Newland Archer had barely settled into his chair at the family dinner table when his sister Janey made her announcement.

"Your cousin the Countess called on Mother while you were away."

Newland’s fork stilled mid-air. Across from him, his mother’s gaze remained fixed on her plate, her expression unreadable. Mrs. Archer, though withdrawn from society, had no desire to be forgotten by it—and Newland suspected she was faintly irritated that he should seem surprised by Ellen Olenska’s visit.

Janey, oblivious to the tension, continued. "She wore a black velvet polonaise with jet buttons and carried the most charming green monkey muff. I’ve never seen her look so fashionable. She arrived alone on Sunday afternoon—thankfully, we had the drawing-room fire lit. And she had one of those new card-cases." A pause. "She said she wished to know us better because you’d been so kind to her."

Newland laughed softly. "That’s Ellen’s way—she always speaks warmly of her friends."

"She seemed truly glad to be here," Mrs. Archer added, though her lips tightened slightly.

"I hope you liked her, Mother."

"She is certainly... *effortless* in her charm," Mrs. Archer conceded.

Janey leaned forward, eyes bright with mischief. "Mother thinks she’s too polished to be sincere."

Mrs. Archer sighed. "I’m old-fashioned, dear. May Welland is my ideal."

Newland’s smile was wry. "They are nothing alike."

---

Days later, Newland paid a visit to old Mrs. Mingott, who received him with unusual enthusiasm. She was grateful he had persuaded Ellen to abandon thoughts of divorce—and when he confessed he’d fled to St. Augustine without leave simply to see May, the old woman cackled, patting his knee with her plump hand.

"So you broke free, did you? And I suppose the Wellands were scandalized? But May—she understood, didn’t she?"

Newland exhaled. "Not entirely. She refused what I went there to ask."

Mrs. Mingott’s eyes gleamed. "And what was that?"

"That we marry in April. Why waste another year?"

She mimed shock, her laughter rich with amusement. "Ah, the classic ‘Ask Mamma.’ These Mingotts—all the same! So afraid of breaking tradition. The only one with any spirit is my Ellen." She studied him, suddenly sly. "Why *didn’t* you marry her?"

Newland chuckled. "For one thing, she wasn’t here to marry."

"A pity," Mrs. Mingott mused, her tone chillingly dismissive. "Now it’s too late. Her life is over."

The words struck like ice. Before he could reply, the portières parted—and there stood Ellen, radiant, her hand outstretched to him.

"Granny’s been interrogating you, I see," she said, smiling.

Mrs. Mingott cackled. "I asked him why he didn’t marry you!"

Ellen’s gaze held Newland’s. "And what did he say?"

"Oh, I’ll let you tease that out of him," the old woman said. "He’s just back from chasing his sweetheart."

Ellen’s expression didn’t waver. "I know. I went to see your mother to ask where you’d gone. You never answered my note."

He muttered an excuse—unexpected departure, meant to write—but she only laughed, light and unreadable.

*If she needs me, she won’t show it*, he thought bitterly.

Mrs. Mingott, oblivious, prattled on about impetuous love, but Newland barely heard. Ellen’s eyes never left him, thoughtful, probing.

When he rose to leave, she walked him to the door.

"Come see me soon," she murmured. "I’m moving next week."

The thought of her leaving the little house—their sanctuary—pierced him. "Tomorrow evening?"

She nodded. "Early. I’m going out."

*Mrs. Struthers’s*, he realized. Where Beaufort would be.

"Tomorrow, then," he agreed, already resolved to arrive late—too late to accompany her.

---

Yet by half-past eight, restless, he stood at her door. Inside, he found not solitude but a gathering: an eccentric marchioness, a shabby philosopher, and a towering mystic—all admiring an extravagant bouquet of crimson roses on Ellen’s sofa.

"Ah, Mr. Archer!" the marchioness trilled. "We were just admiring these *marvelous* flowers. Such sentiment!"

As the odd assembly departed, the marchioness detained him, her voice dropping to a whisper.

"The Count wants her back."

Newland recoiled. "You can’t be serious."

She pressed a letter into his hands. "He begs forgiveness. Think what she abandons—gardens, jewels, art! And yet..." Her gaze flicked to the roses. "Do you prefer *that*, Mr. Archer? Marriage is marriage, after all."

Before he could respond, footsteps sounded. Ellen appeared—and the marchioness’s final words hung between them like a challenge.
