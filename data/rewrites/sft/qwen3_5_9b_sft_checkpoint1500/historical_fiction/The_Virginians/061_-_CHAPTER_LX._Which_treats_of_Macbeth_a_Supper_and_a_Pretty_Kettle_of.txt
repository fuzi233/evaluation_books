Once the theatrical performances drew to a close, our friends departed for Mr. Warrington's lodging, where the Virginians had arranged a sumptuous supper. Mr. Warrington, eager to honor them in the finest style, extended an invitation that the General and his wife gladly accepted, delighted at the prospect of making their young friends happy. The gathering comprised the General and Mrs. Lambert, their collegiate son, their two radiant daughters, and Mr. Spencer of the Temple—a newfound acquaintance George had met at the coffee-house—and all shared the landlady's fare with high spirits. While the precise arrangement of seats remains somewhat elusive, it is evident that Miss Theo sat beside the chickens, while Mr. George Warrington found himself opposite Miss Hetty and a substantial ham, both of whom vied for Mr. Harry's attention. Mrs. Lambert likely occupied George's right hand, leaving the central trio of the General, his son, and the Templar to fill the remaining spaces.

Mr. Spencer, having attended the rival theatre previously, had once introduced George to the actors' greenroom. Conversation naturally drifted back to the play, with several members of the party adamant in their delight. "As for what our gentlemen say, sir," Mrs. Lambert interjected to Mr. Spencer, "you must not credit a word of it. 'Tis a masterpiece, yet my husband and Mr. George conducted themselves abysmally." "We laughed at the wrong moments and wept when silence was required," the General admitted candidly. "You caused the spectators in the boxes to shout 'Hush!' and the pit to demand 'Silence!' I have never witnessed such behavior, and I blush for you, Mr. Lambert!" "Mother considered it a tragedy, whereas we viewed it merely as amusement," the General explained. "George and I acted perfectly, did we not, Theo?" "Not when I was looking your way, Papa!" Theo replied. The General retorted, "Was there ever such a saucy creature?" To which Theo demurred, "You know, sir, I spoke only when prompted. I was deeply moved by the play, particularly by Mrs. Woffington's beauty and acting. I felt sorrow for the mother losing her child, and I am sorry, Papa, if I erred in feeling so!" "Women lack the cunning of men, you know, Theo," Hetty chimed in from across the table, glancing slyly at Harry. "Next time we attend a play, Brother Jack, please pinch us when we ought to weep or nudge us when laughter is due." "I wish we had seen the duel," the General mused. "The clash between little Norval and the towering Norwegian would have been exquisite sport. You should write to Jack and propose it to Mr. Rich, the manager." "I have not seen that scene," Harry said solemnly, wondering if his comment carried wit amidst the general mirth. "However, I saw Slack and Broughton at Marybone Gardens!" he added. "It requires no giant," Harry continued, "to topple that diminutive figure in red boots. For my part, I could hoist him over my shoulder." "Mr. Garrick is small, yet he possesses a presence that commands awe," Mr. Spencer noted. "Recall his portrayal of Macbeth! That dagger scene was terrifying! You should have observed our host, ladies! I introduced Mr. Warrington to Mr. Garrick and Mrs. Pritchard in the greenroom, and Lady Macbeth graciously accepted a pinch from his snuffbox." "Did the Wife of the Thane of Cawdor sneeze?" the General inquired with dramatic gravity. "She thanked Mr. Warrington in a voice so hollow and tragic that he recoiled, spilling his rappee, for Macbeth sneezed three times." "Macbeth! Macbeth! Macbeth!" cried the General. "And the philosopher standing near Mr. Johnson declared, 'Mind, Davy, lest thy sneeze awaken Duncan!' who, coincidentally, was conversing with the three witches seated against the wall." "Have you been behind the scenes?" Theo exclaimed, eyes wide with longing. "To see the ropes, smell the tallow, gaze upon the pasteboard gold and tinsel jewels, and behold the painted matrons?" "Do not look too closely," warned the skeptical host, sipping his hock. "You were angry with your father and me." "Nay, George!" the girl protested. "Nay? I say yes! You angered us because we laughed when you sought to weep. Speaking for myself and others," George bowed to the General, "we refrained from tears not out of unfeeling hearts, but because we stood behind the author's curtain. Up close, we saw through the young hero's rant and tinsel; the pale mother's pallor was mere chalk, her grief nothing more than a pocket-handkerchief. Admit it, Theo, you deemed me unfeeling?" "If you deduce it without confession, what purpose serves my admission?" Theo countered. "Suppose I were to perish," George ventured, "and saw Harry grieve. Then you would witness genuine affliction, a true tragedy, and mourn accordingly. Yet you would remain unmoved if you saw the undertaker in his weeds and black cloak!" "Indeed, but I should," Mrs. Lambert assured, "as would any daughter of mine." "Perhaps we might find weepers of our own, Mr. Warrington," Theo suggested, "in such a dire event." "Would you?" George cried, his cheeks flushing alongside Theo's, both aware of Hetty's watchful gaze. "The older writers grasped little of the pathos," Mr. Spencer, the wit of the Temple, observed. "Consider Sophocles and Antigone," John Lambert called out. "Faith, our wits ignore him save when an Oxford scholar reminds us!" Spencer dismissed the ancients, preferring the moderns. "Shakespeare, however, fails to capture the elegance and pathos of contemporary dramatists. Has he ever matched Belvidera, Monimia, or Jane Shore? Can his comic females rival Congreve?" As he distributed snuff, someone queried if Spencer himself had penned a play. "Many idle gentlemen have tried," Spencer conceded. "Mr. Garrick returned a piece of mine." "I confess I possess four acts of a play in one of my boxes," George revealed. "I wager it rivals the best," Harry whispered to his neighbor. "Is it a tragedy or comedy?" Mrs. Lambert asked. "A tragedy, rife with two or three grisly murders!" George replied. "Let us stage it and have the audience watch their steps! Though my favorite genre remains tyranny," the General declared. "The tragedy! Fetch it instantly, Gumbo!" Mrs. Lambert commanded the footman. Gumbo bowed, confirming the request. "In the large cowhide trunk," George instructed gravely. "Yes, sir," Gumbo responded with elevated formality. "But my tragedy lies buried beneath layers of linen, packages, books, and boots, Hetty." "Fret not; retrieve it and cast the linens from the window!" Hetty cried. "The trunk resides with our agent in Bristol," George announced. "Gumbo must secure post-horses, and we shall await its return until the morrow thereafter." The ladies emitted a comical "Oh!" while the General, perhaps more seriously, proposed gratitude for their escape. "Let us consider returning home. Our young hosts have treated us nobly. We shall drink a final toast to Madam Esmond Warrington of Castlewood, Virginia. Imagine, boys, finding a tall, handsome stepfather upon your return! Ladies of her age have long since remarried." "To Madam Esmond Warrington, my old schoolmate!" Mrs. Lambert toasted. "I shall pen a letter detailing this splendid supper her sons bestowed upon us, and, Mr. George, I shall omit mention of your playhouse misbehavior!" With this, the company dispersed; the General's coach, accompanied by a servant holding a flambeau, awaited his family.

Following such an illustrious entertainment, it seemed natural for Mr. Warrington to visit his guests, ensuring their safe arrival and rest. Their coach could easily have traversed the countryside behind Montague House toward Oxford Road, potentially falling prey to highwaymen, or the ladies might have succumbed to chills or restless sleep following the emotional exertion. Thus, no excuse existed for his inquiry, nor for delaying his departure from the Sloane Museum. Perhaps, as he strolled away, his thoughts wandered, remembering little of what he had read.

"What signifies this eagerness, this hesitation, this shilly-shallying?" the narrator pondered, observing Warrington's trembling hand as he knocked at Mr. Lambert's lodgings in Dean Street. "Does any young man read? Does any aged soul recall the vigor of youth? Time has passed, yet memory endures. Like Shakespeare's Harry promising survival to his soldiers at Agincourt, we may, even now, gallop briefly in the paddock, kicking up our old heels before being halted. I care less for clowns and Pantaloons now, nor for the fairy whose verses I find tedious; yet I delight in witnessing children at a pantomime. I do not dance or dine, but I love to watch Eugenio and Flirtilla twirl, or Lucinda and Ardentio crack a cracker. Burn your little fingers, children!
