So the mighty Empress was jealous of a mere prisoner—woman though she was.

In an instant, my mind, trained to act with soldierly precision under pressure, had decided what I must do. I was sacrificing myself for Atlantis, as ordered by the High Council of Priests. If necessary, Nais would be sacrificed too—but even as that thought crossed my mind, another plan formed: one that might save her.

I bowed low before Phorenice and said, “In this, and in all matters where human hands can act, I will fulfill your wishes, Phorenice.” She patted my arm, and a surge of emotion rose from the depths of her eyes—eyes so full of fire and mystery. Truly, the gods had granted her half her victories when they gave her beauty, voice, and the shape of her face.

By then, merchants and others near the royal quay had gathered, offering dry clothes and shelter. Though I cared little for my wet coat, the area we’d been tossed into by the mammoth was anything but pleasant. I followed her lead without word or glance toward Nais. But Phorenice wasn’t satisfied with such a small gesture. Before leaving, she gave strict orders to the port-captain to guard his prisoner well.

At the house where I was taken, I bathed and exchanged my damp garments for the plainest robe available. The host was delighted at the bargain. Still, I had an hour to wait outside on the pavement, listening to distant bombardments, before Phorenice returned. I couldn’t help smirking at the merchant who trailed behind her—clearly ruined. He’d owned a collection of priceless jewels, bought in partnership with others. Phorenice had taken them all, adorning herself with them.

“Are they not lovely?” she asked, showing me her new treasures. “Look how they sparkle in the sun. I’m glad now, Deucalion, that the mammoth threw us about. It brought me such a fine gift. Tell him I’ll return when he’s earned more—and perhaps I’ll dine with him again. Ah! Litters have arrived. Send one away, and share mine with me. We’ll pretend to be lovers today, even if true love comes easier tomorrow. No, don’t summon more bearers—my slaves will carry us well enough. I’m glad you’re not one of those overfed, clumsy men, Deucalion. I’m slight and quick, and I don’t want to be overshadowed.”

“Back to the royal pyramid?” I asked.

“No,” she replied. “Not there, nor to the walls. I don’t wish to fight or rule today. As I’ve told you, I want to be Phorenice, the maiden, for a few hours. If someone I know were to court me now—like any other girl—I’d call it a pleasure. Have the slaves carry us along the edge of the harbor. And tell these gawkers—if they follow, I’ll rain fire upon them, just as I did with the sacrifice.”

I knew something of the chaos in the streets. That night at the royal banquet, the gate officer had hinted at unrest. But since Phorenice already knew, I didn’t remind her. I dismissed the guards and shooed away the crowd of sightseers, those foolish creatures always drawn to watch their superiors. Then I sat in the litter where invited, and the bearers lifted the pole.

They carried us along the wide walkway between merchant houses, fisher huts, and the rippling waters of the harbor. I glanced sadly at the few ships still moored in the grand basin, and the sparse signs of trade on the docks. Once, ships crowded so tightly that many waited in the estuary beyond the walls. Petitions had gone to the King asking to double the port’s size. Even in those days of oar and sail, solar-powered machines were rare—only used occasionally to speed vessels.

An Egypt voyage took a year then—now, just two months. Of every three ships that set out, at least one never returned, lost to sea beasts or storms. Yet trade once thrived, growing ever stronger. Merchants were stout and generous to the gods. Now, war consumed everything. Wealthy traders abandoned goods, calling bargaining indecent, adopting courtly airs, spending days choosing fabrics and nights feasting.

The lesser merchants, without savings, were crushed by taxes and war. With markets scarce and no exports, industry collapsed. A weaver drafted into fire-tube duty made no cloth; a farmer rebelling didn’t buy slaves. Just a month before my return, a magnificent cargo of slaves had arrived from Europe—only to be released across the estuary, left to fend for themselves. No Atlantean wanted another slave, even as a gift.

Though I mourned this decline, I kept silent. Speaking of it now would only sour our ride. I was learning Phorenice’s moods. And she, in turn, seemed to read mine, trying to hold my interest between her playful fits.

We reached the westernmost harbor wall, where the bearers set us down. She led me to a stone house perched on a promontory, constantly lashed by tide. After much unlocking, she showed me her gold collectors.

At the Sacred Mountain colleges, we’d learned that gold existed dissolved in seawater—but no method had ever been devised to extract it. Yet here, this self-made Empress had stumbled upon the secret as if it were written in a book.

She laughed, patting my arm as I stared. “I tell everyone in Atlantis that only the gods know this secret—and that they gave it to me,” she said. “But I’m no goddess to you, am I, Deucalion? And honestly, I can’t explain how I came up with it. Let’s say I dreamed it. See? Seawater flows in through that culvert, passes over rough metal plates, and exits naturally. The gold collects in the ridges. My men melt it into bars and take it to my smiths. Tides vary with the moon—you priests know that. Simple math tells how much water passes and how much gold is captured. At first, my workers tried to cheat. I dragged two behind a galley until cannibal fish devoured them. Since then, they’ve believed in… well, what do you suppose?”

“More divinity.”

“I suppose so. But I’m letting you see how it works. Do the math. You could become a god among these Atlanteans, Deucalion—just by using your wit.”

Was she mocking me? Or serious? Sometimes she claimed her triumphs came from intelligence alone; the next moment, she offered impossible explanations, forcing me to believe she was divine. This woman—clever, agile, beautiful—mystified me more each day. And the more I stayed near her, the less hope I felt that Atlantis could ever be restored to peace and the old gods—even after I married her and fulfilled the Council’s decree.

Only one path seemed possible: end her mischief with death and let the land recover on its own. It would be a single stroke—a dagger, and done. Yet the very thought revolted me. When it surfaced, I clung to the idea that if such an act were truly meant, the High Gods would have given me clear command.

Still, Phorenice held a strange power. When we left the gold house and stumbled into danger—when a single hesitation would have doomed her—I found myself fighting beside her, as fiercely as I ever had. Yes, battle lust stirs even the coldest hearts when blows fly and cries ring out. But protecting her, refusing to see her torn apart by these brutish fishers—those acts added strength to every strike.

The cause was the unrest I’d mentioned—the desperate poverty driving people to risk everything for a meal. We remounted the litter, bearers swinging forward. Phorenice spoke of her gold supply, of new laws.

“In the old days,” she said, “gold was painstakingly dredged from riverbeds in dangerous lands. A featherful cost a rich man’s fortune. Only nobles wore gold. But when the sea gives me gold by handfuls daily, the price dropped. One day—can you believe it?—a common smith came to me wearing a gold collar. I had his neck severed. I promised the same fate to any who dared repeat it. For now, the madness seems halted. Governing common folk has its trials, Deucalion.”

She went on, but then I saw two crude fishing boats paddling toward us. From narrow lanes between huts, fierce-faced men began running after the litter in threatening groups.

“With your leave,” I said, “I’ll step out and disperse them.”

“Oh, they always gather around me,” she said lightly. “Poor ugly souls—they seem to enjoy staring at my beauty. But scatter them. I said I didn’t want to be followed. I’m on holiday, Deucalion, while you learn to court me.”

I stepped down. The fishers in boats shouted warnings to those hiding in the huts. Onshore, numbers grew fast. We’d passed the merchants’ quarter, now level with mud-and-grass huts—the fishermen’s domain, always prone to violence. Even in the old King’s peaceful reign, riots broke out weekly here.

Fishing is the hardest life imaginable. Storms, firestones from the sky, sea beasts—these are daily threats. Each fisherman trains a large seabird from youth. A bronze ring prevents it from swallowing caught fish. For each catch, the bird gets head, tail, and innards—while the ring is removed during its meal.

These birds are loyal once trained, rarely deserting owners. Yet they’re hunted like prey, and often die suddenly. If both a fisher’s birds die, he loses his livelihood. His guild sees this as divine judgment—Gods rejecting him. So they abandon him. He’s left to starve or steal.

Such hardships make fishers ruthless, reckless men—driven into the trade because all else rejected them. They spend months’ earnings in a single night’s binge, fearing tomorrow may take their lives. And honor demands they defy fear, no matter the cost.

I moved quickly toward the largest group lurking behind huts, leaving the litter behind. “Disperse!” I commanded. “Clean your lanes, repair your homes. Tomorrow I’ll inspect. But today, the Empress desires privacy. Cease your stares!”

“Then give us money!” shrieked a voice from the huts.

“I’ll send a torch and gallows in an hour,” I growled. “To your dens, you dogs!”

They might have obeyed—had they not heard the boatmen shouting. The two hide-covered boats paddled into the surf, men leaping ashore, abandoning their fragile craft to the waves.

From huts and filthy alleys, crowds poured out—men and women alike—surrounding us menacingly.

“Beware,” warned one on the edge. “She called down fire on the sacrifice today. She can burn us all.”

“So much the better,” retorted another. “She can’t burn everyone.”

“Nay, I won’t burn anyone,” Phorenice declared. “But you’ll see my sword-play until you’re tired.”

I heard her with a flicker of amusement—knowing, as one of the Seven, that she had summoned skyfire to destroy a sacrificial beast. I also knew she couldn’t summon fire now. But they gave us no time for words.

Her throne had never been guarded far. They feared an escort might arrive. So they struck fast.

They surged forward, jostling to reach her. Their stench made us gag. A grimy hand lunged for her jewels. I sliced it off at the elbow. It fell at her feet. Moments later, we were locked in combat.

“Back to mine, comrade!” she laughed, drawing her sword and spinning with deadly grace. Bah—it was slaughter from the start.

They rushed in so greedily that none could retreat. We carved a circle with whirling steel. In such fights, wounded left unattended become deadly traps. Yet every foe Phorenice struck lay still. By the gods—she fought like a queen. Every thrust touched life.

Now it was clear: her throne wasn’t won by beauty alone. And as a warrior, I respected her more for it. I glimpsed her only in flashes—her movements swift, precise. The fishers, though clumsy and poorly armed, had numbers and savage fury. Their confidence made it hard to hold ground. Had I not snatched a shield from a fool who charged too boldly, I might have been pulled down by sheer force.

Above the din, her cry rang out: “Deucalion!”—her battle shout. I knew her flair for flattery. Hearing my name roared above chaos was nothing new. But this time, under such circumstances, I couldn’t help but feel flattered.

Condemn me if you must, but I nearly liked her—on her own terms. And Nais? Her memory faded entirely in the heat of battle. Such is our frailty—even the strongest among us.

Few born to the sword resist the urge to fight for the sake of fighting. And it seems women can catch the same fever. The attack slowed. We stood in a ring of fallen foes, the rest backing away.

This was the moment for mercy. A few sharp words could have sent them fleeing. But no—Phorenice must sing a hymn to our swords, gloating over our skill. Then she demanded payment for the slain bearers. She threatened fire, skinning, and dismemberment for the entire quarter—until they learned manners.

“It makes your skin crawl, doesn’t it?” she asked. “I understand. Severity has its cost. But why don’t you stop me? Either you think yourselves gods—or you believe me no goddess. Otherwise, you wouldn’t have come this far. Come now, you foul-smelling rabble—follow your theory. If you fight well, I swear by my face, I’ll spare those who survive.”
