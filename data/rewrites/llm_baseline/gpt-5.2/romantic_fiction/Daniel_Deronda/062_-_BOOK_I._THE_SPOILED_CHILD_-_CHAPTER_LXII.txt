Something Mirah had begun to watch for—as one listens for a familiar footfall in a house that has known both feasting and famine—came upon her again that morning, not like a single pang but like a sorrow returning with trunks and bundles, prepared to lodge.

She was turning out of Knightsbridge after singing at a charitable morning concert in a wealthy home, where Klesmer’s recommendation had won her the invitation and where the air still seemed sweet with violets and hot-house blossoms, mixed with the faint animal warmth of fur collars and the sharper tang of carriage-grease waiting at the curb. Outside, there had been the usual little crowd to stare at departing company, their voices a restless tide against the clatter of wheels. Mirah, in her simple black concert dress with a dust-cloak thrown over it, was not dressed to provoke notice. But it was not the eyes of strangers that tightened her breath: it was a sound.

Footsteps.

They kept an even pace with her own, neither hurrying nor lagging, as if they had learned her rhythm and meant to make it theirs. London’s din had its own ecology—iron rims on stone, the puff and hiss of horses, the barking of a newsboy—yet this steady accompaniment seemed to stand out as a single, obstinate note in the whole city’s chord, and it made her blood go cold. She felt dogged, and instantly, without looking, she knew whom she feared.

Her father.

The thought was so vivid that to turn her head felt like turning to meet a ghost. If it was he, he would claim her—claim recognition—force her to that encounter whose rush of shame and pity had been her nightly nightmare. She walked on, not quickening her pace—what was speed against fate?—and pictured what must happen as if certainty marched behind her. With the picture came a sting of regret: she had given her word to Mrs. Meyrick that she would use no concealment about him. It was that promise, bitter as an herb that nevertheless saves from poison, which urged her at last to do what she most dreaded. Better to choose the moment than to be seized by it.

She had reached the entrance of the small square where her home lay and had made up her mind to turn, when the embodied presentiment came closer, slid to her side, caught her wrist, and said, with that persuasive curl of accent which always tried to sweeten command into affection—

“Mirah!”

She paused without starting. It was the voice she expected, and the eyes were the eyes she expected. Her face was grave, as if she were looking at her executioner. His was arranged for soothing and propitiation. Once he had been handsome, bright with color; now he was sallow and deep-lined, wearing that impudent suavity which comes from courting favor while accepting disrespect. He was lightly made and active, with something of youth about him that made the signs of age seem almost like a disguise; and in truth he was hardly fifty-seven. His clothes were shabby as ever, and yet he carried his shabbiness with a jaunty air—the bushy grizzled curls, the small hands and feet, the light walk that gave him the look of a man who would always contrive a stage for himself.

His presence struck Mirah more keenly than before with the mingled anguish of shame and grief, repulsion and pity—more keenly now that she lived among people to whom he would be only a foreign, broken-down adventurer. Slowly, in a sad, trembling voice, she said,

“It is you, father.”

“Why did you run away from me, child?” he began at once, rapid and practiced, as if he were pouring out a speech he had rehearsed on lonely pavements. His quick gestures flickered like an abbreviated finger-language. “What were you afraid of? You knew I never made you do anything against your will. It was for your sake I broke up your engagement in the Vorstadt, because I saw it didn’t suit you, and you repaid me by leaving me to the bad times that came in consequence. I had made an easier engagement for you at the Vorstadt Theater in Dresden—I didn’t tell you, because I wanted to take you by surprise. And you left me planted there—obliged to make myself scarce because I had broken contract. That was hard lines for me, after I had given up everything for the sake of getting you an education which was to be a fortune to you. What father devoted himself to his daughter more than I did to you? You know how I bore that disappointment in your voice, and made the best of it: and when I had nobody besides you, and was getting broken, as a man must who has had to fight his way with his brains—you chose that time to leave me. Who else was it you owed everything to, if not to me? and where was your feeling in return? For what my daughter cared, I might have died in a ditch.”

He stopped, not from lack of invention but because he had reached a pathetic summit; and he sobbed suddenly, like a woman’s sob, pulling out an old yellow silk handkerchief. He truly felt himself ill-used: unscrupulous people keep a clear account of what is owing to them, with no reckoning on the other side.

Mirah, though the sob moved her by old habit into pity, had strength enough not to let him suppose she was deceived. For the first time in her life she forced accusing words through her filial tenderness.

“You know why I left you, father; and I had reason to distrust you, because I felt sure that you had deceived my mother. If I could have trusted you, I would have stayed with you and worked for you.”

“I never meant to deceive your mother, Mirah,” he said, putting back the handkerchief, and speaking in a voice that struggled as if more sobs would come to help his story. “I meant to take you back to her, but chances hindered me just at the time, and then there came information of her death. It was better for you that I should stay where I was, and your brother could take care of himself. Nobody had any claim on me but you. I had word of your mother’s death from a particular friend, who had undertaken to manage things for me, and I sent him over money to pay expenses. There’s one chance to be sure—he may have written me lies for the sake of getting the money out of me.”

Mirah could not bear to utter the only true answer—*I don’t believe you.* She only showed, by the direction of her steps, that she wished them to go on. To stand still here, where servants and coachmen and passing eyes made a constant little theatre, was unbearable. Even walking together, they were a spectacle: her quiet English-lady dress setting off her beauty like a plain dark frame, and beside her this shabby, eager, foreign-looking man, with his restlessness and his jaunty air, who made the square seem suddenly narrower, as if the city itself drew closer to listen.

“You seem to have done well for yourself, Mirah? *You* are in no want, I see,” he said, examining her with emphasis.

“Good friends who found me in distress have helped me to get work,” she answered, scarcely knowing what she said for the crowding of what she must say. “I give lessons. I have sung in private houses. I have just been singing at a private concert.” Then, with a meaning she wanted him to feel, she added, “I have very good friends, who know all about me.”

“And you would be ashamed they should see your father in this plight? No wonder. I came to England with no prospect, but the chance of finding you. It was a mad quest; but a father’s heart is superstitious—feels a loadstone drawing it somewhere or other. I might have done very well, staying abroad: when I hadn’t you to take care of, I could have rolled or settled as easily as a ball; but it’s hard being lonely in the world, when your spirit’s beginning to break. And I thought my little Mirah would repent leaving her father when she came to look back. I’ve had a sharp pinch to work my way; I don’t know what I shall come down to next. Talents like mine are no use in this country. When a man’s getting out at elbows nobody will believe in him. I couldn’t get any decent employ with my appearance. I’ve been obliged to get pretty low for a shilling already.”

London’s sounds seemed to take the words and make a rough chorus of them—the rattle of a cart, the rasp of a saw from some distant yard—no soft shelter anywhere, only the city grinding on. Mirah’s imagination was quick, and it showed her her father sinking to further degradation, each step downward like a stain spreading on cloth that cannot be washed clean.

Before she could reply to his glib string of sentences, he added, sharp as a knife laid suddenly on the table—

“Where do you live, Mirah?”

“Here, in this square. We are not far from the house.”

“In lodgings?”

“Yes.”

“Any one to take care of you?”

“Yes,” said Mirah again, and turned her face fully toward him, forcing herself to meet the keen look in his eyes. “My brother.”

His eyelids fluttered as if lightning had passed across them; his shoulders moved slightly. Then, after a pause just long enough for calculation, he said—

“Ezra? How did you know—how did you find him?”

“That would take long to tell. Here we are at the door. My brother would not wish me to close it on you.”

She was already on the step, but turned toward him as he stood below on the pavement. Her heart began to beat faster with the prospect of Ezra’s presence meeting this man—this father whose mere nearness could make Mirah feel the “stabbed heart of reverence,” the shame that comes because one has loved and cannot wholly stop loving. And now, even in the attitude of giving leave to the father she had been used to obey, seeing him there below her with a perceptible shrinking from the admission he had almost demanded, she felt that pang of sympathetic humiliation which belongs to an intensely filial nature.

“Stay a minute, *Liebchen*,” Lapidoth said, lowering his voice. “What sort of man has Ezra turned out?”

“A good man—a wonderful man,” Mirah answered with slow emphasis, struggling to master the agitation that made her voice tremble more as she went on. She felt urged—almost as if by a stern inward guardian—to prepare her father for the complete penetration of himself that awaited him. “But he was very poor when my friends found him for me—a poor workman. Once—twelve years ago—he was strong and happy, going to the East, which he loved to think of; and my mother called him back because—because she had lost me. And he went to her, and took care of her through great trouble, and worked for her till she died—died in grief. And Ezra, too, had lost his health and strength. The cold had seized him coming back to my mother, because she was forsaken. For years he has been getting weaker—always poor, always working—but full of knowledge, and great-minded. All who come near him honor him. To stand before him is like standing before a prophet of God—falsehoods are no use.”

She dropped her eyes on the last words, unable to endure the ignoble look of frustration gathering in his face. Yet he was quick—quick as a cook who, finding one dish spoiled, turns at once to another and disguises the lack with sauce. His invention never waited for feeling.

“Mirah, *Liebchen*,” he said, with the old caressing tone, “shouldn’t you like me to make myself a little more respectable before my son sees me? If I had a little sum of money, I could fit myself out and come home to you as your father ought, and then I could offer myself for some decent place. With a good shirt and coat on my back, people would be glad enough to have me. I could offer myself for a courier, if I didn’t look like a broken-down mountebank. I should like to be with my children, and forget and forgive. But you have never seen your father look like this before. If you had ten pounds at hand—or I could appoint you to bring it me somewhere—I could fit myself out by the day after to-morrow.”

The temptation struck Mirah like the scent of warm bread to one who has fasted: the instant desire to give, to relieve, to quiet trouble by a simple offering. But with it came the remembered taste of past bitterness—promises broken, lives strained thin as cheap cloth. She forced herself to look at him again.

“I don’t like to deny you what you ask, father; but I have given a promise not to do things for you in secret. It is hard to see you looking needy; but we will bear that for a little while; and then you can have new clothes, and we can pay for them.”

Now she understood, with a practical clearness, the wisdom that had made Mrs. Meyrick exact that promise: secrecy was the old door through which her father always entered and always escaped, leaving shame and confusion behind him like dirty footprints.

Lapidoth’s good humor gave way. A sneer tightened his mouth.

“You are a hard and fast young lady—you have been learning useful virtues—keeping promises not to help your father with a pound or two when you are getting money to dress yourself in silk—your father who made an idol of you, and gave up the best part of his life to providing for you.”

“It seems cruel—I know it seems cruel,” Mirah said, and the moment was to her worse than the hour when she had thought of drowning herself. The blood left her lips; her breath came thin. “But, father, it is more cruel to break the promises people trust in. That broke my mother’s heart—it has broken Ezra’s life. You and I must eat now this bitterness from what has been. Bear it. Bear to come in and be cared for as you are.”

“To-morrow, then,” Lapidoth said, almost turning away, as if her paleness and trembling meant the inconvenient world had taken her side. But he turned back at once, his hands feeling restlessly in his pockets, and his tone sliding again into appeal.

“I’m a little cut up with all this, Mirah. I shall get up my spirits by to-morrow. If you’ve a little money in your pocket, I suppose it isn’t against your promise to give me a trifle—to buy a cigar with.”

Mirah could not ask herself another question. She did what her pity and habit compelled: put her cold trembling hands into her pocket for her *portemonnaie* and held it out. He grasped it at once, pressed her fingers while he took it, and said in the old easy cadence, as if nothing had been torn or altered—

“Good-bye, my little girl—to-morrow then!”

And he left her.

He had not gone many steps before he opened the purse, searching every fold. He found two half-sovereigns, odd silver, and, pasted against the folding cover, a slip of paper on which Ezra had written, in beautiful Hebrew characters, the name of their mother, the dates of her birth, marriage, and death, and the prayer: “May Mirah be delivered from evil.” Mirah liked to have that inscription on many things she used—an intimate memorial clinging to daily habit, as if the dead could keep watch through the very objects her hands touched.

Lapidoth read it and had a quick vision of his marriage day: himself young, bright, unblamed, teaching many things, expecting to make money more easily by writing, very fond of Sara—his beautiful bride—who cried when he expected him to cry, and reflected each phase of his feeling with a mimetic readiness. But he had traveled far from that self; and the memory came without tenderness, as if words about nourishment lay on a tongue that can no longer taste. The shapes were there, the grain, the mere outline of what once had flavor; yet the savor—the compunction, the sweetness of regret—was gone.

The purse itself was handsome—a gift she had been unable to calculate about giving away. Presently his reverie slipped off like a cloak, and he was considering what the purse would fetch in addition to its contents, and what chance there might be of drawing more from his daughter without submitting to any penitential form of life under the eyes of that formidable son. On such a subject his susceptibilities remained lively enough.

Meanwhile Mirah went into the house, her power of reticence broken by pain too sharp to be carried alone. The noises of the street fell behind her; inside there was that quieter sound-world which Ezra guarded like a sanctuary—the faint turning of paper, the soft settling of a book, the breath between thoughts. She found him seated, quietly reading and sifting old manuscripts of his own, those pages he meant to consign to Deronda, as one might sort herbs carefully dried for a future healing.

In the reaction from her long effort to master herself, she sank before him and clasped his knees, sobbing out his name as if it were bread to keep her from starving.

“Ezra, Ezra!”

He did not speak at once. His alarm spent itself in searching for the cause—alarmed the more because this violent outburst was new in her. But Mirah’s longing was to speak, to pour out the reason and be relieved by truth spoken in the presence of truth.

Presently she raised her head, still sobbing, and said brokenly, “Ezra, my father! our father! He followed me. I wanted him to come in. I said you would let him come in. And he said No, he would not—not now, but to-morrow. And he begged for money from me. And I gave him my purse, and he went away.”

To her, the words were misery itself, every syllable salted with shame. To Mordecai, they were less grievous than what his imagination had prepared; and he answered gently, as he laid his hands tenderly on her head and took off her hat with the care of one who knows that even small acts can soothe an anguished spirit—

“Wait for calm, Mirah, and then tell me all.”

In a few minutes, under that quiet influence, she told him as exactly as she could what had passed—the footsteps, the grasp on her wrist, the request, her promise, the purse. Mordecai listened without interruption, his stillness like a deep place where turbulence can fall and lose its noise.

“He will not come to-morrow,” Mordecai said at last.

Neither spoke aloud what both thought: that he might watch for Mirah’s goings-out and beg again, as a hungry man watches a kitchen door.

After a silence, Mordecai added, and his voice had that cadence which made the small Brompton lodging seem for an instant linked to elder rooms where old griefs and hopes had been spoken in another tongue—

“Seest thou, our lot is the lot of Israel. The grief and the glory are mingled as the smoke and the flame. It is because we children have inherited the good that we feel the evil. These things are wedded for us, as our father was wedded to our mother.”

The place was Brompton, the world outside full of iron and commerce and the ceaseless buying and selling of every sound and every art; yet the words might have come from a Rabbi handing down sentences meant to be kept alive when languages perish and memories are worn thin by the dominant speech. “The Omnipresent,” a Rabbi had said, “is occupied in making marriages.” The levity of that saying lived only in the ear that heard it wrongly; for “marriages” meant all the strange joinings in the universe, the binding together of our good and evil, so that even in a plain room where paper rustled and a sister wept, the mingling went on—smoke and flame—inseparable.
