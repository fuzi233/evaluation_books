As I stood on the worn deck of the steamer, the scorching sun beating down relentlessly, I couldn't help but feel a sense of unease wash over me. The desolate landscape of the Soudan stretched out before us like an unforgiving canvas, punctuated only by the skeletal forms of ancient pyramids and the meager strip of greenery clinging to the riverbanks. It was a callous country, inhabited by a people seemingly devoid of compassion or empathy. The stench of decay and neglect hung heavy in the air, a morbid reminder of the countless lives lost to the unforgiving climate and the apathy of those around them.

My gaze drifted down to the native barge lashed alongside, where a lone figure lay shrouded in a black veil on a traditional angareb. The Arabs seated nearby seemed oblivious to their charge, their laughter and chatter carrying on the wind as they lounged in the stern. I felt an inexplicable sense of fascination with the motionless form, my eyes drawn inexorably to the veiled face. Was it a man or a woman? I couldn't tell, but the stillness of the body was unnerving, a stark contrast to the vibrant colors and sounds that filled the air.

As the sun continued its relentless march across the sky, casting a pitiless glare over the angareb, I felt a growing sense of discomfort. The natives seemed indifferent to the suffering of their fellow human being, leaving the angareb exposed to the elements. I called out to the Arabs, my voice carrying across the water, urging them to move the angareb into the shade. They rose reluctantly, their faces expressionless, and complied with my request.

When I asked if it was a man or a woman, one of the Arabs replied matter-of-factly that it was a man, being taken to the hospital at Assouan, but unlikely to survive. I pressed for more information, learning that he had fallen from a palm tree three weeks prior and received no medical attention since. The man's fate was sealed, a tragic consequence of a society that seemed to view life and death as predetermined, inevitable outcomes.

I couldn't shake the image of the angareb and its occupant from my mind, the thought of the man's slow decline into gangrene and despair haunting me. When a German student of medicine took his seat beside me at dinner, I turned to him impulsively, asking if he might be willing to offer his expertise.
