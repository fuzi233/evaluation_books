It wasn't until the following Friday that Sally managed to drag herself away from her duties and head for Detroit. She arrived on Saturday morning, checked into the Hotel Statler, and immediately phoned Gerald's room to summon him. Once she confirmed he was staying there, she marched into the dining room and ordered breakfast, though her stomach was the only thing that seemed to be moving.

Nursing Mr. Faucitt had drained her completely, and the train ride hadn't helped her sleep. But the real weight on her shoulders wasn't fatigue; it was Gerald's voice on the phone. He had sounded listless, as if her return after weeks of absence was merely a minor administrative detail rather than a joyous reunion. It stung.

Coffee, however, is a powerful alchemist. It transformed her gloom into a manageable buzz. "Men are like this in the morning," she muttered to herself, watching the steam rise from her cup. "Once he gets a cold shower, he'll probably be bouncing off the walls." For now, she needed calories.

Just as she poured her second cup, a stout young man entered the dining room, scanning the lobby with the frantic energy of a man searching for a lost sock. Sally recognized him instantly. He looked exactly like her brother, Fillmore. But Fillmore lived in New York. Was he flitting westward like a butterfly on a caffeine binge?

"Fillmore!" she called out.

He froze in the doorway, looking left, right, and everywhere except toward her table. Then he spotted her and lumbered over, his face a mask of nervous embarrassment.

"Why, Sally?" he stammered. "I thought you were in Europe."

"I got back a week ago," she lied smoothly, "but I've been nursing poor old Mr. Faucitt. I'm here to see Mr. Foster's play, *The Primrose Way*. Is it a hit?"

"It hasn't opened yet," Fillmore said, his voice tight.

"Don't be absurd, Fill. It opened last Monday."

"No, it didn't. Haven't you heard? The Spanish flu has closed all the theaters. Nothing's playing this week."

"I haven't had time to read the papers. Oh, Fill, what a shame!"

"Yes, it's tough. Makes the whole company on edge. I've had the darndest time."

"Why are you mixed up in this?"

Fillmore coughed. "Well, er, Cracknell—remember him from college?—suggested I come down to look at it. Maybe he wants me to put some money in."

"I thought he had all the money in the world."

"Yes, he does, but these fellows like to let a pal in on a good thing."

"Is it a good thing?"

"The play's fine."

"That's what Mr. Faucitt said. But Mabel Hobson..."

Fillmore's face crumpled. "She's an awful woman, Sally! She can't act, and she throws her weight around constantly. The other day there was a fuss about a paper-knife..."

"How could a paper-knife cause a fuss?"

"One of the props. It got mislaid. I'm certain it wasn't my fault..."

"Love seems to have the worst effects on your brain, Fill."

"Well, angry woman... blames the first person she sees..." His voice trailed off into a pained silence.

"Mr. Faucitt said Elsa Doland was good."

"Oh, she's all right," Fillmore dismissed. "But the girl you want to watch is Miss Winch. Gladys Winch. She plays the maid. She only appears in the first act and says very little, mostly just 'Did you ring, madam?' But the way she says it! Sally, that girl is a genius! The greatest character actress in a dozen years! You'll see her name in electric lights on Broadway soon. Personality? Ask me! Charm? She wrote the words and music! Looks?..."

"All right, all right! I know all about it, Fill. And will you kindly inform me how you dared to get engaged without consulting me?"

Fillmore blushed a deep crimson. "Oh, do you know?"

"Yes. Mr. Faucitt told me."

"Well..."

"Well?"

"Well, I'm only human," Fillmore argued weakly.

"I call that a very handsome admission. You've got quite modest, Fill."

He had changed since their last meeting. Someone had apparently punctured his ego and let out all the pomposity. If this was due to Miss Winch's influence, Sally approved of the romance.

"I'll introduce you sometime," Fillmore said.

"I want to meet her very much."

"I've got to see Bunbury. I thought he might be in here."

"Who's Bunbury?"

"The producer. I suppose he's breakfasting in his room. I'd better go up."

"You're busy, aren't you? Little marvel! It's lucky they've got you to look after them."

Fillmore retreated, and Sally settled down to wait for Gerald, her earlier hurt replaced by a strange sense of pity for the poor man.

Minutes later, Gerald arrived.

"Oh, Jerry darling," Sally said, masking her irritation. "I'm so sorry. I've just been hearing about it."

Gerald sat down, looking like a deflated balloon wrapped in a gray fog. "It's just my luck," he gloomed. "The kind of thing that couldn't happen to anyone but me. Damned fools! Where's the sense in shutting the theaters, even with the flu? They let people jam against each other all day in the stores. If that doesn't hurt them, why should it hurt them to go to the theater? Besides, it's all infernal nonsense. I don't believe there is such a thing as Spanish influenza. People get colds in their heads and think they're dying. It's a fake scare."

"I don't think it's that," Sally said gently. "Poor Mr. Faucitt had it quite badly. That's why I couldn't come earlier."

Gerald ignored her news about Faucitt and the fact that she had finally arrived. He stabbed a spoon into his grapefruit with somber determination. "We've been hanging about here day after day, bored to death. The company is falling apart. They're sick of rehearsing when nobody knows if we'll ever open. They were keyed up a week ago, and now they're sagging. It will ruin the play. My first chance! Just chucked away."

Sally felt a growing desolation. She tried to be fair, remembering his terrible disappointment, but self-pity was a trait she despised in men. Her own vanity was bruised; her arrival seemed to have done nothing to lift his spirits. She recalled Mr. Faucitt's comments about Gerald. He was remarkably self-centered, thrusting his misery in her face.

"That Hobson woman is beginning to make trouble," Gerald continued, poking at scrambled eggs. "She ought never to have had the part. Elsa Doland could play it a thousand times better. I wrote Elsa in a few lines the other day, and the Hobson woman went right up in the air. You don't know what a star is till you've seen one of these promoted clothes-props from the Follies trying to be one. It took me an hour to talk her round and keep her from throwing up her part."

"Why not let her throw up her part?"

"For heaven's sake, talk sense," Gerald snapped. "Do you suppose that man Cracknell would keep the play on if she wasn't in it? He'd close the show in a second, and where would I be then? You don't seem to realize this is a big chance for me. I'd look a fool throwing it away."

"I see," Sally said shortly. She had never felt so wretched. Foreign travel, she decided, was a mistake. It put you so out of touch with reality. She analyzed her feelings and realized Gerald was trying to have his cake and eat it too. He wanted her sympathy while erecting a barrier against it. He demanded her pity while holding himself aloof. She felt shut out and useless.

"By the way," Gerald added, "I have to keep her jollying along all the time, so for goodness' sake don't go letting it out that we're engaged."

Sally's chin jerked up. "If you find it a handicap being engaged to me..."

"Don't be silly." Gerald took refuge in pathos. "Good God! It's tough! Here am I, worried to death, and you..."

Before he could finish, Sally's mood shifted with the speed of a lightning bolt. A simple, comforting thought altered her entire outlook. She had come off the train tired and gritty, and the world's out-of-jointness was entirely due to the fact that she hadn't had a bath and her hair was a mess. She felt suddenly tranquil. If it was merely her grubby condition that made Gerald seem so different, all was well. She put her hand on his with a quick gesture of penitence.

"I'm so sorry," she said. "I've been a brute, but I do sympathize, really."

"I've had an awful time," mumbled Gerald.

"I know, I know. But you never told me you were glad to see me."

"Of course I'm glad to see you."

"Why didn't you say so, then, you poor fish? And why didn't you ask me if I had enjoyed myself in Europe?"

"Did you enjoy yourself?"

"Yes, except that I missed you so much. There! Now we can consider my lecture on foreign travel finished, and you can go on telling me your troubles."

Gerald accepted the invitation. He spoke at length, though with little variety. It was definitely established in his mind that Providence had invented Spanish influenza solely to wreck his future. But now he seemed less aloof, more open to sympathy. The brief thunderstorm had cleared the air. Sally lost that sense of detachment.

"Well," Gerald said, checking his watch, "I suppose I had better be off."

"Rehearsal?"

"Yes, confound it. It's the only way of getting through the day. Are you coming along?"

"I'll come directly I've unpacked and tidied myself up."

"See you at the theatre, then."

Sally went out and rang for the lift to take her up to her room.

The rehearsal had started by the time she reached the theater. As she entered the dark auditorium, voices came to her with that thin, reedy echo of people talking in an empty building. She sat at the back, and as her eyes adjusted to the gloom, she saw Gerald sitting in the front row beside a man with a bald head fringed with orange hair—Mr. Bunbury, the producer. Dotted about the house were members of the company whose presence wasn't required for the first act.

On stage, Elsa Doland, looking radiant, was playing a scene with a man in a bowler hat. She spoke a line as Sally entered.

"Why, what do you mean, father?"

"Tiddly-omty-om," was the bowler-hatted man's surprising reply. "Tiddly-omty-om... long speech ending in 'find me in the library.' And exit," he said, starting to move.

For the first time, Sally sensed the atmosphere of nerves. Mr. Bunbury, a man of volatile temperament, picked up his walking-stick and flung it violently across the house.

"For God's sake!" Mr. Bunbury shouted.

"Now what?" inquired the bowler-hatted man, pausing halfway across the stage with genuine interest.

"Do speak the lines, Teddy," Gerald snapped. "Don't skip them in that sloppy fashion."

"You don't want me to go over the whole thing?" the man asked, amazed.

"Yes!"

"Not the whole damn thing?" he queried, fighting with incredulity.

"This is a rehearsal," Mr. Bunbury snapped. "If we are not going to do it properly, what's the use of doing it at all?"

This struck the erring Teddy as reasonable, if not entirely logical. He delivered the speech in an injured tone and shuffled off. The atmosphere of tenseness was unmistakable. The world of the theater is simply a large nursery, and its inhabitants are children who readily become fretful if anything goes wrong. The waiting, the uncertainty, the loafing in strange hotels, the dreary rehearsing of lines polished to the last syllable a week ago—these things had sapped the nerve of the *Primrose Way* company, and demoralization had set in. It would require only a trifle to produce an explosion.

Elsa Doland moved to the door, pressed a bell, and sat down near the footlights with a magazine. A moment later, a young woman entered, greeted instantly by an impassioned bellow from Mr. Bunbury.

"Miss Winch!"

The new arrival stopped and looked out over the footlights, not with pain, but with the genial indulgence of someone coming to a juvenile party to amuse the children. She was a square, wholesome, good-humored-looking girl with a serious face, contradicted by a faint smile lurking at the corners of her mouth. She wasn't pretty, and Sally was surprised that Fillmore had the sense to disregard her surface homeliness and recognize her charm. Deep down in Fillmore, Sally decided, there must lurk an unsuspected vein of intelligence.

"Hello?" said Miss Winch, amiably.

"Miss Winch, did I or did I not ask you to refrain from chewing gum during rehearsal?" Mr. Bunbury asked, profoundly moved.

"That's right, so you did," admitted Miss Winch, chummily.

"Then why are you doing it?"

Fillmore's fiancée revolved the criticized refreshment about her tongue before replying. "Bit o' business," she announced.

"What do you mean, a bit of business?"

"Character stuff," explained Miss Winch in her pleasant, drawling voice. "Thought it out myself. Maids chew gum, you know."
