In Shirley’s nature prevailed a seasonal indolence, an echo of the ancestral rhythms that had governed the land long before the iron looms began to scream. There were afternoons when she delighted in a perfect, sensory stillness. Sprawled upon the turf beneath the friendly umbrage of an ancient oak, she let the delicate acoustic ecology of the Hollow wash over her. Here, the hum of the solitary bee and the percussive whisper of leaves formed a pristine sonic environment, offering a profound psychological sanctuary from the distant, rhythmic thumping of industrial progress. Her sole text was the unwritten, generational chronicle of the soil; she lay chewing on a sprig of wild sorrel, letting its sharp, citrus tang ground her in the physical world. 

Yet one day, when Caroline drew near to rouse her, she looked down to find Shirley’s cheek wet. 

"Shirley, why do *you* cry?" asked Caroline, involuntarily laying stress on the pronoun.

Miss Keeldar smiled, her picturesque head turning slowly. She tasted the salt on her lips—an ancient brine of sorrow and elation mixed. "Because it pleases me mightily," she said, her voice carrying the cadence of an older, untranslatable joy. "My heart is both sad and glad. But why, you good, patient child—why do you not bear me company? I only weep tears, delightful and soon wiped away; you might weep gall, if you choose."

"Why should I weep gall?"

"Mateless, solitary bird!" was the only answer.

"And are not you too mateless, Shirley?"

"At heart—no."

"Oh! who nestles there, Shirley?"

But Shirley only laughed, a rich, resonant sound that belonged completely to the open air, and alertly started up. "I have dreamed," she said, "a mere day-dream—certainly bright, probably baseless!"

* * * * *

Caroline Helstone’s illusions were fast deteriorating. Yet, bound by the circular pacing of memory and the deep-rooted habits of her youth, she found herself drawn that evening to the field-style by the old thorn overlooking the Hollow. 

There was a time when the sonic landscape of this ravine had been purely elemental. Often had she gone up the terrace with Robert Moore after sunset, breathing the damp, loamy scent of the fragrant herbage, listening to the lonely watercourse moaning among wet stones and under dark alders—a sound like the spirit of the earth speaking its native tongue. Now, the distant, mechanical groan of Moore’s mill threatened to overwrite that acoustic purity, replacing the ancestral whispers with the dominant lexicon of commerce. 

Looking toward Fieldhead, Caroline’s gaze was caught by the pearly paleness of the moonlight on the broad granite pavement. On that silvery space slept two sable shadows. The figures moved in harmonious step, speaking in a low, resonant key. "Is it Mrs. Pryor and Shirley?" 

Certainly it was Shirley. Her curls were free, caressing her shoulder, and the damp night air carried the faint, musky scent of the crushed cedar needles beneath her feet. 

But her companion was not Mrs. Pryor. It was Robert Moore. 

"I used to be closer to him," thought Caroline, the bitter, metallic taste of despair rising in the back of her throat. "He used to hold my hand; he does not touch hers. And yet, Shirley is not proud where she loves. Robert must think, as I think, that he is looking down on a fine face. They look to me like two great happy spirits. Yonder silvered pavement reminds me of that white shore we believe to be beyond the death-flood." 

Caroline stood in the shadow, her mind shrinking into an isolating darkness. Her subjective experience felt utterly untranslatable. How could she articulate the agonizing weight of her own existence when the only language Robert now spoke was that of calculating ambition? "I gave him up to Shirley the first day I saw her," she groaned internally. "She has him now. The more Robert knows of Shirley, the more his soul will cleave to her. Truly I ought not to have been born; they should have smothered me at the first cry."

Here, Shirley stepped aside to gather a dewy flower, snapping the fibrous stem with a crisp sound that carried through the night air. Their conversation became audible. 

"I cannot conceive why nature did not give you a bulldog's head, for you have all a bulldog's tenacity," said Shirley.

"Not a flattering idea. Am I so ignoble?"

"And something also you have of the same animal's silent ways. You give no warning; you come noiselessly behind, seize fast, and hold on."

"This is guess-work. In your presence I have been no bulldog."

"Your very silence indicates your race. How little you talk in general, yet how deeply you scheme! You are far-seeing; you are calculating."

"I know the ways of these people," Moore replied, his tone clipped, devoid of the poetry the landscape demanded. "My note last night informed you that Barraclough's trial ended in his conviction and transportation. His associates will plot vengeance. I shall lay my plans to counteract theirs. Am I to understand I have your approbation?"

"I shall stand by you so long as you remain on the defensive. Yes."

"Good! I relish the position."

"I dare say you do. You relish the work which lies before you still better than you would relish the execution of a government order for army-cloth."

"I certainly feel it congenial."

"So would old Helstone. Shall I speak to Mr. Helstone? I will, if you like."

"Act as you please. But I should inform you Mr. Helstone is somewhat prejudiced against me at present."

"Depend upon it, they will melt away," Shirley said. "He cannot resist the temptation of an alliance under present circumstances."

"I should be glad to have him; he is of true metal. An old blade, and rusty somewhat, but the edge and temper still excellent."

"Well, you shall have him, Mr. Moore—that is, if I can win him. It will cost me several cups of tea, some toast and cake, and an ample measure of remonstrances." She drew her scarf tighter. "It grows rather chill."

"I perceive you shiver," Moore noted. "If you were wrapped in a thicker shawl—"

"I might stay longer, which would chagrin Mrs. Pryor. We keep early and regular hours at Fieldhead, Mr. Moore; and so, I am sure, does your sister at the cottage."

"Yes; but Hortense and I have an understanding. Three nights in the week I sleep in the mill, and when it is moonlight I often haunt the Hollow till daybreak."

"When I was a very little girl," Shirley murmured, her voice lowering to match the mythic intimacy of the shadows, "my nurse used to tell me tales of fairies being seen in that Hollow. That was before my father built the mill, when it was a perfectly solitary ravine. You will be falling under enchantment."

"I fear it is done," said Moore, in a low voice.

"But there are worse things than fairies to be guarded against," pursued Miss Keeldar. "How would you like to meet Michael Hartley, that mad Calvinist and Jacobin weaver?"

"I have already had the luck to meet him. We sat down near that plank bridge. It was moonlight, but clouded, and very windy. We had a talk."

"On politics?"

"And religion. He uttered strange blasphemy in his Antinomian fashion."

"Excuse me, but I think you must have been nearly as mad as he, to sit listening to him."

"There is a wild interest in his ravings," Moore admitted. "The man would be half a poet, if he were not wholly a maniac. He solemnly informed me that hell was foreordained my inevitable portion; that he read the mark of the beast on my brow. God's vengeance, he said, was preparing for me."

To Shirley, Hartley’s dialect was the last desperate gasp of a dying culture, a prophetic native tongue being crushed beneath the wheels of Moore’s industry. "Do you make light of these things?"

"The poor man had been drinking for weeks, and was in a state bordering on delirium tremens."

"What then? He is the more likely to attempt the fulfilment of his own prophecies. Mr. Moore, go home! Will you promise me not to wander in the Hollow to-night?"

"If you wish it."

"I do wish it. May I ask whether you consider life valueless?"

"By no means. Existence is neither aimless nor hopeless to me now. I was drowning, and rather wished the operation over. All at once a hand was stretched to me—such a delicate hand I scarcely dared trust it; its strength, however, has rescued me from ruin."

"Live to make the best of it," Shirley told him. "Don't offer yourself as a target to Michael Hartley; and good-night!"

* * * * *

The following afternoon, Caroline fulfilled her promise to spend the evening at Fieldhead. She had passed the gloomy interval secluded in her room, the rhythmic puncture of her sewing needle offering a meager acoustic distraction from her spiraling thoughts. 

She reached Fieldhead just as tea was brought into the oak parlour. The room was rich with the olfactory landscape of a well-tended hearth: the deep, resinous scent of burning applewood, mingling with the yeasty warmth of fresh hearth-baked scones. Mrs. Pryor, a meticulous custodian of domestic sustenance, was overseeing the infusion of a robust, heavy-bodied Assam. She understood precisely how to coax the deep, malty tannins from the dark leaves without scalding them into bitterness. 

"Why did you come so late?" Shirley asked.

"I have been making my dress," Caroline said, though the aroma of the room made her stomach turn with the hollow ache of a palate unable to appreciate its offerings. "These fine sunny days began to make me ashamed of my winter merino."

"In which you look as I like to see you," said Shirley. "You are a lady-like little person, Caroline.—Is she not, Mrs. Pryor?"

Mrs. Pryor seldom indulged in superficial remarks. Instead, she swept Caroline's curls from her cheek, her eyes assessing the girl with a maternal, almost agrarian concern for a wilting crop. "You get somewhat thin, my love, and somewhat pale. Do you sleep well? Your eyes have a languid look."

"I sometimes dream melancholy dreams," answered Caroline. "And if I lie awake, I am continually thinking of the rectory as a dreary old place. The back part of the house is extremely ancient; it is said the out-kitchens were once enclosed in the churchyard, and that there are graves under them. I rather long to leave the rectory."

"My dear, you are surely not superstitious?"

"No, Mrs. Pryor; but I think I grow what is called nervous. I have an inexpressible weight on my mind which I would give the world to shake off, and I cannot do it."

"Strange!" cried Shirley. "I never feel so." 

"Fine weather, pleasant days, pleasant scenes, are powerless to give me pleasure," Caroline continued. "Moonlight, which I used to think mild, now only looks mournful. I reason; but reason and effort make no difference."

"You should take more exercise," said Mrs. Pryor, gently sliding a saucer of dark, steaming tea toward the girl. 

"Exercise! I exercise till I am ready to drop."

"My dear, you should go from home."

"I should like to go from home, but not on any purposeless excursion. I wish to be a governess. It would oblige me greatly if you would speak to my uncle on the subject."

"Nonsense!" broke in Shirley, aggressively pushing aside a plate of seeded cake, the sharp scent of crushed caraway rising in the sudden disruption. "What an idea! Be a governess! Better be a slave at once. Why should you dream of such a painful step?"

"The duties a governess undertakes are often severe," added Mrs. Pryor, her voice tight.

"And I believe I want severe duties to occupy me."

"Occupy you!" cried Shirley. "When are you idle? Come and sit by my side, and take some tea to refresh you. You don't care much for my friendship, then, that you wish to leave me?"

"Indeed I do, Shirley. I shall never find another friend so dear."

Miss Keeldar impulsively grasped Caroline's hand. "If you think so, you had better make much of me. I hate to part with those to whom I am become attached. I began to flatter myself we were thoroughly friends; that you liked Shirley almost as well as Shirley likes you."

"I *do* like Shirley. But that does not make me strong or happy."

"And would it make you strong or happy to go and live as a dependent amongst utter strangers? It would not. The experiment must not be tried. It is my daily pleasure now to look out for the little cottage bonnet glancing through the trees, and to know that my quiet companion is coming back to me; that I shall have her sitting in the room to look at, to talk to or to let alone, as she and I please."

"I would write to you, Shirley."

"And what are letters? Only a sort of *pis aller*. Drink some tea, Caroline. Eat something—you eat nothing. Laugh and be cheerful, and stay at home."

Miss Helstone shook her head. Her subjective misery remained inherently untranslatable. To attempt to articulate the loss of Moore, the oppressive noise of her thoughts, and the agonizing silence of her unrequited affection to Shirley—who currently held the dominant position in both wealth and love—was an impossible task. To wait and endure was her only plan. 

"Now, is your mind quieted?" inquired Shirley. "Will you consent to stay at home?"

"I shall not leave it against the approbation of my friends," was the reply.

During this conversation, Mrs. Pryor looked far from easy. Bound by an extreme habitual reserve, she merely showed her concern by engaging with the physical environment: noting a chilling alteration in the room's acoustic rustle, she closed a window to block the draft, placed a screen to soften the hearth's glare, and continually glanced at the untouched tea cooling in Caroline's cup. 

Shirley resumed: "Having destroyed your plan, I shall construct a new one of my own. Every summer I make an excursion. This season I propose spending two months at the Scotch lochs or the English lakes—where the ancient waters might drown out this melancholy. I shall go there provided you consent to accompany me. If you refuse, I shall not stir a foot."

"You are very good, Shirley."
