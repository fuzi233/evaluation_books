In the realm of Flatland, the fabric of existence was woven from the threads of simplicity and utility. Our world, devoid of the radiant glow of celestial bodies, relied on subtle whispers to guide us through the expanse. The four cardinal directions – North, South, East, and West – were etched into our consciousness, yet we lacked the familiar beacon of the sun to illuminate our path. Undeterred, we developed an innate affinity for the South, a gentle allure that tugged at our essence like an unseen thread. This soft pull, though hardly noticeable in temperate climates, proved sufficient to serve as a compass, guiding us through the vast, open plains.

In our towns, the sturdy silhouettes of houses stood sentinel, their north-south facades a testament to our ingenuity. The roofs, a seamless expanse of pentagonal curves, defied the relentless rains that swept in from the north, their rhythmic patter a soothing lullaby to our ears. When venturing into the countryside, the gnarled trunks of trees stood as sentinels, their rugged forms a reminder of the unforgiving power of nature.

As we navigated the rolling hills and expansive plains, we discovered that the force of the southward attraction was not uniform. The weak and aged, particularly the delicate females, felt its weight more acutely, their steps faltering beneath the insistent pull. A mark of respect, a sign of our consideration, was to yield the northern side of the path to those who traversed it with greater difficulty. This courtesy, born from a deep understanding of our world's peculiarities, became an integral part of our daily lives.

Our dwellings, simple and unadorned, were designed with functionality in mind. Windows, a luxury we could ill afford, were nonexistent, for the light that illuminated our homes was the same as that which bathed the outdoors. It was a mystery we had long grappled with, a riddle that had confounded our scholars for centuries. The solution, a truth that lay beyond the boundaries of our flat world, remained elusive, relegated to the dusty shelves of forgotten knowledge.
