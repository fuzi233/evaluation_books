VI. Oleron understood the silent verdict in Elsie's words when she mentioned the postcard preceding her next visit. He realized, with a crushing finality, that she had seen it too—that he knew, and that his knowing was a rejection. It was a misery that gnawed at him, a pitiful ache, when she returned within a week, knocking at his door without announcement. Standing on the landing, she declared she intended no stay; yet he had to press her, to physically urge her forward, before she would deign to cross the threshold. Her pretext was a query regarding short-story inquiries, a suggestion he should pursue. He thanked her politely. Once this business concluded, however, her anxiety to depart became palpable. Even Oleron, seeing through the thin veil of the literary excuse, did not attempt to detain her. He accompanied her down the stairs, but Elsie Bengough met no fortune in that house. A second catastrophe awaited her. Halfway down, a sharp report of splintering wood shattered the silence, arresting her with a stifled cry. Oleron knew the timber was ancient, yet he had climbed and descended these same steps countless times without mishap. Now, Elsie had driven her heel through a stair tread. He sprang to her side, alarm seizing him. "Oh, I say! My poor girl!" she cried, but immediately dissolved into hysterical laughter. "It's my weight—I know I'm getting fat—" "Be still—let me clear these splinters," he muttered, grinding his teeth. She continued to laugh and sob simultaneously, fixating on her expanding figure. He thrust downward at the broken boards. The extraction proved arduous; her torn boot revealed the severity of the abrasions on her foot and ankle. "Good God—good God!" he chanted, his voice trembling. "I shall be too heavy for anything soon," she sobbed, punctuated by nervous giggles. Yet she refused to ascend or properly inspect the injury. "No, let me go quickly—let me go quickly," she repeated, panic rising. "But it's a frightful gash!" "No—not so bad—let me escape quickly—I'm—I'm not wanted." At those words, that she was unwanted, Oleron's head dropped as if struck by a physical blow. "Elsie!" he choked out, broken and shocked. She, too, made a violent gesture, as if discarding a burden. "Oh, Paul, not *that*—not *you*—of course I do mean that too in a sense—oh, you know what I mean!... But if the other cannot be, spare me this now! I—I wouldn't have come, but—but—oh, I did, I *did* try to keep away!" It was intolerable, heartbreaking; yet what could he do? What could he say? He did not love her... "Let me go—I'm not wanted—let me take away what's left of me—" "Dear Elsie—you are very dear to me—" Again, she made the gesture of violent dismissal. "No, not that—not anything less—don't offer me anything less—leave me a little pride—" "Let me get my hat and coat—let me take you to a doctor," he murmured. She refused. She refused even the support of his arm. Another unsteady laugh escaped her. "I'm sorry I broke your stairs, Paul.... You will go and see about the short stories, won't you?" He groaned. "Then, if you won't see a doctor, will you go across the square and let Mrs. Barrett look at you? Look, there's Barrett passing now—" The long-nosed Barrett peered curiously down the alley, but as Oleron opened his mouth to summon him, the man fled without a word. Elsie seemed desperate for nothing more than to clear the premises. Finally, she promised to go straight to a doctor, insisting on doing so alone. "Good-bye," she said. Oleron watched her until she passed the hatchet-shaped "To Let" boards hanging outside, watching with a superstitious fear that even they might fall upon her and maim her further. That night, Oleron did not dine. His mind was cluttered with too much turmoil. He paced from room to room of his flat, as if walking might allow him to flee Elsie Bengough's haunting cry that still echoed in his ears. "I'm not wanted—don't offer me anything less—let me take away what's left of me—" Oh, if only he could persuade himself that he loved her! He walked until twilight swallowed the street, then, without lighting candles, he stirred the fire and collapsed into a chair. Poor, poor Elsie!... Yet even as his heart ached for her, it was impossible. If only he had known! If only he had exercised common observation! But those walks, those sisterly arms taken—what a fool he had been!... Well, it was too late now. It was she, not he, who must now act—act by keeping away. He would help her all he could. He himself would not sit in her presence. If she came, he would hurry her out again as fast as possible.... Poor, poor Elsie! Darkness crept into the room; the fire burned low and died; and he continued to sit, wincing periodically as a fresh, tortured phrase rang in his ears. Then, suddenly, for reasons unknown, he found himself anxious for her in a new sense—unsettled by her personal safety. A horrible fancy took root: that even then she might be looking over an embankment into dark water, that she might even now be glancing up at the hook on the door. Women had been known to do such things.... Then there would be an inquest, and he himself would be summoned to identify her, questioned about an ill-healed wound on the hand and a severe abrasion of the ankle. Barrett would testify that he had seen her leaving his house.... Then he recognized his thoughts were morbid. With an effort of will, he pushed them aside, sitting for a while to listen to the faint creakings, tickings, and rappings within his panelling.... If only he could have married her!... But he couldn't. Her face rose before him again as he had seen it on the stairs, drawn with pain, ugly and swollen with tears. Ugly—yes, positively blubbered; if tears were women's weapons, as they were said to be, such tears were weapons turned against themselves... suicide again.... Then, all at once, he found himself attentively considering her two accidents. They were extraordinary, both of them. He *could not* have left that old nail protruding in the wood; indeed, he had fetched tools specially from the kitchen, and he was convinced that the step which had broken beneath her weight had been as sound as the others. It was inexplicable. If these things could happen, anything could happen. There was not a beam nor a jamb in the place that might not fall without warning, not a plank that might not crash inward, not a nail that might not become a dagger. The whole place was alive even now; as he sat there in the dark, he heard its crowd of noises as if the house were a great microphone.... Only half-conscious of his action, he had been sitting for some time identifying these sounds, attributing to each crack or creak or knock its material cause; but there was one noise which, again not fully conscious of the omission, he had failed to account for. It had last come some minutes ago; it came again now—a soft, sweeping rustle that held an almost inaudible crackling. For half a minute or so it held Oleron's attention; then his heavy thoughts drifted back to Elsie Bengough. He was nearer to loving her in that moment than he ever had been. He thought how, for some men, their loved ones were dearer precisely because of those poor mortal blemishes that remind us we are but sojourners on earth, with a common fate not far distant that renders it hardly worth while to do anything but love for the time remaining. Strangling sobs, blurring tears, bodies buffeted by sickness, hearts and minds calloused by the world's rubs—how little love there would be were these things barriers to love! In that sense, he did love Elsie Bengough. What her happiness had never moved in him, her sorrow almost awakened.... Suddenly, his meditation broke. His ear had once more become conscious of that soft, repeated noise—the long sweep with the almost imperceptible crackle within it. Again and again it came, with a curious insistence and urgency. It quickened slightly as he became increasingly attentive... it seemed to Oleron that it grew louder.... All at once, he shot bolt upright in his chair, tense and straining. The silky rustle came again; he tried to attach it to something tangible.... The next moment, he had leaped to his feet, unnerved and terrified. His chair hung poised for a heartbeat, then toppled over, sending the fire-irons clattering as it crashed. There was only one noise in the world like that which had caused him to spring thus to his feet.... The next time it came, Oleron reached behind him at the empty air with his hand, backing slowly until he pressed against the wall. "God in Heaven!" The ejaculation burst from Oleron's lips.
