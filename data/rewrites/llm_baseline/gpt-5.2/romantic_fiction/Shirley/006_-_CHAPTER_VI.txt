Mademoiselle Moore had that morning a pupil whose thoughts would not keep to their prescribed furrow. Caroline’s eyes wandered from the page as if some other lesson—unwritten, unsanctioned—were being whispered to her from beyond the glass. Yet when Hortense rebuked her, the girl took it with a docility that seemed warmed and sweetened by the very sun slanting in at the window. It lay on her like a benediction: not a grand, churchy thing, but a domestic grace—heat on skin, light on hair—making her quiet face both happier and better.

She was fair, and it was the sort of fairness that did not insist upon being understood before it was liked. Youth had shaped her gently: slight and pliant, every movement neat without effort, every curve in proper measure. Her eyes—brown and clear—had at times a beam that spoke where speech failed, winning not by boldness, but by that soft, untranslatable language some looks carry straight to the affections. Her mouth was pretty, her skin delicate, her brown hair abundant; she arranged it with a taste that made its curls seem less an adornment than a natural right. Her dress, too, had that modest correctness which is its own refinement: not costly, not loud in fashion, but chosen in colour and cut to suit the wearer. To-day she wore winter merino of the same soft brown as her hair; a little collar lay over a pink ribbon, tied in a pink knot; she had no other ornament, and needed none.

Of what lay beneath—character, intellect—time would speak. But her place in this household could be told at once, as one tells the plain ingredients before the cooking begins.

She was the child of parents who had separated soon after her birth, their temperaments having proved a discord that no patience could reconcile. Her mother had been half-sister to Mr. Moore’s father; thus Caroline stood in a distant cousinship to Robert, Louis, and Hortense—no blood mixed close, yet kin enough for duty to have a voice. Her father, brother to Mr. Helstone the rector, was of a kind whose memory friends prefer to leave buried once death has closed the account: a man who made his wife unhappy, and whose true faults lent plausibility to false stories whispered of his better-principled brother. Caroline had not known her mother; she was taken from her in infancy and never saw her again. Her father died while she was still young, and then her uncle, Mr. Helstone, became her guardian.

He was not made—by nature or habit—for the charge of a young girl. He neglected what others would have managed with care; and had Caroline not felt in herself the ache of want—want of knowledge, want of guidance—he might have let her grow up with scarcely more teaching than chance threw in her way. Even with her own efforts, a depressing sense haunted her: that she was behind other girls of her age and station, irregularly taught, inferior. So when Hortense, newly arrived at Hollow’s Mill, offered to teach her French and fine needle-work, Caroline seized the offer eagerly, as one catches at bread when hunger has made pride small.

Mademoiselle delighted in the arrangement—not merely from kindness, though she was capable of that in her fashion, but because it placed her in command. She liked to be important; she liked to rule a docile pupil. She took Caroline precisely at her own estimate: an ignorant English girl, unevenly brought up, to be corrected and formed. When she discovered Caroline learned quickly and with keen eagerness, she attributed it not to the scholar’s mind or application, but to the teacher’s superior method. When she found, too, that Caroline possessed stray knowledge—desultory, varied, gathered who could tell where—Hortense still contrived to believe it had all been gleaned unconsciously from herself, even when Caroline knew much on subjects whereof Hortense knew little. It was not logical; but Hortense’s faith in her own influence was complete.

Proud of having “un esprit positif,” and professing preference for dry studies, Mademoiselle held her cousin close to grammar as if it were virtue itself. She assigned, as the most improving exercise imaginable, interminable analyses logiques. Caroline would have learned French, she thought, without forever being driven through that thorny hedge of propositions principales and incidents; of hunting down the incidente déterminative and the incidente applicative; of deciding whether a sentence was pleine, elliptique, implicite. When she grew lost in the maze—and she did, more than once—she sometimes took advantage of those odd intervals when Hortense, in an unaccountable fervour, would be upstairs rummaging drawers: arranging, disarranging, rearranging, as if domestic order were a puzzle she must solve anew each day. Then Caroline would slip her book away to the counting-house, where Robert sat among ledgers and samples, ink and figures, the industry of the place making its own austere music: the scratch of pen, the soft thud of a book closed, the measured creak of floorboards under a man who kept his step controlled.

Mr. Moore had a clear, tranquil brain. He glanced at her difficulty, and it seemed to melt—two minutes, two words, and the knot was untied. She would stand there with her lesson open, warmed by the strange comfort of his competence, thinking how swiftly she might learn if Hortense taught like him. When she left, she repaid him with an admiring smile that fell, shyly, rather at his feet than lifted to his face. And walking back to the rectory cottage, she would wish—foolishly, fervently—that nature had made her a boy, so she might ask to be his clerk and sit in the counting-house beside him, instead of sitting in the parlour under Hortense’s rule.

Sometimes—rarely—she spent an evening at Hollow’s Cottage. Often Robert was away: at market, at Mr. Yorke’s, or shut up with male visitors in another room. But sometimes he was at home, free, and then those hours passed with a lightness that made the clock seem indulgent. There was no room in England, Caroline thought, so pleasant as that little parlour when the three cousins occupied it. Hortense, when not teaching, scolding, or cooking, could relax into something almost kindly. And there was a sure way to soften her further: persuade her to take her guitar and sing. Then her voice—well-toned, trained—filled the room. It might have been wholly agreeable, had not her formal, self-important nature shaped even her music, as it shaped her manners and stamped her features: the notes came correct, yet with a certain stiffness, as if even melody must be kept respectable.

Robert, when released for an hour from the yoke of business, was not precisely lively; but he could be willing—willing to watch Caroline’s liveliness, to listen while she talked, to answer her questions with patient exactness. It was agreeable merely to sit near him, to hover on the edge of his notice, to address him and look at him. Sometimes he became more than agreeable—almost animated, certainly gentle, friendly.

Yet the next morning would freeze him again. However much he seemed, in his quiet way, to enjoy those evenings, he rarely contrived their recurrence. Caroline, inexperienced, puzzled over it. If happiness lay ready to hand, why not take it often, keep it bright with use, instead of letting it lie aside until it rusted?

Still she never acted on her own thought. She did not go to Hollow’s Cottage unasked. Even when Hortense pressed her to come, Caroline would refuse if Robert did not second the request—or did so only faintly. To-day was different. That morning, for the first time, he had invited her of his own unprompted will; and he had spoken so kindly that the sound of his voice had been like a sustaining draught, keeping her glad through the day’s dull stretches.

The hours moved in their usual manner. Mademoiselle, perpetually breathless with occupation, bustled between kitchen and parlour: scolding Sarah, then bending over Caroline’s exercise, then demanding a repetition lesson. However faultlessly Caroline performed, Hortense never praised. Praise, she held, was beneath a teacher’s dignity; blame—severe or slight—was indispensable to it. Reprimand was the rail that kept authority upright. If there were no fault in the lesson, then there was always something else: the pupil’s carriage, her air, her dress, her mien.

The customary skirmish over dinner took place. When Sarah finally brought the meal in, she set it down with a look that said plainly, I never dished such stuff in my life, and it’s not fit for dogs. Yet the repast was savoury enough in its foreign way. There was soup first: a purée of dried peas, patiently simmered until the hard little beads surrendered into a thick, smooth body, and seasoned so that it smelled of warmth and thrift together. Hortense prepared it amidst lamentations that in this desolate England no haricot beans could be had—no proper beans, as she called them, with the conviction of a food historian defending an ancestral pantry.

Then came a dish of meat—its nature not declared, though its chopped miscellany suggested odds and ends gathered without ceremony. It was minced small and mixed with crumbs, then seasoned with something sharp and aromatic, and baked in a mould so that it turned out in a compact, respectable shape, queer to the eye but not unpalatable. Greens, bruised oddly as if they had been punished rather than cooked, accompanied it. And last there was a pâté of fruit conserved by a recipe credited to Madame Gérard Moore’s grand’mère; its sweetness had a dark, treacly edge, as if mélasse had stood in for sugar.

Caroline did not dislike this Belgian cookery; she rather liked it for its difference, for the way it broke the sameness of English plainness. It was well she did, for had she shown any distaste, Hortense would have marked it as a crime. A symptom of disrelish for foreign comestibles would have injured Caroline in her good graces beyond repair. Here, taste was loyalty; appetite was a kind of speech, and to speak wrongly at table was to offend a whole lineage.

After dinner, Caroline coaxed her cousin-governess upstairs to dress. The manoeuvre required care. To hint that the jupon, camisole, and curl-papers were odious would have been felony. One must not attack what Hortense considered merit. So Caroline pretended she needed a change of scene, drew Hortense aloft, and once in the bedroom persuaded her it was not worth returning again: she might as well make her toilet now. While Mademoiselle delivered a solemn homily on her own surpassing virtue in disregarding frivolities of fashion, Caroline’s hands did their quiet work: off came the camisole, on went a decent gown; collar adjusted, hair smoothed, the whole made presentable with a deftness born of longing to please.

But Hortense insisted on her own finishing touches. These consisted of a thick handkerchief tied round the throat and a large black apron, servant-like in its amplitude, spoiling the grace Caroline had tried to secure. Hortense would not appear in her own house without those two things. The fichu was morality itself—improper to go without; the apron was the ensign of a good housewife, as if by wearing it she saved money simply by being seen in it. She had made and presented similar items to Caroline; and the only serious quarrel they had ever had, a sore still living in Hortense’s mind, had arisen from Caroline’s refusal to accept and wear them.

“I wear a high dress and a collar,” Caroline had pleaded on that occasion; “I should feel suffocated with a handkerchief besides. And my short aprons do quite as well as that very long one. I would rather make no change.”

Hortense, by perseverance, might have forced her, but Mr. Moore chanced to overhear their dispute and decided that Caroline’s little aprons would suffice. As she was still a child, he said, she might dispense with the fichu for the present—especially as her curls were long and almost touched her shoulders.

There was no appeal from Robert. Hortense yielded, but disapproved deeply of the piquant neatness of Caroline’s dress and the ladylike grace of her appearance. Something more solid, more homely, she would have pronounced beaucoup plus convenable.

The afternoon was devoted to sewing. Hortense, like most Belgian ladies, was skilful with the needle; she considered it no waste to spend long hours on fine embroidery, lace-work that strained the eyes, netting and knitting, and above all on elaborate stocking-mending. She would give a day to the repair of two holes and count her mission nobly fulfilled. To Caroline it was another trial to be condemned to learn that foreign style of darning—stitch by stitch, imitating exactly the knit of the stocking, as if one were rebuilding cloth fibre by fibre. Wearisome, yes; but to Hortense and her ancestresses for generations it was one of the first duties of a woman. She herself had been given a needle and a fearfully torn stocking while still wearing a child’s coif on her little black head; her haut faits in the darning line had been displayed to company before she was six; and when she discovered Caroline was profoundly ignorant of this essential attainment, she could have wept with pity over such a neglected youth.

No time was lost in hunting up a hopeless pair of hose with heels entirely gone and setting the ignorant English girl to repair the deficiency. Caroline had begun the task two years ago, and the stockings were still in her work-bag. Each day she did a few rows as penance, expiating her sins stitch by stitch. They were a grievous burden. She would have liked to fling them into the fire; once Robert, seeing her sitting and sighing over them, had proposed a private cremation in the counting-house. But Caroline knew it would be impolitic to agree; the ashes would only be replaced by a fresh pair in worse condition. She kept, therefore, to the ills she knew.

All afternoon they sat, fingers moving, heads bent, until eyes and hands—and even the spirits of one of them—grew weary. After dinner the sky had darkened; now rain poured fast again, a dense rush on the panes and roof that made the world outside sound like a river. The cottage had its own small acoustic ecology: the rain’s drumming, the wind’s long sighing in the roof-trees, the occasional click of a needle, the faint scrape of thread pulled tight. Inside, the parlour fire threw a ruddy glow on the clear hearth, as if twilight had arrived early.

As five o’clock struck and slipped away, Caroline’s secret fears rose. Robert might be persuaded by Mr. Sykes or Mr. Yorke to remain at Whinbury till it cleared—and there seemed no chance of clearing.

“It will not be fair till the moon rises,” pronounced Mademoiselle Moore. “Consequently I feel assured that my brother will not return till then. Indeed I should be sorry if he did. We will have coffee. It would be vain to wait for him.”

Caroline’s fingers paused on the hateful stocking.

“I am tired. May I leave my work now, cousin?”

“You may, since it grows too dark to see to do it well. Fold it up; put it carefully in your bag; then step into the kitchen and desire Sarah to bring in the goûter, or tea, as you call it.”

“But it has not yet struck six. He may still come.”

“He will not, I tell you. I can calculate his movements. I understand my brother.”

Suspense is a poor diet, and disappointment bitter; Caroline felt both. She obeyed and went into the kitchen.

Sarah sat at the table making a dress for herself, her scissors snipping with brisk, cheerful defiance of the rain.

“You are to bring in coffee,” Caroline said spiritlessly. Then, as if she could not carry her own weight of waiting, she leaned her arm and head against the mantelpiece and hung over the fire.

“How low you seem, miss! But it’s all because your cousin keeps you so close to work. It’s a shame!”

“Nothing of the kind, Sarah,” Caroline answered briefly.

“Oh! but I know it is. You’re fit to cry this minute, for nothing else but because you’ve sat still the whole day. It would make a kitten dull to be mewed up so.”

“Sarah,” Caroline asked, forcing steadiness, “does your master often come home early from market when it is wet?”

“Never, hardly; but just to-day, for some reason, he has made a difference.”

“What do you mean?”

“He is come. I am certain I saw Murgatroyd lead his horse into the yard by the back-way when I went to get water at the pump five minutes since. He was in the counting-house with Joe Scott, I believe.”

“You are mistaken.”

“What should I be mistaken for? I know his horse surely?”

“But you did not see himself?”

“I heard him speak, though. He was saying something to Joe Scott about having settled all concerning ways and means, and that there would be a new set of frames in the mill before another week passed, and that this time he would get four soldiers from Stilbro’ barracks to guard the wagon.”

The words frames, soldiers, guard fell like hard pellets into Caroline’s mind; even in the warm kitchen, with the domestic smell of cloth and starch and the faint comfort of the fire, industry and conflict found their way in, a harsh undertone beneath the rain.

“Sarah, are you making a gown?” Caroline asked suddenly, as if to steady herself by fastening on some homely thing.

“Yes. Is it a handsome one?”

“Beautiful. Get the coffee ready. I’ll finish cutting out that sleeve for you, and I’ll give you some trimming for it. I have some narrow satin ribbon of a colour that will just match it.”

“You’re very kind, miss.”

“Be quick; there’s a good girl. But first put your master’s shoes on the hearth: he will take his boots off when he comes in. I hear him; he is coming.”

“Miss, you are cutting the stuff wrong.”

“So I am; but it is only a snip. There is no harm done.”

At that moment the kitchen door opened. Mr. Moore entered wet with rain and chilled with wind. The smell he brought in was of cold outdoors—damp wool, horse, and air sharpened by weather. Caroline half turned, but bent again over the dress for an instant, as if to gain one more second to settle her face, to veil whatever might betray her too plainly. The attempt failed. When she looked up at last, her countenance beamed.

“We had ceased to expect you. They asserted you would not come,” she said.

“But I promised to return soon. You expected me, I suppose?”

“No, Robert; I dared not when it rained so fast. And you are wet and chilled. Change everything. If you took cold, I should—we should blame ourselves in some measure.”

“I am not wet through: my riding-coat is waterproof. Dry shoes are all I require. There—the fire is pleasant after facing the cold wind and rain for a few miles.”

He stood on the hearth, letting warmth creep back into him. Caroline stood beside him. Robert’s eyes, as he enjoyed the glow, rested a moment on the glittering brasses on the shelf above—bright, scrubbed, domestic trophies. Then he glanced down and saw, uplifted towards him, a face flushed with relief and happiness, shaded by silky curls, lit by eyes that could not hide what they felt.

Sarah had gone into the parlour with the tray; a lecture from her mistress detained her there. The kitchen’s sounds softened: only the rain’s steady assault outside, and the fire’s small crackle within.

Mr. Moore laid his hand for a moment on Caroline’s shoulder, stooped, and kissed her forehead.

The touch seemed to unseal her, as if some long-held restraint broke like a thread.

“Oh!” she said. “I was miserable when I thought you would not come. I am almost too happy now. Are you happy, Robert? Do you like to come home?”

“I think I do—to-night, at least.”

“Are you certain you are not fretting about your frames, and your business, and the war?”

“Not just now.”

“Are you positive you don’t feel Hollow’s Cottage too small for you, and narrow, and dismal?”

“At this moment, no.”

“Can you affirm that you are not bitter at heart because rich and great people forget you?”

“No more questions. You are mistaken if you think I am anxious to curry favour with rich and great people. I only want means—a position—a career.”

“Which your own talent and goodness shall win you. You were made to be great; you shall be great.”

“I wonder now,” he said, a darker edge coming into his voice, “if you spoke honestly out of your heart, what recipe you would give me for acquiring this same greatness; but I know it—better than you know it yourself. Would it be efficacious? Would it work? Yes—poverty, misery, bankruptcy. Oh, life is not what you think it, Lina!”

“But you are what I think you.”

“I am not.”

“You are better, then?”

“Far worse.”

“No; far better. I know you are good.”

“How do you know it?”

“You look so, and I feel you are so.”

“Where do you feel it?”

“In my heart.”

“Ah! You judge me with your heart, Lina: you should judge me with your head.”

“I do; and then I am quite proud of you. Robert, you cannot tell all my thoughts about you.”

Colour rose in his dark face. His lips smiled, and yet pressed together as if he mistrusted the smile. His eyes laughed, and yet he knit his brow, resisting the softness he had just allowed himself.

“Think meanly of me, Lina,” he said. “Men, in general, are a sort of scum, very different to anything of which you have an idea. I make no pretension to be better than my fellows.”

“If you did, I should not esteem you so much. It is because you are modest that I have such confidence in your merit.”

“Are you flattering me?” he demanded suddenly, turning sharply, searching her face with that acute look which seemed to pierce past words to intention.

“No,” she answered softly, laughing at his swiftness. She did not seem anxious to defend herself; as if truth, once spoken, needed no adornment, no further translation.

“You don’t care whether I think you flatter me or not?”

“No.”

“You are so secure of your own intentions?”

“I suppose so.”

“What are they, Caroline?”

“Only to ease my mind by expressing for once part of what I think, and then to make you better satisfied with yourself.”

“By assuring me that my kinswoman is my sincere friend?”

“Just so. I am your sincere friend, Robert.”

“And I am—what chance and change shall make me, Lina.”

“Not my enemy, however?”

The answer did not come whole. Sarah and her mistress entered the kitchen together in a small commotion. They had employed the time in disputing over café au lait: Sarah declaring it the queerest mess ever seen, a waste of God’s good gifts—coffee’s nature, she insisted, was to be boiled in water; Hortense maintaining it was un breuvage royal, far too good for a mean person who objected to it. Their voices cut across the intimate quiet like a sudden clang, making the kitchen seem smaller.

Robert and Caroline withdrew to the parlour. Before Hortense followed, Caroline had only time to repeat, quick as a pulse:

“Not my enemy, Robert?”

And Mr. Moore, Quaker-like, answered with another question, low and steady:

“Could I be?”

Then he seated himself at the table and, with a quiet authority that felt like kindness, settled Caroline at his side.
