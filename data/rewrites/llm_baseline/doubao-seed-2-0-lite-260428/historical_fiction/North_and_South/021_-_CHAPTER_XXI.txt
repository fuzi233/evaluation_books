“On earth is known to none
The smile that is not sister to a tear.”

Margaret Hale walked home at her father’s side, the cool fine night air stinging her cheeks, her pretty white silk gown hitched up above her ankles just as the old ballad’s Leezie Lindsay kilted her green satin gown, light enough on her feet that she might have broken into a giddy dance down the clean, lamplit Milton pavements, dizzy with the sharp, fresh thrill of the evening.

“I rather suspect Mr. Thornton is far less easy in his mind over this strike than he lets on,” Mr. Hale said, breaking their quiet stroll. “He looked anxious, all through the dinner conversation tonight.”
“I should wonder if he were not,” Margaret replied. “Though he kept that usual unshakable cool of his when the other manufacturers threw out their wild suggestions, just before we left the dining room.”
“He did the same all through dinner. It would take an earthquake to rattle his measured tone, but I could not miss the tight line around his mouth, the furrow between his brows. He is frightened, for all his steely calm.”
“I would be, too, were I in his shoes. He must see how the anger of his workers swells, barely smothered under their polite faces—all of them think him the ‘hard man’ of the Bible, not so much cruel as utterly unfeeling. Clear-judging, rigidly clinging to his own ‘rights’ in a way no mortal ought, when you consider how tiny all our petty claims look in the sight of God. I am glad you see his anxiety, too. When I think of Boucher, half-mad with hunger and rage the other day, I can scarcely bear how lightly Mr. Thornton dismissed the workers’ grievances tonight.”
“Hold, child,” Mr. Hale soothed. “I do not deny the man is hard pressed, and Boucher is certainly suffering at this moment. But the Unions have a never-ending, mysterious supply of funds for their members, from all I hear. And you yourself said Boucher is a man of hot, passionate temperament, who makes every feeling he feels twice as loud as it ought to be.”
“Oh, papa!” Margaret protested, half-laughing.
“I only ask you to do Thornton justice,” he went on. “He is the exact opposite of Boucher—proud to a fault, far too proud to ever show a sliver of what he feels beneath that stony surface. The very sort of character I would have thought you would admire, Margaret.”
“I do admire him! I do,” she said quickly. “I just do not feel as certain as you are that there is any softness under that granite exterior. He is a man of immense strength of character, sharp, unusual intellect for all the lack of formal schooling he had as a boy.”
“His life has been no idle one,” her father corrected. “He has had to exercise judgment and self-control every single day since he was a child, and that builds a kind of intelligence no book can teach. To be sure, he lacks that grounding in the history of past ages that gives you a sure footing to guess what the future may bring—but he knows that gap in his own knowledge, and that is no small thing. You are quite prejudiced against him, Margaret, I think.”
“He is the first manufacturer, the first man who makes his living in trade, that I have ever gotten to know well,” she smiled. “He is my first olive, papa—let me make a face while I swallow the first bitter bite. I know he is good of his kind, and by and by I shall grow to like the whole kind. I think I am already halfway there. I was so absorbed in all their talk of labour and mills tonight, I hardly understood half of the jargon, and Miss Thornton had to come fetch me away to the other end of the drawing room, saying she was sure I would be uncomfortable being the only lady among so many gentlemen. I had not even noticed. And the other ladies there were so dull, papa—so dreadfully dull! Their talk reminded me of that old game we used to play at Helstone, where each of us had to weave a list of set nouns into a natural-sounding sentence.”
“What on earth do you mean, child?” Mr. Hale asked, bewildered.
“Why, they took turns dropping little words that marked wealth—their under-gardeners, the number of panes of glass in their greenhouses, the price of their lace shawls, their diamonds—each of them working every single one of those boastful little details into their conversation as if they had stumbled on it by accident, not planned it out for an hour beforehand.”
“You will be just as proud of our one little servant when we get her, if half of what Mrs. Thornton says about her is true,” he laughed.
“I am sure I shall. I felt like such a hypocrite tonight, sitting there in my fine white silk gown with my idle hands folded in my lap, thinking of all the hard, messy housework Dixon and our new girl will have done all day. I’ll warrant they all thought me a very grand lady indeed.”
“Even I was fool enough to think you looked every inch a lady, my dear,” Mr. Hale said, smiling gently.

The smile died on his lips, turning his face white and trembling, the second Dixon pulled open their front door.
“Oh master! Oh Miss Margaret! Thank the good Lord you are home!” the maid gasped, her face streaked with tears. “Dr. Donaldson is here. The neighbour next door fetched him, for the charwoman had gone home for the night. She’s easier now—but sir, I thought she’d die an hour ago.”
Mr. Hale’s hand clamped tight on Margaret’s arm to keep himself from falling. He looked at her, and saw not the wild terror that had seized his own unprepared heart, but quiet, awed sorrow, as if she had half suspected this blow was coming all along.
“Oh! Wicked, wicked daughter that I am, I should never have left her!” Margaret moaned, as she half-supported her trembling father, hurrying him up the stairs.

Dr. Donaldson met them on the landing, his face grave. “The opiate has taken effect,” he whispered. “The spasms were very severe—no wonder your maid was terrified. But she will rally this time.”
This time. The words hung sharp in the air. “Let me go to her,” Margaret pleaded.
Half an hour before, Mr. Hale had been a sturdy middle-aged man. Now his eyes were dim, his senses reeling, his steps tottering as if he had already aged to seventy. Dr. Donaldson guided him into the bedroom, Margaret at their heels. There lay Mrs. Hale, the faint, unmistakable stamp of coming death on her sleeping face. She might be easier for now, she might rest—but Death had already marked her for his own, and it was plain he would soon return to claim what was his.
Mr. Hale stared at her for a long, silent minute. Then he began to shake all over, pulling away from the doctor’s steadying hand to grope for the drawing room door—though a dozen candles, lit in the first wild panic of the crisis, flared bright all around him, he could not see a thing. He staggered into the front room, and fumbled blindly for a chair. Dr. Donaldson wheeled one over, sat him down, and felt his racing pulse.
“Speak to him, Miss Hale,” the doctor said. “We must rouse him.”
“Papa!” Margaret cried, her voice thick with sobs. “Papa, speak to me.”
The blank daze cleared a little from Mr. Hale’s eyes, and he forced himself to focus on her face. “Margaret—you knew this was coming, did you not? Oh, it was cruel of you to hide it from me.”
“No, sir, it was not cruel,” Dr. Donaldson cut in, sharp and decisive. “Miss Hale acted entirely under my orders. It may have been a mistake to keep it from you, but it was never cruel. Your wife will wake a much easier woman tomorrow, I promise you. She had those spasms exactly as I feared she might, though I did not speak of my apprehensions to Miss Hale for fear of distressing her. The opiate I brought with her will give her a long, deep, painless sleep, and that ghastly look on her face that has frightened you so will be gone by dawn.”
“But the disease will not be gone, will it?”
Dr. Donaldson glanced at Margaret. She lifted her head, no plea for soft half-truths in her eyes, and the seasoned doctor who read faces so well saw at once she would rather have the whole unvarnished truth out in the open. “No. The disease is still there. For all our boasting of our skill, we cannot touch it. We can only slow its progress, and ease the pain it brings. Be a man, sir. Be a Christian. Remember the immortality of the soul, which no pain, no mortal sickness, can ever harm or touch.”
All Mr. Hale could reply was choked, broken: “You have never been married, Dr. Donaldson. You do not know what this loss will mean,” and the deep, manly sobs that tore out of him rang through the still dark house like heavy pulses of raw agony.
Margaret knelt beside him, wrapping her arms around him, her tears falling on his hands. No one there could tell how many hours slipped by, lost in grief. It was Mr. Hale who roused himself first, to speak of the hard, practical necessities of the moment. “What must we do?” he asked. “Tell us both. Margaret is my staff—my right hand, now.”
Dr. Donaldson gave clear, sensible instructions. There was no danger for that night—indeed, no immediate danger for many days yet. But there was no hope of full recovery. He advised Mr. Hale to go to bed, to leave only one watcher by the sickbed to conserve their strength. He promised to return before sunrise, and left them with a warm, firm handshake.
They spoke very few words after he was gone, too worn thin by terror and sorrow to do more than lay out a simple plan. Mr. Hale swore he would sit up all night with his wife, and all Margaret could do was persuade him to rest on the drawing room sofa. Dixon bluntly refused to leave her mistress’s side for a second, and Margaret would not have dreamed of going to bed herself, no matter how many times the doctor spoke of husbanding their strength. So Dixon sat in her chair, nodding off, jerking herself awake every few minutes, until at last she surrendered entirely and fell snoring softly. Margaret had thrown off her fine white silk gown in a fit of impatient disgust, and pulled on her soft dressing gown. She felt as if she would never sleep again, all her senses sharpened to twice their normal keenness, tuned to every sound and stir from her mother’s bed.
For two hours, she heard her father restlessly pacing the next room, creeping to the sickbed door every few minutes to listen, too afraid to knock, until Margaret noticed his unseen presence and opened the door to reassure him all was well. At last he fell asleep, and the whole house went still. Margaret sat behind the curtain by the bed, lost in thought. All the interests that had filled her life only a day before felt like faint, distant memories from some other life—Bessy Higgins, poor starving Boucher, the fine dinner at the Thorntons’ house, the streets of Milton outside their door—none of it felt real, next to her mother’s sleeping face. Even their old life in Harley Street felt far away, the days she used to sit tracing her mother’s features in her Aunt Shaw’s face, pining for the quiet cottage at Helstone. The dull gray uneventful winter days back home, that she had once thought tedious beyond bearing, were now the most precious thing in the world. She would have given anything to turn back the clock, to return to those quiet days she had never appreciated half enough. Life felt like nothing but a vain, flickering show, all shadows, all slipping away. It was as if a bell in some high, empty belfry far above the noise of the world was tolling nonstop in her head: All are shadows. All are passing. All is past.
When the gray cool dawn broke, no different from any other happier dawn that had come before, Margaret looked at the three sleeping people in the house one by one. The terrible dark night felt as unreal as a dream itself, a shadow that was already slipping away into the past.
When Mrs. Hale woke, she had no idea how near she had come to death the night before. She was only surprised to see Dr. Donaldson arriving so early, and confused by the anxious, worn faces of her husband and daughter. She agreed to stay in bed that day, complaining only that she felt tired—but the next morning, she insisted on getting up, and the doctor gave his permission for her to sit in the drawing room. She was restless, no position feeling comfortable, and by nightfall she was burning with fever. Mr. Hale was utterly listless, unable to make even the smallest decision on his own.
“What can we do to spare mamma another such terrible night?” Margaret asked the doctor on the third day.
“It is the natural reaction to the strong opiates I was forced to give her,” Dr. Donaldson replied. “I think it is far more painful for you to watch her than it is for her to bear. But there is something that may help—if we can get a water-bed, that will take the pressure off her aching joints. I know Mrs. Thornton has one. I was going to call there this afternoon to ask, but I have a very long round of patients today, I may not have time to stop. You look pale from all your nights of watching, Margaret—a brisk two-mile walk to Marlborough Street will do you good. Go ask Mrs. Thornton if she can lend it to us.”
“Of course,” Margaret said at once. “I can go while mamma is napping this afternoon. I am sure she will be happy to lend it to us.”
The doctor’s prediction was right. Mrs. Hale rallied fast that afternoon, looking brighter and more like her old self than Margaret had dared hope. She left her sitting in her easy chair, her hand resting in Mr. Hale’s, who looked far more worn and haggard than his wife. He could even smile now, faint and slow though it was—something Margaret had never thought to see again, only a few nights before.
It was a two-mile walk from their house in Crampton Crescent to Marlborough Street. The August sun beat down hot and heavy on the pavements at three o’clock in the afternoon, too warm to hurry. Margaret walked the first mile and a half lost in her own sombre thoughts, so used to weaving through the crowds of Milton’s busy streets that she scarcely noticed anything unusual. But soon, she realized something was different. The mass of people clogging the road ahead were not moving along to their errands, they were clustered in tight little groups, talking in low, heated voices, buzzing with a kind of roiling, suppressed excitement that hung thick in the air. They made way for her as she passed, and she was too wrapped up in thoughts of her mother to pay much mind at first. It was only when she turned onto Marlborough Street that the full weight of the oppressive, angry atmosphere hit her. A low, distant roar drifted up from every narrow side lane, like the growl of ten thousand furious, indignant voices. The inhabitants of every squalid little cottage in the lanes were clustered on their doorsteps and windowsills, every single eye turned in the direction of the Thornton mill. Some faces were blazing with unbridled rage, others set with cold, relentless threat, others wide with fear or desperate plea. She reached the small side entrance set into the high blank mill wall, and rang the bell, waiting for the porter to answer. Glancing back, she saw the first slow, surging wave of the dark crowd roll down the far end of Marlborough Street, threatening and cresting, before it receded, leaving a dead, ominous quiet hanging over the whole road. None of it sank deep into her preoccupied mind, her thoughts still fixed on her sick mother at home, trying to brace herself for the inevitable loss ahead.
The porter pulled the door open just a crack, barely wide enough for her to slip through. “It’s you, is it, miss?” he breathed, relief flooding his face as he wrenched the door a little wider to let her in. He shot the heavy bolt home hard behind her. “I reckon that lot out there are all coming this way, soon enough.”
“I did notice something unusual,” Margaret said. “But the street looks empty now.”
She crossed the mill yard, and climbed the steps to the Thorntons’ front door. There was no sound of the steam engine’s steady thud and pant, no click of the looms, no clatter of workmen’s voices—only that far, deep, rising roar of the angry crowd, drawing closer every second.
