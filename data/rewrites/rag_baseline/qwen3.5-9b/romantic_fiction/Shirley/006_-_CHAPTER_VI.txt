That morning, Mademoiselle Moore presided over a pupil whose mind wandered as frequently as the clouds across the English sky. Caroline forgot, again and again, the explanations laid before her, yet she endured the chidings born of her inattention with a spirit unclouded by resentment. Seated in the slanting sunshine near the window, she seemed to absorb the warmth of the sun as a balm, a kind influence that softened her edges and rendered her both happy and benevolent. Thus disposed, she presented her finest visage, a pleasing vision that commanded affection simply by existing.

Nature had not withheld its gifts from Caroline Helstone. Beauty was hers not merely as a possession but as an atmosphere; one did not need to know her intimately to find oneself drawn to her. At first glance, she was fair enough to satisfy the eye. Her form suited her youth perfectly: girlish, light, and pliant, every curve neat, every limb proportionate. Her face was expressive and gentle, framed by eyes that were handsome and capable, at moments, of casting a winning beam that stole directly into the heart, speaking a silent language to the affections. Her mouth was lovely, her skin delicate, and her hair—a fine cascade of brown—fell in picturesque profusion, arranged with a natural taste that favored curls. Her attire announced a refined sensibility; unobtrusive in fashion, modest in cost, yet harmoniously colored to contrast with her pale complexion and tailored to drape her slight frame. Her current winter garb was of merino wool, the same soft shade of brown as her hair. A little collar rested round her neck, overlaid by a pink ribbon fastened with a pink knot. She wore no other ornamentation, and yet she was complete.

Thus much for Caroline Helstone's appearance. As to her character or intellect, if she possessed either, they must reveal themselves in due course.

Her lineage requires brief explanation. She was the child of parents separated shortly after her birth, victims of a fundamental disagreement of disposition. Her mother was the half-sister of Mr. Moore's father; thus, though there was no mixture of blood, she stood in a distant kinship to Robert, Louis, and Hortense. Her father was the brother of Mr. Helstone—a man of such character that friends, once death has settled all earthly accounts, prefer not to recall. He had rendered his wife unhappy. The truths known regarding him lent credibility to the falsehoods circulated about his more principled brother. Caroline had never known her mother, taken from her in infancy, nor had she seen her since. Her father died comparatively young, leaving her uncle, the rector, as her sole guardian for several years. Yet he was ill-adapted, by nature and habit, to the charge of a young girl. He took little trouble regarding her education; indeed, he would have taken none had she not, sensing her neglect, grown anxious on her own behalf, periodically demanding attention and the means to acquire the knowledge deemed indispensable. Still, a depressing sense of inferiority weighed upon her, a conviction that her attainments were fewer than those of girls of her age and station. Glad, therefore, was she to avail herself of the kind offer extended by her cousin Hortense, soon after the latter's arrival at Hollow's Mill, to instruct her in French and fine needlework.

Mdlle. Moore, for her part, delighted in the task, for it conferred upon her a certain importance. She relished the opportunity to lord it, however gently, over a docile yet quick pupil. She took Caroline precisely at her own estimation: an irregularly taught, even ignorant girl. When she discovered that the student made rapid and eager progress, she ascribed the improvement not to the scholar's talent or application, but entirely to her own superior method of instruction. Even when she found that Caroline, unskilled in routine, possessed a knowledge of her own—desultory but varied—the discovery caused her no surprise. She still imagined that from her conversations the girl had unawares gleaned these treasures. She maintained this delusion even when forced to admit that her pupil knew much on subjects whereof she knew little. The idea was illogical, yet Hortense possessed perfect faith in it.

Mademoiselle, who prided herself on possessing "un esprit positif" and entertained a decided preference for dry studies, kept her young cousin under the closest surveillance. She worked her relentlessly at the grammar of the French language, assigning as the most improving exercise she could devise interminable "analyses logiques." These analyses were by no means a source of pleasure to Caroline. She believed she could have learned French just as well without them and grudged excessively the time spent pondering "propositions principales et incidentes," deciding between the "incidente déterminative" and the "incidente applicative," or examining whether a proposition was "pleine," "elliptique," or "implicite." Sometimes she lost herself in the labyrinth of syntax. When so lost, and while Hortense rummaged through her drawers upstairs—an unaccountable occupation in which she spent a large portion of each day, arranging, disarranging, rearranging, and counter-arranging—Caroline would carry her book to Robert in the counting-house. There, she sought to smooth the rough places with his aid. Mr. Moore possessed a clear, tranquil brain of his own. Almost as soon as he looked at Caroline's little difficulties, they seemed to dissolve beneath his eye. In two minutes he would explain all, giving the key to the puzzle in two words. She thought, with longing, if Hortense could only teach like him, how much faster she might learn! Repaying him with an admiring and grateful smile, shed at his feet rather than lifted to his face, she would leave the mill reluctantly to return to the cottage. Then, while she completed the exercise or worked out the sums—for Mdlle. Moore also taught her arithmetic—she would wish that nature had made her a boy instead of a girl, that she might ask Robert to let her be his clerk, to sit with him in the counting-house, instead of sitting with Hortense in the parlour.

Occasionally—and this happened very rarely—she spent the evening at Hollow's Cottage. Sometimes Moore was away attending a market; sometimes he was gone to Mr. Yorke's; often he was engaged with a male visitor in another room. But sometimes, too, he was at home, disengaged, free to talk with Caroline. When this occurred, the evening hours passed on wings of light; they were gone before they were counted. There was no room in England so pleasant as that small parlour when the three cousins occupied it. Hortense, when she was not teaching, scolding, or cooking, was far from ill-humored. It was her custom to relax towards evening and to be kind to her young English kinswoman. Furthermore, there was a means of rendering her delightful: inducing her to take her guitar and sing and play. She then became quite good-natured. With skillful playing and a well-toned voice, it was not disagreeable to listen to her. It would have been absolutely agreeable, except that her formal and self-important character modulated her strains, just as it impressed her manners and molded her countenance.

Mr. Moore, released from the business yoke, was, if not lively himself, a willing spectator of Caroline's liveliness, a complacent listener to her talk, a ready respondent to her questions. He was something agreeable to sit near, to hover round, to address and look at. Sometimes he was better than this—almost animated, quite gentle and friendly.

The drawback was that by the next morning he was sure to be frozen up again. However much he seemed, in his quiet way, to enjoy these social evenings, he rarely contrived their recurrence. This circumstance puzzled the inexperienced head of his cousin. "If I had a means of happiness at my command," she thought, "I would employ that means often. I would keep it bright with use, and not let it lie for weeks aside, till it gets rusty."

Yet she was careful not to put into practice her own theory. Much as she liked an evening visit to the cottage, she never paid one unasked. Often, indeed, when pressed by Hortense to come, she would refuse, because Robert did not second, or but slightly seconded, the request. This morning was the first time he had ever, of his own unprompted will, given her an invitation; and then he had spoken so kindly that in hearing him she had received a sense of happiness sufficient to keep her glad for the whole day.

The morning passed as usual. Mademoiselle, ever breathlessly busy, spent it bustling from kitchen to parlour, now scolding Sarah, now looking over Caroline's exercise or hearing her repetition-lesson. However faultlessly these tasks were achieved, she never commended. It was a maxim with her that praise is inconsistent with a teacher's dignity, and that blame, in more or less unqualified measure, is indispensable to it. She thought incessant reprimand, severe or slight, quite necessary to the maintenance of her authority. And if no possible error was to be found in the lesson, it was the pupil's carriage, or air, or dress, or mien, which required correction.

The usual affray took place about the dinner. When Sarah at last brought it into the room, she almost flung it upon the table with a look that expressed quite plainly, "I never dished such stuff in my life afore; it's not fit for dogs."
