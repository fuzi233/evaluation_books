As Foker sat at the head of the table, surrounded by the remnants of a dinner that had long since lost its allure, he couldn't help but feel a sense of disillusionment wash over him. The gaudy decorations, the forced laughter, and the affected mannerisms of their hosts all combined to make the evening feel like a travesty. "Good heavens," he thought to himself, "what a farce this all is. That Rougemont fellow looks like a painted doll, and his wife's conversation is nothing more than a series of trite platitudes. And as for Calverley, his slang is an affront to good taste."

Miss Rougemont, never one to miss an opportunity to engage her guests, leaned forward and playfully teased Foker about his distant demeanor. "Twopence-halfpenny for your thoughts, Fokey!" she exclaimed, her voice dripping with amusement as she surveyed the scattering of cigar ashes, melting ices, and abandoned dessert.

Foker's companion, Poyntz, chuckled knowingly and tossed out a wry remark about the young man's tendency to overthink things. "Foker, my dear chap, I believe a considerable sum of money has been offered to you for the privilege of listening to your esteemed intellect. Do tell us, what are your thoughts on the matter?"

Foker's neighbor, Calverley, rolled her eyes in exasperation. "Good heavens, what is Poyntz prattling on about now?" she whispered to her companion. "He's a detestable man, always sneering and drawing attention to himself."

Coralie, meanwhile, was giggling uncontrollably at Foker's expense. "Mon dieu, qu'il est drôle ce petit homme, monsieur Fokare," she cooed, her Gascon accent thick and rich as she spoke. "Il ne paraît pas avoir plus de vingt ans." (What a funny little man, Mr. Fokare is, she said to her mother. He doesn't seem to have more than twenty years.)

Lord Colchicum, resplendent in his purple finery, sighed wistfully and raised his goblet in a toast. "C'est la jeunesse, c'est la vie," he murmured, his eyes clouding over with nostalgia.
