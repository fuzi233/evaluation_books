I turned to Eve and spoke, my voice trembling with a mixture of shame and desperate need. "Mother, there is an empty couch beside Lona. I know I am unworthy, yet may I not spend this night among the dead? Forgive my cowardice and my arrogance; take me in. I am sick of myself and crave only the silence of sleep."

"The couch beside Lona is prepared for you," she replied, her tone gentle but firm. "Yet, something must be done before you rest."

"I am ready," I said.

"How do you know you possess the strength to do it?" she asked, a faint smile playing on her lips.

"Because you require it," I answered. "What is the task?"

She turned to Adam. "Is he forgiven, husband?"

"From my heart," Adam replied.

"Then tell him what he must do."

Adam stepped forward and took Mara's hand, holding it tenderly in his lap. "Come, my child, let us go to the cottage. There, I shall instruct you."

As we moved away, a sudden, violent gust of wind battered the roof, accompanied by a great flapping noise, only to die away into a deep, mournful sigh. Once the door of the death-chamber was shut behind us, Adam sat, and I stood before him, waiting.

"You will remember," he began, his voice steady, "how, after leaving your mother's house, you arrived at a dry rock marked by the scars of an ancient waterfall? You climbed it and found a sandy desert. Go to that rock now. From its summit, walk deep into the desert. Do not take many steps before lying down and placing your head upon the sand. If you hear the murmur of water beneath, proceed a little farther and listen again. If the sound persists, you are on the right path. Every few yards, stop, lie down, and hearken. Should you ever hear no sound, you have strayed; you must listen in every direction until the water calls you again. Follow the sound, never retracing your steps, and it will grow louder, leading you to its source. That is the spot you seek. Dig there with the spade I will give you until you reach moisture. Place the hand within, cover it flush with the desert surface, and return home."

He handed me the hand, then produced a spade. "This is my gardening spade," he said softly. "With it, I have brought many lovely things into the sun."

I took the tools and stepped out into the night. The air was biting cold, and darkness was absolute. Falling would have been a terror, and the path ahead seemed treacherous even in daylight. Yet, I had not undertaken this task lightly. The moment I started, I realized I was guided by no chance. A pale light erupted from the ground with every step, illuminating exactly where to place my foot. Through the heather and low rocks, I walked without a single stumble. The bad burrow was utterly still; no waves rose, no heads appeared as I crossed it.

A moon rose, casting a silver glow that revealed the easy way forward. By morning, I was nearly past the dry channels of the river's first branch, judging I was close to Mara's cottage.

The moon hung low, the sun not yet risen, when I saw ahead, where the path narrowed between rocks, a figure veiled from head to foot in moonlit mist. I pressed on as if blind to it. The figure threw aside its veil.

"Have you forgotten me already?" the voice asked—princess, or something pretending to be one.

I neither hesitated nor answered; I walked straight on.

"You meant then to leave me in that horrible sepulchre! Do you not understand that where I please to be, there I am? Take my hand; I am alive as you!"

I was on the verge of saying, "Give me your left hand," but I caught myself, held my peace, and advanced steadily.

"Give me my hand!" she shrieked suddenly, "or I will tear you in pieces: you are mine!"

She flung herself upon me. I shuddered but did not falter. Nothing touched me, and she vanished from sight.

With measured tread, a body of armed men filled the path ahead. I walked through them—I knew not if they parted for me or were mere phantoms. They turned and followed, their march audible and felt at my heels, but I cast no look behind. Their steps and the clash of armor faded into the night.

A little farther, where the moon dipped near the horizon and shadows deepened, I saw a woman seated across the narrow path, her face muffled.

"Ah," she said, "you are come at last! I have waited an hour or more. You have done well! Your trial is over. My father sent me to meet you so you might rest. Give me your charge, and lay your head in my lap; I will guard both until the sun rises. I am not always bitterness, nor to all men!"

Her words were a terrible temptation, for I was exhausted. What was more likely to be true? If I were to trample the very person of the Lady of Sorrow underfoot through slavish obedience and a lack of pure insight, my heart would break. My heart grew faint, then hammered against my ribs as if to burst.

Nevertheless, my will hardened against my heart, and my step did not falter. I bit my tongue to keep from answering and pressed on. If Adam had sent her, he could not complain of my refusal. Nor would the Lady of Sorrow love me less for the fact that even she could not turn me aside.

Just as I reached the phantom, she pulled the covering from her face. Great indeed was her loveliness, but those were not Mara's eyes! No lie could truly or for long imitate them. I advanced as if she were not there, and my foot found empty space.

I had almost reached the other side when a Shadow barred my way. I believe it was The Shadow. He wore what looked like a helmet, but as I drew closer, I saw it was his own head—distorted beyond recognition, bearing only a doubtful resemblance to humanity. A cold, dank wind smote me, sickening and repulsive as the air of a charnel house. Firmness left my joints, and my limbs trembled as if they would collapse into a helpless heap. I seemed to pass through him, though perhaps he passed through me; for a moment, I was one of the damned. Then, a soft wind, like the first breath of a new spring, greeted me, and before me arose the dawn.

My path now led past the door of Mara's cottage. It stood wide open, and upon the table lay a loaf of bread and a pitcher of water. There was no howl, no wail within or around the cottage.

I reached the precipice that testified to the vanished river. I climbed its worn face and entered the desert. Finally, after much listening back and forth, I determined the spot where the hidden water roared loudest. I hung Lilith's hand about my neck and began to dig. It was long labor, for the sand was loose and required a large hole, but eventually, I threw up a damp spadeful. I tossed the sexton-tool onto the edge and laid the hand down. Water began to ooze from beneath its fingers. I sprang out and hastened to fill the grave. Utterly fatigued, I dropped beside it and fell asleep.
