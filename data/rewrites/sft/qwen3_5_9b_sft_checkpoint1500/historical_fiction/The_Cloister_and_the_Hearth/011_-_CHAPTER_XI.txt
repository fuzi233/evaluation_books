A look of venomous hatred from the burgomaster, coupled with his brutal incarceration, had convinced Gerard that Ghysbrecht sought nothing less than his death. He slipped around the corner of the tower, bracing himself not for any divine intervention, but for some cruel, treacherous trap devised by a wicked man to destroy him within those walls, an escape from which would remain a mystery.

Yet, as he moved forward, a soft yet courageous hand slipped into his own. Margaret stood beside him, ready to share this newfound peril.

Before they could reach the base of the haunted tower, a vision froze them in their tracks. Halfway up the stone spiral, a creature resembling a giant glow-worm climbed steadily. Its head blazed with a fiery light, illuminating its dark form, which stretched nearly four feet in length. At the foot of the tower stood a figure dressed in white, indistinguishable from a living woman. Gerard and Margaret quaked with awe.

"The rope!" Gerard gasped, his breath catching in his throat. "The rope! It ascends the ladder."

As they stared, the luminous creature vanished into Gerard's former prison, its light flooding the cell and turning the windowpane a deep, bloody red. The white-clad figure remained motionless below.

Those who survive the initial shock of the supernatural often succumb to a terrifying, primal urge to rush toward the horror, just as a bird might fly blindly at a serpent. This fascination had gripped Margaret. She gently released Gerard's hand, standing bewildered for a heartbeat, before suddenly letting out a wild cry and darting toward the specter. Gerard, unaware of this natural instinct, instantly believed the devil was leading her to ruin. He fell to his knees.

"Exorcizo vos! In the name of Blessed Mary, I exorcise you!"

Though the exorcist screamed his invocations in sheer terror, he was relieved to hear the specter emit a weak, frightened cry. Discovering that hell, too, possessed vulnerabilities offered a glimmer of hope. Redoubling his prayers, Gerard soon saw the ghastly shape kneeling at Margaret's feet, pleading pitifully for mercy.

Soon after, Kate and Giles arrived at the scene. Their surprise was immense when they discovered a new rope dangling from the prisoner's window down to the earth.

"I understand now," declared the younger brother, accepting reality as it presented itself. "Our Gerard descended this rope. He is free. I shall ascend and verify."

"No, Giles, no!" cried the older brother, his judgment clouded by superstition. "Do you not see this is sorcery? This rope is a line cast by the Evil One to lure you to destruction. He knows every weakness of our hearts; he sees how fond you are of climbing. How could Gerard procure such a rope? How could it be fastened in the sky like this? It defies nature. Holy saints guard us tonight, for hell is loose upon the world."

"Nonsense!" retorted the dwarf. "Hell is downward, and this rope goes upward. I have never had the fortune to climb a rope of such length. It may be years before I encounter one prepared for me. Better to be knocked senseless at once than to wait forever for happiness."

With a cry of pure delight, akin to a cat leaping onto a table laden with fish, Giles sprang onto the rope. His athletic vigor burned brightly, and the only concession Kate could extract from him was permission to attach a lantern around his neck.

"A light frightens evil spirits," she explained.

Thus, with massive arms and legs moving with feather-like agility, Giles ascended the rope faster than Gerard had descended. The lantern strapped to the back of his neck transformed him into a glowing beacon, a living glow-worm. His sister watched his progress with trembling anxiety. Suddenly, a female figure burst from the solid masonry, charging at her with superhuman speed.

Kate emitted a faint, strangled cry—the limit of her vocal cords under the weight of terror. Her crutches clattered to the ground, and she sank to her knees, burying her face and moaning, "Take my body, but spare my soul!"

"By heavens, it is a woman!" Margaret exclaimed, panting.

"Why, it is a woman!" Kate stammered, quivering.

"How terrified you make me!" Margaret cried.

"And I am terrified enough!" Kate sobbed. "Oh! Oh! Oh!"

"This is bizarre!" Margaret continued, regaining her composure slightly. "But the fiery-headed thing? It was with you, yet you are unharmed! Why are you here at this hour?"

"Nay, tell me why *you* are here?"

"Perhaps we share the same mission? Ah! You are his dear sister, Kate!"

"And you are Margaret Brandt."

"Yes."

"All the better. You love him; you are here. Then Giles was correct. He has gained his freedom."

Gerard stepped forward to confirm the truth, but their conversation was abruptly interrupted by a horrific, unearthly sound, echoing as if from a tomb: "PARCHMENT!—PARCHMENT!—PARCHMENT!"

With each repetition, the volume swelled. They looked up to see the dwarf, clutching handfuls of parchments, his face alight with fiendish joy and burning with a diabolical fire. The lantern around his neck rendered him a translucent nightmare, startingling the mortals below. As he spoke the word, he hurled the ancient documents at their stunned heads. The papers rained down like wounded ducks; some collapsed immediately, others fluttered helplessly, while still others spiraled slowly in wide, airy circles until they settled on the grass. Hardly had these fallen when the sepulchral roar returned—"Parchment! Parchment!"—and another flock of documents descended, followed by another, blanketing the ground in white.

Finally, the fire-headed imp, with his luminous body and horned hands, slid down the rope like a falling star. Prioritizing business over sentiment, he proposed an immediate settlement for the merchandise he had just delivered to his rescued brother.

"Hush!" Gerard warned sharply. "Speak too loudly. Collect them, and follow us to a safer place than this."

"Will you return home with me, Gerard?" asked little Kate.

"I have no home."

"You shall not say so. After such cruelty, who could be more welcome to your father's house?"

"Father!" Gerard replied sternly. "I have no father. He who was my father has become my jailer. I have escaped from his grasp; I shall never enter his reach again."

"An enemy did this, not your father," Kate insisted, recounting what she had overheard Cornelis and Sybrandt say. Yet, the injury was too fresh to be soothed. A bitterness of indignation, previously unseen in Gerard, surged to the surface.

"Cornelis and Sybrandt are two vile curs who have bared their teeth and hearts to me for a long time, yet they could achieve no more. My father is the culprit. He granted the burgomaster his authority, or the man would not dare touch me, a free burgher of this town. So be it. I was his son; I am now his prisoner. He has played his part; I shall play mine. Farewell to the borough where I was born, lived honestly, and was imprisoned. Until there remains another town in existence, I shall never trouble you again, Tergou."

"Oh! Gerard! Gerard!" Kate cried.

Margaret whispered urgently, "Do not oppose him now. Allow his anger to cool."

Kate turned quickly to her. "May I look upon your face?" The inspection seemed favorable, prompting her to whisper, "It is a comely countenance, and not that of a mischief-maker."

"Fear me not," Margaret said in the same gentle tone. "I could not find happiness without your love, as well as Gerard's."

"These are comforting words," Kate sobbed. Then, lifting her gaze, she added, "Little did I think I would like you so well. My heart is willing, but my infirmity forbids me to embrace you."

At this, Margaret gently wound her arm around Gerard's sister and kissed her tenderly.

"He has often spoken of you to me, Kate," Margaret murmured. "And often I longed for this moment."

"You, too, Gerard," Kate said, "Kiss me before you depart; for my heart grows heavy at the thought of leaving you this night."

Gerard kissed her, and she departed on her crutches toward home. The final sound they heard from her direction was a small, patient sigh. Then, tears welled in Margaret's eyes, though Gerard, a man, paid them no mind.

As they turned toward Sevenbergen, the dwarf nudged Gerard with his bundle of parchments and extended a curved claw.

Margaret dissuaded him. "Why accept what belongs to none of us?"

"Oh, spoil an enemy however you can."

"But might they not use this as a pretext for renewed violence?"

"How could they? Do you truly believe I will remain in Tergou after this? The burgomaster has stolen my liberty; I suspect I would not hesitate to take his life for it, if I could."

"Oh, fie, Gerard!"

"What! Is life worth more than liberty? Well, I cannot take his life, so I shall take the first opportunity that presents itself."
