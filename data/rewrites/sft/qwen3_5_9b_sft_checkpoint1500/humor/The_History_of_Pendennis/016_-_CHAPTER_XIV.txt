The theater in Chatteris was thinner than a winter mouse, so sparse that the house itself seemed to be holding its breath. Mr. Manager Bingley, straining to breathe life into the corpse of *Pizarro*, played his famous 'Rolla' to an audience that consisted mostly of echoes and the occasional cough. Yet, amidst the Spanish battles and Peruvian processions, the air was thick with a singular, suffocating heat. Poor Pen sat alone in the boxes, his eyes bloodshot, leaning over the ledge as if trying to pry the world open. To him, the stage was a blur of costumes and shouting; the only focus was Cora. He later claimed he wished he'd grabbed a pistol right then, so mad was he with a love that had curdled into pure rage. Had it not been for his mother, watching silently from afar with a sympathy that felt like a cold blanket, he might have ended his days prematurely in front of the local gaol. He simply sat, miserable, staring at her back.

And she? She looked like a goddess carved from moonlight and leopard skin, dressed in white raiment with a golden sun pinned to her chest. Her bracelets, a tawdry but dazzling display, caught the light as she moved. Her eyes, the ones that had once nearly undone Pen's soul, rolled and gleamed with a ferocious vitality. But tonight, they were not fixed on him. Pen didn't notice, nor did he realize that the moment two gentlemen entered Box Number Three, the entire theater shifted its axis.

First, the atmosphere changed. With fewer than a dozen souls in the house, the first act had dragged on like molasses, and there had even been whispers of returning tickets. Actors yawned openly, talking over their lines, while even the formidable Mrs. Bingley spoke under her breath. Then, suddenly, the mood snapped. Mrs. Bingley began to bellow like a bull of Bashan; Bingley, usually listless, darted across the stage yelling like a man possessed. Every actor, from Garbetts to Miss Rouncy, poured their charm, swagger, and scowls directly at the two men in Box 3.

One was a quiet little man in black with a grey, shrewd face. The other, however, was a spectacle. He was tall and portly, draped in velvet coats adorned with the richest frog-braiding, his wrists heavy with jewels and his fingers encased in white kid gloves. When he unfolded his yellow pocket-handkerchief, a cloud of musk and bergamot drifted through the stale air, drowning out the smell of cheap tobacco. This was Mr. Dolphin, the great impresario from London, accompanied by his faithful secretary, the sardonic old gentleman Mr. William Minns.

Before ten minutes had passed, the whole troupe knew why they were there. They weren't putting on a show anymore; they were auditioning. Even Miss Fotheringay, whose heart was usually as dull as a brick, felt a strange flutter. She had little to do but look beautiful and strike picturesque poses around a prop child, and she did it to perfection. The actors tried everything to catch the eye of the "Stage Sultan"—hand gestures, dramatic flourishes, sheer volume—but Mr. Dolphin merely took a pinch of snuff from his great gold box.

Nothing worked until the final scene. Rolla stumbles onto the stage, staggering under the weight of his infant son, a monstrous toddler named Master Talma Bingley. Cora rushes forward, screaming, "Oh God, there's blood upon him!" At that precise moment, Mr. Dolphin clapped his hands, breaking into an enthusiastic bravo. He turned to his secretary, slapped him on the shoulder, and declared, "By Jove, Billy, she'll do!"

Old Billy, however, couldn't resist a jab. "Who taught her that dodge?" he asked. "I remember her at the Olympic, and hang me if she could say 'bo' to a goose." It was revealed to be little Mr. Bows in the orchestra who had coached her. As the curtain fell, the entire company swarmed around Miss Fotheringay, mixing congratulations with a distinct undercurrent of hatred.

But how had the mighty Mr. Dolphin descended to a dusty provincial theater on the verge of ruin? His London Museum Theatre was failing fast. Despite advertising perpetual triumphs and victories of old English comedy, the box office remained stubbornly empty. The legendary Hubbards had failed to pay anyone but themselves; the famous Crawdors had bombed in tragedy; Herr Garbage's lions and tigers had drawn briefly until one bit off Herr's shoulder, forcing the Lord Chamberlain to shut them down; and his grand Lyrical Drama, though spectacular, had almost crushed him financially. He was dragging out his season on half-salaries and feeble comedies, waiting for the day the newspapers announced his bankruptcy.

His salvation arrived in the form of the Most Noble the Marquis of Steyne. Lord Steyne, a refined patron of the arts, rarely attended plays unless it was for the ballet, but when he did, he commanded the proscenium box. Surrounded by a circle of friends, including the ever-joking Mr. Wagg and the serious Mr. Wenham, Lord Steyne often amused himself by reading letters from Major Pendennis, who was currently absent in the country.

"The secret is out," Mr. Wenham had recently declared, reading a letter aloud. "There's a woman in the case."

"Why, d—— it, Wenham, he's your age," Lord Steyne had chuckled.

"Pour les âmes bien nées, l'amour ne compte pas le nombre des années," Mr. Wenham had replied gallantly. "For my part, I hope to be a victim till I die."

"And yet," Lord Steyne had mused, "I'm told there is a young friend of Pendennis's who has gone wild about a certain Miss Fotheringay. She is as handsome, and apparently as good an actress, as ever put on rouge. I want a London engagement for her. Can't you get your friend Dolphin to come and see her?"

So, Mr. Wenham became the emissary. He slipped past the stagehands, who were currently cursing the corps-de-ballet, and found Mr. Dolphin. The manager's curses instantly died on his lips as he saw the visitor. He smoothed his coat, wiped his brow, and greeted Mr. Wenham with a smile that suggested he had never known anger in his life. He followed the messenger immediately to pay his respects to the Great Man.

Back in Chatteris, the news spread faster than gossip. Pen read the announcement in the local paper, where the editor lavishly praised Miss Fotheringay's talent and beauty, predicting her meteoric rise in the metropolis. Bingley, seizing the opportunity, began advertising "The Last Night of Miss Fotheringay's Engagement."

As the date approached, the town split into two camps. Sir Derby Oaks, sitting in the stage box, threw bouquets and caught glances with the enthusiasm of a schoolboy. Pen, meanwhile, remained in the almost deserted boxes, looking haggard and wretched. It seemed the only ones who truly cared if she stayed or left were Pen, Sir Derby, and perhaps Mr. Bows of the orchestra.

One evening, Mr. Bows emerged from his seat and ventured into the box where Pen sat. Holding out a gnarled hand, he invited Pen to walk. They strolled down the street and sat on Chatteris Bridge under the pale moonlight, discussing the girl.

"We may sit on the same bridge," Mr. Bows said, puffing on a pipe. "We've been in the same boat for a long time. You aren't the only fool who's made a mess of himself over that woman. And I have less excuse than you, being older and knowing her better. She has no more heart than the stone you're leaning against. If either of us—or the stone—fell into the water, she wouldn't care. Yes, she would care for me, because she needs me to teach her. She can't get along without me, so eventually, she'll send for me from London. But if she didn't need me? Well, she has no heart, no head, and no sense. She likes her dinner, and she's pleased when people admire her. That's all."

"And you do?" Pen asked, startled by the brutal honesty of the crabbed old man.

"It's a habit, like taking snuff or drinking drams," Bows replied calmly. "I've been taking her these five years, and I can't do without her. If she doesn't send for me, I'll follow her. But I know she will. Someday she'll marry and fling me over, just as I discard the end of this cigar."

With a flick of his wrist, the little flame dropped into the dark water below and vanished. Pen rode home that night, and for the first time in months, his thoughts drifted to someone other than himself.
