Some grow weary of the world before their fortieth year, while others retain the vigor of youth until eighty. Yet Margaret Van Eyck's heart remained perpetually green, burning with a youthful fervor for her young namesake. This affection was no fleeting whim; Margaret Van Eyck possessed a sharp insight into character, recognizing in Margaret Brandt a quality rare among women: sincerity. Beyond this inherent virtue, the younger girl enjoyed a powerful ally within the older artist's bosom.

Human nature operates on a peculiar paradox, one that puzzled the ancient Greeks but which the bard of Britain immortalized in verse. We warm to those whom we have served rather than to those who serve us. Consider the memories that haunt the warrior's mind: standing shoulder to shoulder against the tide of battle, shielding a friend's breast; sharing the bleak deserts of Darien where death stalks the evening wind; throwing one's cloak over a fallen companion to face the deadly dew alone; rescuing a friend from a foundering ship amidst breaking waves, only to suck the venom from an arrow wound. These recollections do not stem from gratitude for past kindnesses received, but from the indelible imprint of duties fulfilled.

Margaret Van Eyck had indeed been extraordinary in her kindness. She had broken through her solitary habits to visit the sickbed, nursing and soothing the invalid with a tenderness that surpassed all earthly medicines. Consequently, her heart opened fully to the recipient of her charity. Though her initial visits were motivated by love for Gerard, her devotion to Margaret now eclipsed even that, surpassing the depth of feeling she had ever harbored for Gerard himself.

When Margaret Brandt presented the roses blooming on her cheeks and the slip of parchment that caused them, the elder lady yielded her original plans without a murmur. "Sweetheart," she said gently, "I wished him to remain in Italy for five or six years, returning wealthy and a master artist. But your happiness comes first. Since you cannot live without him, we must hasten his return."

"Oh, madam!" the young woman replied, hanging her head and blushing. "But how shall I let him know? My skill fails me. He has departed for Italy, though I know not his destination. Wait! He listed the cities he would visit—Florence and Rome." Then, sensing the impracticality of sending a vague letter labeled "To Gerard, Italy," which might easily go astray, she cast an imploring gaze at her friend.

"You have arrived at the right place and time," the old lady declared. "Hans Memling was here today; he departs for Italy no later than next week, ostensibly to 'improve his hand.' I assure you, it is not premature."

"But how shall he locate Gerard?"

"Why, he knows Gerard intimately," the old artist explained. "They have dined together more than once, bound as hand and glove. Since their trade is identical, they will traverse the same paths and inevitably meet. Therefore, compose a long letter, copy this pardon into it, and I vouch for the messenger. Within six months, at the latest, Gerard shall receive it. Upon receipt, he will kiss it, press it to his heart, and fly home. What is this smile? And why do your cheeks burn? Why do you clutch me? Ah, yes! Happy days approach for my little pearl."

Meanwhile, in the kitchen, Martin sat before his black-jack, Reicht Heynes spinning beside him. The night proved eventful for Reicht; she interrogated him relentlessly.

Hans Memling, an old pupil of Jan Van Eyck and his sister, was a painter by profession—a fact Margaret's sneer did not negate—and a man of considerable integrity, save for one flaw. His passion for ale, wine, and strong beer exceeded all reason. This singular predilection prevented him from accumulating wealth and frequently compelled him to seek sustenance from Margaret Van Eyck, occasionally even a groat. Yet, this dependence forged a bond; she knew he would honor any commission she entrusted to him.

The letter was penned and delivered to Margaret Van Eyck the following week, alongside a piece of gold for Hans's travel expenses. He appeared eager to depart.

"All the better," remarked the old artist. "He will reach Italy the sooner."

However, just as horses may burn with restless energy to start, only to require a whip after the first few yards, so Hans's urgency cooled into inertia once he reached the principal inn at Tergou. There, two of his boon companions sat in the bay window. Entering for a farewell drink, Hans initially insisted on paying, but they refused, declaring they would treat him; everyone must treat him, including the landlord.

The result of this hospitality loosened Hans's tongue as if filled with oil. Amidst boasts of showing the Italians how to paint, tales of his martial exploits with a pike, and exaggerated accounts of his romantic conquests, he inadvertently revealed the contents of his mission. He confessed he carried a letter to a townsman of theirs, Gerard, calling him a good fellow. "You are all good fellows," he added, slapping Sybrandt on the back with such force as to nearly expel his breath.

Sybrandt, circling the table to evade this muscular greeting, listened intently, learning for the first time that Gerard had traveled to Italy. Seeking certainty, he feigned disbelief. "My brother Gerard is never in Italy."

"Ye lie, ye cur!" Hans roared, instantly turning irascible and failing to recognize that his opponent was the same man he had just praised and struck. "If he is ten times your brother, he is in Italy. Read the superscription!" He flung the letter onto the table.

Sybrandt examined it gravely but claimed inability to read it. However, a member of the company, boasting a rare accomplishment, took the document and read aloud: "To Gerard Eliassoen, of Tergou. These by the hand of the trusty Hans Memling, with all speed."

"'Tis excellently well writ," observed the reader, scrutinizing every character.

"Ay!" Hans boomed. "Small wonder! It is written by a famous hand—that of Margaret, sister of Jan Van Eyck. Blessed and honored be her memory! She is an old friend of mine." Hans then diverged into forty disparate topics.

Sybrandt slipped away to join Cornelis. Together, they pondered the news. Italy was an immense distance. Could they keep him there? "Keep him there?" Cornelis scoffed. "Nothing would keep him long from his Margaret." "Curse her!" Sybrandt cried. "Why didn't she die when she was about it?" "She die? She would outlive the plague to vex us," Cornelis retorted, furious at her refusal to succumb to death for their benefit.

These two black sheep, infecting one another with malice, eventually hatched a scheme to keep Gerard in Italy forever, thereby securing his share of their father's inheritance. Yet, planning required no talent, execution demanded it. Iniquity stalled until, as if Satan whispered into their ears simultaneously, they both shouted, "THE BURGOMASTER!"

They approached Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, who received them eagerly. Suspense, he knew, was intolerable. "You have news of Gerard?" he asked. They recounted the letter and Hans Memling. He listened with restless eyes. "Who wrote the letter?" "Margaret Van Eyck," they replied, assuming the content matched the signature. "Are you sure?" Ghysbrecht moved to a drawer, producing a paper written by Margaret Van Eyck during negotiations for her house. "Did she write like this?" "Yes," Sybrandt affirmed boldly.

"Good. Now, what would you have me do?" Ghysbrecht asked, his heart beating beneath a mask of feigned carelessness that bewildered them. They fumbled with their bonnets, stammering, hesitating, beating about the bush, gradually revealing their desire for a letter to detain Gerard in Italy—one to be substituted for the real one in Hans's wallet. As these rogues vacillated between respect for the burgomaster and suspicion that he was as crooked as they, a storm raged within Ghysbrecht. Vengeance urged Gerard to return and taste the law's weight. Prudence argued for keeping him a thousand miles away. But prudence also questioned why perform dirty work on a doubtful chance? Why empower these knaves to tarnish his name? Furthermore, Ghysbrecht suspected Gerard held a secret that could wound him deeply. Caution and vindictiveness shaped his response: "It is my duty to aid citizens who cannot write. But for the matter itself, I assume no responsibility. Tell me, then, what I shall write."

"Something concerning this Margaret," Sybrandt suggested.

"Ay, ay!" Ghysbrecht declared. "That she is false, that she is married to another—I will bail."

"Nay, burgomaster, nay!" Sybrandt cried. "Gerard would not believe it, or only half-believe it, and then he would return to verify. Say instead that she is dead."

"Dead! What, at her age, will he credit that?"

"Sooner than the other. She was nearly dead; thus, it is not a downright lie."

"Humph! And you think this will keep him in Italy?"

"We are sure of it, are we not, Cornelis?"
