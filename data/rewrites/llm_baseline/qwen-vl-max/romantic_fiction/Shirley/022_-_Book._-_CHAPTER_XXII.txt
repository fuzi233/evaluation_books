Moore’s strength had shown itself fully when defending his mill—yet it was in the relentless pursuit of the riot’s leaders that he revealed his true nature, fierce and unyielding. He spared the common folk who had joined the mob, those driven by hunger and false promises. To him, they were not villains, but victims—men misled by others, their spirits broken by hardship. He believed vengeance on such souls was tyranny, not justice. Though he recognized many of them as the sun rose over the wreckage, he let them pass without a word, without a glance, as if they were shadows slipping through his life.

But the ringleaders? They were strangers—men from distant cities, emissaries of chaos. Most were not workers at all, but outcasts: men who had lost everything—credit, home, dignity—and now sought to burn down what remained. These were the ones Moore hunted with the ferocity of a bloodhound. And he relished it. There was something thrilling in the chase, something that stirred his spirit more than the drudgery of weaving cloth ever could. His horse must have known it too—for it was ridden hard and often, its breath ragged under the strain. Moore lived on the road now, breathing in the wind and cold air like a man born to freedom. The scent of damp earth, the cry of the curlew, the vastness of the moors—it all fed his soul. Even the magistrate’s fear pleased him. They were timid men, slow to act, quick to tremble. He liked to see them flinch, to feel the weight of danger press upon them—because he knew the truth: they feared assassination. That dread had long held back every manufacturer, every official in the region. Only Helstone had ever stood firm. The old warrior knew death might come swiftly, but he faced it without trembling. It would have been his choice, had he had one.

Moore, too, knew the risk. But he wore it like armor. The knowledge that he pursued men who meant to kill him only sharpened his resolve. Fear? He did not know it. Not because he lacked feeling, but because his nature was hard, proud, and quiet—like stone beneath a storm. He rode late into the night across the moors, moonlit or dark, and each journey left him more alive than any day spent behind the counting-house. He felt stronger, clearer, freer.

Four leaders were wanted. Two were cornered near Stilbro’ within two weeks. The other two vanished toward Birmingham, and Moore set off after them, determined to bring them down. Yet even as he chased justice, he did not forget his mill. Repairs were simple—just carpenters and glaziers needed. The machines, his “metal darlings,” had survived intact, untouched by the mob’s fury. Still, one wondered—amid this fever of duty and danger—did he ever pause? Did he ever allow himself a moment to remember something softer, something quieter? He rarely visited Fieldhead. When he did, it was brief. At the rectory, he came only for business, never lingering. His course was rigid, unbroken.

Meanwhile, the world outside grew no calmer. War still roared across Europe, its storms unrelenting. No peace broke through the clouds of smoke and battle-dust. No rain fell to bless the olive; only the red rain of war nourished the laurel. Beneath Moore’s feet, Ruin dug tunnels, sapping the foundations of his life. Whether he walked or rode, whether he crossed his hearth or galloped over Rushedge, he heard the hollow echo and felt the ground tremble beneath him.

While Moore lived thus, how passed the summer with Shirley and Caroline?

Let us first go to Shirley.

Does she sit pale and sorrowful, longing for a lover who ignores her? Does she spend her days sewing or reading, eyes fixed on the page, heart closed to the world? Not at all. Shirley is radiant. Her face still holds its wistful look—but also her careless smile. She fills the ancient manor house with light and sound. The gallery echoes with her laughter. The dim hall, once silent, now rustles with the sweep of silk as she moves from room to room—carrying flowers to the peach-bloom salon, opening casements to let in the scent of mignonette and sweetbriar, bringing plants from the staircase to bathe in sunlight at the porch.

She sews sometimes—but never for long. Her thimble is barely on, her needle threaded, when a sudden thought calls her away. Perhaps she runs upstairs to find an old ivory needle-book, though she doesn’t need it. Or maybe to fix her hair, or tidy a drawer she remembers was messy. Or just to stand at a certain window, watching Briarfield church and rectory nestled in green trees.

She returns—only to be summoned again. Tartar, her dog, scrapes and whines at the door. She must open it, see to his water, watch him drink. Then, through the kitchen, she sees the courtyard: turkeys with their poults, peahens with chicks, Guinea fowls spotted with pearl, pigeons white and blue and purple. She cannot resist. She fetches a roll from the pantry and scatters crumbs. The birds rush to her, eager, plump, joyful—her feathered vassals.

John is at the stables. She must talk to him, pet her mare. As the cows return to be milked, she stays to inspect them—perhaps there are newborn calves, rejected lambs. She feeds them herself, guided by John. Then comes a question about farming—the "crofts," the "ings," the "holms." She dons her gipsy straw hat and walks with him over stiles and along hedgerows, discussing the land as if it were her own.

The afternoon slips into evening. She returns for tea. Afterward, she reads—not sitting upright, but curled on the rug, her seat a footstool, or perhaps beside Mrs. Pryor’s feet—where she always sat as a child. Old habits hold strong.

Tartar lies beside her, massive and calm, his black muzzle resting on his paws, like a wolf’s. One hand rests gently on his head. If she removes it, he groans.

Her mind is lost in the book. She does not move, does not speak—unless Mrs. Pryor says, “My dear, you should not have that great dog so near. He’s crushing your dress.”

“Oh, it’s only muslin. I can wear another tomorrow.”

“My dear, you should sit at a table when you read.”

“I’ll try, ma'am—but it’s so comfortable to do as I’ve always done.”

“My dear, please put the book down. You’re straining your eyes in this dim light.”

“No, ma'am. My eyes never tire.”

At last, the moon rises. A pale glow falls on the page. She closes the book, stands, and walks through the room. The story has stirred her—refreshed her, warmed her. Her thoughts soar. The quiet parlour, the clean hearth, the twilight sky—these things make the world seem like Eden. A deep joy flows through her veins—pure, untouched, given freely by God and Nature. She feels almost divine, lifted high on green hills, standing near where angels once looked down. She sees life as she wishes it—no, not as she wishes, for she does not wish. The vision spreads faster than thought, brighter than desire.

She says nothing. But if Mrs. Pryor speaks, she quietly leaves, walking up the dim gallery.

If Shirley were less idle, less reckless, less ignorant, she might write down these moments. She might capture the beauty, fix the vision. But she does not. She does not know her dreams are rare, her feelings extraordinary. She does not realize that the spring within her heart keeps it forever green.

She takes life easily. Is it not written in her eyes? In her calm moments, they are soft and lazy; in anger, they blaze like fire. Her nature lives in her gaze. So long as she is at peace, indolence, humor, tenderness fill that large gray sphere. But provoke her, and a red spark ignites—flame in an instant.

By mid-July, Shirley and Caroline were to begin their northern tour. But fate intervened. An invasion came to Fieldhead—a genteel raid. An uncle, an aunt, and two cousins from the south—Mr., Mrs., and Misses Sympson of Sympson Grove—arrived in full state. Hospitality demanded surrender. Shirley gave in—easily, almost willingly. This surprised Caroline, who knew her to be decisive and clever when victory was hers. Even Miss Helstone asked why she submitted so readily.

“Old feelings still have power,” Shirley replied. “I spent two years of my youth at Sympson Grove.”

“And do you like them?”

“Not really. Nothing in common. Little Harry Sympson, the only son, was different—he was kind. But he’s not coming here.”

On Sunday, the Fieldhead pew in Briarfield Church held a prim gentleman shifting his spectacles every three minutes, a placid woman in brown satin, and two young ladies in perfect, patterned attire. Shirley stood among them like a black swan—out of place, lonely. We leave her now in this respectable company, and turn to Caroline.

Separated from Shirley, Caroline returned to the rectory. She walked alone in remote paths, sat in the quiet parlour, or in the garden alcove where the sun shone bright yet sad on ripening currants and climbing roses. Their shadows fell in chequered patterns across her white summer dress—still as a statue.

She read books from her uncle’s library. The Greek and Latin meant nothing to her. The rest—Lady’s Magazines stained with saltwater from a sea voyage, mad Methodist tracts full of visions and miracles, letters from the dead, old English classics—had once nourished her childhood. Now they tasted flat.

For change, and to do good, she sewed—clothes for the poor, according to Miss Ainley’s plans. Sometimes, as tears fell slowly onto the fabric, she wondered how Miss Ainley managed to stay so calm. Her cottage was dim, her life solitary, yet she never seemed despondent.

“She says she has trained her thoughts to rise to heaven,” Caroline mused. “She admits this world offers little joy. She looks forward to the next. Like nuns—locked in cells, lit by iron lamps, wrapped in robes like shrouds. She says she fears death not at all. But then, neither did St. Simeon on his column, nor the Hindu on his bed of spikes. They have broken nature—they grow morbid. I still fear death. But perhaps it’s because I am young.”

She continued: “Nobody is to blame for this state of things. I cannot say how to fix it. But I feel something is wrong. Women—especially single women—should have more to do. Better chances. More purpose. I don’t think I offend God by saying this. I’m not impious. I believe God hears the groans we ignore, the sorrows we scorn. Society forbids us to speak of such ills—because speaking of them reminds people of their own helplessness. They hate to be reminded. They’d rather laugh than act.

“Look at the girls here—the Armitages, the Birtwhistles, the Sykeses. Their brothers are in business, in professions. Their sisters? Sewing, cooking, visiting—nothing else. No hope. They grow weak, their minds shrink. Their only dream is marriage. Most will never marry. They will die as they live. They scheme, dress, flirt. Men mock them. ‘The market is overstocked,’ they say. Fathers scold them, send them home. What do they expect them to do? Sew and cook? Forever? As if they had no talents beyond that. As if men themselves could endure such lives!

“Lucretia spinning at midnight? Solomon’s virtuous woman? They are held up as ideals. But Lucretia kept her maidens awake late. I wouldn’t want to be one of them. And the virtuous woman—she wasn’t just spinning. She made linen, sold it, bought land, planted vineyards. She was a manager. A real woman. I like her better. Strength and honor were her clothing. Her husband trusted her. She spoke wisely, ruled kindly. Her children blessed her. That’s a model worth following.

“But are our daughters taught to be like her? Can they reach such heights? Can you give them a field to grow in? Men of England—look at your girls. Many fade into consumption, or become bitter, sour old maids, envious, hateful, because life is a desert. Or worse—driven to use tricks and artifice to gain respect through marriage. Fathers—can you not change this? Not all at once. But think on it. Do not dismiss it with a joke or insult. You want to be proud of your daughters. Then give them work, give them purpose. Let them grow. Keep their minds narrow, and they will be a burden. Cultivate them—and they will be your joy, your comfort, your strength.”
