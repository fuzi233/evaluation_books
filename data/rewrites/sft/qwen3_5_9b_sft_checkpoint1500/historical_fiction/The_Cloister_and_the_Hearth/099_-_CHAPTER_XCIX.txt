Jerome remained as stern and gloomy as ever in his nature, yet his regard for Gerard had softened considerably. Throughout his time in England, Jerome had missed his brother far more than he had anticipated, frequently wondering where Gerard had gone. The news he received in Gouda elevated Jerome's esteem for him further, particularly the account of Gerard standing firm against the Pope and the Minorites regarding "the infernal heresy of the immaculate conception," a phrase Jerome used with vehemence. However, when a young novice tearfully recounted the cause of Gerard's sickness, Jerome's lingering contempt flared anew. "Dying for a woman?" he exclaimed.

Driven by a desire to prevent such a scandal, Jerome visited Brother Clement twice daily in his cell. He deployed all his former influence and eloquence, urging Clement to shed this unspiritual despondency and not to deprive the Church of his piety and gift at such a critical juncture. Gerard listened, acknowledging the validity of Jerome's reasoning and admiring his fortitude. He confessed his own frailty, appearing visibly to waste away toward the land of the faithful. On one occasion, Jerome declared that he had heard Gerard's story and heard it with pride. "But now," Jerome argued, "you ruin everything, Clement. This is the victory of earthly passion. It would have been better to yield to it and repent, rather than resisting it while she lived and succumbing to it now, both body and soul."

"Dearest Jerome," Clement replied, his tone so gentle that it stripped Jerome's warning of its harsh edge, "perhaps you misunderstand me. There is no passion here, but a profound affection for which I need not blush now, nor will I blush in heaven. Consider, Jerome, does the poor hound that dies of grief beside his master's grave commit sin through passion? Nor do I. Passion would have saved my life, but it cost me my soul. She was my guardian angel; she upheld me in my duties and acts of charity. Her countenance encouraged me in the pulpit, and her lips soothed me amidst ingratitude. She became intertwined with all that was noble in my existence. After leaning on her so long, I could not stand alone. And, dear Jerome, believe me, I am no rebel against Heaven. It is God's will to release me. When the earth was thrown upon her poor coffin, something snapped within my chest that can never be mended. I heard it, and I felt it. From that moment, Jerome, no food I placed in my mouth held any flavor. Even with my eyes bandaged, I could not distinguish bread from flesh by taste."

"Holy saints!" Jerome gasped.

"And from that same hour," Clement continued, "my deep depression vanished, and I smiled again. I often smile—what is the reason? I read it thus: He who holds the issues of life and death gave me that minute the great summons. Some cord of life snapped within me. He is exceedingly merciful. I should have lived unhappy, but He said, 'No; enough has been done, enough has been suffered. Poor, feeble, loving servant, thy shortcomings are forgiven, thy sorrows bring thee to thy end; come thou to thy rest!' I come, Lord, I come!"

Jerome groaned. "The Church has always possessed holy yet feeble servants," he murmured. "Now I would give ten years of my life to save yours. But I see it cannot be. Die in peace."

It was thus that within a few days, Gerard lay dying in a state of mind so holy and radiant that many aged saints gathered around to hear his final words. That evening he had seen Giles, begging him not to let poor Jack starve, ensuring that Little Gerard's trustees fulfilled their obligations, asking Giles to kiss his parents for him, and sending Denys to his friends in Burgundy. "Poor fellow, he will feel so strange here without his companion." Following this, Gerard had an audience with Jerome alone. What transpired between them remains unclear, though it must have been extraordinary, for Jerome emerged from the door with his hands crossed over his breast, his high head bowed low, sighing deeply as he walked away.

Two monks who stayed with him until Matins reported that throughout the night he broke out repeatedly into pious ejaculations, praises, and thanksgivings. Once, however, they noted that he wandered, seemingly thinking he saw her walking in green meadows with other spirits clad in white, beckoning to him. They all smiled and waved back, offering comfort. Both monks claimed—and perhaps it was merely fancy—that just before dawn, three light taps echoed against the wall, one after another, very slowly. The dying man heard them and whispered, "I come, love, I come."

This much is certain: Gerard did utter those words and prepared for his departure. He summoned all the monks keeping vigil at that hour. They arrived, hovering like gentle spirits around him, speaking holy words. Some prayed in silence, faces touching the ground, while others tenderly supported his head. When one of them mentioned Gerard's life of self-denial and charity, Gerard stopped him. Addressing them all, he said, "My dear brethren, take note that he who dies here so happily does not hold these new-fangled doctrines of man's merit. Oh, what a miserable hour would it be for me if I did! Nay, I hold, with the Apostles, their disciples in the Church, and the ancient fathers, that we are justified not by our own wisdom, or piety, or the works we have performed in holiness of heart, but by faith."

A profound silence fell, and the monks exchanged significant glances.

"Pray you, sweep the floor," the dying Christian said, his voice clear and strong, seeming supernaturally restored.

They immediately obeyed, filled with awe and curiosity.

"Make me a great cross with wood ashes."

They strewn the ashes in the shape of a large Cross upon the floor.

"Now lay me down on it, for so will I die."

Gently lifting him from his bed, they laid him upon the ash-cross.

"Shall we spread out thine arms, dear brother?" someone asked.

"Now God forbid! Am I worthy of that?"

He lay silent, his eyes raised in ecstasy. Soon afterward, speaking half to them, half to himself, he said, "Oh," with a subdued yet concentrated rapture, "I feel it buoyant. It lifts me, floating in the sky whence my merits had sunk me like lead."

Day broke, displaying his face cast upward in silent rapture, his hands clasped together, resembling Margaret's. Just about the hour she died, he spoke his last word in this world. "Jesu!" And with that word, he fell asleep.

They laid him out for his final resting place. Beneath his linen, they discovered a shirt made of horsehair. "Ah!" cried the young monks, "Behold a saint!" Beneath the haircloth, they found a long, thick tress of auburn hair. They started, horrified, and a babble of voices erupted, some condemning, some excusing.

Amidst the commotion, Jerome entered. Hearing the dispute, he turned to an ardent young monk named Basil, who was crying scandal the loudest. "Basil," Jerome said, "is she alive or dead that owned this hair?" "How may I know, Father?" Basil replied. "Then, for all you know, it may be the relic of a saint?" "Certes it may be," Basil said skeptically. "Have you then broken our rule, which says, 'Put ill construction on no act done by a brother which can be construed innocently'? Who are you to judge such a man as this was? Go to your cell, and stir not out for a week by way of penance." Jerome then removed the lock of hair.

When the coffin was to be closed, Jerome cleared the cell and placed the tress upon the dead man's bosom. "There, Clement," he said to the pale, lifeless face. Setting himself a penance for the action, Jerome nailed the coffin shut himself.

The following day, Gerard was buried in the Gouda churchyard. The monks followed him in procession from the convent. Jerome, clearly executing the wishes of the deceased, read the service. The grave was deep, containing a leaden coffin at the bottom. Poor Gerard, light as a feather due to his wasting condition, was lowered and placed by the side of it.

After the service, Jerome addressed the crowd of parishioners who had come to pay their last respects to their beloved friend. Speaking of the virtues of the departed, loud wailing and weeping burst forth, and tears fell upon the coffin like rain. The monks returned home, gathering in the refectory where Jerome spoke to them: "We have today laid a saint in the earth. The convent will keep his trentals, but we will feast, not fast; for our good brother is freed from the burden of the flesh; his labors are over, and he has entered into his joyful rest. I alone shall fast and do penance; for to my shame I confess, I was unjust to him, and knew not his worth until it was too late. And you, young monks, do not be curious to inquire whether the lock he bore on his bosom was a token of pure affection or the relic of a saint; but remember the heart he wore beneath: most of all, fix your eyes upon his life and conversation, and follow them as you may: for he was a holy man."
