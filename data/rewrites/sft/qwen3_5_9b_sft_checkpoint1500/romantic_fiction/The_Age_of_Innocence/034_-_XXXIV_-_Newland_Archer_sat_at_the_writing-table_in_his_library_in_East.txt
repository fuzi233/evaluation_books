Newland Archer sat alone at the writing-desk in his library, the heavy silence of the room pressing gently upon him. Outside, the city hummed with its usual frantic energy, but within these walls, time seemed to have folded inward, creating a sanctuary where the ghosts of the past lingered. He had returned from the Metropolitan Museum that morning, a grand affair celebrating the new galleries, and the vision of those vast halls—crowded with the spoils of centuries, buzzing with the fashionable crowd—had struck a chord deep within his memory. It had transported him, instantly, back to the old Cesnola rooms, where he remembered sitting on a hard leather divan, watching a slender figure in a sealskin cloak drift away into the museum's sparse corridors. That fleeting image had unlocked a floodgate of recollections, transforming the familiar, comfortable space around him into a theater of his entire life.

For over thirty years, this library had been the stage for the most significant moments of his existence. Here, nearly twenty-six years prior, his wife, May, had broken to him, with a modest blush and careful words, that she was expecting a child. It was here that their eldest son, Dallas—a boy of such delicate constitution he could not endure a midwinter church service—had been baptized by the Bishop of New York, a man whose magnificence and piety had long been the envy of his diocese. Dallas had first stumbled onto the floor, shouting "Dad," with the unbridled joy of childhood, while May and the nurse laughed softly behind the door. Later, it was here that their second child, Mary, who bore her mother's resemblance, had announced her engagement to Reggie Chivers's sensible son, and where Newland had kissed her through her wedding veil before sending her off to Grace Church. In a world that constantly shifted beneath one's feet, the institution of the "Grace Church wedding" remained a steadfast anchor, a tradition that never wavered.

It was in this same room that Newland and May had debated the futures of their children. They had discussed Dallas's studies, Mary's fierce disdain for "accomplishments" in favor of sports and charity, and Dallas's restlessness, which eventually steered him toward architecture. The younger generation, they noted, was breaking free from the rigid lanes of law and commerce, venturing into archaeology, landscape engineering, and a renewed fascination with the architectural heritage of their own country. They protested the meaningless use of the word "Colonial," reserving it only for the suburban millionaires who built imitation antiques.

Yet, above all, it was in this library that the Governor of New York, visiting from Albany, had once slammed his fist onto the desk and shouted, "Hang the professional politician! Men like you, Archer, are what the country needs!" The memory of that exhortation burned within him. How eager he had been then to roll up his sleeves and dive into the mud of public service, echoing the old call of Ned Winsett. However, looking back now, Newland was uncertain if men like himself truly possessed what the nation required. After a single term in the State Assembly, he had not been re-elected, retreating gratefully into municipal work and the writing of occasional articles for reforming weeklies. Yet, as he reflected, even his small contributions counted. Each brick mattered in the wall of progress. He was, by all accounts, a "good citizen"—his opinion sought for schools for crippled children, the reorganization of the Museum of Art, the founding of the Grolier Club, and countless other causes. His days were full, filled decently, and he believed it was all a man ought to ask.

And yet, there was something he knew he had missed: the flower of life. He viewed it now with a serene detachment, much like observing a beloved character in a novel. Ellen Olenska had become the composite vision of everything he had lacked. That faint, tenuous image had kept him from seeking comfort in other women. He had been a faithful husband; when May died, suddenly carried off by pneumonia while nursing their youngest child, he had mourned her honestly. Their long years together had taught him that marriage need not be a battlefield of ugly appetites if it maintained the dignity of a duty. As he looked around the room, adorned with Dallas's English mezzotints, Chippendale cabinets, and soft electric lights, he honored his past and mourned for it. There was goodness in the old ways.

His eyes drifted back to the old Eastlake writing-table, which he had never been willing to discard, and to the first photograph of May that still stood beside his inkstand. There she was, tall and willowy in her starched muslin, as he had seen her beneath the orange trees in the Mission garden. She had remained, in spirit, at that same height: generous, faithful, unwearied. But she lacked imagination, incapable of growth, blind to the shifting tides of the world around her. This blindness had forced her children to hide their true selves from her, just as Newland had hidden his. From the start, there had been a joint pretense of sameness, an innocent family hypocrisy in which father and children colluded. She had died believing the world was a good place, convinced that Newland would ensure Dallas passed on the same principles that had shaped their parents' lives, and that Dallas would, in turn, pass them to little Bill. Of Mary, she was certain. Having snatched little Bill from the grave and devoted her life to saving him, she had gone peacefully to her place in the Archer vault at St. Mark's, safe from the terrifying "trend" that her daughter-in-law, Fanny, had never fully comprehended.

Opposite May's portrait stood one of Mary Chivers. She was as tall and fair as her mother, yet large-waisted, flat-chested, and slightly slouching, conforming to the altered fashions of the day. Mary's mighty feats of athleticism would have been impossible in her mother's twenty-inch waist. The difference seemed symbolic: the mother's life had been tightly laced, whereas Mary, though equally conventional and no more intelligent, led a broader life and held more tolerant views. There was goodness in the new order, too.

The telephone clicked, startling Newland from his reverie. He reached for the receiver. "Chicago wants you," the operator announced.

It must be Dallas, who had been sent to Chicago to discuss plans for the Lakeside Palace. "Hallo, Dad—Yes: Dallas," Newland answered, listening. The voice was close, natural, as if Dallas were lounging in his favorite armchair by the fire. The laughter that broke into the conversation startled him; it was wonderful that across thousands of miles of forest, river, and bustling city, Dallas's laugh could still sound so intimate. "Do come," Dallas urged, his tone urgent. "It'll be our last time together in this kind of way."

Newland paced the room, his heart stirring. Dallas was right; their bond was unique. Though Newland felt drawn toward his future daughter-in-law, Fanny Beaufort, it was tempting to seize this final opportunity for solitude with his son. There was no reason he couldn't, save for the profound realization that he had lost the habit of travel. May had abhorred moving unless necessary, and after Dallas graduated, the family had undertaken the traditional European tour. Newland had refused to go to Paris, insisting they stay together, a decision that had pleased May. Since her death, however, inertia had held him fast. Habit, memory, and a sudden, instinctive shrinking from new things had anchored him.

Now, reviewing his past, he saw how deeply he had sunk into a rut. The rigid moral divisions of his generation—right versus wrong, honest versus dishonest—had left little room for the unexpected. Yet, sometimes, a man's imagination rose above its daily level, surveying the winding paths of destiny. What remained of the little world he had grown up in? He recalled Lawrence Lefferts's sneering prophecy: "If things go on at this rate, our children will be marrying Beaufort's bastards." And yet, here was Dallas, engaged to Fanny Beaufort, and the world accepted it without a murmur. Even Aunt Janey, still so precisely herself, had brought out her emeralds and seed-pearls, feeling them twinkle beautifully on Fanny, who wore them with the grace of an Isabey miniature.

Fanny Beaufort, appearing in New York at eighteen after her parents' death, had won the heart of the city much as Madame Olenska had thirty years prior. Society took her joyfully for granted, ignoring the obscurity of her father's past and her origins. Nothing could more clearly illustrate the distance the world had traveled. People were too busy with reforms, fads, and frivolities to trouble themselves about neighbors' histories.

Newland found himself standing by the window of his hotel in Paris, looking out at the stately gaiety of the streets. His heart beat with a confusion and eagerness he hadn't felt in decades. He wondered if it was thus that his son's heart behaved in the presence of Miss Beaufort—and decided it was not. Dallas acted with a cool composure, taking for granted that his family would approve. These young people, Newland realized, assumed they *would* get what they wanted, whereas his generation had always assumed they *shouldn't*. Could the certainty of the former ever make one's heart beat as wildly as the uncertainty of the latter?
