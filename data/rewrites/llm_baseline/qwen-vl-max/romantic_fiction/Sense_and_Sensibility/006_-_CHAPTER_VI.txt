The journey to Barton had begun in silence and sorrow, the weight of their recent losses pressing heavily upon each of them. The miles stretched on with little joy, the landscape passing by unnoticed as grief lingered in their hearts. But as they neared their destination, a quiet hope stirred within them—this was to be their new home, a place where life might yet unfold with warmth and meaning. When at last they caught sight of Barton Valley, the bleakness gave way to a gentle cheerfulness. It lay before them like a promise: green and fertile, dotted with trees and rich with pasture, cradled between rolling hills.

After winding through the valley for over a mile, they arrived at Barton Cottage. A small, grassy courtyard greeted them, enclosed by a neat wicket gate that opened into a modest but tidy space. The cottage itself was unassuming—small, yes, but well-built and snug. Its walls were clean, its roof tiled, its windows bright. Yet it lacked the charm of true rusticity: no honeysuckles climbed the sides, no green shutters adorned the frames. Still, it felt solid, secure, and full of potential.

A narrow passage ran straight through the house, leading to the garden behind. On either side of the entrance stood a sitting room—each about sixteen feet square—simple but inviting. Beyond lay the kitchen, servants’ rooms, and the staircase. Four bedrooms and two garrets completed the structure. Though newly built and in excellent repair, it seemed almost insignificant compared to the grandeur of Norland. Yet as they stepped inside, tears threatened once more—not from sadness, but from memory. But those tears soon dried. The servants welcomed them with open arms and eager smiles, and each of the Dashwoods resolved to meet the moment with grace and courage. They would not let sorrow claim this new beginning.

It was early September, and the weather was kind. Sunlight bathed the cottage in golden light, making the surroundings seem even more welcoming than they might have otherwise appeared. The view from the windows was especially enchanting. Behind the house, high hills rose steeply—some covered in wild downland, others thick with trees and fields. The village of Barton nestled on one of these slopes, forming a peaceful picture visible from the cottage’s windows. Before them, the valley spread wide, stretching into distant lands. To the south, the hills closed the valley, but beyond them, another stretch of land wound between towering peaks, offering a glimpse of the wider world.

Mrs. Dashwood surveyed the house with thoughtful eyes. While it was plainly too small for her accustomed way of life, she found comfort in its simplicity. She was pleased with the layout, though she already imagined ways to improve it. “It’s certainly not large enough,” she said, “but we can make do for now. And come spring—if fortune favors us—we may consider building.” Her mind raced with plans: combining the passage with one parlor, adding a new drawing room, expanding the upstairs chambers. “I’d love to have finer stairs,” she mused. “But one cannot expect everything. With time and money, we’ll make this a home worth loving.”

For the present, however, they accepted the cottage as it was. Each member of the family busied themselves settling in—arranging books, hanging pictures, placing familiar objects around the rooms to bring a sense of belonging. Marianne’s piano was carefully unpacked and set in its proper place; Elinor’s drawings were pinned to the walls, bringing quiet beauty to their sitting room.

Just after breakfast the next morning, their peace was gently interrupted by the arrival of Sir John Middleton, their landlord. He came not only to welcome them but to offer his hospitality freely. Tall and pleasant-faced, he was about forty, with a warm smile and a manner as cordial as his letter had suggested. Though he had once visited Stanhill, the girls did not remember him. Still, his presence brought immediate comfort. He spoke of his desire for friendship, insisted they dine daily at Barton Park until settled, and pressed his invitations with such sincerity that refusal would have been rude.

His kindness went beyond words. Within hours, a basket brimming with fresh vegetables and fruit arrived from the park, followed later by a generous gift of game. He offered to carry their letters to and from the post, and insisted on sending them his newspaper every day. Lady Middleton, too, had sent a message—polite and thoughtful—expressing her wish to call upon Mrs. Dashwood when convenient. The invitation was returned with equal courtesy, and the following day, Lady Middleton herself made her appearance.

The Dashwoods had been eager to meet her, knowing her disposition would greatly influence their time at Barton. She was lovely—perhaps twenty-seven or twenty-eight—with a graceful figure and a refined face. Her manners were polished, elegant, and composed. But beneath the surface lay a reserve that cooled the initial impression. She spoke little, offering only the most ordinary remarks, and her conversation lacked warmth. She was well-bred, yes, but cold, cautious, and seemingly unable to share anything truly personal.

Yet Sir John, ever cheerful, filled the silence with stories and laughter. And Lady Middleton, wise in social matters, had brought their eldest son—a bright-eyed boy of six—to aid in conversation. The child clung to his mother’s side, shy and quiet, which surprised her ladyship, who could not understand why he was so timid in company when he was so lively at home.

In such visits, children often serve as a lifeline to conversation. Here, ten minutes passed debating whether the boy resembled his father or mother more—and if so, in what way. Opinions differed wildly, and each person expressed astonishment at the others’ views. The topic kept the ladies engaged just long enough to avoid awkward silences.

And as Sir John prepared to leave, he made sure to secure their promise to dine at Barton Park the very next day. The Dashwoods, still adjusting to their new life, smiled politely—but already, the future held new connections, new expectations, and perhaps, a fragile hope for happiness.
