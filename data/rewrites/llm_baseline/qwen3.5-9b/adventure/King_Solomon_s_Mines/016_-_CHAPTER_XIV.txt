The flanking regiments vanished into the silence of the hills, slipping unseen behind the ridges to avoid Twala's sharp-eyed scouts. For nearly thirty minutes, the battlefield held its breath. Only then did the main force—the "Greys" and their supporting "Buffaloes"—begin to stir. These were the army's chest, the vanguard destined to absorb the initial blow.

Both units were fresh and full-strength. The Greys had rested during the morning's skirmish, losing only a handful of men while repelling a partial attack. The Buffaloes, holding the third defensive line on the left, had barely engaged at all.

Infadoos, a seasoned general who understood the psychology of war, addressed his men. He spoke in poetic terms, praising the honor of leading the charge and highlighting the presence of the "Great White Warrior" among their ranks. He promised riches and rank to survivors, urging them to fight for King Ignosi.

I gazed down the endless rows of black plumes and stoic faces. A chill ran through me. Within an hour, most of these magnificent veterans—all men under forty—would lie dead or dying in the dust. It was a calculated sacrifice, a reckless gamble common to great generals who know that sacrificing a few saves the many. They knew their fate was sealed; they were walking toward extinction to buy a chance for the rest of the army. Yet, there was not a tremor in their step, not a flicker of fear on a single face. They faced certain death with absolute calm. My own heart hammered with dread, and I breathed a sigh of envy at their terrifying devotion to duty.

"Behold your king!" Infadoos cried, pointing to Ignosi. "Fight and fall for him, as brave men do. Cursed be the name of any who shrink from death for their sovereign!"

A hush fell over the phalanx, broken only by the rhythmic *tap-tap-tapping* of six thousand spear handles against shields. The sound swelled into a thunderous roar that echoed off the mountains, then faded into silence. Finally, the royal salute cracked through the air.

Ignosi raised his battle-axe in acknowledgment. The Greys moved forward in a triple line, each file containing roughly a thousand warriors. Once they had advanced five hundred yards, Ignosi took command of the Buffaloes, who formed up similarly behind them. With a collective prayer for survival echoing in my mind, we marched on. I had never felt so exposed, my odds of survival lower than at any other moment in my life.

By the time we reached the plateau's edge, the Greys were halfway down the steep slope onto a narrow tongue of grassland jutting into the valley below. The plains beyond were alive with chaos; Twala's camp erupted in activity as regiment after regiment trotted forward to meet us. This bottleneck was deadly: only four hundred yards wide at the base and narrowing to ninety yards at the tip.

The Greys formed a column descending the hill, then spread out into their lethal triple line just as the terrain opened up. Behind them, the Buffaloes took cover on slightly higher ground, one hundred yards back. From here, we observed Twala's massive force—forty thousand strong, reinforced since the morning. They halted at the gorge's entrance, realizing only one regiment could pass through the narrow passage at a time. Blocking the throat of the canyon stood the Greys, ready to hold the line like the legendary Romans defending a bridge.

Twala hesitated, unwilling to risk his elite troops against these grim, immovable ranks. Finally, a tall general bearing ostrich plumes emerged—it was Twala himself. He signaled, and the first regiment charged.

The Greys waited silently until the attackers were within forty yards. Then, a storm of throwing knives (*tollas*) rained down. Immediately, the Greys surged forward with a roar, meeting the enemy in a clash of steel and bone. The sound of colliding shields rolled like thunder, and the plain flashed with the gleam of spears. The fighting was brutal and swift. The attacking line thinned rapidly, swept aside by the Greys like a wave washing over a ridge. That regiment was annihilated, leaving the Greys with only two lines intact—a third of their number dead.

Silence returned, save for the heavy breathing of the survivors. I spotted Sir Henry's yellow beard moving among the ranks, organizing the defense. He was alive!

We moved closer to the carnage, where four thousand bodies littered the ground, staining the earth red. Ignosi ordered that no enemy wounded be killed; the command was obeyed with grim precision.

Before we could process the horror, a second regiment, distinguished by white plumes, charged the remaining two thousand Greys. The pattern repeated: the enemy closed to forty yards, unleashed knives, and the Greys counter-attacked. This time, the outcome hung in the balance. Young warriors fought with ferocious intensity, seemingly overwhelming the veterans by sheer weight of numbers. Hundreds fell every minute, the air filled with the hiss of victory cries ("*S'gee, s'gee!*") and the groans of the dying.

Yet, discipline and experience prevailed. Just as defeat seemed inevitable, Sir Henry's voice cut through the din. His battle-axe flashed overhead, rallying the line. The Greys stiffened, becoming an immovable object against which the enemy waves shattered. Slowly, they pushed forward. Without the obscuring smoke of gunpowder, the slaughter was visible to all. In another minute, the assault faltered.

"These are *men*, indeed!" Ignosi shouted, grinding his teeth in excitement. "They will conquer again!"

Like smoke blown from a cannon, the white-plumed regiment broke and fled. The Greys, though reduced to a ragged six hundred from their original three thousand, stood victorious. Instead of retreating, they chased the fleeing enemy, securing a small knoll and forming a protective ring around it. Standing atop the mound, I saw Sir Henry, unharmed, alongside Infadoos.

Then, Twala's remaining regiments poured down upon the isolated band. The battle resumed with renewed fury.

I, usually a coward who preferred to flee, felt a strange transformation. Watching the ferocity of the warriors, my blood heated, and a savage desire to kill seized me. I looked at the ranks behind us—hands twitching, eyes burning with the hunger of battle—and wondered if I too had changed. Ignosi remained remarkably calm, though his teeth still ground together.

"Shall we stand here and rot?" I asked. "While Twala devours our brothers?"

"Now is the moment," Ignosi replied. "Let us strike."

As a fresh regiment rushed past the mound, Ignosi signaled the Buffaloes to charge. Screaming their war-cry, they surged forward like a tidal wave.

Chaos ensued. An irregular but powerful advance shook the ground, followed by a deafening clash of spears and a red mist of blood. When clarity returned, I found myself amidst the remnants of the Greys, directly behind Sir Henry. He had told me later that the Buffaloes' initial charge had thrown me clear, allowing him to drag me into safety.

The fight continued in a cycle of heroic desperation. Enemy after enemy surged against our shrinking circle, only to be beaten back. The scene was a testament to courage: battalions marching over heaps of their own dead, lifting corpses to gain purchase, only to add new layers to the pile. Infadoos remained cool, issuing orders and jokes to keep spirits high, stepping personally into the thickest of the fray.

Sir Henry was equally formidable. His ostrich plumes had been torn away, revealing his long yellow hair streaming in the wind. Clad in armor slick with blood, wielding his axe with the fury of a berserker, he was unstoppable. Every time an enemy dared approach, Henry roared "*O-hoy!*" and cleaved through shield, helmet, and skull alike. He was the "wizard" who neither failed nor spared.

Suddenly, a cry rang out: "*Twala, y' Twala!*"

From the melee stepped the one-eyed giant himself, armored in chainmail and wielding axe and shield.

"Where art thou, Incubu, thou white man who slew Scragga my son?" Twala screamed, hurling a knife at Sir Henry. Henry deflected it, the blade lodging harmlessly in his shield. Undeterred, Twala lunged, striking Henry's shield with such force that the Dane sank to his knees.

Just as the duel seemed poised to decide the battle, a shout of dismay rose from the surrounding regiments. Looking up, I saw the turning tide. To our right and left, the plains were alive with charging warriors. The outflanking squadrons had arrived.

Ignosi had predicted this trap. While Twala's army focused entirely on the bloody struggle in the center, believing our flankers were hidden reserves, the Impi cavalry leaped like greyhounds onto their unprotected sides.

Within five minutes, the battle was won. Surrounded and terrified by the slaughter, Twala's forces broke into a panicked rout. The plain between us and the town of Loo was scattered with fleeing soldiers. Our enemies melted away as if by magic, leaving us standing alone amidst the devastation.

Of the noble Greys, only ninety-five men remained on their feet. Over three thousand four hundred had fallen.

"Ye have upheld the reputation of your regiment," Infadoos said calmly, tending to a wound on his arm. "Your deeds today will be sung by your grandchildren."
