Elinor waited till the room had settled into its usual evening order—the cards laid straight, the candles trimmed, the air faintly warmed by the last of the tea—and then, with a steadiness that was careful rather than bold, turned again to the confidence Lucy had pressed upon her.

“I should be unworthy of what you have honoured me with,” she said, “if I did not wish for its continuance, or if I pretended to have no further curiosity in what so deeply concerns you. I shall not apologize, therefore, for speaking of it again.”

“Thank you,” Lucy cried, all glow and gratitude at once, as if Elinor had unclasped a tight bodice for her. “You have set my heart at ease. For I was—somehow or other—afraid you had took offence at what I told you on Monday.”

“Offended!” Elinor answered with sincere astonishment, and an honesty that made her voice warmer than her face. “How could you suppose it? Nothing could be farther from my intention than to give you such an idea. Could you have any motive for trusting me, that was not—at once—honourable to you, and flattering to me?”

“And yet I do assure you,” Lucy persisted, her little sharp eyes bright with meaning, “there seemed to me a coldness—something displeased—in your manner, that made me quite uncomfortable. I felt sure you was angry with me; and I have been quarrelling with myself ever since for taking such a liberty as to trouble you with my affairs. But I am very glad to find it was only my own fancy, and that you really do not blame me. If you knew what a consolation it is to me, to relieve my heart by speaking to you of what I am thinking of every moment of my life, your compassion would make you overlook every thing else, I am sure.”

Elinor, who had listened as one listens to a soundscape one cannot leave—a steady drip, a wheel’s incessant creak—answered with gentleness, because gentleness was her habit even when her feelings were sore.

“Indeed, I can easily believe it was a great relief to you, to acknowledge your situation to me; and you shall never have reason to repent it. Your case is unfortunate. You seem surrounded with difficulties, and you will need all your mutual affection to support you under them. Mr. Ferrars, I believe, is entirely dependent on his mother.”

“He has only two thousand pounds of his own,” Lucy said quickly, as if she had counted it out coin by coin on her tongue. “It would be madness to marry upon that; though for my own part I could give up every prospect of more without a sigh. I have been always used to a small income, and could struggle with any poverty for him. But I love him too well to be the selfish means of robbing him—perhaps—of all that his mother might give him if he married to please her. We must wait—maybe for many years. With almost every other man in the world, it would be alarming; but Edward’s affection and constancy—nothing can deprive me of that, I know.”

“That conviction must be every thing to you,” Elinor said, “and he is undoubtedly supported by the same trust in yours. If the strength of your reciprocal attachment had failed—as between many people, and under many circumstances it naturally would during a four years’ engagement—your situation would have been pitiable indeed.”

Lucy lifted her eyes at this; but Elinor kept her own countenance guarded, as a cook guards a simmering pot from boiling over—watchful, silent, determined that no sudden gust of feeling should spill what must not be shown.

“Edward’s love for me,” Lucy continued, “has been put to the test by our long—very long—absence since we were first engaged; and it has stood the trial so well, that I should be unpardonable to doubt it now. I can safely say he has never gave me one moment’s alarm on that account from the first.”

Elinor hardly knew whether to smile at the complacency, or sigh at the blindness—or the skill—with which it was spoken. Lucy, encouraged by her own certainty, went on as if she were setting out proofs like ingredients upon a board.

“I am rather of a jealous temper by nature, too; and from our different situations in life—his being so much more in the world than me—and our continual separation, I was enough inclined for suspicion, to have found out the truth in an instant, if there had been the slightest alteration in his behaviour when we met, or any lowness of spirits that I could not account for; or if he had talked more of one lady than another, or seemed in any respect less happy at Longstaple than he used to be. I do not mean to say that I am particularly observant or quick-sighted in general; but in such a case, I am sure I could not be deceived.”

All this, Elinor thought, may be very prettily said; but it can impose upon neither of us.

After a short silence—filled, as so many silences are, by the small domestic noises that keep a household alive: the light scratch of a chair-leg, the soft fall of a card on felt, the crackle of a coal that remembered it had once been wood—Elinor asked, because she must.

“But what are your views? Or have you none but that of waiting for Mrs. Ferrars’s death—which is a melancholy and shocking extremity? Is her son determined to submit to this, and to all the tediousness of the many years of suspense in which it may involve you, rather than run the risk of her displeasure for a while by owning the truth?”

“If we could be certain it would be only for a while!” Lucy returned. “But Mrs. Ferrars is a very headstrong proud woman; and in her first fit of anger, upon hearing it, would very likely secure every thing to Robert. And the idea of that—for Edward’s sake—frightens away all my inclination for hasty measures.”

“And for your own sake too,” Elinor said, unable to leave the point untouched, “or you are carrying disinterestedness beyond reason.”

Lucy looked at her again and said nothing, the silence held between them like a dish set down and not tasted.

“Do you know Mr. Robert Ferrars?” Elinor asked at last.

“Not at all. I never saw him,” Lucy replied, with a quick little shrug. “But I fancy he is very unlike his brother—silly, and a great coxcomb.”

“A great coxcomb!” Miss Steele repeated at once, her ear catching at the words as a spoon catches at a pot’s rim. Marianne’s music had paused for a breath, and Nancy’s voice darted into it. “Oh, they are talking of their favourite beaux, I dare say.”

“No, sister,” Lucy cried, colouring with vexation, “you are mistaken there. Our favourite beaux are not great coxcombs.”

“I can answer for it that Miss Dashwood’s is not,” said Mrs. Jennings, laughing heartily, in that full-bodied, comfortable manner of hers, as if her mirth were a kind of hospitality. “For he is one of the modestest, prettiest behaved young men I ever saw. But as for Lucy—she is such a sly little creature, there is no finding out who she likes.”

“Oh,” Nancy cried, glancing round with significance, “I dare say Lucy’s beau is quite as modest and pretty behaved as Miss Dashwood’s.”

Elinor blushed in spite of all her composure. Lucy bit her lip, and turned on her sister a look sharp enough to cut thread. A mutual silence followed; and then Marianne, as if she felt the room growing coarse with talk, threw herself again into her concerto with such power that the notes rose like a wind through the trees—sound made clean, sound made shelter. Under that magnificent cover, Lucy leaned closer, and lowered her voice.

“I will honestly tell you of one scheme,” she whispered, “which has lately come into my head for bringing matters to bear. Indeed I am bound to let you into the secret, for you are a party concerned. I dare say you have seen enough of Edward to know he would prefer the church to every other profession. Now my plan is that he should take orders as soon as he can; and then, through your interest—which I am sure you would be kind enough to use out of friendship for him, and I hope out of some regard to me—your brother might be persuaded to give him Norland living; which I understand is a very good one, and the present incumbent not likely to live a great while. That would be enough for us to marry upon; and we might trust to time and chance for the rest.”

“I should always be happy,” Elinor replied, “to show any mark of my esteem and friendship for Mr. Ferrars. But do you not perceive that my interest on such an occasion would be perfectly unnecessary? He is brother to Mrs. John Dashwood—that must be recommendation enough to her husband.”

“But Mrs. John Dashwood would not much approve of Edward’s going into orders.”

“Then I rather suspect that my interest would do very little.”

Again they were silent, while the music filled the spaces between them with a kind of living hush, like the steady pulse of a well-managed kitchen where every hand knows its task. At length Lucy sighed, deep enough to seem practiced.

“I believe it would be the wisest way to put an end to the business at once by dissolving the engagement. We seem so beset with difficulties on every side, that though it would make us miserable for a time, we should be happier perhaps in the end. But you will not give me your advice, Miss Dashwood?”

“No,” Elinor answered, and forced a smile to cover feelings that struggled like a flame in draught. “On such a subject I certainly will not. You know very well my opinion would have no weight with you, unless it were on the side of your wishes.”

“Indeed you wrong me,” Lucy said, with great solemnity, as if she were pronouncing a vow. “I know nobody of whose judgment I think so highly as I do of yours; and I do really believe that if you was to say to me, ‘I advise you by all means to put an end to your engagement with Edward Ferrars; it will be more for the happiness of both of you,’ I should resolve upon doing it immediately.”

Elinor blushed—not now for herself, but for the insincerity of the woman who could speak so, and yet hold so tightly.

“This compliment,” she said, “would effectually frighten me from giving any opinion, had I formed one. It raises my influence much too high. The power of dividing two people so tenderly attached is too much for an indifferent person.”

“’Tis because you are an indifferent person,” Lucy returned, with a little pique, laying a pointed stress on the word, “that your judgment might justly have such weight with me. If you could be supposed to be biased in any respect by your own feelings, your opinion would not be worth having.”

Elinor thought it wisest to make no answer. There are moments when speech is a kind of seasoning that will only draw out bitterness; and she had no desire to provoke them both into an ease and unreserve that could end only in something unworthy. She was even half determined never to mention the subject again.

A pause followed; long enough for the room’s ordinary life to assert itself—the gentle slap of cards, Mrs. Jennings’s contented chuckle, and, beyond the walls, the more ancient sounds of the place: a distant gate’s complaint, the soft animal breath of the night wind over the park. Then Lucy, as if she had never been piqued at all, returned to her accustomed complacency.

“Shall you be in town this winter, Miss Dashwood?”

“Certainly not.”

“I am sorry for that,” Lucy said, and Elinor saw her eyes brighten at the refusal as plainly as if a candle had been raised under them. “It would have gave me such pleasure to meet you there! But I dare say you will go for all that. To be sure, your brother and sister will ask you to come to them.”

“It will not be in my power to accept their invitation, if they do.”

“How unlucky that is! I had quite depended upon meeting you there. Anne and me are to go the latter end of January to some relations who have been wanting us to visit them these several years. But I only go for the sake of seeing Edward. He will be there in February; otherwise London would have no charms for me. I have not spirits for it.”

Before Elinor could reply, the first rubber ended, and she was called to the card-table. Thus the confidential discourse concluded—not with reluctance on either side, for nothing had been said to make them dislike each other less than they had done before. Elinor sat down with a melancholy persuasion, heavier than any mere jealousy: that Edward was not only without affection for the person meant to be his wife, but had not even the chance of being tolerably happy in marriage—the sort of happiness sincere affection on her side might have given him; for self-interest alone could induce a woman to hold a man to an engagement of which she seemed so thoroughly aware that he was weary.

From this time, Elinor never revived the subject. When Lucy entered upon it—and she seldom missed an opportunity, being particularly careful to inform her confidante of her “happiness” whenever a letter came from Edward—Elinor treated it with calmness and caution, and dismissed it as soon as civility would allow. She felt such conversations to be an indulgence Lucy did not deserve, and a danger to herself.

Meanwhile, the Miss Steeles’ visit at Barton Park stretched itself far beyond the first invitation, as dough stretches under a patient hand—pressed, yielded, pressed again. Their favour increased; they could not be spared. Sir John would not hear of their going. In spite of their numerous engagements in Exeter—in spite of the absolute necessity of returning immediately, which rose in full force at the end of every week and was as regularly forgotten at the beginning of the next—they were prevailed on to stay nearly two months, and to assist in the due celebration of that season which seems to require, to proclaim its importance, more than an ordinary share of private balls and large dinners; nights of bright rooms and louder laughter, while outside the older world held to its own quieter language of wind in the trees and distant hoofbeats on winter ground.
