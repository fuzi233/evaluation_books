As Newland Archer navigated the crowded opera house, he found himself in a state of heightened self-consciousness. The commotion surrounding the box where his fiancée, May Welland, sat alongside her mother and aunt drew his attention. At first, he couldn't quite place the woman in the Empire dress, her presence sparking whispers among the other patrons. It wasn't until the truth dawned on him that a wave of indignation washed over him. The Mingotts, of all people, wouldn't dare.

But they had. The hushed conversations behind him left little doubt in Archer's mind that the young woman was none other than May's cousin, the enigmatic Ellen Olenska, known affectionately as "poor Ellen" within the family. Archer recalled hearing from Miss Welland, without any hint of disapproval, that Ellen had recently returned from Europe and had visited her cousin, who was staying with old Mrs. Mingott. As someone who valued family solidarity, Archer appreciated the Mingotts' unwavering support for their wayward relatives. His own fiancée's kindness towards her cousin was something he wholeheartedly approved of, but he couldn't help feeling that introducing Ellen to the public, especially at the opera, was a step too far. The thought of announcing his own engagement to May, mere weeks away, amidst this spectacle made him feel uneasy.

Archer couldn't help but think of old Sillerton Jackson's sentiments on the matter. He didn't think the Mingotts would have attempted such a bold move. Yet, he knew that anyone who dared challenge the social norms within the rarefied world of Fifth Avenue would have to contend with the formidable matriarch, Mrs. Manson Mingott. Her audacious marriage to the head of the wealthy Mingott line, followed by the union of her daughters to foreign husbands, had long been the subject of whispered conversations. The construction of her imposing house, with its pale cream-colored stone façade, stood as a testament to her unwavering confidence. The once-isolated mansion near the Central Park now served as a symbol of her unapologetic individuality.

The Mingott daughters, now grown, had chosen to maintain their distance from their mother, content to live abroad rather than submit to the constraints of New York society. Meanwhile, Mrs. Mingott remained at home, surrounded by the remnants of her European sojourns and the pre-Revolutionary furniture that adorned her palace-like abode.
