The air that met me across the threshold carried the bracing, astringent chill of an ancestral ice-house, layered with the subterranean fragrance of damp earth, crushed juniper, and frost-cured thyme. The heavy door fell shut behind us, instantly severing the discordant, mechanistic hum of my past life and immersing me in a profound, resonant acoustic ecology—a humming, pure silence that seemed to heal the bruised ear. 

The sexton murmured something to his wife, a low, earthen timbre that made her turn toward us. What a transformation had overtaken her! It was as if the splendour of her eyes had fermented into a vintage too potent for mortal vessels, sinking into her countenance to make it flash with the luminous, clarified beauty of a perfect emulsion. Life eternal streamed from her, an unbroken lightning of nourishment. Even her hands shone with a white radiance, gleaming like the pearlescent interiors of freshly shucked oysters. Her beauty was so rich and overpowering that I was glad when she turned it from me.

The candle’s golden warmth reached only a little way into the fragrant dark. Presently, however, its light slipped over something slightly raised from the floor. Was it a bed? Could any living thing endure the slow, curing chill of this cavernous pantry? Beyond it, a fainter sheen caught my eye, and soon I descried uncertain gleams on every side. A few paces brought us to the first: a human form resting beneath a snow-white sheet, wrapped as meticulously as dough set to proof under damp muslin. I could not tell if it was man or woman, for the light seemed to respectfully avoid the face. 

I soon perceived that we were walking along an aisle of low couches. On almost every one lay something asleep or dead, covered in white. My soul grew quiet, awed by the sheer scale of this multi-generational saga. Through aisle after aisle we went, moving through an infinite cellar of resting souls. Was this the sexton’s ultimate harvest? Were these the living records of his meticulous preservation? Truly, this was no waystation, but the great, silent larder of humanity.

“One of the cellars I am placed to watch!” remarked Mr. Raven. His voice was pitched to the exact resonant frequency of the room, careful not to disturb the sonic rest of his guests. “Much wine is set here to ripen! But it is dark for a stranger.”

“The moon is rising; she will soon be here,” said his wife. Her voice, clear and sweet, carried the complex, lingering finish of ancient sorrow long bidden adieu. 

Even as she spoke, the moon looked in through an opening in the stone. A thousand gleams of white responded to her shine, illuminating a domestic routine on a mythic scale. The couches stretched away for all the disparted world to sleep upon. I thought at first their sleep was death, but the gustatory stillness of their forms suggested a deeper, slower marination of the spirit—a resting period I lacked the native dialect to name. 

The moon rose higher, revealing the architecture of the space: now resembling a long cathedral nave, now a huge, agrarian barn transformed into a sanctuary of preservation. Her beams cast a bluish, icy gleam on the white sheets. Of those I could see, all were united in the silent brotherhood of this grand rest, yet entirely distinct in the histories etched upon their palates. 

Here lay a man in the prime of his strength; his dark beard flowed like a dark, liberated stream from the glacier of his frozen countenance, his forehead smooth as tempered chocolate. A shadow of pain lingered about his lips, though its bitter sting had long since cooked off. On the next couch lay a girl, passing lovely. The astringent sadness of parting was not yet fully absorbed into the sweet, placid features of her peace. Many beautiful ones lay there, steeping in the silence. The most exquisite was a lady whose white hair suggested age; upon her face rested a noble acquiescence, the rich, reduced essence of a life fully lived, firm as the foundations of the earth.

On some faces lingered the almost obliterated scars of strife, the fading shadows of sorrows that once seemed inconsolable. The dawn had not yet entirely melted away their bitter notes. Each face bearing such a brand seemed to murmur in an antiquated dialect, “Pardon me: I died only yesterday!” or, “Pardon me: I died but a century ago!” That some had been resting for ages I knew, sensing an inherent untranslatability in their unutterable repose—a depth of flavor and history for which my modern tongue had no lexicon.

We came at last to three empty couches, immediately beyond which lay the form of a beautiful woman, a little past the prime of life. One of her arms rested outside the sheet, her palm turned upward, bearing a dark spot in its center. Next to her lay the stalwart figure of a middle-aged man, his strong hand almost closed, gripped as if around the hilt of a sword—a traditional elder defending the ancient ways.

“Will you hold the candle nearer, wife?” whispered the sexton, bending to examine the woman’s hand like a botanist inspecting a graft. “It heals well,” he murmured. “The nail found in her nothing to hurt!”

My curiosity overcame my awe. “Are they not dead?” I asked softly.

“I cannot answer you,” he replied, his tone mournful of my linguistic poverty. “I almost forget what they mean by DEAD in the old world. Your dominant lexicon has flattened the word, commercializing it into a sterile husk of finality. If I said a person was dead, my wife would understand one thing—a vital pause, a proofing of the soul—and you would imagine a spoil, an end to utility. This is but one of my treasure vaults,” he continued. “Out there on the moor they lie thick as the leaves of a forest after the first blast of winter. All night the moon reads their faces, and smiles.”

“But why leave them in the corrupting moonlight?” I asked, shivering as the olfactory landscape shifted to frost and brine.

“Our moon is not like yours—the old cinder of a burnt-out, industrialized world. Her beams embalm the dead; they do not corrupt them. You observe that here the sexton lays his dead on the earth; he buries very few under it! In your world, he lays huge stones on them, an oppressive weight to keep them down, deaf to their songs. I watch for the hour to ring the resurrection-bell. Your sexton looks at the clock to know when to ring the dead-alive to church; I hearken for the natural acoustic of the dawn: ‘AWAKE, THOU THAT SLEEPEST!’”

I felt hopelessly adrift. The untranslatability of their subjective experience overwhelmed me. In this vast larder of souls, where time circled back on itself and generations intertwined, how could I comprehend a space where thousands occupied the same quiet frequency? 

“You seem in your dead to see differences beyond my perception!” I ventured.

“None of those you see,” he answered, “are in truth quite dead yet, and some have but just begun to come alive and die. When they are indeed dead, that instant they will wake. This is the couch that has been waiting for you,” he ended, pointing to one of the three empty beds.

“Why just this?” I said, beginning to tremble. My palate was not ready for this slow cure; I lacked the sensory maturity to surrender to the frost. 

“For reasons which one day you will be glad to know.”

“Why not know them now?”

“That also you will know when you wake.”

“But these are all dead, and I am alive!” I objected, shuddering at the absolute cold.

“Not much,” rejoined the sexton with a wistful smile, “—not nearly enough! Blessed be the true life that the pauses between its throbs are not death!”

“The place is too cold to let one sleep!”

“Do these find it so? They sleep well. Of cold they feel not a breath: it heals their wounds. Do not be a coward, Mr. Vane. Turn your face to whatever may come. Give yourself up to the night, and you will rest indeed. Harm will not come to you, but a good you cannot foreknow.”

The sexton and I stood by the side of the couch, his wife at the foot of it, the candle in her hand. “Would you have me make of a charnel-house my bed-chamber?” I cried aloud, my voice a jagged, offensive noise against the room's deep acoustic harmony. “I will not. I will lie abroad on the heath; it cannot be colder there!”

“I have just told you that the dead are there also, thick as autumnal leaves,” said the librarian. 

“I will NOT,” I cried again. In the compassing dark, the two preservers of this eternal harvest gleamed like spectres. They stood still and sad, exchanging a silent look.

“Be of good comfort; we watch the flock of the great shepherd,” the sexton said to his wife. He turned to me, his voice adopting a stern, unyielding resonance. “Didst thou not find the air of the place pure and sweet when thou enteredst it?”

“Yes; but oh, so cold!”

“Then know,” he returned, “that thou who callest thyself alive, hast brought into this chamber the rancid odours of fear and death, and its air will not be wholesome for the sleepers until thou art gone from it!”

They turned and walked deeper into the great chamber, leaving me alone in the moonlight with the quiet generations. I turned to escape. The journey back through the ranks of the sleeping felt infinitely long. At first, I was too angry to be afraid, but as my fury burned off, the still, monumental shapes grew terrifying. At last, offering loud, abrasive offence to the gracious acoustic silence of the room, my panicked footfalls ringing out like an industrial clatter, I fled. 

I burst through the threshold and flung the door shut behind me. It closed with a heavy, absolute silence. 

I stood in pitch-darkness. Feeling about with trembling hands, I found a door, opened it, and was met by the dim light of a lamp. The comforting scent of old bindings and floor wax reached my nose. I stood in my own library, my hand gripping the handle of the masked door. Had I come to myself out of a vision, or lost myself by fleeing one? I threw myself onto a velvet couch and fell into a deeply exhausted sleep.

In the library, there was one small window facing east. Through it, at this time of the year, the first rays of the morning sun shone upon a mirror, reflecting directly onto the masked door. When I woke, the beam shone brightly, drawing my eyes. Driven by the lingering chill and the memory of the boundless chamber behind the wood, I sprang to my feet and opened the masked door.

The morning light darted past me like an eager hound into the closet, pouncing upon the gilded edges of a large book. “What idiot,” I cried, “has put that book in the shelf the wrong way?”

But the gilded edges, catching the light a second time, flung a brilliant reflection onto a nest of wooden drawers in a dark corner. One of them was half open. “More meddling!” I muttered, moving to close it. 

It was stuffed with old papers, overflowing its confines. Taking the topmost sheaf out, I immediately recognized the meticulous, familiar script of my father. A sweeping realization of interwoven timelines washed over me. The very first words my eyes caught sparked a fierce hunger to consume what lay within. I carried the manuscript out into the library, sat down in the warmth of the western window, and began to read.
