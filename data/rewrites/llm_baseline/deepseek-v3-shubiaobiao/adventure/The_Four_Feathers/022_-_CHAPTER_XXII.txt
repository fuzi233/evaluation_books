Captain Willoughby was a man who could clear a room without raising his voice. At his club, he was infamous for his relentless monologues—long, winding tales about people no one knew, delivered with the gravity of a man unveiling profound truths. His fellow members had long since learned to scatter at his approach, leaving him in solitary possession of the best-lit corner, a cigar clenched between his teeth, and the air thick with unspoken relief at his isolation.

It was in this state of splendid abandonment that the waiter found him one October afternoon, presenting a calling card with a deferential cough.

"Colonel Durrance?" Willoughby frowned, turning the card over in his fingers. Durrance had never sought him out before. Why now? A prickle of unease ran down his spine—had word of his visit to Kingsbridge somehow reached the man?

"Does Colonel Durrance know I'm here?" he asked sharply.

"Yes, sir."

Willoughby sighed. "Show him in, then."

He braced himself. If Durrance had come to pry into Ethne Eustace’s affairs, he’d find no willing informant here. Feversham and Durrance could settle their own quarrels.

Yet when Durrance entered, his demeanor gave nothing away. He moved with the deliberate ease of a man who had long adapted to blindness, accepting a cigar without hesitation.

"I’ll smoke," he said, striking a match. "They say a blind man can’t enjoy tobacco without seeing the smoke, but I’ve found that’s nonsense. If I let this go out, I’ll know." He exhaled slowly, the scent of good Havana filling the space between them. "Lucky to catch you, Willoughby. I’m only in town for the day."

Willoughby relaxed slightly. "Mather told me you’d been asking after me."

"Mather? Yes, from Suakin." Durrance leaned back. "We were wondering—did you ever meet an Arab called Abou Fatma? Kabbabish tribe?"

Willoughby’s pulse jumped. "Abou Fatma? No, can’t say I have."

Durrance’s voice remained casual. "Mather and I found him years ago near Sinkat. He’d hidden Gordon’s letters in Berber. You remember—they were brought to you in Suakin."

Willoughby’s fingers tightened around his pipe. "Ah, yes. But it wasn’t Abou Fatma who recovered them."

"No? Pity. I’d have liked to know he’d managed it." Durrance’s tone was light, but Willoughby wasn’t fooled. The man’s blindness had sharpened his other senses—he missed nothing.

"Gordon was dead," Willoughby said, too quickly. "No point in risking lives for old letters."

"For no *obvious* reason," Durrance corrected softly.

Willoughby stiffened. A trap? He fumbled for his matches, struck one, and sucked at his pipe to hide his unease.

Durrance let the silence stretch before shifting tactics. "Actually, I wanted to speak of Harry Feversham."

Willoughby nearly choked on his smoke.

"I ran into him at Wadi Halfa," Durrance continued, as if oblivious. "Fallen on hard times. I hoped you might keep an eye out for him in the Soudan."

Relief washed over Willoughby. So that was all. "Of course. Old friend of yours, wasn’t he?"

"One of the few." Durrance’s voice warmed. "Remember that dinner at his flat? Just before we left England. You, me, Feversham—and Trench."

Willoughby’s pipe gurgled. He swore as the match burned his fingers.

"Castleton was supposed to join us," Durrance mused.

"No, he wasn’t," Willoughby blurted. "Sent a telegram—dining elsewhere."

"Ah, yes. With someone from the War Office, wasn’t it?"

Willoughby’s blood ran cold. Durrance wasn’t just reminiscing—he was *hunting*.

A few pleasantries later, Durrance rose, leaving his cigar cold on the table. "Forgot about it," he said lightly. "Too busy thinking of Feversham."

As the cab carried him away, Durrance pieced together the truth. That telegram had been Feversham’s undoing—news of his regiment’s deployment, panic at the brink of war. And now, five years later, the man was redeeming himself in the harshest crucible imaginable.

Durrance smiled. Sutch would get the shock of his life.

He caught the night train to Devonshire, the puzzle finally complete.
