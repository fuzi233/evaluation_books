"No man," declared a Rabbi, citing an undeniable law, "shall turn the bones of his father and mother into spoons." The sentiment was clear: such an act of economy struck a chord that silenced the marketplace. No one dared argue that human progress lay in grinding ancestors into silverware; the only barrier was a feeling, a reverence for the non-material wealth of the world that coerced only those who believed in it.

Daniel Deronda, however, sought a different kind of exploration. He abandoned the fashionable rides of Rotten Row for the winding, crowded arteries of London where common Jews lived. He wandered to synagogues during service, peered into shop windows, and studied faces—a search that offered little promise of the specific discovery he craved. Why not consult an influential Rabbi? He considered it, pushing the inquiry to the week after Christmas. Yet, despite his poetic sensitivity to the world around him, Deronda found himself trapped by the harsh, unyielding reality. When a personal stake was involved, he could not escape the pressure of the Actual, a force indifferent to taste or selection. Enthusiasm thrives comfortably among abstract ideas, tolerating the garlic of the Middle Ages or the pomp of classic processions without flinching. But when ideals press against reality, becoming warm and tangible flesh, enthusiasm grows squeamish, unable to face the mundane without fainting.

Lying in a boat, dreaming of seeking a maiden's kin in Cordova amidst the era of Ibn-Gebirol, one bears the physical inconveniences without shock. Similarly, if the grim streets of St. Mary Axe and Whitechapel were transported to the Rhine in the eleventh century, transforming the cries of Crusaders into the bay of bloodhounds and the reviled Jew into a heroic figure facing torture, the scene would ignite contemplative emotion. Yet, the fervor we feel for such grandiose martyrdom is pale compared to the quiet, unslacking enthusiasm required to sympathize with the commonplace, the perhaps half-repulsive objects that are beloved ideas made flesh. This is the true poetic energy: the imagination piercing solid fact rather than floating among clouds. To glory in a prophetic vision of knowledge covering the earth is easier than witnessing its beginning in newspaper placards staring from a bridge beyond the cornfields. For many delicate souls, it is possible to be in the thick of Armageddon without noticing more than the annoyance of explosive smoke and the struggle on the ground immediately about them.

Usually, Deronda despised the feeble, fastidious sympathy that shrank from the broad life of mankind. But now, with Mirah standing before him as a living reality, he viewed every common Jew and Jewess through the lens of her existence. He sensed a looming collision between her idealized notion of an unknown mother and brother and the discovered fact—a presentiment sharpened by a suppressed fear that a similar collision might hide within his own destiny. He did not expect to find Mirah's friends among wealthy Jews who outdid the lords of the Philistines in sport, but since that class held no likelihood for her, their habits did not trouble him. In this mood, he rambled, preparing his own mind for future theorizing and practice, much as one might study Welsh miners to understand strikes, seeking details of history in the lives of others.

He did not long to find anyone specific. When he glanced at shop signs, he was content they did not bear the name Ezra Cohen. He confessed a peculiar desire that Ezra Cohen should not keep a shop, a wish he knew could be ominous. Desponding views of probability hold that if you object to a squint, your child is likely to be born with one; the hopeful reject this, taking their wishes as security for fulfillment. But fate had other plans. One morning, turning into a quiet side street to escape the noise of Holborn, Deronda felt the scales tip.

Tired of the streets, he paused to hail a cab when his eye caught fine old clasps in chased silver displayed in a window. His first thought was of Lady Mallinger, who might appreciate such Catholic spoils turned into a bracelet. Looking closer, he saw the shop was a pawnbroker's dealing in jewelry, lace, and equivocal bric-à-brac. A sign announced watches and jewelry exchanged and repaired. His survey was noticed from within; a figure appeared at the door, looking round with cordial encouragement, "Good day, sir." In an instant, Deronda recognized the unmistakably Jewish face of a young man about thirty. Wincing from the shopkeeper's likely persuasiveness, he returned the greeting and hurried to the other side of the street, beckoning the cabman. From there, he saw the name above the window: *Ezra Cohen*.

There might be a hundred Ezra Cohens, but Deronda had not seen them. The young man, interested in a possible customer, was likely Ezra himself, of an age expected for Mirah's brother, who had grown up while she remained a child. Yet, as he drove homeward, Deronda's first endeavor was to convince himself there was no warrantable presumption this was Mirah's brother. Even if he proved to be the brother, and the mother was found dead, it was not his duty to reveal the discovery to Mirah. This conclusion was disturbed by his new knowledge that Mirah possessed a religious desire to know of her mother's death and whether her brother lived. How far was he justified in determining another life by his own notions? Was it not his secret complaint that others had ordered his life without open daylight, denying him the full guidance of primary duties?

Immediate relief came from the reflection that he had made no real discovery yet. By looking closer, he would be certified of his freedom from any demand for decision. He intended to return to the shop soon to buy the clasps for Lady Mallinger. But for several days, Sir Hugo hindered him. About to deliver an after-dinner speech on a burning topic, Hugo wanted Deronda to research the legal aspects, wasting days in arguments that always ended in a draw. On many questions they held different sides, but Hugo did not mind; when Deronda put his point well, Hugo said with a mixture of satisfaction and regret, "Confound it, Dan! why don't you make an opportunity of saying these things in public? You're wrong, you know. You won't succeed. You've got the massive sentiment—the heavy artillery of the country against you. But it's all the better ground for a young man to display himself on. When I was your age, I should have taken it. And it would be quite as well for you to be in opposition to me here and there. It would throw you more into relief. If you would seize an occasion of this sort to make an impression, you might be in Parliament in no time. And you know that would gratify me."

"I am sorry not to do what would gratify you, sir," said Deronda. "But I cannot persuade myself to look at politics as a profession."

"Why not? if a man is not born into public life by his position in the country, there's no way for him but to embrace it by his own efforts. The business of the country must be done—her Majesty's Government carried on, as the old Duke said. And it never could be, my boy, if everybody looked at politics as if they were prophecy, and demanded an inspired vocation. If you are to get into Parliament, it won't do to sit still and wait for a call either from heaven or constituents."

"I don't want to make a living out of opinions," said Deronda; "especially out of borrowed opinions. Not that I mean to blame other men. I dare say many better fellows than I don't mind getting on to a platform to praise themselves, and giving their word of honor for a party."

"I'll tell you what, Dan," said Sir Hugo, "a man who sets his face against every sort of humbug is simply a three-cornered, impracticable fellow. There's a bad style of humbug, but there is also a good style—one that oils the wheels and makes progress possible. If you are to rule men, you must rule them through their own ideas; and I agree with the Archbishop at Naples who had a St. Januarius procession against the plague. It's no use having an Order in Council against popular shallowness. There is no action possible without a little acting."

"One may be obliged to give way to an occasional necessity," said Deronda. "But it is one thing to say, 'In this particular case I am forced to put on this foolscap and grin,' and another to buy a pocket foolscap and practice myself in grinning. I can't see any real public expediency that does not keep an ideal before it which makes a limit of deviation from the direct path. But if I were to set up for a public man I might mistake my success for public expediency."

It was after this dialogue, which jarred him, that Deronda set out on his meditated second visit to Ezra Cohen's. He entered the street at the end opposite the Holborn entrance, and an inward reluctance slackened his pace while his thoughts transferred what he had just said about public expediency to the entirely private difficulty bringing him back into this unattractive thoroughfare. It might soon become an immediate practical question how far he could call it wise expediency to conceal the fact of close kindred. Such questions are often decided in a rough-and-ready way; to many, it will appear an over-refinement in Deronda that he should make any great point of a matter confined to his own knowledge. But we have seen the reasons why he regarded concealment as a bane of life, and the necessity of concealment as a mark by which lines of action were to be avoided. The prospect of being urged against the confirmed habit of his mind was naturally grating. He paused before plausible shop-windows, half inclined to decide he would not increase his knowledge about this modern Ezra, who was certainly not a leader among his people—a hesitation proving how, in a man much given to reasoning, a bare possibility may weigh more than the best-clad likelihood. For Deronda's reasoning had decided that all likelihood was against this man's being Mirah's brother.

One window he paused before belonged to a second-hand bookshop, where the literature of the ages was represented in judicious mixture on a narrow table outside, from the immortal verse of Homer to the mortal prose of the railway novel. The mixture was judicious; Deronda found exactly what he wanted: the wonderful autobiography of the Polish Jew, Salomon Maimon. Slipping it into his pocket, he entered the shop to pay, expecting to see behind the counter a grimy personage showing the *nonchalance* about sales universal to the second-hand book-business. In most trades, you find generous men anxious to sell you their wares for your welfare; but even a Jew will not urge Simson's Euclid on you with affectionate assurance. One is led to fear that a second-hand bookseller may belong to that unhappy class of men who have no belief in the good of what they get their living by, yet keep conscience enough to be morose rather than unctuous in their vocation.

Instead of the ordinary tradesman, Deronda saw a figure startling in its unusualness. A man in threadbare clothing, whose age was difficult to guess—from the dead yellowish flatness of the flesh, something like an old ivory carving—sat on a stool against bookshelves projecting beyond the short counter, reading yesterday's *Times*. When he let the paper rest on his lap and looked at the incoming customer, the thought glanced through Deronda that precisely such a physiognomy might have been seen in a prophet of the Exile or a New Hebrew poet of the medieval time. It was a fine typical Jewish face, wrought into intensity of expression apparently by strenuous, eager experience in which all satisfaction had been indirect and far off, and perhaps by bodily suffering involving an absence of ease in the present. The features were clear-cut, not large; the brow not high but broad, fully defined by crisp black hair. It might never have been particularly handsome, but it must always have been forcible; and now, with its dark, far-off gaze and yellow pallor in relief on the gloom of the backward shop, one might have imagined coming upon it in some past prison of the Inquisition, suddenly burst upon by a mob. The look fixed on an incidental customer seemed eager and questioning enough to have been turned on one who might have been a messenger of delivery or of death. The figure was probably familiar and unexciting to the inhabitants of this street; but to Deronda's mind, it brought a strange blending of the unwonted with the common, creating a perceptible interval of mutual observation before he asked, "What is the price of this book?"

Taking the book and examining the fly-leaves without rising, the supposed bookseller said, "There is no mark, and Mr. Ram is not in now. I am keeping the shop while he is gone to dinner. What are you disposed to give for it?" He held the book close on his lap, looking examiningly at Deronda, over whom came the disagreeable idea that this striking personage wanted to see how much could be got out of a customer's ignorance of prices. Without further reflection, Deronda said, "Don't you know how much it is worth?"

"Not its market-price. May I ask have you read it?"

"No. I have read an account of it, which makes me want to buy it."

"You are a man of learning—you are interested in Jewish history?" This was said in a deepened tone of eager inquiry.

"I am certainly interested in Jewish history," said Deronda, quietly, curiosity overcoming his dislike to the sort of inspection and questioning he was under.

Immediately, the strange Jew rose from his sitting posture. Deronda felt a thin hand pressing his arm tightly, while a hoarse, excited voice, not much above a loud whisper, said, "You are perhaps of our race?"

Deronda colored deeply, not liking the grasp, and answered with a slight shake of the head, "No." The grasp was relaxed, the hand withdrawn, the eagerness of the face collapsing into uninterested melancholy, as if some possessing spirit which had leaped into the eyes and gestures sank back again to the inmost recesses of the frame. Moving further off as he held out the little book, the stranger said in a tone of distant civility, "I believe Mr. Ram will be satisfied with half-a-crown, sir."
