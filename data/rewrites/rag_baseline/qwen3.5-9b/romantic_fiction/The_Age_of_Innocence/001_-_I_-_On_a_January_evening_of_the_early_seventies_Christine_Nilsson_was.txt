The air inside the Academy of Music was thick with the scent of beeswax and suppressed anxiety, a heavy atmosphere that clung to the red and gold upholstery of the boxes. Outside, the snow fell in a relentless, white silence, muffling the city's frantic pulse, while inside, the world of fashion reassembled itself around the familiar, shabby grandeur of the old institution. It was a sanctuary for conservatives who prized its inconvenience as a shield against the "new people" rising in the distant districts beyond the Forty-second Street, and for sentimentalists who clung to its ghosts, and musicians who tolerated its flawed acoustics as a necessary tax on tradition.

Christine Nilsson sang. Her voice, a brilliant instrument cutting through the hush, drifted down from the stage where the garden scene unfolded. The audience, transported through private carriages and humble Brown coupes alike, leaned forward in a collective suspension of disbelief. Newland Archer stood at the rear of the club box, his posture relaxed, a deliberate delay in his movements. He had dined alone with his mother and sister hours prior, lingering over a cigar in the Gothic library—a room defined by its black walnut bookcases and the singular rule that permitted smoke. Yet, in New York, punctuality was a performance; to arrive too early was to break the rhythm of the evening. Furthermore, Newland was a dilettante by nature, deriving a sharper, more refined pleasure from anticipating a delight than from consuming it.

He watched the stage. Madame Nilsson, dressed in white cashmere slashed with pale blue satin, performed the Daisy Song. She sang of love and rejection, translating the German text into the fluid Italian required by convention, her large eyes fixed on the disheveled Faust, Capoul, who struggled in his purple velvet to appear pure amidst his artifice. As she reached the climax—"M'ama!"—she pressed a daisy to her lips, her voice triumphant.

Newland's gaze shifted from the stage to the opposite side of the house. There, in the box of the Mingotts, sat a tableau of brocaded matrons and a young girl in white. Her name was May Welland. A flush of pink mounted her cheeks, spreading from her chin to the roots of her fair braids, illuminating the modest slope of her breast where a single gardenia pinned a tulle tucker. Her eyes, ecstatic and fixed on the lovers, softened as she glanced down at the immense bouquet of lilies-of-the-valley resting on her lap. Her gloved fingers touched the stems gently, a gesture of innocent absorption.

Newland breathed out, a sigh of satisfied vanity mixing with a tender reverence. He saw in her an abysmal purity, untouched by the complexities of the world he inhabited. "We'll read Faust together... by the Italian lakes..." the thought floated through his mind, a hazily constructed vision of a future where he would guide her through the masterpieces of literature, revealing the world to her as a man reveals a treasure. Only that afternoon had May hinted that she "cared"—the consecrated phrase of maiden avowal—and now his imagination raced ahead of the engagement ring and the march from Lohengrin, picturing her beside him in scenes of old European witchery.

He did not wish for her to remain a simpleton forever. His plan was to cultivate in her a social tact, a readiness of wit that would allow her to navigate the "younger set," attracting homage while playfully deflecting it. Deep down, perhaps dangerously so, he wished for her to possess the worldly wisdom of the married woman whose charms had once captivated him, though he carefully excluded any trace of the frailty that had ruined that previous life. How such a creature of fire and ice might survive the harsh realities of New York society was a problem he had never bothered to solve; he preferred to hold his idealized view without dissecting it, trusting that it aligned with the consensus of the other gentlemen in the box—their white waistcoats, their buttonholes, their shared doctrine of morality.

Suddenly, Lawrence Lefferts, the arbiter of "form" in New York, broke the spell. With a sharp turn of his head, he lowered his opera-glass, his expression shifting from appreciation to scrutiny. From the slant of his bald forehead to the careless elegance of his patent-leather shoes, Lefferts embodied the knowledge of propriety as if it were written in his bones. "My God!" he exclaimed, handing his glass to Old Sillerton Jackson.

Newland followed the direction of Lefferts's gaze. Standing in the center of the Mingott box was a new figure: a slim young woman, slightly shorter than May, with brown hair curled tightly against her temples and secured by a narrow band of diamonds. The "Josephine look" suggested by her headdress was echoed in the dramatic gathering of her dark blue velvet gown under a girdle with a large, antique clasp. She seemed unaware of the attention she commanded, pausing briefly to discuss seating arrangements with Mrs. Welland before yielding her spot to sit alongside Mrs. Lovell Mingott.

Mr. Jackson returned the opera-glass to Lefferts, and the entire club box turned in unison, waiting for the verdict. Jackson was the keeper of New York's genealogy, a living map of cousinships and forbidden connections, from the stingy Leffertses of Long Island to the mentally unstable Chiverses of Albany. Between his silver hair and hollow temples, he carried the weight of fifty years of scandals and secrets, including the mysterious disappearance of handsome Bob Spicer and the tragic fate of Medora Manson. He scrutinized the newcomers with his filmy blue eyes, his expression unreadable, before giving his moustache a thoughtful twist.

"I didn't think the Mingotts would have tried it on," he said simply. The sentence hung in the air, heavy with implication, sealing the moment in a silence that matched the falling snow outside.
