Mr. Welland laid down his knife and fork as if the silver had suddenly turned too heavy for his hands. Across the luncheon-table his wife, having adjusted her gold eye-glasses with a practised click, held up a card and read it aloud in the tone she reserved for the social grotesque.

“Professor and Mrs. Emerson Sillerton request the pleasure of Mr. and Mrs. Welland’s company at the meeting of the Wednesday Afternoon Club on August 25th at 3 o’clock punctually. To meet Mrs. and the Misses Blenker. Red Gables, Catherine Street. R.S.V.P.”

“Good gracious—” Mr. Welland breathed, as though the words were a draught too sharp for his throat.

“Poor Amy Sillerton,” Mrs. Welland sighed, her voice smoothing itself into pity that had long ago become a habit of the table, like passing the bread. “You never can tell what her husband will do next. I suppose he’s just discovered the Blenkers.”

Emerson Sillerton had always been spoken of in Newport as one speaks of a persistent burr caught in good cloth: not dangerous, only irritating—and impossible to remove because the fabric itself was venerable. Everyone agreed he had had “every advantage.” Wealth, position, the correct alliances, the right names on both sides—there was nothing, as Mrs. Welland was fond of observing, that obliged him to become an archaeologist, or indeed a Professor of any sort, or to live in Newport in winter, or to fill a house with long-haired men and short-haired women whose voices carried, untempered, through open windows. And if he chose to affront tradition, why—why must he have done it to poor Amy Dagonet, who had a right to expect something different, and money enough for her own carriage and the soothing clip-clop of familiar horses in the drive?

Newport’s old families endured the Sillerton-Pennilow-Dagonet connection as they endured certain winds: by drawing lots and sending one reluctant representative to the Sillertons’ annual garden-party, to stand among untidy flower-beds and hear conversation that did not pause politely at the edge of acceptable subjects. Mrs. Welland, who liked her afternoons to proceed as evenly as a well-made sauce, remembered every affront by date.

“It’s a wonder,” she remarked, “that they didn’t choose Cup Race day. Do you remember, two years ago, their giving a party for a black man on the day of Julia Mingott’s thé dansant? Luckily this time there’s nothing else going on that I know of—for of course some of us will have to go.”

Mr. Welland’s fingers worried the edge of his napkin. “ ‘Some of us,’ my dear—more than one? Three o’clock is such a very awkward hour. I have to be here at half-past three to take my drops. It’s really no use trying to follow Bencomb’s new treatment if I don’t do it systematically; and if I join you later, of course I shall miss my drive.”

The thought flushed his finely wrinkled cheek with the anxious pink of a man whose body had become, in its small requirements, an exacting household god.

“There’s no reason why you should go at all,” Mrs. Welland returned, cheerfulness snapping into place like a napkin-ring. “I have some cards to leave at the other end of Bellevue Avenue, and I’ll drop in at about half-past three and stay long enough to make poor Amy feel she hasn’t been slighted.” Her eyes moved, after a slight hesitation, to her daughter. “And if Newland’s afternoon is provided for perhaps May can drive you out with the ponies, and try their new russet harness.”

To be “provided for” was, in the Welland household, as necessary as having the lamb properly carved or the coffee poured at the right minute. Mrs. Welland dreaded unfilled hours as she dreaded bad drainage: something unhealthy, something that produced disorders in the mind. Yet she also believed, with the same firmness, that parents should not visibly interfere with the plans of their married children. Her ingenuity, therefore, was spent in arranging days like trays of carefully separated sandwiches—each element in its place, no jostling, no empty gaps.

“Of course I’ll drive with Papa,” May said, with the gentle firmness that reminded her husband that he had not spoken. “I’m sure Newland will find something to do.”

It had distressed Mrs. Welland, during the fortnight of his stay, to discover that Newland Archer did not look ahead. When asked how he meant to spend an afternoon he answered with that perverse lightness she never knew how to meet: “Oh, I think for a change I’ll just save it instead of spending it—” And once, when she and May had been obliged to make a long-postponed round of calls, he had confessed to having lain all afternoon under a rock on the beach below the house, as if time were something one could cool on stone and salt air.

May had defended him with her serene logic. “No; but you see it doesn’t matter, because when there’s nothing particular to do he reads a book.”

“Ah, yes—like his father!” Mrs. Welland had agreed, consoling herself with heredity, as one excuses an odd flavour in a familiar dish by remembering the cook’s native province. After that, the subject was dropped; but as the day of the Sillerton reception approached, May’s conscience began to prickle at the thought of deserting him to an unprovided-for afternoon, and she proposed tennis at the Chiverses’, or a sail on Julius Beaufort’s cutter—small compensations offered with a wife’s desire to keep the air around her marriage clear of dissonance.

“I shall be back by six, you know, dear: Papa never drives later than that—” she reminded him, and seemed not fully reassured till Archer announced, with a show of foresight, that he thought of hiring a runabout and driving up the island to a stud-farm to look at a second horse for her brougham.

They had been looking for this horse for some time. The idea pleased May so much that she glanced at her mother as if to say: You see—he can plan out his time as well as any of us.

But Archer’s plan had germinated the very day he had heard of the Sillertons’ invitation, and he had kept it secret with the odd caution of a man carrying something contraband in his pocket. He had even engaged in advance a runabout with a pair of old livery-stable trotters—stout, seasoned creatures that could still do their eighteen miles on level roads if not pressed too hard. At two o’clock he hastily deserted the luncheon-table and sprang into the light carriage.

The day itself had that clean brightness which makes every object seem newly washed. A north breeze drove little puffs of white cloud across an ultramarine sky; below it the sea ran in bright ridges, as if it had been combed. Bellevue Avenue lay empty at that hour. After dropping the stable-lad at the corner of Mill Street, Archer turned down the Old Beach Road and drove across Eastman’s Beach, the wheels running with a soft crunch where sand had strayed onto the track. He felt the old, schoolboy excitement of a half-holiday—time unlatched, opening into the unknown.

He took his pair at an easy gait. He meant to reach the stud-farm beyond Paradise Rocks before three; then, after looking over the horse and perhaps trying him, he would still have four hours—four bright coins of time—to spend as he chose.

When he had first heard of the Sillertons’ party he had instantly said to himself that the Marchioness Manson would certainly come to Newport with the Blenkers, and that Madame Olenska might take the opportunity of spending the day with her grandmother. In that case the Blenkers’ house would likely be deserted, and he could, without indiscretion, satisfy a curiosity that had become, without his willing it, a kind of hunger.

He was not sure he wanted to see the Countess again. Yet ever since he had looked at her from the path above the bay he had wanted—irrationally, insistently—to see the place she lived in, to know the exact arrangement of earth and sky and water that held her, to be able to follow, in imagination, the movements of her figure in its daily setting. It was not so much her voice he craved—he told himself he did not even wish to speak to her—as the sense of her existence made tangible: the chair she might sit in, the window she might look from, the air that touched her hair. The longing was like the sick man’s sudden whim for a particular taste once known: something remembered by the body more than by the mind.

At the stud-farm a glance told him the horse was not what he wanted. Nevertheless he took a turn behind it, as if to prove to himself that he was not in a hurry, that he could be deliberate, normal. At three he shook out the reins over his trotters and turned into the by-roads leading to Portsmouth.

The wind had dropped. A faint haze lay on the horizon, the first sign of a fog waiting to creep up the Saconnet with the turn of tide. All around him the fields and woods were steeped in golden light. He drove past grey-shingled farm-houses sunk in orchards where late apples reddened, past hay-fields giving off the sun-warmed smell of cut grass, past oak-groves where the leaves made a low, steady whisper like a careful stirring with a wooden spoon. In the villages he passed, white steeples rose sharply into the fading blue, and from open doorways came the muted clang of pans, the far-off thud of a chopping block, domestic sounds that seemed to hold the day together better than any sermon.

At last, after stopping to ask the way of men at work in a field—voices carried on the even air, unhurried, as if the land itself set the tempo—he turned down a lane between high banks of goldenrod and brambles. At the end of the lane was the blue glimmer of river. To the left, in front of a clump of oaks and maples, stood a long tumble-down house, white paint peeling from its clapboards like dried icing flaking from an old cake.

Facing the gateway on the roadside stood one of the open sheds in which the New Englander shelters farming implements and where visitors hitch their teams. Archer jumped down, led his pair into the shade of it, tied them to a post, and turned toward the house.

The patch of lawn before it had relapsed into a hay-field. But to the left an overgrown box-garden, edged with the stubborn green of old hedges, held dahlias too large and heavy for their stems and rose-bushes gone rusty and wild. In it, like a relic of a forgotten fête, stood a ghostly summer-house of trellis-work that had once been white. Above it was a wooden Cupid who had lost his bow and arrow but still leaned forward in eternal, ineffectual aim.

Archer leaned against the gate a while. No one was in sight. No sound came from the open windows: no laughter, no footfall, no clink of china—only the soft insect-saw of late summer and, farther off, the river’s thin lap against reeds. A grizzled Newfoundland dozed before the door, as ineffective a guardian as the arrowless Cupid. It was strange to think this silence and decay belonged to the turbulent Blenkers, yet Archer was sure he had found the place.

For a long time he stood there, content to take in the scene, falling gradually under its drowsy spell; and then he roused himself to the sense of time slipping through his fingers. Should he look his fill and drive away? Suddenly he wanted to see inside, to fix the interior in his mind—to be able to say: this is the room she sits in. There was nothing to prevent his walking up to the door and ringing. If she was away, as he supposed, he could give his name and ask leave to go into a sitting-room to write a message.

Instead, he crossed the rough grass and turned toward the box-garden. As he entered it he caught sight of something bright-coloured in the summer-house, and a moment later made it out to be a pink parasol.

It drew him as if by a physical pull. He was sure it was hers. He went into the summer-house and sat down on the rickety seat. He picked up the silken thing and examined the carved handle, made of some rare wood that, when warmed in his hand, gave out a faint aromatic scent, like spice stored long in a closed tin. The smell rose intimately, as if it belonged to skin and hair, to drawers of folded linen, to a woman’s private world.

He lifted the handle to his lips.

A rustle of skirts brushed the box hedge. Archer sat motionless, leaning on the parasol handle with his clasped hands, letting the sound come nearer without raising his eyes. He had always known that this must happen—

“Oh, Mr. Archer!” exclaimed a loud young voice.

He looked up and saw before him the youngest and largest of the Blenker girls, blonde and blowsy, in bedraggled muslin. A red blotch on one cheek showed where it had lately been pressed against a pillow; her eyes, half-awakened, stared at him with a hospitable confusion.

“Gracious—where did you drop from? I must have been sound asleep in the hammock. Everybody else has gone to Newport. Did you ring?” she asked incoherently, as if he might have walked straight through the trellis like a character in one of the plays her parents admired.

Archer’s confusion was greater than hers. “I—no—that is, I was just going to. I had to come up the island to see about a horse, and I drove over on a chance of finding Mrs. Blenker and your visitors. But the house seemed empty—so I sat down to wait.”

Miss Blenker, shaking off sleep, looked at him with increasing interest. “The house is empty. Mother’s not here, or the Marchioness—or anybody but me.” Her glance took on a faint reproach. “Didn’t you know Professor and Mrs. Sillerton are giving a garden-party for mother and all of us this afternoon? It was too unlucky I couldn’t go; but I’ve had a sore throat, and mother was afraid of the drive home this evening. Did you ever know anything so disappointing? Of course,” she added gaily, “I shouldn’t have minded half as much if I’d known you were coming.”

A lumbering coquetry he could not return began to show itself in her pose, and Archer, feeling trapped by the pink silk in his hand, found strength to break in: “But Madame Olenska—has she gone to Newport too?”

Miss Blenker stared. “Madame Olenska—didn’t you know she’d been called away?”

“Called away?—”

“Oh, my best parasol!” she cried suddenly, as if the object itself had only now come into focus. “I lent it to that goose of a Katie, because it matched her ribbons, and the careless thing must have dropped it here. We Blenkers are all like that… real Bohemians!”

She recovered the sunshade with a powerful hand, unfurled it, and lifted its rosy dome above her head, the silk catching the light and turning the shade into a small, moving piece of sunset.

“Yes—Ellen was called away yesterday,” she continued, already wandering off into pleasure at her own news. “She lets us call her Ellen, you know. A telegram came from Boston: she said she might be gone for two days. I do love the way she does her hair, don’t you?”

Archer continued to stare through her as though she were transparent. All he saw was the trumpery parasol arched in pink above her giggling head, and all he heard was the absence where another voice ought to have been. In an instant his mind seemed to unroll a long strip of future—day after day laid out like a table set without variation—and on it he saw himself dwindling, moving farther and farther away, the man to whom nothing was ever to happen.

He glanced about at the unpruned garden, the tumble-down house, the oak-grove where dusk was thickening. It had seemed exactly the place where he ought to have found Madame Olenska. And she was far away. Even the pink parasol was not hers.

He frowned, hesitated, and then said, as if testing the words on his tongue: “You don’t know, I suppose—I shall be in Boston tomorrow. If I could manage to see her—” It was half an appeal, half a confession, and he hated that he had to make it to this girl with sleep still clinging to her face.

Miss Blenker’s interest wavered, though her smile persisted with good nature. “Oh, of course; how lovely of you! She’s staying at the Parker House; it must be horrible there in this weather.”

After that Archer was only intermittently aware of what passed between them. He remembered, as through a mist, stoutly resisting her entreaty that he should wait for the returning family and have high tea with them before driving home. The word “tea” brought before him for an instant the smell of toast and weak China tea, the clatter of cups, voices in a room already tired with the day; it was unbearable. At last, with his hostess still lingering at his side, he walked out of range of the wooden Cupid, unfastened his horses, and drove off.

At the turn of the lane he looked back. Miss Blenker stood at the gate waving the pink parasol, its bright curve bobbing against the dimming green like a signal no one had meant to send. The river glimmered beyond her; behind her the old house kept its silence, and the only sound Archer carried away was the steady, familiar beat of hooves on hardening road, as though the island itself were counting out, in measured strokes, the hours he could not keep from slipping.
