One might be tempted to envy Deronda the quiet theater of preparation, watching as he draped fresh gray flannel shirts and a dressing gown reminiscent of a Franciscan's brown frock over Mordecai's frame. Half of his delight lay in seeing Mirah's brother through her eyes, shielding her fervent joy from any shadow of doubt. Yet, even as he arranged the scene, a creeping suspicion gnawed at him: was he mistaking the lower for the higher? Was Mirah truly incapable of appreciating the distinction in her brother precisely *because* of the poverty that marked his past, or was he projecting his own need to sanitize history onto her? The Meyricks demanded appeasement; their minds, untouched by that sacred "seeing"—that bath of solemnity born of reverent love—were prone to immediate repugnance. Deronda, driven by this social friction, resolved to hide every trace of the Jewish identity that might trigger their instinctive recoil. This impulse would have hardened further had he listened to the dialogue crackling around Mrs. Meyrick's hearth that evening, after Mirah retired.

Hans, now settled in his Chelsea rooms, lingered late. As Mrs. Meyrick poked the fire into a roaring blaze, she announced, "Now, Kate, extinguish your candle. Come all round the fire cosily. Hans, dear, cease your laughter at those poems for the ninety-ninth time and join us. I have something wonderful to tell."

"As if I didn't know that, ma," Kate retorted, her voice dripping with practiced sarcasm. "I've seen it in the corner of your eye for ages, hiding behind your endless 'errands.'" The girls gathered, propping their feet on the fender, while Hans swung his chair forward, resting his fists and chin on the backrest.

"Well, since you're so wise," Mrs. Meyrick declared, her voice clear and bright, "perhaps you know Mirah's brother has been found!"

"Oh, confound it!" Hans exploded instantly.

"Hans, that is wicked," Mab whispered, horrified. "Suppose we had lost you?"

"I cannot help being rather sorry," Kate admitted, her tone shifting to genuine regret. "And her mother? Where is she?"

"She is dead."

"I hope the brother isn't a bad man," Amy ventured timidly.

"Nor a fellow all smiles and jewelry—a Crystal Palace Assyrian with a hat on," Hans sneered, his mood souring. "Were there ever such unfeeling children?"

"You don't think the least bit of Mirah's joy in this, do you?" Mrs. Meyrick pressed, emboldened by the need for opposition.

"You know, ma, Mirah hardly remembers her brother," Kate countered. "People lost for twelve years shouldn't come back. They're always in the way."

"Hans!" Mrs. Meyrick reproached sharply. "If you had lost me for twenty years, I should have thought—"

"I said twelve years," Hans interrupted, pacing with agitation. "Anywhere near twelve is the exact duration lost relations should keep out of the way."

"But it's nice finding people—there's something to tell," Mab interjected, clutching her knees. "Did Prince Camaralzaman find him?"

Mrs. Meyrick, in her neat, narrative style, recounted everything she knew without pause. "Mr. Deronda holds the highest admiration for him," she concluded. "He seems quite to look up to him. And he says Mirah is just the sister to understand this brother."

"Deronda is getting perfectly preposterous about those Jews," Hans spat, rising and banging his chair back with violent finality. "He wants to do everything he can to encourage Mirah in her prejudices."

"Oh, for shame, Hans! To speak thus of Mr. Deronda!" Mab cried out. A subtle, undercurrent of pain flickered across Mrs. Meyrick's face, suppressed but visible.

"And now we shall never be all together," Hans rambled, wandering the room with his hands buried in the pockets of his brown velveteen coat. "But we must invite this prophet Elijah to tea. Mirah will think of nothing but sitting on the ruins of Jerusalem. She'll be spoiled as an artist—mind that—she'll get as narrow as a nun. Everything will be ruined—our home and everything. I shall take to drinking."

"Oh, really, Hans," Kate snapped, her patience fraying. "I do think men are the most contemptible animals in all creation. Every one of them must have everything to his mind, or he becomes unbearable."

"Oh, oh, oh, it's very dreadful!" Mab wailed. "I feel as if ancient Nineveh were come again."

"I should like to know what's the good of attending university and knowing everything if you're so childish, Hans," Amy chided. "You ought to put up with a man Providence sends you to be kind to. We shall have to put up with him."

"I hope you'll all like the new *Lamentations of Jeremiah*—'to be continued in our next'—that's all," Hans muttered, grabbing his wide-awake. "It's no use being anything special if you have to endure the company of those men with fixed ideas, staring blankly at you and demanding your remarks serve as mere footnotes to their text. If you're trapped under a petrifying wall, you'd better be an old boot. I don't feel myself an old boot."

Abruptly, he leaned forward to kiss his mother's brow in a hasty, desperate gesture, then offered a condescending nod to the girls on his way out. "Good night, little mother... Good night, girls."

"Suppose Mirah knew how you're behaving," Kate called after him.

His reply was the slam of the door. Turning back to her mother, she added, "I *should* like to see Mirah when Mr. Deronda tells her. I know she will look so beautiful."

Yet Deronda, reconsidering his approach, had penned a letter the previous day. He instructed Mrs. Meyrick to reveal the news herself rather than waiting for his arrival, omitting the true reason: his shrinking from re-entering a narrative where he risked appearing as a benefactor playing a role of general goodness. Instead, he framed it as a desire to stay with Mordecai while Mrs. Meyrick brought Mirah for a visit, creating a necessary buffer before the inevitable move to a new dwelling that Mirah herself would soon propose. Secretly, Deronda harbored anxious wonderment regarding Mordecai. After years of solitary immersion in ideas that had narrowed with his diminishing physical strength, would the elder Cohen allow space for Deronda's tender interest in his sister beyond the bounds of pious duty?

Mordecai's feelings for the Cohens, particularly little Jacob, displayed a persistent warmth, yet these were familiar presences woven into his daily life for years. Deronda noted with concern that Mordecai asked no new questions about Mirah, maintaining an unusual silence on all subjects and seemingly submitting passively to the upheaval of his personal life. Obediently, Mordecai donned the new clothes, but afterward, he smiled faintly at Deronda. "I must keep my old garments by me for a remembrance."

When they sat awaiting Mirah, Mordecai remained silent, his eyelids lowered, yet his face and hands betrayed a restless turmoil. Indeed, Mordecai was undergoing a peculiar nervous perturbation, known only to those whose minds, long flowing with strong impetus in a single current, are suddenly forced into a new or reopened channel. People susceptible because their strength has been drained by dormant biases dread interviews that imperiously revive the past with the same terror they feel facing a threatening illness. Joy may be present, but joy, too, is terrible.

Deronda caught the infection of this excitement. Upon hearing the ring at the door, he stepped out, unsure why, needing only to see and greet Mirah beforehand. He was startled to find her wearing the hat and cloak in which he had first encountered her—the memorable cloak that had once been wetted to serve as a winding sheet. She had descended stairs clad in this attire. When Mrs. Meyrick asked in a questioning tone, "You like to go in that dress, dear?" Mirah replied, "My brother is poor, and I want to look as much like him as I can, else he may feel distant from me," imagining she would meet him in a worker's garb.

Deronda could offer no comment, feeling a secret shame for his own fastidious preparations. They shook hands silently; Mirah appeared pale and awestruck. As Deronda held the door open, Mordecai had risen, his eyes turning toward the entrance with an eager intensity. Mirah took only two or three steps before stopping. They stood motionless, locked in a gaze that felt less like their own presence than the collision of another's memory. Compared to the weight of shared recollection, physical touch was no union.

Mirah broke the silence first, standing firm where she was. "Ezra," she said, using the exact tone she employed when recounting her mother's call to him. With a sudden movement, Mordecai advanced and laid his hand on her shoulders. He towered over her, looking down tenderly. "That was our mother's voice. Do you remember her calling me?"

"Yes, and how you answered her—'Mother!'—and I knew you loved her."
