The aroma of freshly brewed coffee wafted through the cramped, dimly lit room, mingling with the scent of worn leather and stale paper. It was here, amidst this makeshift sanctuary, that I crafted my craft, honing the art of words like a skilled chef perfects a delicate sauce. Three years had passed since my arrival in Deptford, and though I'd secured a modest foothold in the literary world, the weight of debt still lingered, a constant reminder of my precarious financial standing. One hundred and fifty pounds, a sum that, when tallied, seemed to stretch toward two hundred pounds – a figure that haunted me like an uninvited guest. Yet, driven by necessity, I persisted, leaving the realm of sentiment to those better equipped to navigate its turbulent waters.

My manuscripts, carefully crafted and polished, were dispatched to Norah, a guardian of sorts, who saw to their reproduction, accounted for expenses, and forwarded the balance. Initially, my earnings were meager, but Norah's astute management ensured their steady increase. Dan, ever the pragmatist, chafed at her rates, decrying them as excessive, while Miss Deleglise, a formidable businesswoman, remained unmoved, her responses as curt as a winter's morning. Still, I knew that to yield would be to invite the uncertainty that haunted my every waking moment. I needed stability, and Norah's efficiency brought it, incrementally.

As the weeks turned into months, my reputation as "Jack Homer" began to spread, a whispered name that commanded attention. Editors like Mr. Brian, though initially wary, soon recognized the value I brought to their publications. He grumbled, but paid, and I learned to gauge the market, adapting my craft to the demands of a hungry public. Competition, however, was a double-edged sword, and Norah, aware of its potential, introduced it into our negotiations, nudging me toward greater productivity.

I worked tirelessly, yet humor, that elusive muse, proved a capricious mistress. She arrived when she pleased, departing as suddenly as she appeared, leaving me to chase the faint trail of inspiration. Journalism became a necessary supplement, a means to fill the gaps between her whimsical visits. I joined the ranks of the penny-a-liners, a motley crew of writers who lived precariously on the misfortunes of others.
