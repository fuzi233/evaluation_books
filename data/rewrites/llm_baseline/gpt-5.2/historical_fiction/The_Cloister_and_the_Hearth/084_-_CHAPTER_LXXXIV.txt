Sunday, after mass, Catherine’s house in the Hoog Straet seemed to breathe like a great kitchen at festival-time—every corner alive, every board in use, every sound answering another. The shop below had at last the look of a settled thing: planks scrubbed pale, shelves set straight as psalm-lines, and above the door the new names, Cornelis and Sybrandt, painted bold for all the street to read. Their sign swung in the soft air—a white lamb, lips at a gilt ewe—creaking gently on its iron, as if even the wood wished them profit.

Eli had come early, bringing from his own store more wares to give them their first good start: bundles and packets that smelt of dye and new cloth, and the faint peppery bite of imported things. He laid them down with the calm of a man who knew the weight of goods and the weight of beginnings. Catherine’s eyes shone at each piece, not for greed, but for the comfort of order—bread on the table to-morrow because cloth lay ready to-day.

In the little garden behind, Cornelis and Sybrandt walked together between the cabbages and the last of the herbs, speaking low. They had been wild enough in their season—“agriculture” in that unremunerative form that meant oats sown for folly rather than for bread. But the smell of clean timber, the sight of their own names above a door, had sobered them more than sermon. They agreed they were sick of the old waste; it was better to make money than throw it after sport. They vowed, with the vehemence of the newly converted, to stick to business like wax to a seal.

Catherine, at an open window, heard them as one hears a mouse behind the flour-sack: her quick, ever-watchful ear caught the very turn of their words. She did not call out—she only stored the sound away, as she stored spice, to use when it should season something else. She went to Eli with the look of a woman whose hearth was as much her throne as any duke’s chair.

The supper planned for that night was to be a kind of inaugural feast: Margaret and her boy were to come, and the elders would pour out trade counsel as freely as the wine. It was to be a warm blessing on the young men’s new life—Commerce instead of the wild-oats husbandry that had cost Catherine more sighs than any boiling pot.

So Margaret came over to help her mother-in-law, and perhaps, too, to shake off the heavy languor that had lain upon her like damp linen. Catherine put her straight to work—not cruelly, but as one sets a log on the fire. The kitchen soon grew red with heat and faces. A pot sang on the trivet; fat hissed where it met hot iron; onions split and softened under the knife, their sharpness rising and sweetening as they surrendered. Catherine’s hands moved in their old dominion—measuring by feel, tasting by instinct, hearing the very point at which a simmer would turn to a boil. Even the house sounded wholesome: the scrape of a peel on carrots, the steady thud of a pestle, the small clink of crockery—sounds that made a human peace, unlike the harsh racket of carts and hammers that sometimes bruised a street into bad temper.

Presently in came Joan with a salad from Jorian’s garden, the leaves still holding a cool, wet scent as if morning had not quite let them go. She had wrapped it in cloth, and when she opened it the green smelled like earth after rain.

“He cut it for you, Margaret,” she said, setting it down. “You are all his chat; I shall be jealous. I told him you were to feast to-day. But oh, lass—what a sermon in the new kerk! Preaching? I never heard it till this day.”

“Would I had been there then,” said Margaret, and her voice had in it the thirst of a dry mouth. “For I am dried up for want of dew from heaven.”

“Why, he preacheth again this afternoon. But mayhap you are wanted here.”

“Not she,” said Catherine briskly, without lifting her eyes from the pan. “Come, away ye go, if y’are minded.”

“Indeed,” said Margaret, “methinks I should not be such a damper at table if I could come to ’t warm from a good sermon.”

“Then you must be brisk,” observed Joan, always counting time by other folk’s feet. “See the folk are wending that way, and—as I live—there goes the holy friar. Oh, bless us and save us, Margaret; the hermit! We forgot.”

And away she went—out of the house like a gust through a doorway, across the road, stopping the friar as lightly as she would stop a child that ran too near the canal. She returned almost as soon, breath warm, eyes bright with the pleasure of having looked close at something talked of.

“There,” she cried, “I was bent on seeing him nigh hand.”

“What said he to thee?” asked Catherine, not unkindly, but with the impatience of a woman whose broth must not be forgotten for sermons.

“Says he, ‘My daughter, I will go to him ere sunset, God willing.’ The sweetest voice. But oh, my mistresses, what thin cheeks for a young man, and great eyes—not far from your colour, Margaret.”

Margaret’s mouth trembled as if she had tasted something bitter and necessary. “I have a great mind to go hear him,” she said. “But my cap is not very clean, and they will all be there in their snow-white mutches.”

“There, take my handkerchief out of the basket,” said Catherine, practical as salt. “You cannot have the child; I want him for my poor Kate. It is one of her ill days.”

Margaret answered by taking the boy upstairs.

The air changed on the stair—cooler, stiller. In Kate’s chamber the sounds of the kitchen fell away until they were only a distant murmur, like a river heard through closed shutters. Kate lay in bed; her face was drawn fine, and yet when she saw Margaret she smiled with that strange, unearthly sweetness pain sometimes cannot steal.

“How art thou, sweetheart?” said Margaret; then stopped, for the answer was written plain. “Nay, I need not ask. Thou art in sore pain; thou smilest so. See, I have brought thee one thou lovest.”

“Two, by my way of counting,” said Kate, and her smile did not falter.

At that very moment a spasm seized her that would have wrung a cry from a stronger body and a less patient soul; it passed, leaving her breath short, her brow damp. Margaret stood with hands helpless for an instant—she who could chop and stir and mend, and yet could not mend this.

“What, in your lap?” said Margaret, following a gesture of the suffering girl. “Nay, he is too heavy, and thou in such pain.”

“I love him too dear to feel his weight,” Kate replied simply, as if love were a cushion the body could lean on.

Margaret laid the boy where Kate wished, and while Kate’s thin fingers found his sleeve and held it as if it were a cord to the living world, Margaret took the chance to make herself fit for kirk. She washed her face quickly, turned her hair, smoothed what could be smoothed. It was a poor toilet beside those rich white mutches she feared; yet the clean water and the small act of care steadied her.

“I am for the kerk,” said she at last, “to hear a beautiful preacher.”

Kate sighed, and the sigh seemed older than her years. “And a minute ago, Kate, I was all agog to go; that is the way with me this month past—up and down, up and down, like the waves of the Zuyder Zee. I’d as lieve stay aside thee; say the word!”

“Nay,” said Kate, and there was command in her gentleness. “Prithee go; and bring me back every word. Well-a-day that I cannot go myself.”

Tears stood in her eyes—not loud tears, but quiet, like dew on a bruised leaf. That decided Margaret more than any urging. She kissed Kate; then, looking under her lashes at the boy, she heaved a little sigh.

“I trow I must not,” she whispered. “I never could kiss him a little; and my father was dead against waking a child by day or night. When ’tis thy pleasure to wake, speak thy aunt Kate the two new words thou hast gotten.”

Then she went out, looking back with love that was also pain, and shut the door so softly that even the house’s hush did not notice.

Below, Catherine had not wasted the time. She had the vegetables ready—roots pared, greens washed, the salad awaiting its dressing. The kitchen smelled of thrift and good intention: vinegar sharp in a small jug, herbs bruised between fingers to wake their oils, bread warming near the fire so that its crust gave out a nutty scent. A house’s memory lives in such smells; long after names fade from signs, the nose can summon back a Sunday like this.

“Joan, you will lend me a hand, and peel these?” said Catherine, turning a heap of pears or apples in the basket so that the sound of them—a soft knocking—mixed with the pot’s steady song.

“That I will, dame,” said Joan, and set to with a will.

They worked with silent vigour for a while, the knives whispering, the peel falling in curls. Outside, the street carried its own Sabbath noises—footsteps in clusters toward the new kerk, a distant child’s call, the brief hush when a group passed as if the houses themselves listened. Catherine valued that Sabbath sound: it was human, it had room for breath. Not the clang and roar that merchants sometimes praised as “progress,” as if noise itself were coin. A town that cannot hear its own prayers, she thought, is a town that will soon forget its own tongue.

“Now, Joan,” said she at last, “them which help me cook and serve the meat, they help me eat it; that’s a rule.”

“There’s worse laws in Holland than that,” said Joan, pleased. “Your will is my pleasure, mistress; for my Luke hath got his supper i’ the air. He is digging to-day by good luck.”

Margaret came down then, the borrowed handkerchief fresh about her, her face brightened by washing, her hair obedient. For an instant even Catherine, who could be sharp as mustard, softened at the sight; it was as if the hearth’s glow had risen into Margaret’s cheeks.

“Eh, woman,” Catherine exclaimed, half laughing, “yon is an ugly trade. There she has just washed her face and gi’en her hair a turn, and now who is like her? Rotterdam, that for you!” and she snapped her fingers at the capital, as if to say the great city could not outdo this small domestic miracle.

“Give us a buss, hussy!” she added, and kissed her. “Now mind, Eli won’t wait supper for the duke. Wherefore, loiter not after your kerk is over.”

Joan and Catherine both followed Margaret to the door and stood there, watching her go a good way down the street. Among homely housewives, the going-out is half an incident; it alters the sound of the house, like lifting a lid off a pot. Margaret’s steps joined the stream of others toward the church, and for a moment the street’s cadence was like a psalm: many feet, one purpose.

Catherine watched until Margaret was a smaller figure among larger ones, then spoke, not loudly, but with that confidential tone women use when they speak of what troubles them most.

“There, Joan,” said she, “it is almost to me as if I had just started my own daughter for kerk, and stood a-looking after: the which I’ve done it manys and manys the times. Joan, lass, she won’t hear a word against our Gerard; and he be alive, he has used her cruel; that is why my bowels yearn for the poor wench. I’m older and wiser than she; and so I’ll wed her to yon simple Luke, and there an end. What’s one grandchild?”
