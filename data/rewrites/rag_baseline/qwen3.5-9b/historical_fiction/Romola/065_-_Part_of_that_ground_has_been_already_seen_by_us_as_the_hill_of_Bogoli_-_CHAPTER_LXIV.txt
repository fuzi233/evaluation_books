The announcement of Tito's arrival preceded him, and Fra Niccolò, Savonarola's secretary, ushered him up the spiraling stone stairs into the monastery's labyrinthine corridors. These passages, lined with cells, bore witness to Fra Angelico's frescoes—delicate as rainbows suspended on melting clouds—which occasionally startled the unaccustomed eye. Here, amidst the gloom, the painted Madonnas seemed to sit crowned in radiant glory, their divine infants gazing forth with perpetual promise, casting sudden reflections from an ethereal world onto the bare, whitewashed walls.

It was an hour of relative quiet in the monastery; most cells stood empty, illuminated only by the pale, filtered light of narrow windows falling upon nothing but stark plaster, a hard pallet, and a crucifix. Even beyond the heavy door at the end of the corridor—the inner sanctum where the Prior typically sat at his desk or received private visitors—a shaft of high light revealed only one additional object: the broad back of a figure clad in the long white Dominican tunic and scapulary. A man knelt there, head bowed before a crucifix. To the casual observer, he appeared indistinguishable from any ordinary Fra Girolamo, a soul burdened only by minor sins: fleeting distractions during choir practice or a petty resentment when a fellow friar spilled ink upon a miniature. Such a man sought only the narrow ladder of prayer, fasting, and obedience to climb safely into Paradise. Yet, beneath this pristine white garment beat a heart pulsing with a consciousness alien to the average monk, perhaps incomprehensible to any man who had not forged self-knowledge through a tumultuous inner storm. Within this vessel, irrevocable errors and lapses from truth were inextricably entwined with noble purposes and sincere convictions. Self-justifying expediency was woven so tightly into the fabric of a grand design that the entire being seemed incapable of abandoning it, just as the body cannot cease trembling before the objects of hope and fear. Consequently, regardless of the path chosen, the conscience found no perfect repose.

Savonarola was not merely in the posture of prayer; Latin supplications rolled from his lips. Yet, he prayed in name only. Having collapsed to his knees, he had erupted into words of beseeching, seeking a surge of calmness that would serve as divine warrant against the resolutions urged upon him by crowding thoughts and passions. However, the premonitions and impulses working within him for the past hour remained imperious. As he pressed his hands against his face and muttered aloud, *"Cor mundum crea in me,"* his mind remained cluttered with the imagery of the trap his enemies had set, busy constructing arguments to justify himself against their taunts.

His defense extended beyond his adversaries. That morning, he had received fresh evidence that his own followers were as eager to push for the Trial by Fire as his detractors. They desired, tacitly expecting, that he finally accept the challenge to evoke the long-awaited miracle destined to dispel doubt and crush malignity. Had he not proclaimed that God would reveal Himself at the fitting time? To the pragmatic Florentines, eager to settle partisan disputes, no moment seemed more fitting than this. Indeed, if Fra Domenico emerged from the flames unscathed, *that* would be a miracle, and the fervent faith of that good brother served as a cheering omen. Yet, Savonarola was acutely aware that his explanations had failed to quell the secret longing of his followers to see him step forward.

It was impossible for him to satisfy them. With bitter distress, he realized he could no longer resist the prosecution of the trial regarding Fra Domenico. He had not lied when declaring his belief in a future supernatural attestation; however, his constitution rendered such belief conditional. While it was easy for him to envision a distant, undefined miracle shielded by logical necessity, it was equally easy to trust in inward miracles—his own prophetic inspiration and divinely wrought intuitions. Conversely, believing in the probability of a specific, tangible miracle—one requiring him to walk through fire, pressing every detail upon his imagination and demanding exceptional action—was insurmountably difficult.

Savonarola's nature harbored opposing tendencies in nearly equal measure: a passionate sensibility, impatient of rigid thought, flooding every concept with emotion and drifting toward contemplative ecstasy, alternating with a keen perception of external facts and a vigorous, practical judgment of men and things. In this instance, the latter traits were activated by an acute physical sensitivity that amplified the conception of pain and destruction as an inevitable consequence of prior suffering. The speed with which men agree to touch red-hot iron with a wet finger cannot be measured by their theoretical acceptance of the danger; practical belief hinges on what is most vividly represented in the mind at that moment. For the Frate, when the Trial by Fire was presented as an immediate demand, it became impossible for him to believe that he—or anyone—could traverse the flames unharmed. Impossible, furthermore, to believe that even if he resolved to offer himself, he would not recoil at the final threshold.

The Florentines, however, were unlikely to make such fine distinctions. To the common mind, finding moral questions easy and judging conduct via concise alternatives constitutes proof of mental vigor. Nothing appeared plainer than that a man who once declared God would not leave him without a miraculous guarantee, yet retreated when the test was proposed, had spoken falsely. Were not Fra Domenico and Fra Mariano, along with countless *Piagnoni*, ready to enter the fire? What drove their superior courage if not superior faith? Savonarola could not adequately explain his conduct to his friends, nor to himself. Naked emotions rush to clothe themselves in convenient propositions; articulating the true state of one's interior requires more than mere sincerity, even when unmixed. In these very moments, as Savonarola knelt in audible prayer, he had ceased to hear the words. They were drowned by internal argumentative voices shaping reasons for an outward audience.

"To appeal to heaven for a miracle by rashly accepting a challenge, a mere snare prepared by ignoble foes, would be tempting God, and the appeal would go unanswered. Let the Pope's legate arrive, let the ambassadors of all great powers come promising that the convocation of a General Council and the reform of the Church depend upon this miracle, and I will enter the flames, trusting God will not withhold His seal from that great work. Until then, I reserve myself for higher duties directly laid upon me: it is not permitted to leap from the chariot to wrestle with every loud vaunter. Yet, Fra Domenico's invincible zeal to enter the trial may signal a Divine vocation, a pledge that the miracle—"

"No!" When Savonarola focused his mind on the threatened scene in the Piazza, imagining a human body entering the fire, his belief recoiled violently. It was not an event his imagination could simply visualize; he felt it with shuddering vibrations traveling to the extremities of his sensitive fingers. The miracle could not occur. Nay, the trial itself was not meant to happen; he was warranted in using all his power to prevent it. Fuel might be gathered in the Piazza, crowds assembled, formalities completed—all this might now be inevitable, and he could no longer resist without bringing dishonor upon himself, yes, and thereby upon the cause of God. But it was not truly intended that the Franciscan should enter the fire; while *he* hesitated, there existed means to block Fra Domenico's entry. At the absolute worst, if Fra Domenico were compelled to proceed, he should carry the consecrated Host, and with that Mystery in hand, there might be a warrant to expect the ordinary effects of fire to cease; or, more likely, this demand would be resisted, becoming a final obstacle to the trial.

These intentions could not be avowed. He must appear frankly to await the trial and trust in its outcome. This divergence between inward reality and outward appearance was not the Christian simplicity he had strived for through his youth and prime, preaching it as a chief fruit of the Divine life. In the stress and heat of the day, with cheeks burning and shouts ringing in the ears, who remains blessed enough to recall the yearnings of the cool, silent morning and realize they have betrayed them?

"O God, it is for the sake of the people—for they are blind—for their faith depends on me. If I don sackcloth and cast myself among the ashes, who will take up the standard and lead the battle? Have I not been led by a path unknown to the work lying before me?"

The conflict was endless. In the effort of prayerful pleading, his uneasy mind constantly washed its wounds in thoughts of the magnitude of that task, unique to a man inspired with the vision and daring of a sacred rebel. Even the words of prayer had faded. He continued to kneel, but his mind was filled with visions of repercussions rippling across Europe, the sense of immediate difficulties lost in the glow of that grandeur, until the knock at the door announced the anticipated visitor.

Savonarola drew on his mantle before exiting his cell, adhering to custom when receiving guests. With that immediate responsiveness to external appeals characteristic of a power-loving nature accustomed to making its influence felt through speech, he met Tito with a gaze as self-possessed and strong as if he had risen from resolution rather than conflict.

Tito did not kneel, offering instead a greeting of profound deference, which Savonarola received quietly, devoid of sacerdotal formulae. Desiring Tito to sit, he asked immediately:

"Your business carries some weight, my son, unsuitable for transmission through intermediaries?"
