Martin had plotted with considerable skill. He had devised a cunning scheme for his private enjoyment. Yet, seasoned schemers far more experienced than he often find their most intricate plans swept away by the sudden broom of Fate—that domestic housekeeper whose red arm no human can restrain. In this instance, the broom was crafted from the tough fibers of Moore's own stubborn will, tightly bound with his determination. He was regaining his strength and making strange headway against Mrs. Horsfall. Each morning he astonished that matron with a fresh surprise. First, he dismissed her from her valet duties; he would dress himself. Next, he refused the coffee she brought; he would breakfast with the family. Finally, he barred her from his chamber. On the very same day, amid the shrieks of every woman in the house, he thrust his head outdoors. The following morning, he accompanied Mr. Yorke to his counting-house and requested an envoy to summon a carriage from the Red House Inn. He was resolved, he declared, to return home to the Hollow that very afternoon. Mr. Yorke, instead of opposing, aided and abetted him. The carriage was ordered, despite Mrs. Yorke's declaration that the step would prove fatal. It arrived. Moore, little inclined to speak, allowed his purse to serve as his tongue. He expressed his gratitude to the servants and to Mrs. Horsfall through the clinking of coins. The latter figure approved and understood this language perfectly; it made amends for all prior insolence. She and her patient became the best of friends.

Having visited and soothed the kitchen staff, Moore retired to the parlour. He had Mrs. Yorke to appease—a task less simple than pacifying her maids. There she sat immersed in sullen anger, lost in gloomy speculations regarding the depths of man's ingratitude. He approached and bent over her; she was forced to look up, merely to bid him "go away." Beauty remained in his pale, wasted features; there was earnestness and a certain sweetness—since he was smiling—in his hollow eyes.

"Good-bye!" he said, and as he spoke, the smile glittered and melted. He possessed no iron mastery over his emotions now; a trivial sentiment surfaced in his weakened state.

"And what are you leaving us for?" she asked. "We will keep you, and do anything in the world for you, if you will only stay until you are stronger."

"Good-bye!" he said again, adding, "You have been a mother to me; give your wilful son one embrace."

Like a stranger, as he was, he offered her first one cheek, then the other. She kissed him.

"What a trouble—what a burden I have been to you!" he murmured.

"You are the greatest trouble now, headstrong youth!" was the reply. "I wonder who is to nurse you at Hollow's Cottage? Your sister Hortense knows no more about such matters than a child."

"Thank God! For I have had nursing enough to last me a lifetime."

At this moment, the little girls entered—Jessie crying, Rose quiet but grave. Moore led them out into the hall to soothe, pet, and kiss them. He knew it was not in their mother's nature to endure seeing any living thing caressed save herself. She would have felt annoyed had he fondled a kitten in her presence.

The boys stood around the carriage as Moore entered it; yet for them he held no farewell. To Mr. Yorke he merely said, "You have a good riddance of me. That was an unlucky shot for you, Yorke; it turned Briarmains into a hospital. Come and visit me at the cottage soon."

He drew up the glass; the carriage rolled away. Within half an hour, he alighted at his own garden wicket. After paying the driver and dismissing the vehicle, he leaned against the wicket for a moment, resting and musing.

"Six months ago I passed out at this gate," he said, "a proud, angry, disappointed man. I return sadder and wiser; weakly enough, but not worried. A cold, gray, yet quiet world lies around—a world where, if I hope little, I fear nothing. All slavish terrors of embarrassment have departed. Let the worst come, I can work, as Joe Scott does, for an honorable living; in such doom I yet see hardship but no degradation. Formerly, financial ruin was equivalent in my eyes to personal dishonor. It is not so now; I know the difference. Ruin *is* an evil, but one for which I am prepared; the day of its arrival I know, for I have calculated. I can yet postpone it six months—not an hour longer. If circumstances alter by then, which is improbable; if fetters, which now seem indissoluble, should loosen from our trade (of all things the least likely to happen), I might conquer in this long struggle yet—I might—good God! what might I not do? But the thought is a brief madness; let me view things with sane eyes. Ruin will come, lay her axe to my fortune's roots, and hew them down. I shall snatch a sapling, cross the sea, and plant it in American woods. Louis will go with me. Will anyone else go? I cannot tell—I have no right to ask."

He entered the house.

It was afternoon, twilight yet lingering outdoors—starless and moonless; for though keenly freezing with a dry, black frost, heaven wore a mask of clouds congealed and firmly locked. The mill-dam was also frozen. The Hollow was very still. Indoors, it was already dark. Sarah had lit a good fire in the parlour; she was preparing tea in the kitchen.

"Hortense," said Moore, as his sister hurried up to help remove his cloak, "I am pleased to come home."

Hortense did not feel the peculiar novelty of this expression coming from her brother, who had never before called the cottage his home, and to whom its narrow limits had always seemed restrictive rather than protective. Still, whatever contributed to his happiness pleased her, and she expressed herself accordingly.

He sat down, but soon rose again. He walked to the window; he returned to the fire.

"Hortense!"

"My brother?"

"This little parlour looks very clean and pleasant—unusually bright, somehow."

"It is true, brother; I have had the entire house thoroughly and scrupulously cleaned during your absence."

"Sister, I think on this first day of your return home you ought to have a friend or two for tea, if only to see how fresh and spruce you have made the little place."

"True, brother. If it were not late, I might send for Miss Mann."

"So you might; but it really is too late to disturb that good lady, and the evening is far too cold for her to venture out."

"How thoughtful in you, dear Gérard! We must postpone it to another day."

"I want someone today, dear sister—some quiet guest who would tire neither of us."

"Miss Ainley?"

"An excellent person, they say; but she lives too far away. Tell Harry Scott to ride to the rectory with a request from you that Caroline Helstone should come and spend the evening with you."

"Would it not be better tomorrow, dear brother?"

"I should like her to see the place as it is just now; its brilliant cleanliness and perfect neatness are so much to your credit."

"It might benefit her in the way of example."

"It might and must; she ought to come."

He went into the kitchen.

"Sarah, delay tea half an hour." He then commissioned her to dispatch Harry Scott to the rectory, handing her a hastily scribbled note in pencil, addressed "Miss Helstone."

Scarcely had Sarah time to grow impatient under the fear of damaging her already prepared toast when the messenger returned, bringing the invited guest.

She entered through the kitchen, quietly tripped up Sarah's stairs to remove her bonnet and furs, and came down as silently, with her beautiful curls neatly smoothed, her graceful merino dress and delicate collar all trim and spotless, her gay little work-bag in her hand. She lingered to exchange a few kindly words with Sarah, to look at the new tortoiseshell kitten basking on the kitchen hearth, and to speak to the canary-bird, which a sudden flare from the fire had startled on its perch; and then she proceeded to the parlour.

The gentle greeting, the friendly welcome, were exchanged in such a tranquil manner as befit cousins meeting; a sense of pleasure, subtle and quiet as a perfume, diffused itself through the room; the newly-kindled lamp burned brightly; the tray and the singing urn were brought in.

"I am pleased to come home," repeated Mr. Moore.

They assembled round the table. Hortense chiefly talked. She congratulated Caroline on the evident improvement in her health. Her color and plump cheeks were returning, she remarked. It was true. There was an obvious change in Miss Helstone. Everything about her seemed elastic; depression, fear, forlornness, were withdrawn. No longer crushed, saddened, slow, and drooping, she looked like one who had tasted the cordial of heart's ease and been lifted on the wings of hope.
