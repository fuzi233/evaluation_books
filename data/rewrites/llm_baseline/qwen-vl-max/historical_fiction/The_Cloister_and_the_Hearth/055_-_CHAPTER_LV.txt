Two months before the scene in Eli’s home, the people of a small coastal village between Naples and Rome gathered on the shore, their eyes fixed upon a ship battling a fierce gale that drove straight toward the land. At times, the vessel seemed to gain ground against the storm, and the watchers cheered aloud; then, as the wind and waves pressed harder, they watched with silent satisfaction—though none would admit it even to themselves—that she might not survive. For though no one delights in another’s suffering, there is a strange comfort in seeing others endure what one has escaped.

The ship, though poorly built for navigation, was perfectly made for wrecking: her towering stern caught every gust, and her lines curved like an inverted hat. To those on the beach, she appeared alive—a creature gasping for breath, fighting death with a trembling heart. But had they stood upon her deck, they would have seen not one soul, but many, each revealing their true nature in the hour of peril.

On the deck, sailors stumbled about, hauling ropes without order, cursing one moment, praying the next. Passengers huddled near the mast—some sitting, some kneeling, others lying flat—clutching the railings as the ship heaved and rolled through monstrous waves. Among them stood a young man, pale as ash but with lips tightly pressed, his face a mask of fear and pride. It was Gerard, doomed by fate to this night.

Amidst the throng, prayers rose from every corner—vows offered to gods both known and imagined. The sailors called only to one: “Queen of Heaven,” “Star of the Sea,” “Mistress of the World,” “Haven of Safety.” But among the landfolk, polytheism ran wild. An English merchant vowed gold to Our Lady of Walsingham; a Genoese promised a silver collar to Our Lady of Loretto; a Tuscan noble pledged wax candles to Ravenna’s Virgin. Each clung to their own version of salvation, swearing not on the True Cross, but on its replica in some distant city.

Then came a sudden gust—stronger than any before. The rotten shrouds snapped with a crack, and the sail tore free, flapping downwind like a blackened sheet until it vanished into the sea half a mile off. Before the helmsman could turn the ship, a wave struck her quarter, drenching all aboard and offering a chilling preview of death.

Now vows multiplied. One swore to become a Carthusian monk if St. Thomas spared him. Another promised a barefoot pilgrimage to Compostela, clad only in chain mail, if St. James would save him. Others invoked Thomas, Dominic, Denis, and above all, Catherine of Sienna. Two Neapolitan traders stood shivering. One shouted: “I vow to St. Christopher at Paris a waxen image of his own weight—if I reach land!” His companion nudged him. “Brother, take care! Even if you sold all your possessions, you couldn’t buy his weight in wax.” “Hold your tongue,” snapped the other. Then, in a whisper: “Do you think I meant it? Let me live, and I’ll give him not a single rush.”

Others lay prostrate, pleading with the sea itself: “Oh, merciful sea! Generous sea! Bountiful sea! Beautiful sea! Be gentle! Preserve us!” Some wailed with animal terror at each new plunge of the ship, now nothing more than a toy tossed by titanic waves.

Yet among the chaos sat a Roman woman of humble birth, her child at her breast. Her face was ashen, but her eyes calm. Her lips moved in silent prayer—no tears, no cries, no bargains with the gods. When the ship dipped so low that men screamed, she kissed her child. That was all. She sat still, feeding him in the jaws of death—for why should he lose joy she could give him, even as she died?

There, amid the medieval crowd, sat Antiquity. Sixteen hundred years had not dulled the blood of Rome in her veins. Instinct, perhaps forgotten by name, taught her to die with dignity.

Nearby, a giant friar stood on the poop, feet wide apart like the Colossus of Rhodes—not defying danger, but ignoring it. With a steady voice, he recited verses from the Canticles and invited passengers to confess. Some knelt, and he absolved them as though in a quiet sacristy, not a sinking ship. Gerard drew closer, drawn by instinct to strength. And indeed, the courage of heroes might have faltered beside this friar—whose composure was greater than his size.

Thus, two upheld the dignity of humanity: a woman, tender yet unbroken; a monk, steeled by faith.

With the sail gone, the sailors cut down the mast just above the deck. It fell overboard with a crash, leaving the ship lighter—but now helpless. Without canvas, she could no longer outrun the sea. Wave after wave struck her stern, each blow like a mountain falling. The captain left the helm, pale as death. “Lighten her!” he cried. “Throw everything overboard—or we sink before we strike, and lose our last chance.”

The crew obeyed. Passengers seized whatever they could find and hurled it into the sea. Soon they came upon a heavy sack. Beneath it lay an old man, sick and weak. They dragged the sack from under him—it rattled. Two men pulled it to the side. Up leapt the owner, shrieking: “Holy Moses! What do you mean? This is my fortune—silver candlesticks, plates, brooches, hanaps—everything!” “Let go, you old thief!” shouted the others. “Will you risk our lives for your stolen goods?” “Throw him in too!” someone yelled. “It’s this Jew who’s drowning us!”

They wrestled the sack away and cast it into the water. It sank. The man stared, white-haired, hair streaming in the wind, ready to leap after it—had it floated. But it disappeared forever. He staggered, tearing at his hair, cursing the sea, the ship, the heavens, and hell itself.

Then the captain pointed: “A church! Steer for it, mate—and pray to the saint it honors!”

They turned the ship. A massive wave broke the rudder, jammed it fast, and flooded the deck. Panic surged. Some turned on Gerard. “He’s the cause!” they roared. “He never prayed to a saint. He’s a pagan!”

“Alas, good friends,” Gerard stammered, teeth chattering, “I honor the saints—but now there’s no time. What good are Dominic or Thomas? Saint Peter sits nearer God’s throne. If I pray to Him, He may not have time to plead for me. I must speak directly to the Creator of sea and sky and man. Our Father in heaven, save these souls—and me! Oh, sweet Jesus, who walked on Galilee when Peter sank, who wept for Lazarus when others were dry—save poor Gerard—for Margaret’s sake!”

At that moment, sailors began loading the lifeboat. Thirty souls crowded in—men driven by self-preservation. Left behind: three bewildered, two paralyzed by terror. The paralyzed sat like wet rags; the bewildered ran back and forth, watching the boat pull away, but making no move to follow.

One remained on his knees, clutching a wooden statue of the Virgin Mary—life-sized, torn from the mast. It bobbed in the rising water as he followed it on his knees, hands clasped, water swirling around them.

And the Jew—palsied not by fear, but grief—sat cross-legged, mourning his lost bag. Whenever spray hit him, he raised a fist and cursed the Nazarenes, their gods, their ships, their waters—forever.

The Dominican friar, having confessed all, stood serene, communing with himself.

The Roman woman sat pale, drawing her child closer.

Gerard saw this—and his spirit stirred. “Look!” he said. “They’ve taken the boat—and left the woman and child to die.”

His mind worked quickly. “Wife, I’ll save you—God willing.” He searched for a plank or cask—none. Then his eye fell on the Virgin’s image. He snatched it up, heedless of the cry from its worshipper, like a child robbed of a toy. He ran aft.

“Come, wife,” he called. “I’ll lash you and the child to this. It’s rotten, but it will float.”

She looked at him—her great dark eyes full of sorrow. “Thyself?”

With quiet nobility, he answered: “I am a man—no child to care for.”

She nodded. “Ah.”

He tied the statue to her side. She trembled slightly—yet her gaze remained firm. The ship was nearly submerged. Using an oar as a lever, he slid her into the waves.

“Come,” he said. “While there’s time.”

She turned to him, eyes now wet. “Poor youth—God forgive me—my child!”

He launched her onto the surge, using the oar to keep her from smashing against the hull.

Then a strong hand gripped his shoulder. A deep voice spoke: “It is well. Now come with me.”

It was the friar.

Gerard turned. The friar took two strides and grasped the broken mast. Gerard followed, instinctively. Together, after immense effort, they hoisted the mast and carried it off.

“Throw it in,” said the friar. “And follow.”

They did. But one bewildered passenger rushed after them and jumped on one end. Gerard seized the other—the friar the middle. They drifted, the mast rising and plunging like a horse, waves lashing their faces, blinding them, threatening to knock them off.

Suddenly, ahead, a long grating sound. The ship had struck. Moments later, she lay still—and they were thrown against her with terrible force. Their companion’s head cracked against the rudder—like a coconut under a hammer. He sank instantly, leaving only a stain on the water, a clot on the wood, and a dying cry echoing in their ears as they drifted clear.

The friar murmured a short Latin prayer, then stepped forward calmly. They tumbled along—sometimes swallowed by waves, sometimes glimpsing the shore, where people waved and shouted. Between them and the shore, the woman bobbed, paddling with one arm, holding her child tight.

After a long time, the friar said quietly: “I touched the ground.”

“Impossible, father,” said Gerard. “We’re over a hundred yards out. Please—don’t leave the mast.”

“My son,” said the friar, “you speak wisely. But I have duties to Holy Church. I cannot waste time floating when I can walk. There—I felt it again. See the benefit of sandals. Again—sandy. You are shorter than I. Stay with the mast. I walk.”

He stepped off and plunged forward, arms wide, pushing through the water. Gerard followed. Each wave swept over them, but the friar stood firm, head back, mouth closed, disappearing beneath the foam, then reemerging, swimming powerfully.

At last, they neared the shore—but the current pulled them outward. Then fishermen came, linked by long spears, forming a chain. They dragged the two ashore.

The friar shook himself, gave a brief blessing, and walked on—eyes fixed forward, no glance back at the sea that had nearly claimed him. For him, the Master’s will alone determined harm or safety.

As he strode alone toward Rome, I—no servant of the Church—pause to note that we met this giant once before, narrowly escaping him. Now, I fear, we are fated to meet him again.

Gerard grasped every hand on the beach. They brought him to a fire and, with rare delicacy, left him alone to dry. Only then did he retrieve a parchment and paper from his breast, drying them carefully. When satisfied, he accepted fisherman’s clothes and returned to the shore.

What he saw was brief. The captain lingered by the wreck—not from bravery, but because he believed Castor or Pollux had come for him in fire. Yet the sea broke the ship apart, sweeping him and the poop clear—and carried him safely ashore. Gerard helped pull him from the water.

The Jewish trader landed on a fragment and, touching earth, offered a reward for his bag—drawing laughter, not pity. Two others survived on wreckage. The thirty who fled in the boat arrived—each one dead, except one who still breathed. The natives, meaning well, placed him near a hot fire—and he too passed on.

As Gerard stood by the sea, watching his former companions washed ashore, a hand gently touched his shoulder. He turned.

It was the Roman woman—glowing with gratitude. She took his hand, lifted it slowly to her lips, and kissed it—not as a beggar, but as a queen conferring honor. Then, radiant and tearful, she held up her child and made him kiss his savior.

Gerard kissed the child again and again. He loved children. But he said nothing. He was deeply moved—for she spoke not a word, only with her eyes, her cheeks, her ancient gesture. Perhaps she was right. Gratitude needs no words.

It was an ancient Roman matron thanking a modern man from the depths of her soul.

The next day, late afternoon, Gerard—twice as old as the year before, thrice as wise in human ways, no longer a boy, but a man who had fought, bled, and faced death by land and sea—arrived in Rome. Safe, at last, after all storms. *Post tot naufragia tutus.*
