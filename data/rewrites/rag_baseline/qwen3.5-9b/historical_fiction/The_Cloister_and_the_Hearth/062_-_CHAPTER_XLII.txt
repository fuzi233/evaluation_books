Gerard returned to consciousness, and with it, the crushing weight of despair settled upon his spirit. By the second day, fever had seized his brain, driving him into a ravishing delirium. Beside his bed, upon a stark table, lay his rich auburn hair, cascading long and luxuriant as a woman's tresses—a severed crown marking the end of his former self. One deadly symptom swiftly followed another, accelerating toward the grave. On the fifth day, the leech withdrew, declaring the patient beyond aid. As the sun dipped below the horizon that same evening, Gerard succumbed to a profound slumber. Whispers circulated among the townsfolk that he would awaken only to meet his final breath. Yet, an elderly gossip, whose counsel held sway due to her experience as a professional nurse, insisted that his youth might yet be his salvation, provided he could endure a single cycle of twelve uninterrupted hours.

On this promise, his aged landlady swept the room clear, leaving him to rest in solitude. She vowed a wax candle to the Virgin Mary for every hour he slept. Twelve hours passed. The good soul rejoiced, falling to her knees to thank the Virgin. Twenty-four hours elapsed. Doubt crept into the hearts of the nurses. At the thirtieth hour, she summoned the physician. "Thirty hours!" she cried anxiously. "Shall we wake him?" The doctor examined him closely, his brow furrowed. "His breath is steady, his hand moist," he observed. "There are learned leeches who would wake him merely to inspect his tongue, finding nothing but confusion; but we, being women, possess the wisdom to let nature take its course. When has sleep ever harmed a racked brain or a torn heart?"

When the clock struck forty-eight hours, the news spread, and the curious thronged the doors, requiring great effort to keep them at bay. They were admitted only to Fra Colonna and his companion, the gigantic Fra Jerome. These two men relieved the anxious women, sitting in silent vigil. Fra Colonna gazed upon his young friend with tears streaming silently down his cheeks, while Fra Jerome, beads clasped tightly in his hand, regarded the sleeper with the same calm detachment he had once shown the churning sea during their encounter hand-to-hand.

It was likely near the sixtieth hour of this unnatural slumber that the landlady nudged Fra Colonna with her elbow. He leaned forward. Gerard had opened his eyes as gently as if he had merely been dozing. He stared blankly, drawing himself up slightly in the bed. He reached for his head and discovered his hair was gone. He noticed Fra Colonna and smiled with a fleeting pleasure. But midway through the smile, his expression froze, and in an instant, his face convulsed with anguish unspeakable. A loud cry escaped his throat, and he turned his face firmly to the wall. His good landlady wept openly; she knew the hollow ache of awakening bereft. Fra Jerome began to recite canticles and prayers from his breviary. Gerard curled himself into the bedclothes, retreating further inward. Fra Colonna approached him, whimpering softly, reminding him that all was not lost. "The divine Muses are immortal," he urged. "You must transfer your affection to them; they will never betray you nor fail you like fragile creatures of clay." Overcome by his own emotion, the simple father hurried away.

Fra Jerome remained. "Young man," he said, his voice low and grave, "the Muses exist only in the minds of pagans and visionaries. The Church alone grants repose to the heart on earth and happiness to the soul hereafter. Has the earth deceived thee? Has passion broken your heart after tearing it apart? The Church opens her arms: consecrate thy gifts to her! The Church is peace of mind." He spoke these words solemnly at the threshold and departed the moment they were uttered.

"The Church!" Gerard screamed, rising furiously and shaking his fist at the departing friar. "Malediction on the Church! But for the Church, I should not lie broken here, while she lies cold, cold, cold in Holland. Oh, my Margaret! Oh, my darling! My darling! And I must flee from thee for the few months thou hadst to live. Cruel! Cruel! The monsters allowed her to die. Death does not come without signs; these blind, selfish wretches saw them not, or cared not; but I had seen them, I who loved her. Oh, had I been there, I would have saved her, I would have saved her. Idiot! Idiot! To leave her for a mere moment!" He wept bitterly for a long time. Then, suddenly bursting into fresh rage, he cried vehemently, "The Church! For whose sake I was driven from her! My malediction be on the Church! And on the hypocrites who name it to my broken heart. Accursed be the world! Ghysbrecht lives; Margaret dies. Thieves, murderers, harlots, live forever. Only angels die. Curse life! Curse death! And whosoever made them what they are!"

The friar did not hear these mad and wicked words, catching only the raw yell of rage flung after him. It was fortunate, for had he heard them, he would have ordered the late shipmate burned in the forum with as little hesitation as one might roast a kid. The old landlady, having accompanied Fra Colonna downstairs, heard the raised voice and returned in some anxiety. She found Gerard dressing, tears streaming down his face. She remonstrated gently.

"What avail lies in my lying here?" he said gloomily. "Can I find here that which I seek?"

"Saints preserve us! Is he distraught again? What seek ye?"

"Oblivion."

"Oblivion, my little heart? Oh, but you are young to talk so."

"Young or old, what else have I to live for?"

He donned his finest clothes. The good dame protested. "My pretty Gerard, know that it is Tuesday, not Sunday."

"Oh, Tuesday is it? I thought it had been Saturday."

"Nay, thou hast slept long. Thou never wearst thy brave clothes on working days. Consider."

"What I did when she lived, I did. Now I shall do whatever erst I did not. The past is the past. There lies my hair, and with it my way of life. I have served one Master as well as I could. You see my reward. Now I'll serve another, and give him a fair trial too."

"Alas!" sighed the woman, turning pale. "What mean these dark words? And what new master is this whose service thou wouldst try?"

And with this horrible declaration on his lips, the miserable creature walked out. His cap and feather were set jauntily on one side, masking a sinister face pale as ashes, drawn down as if by premature age, his feeble limbs carrying him forward into the unknown.
