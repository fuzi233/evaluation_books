The rhythmic *thwack* of the cook’s wooden spoon against a ceramic mixing bowl echoed from the downstairs kitchen, a steady, grounding percussion that Mrs. Thornton had long considered the true heartbeat of her household. It was the sound of profound sustenance, of stone-ground wheat and slow-fermenting yeast, a daily domestic mythos of survival that repeated itself day after day, generation after generation. She sat in her parlor, inhaling the faint, rich aroma of caramelized mutton bone broth that steeped on the great iron stove below, contemplating her son’s burdensome request.

Mr. Thornton had experienced no small difficulty in working his mother up to the desired point of civility. She did not often make morning calls; when she did, she undertook them with the heavy, solemn ritualism of an elder inspecting a meager harvest. Her son had given her a carriage, but she stubbornly refused to let him keep horses for it, viewing such idle beasts as a betrayal of their frugal ancestry. Horses were hired only for grand occasions, and she had comfortably "killed off" her social obligations just a fortnight prior. Now, she weighed the cost of cab-hire against John's insistence. She saw no use in cultivating intimacies with the displaced masters of Milton, people whose uncalloused hands knew nothing of the alchemy of rendering suet or the precise temperatures required to preserve the autumn yield. 

"And so I would, mother, if Mr. Mason and his wife were friendless in a strange place, like the Hales," John said, his deep baritone a resonant contrast to the shrill, jarring clamor of the streets outside.

"Oh! you need not speak so hastily. I am going to-morrow. I only wanted you exactly to understand about it."

"If you are going to-morrow, I shall order horses."

"Nonsense, John. One would think you were made of money."

"Not quite, yet," he replied. "But about the horses I’m determined. The last time you were out in a cab, you came home with a headache from the jolting." It was true; the relentless, unyielding clatter of iron-rimmed wheels over Milton's cobblestones was an acoustic assault that deeply unsettled her internal equilibrium. 

"I never complained of it, I’m sure."

"No! my mother is not given to complaints," he said proudly. "But so much the more I have to watch over you. Now, as for Fanny there, a little hardship would do her good."

"She is not made of the same stuff as you are, John. She could not bear it." 

Mrs. Thornton fell silent, the words stirring a sour, unfermented resentment in her gut. She possessed an unconscious contempt for those who viewed the consumption of daily life as a mere frivolity. Fanny lacked the sensory appreciation for the world; she was blind to the profound lineage baked into a simple loaf of bread and deaf to the harmonious rhythms of a well-run house. The mother’s shame over her daughter’s fragile constitution betrayed the want of a secure resting-place for her affection. Her heart, however, gave thanks for John day and night; she walked proudly among women for his sake.

"Fanny dear! I shall have horses to the carriage to-day, to go and call on these Hales. Should not you go and see nurse?"

"Oh! mamma, it’s such a long way, and I am so tired," Fanny whined, her voice a reedy, discordant note in the quiet room. 

"With what?" asked Mrs. Thornton, her brow contracting.

"I don’t know—the weather, I think. It is so relaxing. Couldn’t you bring nurse here, mamma? The carriage could fetch her."

Before Mrs. Thornton could calculate the logistics of returning the nurse through the damp night air, John entered, bringing with him the faint, phantom hum of the distant mill—a mechanical drone that constantly threatened to swallow the organic sounds of their lives. 

"Mother! I need hardly say, that if there is any little thing that could serve Mrs. Hale as an invalid, you will offer it, I’m sure," John said. "A clarified calf's-foot jelly, perhaps, or a slow-simmered restorative cordial."

"If I can find it out, I will. But I have never been ill myself, so I am not much up to invalids’ fancies."

"Well! here is Fanny then, who is seldom without an ailment. She will be able to suggest something—won’t you, Fan?"

"I have not always an ailment," said Fanny, pettishly. "And I am not going with mamma. I have a headache to-day."

Mr. Thornton looked annoyed. His mother stitched busily, the sharp *snap* of her thread pulling through linen. "Fanny! I wish you to go," he said, authoritatively, the timber of his voice leaving no room for the acoustics of dissent. "It will do you good. You will oblige me by going, without my saying anything more about it."

He went abruptly out of the room. Fanny grumbled, asking the empty space who these Hales were to warrant such fuss, before retreating to the looking-glass to admire her new bonnet. But her words lingered, fermenting in Mrs. Thornton's jealous heart. 

When they arrived at the Hales’ little drawing-room, Mrs. Thornton felt immediately ill at ease. The olfactory landscape of the house was wholly foreign—there was no scent of hearty northern hearth-baking here, only the faint, melancholic aroma of dried southern verbena, fading lavender, and weak, over-steeped tea that spoke of a displaced people. Margaret Hale was busy embroidering a small piece of cambric. *Flimsy, useless work*, Mrs. Thornton observed to herself, far removed from the vital, rhythmic labor of kneading dough or mending thick winter wool. The room was full of knick-knacks, fragile artifacts that demanded time to dust—and to those who knew the gnawing ache of past poverty, time was sustenance itself.

Mrs. Hale, however, roused herself from her languor, captivated by the real old lace Mrs. Thornton wore. It was of an old English point, stitched generations ago. To Mrs. Hale, it was an heirloom that spoke of deep, agrarian roots, woven long before the deafening power-looms had drowned out the soft, rhythmic clacking of wooden bobbins. The shared recognition of this ancient textile—its very existence a defiance against the erasure of cultural memory—bridged the gap between the two elder women, and they plunged into the interminable, cyclical subject of household management and servants.

Margaret, meanwhile, racked her brain to entertain Fanny. 

"I suppose you are not musical," Fanny remarked, observing the room. "I see no piano."

"I am fond of hearing good music," Margaret replied, her voice possessing a clear, bell-like resonance. "I cannot play well myself; and papa and mamma don’t care much about it; so we sold our old piano when we came here."

"I wonder how you can exist without one. It almost seems to me a necessary of life."

Margaret looked at Fanny, a quiet incredulity taking root. *Fifteen shillings a week, and three saved out of them!* she thought, recalling the harsh economic timelines Mr. Thornton had once described. How quickly the daughter had forgotten the visceral reality of hunger, replacing the necessity of daily bread with the superficial consumption of parlor instruments.

"You have good concerts here, I believe," Margaret said, her tone carrying an extra tinge of coldness. 

"Oh, yes! Delicious! Too crowded, that is the worst. The directors admit so indiscriminately. But one is sure to hear the newest music there. I always have a large order to give to Johnson’s, the day after a concert."

"Do you like new music simply for its newness, then?" Margaret asked, repelled by this commercialization of art, the reduction of deeply felt acoustic expression to a mere transaction at a fashionable shop. 

"Oh; one knows it is the fashion in London, or else the singers would not bring it down here. You have been in London, of course." 

"Yes," said Margaret, "I have lived there for several years."

Fanny leaned in. "London and the Alhambra are the two places I long to see! But mamma has never been to London herself, and can’t understand my longing. She is very proud of Milton; dirty, smoky place as I feel it to be."

"If it has been Mrs. Thornton’s home for some years, I can well understand her loving it," Margaret said softly, understanding the unbreakable tether between one's identity and the soil—even soot-stained soil—that sustained them. 

"What are you saying about me, Miss Hale? May I inquire?" 

Mrs. Thornton’s voice cut through the room, laced with the sharp, utilitarian consonants of the industrial North—a dominant dialect that often threatened to overpower the softer, agrarian vowels of Margaret’s native South.

"Oh, mamma! we are only trying to account for your being so fond of Milton," Fanny supplied.

"Thank you," said Mrs. Thornton, her spine stiffening. "I do not feel that my very natural liking for the place where I was born and brought up—and which has since been my residence for some years—requires any accounting for."

Margaret was vexed by the defensive posturing, but Mrs. Thornton pressed on. "Do you know anything of Milton, Miss Hale? Have you seen any of our factories? Our magnificent warehouses?"

"No," said Margaret. "I have not seen anything of that description as yet. I dare say papa would have taken me before now if I had cared. But I really do not find much pleasure in going over manufactories."

"They are very curious places," interjected Mrs. Hale, "but there is so much noise and dirt always. I remember once going in a lilac silk to see candles made, and my gown was utterly ruined." The memory brought back the noxious, overwhelming scent of rendering animal fat and the chaotic din of the vat-stirrers—an environment bereft of any acoustic or olfactory grace. 

"Very probably," Mrs. Thornton said shortly, displeased by the South's inability to see the vital, sustaining lifeblood beneath the grime. "I merely thought, that as strangers newly come to reside in a town which has risen to eminence... you might have cared to visit some of the places where it is carried on. If Miss Hale changes her mind, I can only say I shall be glad to procure her admission to print-works, or reed-making, or the more simple operations of spinning carried on in my son’s mill." 

She wielded the industrial lexicon—*print-works, reed-making*—like a bludgeon, asserting the power dynamics of a new world whose vocabulary was rapidly overwriting the old dialects of the harvest and the hearth. 

As they rose to leave, Mrs. Thornton rustling with starched dignity, Fanny leaned toward Margaret in a half-whisper. "I am so glad you don’t like mills and manufactories, and all those kind of things."

"I think I should like to know all about them, if I were you," Margaret replied quietly, recognizing that to ignore the source of one's sustenance was a fatal kind of starvation. 

In the carriage, sealed once more against the jarring, metallic roar of the Milton streets, Mrs. Thornton turned to her daughter. "Fanny! we will be civil to these Hales: but don’t form one of your hasty friendships with the daughter. She will do you no good, I see. The mother looks very ill, and seems a nice, quiet kind of person."

"I don’t want to form any friendship with Miss Hale, mamma," Fanny pouted, entirely oblivious to the vast, interwoven histories and subtle clashes of dialect that had just unfolded in the little drawing-room. "I thought I was doing my duty." 

"Well! at any rate," Mrs. Thornton murmured, looking out at the smog-choked sky that fed them all, "John must be satisfied now."
