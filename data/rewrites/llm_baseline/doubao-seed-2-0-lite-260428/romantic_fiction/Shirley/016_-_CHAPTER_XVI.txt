The relief fund thrived. Fuelled by Shirley Keeldar’s fiery, generous example, the tireless efforts of the three parish rectors, and the quiet, unshakable diligence of their spectacled, spinster lieutenants Mary Ann Ainley and Margaret Hall, a substantial sum was soon raised. Judiciously distributed, it softened the sharp edge of want for the unemployed mill workers, and the whole neighbourhood seemed to breathe easier. For a full fortnight, no cloth had been slashed in the looms, no mill walls or manor gates had been marked by outrage across the three parishes. Shirley walked her lands with a light step, convinced the threat of storm had nearly passed. Summer was drawing close, and trade always picked up with the sun, did it not? That weary, dragging war could not rage forever. Peace would come, and when it did, it would send commerce surging forward like a river freed from ice.

This was the line she always pressed on her tenant, Gérard Moore, whenever they crossed paths somewhere private enough to talk. He listened so quiet, so still, that it never failed to fray her patience. She would dart him an impatient glance, demanding more than that measured silence — a word of agreement, an explanation, something. He would smile then, that soft, rare smile that lent his mouth a surprising sweetness even as his brow stayed set and grave, and answer that he too staked all his hopes on the war ending soon, that every one of his risky speculations was anchored to that belief.

“You know full well,” he told her, the low rumble of his voice holding a quiet edge of gratitude, “that Hollow’s Mill runs on nothing but speculation right now. I can sell nothing — there is no market for it. I weave, I card, I spin all to be ready for the first day the ports open again, the first breath of peace. Three months ago that would have been impossible. I’d run my credit and my capital dry. You know who came to my rescue, whose loan kept the mill doors from closing. It’s that money that lets me play this bold game now, the one I thought I’d never get to play again. I know if I lose I’m ruined, I know the win is far from certain — but I can’t be downhearted. Not while my hands are free to work, to strive. Give me six months of peace, and I am saved. You’re right about that, Shirley. But the quiet you’re counting on? The permanent good your fund will do? I doubt it. Charity never soothed working men’s resentment. It never makes them grateful — it makes them feel humiliated, and who can blame them? They don’t thank us mill owners for it, either. They hate us worse than ever. The disaffected here are in constant correspondence with groups in Nottingham, Manchester, Birmingham. They take orders, they wait, they plan, and no strike is struck without weeks of deliberation. You’ve seen the sultry summer days where thunder threatens for a week straight, and each night the clouds clear and the sun sets soft. But the storm is never gone. It’s just delayed. And it will break.”

“Mr. Moore,” she would say, every one of those conversations ending the same way, “take care of yourself. If you ever owe me any kindness, pay me back by promising to watch over your own safety.”

“I promise. I will guard myself close. I want to live, not die. The future unfurls ahead of me like Eden — and when I look deep into the green shade of that paradise, I see a vision more dear to me than any angel, gliding far off among the trees.”

“Do you? What vision, then?”

“I see —”

The parlour maid bustled in then, arms heaped with tea things, and the soft, charged moment broke.

Early May had been fine, the middle of the month damp with constant rain, but the last week, as the moon turned, the skies cleared all at once. A sharp, sweet wind swept the heavy silver rain clouds east, piling them along the horizon until they vanished behind its rim, leaving nothing but pure, unbroken blue for the summer sun to rule over. That sun rose broad and golden on Whitsuntide morning, just in time for the grand annual gathering of the three parishes’ Sunday schools.

Whit-Tuesday was the big day. The two large Briarfield schoolrooms, built almost entirely at the rector’s own expense, had been scrubbed, whitewashed, repainted, strung with flowers and evergreens — armfuls from the rectory garden, two full cartloads from Fieldhead, even a grudging wheelbarrow’s worth from Mr. Wynne’s stingy De Walden estate. Twenty tables, each set to seat twenty guests, were draped in crisp white linen, and twenty canary cages hung from the rafters, their bright little occupants trilling loud over the hum of voices, a fancy of Mr. Helstone’s clerk who swore the birds sang loudest in the middle of a crowd. These tables were not for the twelve hundred children coming to the feast — the children’s meal would be spread out on the grass under the sun. The seats indoors were reserved for patrons and teachers. By one o’clock the children would march in, by two they would be formed into order, they would parade the parish roads until four, then feast, then gather in the church for speeches and music.

Briarfield had been chosen for the fête for no grander reason than that Mr. Helstone willed it so, and no one dared argue with him. Whinbury was far more populous, Nunnely far older, but neither of the other two rectors had half his stubborn, imperious will. They let him lead.

For years, this holiday had been a torture for Caroline Helstone. Shy, nervous of crowds, she was forced to march at the head of the Briarfield school contingent as the rector’s niece, run the tea service for all the neighbourhood’s most wealthy and respected families, all without a mother, aunt, or chaperone by her side to steady her. The very thought of Whitsuntide used to make her tremble.

But this year, Shirley was to be at her side, and that changed everything. The trial turned into a treat. Shirley Keeldar, bright, self-possessed, never awkward, never presumptuous, was worth a dozen ordinary friends. The only worry Caroline had was that the heiress would be late — Shirley had a habit of losing track of time when a book caught her eye, and Caroline’s uncle would not wait a single second for anyone. The second the church clock struck two, the procession would begin.

She rose almost with the sun that Tuesday. She, Fanny, and Eliza bustled all morning, arranging the rectory parlours to perfection, laying out a spread of cold cakes, ripe fruit, and spiced wine on the dining room sideboard. Then she dressed in her freshest white muslin, tied the new blue sash Margaret Hall had given her for her birthday — the sash she was quietly sure Mr. Cyril Hall had bought himself, the one she had repaid with a fine set of cambric bands in a handsome leather case. Her simple bonnet was trimmed to match, her soft white crape scarf falling light over her shoulders. She looked not dazzling, but tender, sweet, with the quiet grace of a wild rose, all soft pink and white and gentle light, her brown eyes clear and her forehead smooth, modest, pensive, and perfectly at peace.

Off she went then, slipping through hidden green lanes and quiet hedgerow paths across to Fieldhead. The rain had left the ground soft and clean, no dust to stain the hem of her white gown, no mud to dampen her thin sandals. She walked fast across the daisy-sprinkled turf, reached the house, and went straight up to Shirley’s dressing room.

It was a good thing she came. Shirley was sprawled across a couch, completely absorbed in a book, paying no mind at all to Mrs. Pryor’s useless urging that she get dressed. Caroline wasted no words. She took the book right out of Shirley’s hands, and set to dressing her herself, lacing stays, fastening hooks, adjusting frills as fast as her fingers could move. Shirley, warm from the May heat, giddy and playful, kept trying to stop to laugh and chatter, but Caroline chided her gently for being so careless, and finished tying the last ribbon just in time.

Shirley made a striking contrast to Caroline. Every fold of her rich silk gown, every line of her straight, tall figure, had a style all its own. Her deep embroidered scarf hung negligently over one shoulder, the wreath of wild roses on her bonnet sitting perfectly atop her curls. She looked like a bright bird of paradise, next to Caroline’s soft white dove, and the two of them ran hand in hand down the Fieldhead drive, laughing as they hurried back towards Briarfield.

They arrived just in time. Over the treetops they heard the church bell tolling, calling everyone to gather, and the distant sound of hundreds of footsteps, and the low murmur of voices. From a rise in the road they saw the five hundred strong Whinbury school procession coming towards them, led by Dr. Boultby in his full canonicals, his shovel hat perched firmly on his head, his gold-headed cane swinging in his hand, and his curate Mr. Donne trailing after him, prim, self-satisfied, every bit the pedantic young clergyman from his turned-up nose down to his square-toed clerical shoes.

The two girls ran down the hill to join the crowd. The churchyard was packed with children all dressed in their very best, and even in the hard times, every little frock was neat, every bonnet tidy — the English love of decency, and Shirley’s quiet, timely bounty, had seen to that. She had given out new frocks and new boots to dozens of families in the weeks leading up to the holiday, and she glowed with quiet pleasure to see the crowd happy and well-dressed. Caroline was happy too: she had given away more than one of her own favourite ribbons and collars to the girls in her class, sewn late by candlelight to finish new frocks for the poorest children.

The rectory garden was full too, groups of patrons walking among the lilacs and laburnums that hung heavy with yellow blossom. Behind the house, the three parish bands were tuning their instruments, and the maids Fanny and Eliza were handing out quart tankards of the strong ale Mr. Helstone had brewed especially for the occasion — he would tolerate no shabby, half-hearted doings, and Shirley heartily approved of his generous, uncompromising taste.

Caroline, who usually hid in corners at public gatherings, moved easily through the parlours, talking, smiling, even speaking first to people before they spoke to her. Shirley’s easy, unafraid company had transformed her. Shirley herself was the centre of a laughing circle on the sofa, and when Mr. Donne came in, she bowed and smiled at him good-naturedly, ready to forget their old quarrel. But he slunk past her with a sullen, sheepish scowl, too cowardly to make peace and too petty to let the grudge go.

“He’s not even worth a proper quarrel,” Shirley whispered to Caroline. “Crushing him would be like squashing a gnat for stinging a rhinoceros. Malone is far better sport.”

As if on cue, Peter Augustus Malone sauntered over, gloved, scented, his hair oiled and brushed to a shine, holding out a huge bunch of full-blown cabbage roses to her. He presented them with a grand, dramatic flourish, then backed away, so proud of his own gallantry he was chuckling to himself — until he caught sight of the wide, laughing grins on both Shirley and Caroline’s faces. His face turned dark as a thundercloud, angry that his grand romantic gesture had turned into a joke.

“Peter is absolutely worth a quarrel,” Shirley murmured to her friend. “I’ll give him one someday, if he keeps glowering at me like that.”

Just then the three rectors appeared at the dining room door, their black clerical coats solemn against the bright flowers. The big morocco easy chair had been saved for Dr. Boultby, who sank into it happily, surrounded by his adoring congregation, his wife dabbing imaginary sweat from his forehead, Caroline handing him a plate of macaroons and a glass of spiced wine. Mr. Hall stood by the open window, breathing in the scent of lilacs, talking gently to Mary Ann Ainley, and Caroline and Shirley and Margaret Hall clustered around him, all four of them regarding him with the soft, reverent affection he so deserved. The third rector, old Helstone, had a whole crowd of admirers pressing around him, and the three curates huddled together in a little group of their own, watched from afar by a cluster of giggling young ladies.

Mr. Helstone pulled his watch out of his pocket, and held it high for everyone to see.

“Ten minutes to two,” he announced loud and clear. “Line up, everyone. Let’s go.”

He seized his shovel hat and marched out, and the whole crowd followed on his heels. The twelve hundred children were drawn up in three regiments of four hundred each, a band at the back of every group, teachers posted in pairs every twenty paces. Mr. Helstone called out the order for the vanguard:

“Grace Boultby and Mary Sykes lead Whinbury.
Margaret Hall and Mary Ann Ainley lead Nunnely.
Caroline Helstone and Shirley Keeldar head Briarfield.”
