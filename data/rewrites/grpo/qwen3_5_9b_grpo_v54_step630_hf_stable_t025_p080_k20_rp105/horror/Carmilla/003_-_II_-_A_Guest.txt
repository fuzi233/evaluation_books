II. A Guest

I am about to recount a tale so singular that it demands every ounce of your faith in my honesty to accept it as true. Yet, it is undeniably real—a truth witnessed by my own eyes.

It was a sweet summer evening, and my father invited me, as he often did, to accompany him on a stroll through the magnificent forest vista bordering our estate.

"General Spielsdorf cannot arrive sooner than I had hoped," my father remarked as we walked.

He was scheduled to visit for several weeks, with his arrival expected the following day. He was to bring with him a young lady, his niece and ward, Mademoiselle Rheinfeldt. I had never met her, though I had heard her described as a creature of immense charm, a prospect that had filled my days with dreams of delightful companionship. I was far more disappointed than a young woman accustomed to the bustle of a city could possibly imagine. This anticipated visit had been the centerpiece of my reveries for weeks.

"And when does he come?" I asked.

"Not until autumn," he replied. "Perhaps not for two months. And I am truly glad, my dear, that you never met Mademoiselle Rheinfeldt."

"And why?" I asked, feeling a sharp sting of mortification mixed with curiosity.

"Because the poor young lady is dead," he answered simply. "I merely forgot to mention it, but you were not in the room when I received the General's letter this evening."

Shock washed over me. In his first correspondence, written six or seven weeks prior, the General had noted that Bertha was unwell, yet there had been no hint of peril.

"Here is the letter," he said, handing it to me. "He is in profound affliction; the writing seems dictated by sheer distraction."

We sat upon a rough bench beneath a cluster of majestic lime trees. The sun was setting with a melancholy brilliance behind the sylvan horizon, casting long shadows as the stream beside our home wound through noble trees, reflecting the fading crimson of the sky. The General's letter was extraordinary, vehement, and riddled with contradictions. I read it twice, the second time aloud to my father, still unable to make sense of it except to assume that grief had shattered his composure.

*"I have lost my darling daughter, for as such I loved her,"* it began. *"During the final days of dear Bertha's illness, I was unable to write. Before then, I knew nothing of her danger. I have lost her, and now learn _all_ too late. She died in the peace of innocence, and in the glorious hope of a blessed futurity. The fiend who betrayed our infatuated hospitality has done it all. I thought I was welcoming innocence, gaiety, a charming companion for my lost Bertha. Heavens! What a fool have I been! I thank God my child died without suspicion of the cause of her sufferings. She is gone without even conjecturing the nature of her illness or the accursed passion of the agent of this misery. I devote my remaining days to tracking and extinguishing a monster. I am told I may hope to accomplish this righteous purpose. At present, there is scarcely a gleam of light to guide me. I curse my conceited incredulity, my despicable affectation of superiority, my blindness, my obstinacy—all—too late. I cannot write or talk collectedly now. I am distracted. As soon as I recover somewhat, I intend to devote myself to inquiry, which may lead me as far as Vienna. Some time in the autumn, two months hence, or earlier if I live, I will see you—that is, if you permit me; I will then tell you all that I scarce dare put upon paper now. Farewell. Pray for me, dear friend."*

Though I had never seen Bertha Rheinfeldt, tears filled my eyes at the sudden news. I was startled, profoundly disappointed.

The sun had set, and twilight deepened as I returned the letter to my father. It was a soft, clear evening. We loitered, speculating on the violent, incoherent sentences we had just read. We had nearly a mile to walk before reaching the road passing the front of the schloss, and by then the moon was shining brilliantly. At the drawbridge, we encountered Madame Perrodon and Mademoiselle De Lafontaine, who had emerged without their bonnets to enjoy the exquisite moonlight.

We heard their voices gabbling in animated dialogue as we approached. Joining them at the drawbridge, we turned to admire the scene together.

The glade through which we had walked lay before us. To our left, the narrow road wound away under clumps of lordly trees, vanishing into the thickening forest. To the right, the road crossed the steep, picturesque bridge near a ruined tower that once guarded the pass; beyond the bridge, an abrupt eminence rose, covered in trees and showing grey, ivy-clustered rocks in the shadows. Over the sward and low grounds, a thin film of mist stole like smoke, marking distances with a transparent veil, while here and there the river flashed faintly in the moonlight.

No softer, sweeter scene could be imagined. The news I had heard made it melancholy, yet nothing could disturb its profound serenity or the enchanted glory of the prospect. My father, who loved the picturesque, and I, stood in silence gazing over the expanse below. The two governesses stood a little behind us, discoursing eloquently on the moon.

Madame Perrodon was fat, middle-aged, and romantic, talking and sighing poetically. Mademoiselle De Lafontaine, claiming descent from a German father, assumed a psychological and metaphysical tone, declaring that when the moon shone with such intensity, it indicated special spiritual activity. She spoke of the full moon's manifold effects: acting on dreams, on lunacy, on nervous people, exerting marvelous physical influences connected to life. She recounted how her cousin, a merchant's mate, had woken from a nap on deck during such a night to find an old woman clawing at his cheek in his dream; his features had been horribly drawn to one side, and his countenance had never fully recovered its equilibrium.

"The moon, this night," she said, "is full of idyllic and magnetic influence—and see, when you look behind you at the front of the schloss, how all its windows flash and twinkle with that silvery splendor, as if unseen hands had lit the rooms to receive fairy guests."

There are indolent styles of spirits in which, indisposed to speak ourselves, the talk of others is pleasant to our listless ears. I gazed on, pleased by the tinkling of the ladies' conversation.

"I have fallen into one of my moping moods tonight," my father said after a silence, quoting Shakespeare to maintain our English:

*"In truth I know not why I am so sad.
It wearies me: you say it wearies you;
But how I got it—came by it."*

He forgot the rest. "But I feel as if some great misfortune were hanging over us. I suppose the poor General's afflicted letter has something to do with it."

At that moment, the unwonted sound of carriage wheels and many hoofs on the road arrested our attention. They seemed to approach from the high ground overlooking the bridge. Soon, the equipage emerged. Two horsemen crossed the bridge first, followed by a carriage drawn by four horses, with two men riding behind.

It appeared to be the traveling carriage of a person of rank, and we were immediately absorbed in watching this unusual spectacle. Moments later, it became far more interesting. Just as the carriage passed the summit of the steep bridge, one of the leading horses took fright, communicating its panic to the rest. After a few plunges, the whole team broke into a wild gallop, dashing between the horsemen ahead and thundering along the road toward us with hurricane speed.

The excitement was made more painful by the clear, long-drawn screams of a female voice from the carriage window. We all advanced in curiosity and horror; I moved in silence, the others with various ejaculations of terror.

Our suspense did not last long. Just before the castle drawbridge, on the route they were taking, stood a magnificent lime tree on one side and an ancient stone cross on the other. At the sight of the cross, the horses, moving at a pace that was perfectly frightful, swerved violently, causing the carriage wheel to strike the projecting roots of the tree.

I knew what was coming. I covered my eyes, unable to bear the sight, and turned my head away; at the same moment, I heard a cry from my lady friends, who had stepped forward.

Curiosity forced my eyes open, revealing a scene of utter confusion. Two horses lay on the ground. The carriage rested on its side, two wheels in the air. The men were busy removing the traces, while a lady with a commanding air and figure had dismounted, standing with clasped hands, raising the handkerchief within them to her eyes periodically.

Through the open carriage door, a young lady was lifted out, appearing lifeless. My dear old father was already beside the elder lady, hat in hand, evidently offering aid and the resources of our schloss. The older lady did not appear to hear him, nor to have eyes for anything but the slender girl being placed against the slope of the bank.

I approached. The young lady was apparently stunned, but certainly not dead. My father, who prided himself on being something of a physician, had just checked her wrist and assured the lady—who declared herself her mother—that her pulse, though faint and irregular, was undoubtedly still distinguishable. The lady clasped her hands and looked upward, as if in a momentary transport of gratitude; but immediately, she broke out again in that theatrical manner I believe is natural to some people.

She was a fine-looking woman for her time, must have been handsome; tall, but not thin, dressed in black velvet, and possessing a proud, commanding countenance, though now strangely agitated.

"Who was ever born to such calamity?" I heard her say, with clasped hands, as I came up. "Here am I, on a journey of life and death, in prosecuting which to lose an hour is possibly to lose all. My child will not have recovered sufficiently to resume her route for who can say how long. I must leave her: I cannot, dare not, delay. How far on, sir, is the nearest village? I must leave her there; and shall not see my darling, or even hear of her, till my return three months hence."

I plucked my father's coat and whispered earnestly in his ear: "Oh! papa, pray ask her to let her stay with us—it would be so delightful. Do, pray."

"If Madame will entrust her child to the care of my daughter and her good governess, Madame Perrodon, and permit her to remain as our guest, under my charge, until her return, it will confer a distinction and an obligation upon us, and we shall treat her with all the care and devotion which so sacred a trust deserves."

"I cannot do that, sir; it would be to task your kindness and chivalry too cruelly," the lady said, distractedly.

"It would, on the contrary, be to confer on us a very great kindness at the moment when we most need it. My daughter has just been disappointed by a cruel misfortune in a visit from which she had long anticipated great happiness. If you confide this young lady to our care, it will be her best consolation. The nearest village on your route is distant and affords no inn suitable for placing your daughter; you cannot allow her to continue her journey for any considerable distance without danger. If, as you say, you cannot suspend your journey, you must part with her tonight, and nowhere could you do so with more honest assurances of care and tenderness than here."

There was something in this lady's air and appearance—so distinguished and imposing, and in her manner so engaging—as to impress one, quite apart from the dignity of her equipage, with a conviction that she was a person of consequence.

By this time, the carriage was replaced in its upright position, and the horses, now tractable, were back in the traces.

The lady threw a glance at her daughter which I fancied was not quite so affectionate as one might have anticipated from the beginning of the scene. Then she beckoned slightly to my father and withdrew two or three steps with him out of hearing. She talked to him with a fixed, stern countenance, utterly unlike the manner in which she had hitherto spoken.

I was filled with wonder that my father did not seem to perceive the change, and also unspeakably curious to learn what she was saying, almost in his ear, with such earnestness and rapidity.

Two or three minutes at most she remained thus employed, then she turned. A few steps brought her to where her daughter lay, supported by Madame Perrodon. She knelt beside her for a moment and whispered, as Madame supposed, a little benediction in her ear; then, hastily kissing her, she stepped into her carriage. The door was closed, the footmen in stately liveries jumped up behind, the outriders spurred on, the postilions cracked their whips, the horses plunged and suddenly broke into a furious canter that threatened soon again to become a gallop, and the carriage whirled away, followed at the same rapid pace by the two horsemen in the rear.
