"Enter!" Trent called out.

Mr. Cupples stepped into the hotel's sitting-room, where the air hummed with the quiet energy of late evening. Outside, the coroner's jury had already delivered their verdict—a condemnation of unknown parties—but inside, Trent was engaged in a far more intimate battle. With a quick, sharp glance upward, he adjusted his focus on a photographic dish of enamelled metal, rotating it slowly under the window's glow. His complexion was ghastly pale, and his movements betrayed a jittery restlessness.

"Take a seat on the sofa," Trent instructed, gesturing vaguely. "Those armchairs are a bargain bin find from the aftermath of the Spanish Inquisition's suppression. A pretty fine negative, don't you think?" He held the glass up to the light, angling his head with the precision of a hawk. "It appears sufficiently washed now. We'll let it air-dry and clear away this clutter."

As Trent busied himself clearing the table of basins, racks, and chemical bottles, Mr. Cupples examined the debris with innocent fascination.

"That vessel contains hypo-eliminator," Trent explained, watching as Cupples uncorked a bottle and sniffed cautiously. "Extremely handy if you're racing against time with a negative. I wouldn't suggest drinking it, however, despite what it claims to eliminate. Sodium hypophosphite, yes—but who knows if it might also dissolve human beings?"

Once the last item was stowed on the crowded mantelpiece, Trent collapsed onto the table itself, facing his companion. "The true virtue of a hotel sitting-room is that its lack of beauty keeps the mind focused on labor. It offers no sanctuary for the fleeting pleasures of a relaxed intellect. Have you ever occupied this space before, Cupples? I have, countless times. It has haunted me across England for years. I would feel utterly adrift if some distant hotel offered me a different chair. Notice this tablecloth—the ink stain is from Halifax, where I first claimed this suite. The burn in the carpet below belongs to Ipswich. And look here: they've finally repaired the glass above 'Silent Sympathy,' the portrait I kicked with a boot in Banbury. This is where my finest work occurs. Since the inquest this afternoon, I've produced several exceptional negatives. There is a perfectly adequate darkroom downstairs."

"The inquest," Cupples interjected, sensing the electric charge beneath Trent's casual words. "That prompts me to thank you, old friend, for looking after Mabel this morning. I had no notion she'd fall ill after stepping out of the box; she seemed composed, almost unnervingly so. Her self-command is remarkable, leading me to believe I could leave her alone to hear the evidence, which I deemed vital. Fortunately, she found a friend to assist, and she is deeply grateful. She feels entirely restored now."

Trent stood silent, his hands buried in his pockets, a furrow etched between his brows. "Let me tell you something," he said after a heavy pause. "I was just reaching the heart of this matter when you arrived. Would you care to witness a glimpse of high-caliber police work? It is precisely the caliber of duty Old Murch ought to be performing right now. Perhaps he is; but I pray, with every fiber of my being, that he is not."

Springing from the table, Trent vanished into his bedroom. Moments later, he emerged carrying a large drawing board laden with a chaotic array of objects.

"First, acquaint yourself with these curiosities," Trent announced, arranging them on the table. "Behold a substantial ivory paper-knife, two leaves torn from my own diary, a bottle of toothpaste, and a small case of polished walnut. Several of these items require immediate return to their rightful owners' bedrooms at White Gables before sundown. That is the nature of my profession—nothing halts me. I secured these this morning while everyone else was attending the inquest, and I daresay some might view this as a peculiar proceeding if they knew the truth."

His gaze settled on the final object remaining on the board. "Can you identify this, Cupples, without so much as touching it?"

"Certainly," Cupples replied, leaning in with intense curiosity. "It is a standard glass bowl, likely a finger-bowl. I detect nothing unusual about it." After a prolonged scrutiny, he added, "Nothing seems amiss."

"I see little myself," Trent countered, "and therein lies the thrill. Take this stout bottle, Cupples, and extract the cork. Do you recognize the contents? You've likely swallowed pounds of it in your lifetime. They administer it to infants. It is known as 'Grey Powder'—a mixture of mercury and chalk. A formidable substance."

Holding the basin sideways over a sheet of paper, Trent directed Cupples. "While I support the rim, I need you to dust a small quantity of this powder directly onto this section of the bowl—precisely here..."

Cupples executed the motion with a steady hand. "Perfect! Even Sir Edward Henry himself could not have manipulated the powder with greater finesse. I suspect you have performed this trick before, Cupples. You appear to be a veteran."

"Not at all," Cupples declared gravely. "I assure you, it remains a total enigma to me. What was required of me?"

"Treat the powdered area of the bowl gently with this camel-hair brush," Trent directed. "Examine it once more. Did you perceive anything odd prior to this?"

Cupples peered closely. "Remarkable!" he exclaimed. "Yes, there are two distinct grey finger-marks on the bowl. They were absent a moment ago."

"I am Hawkshaw, the detective," Trent observed with a grin. "Shall I deliver a brief lecture on the mysteries of glass finger-bowls? When one lifts such a vessel, invisible traces remain, potentially persisting for days or months. These are the imprints of your fingers. The human hand, even when seemingly clean, retains moisture, especially during moments of profound anxiety—as you might have experienced today, Cupples. Such dampness leaves an indelible mark on any cold, smooth surface touched."

He sprinkled the powder again, revealing further details. "Observe the other side. Here is the thumb-print—excellent impressions throughout." He lowered his voice, though Cupples sensed the feverish intensity radiating from Trent's eyes. "This one corresponds to the index finger. Need I remind a man of your worldly wisdom that the pattern is a single-spiral whorl, flanked by symmetrical deltas? This print from the second finger is a simple loop, featuring a staple core and fifteen counts. I am certain of the count because I possess identical prints on this negative, which I have scrutinized in minute detail."

Trent held up one of the negatives against the waning sunlight, tracing the lines with a pencil tip. "Note the bifurcation of that ridge. It exists here, and it exists there. Observe the tiny scar near the center. It matches perfectly. There are dozens of ridge characteristics sufficient for an expert to testify in court that the marks on this bowl and those photographed on this plate were forged by the same hand."

"And where were these captured?" Cupples asked, his eyes wide. "What significance does this hold?"

"I discovered them on the interior of the left-hand pane of the front window in Mrs. Manderson's bedroom. Unable to transport the window itself, I photographed them, affixing a piece of black paper to the exterior of the glass for contrast. The bowl originates from Manderson's chamber; it is the receptacle where he stored his false dentures nightly. I retrieved it, naturally."

"But those cannot be Mabel's finger-marks," Cupples argued.

"I firmly disagree!" Trent retorted decisively. "They are nearly double the size of any impression Mrs. Manderson could possibly leave."

"Then they must belong to her husband."

"Possibly. Shall we verify our findings once more? I am convinced we can."

Whistling softly, Trent's face a mask of pallor, he opened a small, squat bottle filled with dense black powder. "Lamp-black," he elucidated. "Sustain a piece of paper in your palm for a fleeting second, and this gentleman will reveal the pattern of your digits."

With tweezers, Trent carefully lifted one of the leaves torn from his diary and presented it for examination. Initially, no marks appeared. He then tilted the bottle, dusting one surface of the paper, followed by the reverse, before shaking off the excess. He extended the leaf silently.

On one side, clearly imprinted in stark black, lay the same two finger-prints previously observed on the bowl and the photographic plate. Trent took up the bowl, comparing them side by side. He flipped the paper, revealing a bold, charcoal replica of the thumb-mark, identical to the grey imprint on the glass.

"The same individual," Trent concluded with a short, sharp laugh. "I suspected it, and now I know."

He walked to the window, gazing outward into the twilight. "Now I know," he murmured, his tone laced with bitterness, as if speaking to the shadows behind him. Mr. Cupples, bewildered, watched Trent's motionless back for a long moment.

"I remain entirely in the dark," Cupples ventured eventually. "I have often heard of this fingerprint science and marveled at the police methods, but truly, I cannot comprehend how Manderson's prints factor into—"
