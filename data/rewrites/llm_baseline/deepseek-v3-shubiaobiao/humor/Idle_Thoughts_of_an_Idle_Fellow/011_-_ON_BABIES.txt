Ah, babies! Those enigmatic little creatures who rule households with their tiny, iron fists. I, too, was once a baby—though my tenure in that role was brief, far briefer than my absurdly elongated infant garments. Why must babies be swaddled in enough fabric to outfit a small circus tent? Is it to disguise their true dimensions, as if parents fear the world will judge them for producing something so compact? I once posed this question to a nurse, who replied with the kind of wisdom only the profoundly unbothered possess: *"Lor', sir, they always have long clothes, bless their little hearts."* When I pressed further, she recoiled as if I’d suggested swaddling the child in newspaper. So, the mystery remains.

Frankly, the entire concept of dressing babies strikes me as excessive. Life is already a relentless cycle of buttons and zippers; must we subject these bed-bound beings to the same torment? Why rouse them at dawn merely to swap one meaningless smock for another, only to tuck them back into oblivion? And let’s not pretend there’s any discernible difference between a baby’s "day shirt" and its "nightshirt." It’s all the same shapeless sack, as if fashion declared infancy a no-aesthetic zone.

But clothes are merely the first of many bafflements. Take, for instance, the impossible task of distinguishing a baby boy from a baby girl. Hair? Identical. Wardrobe? Unisex chaos. Conversation? Limited to gurgles, which, while charming, offer no taxonomic clarity. Misgender an infant just once, and you’ll witness a family’s collective gasp of horror, as though you’ve insulted an ancient bloodline. Refer to the child as "it," and you may as well have kicked a puppy in front of a vicar. The only safe recourse is to coo, *"What a little angel!"*—a term so conveniently gender-neutral it should earn me a diplomatic medal.

Ah, but the true trial comes when you are summoned to *"see the baby."* Few ordeals are more harrowing for the uninitiated bachelor. The moment the bell rings and nurse is summoned, a dread settles in. The room erupts in a chorus of *"Isn’t he precious?"* and *"Oh, just look at her toes!"* while you stand there, sweating, wondering if feigning sudden blindness would be plausible. Then, the nurse enters—a stern, towering figure bearing what appears to be a loaf of bread wrapped in a shawl. This, you’re told, is the baby.

Silence falls. All eyes turn to you. Your mind empties. Desperate, you blurt something like, *"Goodness, it’s... very small, isn’t it?"* The nurse fixes you with a glare that suggests you’ve just insulted the royal lineage. *"They generally are at this stage,"* she replies, as if you’ve asked why the sky is blue. Then comes the inevitable command: *"Take her in your arms, sir."* You comply, stiff as a mannequin, while the child eyes you with the suspicion of a cat near a bathtub. The moment you attempt a tentative bounce, the nurse snaps, *"I wouldn’t jig her, sir,"* as if you’ve been caught attempting amateur acrobatics. Then, of course, the baby wails—a sound so piercing you’re instantly branded a villain. *"What did you *do* to her?"* the mother demands, as though you’ve been caught teaching the infant poker.

Yet for all their chaos, babies are life’s tiny comedians. They toddle with the gravity of statesmen, puddle-jump in Sunday best, and hold profound debates with spoons. They adore the most inedible items (candle wax! shoelaces!) and express affection by yanking the dog’s ears. They turn orderly homes into obstacle courses and wallets into relics of a bygone era. And yet—would we trade them? A house without their shrieks and sticky fingerprints would be a museum, not a home.

Still, a word to the wise new mother: Do not let the baby monopolize your heart like a greedy landlord. Your husband, that overgrown child, still needs a kind word, a shared laugh. The world will not end if you step away from the crib for an evening. The first baby consumes all; the fifth barely rates a glance. Balance, dear chaos-bringer, balance.

But enough scolding. Who could truly resent these small, solemn-faced explorers of staircases and dust bunnies? They gaze upon the world with the wonder of astronauts, every sidewalk crack a canyon, every cat a mythical beast. And when they pause, staring down some dim street of the future, you wonder—what do they see? Hope? Fear? Or just the next shiny thing to grab?

Godspeed, little travelers. The road ahead is long, and we, farther along, can only wave and smile. You’ll figure it out. Probably.
