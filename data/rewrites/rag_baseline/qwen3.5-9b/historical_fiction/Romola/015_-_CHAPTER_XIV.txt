The cacophony of the Piazza exploded against Tito's ears—a chaotic symphony of shouting, hooting, and clashing instruments that instantly triggered the memory of Nello's warning regarding the *Fierucoloni*. Instead of recoiling, Tito surged forward into the throng, deriving a grim satisfaction from the physical jostling. The collision of bodies served as a necessary distraction, numbing the paralyzing dread of solitude that threatened to consume him as he contemplated his future: a lonely wanderer chasing phantom fortunes, or perhaps, finally turning back to seek out Baldassarre.

From the opposing archways, fresh waves of humanity poured into the square, illuminated by crude paper lanterns bobbing precariously on wooden poles. A rhythmic, monotonous chant provided a structural backbone to the noise, overlaid by shrieks, whistles, mocking songs from boys' high voices, the thunder of drums, and the tinkling of bells. Periodically, a floating lantern would be shattered by a stray stone hurled in a fit of vandalism, eliciting a fresh wave of panic and renewed jeering. Yet, amidst this whirlwind of destruction, pockets of order remained. On the periphery, ragged men darted sharp, predatory glances around them while their tongues clicked with manic glee, offering games of chance to the countryside folk on terms of deceptive generosity. Two figures masked and elevated on stilts, having snatched lanterns from the crowd, swayed the lights in erratic, meteoric arcs as they patrolled the space. Nearby, a shrewd merchant conducted brisk trade at a covered stall, dispensing *berlingozzi*, a dense flour-based delicacy favored by the masses. Another vendor, perched atop a barrel with his back braced against a pillar of the Loggia before the Foundling Hospital (*Spedale degli Innocenti*), hawked miracle pills concocted by a physician from Salerno, guaranteed to cure toothaches and prevent drowning deaths. Further down, a juggler displayed his acrobatic feats on a makeshift platform, while a group of apprentices, bored by the guerrilla-style stone-throwing, engaged in a concentrated, private tournament of their favorite Florentine sport at the narrow entrance of Via de' Febbrai.

Driven by the current, Tito found himself momentarily hemmed in beside a procession of barefoot peasant women. Their feet, hardened by calluses, struck the pavement with a heavy rhythm. Their faces were sun-leathered and bronze-toned, framed by garments of faded, obsolete fabrics caked with generations of dust. To the city dwellers, they appeared as spectral ancestors returning from a pilgrimage undertaken a century prior. These were the hardy women from the mountain ranges of Pistoia, carrying the tangible fruits of a year's labor in modest bundles of yarn strapped to their backs. Behind their stoic expressions lay a fragile calculus of hope and terror, anxieties entrusted solely to the intercession of the Blessed Virgin. It was believed that on this Eve of her Nativity, the miraculous image, painted by angels, would have its veil lifted, allowing its divine potency to flow unimpeded.

Elsewhere, Tito was shoved toward the quieter edges of the square, where opportunists had strategically positioned themselves to maximize profit while minimizing risk. Among these was Bratti, a known associate, leaning casually against a pillar. His lips were pursed in a mask of disdainful silence, scanning approaching customers with a cold, superior gaze, his hand gripping the edge of a serge cloth that concealed the contents of his basket. Surprised by such unusual composure in a desperate trader, Tito stepped closer. Two women approached the basket with tentative curiosity, prompting Bratti to pull the cloth tighter and avert his eyes. Provoked, one woman demanded to know the nature of his wares.

"Before I answer, Monna," Bratti retorted, his voice dripping with feigned pity, "I must ascertain if you intend to purchase. I cannot expose such select merchandise to every idle fly hoping for free inspection. My goods are too refined for that. Furthermore, I possess only two items remaining, and I have no desire to soil them. Given the rumors of pestilence circulating among the wise, these pieces could prove worth their weight in gold. So, *andate con Dio*."

The women exchanged uneasy glances. "And what is the price?" asked the second.

"Not within the reach of your purse, *buona donna*," Bratti replied, adopting a tone of compassionate arrogance. "I advise you to place your faith in Messer Domeneddio and the saints; the poor can do little for themselves."

"Not so poor!" the second woman snapped indignantly, producing her money-bag. "Come, then! What do you say to a *grosso*?"

"I state you may acquire it for twenty-one *quattrini*," Bratti countered coolly, "but not from me, for I lack such small change."

"Then two?" the woman insisted, her temper flaring as her companion watched with envy. "It will barely exceed two, I suspect."

Following a brief negotiation marked by mercantile coquetry, Bratti adopted an air of reluctant concession. "Since you are determined," he said, slowly lifting the cover, "I would be loath to cause you harm. Maestro Gabbadeo often remarked that when a woman desires something and fails to obtain it, she falls into greater peril of the plague than before. *Ecco!* I have but two left. Let me assure you, the charm resides on the finger of Maestro Gabbadeo, who has departed for Bologna—a physician as sagacious as any in the land."

The artifacts revealed were two clumsy iron rings, hammered into the style of ancient Roman jewelry occasionally unearthed. The thick rust coating them and the completely obscured mechanism of their supposed power rendered them perfectly satisfactory. The *grossi* changed hands without complaint. The first woman, lacking such currency, managed to drive a bargain using some of her yarn after displaying considerable reluctance, eventually departing with the remaining ring in triumph. Bratti quickly resealed his basket, now filled with miscellaneous odds and ends likely acquired under similar duress. As he moved away from the pillar, he collided unexpectedly with Tito, who, given the choice, would have preferred anonymity.

"By the head of San Giovanni," Bratti exclaimed, pulling Tito back against the stone column, "what a stroke of luck! I was discussing you this morning, Messer Greco. I noted he has ascended among the *signori*—a development I welcome, for I was at the bottom of his fortune. However, securing an audience with him is nearly impossible now; he is no longer to be found lounging on the stones. But this is your luck, Messer Greco, minus a trifling fee to compensate me for my efforts in this transaction."

"You speak in riddles, Bratti," Tito replied sharply. "Recall that I do not hone my intellect by haggling over iron rings; you must be direct."

"By the Holy Gospel! It was a generous deal I extended. If a Hebrew accepts thirty-two percent interest, surely a Christian deserves a marginally higher return. Had I possessed a clearer conscience, I would have doubled both the coin and the yarn. But speaking of rings... it is your ring—the very one on your finger—for which I can find a buyer. Yes, a buyer with a substantial purse."

"Really?" Tito asked, examining his own ring and listening intently.

"A Genoese traveler heading directly to Hungary, as I understand. He visited my shop recently, scrutinizing every item to determine its value; I wager he thought I carried a pumpkin on my shoulders. He had been scouring the shops of Florence. He wore a ring—not identical to yours, yet of similar design. Discussing rings, I mentioned knowing a promising young man, a close friend of mine, who owned such a piece. He responded, 'Who is he? Tell him I will pay top dollar.' I considered pursuing you to Nello's tomorrow. In my estimation, Messer Greco, you are not the type to watch the Arno turn to broth and remain passive without dipping a finger in."

Tito absorbed every word, though his mind raced elsewhere. Why retain the ring? It had been a sentimental impulse preventing its sale alongside his other gems. Had he acted with prudence earlier, selling it, he might have avoided the identification by Fra Luca. True, the ring had originally belonged to Baldassarre and transferred to Tito as his hand grew, yet superstition regarding inanimate objects held no logical validity. The ring had facilitated his exposure; Tito had developed a distaste for recognition, a demand imposed by the past. This foreigner's offer, assuming a genuine valuation, represented a viable exit strategy, eliminating the burden of sourcing a purchaser.

"You speak with your customary wisdom, Bratti," Tito conceded. "I have no objection to hearing your Genoese's bid. When and where shall we meet?"

"Tomorrow, three hours after sunrise. He will await you at my shop. If your faculties possess the acuity I have always attributed to you, Messer Greco, you will demand a steep price; he cares little for coin. My suspicion is he purchases on behalf of someone else, perhaps a high-ranking noble."

"It is settled," Tito agreed. "I shall attend your shop, barring unforeseen hindrances."

"And you will undoubtedly treat me honorably, considering our long-standing acquaintance, Messer Greco. Thus, I need not linger to negotiate the token sum you will provide for my services. It feels as though a millennium has passed since I left this square; a fair becomes tedious, bordering on malevolent, once one has exhausted one's inventory."
