Three nights after, when the moon should have been climbing above the roofs that hemmed in the great square before the Old Palace, it found no pavement to silver and no orderly alternation of light and shadow to lay down like a patterned cloth. The stones were hidden as if the city had poured its own living substance over them: only heads, shoulders, hands lifted and clenched, the glimmer of eyes and steel, the heaving of breath in a mass that struggled and swayed, filled the piazza to its edges. Florence, that could on other nights be heard in separate threads—the quick patter of feet, a lute’s thin trembling, the soft scamper of late lovers along a wall—was now one single harsh instrument. A roar made of shouts and curses, of trampling soles and bodies thrust aside, of the accidental clank of arms, rose and fell; and across that tumult, nothing came clean except, now and then, a sudden shriek like a bird pierced, or the heavy drop of a bell’s toll that seemed to strike the air itself into dull pieces.

Almost all who could call themselves the public were awake. Those not already wedged within the piazza were pushing to enter it, finding the very act of standing still impossible, because the crowd had become a kind of tide. Within the palace, in the council-chamber, all the chief magistracies were still gathered: the Eighty, and the selected citizens who had argued through the long daylight and the torchlit hours whether the prisoners should have the Appeal, or whether the sentence must be carried out at once to forestall the perilous chances of waiting. Their debate had not been mere debate; it had been quarrel and menace, loud enough that the harshness of it leaked through walls and corridors to the multitude outside, so that the square’s roar was answered by another roar from within, like two seas meeting.

Only in the last hour had the question been decided. Four of the Signoria had stood obstinately for the Appeal even when the argument ceased to be law and became threat—houses to be sacked, families to be turned into targets for the crowd’s rage. And then Francesco Valori—brief, furious, as men are when their anger is a weapon they mean to use at once—had made his party’s meaning plain by declaring that if the Signoria would not defend the liberties of Florence by executing those five perfidious citizens, there would be others ready to take the cause in hand, to the peril of all who opposed it. The Florentine Cato triumphed. When the beans were counted again, the four white that had stood for mercy did not appear; all nine were black—fatal, unanimous. They decided the death of the five without delay, and they decided also, though no one spoke it aloud and it would ripen more slowly, the death of Francesco Valori.

Now the judicial Eight had gone to the Bargello to prepare the execution; and the five condemned men were being led barefoot and in irons through the midst of the council. Their friends had contrived it, with that desperate hope which clings to the visible. Would not Florentines be moved by seeing such ignominy hung on two venerable men like Bernardo del Nero and Niccolò Ridolfi—men whose bias had been taken long before the new order made Mediceanism retrograde? Would not hearts soften at the sight of two brilliant, popular young men like Tornabuoni and Pucci, whose places would be left like empty chairs at every gathering of chief citizens? It was useless. The pity that stirred now was the kind that does not rescue: it only ripens late into revenge.

While that bitter procession passed upstairs, Romola stood below in the palace court, her back against one of the massive pillars, as if she needed the old stone’s steadiness. She was waiting for the moment when her godfather would appear on his way to death. By the use of strong interest she had won permission to visit him in the evening and remain with him until the council’s result should be determined. And now she waited with his confessor, meaning to follow the guard that would lead him to the Bargello. Her heart was bent on clinging to the childless old man to the last instant, as her father would have clung; and she had overborne every remonstrance.

Giovan Battista Ridolfi, a disciple of Savonarola, was beside her. He was going in bitterness to see the death of his elder brother Niccolò, yet he had promised Romola should be guarded, as if in that service he could keep hold of some shred of decency amid the city’s frenzy. Tito, too, was in the palace, but Romola had not seen him. Since the evening of the seventeenth they had avoided each other. Tito knew only by inference, from the report of the Frate’s neutrality, that her pleading had failed.

He was now among official personages—Florentine and foreign—who had waited out the long council. He wore, except when addressed, the subdued gravity of a man in painful conflict between public duty and private feeling. And when someone alluded to his wife, in connection with the condemned, he implied that her mind was violently excited; that the mere fact of his continuing to hold office under a government concerned in her godfather’s condemnation roused in her a diseased hostility; and that for her sake it was best he should not approach her.

“Ah, the old Bardi blood!” said Cennini, shrugging. “I shall not be surprised if this business shakes her loose from the Frate, as well as some others I could name.”

“It is excusable in a woman,” said a young French envoy—smiling, and bowing to Tito—“to think her affections must overrule the good of the State, and that nobody is to be beheaded who is anybody’s cousin; but such a view is not to be encouraged in the male population. It seems to me your Florentine polity is much weakened by it.”

“That is true,” said Niccolò Macchiavelli. “But where personal ties are strong, the hostilities they raise must be taken due account of. Many of these half-way severities are mere hot-headed blundering. The only safe blows to be inflicted on men and parties are the blows that are too heavy to be avenged.”

“Niccolò,” said Cennini, “there is a clever wickedness in thy talk sometimes that makes me mistrust thy pleasant young face as if it were a mask of Satan.”

“Not at all, my good Domenico,” Macchiavelli answered, smiling, laying his hand on the elder’s shoulder. “Satan was a blunderer, an introducer of novità, who made a stupendous failure. If he had succeeded, we should all have been worshipping him, and his portrait would have been more flattered.”

“Well, well,” said Cennini, “I say not thy doctrine is not too clever for Satan: I only say it is wicked enough for him.”

“I tell you,” said Macchiavelli, “my doctrine is the doctrine of all men who seek an end a little farther off than their own noses. Ask our Frate, our prophet, how his universal renovation is to be brought about: he will tell you, first, by getting a free and pure government; and since it appears that this cannot be done by making all Florentines love each other, it must be done by cutting off every head that happens to be obstinately in the way. Only if a man incurs odium by sanctioning a severity that is not thorough enough to be final, he commits a blunder. And something like that blunder, I suspect, the Frate has committed. It was an occasion on which he might have won some lustre by exerting himself to maintain the Appeal; instead of that, he has lost lustre, and has gained no strength.”

Before any reply could rise, the expected announcement came: the prisoners were about to leave the council-chamber. The majority hurried toward the door, eager to secure place behind the guard—intent, with passions hot or curiosity cold, on seeing the execution at the Bargello. Tito was among those who remained behind. He had a native repugnance to sights of death and pain, and five days earlier, when execution had seemed possible, he had hoped for exile as the utmost. His safety demanded no more.

But now he felt a grim ease in the thought that Bernardo del Nero’s head off his shoulders would be a guarantee. Romola’s new knowledge, her new attitude since his return, had planted in him a dread of what she could do—how a single act of hers might make him an object of suspicion and odium, bringing frustration under unpleasant circumstances. Her belief in Baldassarre had plainly fixed her wavering feelings against further submission; and if her godfather lived, she would win him to share her belief without much trouble. Romola was more than ever an unmanageable fact in his destiny. But with Bernardo dead, the difficulties she would face in setting herself against her husband would likely be too great for her shrinking pride. So he had breathed easier when he learned the Eight had gone to order the instant erection of the scaffold.

Four other men—his intimates and confederates—were to die beside Bernardo. But a man’s safety is a god that makes grim demands. Tito felt them grim; and yet, even pursuing what he found agreeable, life forced him into desiring what was disagreeable. He had known that paradox before. Now, as through the open doorway came the shuffle of many feet and the clank of metal on the stairs, he could still answer the French envoy’s questions without showing more than sad resignation to State necessity.

Those sounds fell on Romola as if her hearing had been sharpened until each noise had a blade. In that courtyard the echoes did not blur; they came back hard from stone—the ring of iron, the scrape of a sandal sole, the low murmur that swelled and broke like water thrown against a quay. She needed no arm. She shed no tears. It was as if her whole being were strung past the point where grief and joy are separate things: intensity without softness, like a flame that has burned away smoke.

Since her godfather’s fate had been decided, the struggle within her had given way to an inward union with him. She was asserting for him, as if the words were a shelter she could set up against the world, that though he suffered as a traitor he did not deserve the name. He was the victim of a collision between two fidelities. It was not given him to die for the noblest cause, and yet he died because of his nobleness: he might have been meaner and found it easier to avoid this guilt. In her, sympathy for the individual lot rose up against the formulas by which parties judged actions. She would tread the way with her second father to the scaffold and defy ignominy by knowing it was undeserved.

The way was fenced by three hundred armed men, placed there by Valori’s orders—another contradiction in the night’s contradictions: alarm proclaimed at once that the people would tear the conspirators limb from limb, and alarm also that some might try to rescue them in the midst of a hostile crowd. The press of bodies along the passage breathed heat and stale wine and the sharpness of torches. And always the sound—sound that was almost a physical weight—rolled over them, as if Florence’s own throat were hoarse with its need.

When they reached the court of the Bargello, Romola was allowed to approach Bernardo with his confessor for a moment of farewell. Even in that agitated throng, many eyes turned to them, as the aged man, forgetting his irons, lifted his bound hands toward the golden head bent down to him—then checked himself, as if the very gesture must be obedient now, and leaned to kiss her. She took the fettered hands when they dropped again and kissed them as if they were sacred things.

“My poor Romola,” Bernardo said low, “I have only to die, but thou hast to live—and I shall not be there to help thee.”

“Yes,” Romola answered, hurried, as if the words must be spoken before time could snatch them away. “You will help me—always—because I shall remember you.”

They took her away and led her up the steps to the loggia that ran round the grand old court. There she stood under a dark arch, choosing her place as one chooses a vow. She meant to look until the moment her godfather laid his head on the block. Bernardo was to suffer first. Battista Ridolfi, close beside her, had promised that once she had seen the last look of the man who, alone in all the world, had shared her pitying love for her father, he would lead her out through a door behind them.

And still, behind the iron of her resolve, a possibility worked like a seed that refuses to die: that rescue might yet come, some hand might thrust itself between justice and blood, something would keep that scaffold unstained. Time dragged and snapped. There was constant movement below—torches wavering in their rings, light jerking across carved stone faces that looked down with their old, indifferent mouths; then the same light striking a pale cheek tightened with rage, or a mouth bitten white with held-back sobs—near kin of the condemned who would soon receive the dead and carry them home. The crowd pressed denser about the black scaffold until the court seemed a trough filled to the brim with humanity.

Romola’s face, against the darkness of the arch, looked like marble—still, colorless, a thing carved to endure. The sound in the court was not one sound but many, knotted together: muttered prayers, a sharp laugh that came and was strangled, the thick breathing of men pressed chest to back, and the heavy waves of noise rushing in through the entrance from the piazza beyond. It was as if she stood in a storm-tossed sea and cared nothing for the storm, caring only to hold out a signal until the eyes that sought it could seek it no more.

Suddenly the tumult sank. The very tapers seemed to tremble themselves into quiet. On the scaffold the executioner was ready. Bernardo del Nero appeared, ascending with a slow, firm step.

Romola did not move. No cry escaped her—not even a stifled sound. She stood more firmly, as if her steadiness could lend him steadiness. She saw him pause, the white head kept erect, and she heard his voice distinctly, carrying across that brief hush:

“It is but a short space of life that my fellow-citizens have taken from me.”

As he spoke, his gaze moved slowly round. She felt—felt as one feels a hand laid on the heart—that his eyes rested on her. Her arms stretched out toward him, but whether in the air or only within her, she did not know. Then she saw no more.

After what seemed a long while, a voice said, “My daughter, all is peace now. I can conduct you to your house.”

She uncovered her head and found Bernardo’s confessor beside her, and they were no longer in the open loggia but in a room where grave men talked in subdued tones, as if words themselves must now tread carefully.

“I am ready,” Romola said, starting up. “Let us lose no time.”

All clinging, she thought, was ended. All strength now must be gathered for one purpose: to escape from a grasp under which she shuddered.
