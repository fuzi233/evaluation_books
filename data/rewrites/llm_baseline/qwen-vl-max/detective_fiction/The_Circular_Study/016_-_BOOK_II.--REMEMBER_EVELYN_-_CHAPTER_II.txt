A sigh escaped Amos Cadwalader—deep, shuddering, as if drawn from the very core of a man long buried beneath grief. It spoke louder than words: a wound still raw after thirty years, an ache that time had not healed. Felix, who had just spoken, echoed the breathless pause and pressed on, his voice low but steady.

"It was dusk when I told my story. From darkness to dawn, we sat facing one another—no sleep, no rest—our eyes locked in silent torment. Then we went to John Poindexter."

" Had he fled, we might have spared ourselves. But he met us openly, calmly, with the cold detachment of a man who cannot feel because he has never known feeling. His first words proved it: 'Spare yourselves, spare me—no more recriminations. The girl is dead. I cannot bring her back. Live your lives—eat, drink, spend, enjoy—there’s little time left for any of us. Why dwell on old sorrows?'"

Felix paused, then continued, his tone thick with bitterness. "That last word—'sorrows'—was a triumph. He meant to say, *When a man cares for nothing, no one, not even himself, there is no point in cursing him.*"

Ah, yes—that was the secret. That was what made him so powerful. He felt nothing. Not love. Not loss. Not guilt. And we knew it. We felt the blow—and we bowed under it. But before we left him, before we left the town, your father and I swore a vow: that we would make that heart bleed, however hard it was; that one day, by whatever means, we would force that soulless man to suffer as we had suffered—no matter how prosperous he seemed, how blessed his life appeared."

He stopped. Thomas stirred, but Amos silenced him with a gesture.

"Some men forget in a month. Others in a year. I have never forgotten. Neither has Felix. When you were born—after I married again, hoping joy might return—I felt something strange. I don’t know why, but I believed Evelyn’s avenger had come into the world."

"And when, a year or two later, we heard that God had either forgiven John Poindexter—or remembered his sins—and that he, too, had been given a child after eighteen years of marriage—I looked at your face, Thomas, and saw... I thought I saw... a way to fulfill the vengeance we had sworn to pursue."

Thomas grew. His spirit burned bright, his mind quick, his nature generous. When your mother died, leaving me a widower once more, I hesitated no longer. You were meant for this. You were born for it.

Do you remember the beginning of that journey—the one that took you far from me? How I carried you on my shoulder down a dusty road until we reached the edge of his land? There, among the tombs, I lifted you up and pointed to those distant gables silhouetted against the twilight gold. I said to you then, ‘This is the home of your enemy. This is where happiness will grow beneath a child’s smile—and you shall destroy it.’ Do you remember?"

Thomas nodded slowly. "I remember."

"Then now," Amos whispered, gripping his son's arm, "the hour has come. Fulfill that promise. Avenge our pain. Avenge your sister. _Poindexter’s daughter has grown to womanhood._"

At those words, Thomas recoiled as if struck. But his father misread his horror for hesitation.

"Woo her!" Amos urged, clutching his arm tighter. "Win her! They do not know you. To them, you are Thomas Adams—not Thomas Cadwalader. Take this young girl into your arms. Make her yours. She is only a country girl—you’ve lived in Paris. You can charm her, deceive her, break her. And when she suffers—when she cries out in despair—know this: he will feel it. He must love his child. Through her, we touch his heart. Make her suffer, and if, in doing so, you see him falter—then believe I am watching from beyond the grave. Believe I am smiling. For neither I nor you will have lived in vain."

He gasped, struggling for breath, but his will held firm. His eyes, though dimmed by death, still burned with purpose.

"I shall die tonight. I am dying now. I will never see your triumph. I will never witness John Poindexter’s eyes cloud over with agony—agony so deep it makes a man question whether God exists. But I will know it. Wherever I go, I will feel it. No mound of earth can hold my spirit down when he feels his doom. I will sense his pain—and I will rejoice."

He paused, trembling, then spoke again, his voice rising like a cry from the grave:

"Boy, boy—your life has been shaped for this moment. God gave you beauty. Man gave you strength. You have earned intelligence, grace, skill. All you need to do is show yourself to this girl—and she will fall into your hands. Once she does—remember _me_! Remember _Evelyn_!"

Never had Thomas seen such fire. It swept over him like a tide, pulling him forward despite every instinct. He raised his hand—meaning to speak—but could not form words.

His father misunderstood. "You are silent," he snapped. "You do not want this task. Perhaps you are virtuous—though you’ve lived alone in Paris, unbound by duty or law. Or perhaps you carry some noble instinct, some sense of honor, that blinds you to the wrongs that have screamed to heaven for thirty years. Thomas, Thomas—if you fail me now—"

"He will not fail you," came a calm voice from the side.

Felix. Always composed. Always certain.

"I have watched him. I know him. He will not fail."

Thomas flinched. He had nearly forgotten Felix. Now, turning, he saw the man clearly—for the first time.

Not large, not imposing in stature, but commanding. There was a quiet power in his gaze—gray, unwavering, utterly unshakable. Unlike his father’s frenzy, Felix’s presence was cold precision. Yet beneath the control, Thomas sensed a deeper force—one he could not resist.

And yet—Felix was elegant. Well-dressed, refined, everything a Parisian gentleman should be. Even the poverty that clung to Amos’s room did not touch him. Behind him, a dark-green curtain stretched from floor to ceiling, heavy and mysterious. It framed his face with eerie clarity, as if designed to draw attention.

For a moment, Thomas wondered if it had been placed there for that very reason.

But then Felix’s eyes returned to him—demanding, relentless.

Bowing his head, Thomas stammered, “I am at your command, Felix. At your command, father. I cannot say more. Only—I never saw Evelyn. She died before I was born. I—”

Felix cut in, gently but firmly. “Perhaps there is an obstacle we have not considered. Maybe you already love someone. If so, it need not matter.”

Thomas’s voice rose, sharp with indignation. “No. I am heart-whole. Save for a few fleeting dreams—dreams that fade each day.”

“Your heart will remain untouched,” Felix said. “I have seen the girl. I went there myself, a year ago. She is pale as a snowdrop. Lifeless. Dull. You won’t need to recall the laughter of Parisian women to resist her charm.”

Thomas shrugged. “She must taste the full sweetness of hope,” Felix continued, voice clear and cutting. “Only then can she know despair. She must experience every joy love brings—before we crush it. Can you play this role? One that requires patience, deception, courage, subtlety—and above all, the strength to endure her suffering without breaking?”

“I have the skill,” Thomas murmured. “But—”

“You lack the incentive,” Felix finished. “We understand. Our hatred has been fed by memories of a woman you never knew. Look, then. Look at your sister—how she was, how she remains in our hearts. See her, Thomas. See her as she was—despoiled, murdered, forgotten by Poindexter. Have you ever seen a face more beautiful? More tragic? Beauty and sorrow intertwined—can you not feel it?”

Thomas did not know what to expect. He braced himself—half-expecting to see a ghost.

But when he followed Felix’s hand, the curtain vanished—replaced by light.

And there, in its center, stood a woman.

Not a painting. Not a likeness.

She seemed alive. Tender. Gently sorrowful. Her eyes held a depth of pain that pierced through Thomas’s soul. She was Evelyn—more real than memory, more vivid than dream. He fell to his knees.

"Do not look at me so reproachfully, sweet Evelyn," he whispered. "I was born to avenge you. And I will. John Poindexter shall not rest in peace."

A soft sigh came from the bed.

"Swear it!" Amos cried, extending his arms in the shape of a cross.

"Yes, swear it!" Felix added, placing his hand upon the crossed arms.

Thomas stepped forward. Laid his hand beside Felix’s.

"I swear," he began, raising his voice above the storm raging outside. "I swear to win the affection of Eva Poindexter—and when her heart is mine, I will cast her back into anguish and scorn, into the arms of John Poindexter."

"Good," came a voice from the distance.

Darkness descended.

Thomas collapsed—unconscious—at his father’s feet.

* * * * *

Amos Cadwalader died that night.

But not before one final scene.

Around midnight, he stirred from the sleep that had followed the oath. His eyes opened—fixed on the door.

"Ah!" he breathed. "A visitor. John Poindexter. He comes to beg forgiveness before I leave this world."

The sarcasm in his voice, the mockery in his tone, sent chills through both sons.

They knew—he saw their enemy as clearly as he saw them.

"Does he fear meeting Evelyn? Does he wish to appease me before I join her shade? Let him not be disappointed."

He turned his head toward the door, voice rising with bitter triumph.

"I forgive you, John Poindexter. I forgive you the shame of my daughter. I forgive you my ruined life. I am dying—but I leave behind one who will not forgive. I have a son. An avenger of the dead. Who still lives—to—"

His voice broke.

He fell back.

And with those final words—sealed in blood and silence—Amos Cadwalader died.
