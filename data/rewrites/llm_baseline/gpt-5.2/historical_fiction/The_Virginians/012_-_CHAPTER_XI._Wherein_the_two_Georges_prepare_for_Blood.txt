Colonel George Warrington kept to his chamber of the tavern as if the very boards beneath his boots accused him. The place smelt of wet wool, pipe-ash, and yesterday’s ale, and through the thin wall he could hear—too clearly—the scrape of a chair, the dip of a quill, the low, set voice of his adversary dictating farewells.

In the other room Colonel Washington—General Braddock’s new aide-de-camp, with the same steady hand that could measure a road or lay a camp—thought proper to make his own dispositions. He wrote as a man writes who expects no indulgence from fate: five letters, each folded with care, sealed with his seal as if the wax might keep the world in order. One for his mother at Mount Vernon; one for his brother; one marked only with initials—M. C.; one to his Excellency Major-General Braddock; and the last, the tavern-keeper said, “for your mother, Madam Esmond,” which news, borne across a passage and into a young man’s already inflamed mind, fell like a coal into oil.

But in George Warrington’s room the tragedy was of another key—more boyish in its ornament, more fevered in its vanity. He had set his younger brother Harry to act as clerk and secretary. Harry’s hands shook so that the nib rasped, and his sobs kept time with the scratching. George dictated with a grandeur he borrowed from books, and with a bitterness he made entirely his own.

He framed it as a solemn farewell to their mother—he would not, he said, compromise her by naming any cause; he would speak of “the scheme which she had in view” with a satirical emphasis that made the phrase sound like a lash laid upon flesh.

“My dear, dear George, don’t say that!” Harry broke in, choking.

“‘As probably will be the case,’” George persisted, as if the very probability were a proof of his courage. “You know what a good shot Colonel George is, Harry. I am pretty fair myself, and ’tis probable that one or both of us will drop. —‘I scarcely suppose you will carry out the intentions you have at present in view.’”

Harry’s tears fell hot and salt, and the drops spotted the page. When he came to those words “in view,” he wrote, in vew; and the ink ran where his sleeve smeared it, and the salt water made a blurred little tide.

George peered over with sudden peevishness. “Why, child, what have you been writing, and who taught thee to spell?”

“I can’t think about spelling now, Georgy,” Harry whimpered. “I’m too miserable for that. I begin to think perhaps it’s all nonsense—perhaps Colonel George never—”

“Never meant to take possession of Castlewood?” George flamed, and every grievance he had been stewing like a too-strong sauce boiled over at once. “Never gave himself airs and patronised us there? Never advised my mother to have me flogged? Never intended to marry her? Never insulted me—and was insulted before the king’s officers? Never wrote to his brother to say we should be the better for his parental authority? The paper is there,” he cried, striking his breast-pocket, “and if anything happens to me, Harry Warrington, you will find it on my corse!”

Harry, driven past the point where tears are of any use, rubbed his fists into his eyes and made a mess of ink and salt and grief. “Write yourself, Georgy. I can’t write.”

So George pushed aside the blotted sheet, took a fresh one, and sat down with the air of a young man entering eternity by the gate of rhetoric. He made a will as he would have made a Latin exercise: longest words, grandest quotations, weightiest periods, the satire sharpened till it cut his own tongue. Yet there was tenderness in the bequests, as there is tenderness even in a hot-tempered boy when he thinks the world may be leaving him.

He desired that his negro boy, Sady, should be set free. He named his Horace, and a choice of his books; and—if possible—a suitable provision for his affectionate tutor, Mr. Dempster, who had taught him to scan verses and to speak of virtue with a Scotch precision that could not quite master Virginia heat. He left his silver fruit-knife, his music-books, and his harpsichord to little Fanny Mountain—small domestic relics, bright and intimate, like the scent that rises when an apple is pared cleanly and the peel curls away in one long ribbon.

He sealed the paper with the seal of arms his grandfather had worn, pressing down hard, as if to stamp the old world upon the new.

“The watch, of course, will be yours,” George said then, drawing out his grandfather’s gold watch and looking at it with a kind of wonder, as though time itself were an enemy he could not bring to the field. “Why, two hours and a-half are gone! ’Tis time Sady should be back with the pistols. Take the watch, Harry dear.”

“It’s no good!” Harry cried, and flung his arms round his brother. “If he fights you, I’ll fight him too. If he kills my Georgy—” and the boy’s mouth, in its misery, cast off more than one expression fit to send any recording angel flying.

Down below, the tavern was waking into its brutal holiday. The landlord—Captain Benson, in his old militia uniform, as if a coat could make a slaughter decent—came bustling with news and importance, smelling of stale spirits and damp leather.

He told them the Colonel was in the garden, walking up and down, waiting; and that the “Reg’lars was a’most sober, too, by this time,” which was offered as if sobriety were a fine polish for witnessing blood.

Benson’s had long been a rendezvous for the country’s rough sports—cock-fights, horse-matches, boxing and wrestling—and the very yard held the echoes of old hallooing, the crack of fists on bone, the coarse laughter that follows an eye gouged out. Today the air carried those memories like smoke clinging to rafters.

A plot near the Captain’s log-house had been enclosed with shingles and cleared for a kitchen-garden. The fence was new enough that the sap still showed pale at the cut ends, and within it cabbages sat in dull green patience, their leaves cupping last night’s dew. It was along that narrow strip of ordered earth that Colonel Washington paced, hands behind his back, head bowed, the sorrow on his handsome face as grave as any sermon.

Negro servants crowded at the palings, peering over. Their whispers and suppressed titters ran through the boards like mice. Sound ruled the place: the creak of porch-steps, a half-stifled snore from an officer rousing, the mutter of men trying to speak of death as if it were sport. Under the porch the British officers had bestirred themselves. Captain Waring walked almost steadily. Captain Grace lolled over the railing, eyes wide, unseeing, as if his gaze were fixed upon some floating point in a bottle.

Washington’s mind, for all its disciplined habit, moved in darker circles. There was no honourable way out in that country but the cruel customary one. He had been goaded into fury by a boy’s impertinence and had answered with insulting words. The boy had demanded reparation. Washington, who knew how long a grievance can sit in a young heart and sour, was shocked that Warrington’s jealousy and revenge had rankled so long—but the wrong had been his, and he had accepted the forfeit.

Then, from far off, came a sound that changed the air. A great hallooing and shouting rose from the road—such yelling as negroes love, noise for noise’s sake, and for the sheer joy of hearing the world answer them. Heads—woolly and powdered—turned at once. The shout grew louder; the clatter of hoofs came fast over the hard earth; and presently Mr. Sady burst into view on a foaming horse, riding as if he carried not pistols but a proclamation.

In the midst of the yard, with a flourish meant for drama and mischief alike, Sady fired a pistol. The crack snapped the morning, and the yard exploded in answering uproar. He fired the other—another sharp bite of sound. His horse, which had carried Harry on many a hunt, only tossed its head, accustomed to gunfire and to the sick delight men take in it.

Sady plunged into the courtyard amid a chaos of domestic life: fowls fluttering, turkeys scolding, horses kicking at flies and at one another, pigs shrieking as if they sensed that human violence always spills over into animal terror. Negroes crowded round him, bawling, laughing, slapping their thighs, the whole place an acoustic storm.

“Sady, sir, come here!” roared Master Harry.

“Sady, come here! Confound you!” shouted Master George, whose anger now found easy targets.

“Comin’, mas’r,” Sady said, and went right on talking, grinning, showing his teeth, savouring his own importance. He drew the pistols from the holster, snapped the locks with relish, and pointed one at a grunter that bolted squealing through the muck. He pointed down the road again, where the hoofbeats were returning like a refrain.

“Comin’, mas’r,” he repeated. “Everybody a-comin’.”

And indeed the gallop of other horses swelled upon the air. Into the yard clattered little Mr. Dempster, spurring his pony and digging at it as if he could kick knowledge into speed. Behind him rode a lady in a riding-habit upon Madam Esmond’s little horse—at first glance one might have thought it was Madam herself, but the figure was too stout, too full in the shoulders. As the horse’s grey flanks heaved and its bit foamed, recognition came: it was Mrs. Mountain, cheeks blown red by wind, skirts flecked with road-dust, riding as if she had no fear of broken bones.

“O Lor! O Golly! Hoop! Here dey come! Hurray!” rose a chorus from the yard.

Dr. Dempster and Mrs. Mountain flung themselves down from their horses, elbowed through the crowd, and rushed into the house, across the porch where the British officers sat in muzzy astonishment, down the steps, and into the garden, where George and Harry walked with their tall enemy stalking opposite. The cabbages stood between these lives and their possible ruin like innocent witnesses.

Almost before George could set his face into sternness and say, “What do you do here, madam?” Mrs. Mountain had thrown her arms round his neck.

“Oh, George, my darling! It’s a mistake! It’s a mistake, and is all my fault!”

“What’s a mistake?” George asked, drawing himself back with offended majesty, though her embrace had been warm and human as bread straight from an oven.

“What is it, Mounty?” Harry cried, shaking.

“That paper I took out of his portfolio—that paper I picked up, children, where the Colonel says he is going to marry a widow with two children. Who should it be but you, children, and who should it be but your mother?”

George’s face tightened. “Well?”

“Well, it’s—it’s not your mother. It’s that little widow Custis whom the Colonel is going to marry. He’d always take a rich one; I knew he would. It’s not Mrs. Rachel Warrington. He told Madam so to-day, just before he was going away, and that the marriage was to come off after the campaign. And—and your mother is furious, boys. And when Sady came for the pistols and told the whole house how you were going to fight, I told him to fire the pistols off, and I galloped after him, and I’ve nearly broken my poor old bones in coming to you.”

“I have a mind to break Mr. Sady’s,” George growled. “I specially enjoined the villain not to say a word.”

“Thank God he did, brother!” Harry said, and his voice cracked not with grief now but with relief so sharp it hurt. “Thank God he did!”

George, still angered by the humiliation of being dragged back from the brink, found a new indignity to clutch at. “What will Mr. Washington and those gentlemen think of my servant telling my mother at home that I was going to fight a duel?”

“You have shown your proofs before, George,” Harry said, respectful as ever in his love. “And thank Heaven, you are not going to fight our old friend—our grandfather’s old friend. For it was a mistake and there is no quarrel now, dear, is there? You were unkind to him under a wrong impression.”

“I certainly acted under a wrong impression,” George owned, unwillingly, “but—”

“George! George Washington!” Harry cried then, and sprang over the cabbage-garden towards the bowling-green where the Colonel paced. He ran with that heedless agility of youth, scattering the morning’s stillness with his breath and footfalls. Though the words did not reach us, we could see him with both hands out, the whole honest story tumbling from him in blunders and affection.

There was a custom then, now thinned out of manners like an old dialect forgotten: men could take each other in their arms without shame. When Harry had finished, Washington drew him close and held him to his heart. The Colonel’s voice faltered as he said, “Thank God, thank God for this!”

“Oh, George,” Harry answered, feeling in a rush how much he loved this friend, “how I wish I was going with you on the campaign!”

Washington pressed both the boy’s hands in a grasp that promised itself long life. Then, turning, he advanced towards the elder brother.

Harry, watching, perhaps expected the same embrace, that bodily sealing of renewed affection. But the air between the two Georges was not so easily sweetened. They joined hands, yet their salutation held itself stiffly, formal and stern on both sides, like two men tasting the same dish and finding it seasoned too sharply to enjoy.

“I find I have done you a wrong, Colonel Washington,” George Warrington said, “and must apologise—not for the error, but for much of my late behaviour which has resulted from it.”

“The error was mine! It was I who found that paper in your room, and showed it to George, and was jealous of you, Colonel. All women are jealous,” Mrs. Mountain cried, eager to shoulder blame as if she could carry it away like a basket.

“’Tis a pity you could not have kept your eyes off my paper, madam,” Washington said, and though his tone was controlled, it carried the edge of a man who has been made to pay for another’s curiosity. “You will permit me to say so. A great deal of mischief has come because I chose to keep a secret which concerned only myself and another person. For a long time George Warrington’s heart has been black with anger against me, and my feeling towards him has, I own, scarce been more friendly. All this pain might have been spared to both of us, had my private papers only been read by those for whom they were written.”

He checked himself, as if hearing in his own voice the beginnings of those “hasty words” he feared.

“I shall say no more now, lest my feelings again should betray me. Heaven bless thee, Harry! Farewell, George! And take a true friend’s advice, and try and be less ready to think evil of your friends. We shall meet again at the camp, and will keep our weapons for the enemy. Gentlemen! if you remember this scene to-morrow, you will know where to find me.”

He bowed very stately to the English officers on the porch—men still blinking as though wakened from drink into a different world—and then he left the abashed company, crossing the yard where the last echoes of Sady’s pistols still seemed to hang like bitter smoke among the squawking fowls and the frightened pigs, and he mounted and rode away swiftly, as if to outride both scandal and noise.
