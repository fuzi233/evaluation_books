One morning, Mrs. Bretton swept into my room with purpose in her step. "Let me see your dresses," she commanded. I obeyed silently, opening my wardrobe for her inspection.

"That will not do," she declared after a brief survey. "You must have something new."

Before I could protest, she was gone—only to return shortly with a dressmaker in tow. Measurements were taken, fabrics considered. "I intend to follow my own taste in this," she informed me, leaving no room for debate.

Two days later, the result arrived: a dress of soft pink silk.

"That is not for me," I said at once, recoiling as if presented with some outlandish costume.

"We shall see," replied my godmother, undeterred. "You will wear it tonight."

I was certain I would not. No force on earth, I thought, could compel me into such a confection. Pink! It was alien to me, as I was to it.

Yet by evening, without struggle or resistance, I found myself clad in the pink dress, its brightness tempered by a drapery of black lace. I was pronounced *en grande tenue* and directed to survey myself in the mirror. I did so with trepidation, then quickly turned away.

At seven o'clock sharp, Dr. Bretton arrived. My godmother, regal in brown velvet, led the way downstairs, and I followed, envying her stately elegance. Graham stood waiting in the drawing-room doorway.

*Please don’t let him think I’ve dressed to be noticed,* I prayed silently.

"Here, Lucy," he said, offering me a bouquet. His smile was kind, his glance approving—nothing more. No mockery, no amusement at my expense. The dress, I realized then, was simple in cut, free of frippery. It was only the color that had unnerved me. And if Graham found it unobjectionable, perhaps it was not so dreadful after all.

For those unaccustomed to grand entertainments, the anticipation alone is a kind of enchantment. The concert that night was to be held in the grand *salle* of the principal musical society, attended by royalty—the King and Queen of Labassecour themselves. The drive there was a pleasure in itself: the snug warmth of the carriage, the cheerful company, the flicker of stars through the trees as we rolled along the avenue. The city gates, the guards, the brief pretense of inspection—all of it was new, exhilarating.

The hall, when we entered, was a vision of gold and crimson. A great chandelier blazed overhead, scattering light like shattered diamonds. The air hummed with the murmur of the gathering crowd, the rustle of silks, the scent of perfume.

It was then, in a mirrored compartment between two pillars, that I caught an unexpected glimpse of our party—and of myself. For the first time, I saw what others saw: a figure in pink, slight and uncertain beside Mrs. Bretton’s imposing grace. The reflection was not flattering, but it was honest.

We took our seats as the hall filled. The most striking figure among the assembly was a woman of Junoesque beauty—cold, statuesque, utterly indifferent to the admiration she commanded.

"Beware," I murmured to Graham. "She will never love you, no matter how ardently you worship."

He laughed. "And what if that very indifference is the lure?"

His mother cut in dryly. "Spare me the theatrics, John. You’ve been threatening to marry for years."

Their banter was interrupted as the performers assembled—pupils of the Conservatoire, shepherded by none other than M. Paul Emanuel, whose imperious manner contrasted sharply with his brother Josef’s gentler guidance.

Then the royal party arrived. The King, a man marked by melancholy, moved as one burdened. The Queen, graceful and kind, seemed to carry his sorrow as her own. Their young son, the Prince, leaned against her knee, a bright spark in the somber tableau.

Among the guests, I recognized Ginevra Fanshawe—radiant, frivolous, utterly at ease in her lofty circle. She noticed Dr. Bretton, but her gaze slid over him with careless amusement. He grew quiet, his replies to his mother clipped.

The concert itself was a blur of music—some stirring, some merely elaborate. The choruses, at least, were rousing, sung with full-throated vigor by the Labassecourian choir.

As the evening waned, I found myself caught between the glitter of the present and the shadow of what awaited me—the quiet halls of Madame Beck’s, the solitude of the garden, the distant murmur of lessons. But for now, I let the music and the lights carry me, just a little longer.
