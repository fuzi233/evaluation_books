Having secured substantial winnings from his cousin and the reverend chaplain, our young Virginian felt a solemn obligation to afford them a chance at redemption. I fear his mother and other voices of serious morality would have disapproved of his conduct. Indeed, he indulged far too freely in card games. Beyond the daily rounds of whist or quadrille, which commenced shortly after the three o'clock dinner and lingered until supper, there arose stakes of considerable magnitude. Into these high-stakes diversions, wherein all the gentlemen, including Lord Castlewood himself, participated, the young man was drawn. Following their Sunday conversations, his lordship had grown notably more open and confiding toward his kinsman, betting with him affably and engaging him in backgammon and piquet. Both Mr. William and the pious chaplain relished a touch of hazard; however, this pastime was conducted clandestinely, hidden from the ladies of the house, who had repeatedly extracted vows from Cousin Will that he would not entice the Virginian into mischief and that he would himself abstain. Thus, Will promised as much as his aunt or mother could demand—a pledge of total abstinence, repeated with fervor—and when the family retired, Mr. William would stroll over to Cousin Harry's chambers armed with a dice-box and a bottle of rum, where he, along with Hal and the reverend gentleman, would gamble until the break of dawn.

When Harry offered those glowing accounts of his maternal estate in America to Lord Castlewood, he harbored no intention to deceive, boast, or lie, for the lad possessed a nature of profound honesty. Yet, life at home had acquainted him with a motley crew of companions—horse-jockeys, tavern loafers, gamblers, and sportsmen, many of whom populated his native colony. An aristocracy of land, surrounded by a workforce of negroes tilling fields of tobacco and corn, found little amusement save in the hunt or at the card table and punch bowl. The hospitality of the province was boundless; every man's house was his neighbor's. Idle gentlemen rode from mansion to mansion, finding in each the same sport, welcome, and rough plenty. The Virginian squire often sat upon a barefooted valet and a cobbled saddle, yet within the crumbling fences and behind the cracked windows of the hall, there abounded corn for the horses, venison for the master, and limitless drink. Harry had slept on countless straw mattresses and engaged in endless, jovial night bouts over claret and punch in cracked bowls until morning, when it was time to follow the hounds. His poorer brother, conversely, was of a much more sober temperament, a fact the lad admitted with contrition. "Thus does Nature fashion folk," the narrator observed, "for some love books and tea, while others crave Burgundy and a gallop across the countryside." These youthful tastes were quickly revealed to his friends in England. None of them favored the rigid Puritan discipline, nor did they dislike Harry for lacking the humility of a milk-drinking lamb. Manners, one sees, were far looser a century prior; tongues were vastly freer, and names and deeds spoken of then, which we would now shriek to hear mentioned. "Yes, madam," the voice of history seems to whisper, "we are not as our ancestors were. Should we not thank the Fates for improving our morals so prodigiously?"

Maintaining a sharp, calculating eye on those around him, and correctly surmising that his cousins sought to probe his affairs, Harry Warrington wisely kept his own counsel regarding his personal matters. In games of chance or sport, he proved quite a match for the three gentlemen into whose company he had fallen. Even in the noble game of billiards, he could hold his own after a few days of play with his cousins and their revered pastor. His grandfather, a lover of the game, had imported from Europe one of the very few tables existing in His Majesty's province of Virginia. Though Mr. William could defeat him at the outset, Harry refused to yield undue odds to the young gambler. After their first wager, Harry remained vigilant against Mr. William, and Cousin William, speaking with respect, acknowledged that the American was his equal in most things and superior in many. While Harry played so well that he could best the parson and soon rival William—who, of course, could best the girls—it remained a mystery why, in contests with them, particularly with one of them, Mr. Warrington frequently emerged second. He was profoundly courteous to every soul wearing a petticoat; nor has that traditional politeness yet departed from his country. Every woman of the Castlewood establishment adored the young gentleman. The stern housekeeper was mollified by him; the portly cook greeted him with boisterous smiles; the ladies' maids, whether French or English, smirked and giggled in his favor; and the porter's daughter at the lodge invariably returned a kind word. Madame de Bernstein noted all these things, saying nothing aloud, but watching carefully the boy's disposition and behavior.

Who can truly say how old Lady Maria Esmond was? Books of the Peerage were scarce in those days compared to our blessed modern times, and I cannot determine her age to within a few years, or even a lustre or two. When Will claimed she was thirty-five, he spoke abusively and was prone to exaggeration. Maria was Will's half-sister, the child of the late Lord Castlewood's first wife, a German lady whom 'tis known my lord wed during the reign of Queen Anne. Baron Bernstein, who married Maria's Aunt Beatrix (Bishop Tusher's widow), was also a German, a Hanoverian nobleman and relative of the first Lady Castlewood. If Lady Maria was born under George I., and if His Majesty George II. had reigned for thirty additional years, how could she possibly be twenty-seven, as she told Harry Warrington? "I am old, child," she used to say, addressing him with a familiarity reserved for intimates. "I am a hundred years old. I am twenty-seven. I might be your mother almost." To which Harry would retort, "Your ladyship might be the mother of all the cupids, I am sure. You don't look twenty, on my word you do not!"

Lady Maria appeared any age one wished. She was a fair beauty with a dazzling complexion of white and red, an abundance of fair hair flowing over her shoulders, and beautiful, round arms which displayed to uncommon advantage when she played billiards with Cousin Harry. When she stretched across the table to execute a stroke, that youth caught glimpses of a dainty ankle, a laced stocking, and a little black satin slipper with a tiny red heel, filling him with unutterable rapture. He swore there had never been such a foot, ankle, stockinged limb, or satin slipper in the world. And yet, oh, foolish Harry! Your mother's foot was far more slender and half an inch shorter than Lady Maria's. But somehow, boys do not gaze with rapture upon their mothers' slippers and ankles.

No doubt Lady Maria was very kind to Harry when they were alone. Before her sister, aunt, stepmother, or in public, she treated him lightly, calling him a simpleton, a chit, and various trivial names. Behind his back, and even before his face, she mimicked his accent, which bore the distinct mark of his province. Harry blushed and corrected his faulty intonation under the tutelage of his English monitresses. His aunt pronounced that they would soon make him a fine gentleman.

Lord Castlewood, as we have said, became daily more familiar and friendly with his guest and relative. Until the crops were gathered, there was little sport save for an occasional cock-match at Winchester and bull-baiting at Hexton Fair. Harry and Will rode to many jolly fairs and races about the region, where the young Virginian was introduced to several county families—the Henleys of the Grange, the Crawleys of Queen's Crawley, the Redmaynes of Lionsden, and so forth. Neighbors arrived in their great heavy coaches, staying two or three days in country fashion. Fewer might have come had not the entire Castlewood family feared offending Madame de Bernstein. She disliked country company; the rustic society and conversation annoyed her. "We shall be merrier when my aunt leaves us," the young folks declared. "We have cause, as you may imagine, to be very civil to her. You know what a favorite she was with our father? And with reason. She procured his earldom, being very well indeed at Court at that time with the King and Queen. She commands here naturally, perhaps a trifle too much. We are all afraid of her: even my elder brother stands in awe of her, and my stepmother is much more obedient to her than she ever was to my father, whom she ruled with a rod of iron. But Castlewood is merrier when our aunt is absent. At least, we have much more company. You will come to us in our gay days, Harry, won't you? Of course you will: this is your home, sir. I was so pleased—oh, so pleased—when my brother said he considered it your home!"

A soft hand was extended following this pretty speech, a pair of well-preserved blue eyes looking exceedingly friendly. Harry grasped his cousin's hand with ardour. I know not what privileges of kinship he would not have claimed, had he not been so timid. They called the English selfish and cold. He initially thought his relatives so; how mistaken he was!
