We, who had all a hand in the furtive, ill-judged scheme to secure Theo and I a union behind good General Lambert’s back, felt thrice over guilty when we saw what a blow we had dealt the man we loved and honoured beyond all others. He was the kindest soul that ever breathed: his own cherished child had deceived him, his entire household had banded together to keep a secret from him. Looking back now, my dearest, I swear neither of us would ever have found the heart to do it again. To wound that gentle creature, who never spoke a harsh word to any of us, was no better than striking a child or laying a blow on a woman who has nursed you at her breast. When all the deceit came out, we grovelled in shame before him before he, ever ready to pardon, pulled us to his breast, forgave us freely, joined our hands in blessing, and then sailed away for his new colonial governorship, leaving Theo and me standing hand in hand on the shore, silent, flushing hot with guilt, as though we had sent him to his grave before his time.

Our “wedding trip” to Winchester and Hampton was no giddy, laughing affair. For weeks we moved like ghosts, quiet and subdued, half-convinced we had committed a sin so black no amount of General Lambert’s kindness could ever wash it away. We settled into my old Bloomsbury lodgings, quiet, modest, with no fine wedding feast, no parade of guests. I sent a letter back to my mother’s estate in Virginia, laying the news out plainly: I had made an honourable promise to Theo, and when General Lambert departed for his post, I had kept my word, and meant to finish my law studies to earn our keep, no matter if at home or across the Atlantic. Mrs. Mountain wrote back at Madam Esmond’s bidding—my mother would not so much as put pen to paper to acknowledge my marriage, so great was her fury.

That fury spread to every branch of our London relations, and it was almost comical to watch. The butler at my uncle Sir Miles Warrington’s Hill Street door looked as though he were about to drop dead of shock when I presented myself on his threshold with Theo on my arm, stammering “Sir is not at home, madam, sir” before he slammed the door in our faces. We were laughing at that absurd performance, tripping down the street, when we came upon Lady Warrington’s sedan chair stuck fast in a traffic jam. I swept off my hat and bowed as low as I knew how. “I wonder you dare look me in the face!” she gasped. “Nay, your ladyship, do not rob me of that precious privilege!” I teased. She screamed at her chairmen to move on, but the crowd was too thick. “You would not impale your own husband’s flesh and blood on that spiteful glance of yours, surely?” I called. She rattled the glass shut with a snap, and I bowed again, so elaborately that Theo had to bury her face in my shoulder to smother her giggles.

A few days later in Hyde Park, we spotted her little son, my cousin Miles, trotting along on his pony with a groom trailing behind him. The moment he saw us, he dug his heels into his horse’s sides and galloped full tilt over, the groom yelling “Stop, Master Miles, stop!” at the top of his lungs. “Mama says I am not to speak to you,” he panted, grinning at us, “but sending Harry my love isn’t speaking, is it? And you—” he nodded at Theo, “you’re my new cousin! Nobody said I couldn’t talk to you. You’re very pretty!” The groom caught up to him, apologising to us, and the boy trotted off, waving over his shoulder.

“See how all my grand relations have set their faces against me,” I laughed to Theo. She looked up at me, eyes shining brighter than any diamond in a court lady’s jewellery box. “As if I cared a straw for your relations, when I have you!” she said. Oh, those first quiet months of our marriage! That brisk, cozy winter, the kettle singing on the hearth, the rum punch poor Mr. Sampson would stumble in to make of an evening, the trips to the playhouse where we daydreamed that my new tragedy *Pocahontas* would outstrip every hit ever staged at Drury Lane.

Old Madame de Bernstein, that wicked sharp old aunt of mine, loved the story of Lady Warrington and the stuck sedan chair so much I had to act it out for her over tea. The Clarges Street aunt who had first received us with nothing but scorn, once she saw how sweet and unassuming Theo was, fell utterly in love with her, insisting we come for supper every night, even making us use the back stairs so none of her grand company could spy on us—“You are no longer fit for polite society now you’re married for love, dears,” she would say, as if that were the silliest crime in the world.

The Dowager Countess of Castlewood invited us to her card parties in Kensington, but we quickly found that losing half our small fortune at cards and paying for a hackney coach all the way across London was more than our slender budget could bear. We stopped going, and no one missed us a bit, no more than any other poor relation who slips out of the fashionable world. Theo never regretted it for a moment—she looked far lovelier in her simple white gown, her only ornaments the little bits of jewellery her own mother had left her, than all the wrinkled old duchesses blazing in rubies and diamonds at those parties. Only one fool, that silly fop Beau Lothair, bothered her, sending a silly love note to our lodgings. I ran into him in Covent Garden a week later, and promised I’d break his nose if he ever showed his face near Bloomsbury again, and we never heard a peep from him after.

The only guests we had at our little dinners were Hagan the actor and his wife, poor dear Maria, who’d run off and married him for love, and my old friend Spencer from the Temple, who would sigh over his own unlucky love affairs as he sipped our home-made punch. Even great Dr. Johnson would drop by unannounced of an evening, begging for a dish of Theo’s famous tea—though Gumbo would grumble after he left that he drank enough to fill a pail, and ate half the larder. The gruff old doctor would sputter and argue, fling his bread crumbs across the table, and tell Theo every time he saw her: “Madam, you do not love me. I see it in your face. But you are far prettier than any portrait Sir Joshua Reynolds ever painted, and I would have him paint you, if you would let him.” I never dared, for I knew Sir Joshua’s fee was far out of our reach, and I wish now I had—so our grown children might see just how bright Theo’s face was, all those years ago.

The silliest quarrel we ever had that winter was when Hagan offered to read scenes from my new tragedy after tea. Dr. Johnson huffed and refused, saying he would far rather hear Theo chat about her household than listen to any declaimed verses. He ate so much of Theo’s famous butter sauce that he stuffed a whole big dollop of it into the bosom of his dirty dingy shirt, which Hagan teased him about, and the old man flew into a towering rage, yelling that Hagan was rude and uneducated, before he realised he was being ridiculous, laughed, and blew the anger away like a gust of cold wind. Maria, meanwhile, was gazing adoringly at Hagan, as though he were a prince from a play, reciting lines from her favourite tragedy under her breath, lamenting that he would have to play the unromantic Captain Smith in my *Pocahontas*.

Ah, that tragedy—all our hopes were pinned to it. By that point, my small patrimony was almost gone: I had paid off my brother Harry’s old debts, honoured all my mother’s drafts from Virginia, and spent nearly three hundred pounds keeping us fed and housed. I had no other income, save what the play might earn. Foolish youth, to count your fortune on a theatrical hit, to think a single tragedy would keep us housed, fed, and set up for life! But General Lambert had left for his post convinced I had plenty of funds set aside, his wife Aunt Lambert was certain Providence would never let a good, kind couple starve, and Theo believed every word I said, so we threw all our hopes on those five acts of verse, and our three hundred pounds of ready cash.

Opening night drew near. I went with Theo to call on Lord Wrotham, an old friend of General Lambert’s, who gave us his blessing, bought a whole box of seats for his friends, and promised to drum up support. The Baroness said she would send two of her servants, dressed in their finest, to take a front box. The Dowager Countess sent word she was having a card party that night and could not attend. My uncle and Lady Warrington would not have stepped foot in Drury Lane to save my life. Poor Sampson, who had helped negotiate the play’s production, had bragged to the manager that Lords Bute and even the King himself would attend—and now Garrick, the manager, was in a fine fury, taking snuff so fast he sneezed three times in a row. At the final rehearsal that afternoon, he handed me a letter, his face dark as thunder.

I read it. It was from one of Lord Bute’s secretaries. His lordship, the letter said, was a great patron of the arts, but he could not possibly attend the performance of a play whose lead actor, Mr. Hagan, had contracted a clandestine marriage with the daughter of a peer of the realm—far less could he ask his Majesty the King to grace such a performance.

I walked home to Bloomsbury, my face pale as a sheet. Theo had a hot dinner all set out on the table, waiting for me. I told her I was only tired from the long rehearsal. I did not dare tell her that all our hopes, all our plans for our new life, had just shattered at my feet.
