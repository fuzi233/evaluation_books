I mentioned my lady a little while back. And if the Moonstone—God save us from the troubles it brought—was ever to lie under our roof long enough to be missed there, it must first have been laid, like a tempting sweetmeat on a plate, in my lady’s daughter’s hands. And that daughter, of course, could never have drawn breath to receive any gift at all, if my lady had not first borne her into the world—painfully, as women do, and patiently, as only they can. So if you want a beginning that reaches far enough back to hold the whole mischief in it, you begin with my lady. It’s a comfort, when you’ve a knot to untie, to know you’ve taken hold of the right end of the string.

If you know the fashionable world—even by hearsay—you’ve heard of the three Miss Herncastles: Miss Adelaide, Miss Caroline, and Miss Julia; Miss Julia the youngest, and, to my mind, the best of the three. I had my opportunities of judging; you’ll see how, directly.

I went into the old lord’s service as page-boy—fifteen years old and as sharp-set as a farm pup—waiting on the three honourable young ladies. Thank God, in this business of the Diamond we needn’t trouble ourselves with the old lord. He had the longest tongue and the shortest temper of any man, gentle or simple, that ever made the air ring sour with his own voice. There are noises that soothe you—wind in elms, a kettle beginning to sing, a cart creaking honest on a country lane; and there are noises that set your teeth on edge and turn peace out of doors. The old lord’s talk was the second sort—forever clattering over other people, never leaving room for the quiet that keeps a household sweet.

I stayed in that service till Miss Julia married Sir John Verinder. An excellent man—only wanting somebody to manage him; and, between ourselves, he found somebody to do it. He throve on being managed, as a good joint thrives under a steady hand—no scorching, no hurry, just the right turning at the right time. He grew fat and comfortable, lived happy, and died easy; dating, as I say, from the day my lady took him to church to be married, to the day she took his last breath away from him as gently as she had taken his arm, and closed his eyes for ever.

I went with the bride down here to the husband’s house and lands.

“Sir John,” says my lady, “I can’t do without Gabriel Betteredge.”

“My lady,” says Sir John, “I can’t do without him, either.”

That was his way with her; and that was how I came into his service. It was all one to me where I went, so long as my mistress and I went together.

Now, my lady took an interest in the out-of-door work, the farms, and the people on the estate; and I took an interest too—partly because she set the tune and we all kept it, and partly because I was a small farmer’s seventh son myself, and the smell of turned earth and stored apples was in my blood. There’s a sound to good husbandry as well as a smell: the measured chop of a cleaver in a dairy yard, the soft rush of grain poured into a bin, the lowing at milking-time that comes like a hymn through the dusk. Those are the noises that tell you a place is alive and rightly ordered.

My lady got me put under the bailiff. I did my best, gave satisfaction, and got promotion, as you may say, by plain work. Some years later—on the Monday as it might be—my lady says: “Sir John, your bailiff is a stupid old man. Pension him liberally, and let Gabriel Betteredge have his place.”

On the Tuesday as it might be, Sir John says: “My lady, the bailiff is pensioned liberally; and Gabriel Betteredge has got his place.”

You hear enough and more than enough of married people living together miserably. Here’s an example the other way. Let it warn some of you and encourage others. In the meantime, I go on.

So there I was, you’ll say, in clover: a position of trust, a little cottage of my own, rounds on the estate in the morning, accounts in the afternoon; and in the evening my pipe and my _Robinson Crusoe_. What more could I want? Well—remember what Adam wanted when he was alone in Eden; and if you don’t blame it in Adam, don’t blame it in me.

The woman I fixed my eye on kept house for me at my cottage. Her name was Selina Goby.

I agree with the late William Cobbett about picking a wife: see that she chews her food well and sets her foot down firmly when she walks, and you’re safe enough. Selina was all right in both respects. You could hear, with comfort, the steady way she went about the pantry; not a rattling, fussy creature, slamming lids and banging pails as if noise was the same thing as work, but the sort that knows how to lay a knife down quiet and keep a fire up without making a show of it. She had a hand with broth—clear, strong, and honest, with the scum lifted as neatly as a lie taken off a story. She knew where things came from, too: which farmer’s butter would sweeten best, which orchard’s apples held their shape in a tart, which corner of the garden gave you thyme with a bite to it. A household lives by such knowledge; and when it goes, it goes like a dialect—first the old words are laughed at, then they’re forgotten, and then you find you’ve lost a way of tasting the world you can never get back.

But I had another reason for marrying Selina—entirely of my own discovering. Selina, being a single woman, made me pay her so much a week for board and services. Selina, being my wife, couldn’t charge for her board, and would have to give me her services for nothing. That was the point of view I looked at it from. Economy—with a dash of love.

I put it to my mistress, as in duty bound, just as I had put it to myself.

“I have been turning Selina Goby over in my mind,” I said, “and I think, my lady, it will be cheaper to marry her than to keep her.”

My lady burst out laughing, and said she didn’t know which to be most shocked at—my language or my principles. Some joke tickled her, I suppose, of the sort you can’t take unless you’re quality. I understood nothing, except that I was free to put it next to Selina; and I went and put it accordingly.

And what did Selina say? Lord! how little you must know of women, if you ask that. Of course she said yes.

As the time drew nearer—and there got to be talk of my having a new coat for the ceremony—my mind began to misgive me. I’ve compared notes with other men, and they all own that about a week beforehand they privately wish themselves out of it. I went a trifle further; I actually rose up, as it were, and tried to get out of it.

Not for nothing! I was too just a man to expect she’d let me off for nothing. Compensation to the woman when the man backs out is one of the laws of England. In obedience to the laws—and after turning it over careful in my mind—I offered Selina Goby a feather-bed and fifty shillings to be off the bargain.

You will hardly believe it, but it’s true: she was fool enough to refuse.

After that it was all over with me. I got the new coat as cheap as I could, and I went through the rest of it as cheap as I could.

We were not a happy couple, and not a miserable couple. We were six of one and half-a-dozen of the other. How it was, I don’t understand; but we always seemed, with the best motives, to be getting in each other’s way. When I wanted to go upstairs, there was my wife coming down; when my wife wanted to go down, there was I coming up. That is married life, according to my experience: two people trying to keep house in the same space, like two cooks reaching for the same salt at the same moment—no harm meant, but elbows forever meeting.

After five years of misunderstandings on the stairs, it pleased an all-wise Providence to relieve us of each other by taking my wife. I was left with my little girl Penelope, and with no other child.

Shortly afterwards Sir John died, and my lady was left with her little girl, Miss Rachel, and with no other child.

If I have written to any purpose of my lady, you won’t need to be told that Penelope was taken care of under my good mistress’s own eye. She was sent to school, taught, made sharp, and promoted, when old enough, to be Miss Rachel’s own maid. In those days the house had its own proper sounds: the swish of skirts on the stair, the soft clink of china at tea, the low voices that belonged to family life. Not like the new times, when people think a place must be lively if it is loud, and buy noise the way they buy pictures—because the shop tells them it is art. Give me the old quiet that lets you hear a spoon stir and know, by the very note of it, whether the sugar is fine or coarse.

As for me, I went on year after year as bailiff, up to Christmas 1847, when there came a change in my life.

On that day my lady invited herself to a cup of tea—alone with me—in my cottage. She remarked that, reckoning from the year when I started as page-boy in the old lord’s time, I had been more than fifty years in her service; and she put into my hands a beautiful waistcoat of wool that she had worked herself, to keep me warm in the bitter winter weather.

There are gifts that sit on you like a good supper—warming, steadying, making you ashamed of all the sharp words you ever spoke. I took that waistcoat, and for once in my life I was at a loss for the right thing to say.

But, to my great astonishment, it turned out the waistcoat was not an honour; it was a bribe.

My lady had discovered I was getting old before I had discovered it myself; and she had come to my cottage to wheedle me—if I may use such an expression—into giving up my hard out-of-door work as bailiff and taking my ease for the rest of my days as steward in the house.

I made as good a fight against the indignity of taking my ease as I could. But my mistress knew the weak side of me: she put it as a favour to herself. The dispute ended with me wiping my eyes, like an old fool, with my new woollen waistcoat, and saying I would think about it.

The perturbation in my mind, in regard to thinking about it, being truly dreadful after my lady had gone away, I applied the remedy which I have never yet found to fail in doubt and emergency. I smoked a pipe and took a turn at _Robinson Crusoe_.

Before I had been five minutes with that extraordinary book, I came on a comforting bit (page one hundred and fifty-eight), as follows: “Today we love, what tomorrow we hate.”

I saw my way clear directly. Today I was all for continuing to be farm-bailiff; tomorrow, on the authority of _Robinson Crusoe_, I should be all the other way. Take myself tomorrow, while in tomorrow’s humour, and the thing was done.

My mind relieved in this manner, I went to sleep that night in the character of Lady Verinder’s farm-bailiff, and I woke up the next morning in the character of Lady Verinder’s house-steward. All quite comfortable—and all through _Robinson Crusoe_!

My daughter Penelope has just looked over my shoulder to see what I have done so far. She remarks it is beautifully written, and every word of it true. But she points out one objection. She says what I have done so far isn’t in the least what I was wanted to do. I am asked to tell the story of the Diamond—and instead of that I have been telling the story of my own self.

Curious, and quite beyond me to account for. I wonder whether the gentlemen who make a business and a living out of writing books ever find their own selves getting in the way of their subjects, like me? If they do, I can feel for them.

In the meantime, here is another false start and more waste of good writing-paper. What’s to be done now? Nothing that I know of—except for you to keep your temper, and for me to begin it all over again for the third time.
