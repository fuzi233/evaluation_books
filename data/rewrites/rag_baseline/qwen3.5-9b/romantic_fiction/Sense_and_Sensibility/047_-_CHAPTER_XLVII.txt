Mrs. Dashwood received Willoughby's defense with a mixture of relief and sorrow. She rejoiced that he was cleared of the specific charge laid against him; she pitied his plight and genuinely wished him happiness. Yet, the past remained an immutable scar. No amount of vindication could restore Marianne's shattered faith, nor repair the irreparable damage to her character. Nothing could erase the memory of the agony Marianne had endured through Willoughby's machinations, nor absolve the guilt inherent in his treatment of Eliza. Consequently, he held no place in her daughter's former esteem, and Colonel Brandon's reputation remained untarnished. Had Mrs. Dashwood, like her daughter, heard Willoughby's tale firsthand—witnessed the raw desperation in his countenance and the intensity of his manner—her compassion might have been deeper. But it was neither within Elinor's power nor her desire to manufacture such emotions in another through her retelling. Reflection had tempered her judgment, sobering her view of Willoughby's worth; thus, she sought only to state the bare facts, exposing the truths intrinsic to his nature without the embellishments of tenderness that might cloud the mind.

In the evening, as the three sat together, Marianne voluntarily broached the subject again. It required significant effort; the restless, agitated thoughts that had plagued her for days surfaced in her rising flush and unsteady voice. "I wish to assure you both," she declared, "that I see everything—as you can desire me to do." Mrs. Dashwood would have interjected with soothing words, had not Elinor, wishing to hear her sister's unbiased assessment, signaled urgently for silence. Marianne continued slowly. "It is a great relief to me—what Elinor told me this morning. I have now heard exactly what I wished to hear." For a moment, her voice failed her, but regaining composure, she spoke with newfound calm. "I am now perfectly satisfied. I wish for no change. I never could have been happy with him, after knowing, as I must eventually have known, all this. I should have possessed no confidence, no esteem. Nothing could have altered those feelings."

"I know it—I know it!" cried her mother. "Happy with a man of libertine practices! With one who so injured the peace of the dearest of our friends and the finest of men! No—my Marianne has not a heart capable of finding happiness with such a creature! Her conscience, her exquisitely sensitive conscience, would have felt every ounce of the remorse a husband ought to feel." Marianne sighed and reiterated, "I wish for no change."

"You consider the matter," Elinor observed, "precisely as a sound mind and clear understanding must. You surely perceive, as I do, sufficient reason in this instance, and in many others, to conclude that your marriage would have entangled you in inevitable troubles and disappointments, poorly supported by an affection that was anything but certain. Had you wed, poverty would have been your constant companion. His extravagance is admitted even by himself; his entire conduct demonstrates that self-denial is a concept foreign to him. Your youthful inexperience combined with his relentless demands, operating on a meager income, would inevitably have precipitated distresses far more grievous precisely because they were unforeseen and unanticipated. Your innate sense of honor and integrity would undoubtedly drive you, upon realizing your situation, to attempt every form of economy imaginable. Perhaps, so long as your frugality reduced only your own comforts, you might have been permitted to persist; but beyond that point—and how little could your solitary management halt the ruin that had commenced prior to your union? Furthermore, had you attempted, however reasonably, to curtail *his* pleasures, is it not to be feared that, rather than persuading such selfish sentiments to consent, you would have diminished your own influence over his heart, causing him to regret the very connection that had ensnared him in such difficulties?"

Marianne's lips trembled as she repeated the word "selfish" in a tone questioning whether Elinor truly believed it. "Every aspect of his behavior," Elinor replied, "from the inception to the conclusion of this affair, was founded on selfishness. It was selfishness that first allowed him to toy with your affections; it was selfishness that, once his own were secured, delayed his confession; and it was selfishness that ultimately drove him from Barton. His own enjoyment, or his own ease, was his sole governing principle in every particular."

"It is very true. My happiness was never his object."

"At present," Elinor continued, "he regrets his actions. Why does he regret it? Because he discovers it has yielded no personal satisfaction. It has not made him happy. His circumstances are currently unburdened; he suffers no evils of that nature. He merely concludes that he has married a woman of a less amiable temperament than yourself. But does it follow that had he married you, he would have been content? The inconveniences would have differed. He would then have suffered under the financial hardships which, because they are absent, he now dismisses as trivial. He would have had a wife whose temper he could not complain of, yet he would have been perpetually necessitous—perpetually poor. He would likely soon learn to rank the countless comforts of a secure estate and substantial income as far superior to domestic bliss than the mere disposition of a spouse."

"I have no doubt of it," Marianne said. "And I have nothing to regret—nothing but my own folly."

"Rather attribute it to your mother's imprudence, my child," Mrs. Dashwood interjected. "*She* must bear the responsibility." Marianne would not allow her to proceed. Satisfied that both women recognized their respective errors, Elinor wished to avoid revisiting the past lest it further weaken her sister's spirit. Turning to the primary topic, she immediately resumed, "One observation may, I believe, be fairly drawn from the entirety of this story: that all of Willoughby's difficulties stem from the initial offense against virtue in his conduct toward Eliza Williams. That crime was the genesis of every subsequent transgression and the source of all his current discontent." Marianne assented most profoundly to this remark, prompting her mother to enumerate Colonel Brandon's sufferings and virtues with the warmth that friendship and intent could jointly dictate. Her daughter, however, appeared to listen with little interest.

Over the next few days, Elinor noted that Marianne did not regain strength as anticipated. While her resolve remained unyielding and she strove to appear cheerful and composed, Elinor trusted that time would heal her health. Margaret returned, and the family was once again reunited, quietly resettled at the cottage. Though they did not pursue their studies with the same vigor as during their initial stay at Barton, they planned a rigorous resumption of them in the future.

Elinor grew increasingly impatient for news of Edward. Since departing London, she had heard nothing of his plans, nothing certain regarding his current location. Letters had exchanged between her and her brother following Marianne's illness; in the first letter from John, this sentence appeared: "We know nothing of our unfortunate Edward, and can make no enquiries on so prohibited a subject, but conclude him to be still at Oxford." This was the sole intelligence concerning Edward provided by the correspondence, for his name was omitted from all subsequent letters. She was not destined, however, to remain ignorant of his movements for long. One morning, their manservant was dispatched to Exeter on business. Upon returning to the table and satisfying his mistress's inquiries regarding the outcome of his errand, he volunteered this communication: "I suppose you know, ma'am, that Mr. Ferrars is married."

Marianne gave a violent start, fixed her gaze upon Elinor, saw her turn pale, and collapsed into her chair in hysterics. Mrs. Dashwood, whose eyes, while answering the servant's query, had instinctively followed the same direction, was shocked to perceive through Elinor's countenance the depth of her suffering. A moment later, equally distressed by Marianne's condition, she knew not on which child to focus her primary attention. The servant, observing only that Miss Marianne had fallen ill, possessed enough sense to summon a maid, who, with Mrs. Dashwood's assistance, supported her into the adjoining room. By that time, Marianne was somewhat recovered. Her mother, leaving her to the care of Margaret and the maid, returned to Elinor. Although still greatly disordered, Elinor had regained enough use of her reason and voice to begin questioning Thomas regarding the source of his intelligence. Mrs. Dashwood immediately assumed the burden of inquiry, allowing Elinor to receive the information without the exertion of seeking it.

"Who told you that Mr. Ferrars was married, Thomas?"

"I saw Mr. Ferrars myself, ma'am, this morning in Exeter, along with his lady, Miss Steele as she was. They were stopped in a chaise at the door of the New London Inn as I went there with a message from Sally at the Park to her brother, who is one of the post-boys. I happened to glance up as I passed the chaise and saw directly it was the youngest Miss Steele. So I took off my hat, and she recognized me, calling out to inquire after you, ma'am, and the young ladies, especially Miss Marianne. She bid me convey her compliments and Mr. Ferrars's, their best regards and service, expressing how sorry they were they lacked the time to visit, noting they were in a great hurry to proceed further down for a short while, but promising that when they returned, they would ensure to call."
