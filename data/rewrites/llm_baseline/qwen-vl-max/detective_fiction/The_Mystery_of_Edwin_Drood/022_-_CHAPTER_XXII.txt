Mr. Tartar’s chambers were the most immaculate, orderly, and perfectly arranged rooms ever seen under the sun, moon, or stars. The floors gleamed so brightly they seemed scrubbed clean of all earthly stains—so polished that one might have thought the London blacking trade had been abolished for good. Every piece of brass in his possession shone like a mirror, reflecting light with such precision it could almost be used to signal ships at sea. Not a speck, spot, or smear marred any surface; even his household trinkets—large, small, or medium—were kept in flawless condition. His sitting room resembled an admiral’s cabin, his bathroom a dairy, and his bedroom, lined with lockers and drawers, looked like a well-stocked seedsman’s shop. In the center stood his cot, balanced just so, as if it breathed softly with every shift of air.

Everything in Mr. Tartar’s world had its place: maps and charts in their own section, books neatly aligned, brushes hung precisely, boots stored in their designated nook, clothes folded with military care, medicine bottles tucked away, telescopes and instruments each in their assigned compartment. Nothing was out of reach. Shelves, brackets, hooks, and drawers were designed not only for convenience but also for maximizing space—every inch cleverly utilized, every object placed where it fit best. His silverware sat on the sideboard in perfect order, so exact that a misplaced salt spoon would have stood out like a crime. His toiletries on the dressing table were arranged with such precision that a toothpick left askew would have drawn immediate notice. Even his collection of curiosities—birds, fish, reptiles, arms, garments, shells, seaweed, coral fragments—was preserved and displayed according to type and origin, each in its ideal position. Paint and varnish were kept hidden, always ready to erase any accidental smudge. No ship ever sailed more cleanly than these chambers were kept.

On this bright summer day, a neat awning stretched over Mr. Tartar’s flower garden, rigged with nautical precision. The whole scene carried a distinct sea-going atmosphere—so complete that the garden might have belonged to the stern windows of a vessel at sea, and the entire residence might have set sail at any moment if Mr. Tartar had simply picked up the speaking trumpet slung in the corner and bellowed orders to “heave the anchor up” and “get all sail upon her!”

And Mr. Tartar himself, hosting this maritime retreat, matched the setting perfectly. When a man is deeply devoted to a hobby that never disappoints him, there's something charming about seeing him enjoy it with humor and pride. If he’s naturally warm-hearted, genuine, and full of life, then few things are more pleasing than watching him revel in his passion. Rosa, though she hadn’t yet been formally introduced to the role of First Lady of the Admiralty—or perhaps First Fairy of the Sea—would have found it delightful to see Mr. Tartar half-laughing at his inventions, half-delighted by them. She would have thought it especially striking when, after showing her around, he stepped back from his admiral’s cabin, politely inviting her to consider herself its queen, and waved her free into the garden with the same hand that once held Mr. Crisparkle’s life in balance.

“Helena! Helena Landless! Are you there?”

“Who speaks? Not Rosa?” Then another face appeared—handsome, calm, and familiar.

“Yes, my darling!”

“Why, how did you come here, dearest?”

“I—I don’t quite know,” Rosa said, blushing. “Unless I’m dreaming.”

Why blush? Because the two of them were alone among the flowers. Were blushes among the harvest of the magic beanstalk?

“I am not dreaming,” Helena replied, smiling. “I’d take more for granted if I were. How did we end up together—so near, so suddenly?”

Indeed, unexpectedly, amidst the dull gables and chimneys of P. J. T.’s connection, and the blooms born from saltwater soil. But Rosa, coming to, quickly explained how they came to meet, and all the circumstances behind it.

“And Mr. Crisparkle is here,” Rosa added, finishing swiftly. “And do you believe it? He was saved long ago!”

“I could believe anything of Mr. Crisparkle,” Helena answered, her cheeks warming.

(More blushes bloomed in the land of the beanstalk.)

“But it wasn’t Crisparkle,” Rosa corrected quickly.

“I don’t understand, love.”

“It was very kind of Mr. Crisparkle to be saved,” Rosa said, “and he couldn’t have shown more respect for Mr. Tartar. But it was Mr. Tartar who saved him.”

Helena’s dark eyes studied Rosa’s bright face through the leaves. “Is Mr. Tartar with you now, dear?”

“No,” Rosa replied. “He’s given up his rooms to me—to us, I mean. It’s such a beautiful place!”

“Is it?”

“It’s like the inside of the most exquisite ship ever built. Like—like—”

“Like a dream?” Helena suggested.

Rosa nodded slightly and inhaled the scent of the flowers.

After a brief silence, during which Rosa fancied Helena seemed to feel pity for someone unseen, Helena spoke again: “My poor Neville is reading in his room—the sun is too bright on this side right now. I think he should not know you’re so close.”

“Oh, I agree!” Rosa exclaimed at once.

“I suppose,” Helena continued, hesitating, “he’ll have to learn everything you’ve told me eventually—but I’m not certain. Ask Mr. Crisparkle what he thinks. Ask him whether I may tell Neville as much or as little as I choose.”

Rosa retreated into her private chamber and relayed the question. The Minor Canon advised full trust in Helena’s judgment.

“I thank him very much,” Helena said when Rosa returned. “Now ask him whether it would be better to wait until further signs of harm toward Neville appear—such as threats or harassment from this man—or to try to uncover any secret schemes already underway?”

The Minor Canon struggled to offer a confident opinion. After several failed attempts, he suggested consulting Mr. Grewgious. Helena agreed, and he made his way across the quadrangle to P. J. T.’s house—pretending, rather poorly, to be casually strolling. He delivered the request. Mr. Grewgious responded firmly: if you can catch a brigand or wild beast off guard, you should. And he declared without hesitation that John Jasper was both.

With this guidance, Mr. Crisparkle returned to Rosa, who passed it on to Helena. She sat quietly at her window, deep in thought.

“We can count on Mr. Tartar’s help, Rosa?” she asked.

Oh yes, Rosa believed so. Oh yes, she felt sure she could almost guarantee it. But should she ask Mr. Crisparkle? “Your word on the matter is as good as his, my dear,” Helena said calmly. “You needn’t go again.”

Odd, that Helena should say so.

“You see, Neville,” Helena continued after a pause, “knows no one else here. He hasn’t spoken to anyone. If Mr. Tartar would visit him openly, often—even daily—something might come of it.”

“Something might come of it?” Rosa repeated, looking at her friend with puzzled wonder. “Something might?”

“If Neville is truly being watched—and if the goal is to isolate him, to wear down his spirit grain by grain—as you suggested—is it not likely,” Helena asked, “that his enemy would warn Mr. Tartar to stay away? In that case, we wouldn’t just learn of the threat—we might learn exactly what was said.”

“I see!” Rosa exclaimed, and darted back into her chamber.

Soon her face reappeared, flushed and eager. She reported that she had told Mr. Crisparkle, who had brought Mr. Tartar, and that Mr. Tartar—“who is waiting now, in case you want him,” she added, glancing back between the cabin and the garden—had pledged his full cooperation and intended to begin immediately.

“I thank him from my heart,” Helena said. “Please tell him so.”

Again, Rosa hesitated, torn between the garden and the cabin, delivering her message with a flutter of nerves. She returned with renewed assurances from Mr. Tartar, standing uncertainly between Helena and him—a confusion not awkward, but oddly graceful.

“And now, darling,” Helena said, “we must remember our caution. We’ll part for now. I hear Neville moving. Are you going back?”

“To Miss Twinkleton’s?” Rosa asked.

“Oh, I could never return there,” Rosa said, shaking her head. “Not after that terrible meeting!”

“Then where will you go, sweet one?”

“Now that I think of it,” Rosa admitted, “I don’t know. I haven’t decided anything. But my guardian will take care of me. Don’t worry, dear. I’ll be somewhere.”

(It did seem likely.)

“And I’ll hear from my Rosebud through Mr. Tartar?” Helena asked.

“Yes, I suppose so—through—” Rosa glanced back, flustered, unable to finish. “But before we part, tell me one thing, dearest Helena. Tell me—you’re sure, absolutely sure, I couldn’t help it.”

“Help it, love?”

“Help making him malicious and vengeful. I couldn’t talk to him, could I?”

“You know how much I love you,” Helena replied, voice rising with emotion. “But I’d rather see you dead at his wicked feet than betray your soul.”

“That means a great deal to me,” Rosa said. “And you’ll tell your brother so? You’ll give him my thoughts and sympathy? And ask him not to hate me?”

With a slow, sorrowful shake of her head—almost as if the plea were unnecessary—Helena kissed her hands tenderly, and Rosa kissed hers in return. Then, a third hand—a brown one—appeared among the foliage and gently guided her friend from sight.

Later, in the Admiral’s Cabin, Mr. Tartar opened a locker and pulled out a drawer with a single touch. Instantly, a dazzling feast appeared: shimmering macaroons, sparkling liqueurs, exotic spices preserved like jewels, and jellies made from fruits of distant lands. It was a magical repast, conjured from nowhere. But time refused to stand still. With relentless speed, it dragged Rosa back to earth—from the enchanted garden to her guardian’s chambers.

“And now, my dear,” Mr. Grewgious said, “what shall we do next? Or, to put it another way—what shall we do with you?”

Rosa could only look apologetically, aware she was in everyone’s way. A fleeting idea crossed her mind—living fireproof, high above the city in Furnival’s Inn for the rest of her days. That was all she had.

“It has occurred to me,” Mr. Grewgious said, “that Miss Twinkleton occasionally visits London during term breaks, seeking connections and meetings with parents in town. Might we invite her to stay with you for a month—until we can plan better?”

“Stay where, sir?”

“Could we take a furnished lodging in town for a month, and invite Miss Twinkleton to care for you during that time?”

“And afterward?” Rosa asked.

“And afterward,” Mr. Grewgious replied, “we’d be no worse off than we are now.”

“I suppose that could smooth things,” Rosa agreed.

“Then let us go find one,” Mr. Grewgious said, rising. “Nothing would please me more than to have your sweet presence every evening for the rest of my life—but this isn’t suitable for a young lady. Let’s seek adventure and find a lodging. Meanwhile, Mr. Crisparkle, returning home, will kindly speak to Miss Twinkleton and invite her to join us.”

Mr. Crisparkle accepted and left. Mr. Grewgious and Rosa began their search.

His method of inspecting lodgings involved standing across the street from a house with a sign, staring at it; then circling to the back and staring again; then refusing to enter. They moved slowly. Eventually, he remembered a distant cousin of Mr. Bazzard’s—once a lodger in need of influence—who lived in Southampton Street, Bloomsbury Square. Her name, emblazoned in bold letters on a brass plate, was BILLICKIN.

Mrs. Billickin was faint, frail, and astonishingly honest. She emerged from her private back parlour as if summoned from a trance, her breath shallow, her movements languid.

“I hope I see you well, sir,” she said, bowing slightly.

“Quite well, thank you. And you, ma'am?”

“I am as well,” she replied, nearly gasping, “as I hever ham.”

“My ward and an elderly lady wish to rent a modest lodging for a month or so. Do you have anything available?”

“Mr. Grewgious,” she said solemnly, “I will not deceive you. I _have_ apartments.”

She added silently: *Burn me at the stake, but I will not lie.*

“And what are they, ma'am?” he asked, trying to ease the tension.

“There is this sitting-room—which, call it what you will, is the front parlour, Miss,” she said, drawing Rosa into the conversation. “The back parlour is mine, and I never part with it. And there are two bedrooms at the top of the house, with gas laid on. I won’t pretend your floorboards are firm—they aren’t. The gasfitter himself said to make them solid, he’d have to go under your joists, and that would cost too much for a yearly tenant. So the pipes run above the joists. Best you know that.”

Mr. Grewgious and Rosa exchanged glances—worried, though they didn’t grasp the danger.

“Well,” he said, regaining composure, “the roof is sound, no doubt?”

“Mr. Grewgious,” she replied, “if I told you that having nothing above you means having a floor above you, I’d be deceiving you—and I won’t. No, sir. Your slates WILL rattle loose in wind, no matter what you do! I defy you, sir, to keep them tight. Try as you may, they’ll move.”

She paused, cooling slightly, as if fearing she’d overpowered him.

“Consequently,” she continued, softer but still firm, “it would be worse than useless for me to escort you upstairs, only for you to point at the ceiling and say, ‘Mrs. Billickin, what stain do I see?’ and for me to reply, ‘I don’t understand.’ No, sir. I understand before you speak. It’s the wet. It comes in. It doesn’t. You may live dry for half your life—but the time will come. And it’s best you know it now. A dripping sop would be no name for it.”

Mr. Grewgious looked embarrassed, caught in a future disaster.

“Any other rooms, ma'am?”

“Mr. Grewgious,” she said gravely, “I have. You ask, and I answer: I have. The first and second floors are vacant and sweet.”

“Ah,” he said, reassured, “then there’s nothing wrong with those?”

“Mr. Grewgious,” she replied, turning to him with formality, “there is the stairs. Unless your mind is prepared for them, disappointment is inevitable. You cannot, Miss,” she addressed Rosa reproachfully, “place a first floor—or a second—on the same level as a parlour. No, you cannot. It’s beyond your power. Why try?”

She phrased it as if Rosa had stubbornly insisted on the impossible.

“May we see them, ma'am?”

“Mr. Grewgious,” she said, “you may. I will not hide it from you. You may.”

She sent for her shawl—according to ancient custom, she could never leave her home without being wrapped—and led them up. She paused frequently on the stairs, clutching her chest as if her heart might escape.

“And the second floor?” Mr. Grewgious asked, satisfied with the first.

“Mr. Grewgious,” she replied, turning sharply, “the second floor is over this.”

“Can we see it too?”

“Yes, sir,” she said. “It is open as the day.”

It proved satisfactory. Mr. Grewgious stepped aside with Rosa to discuss terms, then requested pen and ink and drafted a short agreement. Mrs. Billickin took a seat and delivered a detailed summary:

“Five-and-forty shillings per week, payable monthly, is fair for both parties. It’s not Bond Street nor St. James’s Palace—but it’s not claimed to be. Nor is it denied—that the Arching leads to a mews. Mews must exist. Attendance: two servants, paid well. There have been disputes with tradesmen, but dirty shoes on fresh hearth-stoning were blamed—not commissions. Coals are either _by_ the fire or _per_ the scuttle.” She emphasized the prepositions, as if they meant worlds.

“Dogs are not welcome. Besides litter, they get stolen, and suspicion spreads. Unpleasantness follows.”

By now, Mr. Grewgious had signed the contract and handed over earnest money.

“I’ve signed for the ladies, ma'am,” he said. “Kindly sign for yourself—Christian name and surname—here.”

“No, sir,” Mrs. Billickin said, bursting into candor again. “You must excuse the Christian name.”

Mr. Grewgious stared.

“The door-plate is a protection,” she explained. “It works, and I won’t remove it.”

He turned to Rosa.

“No, Mr. Grewgious,” she said, “I must refuse. As long as this house remains unknown—Billickin’s, indistinct—no one knows where I hide. Near the door or down the alley, what size or weight I am. I feel safe. But if I commit myself to a single female signature—no, Miss! Nor would you, for a moment, wish to exploit your sex in such a way, if you weren’t forced by thoughtless example.”

Rosa flushed, as if she’d attempted some grave betrayal. She urged Mr. Grewgious to accept any signature. So, in grand baronial fashion, the sign-manual **BILLICKIN** was appended.

Details were settled for taking possession the day after next, when Miss Twinkleton might arrive. Rosa returned to Furnival’s Inn on her guardian’s arm.

And there—walking up and down the inn, checking himself as he saw them approach—was Mr. Tartar.

“It occurred to me,” he said, stepping forward, “that we might go up the river. The weather is lovely, and the tide favors us. I have a boat at the Temple Stairs.”

“I haven’t been up the river in many years,” Mr. Grewgious said, tempted.

“I’ve never been,” Rosa added.

And so, beneath the summer sky, the trio prepared to set forth—not on a ship, but on a quiet river journey—where secrets might float up like bubbles from the deep.
