The news had preceded us. By the time we arrived, the household was in chaos. As we passed Lady Verinder’s chamber, the door burst open from within. My mistress emerged, wild with distress, Mr. Franklin close behind her, struggling in vain to calm her. “You are responsible for this!” she shrieked, pointing a trembling hand at Sergeant Cuff. “Gabriel! Give that man his money—and let me be rid of him!”

Only the Sergeant remained composed—indeed, he was the sole figure among us who could meet such a storm with steady nerve. “I am no more accountable for this tragedy, my lady,” he replied, voice firm yet respectful, “than you are. If, within half an hour, you still demand I leave, I will accept your dismissal—but not your payment.”

His words carried weight. They silenced her fury and struck a chord in me as well. She allowed Mr. Franklin to guide her back into the room. The door closed behind them, leaving the rest of us standing in uneasy silence.

Sergeant Cuff turned his sharp gaze upon the servants. All were pale with fear—except Penelope. She stood apart, weeping quietly. He approached her gently. “When your father has changed into dry clothes,” he said, “come to my room. I wish to speak with you.”

By the time the half-hour passed, I had dressed myself and provided the Sergeant with suitable garments. Penelope came then, her face still damp with tears. At that moment, I felt a deeper pride in her than ever before. I pulled her onto my lap and held her close. “God bless you, my child,” I whispered. She buried her head against me, arms wrapped tightly around my neck. We sat together in quiet grief. I believe both she and I were thinking of Rosanna—the poor girl whose life had ended so tragically.

The Sergeant stood by the window, watching the rain fall. I felt compelled to thank him—for considering us, for giving us space to mourn. It is a privilege reserved for those above us: to grieve openly, to feel without restraint. We of the lower ranks have no such luxury. Necessity spares the rich; it shows no mercy to us. We learn to lock our sorrows away, to carry on with duty, no matter how heavy the heart. I do not resent this—I merely observe it.

When Penelope was ready, we prepared to meet the Sergeant. He asked her if she knew why Rosanna had taken her own life. Without hesitation, she answered, “It was because of Mr. Franklin Blake.” Then he questioned whether she had shared this belief with anyone else. “No,” she said softly, “for Rosanna’s sake.”

I added, “And for Mr. Franklin’s sake too. If Rosanna truly died for love of him, it was not through his fault or knowledge. Let him go today—if he must—without learning the truth. Spare him that pain.”

The Sergeant nodded once. “Quite right.” He fell silent again, his mind clearly weighing her words against some hidden conviction of his own.

At the end of the half-hour, Lady Verinder’s bell rang. On my way to answer it, I met Mr. Franklin stepping out of his aunt’s sitting-room. He informed me that she wished to see the Sergeant—this time with me present, as before—and added that he himself had a few words to say privately to the officer first.

As we walked back toward my room, he paused to glance at the railway timetable in the hall. “Are you truly leaving us, sir?” I asked. “Miss Rachel will recover, given time.”

“She will recover,” he replied, “only when she knows I have gone—when she understands she will never see me again.”

At first, I thought he spoke in anger over her treatment of him. But it was not anger. It was sorrow—deep and clear. From the moment the police arrived, Lady Verinder had reacted violently whenever his name was mentioned. He had loved her too deeply to admit it, until the truth became undeniable—when she fled to her aunt. Once his eyes were opened to that cruel reality, he made his decision. A man of spirit can only choose one path: to withdraw.

He spoke to the Sergeant in my presence. He told him that Lady Verinder acknowledged she had spoken rashly. Then he asked: would the Sergeant agree to accept his fee and abandon the investigation?

“No, sir,” the Sergeant replied firmly. “My fee is paid for fulfilling my duty. I will not take it until that duty is complete.”

“I don’t understand,” said Mr. Franklin.

“I’ll explain,” the Sergeant continued. “When I came here, I promised to uncover what happened to the Diamond. I am now ready to fulfill that promise. I will present the facts to Lady Verinder plainly. I will tell her exactly what steps must be taken to recover the Moonstone. Then the burden will be hers. She may choose whether I continue or not. But I will have done what I undertook. Only then will I accept payment.”

In those words, he reminded us all that even a detective must honor his word—not just for justice, but for his own dignity.

There was nothing left to argue. As I rose to escort him to Lady Verinder’s room, he asked if Mr. Franklin wished to attend. “Not unless Lady Verinder insists,” he replied.

Then, in a low whisper as I followed the Sergeant out, he said, “I know what he’s going to say about Rachel. And I love her too much to hear it and keep my temper. Leave me alone.”

I found him leaning against the window sill, face in his hands, utterly broken. Penelope stood at the door, peering in, longing to comfort him. In his place, I would have called her in. When one woman wrongs you, there is great solace in confiding in another—because nine times out of ten, she will stand by you. Perhaps, when I turned away, he did summon her? If so, I can only affirm that my daughter would have spared nothing to ease his pain.

Meanwhile, Sergeant Cuff and I entered Lady Verinder’s room. The last time we had seen her, she had been unable to lift her eyes from the book on her table. This time, she met the Sergeant’s gaze with equal steadiness. Her face was set—every line showing the strength of her family pride. I knew then that the Sergeant would face a formidable opponent. For when a woman like my mistress is prepared to hear the worst, she is ready to meet it head-on.
