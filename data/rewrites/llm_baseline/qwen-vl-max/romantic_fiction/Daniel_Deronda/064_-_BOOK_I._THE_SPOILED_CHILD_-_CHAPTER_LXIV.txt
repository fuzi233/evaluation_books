CHAPTER LXIV. “Questa montagna è tale, Che sempre al cominciar di sotto è grave. E quanto uom più va su e men fa male.” —DANTE: _Il Purgatorio_.

It had not been long since her mother arrived that Gwendolen agreed to stay in Genoa. The thought of leaving behind the glittering sea and its dazzling beauty stirred something deep within her—a quiet strength she hadn’t known she possessed. For even the most idyllic place can become a prison when the heart is burdened; no matter how lovely the landscape, if our inner world is aflame, we walk through paradise like exiles.

“I’ll never look at the Mediterranean again,” Gwendolen said softly to her mother, whose eyes held a gentle understanding—though she never spoke of the husband who was no more. Mrs. Davilow, though she outwardly mourned the tragedy of her daughter’s life, found herself quietly rejoicing in this unexpected return. Her child was back—not just with love, but with a newfound tenderness toward her presence, as if she’d nearly lost her forever and now clung to every moment.

“Are you there, mamma?” Gwendolen called out one night, her voice small and trembling, just as she might have done as a child afraid in the dark. A bed had been made for her mother beside hers, and their closeness felt sacred.

“Yes, dear,” came the reply. “Can I do anything?”

“No,” Gwendolen whispered, “just knowing you’re here makes it real. Do you mind if I woke you?”

The question was new—unlike the girl she once was.
“I wasn’t asleep, darling.”

“It felt unreal,” Gwendolen murmured. “I needed to hear your voice. I can bear anything if you’re near. But don’t lie awake worrying about me. You must be happy now. Let me make you happy—at last. Or what will I do?”

“God bless you, my dear,” Mrs. Davilow said, her voice thick with emotion. “That’s the greatest happiness I could ever know—when you care for me so deeply.”

But the next night, hearing her daughter restless and sighing, Mrs. Davilow offered gently, “Let me give you your sleeping draught, Gwendolen.”

“No, mamma,” she replied firmly. “I don’t want to sleep.”

“It would help you, my love.”

“Don’t tell me what’s good for me,” Gwendolen snapped, her voice sharp with sudden frustration. “You don know what’s good for me. Uncle and you both keep trying to decide things for me, but I feel it differently. Don’t interfere.”

Mrs. Davilow said nothing. She didn’t wonder at her daughter’s mood. Then, after a pause, Gwendolen spoke again, her voice breaking: “I was always naughty to you, mamma.”

“No, dear,” she answered softly.

“Yes, I was,” Gwendolen insisted, tears rising. “It’s because I was always wicked that I’m suffering now.” And then she wept, her sobs echoing in the stillness of the room.

Her silence about the past—the marriage, the end—had only pushed her emotions into these sudden bursts of pain, half-expressed, half-hidden. Yet slowly, dimly, the truth began to dawn on her mother, carried by fragments of news from Sir Hugo to Mr. Gascoigne, and then, in part, to her. Sir Hugo, while handling the formalities around Grandcourt’s death and the possibility of his body washing ashore, decided it was kinder to speak to the rector—his old friend—about the contents of the will, before the man returned home under illusions.

He broke the news carefully, first suggesting only that the widow might not be as well provided for as expected. Only later, after hints about debts and prior obligations, did he reveal that Grandcourt had left everything to a son born outside marriage—one who now stood to inherit all, since no legitimate heir existed.

The rector was deeply wounded. He remembered now how cold and proud Grandcourt had been—how dismissive of him. He also recalled vague warnings about the young man’s reckless ways, pleasures indulged in secret, things buried beneath a polished surface. Now, those secrets were revealed: not as relics, but as living truths, crawling over the lives of decent people like insects on fresh meat.

Yet he kept his thoughts private. To Sir Hugo, he said calmly, “When a man makes a will in health, he expects to live long. Perhaps Mr. Grandcourt never imagined this would come to pass.”

After a moment, he added, “Still, it’s painful in many ways. Giving such prominence to illegitimate children may harm women’s reputations.”

“Well,” Sir Hugo replied with his usual ease, “since the boy exists, it was really the best option. Grandcourt had no closer relative. It’s chilling to think you leave life only to benefit a cousin. Men enjoy making wills when they’re thinking of their own families—but it’s a nuisance when you’re giving it all to someone you barely care for. That’s worse than having only a life interest. No—I forgive Grandcourt for that part. But what I can’t forgive is how poorly he treated his wife—our niece. Not much better than a doctor’s widow. Nothing angers me more than a man showing posthumous stinginess toward his wife. A man should take pride in providing for her. I would. I see it as a test of character—whether a man feels at peace with death knowing his wife and daughters are secure. I’ve heard of soldiers in the Crimea willing to drown if their widows were cared for.”

“It has surprised me,” the rector admitted, “especially since I, as her uncle, had trusted in Grandcourt’s generosity. I made no claims for her beforehand. That seemed right.”

“Not blamable,” Sir Hugo said. “I respect trust. But take my advice—if you marry another niece, even the Archbishop himself, bind him down. Your niece can’t remarry twice. If he’s a good man, he’ll welcome it. As for Mrs. Grandcourt… I feel closer to her now, knowing she’s been ill-used. I hope you’ll urge her to rely on me as a friend.”

And so the noble-hearted baronet expressed his outrage at the fate of the young, beautiful widow—left with two thousand pounds a year and a house in a coal-mining region.

To the rector, that income seemed less dire. Still, he felt the deeper humiliation—the public exposure of Grandcourt’s relationship with Mrs. Glasher. He saw how it would wound not only Gwendolen, but all who loved her. And like any good husband and father, he felt the shame through the eyes of women.

So when he finally told Mrs. Davilow, the discomfort was profound. He anticipated the pain in Gwendolen’s heart—and dreaded speaking of it.

But Mrs. Davilow had already begun to suspect. She now believed her daughter had known about the other woman, the children, perhaps even before the marriage. She hoped to learn more during their journey home, to prepare her gently. But Gwendolen spared her.

“I hope you don’t expect me to be rich or grand, mamma,” she said soon after the rector’s talk. “Perhaps I’ll have nothing at all.”

She sat dressed, still, lost in thought.

Mrs. Davilow was startled, but replied after a moment, “Oh, dear, you’ll have something. Sir Hugo knows the will.”

“That won’t decide,” Gwendolen said sharply. “Sir Hugo says I get two thousand a year and Gadsmere.”

“What you receive depends on what you accept,” she said. “You and Uncle mustn’t try to pressure me. I’ll do everything to make you happy—but in matters concerning my husband, I must be left alone. Is eight hundred a year enough for you?”

“More than enough,” Mrs. Davilow said quickly. “Don’t think of giving me so much.”

She paused, then asked, “Do you know who inherits the estate and the rest?”

“Yes,” Gwendolen said, waving her hand. “I know everything. It’s all right. I never want to speak of it again.”

Mrs. Davilow looked away, flushed slightly. She rose to fetch a fan-screen, unable to meet her daughter’s gaze. What agony had her child endured—what secrets remained locked inside? She could only imagine.

Gwendolen watched her closely, now sensing her mother’s distress. With softening resolve, she said, “Come sit nearer, mamma. Don’t be unhappy.”

Mrs. Davilow obeyed, biting her lips to hold back tears.

Gwendolen leaned forward, tenderly. “I mean to be wise. Truly. And good—so good to you, dear, sweet, old mamma. You won’t recognize me. Just don’t cry.”

In her heart, Gwendolen had made a decision: she would ask Deronda whether she should accept any of her husband’s money—whether she could take enough to support her mother. She felt strong enough to do anything that would raise her in his eyes. Anything.

Sir Hugo had invited them to stay at Park Lane, where he lived. He urged them to make it their home until mourning duties were settled. Town, he insisted, was quieter than rural retreats now. He would personally arrange for Gwendolen’s belongings to be removed from Grosvenor Square.

This plan suited her perfectly. At Park Lane, she could meet Deronda—if only she could find a way to send him a message.

During the journey, Sir Hugo, aware she knew the terms of the will, spoke openly about her future. He mentioned possible arrangements, suggested mild prospects, and spread a cheerful air around her widowhood. He believed it was more graceful for a woman to recover her spirits after discovering her husband hadn’t treated her generously. It was the testator’s fault if grief turned into sorrow over poverty.

Moved by kindness and sympathy, the baronet became almost paternal. He called her “my dear,” discussed Gadsmere as if it were “we” who would manage it, and praised its comfort—despite the soot.

“I wouldn’t mind the dirt myself,” he declared. “Nothing’s healthier. If your work is there, Gadsmere is a paradise. It’s even featured in Scrogg’s history of the county—with its little tower and lake. The prettiest print in the book.”

“A more important place than Offendene?” asked Mr. Gascoigne.

“Much,” Sir Hugo replied firmly. “I was there with my brother—over twenty-five years ago, but I remember it well. The rooms may not be larger, but the grounds are on a different scale.”

“Our poor dear Offendene is empty now,” Mrs. Davilow sighed. “Mr. Haynes refused it, and no one’s taken it since. I should have accepted Lord Brackenshaw’s offer to stay rent-free—just to keep it aired and warmed.”

“I hope you’ve something snug instead,” Sir Hugo said.

“A little too snug,” Mr. Gascoigne smiled, glancing at his sister-in-law.

Gwendolen turned at her mother’s words, her eyes changing. Outside, the train passed through sun-drenched fields, hedges gone, poplars marking the horizon. The conversation drifted—idle talk about coal pits, telegraph cables, church rates.

But Gwendolen’s mind wandered far beyond. She saw Offendene—the downs, the cattle, the shaded lanes, the neat hedges along the road from the parsonage. She saw the avenue, the hall door opening, her mother or one of her sisters stepping out to greet her.

A quiet home she once fled as dull now appeared as sanctuary—a place of morning breath and birdsong, after wandering through a nightmare masquerade. She had entered with intoxicating belief in illusion, and now, terrified, she saw the end: the masks falling, serpents hissing around her, and the fear that she herself had become one of them.

Offendene became a refuge in her memory. She dwelled on it, yet gave no sign. She listened without reacting to discussions of cable lines or political bills.

How wide our minds roam during a long journey! Even strangers exchange stories from China to Peru, sharing so much they risk feeling poorer in future meetings. But Sir Hugo and Mr. Gascoigne had an even stronger reason to talk—forced together in a mourning coach, they lightened the mood with endless chatter.

“I don’t mind telling you,” Sir Hugo said, confiding in the rector. And the rector, without saying so, shared details of his sons’ struggles.

Through discussing everyone near Diplow, Sir Hugo grew fond of the place again. He decided to bring his family there for a few weeks before autumn. Mr. Gascoigne welcomed the idea.

The journey ended with mutual affection between the men.

Meanwhile, Gwendolen sat apart—like one who had walked among spirits, full of a silent experience that made ordinary talk seem dreamlike. Mrs. Davilow, meanwhile, wondered what her daughter felt, and what lay behind her hesitation about accepting the inheritance.

Gwendolen had a single purpose ahead: to see Deronda. She needed his judgment—to ensure she did nothing he would disapprove of. Without him, might she have forgiven herself? Would secrecy have brought absolution?

But it was Deronda who had become her conscience—the one whose opinion shaped her joy and dread. His presence had pierced her soul, revealing truths she couldn’t face alone. He had judged her at the gaming table, and she had felt it. He could have ruined that influence—but he hadn’t.

She had forgotten to ask for his address. Her only hope was Sir Hugo.

She was not blind to what others might think—of her dependence on Deronda, of seeking him more than he sought her. Grandcourt’s scorn had taught her pride. But her nature clung to this connection. She would not let go, not for appearances, not for fear.

At Park Lane, when she learned Sir Hugo would visit the Abbey briefly, she turned to him—calmly, without hesitation, her mother present.

“Sir Hugo,” she said, “I wish to see Mr. Deronda again—soon. I don’t know his address. Could you tell me, or send him a message?”

A flicker crossed Sir Hugo’s face—but he responded smoothly, “Honestly, I don’t know if he’s at his chambers or the Abbey. But I’ll make sure. I’ll send a note now to his chambers. If he’s at the Abbey, I’ll deliver your message and send him up at once. I’m certain he’ll want to obey your wish.”

He spoke with quiet kindness—as if such a request were natural, even proper.

But inwardly, Sir Hugo was convinced Gwendolen had fallen passionately in love with Deronda—a bond formed long ago. His old suspicion returned, stronger than ever: that her feelings might lead her into imprudence. And so, despite his concern, he meant to protect her.

To him, it was a perfect romance—this brilliant woman and his favorite Dan, destined for each other. And Grandcourt? Well, he had died at just the right time.

Yet Sir Hugo was troubled by a new doubt: had Dan grown too lofty, too distant? Was there some plan in his head—some greater purpose—that would make him reject this lovely widow, even when she reached out?

It was ironic—Sir Hugo, who once warned Deronda against being too tender, now felt irritated that he might not be in love enough.

Of course, all this was premature—only a fortnight after Grandcourt’s death. But minds often rush ahead or lag behind.

Still, he sent the note. And it found Deronda at his chambers.
