The agony of waiting for bad news, however inevitable, was far less terrible than the sting of the truth itself. Elinor realized now that, despite her rational assurances, she had secretly clung to a fragile hope as long as Edward remained unmarried. She had convinced herself that fate would intervene—that some act of his own resolve, the persuasion of friends, or a sudden opening for a better match would save them from this ruin. But the marriage was done. The shock was immediate, yet it was quickly followed by a sharp condemnation of her own heart for the bitter flattery that had lingered in its depths, amplifying the pain of the revelation.

It surprised her, initially, that the wedding had taken place so swiftly, seemingly before Edward could even be ordained or possess the living. Yet, upon reflection, she understood Lucy's frantic energy. In her desperate attempt to secure Edward, she had likely overlooked every obstacle except the mere delay. They were married in town and now hurrying back to her uncle's estate at Delaford. The thought of them settling there sent a jolt through Elinor. That house, where so many threads of her past life intertwined, was a place she wished to know intimately yet desperately wanted to avoid. Her imagination instantly populated the scene: she saw Lucy bustling about as the energetic manager, balancing a desire for fashionable appearances with ruthless thrift, constantly calculating her own interests and courting the favor of Colonel Brandon and the wealthy circle around Mrs. Jennings. As for Edward, she saw nothing clear in her mind. Whether happy or wretched, his image offered her no comfort; she turned her face away from every sketch she might conjure.

Days drifted by without a letter. Elinor pinned her hopes on some connection in London sending word, but silence stretched endlessly. Though she knew no specific fault lay with anyone, she found herself blaming every absent friend for their thoughtlessness or laziness. "When do you write to Colonel Brandon, ma'am?" the question bubbled up from her restless heart, needing some movement, some sign of life.

"I wrote to him last week, my dear," her mother answered gently. "I expected to see him here rather than hear from him. I urged him to come, and I am not at all surprised if he walks in today or tomorrow." This offer of hope, however thin, became her anchor. Colonel Brandon *must* have news.

Her resolve was shattered almost before it formed. A figure on horseback appeared near the window. A gentleman stopped at the gate. Was it Colonel Brandon? If it were, she would finally know the truth. But the silhouette lacked his bearing, his height. Could it be Edward? She squinted, disbelief warring with longing. He had just dismounted. There was no mistake—it was Edward. She sank into her chair, steeling herself. "He has come from Mr. Pratt's specifically to see us," she whispered to herself. "I will remain calm. I will master myself."

Almost instantly, she sensed that the others had seen the error. She watched her mother and Marianne flush, their eyes darting to hers, lips moving in hushed confidences. Oh, how she wished to speak, to assure them that she anticipated no coldness, no slight, in their treatment of him! But words failed her, and she was forced to surrender their conduct to their own judgment.

A heavy silence fell over the room. The ticking of the clock seemed louder than ever. Finally, footsteps crunched on the gravel path, quickened as he approached. He entered the passage, and moments later stood before them.

His countenance was pale, agitated, devoid of the usual cheerfulness. He looked fearful, acutely aware that he deserved no warm welcome. Mrs. Dashwood, trusting implicitly in Elinor's guidance, forced a smile of complacency, extended her hand, and offered her congratulations. Edward blushed, stammering an incoherent response. Elinor's lips had moved in unison with her mother's, and when the gesture ended, she cursed her own compliance, wishing she had shaken his hand. Instead, she sat down abruptly and began discussing the weather, her voice a hollow mask.

Marianne had withdrawn to the shadows, hiding her distress. Margaret, sensing the gravity of the moment, sat as far from him as possible, maintaining a rigid, dignified silence.

When Elinor finally exhausted her polite chatter about the dry season, an awful stillness descended. Mrs. Dashwood broke it, tentatively asking if Mrs. Ferrars was well. "Yes," Edward replied hurriedly, his tone tight. Another pause ensued, stretching like taut wire.

Resolving to speak, though trembling, Elinor asked, "Is Mrs. Ferrars at Longstaple?"

"At Longstaple!" he exclaimed, looking surprised. "No, my mother is in town."

"I meant," Elinor continued, picking up a piece of embroidery, her voice steady despite the storm inside, "to inquire for Mrs. *Edward* Ferrars."

She dared not lift her eyes. Her mother and Marianne turned to stare at him. Edward flushed, looked bewildered, and hesitated. Then, slowly, "Perhaps you mean... my brother? You mean Mrs. *Robert* Ferrars?"

"Mrs. Robert Ferrars!" the words rang out in stunned amazement from both women. Even Elinor, unable to speak, gazed at him with wide, incredulous eyes. Rising from his seat, Edward wandered to the window, fumbling with a pair of scissors lying on the sill. With a frantic, disjointed motion, he snipped the sheath to ribbons as he spoke.

"You may not have heard," he blurted out, "that my brother is lately married—to Miss Lucy Steele."

The news struck the room like a thunderclap. Everyone gasped in unspeakable astonishment, save for Elinor. She sat slumped over her work, her head bowed, consumed by an agitation so profound she scarcely recognized her surroundings.

"Yes," he confirmed, "they were married last week. They are now at Dawlish."

Elinor could endure no longer. She sprang from her chair and fled the room. The moment the door clicked shut, the dam broke. She burst into tears, a flood of pure, unadulterated joy that seemed endless at first. Outside, Edward, having looked anywhere but at her until then, witnessed her departure. Perhaps he saw her, perhaps he heard her sobs. Whatever the case, he fell into a deep reverie, impenetrable to her mother's inquiries or kindnesses. Without a word, he left the room and walked out toward the village, leaving the Dashwoods in a state of bewildered perplexity regarding his sudden and miraculous turn of fortune.
