I reclined beneath the boughs of a great tree, watching as the children drifted away one by one, or in small, murmuring clusters, to climb into their woven nests. They possessed a peculiar rhythm to their existence; so utterly exhausted by day and so miraculously refreshed by dawn, they seemed equally content to surrender to sleep and to wake anew. Though weariness weighed upon me as well, I remained awake, my heart holding vigil. Lona had not bid me goodnight, and I was certain she would return.

The moment I beheld her again, a profound shock had struck me: her resemblance to the Princess was undeniable. There could be no doubt she was the daughter Adam had spoken of. Yet, in Lona, the dazzling, terrifying radiance of Lilith was softened by an innocent childlikeness, deepened by a tender sense of motherhood. "She is likely occupied," I murmured to myself, "with the child of the woman I met fleeing!"—a woman who, Lona had confessed, was not yet half a mother.

She arrived at last, sitting beside me. After a few moments of silent, radiant joy, expressed through the gentle stroking of my face and hands, she began to recount everything that had transpired since my departure. As we spoke, the moon rose, and occasionally, filtering through the leaves, its silver light illuminated her beautiful face—a countenance filled with thought and a care that love alone could redeem and glorify. How such a child could be born of such a mother, of such a woman and such a princess, was a mystery hard to fathom; yet, happily, she had two parents—or perhaps three. She drew my heart toward her with a quality within me that mirrored her own, and I loved her as one who, regardless of the perfection she might achieve, would forever remain a child. I knew then that I loved her even as I left her, and that the hope of reunion had been my sole solace. Every word she spoke seemed to pierce straight to my heart, purifying it like the truth itself.

She told me that after I departed the orchard valley, the Giants began to believe more firmly in the actual existence of their neighbors. Consequently, their hostility grew. Sometimes the Little Ones would witness them trampling furiously, reacting to mere shadows or imagined signs of our presence, while we stood nearby, laughing at their foolish rage. Soon, however, their animosity took a practical form: they began destroying the trees whose fruit sustained us. This drove the Mother of them all to meditate on counteraction. She sent her sharpest scouts to listen at night and learned that the Giants believed I was hidden somewhere nearby, intending to strike them down in their sleep once I had recovered my strength. She concluded that the only way to halt the destruction was to give them grounds to believe we had abandoned the place entirely. The Little Ones must migrate into the forest—beyond the Giants' reach, yet close enough to their own trees, which they would visit only by night. The plan's main flaw was that the forest lacked the dense undergrowth needed to conceal them.

But she reflected that where there were birds, there could be habitation for the Little Ones. They possessed eager sympathies for all modes of life and could learn from the wildest creatures. Why not seek refuge from the cold and their enemies in the tree-tops? Why not, having slept in the low brushwood, now seek the lofty foliage? Why not build nests where scooping hollows was impossible? All that birds could do, the Little Ones could learn—except, indeed, to fly!

She spoke to them on the subject, and they listened with approval. They could already climb the trees and had often watched birds constructing their homes! The forest trees, though massive, did not look unwelcoming. They stretched their branches high toward the sky, some even reaching down, as if inviting us to dwell with them. Perhaps, in the crown of the tallest, they might find the bird that laid baby-eggs, sat upon them until ripe, and then tumbled them down to release the little ones! Yes; they would build sleep-houses in the trees, unseen by the Giants, for never did one of the dull-bearded brutes raise his head to look upward. Thus convinced, the Giants would surely believe we had fled the country, and the Little Ones would gather their apples, pears, figs, mesples, and peaches while the giants slept.

Thus reasoned the Lovers, eagerly adopting Lona's suggestion. Soon, they were as at home in the tree-tops as the birds themselves. The Giants, finding no trace of us, concluded we had been frightened out of the land, forgot their trees, and almost ceased to believe in the existence of their small neighbors.

Lona asked if I had noticed that many of the children had grown. I replied that I had not, though I could readily believe it. She assured me it was true, but added that the certain evidence that their minds had grown alongside their bodies had gone far toward mitigating the alarm the discovery had caused her.

In the final moments of twilight, and later when the moon shone brightly, they descended to the valley to gather enough fruit to sustain them for the next day. For the Giants never ventured out in the twilight; to them, it was absolute darkness, and they hated the moon—they would have extinguished it had they been able. But soon the Little Ones discovered that fruit gathered in the night was not wholly good the following day. The question arose: would it not be better, instead of merely pretending to have left, to make the bad Giants leave themselves?

She said they had already, while exploring the forest, made acquaintance with its animals, meeting most of them personally. Knowing how strong, wise, and docile some were, and how swift and manageable others, they set themselves to secure their aid against the Giants. With loving, playful approaches, they soon made more than friends of most of them. They began by addressing horse and elephant as "Brother Horse" or "Sister Elephant," until before long they had individual names for each. It took a little longer before they called the Bears "Brother Bear" or "Sister Bear," but that followed quickly. The other day she had heard one little fellow cry, "Ah, Sister Serpent!" to a snake that bit him as he played with it too roughly. Most would have nothing to do with a caterpillar except watch it through its changes; but when it finally emerged from its retreat with wings, all would immediately address it as "Sister Butterfly," congratulating it on its metamorphosis—for which they used a word meaning something like *repentance*—evidently regarding it as something sacred.

One moonlit evening, as they went to gather fruit, they encountered a woman seated on the ground with a baby in her lap—the very woman I had met on my way to Bulika. Mistaking her for a Giantess who had stolen one of their babies (for they regarded all infants as their property), they fell upon her in a multitude, beating her in a childish yet sufficiently bewildering fashion. She would have fled, but a boy threw himself down and held her by the feet. Recovering her wits, she recognized in her assailants the children whose hospitality she sought and immediately yielded the baby. Lona appeared and carried it away in her bosom.

While the woman noted that in striking her they were careful not to hurt the child, the Little Ones observed that, as she surrendered her, she hugged and kissed the infant just as they desired to do. They concluded she must be a Giantess of the same kind as the good Giant. The moment Lona secured the baby, they brought the woman fruit and began to shower her with every sort of childish attention.

The woman, previously in perplexity as to where to go, dared not return to the city because the Princess was certain to discover who had lamed her leopardess. Delighted by the friendliness of the little people, she resolved to remain with them for the present. She would have no trouble with her infant and might find a way to return to her husband, who was rich in money and gems and seldom unkind to her.

Here I must supplement, partly from conjecture, what Lona told me about the woman. Like the rest of the inhabitants of Bulika, she was aware of the tradition that the Princess lived in terror of the birth of an infant destined to her destruction. They were all unacquainted, however, with the frightful means by which she preserved her youth and beauty. Her deteriorating physical condition requiring a larger use of those means, they took the apparent increase of her hostility toward children as a sign that she saw her doom approaching. This, although no one dreamed of any attempt against her, nourished in them hopes of change.

Now arose in the woman's mind the idea of furthering the fulfillment of the shadowy prediction, or using the myth at least for her own restoration to her husband. What seemed more probable than that the fate foretold lay with these very children? They were marvelously brave, whereas the Bulikans were cowards in abject terror of animals! If she could rouse in the Little Ones the ambition of taking the city, then, in the confusion of the attack, she would escape from the little army, reach her house unrecognized, and lie hidden there, awaiting the result!

Should the children now succeed in expelling the Giants, she would begin at once, while they were yet flushed with victory, to suggest the loftier aim! By disposition, indeed, they were unfit for warfare; they hardly ever quarreled and never fought; they loved every living thing and hated either to hurt or to suffer. Still, they were easily influenced and could certainly be taught any exercise within their strength. At once she set some of the smaller ones to throwing stones at a mark. Soon they were all engrossed with the new game, growing skilful in it.

The first practical result was their use of stones in my rescue. While gathering fruit, they found me asleep, went home, held a council, and returned the next day with their elephants and horses. They overwhelmed the few Giants watching me and carried me off. Jubilant over their victory, the smaller boys were childishly boastful, the bigger boys less ostentatious, while the girls, though their eyes flashed more, were not so talkative as usual. The woman of Bulika no doubt felt encouraged.

We talked for the greater part of the night, chiefly about the growth of the children and what it might indicate. With Lona's power of recognizing truth, I had long been familiar; now I began to be astonished at her practical wisdom. Probably, had I been more of a child myself, I should have wondered less.

It was far from morning when I became aware of a slight fluttering and scrambling. I rose on my elbow and, looking about, saw many Little Ones descend from their nests. They disappeared, and in a few moments all was still again.

"What are they doing?" I asked.

"They think," answered Lona, "that, stupid as they are, the Giants will search the wood, and they are gone to gather stones with which to receive them. Stones are not plentiful in the forest, and they have to scatter far to find enough. They will carry them to their nests and, from the trees, attack the Giants as they come within reach. Knowing their habits, they do not expect them before morning. If they do come, it will be the opening of a war of expulsion: one or the other people must go. The result, however, is hardly doubtful. We do not mean to kill them; indeed, their skulls are so thick that I do not think we could—not that killing would do them much harm; they are so little alive! If one were killed, his Giantess would not remember him beyond three days!"

"Do the children then throw so well that the thing *might* happen?" I asked.

"Wait till you see them!" she answered, with a touch of pride. "But I have not yet told you of a strange thing that happened the night before last! We had come home from gathering our fruit and were asleep in our nests when we were roused by the horrid noises of beasts fighting. The moon was bright, and in a moment our trees glittered with staring little eyes, watching two huge leopardesses—one perfectly white, the other covered with black spots—which worried and tore each other with I do not know how many teeth and claws. To judge by her back, the spotted creature must have been climbing a tree when the other sprang upon her. When first I saw them, they were just under my own tree, rolling over and over each other. I got down on the lowest branch and saw them perfectly. The children enjoyed the spectacle, siding some with this one, some with that, for we had never seen such beasts before and thought they were only at play. But by degrees their roaring and growling almost ceased, and I saw that they were in deadly earnest, and heartily wished neither might be left able to climb a tree. But when the children saw the blood pouring from their flanks and throats, what do you think they did? They scurried down to comfort them, gathering in a great crowd about the terrible creatures and beginning to pat and stroke them. Then I got down as well, for they were much too absorbed to heed my calling to them; but before I could reach them, the white one stopped fighting and sprang among them with such a hideous yell that they flew up into the trees like birds. Before I got back into mine, the wicked beasts were at it again, tooth and claw. Then Whitey had the best of it; Spotty ran away as fast as she could run, and Whitey came and lay down at the foot of my tree. But in a minute or two she was up again, walking about as if she thought Spotty might be lurking somewhere. I waked often, and every time I looked out, I saw her. In the morning she went away."

"I know both the beasts," I said. "Spotty is a bad beast. She hates the children and would kill every one of them. But Whitey loves them. She ran at them only to frighten them away, lest Spotty should get hold of any of them. No one needs be afraid of Whitey!"
