Harry Warrington awoke to the gentle light of a summer morning, the sun streaming through the window, and the kind faces of Colonel Lambert and his wife smiling at his bedside. Hunger gnawed at him, sharp and insistent, and the doctor, with a nod of approval, permitted him to eat—a bowl of chicken and rice prepared, as Mrs. Lambert proudly informed him, by one of her daughters.

One of her daughters? A fleeting image surfaced in Harry’s mind—two young women, their cheeks flushed, dark curls framing their faces, hovering near his bed before vanishing soon after he had regained his senses. Then, unbidden, came the memory of another woman—lovely, yes, but older, undeniably older, with—oh, horror! He winced, a pang of remorse striking him as he recalled the artifice beneath her charms. How long had it been since he had learned that those pearls were paste, those golden tresses mere trinkets? A lifetime ago, when he was still a boy, an innocent fool. Now he was a man—no, an old man, battered by experience. The fever, the bloodletting, the hours of fasting, the draught that had plunged him into deep, dreamless sleep—all had left him hollowed out, as though years had passed in a single night.

“What troubles you, my dear?” asked Mrs. Lambert as he flinched.

“Nothing, madam,” Harry lied smoothly. “A twinge in my shoulder. I must thank you both for your kindness.”

“We are old friends, Mr. Warrington,” she replied warmly. “My husband served with your father, and I was a girlhood companion of your mother’s. You were no stranger to us, even before your aunt and cousin told us who you were.”

“Are they here?” Harry asked, his voice betraying a flicker of unease.

“They must have stayed at Tunbridge Wells last night. A rider was sent from Reigate yesterday to inquire after you.”

“Ah,” Harry murmured, glancing at his bandaged arm.

“I’ve made a fine recovery of you,” declared the doctor. “Now Mrs. Lambert and the cook shall take charge.”

“Nay, Theo prepared the chicken herself,” corrected Mrs. Lambert.

“Theo?” Harry repeated, curiosity stirring.

“Our eldest,” the Colonel explained, grinning. “She has mended your coat, cut roses for your room, and, if I’m not mistaken, sang you to sleep last night.”

“I thought I dreamed the hymn,” Harry admitted.

“No dream. Though your man attempted to join in from the kitchen, with rather more enthusiasm than melody.”

Harry laughed, but the mention of home—of his grandfather, who had loved that hymn, of his brother, now gone—tightened his throat. He swallowed the sudden ache.

Colonel Lambert, observing the shift in his guest’s mood, steered the conversation elsewhere. “You’ll find no Tories under this roof, Mr. Warrington. Our ancestors fought for Cromwell, and though we’ve softened into good Churchmen, we’ve no love for Jacobites.”

Harry’s chin lifted. “The Esmonds were loyal to the rightful king. Had we been home twenty years ago, my brother and I would have staked our heads for James.”

“Best keep them on your shoulders,” Lambert said dryly. “Temple Bar’s spikes are no pleasant sight.”

Harry’s jaw set. “I’d salute them still, even with the court looking on.”

Lambert’s expression turned thoughtful. “Then you’d best guard such sentiments. Your aunt, the Baroness, has no patience for romantic treason. She’s maneuvering her nephews into the king’s favor, and I doubt she’d welcome a rebel in her household.”

Harry flushed. At Castlewood, such talk had flowed freely. Here, it seemed, even walls had ears.

Soon after, he rose with Gumbo’s assistance—not without threatening the servant another thrashing if he continued to gossip. (Gumbo swore solemn obedience, though Harry suspected the vow would last only until the next audience in the kitchen.)

Dressed in borrowed linen—his own finery lost to his aunt’s departing carriage—Harry was led through Lambert’s home: a sturdy brick house flanked by heraldic beasts, its halls lined with pikes and muskets from wars long past. Portraits of grim-faced Roundheads glowered from the walls.

“Our ancestors,” Lambert said, noting Harry’s frown. “We were rebels once. Now we’re peaceful enough—though my son means to take orders, and my womenfolk are staunch for the Church. But come, you must meet them properly.”

He ushered Harry into the parlor, where the Lambert daughters stood to greet him. Miss Hetty, bright-eyed and impudent, dipped a cheeky curtsey. Miss Theo—the broth-maker, the rose-gatherer, the singer of hymns—blushed furiously as her father introduced her.

Harry, no connoisseur of beauty, still noted the warmth of her smile, the frank kindness in her eyes. She was no ethereal creature, but sturdy and lively, her arms sun-kissed, her laughter easy.

“And which great author do you favor, Mr. Warrington?” Lambert asked, mischief glinting in his gaze.

Harry hesitated. “I fear my book-learning is poor, sir. My brother was the scholar. I know horses better than Homer.”

Lambert clapped him on the back. “Honesty’s worth more than a library. But tell me—what do you know of Prussia’s cavalry?”

Harry’s face lit with eager interest. Campaigns, kings, the clash of armies—here was a language he understood.

And yet, as he spoke, he caught Theo’s glance, swift and unreadable, before she looked away.
