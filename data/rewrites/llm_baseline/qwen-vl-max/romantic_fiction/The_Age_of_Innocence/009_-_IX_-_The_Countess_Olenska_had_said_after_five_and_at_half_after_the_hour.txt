IX. The Countess Olenska had said “after five”; and at half past the hour, Newland Archer rang the bell of a modest house on West Twenty-third Street—its stucco peeling, its cast-iron balcony strangled by a wild wisteria vine. It was an odd place to settle in, far removed from the polished avenues of society. Small dressmakers, taxidermists, and writers—people who lived quietly, almost invisibly—were her neighbors. Down the untidy street, Archer recognized a weathered wooden cottage where Winsett, a journalist he sometimes met, once claimed to live. Winsett never invited guests, but once, during a late-night walk, he’d pointed it out with pride. Archer had shivered then, wondering if this was how the intellectual world lived elsewhere. Madame Olenska’s home was no grander than the rest—only slightly better kept, with a bit more paint around the windows. As he stood before it, he couldn’t help thinking that the Polish count must have stripped her not just of fortune, but of illusion as well.

The day had been heavy. He’d lunched with the Wellands, hoping afterward to take May for a quiet stroll in the park. He wanted to be alone with her—to tell her how radiant she’d looked the night before, how proud he was of her, and how he wished they could marry sooner. But Mrs. Welland had reminded him firmly that family visits were only halfway done. When he suggested moving up the wedding date, she’d raised her eyebrows and sighed: “Twelve dozen of everything—hand-embroidered—” They’d rolled through one household after another in the family landau, like exhibits in a polite parade. By the time they parted, Archer felt like a specimen on display—caged, admired, and passed along. He supposed his anthropology readings made him see things too plainly, but when he thought about the wedding being delayed until autumn, a cold weight settled in his chest. “Tomorrow,” Mrs. Welland called after him, “we’ll do the Chiverses and the Dallases.” He realized then that their social rounds were being followed alphabetically—and they were barely through the first quarter.

He’d meant to tell May about the Countess’s request—that she’d actually *commanded* him to call. But in their brief moments together, other things had mattered more. Besides, it seemed absurd to bring it up. May had always wanted him to be kind to her cousin; hadn’t that very wish helped hasten their engagement? It struck him now that, without Ellen Olenska’s arrival, he might still have been free—or at least less bound. But May had chosen this path, and somehow, that freed him from further obligation. So here he was, standing at her door, unannounced, uninvited into her world.

Curiosity gripped him. The way she’d summoned him—so casually, so directly—suggested a mind sharper than her gentle demeanor implied. The door opened to a dark-haired maid, thick-boned and foreign-looking, wearing a bright scarf over her neck. She smiled broadly, showing white teeth, and gestured toward the inside with a shrug. Her eyes didn’t meet his, nor did she understand his questions. She led him through a narrow hall into a low-ceilinged drawing room lit only by a fire. The room was empty. She left him there, and for a moment he wondered if she’d gone to fetch the Countess—or if she thought he’d come to wind the clock, which sat stopped on the mantel.

He remembered reading that southern people communicated in silence, in gesture and expression. But her movements—her nods, her smiles—meant nothing to him. He felt foolish. Then she returned with a lamp. He managed to cobble together a phrase from Dante and Petrarch, and she responded: “La signora e fuori; ma verra subito.” He took it to mean: “She’s out—but she’ll come soon.”

With the light now, he saw the room clearly. It was unlike any space he’d ever entered. Faded, yes, but full of quiet charm. The Countess had brought fragments of her old life—“bits of wreckage,” she’d called them. A few slender dark tables, a delicate Greek bronze on the mantle, a stretch of red damask nailed over stained wallpaper behind two Italian-style paintings in aged frames. Archer prided himself on knowing art. He’d read Ruskin as a boy, Symonds, Vernon Lee, Hamerton, Pater’s *The Renaissance*. He spoke of Botticelli with ease, mentioned Fra Angelico with mild condescension. Yet these pictures unsettled him. They weren’t like anything he’d seen in Italy—perhaps because he wasn’t seeing them with the eyes of a traveler, but of a man caught in a strange house, waiting for a woman who might not come.

He regretted not telling May. What if she came? What would she think of him sitting alone in the dusk, as if already at home? But he’d come. He meant to wait.

He sank into a chair, stretched his feet toward the logs. The atmosphere was unlike any he’d known—quiet, intimate, charged with something foreign. This shabby rented house, surrounded by pampas grass and Rogers statuettes, had been transformed by touch and taste. He tried to decipher the magic: the way chairs were grouped, the single pair of Jacqueminot roses in a thin vase—never bought singly, yet here, just two. And the scent—something beyond perfume, like a distant bazaar, Turkish coffee, ambergris, dried roses.

His thoughts drifted to May’s future home. Mr. Welland had already picked a new house on East Thirty-ninth Street—greenish-yellow stone, modern, built by young architects rebelling against brownstone. The plumbing was perfect. Archer had hoped to travel, delay the decision. But the Wellands approved of a long European honeymoon—maybe even Egypt—but insisted on a house for the couple upon return. His fate was sealed. Every evening, he’d climb the iron railings, step into a Pompeian vestibule, pass through yellow wood paneling into a hall. Above, a bay window in the drawing room—he couldn’t picture what May would do with it. She accepted the purple satin and gold tufting of her parents’ room, the fake Buhl tables, the gilt cabinets filled with Saxe china. He imagined she’d want the same. His only comfort: she’d probably let him arrange his library—plain bookcases, no glass doors, “sincere” Eastlake furniture.

The maid returned, drew the curtains, pushed a log, and said softly: “Verra—verra.” Then she left.

Archer rose and began pacing. Should he stay? His presence felt increasingly absurd. Had he misunderstood? Was she not expecting him?

A sound echoed down the street—hooves on cobblestones. A carriage stopped. He pulled back the curtain. In the dim glow of a streetlamp, he saw Julius Beaufort’s compact English brougham, drawn by a roan horse. The banker stepped out, helping a woman from the vehicle. It was the Countess.

Beaufort stood, hat in hand, speaking. She shook her head. They exchanged a handshake. He climbed back into the carriage. She walked up the steps.

When she entered, she showed no surprise at finding him there. Surprise was not part of her nature.

“How do you like my funny house?” she asked.

“To me, it’s like heaven,” he replied.

She untied her velvet bonnet, tossed it aside with her cloak, and looked at him with thoughtful eyes. “You’ve arranged it delightfully,” he added, aware of how flat it sounded—but trapped by convention, desperate to say something true.

“Oh, it’s a poor little place. My relations despise it. But at least it’s less gloomy than the van der Luydens’.”

The words stunned him. Few dared speak ill of the van der Luydens’ stately home. Those privileged to enter called it “handsome”—though they trembled within. But suddenly, he was glad she’d said it. She’d named the fear everyone felt.

“It’s delicious—what you’ve done here,” he repeated.

“I like the little house,” she admitted. “But I suppose what I really love is the blessedness of being here—in my own country, my own town—and being alone.”

Her voice was so soft he barely heard the last part. But awkwardly, he picked it up.

“You like being alone?”

“Yes. As long as my friends keep me from feeling lonely.”

She sat near the fire. “Nastasia will bring tea soon,” she said, gesturing for him to return to his chair. “I see you’ve already chosen your corner.”

Leaning back, she folded her arms behind her head, gazing into the flames beneath lowered lids. “This is the hour I like best—don’t you?”

A sense of propriety made him answer: “I was afraid you’d forgotten the hour. Beaufort must have been very engrossing.”

She smiled. “Why—have you waited long? Mr. Beaufort took me to see several houses—since it seems I’m not allowed to stay in this one.”

She dismissed both men—Beaufort and him—and continued: “I’ve never been in a city where there’s such a resistance to living in _des quartiers excentriques_. What does it matter where one lives? I’m told this street is respectable.”

“It’s not fashionable.”

“Fashionable! Do you all care so much about that? Why not make your own fashions? But I suppose I’ve lived too independently. At any rate, I want to do what you all do—I want to feel cared for, safe.”

He was touched—as he had been the night before when she spoke of needing guidance. “That’s what your friends want,” he said. “New York’s an awfully safe place,” he added, with a flash of irony.

“Yes, isn’t it? One feels that,” she exclaimed, missing the sarcasm. “Being here is like—like being taken on a holiday when one has been a good little girl and done all one’s lessons.”

The comparison was meant kindly, but it grated. He didn’t mind mocking New York himself—but hearing others treat it lightly annoyed him. He wondered if she truly grasped how powerful it was, how nearly it had crushed her. The Lovell Mingotts’ dinner, hastily patched together from scraps of society, should have shown her how close she’d come to disaster. But either she hadn’t realized it, or had forgotten in the triumph of the van der Luyden evening. Archer leaned toward the first. He thought her vision of New York still undifferentiated—and that thought irritated him.

“Last night,” he said, “New York laid itself out for you. The van der Luydens do nothing by halves.”

“No—how kind they are! It was such a nice party. Everyone seems to hold them in such high esteem.”

Her words were too mild. She might have spoken of a tea party at the dear Miss Lannings’.

“The van der Luydens,” Archer said, feeling pompous, “are the most powerful influence in New York society. Unfortunately—because of her health—they receive very seldom.”

She unclasped her hands, looked at him thoughtfully. “Isn’t that perhaps the reason?”

“The reason—?”

“For their great influence. That they make themselves so rare.”

He flushed, stared—then understood. With one sentence, she’d undone their power. He laughed, and let them fall.

Nastasia brought tea—Japanese cups without handles, small covered dishes—placing the tray on a low table.

“But you’ll explain these things to me,” Madame Olenska continued, leaning forward to hand him his cup. “You’ll tell me all I ought to know.”

“It’s you who are teaching me,” he said. “Opening my eyes to things I’ve looked at so long I’ve ceased to see them.”

She detached a small gold cigarette case from her bracelet, offered it to him, then lit one herself. On the mantle were long spills for lighting.

“Ah—then we can both help each other. But I need help so much more. You must tell me exactly what to do.”

It was on the tip of his tongue to say: “Don’t be seen driving with Beaufort—” But he was too deep in the room’s spell, in her world. To give such advice would be like warning someone bargaining for attar of roses in Samarkand to pack arctics for winter. New York seemed farther away than Samarkand. And if they were to help each other, she was already doing the first service—making him see his own city from the outside.

Through the wrong end of a telescope, it looked small, distant. But from Samarkand, it would look the same.

A flame leapt from the logs. She bent over the fire, stretching her thin hands close—until a faint halo glowed around her nails. The light touched the dark rings of hair escaping her braids, made her pale face paler.

“There are plenty of people to tell you what to do,” Archer said, vaguely envious.

“Oh—all my aunts? And my dear old Granny?”

She considered it calmly. “They’re all a little vexed with me for setting up on my own—poor Granny especially. She wanted to keep me with her. But I had to be free—”

He was moved by the casual way she spoke of Catherine Mingott—the formidable matriarch. He wondered what had driven her to crave even the loneliest freedom.

Still, he said, “Your family can advise you. Explain differences. Show you the way.”

She lifted her brows. “Is New York such a labyrinth? I thought it so straight up and down—like Fifth Avenue. And with all the cross streets numbered!”

She sensed his disapproval and added, with a rare smile that lit her whole face: “If you knew how I like it for just _that_—the straight-up-and-downness, and the big honest labels on everything!”

He saw his chance. “Everything may be labeled—but everybody is not.”

“Perhaps. I may simplify too much—but you’ll warn me if I do.”

She turned from the fire to look at him. “There are only two people here who make me feel as if they understand what I mean and can explain things: you and Mr. Beaufort.”

Archer winced. Then, quickly adjusting, he understood—sympathized, even pitied. She’d lived so close to darkness that she still breathed easier in its air. But since she believed he understood, his duty was to show her Beaufort as he truly was—with all he represented—and make her abhor it.

“I understand,” he said gently. “But at first, don’t let go of your older friends’ hands—your Granny Mingott, Mrs. Welland, Mrs. van der Luyden. They admire you. They want to help.”

She shook her head, sighed. “Oh, I know—I know! But only on condition that they don’t hear anything unpleasant. Aunt Welland put it exactly that way when I tried... Does no one want to know the truth here, Mr. Archer? The real loneliness is living among kind people who only ask you to pretend!”

She raised her hands to her face. He saw her shoulders shake. “Madame Olenska!” he cried, rising, bending over her. He took one of her hands, clasping it like a child’s, murmuring reassurances. But in a moment, she freed herself, looked up, wet lashes gleaming.

“Does no one cry here, either?” she said, laughing as she straightened her braids. “I suppose there’s no need to, in heaven.”

It was burned into his mind: he had called her “Ellen”—twice—and she hadn’t noticed.

Far down the inverted telescope, he saw the faint white figure of May Welland—here, in New York.

Then Nastasia appeared, saying something in rich Italian. Madame Olenska, touching her hair, flashed: “Gia—gia.” And the Duke of St. Austrey entered, leading a magnificent woman in black wig and crimson plume, swathed in fur.

“My dear Countess,” the Duke announced, “I’ve brought an old friend—Mrs. Struthers. She wasn’t invited to last night’s party, and she wants to know you.”

The Duke beamed. Madame Olenska advanced with a murmur of welcome.

“Of course I want to know you, my dear!” Mrs. Struthers boomed, her voice matching her feathers and wig. “I want to know everyone who’s young, interesting, charming. And the Duke tells me you like music—didn’t you, Duke? You’re a pianist, I believe? Well, do you want to hear Sarasate tomorrow? I have something going on every Sunday—when New York doesn’t know what to do with itself, I say: ‘Come and be amused.’ And the Duke thought you’d be tempted.”

Madame Olenska’s face lit up. “How kind! How good of the Duke to think of me!”

She pushed a chair forward. Mrs. Struthers sank into it with pleasure. “Of course I shall be happy to come.”

“That’s all right, my dear. And bring your young gentleman with you.”

She extended a bold hand to Archer. “I can’t place your name—but I’m sure I’ve met you. I’ve met everybody—here, Paris, London. Aren’t you in diplomacy? All the diplomatists come to me. You like music too? Duke, you must be sure to bring him.”

The Duke rumbled “Rather” from his beard. Archer bowed stiffly, feeling like a schoolboy among careless elders.

He wasn’t sorry the visit ended—only that it had lasted so long, wasting emotion.

As he stepped out into the winter night, New York surged back—vast, immediate. May Welland was again the loveliest woman in it.

He turned into his florist’s to send her the daily box of lilies-of-the-valley—only to realize he’d forgotten them that morning.

As he wrote a note, he glanced around the shop—and saw a cluster of yellow roses. Sun-golden, fiery, unlike any he’d seen. For a moment, he thought of sending them to May instead.

But they didn’t look like her. Too rich, too strong.

In a sudden shift of mood, almost without thought, he signaled the florist to pack the roses in another box. He slipped his card into a second envelope—wrote “Countess Olenska” on it.

Then, just as he turned to leave, he pulled the card out again—and left the envelope blank.

“They’ll go at once?” he asked, pointing to the roses.

The florist nodded.
