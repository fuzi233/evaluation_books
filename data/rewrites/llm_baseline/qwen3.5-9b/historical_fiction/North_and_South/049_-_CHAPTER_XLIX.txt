"Is not Margaret the heiress?" Edith whispered to her husband later that evening, once they were safely locked inside their room following the somber journey to Oxford. She had pulled his tall head down, standing on tiptoes, imploring him not to be shocked before she dared to ask. Captain Lennox, however, remained completely in the dark; if he had ever heard anything, he had surely forgotten it. After all, what could a Fellow of a small college possibly possess? Yet, he had never charged her for her board, and two hundred and fifty pounds a year sounded absurdly generous, especially since she didn't even drink wine.

Edith descended onto her feet a fraction sadder, her romantic illusions deflated. A week later, however, she bounded toward her husband, performing a low curtsey. "I am right, and you are wrong, most noble Captain," she declared. "Margaret has received a lawyer's letter; she is the residuary legatee. The specific legacies total about two thousand pounds, with the remainder valued at approximately forty thousand pounds, depending on current property rates in Milton."

"Indeed!" he exclaimed. "How does she take her good fortune?"

"Oh, it appears she suspected she was due something all along, simply unaware of the magnitude. She looks terribly pale and white, claiming she fears it, but that is merely nonsense—it will pass quickly. I left Mother pouring congratulations down her throat and slipped away to inform you."

It became the universal consensus that Mr. Lennox was naturally destined to serve as Margaret's legal advisor. Being entirely ignorant of business formalities, she referred to him for nearly every matter. He selected her attorney, brought papers requiring her signature, and derived genuine joy from instructing her on the cryptic symbols of the law.

"Henry," Edith teased one day, "what do you suppose all these lengthy conversations with Margaret will eventually lead to?"

"No, I don't," he replied, flushing slightly. "And I request you not to tell me."

"Oh, very well; then I needn't warn Sholto not to invite Mr. Montagu so frequently to our home."

"Just as you choose," he retorted with forced composure. "Whatever you are contemplating may or may not occur. However, before I commit myself, I intend to survey the terrain clearly. Choose whoever you wish. It may seem uncivil, Edith, but if you interfere, you will ruin it. She has been exceedingly stubborn with me for a long time; she is only just beginning to thaw from her 'Zenobia' mannerisms. She possesses the makings of a Cleopatra within her, provided she sheds a bit of her paganism."

"For my part," Edith remarked with malice, "I am delighted she remains a Christian. I know so very few!"

There was no trip to Spain for Margaret that autumn. Although she clung to the hope that a fortunate circumstance might summon Frederick to Paris—a destination where she could easily arrange a meeting with a convoy—she was instead confined to Cromer. Her Aunt Shaw and the Lennoxes were bound for that coastal town. Having always wished her to join them, they made only sluggish attempts to facilitate her separate desires. Perhaps Cromer was, in a certain sense, the ideal location for her. She required physical strengthening and bracing alongside rest.

Among the hopes that had evaporated was the trust she had placed in Mr. Bell revealing the simple facts regarding the family circumstances preceding the tragic accident that claimed Leonard's life. Regardless of how his opinion might have shifted from what Mr. Thornton once held, Margaret had desired it to be founded on a truthful understanding of her actions and motivations. It would have brought her relief; it would have granted her peace on a subject that would otherwise haunt her forever, unless she could learn to stop dwelling on it.

Time had passed so long since those events that explanation was impossible except through the lens of Mr. Bell's death. She might resign herself, like countless others, to being misunderstood. Yet, despite reasoning herself into accepting this as an uncommon fate, her heart still ached with a longing that, years hence, before he perished, he might finally know how deeply she had been tempted. She believed she did not wish to hear that everything was explained to him, provided only that he understood. This wish, however, proved vain, like so many others. Once she had schooled herself into this conviction, she turned her entire heart and strength toward the immediate future, resolving to strive and make the best of it.

She spent long hours sitting on the beach, staring intently at the waves as they churned perpetually against the pebbly shore—or gazing at the distant swell sparkling against the sky, listening unconsciously to the eternal psalm rising continuously. She was soothed without knowing how or why. Listlessly seated on the ground, hands clasped around her knees, she watched while her Aunt Shaw conducted minor shopping expeditions, and Edith and Captain Lennox rode far and wide across the coast and inland. Nurses sauntering with their charges would pass and repass her, whispering in wonderment about what she could possibly find captivating enough to gaze upon for days on end.

When the family gathered for dinner, Margaret was so silent and absorbed that Edith deemed her moody, enthusiastically supporting her husband's proposal to invite Mr. Henry Lennox to stay at Cromer for a week upon his return from Scotland in October.

Yet, this period of solitude allowed Margaret to reorder events in their proper sequence, regarding both their origins and significance in relation to her past and future. Those hours by the seaside were not wasted, as anyone with the perception to observe or the care to understand the transformation in Margaret's countenance could attest. Mr. Henry Lennox was profoundly struck by the change.

"The sea has undoubtedly done Miss Hale an immense amount of good," he remarked when she first entered the room after his arrival in their family circle. "She looks ten years younger than she did in Harley Street."

"That's the bonnet I procured for her!" Edith announced triumphantly. "I knew it would suit her the moment I laid eyes on it."

"I beg your pardon," Mr. Lennox replied in his characteristic half-contemptuous, half-indulgent tone usually reserved for Edith. "However, I believe I discern the distinction between the charms of a dress and the charms of a woman. No mere bonnet could have rendered Miss Hale's eyes so lustrous yet so soft, or her lips so ripe and red—and her face altogether so filled with peace and light. She is like, and yet more..." he lowered his voice "...like the Margaret Hale of Helstone."

From this moment forth, the clever and ambitious man devoted all his energies to winning Margaret's affection. He adored her sweet beauty and perceived the latent breadth of her mind, which he believed could easily be guided to embrace all the objectives on which he had set his heart. He viewed her fortune merely as a component of her complete and superb character and position, yet he was acutely aware of the social elevation it would instantly afford him, the poor barrister. Ultimately, he envisioned earning such success and honors that he could repay her, with interest, that initial financial advancement he owed to her.

He had visited Milton recently on business concerning her property, returning from Scotland. With the keen eye of a skilled lawyer, ever ready to assess contingencies, he observed that significant additional value was accruing annually to the lands and tenements she owned in that thriving, expanding town. He was pleased to note that the current relationship between Margaret and himself, as client and legal advisor, was gradually erasing the memory of that ill-fated, poorly managed day in Helstone. Thus, he possessed unusual opportunities for intimate interaction with her, beyond those arising from the familial connections.

Margaret was only too willing to listen as long as he spoke of Milton, even though he mentioned none of the people she particularly knew. It had been the prevailing sentiment among her aunt and cousin to speak of Milton with disdain and contempt—feelings Margaret was ashamed to recall having expressed and felt upon first moving there. Conversely, Mr. Lennox surpassed Margaret in his appreciation of Milton's character and its inhabitants. Their energy, their power, their indomitable courage in struggle and combat; their lurid vividness of existence—all captivated and arrested his attention. He never grew weary of discussing them, failing to perceive until Margaret pointed it out—even amidst her delight—that too many of the ends they pursued were selfish and materialistic, tainted sins within so much that was noble and admirable.

Still, when other topics bored her and she offered brief replies to numerous inquiries, Henry Lennox discovered that asking about peculiarities of Darkshire character restored the light to her eyes and the glow to her cheeks.

Upon returning to town, Margaret fulfilled one of her seaside resolutions and took control of her own destiny. Prior to departing for Cromer, she had been as obedient to her aunt's dictates as if she were still the frightened little stranger who wept herself to sleep during her first night in the Harley Street nursery. But those solemn hours of reflection had taught her that she must one day answer for her own life and how she had lived it. She endeavored to solve the most difficult problem facing women: determining how much should be wholly subsumed in obedience to authority and how much might be reserved for freedom of action.
