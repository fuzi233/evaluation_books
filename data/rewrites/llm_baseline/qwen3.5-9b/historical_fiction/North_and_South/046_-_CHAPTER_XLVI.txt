"So on those happy days of yore,
Oft as I dare to dwell once more,
Still must I miss the friends so tried,
Whom Death has severed from my side.

But ever when true friendship binds,
Spirit it is that spirit finds;
In spirit then our bliss we found,
In spirit yet to them I'm bound." —UHLAND.

Margaret was ready long before the appointed hour, granting herself a brief, quiet moment to shed a tear unseen, only to compose a radiant smile whenever anyone glanced her way. Her final anxiety was merely the fear of missing the train; yet, relief washed over her instantly. Seated opposite Mr. Bell in the carriage, she watched the landscape blur past. The familiar stations of the south country slid by, bathed in the warm, golden light of a pure sun that reddened the tiled roofs, a stark contrast to the cold slate of the north. Pigeons circled the quaint, peaked gables, settling softly to preen their shimmering feathers in the heat.

The stations themselves were surprisingly empty, devoid of the bustling crowds Margaret recalled from her previous journeys on the London and North-Western line. This route, later in the year, would likely swarm with pleasure-seekers, but currently, it remained the domain of steady industry. Spectators lounged at platforms, hands in pockets, seemingly lost in the simple act of watching the trains depart, leaving behind only tracks, sheds, and distant fields. The hot air danced over the golden stillness of the land. Farm after farm rolled by, each evoking memories of German idylls, of *Hermann and Dorothea*, of *Evangeline*.

From this waking reverie, she was abruptly roused. It was time to alight and take the fly to Helstone. Suddenly, sharp emotions pierced her heart, a mixture of pain and pleasure she could barely define. Every mile was thick with associations she would trade the world for, yet each triggered a profound grief for "the days that are no more." The last time she traversed this road, she had departed with her parents; the season had been gloomy, her spirits crushed, but they had been beside her. Now, she was alone—an orphan. They had vanished from the earth, leaving her solitary witness to the scene. It wounded her deeply to see the Helstone road flooded in sunlight, every turn and familiar tree identical in its summer glory to years past. Nature felt no loss; it remained eternally young.

Mr. Bell understood the turmoil within her and wisely maintained a respectful silence. They arrived at the Lennard Arms, a structure half-farmhouse, half-inn, standing slightly apart from the road as if to declare that the host did not actively court travelers. Facing the village green stood an ancient lime-tree, its branches forming a bench-like canopy beneath which hung the faded Lennard escutcheon. The inn's door stood wide, yet there was no hurried hospitality.

When the landlady finally appeared, she offered a warm, almost familial welcome, apologizing profusely for her delay due to the demands of hay-making and the preparation of provisions for the fields. She had been too busy packing baskets to notice the wheels until it was too late.

"Why, bless me!" she exclaimed, her eyes catching the glint of sunshine on Margaret's face, previously obscured in the shade. "It's Miss Hale, Jenny!" she cried, rushing to the door to summon her daughter. Then, approaching Margaret, she shook her hand with maternal affection.

"And how are you all? How's the Vicar and Miss Dixon? The Vicar above all! God bless him! We've never ceased to mourn his departure."

Margaret attempted to speak of her father's death; regarding her mother, Mrs. Purkis clearly assumed she was deceased given the omission of her name. Choking on her words, Margaret could only gesture to her deep mourning and whisper, "Papa."

"Surely, sir, it's never so!" Mrs. Purkis turned to Mr. Bell, confirming the dark suspicion in her mind. "A gentleman visited us in the spring—or perhaps last winter—who spoke much of Mr. Hale and Miss Margaret. He mentioned Mrs. Hale had passed, poor soul, but never a word of the Vicar being ill!"

"It is so, however," Mr. Bell replied gently. "He died quite suddenly while visiting me in Oxford. He was a good man, Mrs. Purkis, and many of us would be grateful for such a calm ending. Come, Margaret, my dear! Your father was my oldest friend, and she is my goddaughter, so I thought we should journey down together to see the old place. I know you can provide comfortable rooms and a fine dinner. You don't recall me, I see, but my name is Bell. On occasion, when the parsonage was full, I've slept here and enjoyed your excellent ale."

"To be sure! I ask your pardon; I was so preoccupied with Miss Hale." Mrs. Purkis led Margaret to a room, suggesting she remove her bonnet and wash her face. She noted that earlier that morning she had submerged fresh roses in a water-jug, hoping someone might arrive to enjoy the scent. "To think the Vicar is dead! Well, we must all die; that gentleman said he was recovering from the sorrow of Mrs. Hale's passing."

"Mrs. Purkis, please attend to Miss Hale first. I wish to consult with you regarding dinner."

Later, in Margaret's bedroom, the casement window was nearly obscured by climbing roses and vines. Pushing them aside, she caught a glimpse of the parsonage chimneys rising above the trees, recognizable even through the foliage.

"Aye!" Mrs. Purkis said, straightening the bed and sending Jenny for lavender-scented towels. "Times have changed, miss. Our new Vicar has seven children and is building a nursery where the old arbour and tool-house once stood. He has installed new grates and a plate-glass window in the drawing-room. He and his wife are energetic folk, doing much 'good,' though I suspect they are merely turning things upside down for little reason. The new Vicar is a teetotaler and a magistrate; his wife knows recipes for economical cooking and makes bread without yeast. They talk so incessantly, both at once, that one is knocked down before one can form a counter-argument. He'll be nosing into the hay-fields for the men's cans, causing a fuss because it isn't ginger-beer. My mother and grandmother before me sent good malt liquor to haymakers and administered salts and senna for ailments. I must continue in their ways, though Mrs. Hepworth wants to replace my medicines with comfits, claiming they are pleasanter—I simply lack faith in them. I must go now, miss, though I have much to hear; I'll return shortly."

Mr. Bell prepared a rustic lunch for Margaret upon her descent: strawberries and cream, brown bread, milk, Stilton cheese, and a bottle of port for himself. After this simple meal, they set out for a walk, unsure which path to take amidst so many familiar attractions.

"Shall we pass the vicarage?" Mr. Bell asked.

"No, not yet," Margaret replied. "We will circle this way and return via it."

Old trees felled the previous autumn lay bare; a squatter's dilapidated cottage had vanished. Margaret mourned each loss as if bidding farewell to an old friend. They passed the site where she and Mr. Lennox had once sketched. The white, lightning-struck trunk of the venerable beech, beneath whose roots they had sat, was gone. The elderly inhabitant of the ruined cottage was dead, replaced by a tidy, respectable new dwelling. A small garden occupied the space where the beech had stood.

"I did not realize I had grown so old," Margaret murmured after a pause, turning away with a sigh.

"Yes!" Mr. Bell agreed. "It is the initial changes among familiar things that create such a mystery of time for the young. Later, we lose the sense of the miraculous. I accept change as inevitable. The instability of human affairs is familiar to me, but to you, it feels new and oppressive."

"Let us go see little Susan," Margaret suggested, guiding her companion up a grassy lane shaded by a forest glade.

"With all my heart, though I have no idea who little Susan is. But I hold a kindness for all Susans, for simple Susan's sake."

"My little Susan was disappointed when I left without saying goodbye; it weighs on my conscience that I caused her pain which a little more effort might have avoided. But it is a distance. Are you sure you won't tire?"

"Quite sure, provided you don't walk too fast. Here, there are no scenic views to justify pausing for breath. One might think it romantic to walk with someone 'fat and scant o' breath' if I were Hamlet, Prince of Denmark. Have compassion on my infirmities for his sake."

"I shall walk slower for your own sake. I like you twenty times better than Hamlet."

"On the principle that a living ass is preferable to a dead lion?"

"Perhaps so. I do not analyze my feelings."

"I am content to accept your liking for me without scrutinizing its origins. Only let us not walk at a snail's pace."

"Very well. Lead the way, and I will follow. Or, if I move too quickly, we may stop and meditate, like the Hamlet you compare yourself to."
