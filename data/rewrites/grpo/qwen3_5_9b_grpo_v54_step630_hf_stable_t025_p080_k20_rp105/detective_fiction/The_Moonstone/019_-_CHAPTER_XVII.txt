The night passed without incident, and despite the watchful eyes of Sergeant Cuff, neither Miss Rachel nor Rosanna made any attempt to communicate. I had anticipated that the Sergeant would depart for Frizinghall at first light, yet he lingered, seemingly occupied with some unseen task. Leaving him to his own devices, I ventured into the grounds shortly thereafter and encountered Mr. Franklin on his customary stroll along the shrubbery path.

We had scarcely exchanged greetings when the Sergeant appeared unexpectedly. He approached Mr. Franklin with a practiced ease, though the gentleman received him with palpable hauteur. "Have you anything to say to me?" Mr. Franklin asked sharply, offering no warmth to the Sergeant's polite morning wish.

"I have something to say to you, sir," the Sergeant replied, his tone shifting from deference to authority. "Regarding the inquiry I am conducting here. You detected the true direction of this investigation yesterday. Naturally, in your position, you are shocked and distressed. Naturally, too, you project your own anger regarding your family's scandal onto me."

"What do you want?" Mr. Franklin interrupted, his voice cutting through the air.

"I wish to remind you, sir, that I have not yet been *proved* wrong. Keeping that in mind, please recall that I am an officer of the law acting under the sanction of the mistress of this house. Under these circumstances, is it, or is it not, your duty as a good citizen to assist me with any special information you may possess?"

"I possess no special information," Mr. Franklin stated flatly.

The Sergeant dismissed this answer as if it had never been spoken. "You may save my time from being wasted on inquiries at a distance," he continued, pressing forward, "if you choose to understand me and speak out."

"I don't understand you," Mr. Franklin retorted, "and I have nothing to say."

"One of the female servants—I won't mention names—spoke to you privately last night."

Again, Mr. Franklin cut him short. Again, he answered, "I have nothing to say."

Standing silently nearby, I recalled the movement in the swing-door from the previous evening and the coat-tails disappearing down the passage. The Sergeant had undoubtedly gathered enough to suspect that Rosanna had relieved her conscience by confessing something to Mr. Franklin Blake. This thought had barely formed in my mind when Rosanna Spearman herself emerged from the end of the shrubbery walk. She was followed closely by Penelope, who was clearly attempting to guide her back toward the house. Seeing that Mr. Franklin was not alone, Rosanna halted, her face a mask of perplexity. Penelope waited behind her. Mr. Franklin spotted the girls as quickly as I did. The Sergeant, displaying his characteristic cunning, feigned total ignorance of their presence. All of this unfolded in a heartbeat. Before either Mr. Franklin or I could utter a word, the Sergeant stepped in smoothly, maintaining the appearance of continuing our previous conversation.

"You needn't be afraid of harming the girl, sir," he addressed Mr. Franklin, speaking loudly enough for Rosanna to hear every syllable. "On the contrary, I recommend you to honor me with your confidence if you feel any interest in Rosanna Spearman."

Mr. Franklin instantly adopted an air of obliviousness regarding the girls. He responded, his voice projecting across the garden:

"I take no interest whatever in Rosanna Spearman."

I glanced toward the end of the walk. From that distance, I saw only that the moment Mr. Franklin spoke, Rosanna turned abruptly. Instead of resisting Penelope as she had moments before, she allowed my daughter to take her arm and lead her back inside.

The breakfast bell rang just as the two girls vanished, and even the astute Sergeant Cuff was forced to concede defeat. He turned to me quietly. "I shall go to Frizinghall, Mr. Betteredge, and I shall be back before two." With that, he departed without further comment, leaving us free for several hours.

"You must make it right with Rosanna," Mr. Franklin said to me once we were alone. "I seem fated to say or do something awkward whenever that unfortunate girl is near. You saw it yourself; the Sergeant laid a trap for both of us. If he could confuse me or irritate her into breaking down, either she or I might have revealed something serving his purpose. On the spur of the moment, I saw no better escape than the path I took. It stopped the girl from speaking and demonstrated to the Sergeant that I saw through him. He was evidently listening, Betteredge, when I was speaking to you last night."

He had done more than merely listen, as I privately surmised. He had remembered my confession that the girl loved Mr. Franklin, and he had calculated precisely on that emotion when he appealed to Mr. Franklin's supposed interest in Rosanna, speaking within her hearing range.

"As to listening, sir," I remarked, holding back the other point, "we shall all be rowing in the same boat if this continues much longer. Prying, peeping, and listening are the natural occupations of those in our situation. Within a day or two, Mr. Franklin, we shall all be struck dumb together—for this reason: we shall all be listening to surprise each other's secrets, fully aware of what we are doing. Excuse my outburst, sir. The horrid mystery hanging over this house gets into my head like liquor and makes me wild. I will not forget what you have told me. I shall take the first opportunity to make it right with Rosanna Spearman."

"You haven't said anything to her yet about last night, have you?" Mr. Franklin asked.

"No, sir."

"Then say nothing now. I had better not invite the girl's confidence with the Sergeant watching to surprise us together. My conduct is not very consistent, Betteredge—is it? I see no way out of this business, which is dreadful to contemplate, unless the Diamond is traced to Rosanna. And yet I cannot, and will not, help Sergeant Cuff to find the girl out."

Unreasonable, no doubt. Yet it mirrored my own state of mind perfectly. I understood him entirely. If you will, for once in your life, remember that you are mortal, perhaps you will understand him too.

While the Sergeant was en route to Frizinghall, the domestic situation unfolded as follows: Miss Rachel remained obstinately confined to her room, awaiting the carriage for her journey to her aunt's. My lady and Mr. Franklin breakfasted together. Afterward, Mr. Franklin made one of his sudden resolutions and departed precipitately for a long walk to quiet his mind. I was the sole witness to his exit; he assured me he would return before the Sergeant's arrival. The weather, foreshadowed the night before, had shifted dramatically. Heavy rain followed by high winds had swept through at dawn. By mid-morning, the air was fresh and biting, though the clouds, threatening intermittently, failed to release another drop of rain. It was a challenging day for a walk, suitable only for the young and strong capable of braving the great gusts sweeping in from the sea.

I attended to my mistress after breakfast, assisting her in settling our household accounts. She mentioned the Moonstone only once, strictly forbidding any present discussion of it between us. "Wait till that man comes back," she said, referring to the Sergeant. "We *must* speak of it then; we are not obliged to speak of it now."

Leaving my mistress, I found Penelope waiting in my room.

"I wish, father, you would come and speak to Rosanna," she said. "I am very uneasy about her."

I suspected the cause readily enough. However, it is a maxim of mine that men, being superior creatures, are bound to improve women if they can. When a woman requests my assistance—whether my daughter or anyone else—it is essential that I insist on knowing the reason. The more often you compel them to rummage their own minds for a justification, the more manageable you will find them in all relations of life. It is not their fault (poor wretches!) that they act first and think afterwards; it is the fault of the fools who indulge them.

Penelope's reason, on this occasion, was delivered in her own words. "I am afraid, father," she said, "Mr. Franklin has hurt Rosanna cruelly, without intending it."

"What took Rosanna into the shrubbery walk?" I asked.

"Her own madness," Penelope replied. "I can call it nothing else. She was bent on speaking to Mr. Franklin this morning, come what might. I did my best to stop her; you saw that. If I could only have got her away before she heard those dreadful words..."

"There! There!" I said, soothing her. "Don't lose your head. I cannot recall anything happening to alarm Rosanna."

"Nothing to alarm her, father. But Mr. Franklin said he took no interest whatever in her—and, oh, he said it in such a cruel voice!"

"He said it to stop the Sergeant's mouth," I answered.

"I told her that," Penelope said. "But you see, father (though Mr. Franklin isn't to blame), he has been mortifying and disappointing her for weeks and weeks past; and now this comes on top of everything! She has no right, of course, to expect him to take any interest in her. It is quite monstrous that she should forget herself and her station in that way. But she seems to have lost pride, and proper feeling, and everything. She frightened me, father, when Mr. Franklin said those words. They seemed to turn her into stone. A sudden quiet came over her, and she has gone about her work ever since, like a woman in a dream."

I began to feel a distinct unease. There was something in the way Penelope phrased it that silenced my superior judgment. Recalling the events between Mr. Franklin and Rosanna the night before, I saw how she looked cut to the heart then; and now, as ill luck would have it, she had been stung again on a tender spot. Sad! Sad!—all the more tragic because the girl had no reason to justify her feelings and no right to feel them.

I had promised Mr. Franklin to speak to Rosanna, and this seemed the fittest time to keep my word.

We found the girl sweeping the corridor outside the bedrooms. She was pale and composed, neat as ever in her modest print dress. I noticed a curious dimness and dullness in her eyes—not as if she had been crying, but as if she had been staring at something for too long. Perhaps it was a misty haze raised by her own thoughts. Certainly, there was no object about her she had not seen hundreds of times before.

"Cheer up, Rosanna!" I said. "You mustn't fret over your own fancies. I have something to say to you from Mr. Franklin."

I then presented the matter to her in the friendliest and most comforting terms I could devise. My principles regarding the other sex are, as you may have noticed, very severe. Yet somehow, when I face women directly, my practice (I admit) does not conform to those strict rules.

"Mr. Franklin is very kind and considerate. Please to thank him." That was all the answer she gave me.

My daughter had already observed that Rosanna moved about her work like a woman in a dream. I now added to this observation that she also listened and spoke like one. I doubted if her mind was in a fit condition to comprehend what I had said.

"Are you quite sure, Rosanna, that you understand me?" I asked.

"Quite sure."

She echoed me, not like a living woman, but like a creature moved by machinery. She continued sweeping all the while. I gently took the broom from her hands.

"Come, come, my girl!" I said. "This is not like yourself. You have something on your mind. I'm your friend—and I'll stand by you, even if you have done wrong. Make a clean breast of it, Rosanna—make a clean breast of it!"

In days past, speaking to her in such a manner would have brought tears to her eyes. Now, I saw no change in them.

"Yes," she said. "I'll make a clean breast of it."

"To my lady?" I asked.

"No."

"To Mr. Franklin?"

"Yes; to Mr. Franklin."

I hardly knew what to reply. She was in no condition to understand the caution against speaking to him in private, which Mr. Franklin had directed me to convey. Feeling my way cautiously, I merely told her that Mr. Franklin had gone out for a walk.

"It doesn't matter," she answered. "I shan't trouble Mr. Franklin today."

"Why not speak to my lady?" I said. "The way to relieve your mind is to speak to the merciful and Christian mistress who has always been kind to you."

She looked at me for a moment with grave and steady attention, as if fixing my words in her mind. Then she took the broom from my hands and moved off slowly, a little way down the corridor.

"No," she said, continuing her sweeping and speaking to herself; "I know a better way of relieving my mind than that."

"What is it?"

"Please to let me go on with my work."

Penelope followed her, offering assistance.

She answered, "No. I want to do my work. Thank you, Penelope." She looked around at me. "Thank you, Mr. Betteredge."

There was no moving her; there was nothing more to be said. I signaled to Penelope to come away with me. We left her as we had found her, sweeping the corridor, like a woman in a dream.

"This is a matter for the doctor to look into," I declared. "It is beyond me."

My daughter reminded me of Mr. Candy's illness, caused by the chill he caught during the dinner party. His assistant, a certain Mr. Ezra Jennings, was at our disposal, to be sure. Yet, nobody knew much about him in our parts. He had been engaged by Mr. Candy under peculiar circumstances, and right or wrong, none of us liked or trusted him.
