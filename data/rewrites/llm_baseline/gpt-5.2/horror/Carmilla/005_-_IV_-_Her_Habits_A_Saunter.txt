I was captivated by her—so much so that the days began to arrange themselves around her like the courses of a dinner one does not yet understand, but already hungers for. And yet, even as I watched her, even as I touched her hair and listened to her voice, there were customs in her that sat uneasily with me, like a familiar recipe made with some foreign spice whose fragrance is exquisite and whose aftertaste is faintly medicinal.

She was taller than most women, slender, astonishingly graceful. Nothing about her suggested illness; only a languor, a deliberate softness in every motion, as though she moved through water that no one else could see. Her complexion had that deep, living brilliance one associates with health, with blood close to the skin; her features were small, perfectly shaped; her eyes large and dark and burning with a glossy, watchful light. But it was her hair—her hair was the thing that bound my attention like a spell. I had never seen anything so thick, so long. When it was loosened it fell with its own weight, and I used to slide my hands beneath it and laugh, honestly startled, at how heavy it was, how it held warmth the way a fur holds warmth. It was fine as silk, soft as sifted flour, a rich dark brown with a hidden glint, as though threads of gold had been spun into it and then buried again. In her room, when she lay back in a chair and talked in that sweet low voice—so low it seemed made for the ear alone—I would let it down and fold it and braid it, separating it in slow glossy ropes, spreading it like a cloak across her shoulders and my own hands. If I had known then what I know now—if I had understood what a thing it is to lavish such intimacy on a mystery—I think the weight of that hair would have felt like a chain.

She had won me on the first night with her confidence, or what I mistook for confidence; but I discovered, almost at once, a vigilance in her reserve. About herself, her mother, her history, her plans—everything that might give her a true outline—she kept a wakeful silence. Perhaps I was unjust. Perhaps I ought to have obeyed, more faithfully, the injunction my father had received from that stately lady in black velvet. But curiosity has its own appetite, and it is not a polite one. No girl endures patiently to be denied what another girl might grant her with ease. It seemed to me—unaccountably, absurdly—that refusal was a form of coldness. What harm could there be in telling me what I asked? Why offer tenderness and keep truth locked away? Was I to be trusted with her cheek against mine, but not trusted with the name of the place she called home?

What she told me, after all my insinuations and my poorly disguised attacks, amounted—in my unreasonable estimation—to almost nothing. Three disclosures, each like a dish described but never served. First: her name was Carmilla. Second: her family was ancient and noble. Third: her home lay westward. She would not tell me her family name, their bearings, their estate, not even the country. I did not persecute her ceaselessly; I waited for the moments when conversation seemed to open, as dough opens under a patient hand, and I tried to slip my questions in gently. Once or twice I pressed more directly, and always failed. Reproaches failed. Caresses failed. She parried with a melancholy prettiness, a deprecating smile, and always—always—passionate assurances of her liking for me, of her trust in my honor, and promises that one day I should know all. How could I stay offended when she made her refusals sound like sorrow?

At times she would put her arms around my neck, draw me close, lay her cheek to mine, and murmur with her lips at my ear, “Dearest, your little heart is wounded; think me not cruel because I obey the irresistible law of my strength and weakness; if your dear heart is wounded, my wild heart bleeds with yours. In the rapture of my enormous humiliation I live in your warm life, and you shall die—die, sweetly die—into mine. I cannot help it; as I draw near to you, you, in your turn, will draw near to others, and learn the rapture of that cruelty, which yet is love; so, for a while, seek to know no more of me and mine, but trust me with all your loving spirit.”

Then, as though her words must be sealed upon my skin, she would press me closer; her body trembled, and her soft kisses warmed my cheek as if her mouth carried a faint fever. Her language, her agitations, were unintelligible. I wanted to draw away. I did not want to draw away. My will seemed to thin and dissolve, like sugar in hot chocolate, until there was nothing left but a trance-like compliance. Her voice had the quality of a lullaby—yet not the lullabies of my childhood, sung in the homely cadence of our servants’ old dialect, with its round, comforting vowels and its names for herbs and weather that cannot be cleanly translated into courtly speech. Hers was a lullaby that soothed resistance and left behind, when it ended, a silence full of listening. I only recovered myself when her arms released me.

In those moods I did not like her. I felt a tumultuous excitement that was pleasurable, and again and again a vague fear and disgust braided through it like bitter threads. Love growing into adoration; abhorrence rising with it—paradox, yes, but it is the only honest word. I write now after more than ten years, my hand trembling with confused, horrible recollections—vivid in the main current of events, yet dim in the very scenes where feeling had been fiercest. I believe that is common: the mind, scalded by passion, remembers its pain as one remembers a burn—certain that it happened, unable to summon the exact shape of flame.

Sometimes, after an hour of apathy, she would take my hand and hold it, pressing it again and again, as if trying to imprint herself upon my pulse. She would blush, gazing at me with eyes both languid and burning, breathing so fast her dress rose and fell visibly. It had the ardor of a lover; it embarrassed me; it felt hateful and yet overpowering. Her eyes seemed to gloat, and then she would draw me to her and kiss along my cheek, hot lips traveling as if seeking some secret place, and whisper—almost sobbing—“You are mine, you shall be mine, you and I are one for ever.” Then she would fling herself back, cover her eyes with her small hands, and leave me shaking.

“Are we related?” I used to ask. “What do you mean by all this? I remind you perhaps of someone you love; but you must not. I hate it. I don’t know you—I don’t know myself when you look so and talk so.”

She would sigh at my vehemence, turn away, and let my hand fall as if it meant nothing.

I tried to explain these manifestations and could not. They did not look like affectation. They were the breaking out, for a moment, of something suppressed. Was she, despite her mother’s denial, visited by brief insanity? Or was it all romance and disguise? I had read old tales—tales carried by nurses in words that have no elegant equivalents—of lovers in masquerade, of a boyish suitor hidden under feminine dress with the help of an old adventuress. The hypothesis flattered my vanity; but there was too much against it. Her constant languor, for one: it did not belong to a healthy masculine system. Between those passionate outbreaks there were long stretches of commonplace gaiety, then brooding melancholy, in which—except for the melancholy fire of her eyes following me—I might have been nothing to her.

Her habits, too, were odd. Perhaps a lady of a town would not have found them so singular, but to us, living close to the rhythm of fields and kitchens and the small noises by which one measures a day, they struck strangely.

She came down very late, generally not until one o’clock. In a house where the morning has its own music—the clink of pails, the thud of loaves set on boards, the hens fussing, the cook’s knife keeping time on the block—her lateness was like a gap in the score. When she did appear she took only a cup of chocolate and ate nothing. The chocolate, thick and dark, steamed faintly with cinnamon and ground bean; its fragrance filled the room, promising comfort, yet she treated it as one treats a remedy: a few sips, no relish, no desire. It troubled me more than I can account for now. Food in our country is memory made tangible: the same rye bread baked in the same shape for generations, the same sour cream, the same forest mushrooms steeped in butter, the same names spoken over herbs as one strips them from their stems. To refuse eating is to refuse a language. Carmilla drank chocolate and left the rest untouched, as if she had no need of the domestic sacrament by which we recognize each other.

After that we went out to walk—but it was not a walk, not truly. It was a saunter, barely a drift. Almost immediately she seemed exhausted and would either return to the schloss or sit on one of the benches placed among the trees. Her body drooped, yet her mind did not share the languor. She was animated in talk, intelligent, keen. Sometimes she would allude to her home, to an adventure, an early recollection, and in those brief hints there were strange manners, unfamiliar customs, little domestic details that did not fit any country I knew. From them I began to suspect her native place lay much farther than I had first imagined—westward, yes, but westward into a distance that felt less like geography than like time.

One afternoon as we sat under the trees, a funeral passed. It was for a pretty young girl, the daughter of one of our forest rangers—a child I had often seen carrying a basket, cheeks bright from wind and work. She was his only child. He walked behind the coffin with the posture of a man whose spine has been broken without a blow. Behind him peasants walked two-and-two, singing a funeral hymn. The sound moved through the trees in a slow, well-worn channel, and the woods seemed to listen, every leaf holding its breath between notes. In our country, these hymns are older than the neat prayers of printed books; they keep words alive that no official language cares to remember. Their vowels carry soil and smoke; their cadences follow the same arc as scythes and lullabies. I rose to show respect as they passed, and joined in softly.

Carmilla shook me—almost roughly. I turned, astonished.

“Don’t you perceive how discordant that is?” she said.

“I think it very sweet,” I answered, vexed at her interruption and suddenly anxious that the mourners might see us and resent what was passing. I resumed at once—and again she stopped me.

“You pierce my ears,” said Carmilla, almost angrily, and she stopped her ears with her little fingers. “Besides, how can you tell that your religion and mine are the same? Your forms wound me, and I hate funerals. What a fuss! Why you must die—everyone must die; and all are happier when they do. Come home.”

“My father has gone on with the clergyman to the churchyard,” I said. “I thought you knew she was to be buried today.”

“She? I don’t trouble my head about peasants. I don’t know who she is,” Carmilla answered, and there was a flash from her fine eyes like the strike of flint.

“She is the poor girl who fancied she saw a ghost a fortnight ago, and has been dying ever since, till yesterday, when she expired.”

“Tell me nothing about ghosts,” she said quickly. “I shan’t sleep tonight if you do.”

“I hope there is no plague or fever coming; all this looks very like it,” I went on, for the deaths had begun to feel like a pattern. “The swineherd’s young wife died only a week ago, and she thought something seized her by the throat as she lay in her bed, and nearly strangled her. Papa says such horrible fancies do accompany some forms of fever. She was quite well the day before. She sank afterwards, and died before a week.”

“Well, her funeral is over, I hope, and her hymn sung; and our ears shan’t be tortured with that discord and jargon. It has made me nervous. Sit down here, beside me; sit close; hold my hand; press it hard—hard—harder.”

We moved a little back to another bench. She sat, and her face altered so suddenly that my breath stopped. It darkened, turned horribly livid; her hands clenched, her teeth set, and she frowned, compressing her lips, staring at the ground by her feet. A shudder ran through her—continuous, irrepressible, like ague. It was as if every energy in her strained to suppress some seizure that tugged at her from within. At last a low convulsive cry broke from her, and gradually the fit subsided.

“There! That comes of strangling people with hymns!” she said, at last, with a kind of bitter levity. “Hold me, hold me still. It is passing away.”

And it did pass. Then, perhaps to disperse the gloomy impression the spectacle had left upon me, she became unusually animated and chatty, and so we got home. It was the first time I had seen any definable symptom of the delicate health her mother had spoken of. It was also the first time I had seen anything like temper in her. Both vanished like a summer cloud; never but once afterward did I witness, on her part, a momentary sign of anger.

It happened when she and I were looking out from one of the long drawing-room windows. Across the drawbridge into the courtyard came a figure I knew well, a wanderer who visited the schloss generally twice a year: a hunchback with sharp, lean features and a pointed black beard. He smiled from ear to ear, showing white teeth like little fangs. He wore buff, black, and scarlet, crossed with more straps and belts than I could count; from them hung all manner of objects, clinking and tapping as he walked—metal against metal, leather creaking, a whole little industry of noise entering our quiet court. Behind him he carried a magic lantern and two boxes, and I knew them: one held a salamander, the other a mandrake—those stitched-together monstrosities that made my father laugh, compounded of bits of monkey, parrot, squirrel, fish, hedgehog, dried and neatly sewn to startling effect. He had a fiddle; a box of conjuring apparatus; a pair of foils and masks attached to his belt; several other mysterious cases; and a black staff tipped with copper in his hand.

A rough, spare dog followed at his heels, but stopped short at the drawbridge as if the threshold itself had teeth. In a moment it began to howl dismally. The sound poured into the courtyard and bounced off the stone, turning our domestic stillness into something strained and raw. I remember thinking—absurdly, but distinctly—that the dog was the only honest creature among us, responding to something our human manners insisted on ignoring.

The mountebank stood in the middle of the courtyard, lifted his grotesque hat, and bowed ceremoniously, paying compliments volubly in execrable French and German not much better. Then he disengaged his fiddle and scraped a lively air, singing to it with a merry discord, dancing with ludicrous activity. I laughed in spite of the dog’s howling. He advanced to the window, hat in hand, fiddle tucked under his arm, and without pausing for breath he gabbled a long advertisement of his accomplishments—arts, entertainments, curiosities he could display at our bidding.

“Will your ladyships be pleased to buy an amulet against the oupire,” he said at last, dropping his hat on the pavement, “which is going like the wolf, I hear, through these woods. They are dying of it right and left—and here is a charm that never fails; only pinned to the pillow, and you may laugh in his face.”

The charms were oblong slips of vellum covered in cabalistic ciphers and diagrams. Carmilla bought one instantly, and so did I. We leaned down, amused—at least I was. The man looked up, and his piercing black eye seemed to catch on something in our faces. He unrolled, in an instant, a leather case full of odd little steel instruments.

“See here, my lady,” he said, addressing me, “I profess, among other things less useful, the art of dentistry. Plague take the dog! Silence, beast! He howls so that your ladyships can scarcely hear a word. Your noble friend, the young lady at your right, has the sharpest tooth,—long, thin, pointed, like an awl, like a needle; ha, ha! With my sharp and long sight, as I look up, I have seen it distinctly; now if it happens to hurt the young lady, and I think it must, here am I, here are my file, my punch, my nippers; I will make it round and blunt, if her ladyship pleases; no longer the tooth of a fish, but of a beautiful young lady as she is. Hey? Is the young lady displeased? Have I been too bold? Have I offended her?”

Carmilla drew back from the window, her face suddenly hard with anger.

“How dares that mountebank insult us so?” she cried. “Where is your father? I shall demand redress from him. My father would have had the wretch tied up to the pump, and flogged with a cart whip, and burnt to the bones with the cattle brand!”

She stepped back and sat down, and even before the offender had left her sight her wrath drained away as abruptly as it had risen. She returned to her usual tone as if nothing had happened, as if the hunchback and his dog and his jangling straps had been only a gust of wind.

That evening my father was out of spirits. When he came in he told us there had been another case like the two fatal ones already occurred: the sister of a young peasant on his estate, only a mile away, very ill. She described an attack nearly the same, and now she was slowly, steadily sinking.

“All this,” said my father, “is strictly referable to natural causes. These poor people infect one another with their superstitions, and so repeat in imagination the images of terror that have infested their neighbors.”

“But that very circumstance frightens one horribly,” said Carmilla.

“How so?” my father asked.

“I am so afraid of fancying I see such things,” she answered. “I think it would be as bad as reality.”

“We are in God’s hands,” said my gentle father. “Nothing can happen without his permission, and all will end well for those who love him. He is our faithful creator; He has made us all, and will take care of us.”

“Creator! Nature!” Carmilla replied, and the word Nature sounded on her tongue like a doctrine older than my father’s. “And this disease that invades the country is natural. Nature. All things proceed from Nature—don’t they? All things in the heaven, in the earth, and under the earth, act and live as Nature ordains? I think so.”

“The doctor said he would come here today,” my father said after a silence. “I want to know what he thinks about it, and what he thinks we had better do.”

“Doctors never did me any good,” said Carmilla.

“Then you have been ill?” I asked.

“More ill than ever you were,” she answered.

“Long ago?”

“Yes, a long time. I suffered from this very illness; but I forget all but my pain and weakness, and they were not so bad as are suffered in other diseases.”

“You were very young then?”

“I dare say. Let us talk no more of it. You would not wound a friend?” She looked languidly into my eyes, slipped her arm around my waist with a possessive tenderness, and led me out of the room. My father remained by the window, busy with papers, his pen scratching—one of the few sharp sounds in the house that night, like a mouse in the walls.

“Why does your papa like to frighten us?” Carmilla murmured, sighing, with a little shudder.

“He doesn’t, dear Carmilla. It is the very furthest thing from his mind.”

“Are you afraid, dearest?”

“I should be very much,” I confessed, “if I fancied there was any real danger of my being attacked as those poor people were.”

“You are afraid to die?”

“Yes. Every one is.”

“But to die as lovers may—to die together, so that they may live together.” Her voice deepened on the words, and something in it struck the same confusion in me as before, that mingling of sweetness and dread. “Girls are caterpillars while they live in the world, to be finally butterflies when the summer comes; but in the meantime there are grubs and larvae, don’t you see—each with their peculiar propensities, necessities and structure. So says Monsieur Buffon, in his big book, in the next room.”

Later that day the doctor came, and he was closeted with my father for some time. He was a skilful man of sixty and upward, powdered, his pale face shaved smooth as a pumpkin. When they emerged together I heard my father laugh as they came out.

“Well, I do wonder at a wise man like you,” my father said. “What do you say to hippogriffs and dragons?”

The doctor smiled and shook his head. “Nevertheless,” he said, “life and death are mysterious states, and we know little of the resources of either.”

They walked on, and I heard no more. I did not then know what the doctor had been broaching; but I think I can guess it now, as one guesses, too late, the missing ingredient in a dish whose flavor has haunted one for years.
