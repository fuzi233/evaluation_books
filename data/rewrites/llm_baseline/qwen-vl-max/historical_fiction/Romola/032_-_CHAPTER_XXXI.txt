“My Romola,” Tito said on the second morning after his speech in the Piazza del Duomo, “I am expecting important visitors today—the Milanese Count and the Seneschal de Beaucaire, a close favorite of the Cristianissimo. I know you have no desire to endure the hollow courtesies of these grand figures, who may never return to Florence. Since they’ll want to inspect the antiquities and the library, perhaps you’d prefer to rest from your work and visit your cousin Brigida.”

Romola sensed the unspoken wish beneath his words and readily agreed. But soon she returned, wrapped in her hood and mantle, and said, “Oh, what a relief it will be when the gates of Florence swing open and the last Frenchman steps beyond them! Even you, with all your patience, are beginning to tire, my Tito—admit it. Your head feels hot.”

He was bent over his desk, writing, and she had placed her hand gently upon his head, as if to offer a farewell caress. This gesture had been familiar between them; usually, he would lift his face, lean back slightly, and meet her gaze. But now, he felt unable to raise his head—as though her touch were a weight of lead. Instead, he spoke lightly, his pen still gliding across the page: “The French are as eager to leave Florence as wasps are to abandon a ripe pear once they’ve feasted upon it.”

Romola, acutely aware of the absence of his usual response, withdrew her hand. “I’m going, Tito.”

“Farewell, my sweet one,” he replied. “I must stay here. Take Maso with you.”

Still, he did not look up. Romola left without another word.

Small things often mark turning points in marriage—and this morning, for the first time, Romola acknowledged to herself not only that Tito had changed, but that the change was directed toward her. Was it her fault? She might have thought so, had it not been for the lingering mysteries—the armor, the painting—clues to an event she could not understand.

No sooner had she stepped out of the house than Tito laid down his pen and looked up, relieved to see only parchment and broken marble before him. He felt a quiet shame at his inability to meet her glance, to act as he always had. He would have chosen to be more tender than ever—but he could not master the sudden recoil from her presence, a reaction tied deeply to those very qualities that made him long to remain faithful to her.

He was about to take a step that would provoke her deepest anger. He knew he would face discomfort, reproach, even sorrow before winning her forgiveness. And Tito had never found it easy to endure displeasure or rage. His nature recoiled from defiance, from boldness. All his instincts leaned toward preserving Romola’s affection, her tenderness.

He was not tormented by sentimental scruples—those, he had swiftly dismissed as irrelevant to practical life. Yet freedom from such scruples did not shield him from fear of pain, of wounded pride, of loneliness. Unscrupulousness may free one from guilt, but not from toothache, nor from the ache of isolation. And Tito now felt that no trickery, no charm, could protect him from the coming clash with Romola. He was being driven forward like a timid beast toward a desperate leap, pursued by the claws and fangs of fear.

A secret uneasiness had long gnawed at him—the idea that clinging to Bardo’s wishes regarding the library had become, under current circumstances, a foolish sentimentality, costing both himself and Romola real advantages. But it was only recently, amid the upheaval of the past week, that this feeling had crystallized into action. The pressure of new motives and the rare chance of a timely opportunity had pushed him forward.

Indeed, it was not until he saw Baldassarre regaining his sanity, until he began to fear he might be forced to flee Florence, that he resolved to exercise his legal right to sell the library—before the chance slipped away, lost to French and Milanese buyers. For if he had to leave Florence, he did not want to depart as a penniless exile. He had grown accustomed to comfort, and he wished to carry that comfort with him. He wanted to take Romola with him—and if possible, to do so without disgrace.

Success had bred in him a hunger for the pleasures tied to status and influence. To vanish under dishonor, even with riches, seemed not temptation but horror—a descent into the life of a fugitive. He could not sever ties with the Florentines who respected him, nor could he truly separate from Romola. She was the wife of his first love—still loved, still essential. She was part of the fabric of his life, something he dreaded losing.

He winced at the thought of her judgment, uncertain how far her feelings might turn against him. The power a husband holds over his wife—power that allows some men to risk betrayal—could not soothe Tito’s anxiety. That was the weight that held his head down, keeping him from lifting his eyes to hers. Her gaze, clear and pure, brought too vividly the vision of the struggle ahead.

But there was no avoiding it. If they were to leave Florence, they needed money. Indeed, Tito could not imagine shaping a future for himself without substantial funds. And this need—to arrange life according to his own desires—was the root of all his missteps. He would have borne any sacrifice that did not bring discomfort.

The magnates came and went, bargains were sealed, and Romola returned home. That night, nothing serious was spoken. Tito was cheerful, animated, sharing stories and scenes from the French court with a liveliness he had not shown before. Romola noticed the effort behind his cheerfulness and, understanding he was trying to mend the wound of the morning, accepted it as an act of contrition. Yet inside, she ached at the growing distance between them—the silence around a hurt neither dared name.

The next day, Tito stayed away until late. It was a significant evening for Romola. Piero di Cosimo, spurred by guilt over any pain he may have caused her, had delivered her father’s portrait. She had placed it against the back of Bardo’s old chair and stood before it, lost in memory, when the door opened and Bernardo del Nero entered.

“It’s you, godfather! How I wish you had come earlier—it’s growing dark,” Romola said, stepping forward.

“I just stopped by to tell you the good news,” Bernardo said. “Tito hasn’t returned yet, so I thought you should hear it first. The French king is leaving tomorrow—not a moment too soon. There was another clash this morning between our people and his soldiers. But now, there’s hope the city may regain order, and trade may resume.”

“That is wonderful,” Romola said. “But it seems sudden. Yesterday, Tito thought the king would linger much longer.”

“He’s been well scolded,” Bernardo replied, smiling. “His own generals opened their mouths wide enough, and then our Signoria sent Fra Girolamo—the city’s mastiff. The Cristianissimo trembled at that thunder and ordered the retreat. We may quarrel among ourselves when he’s gone, but everyone agrees: we’re glad to be rid of the army choking Florence. And the friar barked with purpose this time. Ah, what is this?” he added, as Romola guided him toward the portrait.

“Let us see,” he said, beginning to unroll his scarf as she brought a stool.

“Don’t you need your spectacles, godfather?” Romola asked, anxious he should see exactly what she saw.

“No, child, no,” Bernardo answered, uncovering his grey hair as he sat upright. “For seeing at this distance, my old eyes may be sharper than yours. Old eyes are like old memories—they remember best what lies far away.”

“It’s better than having no portrait at all,” Romola said softly after a pause. “It doesn’t resemble him as closely as the image in my mind, but that image might fade with time.”

She rested her arm on his shoulder, drawn to him by their shared reverence for the dead.

“I don’t know,” Bernardo said. “I almost think I see Bardo as he was in youth—better than this painting shows him in age. Your father had fire in his eyes when he was young. I never understood how someone so passionate, so impatient, could spend his life among books and shadows. But he gave his heart to it.” He shrugged slightly, and Romola heard in his voice a resonance with her own thoughts.

“And he remained disappointed until the end,” she murmured, then quickly added, fearing she might seem to blame Tito, “If only his greatest, most cherished wish could be fulfilled—just as he imagined it!”

“Well, perhaps it may yet,” Bernardo said kindly, rising and placing his cap on his head. “The times are stormy, but fish are caught by waiting. Who knows? When the wheel turns enough, I may yet be Gonfaloniere before I die. And no creditor can touch these things.” He glanced around the room. Then, turning to her and patting her cheek, he said, “And you needn’t fear my dying—my ghost will claim nothing. I’ve seen to that in my will.”

Romola took his hand and pressed it to her lips, silent.

“Have you been scolding your husband for staying away so much lately?” Bernardo asked, wishing to shift the mood. “I see him everywhere except here.”

A flush crept over Romola’s neck and face as she replied, “He has been in great demand—you know how well he speaks. I’m glad his worth is recognized.”

“You’re content, then, Madonna Orgogliosa?” Bernardo asked, smiling, as he moved toward the door.

“Assuredly.”

Poor Romola! There was one thing that would have made her disappointment in her husband even harder to bear: the knowledge that others might see him as the cause of her pain. It was perhaps a woman’s vulnerability—but one closely linked to her nobleness. For the woman who willingly reveals the private wounds of her marriage turns its sacred space into something common, profaned.
