“Forget the professors.” So said Madame Beck, smooth and assured as she was in all things. She was a shrewd, capable woman—but she should never have spoken those words. It was a fatal misstep. That night she ought to have left me calm, uninvested, sealed off in my own quiet, separate world, not stirred to even a flicker of connection to the very man they claimed I ought to banish entirely from my mind.

Forget him? The wiseacres around me chose the most absurd possible path to that end. They showed me just how good he was. They painted my sharp-tongued, quick-tempered little Paul Emanuel as a stainless, unassailable hero. Then they prattled on, unasked, about the depth and constancy of his capacity to love. Before that day, what certainty had I ever had that he could love deeply at all?

I had seen him jealous, suspicious; I’d caught glimpses of softness that rolled in like a warm summer breeze, and flickers of pity that vanished as quickly as morning dew, dried away in the sudden heat of his irritability. That was all I’d known of him. But Père Silas and Modeste Maria Beck—there was no doubt the two had acted in perfect concert—opened the hidden chamber of his heart to me. They showed me a grand, all-consuming love, born in the fiery ardor of his southern youth, so strong and perfect that it had laughed in Death’s very face, scorned his petty theft of mortal flesh, clung fast to the undying spirit, and stood faithful vigil beside a tomb for twenty long years.

This was no idle, indulgent daydream. He had proven that fidelity by pouring all his best energies into unselfish purpose, sacrificing every worldly comfort without limit for those who had once been dear to the woman he loved. For her sake, he had set aside all thoughts of vengeance, and taken up a cross no other man would have chosen to bear.

As for Justine Marie, I felt I knew her as clearly as if we had met face to face. She was the quiet, phlegmatic sort I had seen a dozen times in Madame Beck’s school: pale, slow, placid, kind-natured, innocent of evil but never remarkable for any great good.
If others pictured her wearing angel’s wings, I knew whose poetic fancy had fastened them to her shoulders. If they claimed her forehead glowed with the reflected light of a holy halo, I knew in whose dark, fiery eyes that ring of sacred flame had first been kindled.

Was I meant to be terrified by the ghost of a pale dead nun, to see her rise as an eternal barrier between Paul and the rest of the world? What of all the charities that had eaten away at his entire fortune? What of the vow that bound his heart to a life of consecrated singleness?
Madame Beck, Père Silas—you ought never to have planted those questions in my mind. They were the deepest puzzle, the sturdiest obstacle, and the sharpest, most thrilling stimulus I had ever known. For a whole week, sleeping and waking, I turned those two questions over and over in my head. There was no answer to be found anywhere in the wide world, except in the place where that short, dark little man stood, sat, walked, lectured, wearing his faded bandit-style bonnet-grec and his shabby old paletôt, spattered with ink and stained with age and smoke.

After that strange visit to the Rue des Mages, I ached to see him again. It felt as if, now that I knew the truth of him, his face would read like a clearer, more fascinating book than it ever had before. I longed to trace in his features the imprint of that early, unshakable devotion, the marks of that half-knightly, half-saintly chivalry the priest’s story had revealed beneath his gruff exterior. He had become my Christian hero, and I burned to see him under that new light.

Opportunity did not take long to arrive. My new impressions were put to the test the very next day. I was granted an audience with my supposed Christian hero—an interview that was far from heroic, sentimental, or biblical, but lively enough in its own chaotic way.

At three o’clock that afternoon, the peaceful hum of the first classe, overseen by Madame Beck herself as she gave one of her calm, clear, useful lessons, was shattered entirely by the wild, unannounced burst of a shabby old paletôt through the door.
I had never been quieter at that moment. Madame Beck’s presence had lifted all responsibility from my shoulders, her even tones soothed me, and I was happily bent over my desk, copying an elaborate line engraving, painstakingly working my copy up to match the perfect finish of the original. I took absurd pleasure in the tedious labour back then, producing fussy, almost Chinese facsimiles of steel engravings—works as trivial as fancy needlework, but which I thought very fine in those days.

In an instant, my drawing, my pencils, my precious half-finished copy were crushed into a crumpled heap and swept from my sight. I felt shaken right out of my chair, like a lone withered nutmeg tipped out of a spice box by an overexcited cook. That chair, and my desk, were seized by the man in the flying paletôt, one tucked under each arm, and borne away across the room. A second later I was following my furniture, and in two minutes the three of us were set down firmly in the very centre of the grand salle—the vast, seldom-used hall reserved for dancing and choral lessons—plunked down with enough emphasis to make me fear we would never be allowed to leave.

When I’d gathered my scattered wits, I saw two men before me, both flourishing full sets of moustaches, sidewhiskers, and imperial beards. One was dark, stiff and half-military in bearing, wearing a braided frock coat; the other had the careless, slouching air of a student or artist. M. Emanuel stood a little apart from them, his face dark with suppressed rage, one hand outstretched in the dramatic, speaking gesture he used at his lecture tribune.
“Mademoiselle,” he said, “you must prove to these gentlemen that I am no liar. You will answer their questions to the best of your ability. You will also write on whatever theme they choose. It seems they believe I am an unprincipled imposter: that I write essays myself, deliberately forge my pupils’ names to them, and boast that the work is theirs. You will disprove this lie.”

Great heavens. The show trial I had spent so long dodging had crashed down on me like a thunderbolt. These two sneering, foppish, braided, mustachioed figures were none other than Messieurs Boissec and Rochemorte, dandy professors from the college—cold-blooded pedants, sceptics, and cruel scoffers. M. Paul had rashly shown them an essay I had written once, a piece he had never praised or even mentioned to me, which I had long thought forgotten. It was not remarkable by any standard, only looked exceptional compared to the average work of foreign schoolgirls; in an English school it would have passed almost unnoticed. The two men had chosen to claim it was a forgery, and now I was to be dragged through their cruel examination to clear M. Emanuel’s name.

What followed was a scene I will never forget.
They began with classics. I drew a total blank. They moved on to French history. I could scarcely tell Mérovée apart from Pharamond. They quizzed me on every branch of learning they could name, and all they got was a shake of my head, and the same unchanging reply: “I do not know.”
After a significant, annoyed pause, they moved on to general information, broaching subjects I knew well, and had thought deeply about. M. Emanuel, who had stood watching dark as the winter solstice, brightened a little. He thought I would finally prove I was no fool.

He was wrong. Answers surged up in my mind fast, filling me like a rising well—all the ideas were there, but no words would come out. I could not, or would not speak; partly I think my nerves had completely tangled, and partly my pride was up in arms. I heard the man in the braided coat whisper to his friend: “Is she actually an idiot?”
Yes, I thought. An idiot I am, and always will be, for the likes of you.

I suffered, cruelly. I saw damp beads of sweat gather on M. Paul’s brow, and his eyes held a bitter, sad reproach. He refused to believe I was naturally slow to speak; he thought I could be quick and sharp if I only tried.
At last, to release him, the professors, and myself from the torture, I stammered out:
“Gentlemen, you had best let me go. You will get nothing useful from me. As you say, I am an idiot.”
I wished I could have spoken with calm dignity, or that I had the sense to hold my tongue. My traitorous tongue tripped and faltered. I saw the two judges cast a triumphant, mocking look at M. Emanuel, heard the shaky distress in my own voice, and burst into choking tears. The emotion was far more anger than grief. If I had been a strong man, I would have challenged both of them to duels on the spot—but it was raw, ungoverned feeling, and I would far rather have been publicly whipped than have let them see it.

The fools! Could they not spot the unpolished work of a novice in that composition they called a forgery? I had started with nothing but a subject M. Paul had dictated to me, entirely new to me. I had hunted through books for facts, painstakingly built a skeleton out of dry, bare history, then clothed those bones in words and tried to breathe life into them. It had taken weeks of anxious work to arrange all the facts properly, to scrub out every flaw or falsehood, but that knowledge was not ripe, ready-stored in my head. I had to glean every bit of it fresh, like wild herbs, and shred them green into the pot. Boissec and Rochemorte could not see that. They mistook my rough, laboured work for that of a full-grown, experienced scholar.

They would not let me go. I had to sit down and write before them. My hand shook as I dipped my pen in ink, and I blinked at the blank page through tear-blurred eyes, as one of the professors began to mince out an apology for causing me pain.
“We act in the interests of truth. We do not wish to hurt you,” he said.
Scorn gave me nerve. I only answered: “Dictate, Monsieur.”
Rochemorte named the theme: “Human Justice.”

Human Justice? It was a blank, cold abstraction, not a single inspiring idea came to me. M. Emanuel stood sad as Saul, stern as Joab, and his accusers gloated.
I looked straight at them. I was gathering my courage to tell them I would not speak or write another word for their amusement, that their theme was hateful and their presence uninspiring, and that anyone who cast a shadow of doubt on M. Emanuel’s honour was the one truly defying the truth they claimed to champion—when a sudden flash of memory hit me.
Those two faces, half-hidden behind a thicket of long hair and whiskers—cold, bold, distrustful, arrogant—were the very same faces I had seen projected in gaslight from behind a portico pillar, the night I arrived desolate and friendless in Villette, the night they had chased me breathless across a whole quarter of the town and half-frightened me to death.
“Pious mentors,” I thought. “Pure guides for the young! If Human Justice were what she ought to be, you two would never hold your posts, or enjoy your fine reputations.”

The idea took root, and I set to work. “Human Justice” rushed before me as a ragged, red-faced old harridan, arms akimbo, holding court in a den of confusion. Servants screamed for her help and orders, and she never answered. Beggars starved on her doorstep, ignored. A swarm of sick, quarrelsome children crawled around her feet, yelling for sympathy and redress, and she cared nothing for any of it. She kept a warm seat by the fire, a short black pipe, and a bottle of soothing syrup for her own comfort. She smoked and sipped, lost in her own small paradise. Whenever a cry of suffering pierced her ears too sharply, she seized the poker or the hearth brush: if the sufferer was weak, wronged, and sickly, she beat them into silence; if they were strong and loud and troublesome, she only threatened, then reached deep into her pocket and tossed a shower of sugarplums to bribe them into quiet.

That was the rough, scathing sketch of “Human Justice” I scratched hastily onto paper, and handed over to Messieurs Boissec and Rochemorte. M. Emanuel read it over my shoulder. I did not wait for their comments. I curtsied to all three, and left.

After school that day, M. Paul and I met again. The meeting was not smooth at first. There was a score to settle between us. That wild, forced examination could not be digested in an instant. Our cross, snappish dialogue ended with him calling me “a little mocker, heartless and cold,” and storming off.
I did not want him to leave entirely, only to feel that the kind of unruly outburst he had indulged in that day could not go unchallenged. I was glad, then, to spot him soon after, digging in the berceau, the garden bower. He approached the glass door, and I drew near too. We spoke first of the flowers growing beside the frame. He laid down his spade. Slowly, he turned the conversation to other subjects, until at last he touched on the thing we had both been avoiding.
He knew his behaviour that day had been wildly extravagant. He half-apologised, half-regretted his own fitful moods, and hinted that I ought to make allowances for him. “But,” he said, “I can hardly expect that from you, Miss Lucy. You know nothing of me, or my position, or my history.”

His history. I seized on the word at once.
“No, Monsieur,” I replied. “Of course, as you say, I know neither your history, nor your position, nor your sacrifices, nor any of your sorrows, trials, affections, or loyalties. Oh no, I know nothing at all about you. You are a complete stranger to me.”
“Hein?” he murmured, arching his brows in surprise.
“You know, Monsieur, I only ever see you in the classroom: stern, dogmatic, hasty, imperious. In town, I hear of you as active and wilful, quick to start new schemes, hasty to lead others, but slow to persuade, almost impossible to bend. A man like you, with no ties, can have no attachments. With no dependants, no duties. All of us you come into contact with are just machines you push around, careless of our feelings. You seek your amusements in public, under the chandelier light of evening. This school and the college are your workshops, where you turn out the goods you call pupils. I don’t even know where you live. It is only natural to assume you have no home, and need none.”

“I see how you judge me,” he said. “Your opinion of me is exactly what I feared it was. To you I am neither a man nor a Christian. You see me empty of affection and religion, with no friends or family to bind me, no faith or principle to guide me. Very well, Mademoiselle. That is the reward we get in this life.”
“You are a philosopher, Monsieur,” I said, glancing pointedly at his shabby paletôt, which he immediately brushed at self-consciously. “A cynical philosopher, who despises humanity’s foibles, rises above its luxuries, and does not need its petty comforts.”
“And you, Mademoiselle? You are neat, soft, cosy, and frightfully unfeeling, on top of everything else.”
“But honestly, Monsieur, you must live somewhere, do you not? Tell me where. What household of servants do you keep?”

He thrust his lower lip out in a scowl of dramatic scorn. “I live in a hole! I inhabit a den, Miss—a cavern you would not dare poke your dainty little nose into. Once, stupidly ashamed to speak the full truth, I called my room at the college my ‘study.’ Now you know that ‘study’ is my entire home. My bedroom is there, my sitting room too. As for my ‘servants’” —he mimicked my prim voice— “there are ten of them. Here they are.”
He spread all ten grimy fingers out under my eyes.
“I black my own boots,” he went on savagely. “I brush my own paletôt.”
“No, Monsieur, it is far too shabby, you never do that,” I put in.
“I make my own bed, do all my own housework. I eat my dinner at a restaurant, my supper is whatever I can scrounge. I pass long days of unremitting work, with no one to love, and long nights of total loneliness. I am ferocious, bearded, almost monkish. Nothing alive in this world loves me, except a few old hearts as worn out as my own, and a handful of impoverished, suffering people, poor in purse and spirit, whom the world does not want, but whom the gospel has promised the kingdom of heaven.”

“Ah, Monsieur—but I know all that.”
“What do you know? A great many things, I don’t doubt—but you do not know me, Lucy!”
“I know you have a pleasant old house in a quiet old square in Basse-Ville. Why do you not go and live there?”
“Hein?” he muttered again, surprised.
“I liked it very much, Monsieur. The steps leading up to the door, the grey flagstones out front, the tall old trees behind—real trees, not shrubs, dark and mature, growing for decades. And the little oratory boudoir—you should turn that into your study. It is so quiet and solemn.”

He stared at me closely, half-smiling, a dark flush rising to his cheeks. “Where did you hear all that? Who told you?”
“Nobody told me. Do you think I dreamed it, Monsieur?”
“How am I to guess a woman’s waking thoughts, much less her sleeping fantasies?”
