For the next three or four days, nothing happened to make Elinor regret her decision to seek advice from her mother; Willoughby neither visited nor sent a letter. By the end of this period, they were scheduled to accompany Lady Middleton to a gathering, an event from which Mrs. Jennings was excused due to the illness of her youngest child. For this occasion, Marianne appeared utterly disheartened, neglectful of her attire, and seemingly indifferent to whether she attended or stayed behind. She sat by the drawing-room fire after tea until Lady Middleton's arrival, remaining motionless in her seat, lost in thought and oblivious to her sister's presence. Only when they were summoned to the door did she start as if she had momentarily forgotten anyone was expected.

Upon arriving at the venue, they alighted as soon as the line of carriages allowed, climbed the stairs, and heard their names called aloud from one landing to the next before entering a room brilliantly illuminated, packed with guests, and stiflingly warm. After offering the customary courtesy to the hostess, they mingled with the crowd, inevitably adding to the discomfort of the heat. After spending some time exchanging minimal pleasantries, Lady Middleton seated herself at the game table. Since Marianne lacked the energy to move about, Elinor and Marianne fortunately secured seats near the table.

It was not long before Elinor spotted Willoughby, standing just a few yards away, engaged in earnest conversation with a highly fashionable young woman. He quickly caught her gaze and bowed, yet made no attempt to speak to her or approach Marianne, despite clearly seeing her, continuing his discourse with the same lady. Elinor instinctively glanced at Marianne to gauge her reaction. It was at that precise moment that Marianne first noticed him; her entire face flushed with sudden joy, and she would have rushed toward him instantly had her sister not seized her arm.

"Good heavens!" she cried. "He is here—he is there! Oh, why doesn't he look at me? Why can't I speak to him?"

"Please, please compose yourself," Elinor urged. "Do not reveal your feelings to everyone present. Perhaps he hasn't seen you yet."

However, this reassurance offered no comfort to Marianne, who found it impossible to remain calm or even desire to do so. She sat trembling with an agony of impatience that distorted every feature. Finally, Willoughby turned around and looked at them both. Marianne sprang up, uttering his name with affectionate emphasis, and extended her hand. He approached, directing his words primarily to Elinor as if wishing to avoid her gaze, deliberately ignoring her gesture, and inquired hurriedly about Mrs. Dashwood and how long they had been in London.

Elinor was so stunned by this greeting that she lost all presence of mind and could not utter a word. Yet, her sister's emotions were immediately evident. Her face turned crimson, and in a voice thick with emotion, she exclaimed, "Good God! Willoughby, what is the meaning of this? Have you not received my letters? Will you not shake hands with me?"

He could no longer avoid it, but her touch seemed painful to him, and he held her hand for only a fleeting moment. Throughout this exchange, he was visibly struggling to maintain his composure. Elinor watched his face and noted its expression growing increasingly tranquil. After a brief pause, he spoke calmly.

"I did myself the honor of calling in Berkeley Street last Tuesday and deeply regretted not finding you and Mrs. Jennings at home. I trust my card was not lost."

"But have you not received my notes?" Marianne shrieked in wild anxiety. "There must be some mistake—some dreadful mistake. What could possibly explain this? Tell me, Willoughby; for heaven's sake, tell me what is wrong?"

He offered no reply. His complexion darkened, and all his earlier embarrassment returned. However, upon catching the eye of the young lady with whom he had been conversing, he felt compelled to exert himself instantly. Recovering his poise, he said, "Yes, I had the pleasure of receiving notice of your arrival in town, which you were so kind as to send me," gave a slight, hasty bow, and turned away to join his friend.

Marianne, now looking ghastly pale and unable to stand, sank back into her chair. Elinor, fearing she might faint, attempted to shield her from the view of others while reviving her with lavender water.

"Go to him, Elinor," Marianne cried as soon as she could speak. "Force him to come to me. Tell him I must see him again—I must speak to him instantly. I cannot rest; I shall know no peace until this is explained. Some terrible misunderstanding must exist. Oh, go to him right now."

"How can that be done? No, my dearest Marianne, you must wait. This is not the place for explanations. Wait only until tomorrow."

With great difficulty, Elinor managed to prevent her from pursuing him herself. Persuading Marianne to curb her agitation and appear composed until she could speak privately and effectively was impossible; Marianne continued to break down in whispers, lamenting her misery. Shortly thereafter, Elinor saw Willoughby leave the room via the staircase. Telling Marianne he was gone, she argued against the possibility of further contact that evening, urging her to remain calm. Instantly, Marianne begged Elinor to ask Lady Middleton to take them home, claiming she was too wretched to stay another minute. Though Lady Middleton was mid-game, she was too polite to object. Exchanging cards with a friend, the sisters departed as soon as a carriage could be found.

Scarcely a word was exchanged on the journey back to Berkeley Street. Marianne was in a silent agony, too overwhelmed even to weep. Fortunately, Mrs. Jennings had not yet returned, allowing them to proceed straight to their rooms, where smelling salts eventually brought Marianne back to herself. Soon undressed and in bed, and apparently desiring solitude, her sister left her. While waiting for Mrs. Jennings' return, Elinor had ample opportunity to reflect on the day's events.

She could not doubt that a prior engagement existed between Willoughby and Marianne, nor that Willoughby was now weary of it. Regardless of how Marianne might still nourish her own hopes, Elinor could not attribute such behavior to error or misunderstanding. Nothing short of a complete change of heart could account for it. Her indignation would have been far greater had she not witnessed the embarrassment that suggested a consciousness of his own misconduct, preventing her from believing him so unprincipled as to have toyed with her sister's affections from the start without cause. Absence might have dulled his regard, and convenience might have driven him to suppress it, but she could never convince herself that such a regard had never existed.

As for Marianne, reflecting on the pain such an unfortunate meeting had already caused, and the even severer suffering likely to follow, filled Elinor with profound sorrow. In comparison, her own situation seemed preferable; while she might love Edward as deeply as ever, whatever their future separation, her mind could find support. Conversely, every circumstance seemed to conspire to intensify Marianne's misery through a final, irrevocable separation from Willoughby—an immediate and unyielding rupture.
