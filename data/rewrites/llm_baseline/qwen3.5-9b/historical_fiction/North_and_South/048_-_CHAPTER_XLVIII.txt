"My own, my father's friend! I cannot part with thee! I ne'er have shown, thou ne'er hast known, How dear thou art to me." — *Anon.*

The atmosphere of the dinner parties hosted by Mrs. Lennox was defined by a specific alchemy: her guests supplied the beauty, Captain Lennox provided the effortless mastery of current topics, and Mr. Henry Lennox, accompanied by a select group of young men regarded as his intimates, contributed wit, sharp intellect, and a breadth of knowledge wielded with such finesse that it appeared neither pedantic nor burdensome. These gatherings were undeniably delightful. Yet, even within this glittering circle, Margaret's dissatisfaction inevitably surfaced. Every talent, every sentiment, every acquired skill—and indeed, every inclination toward virtue—was consumed as fuel for fireworks; the sacred, hidden flame burned itself out in mere sparkle and crackle. They discussed art through a purely sensual lens, fixated on external effects rather than seeking its deeper lessons. They fanned themselves into an artificial fervor regarding lofty subjects in the company of others, only to abandon those thoughts entirely once they were alone. Their capacity for genuine appreciation was squandered in a torrent of socially appropriate words.

One evening, after the gentlemen had retired to the drawing-room, Mr. Lennox approached Margaret. It was nearly the first voluntary address he had directed toward her since her return to Harley Street.

"You did not appear pleased by what Mr. Shirley said at dinner," he observed.

"Did I not?" Margaret replied smoothly. "My countenance must be remarkably expressive. It seems to have retained its eloquence."

"I did not approve," Margaret stated quickly, "of his manner of championing what he knew to be erroneous—so blatantly wrong, even in jest."

"But it was exceedingly clever. How precisely each word landed! Do you recall the felicitous epithets?"

"Yes."

"And yet, you would likely disdain them if you considered them seriously. Please, do not hesitate to express that, despite his being my friend."

"There!" she exclaimed, halting abruptly. She paused, expecting him to complete the thought she had started, but he remained silent, watching to see if she would elaborate. Instead, she simply flushed crimson and turned away. Before she could withdraw completely, she caught the soft, clear murmur of his voice: "If my tone, or my mode of thinking, is what you dislike, will you grant me the courtesy of telling me so directly? That would give me the opportunity to learn how to please you."

For several weeks thereafter, there was no news of Mr. Bell's departure for Milton. He had mentioned the possibility during his stay at Helstone, suggesting it might happen soon, yet he must have handled any necessary arrangements via correspondence. Margaret reasoned that, if able, he would avoid traveling to a place he disliked and which he would struggle to comprehend, especially given the intimate nature of the explanation she felt required personal delivery. She knew he understood the necessity of the meeting, yet the timing mattered less to her than the fact that it had not occurred. It was now August, and there had been no mention of the Spanish journey he had hinted at for Edith. Margaret struggled to reconcile herself to the fading of this hope.

Then, one morning, a letter arrived. It announced that Mr. Bell intended to come to town the following week to discuss a plan forming in his mind. Furthermore, he planned to indulge in some medical treatment, having begun to accept her view that his irritability stemmed from physical frailty rather than character flaws. Reading between the lines, Margaret noted a forced cheerfulness in his tone—a detail she only recognized in retrospect. At the time, her attention was captured by Edith's exclamations.

"He's coming to town! Oh dear! And I am so utterly drained by the heat that I doubt I possess the strength for another dinner. Besides, everyone has departed except our dear, dull selves, who cannot decide where to go next. There will be no one to meet him."

"I am certain he would prefer dining with us in private company rather than among the most agreeable strangers you could possibly assemble. Moreover, if he is unwell, he will not seek invitations. I am glad he has finally admitted it. I was convinced he was ill from the entire tenor of his letters, yet he refused to answer my inquiries, leaving me with no confidant to update me."

"Oh! He is not severely ill, or he would not entertain thoughts of Spain."

"He never mentions Spain."

"No! But the scheme he intends to propose clearly relates to that region. However, would you truly venture out in weather like this?"

"Oh, it will cool down daily. Yes! Consider it. I am only afraid that I have indulged in such absorbing, wilful wishing—a habit destined either for disappointment or for satisfaction that feels hollow in spirit. It is akin to the prayer, 'Give me children, or else I die.' I fear my cry is, 'Let me go to Cadiz, or else I die.'"

"My dearest Margaret! You will surely be persuaded to stay put; and then what shall I do? Oh! I wish I could find someone suitable for you to marry here, ensuring your presence remains secure!"

"I shall never marry."

"Nonsense! Double nonsense! As Sholto remarks, you are such an attraction to the household that he knows countless men who would gladly visit next year solely for your sake."

Margaret straightened her posture with haughty dignity. "Do you know, Edith, I sometimes suspect your life in Corfu has imparted..."

"Well?"

"Just a shade or two of coarseness."

Edith immediately burst into bitter sobs, declaring with vehemence that Margaret had lost all affection for her and ceased to regard her as a friend. Overwhelmed by the sting of wounded pride, Margaret realized she had voiced an opinion too harsh for the relief she sought, and by the end of the day, she had become Edith's devoted slave. Meanwhile, the little lady, overwhelmed by her hurt feelings, lay like a fallen victim upon the sofa, heaving deep sighs until sleep claimed her.

Mr. Bell failed to appear on the day he had postponed his visit a second time. The following morning, a letter from Wallis, his servant, reached them. It stated that his master had been unwell for some time—the true cause of his delay—and that just as he was preparing to depart for London, he suffered an apoplectic seizure. Wallis added that the attending physicians believed he could not survive the night, making it highly probable that by the time Miss Hale received the missive, her former master would already be gone.

Margaret received this letter at breakfast. Her complexion drained instantly as she read it. Silently handing the note to Edith, she rose and withdrew from the room. Edith was profoundly shaken, reading the news with a sobbing, frightened, childlike demeanor that distressed her husband greatly. Mrs. Shaw was eating breakfast in her own quarters, and the responsibility of reconciling his wife to the terrifying proximity of death—for the first time in her life—fell upon him. Here was a man scheduled to dine with them today, now potentially dead or dying! It took considerable time before she could summon the resolve to think of Margaret. Suddenly, she sprang up and followed her daughter upstairs to Margaret's room.

Dixon was packing a few toiletry items, while Margaret hastily donned her bonnet, weeping continuously. Her hands trembled so violently she could barely fasten the ribbons.

"Oh, dear Margaret! How shocking! What are you doing? Are you going out? Sholto would telegraph or do anything you desire."

"I am going to Oxford. A train departs in half an hour. Dixon has offered to accompany me, but I could manage alone. I must see him again. Besides, he may be recovering and require care. He has been like a father to me. Do not detain me, Edith."

"But I must insist. Mother will disapprove entirely. Come and consult her; you do not know where you are going. I would not mind if he had his own residence, but staying in his Fellow's rooms! Come to Mother and ask her permission before you leave. It will not take a moment."

Margaret relented, missing her connection. Caught in the suddenness of the tragedy, Mrs. Shaw became bewildered and hysterical, causing the precious minutes to slip away unnoticed. However, another train departed in two hours. After various debates concerning propriety and impropriety, it was settled that Captain Lennox would accompany Margaret. The one constant in her resolve was her determination to travel by the next available train, regardless of what anyone said about the suitability of the step. Her father's friend, her own friend, lay on the brink of death; the realization struck her with such vivid force that she was astonished at the firmness with which she asserted her right to act independently. Five minutes before departure, she found herself seated in a railway carriage opposite Captain Lennox.

It was always a comfort to her to believe she had acted correctly, even if the outcome was merely to confirm his passing during the night. She recalled the rooms he had occupied, associating them forevermore with fond memories of her father and his single, cherished, faithful companion. They had promised Edith prior to leaving that, should their fears prove true, they would return for dinner. Thus, the long, lingering gaze around the chamber where her father had breathed his last had to be interrupted, replaced by a quiet farewell to the kindly old face that had so often offered pleasant words, merry quips, and eccentricities.
