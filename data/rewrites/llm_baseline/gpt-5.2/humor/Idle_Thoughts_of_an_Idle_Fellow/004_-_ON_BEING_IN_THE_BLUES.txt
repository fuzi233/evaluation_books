I can take a melancholy mood as I can take a glass of bitter beer: with a certain relish, and even a pride in the face I pull while swallowing it. There is an honest comfort, too, in being properly wretched—like sitting down, of your own free will, to a dish that you know will scorch your tongue and punish you afterward, simply because it is the dish that suits the weather.

But nobody enjoys a fit of the blues.

The blues are not sorrow with a pedigree. They are not grief with a reason, nor regret with a name you can pronounce in your mother’s dialect and have it land, warm and exact, on the table between you like a loaf. The blues are a sudden, sour mis-seasoning of the soul; a thing that comes in, uninvited, like a gust from the street that drags soot across your clean cloth. Everybody has them; nobody can tell you why. You are as likely to be seized by them on the morning after you have come into a fortune as on the morning after you have left your new silk umbrella in the train—both events equally capable of throwing the universe out of joint, it appears, provided the universe is in a mischievous mood.

Their effect upon you is what might be produced by toothache, indigestion, and a cold in the head combining into a little band and marching through your person, beating tin pans. The world’s sounds go wrong first. The ordinary hum of living—kettle-lid, cart-wheel, distant children—ceases to be a choir and becomes a factory. The street’s roar is not merely loud; it is intrusive, like a neighbour playing a waltz on a cracked cornet directly into your ear and calling it Art. Your own breathing seems badly tuned. Even the tick of the clock grows insolent, as though it were being paid by the insult.

You become stupid, restless, and irritable; rude to strangers and dangerous to your friends; clumsy, maudlin, quarrelsome; a nuisance to yourself and everybody about you. While it is on you, you can do nothing and think of nothing—though you feel at the time bound to do something, as a cook feels bound to “make a dinner” when the larder is empty and the knives all blunt.

You cannot sit still. You put on your hat and go out for a walk; but before you get to the corner you wish you had not come out, because the pavement has begun to sound like a drum, and the voices of the passers-by are as sharp as grated horseradish. You turn back, convinced the air is not fit for human lungs and that every cart in London has made it its mission to choose your street for rehearsal.

You open a book and try to read, because books are supposed to be soothing; but to a man in the blues literature is like cold tea. Shakespeare tastes trite and commonplace, as though he had been reheated too often. Dickens is dull and prosy, like stew that has been kept on the back of the range all day and has lost its good fat. Thackeray is a bore—dry as yesterday’s crust. Carlyle is too sentimental, which is a curious complaint to make, but the blues are great at taking a reasonable objection and salting it to death. You fling the book aside and call the author names in a vocabulary you would be ashamed to use in any decent kitchen.

Then you “shoo” the cat out of the room, not because she has done anything, but because her quiet padding suggests contentment, and contentment is an offence. You kick the door to after her, making a bang that hurts you more than it hurts the door, and you are instantly furious with the door for being made of wood and not feeling it.

You think you will write your letters. You sit down with paper as white as flour and a pen that has the effrontery to drip. You begin:

“Dearest Auntie: I find I have five minutes to spare, and so hasten to write to you—”

and there you stick, as if the sentence were a dumpling that refuses to cook through. You stare at it for a quarter of an hour, unable to think of another line, because in the blues every word in the language becomes either too big, too small, or in the wrong dialect. There are feelings you can taste clearly enough—like burnt sugar, like stale butter—but when you go to name them in the common tongue they will not translate. They sit in you mutely, as unrenderable as a grandmother’s old field-saying that has no equivalent outside its valley. You tumble the paper into the desk, fling the wet pen down upon the table-cloth, and start up with the resolution of going to see the Thompsons.

While pulling on your gloves, however, you remember that the Thompsons are idiots; that they never have supper; and that you will be expected to jump the baby. It is a terrible thing, in the blues, to think of a house where there is no supper. Even despair draws the line at tea and no solid. You curse the Thompsons (not for being themselves—that would be too philosophical—but for having arranged the universe so that you must meet them) and decide not to go.

By this time you feel completely crushed. You bury your face in your hands and think you would like to die and go to heaven. You picture your own sick-bed with a cook’s attention to details you would never show in health: the position of the pillows, the shade of the light, the hush in the room that is not peace but performance. All your friends and relations are standing round, weeping. You bless them all, especially the young and pretty ones, because the blues have an odd instinct for decoration. You imagine how they will value you when you are gone, and learn too late what they have lost; and you bitterly contrast their presumed reverence then with their decided want of veneration now.

This makes you feel a little more cheerful—for a brief period. Then the blues, who allow no comfort to stand unchallenged, whisper in a colder voice: What a fool you are to suppose anybody would care at anything that might happen to you. Who would care two straws—whatever exact measure of affection two straws represent—whether you are blown up, hung up, married, or drowned? Nobody cares for you. You have never been properly appreciated, never met with your due deserts in any one particular. You review your whole past life, and it is painfully apparent that you have been ill-used from your cradle; and it is astonishing, when you come to think of it, that a creature so persecuted has managed to eat as many dinners as you have.

Half an hour of such thinking works you up into a state of savage fury against everybody and everything—especially yourself, whom anatomical reasons alone prevent your kicking. The blues are remarkably ingenious at turning a man into his own enemy, setting him to scolding himself in a language he would not permit a stranger to use, because self, in these moments, is a conquered province.

Bed-time comes at last, to save you from doing something rash. You spring upstairs, throw off your clothes, leaving them strewn about the room like the wreck of an argument, blow out the candle, and jump into bed as if you had backed yourself for a heavy wager to do the whole thing against time.

There you toss and tumble for a couple of hours, varying the monotony by occasionally jerking the clothes off and getting out and putting them on again, as though trousers were a remedy. You listen to the house’s noises: the settling of boards, the sigh of the fire going out, the distant clink of something in the scullery that is probably nothing at all. In the blues, every small sound is large and every large sound is personal. The world has no right to creak without consulting you. At length you drop into an uneasy, fitful slumber, have bad dreams, and wake up late the next morning—heavy as a pudding that has never known a proper suet.

At least, this is all we poor single men can do under the circumstances.

Married men manage their blues with more variety. They bully their wives, grumble at the dinner, and insist on the children’s going to bed. Which, creating as it does a good deal of disturbance in the house, must be a great relief to the feelings of a man in the blues—rows being the only form of amusement in which he can take any interest. There is a comfort, no doubt, in being able to turn your inward discord into outward noise; it is like having a pot boil over and blaming the fire. Single men must content themselves with internal racket.

The symptoms of the infirmity are much the same in every case, but the affliction is variously termed, because language loves to pretend it has a word for everything even when it hasn’t. The poet says that “a feeling of sadness comes o’er him,” which sounds as if it had arrived in a cloak with a calling-card. ’Arry refers to the heavings of his wayward heart by confiding to Jimee that he has “got the blooming hump,” which is at least honest in its ugliness. Your sister does not know what is the matter with her to-night. She feels out of sorts altogether and hopes nothing is going to happen. The every-day young man is “so awful glad to meet you, old fellow,” for he does “feel so jolly miserable this evening.” As for myself, I generally say that I have “a strange, unsettled feeling to-night,” and think I’ll go out.

It never comes except in the evening. That is one of its peculiarities. In the sun-time, when the world is bounding forward full of life, we cannot stay to sigh and sulk. The day has its own tyranny of sound: the carts, the hammers, the business voices, the purposeful footsteps. The roar of the working hours drowns the voices of those elfin sprites that are forever singing their low-toned miserere in our ears. It is not that the day is kinder; it is simply louder, and there is something in us that takes its cue from the surrounding chorus. In the daytime we are angry, disappointed, indignant; we swear and knock the furniture about; but we are never “in the blues,” never that peculiar, bottomless, unreasonable droop that seems to have been mixed in a bowl no cook could name.

If misfortune comes at ten o’clock in the morning, we—meaning you, of course—swear and knock the furniture about. But if it comes at ten P.M., we read poetry or sit in the dark and think what a hollow world this is. It is less the misfortune than the hour’s acoustics. At night, the city’s great industrial shout sinks to a murmur, and in the lull you hear yourself too clearly. Silence is not always peace; sometimes it is a magnifying glass held to your own internal clatter.

As a rule it is not trouble that makes us melancholy. Actuality is too stern a thing for sentiment. We will linger to weep over a picture, but from the original we should quickly turn our eyes away. There is no pathos in real misery; no luxury in real grief. We do not toy with sharp swords, nor hug a gnawing fox to our breast for choice. When a man or woman loves to brood over a sorrow and takes care to keep it green in memory, you may be sure it is no longer pain to them. However they suffered at first, the recollection has become, by then, a pleasure—like a sauce that once burned, but which, in the telling and retelling, has been tempered into something you can sip and call “rich.”

Many dear old ladies who daily look at tiny shoes lying in lavender-scented drawers, and weep as they think of the tiny feet whose toddling march is done; and sweet-faced young ones who place each night beneath their pillow some lock that once curled on a boyish head that the salt waves have kissed to death—these will call me a nasty cynical brute and say I am talking nonsense. Yet I believe that if they ask themselves truthfully whether they find it unpleasant thus to dwell on their sorrow, they will be compelled to answer “No.” The tears have become seasoned. The grief has been folded and kneaded by time until it is part of the daily bread of the heart. Tears are as sweet as laughter to some natures.

The proverbial Englishman, as we know from old chronicler Froissart, takes his pleasures sadly; and the Englishwoman goes a step further and takes her pleasures in sadness itself. I am not sneering. I would not sneer at anything that helps keep hearts tender in this hard old world. We men are cold and common-sensed enough for all; we would not have women the same. No, no: be always sentimental and soft-hearted as you are—be the soothing butter to our coarse dry bread. Besides, sentiment is to women what fun is to us. They do not care for our humour; surely it would be unfair to deny them their grief.

And who shall say their mode of enjoyment is less sensible than ours? Why assume that a doubled-up body, a contorted purple face, and a gaping mouth emitting ear-splitting shrieks point to a state of more intelligent happiness than a pensive face resting upon a little white hand and a pair of gentle, tear-dimmed eyes looking back through Time’s dark avenue upon a fading past? One sound is merely loud; the other is quiet but deep—more like the hush that falls on a farmhouse kitchen when the old ones begin speaking in the words they learnt first, the words that younger mouths no longer use, and everyone listens because they feel something slipping away that cannot be bought back.

I am glad when I see Regret walked with as a friend—glad because I know the saltness has been washed from the tears, and that the sting must have been plucked from the beautiful face of Sorrow ere we dare press her pale lips to ours. Time has laid his healing hand upon the wound when we can look back upon the pain we once fainted under and no bitterness or despair rises in our hearts. The burden is no longer heavy when for our past troubles we have only that sweet mingling of pleasure and pity that we feel when old knight-hearted Colonel Newcome answers “adsum” to the great roll-call, or when Tom and Maggie Tulliver, clasping hands through the mists that have divided them, go down, locked in each other’s arms, beneath the swollen waters of the Floss.

And talking of poor Tom and Maggie brings to mind a saying of George Eliot’s in connection with melancholy. She speaks somewhere of “the sadness of a summer’s evening.” How wonderfully true—like everything that came from that wonderful pen—the observation is.

Who has not felt the sorrowful enchantment of those lingering sunsets? The day withdraws not with a slam but with a long, slow simmer, as when a pot of stock is drawn down and the kitchen’s steam turns to a faint breath on the window. The world belongs to Melancholy then: a thoughtful, deep-eyed maiden who loves not the glare of day. It is not till light thickens, and the crow wings to the rocky wood, that she steals forth from her groves. Her palace is in twilight land. It is there she meets us. At her shadowy gate she takes our hand in hers and walks beside us through her mystic realm. We see no form, but seem to hear the rustling of her wings—soft as a skirt-brush in a quiet room, or the last leaves of mint being stripped and dropped into a bowl.

Even in the toiling, hum-drum city her spirit comes to us. There is a sombre presence in each long dull street; and the dark river creeps ghostlike under the black arches as if bearing some hidden secret beneath its muddy waves. The noises of trade fall away, and what remains is not music but the memory of noise, a tinnitus of civilisation. In the silent country, when the trees and hedges loom dim and blurred against the rising night, and the bat’s wing flutters in your face, and the land-rail’s cry sounds drearily across the fields, the spell sinks deeper still into our hearts. We seem, in that hour, to be standing by some unseen death-bed; and in the swaying of the elms we hear the sigh of the dying day.

A solemn sadness reigns. A great peace is around us. In its light the cares of the working day grow small and trivial, and bread and cheese—ay, and even kisses—do not seem the only things worth striving for. Thoughts we cannot speak, but only listen to, flood in upon us. And standing in the stillness under earth’s darkening dome—where every sound has room to be itself, not crushed into a jangle by wheels and iron and shouting—we feel that we are greater than our petty lives. Hung round with those dusky curtains, the world is no longer a mere dingy workshop, but a stately temple wherein man may worship; and where, at times, in the dimness, his groping hands touch God’s.
