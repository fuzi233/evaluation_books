The solitude of the day had been a heavy weight upon Caroline. With her uncle away at Whinbury, the long, bright hours of that summer afternoon had stretched out before her like a desolate waste, devoid of sound or shadow, until the mere passage of time felt as exhausting as a journey across the Sahara. She sat in the alcove, her needle moving with mechanical precision over the brown holland of a child's slip, her eyes fixed on the thread, while her mind wandered restlessly.

The silence was broken by Fanny's voice calling from the doorway. "Miss Caroline!"

A low murmur answered from the shadows of the alcove. Fanny hurried forward, holding a letter. She handed it to Caroline, who accepted it with a trembling hand, dropping it carelessly onto her work without looking at it.

"Joe Scott's son, Harry, brought it," Fanny said.

The words struck Caroline like a spark in dry tinder. She lifted her head, her eyes flashing with a sudden, vivid life. "Harry Scott! Who sent him?"

"He came from the Hollow."

Caroline snatched the note, breaking the seal with eager fingers. It was a message from Hortense, declaring her return from Wormwood Wells and her loneliness while Robert was at the market. Hortense expressed a desire for Caroline's company, suggesting that a change of scene would be beneficial to her young cousin, who had been deprived of her "meilleure amie" due to the recent misunderstanding between Robert and Mr. Helstone. The note urged Caroline to come immediately.

Caroline did not hesitate. She abandoned her sewing, climbed the stairs, and donned her straw bonnet and black silk scarf. The simple drapery suited her, accentuating the fairness of her face. She ran down the green lane toward the Hollow, inhaling the sweet scent of the hedge flowers. Though she knew Robert was not there, the act of visiting his home, of stepping into the room where he had sat that morning, felt like a reunion. Illusion, masked as a fairy, whispered in her ear that perhaps he might return while she was there, offering a fleeting moment of connection.

Miss Moore met her at the cottage porch. Dignified and phlegmatic as usual, Hortense welcomed her with a smile, though her heart was likely troubled by the intensity of Caroline's joy. Inside, the atmosphere was warm and crowded. Mrs. Yorke, the formidable matron, sat by the fire in a gown of crimson skirts and a cap of black silk so large it resembled a balloon, adorned with excessive ribbons. Her presence was a grand condescension, a rare favor bestowed upon Miss Moore, whom she admired for her steady, unattractively plain demeanor.

Caroline approached the great lady with diffidence, expecting a cool reception. Instead, she was greeted by Jessie, a small child in a blue frock who threw her arms around Caroline's neck, declaring her mother unfriendly and Rose indifferent. Jessie, however, professed to be Caroline's friend.

Rose Yorke, sitting quietly on the floor with a book, offered a brief, polite greeting before returning to her reading. Caroline, possessing a fine instinct for the peculiar, knelt beside the child. They began to read Mrs. Radcliffe's "The Italian" together. Rose, a spirited twelve-year-old, spoke passionately of her desire to travel, rejecting the static, death-like monotony of life in the rectory. She declared that to bury one's talents in household drudgery was a sin, comparing it to the parable of the talents.

Mrs. Yorke attempted to correct Rose, insisting that solid satisfaction came only from duty. Rose retorted with a bold declaration of her intent to trade her talents, refusing to let them rot in teapots or linen presses. When Mrs. Yorke criticized Caroline's "romantic" nature and lackadaisical expression, Caroline defended her feelings and her capacity for love, asserting that she would love her future husband and children with all her impulses.

Mrs. Yorke, unimpressed and perhaps bilious, turned her attention to Hortense. She questioned Caroline about her cousin's intentions, implying that Hortense might be using her to manipulate the situation regarding Robert. Caroline, sensing the insinuation, rose to her defense. She spoke clearly and firmly, rejecting the suggestion that she was a victim of craft or false sentiment. She told Mrs. Yorke that she disliked her and would answer any future insults without apology. The confrontation ended with Mrs. Yorke acknowledging Caroline's spirit, while Jessie observed that Caroline possessed an armor beneath her silk dress that Mrs. Yorke could not penetrate.

As the visitors departed, Caroline remained by the window, watching the twilight deepen. She saw Frederick Murgatroyd and Joe Scott leaving the mill, locking up for the night. Caroline sighed, resigning herself to the fact that Robert would not arrive. Just as she prepared to leave, Hortense entered, announcing that flowers had arrived from Fieldhead. She spoke of the bustling life at Fieldhead and the refusal of Miss Keeldar to let Mr. Sympson's family depart, noting that Miss Keeldar insisted on bringing her cousin Henry to Yorkshire, perhaps to oblige Robert and Hortense.

Suddenly, the kitchen door opened, and the noise of the household ceased. Joe Scott entered, followed by another man. Hortense cried out in surprise, "Mon Dieu! Est-ce que je m'y attendais?" Robert's voice confirmed the identity of the newcomer.

Caroline fled to the top of the stairs to observe. Hortense called her down, leading her to the room where two men stood. One was Robert, tall and dark, looking somewhat awkward and embarrassed. The other was his brother, Louis, who resembled Robert in height and complexion but possessed a softer, more grave nature. Hortense introduced them as strangers, creating a momentary confusion for Caroline, who realized with a laugh that she had mistaken the resemblance.

Robert and Louis sat with Caroline. Louis, a tutor, spoke quietly, his demeanor calm and reflective. Caroline found herself drawn to him, yet her heart remained anchored to the silent Robert, who leaned against the wall, turning pages of a book of engravings. Even without touching her, his presence exerted a magnetic pull. Hortense, clearing the table for supper, pointed out the flowers from Fieldhead, joking that Robert was the favorite there. When Louis asked if Robert was indeed the favorite, Hortense declared him the most precious thing in the world, while Caroline, feeling her own star dimming, simply agreed.

Realizing it was late, Caroline decided to leave. Though Robert could not detain her, the evening had been a strange mixture of illusion and reality, leaving her with a profound sense of longing and a quiet resolve to face the uncertainties ahead.
