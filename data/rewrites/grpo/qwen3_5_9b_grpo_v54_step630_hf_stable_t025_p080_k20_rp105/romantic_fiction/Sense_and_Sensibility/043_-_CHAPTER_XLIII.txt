The next morning, Marianne rose at her accustomed hour, declaring herself better and attempting to resume her usual duties. Yet, a day spent shivering before the fire, unable to read, or lying listless upon a sofa, offered little evidence of her recovery. By the time she retired early, her condition had worsened. Colonel Brandon marveled at Elinor's composure; despite nursing Marianne all day against her sister's wishes and forcing medicine upon her at night, Elinor, like Marianne, placed her faith in the healing power of sleep and felt no genuine alarm.

However, a restless and feverish night shattered their expectations. When Marianne, after insisting on rising, confessed her inability to sit and returned to bed, Elinor readily accepted Mrs. Jennings's counsel to summon the Palmers' apothecary. Mr. Harris arrived, examined the patient, and while encouraging Miss Dashwood to expect a swift recovery, his mention of a "putrid tendency" and the word "infection" sent immediate panic through Mrs. Palmer regarding her infant. Mrs. Jennings, who had suspected the illness was grave from the start, grew grave indeed at Mr. Harris's report. Confirming Charlotte's fears, she insisted on the immediate removal of the child and her nurse to a relative's house a few miles outside Bath. Mr. Palmer, though dismissing their fears as idle, found his wife's anxiety insurmountable. Her departure was arranged within the hour. She left with her son and nurse, promising to follow in a day or two, and urging her mother to accompany her. Mrs. Jennings, however, with a kindness that earned Elinor's deep affection, resolved to stay at Cleveland as long as Marianne remained ill, offering her own attentive care to replace the absent mother. Elinor found her a willing and active helper, eager to share the burden and skilled in nursing matters.

Poor Marianne, weak and low from the malady, could no longer hope for recovery by tomorrow. The thought of what that day would have brought—without this illness, they were to have begun their journey home, surprising their mother the following morning—made every ailment seem more severe. Her few words were lamentations of this inevitable delay, though Elinor tried to lift her spirits, believing as she truly did then that the delay would be short.

The next day brought little change; Marianne was neither better nor worse. The household was further diminished as Mr. Palmer, though reluctant to leave due to his humanity and dislike of appearing frightened, was finally persuaded by Colonel Brandon to fulfill his promise. As he prepared to depart, Colonel Brandon himself spoke of going as well. Here, Mrs. Jennings intervened with perfect tact. To send the Colonel away while his heart ached for his sister would deprive them both of comfort. She urged him to stay, citing her need for his company in the evenings and his ability to assist Elinor in any emergency. Mr. Palmer warmly seconded her plea, finding relief in knowing someone capable of advising Miss Dashwood remained. Marianne, of course, was kept ignorant of these arrangements. She knew not that she had caused the owners of Cleveland to depart within a week of their arrival. It surprised her nothing that she saw no sign of Mrs. Palmer, nor did it concern her enough to mention the name.

Two days passed since Mr. Palmer's departure, and Marianne's condition remained static. Mr. Harris still spoke boldly of a speedy recovery, and Elinor shared his sanguinity, though the others were far less cheerful. Mrs. Jennings had early decided Marianne would never recover, and Colonel Brandon, listening to her forebodings, found himself unable to resist their influence. He tried to reason away fears that the apothecary's judgment rendered absurd, but the long hours of solitude favored melancholy thoughts, and he could not shake the conviction that he would never see Marianne again.

On the morning of the third day, gloomy anticipations nearly vanished. Mr. Harris declared the patient materially better; her pulse was stronger, and symptoms more favorable. Elinor, confirmed in her hopes, was full of cheerfulness, rejoicing that her letters to her mother had minimized the indisposition. She almost fixed a date for Marianne's travel. But the day did not end auspiciously. Towards evening, Marianne grew ill again, becoming heavier, restless, and uncomfortable. Elinor, still optimistic, attributed the change to the fatigue of sitting up for her bed to be made. Administering cordials, she watched with satisfaction as Marianne sank into a slumber, expecting beneficial results. The sleep lasted a considerable time, though not as quiet as desired. Elinor, anxious to observe the outcome, resolved to stay with her throughout. Mrs. Jennings, unaware of the change, went to bed early; the maid, one of the principal nurses, was resting elsewhere, leaving Elinor alone with Marianne.

Marianne's repose became increasingly disturbed. Her sister, watching her continual shifts in posture and hearing frequent, inarticulate cries of pain, almost wished to rouse her from such a painful sleep. Suddenly, awakened by a noise in the house, Marianne started up with feverish wildness and cried, "Is mama coming?"

"Not yet," Elinor replied, concealing her terror and helping Marianne lie down again. "But she will be here, I hope, before long. It is a great way from here to Barton."

"But she must not go round by London!" Marianne cried in the same hurried manner. "I shall never see her if she goes by London."

Elinor perceived with alarm that Marianne was not quite herself. While attempting to soothe her, she eagerly felt her pulse. It was lower and quicker than ever! Marianne's wild talk of her mother increased Elinor's alarm so rapidly that she determined to send instantly for Mr. Harris and dispatch a messenger to Barton for her mother. Consulting Colonel Brandon on the best means to achieve this followed immediately. As soon as she had rung for the maid to take her place, she hastened to the drawing-room, where she knew he was usually found later in the day.

There was no time for hesitation. Her fears and difficulties were immediately before him. He had no courage or confidence to remove her fears; he listened to them in silent despondence. But her difficulties were instantly resolved. With a readiness that seemed pre-arranged for the occasion, he offered himself as the messenger to fetch Mrs. Dashwood. Elinor made no resistance that was not easily overcome. She thanked him with brief, fervent gratitude. While he hurried his servant to arrange post-horses directly, she wrote a few lines to her mother.

The comfort of such a friend at that moment—Colonel Brandon, or such a companion for her mother—was felt with profound gratitude. A companion whose judgment would guide, whose attendance would relieve, and whose friendship might soothe! His presence would lessen the shock of such a summons. Meanwhile, whatever he felt, he acted with the firmness of a collected mind, making every necessary arrangement with despatch and calculating exactly when she might expect his return. Not a moment was lost. The horses arrived even sooner than expected. Colonel Brandon pressed her hand with a look of solemnity, spoke a few words too low for her to hear, and hurried into the carriage. It was about twelve o'clock. She returned to Marianne's apartment to wait for the apothecary and watch through the night. It was a night of almost equal suffering: sleepless pain and delirium for Marianne, cruel anxiety for Elinor, before Mr. Harris appeared. Her apprehensions, once raised, paid for all her former security. The servant who sat with her, refusing to wake Mrs. Jennings, only tortured her more with hints of what her mistress had always feared.

Marianne's ideas remained fixed incoherently on her mother. Whenever she mentioned her name, it gave a pang to Elinor's heart. Reproaching herself for trifling with so many days of illness and wretched for lack of immediate relief, she fancied all relief might soon be in vain, that everything had been delayed too long, and pictured her suffering mother arriving too late to see this darling child or to see her rational.

She was on the point of sending for Mr. Harris again, or another doctor, when he finally arrived—not until after five o'clock. His opinion made some amends for his delay. Though acknowledging a very unexpected and unpleasant alteration in his patient, he would not allow the danger to be material. He spoke confidently of the relief a fresh mode of treatment would procure, and this confidence, in a lesser degree, was communicated to Elinor. He promised to return in three or four hours, leaving both patient and attendant more composed than he had found them.

With strong concern and many reproaches for not being called sooner, Mrs. Jennings heard in the morning of what had passed. Her former apprehensions, now restored with greater reason, left her no doubt of the event. Though trying to speak comfort to Elinor, her conviction of her sister's danger prevented her from offering hope. Her heart was truly grieved. The rapid decay and early death of a girl so young and lovely as Marianne must strike even an uninterested person with concern. On Mrs. Jennings's compassion, she had other claims: she had been Marianne's companion for three months, was still under her care, and knew how greatly injured and unhappy Marianne had been. The distress of her sister, particularly a favorite, was before her. And regarding their mother, when Mrs. Jennings considered that Marianne might be to her what Charlotte was to herself, her sympathy was very sincere.

Mr. Harris was punctual for his second visit, but he came to be disappointed in his hopes. His medicines had failed; the fever was unabated. Marianne remained in a heavy stupor, only more quiet—not more herself. Elinor, catching all his fears in a moment, proposed calling in further advice. He judged it unnecessary, claiming he had something more to try, a fresh application of which he was as confident as the last. His visit concluded with encouraging assurances that reached her ear but could not enter Elinor's heart. She was calm, except when thinking of her mother, but almost hopeless. In this state she continued until noon, scarcely stirring from her sister's bed, her thoughts wandering from one image of grief to another, her spirits oppressed by Mrs. Jennings's conversation, who scrupled not to attribute the severity of the attack to the weeks of previous indisposition caused by Marianne's disappointment. Elinor felt the reasonableness of the idea, and it added fresh misery to her reflections.

About noon, however, she began—with a dread of disappointment that kept her silent even to her friend—to fancy, to hope she could perceive a slight amendment in her sister's pulse. She waited, watched, and examined it again and again. Finally, with an agitation more difficult to bury under exterior calmness than all her foregoing distress, she ventured to communicate her hopes. Mrs. Jennings, forced by examination to acknowledge a temporary revival, tried to keep her young friend from indulging the thought of its continuance. Elinor, reciting every injunction of distrust, told herself not to hope. But it was too late. Hope had already entered, and feeling all its anxious flutter, she bent over her sister to watch—she hardly knew for what. Half an hour passed, and the favorable symptom still blessed her. Others arose to confirm it. Her breath, her skin, her lips all flattered Elinor with signs of amendment. Marianne fixed her eyes on her with a rational, though languid, gaze. Anxiety and hope now oppressed her in equal degrees, leaving her no moment of tranquility until Mr. Harris's arrival at four o'clock. His assurances and felicitations on a recovery surpassing his expectation gave her confidence, comfort, and tears of joy.

Marianne was in every respect materially better, and he declared her entirely out of danger. Mrs. Jennings, perhaps satisfied with the partial justification of her forebodings found in their recent alarm, allowed herself to trust in his judgment and admitted, with unfeigned joy and soon unequivocal cheerfulness, the probability of an entire recovery.

Elinor could not be cheerful. Her joy was of a different kind, leading to anything rather than gaiety. Marianne restored to life, health, friends, and her doting mother was an idea to fill her heart with sensations of exquisite comfort and expand it in fervent gratitude; but it led to no outward demonstrations of joy, no words, no smiles. All within Elinor's breast was satisfaction, silent and strong.

She continued by her sister's side with little intermission all afternoon, calming every fear, satisfying every inquiry of her enfeebled spirits, supplying every succour, and watching almost every look and every breath. The possibility of a relapse would occasionally remind her of her anxiety, but when she saw, on her frequent and minute examinations, that every symptom of recovery continued, and saw Marianne sink at six o'clock into a quiet, steady, and apparently comfortable sleep, she silenced every doubt.

Time was now drawing on when Colonel Brandon might be expected back. At ten o'clock, she trusted, or at least not much later, her mother would be relieved from the dreadful suspense in which she must now be traveling towards them. The Colonel, too! Perhaps scarcely less an object of pity! Oh, how slow was the progress of time that yet kept them in ignorance!

At seven o'clock, leaving Marianne still sweetly asleep, she joined Mrs. Jennings in the drawing-room for tea. She had been kept from breakfast by her fears and from dinner by their sudden reverse, so this refreshment, with such feelings of content, was particularly welcome. Mrs. Jennings would have persuaded her to rest before her mother's arrival and allow her to take her place by Marianne, but Elinor had no sense of fatigue, no capability of sleep at that moment, and was not to be kept away from her sister unnecessarily. Mrs. Jennings therefore attended her upstairs to the sick chamber to verify that all continued right, left her there to her charge and her thoughts, and retired to her own room to write letters and sleep.

The night was cold and stormy. The wind roared around the house, and the rain beat against the windows; but Elinor, all happiness within, regarded it not. Marianne slept through every blast; and the travelers—they had a rich reward in store for every present inconvenience.
