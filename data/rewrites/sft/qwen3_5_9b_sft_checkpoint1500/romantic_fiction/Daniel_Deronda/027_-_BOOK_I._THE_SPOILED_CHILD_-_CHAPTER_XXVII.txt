Desire trims the sails, yet circumstance alone provides the wind to fill them.

Outside, Grandcourt rode his magnificent black stallion, Yarico, accompanied by his groom on *Criterion*, enjoying the leisurely journey from Diplow to Offendene. Inside, however, the air was thick with a quiet, internal storm. Gwendolen sat before the mirror, her mother gently gathering the long, light-brown tresses she had just brushed into a soft coil.

"Simply gather it loosely, Mamma," Gwendolen instructed softly. "Make a single coil."

Mrs. Davilow offered to bring earrings, her gaze lingering on the glass where they both appeared. It was impossible to miss the transformation in her daughter's face; the eyes burned with a brightness unseen for days, a shadow lifted from the features to reveal lines of placid youthfulness. A cheerful note slipped into her mother's voice. "You truly desire those earrings?"

"No, Mamma," Gwendolen replied, offering one of her practiced, enigmatic smiles. "I shall wear none. I intend to dress in my black silk. Black is the appropriate attire for refusing an offer." She rose, shedding her dressing gown with a fluid motion.

"And suppose the offer is never made?" Mrs. Davilow ventured, a sly glint in her eye.

"Then it will simply be because I have refused it beforehand," Gwendolen countered, her chin lifting with a proud, defiant toss. "It amounts to the same result."

As Gwendolen descended the stairs in her long, somber robes, her posture was impeccable—a firm poise and an elastic grace that had recently been absent, like sap returning to a parched plant. Her mother watched her pass, thinking, *She is restored. It must be the joy of his arrival. Has she really resolved against him?*

If that thought had been spoken aloud, Gwendolen would have bristled. Indeed, for the last twenty hours, her mind had been a battleground of alternating images and arguments regarding Grandcourt. The resolution she had formed had evaporated, dissolved by the oscillation of counterbalancing desires. She would have maintained her stance, but it felt hollow, drained of blood, no more vital to her quivering existence than a passive prayer for divine will. She did not intend to accept him; since the letter arrived, she had intended to reject him. Yet, this resolve forced her to confront the unwelcome truths head-on, stripping away the veil of ignorance. By staring intently at a dubious future with a constructive imagination, one could conjure twenty different shapes. The vague hesitations she had felt before the meeting at the Whispering Stones now counted for nothing, submerged beneath a final, overwhelming repulsion.

"If it had not been for that day at Cardell Chase," she told herself, "there would be no barrier to our union." That day, and the ones following, had shifted her from reasoning to action. An impulsive force, born not merely of maidenly pride or jealousy, but of a profound, vague dread of wrongdoing, had driven her. Whatever aligned with the external image of a "Lady" she embraced without scruple; but from the shadow realm of disgrace, guilt, and sin, she shrank with a mixture of terror and pride. She placed any deliberate injury to another firmly within the category of guilt.

Yet, a nagging question persisted: Did she truly know the extent of Mrs. Glasher's plight? She had promised not to interfere, but was a second marriage to Grandcourt truly the fatal blow to that promise? Could it not be better for Grandcourt to marry? What limits could a woman not break if she learned to assert herself? Here, her mind wandered into constructive fantasy. Gwendolen possessed as accurate a concept of marital dynamics—as of magnetic fields and storm laws—as she did of reality. "Mamma managed poorly," she summarized her mother's failures. "I shall manage otherwise."

Her inner debate swirled with hypotheticals: *What would Mamma and Uncle say of Mrs. Glasher? What would anyone think if Mr. Grandcourt married someone else and fathered other children?* Considering "what anyone would say" freed her from the paralysis of obscurity. Memories confirmed that society viewed illegitimate children as more shameful than illegitimate fathers. The verdict of the crowd reassured her: she had no business worrying deeply for Mrs. Glasher.

However, there was another burden. Beyond public opinion lay a personal feeling of indignation and loathing—to be expected to wed an "outworn" life, heavy with backward secrets, felt like an affront to her own identity. Though the question of love for Grandcourt had played a minor role, the idea of being "enamored" belonged to him. She had found his approach acceptable until the glimpse of his past, which she resented as a calculated insult. His advances to her were deliberate, and now, viewing them retrospectively, they tasted of disgust. These feelings had sunk deep, dulled by recent troubles into a steady, firm conviction: she would not accept Grandcourt.

Had anything altered this, it would have been the prospect of saving her mother—"poor Mamma"—but no! She was going to refuse. Meanwhile, the knowledge that he was coming to be rejected invigorated her. She held the white reins again. A new current flowed through her, reviving her from the crushed consciousness left by her interview with Klesmer. She would no longer seek validation of her capabilities; she would exercise her power.

Was this why her heart hammered anxiously when she heard the horse's hooves on the gravel? When Miss Merry announced Grandcourt's presence in the drawing-room, Gwendolen realized her preparations were futile. It mattered little whether he arrived suddenly amidst despair or after careful planning.

Walking into the room, she concentrated every ounce of energy into self-control, projecting a grave, gracious demeanor. She handed him her hand, answering his inquiry about her health with a voice low and languid, mirroring his own. Seated opposite on two wreath-painted chairs, Gwendolen sat upright with downcast lids, while Grandcourt leaned back, hat in hand, gazing at her. To an observer, they appeared suspended in a stage of romantic tension. The courtship had indeed begun; she felt herself being wooed by this silent man, enveloped in an atmosphere of subtle roses and undivided attention. He, too, felt himself courting, delighted by the challenge of a girl who remained somewhat uncalculable.

"I was disappointed not to find you at Leubronn," he began, his broken drawl laced with amorous laziness. "The place was unbearable without you. A mere kennel. Do you not agree?"

"I cannot judge what it would be without me," Gwendolen replied, turning her eyes to him with a flicker of mischief. "With me, I like it well enough to stay longer, if I could. But I was forced home by family troubles."

"It was cruel of you to leave," Grandcourt said, ignoring the troubles, though Gwendolen wished to clarify them immediately. "You must have known it would ruin everything. You were the heart and soul of it all. Are you quite reckless about me?"

A "yes" would not be taken seriously; a "no" was impossible. Turning down her eyelids, she blushed from cheek to neck. Grandcourt read this as a sign of inclination and decided to push further.

"Perhaps there is some deeper interest? Some attraction—an engagement—that it would have been fair to disclose? Is there a man standing between us?"

An answer formed internally: *No; but there is a woman.* Yet, how could she speak this? Even if she had promised silence, entering the subject with Grandcourt felt forbidden. How to arrest his wooing with a formal speech? A fish invited to dinner knows how to decline, but what if it swims against a net? Gwendolen had no time to decide. Compelled by silence, she waited. After a pause, Grandcourt pressed, "Am I to understand that someone else is preferred?"

Impatient with her embarrassment, Gwendolen seized the difficulty, freeing herself. Raising her eyes, she spoke with renewed clarity and defiance, "No." She wished him to understand: *What then? I may not be ready to take you.* Nothing could escape Grandcourt's perception if it affected his ego.

"The last thing I would do is importune you," he declared smoothly. "I would not hope to win you by becoming a bore. If there were no hope, I would ask you to tell me so, and I would ride away to—well, anywhere."

To Gwendolen's own shock, the image of Grandcourt riding away sent a sudden alarm through her. What would remain? Only the former dreariness. She needed him there. Snatching at a delaying tactic, she spoke of her circumstances.

"I fear you are unaware of what has befallen us. I have lately been consumed with my mother's troubles, pushing other matters aside. She has lost her entire fortune, and we must leave this house. I ask you to pardon my preoccupation."

By evading a direct appeal, Gwendolen regained some composure. She spoke with dignity, meeting his gaze. His long, narrow, impenetrable eyes locked onto hers, arresting them mysteriously. The drama between them defied simple description, a complex interplay that words like "Love" or "Thought" could not capture. Who held the stronger influence? At that moment, his strongest wish was to master this creature—a piquant blend of maidenliness and mischief.
