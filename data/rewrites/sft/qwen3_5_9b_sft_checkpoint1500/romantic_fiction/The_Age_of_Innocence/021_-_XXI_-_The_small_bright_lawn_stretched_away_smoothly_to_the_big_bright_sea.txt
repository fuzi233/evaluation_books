The small, sun-drenched lawn stretched away in a smooth, inviting expanse toward the vast, brilliant sea. Its edges were trimmed with vibrant scarlet geraniums and coleus, framing cast-iron vases painted a rich chocolate hue. These stood at intervals along the winding path leading to the water, their garlands of petunias and ivy geraniums looping gracefully above the neatly raked gravel.

Halfway between the cliff's edge and the square wooden house—which shared the chocolate color scheme, though its verandah roof was striped in yellow and brown to mimic an awning—two large targets were set against a backdrop of lush shrubbery. Opposite them, pitched upon the grass, was a genuine tent surrounded by benches and garden seats. Numerous ladies in flowing summer dresses and gentlemen in grey frock-coats and tall hats populated the lawn or sat upon the benches. Occasionally, a slender girl clad in starched muslin would emerge from the tent, bow in hand, and shoot her shaft swiftly toward one of the targets, causing the spectators to interrupt their chatter to watch the result with bated breath.

Newland Archer stood on the verandah of the house, gazing down at the scene with a mixture of curiosity and unease. Flanking the shiny painted steps were large blue china flower-pots resting on bright yellow stands, each filled with spiky green plants. Below the verandah, a wide border of blue hydrangeas edged with more red geraniums spilled onto the ground. Behind him, the French doors of the drawing rooms offered glimpses, through swaying lace curtains, of glassy parquet floors dotted with chintz poufs, dwarf armchairs, and velvet tables laden with silver trifles.

The Newport Archery Club traditionally held its August meeting at the Beauforts'. While the sport, once undisputed until croquet arrived, was slowly being replaced by lawn tennis, the latter game was still deemed too rough and inelegant for high society. Thus, the bow and arrow retained their prestige as an occasion to display fine dresses and graceful postures.

Archer looked down with a strange sense of wonder at this familiar spectacle. It startled him to realize that life was proceeding in the old, predictable manner when his own internal reactions had shifted so drastically. Newport was the place that had first revealed the magnitude of this change to him. Back in New York during the previous winter, after settling into their new greenish-yellow house with the bow-window and Pompeian vestibule, he had retreated with relief into the old routines of the office. This daily grind had served as a tether to his former self. He had savored the pleasurable excitement of selecting a showy grey stepper for May's brougham—the gift from the Wellands—and taken pride in arranging his new library, realized despite family doubts, complete with dark embossed paper, Eastlake bookcases, and "sincere" furniture. At *The Century* and *The Knickerbocker*, he had rediscovered Winsett and his peers; balancing hours at the law with dining out, entertaining guests, and occasional nights at the opera, his life had still felt like a solid, inevitable business.

But Newport represented an escape from duty into an atmosphere of unmitigated holiday leisure. Archer had attempted to persuade May to spend the summer on a remote island off the Maine coast, Mount Desert, where a few hardy Bostonians and Philadelphians camped in "native" cottages amidst woods and waters. Yet, the Wellands invariably chose Newport, where they owned a box on the cliffs. With no compelling reason to refuse joining them, Archer had yielded. Mrs. Welland had pointed out tartly that it was hardly worthwhile for May to try on summer clothes in Paris if she couldn't wear them immediately. This argument, logical and pleasant, Archer had found difficult to refute.

May herself could not comprehend his obscure reluctance to embrace such a reasonable arrangement. She reminded him that he had always loved Newport in his bachelor days—an indisputable fact to which he could only counter with a promise that he would enjoy it more now that they were together. However, standing on the Beaufort verandah and watching the brightly peopled lawn, a chill ran through him; he knew, with a sinking heart, that he would not like it at all.

It was not May's fault, poor dear. If they had occasionally drifted slightly out of step during their travels, harmony returned upon returning to the conditions she was accustomed to. He had always anticipated that she would not disappoint him, and he had been right. He had married, as most young men did, because he had met a perfectly charming girl just as a series of aimless sentimental adventures ended in premature disgust. She represented peace, stability, comradeship, and the steadying sense of unescapable duty.

He could not claim he had been mistaken in his choice; she had fulfilled every expectation. It was undeniably gratifying to be the husband of one of New York's handsomest and most popular married women, especially since she was also one of the sweetest-tempered and most reasonable wives. Archer had never been blind to these advantages. As for the fleeting madness that had seized him on the eve of his marriage, he had trained himself to dismiss it as the final experiment of his discarded past. The notion that he could ever, in his sober senses, have dreamed of marrying the Countess Olenska had become almost unthinkable; she remained in his memory merely as the most poignant ghost among a line of shadows.

Yet, these abstractions left his mind feeling empty and echoing, a silence that explained why the bustling, animated crowd on the Beaufort lawn shocked him as if they were children playing in a graveyard.

A murmur of skirts sounded beside him, and the Marchioness Manson fluttered out of the drawing-room window. As usual, she was excessively adorned, wearing a limp Leghorn hat anchored by windings of faded gauze and a little black velvet parasol on a carved ivory handle, absurdly balanced over her massive hat-brim.

"My dear Newland, I had no idea you and May had arrived! You say you came only yesterday? Ah, business—professional duties... I understand. Many husbands find it impossible to join their wives here except for the weekend." She cocked her head sideways, languishing at him through screwed-up eyes. "But marriage is one long sacrifice, as I used to remind my Ellen..."

Archer's heart gave a strange, jerking stop, a sensation he had experienced once before, seeming to slam a door between himself and the outside world. Yet, this break in continuity proved to be fleeting, for he soon heard Medora answering a question he had apparently voiced.

"No, I am not staying here, but with the Blenkers, in their delightful solitude at Portsmouth. Beaufort was kind enough to send his famous trotters this morning, so I could catch at least a glimpse of one of Regina's garden parties; but tonight I return to rural life. The Blenkers, dear original souls, have hired a primitive old farmhouse..." She drooped slightly beneath her protective brim, adding with a faint blush, "This week Dr. Agathon Carver is holding a series of Inner Thought meetings there. A stark contrast to this gay scene of worldly pleasure—but then I have always lived on contrasts! To me, the only true death is monotony. I always tell Ellen: Beware of monotony; it is the mother of all deadly sins. But my poor child is undergoing a phase of exaltation, an abhorrence of the world. You know, I suppose, that she has declined all invitations to stay at Newport, even with her grandmother Mingott? I could hardly persuade her to come to the Blenkers' if you will believe it! The life she leads is morbid, unnatural. Ah, if she had only listened to me when it was still possible... when the door was still open..."

As Stirling Beaufort advanced over the lawn toward them, tall, heavy, and tightly buttoned into a London frock-coat with an orchid in his buttonhole, Archer noted the change in his friend's appearance. After not seeing him for months, Beaufort seemed florid, heavy, and bloated in the hot summer light. Without his erect, square-shouldered walk, he would have looked like an over-fed, over-dressed old man.

Rumors swirled about Beaufort. Earlier that spring, he had cruised the West Indies in his new steam-yacht, allegedly accompanied by a lady resembling Miss Fanny Ring. The yacht, built in the Clyde and equipped with unheard-of luxuries like tiled bathrooms, was said to have cost half a million dollars. Upon his return, he had presented his wife with a magnificent pearl necklace, an offering fitting for someone bearing such financial burdens. Despite his substantial fortune, disquieting rumors persisted in Fifth Avenue and Wall Street regarding unfortunate railway speculations or exploitation by an insatiable member of her profession. To every report of threatened insolvency, Beaufort responded with fresh extravagance: building new rows of orchid-houses, purchasing race-horses, or acquiring new paintings by Meissonnier or Cabanel.

Beaufort approached the Marchioness and Newland with his usual half-sneering smile. "Hullo, Medora! Did the trotters do their business? Forty minutes, eh?... Well, that's not so bad, considering your nerves had to be spared." He shook hands with Archer, then turned back, placing himself on Mrs. Manson's other side. Speaking in a low voice, words that their companion did not catch, he murmured something else.
