My memory of the events at Hotherstone's Farm is vague and fragmented. I recall a warm reception, a supper so abundant it seemed intended to feed a small village, and a bedroom of impeccable cleanliness. The only flaw in that sanctuary was the detestable invention of our ancestors: the featherbed. My nights were restless, punctuated by the frantic striking of matches and the flickering of a single candle, until dawn brought a profound sense of relief and the promise of rising.

Before sleeping, I had arranged with Betteredge that I would fetch him on our way to Cobb's Hole whenever I chose—a directive that, fueled by my eagerness to secure the letter, effectively meant "the earliest possible moment." Skipping breakfast entirely, I grabbed a crust of bread and set off, bracing myself for the possibility of catching the elderly man in slumber. To my delight, he was already awake, dressed, and leaning on his stick, vibrating with equal anticipation.

"How are you this morning, Betteredge?" I asked.

"Very poorly, sir," he replied.

"I'm sorry to hear that. What ails you?"

"I suffer from a new ailment, Mr. Franklin, one I invented myself. I won't frighten you, but you'll contract it before noon."

"The devil I shall!"

"Do you feel a burning heat in the pit of your stomach? A pounding at the crown of your head? Not yet? It will seize you at Cobb's Hole. I call it 'Detective-Fever,' and I contracted it while accompanying Sergeant Cuff."

"Aye, aye! And the cure is simply to open Rosanna Spearman's letter, is it? Come, let us get to it."

Though early, the fisherman's wife was already stirring in her kitchen. Upon my introduction by Betteredge, Mrs. Yolland performed a ritual usually reserved for distinguished guests: she produced a bottle of Dutch gin and two clean pipes, setting them upon the table.

"What news from London, sir?" she asked, launching into a query of vast scope.

Before I could formulate an answer, a figure emerged from a shadowy corner. A pale, wild-eyed girl, possessing hair of remarkable beauty and a ferocity in her gaze, limped forward on a crutch. She approached the table where I sat, regarding me with a mixture of fascination and horror that held me transfixed.

"Mr. Betteredge," she said, refusing to break eye contact, "say his name again, please."

"This gentleman's name," Betteredge responded, stressing the word *gentleman*, "is Mr. Franklin Blake."

Instantly, the girl turned her back and fled the room. Mrs. Yolland offered some apologetic murmurs, which Betteredge likely translated into proper English, though I remain uncertain of their exact content. My attention was wholly captured by the rhythmic *thump-thump* of the crutch echoing up the stairs, across the floor above, and back down again. Finally, the apparition appeared in the doorway, holding a letter, beckoning me outside.

Leaving my apologies trailing behind me, I followed her rapid, limping pace down the beach slope. She guided me behind a cluster of boats, away from the prying eyes of the villagers, and halted, facing me for the first time.

"Stand there," she commanded. "I wish to examine you."

There was no ambiguity in her expression. I radiated to her the deepest feelings of revulsion and disgust. I need not boast that no woman had ever gazed upon me thus; I can safely assert that no woman had ever allowed me to witness such a look. There is a limit to the duration a man can withstand such scrutiny. I attempted to divert her gaze toward something less repulsive.

"I believe you have a letter for me," I began.

"Is it the letter in your hand?"

"Repeat that," she demanded.

I echoed her words dutifully.

"No," she muttered, her eyes remaining mercilessly fixed on my face. "I cannot comprehend why she saw anything in his visage. I cannot guess what she heard in his voice."

Suddenly, she looked away, resting her weary head atop her crutch. "Oh, my poor dear!" she whispered, her tone shifting to a soft, broken lilt. "Oh, my lost darling! What could you possibly see in this man?"

She lifted her head with renewed fury, staring at me once more. "Can you eat and drink?"

I maintained my composure. "Yes."

"Can you sleep?"

"Yes."

"When you encounter a destitute girl in service, do you feel no remorse?"

"Certainly not. Why should I?"

With abrupt violence, she shoved the letter into my face. "Take it!" she shrieked. "I have never set eyes on you before. God Almighty forbid I should ever see you again!"

Spoken with those final words, she limped away at full speed. The obvious explanation—that she was mad—was undoubtedly shared by everyone. Accepting this conclusion, I turned my focus to the contents of Rosanna Spearman's missive.

The address read: *"For Franklin Blake, Esq. To be delivered into his own hands (and not entrusted to any other), by Lucy Yolland."*

Breaking the seal revealed an inner envelope containing a letter, which in turn held a folded piece of paper. I read the letter first:

*"Sir,—If you are curious to understand the reason for my behavior toward you during your stay at my mistress's house, Lady Verinder, perform the instructions detailed in the enclosed memorandum. Do so without any observer present. Your humble servant, ROSANNA SPEARMAN."*

Next, I examined the memorandum, copying its contents verbatim:

*"Memorandum: Go to the Shivering Sand at the turn of the tide. Walk out onto the South Spit until the South Spit Beacon and the flagstaff at the Coast-guard station above Cobb's Hole align perfectly. Lay down a stick, or any straight object, on the rocks precisely in this line of alignment. Ensure one end of the stick rests at the edge of the rocks, overlooking the quicksand. Feel along the stick, through the seaweed (starting from the end pointing toward the beacon), searching for the Chain. Run your hand along the Chain once found, until you reach the section stretching over the edge of the rocks, down into the quicksand. **AND THEN, PULL THE CHAIN.**"*

As my eyes fell upon the underlined command, I heard Betteredge's voice behind me. The inventor of Detective-Fever had finally succumbed to the contagion.

"I cannot bear it anymore, Mr. Franklin. What does her letter say? For heaven's sake, tell us!"

Heavily, I handed him the documents. He skimmed the main letter with little interest, but the memorandum struck him deeply.

"The Sergeant said it!" he cried. "From start to finish, he claimed she possessed a note detailing the hiding place. And here it is! Lord deliver us, Mr. Franklin, the secret that baffled everyone from the great Cuff downward is ready to reveal itself to *you!* It is ebb tide now, as anyone can see. How long until the turn?"

Spotting a boy repairing a net nearby, Betteredge shouted, "Tammie Bright! I hear you!"

"In an hour's time!" the boy called back.

Checking our watches, Betteredge proposed, "We can circle via the coast, Mr. Franklin, reaching the quicksand with ample time. What say you, sir?"

"Come along!"

On the way to the Shivering Sand, I asked Betteredge to jog my memory regarding Rosanna's actions during Sergeant Cuff's initial inquiry. With his assistance, the sequence became clear: her trip to Frizinghall while the household thought her ill; her nocturnal activities with locked doors and burning candles; her suspicious purchases of the japanned tin box and the two dog-chains from Mrs. Yolland; and the Sergeant's firm belief that she had concealed something at the Shivering Sand, though he knew not what. These fragments coalesced as we reached the low rocky ledge known as the South Spit.

Guided by Betteredge, I positioned myself to align the Beacon and the Coast-guard flagstaff. Placing my stick carefully along the uneven rocks according to the memorandum, we checked our watches again. Nearly twenty minutes remained before the tide turned.

Suggesting we wait on the dry sand rather than the slick rocks, I sat down. To my surprise, Betteredge prepared to depart.

"What are you leaving for?" I asked.

"Re-read the letter, sir," he urged. "You will see."

Recalling the instruction to discover the secret alone, I understood. "It is hard to leave you at such a critical moment," he admitted. "But she met a terrible end, poor soul, and I feel compelled to honor her wishes. Besides," he confided, "nothing in the letter forbids you from revealing the secret later. I shall linger in the fir plantation and await your signal. Do not keep me waiting too long; Detective-Fever is difficult to manage under these conditions."

With that warning, he vanished.

The brief interval stretched into an eternity of suspense. Lighting a cigar, I settled on the beach slope. The morning sun bathed everything in unclouded brilliance; the air was fresh enough to make breathing a luxury. Even the isolated bay seemed cheerful, its exposed quicksand glittering gold, masking its treacherous nature beneath a deceptive smile. It was the finest day I had witnessed since returning to England.
