The shadows cast by the setting sun danced across the walls of the Hale residence, a poignant reminder of the passing of time. For days, the family had been suspended in a state of collective grief, each member struggling to come to terms with the loss of Mrs. Hale. Yet, as the hours ticked by, the raw emotion began to give way to a sense of resignation, as if the weight of their sorrow was slowly becoming a familiar companion.

Mr. Hale, still reeling from the shock of his wife's passing, found himself increasingly consumed by anxiety. His fear of being discovered and arrested hung over him like a specter, casting a pall of unease over every moment. The mere sound of a door creaking open sent him jumping, his nerves stretched taut as a bowstring. It was a constant battle to keep his thoughts from wandering back to the day of the funeral, when he had stood by his wife's side, his heart heavy with grief.

As the evening drew in, Mr. Hale's thoughts turned to the morrow, and the impending departure of his son, Frederick. He called Margaret to his side, his voice low and urgent. "You will accompany Frederick to the station, won't you, my dear? I must know that he is safely gone, that he has escaped the notice of those who would do him harm."

Margaret nodded, her eyes welling up with tears. "Of course, Papa. I'll see him off, and bring you word that he is clear of Milton." She added, her voice barely above a whisper, "If you won't be lonely without me, Papa."

Mr. Hale shook his head, his eyes clouded with worry. "No, no, my dear. I'll be thinking of him, wondering if he's been seen, if he's safe. But with you by my side, I'll feel better." He glanced at his watch, his mind racing ahead to the appointed hour. "What time is his train, Fred?"

"Ten minutes past six," Frederick replied, his voice steady, though his eyes betrayed a hint of sadness.

As the evening wore on, Margaret helped her brother into his coat, her hands moving with a sense of purpose. "I'll take a cab to the Outwood station," she said, her voice firm. "It's closer, and less crowded. We'll be safer there."
