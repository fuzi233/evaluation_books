The dawn's pale light caught the iridescent sheen of the raven's feathers as he perched on the rim of the massive stone basin. I lay submerged in the fountain's water, my father's creation, watching the steel stalk above shoot a hundred-foot torrent into the sky before bursting into foam. My face broke the surface, gasping, as the bird looked down with an expression that felt less like avian curiosity and more like judgment.

"You told me nothing!" I croaked, my voice rough from the cold.

"I instructed you to ignore anyone you distrusted," the raven replied, his tone sharp despite the morning chill. "How could a mortal retain such a directive?"

"The consequence of forgetting is what you must not forget," Mr. Raven said, stepping closer until his shadow fell over the water. He reached down a hand that shimmered with unnatural solidity.

I grasped it, and suddenly the sensation of drowning vanished, replaced by the solid ground of the lawn. I stood dripping, water streaming from my robes.

"Change immediately," he commanded, turning toward the house with a stately stride. "A wetting reveals little of your origin, though here it is merely inconvenient."

As he walked, shifting back into his full form, I laughed, shaking my head. "I have little to change. I discarded my robe to climb the tree."

"It has been ages since I molted," the raven noted dryly.

Inside, the house was silent. I found a dressing gown and descended to the library. The librarian emerged from a closet just as I collapsed onto a couch. Mr. Raven pulled a chair close, sitting without a word for a moment before speaking.

"What does it all mean?" I asked, staring at the ceiling.

"A profound inquiry," he answered. "No one truly knows the nature of things; we only grasp their meaning through application. Whether one learns depends entirely on how they utilize what they possess."

"I have applied nothing yet."

"Not much, but you acknowledge the void. Most spend a lifetime realizing they have learned nothing and achieved less. Yet, you possessed the desire to be useful."

"I wanted to help the children—the Little Ones."

"You started down the wrong path."

"I didn't know the right one."

"That is true, but you are culpable for your ignorance."

"I will accept your explanation once I understand it."

"If you had accepted our invitation, you would have known the path. When a man refuses to act where he stands, he wanders far only to find nothing."

"I have wandered far and found nothing. I left the children to learn how to serve them, only to discover the peril they face."

"When you were with them, you were in the position to aid them. You abandoned your post to search for a job that required you to leave."

"Did you mean I could have helped them by staying?"

"Could you teach them anything by abandoning them?"

"No, but how was I to begin? They were far ahead of me!"

"They were not measuring themselves against you as a ruler. If they possessed your knowledge, or even what you should have known, they would be leagues ahead. But you observed they were not growing—or growing so sluggishly that the concept of growth was foreign to them. They feared growing. Have you ever witnessed a child remain static?"

"But I lacked the power to force their growth!"

"You could have removed the obstacles preventing it."

"What obstacles? I suspected a lack of water."

"Exactly. They have no tears to cry with."

"I would have prevented the need for such tears."

"That is the hallmark of foolish philanthropy. Without the weeping of your world, there would be no cause to save it. You admitted you thought they needed water; why did you not dig wells?"

"It never crossed my mind."

"Not even when the sounds of subterranean waters filled your ears?"

"I believed it once. But I feared the giants for them. That fear drove me to endure so much abuse from those beasts myself."

"In doing so, you nearly conditioned the noble creatures to fear the very 'Bags' who oppressed them. While they fed, comforted, and worshipped you, you allowed yourself to be enslaved by bestial men. You gave the darlings a coward to call their hero. A greater betrayal is hard to imagine. They offered you their hearts; you owe them your soul. You could have turned the giants into servants for the Little Ones."

"I fear your words hold truth, Mr. Raven. But I believed more knowledge might harm them, stripping away their innocence and loveliness."

"They gave you no cause for such fear."

"Is not a little knowledge dangerous?"

"One of your world's greatest lies. Is humanity's greatest wisdom more dangerous than a fragment? The notion that knowledge is inherently grand makes any degree of it seem perilous compared to ignorance. Knowing everything is not greatness."

"At least I served the giants out of love, not cowardice."

"Granted. But you should have served the Little Ones, not the giants. You should have given them water; then they would have quickly shown the giants their true place. Meanwhile, you could have ordered the giants to clear two-thirds of their coarse fruit trees to make room for the delicate ones. You missed your chance with the Lovers, Mr. Vane. You speculated about them rather than helping them."
