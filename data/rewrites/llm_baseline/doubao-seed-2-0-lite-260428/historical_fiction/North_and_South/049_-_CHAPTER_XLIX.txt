“And down the sunny beach she paces slowly,
With many doubtful pauses by the way;
Grief hath an influence so hushed and holy.”

“Is Margaret not the heiress?” Edith whispered to her husband late that night, alone in their room after their sombre journey to Oxford. She had tugged his tall head down to her level, balancing on tiptoe, and made him swear not to think her greedy before she dared voice the question. Captain Lennox knew nothing of the matter: if he had ever heard a passing mention of it, he had long forgotten. He assumed a fellow of a tiny, obscure college could not have much to leave behind, joked that he had never expected Margaret to pay her share of household expenses anyway, and scoffed that two hundred and fifty pounds a year of independent income was absurd for a girl who never touched a drop of wine. Edith set her heels back on the carpet, a little sadder, her tiny romantic fancy of Margaret as a great wealthy heiress popped like a soap bubble.

A week later, she came prancing up to her husband, dropping him a deep, playful curtsy. “I was right, and you were wrong, my noble captain. Margaret has had a lawyer’s letter, and she is the residuary legatee. The small specific gifts add up to two thousand pounds, and the rest of the estate, at current booming Milton property values, comes to forty thousand.”

“Indeed? And how does she take this stroke of good fortune?”

“Oh, she acts as if she half expected it all along, though she had no idea the sum was so large. She went white as a sheet the second she read the full details, and says she is afraid of all that money. Which is nonsense, of course—she’ll get over that silly feeling soon enough. I left Mamma smothering her with congratulations and snuck away straight to tell you.”

By unspoken general consent, everyone assumed Henry Lennox would naturally act as Margaret’s informal legal adviser from that point onward. She was entirely unacquainted with every form of business procedure, so she turned to him for guidance in nearly every matter related to the estate. He selected her solicitor, brought her stacks of documents to sign, and was never happier than when walking her through the opaque, seemingly mystical jargon of legal contracts, explaining exactly what every clause and fine line meant.

“Henry,” Edith said one day, arch and teasing, “do you know what I hope and expect all these long closed-door conversations with Margaret will end in?”

“No, I don’t,” he replied, colouring bright red. “And I would thank you not to tell me.”

“Oh, very well, then. I suppose I don’t need to warn Sholto to stop inviting Mr. Montagu over to the house so often.”

“Just as you please,” he said, forcing a tone of cool indifference. “What you are imagining may or may not come to pass. But this time, before I commit myself, I will make absolutely sure my ground is solid. Invite whoever you like. Edith, this is not a thing to meddle in—if you interfere, you will ruin it entirely. She has been icy and distant with me for so long, sharp and unyielding as Queen Zenobia, and she is only just starting to thaw now. She has the makings of a Cleopatra in her, if only she were a little less pious.”

“For my part,” Edith said, a little sharp and mischievous, “I am very glad she is a Christian. I know so very few decent people who are!”

Margaret’s planned trip to Spain fell through that autumn, though she had clung to hope almost to the last that some lucky chance would bring Frederick to Paris, where she could easily travel to meet him with a respectable chaperone. Instead of the coast of Cadiz, she settled for Cromer, where Aunt Shaw, the Lennoxes, and the whole household were already bound for a holiday. They had gently pressed her to join them the whole time, making only half-hearted, lazy efforts to help arrange her separate travel plans, and in the end, Cromer was exactly what she needed: she was run ragged by grief and stress, and the bracing sea air soothed her body as much as her mind.

Among the hopes that had faded in the months after Mr. Bell’s death was the quiet, stubborn trust she had carried that Bell would pass the full, unvarnished truth of the Leonards incident on to Mr. Thornton—all the context of her brother’s long lost return, the desperate lie she had told to protect him, the months of guilt and shame she had carried afterward. She wanted Thornton’s opinion of her, no matter how harsh it had once been, to be rooted in full understanding of what she had done, and why. That small unburdening would have given her the rest she craved, the end to a restlessness she would otherwise carry her whole life, if she could not force herself to stop thinking of it. Now, so long after the events, there was no one left to tell the truth but herself, and she had no way to seek Thornton out to explain it. She tried to reason herself into accepting her fate, that millions of people lived misunderstood their whole lives, but her heart still ached with the quiet, foolish wish that one day—years and years from now, before he died at the very least—he would somehow learn how deeply she had been tempted, how little she had deserved his judgment. When she finally schooled herself to accept that wish would never be granted, she turned all her heart and strength to the life laid out before her, determined to make the very best of it.

She spent hours sitting on the pebbly shore, gazing intently at the waves as they dragged back and forth over the stones in ceaseless motion, or stared out at the distant silver heave of the open sea where it caught the light against the sky, listening half-unconsciously to the low, endless roar that sounded like a perpetual unwritten psalm rising from the water. It soothed her, and she could not have said how or why. She sat on the ground, hands clasped loosely around her knees, while her aunt wandered the town running small errands, and Edith and Captain Lennox rode for miles along the shore and across the inland lanes. Nannies trailing toddlers or pushing strollers sauntered past her again and again, whispering to each other, wondering what on earth she could possibly find to look at for hours, day after day. When the whole family gathered for dinner, Margaret was so quiet and lost in thought that Edith declared her moping, and leapt at her husband’s suggestion that they invite Henry Lennox to spend a week at Cromer when he returned from his Scotland trip in October.

All those long hours of quiet thinking let Margaret sort through every event of her past, untangle their roots and true significance, and shape a clear vision for her future. No one who did not care to look closely noticed, but a soft, steady peace had settled into her face, impossible to miss for anyone paying attention. Henry Lennox was completely stunned by the change when he first saw her.

“The sea has done Miss Hale an immense amount of good, I should say,” he remarked, after she had left the room shortly after his arrival. “She looks ten years younger than she did in Harley Street.”

“That’s the new bonnet I bought her!” Edith said, preening triumphantly. “I knew it would suit her the second I laid eyes on it.”

“I beg your pardon,” Mr. Lennox replied, in that half-indulgent, half-amused tone he always used with Edith. “But I think I can tell the difference between the charm of a dress and the charm of a woman. No mere bonnet could make Miss Hale’s eyes so bright and yet so soft, or her lips so warm and full, or her whole face glowing with such peace and light. She is like the Margaret Hale we knew at Helstone,” he dropped his voice, “and more, far more.”

From that moment, the clever, ambitious young barrister bent every one of his considerable powers to winning Margaret. He loved her soft, gentle beauty, and he saw the deep, latent strength of her sharp mind, which he was certain would easily align with all the grand, ambitious plans he held for his own career. He saw her fortune only as a natural part of the perfect, superb picture of who she was and the life they could share, though he was fully aware the sum would catapult the poor young barrister to a level of social and professional status he could never have reached on his own for decades. He had visited Milton on business related to her property holdings right after his Scotland trip, and as a skilled lawyer with an eye for contingencies, he saw clearly that the value of her land and houses in the booming industrial town was rising higher every single year. He was glad that their new dynamic of client and legal adviser was steadily erasing the awkward, painful memory of that botched, badly managed day at Helstone, when he had bungled his first proposal.

Margaret was only too happy to listen when he talked about Milton, even though he had not met any of the people she had cared for most deeply there. Her aunt and cousin had always dismissed Milton as coarse, ugly, and unpleasant, the very same feeling Margaret had once been ashamed to remember having in her first weeks in the town. But Henry’s admiration for the character of Milton and its people outstripped even Margaret’s own: he was captivated by their endless energy, their ferocious power, their indomitable courage in the face of every struggle, the bright, vivid intensity of their way of life. He never tired of talking of them, until Margaret, with her characteristic candour, gently pointed out that for all their noble, admirable grit, far too many of the goals they poured all that tireless, mighty effort into were selfish and material, a flaw that tainted all their greatness. Still, whenever every other topic paled for Margaret, and she gave only short, distant answers to idle chatter, all Henry had to do was ask about some small Darkshire custom, some quirk of Milton character, to bring the light back to her eyes and the glow back to her cheeks.

When they returned to London, Margaret kept the promise she had made to herself on the Cromer beach: she took full control of her own life. Before their seaside holiday, she had been as docile to her aunt’s rules as that scared little stranger who had cried herself to sleep in the Harley Street nursery her very first night there. But those hours of solemn, quiet thought had taught her that one day she would be held fully accountable for the life she had lived, and she had worked out the most difficult balance any woman ever strikes: how much of herself to yield to the loving authority of the people who cared for her, and how much to claim as her own, free to use for the work she felt called to do. Mrs. Shaw was unfailingly gentle and good-tempered, and Edith had inherited that same sweet domestic kindness. Margaret knew she had once had the sharpest temper of the three, her quick perceptions and overactive imagination making her hasty, her early isolation making her proud—but she had a soft, childlike sweetness of heart that had always made her irresistible even in her rare stubborn moods, and now, chastened even by what the world called her good fortune, she charmed her aunt into full acquiescence to her wishes. Margaret had won the right to follow her own ideas of duty.

“Only don’t get strong-minded,” Edith pleaded. “Mamma wants to buy you your very own footman, you are more than welcome to him—they are such a nuisance, after all. That’s the only thing I beg of you, darling: don’t turn strong-minded. Footman or no footman, don’t be one of those stern, serious bluestockings.”

“Don’t be afraid, Edith,” Margaret laughed. “I will faint dead away in the middle of the servants’ dinner the very first chance I get, and then, between Sholto playing with the fire and the baby screaming, you’ll be begging for a strong-minded woman who can handle any emergency.”

“And you won’t get too pious to joke and be merry with us?”

“Never. I shall be merrier than I have ever been, now that I get to live the life I choose.”

“And you won’t dress all in dull brown and dust-colour, to hide all the Milton dirt you’ll pick up wandering about those dreadful industrial parts, and make me buy all your pretty dresses for you?”

“Indeed I mean to pick out all my dresses for myself,” Margaret said. “You can come shopping with me if you like, but no one else can choose quite what suits me.”

“Oh, I was so afraid you’d give up every last vanity. I’m glad you’re keeping a few of them, just as little reminders of the old Margaret Hale we knew.”

“I am going to be exactly the same girl you have always known,” Margaret told her, “if only you and Aunt Shaw can make yourselves see it. I have no husband or children to give me natural daily duties, after all, so I shall simply make some good work for myself, beyond ordering my own new gowns.”

In their private little family conclave, made up of Edith, her mother, and her husband, they all agreed that Margaret’s new plans would only push her closer and closer to Henry Lennox. They kept her away from all other eligible young men among their acquaintance, and it soon became clear that Margaret did not care for the company of anyone outside their own small family but Henry. Other suitors, drawn to her beauty or the reputation of her fortune, drifted away quickly, put off by her quiet, uninterested gentle disdain, to chase after other less picky beauties, or other heiresses with even greater fortunes. Henry and Margaret grew slowly, steadily closer in their intimacy, and neither of them was the sort of person to let anyone peer too closely or remark on the quiet, tender relationship taking root between them.
