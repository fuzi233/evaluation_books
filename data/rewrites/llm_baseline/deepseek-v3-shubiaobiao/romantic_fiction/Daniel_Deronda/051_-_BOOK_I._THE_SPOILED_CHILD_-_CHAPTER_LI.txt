She held the spindle as she sat,
Errina with the thick-coiled mat
Of raven hair and deepest agate eyes,
Gazing with a sad surprise
At surging visions of her destiny—
To spin the byssus drearily
In insect-labor, while the throng
Of gods and men wrought deeds that poets wrought in song.

When Deronda stood at the threshold of his mother’s apartments in the *Italia*, his pulse quickened with an echo of boyhood’s restless apprehensions. The servants in the antechamber glanced at him curiously, struck by the contrast between the distinguished young man before them and the physician they had expected. But Deronda scarcely noticed them—his attention was fixed beyond the second door, where a figure stood waiting.

She was draped in black lace, the fabric cascading from her silvered hair to the floor, her arms bare save for the glint of bracelets, folded in quiet defiance. Her face, though no longer youthful, held a regal poise, arresting in its expressiveness. As Deronda stepped forward, she extended her hand. He took it, pressing it to his lips, and for a suspended moment, they simply studied one another. Her gaze was piercing, her features so alive with shifting emotion that she seemed to transform before his eyes.

Then, abruptly, she released his hand only to place both of hers upon his shoulders, her face softening into an almost radiant admiration.

*"You are beautiful,"* she murmured, her voice low and melodious, each syllable shaped with an exotic cadence. *"I knew you would be."* She kissed his cheeks, and he returned the gesture—a ritual more formal than tender.

Just as swiftly, the warmth retreated. Her posture stiffened, her tone cooling. *"I am your mother. But you cannot love me."*

*"I have thought of you more than anyone in this world,"* Deronda replied, his voice unsteady.

*"I am nothing like what you imagined,"* she said, withdrawing her hands and folding her arms, as if challenging him to scrutinize her. And he did—searching for traces of himself in her sharp, striking features, yet finding instead an unsettling distance. There was something almost mythic about her, as if she belonged to another realm entirely.

*"I used to fear you were suffering,"* he ventured. *"I wished I could bring you comfort."*

*"I *am* suffering. But not in a way you can soothe,"* she replied, moving to a sofa arranged with cushions. *"Sit."* She gestured to a nearby chair, then softened slightly at his troubled expression. *"Do not distress yourself. I am not in pain now. I can speak."*

Deronda obeyed, but the woman before him felt less like a mother and more like an enigmatic force of fate. He waited, suspended between longing and trepidation.

*"No,"* she began, *"I did not summon you for solace. I had no notion how you would feel toward me—nor do I know now. I do not expect love from a son who never knew me. But believe this: I chose what I thought was best for you. I did not deprive you of anything precious."*

*"You cannot ask me to believe a mother’s love would have been worthless,"* Deronda countered.

*"I do not speak to demean myself,"* she said with sudden fire. *"But I had little affection to give. I wanted freedom, not the weight of another life. You wonder what I was? Not a princess then—I was an artist. A singer, an actress. Men crossed continents for me. I lived a thousand lives in one. A child had no place in them."*

Her words were a shield, flung up against imagined reproach. Deronda gripped the edge of his chair, his breath shallow.

*"I did not choose marriage. My father compelled me—to your cousin, Ephraim. It was my only path to liberty. I could command a husband, but not a father. And the chains I hated for myself, I spared you."* Her voice grew sharper. *"What greater kindness could a mother offer? I freed you from the burden of being born a Jew."*

*"Then I *am* a Jew?"* The words burst from him, raw with emotion. *"My father was Jewish—and so are you?"*

*"Yes,"* she said, watching him closely now, as if gauging a threat.

*"I am glad of it,"* he declared, passion vibrating beneath the words.

Her composure fractured. *"Why?"* she demanded, her pallor deepening. *"You are an English gentleman. I made certain of it."*

*"You chose without knowing what you took from me,"* he shot back, turning away.

A silence, thick and charged, settled between them. When she spoke again, her voice was steel.

*"I chose as I would have for myself. How could I foresee you would inherit my father’s spirit? That you would love what I despised?"* The last words dripped bitterness.

Deronda mastered himself, the heat of his anger giving way to a quieter sorrow. *"Forgive me if I spoke harshly. But why tell me now, after ensuring I grew up in ignorance?"*

*"Ah, motives!"* she said with a scornful laugh. *"When you are my age, nothing is simple. People demand women feel as others do—or brand them monsters. I am no monster. I did not weep when I sent you away. But shadows gather now. Sickness sharpens them. I wronged the dead—and time runs short to mend it."*

Her voice shifted like a skilled performer’s, each word weighted with dramatic truth. Deronda listened, rapt, as she unveiled the past.

*"My father was a learned man—a physician, stern as iron. He wished for a son; I was a disappointment. A daughter must be molded—'the Jewish woman,' obedient, devout. I was to revere scrolls and rituals that meant nothing to me. I craved the world, not gilded cages."*

*"And my father?"* Deronda pressed.

*"Gentle, adoring. He gave up everything for me."* She reached for his hand, studying the ring he wore. *"His ring. You have his hands."*

For the first time, Deronda felt the stirrings of a deeper connection. *"Mother,"* he said fervently, *"let the past rest. Take us into your heart—the living and the dead. Forgive. Let me love you."*

She kissed his brow, her smile wistful. *"I reject nothing. But I have nothing to give."*

And as she leaned back, the chasm between them remained—wide, aching, and unresolved.
