CHAPTER XXVII. Desire has trimmed the sails, and Circumstance brings but the breeze to fill them.

As Grandcourt rode his sleek black Yarico toward Offendene, with Criterion following behind under the groom’s steady hand, Gwendolen sat before her mirror while her mother gently gathered her long, light-brown hair into a loose coil. “Just tie it up simply, mamma,” she said softly, her voice calm but edged with quiet resolve.

“Let me bring you some earrings, Gwen,” Mrs. Davilow offered, stepping back to admire the reflection in the glass.

They both paused—unconsciously drawn by the change in Gwendolen’s face. Her eyes sparkled with a brightness they hadn’t held in days, and the faint shadow that had clouded her features seemed lifted, restoring her youth and composure. A warmth stirred in Mrs. Davilow’s heart. “You do want your earrings?”

“No, mamma,” Gwendolen replied, smiling faintly. “I won forgo any ornaments. I’ll wear my black silk. Black is the only color when one is going to refuse an offer.”

She rose, slipping off her dressing gown with a grace that carried a new strength. The way she moved—head held high, body poised with renewed energy—was like a plant rehydrated after drought. Her mother watched, thinking, *She’s herself again. It must be pleasure at his coming. But can she truly mean to turn him down?*

Gwendolen would have been furious if such thoughts had been spoken aloud. Yet over the past twenty hours—broken only by brief sleep—her mind had swirled with images and arguments, pulling her between desire and duty, pride and fear. She had resolved not to accept Grandcourt from the moment she read his letter. But now, that decision felt hollow—a shell stripped of life, like a prayer whispered without belief. She still meant to refuse him. But the very act of resisting had forced her to confront what she’d once avoided: the weight of his past, the woman he had left behind.

At first, her hesitation had been vague—just a flicker of unease. But then came the day at Cardell Chase. That was when everything shifted. Not with reason, but with instinct. She had reacted not just out of maidenly pride or jealousy, but from a deeper dread—an unspoken horror of wrong, of complicity in something hidden, something dishonorable. She didn’t know exactly what had happened between Grandcourt and Mrs. Glasher. All she knew was that she had promised not to interfere. And yet—what did that promise really mean? If another woman married him, would it truly harm Mrs. Glasher or her children? Or might it even help them?

Her mind spun through possibilities. Marriage, to Gwendolen, was still a mystery—no more real to her than the invisible currents of magnetism or the laws of storms. She saw her mother’s marriage as poorly managed, and vowed she would do better. But she couldn’t speak of marriage’s trials—not with this daughter, whose spirit was already too tangled in silence and suspicion.

*I wonder what mamma or Uncle would say if they knew about Mrs. Glasher,* she thought. Not that she could ever tell them. Nor could she imagine what others might think of Grandcourt marrying someone else—and having other children. What would people say? What would they say about *her*, if she accepted him?

The thought of public opinion brought a strange relief. At least it gave her a measure. She collected her memories: yes, society judged illegitimate children far more harshly than illegitimate fathers. So perhaps, in their eyes, she had no duty to defend Mrs. Glasher. But that didn’t erase the feeling—the deep, almost angry shame—that she should be expected to join a man whose life was steeped in secrets, in broken promises. He had never loved her, not truly. His advances had been calculated, deliberate. And now, knowing what she knew, she felt a kind of retrospective disgust—not just at him, but at the idea of being wooed by someone who laughed at her ignorance.

Yet love had never been her concern. For her, marriage had always been about security, status, escape. To be loved was the man’s role. But now, the idea of being chosen by Grandcourt—this man of power and mystery—felt like a trap. Still, she refused to give in to fear. She had made up her mind. She would not accept him.

And yet—there was a thrill in the thought of refusing him. She had the reins in her hands again. A current ran through her veins, lifting her from the despair of Klesmer’s visit. She wasn’t asking for approval anymore. She was claiming power.

Then came the sound of hooves on gravel. Miss Merry appeared at the door. “Mr. Grandcourt is here. In the drawing-room.”

Gwendolen’s breath caught. The hours of preparation, the triumph of her resolve—it all vanished. It was as if he had burst in upon her in the midst of her deepest sorrow.

She walked into the room with every ounce of willpower focused on control. She met his gaze with calm dignity, offering her hand as he greeted her with a low, languid voice: “I hope you are quite well.”

She answered just as softly. “Yes.”

They sat—she upright, eyes lowered; he relaxed, leaning on the chair back, hat in hand. From the outside, they looked like lovers suspended in a moment of quiet tension. And indeed, the game had begun. He was wooing her—not with words, but with presence, with attention so intense it seemed to breathe around her. And she, though she tried to resist, found herself drawn in.

“I was disappointed not to find you at Leubronn,” he began, his voice slow, tinged with a hint of longing. “It was unbearable without you. A mere kennel of a place. Don’t you agree?”

“I can’t judge what it would be without myself,” she replied, lifting her eyes with a flicker of mischief. “But with me, I liked it well enough to stay longer—if I could. Though I had to return for family troubles.”

“Very cruel of you,” he said, ignoring her mention of hardship. “You knew you were the heart of everything. Are you so reckless with me?”

She couldn’t answer yes—too obvious. No—too final. So she turned her eyes down, flushing crimson.

Grandcourt saw it as a sign of interest. But he wanted more. “Is there someone else? Some engagement? A man standing between us?”

Inwardly, she answered: *No—but there is a woman.* But how could she say it? Even if she hadn’t sworn silence, the subject was forbidden ground. And how could she stop his courtship with formalities? Like a fish trapped in a net, she had no clear path.

After a pause, he asked, “Am I to understand that someone else is preferred?”

That was it. The moment of truth. Gwendolen, tired of hesitation, rose abruptly. “No,” she said, her voice clear and firm. “Not that. But I may not be ready to take *you*.”

He understood instantly. Such things touched his pride deeply. “I would never importune you,” he said, his tone cool, composed. “I wouldn’t ask you to endure me. If there’s no hope, I’d ask only that you tell me so, so I may ride away.”

At those words, something inside her stirred. A sudden fear—what if he left? What then? The old emptiness would return. She needed him there. She clung to the next topic, desperate for delay. “You don’t know what’s happened. Mamma has lost everything. We’re leaving this house. I beg you to forgive my distraction.”

With that, she regained some composure. She spoke with dignity, meeting his gaze. His eyes—long, narrow, unreadable—held hers, creating a silent drama neither could name. Love, like thought, cannot be captured in words. The exchange between them was deeper than speech, charged with unspoken forces.

Then, slowly, he said, “You will tell me now, I hope, that Mrs. Davilow’s loss will not trouble you further. You will trust me to protect her. You will let me provide for her future.”

His voice was measured, deliberate. Each pause allowed time for her imagination to wander. As the words sank in, they felt like wine—warm, soothing, making the world seem lighter. Suddenly, the pain of poverty receded. She imagined herself springing to her mother, laughing again. The repugnance she once felt faded into memory. Here, in this moment, was rescue.

Yet when he finished speaking, she felt the pivot point. “You are very generous,” she murmured, not moving her eyes.

“You accept what will make such things a matter of course?” he asked, no urgency in his voice. “You consent to become my wife?”

This time, she remained pale. Without meaning to, she rose and stepped back, folding her hands before her. Silence stretched.

Grandcourt followed, placing his hat on the chair but holding onto it. The hesitation of this destitute girl—so close to accepting his wealth—sparked a fierce interest in him. He believed it was because of Mrs. Glasher.

“Do you command me to go?” he asked, his voice low, almost intimate.

“No,” she whispered. She couldn’t let him leave. That single word was a grip.

“You accept my devotion?” he asked, holding his hat at his side, eyes locked on hers.

Their gaze held—long, still, full of meaning. How could she contradict herself? What had she waited for? He had closed the door on explanations.

“Yes,” she said, gravely—like a verdict pronounced in court.

He received it quietly. They stood, staring, unchanged in posture.

Was there ever a more solemn way to say “yes”? Grandcourt preferred the distance, the ritual, the unspoken boundary she imposed. But at last, he placed his hat down and stepped forward, taking her hand, pressing his lips lightly against it—then letting go.

She thought his manner perfect. And in that moment, she felt free—almost ready to tease again. Her “yes” meant so little now. The darkness of her future dissolved. She saw herself freed from the Momperts, her mother from Sawyer’s Cottage.

A smile curled her lips. “Will you see mamma? I’ll fetch her.”

“Wait a moment,” he said, falling into his familiar pose—thumb and forefinger in his waistcoat pocket, right hand stroking his whiskers. “Have you anything else to say?”

“Only this,” she said, playfully. “Will it bother you to be asked when we can marry?”

“I think it will, today,” she replied, tilting her chin.

“Not today. Tomorrow. Think on it. In a fortnight—or three weeks—as soon as possible.”

“Oh, you think you’ll tire of me?” she teased. “I’ve noticed married husbands aren’t as present as engaged ones. But perhaps I’ll like that better.”

She laughed, bright and charming.

“You shall have whatever you like,” he said.

“And nothing that I don’t like?” she countered. “Please say that. Because I dislike what I don’t like more than I like what I like.”

He paused. These were nuances he knew well. “I don’t know. This world is a brute place. Things happen you don’t like. I can’t always keep you from boredom. If you want to ride Criterion, I can’t stop him from appearing.”

“Oh, my friend Criterion—how is he?”

“He’s outside. I had the groom ride him so you could see. He wore a side-saddle yesterday. Come to the window.”

They watched the horses circle the drive—beautiful, powerful creatures, groomed to perfection. A surge of joy passed through Gwendolen. They were symbols of command, luxury—everything opposite to the ugliness of poverty she had faced.

“Will you ride Criterion tomorrow?” Grandcourt asked.

“If you wish, everything will be arranged.”

“I should like it above all things,” she said. “I want to lose myself in a gallop again. But now I must go fetch mamma.”

“Take my arm to the door,” he said.

She did. Their faces were nearly level, close together. He looked at her. She thought his manners—his ease, his restraint—were more refined than any lover she’d ever imagined. There was no threat of a kiss. Only quiet intimacy.

Then, halfway across the room, she stopped. “Oh—while I think of it. There’s something I dislike that you can save me from. I do *not* like Mr. Lush’s company.”

“You shall not have it. I’ll get rid of him.”

“You’re not fond of him yourself?”

“Not at all. I let him hang on because he’s always been a poor devil. He traveled with me as a boy. Coarse-haired brute—half hog, half dilettante.”

She laughed. It felt natural, kind—even gentle.

When they reached the door, he opened it for her with effortless grace. Truly, she thought, he might be the least disagreeable husband imaginable.

Mrs. Davilow waited anxiously in her bedroom. When Gwendolen entered, she rushed forward, kissed her cheeks, and whispered, “Come down, mamma. See Mr. Grandcourt. I’m engaged to him.”

“My darling child,” Mrs. Davilow breathed, stunned—not joyful, but solemn.

“Yes,” Gwendolen said, calm, decisive. “Everything is settled. You won’t go to Sawyer’s Cottage. I won’t be inspected by Mrs. Mompert. Everything will be as I choose. Come down with me now.”
