The news of Charlotte Palmer's delivery of a son and heir spread quickly through the social circles of London, and the ladies of the town were abuzz with excitement. Mrs. Jennings, in particular, was overjoyed at the prospect of spending more time with her dear friend, and she made it a point to visit Charlotte every morning as soon as she rose. As a result, her young friends, the Miss Dashwoods, found themselves relegated to the sidelines, spending their days at Conduit Street under the watchful eye of Lady Middleton and her daughters.

While the Dashwoods would have preferred to stay with Mrs. Jennings, they understood that her enthusiasm for Charlotte's new baby took precedence. They attempted to make the best of their situation, but it was clear that their presence was a source of discomfort for the Middletons. Lady Middleton, though polite in her interactions with Elinor and Marianne, harbored a certain disdain for them. She believed they lacked the capacity for flattery, which she saw as an essential trait in a social companion. Her daughters, Lucy and Anne, felt similarly, viewing the Dashwoods as intruders on their family's kindness, which they wished to monopolize for themselves.

Lucy Steele, however, was less affected by the tension, and her sister, Anne, seemed almost indifferent. If only the Dashwoods would engage with her on the subject of Marianne's affair with Mr. Willoughby, she believed she could find a way to reconcile their differences. But this olive branch was not extended, and instead, the sisters continued to maintain a polite but distant relationship. A gentle jest about the Doctor might have eased the atmosphere, but neither the Dashwoods nor the Middletons were inclined to humor her.

Meanwhile, Mrs. Jennings remained blissfully unaware of the undercurrents of tension, seeing only the joy and camaraderie that filled her life. She spent her days visiting her friends and sharing stories of Charlotte's new baby, always eager to recount the smallest details to anyone who would listen. One thing did, however, trouble her – Mr. Palmer's assertion that all infants are alike, which struck her as a strange and unfatherly notion. Despite this, she continued to revel in the company of her friends, spreading her enthusiasm far and wide.
