The following morning, Denys seized an opportunity to reveal the remainder of his tale to Mother and Daughter, carefully excluding Cornelis and Sybrandt from the conversation. "It is not for me to cast a shadow upon them," he explained with his characteristic reserve regarding his comrades. "They are of good stock. Yet, Gerard views them as enemies in this affair. Since I am Gerard's companion, it is a soldier's code to withhold information from the foe—unless, of course, one chooses to speak lies."

Catherine sighed deeply but offered no rebuttal. The adventures Denys recounted stirred within them a tumult of agitation and profound grief; they wept bitterly at the memory of the friends' separation, a scene Denys could barely describe without faltering. Yet, eventually, their sorrow merged into a joyful hope and eager anticipation of Gerard's swift return. Denys shared this confidence wholeheartedly, though he gently reminded them that Gerard's final wishes and instructions must not be neglected simply because of their optimism. Indeed, should Gerard return next week only to find no sign of Margaret, what manner of figure would he cut?

For the first time, Catherine felt a kinship toward the absent Margaret that surpassed her usual demeanor. She was equally anxious to locate her and offer kindness before Gerard's arrival, yet she could not align with Denys's belief that abandoning their neighborhood to search for her was wise. "She must have confided in someone where she was going," Catherine argued. "These are not dishonest folk fleeing the country; they owe not a single stiver in Sevenbergen. And, dear heart, Denys, you cannot hunt the entire province of Holland for one girl."

"Can I not?" Denys asked grimly. "We shall see." After a moment of reflection, he proposed a division of labor: she should remain here with her eyes and ears wide open, while he would scour every town in Holland if necessary. "However," he noted, "she will not be many leagues distant. Three do not fly as fast, nor travel as far, as one."

"That is sound logic," Catherine conceded, though she insisted he visit the Demoiselle Van Eyck immediately. "She and our Margaret were bosom friends. She knows where the girl has gone, if she will but tell us."

Denys was ready to depart instantly, but Catherine, seizing the moment, smoothed her dress slightly and took him with her. They were received graciously by the elderly lady seated in a richly furnished chamber. Upon hearing their news, the tapestry slipped from Margaret Van Eyck's hands.

"Gone? Gone from Sevenbergen without telling me? The thankless girl!"

This reaction startled the visitors. "What? You know nothing? When was she last here?"

"Perhaps ten days ago," the old lady replied. "I had taken out my brushes, after so many years, to paint her portrait. I did not finish it, though; for certain reasons."

Catherine remarked on the strangeness of her departure—"bag and baggage"—without permission or explanation. "Was there ever anything so untoward? Just when our hearts were warm toward her, and here comes Gerard's mate from the ends of the earth with comfort for her, only to find her missing, while Gerard himself awaits... What am I to do? Surely she did not leave without cause. Can you not give us a clue, my good demoiselle? Prithee, speak."

"I have no clue to give," the elder lady said, her tone growing peevish.

"Then I can provide one," declared Reicht Heynes, appearing in the doorway with flushed cheeks. "Have you been listening all along, Mistress?"

"What are my ears for, mistress?"

"True. Very well, then, shed the light of your wisdom upon this dark matter."

"There is no darkness here that I perceive," Reicht stated firmly. "If you call it a two-ply cord, and the ends of it are lying right here in this room, you will not be far astray. Oh, Mistress, I wonder at you sitting there pretending ignorance."

"Marry, come up," the mistress commanded, her own cheeks flushing nearly as red as the servant's.

"So it was I who drove the foolish girl away," Reicht admitted.

"You did your share, Mistress. How did you greet her the last time she visited? Do you think she could fail to notice it, especially when she was all alone and friendless? And you said, 'I have changed my mind about painting you,' turning your nose up at her."

"I did not turn up my nose," the mistress retorted sharply. "My nose is not shaped like yours for gazing heavenward."

"Oh, all noses can follow the heart's inclination, for that matter. Poor soul. She did come to my kitchen. 'I am not to be painted now,' she said, tears in her eyes. She spoke no further. But I knew exactly what she meant. I had seen you."

"Well," said Margaret Van Eyck, "I confess as much and make you the judge, Madam. Understand that young girls can accomplish nothing of their own volition; they are merely adept at mimicking their sweethearts. Your Gerard is reasonably skilled in many things, including the illuminator's craft. Margaret was his pupil, a patient one. Is it a marvel? Having a woman's eye for color and a lover to emulate? 'Tis a trick I despise in my heart. For through it, the great art of color—which should be royal, aspiring, and free—becomes a poor slave to the petty crafts of writing and printing. It is fettered, imprisoned, and diminished, both body and soul, to match the littleness of books, destined to go to church in a rich fool's pocket."

"Naturally, affection governs us all," she continued, her voice softening. "When the poor wench brought me her thorn-leaves, lilies, ivy, dewberries, ladybirds, and butterfly grubs—all the scum of Nature stuck fast in gold-leaf like wasps in a honey-pot—and alongside her daily book, showing she had poured over each singular page for a hundred, or perhaps two hundred hours, certainly I was wroth. That an immortal soul, countless hours of labor, and much manual skill should be flung away on Nature's trash, leaves, insects, and grubs, and on barren letters. Yet, having bowels, I restrained myself, damming my better feelings, and looked kindly at the work to suggest improvements. I said, 'Since Heaven, for our sins, has doomed us to spend time, soul, and color on great letters and tiny beetles, omitting such small fry as saints and heroes—their acts and passions—why not present the scum naturally?'

'I told her,' I said, 'that the grapes I saw hanging in the air did not stick to walls; and even these insects, and Nature's slime in general, do not pass their noxious lives wedged miserably in metal prisons like flies in honey-pots and glue-pots, but crawl or hover at large, infesting the air.'

'Ah, my dear friend,' she replied, 'I see now whither you drive; but this ground is gold; upon it we may not shade.'

'Who said so?' I asked.

'All teachers of this craft,' she answered; and (to make an end of me at once, I suppose) 'Gerard himself!'

'That for Gerard himself,' I retorted, 'and all the gang; give me a brush!'

'So I chose to shade her fruits and reptiles with a color false in nature, but true relative to that monstrous background of glaring gold. In five minutes, out came a bunch of raspberries, stalk and all, almost flying into your mouth; likewise a butterfly grub she had so truly rendered it might turn the stoutest stomach. My lady threw her arms around my neck and exclaimed, 'Oh!'"

"Did she now?"

"The little love!" observed Denys, finally managing to interject.

Margaret Van Eyck stared at him, then smiled. She proceeded to explain how, step by step, she had been led to promise to resume the art she had abandoned with a sigh upon her brothers' deaths—to paint the Madonna once more, using Margaret as her model. Incidentally, she even revealed how girls are transformed into saints.

"Thy hair is adorable," I said.

"Why, 'tis red," she replied.

"Ay," I said, "but what a red! How brown! How glossy! Most hair is not worth a straw to us painters; thine is the artist's very hue. But thy violet eyes, which currently smack of earth—being now languid for lack of one Gerard, now aflame with hope for the same Gerard—I will lift to heaven in fixed and holy meditation. And thy nose, which already somewhat aspires that way (though not so piously as Reicht's), I will debase a trifle, and somewhat enfeeble thy chin."

"Enfeeble her chin? Alack! What may that mean? You go beyond me, Mistress."

"'Tis a resolute chin. Not a jot too resolute for this wicked world; but when you come to a Madonna? No, thank you."

"Well, I never. A resolute chin," Denys interjected.
