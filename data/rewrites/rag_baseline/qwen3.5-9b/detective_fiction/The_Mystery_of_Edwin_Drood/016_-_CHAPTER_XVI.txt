The air in the dining room was thick with the scent of roasted poultry and the metallic tang of fear. John Jasper lay in an easy-chair, his body a map of recent collapse, while Mr. and Mrs. Tope hovered like anxious birds around him. Between them sat Mr. Grewgious, a figure carved from wood and silence, his hands resting rigidly upon his knees. He watched Jasper recover with the detached scrutiny of a clockmaker inspecting a broken gear.

"You've come to nicely now, sir," Mrs. Tope murmured, her voice trembling with a theatrical pity. "Thoroughly worn out. No wonder!"

"A man cannot have his rest broken," Mr. Grewgious recited, his tone flat and instructional, "nor his mind cruelly tormented, nor his body overtaxed by fatigue, without being thoroughly worn out."

Jasper, weak and pale, offered a faint apology for alarming them. Grewgious dismissed it with a repetitive nod, offering thanks that sounded less like gratitude and more like a refusal to acknowledge the gravity of the situation. Mrs. Tope, however, saw only a hungry guest. She announced the arrival of wine, jelly, and a wing of roast fowl that had been reheated twenty times, perhaps once. As she bustled to serve, Grewgious emitted a sharp snort—a sound devoid of meaning, capable of signifying agreement, denial, or total absence.

Jasper ate and drank with a voracious indifference, consuming the food not to savor it, but to anchor himself against the vertigo of his thoughts. Grewgious remained upright, a statue of polite imperturbability, refusing to engage in any discourse that might reveal the cracks in his composure.

When Jasper finally pushed his plate aside, he spoke. "Do you know," he asked, his voice regaining a thread of strength, "that I find some crumbs of comfort in the communication which has so amazed me?"

"Do you?" Grewgious replied, the unspoken clause hanging heavy in the air: *I do not.*

Jasper elaborated. After the shock of learning that his beloved Edwin had withheld a devastating secret, a secret that destroyed the castles he had built for the boy, a sliver of light had pierced the darkness. "Supposing him to have disappeared of his own act," Jasper argued, tracing the logic with desperate fervor, "is not his disappearance more accountable? Less cruel? The fact that he had just parted from your ward provides a reason. It relieves the cruelty from her."

Grewgious assented.

"And even as to me," Jasper continued, his face brightening with a dangerous hope, "he knew you were coming. He knew you were entrusted with this truth. Therefore, he foresaw the inferences I would draw. Even the cruelty to me—John Jasper, Music Master—vanishes!"

Again, Grewgious nodded.

"I begin to believe it possible," Jasper declared, clasping his hands, "that he may have disappeared of his own accord, and that he may yet be alive and well."

At that moment, Mr. Crisparkle entered. Jasper repeated his theory to the Minor Canon. Crisparkle, though troubled, found the argument plausible. He recalled his own conversation with Mr. Sapsea, where Jasper had insisted there was no quarrel between Edwin and Neville Landless at their last meeting. Yet, Crisparkle carried a private burden. He knew of Neville's fiery temper, incited by a romantic delusion regarding Jasper's niece. He remembered two specific incidents: a second outbreak of anger against Edwin, and the jealous flame burning in Neville's breast. These facts weighed heavily against Neville's innocence, yet Crisparkle feared that voicing them now would be tantamount to constructing a falsehood to replace the truth.

Watching Jasper's hopeful transformation, Crisparkle hesitated no longer. He addressed Grewgious, testifying to Jasper's integrity and affirming his own belief in Edwin's clearance from suspicion. He admitted that despite knowing Neville's volatile nature and his infatuation with the girl, he maintained his faith in the young man. This declaration struck Jasper like a blow; he paled, yet clung tenaciously to the hope that Edwin had simply absconded of his own wild will.

Crisparkle left the room, his mind unsettled, and took a night walk. He wandered aimlessly until the sound of falling water reached him—the Cloisterham Weir. Starlight illuminated the scene. The water crashed over the weir with its usual rhythm, a cold, indifferent noise. Crisparkle reasoned with himself, searching for the source of his unease. He listened, he looked, he strained his hawk-like eyes. Nothing was visible. Nothing was audible.

Yet, the place felt haunted.

He returned at sunrise. The frost bit at his skin, but the morning clarity allowed him to survey the weir in its minutest details. Suddenly, his gaze locked onto a single spot. Something glistened there, stationary amidst the moving water. It was not a reflection.

Without hesitation, Crisparkle stripped off his clothes and plunged into the icy depths. He swam to the spot, climbing the timbers to retrieve a gold watch caught in the interstices of the wood. Engraved on its back were the letters: *E. D.*

He dove again, searching for the body, but found only a shirt-pin stuck in the mud.

Returning to Cloisterham, Crisparkle took Neville Landless with him and proceeded straight to the Mayor. Jasper was summoned. The watch and pin were identified. The narrative shifted instantly. Neville was detained, and the wildest frenzies of evil report rose against him. He was painted as a vindictive monster, a man of violent nature who had nearly brought Mrs. Crisparkle to her grave. The Mayor recounted Neville's past atrocities—his whipping of nomadic "Natives" across the globe, his threats to destroy lives, his importation by a Philanthropist who believed him to be a danger to the smallest number. In the court of public opinion, the missing boy was presumed murdered, and the lover was presumed guilty.
