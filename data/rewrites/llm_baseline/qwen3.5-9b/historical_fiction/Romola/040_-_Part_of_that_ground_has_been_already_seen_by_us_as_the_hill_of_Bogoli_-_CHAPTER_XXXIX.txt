As they stepped into the elegant pavilion, Tito's keen eye immediately confirmed his suspicion: the gathering was political. Yet, it was a politics softened by camaraderie, a strengthening of faction through good fellowship. In those days, fine food and vintage wine were believed to sharpen political instincts; it was thought that post-prandial conversation allowed individuals to clarify their views with a lucidity impossible for those who had not first satisfied their hunger. Though the Florentines prided themselves on sobriety and thrift, wherever wealth accumulated, devotees of *Madonna della Gozzoviglia* (Good Cheer) and *San Buonvino* (Good Wine) inevitably arose. The Rucellai family stood among the few Florentine houses capable of sustaining such lavish tables. Consequently, no high-minded philosophical treatises were expected tonight. Nor would anyone object to the bronze bust of Plato surveying the scene, or to the frescoes depicting the Cardinal Virtues adorning the walls.

That bust of Plato had long since ceased to gaze upon transcendent ideals. Removed from Lorenzo's villa after his death, it now presided over the Platonic Academy's sessions in these gardens. Every year on the thirteenth of November, the anniversary of Plato's passing, it looked down from beneath laurel leaves upon a select assembly of scholars. They ate and drank moderately, yet discussed the Master's doctrines with perhaps less restraint. Among them were figures of note: Pico della Mirandola, once a Quixotic youth with cascading curls, amazed by his own intellect and later humbled by a consuming quest for inner perfection; Marsilio Ficino, the diligent, youthful philosopher nourished exclusively on Platonism until his mind grew soft and pulpy from the diet; Angelo Poliziano, the era's supreme literary genius, a poet whose words still seem to pulse with life; and Leon Battista Alberti, the revered elder statesman, a robust mind encompassing art, science, invention, and poetry. Behind them lay countless unnamed laborers whose contributions formed the unseen foundation of their heritage, much like the ploughing and sowing of generations past.

Bernardo Rucellai himself held a distinguished place in this academy even before becoming its patron. In his mid-forties, he possessed a haughty, dignified bearing, conscious of his impeccable Latin—a tongue, Erasmus noted, that could not be coaxed into speech by the sharpest German interrogators. He greeted Tito with particular favor, seating him between Lorenzo Tornabuoni and Giannozzo Pucci, both accomplished young members of the Medicean faction.

Conversation flowed lightly while a brass basin of scented water circulated for hand-washing. Rings glinted on white fingers beneath the candlelight, mingling with the fresh scent of imported French damask roses. The topic turned to the inevitable: "What would old Dante think," someone mused, "if he could return to life, strapped in his leather belt, to see silver forks upon the table?" Consensus held that modern customs would astonish ancestors, and vice versa.

As silver forks began to toy with the appetizing delicacies preceding the main course—morsels of liver cooked to a melting tenderness—the guests admired the enamelwork on the brass service centers. They praised the silver dish for *confetti*, a masterpiece by Antonio Pollaiuolo, whom papal patrons had lured from Florence to the opulence of Rome.

"Ah, I recall," said Niccolò Ridolfi, a middle-aged man whose negligent demeanor masked a lifetime of commanding authority. "I remember our Antonio complaining bitterly about his chiseling and enameling of these metal trifles. He threw himself into painting with a fury, declaring, 'The artist who casts his work into gold and silver pours his brain into the melting pot.'"

"That is a grim prophecy," Giannozzo Pucci remarked. "If this war with Pisa continues and the revolt spreads, our silver dishes may follow suit. I suspect Antonio's silver saints around the altar of San Giovanni will one day vanish from the faithful's sight to be worshipped more devoutly as coins."

"The Friar prepares us for this already," Lorenzo Tornabuoni added. "He tells the people that God rejects silver crucifixes alongside starving bellies, insisting the Church is best adorned with the gems of holiness and the golden hue of brotherly love."

"A sound doctrine of war finance," Bernardo Rucellai replied dryly. "But politics wait for the wine, Lorenzo. These matters are too heavy for roast meat and boiled dishes."

"Indeed," Niccolò Ridolfi agreed. "Luigi Pulci would insist this delicate kid requires an impartial palate. I recall him at Careggi, in his rambunctious mood, claiming that nothing corrupts the tongue like opinion. 'Opinion,' he said, 'spoils the saliva—that is why men crave pepper. Only skepticism offers a flavorless philosophy.' 'No,' retorted poor Lorenzo de' Medici, 'you are mistaken. Here sits Matteo Franco, an untainted skeptic demanding hotter sauce than any of us.' 'Because he holds a strong opinion of himself,' Luigi flashed back. 'He believes in the immortality of his own verses. He is a logician like that preaching friar who mapped the pavement of hell. Poor Luigi! His mind was like sharpest steel, touching nothing without cutting.'"

"And yet a gentle soul," Giannozzo Pucci countered. "His talk seemed merely bubbles bursting in the wind. What dithyrambs he sang of eating and drinking, yet he was as temperate as a butterfly."

The light chatter and substantial fare continued for some time. Following the roasts came the essential capon and game, crowned by the spectacular presentation of a peacock prepared à la Apicius. Cooked with feathers intact and arranged to resemble a living bird resting on the platter, it demanded the skill of a master carver to flip the classical, albeit insipid, bird onto its back, exposing the plucked breast for slicing. Unless one possessed an independent spirit willing to reject this expensive display for the simple digestibility of capon, everyone partook of the peacock. Few dared refuse. Tito quoted Horace and scattered his slice in tiny fragments; Bernardo Rucellai offered a learned comment on the ancient price of peacock eggs but declined to eat his portion; Niccolò Ridolfi balanced a bite on his fork while recounting a favorite tale of Luigi Pulci regarding a Sienese man who boiled a wild goose in its feathers to deceive guests into thinking it a pheasant.

Little actual peacock was consumed, yet the satisfaction lay in witnessing such extravagance, accessible only to the wealthiest. It would have been reckless to mock the flesh of peacocks or similar traditions when Fra Girolamo was preaching the disruptive doctrine that luxury was not the duty of the rich toward the poor.

Outside, amidst the chill shadows surrounding this center of warmth and savory smells, the lonely outcast walked in tightening circles. He paused among the trees, peering into the windows where the interior glowed brightly. He heard the laughter, saw Tito gesturing with careless elegance, and heard his voice rising and falling in the merry chaos of overlapping speech. Baldassarre's mind was agitated. He prepared himself for the moment he might breach this brilliant circle, deriving a savage pleasure from watching Tito's effortless gaiety, which seemed to be blinding the unsuspecting victim to coming torment.

Yet, the men seated among the flickering candles and gleaming goblets knew nothing of the pale, fierce face watching from the dark. Light can serve as a curtain as effectively as shadow.

As the conversation deepened, shedding its triviality, the sense of civic duty pressed heavily upon even the most indifferent minds. The teachings of the overpowering Fra Girolami dominated their thoughts. Before the stewed fish was cleared and the sweets presented, the Friar's name surfaced, and despite Rucellai's earlier warning against politics, the discussion resumed. Initially, with servants still present, it remained mere gossip: the results of the first day's voting for the Great Council; the fiery, domineering nature of Francesco Valori, who seemed to demand absolute rule by virtue of his austerity; and the obvious alignment between Soderini's speeches supporting the Great Council and the Friar's sermons, suggesting they were molded from the same dough.

"In my view," Niccolò Ridolfi stated, "the Friar possesses a sharper mind for public affairs than Soderini or any of the *Piagnoni*. You can count on Soderini being his mouthpiece rather than the other way around."

"No, Niccolò; I disagree," Bernardo Rucellai corrected. "The Friar has an acute mind and readily identifies what serves his ends, but it is unlikely that Pagolantonio Soderini, with his extensive experience and study of the Venetian Council, owes much to a monk for his ideas. No, Soderini loads the cannon; I admit the Friar provides the powder and strikes the match. He masters the people, and the people are beginning to master us. Behold!"

"Well," Lorenzo Tornabuoni said shortly after the servants departed and only wine circulated, "whether Soderini is indebted or not, *we* owe the Friar the general amnesty accompanying the Council scheme. We might have endured the fear of God and moral reforms passed by a majority of black beans, but the proposal that allows our Medicean heads to remain safely on our shoulders, sparing us the loss of property to fines, earns my warm approval.
