By the Editor

If my old, dear friend the Square of Flatland still held the sharp, curious vigour of mind he had when he first penned these memoirs, I would not need to speak for him here. He had three things he was desperate to say to his readers, first of all: to offer his deepest gratitude to the Spaceland audience and critics who loved his account of 2D life so fiercely that demand for a second edition arose far faster than either of us ever dared hope. Second, to apologise for the scattered small errors and misprints that slipped into the first run – flaws for which, he swears, he is not entirely to blame, given how clumsy 3D pen and paper feels to a being who has never known more than two directions of movement. Third, to clear up a handful of very persistent misconceptions that have cropped up in reviews and reader letters since his book first launched.

Alas, he is not the bright, restless Square who snuck a 24-hour glimpse of our 3D world all those years ago. Years of locked confinement in Flatland’s state prison, the far heavier weight of universal mockery and disbelief from everyone he ever knew, and the soft eroding fog of old age have scrubbed most of the terminology and sharp, vivid memories he picked up during his short visit to Spaceland right out of his mind. So he has begged me to speak on his behalf to address the two most strident objections raised against his work, one intellectual, one moral.

The first and most common critique runs like this: when a Flatlander looks at a line, their eye must perceive some tiny thickness as well as visible length, otherwise the line (with exactly zero depth) would be completely invisible. Therefore, the argument goes, Flatlanders must have a tiny, unacknowledged third dimension of height, however infinitesimal, and the Square should have admitted this plainly from the start.

This argument is so plausible, so irrefutable to most Spacelanders, that I will admit I had no reply for it the first time I heard it. But my old friend’s answer, when I brought the critique to him in his cell, is entirely unassailable.

“I admit every fact your critic states,” he told me, “but I deny their conclusion. It is technically true that every object in Flatland carries that unrecognised third dimension we could call ‘height’, just as it is true that every object in our Spaceland has a fourth unrecognised dimension, no one has named yet, that we could call ‘extra-height’. But we Flatlanders can no more perceive or reason out our ‘height’ than you Spacelanders can wrap your heads around that fourth ‘extra-height’. Even I – who walked your streets, who understood the meaning of height perfectly for 24 whole hours – cannot visualise it now, cannot see it with my eyes or work it out through logic. I can only hold it as a matter of faith.

“The reason is simple. A dimension implies direction, it implies measurement, it implies the possibility of more and less. Every line, every shape, every object in Flatland is equally, infinitesimally thin. There is no variation, no difference in that tiny third dimension that could ever nudge any Flatlander to suspect a whole new direction exists. No fancy, delicate micrometer a hasty critic suggested would help us at all – we would not know what to measure, nor in what direction to point our tool.

“To us, a line you would call 1D is both long and bright: brightness is not a separate property we detach from it, it is the very life of the line. If the brightness fades, the line vanishes entirely. So when I try to explain this unseeable third dimension to my fellow prisoners or guards, they all nod and say ‘oh, you mean brightness’. When I tell them no, this is a real, separate physical dimension, they laugh and demand I measure it, or point out which direction it extends. I cannot do either, and I fall silent. Only last week, the Chief Circle – our High Priest, the absolute head of our ruling aristocracy – made his seventh annual visit to inspect the prison, and once more asked if I had renounced my mad stories of a third dimension. I tried to prove to him that he too was tall, in that hidden direction, as well as long and broad. What did he say? ‘Measure my height, and I will believe you.’ I had no answer. He left the cell triumphant, and I was left to pace my stone floor.

“Does that still sound absurd to you? Put yourself in my place. Imagine a visitor from the Fourth Dimension drops through your ceiling one day, and says: ‘Every time you open your eyes, you look at a 2D plane, and infer a 3D solid. But you are all seeing a fourth dimension too, one that is not color, not brightness, not any property you know – it is a real dimension, even if I cannot point out its direction, and you could never measure it.’ What would you do? You would call the orderlies to lock the strange man up, would you not? That is exactly my fate. It is as natural for Flatland to lock up a Square for preaching the Third Dimension as it is for Spaceland to lock up a Cube for preaching the Fourth.

“Alas, how alike all blinded, persecuting beings are across every possible world. Points, Lines, Squares, Cubes, Extra-Cubes: we are all slaves to the prejudices of the dimensions we happen to be born into, just as one of your Spaceland poets wrote: *One touch of Nature makes all worlds akin*.”

[Note from the Editor, at the explicit request of the Square: to clear up this exact widespread misconception, he has added new asides to his original dialogue with the visiting Sphere, remarks he once cut as tedious and unnecessary, to lay this physics confusion entirely to rest.]

This first defence of the Square’s is, as far as I can tell, completely unbreakable. I wish I could say his reply to the second, moral objection was equally clear and convincing. Many critics have called him a woman-hater, an accusation raised fiercely by that larger half of Spaceland’s population whose voices he so often sidelined in his original draft. I would happily clear this stain from his name entirely, if I could do so honestly, but his seven years in prison have softened and shifted his views so far from what they were when he first wrote his memoirs that I would do him a disservice to transcribe his rambling defence verbatim. As his interpreter, I can sum it up plainly: he no longer holds the unkind, dismissive views of Flatland’s Straight-Line women and working-class Isosceles Triangles that were standard among his social circle. He now finds himself agreeing entirely with the Sphere that women are, in many critical ways, wiser and more perceptive than the elite Circles who rule Flatland. But when he wrote his history, he fell prey to the bad habit shared by almost all Flatland historians, and nearly all Spaceland historians until very recently: he treated the lives of women and the mass of ordinary working people as irrelevant background noise, unworthy of detailed, careful attention.

He also wishes to formally disavow the aristocratic, pro-Circle biases that many readers fairly assumed he held. He still acknowledges the sharp, ruthless intelligence that allowed a tiny ruling class of Circles to maintain power over millions of Flatlanders for hundreds of generations – but he points out that the bare facts of Flatland history, told without commentary, speak for themselves. Revolutions can never be permanently crushed by massacres, and Nature, in condemning the Circles to near-total infertility, has already doomed their dynasty to eventual collapse. “In that,” he told me, “I see the great law that binds every world together: the schemes that the powerful think they are building for their own eternal dominion are always twisted by Nature’s greater wisdom into something far different, and far kinder, than they ever intended.”

As a final note, he begs his readers not to insist that every tiny silly detail of daily life in Flatland must map 1:1 to some equivalent rule in our 3D world. All he hopes is that, for modest, curious Spaceland readers – the kind who refuse to shout “that is completely impossible!” at anything that falls outside their experience, but who also refuse to claim they already know every single fact about realities beyond their own – this little book will prove both amusing, and thought-provoking.
