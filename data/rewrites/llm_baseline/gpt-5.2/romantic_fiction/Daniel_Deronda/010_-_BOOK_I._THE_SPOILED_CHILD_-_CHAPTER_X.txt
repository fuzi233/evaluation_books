“What woman should be?” one gentleman drawled, as if he were tasting the phrase before swallowing it. “Sir, consult the appetite of marriageable men. The world’s store—iron, cotton, wool, and all the rest—gets hammered and spun into whatever the market wants. So with our daughters. They must be wives; and to be wives they must be what men will choose. Men’s taste is woman’s test. You mark the phrasing? The little click of t’s and s’s—excellent.”

“Nay,” said another, amused, “turn it round. Give us the test of taste. A fine menu—does it mean to-day what Roman epicures meant when they insisted a gentleman must know how to eat before he could pretend to dine?”

Their talk ran on with the lightness of champagne bubbles, while Brackenshaw Park spread itself under a July sky like a great green table laid for company. From the gentle heights the view fell away over the neighboring valley to the eastern downs, and westward the cultivated country rose broad and slow, like a curtain hung in folds of wheat, hedgerow, and pale road-dust. The castle, rough-hewn of limestone, held the sun in pits and angles where rain and lichen had worked their patient stains; beech and fir sheltered it on the north, and slid down the slopes in dark masses that looked, at a distance, like flocks moving toward the glitter of water.

At the far end of the park the archery-ground lay as carefully tended as a kitchen garden before a fête-day: the gravel walk combed smooth, the turf newly shorn, the targets set out with the punctuality of plates. Tall elms and a dense screen of hollies threw an agreeable afternoon shade—shade that softened everything, even vanity. The Archery Hall, white with an arcade in front, showed like a small temple against the green; and before it the ladies moved in flower-groups, bowing and turning their necks with that leisurely grace lilies might have, if lilies decided to stroll.

The soundscape was as carefully composed as the scene. When the military band from Wanchester paused, the air did not collapse into emptiness; it filled instead with bright, layered laughter, with speech that rose now into mild excitement, now sank to a contented murmur. There were no coarse roars, no pushing crowd, none of the harsh modern din that makes pleasure feel like an engine-room. Here the human voices kept their musical proportion to the birds in the trees and the soft, periodic thud of arrows finding their mark. Even the tenants admitted as spectators—Lord Brackenshaw’s own people with their families—seemed to keep a natural hush, as if the place itself required a certain gentleness of sound.

The men among the tenants made sport for themselves with imaginative betting—“anything you like” laid down on their favorite archer—while the young maidens, ruled by another kind of hunger, chose silently which sweetly-dressed lady they would like to be if the choice were offered. Perhaps their rustic fancy would have picked a face with more pink and hair of a more fashionable yellow than Gwendolen Harleth’s; but the male judges pressed nearest her were unusually unanimous: she was, to them, the finest girl present.

No wonder she enjoyed her existence that day. Pre-eminence is sweet to those who love it. And for complete enjoyment the outward must answer to the inward: the body’s ease must match the mind’s bright certainty. That concurrence was happening for Gwendolen, as if everything—light, air, eyes upon her—had been prepared like a well-timed course.

Bows and arrows, too, are among the prettiest weapons for a feminine figure to play with: they demand grace, and that fine concentration seen in all marksmanship, but without the associations of blood and powder. Archery carried no acrid breath of brimstone on it; it broke nobody’s shins, bred no hulking athletic monsters. Its only danger was failure—and for generous blood that danger is enough to mould skill.

At Brackenshaw the prizes were of the symbolic kind, not something to wrap and carry home like a joint from market. The gold arrow and the silver, the gold star and the silver, were to be worn awhile in sign of victory and then passed on to the next who deserved them. Like wreaths, only without the inconvenience of wilting in a ballroom. The whole institution had been framed with good taste so that, by necessity, it need not admit ridiculous incidents.

And to-day everything incalculable was in its favor. The warmth was mild; there was no wind to disturb hair, drapery, or the arrow’s course. Skill had fair play. When all marched together to extract the arrows, the movement of bright young creatures toward a common object—light speech, quick laughter, the swish of skirts, the subdued tap of shoes on gravel—made a kind of ritual, pleasant to the eye and, in its gentle rhythm, soothing to the ear.

Here Gwendolen seemed a Calypso among her nymphs. It was in her attitudes and movements that all were obliged to admit her surpassing charm.

“That girl is like a high-mettled racer,” said Lord Brackenshaw to young Clintock, one of the invited spectators.

“First chop! tremendously pretty too,” said the elegant Grecian, who had been paying her assiduous attention. “I never saw her look better.”

Perhaps she had never looked so well. Her face beamed with young pleasure without that stray ray of discontent which can spoil even beauty. Satisfied with her own chances, she felt kindly toward everybody and, for the hour, toward the universe.

Not to have the highest distinction in rank, not to be marked out as an heiress like Miss Arrowpoint, gave an added triumph to her eclipse of such advantages. She would not have exchanged her own accompanying group for any other: her mother’s appearance might have suited an amiable duchess; her uncle and aunt Gascoigne with Anna made equally gratifying figures in their way. And she was too full of joyous belief in herself to feel jealousy though Miss Arrowpoint was one of the best archeresses.

Even the reappearance of the formidable Herr Klesmer—which caused surprise through the company—fell in with Gwendolen’s appetite for amusement. Short of Apollo himself, what great musical maestro could be anything but a comic contrast at an archery meeting?

There was a satirical light in her eyes when she looked toward the Arrowpoint party at their entrance. The contrast between Klesmer and the average group of English country people was at its utmost, set close by his hosts—or patrons, as Mrs. Arrowpoint would have liked to hear them called, that she might deny the possibility of patronizing genius now that its royalty was, in her imagination, universally acknowledged.

We English are a miscellaneous people; any chance fifty will show varieties of face and figure. But our prevailing expression is not that of a lively, impassioned race carrying the real as mere make-weight. The strong point of the English gentleman pure is his easy style; he objects to marked ins and outs of costume, and he objects to looking inspired.

Now imagine those ordinary well-bred men watching the entrance of Herr Klesmer: his mane of hair floating backward in massive inconsistency with the chimney-pot hat perched above pronounced, well-modeled features; the powerful clear-shaven mouth and chin; the tall thin figure clothed in a way which, not being strictly English, was all the worse for its apparent emphasis of intention. Draped in some loose Florentine grace with a berretta on his head, he might have stood beside Leonardo; but here the trousers were not what English feeling demanded at the knees; and the fire in his glances, the quick movement of his head as he looked round with curiosity, became comedy under a hat that proclaimed mankind should have well-cropped hair and a staid demeanor. Some present laughed; others felt a mild disgust at what they called the Arrowpoints’ want of judgment in presenting such a figure, unexpectedly, on a July afternoon in exclusive society.

“What extreme guys those artistic fellows usually are!” Clintock said to Gwendolen. “Do look at the figure he cuts, bowing with his hand on his heart to Lady Brackenshaw—and Mrs. Arrowpoint’s feather just reaching his shoulder.”

“You are one of the profane,” said Gwendolen. “You are blind to the majesty of genius. Herr Klesmer smites me with awe; I feel crushed in his presence; my courage all oozes from me.”

“Ah—you understand all about his music.”

“No, indeed,” said Gwendolen, laughing lightly. “It is he who understands all about mine and thinks it pitiable.”

Klesmer’s verdict on her singing had been an easier joke since he had been struck by her plastik.

“It is not addressed to the ears of the future, I suppose. I’m glad of that: it suits mine.”

“Oh, you are very kind. But how remarkably well Miss Arrowpoint looks to-day! She would make quite a fine picture in that gold-colored dress.”

“Too splendid, don’t you think?”

“Well, perhaps a little too symbolical—too much like the figure of Wealth in an allegory.”

The malice of it was only surface—one of those bubbles of fun that sparkle and burst, leaving no bitterness. Gwendolen did not wish Miss Arrowpoint, or any one else, out of the way; she believed in her own luck even more than in her skill. And as the shooting went on, belief in both rose together. She promised a score among the best—astonishing in a new member—and to her temperament one success prepared the next. She trod on air; all pleasant things seemed possible. The hour was enough for her: she did not need to ask herself what she should do next to keep life at the due pitch.

“How does the scoring stand, I wonder?” said Lady Brackenshaw. A gracious personage, adorned with two little girls and a stout boy, she sat as lady paramount while her lord came up during an interval.

“It seems to me Miss Harleth is likely to win the gold arrow,” Lady Brackenshaw added.

“Gad, I think she will, if she carries it on! She’s running Juliet Fenn hard,” said Lord Brackenshaw. “Wonderful for one in her first year. Catherine is not up to her usual mark,” he continued, turning to the heiress’s mother who sat near. “But she got the gold arrow last time. There’s a luck even in these games of skill. That’s better. Gives the hinder ones a chance.”

“Catherine will be very glad for others to win,” said Mrs. Arrowpoint, with the smooth emphasis of a woman serving virtue as if it were a dish she had invented. “She is so magnanimous. It was entirely her considerateness that made us bring Herr Klesmer instead of Canon Stopley, who had expressed a wish to come. For her own pleasure, I am sure she would rather have brought the Canon; but she is always thinking of others. I told her it was not quite en règle to bring one so far out of our own set; but she said, ‘Genius itself is not en règle; it comes into the world to make new rules.’ And one must admit that.”

“Ay, to be sure,” said Lord Brackenshaw carelessly, dismissing the subject with the ease of a man who prefers his entertainment without lectures. “For my part, I am not magnanimous; I should like to win. But, confound it! I never have the chance now. I’m getting old and idle. The young ones beat me. As old Nestor says—the gods don’t give us everything at one time: I was a young fellow once, and now I am getting an old and wise one. Old, at any rate; which is a gift that comes to everybody if they live long enough—so it raises no jealousy.”

He smiled comfortably at his wife.

“Oh, my lord, people who have been neighbors twenty years must not talk to each other about age,” said Mrs. Arrowpoint. “Years, as the Tuscans say, are made for the letting of houses. But where is our new neighbor? I thought Mr. Grandcourt was to be here to-day.”

“Ah, by the way, so he was. The time’s getting on too,” said his lordship, looking at his watch. “But he only got to Diplow the other day. He came to us on Tuesday and said he had been a little bothered. He may have been pulled in another direction. Why, Gascoigne!”—the rector was just then crossing at a little distance with Gwendolen on his arm and turned at the call—“this is a little too bad; you not only beat us yourself, but you bring up your niece to beat all the archeresses.”

“It is rather scandalous in her to get the better of elder members,” said Mr. Gascoigne, with inward satisfaction curling his short upper lip. “But it is not my doing, my lord. I only meant her to make a tolerable figure, without surpassing any one.”

“It is not my fault either,” said Gwendolen, with pretty archness. “If I am to aim, I can’t help hitting.”

“Ay, ay, that may be a fatal business for some people,” said Lord Brackenshaw good-humoredly; then, watch still in hand, he looked again toward Mrs. Arrowpoint. “The time’s getting on, as you say. But Grandcourt is always late. In town he’s always late—and he’s no bowman, understands nothing about it. But I told him he must come; he would see the flower of the neighborhood here. He asked about you—had seen Arrowpoint’s card. I think you had not made his acquaintance in town. He has been a good deal abroad. People don’t know him much.”

“No; we are strangers,” said Mrs. Arrowpoint. “But that is not what might have been expected. For his uncle Sir Hugo Mallinger and I are great friends when we meet.”

“I don’t know; uncles and nephews are not so likely to be seen together as uncles and nieces,” said Lord Brackenshaw, smiling toward the rector. “But just come with me one instant, Gascoigne, will you? I want to speak a word about the clout-shooting.”

Gwendolen chose to go too and be deposited in the same group with her mother and aunt until she had to shoot again. And there—beneath her saucy satire, provoked chiefly by the divination that her friends thought Grandcourt a desirable match for her—there stirred something not at all indifferent: a quick, secret concern as to the impression she would make.

True, she told herself he was to have not the slightest power over her. He would be one of those complimentary, assiduously admiring men whom even her narrow experience had supplied in several specimens: various-colored beards, various styles of bearing, all obedient to her charm. The fact that her friends wanted her to think him delightful gave her a resisting impulse to presuppose him ridiculous. But that was no reason to spare his presence. Even a passing prevision of trouble—if she despised and refused him—raised not the least wish that he should save her the trouble by keeping away. Mr. Grandcourt scarcely noticing her, and shortly becoming engaged to Miss Arrowpoint, was no picture to flatter her imagination.

So she had been all ear to Lord Brackenshaw’s accounting for Grandcourt’s non-appearance; and when he did arrive, no consciousness—not even Mrs. Arrowpoint’s or Mr. Gascoigne’s—was more awake to it than hers. Yet she steadily avoided looking toward any point where he was likely to stand. She would not let the smallest shift of angle betray that it mattered to her whether the much-talked-of Mr. Mallinger Grandcourt appeared or not.

She became absorbed again in shooting, and abstained so resolutely from any observant glance that even if he took a conspicuous place among the spectators it would be clear she was unaware. All the while, the certainty that he was there ran like a distinct thread through her consciousness. Perhaps her shooting was the better for it: it gained in precision; and at last she raised a storm of clapping and applause by three hits running in the gold. Among the Brackenshaw archers the feat had no vulgar reward of coin, but a special gold star to be worn on the breast.

The moment was happy to herself—and it was exactly what her mother and uncle would have chosen. People fell back, giving her space to advance conspicuously and receive the gold star from Lady Brackenshaw. The movement of her fine form in the clear afternoon light—shadows long and still—made her the center of a pretty picture; everyone must gaze at her. That was enough. She was determined to see nobody in particular, to turn her eyes no way except toward Lady Brackenshaw. But thoughts are not bridled like eyes.

It entered into her pleasure that Herr Klesmer must be observing her at a moment when music was out of the question and his superiority far in the background. Vanity, like tenderness, cannot rest easily under a gaze it cannot govern; and the unconquered Klesmer threw a trace of his malign power even across her pleasant certainty that Mr. Grandcourt was seeing her to the utmost advantage and was likely to admire without criticism. She did not expect to admire him; that was not necessary to her peace of mind.

Gwendolen met Lady Brackenshaw’s gracious smile without blushing—blushes came to her only when surprise took her—and bent with easy grace while the star was fixed near her shoulder. The little ceremony ended; she exchanged playful speeches, received congratulations, drifted among the groups now intent on the scoring. Presently she stood outside them, examining the point of an arrow with an absent air, when Lord Brackenshaw came up.

“Miss Harleth,” he said, “here is a gentleman who is not willing to wait any longer for an introduction. He has been getting Mrs. Davilow to send me with him. Will you allow me to introduce Mr. Mallinger Grandcourt?”
