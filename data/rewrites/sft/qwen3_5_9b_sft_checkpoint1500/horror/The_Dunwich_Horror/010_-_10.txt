Rain lashed the muddy road below Sentinel Hill, turning the earth into a slick, sucking mire that slowed every step. Three men from Arkham—Dr. Armitage, a frail elder with a beard like spun snow; Professor Rice, a sturdy man of iron-gray hair; and Dr. Morgan, thin and sharp-eyed—climbed upward alone. They had spent hours teaching the terrified locals how to operate the great brass telescope, leaving the glass in the hands of the villagers who stood shivering in the rain. As the trio ascended, their progress agonizingly slow, the men on the road watched them with wide, fearful eyes. Above them, high on the slope, the massive swath of black shrubbery trembled. Its creator moved within it with a slowness that felt deliberate, a snail-like deliberation that belied the speed of its approach. It was clear now: the pursuers were closing in.

At the critical juncture, Curtis Whateley, representing the undecayed line of the family, gripped the telescope. He directed the crowd, pointing out that the Arkham men had deviated sharply from the path to seize a lower ridge overlooking the swath. His prediction proved correct; shortly after the invisible abomination tore past their position, the three men scrambled onto the minor elevation, placing themselves directly between the horror and the fleeing villagers.

Wesley Corey, now holding the lens, screamed that Armitage was adjusting the spray mechanism Rice clutched. The air grew heavy with anticipation; everyone knew the powder was designed to momentarily manifest the unseen nightmare. A few men squeezed their eyes shut, but Curtis lunged forward, snatching the telescope back. He strained his vision until his eyes watered, desperate to see the truth. Through the lenses, he saw Rice poised perfectly, ready to unleash the potent dust with devastating effect. To those without the glass, it appeared as nothing more than a fleeting flash of gray cloud near the summit—a disturbance the size of a house. But Curtis saw the reality.

With a piercing shriek that cut through the rain, Curtis dropped the instrument. It crashed into the ankle-deep mud, splattering with dirt. He reeled backward, his knees giving way as if struck by a physical blow. Two strong hands caught him before he hit the ground, but he offered no resistance, collapsing into a heap of pure, animalistic terror.

"Oh, oh, great Gawd... *that*... *that*...." he gasped, the words barely audible over the drumming rain.

A cacophony of confused shouting erupted from the crowd. Only Henry Wheeler, trembling with a mixture of pity and dread, managed to scoop up the fallen glass and brush away the filth. Curtis lay broken, his mind shattered, unable to speak coherently save for fragmented, hysterical bursts.

"Bigger 'n a barn!" Curtis wheezed, his voice cracking. "All made o' squirmin' ropes... hull thing sort o' shaped like a hen's egg bigger'n anything, with dozens o' legs like hogsheads that haff shut up when they step... nothin' solid abaout it—all like jelly, an' made o' sep'rit wrigglin' ropes pushed clost together... great bulgin' eyes all over it... ten or twenty maouths or trunks a-stickin' aout all along the sides, big as stovepipes, an' all a-tossin' an' openin' an' shuttin'... all gray, with kinder blue or purple rings..."

His breath hitched, ragged and wet. "*An' Gawd in Heaven—that haff face on top!*..."

That final image broke the dam. Curtis collapsed completely, his body convulsing as the vision burned itself into his soul. Fred Farr and Will Hutchins dragged him to the side of the road, laying him gently on the damp grass. Henry Wheeler, shaking uncontrollably, lifted the telescope again to peer at the mountain. Through the lenses, he saw only three tiny, frantic figures scrambling toward the summit, their movements blurring against the steep incline. Nothing else. Yet, a strange, unnatural silence fell over the deep valley behind them, followed by a sound that chilled the blood: the piping of countless whippoorwills. Their shrill chorus, usually a sign of summer, now sounded twisted, threaded with a note of tense, malignant expectancy.

Earl Sawyer seized the glass, scanning the peak. "Three figures," he reported, his voice tight. "They're standin' on the topmost ridge, right next to the altar-stone. One o' 'em... he seems ta be raisin' his hands above his head at rhythmic intervals." As Sawyer spoke, the crowd seemed to hear it too—a faint, half-musical hum drifting from the distance, as if a loud chant accompanied the wild gestures. The silhouette on that remote peak must have been a spectacle of infinite grotesquery, but no one present possessed the capacity for aesthetic appreciation. "I guess he's sayin' the spell," Wheeler whispered, snatching the telescope back from Earl. The whippoorwills were piping wildly, their rhythm singularly irregular, clashing with the visible frenzy of the ritual.

Suddenly, the sunshine seemed to dim, not due to clouds, but as if the light itself had been poisoned. A peculiar, suffocating gloom descended, marked by every observer. Beneath the hills, a deep rumble brewed, mixing strangely with a discordant thrumming that seemed to emanate from the sky itself. Lightning flashed overhead, illuminating the scene with a jagged, unnatural brilliance, though the heavens offered no promise of a storm. The chanting of the men from Arkham grew louder, unmistakable now. Wheeler saw through the glass that they were raising their arms in a synchronized, rhythmic incantation. Far away, from some lonely farmhouse, the frantic barking of dogs pierced the air.

The quality of the daylight shifted again, growing heavier, pressing down on the rumbling hills. A purplish darkness, born of a spectral deepening in the sky, enveloped the landscape. Lightning flashed again, brighter and more violent, and the crowd, staring in helpless wonder, fancied they saw a certain mistiness surrounding the altar-stone on the distant height. No one had touched the glass at that precise moment. The whippoorwills continued their erratic, pulsating song, while the men of Dunwich braced themselves, muscles taut, against some imponderable menace that seemed to saturate the atmosphere.

Then, without warning, the air filled with those deep, cracked, raucous vocalizations that would haunt the stricken group forever. They did not emerge from any human throat; the organs of man could produce no such acoustic perversions. If one had to assign a source, it would have been the pit itself, yet the origin was unmistakably the altar-stone on the peak. It was almost wrong to call them *sounds*, for so much of their ghastly, infra-bass timbre vibrated through the bones, striking dim seats of consciousness and terror far subtler than the ear. Still, they took the form of half-articulate *words*. They were loud—so loud as to echo the rumblings and thunder above—and yet they appeared to come from nowhere. Because the imagination conjured a source in the realm of non-visible beings, the huddled crowd at the mountain's base pressed closer together, wincing as if expecting a physical blow.

"*Ygnaiih ... ygnaiih ... thflthkh'ngha ... Yog-Sothoth....*" The hideous croaking rang out, vibrating through the earth. "*Y'bthnk ... h'ehye ... n'grkdl'lh....*"

The speech impulse seemed to falter, as if some frightful psychic struggle were raging within the speaker. Henry Wheeler strained his eye at the telescope, seeing only the three grotesquely silhouetted figures on the peak, flailing their arms in strange, frantic gestures as the incantation neared its climax. From what black wells of Acherontic fear, from what unplumbed gulfs of extra-cosmic consciousness, were those half-articulate thunder-croakings drawn? Presently, they gathered renewed force and coherence, swelling into stark, utter, ultimate frenzy.

"*Eh-ya-ya-ya-yahaah ... e'yaya-yayaaaa ... ngh'aaaa ... ngh'aaaa_ ... h'yuh ... h'yuh ... HELP! HELP! ... _ff--ff--ff_--FATHER! FATHER! YOG-SOTHOTH!..."*

And then, silence. The pallid group in the road, still reeling from the *indisputably English* syllables that had poured thickly and thunderously down from the frantic vacancy beside the altar-stone, were never to hear such words again. Instead, they jumped violently at the terrific report that rent the hills—a deafening, cataclysmic peal whose source, whether inner earth or sky, no hearer could ever place. A single lightning bolt shot from the purple zenith to strike the altar-stone. Immediately, a great tidal wave of viewless force and indescribable stench swept down from the hill, washing over the countryside.

Trees, grass, and underbrush were whipped into a fury, tearing apart in the wind. The frightened crowd at the mountain's base, already weakened by the lethal fetor that threatened to asphyxiate them, were almost hurled off their feet.
