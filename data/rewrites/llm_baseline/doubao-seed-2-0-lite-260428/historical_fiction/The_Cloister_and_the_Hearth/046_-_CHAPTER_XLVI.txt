Catherine was the most careful housewife in Tergou, who scarcely ever left her hearth for a full day—for every time she did, some mishap or other was sure to spring up in her absence. Keenly aware of this, she waited for a lull in all her domestic duties, and put off her planned visit to Sevenbergen to call on Margaret Brandt from one day to the next. Even the rare afternoon when all chores were done and she could have spared the time, Peter Buyskens’ mule was gone off on an errand somewhere, and no other transport could be found.

At last, one day Eli asked her bluntly, right in front of the whole family, if the rumor was true that she’d been meaning to go visit Margaret Brandt.
“Ay, my man,” she said, flushing a little.
“Then I forbid you to go.”
“Oh, do you now?”
“I do.”
“Then I suppose there’s no more to be said on the matter,” she replied, her cheeks hot with surprise.
“Not a word,” Eli answered, stern and unyielding.

When she was alone with her daughter Kate, she raged not against her husband, but against her own foolishness.
“Fool that I am! I should have snuck off there like a cat creeping up on a robin before anyone could stop me. That’s always my way—like a silly hen that can’t lay an egg without cackling loud enough to call the whole house to come steal it away. Next time you and I have a mind to do even the smallest harmless thing, let’s do it right then and there, no waiting, no talking it over with anyone else. That way, if anyone questions us, we can honestly say we never meant a bit of harm, and no one could fault us. For two pins I’d march over there right this minute, defiance to the whole household!”

Kate, gentle as ever, was startled by her mother’s wild outburst. “Nay, mother, be patient. Father will come around in time.”
“Ay, and Michaelmas will come round too—but who knows when? I was so set on you seeing the girl for yourself, so we could put our heads together and judge her fairly. You can never know a body, man or beast, till you’ve looked them in the eye. If I had fifty more sons, I’d never thwart a single one’s fancy before I’d seen the lass they loved for myself, and spotted if there were quicksands underfoot. People tell me I was wrong to condemn Gerard’s choice sight unseen, and they’re right. Life’s a long school, and the last lesson’s never learned—we kick out one fault, and pick up another, blundering here, blundering there, till we blunder straight into our graves and that’s the end of it.”

“Mother,” Kate said, timid and soft.
“What is it now? You look as if you’ve got bad news, poor thing, all white and scared.”
“I have an avowal to make,” Kate faltered, her voice barely above a whisper.
“Now there’s a fine grand word for you!” Catherine said, proud at once. “Our Gerard taught you that, I’ll wager. Go on, out with it.”
“Well, then, truth to tell: I have already seen her. And spoken with her, too.”
“And you never told me? After that, nothing in the world can surprise me ever again.”
“Mother, you were so sharp against her back then. I waited till I could tell you without making you angrier still.”
“Ay,” Catherine said, half sad, half bitter, “like mother, like daughter—cowardice is the curse of us both. I can snap and snarl at everyone else to keep this house running smooth, but Kate, did you ever get a harsh word or cross look from your poor old mother that made you fear to tell me the truth? Nay, don’t cry, lass. Ten to one you had your good reasons. Rise up now, brave heart, tell me the whole story—better late than never. First off: when and how did you hobble all the way to Sevenbergen on your poor crutches without me knowing?”
“I never set foot there in my life,” Kate said. “I won’t say I never wanted to see her, but I never went looking for her there.”
“There now,” Catherine said, nodding, “I knew my girl would never sneak off behind my back. Go on, I’m all ears, dying to hear.”

“Word came to me somehow—please, mother, I beg you on my knees, never ask me how I heard— that Gerard was locked up in the Stadthouse tower, a prisoner.”
“Ah!” Catherine gasped.
“Pretended to be by father’s orders, they said.” Catherine sighed so deep it was almost a moan, and whispered that things were far blacker than she’d ever guessed.
“So Giles and I snuck out after dark to go cheer him up. And there at the foot of the tower, there was a brave lass I’d never seen before, come on the very same errand. At first we scared each other half out of our wits, what with the tower’s bad reputation and our heads full of old tales of devils, and we both went as white as sheets in the moonlight. But the next moment, I said ‘You must be Margaret,’ and she said ‘And you are Kate.’ Think of that!”
“Did anyone ever hear the like? It was Gerard, of course—he’s been chattering about you to her, and about her to you, back and forth for months!”

Kate rewarded her mother’s quick guess with one of those soft, composite kisses pressed to her cheek that said all at once: You’re exactly right, you’re the cleverest mother alive, it’s so sweet we’re of one mind after never having been anything else, and a dozen other tender little things too soft for words.
“Now speak your mind, lass. Gerard’s not here—God only knows I wish he was—so we can talk freely.”
“She’s comely,” Kate said, “and her looks do her no wrong, no matter what anyone’s painted of her.”
“Eh, listen to young folk!” Catherine laughed. “I care more for good acts than good looks. Does she love my boy the way he deserves to be loved?”
“Sevenbergen is farther from the Stadthouse than our own house is,” Kate said thoughtfully, “and she got there before I did.” Catherine nodded, she understood perfectly.
“Nay, more—she’d already got him out before I even arrived. Climbed down from that captive’s tower, all the way.” Catherine shook her head, unbelieving. “That’s the highest tower for miles around! That can’t be done.”
“But it’s true,” Kate insisted. “She and an old man she’d brought with her figured out a way to run a rope up to his cell. There it was, dangling out his window when we got there, and Giles climbed right up it. At first I thought it must be magic. But then that frank sweet lass threw her arms around me, and her heart was beating hard against mine, and her cheeks were all wet with tears, and I knew it wasn’t glamour at all. It was love. She’s strong and hearty, not frail like me, but even I, poor weak thing that I am, know what it feels like to want to move the whole world just to help the people I love. And she loves Gerard, no doubt of it.”
“God bless her for it! God bless her!” Catherine cried.
“But—” Kate hesitated.
“But what, my lamb?”
“But is her love perfectly honest? It’s strange, but that very act that warmed your heart made mine cool a little. She’s not his wife, not really, mother. No matter what people say.”
“Humph. They stood at the altar together, everyone says.”
“Ay, but they left the church just as they came, maid and bachelor, no vows spoken.”
“The parson himself said that?” Catherine asked.
“No, I don’t know that for a fact.”
“Then I’ll take no man’s word for such a tangled skein but Gerard’s own.” She thought for a minute, then made up her mind. “Still, you’re right, lass. I’ll go to Sevenbergen alone. I’m a wife, not a slave. We’re all in the dark here, and she’s the one who holds the clue. I’ll question her with no one else around—least of all you. I won’t bring a good innocent lass like you into any house that’s got even a shadow of a spot on it, not even a palace made of solid gold.”

The more Catherine turned their conversation over in her head, the more drawn she felt to Margaret, and curiosity— that most potent of human passions, sharpened all the more by the fact Catherine never read books to slake it—was burning hot in her breast. At last, one fine sunny day right after dinner, she whispered to Kate: “Try to keep the house from falling to bits while I’m gone, won’t you?” She pulled on her very best kirtle and hood, her bright scarlet clocked hose, and her brand new shoes, and set off briskly down the road to Sevenbergen, not troubling anyone to lend her a mule.

When she got to the village, she asked the first person she saw where Margaret Brandt lived. The man shook his head, said the name was strange to him. She walked a little farther, and asked a girl of about fifteen who was lingering in a doorway. “Father!” the girl called into the house, “there’s another one come looking for that magician’s daughter!” The girl’s father came out, and told Catherine that Peter Brandt’s cottage was just outside the village on the east side. “You can see its chimney from here,” he said, pointing. “But you won’t find them there, neither father nor daughter. They moved out of town a whole week ago, bless you.”
“Say it’s not so, good man! I’ve walked all the way from Tergou.”
“From Tergou? Then you must have met that soldier on the road, didn’t you?”
“What soldier? Ay, I did pass a soldier on the way.”
“Well, that same soldier was here yesterday looking for that very Margaret Brandt.”
“And he was mad as fire that she was gone, too!” the girl put in. “That long beard of his and her pretty cheek—they’re no strangers, I’ll wager.”
“Don’t speak slander of people you don’t know, little one,” Catherine said sharply. “You’re far too young to be spreading tales. Now tell me more about this soldier, good man.”
“I don’t know much more,” the man said. “He turned up looking for Margaret, I told him she and her father had run off in the middle of the night a week prior, and some folks say the devil flew away with them, seeing as they’re both magicians. ‘The devil fly away with you and your bad news,’ he shouts at me, that’s all the thanks I get. But then he says he doesn’t believe me, so off he storms to the cottage. Presently he comes back, singing a different tune. ‘You were right and I was wrong,’ he says, and shoves a silver coin in my hand to prove it. My wife still won’t believe I told the truth all day, till I show it her.”
Catherine pretended not to care, but she leaned in to glance at the coin anyway.
“And he was quiet and sad, wasn’t he, father?” the girl said warmly. “He was the handsomest man I ever saw. Cheeks like a rose, a shiny full beard, eyes as dark and bright as sloe berries.”
“I saw he had a fine beard,” Catherine said, “but at my age, I don’t go gawking at young men the way I did when I was young and foolish. He was very polite, though, doffed his hat to me like I was a queen, and I curtsied right back to him, for good manners breed good manners. I never dreamed he was her sweetheart, though. Where is the baker in this town?”

The man blinked at the sudden, sharp turn of her question, and told her there were two bakers on the street, John Bush and Eric Donaldson. Catherine bade them goodbye, and set off again—but her sprightly little step dragged now, no longer tapping the ground with her heel in quick, eager jerks. She asked the bakers if Peter Brandt had left town owing anyone money. Bush said they’d never been his customers. Donaldson said not a single stiver: Margaret had come round the very night they left and paid every penny they owed, and he didn’t believe they owed a copper to anyone in the whole village.
“Can you tell me what sort of lass Margaret was?” Catherine asked as she turned to leave.
“Well, a bit too quiet and reserved for my taste,” Donaldson said. “I like a customer who’ll stop and chat when I’m not busy. But everyone says she’s a very good, dutiful daughter.”
“That’s no small praise,” Catherine said. “And they say she’s very pretty?”
“Wherever have you come from, old dame?” he laughed.
“From Tergou.”
“Oh, that explains it. The local lads call her the Beauty of Sevenbergen. The lasses, though, all scoff at that, and tear her to pieces so thoroughly no two of them can even agree what her faults are.”
“That’s all the proof I need,” Catherine said. “The bakers of Sevenbergen aren’t fools, and the young girls here are no shallower than they are in every other town in the world.”

She bought a small loaf of manchet bread, partly out of fellow-feeling for another shopkeeper, partly to prove to her own household that the bread she baked for them every day was far better than what they sold in Sevenbergen, and trudged back home to Tergou, heavy and dejected.

Kate met her just outside the town, her eyes bright and hopeful.
“Well, Kate lass, it’s a good thing I went,” Catherine said, her voice hollow. “My heart’s broken. Our Gerard has been cruelly wronged. That girl is no kin of ours, not from this hour on.”
“Alas, mother, I don’t understand what you mean.”
“Ask me no more questions, lass. Never speak her name to me again. That’s all.” Kate sighed soft and humble, and the two of them walked home together.

When they stepped through their door, they found a soldier sitting calmly by their hearth. The moment they came in, he stood up and saluted them politely. They stared at him—Kate a little surprised, Catherine full of rising, boiling indignation.
“What are you doing in my house?” Catherine snapped at him.
“I came looking for Margaret.”
“Well, we know no such person.”
“Don’t say that, dame. You know very well who Margaret Brandt is.”
“We’ve heard of her, and all to our cost.”
“Come, dame, I beg you, just tell me where she lives, that’s all I ask.”
“I don’t know where she lives, and I don’t care.”

Denys could tell she was lying on purpose, and bit his lip in frustration. “I expected to find myself in enemy territory here in Tergou, but if you knew the whole truth, you wouldn’t be so harsh to me.”
“I do know the whole truth,” Catherine said bitterly. “This morning I knew nothing at all.” She planted her hands on her hips, voice raised and eyes flashing, and demanded to know what impudence he had to sit down by her hearth, the last hearth on earth he should ever darken.
“May the devil fly away with your hearth to the lake of fire and brimstone!” Denys roared back, fluent in the local Flemish tongue. “Your own servant bade me sit here and wait for you, else I never would have set foot in your house. A curse on this churlish roof that treats an innocent stranger so!” He turned, scowling, and stormed toward the door.

Catherine gasped, suddenly frightened and a little guilty, and the fiery virago sat straight down and burst into tears. Kate sat down beside her, crying quiet too, not a moment behind her.
There’s an old saying from a wise writer long lost to us: She says “I feel all the weakness of a woman,” and he replies “Then you are invincible.” Denys learned that truth that day, right there on their doorstep. He stood looking ruefully at the mess his angry outburst had made.
“Nay, good wife,” he said, softening at once. “Don’t cry over a fool’s hasty words. I don’t mean half what I shout. Your house is your own, I’ve no right to be here at all. I’ll go now.”

“What is all this commotion?” said a grave, steady man’s voice. It was Eli, just come in from his shop.
“Here’s a ruffian been shouting at your wife and daughter and making them cry,” Denys explained.
“Little Kate, tell me what really happened,” Eli said, sensible as ever. “Ruffians don’t usually call themselves ruffians.”
Before Kate could speak, Catherine cut in: “Hush, girl. Muriel bade him sit down, and I didn’t know it, and snapped at him for intruding. Then he started storming about laying a curse on our whole house, root and branch. I’ve never been so cursed in all my life!”
“You were both a little to blame, the two of you,” Eli said calmly. “But what my servant promises, I will stand to. We don’t keep an open house for every stranger, but we’re not so poor we can’t give a seat by the fire on a cold day to a wayfarer with an honest face. And I see you are wounded, man.”
“Wounded?” Catherine and Kate both cried out at once.
“Do you think a soldier slings his arm up in a sling for fun?”
“Nay, it’s only a little arrow wound,” Denys said cheerfully.
“Only an arrow?” Kate cried, horrified. “Mother, how did we not see it before?”
“Truly, it’s nothing at all. But let me use it as an excuse for my bad temper just now. These little silly trifling wounds are the ones that make a man fretful. Why, your Gerard— he’s the sweetest tempered soul that ever breathed, but once when a bear took a chunk no bigger than a crown out of his calf, he turned so hot and choleric you’d have sworn he wasn’t your son at all, but sired by Sir John Pepper on Dame Mustard, that fiery knight and his hot wife. Who’s this?
