"You, Captain Miles Warrington, possess the rare honor of securing the favor of a lady—indeed, of several ladies simultaneously. Let us name the Duchess of Devonshire, Mrs. Crew, Mrs. Fitzherbert, the Queen of Prussia, the Goddess Venus herself, Mademoiselle Hillisberg of the Opera—it matters not whose affections you have captured. Now, if you win a lady's good graces, do you invariably retreat to the mess to recount every detail?"

"Not such a fool, Squire!" retorted the Captain, admiring his sideburns in the mirror. "Have you, Miss Theo, whispered every word spoken to Mr. Joe Blake, junior, in the shrubbery this morning?"

"Joe Blake, indeed!" cried Theo Junior. "And you, Mademoiselle? Did you reveal the contents of that scented note delivered under Sir Thomas's seal? Observe how crimson she turns! Red as the curtains, I swear! Well, Mademoiselle, we all harbor our secrets." (The Squire executed a particularly elegant French bow here.)

"No, Theo, there was nothing in the shrubbery save nuts, my child! No, Miles, my son, we do not disclose everything, even to the most indulgent of fathers—and if I recount what transpired in a landau on the Hampstead Road on the twenty-fifth of May, 1760, may Chevalier Ruspini extract every tooth from my skull!"

"Please, Papa!" urged Mama. "Or, since Jobson, who drove us, is now in your employ, perhaps you will summon him from the stables! I insist upon hearing the tale!"

"What is this mystery, then?" asked Mademoiselle, in her charming French accent, addressing my wife.

"Eh, ma fille!" the lady whispered. "Wouldst thou ask me what I said? I said 'Yes!'—behold, that is all I said."

Thus, it was my wife who had betrayed us, not I. This was the entirety of our exchange as the carriage, faster than I deemed prudent, galloped toward Hampstead and returned. Theo had not agreed to defy her honored parents—such a notion was unthinkable. Yet, we resolved to wed no other; nay, not even the Prince of Wales himself could command her hand. Her heart had been surrendered with her father's consent—nay, his direct order; it was not hers to reclaim. Such a benevolent father must eventually soften; and if George kept his promise—whether today, in twenty years, or in another world—she knew she would never break hers.

Hetty's face beamed with delight when, upon my departure, she observed Theo's countenance settling into a sweet tranquility. "All the physician's medicine has not wrought such improvement," the devoted sister remarked. After their act of disobedience, the girls departed home. I relinquished the position I had briefly occupied beside my dear invalid. Hetty skipped back into her seat, and Charley mounted to the box. He later confessed that the sermon he attended was exceedingly dull and obtuse; the little chap was too orthodox to appreciate dissenting preachers. Hetty was not the sole family member to notice her sister's transformed demeanor and elevated spirits. I was informed that upon their return, their mother embraced both, especially the invalid, with uncommon fervor.

"There is nothing comparable to a country ride," Aunt Lambert declared. "It has done her dear Theo wonders. She rode to Hampstead, did she? She must ride again tomorrow. Blessed be Heaven, Lord Wrotham's horses are at our disposal three or four times weekly, and the sweet child may enjoy their company!"

As for the notion that Mr. Warrington might have encountered the children during their drive, Aunt Lambert never once entertained it—or at least, never voiced such a suspicion. I leave it to the curious to conjecture whether Mrs. Lambert could possibly have imagined her daughter and her sweetheart reuniting. Do women assist one another in matters of romantic entanglement? Do they scheme, intrigue, devise small plans, spin little falsehoods, create amorous opportunities, dangle the rope-ladder, coax, wheedle, mystify the guardians or Abigails, and divert their attention while Strephon and Chloe whisper in the twilight or dash off to Gretna Green in a postchaise? My dear young friends, some individuals are thus constituted; and some kind souls, having loved tenderly and truly in their own time, remain forever disposed with kindness and tenderness toward their young successors as they commence similar games. Miss Prim is not among them. Should she learn of two youths attached to one another, she will sneer at them as fools or conjure up every conceivable evil. Possessing a hunchback herself, she seeks to bite everyone else's. I believe if she witnessed a pair of turtles cooing in a wood, she would cast her gaze downward or hurl a stone to frighten them; yet I speak, you see, young ladies, of your grandmother, Aunt Lambert, who was a veritable syllabub of human kindness. Moreover, regarding the affair currently under discussion, how am I to determine whether she possessed knowledge of it or not? Thus, all she tells Theo upon her return is, "My child, the country air has restored you completely, and I hope you will take another drive tomorrow, and another, and another, ad infinitum."

"Do you not think, Papa, that the ride has performed miracles for the child, and ought she not be compelled to breathe the fresh air?" Aunt Lambert inquired of the General upon his arrival for supper.

"Yes, certainly. If a coach-and-six benefits little Theo, she shall have it," Lambert replied. "Or he will drag the landau up Hampstead Hill himself if no horses are available;" and so the good man would willingly spend his guineas, his breath, or his blood to grant his child pleasure. He was charmed by his daughter's altered complexion; she ate a piece of chicken with relish; she drank a small measure of negus, which he prepared for her. Indeed, it seemed superior to the physician's finest remedies, which, God knows, had yielded little benefit thus far. Mama was gracious and happy. Hetty was radiant and laughing. It resembled an evening at home at Oakhurst. Never for months past, never since that fatal, cruel day—the one no one dared mention—had they spent such a delightful night.

However, if the other women chose to coax and cajole the good, simple father, Theo herself was too honest to sustain that sweet and fond delusion for long. When, for the third or fourth time, he returned to the delightful topic of his daughter's improved health and asked, "What has effected this? Is it the country air? Is it the Jesuit's bark? Is it the new medicine?"

"Cannot you divine, dear, what it is?" she said, placing a hand upon her father's with a tremor in her voice, though her eyes remained wide and bright.

"And what is it, my child?" asked the General.

"It is because I have seen him again, Papa!" she exclaimed.

The other two women turned pale, and Theo's own heart began to palpitate, her cheeks flushing white as she continued to gaze into her father's terrified visage. "It was not wrong to see him," she continued more quickly; "it would have been wrong not to inform you."

"Great God!" groaned the father, recoiling his hand, his countenance etched with such profound grief that Hetty rushed to her nearly swooning sister, clutching her to her bosom and crying out rapidly, "Theo knew nothing of it, sir! It was my doing—it was entirely my doing!"

Theo lay upon her sister's neck, kissing it twenty, fifty times.

"Women, women! Are you playing with my honor?" the father shrieked, bursting forth with a fierce exclamation. Aunt Lambert sobbed wildly, "Martin! Martin! Say not a word to her!" Again Hetty cried out, "Martin!" and staggered backward toward the wall, for Theo had fainted upon her shoulder.

I was taking my breakfast the following morning with whatever appetite I might muster when my door opened and my faithful black servant announced, "General Lambert." Instantly, by the General's expression, I perceived that the events of the previous day were known to him.

"Your accomplices did not confess," the General stated as soon as my servant had departed, "but sided with you against their father—a testament to how desirable clandestine meetings are. It was from Theo herself I learned that she had seen you."

"Accomplices, sir!" I replied (perhaps not unwilling to shift the focus from the core issue). "You know how fondly and dutifully your young people regard their father. If they oppose you in this instance, it must be because justice lies against you. A man of your stature cannot establish *sic volo, sic jubeo* as the sole law within his household!"

"Pshaw, George!" cried the General. "Though we are parted, God forbid I should wish for our love to cease. I received your promise that you would not seek to see her."

"Nor did I go to her, sir," I said, turning red, undoubtedly; for while this was truth, I admitted it was also a lie.

"You mean she was brought to you?" asked Theo's father, greatly agitated. "Will you hide behind Hester's petticoat? What a splendid defense for a gentleman!"
