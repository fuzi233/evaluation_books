Few words are needed from me to bring to an end what Ezra Jennings has already set down.

On the morning of the twenty-sixth I woke, as a man wakes from chloroform—alive, sane, and utterly dispossessed of memory. From the first moment when opium took me by the hand, to the instant I opened my eyes in Rachel’s sitting-room, all was blank.

What followed my waking I will not relate in detail. The result is enough. Rachel and I understood each other—before either of us had spoken one word of explanation. I decline to account for the swiftness of that reconciliation; Rachel declines also. Those who have ever had their hearts taken by storm will know what passed between us when Ezra Jennings closed the door and left us alone.

We should, however, have been discovered—certainly discovered—if Rachel’s quick sense had not been quicker than Mrs. Merridew’s footsteps. Rachel heard the soft rustle of the old lady’s gown along the corridor: the particular sound of stiff silk that whispers, even when it means well. In a moment Rachel was out and in her way.

“What is the matter?” I heard Mrs. Merridew ask.

“The explosion!” Rachel answered, with a steadiness that might have carried off any domestic emergency from a scorched pudding to a broken heart. She took Mrs. Merridew by the arm and led her down into the garden “out of the way of the impending shock,” as if Ezra Jennings’s scientific contrivance had been a cannon to be fired off.

When they returned, Mrs. Merridew met me in the hall and spoke with the benign superiority of a lady who has been at school when “science” was a new word.

“Explosions, Mr. Blake, are infinitely milder than they were. I assure you I barely heard Mr. Jennings’s explosion from the garden. And no smell afterwards, that I can detect, now we have come back to the house! I must really apologise to your medical friend. It is only due to him to say that he has managed it beautifully!”

So—after vanquishing Betteredge and Mr. Bruff, Ezra Jennings vanquished Mrs. Merridew too. There is more liberal feeling in the world than we give it credit for; and it is sometimes won by nothing grander than the absence of an after-smell.

At breakfast Mr. Bruff made no secret of his reason for wanting me in London by the morning train. He was watching the bank; and the possible issue of that watch laid hold of Rachel’s curiosity with a clasp that no prudence could loosen. If Mrs. Merridew had no objection, Rachel would go with us—she said—so as to be within reach of the first news.

Mrs. Merridew proved all pliancy and indulgence, after the considerate manner in which the “explosion” had conducted itself; and Betteredge was told that we were all four to travel back together. I expected him to ask to come with us; but Rachel had wisely given him work that would keep his mind at home. He was charged with completing the refurnishing of the house; and the comfortable burdens of domestic arrangement—like the steady simmer of a broth that must not be left—kept him from that “detective-fever” which might otherwise have seized him.

Our one regret in returning was the necessity of parting, so abruptly, from Ezra Jennings. We could not persuade him to accompany us. I promised to write; Rachel insisted that he must come to her when she returned to Yorkshire. There seemed every chance of meeting again within a few months—and yet there was something unreasonably sad in seeing him left standing alone upon the platform while the train moved out: a man as quietly indispensable as salt, and as little thanked.

On our arrival in London Mr. Bruff was accosted at the terminus by a small boy in threadbare black—jacket and trousers that had known too many errands and too few holidays. His eyes were his wonder: they thrust forward and rolled about so loosely that you looked at them with the uneasy thought that they might drop out, like marbles from a torn pocket.

After listening, Mr. Bruff asked the ladies if they would excuse our accompanying them back to Portland Place. I had barely time to tell Rachel I would return and report all that happened, when Mr. Bruff seized my arm and hurried me into a cab. The boy with the ill-secured eyes mounted the box beside the driver, and we were ordered to Lombard Street.

“News from the bank?” I asked.

“News of Mr. Luker,” said Mr. Bruff. “An hour ago he was seen leaving his house at Lambeth in a cab with two men—recognised by my men as plain-clothes police. If his dread of the Indians is at the bottom of it, the inference is plain enough. He is going to take the Diamond out of the bank.”

“And we go to see what comes of it?”

“Yes—or to hear what has come of it, if it is over already. Did you notice my boy on the box?”

“I noticed his eyes.”

Mr. Bruff laughed. “They call the poor little wretch ‘Gooseberry’ at the office. I employ him on errands; and I only wish my clerks who nicknamed him were as thoroughly to be depended on as he is. Gooseberry is one of the sharpest boys in London, in spite of his eyes.”

It was twenty minutes to five when we drew up before the bank. Gooseberry looked longingly at his master as Mr. Bruff opened the cab door.

“Do you want to come in too?” Mr. Bruff asked kindly. “Come in then—and keep at my heels till further orders. He’s quick as lightning,” he added to me in a whisper. “Two words will do with Gooseberry where twenty would be wanted with another boy.”

We entered. The outer office—long counter, cashiers behind it—was packed with people, all jostling time itself to draw money out or pay money in before the bank closed at five. The murmur was like a hive; the scraping of boots and the clicking of coin made a hard, restless music that set one’s teeth on edge. I have known quieter seas.

Two men in the crowd approached Mr. Bruff as soon as he showed himself.

“Well?” asked the lawyer. “Have you seen him?”

“He passed us here half an hour since, sir, and went into the inner office.”

“Has he not come out again?”

“No, sir.”

Mr. Bruff turned to me. “Let us wait,” he said.

I looked for the three Indians. Not a sign. The only person present with a noticeably dark complexion was a tall man in a pilot-coat and round hat who looked like a sailor. Could that be one of them in disguise? No. He was taller than any of the Indians; and the face that showed beneath his bushy black beard was twice the breadth of theirs.

“They must have their spy somewhere,” said Mr. Bruff, and his eye fell, as mine had, on the dark sailor. “And he may be the man.”

Before he could add another word his coat-tail was respectfully pulled. Mr. Bruff looked down, and then where Gooseberry was looking.

“Hush!” he said. “Here is Mr. Luker!”

The money-lender emerged from the inner office, with his two plain-clothes policemen behind him. He began working his way towards the door—now in the thick of the crowd, now in a thinner lane between shoulders. Mr. Bruff leaned close to me.

“Keep your eye on him. If he passes the Diamond to anybody, he will pass it here.”

Without noticing us, Mr. Luker moved on. As he passed a short, stout man in a sober grey suit, I saw Luker’s hand stir—quick as a swallow’s dip. The grey-suited man started, then looked after him. Luker went on to the door; his two guards set themselves one at either side; one of Mr. Bruff’s men followed; and they were gone.

I looked at Mr. Bruff and then, without speaking, looked towards the man in grey.

“Yes,” whispered Mr. Bruff. “I saw it too.” He turned sharply in search of his second man. The second man was nowhere. He looked round for Gooseberry. Gooseberry had vanished.

“What the devil does it mean?” Mr. Bruff said, and for him the words were violence. “They have both left us at the very time when we want them most.”

The man in grey came to the counter, paid in a cheque, received his receipt, and turned to go.

“What is to be done?” asked Mr. Bruff. “We can’t degrade ourselves by following him.”

“I can,” I said. “I wouldn’t lose sight of that man for ten thousand pounds.”

“In that case,” rejoined Mr. Bruff, “I wouldn’t lose sight of you for twice the money.” And, muttering to himself, “A nice occupation for a man in my position—For Heaven’s sake don’t mention it. I should be ruined if it was known,” he followed me out.

The man in grey got into an omnibus going west. We got in after him. There were reserves of youth left in Mr. Bruff; I declare it. When he took his seat he blushed.

The man in grey stopped the omnibus in Oxford Street and got out. We followed. He went into a chemist’s shop.

“My chemist!” Mr. Bruff exclaimed, stopping short as if the pavement had betrayed him. “I am afraid we have made a mistake.”

We entered. Mr. Bruff exchanged a few words in private with the proprietor. He returned to me crestfallen.

“It’s greatly to our credit,” he said, taking my arm to lead me out—“that’s one comfort!”

“What is to our credit?”

“Mr. Blake, you and I are the two worst amateur detectives that ever tried their hands at the trade. The man in the grey suit has been thirty years in the chemist’s service. He was sent to the bank to pay money to his master’s account—and he knows no more of the Moonstone than the babe unborn.”

What was to be done next?

“Come back to my office,” said Mr. Bruff. “Gooseberry and my second man have evidently followed somebody else. Let us hope they had their eyes about them at any rate!”

At Gray’s Inn Square the second man had been waiting for us more than a quarter of an hour.

“Well?” asked Mr. Bruff. “What’s your news?”

“I am sorry to say, sir,” the man answered, “that I have made a mistake. I could have taken my oath I saw Mr. Luker pass something to an elderly gentleman in a light-coloured paletot. The elderly gentleman turns out, sir, to be a most respectable master iron-monger in Eastcheap.”

“Where is Gooseberry?” asked Mr. Bruff, as one who expects disappointment.

The man stared. “I don’t know, sir. I have seen nothing of him since I left the bank.”

Mr. Bruff dismissed him.

“One of two things,” he said to me. “Either Gooseberry has run away—or he is hunting on his own account. What do you say to dining here, on the chance that the boy may come back in an hour or two? I’ve got some good wine in the cellar, and we can get a chop from the coffee-house.”

We dined at his chambers. Before the cloth was removed a “person” was announced as wanting to speak to the lawyer. Gooseberry? No—only the man who had been employed to follow Mr. Luker after he left the bank.

His report held no feature of interest. Mr. Luker had returned to his own house and dismissed his guard. He had not gone out again. Towards dusk the shutters went up and the doors were bolted. The street before the house and the alley behind it had been watched. No Indians were seen; no loiterer; no shadow worth noticing. The man waited for further orders and was dismissed for the night.

“Do you think Mr. Luker has taken the Moonstone home with him?” I asked.

“Not he,” said Mr. Bruff. “He would never have dismissed his two policemen if he had run the risk of keeping the Diamond in his own house again.”

We waited another half-hour for Gooseberry. He did not come. It was then time for Mr. Bruff to go to Hampstead and for me to return to Rachel in Portland Place. I left my card with the porter at the chambers and wrote on it that I should be at my lodgings at half-past ten that night; the card was to be given to the boy if he came.

Some men have a knack of keeping appointments; others have a knack of missing them. I am one of the others. Add to that: I spent the evening at Portland Place on the same seat with Rachel, in a room forty feet long, and Mrs. Merridew at the farther end of it—like a sentry posted to guard propriety by sheer endurance. Does anybody wonder I got home at half-past twelve instead of half-past ten? Only a heartless person; and I sincerely hope never to make that person’s acquaintance.

My servant handed me a morsel of paper as he let me in. In neat legal handwriting I read:

“If you please, sir, I am getting sleepy. I will come back tomorrow morning, between nine and ten.”

The boy with the extraordinary eyes had called, produced my card and message, waited an hour, done nothing but fall asleep and wake up again, written that line, and gone—after gravely informing my servant that he was “fit for nothing unless he got his night’s rest.”

At nine the next morning I was ready. At half-past nine I heard steps outside my door.

“Come in, Gooseberry!” I called.

“Thank you, sir,” answered a grave, melancholy voice.

The door opened. I started to my feet and confronted—Sergeant Cuff.

“I thought I would look in here, Mr. Blake,” he said, “on the chance of your being in town, before I wrote to Yorkshire.”

He was as lean and dreary as ever; and his eyes still had that old trick Betteredge had so subtly noticed—looking as if they expected from you something you did not know you possessed. But dress can play its own sort of masquerade; and Sergeant Cuff was altered beyond recognition. He wore a broad-brimmed white hat, a light shooting-jacket, white trousers, drab gaiters; he carried a stout oak stick. He looked as if he had lived in the country all his life. When I complimented him on his metamorphosis he refused the jest. Quite gravely, he complained of the noises and the smells of London—noises that bite into the ear, smells that lie like soot on the tongue. I declare I am not sure he did not even put a faint rustic twist into his speech. I offered him breakfast; the innocent countryman was shocked. His breakfast hour was half-past six; he went to bed with the cocks and hens.

“I only got back from Ireland last night,” he said, coming round to business in his own impenetrable manner. “Before I went to bed I read your letter, telling me what has happened since my inquiry after the Diamond was suspended last year. There’s only one thing to be said about it on my side. I completely mistook my case. How any man living was to have seen things in their true light in such a situation as mine was at the time, I don’t profess to know. But that doesn’t alter facts. I own I made a mess of it. Not the first mess, Mr. Blake, that has distinguished my professional career. It’s only in books that detective officers are superior to the weakness of making a mistake.”

“You have come in the nick of time to recover your reputation,” I said.

“I beg your pardon, Mr. Blake. Now I have retired from business, I don’t care a straw about my reputation. I have done with my reputation, thank God! I am here, sir, in grateful remembrance of the late Lady Verinder’s liberality to me. I will go back to my old work—if you want me, and if you will trust me—on that consideration, and on no other. Not a farthing is to pass from you to me. This is on honour. Now tell me how the case stands since you wrote last.”

I told him of the opium experiment and of what followed at the bank in Lombard Street. The experiment struck him greatly—new ground even to him. And he listened with particular attention to Ezra Jennings’s theory of what I had done with the Diamond after leaving Rachel’s sitting-room on the birthday night.

“I don’t hold with Mr. Jennings that you hid the Moonstone,” Cuff said. “But I agree with him that you certainly took it back to your own room.”

“Well?” I asked. “And what happened then?”

“Have you no suspicion yourself of what happened, sir?”

“None.”

“Has Mr. Bruff no suspicion?”

“No more than I have.”

Sergeant Cuff rose and went to my writing-table. He returned with a sealed envelope. It was marked “Private,” addressed to me, and had the Sergeant’s signature in the corner.

“I suspected the wrong person last year,” he said, “and I may be suspecting the wrong person now. Wait to open the envelope till you have got at the truth. And then compare the name of the guilty person with the name I have written in that sealed letter.”

I put it in my pocket and asked his opinion of what we had done at the bank.

“Very well intended, sir, and quite the right thing. But there was another person who ought to have been looked after besides Mr. Luker.”

“The person named in the letter?”

“Yes. It can’t be helped now. I shall have something to propose to you and Mr. Bruff when the time comes. Let’s wait first and see if the boy has anything worth hearing.”

It was close on ten and Gooseberry had not appeared. Sergeant Cuff talked of other matters—asked after Betteredge and his old enemy the gardener. Another minute and he would have wandered, by the natural turn of his mind, to the subject of roses, when my servant interrupted to say the boy was below.

Gooseberry, brought in, stopped on the threshold and looked distrustfully at the stranger with me. I told him to come nearer.

“You may speak before this gentleman,” I said. “He is here to assist me and knows all that has happened. Sergeant Cuff—this is the boy from Mr. Bruff’s office.”

In our modern civilisation, celebrity is a lever that will move anything. The fame of the great Cuff had reached even Gooseberry. The boy’s projecting eyes rolled at the name until I thought, in sober earnest, that they must drop upon the carpet.

“Come here, my lad,” said the Sergeant. “Let’s hear what you’ve got to tell us.”

The notice of the great man—the hero of many a story told in whispers behind many a lawyer’s desk—fascinated the boy. He placed himself before Cuff, hands behind him, like a pupil saying his catechism.

“What is your name?” asked the Sergeant, beginning at the beginning.

“Octavius Guy,” said the boy. “They call me Gooseberry at the office because of my eyes.”

“Octavius Guy, otherwise Gooseberry,” pursued the Sergeant with the utmost gravity, “you were missed at the bank yesterday. What were you about?”

“If you please, sir, I was following a man.”

“Who was he?”

“A tall man, sir, with a big black beard, dressed like a sailor.”

“I remember the man,” I broke in. “Mr. Bruff and I thought he was a spy employed by the Indians.”

Sergeant Cuff showed no respect whatever for what Mr. Bruff and I had thought. He went on.

“Well? And why did you follow the sailor?”

“If you please, sir, Mr. Bruff wanted to know whether Mr. Luker passed anything to anybody on his way out of the bank. I saw Mr. Luker pass something to the sailor with the black beard.”

“Why didn’t you tell Mr. Bruff what you saw?”

“I hadn’t time, sir. The sailor went out in such a hurry.”

“And you ran out after him—eh?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Gooseberry,” said the Sergeant, patting his head, “you’ve got something in that small skull of yours—and it isn’t cotton-wool. I’m pleased with you so far.”

The boy blushed. Cuff continued.

“Well? What did the sailor do when he got into the street?”

“He called a cab, sir.”

“And what did you do?”

“Held on behind, and run after it.”

Before the Sergeant could put his next question, another visitor was announced—the head clerk from Mr. Bruff’s office. Not wishing to interrupt the examination, I received the clerk in another room.

He brought bad news. The agitation of the last two days had proved too much for Mr. Bruff. He had awoken with an attack of gout; he was confined to his room at Hampstead; and in the critical state of our affairs he was uneasy at leaving me without an experienced adviser. The chief clerk had been ordered to hold himself at my disposal.

I wrote at once to quiet Mr. Bruff’s mind—telling him of Sergeant Cuff’s visit, adding that Gooseberry was at that moment under examination, and promising to report whatever occurred later. The clerk took my note away; I returned to the room I had left.

Sergeant Cuff stood at the fireplace ringing the bell.

“I beg your pardon, Mr. Blake,” he said. “I was just going to send word by your servant that I wanted to speak to you. There isn’t a doubt in my mind that this boy—this most meritorious boy,” he added, laying his hand again on Gooseberry’s head, “has followed the right man. Precious time has been lost, sir, through your unfortunately not being at home at half past ten last night. The only thing to do now is to send for a cab immediately.”

Within five minutes Sergeant Cuff and I were on our way eastward towards the City, with Gooseberry on the box to guide the driver.

“One of these days,” said the Sergeant, pointing through the front window, “that boy will do great things in my late profession. He is the brightest, cleverest little chap I have met with for many a long year past. You shall hear the substance, Mr. Blake, of what he told me while you were out. You were present, I think, when he mentioned that he held on behind the cab and ran after it?”

“Yes.”
