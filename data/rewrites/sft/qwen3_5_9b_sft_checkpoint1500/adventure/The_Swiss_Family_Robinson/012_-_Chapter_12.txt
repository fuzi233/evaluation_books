The greatest peril we had faced thus far had finally passed, yet a shadow of dread lingered in my heart. Could another serpent have slipped unseen into the swamp, or perhaps emerged from the lands beyond Falconhurst, just as the first had done? To ensure our safety, I planned two vital expeditions: first, a thorough inspection of the marsh and thicket, and second, an urgent journey to the Gap—the only route by which such a deadly enemy could ever breach our sanctuary.

Summoning my sons, I found Ernest reluctant, while Jack trembled visibly, swearing he felt the cold shivers whenever he recalled the crushing force of the snake's tail. Though they hesitated, I urged them forward. Together, we constructed a makeshift bridge using planks and wicker hurdles laid across the treacherous marsh, shifting them as we advanced. Our search yielded little but flattened reeds and evidence of the beast's lair, confirming the area was clear of active threats.

Beyond the tangled thicket, the terrain hardened into solid ground near a sheer cliff wall. There, a crystal-clear brook bubbled forth from a massive opening, revealing a cavern of immense proportions. Inside, the vaulted ceiling dripped with stalactites that formed majestic pillars, seemingly propping up the roof above. The floor was blanketed in a fine, snow-white powder with a smooth, soapy texture—a deposit of fuller's earth, perfect for laundry. "A delightful discovery!" I declared. "This will spare me the labor of boiling soap."

Fritz, eager to explore the source of the stream, ventured deeper into the widening grotto. He called for me to follow, leaving the younger boys behind. I discharged a pistol shot, the echoing reverberations bouncing off the vast walls, proving the chamber's staggering size. Lighting my candle, which burned steadily despite the gloom, we pressed on. Suddenly, Fritz gasped, "I believe this is a second salt mine! Look how the walls gleam!"

"The reflections suggest crystal," I corrected gently. "But the water flowing over them leaves no track and tastes sweet. I suspect this is a cave of rock crystal." "Splendid!" Fritz exclaimed. "A treasure beyond measure!" "Unless we can utilize it," I countered, noting our precarious situation, "it is as worthless as the gold lump found by old Robinson Crusoe." Still, Fritz insisted on breaking off a specimen. "Carefully," I warned, "or you'll ruin its natural pyramidal form." After a careful strike, he secured a large lump containing perfect crystals, and we departed, Fritz firing a final salute to echo through the silent depths.

Upon exiting, we found Jack weeping bitterly. "I thought you were lost!" he sobbed, clutching me. "I heard the rocks shatter and feared you were crushed!" I soothed him, explaining the echoes, and learned that Ernest had stayed behind. Indeed, there he was, busy weaving a funnel-shaped basket designed to trap fish.

"A serpent!" I cried, rushing toward what he had shot. "A large eel?" It lay coiled amidst the rushes, nearly four feet long and as thick as my arm. "Excellent," I said, relieved by his courage. "Tenacious creatures like eels require a blow to the neck and a sharp slap against a stone to die properly."

Returning via the firmer cliffs, we found my wife washing clothes at the fountain. She was overjoyed at our safe return and thrilled by the Fuller's Earth, noting our soap reserves were dwindling. The eel was prepared for supper, and that evening we recounted our discoveries, including the magnificent crystal cavern.

Before our extended journey, I deemed it crucial to verify if any snakes still haunted the woods between the cliffs and the sea. Preparations for the larger expedition to the Gap began immediately. We packed the cart with tents, provisions, firearms, tools, and torches, drawing it by our sturdy horses, Storm and Grumble. Jack and Franz guided the team, while my wife sat comfortably inside. Fritz led the way, walking alongside me, with Ernest close by. Our flanks were guarded by the dogs and Fangs, the tame jackal.

Heading toward Woodlands, we traced numerous signs of the serpent's recent passage. At Falconhurst, the poultry and livestock greeted us warmly. By nightfall, we reached Woodlands, a peaceful and thriving settlement untouched by the boa or the mischievous apes. The following day, we surveyed the neighborhood, gathering cotton for new pillows. Franz, entrusted with a small gun for the first time, accompanied me along the lake's edge. The dogs flushed ducks and herons, but Franz missed every chance. Impatient, he suggested shooting the black swans, but I intervened.

Suddenly, a harsh, booming sound erupted. Franz guessed it was Swift, the young onager, but I identified it as a bittern, a rare bird known for its unique, roaring cry. Franz begged permission to shoot it, fascinated by the mechanics of such a powerful vocalization. Just then, the thicket rustled violently. Franz fired, shouting, "I've hit him!" "What?" I demanded. "A wild pig!" he replied. However, upon approaching, I found it was not a hog, but a capybara—a South American rodent with webbed feet and blunt snouts. Franz was ecstatic, believing he had slain a "new creature."

Back at Woodlands, we discovered Ernest surrounded by a dozen dead rats. He explained how Knips had triggered a hidden mound, releasing a swarm of burrowing "beaver rats" from their clay-lined tunnels. The commotion had driven the rodents into a frenzy, attacking anyone nearby until Juno the dog arrived to dispatch them swiftly. I examined the site, identifying it as a colony of muskrats, valuable for their fur and the musk glands they possess.

Later that evening, we dined on Franz's capybara, which he found oddly flavored due to the musk. I educated the boys on the various sources of musk—from the deer to the Indian muskrat—and then described the civet cat, explaining how its aromatic secretion was harvested from a specialized pouch using a clever, humane trapping method involving a narrow cage and a spoon.

Before bedtime, Jack retrieved a game-bag containing pale green apples, unknown fruits he had dared not eat without Knips' approval. Cutting one open, I revealed a circle of seeds rather than a single stone, suggesting it was likely a cinnamon apple. Knips, seizing an opportunity, snatched a slice and ate it with gusto, prompting a chaotic scramble among the boys. Exhausted, we retired to our mattresses.

The next morning, we traveled to a former arboreal shelter between the sugar-brake and the Gap, spreading a sailcloth overhead for protection. Our mission: to scour the area for the boa constrictor. While Fritz, Jack, and Franz searched the cane brakes, I refreshed myself with fresh sugarcane juice. Suddenly, barking dogs alerted us to a disturbance. Emerging from the thicket was a herd of peccaries—grey, tusked creatures resembling wild pigs. I dispatched two with my double-barrel, and the survivors fled in a disciplined line. Realizing the need to remove their odoriferous glands immediately to prevent the meat from spoiling, I instructed the boys to assist.

While Jack fetched the cart, I cleaned the carcasses. Meanwhile, news arrived that Ernest and his mother had secured three additional peccaries near the hut. We loaded the cart, decorated it with flowers, and marched triumphantly back to camp, where my wife awaited. Over dinner, we processed the meat, singeing off the bristles and salting the hams. The boys then attempted to cook a peccary in an Otaheitean style, digging a pit, heating stones, and burying the stuffed animal under hot embers. The result was a surprisingly delicious roast, seasoned naturally by the wrapping leaves.

Simultaneously, we cured a large supply of ham and bacon in a newly built smoking shed. During this time, we explored the surroundings, discovering giant bamboo stalks ideal for casks and noting the absence of the serpent. However, a visit to the farm at Prospect Hill revealed terrible damage wrought by the vagrant apes, scattering animals and ruining the cottage. Reluctantly, we left the mess to be repaired later, barricading the hut where our bacon was stored before resuming our march toward the Gap.

Having cleared a path through the cane thicket, we reached the defile, expecting fierce defenses. Instead, the barricades lay broken, evidence that the great snake and the peccaries had recently breached this barrier. Undeterred, we resolved to fortify the position anew. Beyond the rocky pass, the landscape shifted dramatically. Crossing the East River, we found ourselves entering an arid, parched desert, a stark contrast to the lush valley below. The boys groaned at the "Arabian Petraea" and "Sahara-like" desolation, but I encouraged them, pointing to the distant mountains promising water and refuge.

Resting briefly under an overhanging rock, we continued. Hunger spurred us onward when Knips, sniffing suspiciously, raced ahead toward a glen. Following him, we found a pool where he and the dogs had quenched their thirst. Suddenly, Fritz pointed to the horizon. "Horsemen!" he cried. Through the spyglass, Jack and Ernest saw riders, but I, peering closer, discerned them as large ostriches. Determined to capture one for its feathers, I stationed myself with Ernest behind some shrubs while the boys recalled the dogs. The ostriches approached, varying their pace playfully.
