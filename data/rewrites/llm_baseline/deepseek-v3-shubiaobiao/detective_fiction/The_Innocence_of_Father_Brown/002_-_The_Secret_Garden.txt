Aristide Valentin, Chief of the Paris Police, was running late. His guests had already begun to arrive, greeted by his ever-vigilant servant, Ivan—a grizzled old man with a scar and a table in the weapon-lined entrance hall. Valentin’s home was as enigmatic as the man himself: an old estate with high walls and towering poplars overlooking the Seine. Its design was peculiar, almost paranoid—doors led from the house into the garden, but the garden had no exit to the outside world. The walls were smooth, unscalable, crowned with spikes. A fitting retreat for a man who had made countless enemies.

When Valentin finally arrived, he moved swiftly through his duties—locking away official documents, pausing only briefly at the open garden door. The storm had passed, leaving a sharp moon to pierce the remnants of clouds. For a moment, the rationalist in him wavered, as if sensing the approach of something fateful. Then, remembering his guests, he turned away.

The drawing-room was alive with conversation. There was Lord Galloway, the English Ambassador, ruddy-faced and irritable; his wife, Lady Galloway, slender and silver-haired; their daughter, Margaret, pale and copper-haired, with an air of quiet defiance. The Duchess of Mont St. Michel and her daughters exuded opulence, while Dr. Simon, the scientist, adjusted his glasses with an air of perpetual skepticism. Then there was Father Brown, the unassuming priest from Essex, and—most intriguing to Valentin—Commandant Neil O’Brien of the French Foreign Legion. The Irishman’s uniform was dashing, his demeanor a mix of melancholy and bravado. The Galloways greeted him stiffly; Lady Margaret pointedly looked away.

But Valentin’s attention was reserved for one man: Julius K. Brayne, the American millionaire whose eccentric philanthropy had made headlines. Brayne was a mountain of a man, white-haired, red-faced, with an almost cherubic intensity. He believed in every religion and none, funding anything he deemed "progressive"—including, it seemed, Valentin himself.

Dinner passed without incident—until the cigars. The younger men dispersed, leaving Galloway simmering over his coffee. His suspicions festered. Where was O’Brien? Where was Margaret?

He found his answer in the moonlit garden.

There, sprawled in the grass, was a corpse—a massive figure in black evening wear, head nearly severed. Blood darkened the white of his shirt. Galloway’s shout brought the others running.

Dr. Simon confirmed the obvious: the man was dead, his neck cleanly cut. "Not with a knife," the doctor murmured. "Something heavier. A sabre, perhaps."

Valentin’s mind raced. A stranger, decapitated in his garden. No weapon in sight. No gates in the walls.

Then Father Brown spoke, meek but pointed: *"There are no gates to this garden."*

The implications settled like a chill. If the killer hadn’t left—then they were still here.

Suspicion fell quickly. O’Brien’s sword was missing. Brayne had vanished. And when Ivan returned from the road outside, he carried a bloodied cavalry sabre—discarded, as if by a fleeing man.

But Valentin knew better than to trust appearances. The real mystery had only just begun.
