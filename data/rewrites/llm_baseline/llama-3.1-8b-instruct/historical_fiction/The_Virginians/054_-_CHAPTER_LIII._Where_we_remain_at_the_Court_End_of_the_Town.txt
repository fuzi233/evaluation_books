As George Warrington finished recounting the tale of his adventures to Madame de Bernstein, the old lady's eyelids began to droop, only to snap open once more whenever George paused, her expression a mixture of interest and disinterest. She nodded along, her face a mask of polite attention, as George struggled to find the right words. He stuttered and blushed, his voice faltering, but he pressed on, driven by the faint applause of his audience at home – Hetty's shining eyes, Theo's sympathetic glances, and his mother's warm smile. It was a far cry from the stern scrutiny of Madame de Bernstein, whose approval seemed as elusive as the morning dew.

"I daresay, my dear sir," she said, extending a hand adorned with a delicate lace mitten, "your story was quite captivating. Your mother must have been overjoyed to see you return." Her tone was dry, her words laced with a subtle barb.

George shrugged, his shoulders barely moving, as he bowed low in response. His aunt's gaze locked onto his, her eyes piercing in their intensity. "Overjoyed, perhaps," she continued, "though I suspect she might have been more inclined to rejoice at the return of a prodigal son, rather than one who had merely stumbled upon the world anew."

George's face reddened, and he spoke with a hint of defensiveness. "He came to his family in England, madam, and found solace in the company of my friends."

Madame de Bernstein raised an eyebrow, her expression a mixture of disdain and amusement. "Alas, dear nephew Warrington, I fear he came to worse counsel, and I should have warned my sister sooner, had she seen fit to share such tidings with me. 'Twas a kindness, indeed, that she wrote to me through you, but a kindness that only serves to highlight her neglect of my own correspondences."
