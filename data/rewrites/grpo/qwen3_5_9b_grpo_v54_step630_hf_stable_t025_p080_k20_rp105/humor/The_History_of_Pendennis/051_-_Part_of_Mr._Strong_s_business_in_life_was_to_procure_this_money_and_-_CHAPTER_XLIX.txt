Arthur Pendennis spotted Mr. Bows and immediately switched on his "loud, cheery" social mode, offering a handshake that was as dashing as it was perfunctory. Yet, beneath the surface of this performance, a distinct blush bloomed on Arthur's cheeks, mirrored instantly by a fluttering crimson signal on Fanny's face. After the initial pleasantries—where Arthur ironically confirmed his intention to visit Mr. Costigan's chambers—the atmosphere in the room curdled into a heavy, guilty silence. To break the tension, Pendennis attempted to recount the previous night's escapades, mimicking Costigan's futile arguments with the Vauxhall check-taker. It was a disaster. No one laughed. Mrs. Bolton was utterly confused by the sketch, Fanny wore an expression of terrified alarm, and old Mr. Bows sat as glum as a musician forced to play a difficult concerto on a broken piano. Realizing his comedy had failed, Pendennis's voice sputtered and died, leaving the room shrouded in darkness once more.

"You were coming to see me, sir," Mr. Bows interjected, his voice dripping with practiced melancholy. "Won't you honor my chambers? They are high up, but—"

"Oh! I live in a garret myself," Pendennis interrupted, seizing the chance to deflect. "And Shepherd's Inn is twice as cheerful as Lamb Court."

"I knew you had third-floor apartments," Bows countered, adopting a theatrical pose with a trembling finger and a hat in hand. "I merely meant the air up three flights of stairs is wholesomer for gentlemen than the stagnant air of a porter's lodge."

"Sir!" Pendennis snapped, his candle flame flaring in sudden indignation. "Will you permit me to choose my society without—"

"You were so polite to mention your visit," Bows said, ignoring the interruption with the smooth cadence of a man who has spent a lifetime listening to orators on stage. "Shall I show you the way? Mrs. Bolton, Mr. Pendennis and I are old friends; we crossed paths at the dawn of his life."

With a final, melodramatic bow, Bows ushered Pendennis out, promising to remember the theater tickets and pressing Fanny's hand before vanishing into the London fog.

"What a nice young man, to be sure!" Mrs. Bolton exclaimed, watching them ascend the court.

"D'you think so, ma?" Fanny asked, her eyes fixed on the retreating figures.

Mrs. Bolton, however, was already lost in a different reverie, comparing Pendennis to a young gentleman she'd known years prior—a city sugar-baker with black whiskers who had driven a dancer named Emly Budd in a tilbury. She recounted their private wedding at St. Bartholomew's and Emly's subsequent rise to becoming a patroness of the Manshing-House Ball, contrasting the commoner's fate with the professional glamour of Lady Mirabel, Costigan's daughter. As she finished her tale, folding the dinner cloth with Fanny, the two women came close. Mrs. Bolton lifted Fanny's chin and kissed her. This time, the blush on Fanny's cheek wasn't born of alarm, but of a sweet, confusing pleasure. *What is love?* she wondered, the question tasting both sweet and bitter.

Once Mrs. Bolton departed on her market errand, Fanny retreated to the window overlooking Bows's door, her gaze locked onto that specific patch of pavement. Inside, her sisters Betsy-Jane and Amelia-Ann were pretending to read a grave tract upside down, oblivious to Fanny's turmoil. Finally, a movement caught her eye. Bows had emerged. He passed the door, but Pendennis followed him. They bowed under the archway together, but Bows did not stop to speak. Minutes later, Bows returned alone, entering the porter's room with a gingerbread cake for the girls. He sat by the door, ostensibly guarding the entrance, waiting for a guest who never appeared. When Mrs. Bolton returned from the market, she found Bows there instead of the man she expected.

The meeting in Bows's attic room was far from satisfactory. Pendennis was sulky, unwilling to engage with Costigan, who had moved from his bedroom only moments before, smelling distinctly of otto-of-whisky. Costigan, however, was in high spirits, shaking a wobbly hand that still managed to hold a razor. He regaled Pendennis with tales of Baden-Baden and Dublin Castle, speaking with the grandiose confidence of a man who believes his own legends. For a moment, the old memories of Chatteris and the bridge returned, softening Pendennis's heart. He shook hands warmly with both men, forgetting entirely about Fanny until Bows began hobbling down the stairs, clearly intent on following him out.

This persistence was the final straw. Pendennis's patience snapped. "Confound him," he thought, "what does he mean by dogging me?" He burst out laughing in the Strand, a sound that was less honest than it was self-mocking. He tried to drown his thoughts in the Panorama, where Care followed him to the top; in the Club, writing a sarcastic letter home; and in the library, where even the serious Mr. Jawkins failed to hold his attention. Finally, passing a Comedy Theatre advertising "Roars of Laughter," he entered the pit. There, dressed in a man's suit, was the lovely Mrs. Leary. But the sight produced no effect. He laughed again, a bitter, hollow sound, remembering the spell she had cast upon him just weeks prior. The magic was gone, leaving only the sharp edge of reality.
