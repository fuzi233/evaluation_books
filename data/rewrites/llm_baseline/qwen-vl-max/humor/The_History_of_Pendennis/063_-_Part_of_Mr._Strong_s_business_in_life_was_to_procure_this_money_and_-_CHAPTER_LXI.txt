Our dear, long-suffering Begum was so furious at her husband’s latest act of deceit and idiocy that she flatly refused to help Sir Francis Clavering pay his debts of honour. She declared she’d leave him to face the consequences of his unrelenting folly—and possibly even divorce him. After the disastrous day at the Derby, the poor gambler was in such a state of mental collapse he wanted to vanish from the face of the earth—avoiding both his fellow racers (who had seen him lose everything) and his wife, whose bank account he now feared might be closed forever.

The next morning, when Lady Clavering asked if Sir Francis was home, she was told he hadn’t returned—and had sent a messenger with instructions to pack his clothes and letters and send them to Short Hotel in Blackfriars. Strong knew full well that the Baronet would soon be reaching out for help, and sure enough, a note arrived begging him to visit “Mr. Francis” at the inn. The Baronet, it seemed, had a peculiar talent for lying when confronted with reality, and always chose flight over facing facts.

The hotel’s boots, carrying Clavering’s message to Grosvenor Place and returning with his carpet bag, immediately recognized the owner. He passed the news to the footman, who relayed it to the servants’ hall, then to Mrs. Bonner—the housekeeper and confidante of Lady Clavering—who finally delivered the bombshell to her ladyship. Before long, every servant in the household knew: Sir Francis was hiding under an alias at a dingy inn on the Blackfriars Road. And the coachman? He blabbed it to other coachmen, who told their masters, who whispered it at Tattersall’s, where everyone assumed Sir Francis was about to flee to the Levant—probably to avoid creditors, or maybe just to escape himself.

That same day, a suspicious number of bills piled up on the Baronet’s hall table. The French cook submitted his invoice. So did Messrs. Finer and Gimcrack, the ornamental dealers; Madame Crinoline, the famed milliner; and Miss Amory herself—whose accounts at each establishment were by no means negligible. It was clear: someone was running out of money fast.

In the afternoon, Strong dropped by Grosvenor Place as usual. He found all these documents neatly arranged in Sir Francis’s study and began leafing through them with a look of profound sorrow.

Mrs. Bonner descended upon him mid-examination. As Lady Clavering’s right hand and emotional anchor, she was firmly on her side—and more furious than the lady herself could be.

“She won’t pay,” she announced, arms crossed. “Not if I have anything to say about it. You go back to Sir Francis—this coward hiding in a cheap inn like some common sneak—and tell him we’re done. We made him a gentleman, pulled him out of prison (and others too), paid his debts again and again, gave him a seat in Parliament, houses in town and country—yet he can’t even show his face! We’ve given him his horse, his dinner, his clothes—everything! And we’re done. Our fortune is ours now. We’ll give him enough to live on, and then let him be. That’s what you tell him—from Susan Bonner.”

Lady Clavering, hearing of Strong’s arrival, summoned him upstairs. The Chevalier hoped she might be more reasonable than her loyal factotum. He’d convinced her before—many times—of Clavering’s worthiness, though not without effort. This time, he painted a bleak picture: Sir Francis was distraught, nearly suicidal, trembling at the thought of failing his creditors.

“Kill himself?” Mrs. Bonner snorted. “He’ll die happy—when he’s got one last chance to rob us.”

Strong insisted he’d found razors on the table. But Lady Clavering laughed—a cold, bitter laugh. “Oh, he’ll never harm himself. Not while there’s a shilling left to steal. His life’s safe. Depend on it. Ah, what a terrible day it was when I ever saw him!”

“The first man was better,” said the aide-de-camp. “At least he was wild—but he had courage. This one? What good does it do my lady to pay his bills, sell her diamonds, forgive him? He’ll be just as bad next year. He’ll cheat her again, rob her again, and use her money to feed thieves—though not you, Captain—you’ve been kind enough, despite our regrets.”

Strong noticed the mention of diamonds. A flicker of hope. Maybe Lady Clavering wasn’t entirely beyond mercy.

“Honestly, ma’am,” he said, genuinely moved by her patience, “anything either of you says against Clavering—or Mrs. Bonner against me—is richly deserved. We both know we’ve been lucky to survive this far. He’s treated you cruelly. But you’re the most forgiving woman in England. If you weren’t, he’d be finished. But you can’t let your son grow up with a disgraced father. Tie him down—make him promise anything. I guarantee he’ll sign.”

“And break it,” said Mrs. Bonner.

“And keep it *this* time,” Strong shot back. “He must. You should see how he wept—‘Oh, Strong,’ he said, ‘it’s not for myself—I’m thinking of my boy… and of the best woman in England, whom I’ve wronged.’ He didn’t mean to bet—he was tricked into it. Everyone in the ring was fooled. He thought it was safe. It’ll teach him for life. To see a man cry—well, it breaks your heart.”

“He doesn’t care much about making *you* cry,” Mrs. Bonner muttered. “Poor soul—look at him now, Captain.”

Later, Strong shared the scene with Clavering at Short’s Hotel, where the Baronet sat weeping into a glass of curacoa.

“If you’ve any manhood left, Clavering,” Strong said, “keep your word. Or by Heaven, I’ll turn on you and tell all.”

“What all?” Clavering gasped.

“Psha!” Strong snapped. “Do you think I’m a fool? I’ve lived long enough to know secrets. You know I only need to speak, and you’re penniless tomorrow. And I’m not the only one who knows.”

“Who else?”

“Old Pendennis. Or I’m mistaken. He recognized the man the first night he came drunk into your house.”

“He knows it?” Clavering shrieked. “Damn him—kill him!”

“You’d like to kill us all, wouldn’t you, old boy?” Strong sneered, puffing his cigar.

Clavering slammed his hand against his forehead. “Oh, Strong! If I dared, I’d end it all. I’m the most miserable dog in England. That’s why I’m reckless. That’s why I drink.” He raised the curacoa with a shaking hand. “And hang around with thieves. I know they’re thieves—every last one. But what can I do? I didn’t know—really, I didn’t! Until I saw the scoundrel, I knew nothing. And now I’ll run away—go abroad—bury myself in a forest—hang myself from a tree—oh, I’m the most wretched beggar alive!” He cried, cursed, groaned, and vowed repentance—all in the same breath.

And yet, irony struck: while Clavering sank deeper into despair, another tenant of Strong’s chambers—Colonel Altamont—was riding high. He’d won big at the Derby, thanks to a lucky bet. When Captain Clinker settled Clavering’s debts (on Lady Clavering’s orders), Altamont collected thirty-to-one odds in fifties—his winnings gleaming like gold in the sun.

His friends swarmed in to congratulate him. Tom Driver suggested reviving the sunken treasure ship in the Gulf of Mexico. Mr. Keightley pushed the Tredyddlums shares. Jack Holt offered a tobacco-smuggling scheme. Jack Rackstraw had two horses he *must* buy. Tom Fleet had a satirical paper, *The Swell*, needing £200 to become a thousand-pound-a-year venture. Little Moss Abrams urged caution: “Invest in solid bills—safe as the Bank of England, fifty per cent return!”

But Altamont resisted. He tucked his notes away and went straight to Strong’s chambers, where he “sport”-ed the outer door. Strong had warned him about his friends—and though he took twenty pounds off the Colonel’s winnings (no shame in that), he refused to let anyone take advantage of him.

Altamont wasn’t a bad sort when things were going well. He bought Grady a smart livery, made Costigan weep with gratitude over a five-pound note, gifted Mrs. Bolton a green shawl and Fanny a yellow one—both from the brightest window display in Regent Street. Then, on Miss Amory’s birthday in June, she received a parcel from “a friend”: a massive brass writing-desk with amethysts that looked like evil eyes, a musical snuff-box, two outdated Keepsakes, and two dresses in colours so outrageous they made her laugh uncontrollably.

It turned out Altamont had also bought cigars and silks from some Fleet Street charlatans. And later, Strong found him in Cheapside’s Auction Room, investing in desks, candlesticks, a dinner epergne, and a bagatelle board. The epergne stayed at chambers and became a centerpiece at Altamont’s frequent dinners—until Jack Holt remarked it looked like it had been “taken in a bill.” Which, naturally, it had.

These dinners became a weekly ritual. Sir Francis Clavering, now reduced to living in a half-shut-down house, joined them regularly. His estate was stripped bare—Mirobolant’s successor fired, footmen dismissed, one quitting because “on’y one footman was kep’”—and Lady Clavering enforced strict economy across the entire household. Major Pendennis, Strong, the lawyer, and the Begum herself carried out the reforms with military precision.

After settling Clavering’s debts—publicly scandalous, and further tarnishing his reputation—Lady Clavering fled to Tunbridge Wells in high dudgeon, refusing to see her reprobate husband. Clavering stayed in London, avoiding her wrath, slipping in and out of Parliament, playing billiards with Raff and Marker, haunting sporting pubs, or lurking near Lincoln’s Inn, waiting hours for lawyers who barely acknowledged his existence.

No wonder he loved Strong’s dinners. There, he was the guest of honour—called “Yes, Sir Francis,” “No, Sir Francis.” He cracked jokes, sang his sad little French tune after Strong’s jolly chorus and Costigan’s Irish ballads. In that warm, chaotic circle, he felt human again. At home, his grand house echoed with silence, attended only by a sour-faced housekeeper and a valet who mocked him daily.

“Damn it,” he’d complain to his friends, “that valet—must fire him. But I owe him two years’ wages. Can’t ask my lady. He brings me tea cold, with a leaden teaspoon—says she sent all the plate to the banker’s because it ain’t safe. How dare she not trust me with a single spoon? Unfair, Altamont? You know my lady’s low-born—wait, I beg your pardon—hem—still, it’s cruel. And the servants laugh—those damn rascals! I’ll break every bone in their bodies. They don’t answer my bell. And last night—my man was at Vauxhall, dancing in one of my dress-shirts and velvet waistcoats—confound him! I swear he’ll be hanged. All those infernal valets are scoundrels.”

He grew oddly fond of Altamont now. Listened eagerly to his tales: how he’d slipped aboard a ship at night to escape his wives; how they’d paddled madly after it in canoes; how he’d been lost in the bush for three months in New South Wales; how he’d met Napoleon at Saint Helena and been presented to him as a mate of the Indiaman.

Over wine, Altamont spun wild yarns—some true, many false. But Clavering drank them in, nodding solemnly, clinking glasses.

“Leave him alone,” Altamont chuckled to Strong. “I know what he’s coming to. And I know what I’m telling. I was officer on an Indiaman. I traded to New South Wales. Lost my ship. Became officer to the Nawaub. Only me and my royal master had a falling-out. Who’s hurt? Who knows? The other chap’s dead—shot in the bush, body identified at Sydney. If I thought someone’d betray me, do you think I wouldn’t wring his neck? I’ve done worse—told you about the overseer. Fair fight. He had gun and bayonet. I had an axe. Fifty men saw it—cheered me! I’d do it again. Him, wouldn’t I? I ain’t afraid. I’d kill the man who spills my secret. That’s my rule. Pass me the liquor.”

Strong stood up and removed the brandy bottle.

“Ha, ha!” Altamont laughed. “You’re right, old boy. You always keep your head. When I start pitching, I authorize you—to put away the rum-bottle.”

“Take my advice, Altamont,” Strong said gravely. “Don’t make it too tempting for Clavering to get rid of you. Who knows what he might do?”

And the event Altamont had been waiting for arrived swiftly.

One day, Strong was out on business. Clavering showed up at the chambers alone—finding only the envoy of the Nawaub.

He ranted about the world’s cruelty, his wife’s ingratitude, Strong’s betrayal. “Hundreds of pounds I’ve given Ned Strong—kept him out of jail—been his friend for life—and now he sides with her! They’ve conspired to ruin me! They don’t give me pocket-money—less than Frank has at school!”

“Why don’t you go to Richmond and borrow from him?” Altamont laughed savagely. “He wouldn’t refuse his poor old beggar of a father, would he?”

“I’ve humiliated myself,” Clavering moaned. “Look—look at these pawn-tickets! A Member of Parliament, an English Baronet—forced to pawn a drawing-room clock, a buhl inkstand, a gold duck’s-head paper-holder—worth five pounds, sold for fifteen-and-six! It’s humiliating! Made me cry—tears! And that damned valet—curse him, I wish he was hanged—he threatened to tell my lady! Said the things in my house weren’t mine to sell or throw away! By Gad, the scoundrel!”

“Cry a bit,” Altamont said kindly. “It’ll help. Why, I used to think you were a happy man. Now you’re a miserable son of a gun.”

“It’s a shame,” Clavering whined. “And I haven’t got the money to pay the cab outside. Mrs. Bolton lent me three shillings. I don’t want to ask her again. I asked Costigan—old Irish miscreant—no money. Campion’s out of town.”

“I thought you swore to your wife you wouldn’t sign any more paper,” Altamont said, puffing his cigar.

“Then why doesn’t she give me pocket-money? Damme, I need it!”

“Oh, Am——, oh, Altamont,” he sobbed. “I’m the most miserable beggar alive.”

“You want a loan?” Altamont asked. “Twenty pounds?”

“If you would,” Clavering cried, “I’d be grateful forever—forever, my dearest friend.”

“How much will you give? Fifty-pound bill, six months, half down, half in plate?”

“Yes! Yes! I’ll pay it—on the day! I’ll make it payable at my banker’s! Do anything you like!”

“Well, I was only joking. I’ll give you twenty.”

“You said a pony,” Clavering protested. “My dear fellow, you said a pony. I’ll never forget it. I’ll take it as a loan—pay you back in six months. I swear it.”
