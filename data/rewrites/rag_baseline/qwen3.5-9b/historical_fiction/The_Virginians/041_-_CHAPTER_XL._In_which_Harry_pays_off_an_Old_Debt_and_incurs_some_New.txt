The autumn air carried the scent of damp earth and departing summer, signaling the end of the Tunbridge season. Madame de Bernstein's destination was clear: Bath. It offered cards, company, and the vibrant pulse of life that the countryside lacked. Harry agreed to escort Lady Maria and the chaplain to Castlewood, though the promise tasted bitter on his tongue.

They passed Oakhurst village, the site of Harry's recent hospitality. Lady Maria's commentary on the local young ladies—her observations regarding their attempts to capture Harry's attention and the mother's obvious eagerness to secure a match—irritated him. Mr. Warrington, emboldened by this perceived slight, declared he would bypass their hosts' door. That evening, at the inn, the atmosphere curdled. Lady Maria's smiles failed to thaw Harry's expression; her manufactured tears elicited no sympathy. To her querulous remarks, he offered only grunts. Eventually, she retired to her chamber, sighing heavily. Harry bowed her out with a rigid politeness, consigning her to the care of the landlady and her maid.

Ten minutes after her departure, a horse galloped out of the yard. Later, when Mrs. Betty entered to retrieve smelling salts, she found Parson Sampson smoking a solitary pipe. Mr. Warrington had vanished, ostensibly for a moonlit walk, but his whereabouts were unknown. Sampson confirmed that Mr. Warrington intended to depart the following morning, seated beside Lady Maria's carriage. Yet, the darkness lingered on his brow. During the journey, silence reigned between them. Lady Maria, convinced he knew of her theft, fretted silently.

The truth lay elsewhere. On a steep hill near Oakhurst, three miles from the road, Lady Maria, leaning heavily on Harry's arm, had poured out sentimental vows. She was genuinely in love, unaware that her affection only deepened his coldness. Her face, fresh despite the lines of twenty years, cast shadows in the moonlight. Harry felt the weight of her dependence as an unbearable burden. He cursed the walk, the heat, and the wine that had compelled him to make a fatal pledge.

Her praises became noise. She recited scraps of plays, calling herself his champion, her Henrico, her Molinda, vowing eternal faithfulness. She clung to him, asking if his precious image and hair, kept in a silken case, would accompany her to the grave. Harry realized then that Madame Bernstein had burned the contents of that case—the hair and the note—and Maria still held onto the fiction. It was at that moment, as he suddenly jerked his arm free, that she felt the sharp pang of remorse for her deception.

At Castlewood, Harry's mood did not improve. Lord Esmond was absent, as were the ladies. Only Mr. Will returned from partridge shooting, turning pale at the sight of his cousin and receiving a sulky scowl in return. Despite this, they met at supper. The conversation was civil but strained until Harry mentioned the brown horse.

"Your horse! What horse?" Will asked, confused.

"The brown horse," Harry stated curtly. "For which I played you, and which I won the night before you rode away."

Will feigned ignorance. Harry summoned Parson Sampson to act as umpire. The chaplain confirmed Harry had wagered fifty guineas against the horse. Will admitted he had won the other horse but dismissed the value of the brown steed, laughing at the low sale price of thirty shillings. Harry maintained a studied courtesy, offering chicken to Lady Maria, locking the servants out, and pocketing the key.

Once the door clicked shut, the tension snapped. Will, gripping a knife, demanded to know the meaning of the maneuver. Harry revealed the stakes: fifty guineas against the horse. Will denied the debt, claiming it cost Harry no more effort to lie than to cheat. Harry stepped closer, warning the chaplain to ensure fair play, before launching into an insult Will could not hear.

Will retaliated, hurling a wine bottle. It missed Harry's head, shattered against the wainscot, destroying a portrait of an Esmond ancestor, and splashed port wine over the chaplain's wig. The parson cried out for quiet, but the gentlemen ignored the Church's commands. Harry seized a large silver-handled knife, driving it toward his cousin. He intercepted the thrust, catching Will's wrist, and delivered a rapid blow to the nose. Will reeled backward, dropping his weapon, which Harry's opponent kicked under the table.

Will recovered, charging forward like a battering ram. Harry, recalling the brutal combat styles of Marybone and the plantations, sidestepped. He struck Will's right ear with tremendous force, knocking him unconscious against the heavy oak table.

"Chaplain, bear witness to a fair fight!" Harry shouted, drawing the key to unlock the lobby where servants had heard the commotion. They entered to find two victims: the chaplain drenched in port, and Will Esmond lying in his own blood.

Harry ordered the neckcloth undone and a fleam fetched. Will soon regained consciousness, his eye swollen shut, cursing freely. Harry noted his recovery with grim humor, asking if he desired more drink. Will, enraged, struck the black servant Gumbo, who had approached to offer assistance. Gumbo explained that Harry had nearly cut his tongue. The household servants, who loved Harry, refused to eject the guest, declaring the fight fair and noting that Will was accustomed to such treatment.

Betty mocked Will's departure, saying he had lost his zipper and was going to bed. Will retreated, leaving a trail of curses. The next morning, Harry sent a polite note inviting Will to join him in London or elsewhere, threatening to frighten highwaymen on Hounslow Heath. Will responded with violence, demanding Harry leave Hounslow.

Simultaneously, a letter arrived from Molinda, written in a mix of English and Greek, pleading for peace and forgiveness. Harry read it with weariness, reflecting on how the golden tablets of romance had faded into leaves. His dignity forbade a prolonged stay at Castlewood after blinding a son of the house. He drafted a formal explanation, supervised by Parson Sampson, ensuring the spelling remained impeccable. He informed Maria that remaining would be improper and that his absence was necessary. She wept profusely at the prospect of losing him, imagining him finding no one who loved him as she did.

Harry left, cheerful and singing to his horses, nodding at roadside beauties and chuckling at the landlady. He was not inconsolable; he craved the glitter of London, the crowds of St. James's, and the thrills of Newmarket. By the time he arrived, the forty-four pounds he had carried from Tunbridge were gone. He needed funds. With two sums of five thousand pounds in his and his brother's name, he felt secure. He would borrow, rely on a streak of luck at the gaming tables, and restore his capital. For now, he must maintain the appearance of a gentleman, proving that a man of his rank could not survive on a miserly two hundred a year.
