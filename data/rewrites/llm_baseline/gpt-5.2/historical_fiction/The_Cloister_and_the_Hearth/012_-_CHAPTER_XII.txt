Ghysbrecht Van Swieten carried the key of Gerard’s chamber in the greasy warmth of his pouch, as a man carries a bone meant for a dog he dislikes. He would not go early. “A little hunger does good,” he told himself, and, as if hunger were a spice he could measure with two fingers, he let the morning simmer.

Below, the town had its usual broth of sounds: the thin clack of wooden pattens on damp stone; the cough of a cartwheel; a smith’s far hammer answering another hammer as though the streets had learned a trade-song. From a baker’s door drifted the round, honest scent of rye crust and hot yeast, and it made Ghysbrecht’s malice feel the more unnatural—like vinegar poured into milk. Even so, he waited until ten of the clock.

Then he climbed, one stair at a time, with a loaf under his arm and a pitcher in his hand, and behind him his servant Dierich went close, trusty, well-armed, boots making a dull, disciplined thud against the boards. At the prison door Ghysbrecht paused and listened. No shuffling. No groan. No beggar’s prayer. Only the house’s own small noises—timber settling, a faint draft humming as it worried at a crack.

A grim smile gathered on him. “By this time he will be as down-hearted as Albert Koestein was,” he thought, tasting the thought as one tastes a bitter herb and likes it.

He lifted the latch and pushed.

The cell stood empty.

For a breath he did not understand what his eyes told him. His body stiffened in a queer, arrested way—as if the sinews that moved him had been cut. Then the strength went out of him all at once; he trembled so that the loaf slipped in his grasp. Dierich, seeing his master’s back go rigid and then shake, crept nearer and peered over his shoulder.

There was the rope. There the iron bar drawn out. The blank pallet. The open space where a young man should have been.

Dierich let out a loud cry of wonder. But his surprise doubled when Ghysbrecht, disregarding rope and bar and everything that spoke of escape, flung himself down before the chest that stood in the cell—an old, blunt-nosed coffer—and began to feel it all over with quivering hands, patting and pawing it as if the wood might still be warm with what it had held. He bent his face into it and stared, not at the chamber, not at the means of flight, but into that hollow belly.

Dierich stared at him as at a man suddenly struck by some planet. “Why, master—what is the matter?”

Ghysbrecht’s pale lips worked and made no sound. His hands fell. He kept staring into the chest, as if by staring he could make parchment grow again as dough rises under a cloth.

“Why, master,” said Dierich, bewildered, “what avails glaring into that empty box? The lad is not there. See here—note the cunning of the young rogue; he hath taken out the bar, and—”

“GONE! GONE! GONE!” screamed Ghysbrecht, and the word was not for Gerard alone.

“Gone!” echoed Dierich. “What is gone, Holy saints! he is planet-struck!”

“STOP THIEF!” shrieked Ghysbrecht. He whirled on Dierich and clutched him by the collar, shaking him with such fury that the servant’s teeth clicked. “D’ye stand there, knave, and see your master robbed? Run! fly! A hundred crowns to him that finds it me again. No—no! ’tis in vain. Oh, fool! fool! to leave that in the same room with him. But none ever found the secret spring before. None ever would but he. It was to be. It is to be. Lost! lost!”

The burst burned out as suddenly as it flared. His years and his ailments took him by the shoulders and sat him down. He sank against the chest, muttering like a man repeating a charm that has failed him. “Lost… lost…”

“What is lost, master?” Dierich asked, and in his voice there was a rough kindness, the way a man speaks to a pot that has boiled over—half pity, half impatience.

“House and lands and good name,” groaned Ghysbrecht, and wrung his hands feebly.

“WHAT?” Dierich’s one word rang out with such sharp curiosity that it pricked Ghysbrecht back into craft, like a pin into a bladder.

Ghysbrecht blinked, gathered himself, and let cunning smooth his face. “I have lost the town records,” he stammered; and he looked askant at Dierich like a fox caught too near a hen-roost.

“Oh—is that all?”

“Is’t not enough?” Ghysbrecht seized on the safer grievance at once. “What will the burghers say to me? What will the burghs do?” Then, with a new urgency: “A hundred crowns to him who shall recover them; all, mind, all that were in this box. If one be missing, I give nothing.”

“’Tis a bargain, master,” said Dierich promptly. “The hundred crowns are in my pouch already—if I find the skins. See you not that where Gerard Eliassoen is, there are the pieces of sheepskin you rate so high?”

“That is true; that is true, good Dierich—good faithful Dierich. All, mind—all that were in the chest.”

“Master, I will take the constables to Gerard’s house, and seize him for the theft.”

“The theft?” Ghysbrecht caught at the word as if it were a handle. “Ay! good; very good. It is theft. I forgot that.” Then the malice in him leaned down into its own shadow. “So, as he is a thief now, we will put him in the dungeons below, where the toads are and the rats. Dierich, that man must never see daylight again. ’Tis his own fault; he must be prying. Quick, quick! ere he has time to talk—you know, time to talk.”

In less than half an hour Dierich Brower and four constables stood in the hosier’s house, their boots bringing in the street’s damp and their iron clinking softly—a hard little music that turned the domestic air sour. Catherine, pale with that dread which comes of loving an impulsive boy, faced them with floury hands as if she had been interrupted at kneading.

They demanded young Gerard.

“Alas! what has he done now?” she cried. “That boy will break my heart.”

“Nay, dame, but a trick of youth,” said Dierich smoothly; his voice was like oil on a pan—quieting, slippery. “He hath but made off with certain skins of parchment, in a frolic doubtless; but the burgomaster is answerable to the burgh for their safe keeping, so he is in care about them. As for the youth, he will doubtless be quit for a reprimand.”

The gentleness of the words, and the familiarity of the house, imposed on Catherine’s honest fear; she clutched at the hope of “a reprimand” as a poor cook clutches at a crust. But her daughter Kate, who listened with a sharper hunger and a finer ear, heard the strain beneath the smoothness. And when it appeared Gerard was not at home—had not been at home that night—Dierich’s mask slipped. His disappointment came out of him in a harshness too big for “a frolic.”

“Come away then,” said he roughly to the constables. “We are wasting time.” And, with a vehemence that betrayed a private stake: “I’ll find him if he is above ground.”

As he turned to go, Kate made him a small signal. He bade his men move on, and waited outside the door where the air sounded wider—the street murmuring, a child’s cry, the soft slap of water in a gutter. Kate joined him quickly.

“Hush!” she said. “My mother knows not. Gerard has left Tergou.”

Dierich’s eyes sharpened. “How?”

“I saw him last night.”

“Ay! Where?” The eagerness jumped in him.

“At the foot of the haunted tower.”

“At the foot—” Dierich’s mind ran at once to rope and bar. “How did he get the rope?”

“I know not; but this I know: my brother Gerard bade me there farewell, and he is many leagues from Tergou ere this. The town, you know, was always unworthy of him; and when it imprisoned him, he vowed never to set foot in it again. Let the burgomaster be content, then. He has imprisoned him, and he has driven him from his birthplace and from his native land. What need now to rob him and us of our good name?”

It was sense, and had Dierich been only a townsman he might have felt it. But he was mortified—mortified at the escape, and mortified at the thought of the hundred crowns slipping away like fish in a net.

“What need had he to steal?” he flung back bitterly.

“Gerard stole not the trash,” Kate answered hotly. “He but took it to spite the burgomaster, who stole his liberty. But he shall answer to the Duke for it, he shall. As for these skins of parchment you keep such a coil about—look in the nearest brook or stye, and ’tis odds but you find them.”

Dierich’s eyes flashed, not with piety for the records, but with a sudden greedy hope. “Think ye so, mistress?—think ye so?” And the words came out as though he were tasting them, turning them on his tongue, as one turns a rumour that might lead to profit.

“Mayhap you know ’tis so,” said Kate, and her voice lowered. “This I know, that Gerard is too good to steal, and too wise to load himself with rubbish, going a journey.”

“Give you good day, then,” snapped Dierich. “The sheepskin you scorn, I value it more than the skin of any in Tergou.” And he went off hastily on the false scent she had thrown him—toward brooks and styes, toward any place where wet and filth might hide what a man in fear had cast away.

Kate went back into the house, and the familiar noises there—spoons, a creak of a stool, the soft breathing of stored linen—felt suddenly fragile, as if the house were a loaf that might collapse if jarred. She drew Giles aside—Giles, whom folk called Perpusillus, small in name if not in bone.

“Giles, my heart misgives me. Breathe not to a soul what I say to you. I have told Dirk Brower that Gerard is out of Holland, but much I doubt he is not a league from Tergou.”

“Why, where is he, then?” asked Giles, his blunt face earnest.

“Where should he be, but with her he loves?” Kate said; and the thought was like smelling a remembered herb—sweet, piercing, and sorrowful. “But if so, he must not loiter. These be deep and dark and wicked men that seek him. Giles, I see that in Dirk Brower’s eye makes me tremble.” She clasped her hands, angry at her own body. “Oh, why cannot I fly to Sevenbergen and bid him away? Why am I not lusty and active like other girls? God forgive me for fretting at His will; but I never felt till now what it is to be lame and weak and useless.”

Then, forcing tenderness into a coaxing tone, she laid her hope on him. “But you are strong, dear Giles—you are very strong.”

“Yes, I am strong,” thundered Perpusillus, swelling with it; then, catching what she meant, he fell back into the sulk of a boy asked to labour. “But I hate to go on foot.”

“Alas! alas!” Kate breathed. “Who will help me if you will not? Dear Giles—do you not love Gerard?”

“Yes,” said he, and the sulk cracked. “I like him best of the lot. I’ll go to Sevenbergen on Peter Buyskens his mule. Ask you him, for he won’t lend her me.”

Kate began to remonstrate at once. A mule taken openly would be a bell rung in the market. The whole town would have ears for it; folk would follow with their eyes; and in a place where every voice knew every name, it would soon be known whither Giles had gone—toward Sevenbergen, toward danger.

Giles parried it with a plan of his own, half-boyish, half-shrewd. He would ride out of town the opposite way, as if bound on some idle errand, and not turn the mule’s head toward Sevenbergen till he had shaken off the curious. Kate, measuring the town’s hunger for gossip as carefully as she would measure spice into broth, saw it was the best she could do. She assented, and borrowed the mule.

Then she charged Giles with a short message—few words, but heavy with meaning—and made him repeat it after her again and again until he could say it word for word, as a cook repeats a recipe that must not fail: no ingredient forgotten, no step altered, or the whole dish spoiled and the eater poisoned.

Giles mounted and started, the mule’s hooves making a steady, living percussion down the street—sound carrying farther than sight, and Kate listening until it thinned into the town’s general murmur.

Little Kate withdrew to a corner of the house where the daylight fell like a pale cloth. She knelt, and did the last thing now left in her power for her beloved brother: she prayed, long and earnestly, for his safety—sending the prayer after him as one sends bread after a traveller, knowing it may be all that stands between him and hunger.
