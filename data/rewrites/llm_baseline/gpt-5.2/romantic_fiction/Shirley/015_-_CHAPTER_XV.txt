The morning after, Shirley sat with Caroline where the light fell kindly, warming the polish of the old furniture and laying a soft gilt edge on the very air. Through the half-open window came the garden’s mingled breath—box, damp earth, and some late wallflower refusing to be done with spring. A rook cawed beyond the trees; nearer at hand a bee worried at a blossom with the steady industry of a cook who knows exactly what must be done and when.

Shirley, still in that pleased humour which follows a well-managed table, spoke with the bright ease of a hostess who liked her own skill.

“I was glad last night answered,” she said. “I do like a little circle of gentlemen round a judiciously contrived meal. It is diverting to watch them grow amiable over what has been honestly considered—what has been seasoned, and warmed, and timed. Men pretend to live on glory and business; give them a well-roasted bird and a glass properly chosen, and you see what truly reaches them. We—women—are expected to be content with the thinking of it; but I own I take pleasure in the contrivance.”

Caroline smiled; she had enjoyed the dinner too, though her enjoyment was of the quiet kind that does not ask to be observed.

“And yet,” Shirley went on, turning the subject with that suddenness which was her way, “I watch Moore sometimes—your cousin Moore—to discover where he may be touched. But he has none of that child’s simplicity about food, of being pleased on the surface and showing it. Did you ever find his accessible point, Lina? You have seen more of him than I.”

“It is not, at any rate, like my uncle’s, nor Dr. Boultby’s,” Caroline returned, and because Shirley had led her there, she allowed herself that shy indulgence—the speaking of Robert’s name without being blamed by her own reserve. “But I cannot tell what it is. I never watched him in my life but my watching was soon baffled by finding he was watching me.”

“There!” Shirley’s eyes kindled with the quick triumph of recognition. “You cannot fix your gaze on him but his will presently flash on you. He is never off his guard. Even when he does not look, his mind seems moving among your own thoughts—tracing words back to their spring, weighing motives as if they were grain in a measure. I know that sort of man. It piques me. How does it affect you?”

Caroline had once been fluttered by these direct thrusts; now she had learned, like a little Quaker in a plain gown, to parry with gentleness.

“Pique you? In what way does it pique you?” she asked, letting the question return upon its owner.

Shirley’s attention flew elsewhere as suddenly as a bird startled from a hedge.

“Here he comes!” she cried, springing up and running to the window. “A diversion! I never told you of a conquest I have made lately—made at those parties you never will come to with me; and it was done without effort, without intention—so I protest. There is the bell—and by all that is delicious! there are two of them. Do they never hunt except in couples? You may have one, Lina, and you may take your choice. Listen to Tartar!”

Down the hall burst the deep bay of her great dog—black-muzzled, tawny, and huge—filling the house as though the very boards had learned to bark. After the bay came a growl, low and long, like thunder muttering behind a hill.

“Listen!” Shirley laughed. “You would think it the prelude to a bloody onslaught. They will be frightened. They do not know old Tartar as I do. They are not aware his uproars are all sound and fury, signifying nothing.”

But in the hollow of the hall there was indeed a scuffle, a sharp command, and then a crack of a cane or whip; immediately followed a yell, a run, the clatter of hurried feet, and the startled cries of servants.

“O Malone—Malone!”

“Down, sir! Down!” rang out a high, imperious voice—more indignant than brave.

“He really is worrying them,” Shirley exclaimed, half amused, half alarmed now. “They have struck him. A blow is what he is not used to, and will not take.”

Out she ran. The scene, for all its comic frenzy, had something too true of panic in it. One gentleman fled up the polished oak staircase with the speed of terror; another backed towards the stairfoot, flourishing a knotty stick as though he believed noise and timber could keep teeth away. Tartar—big as a calf, stubborn as a stone—bellowed, sprang, and set the hall in uproar. From the kitchen came the servants, tumbling out with aprons and startled faces, as if the very business of preparing dinners were being invaded by war.

The dog made a spring; the second gentleman turned and rushed after the first. The first was already safe in a chamber and held the door against his companion—nothing so merciless as fear. The other struggled hard; the door was about to yield.

“Gentlemen,” Shirley’s voice rang clear, silvery, and vibrating, “spare my locks, if you please. Calm yourselves! Come down. Look at Tartar—he won’t harm a cat.”

She was already at the dog. At her touch he crouched, fore paws stretched before him, tail still switching in stubborn resentment, nostrils snorting, dull fire in his eyes. He was honest, phlegmatic, stupid, and resolute; he loved his mistress and the man who fed him, and cared for little else. Quiet enough—unless threatened with a stick. Then some bulldog inheritance rose and made a demon of him.

“Mr. Malone, how do you do?” Shirley called up to the gallery, lifting her mirth-lit face. “That is not the way to the oak parlour. That is Mrs. Pryor’s apartment. Request your friend Mr. Donne to evacuate. I shall have the pleasure of receiving him in a lower room.”

A hollow laugh answered—Peter Augustus Malone leaning over the balustrade with offended dignity.

“Really that animal alarmed Donne,” he said, stiffening himself with an effort at composure. “He is—ah—a little timid. I thought it better to follow in order to reassure him.”

“It appears you did. Now come down, if you please. John—go upstairs and liberate Mr. Donne. And take care, Mr. Malone; the stairs are slippery.”

The caution came late. The oak had been polished by years of careful housewifery until it shone like a table newly rubbed for company. Malone slipped in his stately descent and saved himself only by clutching the banisters so that the whole great framework groaned.

Tartar, sensitive to any commotion, growled again. Malone, for all his surprise, was no coward; now he passed the dog with suppressed fury rather than fear, and if looks could have strangled, Tartar would have died where he lay. Forgetting politeness in his rage, Malone pushed into the parlour before Miss Keeldar, glanced at Caroline as if bending his head were a personal indignity, and glared at both ladies with such a dark promise of domestic tyranny that Shirley thought, with a momentary pity, how certain men look when crossed—as if they had been born to clutch and punish.

Shirley stopped laughing. Caroline, true lady, would not smile at any mortification, however deserved. Tartar was dismissed with a word; Malone was soothed—for Shirley could put a bridle on tempers with her voice, as deftly as she could order a meal. Malone had sense enough to perceive that, since he could not challenge the owner of the dog, he had better be civil. He tried to be civil; being received, he became very civil indeed, and presently quite himself. He had come, in truth, meaning to be charming and fascinating. If his entrance had been lion-like, he resolved to depart lamb-like.

He chose his seat for air—or for escape—neither on the sofa where Shirley offered him a sort of throne, nor near the fire, to which Caroline, by a gentle sign, invited him, but on a chair close to the door. Once his sullen heat had cooled, constraint returned; he spoke by starts, choosing the most painfully common topics, and between each fragment of talk he sighed as if the world were a burden and he a martyr to sensibility.

To help himself to grace, he drew from his pocket a large silk handkerchief—red and yellow—an ample bandana. His hands, having nothing wiser to do, set to work on it with earnest invention. He folded it cornerwise, whipped it open with a flourish, folded it narrower, made of it a band; and then, with solemn originality, he bound it round his crossed legs—circled his athletic Irish calves and tied them together as though he feared his limbs might run away without permission. He repeated this device more than once, as if expecting applause. Shirley fled to the window to laugh unseen; Caroline turned her face aside so her curls might hide the smile rising in spite of herself.

And Caroline observed, too, what was more instructive than comic: how completely, abruptly, and without shame Malone’s homage had wheeled from herself to the heiress. The little sum he had once calculated upon in Miss Helstone’s future dwindled to nothing beside Miss Keeldar’s estate and hall. He did not even trouble to disguise his arithmetic; it was as open as a butcher’s scale.

John’s steps were long absent above; Mr. Donne, it seemed, did not descend willingly. At length he appeared at the parlour door—cold, calm, and not in the least ashamed. Shame, indeed, required some warmth of nature, and Donne had none. He was of that immovable, complacent, self-satisfied kind which cannot blush because it cannot feel. No humiliation could touch him; he had no fire in his blood, no modesty in his soul—decorous, arrogant, insipid, and inane; and yet he had arrived at the notion of wooing Miss Keeldar as one might decide to purchase a chair.

His idea of courtship was mechanical: a certain number of visits, then a letter, then acceptance for love of his office; then marriage; then mastery of Fieldhead, servants at command, the best eating and drinking, and importance. He would have concealed his aims better had he possessed even that small instinct which teaches men how to conceal; but he entered with an injured impertinence, as if Fieldhead had wronged him.

“A very dangerous dog that, Miss Keeldar,” he said. “I wonder you should keep such an animal.”

“Do you?” Shirley replied coolly. “Perhaps you will wonder more when I tell you I am very fond of him.”

“I should say you are not serious. Can’t fancy a lady fond of that brute—’tis so ugly—a mere carter’s dog. Pray hang him.”

“Hang what I am fond of!”

“And purchase instead some sweetly pretty pug or poodle—something appropriate. Ladies generally like lap-dogs.”

“Perhaps I am an exception.”

“Oh, you can’t be, you know. All ladies are alike in those matters. That is universally allowed.”

“Tartar frightened you terribly, Mr. Donne. I hope you won’t take harm.”

“That I shall, no doubt. He gave me a turn I shall not soon forget. When I sor him”—so Mr. Donne pronounced it—“about to spring, I thought I should have fainted.”

“Perhaps you did faint in the bedroom; you were a long time there.”

“No. I bore up that I might hold the door fast. I was determined not to let any one enter. I thought to keep a barrier between me and the enemy.”

“But what if your friend Mr. Malone had been worried?”

“Malone must take care of himself.”

At that moment, past the glass door opening on the garden, Tartar walked, stiff and tawny and black-muzzled as ever, still in bad humour, emitting a growl and a half-strangled whistle—some stubborn note from his bulldog ancestry.

“What is that?” Donne cried. “I declare your man told a falsehood! The dog is there!”

“There are other visitors coming,” Shirley observed, with the provoking serenity of those who own the terror and can command it.

Tartar sprang down the paved walk towards the gate, bellowing with explosion. Shirley opened the glass door and stepped out, chirruping to him as a cook might hush the sudden roar of a kettle. The bellow was silenced at once; the great brute lifted his blunt head to be patted.

“What! Tartar, Tartar!” said a cheery, rather boyish voice. “Don’t you know us? Good-morning, old boy!”

Little Mr. Sweeting came through the gate, fearless through sheer good nature, caressing the guardian as if they were old friends. His vicar, Mr. Hall, followed with quiet assurance; Tartar had no ill-will to him. After sniffing both with grave ceremony, the dog drew aside to the sunny front of the hall, leaving the archway clear. Sweeting would have played with him, but Tartar, obstinately indifferent to all hands save Shirley’s, ignored the caresses as if they were air.

Shirley met Messrs. Hall and Sweeting cordially, shaking hands, and heard their news—success that morning in obtaining subscriptions for the fund. Mr. Hall’s plain face shone with benignity; his eyes beamed through his spectacles. When Caroline, seeing who had come, ran out and put both her hands into his, he looked down on her with a gentle affection which made him seem at once grave and fatherly—like some mild scholar who had carried peace about with him into a rough world.

Instead of returning to the house, they wandered into the garden, the ladies on either side of Mr. Hall. The day was breezy and sunny; the wind freshened their cheeks and lifted their ringlets into graceful disorder. Shirley looked gay; Caroline looked quietly pretty. Mr. Hall spoke most to his brilliant companion, yet his eyes often sought the quieter one, as if accustomed to read her silence.

Flowers grew profusely in the enclosure, and their perfume—rose, mignonette, damp leaves bruised underfoot—rose and fell with the passing gusts. Shirley gathered handfuls, pressing the stems into her palm till the sap scented her fingers. She gave some to Caroline.

“Choose a nosegay for Mr. Hall,” she said.

Caroline, with her lap filled with delicate and splendid blossoms, sat down on the steps of a summer-house. Mr. Hall stood near, leaning on his cane, and there, in that ordinary garden ease—talk, wind, scent, and bird-notes—there was something almost sacred in their quiet companionship, as if the day were repeating itself from year to year, and friendship were a tradition kept like a household recipe, handed down and never improved upon because it was already right.

Shirley, remembering hospitality even while she strayed, called out the neglected pair still in the oak parlour. She convoyed Donne past his dread enemy, who lay snoring under the meridian sun, nose on fore paws, as harmless as any dog could look when asleep. Donne was glad of the safeguard; gratitude, as usual, did not occur to him.

Desiring to be impartial, Shirley offered the curates flowers. They accepted them with native awkwardness. Malone, holding bouquet in one hand and shillelah in the other, looked as if the two objects were sworn enemies and he did not know which to honour. Donne’s “Thank you!” was rich to hear—so fatuous, so self-important, as though a handful of blossoms were homage to his virtues and an attempt to win his priceless affection. Sweeting alone received his posy like the smart, sensible little man he was, and popped it gallantly into his buttonhole as if he had always belonged among gardens and ladies.

As a reward for that good manner, Shirley beckoned him apart and gave him some commission. Sweeting’s eyes sparkled; away he flew round by the courtyard to the kitchen, as much at home there as if he had been born to carry dishes. Presently he reappeared with a round table, which he set under the cedar; then he collected six garden-chairs from nooks and bowers, arranging them in a circle with cheerful fuss. The parlour-maid—Shirley kept no footman—came out with a tray covered by a napkin. Sweeting’s nimble fingers helped dispose glasses, plates, knives, and forks; then the napkin was lifted, and there lay the neat luncheon: cold chicken with its skin pale and savoury, slices of ham cut clean, and tarts whose browned edges promised crispness before the tongue even tried them.

This impromptu regale was Shirley’s delight. Nothing pleased her more than to have an alert little friend like Sweeting running about her hand, catching hints and executing them briskly. Between the house and the kitchen there was a little world of sound—soft clink of glass, the hush of linen unfolded, the knife’s clean tap against a plate—domestic music, wholesome and unpretending. It contrasted with the harsher noises beyond Fieldhead: the memory of factory clatter and public tumult that could batter the nerves like incessant hammering. Here, under the cedar, sound was proportioned—voices, birds, wind, and the small civil noises of eating—each in its place, each permitting thought and feeling to breathe.

Sweeting’s devotion to the heiress was, for once in such a world, quite disinterested; it did not shake his faithful allegiance to the magnificent Dora Sykes.

The repast proved merry. Donne and Malone contributed little to its gaiety—except what concerned knife, fork, and wine-glass; but where four such natures as Mr. Hall, David Sweeting, Shirley, and Caroline met in health and amity on a green lawn, under sun, amid a wilderness of flowers, no dulness could long hold ground. Even the very food seemed to set a tone—honest, kindly, and communal, as if salt and bread had a way of making people speak more truly.

In the course of talk Mr. Hall reminded them Whitsuntide was approaching, when the grand united Sunday-school tea-drinking and procession of the three parishes—Briarfield, Whinbury, and Nunnely—were to take place. Caroline would be at her post as teacher, he knew, and he hoped Miss Keeldar would not be wanting. He hoped she would make her first public appearance amongst them then.

Shirley’s eyes brightened. She liked festive excitement—the gathering of happiness, the arrangement of pleasant details, the sight and sound of gladness in a mass: children’s voices, hymns, the shuffle of many feet on grass, the rustle of holiday clothes, the clatter of cups and the sweet steam of tea rising from urns like a homely incense.

“They may count on me with security,” she said. “I do not know what I shall have to do, but they may dispose of me as they please.”

“And,” Caroline added, “you will promise to come to my table and sit near me, Mr. Hall?”

“I shall not fail, Deo volente,” he replied. Then, turning to Shirley, with a smile that showed how long memory can keep the same affection under altered years: “I have occupied the place on her right hand at these monster tea-drinkings for the last six years. They made her a Sunday-school teacher when she was a little girl of twelve. She is not particularly self-confident, as you may have observed; the first time she had to ‘take a tray’ and make tea in public there was piteous trembling and flushing. I saw the speechless panic—the cups shaking in the little hand, the teapot too full from the urn, slopping over in spite of her care. I went to her aid, sat near her, managed the urn and slop-basin, and in fact made the tea for her like any old woman.”

“I was very grateful,” Caroline interposed, and the quiet warmth of her tone did more than protestations.

“You were,” he said. “And you told me so with an earnest sincerity that repaid me well; for there are little ladies of twelve whom you may help and caress for ever without their showing a quicker sense of kindness than if they were wax and wood. She kept close to me the rest of the evening, Miss Keeldar—walked with me over the grounds where the children played; followed me into the vestry when all were summoned into church; she would, I believe, have mounted with me to the pulpit, had I not taken the precaution of conducting her to the rectory pew.”

“And he has been my friend ever since,” Caroline said.

“And always sat at her table, near her tray, and handed cups—that is the extent of my services,” Mr. Hall continued. “The next thing I do for her will be to marry her some day to some curate or mill-owner. But mind, Caroline”—and now there was a gentle seriousness under the jest—“I shall inquire about the bridegroom’s character; and if he is not a gentleman likely to render happy the little girl who walked with me, hand in hand, over Nunnely Common, I will not officiate. So take care.”

“The caution is useless,” Caroline answered, half smiling, half earnest. “I am not going to be married. I shall live single, like your sister Margaret.”

“You might do worse,” he said. “Margaret is not unhappy. She has her books for a pleasure and her brother for a care, and she is content. If ever you want a home—if the day should come when Briarfield rectory is yours no longer—come to Nunnely vicarage. Should the old maid and bachelor be still living, they will make you tenderly welcome.”

Caroline, who had kept aside the nosegay she had chosen, offered it now.

“There are your flowers. You don’t care for a bouquet, but you must give it to Margaret; only—let me be sentimental for once—keep that little forget-me-not. It is a wild flower I gathered from the grass; and—to be still more sentimental—let me take two or three of the blue blossoms and put them in my souvenir.”

She drew out a small book with an enamelled cover and silver clasp, opened it, and laid the flowers between the leaves, writing round them in pencil: “To be kept for the sake of the Rev. Cyril Hall, my friend. May —, 18—.”

Mr. Hall, on his part, also placed a sprig between the leaves of his pocket Testament. He wrote only on the margin one word: “Caroline.”

“Now,” he said, smiling, “I trust we are romantic enough. Miss Keeldar, I hope you are laughing at this trait of ‘exaltation’ in the old gray-headed vicar; but the fact is, I am so used to comply with the requests of this young friend of yours, I don’t know how to refuse her when she tells me to do anything. You would say it is not much in my way to traffic with flowers and forget-me-nots; but, you see, when requested to be sentimental, I am obedient.”

“He is naturally rather sentimental,” Caroline said softly. “Margaret told me so, and I know what pleases him.”

“That you should be good and happy? Yes—one of my greatest pleasures. May God long preserve to you the blessings of peace and innocence!” His tone deepened, as if the garden’s brightness had drawn out, by contrast, a thought solemn and enduring. “By which phrase I mean comparative innocence; for in His sight none are pure. What to our human perception looks spotless as we fancy angels, is to Him but frailty, needing the blood of His Son to cleanse, and the strength of His Spirit to sustain. Let us each and all cherish humility—my young friends, as well as I; we may well do it when we look into our own hearts and see there temptations, inconsistencies, propensities even we blush to recognize.”

He paused a moment; the wind sighed in the cedar above them; somewhere nearer, a finch repeated its small phrase, clear and unbought. In the distance, beyond Fieldhead’s shelter, there came faintly—like an ill omen of another world—the harsh, far-off grind of industry, a sound that had no tenderness in it and offered no rest.

“It is not youth,” Mr. Hall went on, “nor good looks, nor grace, nor any gentle outside charm that makes either beauty or goodness in God’s eyes. Young ladies, when your mirror or men’s tongues flatter you, remember that, in the sight of her Maker, Mary Ann Ainley—a woman whom neither glass nor lips have ever panegyrized—is fairer and better than either of you. She is indeed,” he added, after a pause—“she is indeed. You young things, wrapt up in yourselves and in earthly hopes, scarcely live as Christ lived. Perhaps you cannot yet, while existence is so sweet and earth so smiling to you; it would be too much to expect. She, with meek heart and due reverence, treads close in her Redeemer’s steps.”
