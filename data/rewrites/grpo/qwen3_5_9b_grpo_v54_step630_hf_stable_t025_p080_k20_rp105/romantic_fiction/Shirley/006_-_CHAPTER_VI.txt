The morning sun filtered through the windowpane, warming the small room where Caroline Helstone sat with her eyes fixed on a point somewhere beyond the glass. Her governess, Mademoiselle Moore, watched her with a critical eye, noting the pupil's recurring lapse into silence. Caroline forgot the explanations offered to her, her mind drifting despite the chiding that followed. Yet, she bore the reproofs with a serene, unclouded patience. Bathed in the sunlight, she seemed to absorb a gentle influence that rendered her both happy and kind. In this state, she was at her most pleasing.

Caroline possessed a beauty that required no introduction to be appreciated. She was fair enough to captivate at first sight, her figure light and pliant, every curve neat and proportionate. Her face was expressive and gentle, framed by a profusion of brown curls that she arranged with taste. Her eyes held a winning beam that spoke softly to the affections, and her mouth was very pretty. She wore a simple winter garb of merino in a soft brown shade that matched her hair, accented only by a pink ribbon and knot at her neck. Her style was unobtrusive yet tasteful, suiting her slight frame perfectly.

While her appearance was undeniable, her intellect remained a mystery for the present. Her origins were soon revealed: the daughter of parents separated shortly after her birth due to incompatible dispositions. Her mother, the half-sister of Mr. Moore's father, had been taken from her in infancy. Her father, the brother of the rector, was a man whose character friends preferred not to recall after death; he had rendered his wife unhappy, and rumors of his conduct had tainted the reputation of his more principled brother. Having known neither parent, Caroline had been raised solely by her uncle, the rector. He was ill-suited to guide a young girl, neglecting her education until she herself grew anxious to acquire knowledge. Consequently, she felt inferior to her peers, a sentiment that made her eagerly accept the offer from her cousin Hortense to teach her French and needlework.

Mademoiselle Moore, proud of her "positive spirit" and preference for dry studies, delighted in the task. She viewed Caroline as an irregularly taught, nearly ignorant girl, attributing the pupil's rapid progress entirely to her own superior methods. Hortense kept her young cousin closely bound to the grammar of the French language, assigning endless "logical analyses." These exercises were a source of little pleasure to Caroline, who grudgingly spent hours pondering propositions and incident clauses. Occasionally, lost in the maze of syntax, she would seek aid from Robert in the counting-house. Mr. Moore, with his clear and tranquil mind, could dissolve her difficulties in moments, offering keys to puzzles that baffled Hortense. Caroline admired him deeply, wishing she were a boy so she might sit with him as a clerk rather than endure Hortense's lessons in the parlour.

Evenings at Hollow's Cottage were rare but precious. When Robert was home and free from business, the hours passed swiftly. Hortense, usually formal and self-important, relaxed in the presence of her kin, becoming kind and musical when she played the guitar. Robert, though not naturally lively, was a willing spectator of Caroline's vivacity, a complacent listener to her talk. Yet, these moments were fleeting; by the next morning, Robert was often "frozen up" again, rarely contriving their recurrence. This puzzled Hortense, who believed happiness should be cultivated constantly, yet she never dared to invite Caroline unasked, waiting for Robert's cue.

This morning marked a change. For the first time, Robert had invited Caroline unprompted, speaking with such kindness that she carried a sense of happiness throughout the day. The morning passed as usual, filled with Hortense's bustling supervision and Caroline's diligent repetition. During dinner, Sarah, the mill worker, served a meal with disdain, calling it unfit for dogs, yet the food—pea purée, chopped meat, greens, and a fruit pâté—was savory enough. Caroline accepted the Belgian cookery without complaint, knowing that showing distaste would incur her cousin's wrath.

Afterward, Caroline managed to coax Hortense upstairs to dress. Avoiding any hint that the camisole and curl-papers were odious, she persuaded her cousin to make her toilet in the bedroom. While Hortense delivered a homily on her own disregard for fashion, Caroline undressed her, investing her in a decent gown and arranging her hair. However, Hortense insisted on finishing with a thick handkerchief and a voluminous black apron, symbols of morality and thrift to her. Caroline protested, citing suffocation and the adequacy of her own short aprons, but Robert intervened, overhearing the dispute. He deemed Caroline's attire sufficient for a child, compelling Hortense to yield, though she disapproved of the resulting neatness.

The afternoon was devoted to sewing. Hortense, skilled with her needle, condemned Caroline to learn the tedious art of darning stockings stitch by stitch, a duty she considered essential for women. Caroline found the task grievous, often sighing over the work, but she adhered to it, knowing that burning the stockings would only result in a fresh pair. As the sky darkened and rain poured down, suspense gripped Caroline. She feared Robert would be detained at the mill by Mr. Sykes or Mr. Yorke.

At five o'clock, with the clouds streaming and the wind whispering in the roof-trees, Mademoiselle Moore declared that Robert would not return until moonrise. "We will have coffee," she decided, dismissing the hope of his arrival. Caroline obeyed, retreating to the kitchen where Sarah was making a dress. Sarah noted Caroline's listlessness, suggesting she was fit to cry from being cooped up. When Caroline asked if her master often returned early in wet weather, Sarah revealed she had seen Murgatroyd lead Robert's horse into the yard and heard him speaking of new frames and guards.

Caroline dismissed Sarah's claims as mistaken, but Sarah persisted, claiming to have heard Robert's voice. "He is coming," Sarah announced as the kitchen door opened. Mr. Moore entered, wet and cold. Caroline, pretending to focus on her dressmaking, hid her face before turning to meet him, her countenance instantly beaming.

"We had ceased to expect you," she said. "They asserted you would not come."

"But I promised to return soon. You expected me, I suppose?"

"No, Robert; I dared not when it rained so fast. And you are wet and chilled. Change everything. If you took cold, we should blame ourselves."

"I am not wet through: my riding-coat is waterproof. Dry shoes are all I require."

Standing by the hearth, Robert enjoyed the fire's glow. His glance rested on Caroline's uplifted face, flushed and smiling, shaded by silky curls. Sarah withdrew to the parlour with the coffee tray. Robert placed a hand on Caroline's shoulder, stooping to kiss her forehead.

"Oh!" she exclaimed, as if the kiss had unsealed her lips. "I was miserable when I thought you would not come. I am almost too happy now. Are you happy, Robert? Do you like to come home?"

"I think I do—to-night, at least."

"Are you certain you are not fretting about your frames, your business, and the war?"

"Not just now."

"Are you positive you don't feel Hollow's Cottage too small for you, and narrow, and dismal?"

"At this moment, no."

"Can you affirm that you are not bitter at heart because rich and great people forget you?"

"No more questions. You are mistaken if you think I am anxious to curry favour with rich and great people. I only want means—a position—a career."

"Which your own talent and goodness shall win you. You were made to be great; you *shall* be great."

"I wonder now, if you spoke honestly out of your heart, what recipe you would give me for acquiring this same greatness; but I know it—better than you know it yourself. Would it be efficacious? Would it work? Yes—poverty, misery, bankruptcy. Oh, life is not what you think it, Lina!"

"But you are what I think you."

"I am not."

"You are better, then?"

"Far worse."

"No; far better. I know you are good."

"How do you know it?"

"You look so, and I feel you *are* so."

"Where do you feel it?"

"In my heart."

"Ah! You judge me with your heart, Lina: you should judge me with your head."

"I do; and then I am quite proud of you. Robert, you cannot tell all my thoughts about you."

Robert's dark face flushed; his lips smiled yet remained compressed, his eyes laughed while his brow knitted. "Think meanly of me, Lina," he said. "Men, in general, are a sort of scum, very different to anything of which you have an idea. I make no pretension to be better than my fellows."

"If you did, I should not esteem you so much. It is because you are modest that I have such confidence in your merit."

"Are you flattering me?" he demanded, turning sharply upon her with an eye of acute penetration.

"No," she said softly, laughing at his sudden quickness. She seemed to think it unnecessary to proffer any eager disavowal.

"You don't care whether I think you flatter me or not?"

"No."

"You are so secure of your own intentions?"

"I suppose so."

"What are they, Caroline?"

"Only to ease my mind by expressing for once part of what I think, and then to make you better satisfied with yourself."

"By assuring me that my kinswoman is my sincere friend?"

"Just so. I am your sincere friend, Robert."

"And I am—what chance and change shall make me, Lina."

"Not my enemy, however?"

The answer was cut short by Sarah and her mistress entering the kitchen in commotion. They had been arguing over "café au lait," which Sarah deemed a waste of God's gifts, while Mademoiselle affirmed it was a royal beverage. The former occupants withdrew to the parlour. Before Hortense followed, Caroline questioned Robert once more, "Not my enemy, Robert?" Moore, Quaker-like, replied with another query, "Could I be?" and then seated himself beside her.

Caroline scarcely heard Hortense's explosion of wrath upon their reunion; the long declamation about the "indign conduct of this wicked creature" sounded confusedly like rattling china. Robert laughed in subdued tones, politely entreating his sister to be tranquil. He assured her that if she desired satisfaction, she could choose any girl from the mill as an attendant, though he feared they were mostly ignorant of household work. Hortense admitted the truth of this, complaining of the "insupportable" English peasants and wishing for an Antwerp girl. Robert, stern in public but kind in private, offered to fetch her an Antwerp servant, but Hortense declined, noting such a girl would not stay long amidst the factory's young coquettes. She softened, acknowledging her domestic trials as her destiny, recalling her mother's similar sufferings in Antwerp. Robert, remembering his mother's hot kitchen in Belgium, let the subject drop.

When the coffee-service was removed, Robert fetched Hortense's music-book and guitar. Arranging the ribbon around her neck with fraternal kindness, he asked for her mother's favorite songs. Nothing refines like affection; family union elevates. Hortense, pleased and grateful, looked almost graceful, her fretful look replaced by a "sourire plein de bonté." She sang with feeling, the songs reminding her of her young days and a parent she had loved. Observing Caroline's naïve interest, Hortense's good-humour increased, and the exclamation, "I wish I could sing and play like Hortense!" rendered her charming for the evening.

A lecture followed on the vanity of wishing versus the duty of trying. "As Rome," it was suggested, "had not been built in a day, so neither had Mademoiselle Gérard Moore's education been completed in a week, or by merely *wishing* to be clever. It was effort that had accomplished that great work." Once on the theme of her own merits, Hortense was fluent, taking her knitting and sitting down tranquil in the blissful self-complacency of the warm parlour.

"What shall we do now, Caroline?" asked Mr. Moore, returning to his seat.

"What shall we do, Robert?" she repeated playfully. "You decide."

"Not play at chess?"

"No."

"Nor draughts, nor backgammon?"

"No, no; we both hate silent games that only keep one's hands employed, don't we?"

"I believe we do. Then shall we talk scandal?"

"About whom? Are we sufficiently interested in anybody to take a pleasure in pulling their character to pieces?"

"A question that comes to the point. For my part, unamiable as it sounds, I must say no."

"And I too. But it is strange, though we want no third—fourth, I mean (she hastily and with contrition glanced at Hortense)—living person among us—so selfish we are in our happiness—though we don't want to think of the present existing world, it would be pleasant to go back to the past, to hear people that have slept for generations in graves that are perhaps no longer graves now, but gardens and fields, speak to us and tell us their thoughts, and impart their ideas."

"Who shall be the speaker? What language shall he utter? French?"

"Your French forefathers don't speak so sweetly, nor so solemnly, nor so impressively as your English ancestors, Robert. To-night you shall be entirely English. You shall read an English book."

"An old English book?"

"Yes, an old English book—one that you like; and I will choose a part of it that is toned quite in harmony with something in you. It shall waken your nature, fill your mind with music; it shall pass like a skilful hand over your heart, and make its strings sound.
