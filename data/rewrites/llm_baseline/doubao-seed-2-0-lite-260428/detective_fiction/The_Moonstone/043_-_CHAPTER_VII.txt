The second I stepped across the threshold, Rachel sprang up from the piano stool as if jolted by a live wire.
I shut the door softly behind me. For a long, taut beat, we stood in total silence, the full length of the parlour stretching between us. The small exertion of rising seemed to be the only movement her body was capable of making—every other muscle, every thread of thought, all of her faculties, bodily and mental, had narrowed down to one single act: staring at me.

A cold lurch of fear told me I’d shown myself too suddenly. I advanced a few slow steps toward her. “Rachel,” I said, gentle as I could speak.

The sound of my name on my lips dragged the life back into her limbs, flooded colour back into her pale cheeks. She moved toward me too, still not uttering a word. Slow, as if pulled by a force outside her own will, she drew closer and closer, that warm dusky flush darkening across her face, the light of returning awareness brightening in her eyes with every step. I forgot the errand that had brought me to her. I forgot the vile suspicion that had stained my name. I cast aside every consideration, past, present, and future, I had sworn to honour. All I saw was the woman I loved coming nearer, trembling, undecided. I couldn’t resist it a moment longer. I pulled her tight into my arms, and covered her face with kisses.

For one split second I thought she was kissing me back. For one breath, it felt like she too might have forgotten everything that stood between us. Before that thought could even fully form in my head, her first intentional movement tore the illusion apart. With a cry that rang like a shriek of horror, with a strength I doubt I could have resisted even if I’d tried, she shoved me hard away from her. I saw unforgiving, merciless anger in her eyes. I saw cold, biting contempt curl her lips. She looked me over from head to toe the way she might have stared at a random stranger who had just insulted her in the street.

“You coward!” she said. “You mean, miserable, heartless coward!”

Those were her first words. The most unendurable reproach a woman can level at a man, and she had chosen it to hurl at me.

“I remember a time, Rachel,” I said, “when you would have told me I’d offended you with far more dignity than that. I beg your pardon.”

Some of the sharp bitterness I felt must have seeped into my voice. At the first words of my reply, her eyes, which had darted away a moment before, snapped back to me unwillingly. She answered in a low, sullen tone, a quiet submission to her own rage I had never seen from her before.

“Maybe I owe you some small excuse,” she said. “After what you’ve done, is it manly of you to sneak your way in to see me like this today? It feels like a coward’s trick, preying on the weakness I’ve always had for you. Like a coward’s cheap surprise, catching me off guard so I’d let you kiss me. But that’s just a woman’s silly way of seeing it. I should have known you’d never think of it that way. I should have controlled myself, and said nothing at all.”

That half-apology stung worse than the insult. The most degraded wretch alive would have felt humiliated by it.

“If my honour wasn’t entirely in your hands right now,” I said, “I’d walk out that door this instant and never see you again. You spoke of what I’ve done. What, exactly, have I done?”

“What have you done? You dare ask that question of me?”

“I do.”

“I’ve kept your infamy a secret this whole time,” she snapped. “And I’ve suffered for it, hiding what I know. Don’t I have the right to be spared the insult of you pretending you have no clue what I’m talking about? Is every scrap of gratitude you ever had dead in you? You were once a gentleman. You were once dear to my mother, and far, far dearer to me—”

Her voice broke. She collapsed into a chair, turned her back on me, and covered her face with her hands.

I waited a long moment before I trusted myself to speak again. In that silence, I could barely tell which stung sharper: the lash of her contempt, or the stubborn, proud resolve that kept me from reaching out to soothe her distress.

“If you won’t speak first,” I said, “I will. I came here with something very serious to tell you. Will you do me the basic justice of listening while I say it?”

She didn’t move, didn’t answer. I didn’t beg a second time, didn’t advance an inch closer to her chair. With a pride every bit as stubborn as hers, I laid out my full discovery at the Shivering Sand, and every step that had led me there. The story took a while to tell. From the first word to the last, she never turned to look at me, never made a single sound.

I kept my temper clamped down tight. My whole future depended, more than likely, on me not losing my head right then. The moment had come to test Mr. Bruff’s theory. Caught up in the sharp, breathless tension of that experiment, I stepped around to stand directly in front of her.

“I have one question to ask you,” I said. “It forces me to revisit a very painful subject. Did Rosanna Spearman show you my stained nightgown? Yes, or No?”

She shot to her feet, and walked right up to me of her own accord. Her eyes searched my face, sharp and intent, as if she was trying to read something there she had never seen before.

“Are you mad?” she asked.

I still held myself back. “Rachel,” I said quietly, “will you answer my question?”

She kept going, ignoring me entirely. “Do you have some hidden agenda I don’t know about? Some cowardly fear for your future that drags me into it? They say your father’s death left you a very rich man. Did you come here to compensate me for losing my Diamond? And do you still have enough shame left to hide what you’re really after? Is that the secret behind this whole fake act of innocence, this ridiculous story about Rosanna Spearman? Is petty, ashamed greed the root of all these lies?”

I snapped. I couldn’t control myself a second longer. “You have done me an infamous wrong!” I burst out hotly. “You suspect me of stealing your Diamond. I have a right to know, and I will know, the reason why!”

“Suspect you!” she yelled, her anger flaring to match mine. “You villain, I saw you take the Diamond with my own two eyes!”

That revelation crashed over me, shattering every last piece of Mr. Bruff’s carefully built theory, leaving me completely helpless. Innocent as I was, I stood frozen in total silence before her. To her eyes, to any eyes, I must have looked like a man struck dumb by the proof of his own guilt.

She recoiled from the sight of my humiliation, and of her own triumph. The sudden, heavy silence that had fallen over me seemed to frighten her. “I spared you back then,” she said. “I would have spared you now, if you hadn’t forced me to speak.” She moved as if to leave the room, but hesitated before she reached the door. “Why did you come here just to humiliate yourself?” she whispered. “Why did you come here just to humiliate me?” She took a few more steps, then paused again. “For God’s sake, say something!” she cried, passionately. “If you have any mercy left, don’t make me degrade myself any further like this! Say something, and drive me out of the room!”

I advanced toward her, barely aware of what I was doing. I had some vague, confused idea of stopping her until she told me the full story. The second I learned that the evidence condemning me in Rachel’s mind was the testimony of her own eyes, nothing—not even my absolute certainty of my own innocence—made sense any more. I took her hand, tried to speak firmly, to the point. All I could get out was: “Rachel, you once loved me.”

She shuddered, and looked away from me. Her hand lay limp, trembling, in mine. “Let go of it,” she said faintly.

My touch had the same effect on her that the sound of my voice had when I first walked in. Right after she’d called me a coward, right after she’d declared she’d seen me steal the Diamond—while her hand lay in mine, I still had some hold over her.

I drew her gently back to the centre of the room, and sat her down beside me. “Rachel,” I said, “I can’t explain how contradictory what I’m about to tell you sounds. I can only speak the truth, just as you did. You saw me—with your own eyes, you saw me take the Diamond. Before God who is listening to us right now, I swear to you: I only just learned that I supposedly took it, this exact second!”

She didn’t even seem to hear me. “Let go of my hand,” she repeated faintly. That was her only answer. Her head sank onto my shoulder, and her hand unconsciously curled tight around mine the very moment she asked me to release it.

I didn’t press her on my innocence. That was as far as my forbearance went. My only chance of ever holding my head up again among honest men depended on getting her to tell me every last detail of what happened that night. The only hope I had left was that she might have missed some tiny, trivial scrap of evidence, something small that a full investigation could use to prove I was innocent in the end. I admit I didn’t let go of her hand. I admit I spoke to her with every scrap of the old sympathy and trust we’d shared in better days.

“I want you to tell me something,” I said. “I want you to walk me through every single thing that happened, from the moment we said goodnight to each other after your birthday dinner, up to the moment you saw me take the Diamond.”

She lifted her head from my shoulder, and tried to tug her hand free. “Oh, why go back to that!” she said. “Why dig all that up again!”

“I’ll tell you why, Rachel. We’re both the victims of some monstrous, cruel lie that’s pretended to be the truth this whole time. If we look at what happened on your birthday night together, we might still end up understanding each other.”

Her head dropped back onto my shoulder. Tears welled in her eyes, and rolled slowly down her cheeks. “Oh,” she whispered, “do you think I never had that hope? Don’t you know I’ve tried to see a way out of this, all on my own, just like you are now?”

“You tried alone,” I said. “You never tried it with me there to help you.”

Those words seemed to wake the same spark of hope in her that I felt when I spoke them. She answered my questions not just obediently, but eagerly, leaning her full intelligence into it, opening her whole mind to me willingly.

“Let’s start,” I said, “right after we said goodnight. Did you go straight to bed? Or did you stay up?”

“I went to bed.”

“Do you remember what time it was? Was it very late?”

“Not really. Around twelve, I think.”

“Did you fall asleep?”

“No. I couldn’t sleep that night.”

“Were you restless?”

“I was thinking about you.”

That answer almost broke me. The tone of it, even more than the words, went straight through my chest. I had to pause for a long moment before I could go on.

“Did you leave any light on in your bedroom?” I asked.

“None at all—until I got up again, and lit my candle.”

“How long after you got into bed was that?”

“About an hour, I think. Around one o’clock in the morning.”

“Did you leave your bedroom then?”

“I was just about to. I’d put on my dressing gown, and I was heading into my sitting room to get a book—”

“Had you opened your bedroom door yet?”

“I’d just turned the handle and pulled it open.”

“But you hadn’t stepped into the sitting room?”

“No. I stopped right before I could.”

“What stopped you?”

“I saw a light glowing under the sitting room door, and I heard footsteps coming toward it.”

“Were you frightened?”

“Not then. I knew my mother was a terrible insomniac, and I remembered she’d begged me all evening to let her keep the Diamond safe for me. I thought she was being unreasonably anxious about it, and I figured she was coming to check if I was in bed, to nag me about the Diamond again if she found I was still awake.”

“What did you do?”

“I blew my candle out, so she’d think I was asleep. I was being stubborn, too—I was dead set on keeping my Diamond in the hiding spot I’d chosen, not giving it to her.”

“After you blew the candle out, did you climb back into bed?”

“I didn’t have time. The exact second the flame went out, the sitting room door swung open, and I saw—”

“You saw who?”

“You.”

“Dressed in my usual clothes?”

“No.”

“In my nightgown?”

“In your nightgown, with your bedroom candle in your hand.”

“Were you alone?”

“Completely alone.”

“Could you see my face clearly?”

“Yes.”

“Plainly?”

“Plain as day. The candle in your hand lit it right up for me.”

“Were my eyes open?”

“Yes.”

“Did you see anything strange about them? A vacant, faraway look, like I was sleepwalking?”

“Nothing like that. Your eyes were bright—brighter than usual. You glanced around the room like you knew you weren’t supposed to be there, like you were scared someone would catch you.”

“When you stepped into the sitting room, did you notice how I walked?”

“You walked exactly like you always do. You walked straight to the middle of the room, then stopped and looked around.”

“What did you do first, when you saw me?”

“I couldn’t move. I was frozen solid. I couldn’t speak, couldn’t call out, couldn’t even step back and shut my bedroom door.”

“Could I have seen you standing there?”

“You definitely could have. But you never looked in my direction. There’s no point even asking. I’m sure you never saw me.”

“How can you be so sure?”

“Would you have still taken the Diamond? Would you have acted the way you did after? Would you be here right now, if you’d known I was awake watching you? Don’t make me talk about that part! I want to answer you calmly. Help me stay as calm as I can. Ask me something else.”

She was right, in every possible way. I moved on.

“What did I do after I stopped in the middle of the room?”

“You turned away, and walked straight to the corner by the window—where my Indian cabinet stands.”

“When I was at the cabinet, my back was to you. How did you see what I was doing?”

“I moved, too, so I could see.”

“So you could watch what I was doing with my hands?”

“There are three mirrors in my sitting room. While you stood there, I saw every single thing you did, reflected in one of them.”

“What did you see?”

“You set your candle down on top of the cabinet. You opened drawer after drawer, and shut each one, until you got to the drawer I’d hidden the Diamond in. You stared down at that open drawer for a second, then reached your hand in, and pulled the Diamond out.”

“How do you know I pulled the Diamond out?”

“I saw your hand go into the drawer. And I saw the glint of the stone between your thumb and fingers when you pulled your hand back out.”

“Did I reach back toward the drawer after that? To close it, for example?”

“No. You had the Diamond in your right hand, and you picked the candle back up off the top of the cabinet with your left.”

“Did you glance around the room again after that?”

“No.”

“Did I leave the room right away?”

“No. You stood completely still for what felt like forever. I could see your face sideways in the mirror. You looked like a man deep in thought, unhappy with whatever you were thinking about.”

“What happened next?”

“You snapped out of it all of a sudden, and walked straight out of the room.”

“Did I shut the door behind me?”

“No. You hurried out into the passage, and left the door wide open.”

“And then?”

“Then your light vanished, the sound of your footsteps faded away, and I was left all alone in the dark.”

“Did anything happen between that moment, and the time the whole house found out the Diamond was gone?”

“Nothing.”

“Are you sure? Might you not have fallen asleep for a little while?”

“I never slept. I never climbed back into bed. Nothing at all happened until Penelope came to my room at her usual time the next morning.”

I dropped her hand, stood up, and paced the length of the room. I’d asked every possible question I could think of, and she’d answered all of them. I had every last detail laid out in front of me. I’d tested the theory of sleepwalking, the theory of intoxication—both had been proven completely worthless, by the very witness who had seen me that night. What was there to say? What was there left to do? The terrible, undeniable fact of the theft stood right in front of me, the only solid thing in a sea of total, impenetrable darkness. Not a single glimmer of light to guide me, even after I’d uncovered Rosanna Spearman’s secret at the Shivering Sand. Not a single glimmer of light now, after I’d come to Rachel, and heard the whole hateful story straight from her own lips.

She was the first to break the silence.

“Well?” she said. “You asked all your questions, and I answered every one. You made me hope we’d find something out of this, just because you hoped you would. What do you have to say now?”

The tone of her voice told me the small influence I’d had over her was gone again.

“We were going to look at what happened on my birthday night together,” she went on, “and then we were supposed to understand each other. Did we do that?”
