On a Thursday afternoon, Madame Beck summoned me to her side, asking if I had any pressing duties that would prevent me from running errands in town for her. With nothing occupying my time, I offered myself willingly. She handed me a list of silks, wools, and embroidery threads needed for the students’ projects, and soon I was ready—dressed appropriately for the brooding sky above, heavy with clouds and heat—when she called me back into the dining room.

“Forgive me, Miss Lucy,” she said, as though struck by a sudden thought, “I’ve just remembered one more task—if you’re not too burdened already?”

Of course, I assured her I was far from overtaxed. She hurried into the small salon and returned with a lovely basket brimming with rare greenhouse fruits—rosy, perfect, and luscious, nestled among dark green waxen leaves and pale yellow star-shaped blossoms of some exotic plant.

“There,” she said, “it’s light enough not to mar your fine appearance, unlike a servant’s chore. Please deliver this basket to Madame Walravens, along with my congratulations on her celebration. She lives in the old town, Number 3, Rue des Mages. The walk may be long, but you have the entire afternoon. Don’t rush—should you miss dinner, I’ll have a portion saved, or Goton, who holds you in affection, will prepare something special for you. You won detail, my dear Miss Lucy. And oh—please!” (calling me back once more), “be sure to see Madame Walravens herself and hand her the basket directly. She is quite particular about such things. Goodbye! Until we meet again!”

At last, I stepped out. The shopping took longer than expected—choosing and matching threads and fabrics being an endlessly tedious task—but eventually, every item was secured. The patterns for slippers, bell-ropes, and baskets were selected; tassels and slides for purses chosen; all the little details settled. Only the fruit and message remained.

I welcomed the idea of a long walk through the ancient, shadowed Basse-Ville. And I liked it even more as the evening sky deepened into a mass of black-blue metal, glowing at the edges, slowly turning crimson like molten iron.

Storms frighten me—not because of the thunder or lightning, but because they demand effort, movement, strength. But rain? That I can endure. A steady downpour asks only surrender—letting clothes and body grow wet, yielding quietly to nature’s will. In return, it cleanses the city, clears the streets, stills the world like a spell. It turns Villette into a forgotten desert city, Tadmor. Let the rains fall, let the floods rise—just so long as I first deliver this basket of fruit.

A distant clock, its voice unknown now that Jean Baptiste’s tower was too far away, chimed the quarter past five when I reached the address Madame Beck had given. It wasn’t really a street at all, more like a quiet square. Grass grew between the wide grey stones, the houses stood large and ancient, their backs hidden by trees suggesting gardens behind. Time seemed to linger here—business had fled. Once, wealthy men lived here; once, grandeur dwelled within these walls. The church rising above, with its dark, crumbling towers, was once the opulent shrine of the Magi. But wealth had flown, leaving behind empty nests—perhaps to shelter poverty, perhaps to stand cold and abandoned through endless winters.

Crossing the silent square, where drops as large as five-franc coins fell slowly onto the pavement, I saw no sign of life—except for an elderly priest, bent and leaning on a staff, passing by. He looked like a figure carved from age itself.

He had come from the very house I was meant to visit. When I paused before the door he had just closed, ringing the bell, he turned and stared. His gaze lingered—perhaps he found me incongruous: young, well-dressed, bearing summer fruit in such a place. Had a bright-faced maid opened the door, I might have felt out of place too. But when an old woman appeared—wearing a faded peasant dress, a cap both ugly and costly, lace flaps hanging long, cloth petticoat and jacket, and wooden clogs shaped like tiny boats—I felt the scene made sense. It was fitting, even comforting.

Her face, however, was anything but soothing. I’d rarely seen such a sour expression. She barely answered my question about Madame Walravens, and I feared she’d snatch the basket from my hands—until the old priest limped forward, stopped her, and listened carefully to my message.

His hearing seemed poor, making it hard to make him understand I must speak to Madame Walravens personally and give her the basket myself. After some patience, he grasped the point and, speaking to the old woman in the native tongue of Labassecour, persuaded her to let me enter. He then escorted me upstairs and left me in a sort of drawing-room.

The room was large, with a magnificent old ceiling and stained-glass windows like those in a chapel. But it was empty, and under the gathering storm, it looked ominous. A smaller room opened off it, its single window shuttered tight. In the dimness, I could barely make out the furniture. My attention was drawn to a painting on the wall.

Then—strangely—the image began to shift. It trembled, sank, rolled inward until it vanished entirely, revealing an arched opening leading into a stone passage and a winding staircase below. Cold, bare stone—no carpet, no paint. From the stairs came a soft tapping, like a stick on stone. Then a shadow crept down, followed by a form.

Was it real? This figure emerging from the arch, dimming the light?

It drew near. I saw it clearly. I understood where I was. No wonder this square was called the Quarter of the Magi—no wonder the three towers above had been blessed by ancient sages of lost arts. Magic ruled here. A spell had opened a fairy realm—the shrinking portrait, the secret passage, the stone stair—all part of a tale. And standing at the center was Cunegonde, the sorceress. Malevola, the evil fairy. What did she look like?

She might have been three feet tall, yet shapeless. Her thin hands rested atop each other, gripping a golden knob on a slender ivory staff. Her face was enormous, set not on her shoulders but directly before her chest—no neck at all. I could swear a hundred years lived in her features, and more in her eyes—those cold, hostile eyes, framed by thick grey brows and livid lids. How sternly they regarded me, filled with dull disdain!

She wore a blue brocade gown, dyed the color of gentian flowers, patterned with satin leaves. Over it, a rich shawl, so large it swept the floor with its multicolored fringe. But her jewels stole the eye: long, clear earrings that sparkled with a light no imitation could mimic; rings on her bony fingers, thick gold bands set with purple, green, and blood-red stones. Hunchbacked, dwarf-like, and frail, she was dressed like a barbarian queen.

“Que me voulez-vous?” she rasped, her voice more masculine than feminine—indeed, a silver beard sprouted from her chin.

I delivered the basket and the message.

“Is that all?” she demanded.

“That is all,” I replied.

“Truly, it was worth the trouble,” she said. “Return to Madame Beck and tell her I can buy fruit when I choose. As for her congratulations—je m’en moque!” And with that, she turned her back.

Just as she did, thunder cracked, and lightning blazed across the room—illuminating salon and inner chamber alike. The magic story unfolded with the fury of the elements. The traveler, lured into the enchanted castle, heard the storm awaken outside.

What was I to think of Madame Beck? She kept strange company. She sent gifts to a mysterious shrine—and the recipient seemed neither kind nor noble. There went Sidonia, trembling like disease incarnate, tapping her staff on the mosaic floor, muttering curses as she vanished.

Rain poured down. The sky, red moments before, now glowed deathly pale beneath its blackness, as if afraid. Despite my earlier confidence in facing storms, I hesitated to step back into this downpour. Lightning flashed fiercely, thunder rumbled close—this tempest had gathered directly over Villette, bursting at the zenith. Forked bolts pierced vertical torrents; red zigzags crossed white streaks of rain. All erupted from a sky swollen and black.

Leaving Madame Walravens’ cold, unwelcoming salon, I descended the staircase. A seat waited on the landing—I sat there, waiting. Someone glided along the gallery above. It was the old priest.

“Mademoiselle should not sit here,” he said gently. “Our benefactor would be displeased if he knew a stranger was treated thus in this house.”

He urged me so earnestly to return to the salon that I could not refuse without rudeness. The smaller room proved better furnished, warmer, more inviting. He led me in. Partially lifting the blind, he revealed what looked more like a chapel than a private chamber—a solemn, reverent space, more suited to relics than comfort.

The priest sat beside me, as if keeping company. But instead of speaking, he opened a book, fixed his eyes upon the page, and whispered softly—what sounded like a prayer or litany. A yellow glow from the storm-lit sky gilded his bald head; his figure remained in deep purple shadow. Still as a statue, he seemed to forget me altogether—only raising his eyes when thunder cracked or lightning flared, not in fear, but in quiet awe. I, too, was awestruck—but free from terror, my thoughts flowed freely.

Truthfully, I began to feel a resemblance between this man and Père Silas, the confessor before whom I had knelt in the Béguinage church. The likeness was faint—I’d seen him only in dim light, in profile—but still, I sensed it. I thought I even recognized his voice.

As I watched, he caught my gaze, lifting his eyes briefly. I turned to study the room—its own half-mystic charm.

Beside a cross of aged ivory, carved with intricate detail and tilted over a dark-red prie-dieu adorned with a rich missal and ebony rosary—hung the picture whose outline had drawn me earlier. Now, clearer, it was no Madonna. It was a portrait of a woman in nun’s garb. Her face, though not beautiful, was gentle—pale, young, shadowed by sorrow or illness. Not intellectual, not striking—yet sweet in a way that spoke of weakness, passivity, resignation. Yet I could not tear my eyes from it.

The priest, who seemed deaf and frail at first, clearly retained his faculties. Without looking up, without turning his head, he noticed where my gaze fell. In slow, clear tones, he murmured four words:

“She was much beloved.

“She gave herself to God.

“She died young.

“She is still remembered, still wept.”

“By that old lady, Madame Walravens?” I asked, thinking I’d uncovered the source of her bitter temper—the grief of loss.

He shook his head, half-smiling.

“No, no,” he said. “A noblewoman’s love for her grandchildren may be deep, her sorrow keen—but only the betrothed lover, denied by Fate, Faith, and Death, mourns as Justine Marie is mourned.”

I sensed he wished to be questioned, so I asked who had loved her, and who still mourned her. He told me a small romantic tale, spoken with quiet emotion, against the fading storm. I admit it could have been more moving had it avoided French sentimentalism and over-dramatization. But the priest was plainly French-born, bred in Rome—a true son of the Church. When he did lift his eyes, they glanced at me with sharpness and depth—one would not expect after seventy years. Yet I believed him good.

The hero of his tale was a former pupil—now his benefactor—who had loved Justine Marie, daughter of wealthy parents, when his prospects allowed him hope. But his father, once a banker, failed, died, leaving debts and ruin. The son was forbidden to pursue Marie—especially by the hunchbacked, monstrous grandmother, Madame Walravens, whose temper, twisted by deformity, became demonic. The gentle Marie had neither the courage to betray her lover nor the strength to remain faithful. She abandoned him, refused another suitor with greater wealth, withdrew to a convent, and died in her noviciate.

Yet the heart that loved her never healed. The truth of his devotion showed in how he later cared for her family.

Years after Justine’s death, disaster struck her household. Her father, a jeweler by trade but active on the stock exchange, fell into financial ruin and disgrace. He died of grief and shame. Left penniless, his widow and mother faced starvation—until their once-rejected suitor, hearing of their plight, arrived with extraordinary devotion. He took on their prideful scorn with pure charity—housing them, caring for them, supporting them with tenderness no son could surpass. The mother, a decent woman, died blessing him. The grandmother, cruel and godless, lived on, sustained entirely by this self-sacrificing man. He treated her—with whom he’d suffered lifelong pain and solitude—as a mother. He brought her to this house, “and,” the priest added, tears rising in his eyes, “here, too, he shelters me, his old tutor, and Agnes, a servant from his father’s household. Three-quarters of his income goes to our upkeep and other charities—he keeps only one-fourth for himself, bread and modest lodging. By this choice, he has made marriage impossible. He has given himself to God and to his angel-bride, as fully as I, a priest.”

He wiped his tears, then raised his eyes to mine—for a fleeting moment. I caught the glance, and in it, a meaning struck me.

These Romanists are astonishing creatures. One you know no more than a distant emperor or lost king knows you—and yet, sees you wholly. He plans your meeting, your path, your words, as though fate were merely a cover for design. Madame Beck’s sudden errand, my innocent journey to the Place of the Magi, the priest’s chance descent, his intervention, my invitation into the room, the portrait, the story—each incident seemed separate, random. Like loose beads. But threaded by that quick, cunning glance, they formed a chain, like the rosary on the prie-dieu. Where was the link? I sensed connection, but couldn’t trace it.

Perhaps my silence seemed odd. He broke it gently: “Mademoiselle, I trust your journey home isn’t too long through these flooded streets?”

“More than half a league.”

“You live——?”

“In the Rue Fossette.”

“Not” (with sudden warmth), “not at Madame Beck’s pensionnat?”

“The same.”

“Donc” (clapping his hands), “donc, you must know my noble student, my Paul?”

“Monsieur Paul Emanuel, Professor of Literature?”

“He and none other.”

A pause. The thread of connection suddenly snapped into focus. I felt it yield.

“Were you speaking of M. Paul?” I asked. “Was he your pupil—and the benefactor of Madame Walravens?”

“Yes, and of Agnes, the old servant. And more—(with emphasis)—he was and _is_ the true, constant, eternal lover of that saint in heaven—Justine Marie.”

“And who, father, are _you?_” I asked, though I already expected the answer.

“I am Père Silas,” he said, “that unworthy son of Holy Church whom you once honored with a noble and touching confidence—revealing to me the core of your heart, the inner shrine of your mind, which I, in truth, desired to guide toward the only true faith. I have never ceased to watch you, never lost interest. Trained by Rome, shaped by her teachings, inoculated with her truths, inspired by her zeal—I see what you might become. And I envy Heresy her prize.”

This startled me. I felt it too—shaped, trained, molded. “Not so,” I thought—but I held my peace.

“I suppose M. Paul does not live here?” I asked, shifting to safer ground.

“No. He comes only occasionally—to worship his beloved saint, to confess to me, to pay respects to the woman he calls his mother. He lives in two small rooms, without a servant. Yet he refuses Madame Walravens selling those jewels you see her wear—objects she clings to with childish pride, as relics of her son’s wealth.”

“How often,” I murmured, “has this man, M. Emanuel, seemed petty in small things—yet how great in the larger ones!”

I didn’t count confession or saint-worship among his greatness.

“How long since that lady died?” I asked, looking at Justine Marie.

“Twenty years. She was older than M. Emanuel. He was very young then—he is not much past forty.”

“Does he still weep her?”

“His heart will weep her always. The essence of Emanuel’s nature is—constancy.”

He said it with firm emphasis.

Then the sun broke through, pale and watery. Rain still fell, but the storm had passed. The heated sky had split, pouring out its lightning. Delaying further would leave no daylight for my return. I rose, thanked the priest for his hospitality and his story. He responded kindly with “pax vobiscum”—a blessing I welcomed, for it seemed sincere. But less so the cryptic phrase that followed.

“Daughter,” he said, “you _shall_ be what you _shall_ be.”

An oracle that made me shrug as soon as I stepped outside. Few of us know our destiny. For all that had happened, I still hoped to live and die a sober Protestant. There was something hollow within “Holy Church,” and something showy around it—that tempted me little.

I walked on, lost in thought. Whatever Romanism may be, there are good Romanists. This man, Emanuel, seemed among the best—touched by superstition, influenced by priestly power, yet remarkable for his faith, devotion, sacrifice, and boundless charity. The question remained: how did Rome treat such qualities? Did she cherish them for God’s sake—or exploit them for her own gain?
