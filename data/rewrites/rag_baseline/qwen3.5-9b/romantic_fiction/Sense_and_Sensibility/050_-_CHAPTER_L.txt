Edward Ferrars was finally admitted to his mother's presence, emerging victorious from a battle of wills that had lasted far too long. Mrs. Ferrars, having deployed a defense of such ferocity and steadiness, successfully preserved herself from the stigma of being "too amiable," a label she seemed perpetually terrified of acquiring. Consequently, she pronounced him once more her son. Yet, the Ferrars household remained a landscape of volatile tides; for years she had nurtured two sons, only for the recent annihilation of Edward to rob her of one, and the prior death of Robert to leave her bereft of both. Now, with Edward resuscitated in spirit if not in health, she possessed a single heir again. However, Edward's sense of security remained precarious until he disclosed his engagement. He reasoned that the mere publication of his intent to marry Miss Dashwood might precipitate a fatal collapse in his constitution, carrying him off as swiftly as his previous misfortunes. Thus, with apprehensive caution, he revealed the truth, met with a calmness that exceeded his expectations.

Mrs. Ferrars initially attempted to dissuade him, marshaling every argument available to her. She posited that Miss Morton represented a superior option—a woman of higher rank and greater fortune. She reinforced this assertion with a brutal arithmetic of status: Miss Morton was the daughter of a nobleman possessing thirty thousand pounds, whereas Miss Dashwood was merely the offspring of a private gentleman holding a paltry three thousand. When she observed that Edward, while acknowledging the factual accuracy of her representation, showed no inclination to be guided by it, she deemed further resistance futile. Drawing upon the bitter lessons of the past, she submitted. After an ungracious delay necessitated by her own wounded dignity, designed to preempt any suspicion of genuine goodwill, she issued her decree of consent to the union of Edward and Elinor.

The subsequent consideration of financial augmentation yielded disappointing results. Although Edward was now her sole surviving son, he was not her eldest; Robert remained the primary beneficiary, inevitably endowed with a thousand pounds a year. No objections were raised against Edward accepting the clerical path for a modest stipend of two hundred and fifty pounds at most. Beyond the ten thousand pounds already advanced with Fanny, nothing was promised for the present or future. This sum, however, satisfied Edward and Elinor, exceeding their expectations. Even Mrs. Ferrars, through her evasive maneuvers, appeared the only party genuinely surprised by her stinginess.

With their income secured, the couple awaited only the completion of the parsonage at Barton, a project Colonel Brandon pursued with an eager desire to accommodate Elinor. As usual, the construction suffered from the unaccountable dilatoriness of the workmen, plagued by a thousand disappointments and delays. Eventually, Elinor breached her firm resolution to postpone marriage until the house was ready. The ceremony took place in Barton church early in the autumn. Their first month was spent at the Mansion-house, allowing them to supervise the finishing touches of the parsonage, select wallpapers, design shrubberies, and plan the layout of the grounds. By Michaelmas, Mrs. Jennings arrived to visit them, confirming her prophecies with remarkable accuracy. She found in Elinor and her husband what she truly believed to be one of the happiest couples in the world. Indeed, they lacked nothing save the marriage of Colonel Brandon and Marianne, and perhaps slightly better pasture for their cows.

Upon their settlement, they were besieged by visitors. Almost all relations and friends paid homage. Mrs. Ferrars descended to inspect the happiness she had reluctantly authorized, treating them with a facade of decent affection that never masked her true disdain. That favor was reserved exclusively for Robert and his wife, whose rise to prominence was swift and total. Lucy Steele's behavior in this affair stands as a testament to the efficacy of relentless self-interest. Her respectful humility, assiduous attentions, and endless flattery, once a small opening permitted, reconciled Mrs. Ferrars to Robert's choice and re-established him completely in her mother's grace. While Edward was never cordially forgiven for his intention to marry Lucy, and Elinor, despite her superior fortune and birth, was regarded as an intruder, Lucy was elevated to the status of a favorite child. She became indispensable to Mrs. Ferrars, receiving liberal assistance and maintaining harmonious relations with the Dashwoods, barring the inevitable jealousies between Fanny and Lucy and the occasional domestic friction between her own husband and herself.

Robert's ascent was built on a foundation of deception. When he first courted Lucy in Bartlett's Buildings, his stated aim was merely to persuade her to abandon her engagement to Edward. Expecting that a few interviews would suffice to overcome their mutual affection, he erred in his estimation. Lucy required repeated assurances; doubts lingered in her mind until another half-hour of discourse could dispel them. This demand for constant attendance secured his presence, and the rest followed logically. Gradually, the conversation shifted from Edward to Robert, a topic on which he possessed infinite material. Soon, it became evident that he had entirely supplanted his brother. He was proud of his conquest, proud of having tricked Edward, and very proud of his clandestine marriage without maternal consent.

Following this, they enjoyed several months of profound happiness at Dawlish, where Lucy cultivated her extensive network of relations and Robert drafted plans for magnificent cottages. Returning to town, Robert secured his mother's forgiveness through the simple expedient of asking for it—an act initiated by Lucy's strategic counsel. Initially, this pardon extended only to Robert. Lucy, owing no filial duty to her mother and thus committing no transgression, remained unpardoned for some weeks. However, her persistent display of humility, self-condemnation regarding Robert's offense, and feigned gratitude for her mother's perceived unkindness eventually procured the haughty notice that overwhelmed her with graciousness. Within a short time, she ascended to the highest state of affection and influence.

Lucy became as essential to Mrs. Ferrars as either Robert or Fanny. Meanwhile, Edward, though never fully absolved of his past error, displayed a ready discharge of his duties, an increasing attachment to his wife and home, and a regular cheerfulness of spirit, suggesting he was no less content with his lot than Robert. Elinor's marriage divided her from her family as little as circumstances permitted; her mother and sisters spent more than half their time at Barton. Mrs. Dashwood's frequent visits to Delaford were driven by both policy and pleasure; her desire to bring Marianne and Colonel Brandon together was a cherished objective, arguably more fervent than John's earlier suggestions.

Marianne Dashwood was destined for an extraordinary fate. Born to discover the falsity of her own opinions, she was compelled to counteract her most favored maxims through her conduct. She was born to overcome an affection formed late in life—at seventeen, based solely on strong esteem and lively friendship—and voluntarily offer her hand to another. That other was a man who had suffered no less than herself under the events of a former attachment, whom she had previously deemed too old to marry, and who still clung to the constitutional safeguard of a flannel waistcoat. Yet, so it was. Instead of succumbing to an irresistible passion or retiring into solitude, she found herself at nineteen submitting to new attachments, entering new duties, and becoming the mistress of a family and the patroness of a village.

Colonel Brandon, in Marianne, found consolation for every past affliction. Her regard and society restored his mind to animation and his spirits to cheerfulness. Equally, Marianne discovered her own happiness in forming his. Willoughby could not hear of her marriage without a pang; his punishment was completed shortly thereafter by the voluntary forgiveness of Mrs. Smith. By attributing her clemency to Willoughby's marriage to a woman of character, she provided him with the reason to believe that had he behaved honorably toward Marianne, he might have achieved both happiness and wealth. His repentance was undoubtedly sincere, and he long viewed Colonel Brandon with envy and Marianne with regret. Yet, he was not forever inconsolable, nor did he flee society or die of a broken heart. He lived to exert himself and frequently to enjoy life, finding domestic felicity in breeding horses and dogs and various sports. Nevertheless, for Marianne, he retained a decided regard, considering her his secret standard of perfection in womanhood; many a rising beauty was subsequently slighted by him as bearing no comparison to Mrs. Brandon.

Mrs. Dashwood wisely remained at the cottage, avoiding a move to Delaford. Fortunately for Sir John and Mrs. Jennings, when Marianne departed, Margaret had reached an age highly suitable for dancing and not ineligible for the presumption of having a lover. Between Barton and Delaford, a constant communication flowed, dictated by strong family affection. Among the merits and happiness of Elinor and Marianne, it cannot be ranked as the least considerable that, though sisters living almost within sight of one another, they managed to coexist without disagreement, thereby preventing any cooling of relations between their respective husbands.
