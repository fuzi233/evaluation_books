Not everyone who dares to fight a divine battle comes out victorious. For nights on end, the sweat of anguish breaks dark upon the forehead, and the suppliant cries out for mercy with a silent voice—the soul's appeal to the Unseen. "Spare my beloved," it pleads. "Heal the life within me. Do not tear apart what long affection has woven into my very nature. Oh, God of heaven, bow down, listen, show mercy!" Yet after this cry and this struggle, the sun may rise to find him defeated.

That opening morning, accustomed to greet him with the whispers of gentle breezes and the songs of skylarks, breathed instead a chilling reality from the lips that warmth and color had abandoned. "Oh, I have suffered a terrible night," they whispered. "This morning I am worse. I have tried to rise, but I cannot. Strange dreams trouble me."

The watcher approached the patient's pillow and beheld a new, strange molding of familiar features. Instantly, he sensed that the unbearable hour drew near; he knew it was God's will that his idol be broken. Bowing his head, he subdued his soul to the sentence he could not avert and scarcely endure.

How happy Mrs. Pryor was! Still kneeling in prayer, unaware that the summer sun hung high above the hills, her child softly awakened within her arms. There was no pitiful, unconscious moaning—that sound so draining of strength that even a sworn vow of firmness dissolves into a rush of unquenchable tears—before her waking. Nor was there any interval of deafening apathy. The first words spoken were not those of one drifting away from this world, already permitted to wander into realms foreign to the living. Caroline clearly remembered with lucid precision what had transpired.

"Mamma, I have slept *so* well. I only dreamed and woke twice."

Mrs. Pryor rose with a start, concealing the joyful tears summoned to her eyes by that affectionate word "mamma," and the welcome reassurance that followed.

For many days, the mother dared to rejoice only with trembling. That initial revival seemed like the flicker of a dying lamp. If the flame streamed upward brightly for a moment, the next instant it sank dim within the socket. Exhaustion swiftly followed excitement.

There was always a tender endeavor to *appear* better, yet too often ability refused to second the will; too frequently the attempt to hold oneself together collapsed. The efforts to eat, to speak, to maintain cheerfulness proved unsuccessful. Many an hour passed wherein Mrs. Pryor feared the chords of life could never again be strengthened, though the timing of their breaking might be deferred.

During this season, the mother and daughter seemed left almost entirely alone in the neighborhood. It was late August; the weather was fine—or rather, exceedingly dry and dusty, for an arid wind had blown from the east for over a month. Very cloudless, too, though a pale haze, stationary in the atmosphere, seemed to steal all depth of tone from the blue of heaven, all freshness from the verdure of earth, and all glory from the light of day. Almost every family in Briarfield was absent on an excursion. Miss Keeldar and her friends were at the seaside; so was Mrs. Yorke's household. Mr. Hall and Louis Moore, between whom a spontaneous intimacy had seemingly arisen—likely the result of a harmony of views and temperament—had gone "up north" on a pedestrian journey to the Lakes. Even Hortense, who would gladly have stayed behind to aid Mrs. Pryor in nursing Caroline, had been so earnestly entreated by Miss Mann to accompany her once more to Wormwood Wells, hoping to alleviate sufferings greatly aggravated by the unhealthy weather, that she felt compelled to comply. Indeed, it was not in her nature to refuse a request that appealed so directly to her goodness of heart and, through a confession of dependency, flattered her *amour propre*. As for Robert, from Birmingham he had traveled onward to London, where he still sojourned.

As long as the breath of Asiatic deserts parched Caroline's lips and fevered her veins, her physical convalescence could not keep pace with her returning mental tranquility. But there came a day when the wind ceased to sob against the eastern gable of the rectory and the oriel window of the church. A small cloud, shaped like a man's hand, arose in the west; gusts from the same quarter drove it forward and spread it wide; wet and tempest prevailed for a while. When that storm passed, the sun broke forth genially, heaven regained its azure, and earth its green; the livid cholera-tint had vanished from the face of nature; the hills rose clear around the horizon, absolved from that pale malaria-haze.

Caroline's youth could now serve her, as could her mother's nurturing. Both, crowned by God's blessing, sent in the pure west wind blowing soft as fresh through the ever-open chamber lattice, rekindled her long-languishing energies. Finally, Mrs. Pryor saw that it was permitted to hope: a genuine, material convalescence had begun. It was not merely Caroline's smile that was brighter, or her spirits that were cheered, but a certain look had passed from her face and eye—a look dread and indescribable, yet easily recalled by those who have watched the couch of dangerous disease. Long before the emaciated outlines of her aspect began to fill out, or her departed color returned, a more subtle transformation occurred; all grew softer and warmer. Instead of a marble mask and glassy eye, Mrs. Pryor saw laid upon the pillow a face pale and wasted enough, perhaps more haggard than the previous appearance, but far less awful; for it was a sick, living girl, not a mere white mold or rigid piece of statuary.

Now, too, she was not constantly petitioning to drink. The words, "I am *so* thirsty," ceased to be her complaint. Sometimes, after swallowing a morsel, she would remark that it had revived her. All descriptions of food were no longer equally distasteful; she could be induced, occasionally, to indicate a preference. With what trembling pleasure and anxious care did not her nurse prepare what was selected! How intently she watched as Caroline partook of it!

Nourishment brought strength. She could sit up. Then she longed to breathe the fresh air, to revisit her flowers, to see how the fruit had ripened. Her uncle, always liberal, had purchased a garden-chair for her express use. He carried her down in his own arms and placed her in it himself, while William Farren stood ready to wheel her around the walks, to show her what he had done among her plants, and to take her directions for further work.

William and she found plenty to discuss. They shared a dozen topics—interesting to them, unimportant to the rest of the world. They held a similar interest in animals, birds, insects, and plants; they maintained similar doctrines regarding humanity's relation to the lower creation, and possessed a similar turn for minute observation on points of natural history. The nest and proceedings of certain ground-bees, which had burrowed in the turf beneath an old cherry-tree, became one subject of fascination; the haunts of certain hedge-sparrows, and the welfare of certain pearly eggs and callow fledglings, another.

Had *Chambers's Journal* existed in those days, it would certainly have formed Miss Helstone's and Farren's favorite periodical. She would have subscribed for it, and to him each number would duly have been lent; both would have put implicit faith in and found great savor in its marvellous anecdotes of animal sagacity.

This is a digression, but it suffices to explain why Caroline would have no other hand than William's to guide her chair, and why his society and conversation sufficed to give interest to her garden-airings.

Mrs. Pryor, walking nearby, marveled at how her daughter could be so at ease with a "man of the people." *She* found it impossible to speak to him otherwise than stiffly. She felt as if a vast gulf lay between her caste and his, and that to cross it or meet him halfway would be to degrade herself. She gently asked Caroline, "Are you not afraid, my dear, to converse with that person so unreservedly? He may presume, and become troublesomely garrulous."

"William presume, mamma? You don't know him. He never presumes. He is altogether too proud and sensitive to do so. William has very fine feelings."

And Mrs. Pryor smiled skeptically at the naive notion of that rough-handed, rough-headed, fustian-clad clown possessing "fine feelings."

Farren, for his part, showed Mrs. Pryor only a very sulky brow. He knew when he was misjudged and was apt to turn unmanageable with those who failed to give him his due.

The evening restored Caroline entirely to her mother, and Mrs. Pryor relished the evening; for then, alone with her daughter, no human shadow came between her and what she loved. During the day, she maintained her stiff demeanor and cool moments, as was her wont. Between her and Mr. Helstone, a very respectful but most rigorously ceremonious intercourse was kept up. Anything resembling familiarity would have bred contempt instantly in either party; but by dint of strict civility and well-maintained distance, they managed to proceed very smoothly.

Toward the servants, Mrs. Pryor's bearing was not uncourteous, but shy, freezing, ungenial.
