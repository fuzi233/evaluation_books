No one in the pensionnat doubted M. Paul Emanuel’s hatred of interruption. It was not a dislike merely, but an irritability so fine-drawn that the scrape of a chair, the click of a latch, the accidental drift of a skirt across the threshold, went to him like grit between the teeth. To cross his class while he taught was accounted a trial of courage; to cross it five times in one morning, as Rosine the portress had done, was to tempt Providence with French slippers for armour.

That morning I sat in the carré, with an embroidery-frame on my lap—one of those pieces a pupil begins with ardour, then abandons when the patient sameness of stitches refuses to flatter her vanity. My fingers kept the obedient rhythm of silk through linen, and my ears, less dutiful, took their pleasure from the voice next door. It rose and fell, sharpened and broadened, like a kitchen fire that suddenly catches a new draught: quiet coals one moment, a flare and crackle the next. There was a stout wall between me and the storm; and, besides, the glass-door to the court stood ready, a clean escape like a back-stair. I confess I listened with more curiosity than alarm.

Poor Rosine had no such refuge. Four times already she had been compelled to dart in, snatch a girl from beneath his very hand—“Mademoiselle, au piano!”—and vanish again as if the floor were hot. Each venture left her paler, her mouth emptier of words, her eyes fuller of the kind of fear that lives in servants who must obey two masters. When, for the fifth time, she approached, she came as one walks to the chopping-block.

“Mon Dieu! Mon Dieu!” she whispered at my door. “Que vais-je devenir? Monsieur va me tuer, je suis sûre; car il est d’une colère!”

She flung open the class-door.

“Mademoiselle La Malle au piano!”

Before she could retreat and pull it to, his voice shot out—hard, metallic, like the clang of a pan dropped in a silent scullery.

“Dès ce moment!—la classe est défendue. La première qui ouvrira cette porte, ou passera par cette division, sera pendue—fut-ce Madame Beck elle-même!”

The decree hung in the corridor like smoke after a burnt sauce: acrid, lingering, and impossible to pretend away. Ten minutes had scarcely passed when Rosine’s slippers shuffled again—only now the sound of them had the desperate soft haste of someone carrying boiling water.

“Mademoiselle,” she said, coming to my side as if my presence could shield her, “I would not for a five-franc piece go into that classe again just now. Monsieur’s lunettes are really terrible; and here is a commissionaire come with a message from the Athénée. I have told Madame Beck I dare not deliver it, and she says I am to charge you with it.”

“Me?” I returned, half amused, half affronted. “That is not in my duty. Come, Rosine—carry your own burden. Be brave—charge once more!”

“I, Mademoiselle? impossible! Five times I have crossed him this day. Madame must really hire a gendarme for this service. Ouf! Je n’en puis plus!”

“Bah! You are only a coward. What is the message?”

“Precisely of the kind with which Monsieur least likes to be pestered: an urgent summons to go directly to the Athénée—there is an official visitor—inspector—I know not what—arrived, and Monsieur must meet him. You know how he hates a must.”

Yes; I knew. Whatever tasted of constraint—an order, an obligation, a summons—was to him what a harsh, factory-throb is to a listening ear: a mechanical, imposed noise, insulting to his own chosen cadence. He would resist, not always from malice, but from that proud need to keep his soul’s footing on ground he had named his own.

Still, he must go. His duty, his interest, and Madame Beck’s quiet calculation all pointed one way. I took the message from Rosine, not without fear; yet in that fear there was also a stirring curiosity—like leaning over a pot to see whether the first bubbles have begun.

I opened the door, entered, and closed it behind me with what silence my unsteady hand could manage; for in that room, the manner of a fault could be worse than the fault itself. To rattle the latch, to hesitate, to leave a gap of light—those were provocations.

There he sat, bent above his desk upon the estrade, teaching arithmetic. Figures—those dry grains—never agreed with him; they parched his temper as stale bread parches the throat. The pupils’ heads were bent like stems under hail. He did not even raise his face at my entrance; to look up and acknowledge a breach of his law was an effort his anger refused him. It served me well. I walked the length of the room; and I have always found it easier to meet wrath close, where one can see its edge, than to endure it at a distance, where it looms and gathers.

I stopped at the estrade, directly before him. He continued his lesson as if I were air.

As I was not quite tall enough to see his countenance over the desk, I leaned and peeped round. At first I meant only to observe him: he had struck me, at my entrance, with a certain dark, fierce picturesqueness—like a black tiger in ill-humour. Twice I looked and withdrew, unchallenged. The third time, the instant my eye cleared the desk’s shadow, it was caught—pinned through the very pupil—by the “lunettes.” Rosine had spoken truth: those glasses had a blank, unmoving terror in them, more appalling than the living play of his naked eyes.

But proximity was my ally. The short-sighted “lunettes” could not well inspect a culprit under his nose. He took them off, and thus, oddly, we came nearer equality.

I was glad I did not truly fear him. When, in grim literal jest, he demanded cord and gibbet to hang the offender he had threatened, I could answer with my own weapon: I slipped from my work a needleful of thread and handed it round the corner of the desk with such civil readiness that it disarmed something in his surplus ire. I did not do it for the room to see; I merely attached the noosed thread to the barred back of his chair, as one might silently set in place a jesting garnish on a stern dish.

“Que me voulez-vous?” he said at last, in a growl that seemed to grind within his throat, his teeth clenched as though he had sworn no smile should escape.

“Monsieur,” I replied, “je veux l’impossible, des choses inouïes;” and then, without any tender preparation, I gave him the message, brisk and urgent, heightening its necessity—as one shakes a reluctant man by the shoulders with words.

He would not hear of it. He would not go; he would not leave his class; let all the officials of Villette send. He would not stir at the bidding of king, cabinet, and chambers together.

I knew, however, that his revolt was only his first seasoning—bitter, sharp, inevitable. In the end he must comply. So I stood silent, as though he had not spoken, and waited.

He demanded, “What more do you want?”

“Only Monsieur’s answer, to deliver to the commissionaire.”

He waved an impatient negative.

Then I made a bold movement. On the window-sill lay his bonnet-grec, in grim repose; I stretched my hand towards it. He followed my hand with a look that held both amazement and disdain.

“Ah!” he muttered, “if it came to that—if Miss Lucy meddled with my bonnet-grec—she might just put it on herself, turn garçon for the occasion, and benevolently go to the Athénée in my stead.”

With much respect, I laid the bonnet on the desk. Its tassel seemed to nod at me—solemn, admonitory, like a blackcap worn by some ancient judge.

“I’ll write a note of apology—that will do!” he said, still bent on evasion.

Knowing it would not do, I pushed the bonnet gently towards his hand. It slid down the smooth varnish of the desk—pushing before it the steel-framed “lunettes”—and, terrible to confess, those glasses went over the edge and fell upon the estrade.

Often had I seen them fall and survive. This time, by some spiteful turn of chance—my chance—it was different. When I picked them up, each clear pebble of glass was cracked into shivered stars.

Dismay seized me; but beneath dismay there was regret more real. I knew the value of those “lunettes.” His sight was peculiar; he called them his treasures. Holding them, ruined, I trembled, as if I had spilt something precious and irrecoverable—not wine, but the last of a rare oil one has guarded for years.

For some seconds I could not look at him. He spoke first.

“Là!” said he. “Me voilà veuf de mes lunettes! Now Mademoiselle Lucy will confess the cord and gallows are earned; she trembles in anticipation of her doom. Ah, traitress! traitress! You are resolved to have me quite blind and helpless in your hands!”

I lifted my eyes. Instead of fury, his face overflowed with a smile—coloured with a warm bloom I had once seen at the Hôtel Crécy. He was not angry—not even truly grieved. Under the real injury he showed clemency; under the real provocation, patience. And this mishap, which I had thought would ruin my errand, became my best assistance. While no harm had been done, he had been unmanageable; as soon as I stood before him a conscious offender, he grew graciously pliant, as if contrition were a seasoning he could not resist.

Still railing gently—“une forte femme… une Anglaise terrible… une petite casse-tout”—he declared he dared not disobey one who could work such havoc; it was like the Grand Emperor smashing the vase to inspire dismay. So he crowned himself with his bonnet-grec, took the broken “lunettes” from my hand with a clasp that pardoned and encouraged, bowed, and went off to the Athénée in excellent humour.

After such amiability, it is hard to hear that before night we quarrelled again; yet it happened, and I could not help it.

It was his custom sometimes—an admirable custom, too—to arrive of an evening without warning, burst upon the silent hour of study, and set up a sudden despotism over our tranquillity. Books were put away; work-bags brought out. He drew forth a thick volume or a handful of pamphlets, and, instead of the sleepy “lecture pieuse” drawled by a pupil, he poured into our conventual stillness some tragedy read grandly, acted with a fiery voice—some drama used as a vessel for his native passion, filled to the brim like a cup of potent brew. Or he would flash us a glimpse of current literature: a tale that had charmed Paris, a feuilleton that had waked laughter in salons—always pruning with a severe hand whatever could be deemed unfit for “jeunes filles.” I had noticed he could improvise whole paragraphs where he cut—clean, vigorous, irreproachable—better often than what he removed. It was a kind of translation, not from language to language, but from one moral lexicon to another; and he did it as a man does kneading—pressing, folding, shaping—until the coarse grain becomes fit for the oven.

That evening we sat quiet as nuns in retreat: pupils bent over tasks, teachers occupied with work. Mine was a small piece of fancy-work which, unusually, interested me; it was not mere time-killing. I meant it as a gift; the day of giving was near; my fingers hastened, drawing silk through with a little eager friction, as if the stitch itself could shorten the waiting.

We heard the sharp bell-peal we all knew; then the rapid step each ear could name. “Voilà Monsieur!” passed round in one breath; and the double door did not open—it split, as it always did for him, for “open” is too slow a word for his motion. He stood among us.

Two long study tables stretched under hanging lamps. Teachers sat at the centres; girls lined both sides—eldest nearest the lamp’s warmth, idlers and little ones pushed towards the dimmer ends. M. Paul’s habit was to hand a chair to some teacher—usually Zélie St. Pierre, the senior mistress—take her place, and thus secure the full lamp-light, which his near-sightedness demanded.

As usual, Zélie rose quickly, smiling with all her teeth—a smile that ran from ear to ear like a thin cut, lighting neither cheek nor eye. Either he did not see her, or chose not to. His new “lunettes” could excuse any oversight, and he used them. He passed Zélie, came straight to my side, and before I could rise, whispered, “Ne bougez pas,” and established himself between me and Miss Fanshawe—Ginevra—who would always contrive to be my neighbour, elbowing my ribs with her small, restless vanity, keeping herself “warm,” as she said, on winter evenings, till I sometimes protected myself with an artful pin at my girdle.

“Ne bougez pas” was easily commanded; it was less easily obeyed. I must make him room; I must ask the pupils to draw back that I might move. I swept aside my working things to clear space for his book, and edged away a little—not more than a yard, the allowance any reasonable man might accept. But M. Paul was never reasonable. Flint and tinder: the least friction sparked him.

“Vous ne voulez pas de moi pour voisin,” he growled. “Vous vous donnez des airs de caste; vous me traitez en paria.” He scowled. “Soit! je vais arranger la chose!”

“Levez-vous toutes, Mesdemoiselles!” he cried.

The girls rose. He drove them all to the other table, like a shepherd with a sharp stick. Then he placed me at one extremity of the long bench; brought me my work-basket, silk, scissors—every implement set in order; and fixed himself at the other end, a whole stretch away.

It was absurd; yet no one dared laugh. A giggle would have been a dropped plate in a tense kitchen—heard, noted, punished. As for me, I took it coolly. Isolated, cut off, I sat and minded my work, quiet, not unhappy. There was, indeed, a snug composure in it—new to me—as though his restless nature drew all fever out of the room like a magnet, leaving me only a calm that let the needle travel.

“Est-ce assez de distance?” he demanded.

“Monsieur en est l’arbitre,” I answered.

“Vous savez bien que non. C’est vous qui avez créé ce vide immense: moi je n’y ai pas mis la main.”

And with that he began to read.

For his misfortune, he had chosen a French translation of what he called “un drame de Williams Shackspire”—“le faux dieu,” he added, “de ces sots païens, les Anglais.” Had his temper been sweeter, perhaps his verdict would have been different; but to-night he spoke as a man speaks of a dish served ill: not content to say it is bad, he must insult the cook and the country besides.

The translation was indeed poor—thin where it should have been rich, pale where it should have bled. It had the wrong salt. It tasted to my mind like broth made from bones long boiled and strained twice: the nourishment gone, only a suggestion remaining. I said nothing; but the muscles about the eyes and mouth will sometimes betray what the tongue is forbidden to utter. His “lunettes” gleaned every stray look; I do not think they missed one. Soon he discarded them that his own eyes might blaze more freely; and though he sat at the cold north end to which he had exiled himself, he grew hotter there than reason would have allowed beneath the lamp’s very tropic.

When he had finished, it was doubtful whether he would depart in silence or let his anger escape. Silence was not his habit; yet what had I done, openly, to merit reproof? I had not spoken.

Supper was brought: bread, and milk diluted with tepid water—poor fare, softened and thinned, like many things in that house. In consideration of his presence, the rolls and glasses stood untouched until he spoke.

“Take your supper, ladies,” he said, busying himself with marginal notes to “Williams Shackspire.” They ate. I took my roll and glass too; but my work had seized me more strongly than hunger. I stayed in my seat of punishment and stitched while I chewed, sipping the warm, weakened milk, my whole manner easy—almost serenely domestic. The room had its evening sounds: the low talk at table, the clink of glass, the soft tearing of bread, the distant hush of the house settling. Beneath those human noises lay, as always, the heavier city—wheels, a far shout, the iron pulse of Villette. I felt, as I often did, that peace depends on what the ear must bear; and here, strangely, M. Paul’s presence—so rasping, so quick to spark—seemed to swallow up the jarring influences and leave me a pocket of quiet in which my needle could sing.

He rose and turned to the door. I thought he would go without another word.

He returned—perhaps only for the pencil-case left on the table. He took it, clicked the pencil in and out, broke its point against the wood, re-cut it, pocketed it—and then walked straight to me.

At the other table, teachers and girls talked freely; at meals they always did, their voices habitually fast and loud. The sound made a kind of crowded canopy under the lamp—busy, human, heedless. He came and stood behind me, and asked what I was making.

“A watchguard,” I said.

“For whom?”

“For a gentleman—one of my friends.”

He stooped; and then, close to my ear, he spoke in that low, cutting way which, in him, seemed less like speech than like a sharp whisper of steel.

Of all the women he knew, I was the one who could make herself the most consummately unpleasant. With me it was least possible to live on friendly terms. I had a “caractère intraitable,” perverse to a miracle. Whoever approached me with pacific intentions—crac!—I turned concord to discord, good-will to enmity. He, M. Paul, wished me well enough. He had never harmed me knowingly. He might claim to be at least a neutral acquaintance—guiltless of hostile sentiment; and yet—how I behaved to him! What pungent vivacities, what an impetus of mutiny, what a “fougue” of injustice!

At that I could not help widening my eyes a little, slipping in a small protest, half incredulous.

“Vivacities? Impetus? Fougue? I didn’t know—”

“Chut! à l’instant! There! there I went—vive comme la poudre!” He was sorry—very sorry. For my sake he grieved over this peculiarity. This “emportement,” this “chaleur”—generous, perhaps, but excessive—would yet do me mischief. It was a pity: I was not, he believed, wholly without good qualities. If only I would hear reason; be more sedate, more sober; less “en l’air,” less “coquette,” less taken by show; less prone to value outside excellence—to make much of attentions from people remarkable chiefly for so many feet of stature, “des couleurs de poupée,” “un nez plus ou moins bien fait,” and an enormous stock of fatuity—then I might yet prove useful, perhaps exemplary. But as it was—and here his voice caught, as though some feeling had stuck in his throat like a crumb that will not go down.

I might have looked up, might have held out my hand, might have offered one soft word to settle him; but I feared if I moved I should either laugh or cry, so strangely did the touching mingle with the absurd.

I thought him nearly done. He was not. He sat down, as if to speak more at ease—like a man settling to carve and serve, unwilling to leave his dish unfinished.

While he was on these painful topics, he said, he would dare my anger for my good, and refer to a change he had noticed in my dress. When he first knew me—when he used only to catch a passing glimpse from time to time—I satisfied him. The gravity, the austere simplicity, inspired the highest hopes. What fatal influence had lately impelled me to put flowers under my bonnet-brim, to wear “des cols brodés,” and even, on one occasion, to appear in a scarlet gown—he could conjecture, but would not yet openly declare.

This time I interrupted with a sharp indignation, and a sort of honest horror.

“Scarlet, Monsieur Paul? It was not scarlet. It was pink—and pale pink too—and further subdued by black lace.”

“Pink or scarlet, yellow or crimson, pea-green or sky-blue—it is all one. These are flaunting, giddy colours. And the lace—_that_ is but a colifichet de plus.” He sighed over my degeneracy. He could not be as particular as he wished; not possessing the exact names of these “babioles,” he might fall into small verbal errors which would give my sarcasm an opening and excite my unhappily sudden disposition. He would merely say, in general terms—and he knew these terms were correct—that my costume had lately assumed “des façons mondaines,” and it wounded him to see it.

What “façons mondaines” he discovered in my winter merino and plain white collar I could not tell; and when I asked, he said it was all too studied for effect—and besides, had I not a bow of ribbon at my neck?

“And if you condemn a bow of ribbon for a lady, Monsieur,” I said, holding up the little chainlet of silk and gold bright in the lamplight as if it were a thread of sunshine spun into cord, “you would necessarily disapprove of a thing like this for a gentleman?”

His only answer was a groan—whether for my levity, or for some deeper vexation, I could not then decide.

He sat silent a few minutes, watching the progress of the chain as I worked more assiduously than ever—stitch after stitch drawn tight, each loop made fast as memory is made fast when one binds it to an object meant to be worn close and daily. Then he asked, more low than before, whether what he had said would have the effect of making me entirely detest him.
