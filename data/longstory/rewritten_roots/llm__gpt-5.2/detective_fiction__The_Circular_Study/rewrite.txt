Mr. Gryce had grown tired in a way that did not show in the stiffness of his shoulders so much as in the flatness of everything that reached his senses. The city’s autumn had turned to a long gray exhalation—coal smoke in the throat, damp grit underfoot, and, always, that hard modern racket that worried the ear without ever satisfying it: drays clattering, elevated trains gnashing above the streets like iron teeth, street cries sharpened into commerce. Even inside Headquarters the noise seemed to follow him, thinned but persistent, as if the building itself had learned to hum with the same indifferent industry as the shops outside.

He had been thinking—more often than he cared to admit—of the little farm in Westchester he had bought with a man’s last prudent hopes. In his mind it smelled of turned earth and cider apples, and it sounded the way a life ought to sound: wind through hedges, the honest rasp of a hoe, a kettle beginning to sing. Yet the thought did not cheer him. Action had been his daily bread; without it, he grew hungry and old.

So when the telephone bell cut through the room with its sharp, insistent ring, it was like a clean knife through stale air. A message from Carter’s drug store—Carter’s, with its varnished counters and the medicinal sweetness of licorice and camphor—had come in to say that a lady had sent a boy off the street with news for the police: a strange crime in the old mansion round the corner.

Strange.

That word lodged in Mr. Gryce’s mind the way an unfamiliar spice does on the tongue—something not at all unpleasant, but unmistakably present, and demanding to be named. He forgot Westchester. He forgot his years. He felt, for the first time in weeks, the return of appetite.

Men about him began speaking of the house itself, as if its name were a dish everyone had once tasted and half-remembered. One of the few landmarks left from the preceding century, it stood squeezed between newer structures—shops, club-houses, restaurants—pressing against it on either side like loud diners at a quiet table. Its brick front and brownstone trimmings had held their composure for decades while the neighborhood’s soundscape thickened with profit. And now—crime in that house.

Mr. Gryce caught the inspector’s eye. The order to investigate came without flourish. He took it as he always had, like a tool handed across a bench.

He went first to Carter’s.

The drug store met him with its familiar mingling of odors: alcohol, peppermint, soap, and the faint dusty perfume of patent powders. At the back, near the telephone clerk, waited the boy—thin, watchful, and trying to look careless while fear made his hands too busy. Mr. Gryce took him along at once toward the mansion.

As they walked, the city’s sounds shifted around them, some falling away, others rising: a street organ grinding out a tune that had once belonged to another language of streets, now dulled by repetition; a cook’s voice from an open basement window calling for parsley; the tinny laughter of men outside a restaurant where art had been reduced to signage and slogans. Mr. Gryce listened without meaning to. A man who has spent his life reading faces learns, late in the game, to read the world’s other expressions too—its silences, its false notes.

He made the boy talk.

There was little to add beyond what had already been telephoned. The child insisted it was a lady—not a “woman,” not the sort who would bargain over pennies at a stall. He had been staring at toys in a window when she came up, close enough for him to see the shine of her clothes and the cold flare of jewels. She put money in his hand—enough to make him swallow—and drew him along the street to Carter’s as if she had every right to steer his small life for a moment.

Inside the drug store she showed him another coin and promised it if he would run to the telephone clerk with a message for the police. When he grabbed at it, she said all he had to do was tell the clerk that a strange crime had been committed in the old house on ---- Street. The word “crime” scared him; he started to slide away. Then she caught him again and shook him—hard enough to rattle his thoughts into place—until he did what she wanted and ran.

He spoke with the plain candor of a child who has been bought and frightened in the same breath. Mr. Gryce believed him. But when he asked for a description of the lady, the boy’s attention failed where need demanded precision. “A stunner,” he said, with a poor man’s vocabulary for splendor. “Shiny clothes. Jewels.”

That was all.

They were nearing the mansion now, and Mr. Gryce let the questioning drop. He gave his whole mind to the building ahead.

Nothing on the outside confessed to trouble. The door was shut; the stoop was clean; the windows were heavily curtained, some further dulled by closely drawn shades. The brick face held its calm against the bustle that crowded it—the neighboring buildings alive with signs, glass, and the constant busy hum of trade. It was a quiet front in a loud street, a place that seemed to insist on its own older grammar.

“Some mistake,” Mr. Gryce muttered, because the stillness was so complete it felt deliberate.

Then movement stirred in the area under the stoop. An officer stepped out, his expression a mix of confusion and restraint.

“Anything wrong?” Gryce asked, sharp. “Any blood shed? All seems quiet here.”

The man touched his hat. “Can’t get in. Rang all the bells. Would think the house empty, only I saw something like a stir in one of the windows overhead. Shall I try the rear yard through one of the lower windows of Knapp & Co.’s store next door?”

“Yes,” Gryce said at once. “Take this boy with you. Lock him up in one of their offices, then break your way in from the back. It ought to be easy enough from the yard.”

The officer nodded, took the boy by the arm, and disappeared into the adjoining store with him.

Mr. Gryce remained in the area, and the street, as streets will, began to gather itself into a crowd. Passers-by slowed, then stopped, curiosity swelling like a smell at the first hint of smoke. Questions came at him—what was it, who lived there, was it murder?—and he had nothing to feed them. The city always wanted a story at once; it did not care whether the telling trampled the truth.

The door opened from within before the crowd could press him harder. That simple fact—wood yielding, hinges speaking softly—changed everything. He slipped inside and let the door close behind him, shutting out the street’s coarse chorus.

The quiet struck him almost physically.

He found himself in a basement hall leading toward a kitchen. Even in that first glance he registered its order: surfaces wiped, utensils put away, nothing left to sour or crust. A kitchen that had been kept like a museum display rather than lived in—no steam in the air, no onion-skin smell in the corners, no bread cooling, no recent fire-talk from pots. Domesticity without warmth. Routine without the human mess of it.

He saw no sign of struggle, no shattered dish, no spilled broth, no broken chair. If tragedy had stepped into this house, it had done so without upsetting the commonplace objects meant to sustain life.

Dissatisfied with the emptiness of the lower rooms, he followed the officer upstairs when the man returned by whatever rear entry he had found. Their feet made minimal sound on the old stair, as if the wood itself wished to keep secrets.

As they reached the parlor floor, the officer spoke, eager to add context as men do when faced with something they cannot interpret.

“Mr. Raffner of the firm next door says the man here is an odd sort. Nobody knows him. A bookworm, they call him. He’s been here six months. They’ve never seen anyone about the place but him and a strange old servant—just as peculiar and close as his master.”

“I know,” Mr. Gryce murmured.

He did. Everyone who paid any attention to the city’s quieter mysteries knew of Mr. Adams: personally attractive, wealthy, and so uncongenial by temperament that he seemed to have built his life to avoid the necessity of being understood. A man who lived as if language itself were a hostile country—keeping to his own dialect of silence, refusing the easy words that made other men neighbors.

To enter such a house was an event. To enter it on an errand of life and death—Mr. Gryce felt, in the thin blood of his age, the old quickening. There are men who retire because the world grows dull; there are others to whom the world only grows dull when it stops demanding their best attention.

The hall they stepped into was wide and old-fashioned, furnished sparsely in the ancient style a house like this insisted upon. The place carried its past the way a well-kept pantry carries its dried herbs—silent, but potent, and ready to release an aroma when disturbed. Two doors stood open into the hall.

They went into the nearest, almost opposite the top of the staircase. The room beyond was bare as a doctor’s outer office: nothing to touch, nothing to soften sound, no hint of personal habit. They would have left at once if not for the suggestion, beyond a half-drawn portière, of richer things—color, texture, the promise of purpose.

Mr. Gryce moved forward and put his hand through the folds.

The next room stopped them cold.

It was small and circular—an odd choice in a city that liked its straight lines and practical corners—and it seemed made to gather sound and hold it. The street’s din could not find its way in; even their breathing felt magnified, the hush around it like a held note. Strange tapestries hung on the walls, their threads old and darkly lustrous, broken here and there by curios that had the look of priceless objects taken far from their native meanings and pinned here like exotic specimens. Books ran in rows around the lower portion of the room, as if the place were a study and a shrine at once.

And yet none of those things drew the eye.

It was still daylight, but the room was lit by a jet of rose-colored light—a concentrated, unnatural glow that fell not on the shelves or the great picture that might have commanded attention on any other day, but on a man lying on a bearskin rug.

He lay on his back with a dagger in his heart.

On his breast rested a cross, and its golden lines stood out sharply against a long, dark garment that wrapped him in folds. In that rose light he looked, for an instant, like a saint arranged for burial in some holy place—until the dagger denied sanctity and proclaimed violence. His face was upturned, fixed in an anguish Mr. Gryce could not readily translate into any common emotion. It did not speak plainly of fear, or pain, or surprise. It was something else—something private, perhaps unnameable, as if the last thing that had crossed his mind belonged to a language Mr. Gryce had never learned.

Mr. Gryce and the officer stood there, appalled, while the room held its breath around them and the rose-colored light continued to burn, steady as a signal no one outside could hear.

Mr. Gryce stood a heartbeat too long on the threshold, held there by a hush so complete it seemed manufactured—an airless quiet, like a kitchen after the ovens have gone cold and every pot has been scrubbed back into its place. The study’s dim glow, its tapestries and clustered curios, the roundness of the room closing in like a kept secret—everything conspired to make the body on the floor look less like a man felled in violence and more like an offering arranged with care.

Then the habit of his trade took him by the shoulders and pushed him forward.

He crossed to the master of the house as if approaching a table set for inspection, and though the stillness of the face told its own story, he knelt and verified it with touch: fingers at the pulse, eyes studied close. Warmth lingered in the flesh, but life did not. The upturned gaze—fixed in a reproach too human to ignore—forced him to draw up all his practiced composure, as a man might brace himself against the sudden sour sting of smoke in a room that should smell only of wax and old leather.

The dagger he did not pull. He examined it where it stood, sunk home. It was no common blade: an oriental curio, the sort of ornate purchase men made to prove they had been places they’d only read about. That detail flirted with suicide, and yet the angle of entry and the wound’s position refused to cooperate with the idea. It had the wrong logic. Bodies, like recipes, reveal when a hand has cheated.

He looked for the usual mess—the scuffed carpet, the overturned chair, the broken ornament that tells you a struggle happened—but the apartment held itself in a discipline almost domestic. Beyond a few rose leaves scattered as if dropped from a bouquet carried too quickly, nothing was disturbed. The circular chamber wore its luxury as neatly as a butler’s gloves.

The large central table, close by the place where the man had been struck down, should have borne the shock. Instead, its crowded surface seemed curated rather than ransacked: pipes laid as if their stems had been aligned by a fastidious thumb; pens; small portraits; weights and measures; Roman lamps; Venetian glass; rare porcelains; medals; rough metalwork; manuscript; a scroll of music; and a pot of growing flowers that gave off a faint green, damp smell—earth in a room otherwise all old fabric and metal and polish.

Oddest of all, set in a row like the buttons of a modern stove, were electric switches.

Mr. Gryce, curious in spite of himself, touched one. The lamp above—caged in fretted ironwork—shifted at once from its red ember-glow to a greenish glare that turned the dead man’s skin the color of spoiled milk and bruised herbs. The sudden tint made the whole place look sick. Mr. Gryce pressed another button quickly. A mild white radiance returned, and with it his own breath.

“Childish tricks,” he thought, and yet the sharply cut mouth and purposeful chin of the victim contradicted the word. Nothing about that face suggested frivolity. The shifting light, the electric contrivance, belonged to a mind that played with effects the way a chemist plays with flame—precise, not playful.

He forced his attention away from the body and onto the walls. Tapestries, curios, the bric-a-brac of a collector with money and time—none of it spoke plainly until one small violence did: a nail bent down and wrenched from its place.

The cross that ought to have hung there now lay on the dead man’s breast, its cord still attached, red threads tangled with the darker scarlet seeping from the wound. The distance between the empty place on the wall and the body was too great. No man with that injury could have gone there, torn the symbol down, and returned to arrange it on his own heart.

Another hand had done it.

Hope, remorse, mockery—Mr. Gryce could not yet afford to choose which. Time, in such rooms, was always the first thing to die.

Behind the table, half-way up the wall, hung the only large painting: the portrait of a young girl, beautiful in the grave, wistful manner of the years at the end of the Civil War. Her hair and dress fixed her era as surely as any date, and in her face—fair where the man on the floor was dark—there lived the same fine, haunting intelligence. A kinship suggested itself, the sort that persisted in bone and gaze even when everything else had changed.

Below her portrait were mounted a sword, a pair of epaulettes, and a valor medal. Mementos. Evidence, perhaps, of a life that had once been simple enough to measure in ranks and ribbons.

He moved on and nearly missed a narrow curtain, so dark and so close in pattern to the surrounding hangings that it hid like a secret seam in a well-made coat. It was not a window; the windows in this room were high, nearly under the ceiling, as if meant to admit light without admitting the world. This drapery covered an opening.

He pushed it aside.

A narrow closet-room lay beyond: a bachelor’s cot made with tight, spare precision; a dresser that spoke of luxury and exact habits; and a small table. Everything was in order—too in order—except for a silver-backed comb missing from the dresser’s arrangement. His eye found it almost at once, lying on the floor at the far end, as if knocked down in haste.

And there, near the door, on a small stand: a pearl-handled parasol.

A woman had been here, and not long ago. The air held a faint, sweetened trace—powder and something floral, like crushed petals on warm skin—subtle but distinct against the room’s older smells of cloth and wood and metal.

Mr. Gryce turned back toward the study, and in the threshold his gaze caught a small black sparkle. He bent and picked it up. A sequin. Another lay beyond, and another still, each one fallen like a dropped seed, marking a path from the little room toward the central table. He knew that kind of trim: spangles strung on a thread that, once broken, surrendered its glitter one by one. Vanity made an excellent informant.

The delicacy of the clue—bright, feminine, ridiculous in the shadow of murder—brought a brief, strange smile to his mouth. A trap made out of ornament.

He followed the trail back, trying to locate the snag that had started the loss. Doorway? Furniture? The chairs were cushioned, their edges soft. Nothing sharp. Then his eyes went to the cross. At its base was gold fretwork, fine as filigree—exactly the sort of thing a sequined gown might catch on if a woman, in haste or fury, tore it from its nail.

He went to the place where the cross had hung and searched. Nothing there. But when he reached the bearskin rug beside the body, his fingers found one more sequin caught among the long hairs.

So she had stooped here. Over him. Near him. After him.

He returned to the central table with a patience that had been honed on smaller puzzles. This time he examined it thoroughly, not merely as a collector’s display but as a work surface—where hands had been, where they had hesitated, where they had placed and lifted. Near the inkstand he drew his finger across a spot and looked at the faint residue that clung—something slick, not dust. The smallest smear that told of contact, of pressure, of something set down and moved again.

He lifted the inkstand, careful as a cook lifting a lid to check a simmer without losing heat, and replaced it in precisely the same spot. If he expected to find anything beneath, his face gave nothing away. He stood there, thinking hard, while the white light burned above with a steady, unemotional honesty.

A quick step behind him made him turn.

Styles had returned from his sweep of the house and stood with the rigid eagerness of a man who had discovered something he didn’t know how to name. He didn’t speak at first; he pointed toward the staircase visible through the antechamber door.

Mr. Gryce’s glance flicked once around the room—at the body, the cross, the fallen rose leaves, the glittering trail—reluctant to abandon a scene that felt poised to confess if given one more minute.

Styles’ restraint broke. “Come,” he whispered, excitement pressing the word. “I’ve lighted on the guilty party. He’s in a room upstairs.”

“He?” Mr. Gryce repeated, the pronoun pricking him.

“Yes. There can be no doubt. When you see him—” Styles’ eyes widened. “But what is that? Is he coming down? I’m sure there’s nobody else in the house. Don’t you hear footsteps, sir?”

Mr. Gryce nodded. Someone was descending. The sound came measured but strained, as if the person were counting steps without meaning to. With it came breath—quick, labored—slipping through the doorway like steam in cold air.

“Let us retreat,” Styles murmured. “Not because he’s dangerous, but because you must see him before he sees you. He’s a strange-acting man. If he comes in here, he’ll do something to incriminate himself. Where can we hide?”

Mr. Gryce drew him toward the little closet-room and pulled the curtain back into place, leaving a narrow slit—just enough to watch. They waited, the study’s silence now filled with the soft, inevitable percussion of feet on stairs.

The portière at the study entrance lifted.

A man stepped through.

Mr. Gryce and Styles stared.

Was this the murderer Styles had promised? It was a pale, lean servitor, hollow-cheeked and neat, carrying a tray with a single glass of water set upon it—an offering as harmless as hospitality. The sight was so at odds with Styles’ certainty that Mr. Gryce turned a questioning look toward his colleague. Styles, startled but stubborn, pressed for patience with a tight gesture.

The servant advanced. He stopped. His eyes fell on the figure on the floor.

The glass slipped from the tray and shattered with a sound that seemed shockingly loud in the room’s long-held quiet. The servant gave a low cry that warped abruptly into something animal, a howl scraped raw from the throat.

“Look at him,” Styles breathed, urgent. “Watch what he will do now. You will see a murderer at work.”

And then the man changed.

As if some inner gear had caught and begun to turn, he launched into a feverish pantomime—so vivid, so charged with passion, that it made the air feel crowded. He stood staring at empty space, his expression twisted as if he saw a person there. One hand stretched out behind him in a cramped, unnatural way, he plunged the other toward the table, snatching at nothing—then clenching his fist as though around a handle.

He made a quick side-thrust into the empty air, fierce and precise. The invisible target seemed to recoil simply from the force of his intent. He held the pose a fraction, then slowly opened his hand as if releasing an imaginary dagger. His body tottered backward; his eyes widened into horror at what he had “done.” Remorse—terror—something like both—took him apart piece by piece until his knees buckled.

Lower and lower he sank, that one arm still stretched as if in accusation or self-defense, until he collapsed in an inert heap on the floor, echoing the dead man’s posture in a dreadful parody.

“It is what I saw him do upstairs,” Styles whispered into Mr. Gryce’s ear. “He has been driven insane by his own act.”

Mr. Gryce did not answer. In all his years of weighing evidence and reading the residues people left behind—like flour on cuffs, like grease on fingertips—he had never been handed a mystery that behaved like this: a room arranged, a symbol torn down and placed with care, a woman’s glittering trail, and now a servant acting out murder against a ghost, as if the very air had learned the crime and was teaching it back.

The man who had so lately flung himself into that grotesque pantomime of murder and grief straightened as if he had been wrenched awake from a fever. The fury that had made his face a mask a moment before drained out of it, leaving only a blanched, bewildered vacancy. He stooped, fingers groping along the carpet, found the fallen glass, and with the obedient drift of habit began to carry it from the room—as quietly as if the air did not still taste of ozone from the lamp’s contrivance, as if the study did not hold, in its warm, close breath, the heavy animal reek of bearskin and the colder, sharper tang that clings to sudden death.

“Wait, man!” Mr. Gryce’s voice snapped across the hush. “You needn’t take that glass away. We first want to hear how your master comes to be lying here dead.”

A summons like that would have struck any servant to the marrow. This one did not even start. He moved on, unheeding, until Styles stepped in and clamped a hand on his shoulder.

“Stop!” Styles demanded. “What do you mean by sliding off like this? Don’t you hear the gentleman speaking to you?”

The touch told where words had not. The glass slipped from the man’s hand again; this time it fell hard, struck the floor, and broke with a bright, brittle clink that seemed indecently cheerful in that chamber. The sound was answered at once by a noise from the man himself—not quite a cry, not quite a groan, but a strangled, torn note between them, as if pain and alarm had found no settled language in his throat. He had seen the men who barred his way; his body folded in upon itself, shoulders caving, head drawing down, the whole figure cringing as if it had learned terror by long instruction.

He wrenched once toward escape. Styles tightened his grip and dragged him back, step by unwilling step, to the bearskin rug where the master lay.

At the sight of that outstretched form a second transformation ran over the servant like a gust through a flame. His eyes, which had been wild with fear, kindled with a light so quick and so ugly that it seemed to stain the air around him. The corners of his mouth twisted; and then, with no effort to hide it, he let out a low laugh—thin, thrilling, irreverent—so out of place that it echoed up into the high spaces above the windows like blasphemy spoken in a church.

Mr. Gryce waited until the last thread of that laughter had died among the room’s recesses. Something in him recoiled, not merely from the sound but from the satisfaction behind it. Then he fixed the man with a stare as steady as it was rare with him, and said, with the hard patience of one who will have facts though all decencies fail:

“There now. Speak. Who killed this man? You were in the house with him, and should know.”

The servant’s lips opened. What came out was not speech but a rope of harsh gutturals, broken and eager, meaningless to any ear trained in English. While he made them, he jabbed two fingers toward his ears, then his mouth, shaking his head with violent insistence—as if to fling the very notion of answering away from him.

There was only one reading: he could not hear; he could not speak.

Styles’s grasp slackened in sheer shock. His hand dropped away. Freed, the old servant slid back to his earlier place and, with an unnerving fidelity, began again—more vivid now, more urgent—the same dumb re-enactment: the killing, the sudden collapse, the show of mourning. When he had finished, he stood waiting, eyes restless and bright, with that under-glint of infernal joy still alive in them, making his presence beside the dead seem not only improper but profaning.

Styles, goaded past restraint, shouted at him, the words flung like stones. “Can’t you speak? Can’t you hear?”

The man answered with a smile—slow, gloating, and wholly without shame.

Mr. Gryce cut it short. “Take him away. Examine him carefully for blood marks. I am going up to the room where you saw him first. He is too nearly linked to this crime not to carry some trace of it away with him.”

They searched the old servant as one searches a man who has handled raw meat—palms, cuffs, the creases where stains hide. Nothing. Not a smear, not a rusty print, not so much as a darkened nail. Above, in the rooms where he had first been found, the detective’s practiced eye met only orderly surfaces and the stubborn absence of the one thing he wanted: a sign that would bind this remaining inmate of the house to what had happened in the study.

For once, method met blankness. Mr. Gryce returned, thoughtful, and began again that slow, merciless re-examination of the two rooms which, to his mind, held all the clues the crime intended to yield. He moved as if measuring a recipe that had failed—back to the same ingredients, the same implements, the same heat—wondering what had been altered in secret. The queer lamp’s apparatus, the curtained windows, the table where his hand had found the hidden buttons; the rug’s coarse nap underfoot; the stale warmth of the closed room; even the outside world’s muffled murmur pressing at the walls like a crowd at a kitchen door, hungry for whatever scandal was being cooked within.

He was bending again over the dead man’s upturned face—over the fixed mouth and the expression that looked less like peace than like suffering held in a vice—when a sound cleaved the air above him.

Not the street’s impatient bell. Not Styles’s boots. A cry from the dim height near the windows; two words formed with startling clearness and flung down into the room like a command:

“Remember Evelyn!”

Mr. Gryce straightened as if struck. Remember Evelyn. Who was Evelyn? And who, in this house already searched, could have found breath enough to call to them from overhead?

It seemed to come from the roof itself. And then he saw it.

Near the top of one of the tall windows hung a cage, swung high—nearly level with the curtain-rod—where the light pooled thinly. Inside, an English starling clung to its perch, neck craned, feathers sleeked down as it watched. When Mr. Gryce’s eyes lifted to it, the bird seemed to recognize attention as its cue. It opened its beak and shrieked again, harsher, more insistent, as if the phrase had been drilled into it until it carried the weight of a prophecy:

“Remember Evelyn!”

The room had already given him enough unnerving touches—this mute mirth, this color-shifting lamp, this crime staged in silence—and yet the bird’s human syllables were the last, uncanny garnish, like a wrong spice in a familiar broth: impossible to ignore once tasted.

With an odd, oppressive sense of nightmare, Mr. Gryce leaned forward on the study table to get a better view of the cage. His hand, seeking balance, found—without his noticing at first—the neat little boss of a button. At once the light that had been clear and white since his last contact with the electrical device slid into green, bathing the rug, the walls, the dead face, and even the starling’s cage in a sickly tint, as if the room were being shown through bottle-glass.

Annoyance pricked him. The shifting hues were a problem in themselves, but not one he could afford at this moment. He drew back from the table, turned toward the door, and would have left the study when Styles’s voice rose from the antechamber where he kept watch over the old butler.

“Shall I let him go, Mr. Gryce? He seems very uneasy; not dangerous, you know, but anxious—as if he had forgotten something, or recalled some unfulfilled duty.”

“Yes, let him go,” Mr. Gryce answered instantly. “Only watch and follow him. Every movement he makes is of interest. Unconsciously he may be giving us invaluable clews.”

He stepped toward the curtains to see for himself what the man would do.

Above, the starling screamed once more as he passed between the heavy folds: “Remember Evelyn!”

Irresistibly Mr. Gryce glanced back and up. To whom was this appeal directed with such sharp persistence—himself, whose ears were open, or the servant below, whose ears were forever shut? The phrase might be nothing more than a household trick, a parlor marvel kept like any other ornament. Or it might be a name at the root of the whole tragedy, forced into the only mouth in the house that could still produce sound.

He could not pause to decide. The old butler, released, had hurried down the hall. Now he returned from the front door with the soft, swift noiselessness of long training, carrying in one hand his master’s hat, in the other his master’s umbrella. His face and bearing had resumed that respectful immobility a servant wears like a livery. He came straight to Mr. Gryce, offered the articles, and when they were taken, retreated at once—quiet as a shadow—to his seat in the antechamber. There he sank down again, and almost immediately the dazed vacancy returned, as if whatever had driven him to that small errand had spent itself.

Mr. Gryce replaced Mr. Adams’s hat and umbrella upon the hall rack with a displeased deliberation. “Humph,” his thoughts ran, bitter with the check to his hopes. “Mind quite lost, memory uncertain, testimony valueless.” Then, with the relentless inward turn that made him dangerous: Had the tragedy shattered the man into this state? Or had this broken, irresponsible condition been the forerunner—the very cause—of the deed?

He might have pursued that question into some workable conclusion if, at that moment, the fitful clanging of the front-door bell—ringing off and on with the crowd’s impatience—had not been broken by a different sound: an authoritative knock, measured and final, that silenced all other appeals. The coroner, or someone bearing equal weight of office, had arrived.

Mr. Gryce felt the shift at once. The solitary hour in which a detective can move freely through a house of secrets had ended; the world, with its procedures and interruptions, was at the door.

Mr. Gryce had walked into stranger houses than this one and taken stranger statements than Jake the errand boy’s; but never, in all the long years his mind had moved by habit along the grooves of crime, had he felt himself so hampered at the start.

It was not only that the dead man—Adams—had lived like a hermit with no household but a man-of-all-work who could not speak, and, worse, could not hear. It was not only that the house sat in a quarter where “neighbor” was an empty word and the street itself seemed built to keep curiosity at bay. It was the silence of the world around the victim. The news had gone abroad; the coroner had come and gone; officials, doctors, and the ink-stained swarm of reporters had tramped through the hall and out again—yet no friend, no kinsman, no anxious creditor, no woman with red eyes and a story to pour out, had appeared at the door. For a man who had been, for months, a marked figure in this part of town, it was an absence that rang louder than any knock.

Near midnight the last of the outsiders filed away, their footsteps thinning into the street until the house seemed to draw its breath in a long, stale exhale. Mr. Gryce, who had been holding himself to the case by sheer will, felt the smallest easing of the shoulders. There were two points he meant to settle before he allowed himself even the pretence of rest. The first lay in the keeping of a dozing boy in the hall.

Jake sat in a chair like a dropped bundle, cap tilted, hands lax in his lap. Mr. Gryce stopped before him and spoke with that quiet authority which did not need volume to be obeyed.

“Come, Jake. Time to look lively. You’re going with me to the exact place where that lady ran across you to-day.”

The boy started, blinking, searching with clumsy fingers for his cap as if it might have rolled away like a coin. “I’d like to see my mother first,” he pleaded, the sleep still thick in his voice. “She must be done up about me. I never stayed away so long before.”

“Your mother knows where you are. I sent her word hours ago.” Mr. Gryce watched the boy’s face change under that assurance. “She gave a good report of you, Jake. Says you’re obedient, and you never told her a falsehood.”

“She’s a good mother,” Jake said at once, with the fierce loyalty of the very young. “I’d be as bad—as bad as my father was—if I didn’t treat her well.” His fingers found the cap; he crammed it on as if it were a helmet. “I’m ready.”

They went out. The city at that hour was a patchwork: stretches of deadened pavement where the lamps seemed to burn without purpose, and sudden veins of activity where late carts rattled and men moved with the restless hunger of those who work when others sleep. Mr. Gryce led Jake into one of these livelier thoroughfares and halted before a shop window gone dark for the night.

Inside the glass, two stuffed frogs—ridiculous, theatrical things—were posed with tiny swords in a perpetual duel. Jake pointed at them as if this absurd combat were a landmark on a map.

“Right there,” he said. “I was looking at them when she come.”

Mr. Gryce’s eyes stayed more on Jake than on the frogs. “Was she a pretty lady?” he asked, as if beauty might be a clue and not merely a lure.

Jake scratched his head, his brow furrowing with honest uncertainty. “She made me a good deal afraid of her,” he admitted. “But her clothes—oh, gorgeous!” There his confidence returned; finery was a language he could read. “And she was—”

“Young?” Mr. Gryce put in quickly. “Carried flowers? Seemed troubled—anxious and trembling?”

Jake shook his head, thrown off by the picture the detective was trying to paint onto his memory. “Not young like that, sir. And I didn’t notice no flowers. But… I think as she was what you might call troubled. She wasn’t crying. When she spoke to me, she put more feeling into her grip than into her voice.” He rubbed his arm as if the pressure still lived there. “She just dragged me to the drug-store. If she hadn’t give me money first, I’d have wriggled away, I would. But I likes money, sir; I don’t get too much of it.”

Mr. Gryce moved on, his mind turning that single phrase—more feeling in the grip than in the voice—over and over like a seed between thumb and forefinger. “Not young,” he muttered, mostly to himself. “Some old flame, then, of Mr. Adams. They’re apt to be dangerous—more dangerous than the young ones.”

They stopped before the drug-store, its odor of camphor and soap faintly seeping even through closed doors. Mr. Gryce turned.

“Show me where she stood while you went in.”

Jake pointed to a spot on the pavement, near the curbstone. His drowsiness had been scoured away by the importance of being useful. “There.”

“And was she there when you came out?”

“Oh, no, sir. She went away while I was inside.”

“Did you see her go? Can you tell me whether she went up street or down?”

Jake’s gaze flicked to the street as if he could replay it there. “I had one eye on her, sir. I was afraid she’d come into the shop after me, and my arm was too sore to want her to clinch me again. So when she started to go, I took a step nearer and seen her move toward the curbstone and hold up her hand.” He hesitated, trying to please. “But it wasn’t a car she was after. None come by for several minutes.”

The crease between Mr. Gryce’s eyes softened. “Then it was some cabman—or hack-driver—she hailed. Were there any empty coaches about?”

Jake could only shake his head. His observations had run to the edge of what he could honestly claim. Mr. Gryce saw it and did not punish the boy for being simply a boy.

He turned Jake over to one of his men stationed near by, with a few low instructions, and set his own course back to the house that seemed to swallow sound. The street grew emptier as he went, the air cooling, the city’s late noises thinning until even a distant wheel on stone sounded like an intrusion.

At the door Styles admitted him at once.

“Any one waiting for me?” Mr. Gryce asked.

“Yes, sir. A young man. Name, Hines. Says he’s an electrician.”

“That’s the man I want. Where is he?”

“In the parlor, sir.”

“Good. I’ll see him. But don’t let any one else in. Anybody upstairs?”

“No, sir. All gone. Shall I go up or stay here?”

“You’d better go up. I’ll look after the door.”

Styles nodded and went toward the stairs, his boots sounding briefly and then swallowed by the house.

In the parlor a dapper young man rose promptly—clean collar, quick eye, a look of competence worn like a badge.

“You sent for me,” he said.

Mr. Gryce asked his few questions with the economy of one who did not waste words on preliminaries. Satisfied, he led Hines into Adams’s study—the room where the tragedy had been discovered and where the dead man’s peculiar genius, if genius it was, had arranged itself in wires and hidden buttons like nerves in flesh.

A candle, set by order near the door, gave a mild, domestic light—one small flame trying to soften the severity of the place. Mr. Gryce went straight to the central table and laid his hand on a button embedded there as if it belonged among inkstands and papers.

“Blow out the candle,” he told Hines.

Hines did so, and for a beat the room became pure night, the kind that presses close and makes a man suddenly aware of his own breathing.

Then Mr. Gryce pressed the button.

A deep violet illumination burst through the study—an uncanny bloom, as if the air itself had been steeped in some dark berry dye and set alight. The walls, the table, the built-up window places—everything swam in that color. Hines turned sharply, startled into silence, and Mr. Gryce, though he had expected an effect, watched with the same hard attention he gave any new fact.

“That is but one slide,” he remarked. “Now—” He pressed another. “Pink, as you see. This one produces green. This one white.” Another touch and the room sickened into a bilious yellow that made skin look sallow and shadows look bruised. “Not becoming to either of us, I am sure. Now examine the connections. See if there is anything peculiar.”

Hines bent to his work, fingers deft, his eyes following the runs of wire and the amateurish joins. He found—after the careful, reluctant time an expert gives to disproving his own first impressions—nothing beyond the obvious: the whole contrivance had been planned without modern skill, and yet it functioned with exasperating success.

“It’s amateur work,” Hines said at last, straightening. “But it works. That’s the oddest part.”

“He made it himself?” Mr. Gryce asked.

“Undoubtedly. Or someone equally behind the times in wiring.”

Mr. Gryce’s gaze went to the shelf of books that sat like silent tutors against the wall. “Look at those. Is there enough in them for a man to rig up such an arrangement?”

Hines merely glanced, as if reading titles were enough. “A man with a deft hand and a scientific turn might—by the aid of these—do all you see here and more. The aptitude is all.”

“Then I’m afraid Mr. Adams had the aptitude,” Mr. Gryce returned, dryly. There was disappointment in him—not because the dead man had been clever, but because cleverness of that sort complicated motives and widened the possible roads out of the crime.

He moved, and his attention shifted to the other curiosities the room held. “A man with a turn for contrivances often wastes time and money on useless toys fit only for children,” he said, with a glance at the shifting colors still ready beneath his hand. “Look at that bird cage.”

High up, beyond easy reach, a cage hung where no sensible owner would have placed it if he intended to clean it or feed its occupant. Yet there was a lively movement inside. Mr. Gryce continued, almost as if speaking to keep his thoughts from scattering. “Perched like that, it must owe its usefulness to some means of being raised and lowered. Where is the contrivance? Can you find it?”

Hines searched, first confidently, then with the slight irritation of a professional confronted with a trick. At last his fingers found a hidden button amid the tapestry. He pressed it. Something disengaged with a muted click, and the cage descended slowly, obedient as a servant.

“We will not send this poor bird aloft again,” Mr. Gryce said. He detached the cage and held it for a moment, weighing it, studying the creature within. “An English starling is none too common here. Hark—he is going to speak.”

The bird flicked and turned, bright-eyed, sharp as a thought, then—whether warned by the detective’s steady gaze or by some instinct that recognized danger—fell suddenly quiet and tucked itself into a doze, real or feigned.

Mr. Gryce set the cage on the floor.

Perhaps idly, perhaps because he could not resist testing every concealed nerve of this room, he pressed another button on the table—one he had not yet touched—and looked for a new color to wash over them.

The bilious yellow remained. The recent handling had likely jarred some connection. He shrugged, turning away, when his body stopped of its own accord.

The opening into the antechamber—the only passage back to the rest of the house—was moving.

Not swinging like a door, but sliding. From a yard-wide gap it narrowed to a foot. From a foot to an inch. From an inch—

Hines let out a short laugh, more startled than amused. “Well, that’s certainly the contrivance of a lazy man. Seated here, he could close his door at will—no shouting for a deaf servant, no stumbling over rugs. I don’t know but I approve of it, only—” His voice changed as he took in the surface. “The slide’s a curious construction. Not wood, as any sensible door ought to be, but of—”

“Steel,” Mr. Gryce finished, and there was something in his tone that made the word feel colder than the metal. He stepped close, palm on the smooth plate. “This is the strangest thing yet. It begins to look as if Mr. Adams was daft on electrical contrivances.”

“And as if we were prisoners here,” Hines added, the laugh gone now. He ran his hand along the edges. “I do not see any means for drawing this slide back.”

“Oh, there’s another button for that, of course,” Mr. Gryce said lightly, as if refusing to lend the situation his fear.

They searched. Five minutes, then more. Fingers probed tapestry, woodwork, the table edges, the places where an inventor might hide a secret with childish delight. Nothing responded. The steel plate stood flush, seamless in its purpose.

“If you don’t object,” Mr. Gryce said at last, “I will turn a more cheerful light upon the scene. Yellow does not fit the occasion.”

“Give us rose,” Hines said, with a tightness that could not be disguised by briskness, “for unless you have someone on the other side of this steel, we seem likely to remain here till morning.”

“There is a man upstairs whom we may perhaps make hear,” Mr. Gryce returned, and the faint irony in the word hear did not escape either of them. “But what does this contrivance portend? It has a serious look to me, when you consider that every window in these two rooms has been built up almost under the roof.”

“Yes,” Hines admitted, and his breath made a small sound in the yellow light. “A very strange look. But before engaging in its consideration I should like a breath of fresh air. I cannot do anything while in confinement. My brain won’t work.”

Mr. Gryce did not answer at once. His hand traced the steel. It had been made—at cost, and with care—to follow the curve of the walls through which it passed. The fit was too exact, too deliberate, for a whim. The thought that formed in him was not comforting, and he kept it under his tongue.

“Mr. Adams carried his taste for the mechanical to great extremes,” he said finally. “This slide is carefully fitted, and if I am not mistaken it will stand some battering before we are released.”

“I wish his interest had led him to attach something as simple as a bell,” Hines muttered, half to himself.

“True. We have come across no bell.”

“It would have smacked too much of the ordinary to please him.”

“Besides,” Mr. Gryce said, “his only servant was deaf.”

Hines’s gaze fell on an alpenstock leaning near by, silver-mounted, the sort of thing an idle man might buy for the look of it. “Try a blow,” he suggested. “A quick blow with that. Someone should hear.”

“I will try my whistle first,” Mr. Gryce said. “It will be better understood.”

He whistled—sharp, practiced, a sound meant to slice through distance and door. Then, when that failed to raise an answering stir, he struck the steel with the staff. The blow rang oddly, not like wood but like a dull gong muffled by walls. He struck again and again, spacing the knocks with a patience that hid his growing certainty: this house had been built to keep sound in or out as the master wished, and the man who could not hear had been the perfect companion for such a taste.

Time thickened. The yellow light made minutes feel ill. Hines paced once, then stopped, listening to the house that offered no reply. Mr. Gryce whistled again until his lips felt raw with the dryness of the sealed air.

It was an hour before Styles, somewhere above, was made to notice—by vibration, by chance, by some passing step halted at the right moment. And when at last the alarm traveled to hands that could act on it, it was five hours more before an opening could be effected large enough to admit of their escape; so firmly was that barrier of steel fixed across the sole outlet from Mr. Adams’s remarkable room.

The aftertaste of that morning’s strangeness clung to Mr. Gryce as stubbornly as smoke in a winter overcoat. It had all the qualities he disliked in a case: too much silence, too much artifice, too many little contrivances meant to turn a man’s mind away from the plain human motives that usually lay, like a bone on a plate, in the center of the table.

At Headquarters, where men had been glancing at the clock and then at one another, his first question was put with the impatience of one who refuses to be soothed.

“Bartow—has he come to himself?”

The answer came back like a door shutting.

“No change, sir. The specialist says it’s not favorable. Not immediate.”

Mr. Gryce’s mouth drew into a line. He motioned the arresting officer closer.

“How did he take it—when you detained him?”

“Surprised, sir. Shook his head, made queer gestures, then went into that pantomime of his. It’s a spectacle. Poor fool keeps holding his hand back—so.”

The officer demonstrated: the right hand drawn behind him, not quite clenched, not quite open, as if the palm carried a message he could not show.

Mr. Gryce’s eyes narrowed. He had seen that same half-hidden flourish before—at the instant Bartow realized he was being watched. A gesture meant to point and yet to conceal; to proclaim innocence and yet to refuse the one thing that would prove it.

“Does he mope? Fear? Anger?”

“It shifts, sir. One minute he looks ready to fall asleep on his feet. The next he’s up—furious—shaking his head, pounding the wall.”

“Watch him every moment. Note everything. His testimony may be worthless, but his body is talking whether he knows it or not. To-morrow I’ll see him myself.”

When the officer went out, the room settled into the dry hush of paper and ink. Mr. Gryce drew from his pocket a small packet and spilled its contents upon his desk.

Five spangles.

Not coins, not quite sequins in the theatrical sense, but those hard little disks that catch light and throw it back with a cold, elegant glitter—blackened, yes, yet still capable of flashing as if each carried a trapped spark. They looked absurdly domestic on the green blotter, like the dropped decorations from some fashionable bodice brought too close to a candle.

He had a habit—an old man’s habit—of fixing his eyes on something minute while he let his thoughts make their slow, inward circuit. The office around him could clatter and bustle; the object would hold steady, anchoring the mind as surely as a cutting board steadies the knife. Today the five spangles served that purpose. He studied their pinholes, their edges, the faint abrasion where thread had worried them loose. They offered no words, no address, no name—only the stubborn fact of taste and expense.

At last he gathered them back into their envelope with a decisive sweep, glanced at his watch, and went into the outer room where a line of waiting men stood with that restless stillness peculiar to policemen who have been made to wait.

He beckoned one—young, plain-featured, eager-eyed—whose attention had followed his movements too closely to escape the old detective’s notice. He led him back into the private room and shut the door.

“You want a hand in this matter?” he asked.

The young man flushed, not with vanity but with that honest heat of ambition which makes even an unremarkable face briefly compelling.

“Oh, sir—I wouldn’t presume—”

“Enough. You’ve been here six months and had no real test. Now you’ll have one. Are you up to it, Sweetwater? Do you know this city well enough to find a needle in it?”

“I’d like the chance,” Sweetwater said, and the words came out quick, like a boy volunteering to run an errand in a storm. “If I succeed, it will mean more than if I’d grown up on these streets.”

Mr. Gryce’s gaze remained cool. “You’ll have difficulty enough. I’m asking what’s near impossible. We need a woman—of whom we know almost nothing—except that she wore, when last seen, a dress heavily bespangled with black, and that she carried a parasol. I can show it to you before you go, or—”

“Describe it,” Sweetwater cut in, suddenly cautious. “If I fail I’d rather not have the fellows joking that I was sent out with a lady’s frill in my hand. Let me work quietly.”

Mr. Gryce’s mouth twitched—half amusement, half approval. “Very well. A dainty, nonsensical thing—gray chiffon, pearl handle, pink bows. Looks unused. A woman doesn’t mislay that unless her mind is in a whirl. No name on it.”

“Nor maker’s mark?”

“No. That would have been too kind.”

“And the hour this lady was seen—at Hudson’s fruit store, Fourteenth Street?”

“Half-past four. The moment the telephone message came.”

Sweetwater nodded, filing it away. “All right, sir. When do you want to see me?”

“When you have something worth telling. Wait—one more thing. I suspect her first name may be Evelyn.”

Sweetwater’s eyes brightened, but he only said, “I understand, sir,” and was gone as if the city itself had called him.

Left alone, Mr. Gryce did not share the young man’s lightness. The whole affair had the feel of a room carefully sealed—windows stopped, doors plated, even sound muffled—so that the ordinary methods of entry were thwarted and one was forced to crawl in through some crack no one had noticed.

He looked, instead, to the one dependable ally in modern crime: publicity. The papers had already thrown the news of Adams’s death across the country like seed. Wealth and culture attract acquaintances the way a well-set table draws neighbors who have long pretended not to be hungry. Surely someone would come forward—family, friend, claimant—someone to give a name to the strange dead man who had lived like a hermit behind his contrivances.

But the day went by with its hours as bare as an unbuttered plate.

A banker appeared, careful, polished, and unhelpful beyond the simple fact that the accounts were in good order. A lawyer came, equally neat, equally thin of revelations: no known kin; no disclosed history. Even the landlord—summoned, questioned, led through the house where his tenant had died—could offer only the outline of a man who paid on the day, to the hour, and never invited familiarity.

He spoke of first being approached by a Philadelphia lawyer now dead, who had vouched for Adams’s respectability and solvency. He admitted he had been struck, on first meeting, by the tenant’s ascetic look and his restrained intelligence. “A bookworm,” he called him, with the faint resentment landlords feel toward tenants who keep their rent in order but their souls private.

In the death-room the landlord stared at the huge steel plate blocking the doorway as if it were a meat safe bolted where no meat should be. He looked up at the high windows through which only a few reluctant rays found their way.

“These were filled in at Mr. Adams’s request,” he said. “Originally they ran down to the wainscoting.”

He was shown the added lath and plaster, the thick barrier, the entire costly piece of engineering intended to shut the room like a jar. He could not explain it. The lamp with its varying lights astonished him too; he’d never been received in this room, only in the parlor, where nothing advertised the peculiarities of the house beyond a certain sterility of comfort.

The starling—noisy, impertinent witness that it was—was not there to be seen; it had been taken away for Mr. Gryce’s private hearing at Headquarters.

Other lines of inquiry dried up as well. No clue to the owner of the insignia found on the wall could be teased from pension offices or Grand Army posts. The name of the painter of the portrait on the wall rang no bell among New York artists; it was as if the portrait, like the dead man, had been made in a place where names did not carry.

Every drawer, every pigeonhole, every hiding place in the study had been turned out. No will. No business papers. Nothing with a signature that might pin him to a past. Adams had lived as though he wished to leave no crumbs behind him—no trail a hungry world could follow.

Late afternoon brought in the tradesmen’s accounts, and if they could not provide lineage they could at least offer habits. Their testimony had a dull, repeated rhythm: the servant came, the servant gestured, the servant bought the best and would not be cheated. The master was never seen among the counters. Bartow, deaf and dumb, had made his purchases with a fluency of motion that kept mistakes rare, as if his hands and eyes had developed their own dialect.

Yet more than one clerk said the man had changed in the last few weeks—less methodical, more quick to anger, like someone carrying a grievance under his apron, held down by force of will. Mr. Kelly, the grocer, put it bluntly: “He looked like a man wanting to bite.”

None of that was proof. A nervous servant, penned in a silent house, might well grow irritable without murder in his heart. Still, with the master dead and the servant’s connection unexplained, memory sharpened into accusation. Men recalled looks they had once dismissed as odd and now called malevolent.

Mr. Gryce was about to concede the day had yielded nothing but noise when Sweetwater reappeared.

The young man’s face had that tightened brightness of one who has run hard and come upon something—either treasure or trouble. Mr. Gryce’s own fatigue lifted.

“What!” he cried. “You haven’t found her?”

Sweetwater’s smile flickered. “Don’t ask me yet, sir. I came to see if there’s any reason I shouldn’t have the loan of that parasol—about an hour. I’ll return it. I want to make a test.”

“What test?”

“Excuse me, sir. Respectable business houses shut early, and this must be tried in a respectable house.”

Mr. Gryce, who disliked secrecy but respected speed, rose. “Then you shall have it. Wait.”

He brought the parasol—a thing too light and foolish to have caused so heavy a disturbance. Gray chiffon stretched over ribs as delicate as a bird’s bones, the handle pale as a pearl, and little pink bows that looked like the final touch on a cake no man would confess to wanting.

“There. Bring it back soon. I have not the patience I used to have.”

“An hour, sir,” Sweetwater said, already backing toward the door. “Give me an hour, and then—”

The door snapped behind him.

That hour might have been long if it had not been broken—mercifully, suddenly—by Sweetwater’s return in a more excited state than before. He held the parasol out as if it were both proof and apology.

“My test failed,” he said at once. “But the parasol brought me luck anyway. I have found the lady, sir, and—”

His breath caught; he had to take another before he could finish, as if even speaking of her required composure.

“And she is what I said,” Mr. Gryce murmured, half to himself. “A respectable person in a respectable house.”

“Yes, sir. Very respectable. More than I expected. Quite a lady. Not young, but—”

“Her name,” Mr. Gryce broke in. “Is it—Evelyn?”

Sweetwater shook his head, almost boyishly abashed. “I can’t say, sir. I hadn’t the courage to ask. She is here—”

“Here!” Mr. Gryce took a step as if to go at once, then forced himself back, setting his self-control like a lid on boiling water. “I can restrain myself. If she’s here, she won’t leave before I see her. Are you sure it’s the woman we want—the one who was in Adams’s house and sent the warning?”

“Let me tell you how I came to her, sir. Then you’ll judge.”

Mr. Gryce sat again, but his stillness was now the stillness of a dog listening. “Your story, then. You used the clue I gave you?”

Sweetwater’s expression turned rueful. “I’m sorry, sir. I followed my own idea. It would take too long to hunt every hack driver, and I couldn’t be sure she even used a public conveyance.”

“What did you do?”

“I thought of the spangles,” Sweetwater said, and his hands made a small measuring motion, as if he were already handling cloth between finger and thumb. “They weren’t cheap ornaments. That trimming is new in fashion—expensive—sold only in shops where they keep elaborate garnitures. I’ve noticed it in windows. The spangles are sewn on black net in figures—scrolls, wreaths, you might call them. So brilliant that if a stretch is broken—if a few are missing—you’d see the gap at once, especially if the net is worn over a color.”

Mr. Gryce watched him with a patience he rarely granted youthful enthusiasm.

“I said to myself,” Sweetwater went on, “if this woman is respectable—if she’s the kind who would be distressed to have been in a house where a man died—she won’t leave that tear in her dress. She’ll want to restore the missing bit quickly. But where does she get a matching piece? She likely hasn’t spare net at home. She’ll go and buy it—and because she needs only a few spangles, she’ll buy the smallest strip possible.”

Mr. Gryce’s eyes moved, briefly, to the envelope on his desk. Five little black gleams; five crumbs from a costly loaf.

“So I went to the leading shops—Broadway, Twenty-third, Sixth Avenue—and got certain clerks interested. I asked them to watch for any lady buying just a tiny portion of that bespangled net, and to note her if they could. Then I took my stand at Arnold’s.”

“Why Arnold’s?”

Sweetwater spread his hands. “I can’t tell. Luck, maybe. Before the afternoon was half over, one of the men behind the counter gave me a look. I went over and saw him rolling up a small package—handing it to a rosy young girl. ‘For one of our leading customers,’ he whispered. ‘I don’t think she’s your person.’ But I wouldn’t risk it. I followed the girl.”

“And?”

“She went to a fine brownstone in a quiet, aristocratic quarter. Entered by the basement door. I rang the bell above and—well, sir, I had to bite my lip. After such running, such hoping, to be fooled by coincidence—”

“But you weren’t.”

“No, sir.” Sweetwater’s eyes shone. “The maid who answered was the same girl, and she still had that parcel in her hand—like a thread tying the shop to the murder-house. I acted casual. I asked, ‘Has your mistress lost a parasol? One has been found—’ I didn’t even finish, because her face told me enough.”

“What did she say?”

“She said her mistress had lost one. So I said I’d bring it, and I ran down the steps.”

Mr. Gryce made a low sound—impatient approval. “And then?”

“My intention was to come straight to you. But I wanted to be sure. Such a house, sir. Such a respectable servant. I thought: if the mistress recognizes this parasol, then I’ve truly found her. So I begged you for its loan—went back—and rang again.”

“And the maid?”

“She looked at it and took fright, or else I’d made a mistake. ‘That isn’t the one my mistress lost,’ she said. ‘She never carries anything but black.’ And she was closing the door when a voice called from inside—sharp, peremptory: ‘Let me see that parasol. Hold it up, young man. There—at the foot of the stairs. Ah!’”

Sweetwater paused as if he could still hear that single syllable in the air of the hallway, like a spoon tapped once against a glass.

“That ‘ah,’ sir—it told everything. I couldn’t see her at first, but I knew she was leaning over the banisters above. I waited to hear her withdraw, but she didn’t. It was like she was steadying herself for what had to be done. Then she spoke again, and her tone surprised me—dry, controlled, almost—almost as if she were used to command.”

“And?”

“She said: ‘You have come from Police Headquarters.’ I didn’t answer; I didn’t think I needed to. Then she said, ‘From a man called Gryce.’”

Mr. Gryce’s face did not change, but something in his eyes tightened.

“I said,” Sweetwater continued, “‘Since you ask me, yes. Mr. Gryce wished to restore lost property. Is not this parasol yours? Shall I leave it with the maid?’”

“And her reply?”

“‘Mr. Gryce has made a mistake. The parasol is not mine; yet he deserves credit for the use he has made of it in this search. I should like to tell him so. Is he at his office, and do you think I would be received?’”

“You thought she was joking.”

“I did, sir. But she wasn’t. In a moment she came down—very stately, almost severe—already dressed for the street. She asked me, ‘Have you a cab?’ I said no. She told me, ‘Then get one.’”

Sweetwater gave a short, self-mocking laugh. “There was my dilemma: leave her alone and risk her escape—or trust her. She looked honest, sir. Not common-honest, but the sort that comes from being answerable to oneself, not to others. I trusted her. Went for the cab. I promise you, sir, I won’t take such a risk again.”

“But she waited.”

“Yes. She stood on the stoop when I returned—entered the hack—told the driver to come straight here. I saw her just now in the outer office, waiting for you.”

Mr. Gryce rose. For a moment his hand pressed Sweetwater’s with a grip that was oddly warm in so deliberate a man—envy in it, perhaps, and indulgence too, for youth’s luck and youth’s speed.

Then he went out.

He had meant to maintain his composure, to approach the mysterious woman as he approached any witness—quiet, wary, unprovoked. Yet the instant he came face to face with her, his practiced manner altered in spite of himself. Respect remained—he was incapable of rudeness to a lady—but astonishment broke through it, franker than he usually allowed.

For the woman waiting there was no nameless stranger in spangles, no frightened “Evelyn” in gray chiffon and pink bows.

She was known to him—well known; more, almost an old ally in certain bygone matters.

She was Miss Amelia Butterworth of Gramercy Park.

Miss Butterworth met Gryce’s glance as though it were a draught she could not decide to swallow—medicinal, necessary, and bitter on the tongue. He had seen her pleased with herself before, even briskly triumphant; he had never seen her look as if her own name had turned unfamiliar in her mouth.

“Miss Butterworth,” he began, “what does this mean? Have you—”

“There!” she cut in, the single syllable snapped like a broken thread. “You have caught me at my old tricks, and I am accordingly ashamed—and you triumphant. The gray parasol you were good enough to send to my house is not mine. But I was in the room where you picked it up, as you have so cleverly concluded. Since it is useless to fence with your perspicacity, I have come to confess.”

The word confess hung between them with the weight of a kitchen knife laid down too carefully: not abandoned, not surrendered—placed, for the moment, within reach.

“You were there,” Gryce repeated, and drew his chair close, as if by narrowing the space he could narrow the mystery. “And when? I do not presume to ask for what purpose.”

“But I must explain my purpose,” she returned, jaw set with grim domestic resolve, like a woman admitting she has ruined a preserve by daring a new recipe. “Otherwise I should find myself at too great a disadvantage. Not that I like to display weakness; yet I recognize the exigencies of the occasion. You will naturally be surprised to discover that I—Amelia Butterworth—who am a stranger to Mr. Adams and have no neighborly excuse for interference, should so far forget myself as to appear before you in this position.”

She pressed her lips together, then went on, her words coming in measured portions, as if she had weighed each on a scale and found them all too heavy.

“This was no affair that sought me. I sought it, sir. If I had gone to Jericho first, it might have cost me more and taken longer—but it would have spared me humiliation and possible publicity. Mr. Gryce, I never meant to be mixed up with another murder case. I have, I acknowledge, shown aptitude and received—ere now—certain marks of your approval; but my head was not turned by them. At least I thought it was not. I meant to keep to my own metier, and not be allured, by any inducements you might offer, into exercising gifts that may have brought me praise but certainly have not brought me happiness.”

Her voice changed—less formal, more stung—like a palate that has met something scorched.

“But temptation did not come through you, or I might have resisted. It came through a combination of circumstances which found me weak and—yes—unprepared. I was surprised into interest. I am ashamed of it—so ashamed I endeavored to hide my participation. And, thinking I had succeeded, I was congratulating myself, when that parasol was thrust in my face and I understood that you—if no one else—knew I had been in Mr. Adams’s room of death before you.”

She lifted her hands an inch, then dropped them, as though even gesture felt like evidence.

“Yet I thought I had left no traces behind me. Could you have seen—”

“Miss Butterworth,” Gryce said quietly, “you dropped five small spangles from your robe. You wore a dress spangled with black sequins, did you not? Besides, you moved the inkstand—and—well.” His mouth tightened with an honesty that was almost a wince. “I will never put full faith in circumstantial evidence again. I saw those tokens of a woman’s presence; I heard what the boy said of a well-dressed lady who sent him to the drug-store with a message to the police; and I drew the conclusion—yes, I admit it—that this woman had wielded the dagger, not the deaf-and-dumb butler who has borne the blame. I was anxious to find her—little realizing what would come of it, or that I should be obliged to offer my most humble apologies.”

“Do not apologize,” she said with sudden severity, as if apology were a kind of noise. “I had no business there—or at least no business to leave spangles on Mr. Adams’s miserable floor. I was passing the house. Had I been the woman I once was—one who had never dipped into a mystery—I should have continued on. It is a curious sensation,” she added, and for the first time a tremor entered her voice, “to find yourself, however innocent, regarded by a whole city as the cause or motive of a terrible murder—especially when you have spent time, as I have, in the study of crime and the pursuit of criminals. I do not enjoy the experience.”

She drew a breath, as if to steady her thoughts the way one steadies a simmering pot by adjusting the flame.

“But I brought it on myself. If I had not been so—” she halted, bristled. “Not curiosity. I will not own mere curiosity. It was the look on the young man’s face. But I ramble; it is not my habit. Give me time, Mr. Gryce. In a few minutes I shall be coherent.”

“I am giving you time,” he returned with one of his lowest bows. “The half-dozen questions I long to ask have not yet left my lips. I sit here—if you will acknowledge it—a monument of patience.”

“So,” she broke in, seizing on his earlier admission like a cook seizing on a missing ingredient. “You thought the deed perpetrated by an outsider. Most of the journals”—she emphasized this as if newspapers were a sort of cheap seasoning she still tasted—“ascribed the crime to the man you have mentioned. And there seems reason enough. The case is not simple, Mr. Gryce. It has complications. I recognized that at once, and that is why—But I will waste no more moments in apology. You have a right to any little fact I picked up in my unfortunate visit. There is one fact not included in any account of the murder: Mr. Adams had other visitors besides myself in those fatal minutes preceding his death. A young man and a young woman were with him. I saw them come out of the house.”

Gryce’s attention tightened, keen as a blade freshly honed. “Tell your story more simply, Miss Butterworth. What first drew your attention to the house?”

“There!” she exclaimed, not without a flash of offended pride. “That is the second time you have had to remind me. You will not have to do so again. To begin: I noticed the house because I always notice it. I never pass it without giving thought to its ancient history and speculating on its present inmates. Yesterday afternoon, on my way to the art exhibition, I found myself before it and naturally looked up. And—whether providence or mere unfortunate timing—I looked at the precise instant when the inner door of the vestibule burst open.”

Her eyes sharpened, as if the memory itself had edges.

“A young man appeared in the hall carrying a young woman in his arms. He was in a state of intense excitement; she was in a dead faint. Before they attracted notice, he set her on her feet, took her on his arm, and dragged her down the stoop into the crowd, where they disappeared.”

She paused, and the pause was full of street-sounds remembered: hoofs, wheels, voices—an urban roar that smothered the small sharp things that mattered.

“I stood rooted in astonishment. I tried to see which way they went. And I lingered long enough to take a peep into the time-honored interior—which had been left open because the young man forgot to close the door behind him. As I did so, I heard a cry from within. Muffled and remote, but unmistakably terror and anguish. Led by an impulse I may live to regret, I rushed up the stoop and went in—shutting the door behind me lest others should follow.”

She sat very still as she continued, as though her body feared to repeat what her words were forced to repeat.

“Until that moment I acted from instinct. But once in that semi-dark hall, I paused and asked myself what excuse I should give if I encountered any occupant. Then the cry came again—what I am ready to swear was from the farthest room on the parlor floor. I remembered the young man’s wandering eye and drawn countenance. And, despite all feminine instinct for retreat, I went on. Before I knew it I was in the circular study—before a prostrate man who seemed to be dying.”

Gryce did not interrupt; he listened as if listening were a method of search.

“He was lying as you probably found him later: a cross on his breast; a dagger in his heart. Yet his right hand trembled. When I stooped to lift his head, he shuddered—then settled into eternal stillness. I—a stranger from the street—witnessed his last breath, while the young man who had gone out—”

“Can you describe him?” Gryce asked. “Did you see him close enough for recognition?”

“Yes. I think I would know him again. He wore a checked suit—very natty—and was more than usually tall and fine-looking. But his chief peculiarity was his expression.” Her voice lowered, not in delicacy but in unwilling awe. “I never saw on any face—not on the stage at the very crest of tragedy—a greater accumulation of mortal passion struggling with an imperative necessity for restraint. The young girl whose blonde head lay upon his shoulder looked like a saint in the clutch of a demon. She had seen death, but he—” She stopped short, as if her own mouth refused to translate what her eyes had read. “I prefer not to interpret that countenance. It passed from my view almost immediately and likely composed itself in the throng, or we should have heard of him to-day. The girl seemed devoid of almost all feeling. I should not remember her.”

“And was that all?” Gryce asked. “Did you just look at the man and vanish? Did you encounter the butler? Have you any knowledge that settles his innocence or fixes his guilt?”

“I know no more of him than you, except this: he was not in the room when I reached it and did not come into it while I was there. Yet it was his cry that led me—or do you think it was that of the bird I afterward heard shouting in the cage over the dead man’s head?”

“It might have been the bird,” Gryce allowed. “Its call is clear and it seems strangely intelligent. What was it saying while you stood there?”

“Something about Eva. ‘Lovely Eva, maddening Eva! I love Eva! Eva! Eva!’”

“Eva?” Gryce’s brow knit. “Wasn’t it ‘Evelyn? Poor Evelyn?’”

“No. It was Eva. I thought it might mean the girl I had just seen carried out. It was unpleasant—hearing that creature shriek such words above a man dead at my feet.”

“Miss Butterworth,” Gryce said, patient but probing, “you didn’t simply stand over him. You knelt and looked in his face.”

“I acknowledge it—and caught my dress in the filigree of the cross.” Her chin lifted. “I could not stand stock still while a man drew his last breath under my eye.”

“And what else did you do? You went to the table.”

“Yes.”

“And moved the inkstand.”

“Yes—very carefully, sir. Very carefully.”

“Not so carefully,” he returned, “but that I could see where it had been. The square made by its base in the dust did not coincide with its later place.”

“Ah,” she murmured, and there was almost humor in her chagrin. “That comes of your spectacles and my lack of them. I endeavored to set it down precisely.”

“Why take it up at all? What were you looking for?”

“For clews, Mr. Gryce. You must forgive me. I was seeking clews. I moved several things. I hunted for a line of writing which ought to explain this murder.”

“The line of writing?”

“Yes.” She watched him narrowly. “I have not told you what the young girl said as she slipped into the crowd.”

“You have spoken of no words,” he said, suddenly alert in every feature. “Have you such a clew? Miss Butterworth, you are fortunate—very fortunate.”

“I was not to blame for being in the way,” she replied, dignity braced like a well-set crust, “nor could I help hearing. She cried, in what I now remember as affright: ‘You have left that line of writing behind!’ I did not attach much importance then, but when I came upon the dying man—so evidently murdered—I recalled her words and looked about for the writing.”

“And did you find it?” Gryce asked. “I am ready for any revelation.”

“For one that would reflect dishonor on me?” she returned swiftly. “If I had found any paper explaining the tragedy, I should have felt bound to call the police’s attention to it. I did notify them of the crime itself.”

“Yes. And we are obliged,” Gryce said, then pressed the point with unsoftened candor. “But how about your silence regarding the two persons leaving the house immediately upon—or just preceding—the death?”

“I reserved that information,” she admitted, and the admission tasted bitter even to her. “I waited to see if the police would get wind of them without my help. I wished—sincerely—to keep my name out of the inquiry. Yet I feel relief now that I have confessed. I never could have rested properly after seeing so much—and thinking my own thoughts—if I had been compelled to bridle my tongue while false scents were followed and delicate clews overlooked.”

Her gaze held his, and for a moment the two of them seemed to sit within a private chamber of quiet—an acoustic refuge from the city’s incessant grind outside the windows. It was in that hush, more than in her words, that her meaning pressed through: truth needed a clean room to be heard, and noise—whether street-noise or newspaper-noise—spoiled it like soot settling on uncovered food.

“I regard this murder,” she added, “as the most difficult problem that has ever come in my way, and therefore—”

“Yes, madam?”

“I cannot but wonder,” she said, carefully, “if an opportunity has been afforded me for retrieving myself in your eyes. I do not care for any one else’s opinion of my ability or discretion, but I should like you to forget my last despicable failure in Lost Man’s Lane. It is a sore remembrance, Mr. Gryce, which nothing but a fresh success can make me forget.”

“Madam, I understand,” he answered. “You have a theory. You consider the young man with the tell-tale face guilty of Mr. Adams’s death. It is possible. I never thought the butler was rehearsing a crime he committed himself.”

“Do you know who the young man is?”

“Not in the least. You are the first to bring him to my attention.”

“And the young girl with the blonde hair?”

“The first I have heard of her.”

“I did not scatter the rose leaves found on that floor,” Miss Butterworth said, as if setting each fact into place like dishes in a cupboard.

“No. It was she,” Gryce returned. “She probably wore a bouquet in her belt.”

“Nor was that frippery parasol mine,” she continued, “though I did lose a good, stout, serviceable one somewhere that day.”

“It was hers, I have no doubt.”

“Left by her in the little room where she was whiling away the time while the gentlemen conversed—possibly about that bit of writing.”

“Certainly.”

“Her mind was not expectant of evil,” Miss Butterworth said, voice turning oddly intimate with the scene, “for she was smoothing her hair when the shock came—”

“Yes, madam, I follow you.”

“And had to be carried out of the place after—”

“What?”

“After she had placed that cross on Mr. Adams’s breast. That was a woman’s act, Mr. Gryce.”

“I am glad to hear you say so,” he replied, and in his tone was the relief of a man hearing a sound in a wilderness. “The placing of that cross on a layman’s breast was a mystery to me, and is still. Great remorse or great fright only can account for it.”

“You will find many mysteries in this case.”

“As many as I ever encountered.”

“I have to add one.”

“Another?”

“It concerns the old butler.”

“I thought you did not see him.”

“Not in the room where Mr. Adams lay.”

“Ah! Where then?”

“Upstairs. My interest was not confined to the scene of the murder. Wishing to spread the alarm, and not being able to rouse any one below, I crept upstairs and so came upon the poor wretch going through the significant pantomime so vividly described in the papers.”

Gryce’s eyes narrowed. “Unpleasant for you.”

“I fled,” she said simply. “By then I was shaken, and knew only one thing: to escape. But I carried away an enigma. How came I to hear this man’s cries in Mr. Adams’s study and yet find him on the second floor when I searched? He had not time to mount the stairs while I was passing down the hall.”

“It is mistaken impression,” Gryce suggested. “Your ears played you false. The cries came from above, not from the study.”

“My ears are not accustomed to play me tricks,” she replied, and there was steel under the lace. “You must seek another explanation.”

He spread his hands a fraction. “I have ransacked the house. There are no back stairs.”

“If there were, the study does not communicate with them.”

“And you heard his voice in the study?”

“Plainly.”

“Well,” Gryce admitted, “you have given me a poser.”

“And another,” she said, relentless as a woman counting out coins owed. “If he was the perpetrator, how comes it that he was not detected and denounced by the young people leaving? If, on the contrary, he witnessed another man’s blow—a blow that horrified him into madness—how comes it that he was able to slide away from the door where he must have stood, without attracting attention and bringing vengeance on himself from the guilty murderer?”

“He may be one of the noiseless kind,” Gryce said, “or rather may have been such before this shock unsettled him.”

“True,” she returned, “but he would have been seen. Recall the doorway’s position. If Mr. Adams fell where struck, the assailant must have had that door directly before him. He could not have helped seeing any one standing in it.”

“That is true,” Gryce conceded. “Your observations are correct. But those young people were disordered. Their condition proves it. Escape was all they sought. And, you see—they did escape.”

“But you will find them,” Miss Butterworth insisted, and now a spark of her old confidence burned through her shame. “A man who can locate a woman in this great city with no other clew than five spangles will certainly make this parasol tell the name of its owner.”

Gryce’s expression softened, and in the softening was something like affection—carefully rationed, like costly spice. “Ah, madam, the credit is not mine. It was the initial stroke of a young man I propose to adopt into my home and heart—the same who brought you here to-night. Not much to look at, but promising. Yet I doubt even he can discover your young lady with no other aid than this parasol. New York is a big place, ma’am—a big place. Do you know how Sweetwater came to find you? Through your virtues: through your neat, methodical habits. Had you been careless, and not given to mending torn dresses, he might have worn his heart out searching for the lady who dropped those spangles. Luck—or rather your commendable habit—favored him. But in the search you propose, he may have no such assistance.”

“Nor will he need it,” she said, and the confidence in her tone was not vanity but hunger—hunger for order, for the right name put to the right thing. “I have unbounded faith in your genius, which is back of your pupil’s skill. You will discover, by some means, the lady with the dove-colored plumes, and through her the young gentleman.”

“We shall at least put our energies in that direction,” Gryce replied. “Sweetwater may have an idea—”

“And I may have one,” she said.

“You?”

“Yes. I indulged in but little sleep last night. That dreadful room with its unsolved mystery was ever before me. Thoughts would come—possibilities. I imagined myself probing its secrets to the bottom and—”

“Wait, madam,” Gryce interrupted, as if a sudden sound from outside had reminded him of some other note in the case. “How many of its so-called secrets do you know? You said nothing about the lantern.”

“It was burning with a red light when I entered.”

“You did not touch the buttons arranged along the table top?”

“No.” Her distaste was immediate. “If there is one thing I do not touch, it is anything suggesting an electrical contrivance. Mechanisms alarm me. I am intensely feminine, sir, in all my instincts, and to such things I give a wide berth. I have not even a telephone in my house. Some allowance must be made for the natural timidity of woman.”

Mr. Gryce suppressed the smallest smile. “It is a pity,” he remarked. “Had you brought another light upon the scene, you might have been blessed with an idea on a subject as puzzling as any connected with the whole affair.”

“You have not heard what I have to say on a more important matter,” she returned. “When we have exhausted one topic, we may both feel like turning on your fresh lights. Mr. Gryce, on what does this mystery hinge? On the bit of writing these young people were so alarmed at leaving behind.”

“Ah. It is from that you would work,” he said, and nodded as if acknowledging a sound he had been straining to catch. “It is a good point to start from. But we have found no such writing.”

“Have you searched for it?” she demanded. “You did not know till now that importance might attach to a morsel of paper with half a dozen words.”

“True,” he answered, “but a detective searches just the same. We ransacked that room as few rooms have been ransacked in years—not for a known clew but an unknown one. We had first to learn who the man was; his papers were examined. They told nothing. If there had been a scrap of writing within view or in his desk—”

“It was not on his person?” she pressed. “His pockets—his clothes—”

“A man dead of violence is always searched,” he said. “I leave no stone unturned.”

At this her face took on an expression that was not triumph but a kind of inward settling—as if a long-simmering suspicion had finally thickened into certainty.

“I wonder,” she murmured, “if I imagined—or really saw—on Mr. Adams’s face an extraordinary expression. Something more than surprise or anguish after a mortal blow. A look of determination—some superhuman resolve taken at the moment of death, or—Can you read that face for me? Or did you fail to perceive what I say? It would aid me to know.”

“I noted that look,” Gryce admitted. “It was not common. But I cannot read it for you—”

“I wonder if your young man, Sweetwater, can,” she said, and her voice sharpened again. “Such a fold to the lips, such mingled grief and—what was that you said? Sweetwater has not been admitted to the room of death? Well, well. Then I must make my own suggestion—though it may be totally valueless. It has impressed me so that it must out, if I am to have peace to-night.”

She leaned forward. “Mr. Gryce, allow me to whisper in your ear. Some things lose force when spoken aloud.”

And she breathed a short sentence into him—so low it was less a sound than the shape of a sound, like a remembered word in a dying dialect. Whatever it was, it struck him at once. He started, then looked at her with amazement that quickly deepened into admiration.

“Madam!” he cried, rising that he might honor her with a bow as deep as any he had given a person of consequence. “Your idea—valueless or not—is worthy of the acute lady who offers it. We will act on it. At once. Wait while I give my orders. I will not keep you long.”

With another bow, he left the room.

Miss Butterworth had been trained, from the age when a child first learns to hold a teacup without trembling, to keep her body quiet and her feelings quieter. When she sat, she did not shift. When she rose, it was with decision, like a cook who knows precisely when a soufflé has set. Even her silences were properly folded, like napkins in a drawer.

Yet the moment she was left alone after the interview, the restraint that had always served her so well began to feel like a collar too tight at the throat. She walked to the window, halted, turned back again—each movement clipped and orderly, each one betraying the very agitation she despised in others. Outside, the street’s everyday clatter pressed in: wheels striking uneven stone, a far hawker’s call, the thin metallic ringing of a bell some door away. The noise seemed indecently alive beside the dead house she had just left.

She was still standing there, hands locked together as if to prevent them from fluttering, when Mr. Gryce returned. He came in with that air of a man who has been listening longer than speaking, and with a smile—almost sprightly—which sat oddly on his worn, vigilant face.

“Well, that is seen to!” he said. “And all we have to do now is to await the result. Madam, have you any further ideas? If so, I should be glad to have the benefit of them.”

Her composure snapped back into place as neatly as a lid over a simmering pot. “You would?” she repeated, studying him with the suspicious exactitude of a woman weighing flour. “I should like to be assured of the value of the one I have already advanced, before I venture upon another. Let us enter into a conference instead; compare notes; tell, for instance, why neither of us look on Bartow as the guilty man.”

“I thought we had exhausted that topic.” His voice kept its courtesy, but his eyes were intent. “Your suspicions were aroused by the young couple you saw leaving the house, while mine—well, madam, to you, at least, I may admit that there is something in the mute’s gestures and general manner which conveys to my mind the impression that he is engaged in rehearsing something he has seen, rather than something he has done; and as yet I have seen no reason for doubting the truth of this impression.”

“I was affected in the same way,” she answered quickly. “And would have been, even if I had not already had my suspicions turned in another direction. Besides, it is more natural for a man to be driven insane by another’s act than by his own.”

“Yes, if he loved the victim.”

“And did not Bartow?”

“He does not mourn Mr. Adams.”

“But he is no longer master of his emotions.”

“Very true; but if we take any of his actions as a clew to the situation, we must take all. We believe from his gestures that he is giving us a literal copy of acts he has seen performed. Then why pass over the gleam of infernal joy that lights his face after the whole is over? It is as if he rejoiced over the deed, or at least found immeasurable satisfaction in it.”

She frowned. Even in recollection, the scene had an odor: not merely blood, but the close, sickly sweetness of a room shut up with violence in it, as if the air itself had been bruised. “Perhaps it is still a copy of what he saw; the murderer may have rejoiced. But no—there was no joy in the face of the young man I saw rushing away from this scene of violence. Quite the contrary. Mr. Gryce, we are in deep waters. I feel myself wellnigh submerged by them.”

“Hold up your head, madam. Every flood has its ebb. If you allow yourself to go under, what will become of me?”

“You are disposed to humor, Mr. Gryce. It is a good sign. You are never humorous when perplexed. Somewhere you must see daylight.”

“Let us proceed with our argument. Illumination frequently comes from the most unexpected quarter.”

“Very well, then. Let us put the old man’s joy down as one of the mysteries to be explained later. Have you thought of him as a possible accomplice?”

“Certainly; but this supposition is open to the same objection as that which made him the motive power in this murder. One is not driven insane by an expected horror. It takes shock to unsettle the brain. He was not looking for the death of his master.”

“True. We may consider that matter as settled. Bartow was an innocent witness of this crime, and, having nothing to fear, may be trusted to reproduce in his pantomimic action its exact features.”

“Very good. Continue, madam. Nothing but profit is likely to follow an argument presented by Miss Butterworth.”

His tone was perfectly respectful; his manner, as impeccable as a table laid for a rector. But she, who had spent a lifetime detecting condescension in the smallest inflection—like tasting stale oil in a sauce—stiffened, though she allowed no other sign of irritation.

“Let us, then, recall his precise gestures,” she said, “remembering that he must have surprised the assailant from the study doorway, and so have seen the assault from over his master’s shoulder.”

“In other words, directly in front of him. Now what was his first move?”

“His first move, as now seen, is to raise his right arm and stretch it behind him, while he leans forward for the imaginary dagger. What does that mean?”

“I should find it hard to say. But I did not see him do that. When I came upon him, he was thrusting with his left hand across his own body—a vicious thrust—and with his left hand. That is a point, Mr. Gryce.”

“Yes,” Gryce said, his gaze sharpening, “especially as the doctors agree that Mr. Adams was killed by a left-handed blow.”

She drew in her breath. The street-noises outside seemed to recede; in her mind rose the sharp, hideous little sound of metal meeting flesh, though no one had heard it in fact. “You don’t say! Don’t you see the difficulty, then?”

“The difficulty, madam?”

“Bartow was standing face to face with the assailant. In imitating him—especially in his unreasoning state of mind—he would lift the arm opposite to the one whose action he mimics, which, in this case, would be the assailant’s right. Try, for the moment, to mimic my actions. See! I lift this hand, and instinctively—nay, I detected the movement, sir, quickly as you remembered yourself—you raise the one directly opposite to it. It is like seeing yourself in a mirror. You turn your head to the right, but your image turns to the left.”

Mr. Gryce laughed outright. The sound was brief, but honest; it cut through the oppressive hush that clung to the subject like a grease-film to a pan. He was not often caught unprepared, and the admission, when it came, was almost cheerful.

“Very good! You have proved your point quite satisfactorily; but what conclusions are to be drawn from it? That the man was not left-handed, or that he was not standing in the place you have assigned to him?”

“Shall we go against the doctors? They say that the blow was a left-handed one.” Her eyes brightened with a kind of practical hunger. “Mr. Gryce, I would give anything for an hour spent with you in Mr. Adams’s study, with Bartow free to move about at his will. I think we would learn more by watching him for a short space of time than in talking as we are doing for an hour.”

The request came out tentatively, almost with reluctance. Miss Butterworth had, in her way, the strict conscience of a woman who believes a household runs on boundaries; she knew she was proposing to step across one.

“I don’t want to be disagreeable,” she added, with a little smile that tried to soften the audacity.

“I think it can be managed, madam; that is, after the funeral. There are too many officials now in the house, and—”

“Of course, of course,” she agreed at once. “I should not think of obtruding myself at present. But the case is so interesting, and my connection with it so peculiar, that I sometimes forget myself.” Her fingers tightened upon one another. “Do you think—” She rarely let her voice falter; now it did. “—that I have laid myself open to a summons from the coroner?”

Mr. Gryce’s expression changed; his eyes lowered for an instant to her folded hands, and there was something almost compassionate in his pause.

“The facts regarding this affair come in so slowly,” he said at last, “that I doubt if the inquest is held for several days. Meanwhile we may light on those two young people ourselves. If so, the coroner may overlook your share in bringing them to our notice.”

He put a slight, sly weight on the word overlook, and his glance held a humor that was not entirely kind. But Miss Butterworth did not bristle. Her mind had already moved to the next fact, the next disturbance in the pattern—like a housekeeper who discovers an extra place-setting has been used.

“Ah,” she said, “if they had been the only persons I encountered there. But they were not. Another person entered the house before I left it, and I may be obliged to speak of him.”

“Of him? Really, madam, you are a mine of intelligence.”

“Yes, sir,” she returned meekly—meekly, which from Miss Butterworth was itself a kind of confession. “I ought to have spoken of him before, but I never like to mix matters, and this old gentleman—”

“Old gentleman!”

“Yes, sir. Very old and very much of a gentleman, did not appear to have any connection with the crime beyond knowing the murdered man.”

“Ah, but that’s a big connection, ma’am. To find some one who knew Mr. Adams—really, madam, patience has its limits, and I must press you to speak.”

“Oh, I will speak! The time has come for it. Besides, I’m quite ready to discuss this new theme; it is very interesting.”

“Suppose we begin, then, by a detailed account of your adventures in this house of death,” he suggested dryly. “Your full adventures, madam, with nothing left out.”

“I appreciate the sarcasm, but nothing has been left out except what I am about to relate to you. It happened just as I was leaving the house.”

“What did? I hate to ask you to be more explicit. But, in the interests of justice—”

“You are quite right. As I was going out, then, I encountered an elderly gentleman coming in. His hand had just touched the bell handle. You will acknowledge that it was a perplexing moment for me. His face, which was well preserved for his years, wore an air of expectation that was almost gay. He glanced in astonishment at mine, which—whatever its usual serenity—certainly must have borne marks of deep emotion. Neither of us spoke. At last he inquired politely if he might enter, and said something about having an appointment with some one in the study. At which I stepped briskly enough aside, I assure you, for this might mean—”

Gryce’s lips parted as if to interject.

“What did you say? Did I close the door? I assuredly did. Was I to let the whole of — Street into the horrors of this house at a moment when a poor old man—No, I didn’t go out myself. Why should I? Was I to leave a man on the verge of eighty—excuse me, not every man of eighty is so hale and vigorous as yourself—to enter such a scene alone? Besides, I had not warned him of the condition of the only other living occupant of the house.”

“Discreet, very. Quite what was to be expected of you, Miss Butterworth. More than that. You followed him, no doubt, with careful supervision, down the hall.”

“Most certainly! What would you have thought of me if I had not? He was in a strange house; there was no servant to guide him; he wanted to know the way to the study, and I politely showed him there.”

“Kind of you, madam—very. It must have been an interesting moment to you.”

“Very interesting! Too interesting! I own that I am not made entirely of steel, sir, and the shock he received at finding a dead man awaiting him, instead of a live one, was more or less communicated to me. Yet I stood my ground.”

“Admirable! I could have done no better myself. And so this man who had an appointment with Mr. Adams was shocked—really shocked—at finding him lying there under a cross, dead?”

“Yes, there was no doubting that. Shocked, surprised, terrified—and something more. It is that something more which has proved my perplexity. I cannot make it out, not even in thinking it over. Was it the fascination which all horrible sights exert on the morbid, or was it a sudden realization of some danger he had escaped, or of some difficulty yet awaiting him? Hard to say, Mr. Gryce, hard to say; but you may take my word for it that there was more to him in this meeting than an unexpected stumbling upon a dead man where he expected to find a live one.

“Yet he made no sound after that first cry, and hardly any movement. He just stared at the figure on the floor; then at his face, which he seemed to devour—at first with curiosity, then with hate, then with terror, and lastly—how can I express myself?—with a sort of hellish humor that in another moment might have broken into something like a laugh, if the bird, which I had failed to observe up to this moment, had not waked in its high cage, and, thrusting its beak between the bars, shrilled out in the most alarming of tones: ‘Remember Evelyn!’”

At the memory of that cry—thin, sharp, and unnatural in the stillness—Miss Butterworth’s shoulders rose a fraction. The sound had seemed to pierce not only her ears but the room itself, as if it had struck the walls and returned brighter, uglier. In a city that never truly fell silent, that single, caged phrase had felt like a deliberate violation of quiet, a fragment of private history made into a public noise.

“That startled the old man even more than the sight on the floor had done,” she continued. “He turned round, and I saw his fist rise as if against some menacing intruder, but it quickly fell again as his eyes encountered the picture which hung before him, and with a cringe painful to see in one of his years, he sidled back till he reached the doorway. Here he paused a minute to give another look at the man outstretched at his feet, and I heard him say:

“‘It is Amos’s son, not Amos! Is it fatality, or did he plan this meeting, thinking—’”

Her voice caught, not from fear but from the maddening incompleteness of it—like a recipe torn off mid-instruction, leaving the crucial step lost.

“But here he caught sight of my figure in the antechamber beyond, and resuming in an instant his former debonair manner, he bowed very low and opened his lips as if about to ask a question. But he evidently thought better of it, for he strode by me and made his way to the front door without a word. Being an intruder myself, I did not like to stop him. But I am sorry now for the consideration I showed him; for just before he stepped out, his emotion—the special character of which, I own to you, I find impossible to understand—culminated in a burst of raucous laughter which added the final horror to this amazing adventure. Then he went out, and in the last glimpse I had of him before the door shut he wore the same look of easy self-satisfaction with which he had entered this place of death some fifteen minutes before.”

Mr. Gryce’s whole manner tightened with interest. “Remarkable! Some secret history there! That man must be found. He can throw light upon Mr. Adams’s past. ‘Amos’s son,’ he called him? Who is Amos? Mr. Adams’s name was Felix. Felix, the son of Amos. Perhaps this connection of names may lead to something. It is not a common one, and if given to the papers, may result in our receiving a clew to a mystery which seems impenetrable. Your stay in Mr. Adams’s house was quite productive, ma’am. Did you prolong it after the departure of this old man?”

“No, sir. I had had my fill of the mysterious, and left immediately after him. Ashamed of the spirit of investigation which had led me to enter the house, I made a street boy the medium of my communication to the police, and would have been glad if I could have so escaped all responsibility in the matter. But the irony of fate follows me as it does others. A clew was left of my presence, which involves me in this affair, whether I will or no. Was the hand of Providence in this? Perhaps. The future will tell.

“And now, Mr. Gryce, since my budget is quite empty and the hour late, I will take my leave. If you hear from that bit of paper—”

“If I hear from it in the way you suggest I will let you know. It will be the least I can do for a lady who has done so much for me.”

“Now you flatter me—proof positive that I have stayed a minute longer than was judicious. Good evening, Mr. Gryce.”

“What? I have not stayed too long? You have something else to ask.”

“Yes,” he said, and for the first time his careful tone took on a personal edge, like a knife slipping from professional use into private curiosity. “And this time it is concerning a matter personal to yourself. May I inquire if you wore the same bonnet yesterday that you do to-day?”

“No, sir. I know you have a good reason for this question, and so will not express my surprise. Yesterday I was in reception costume, and my bonnet was a jet one—”

“With long strings tied under the chin?”

“No, sir, short strings; long strings are no longer the fashion.”

“But you wore something which fell from your neck?”

“Yes, a boa—a feather boa. How came you to know it, sir? Did I leave my image in one of the mirrors?”

“Hardly. If so, I should not have expected it to speak. You merely wrote the fact on the study table top. Or so I have dared to think. You or the young lady—did she wear ribbons or streamers, too?”

“That I cannot say. Her face was all I saw, and the skirt of a dove-colored silk dress.”

“Then you must settle the question for me in this way. If on the tips of that boa of yours you find the faintest evidence of its having been dipped in blood, I shall know that the streaks found on the top of the table I speak of were evidences of your presence there. But if your boa is clean, or was not long enough to touch that dying man as you leaned over him, then we have proof that the young lady with the dove-colored plumes fingered that table also, instead of falling at once into the condition in which you saw her carried out.”

Miss Butterworth’s lips pressed together. She could summon the feel of the boa even now—soft, foolish, and theatrical against her neck, the very sort of ornament she would have condemned on another woman for inviting notice. That a thing so frivolous might have trailed across a table in a room of murder, might have taken up a smear of blood as easily as it would take up kitchen soot, struck her with humiliation sharper than any rebuke.

“I fear that it is my boa which will tell the tale,” she said. “Another proof of the fallibility of man—or, rather, woman. In secret search for clews I left behind me traces of my own presence. I really feel mortified, sir, and you have quite the advantage of me.”

And with that show of humility—which may not have been wholly sincere—she took her departure, her steps as firm and measured as ever, though the world outside rang too loudly, too carelessly, in her ears.

Did Mr. Gryce suffer from any qualms of conscience at having elicited so much and imparted so little? It is doubtful. Mr. Gryce’s conscience was quite seared in certain places.

Mr. Gryce’s note reached Miss Butterworth in the mild, domestic hour when the household air still smelled of yesterday’s coal-fire and the day’s first toast. Her own answer met his almost at the same moment—two scraps of paper crossing in the city like matched spoons in a drawer.

Hers had been clipped to the point:

You were quite correct. So far as appears, I was the only person to lean over Mr. Adams’s study table after his unfortunate death. I have had to clip the ends of my boa.

His, no longer than a cook’s order in a busy kitchen, carried the sharper seasoning:

My compliments, madam! Mr. Adams’s jaws have been forced apart. A small piece of paper was found clinched between his teeth. This paper has been recovered, and will be read at the inquest. Perhaps a few favored persons may be granted the opportunity of reading it before then, notably yourself.

The second message struck like the first scent of vinegar in a sauce you thought finished—sudden, unmistakable, and calling up questions you could not pretend away. Miss Butterworth’s composure, already a thing of polished habit, became positively lustrous; and because she could not decently ladle the cause of it into conversation, the servants and hangers-on in her orbit began to speculate with that hungry curiosity people display when they have nothing to chew but another person’s good fortune.

At Headquarters the air was thick with talk. Men who had never before found time to notice their own neighbors now discovered they had, each of them, seen someone—some lady, some gentleman, some young couple—come or go from the dead man’s house at the very hour the papers had made famous. The city delivered these stories the way a market delivers bruised fruit: plentiful, varied, and mostly useless once you pressed them.

An old woman declared a genteel lady had opened the door to an elderly gentleman in a great-coat. A young woman with fashionable haste swore a young man, supporting an awfully pale young lady, had run against her as she passed the Adams place. She could not, however, swear to the young man’s face again, and could not settle whether the pale one was blonde or brunette—only that she wore a beautiful gray feather and looked ready to faint. Each account added noise to the street’s chorus, but little music to the case.

Then came the funeral. Mr. Adams, who had filled his rooms with rare objects and strict routine, went to the grave with no kin stepping forward, no intimate friend raising a voice for him. The landlord attended to the necessary rites; the banker stood in the place of chief mourner, as if money could substitute for blood. Detectives threaded the crowd at the door and at the cemetery, watching for faces that might flinch or follow too closely; but the mourners were thin, the passers-by ordinary, and the dead man’s secrets stayed where they had always lodged—in his walls, his habits, and the locked cupboards of his life.

So Mr. Gryce, recognizing that the living would not volunteer what the dead had withheld, went back to the house one evening alone, on foot, when darkness made the windows look blind and shut. He let himself in with a key and, with the method of a careful housekeeper restoring order before a difficult task, lit the rooms one by one until the place stood fully exposed—parlor, hall, stairs—every corner awake under gas.

The study was as he had left it in essentials: the cross hung again in its old place; the bird-cage swung from its hook under the ceiling; the rich, severe objects of the room were restored to their accustomed stations. Only the broken casings by the doorway gaped, ugly as a kitchen wall stripped to lath. The steel plate had been pushed back into its prepared slot, yet the blue surface still showed through the raw hole in the antechamber wall like cold metal glimpsed beneath torn fabric. Mr. Gryce, with an instinct more domestic than men often confess to, pulled two rugs from the parlor floor and draped them over the openings, softening the sight the way one lays a cloth over an unseemly mess when guests are expected.

He consulted his watch. Not yet nine.

He went to the parlor window and waited behind the curtains. The street outside murmured—wheels on stone, a distant shout, the thin, constant hiss of the city’s breath. Industrial noise, he thought, was an easy accomplice; it covered small sounds the way a loud band covers an honest singer. Yet tonight he wanted the house to speak for itself, to hear the old timbers and the stair’s round throat carry what they would.

A carriage stopped. A lady alighted with the brisk certainty of one who had never waited on anyone’s permission to be useful. Mr. Gryce left his post to meet her at the door.

Miss Butterworth came in as if she owned not merely her courage but the air between walls.

“Madam,” said he, “your punctuality is equal to my own. Have you ordered your coachman to drive away?”

“Only as far as the corner,” she returned, following him into the hall. “There he will await the call of your whistle.”

“Nothing could be better. Are you afraid to remain for a moment alone while I watch from the window the arrival of the other person we expect? At present there is no one in the house but ourselves.”

“If I were subject to fear in a matter of this kind, I should not be here at all.” Her glance swept the lighted rooms. “Besides, the house is very cheerfully illuminated. I see you have chosen a crimson light for the study.”

“Because a crimson light was burning when Mr. Adams died.”

From above, from the high-hung cage in the study, the parrot’s voice cut through the gaslit quiet—sharp, bright, and oddly human in its insistence.

“Remember Evelyn!”

Miss Butterworth paused as if a cold spoon had touched her tongue. “Oh! You have brought back the bird.”

“It was here all the time,” Mr. Gryce replied.

“That is not the cry with which it greeted me before. It was ‘Eva! Lovely Eva!’ Do you suppose Eva and Evelyn are the same?”

“Madam, we have so many riddles before us that we will let this one go for the present. I expect Mr. Adams’s valet here in a moment.”

“You relieve me of an immense weight,” she said, the relief real enough to soften her briskness. “I was afraid the privilege of being present at the test you propose to make was not to be accorded me.”

“You have earned a seat at this experiment. Bartow has been given a key and will enter as of old in entire freedom to do as he wills. We have simply to watch his movements.”

“In this room?” She looked toward the study as one might look toward a pantry where something rancid has been discovered. “I do not think I shall like that. I had rather not meet this madman face to face.”

“You will not be called upon to do so. We do not wish him startled by encountering any watchful eye. Irresponsible as he is, he must be allowed to move about without anything to distract him. Nothing must stand in the way of his following those impulses which may yield us a clew to his habits and the ways of this peculiar household.”

He guided her into the parlor—an unused room, the air in it stale with old upholstery and shut-in carpet, like a cupboard that has not been opened since last season. “I propose to place you where the chances are least in favor of your being seen—here, madam, which we have every reason to believe was seldom opened during Mr. Adams’s lifetime.”

“You must put out the gas then,” she said, practical even in discomfort. “An unaccustomed light will attract his attention.”

“I will not only put out the gas but draw the portières close, making this little hole for your eye and this one for mine. A common expedient, madam; but serviceable—serviceable.”

In the sudden dimness behind the curtain, Miss Butterworth felt the absurdity of her position like a blush rising—she, a woman of settled dignity, peering through a slit in velvet. Yet the house was not silent; it was never truly silent. Even in darkness it had its own small ecology of sound: the faint settling of wood, the far-off creak of a stair tread remembering weight, the distant city’s endless mechanical sigh. In such a place, listening became as intimate as tasting—every little note meaningful.

The front door opened.

Mr. Gryce’s faintest “Hush!” confirmed what she already knew. The deaf-and-dumb valet had entered.

Bartow appeared in the antechamber sooner than she expected—a slight, elderly figure with the brisk, trained air of a man whose body still remembers duties his mind cannot name. He laid his hat on a table near the doorway and advanced toward the portière he must have lifted twenty times a day.

But this time he barely touched it.

Something held him back. The memory of what he had last seen there? The unfamiliar draped rugs over the broken casings? Miss Butterworth could not read his face from that angle, but she saw the meek, mechanical turn of his head, the way he took up his hat again as if nothing mattered but the next movement in an old sequence. Without entering the study, he left the antechamber and went softly upstairs.

“I will follow him,” Mr. Gryce whispered. “Don’t be afraid, ma’am. This whistle will bring a man in from the street at once.”

“I am not afraid. I would be ashamed—”

But he was already gone. She remained, listening to his steps and the valet’s, the two sound-tracks threading up the stair. For a moment the parrot muttered to itself, as if the house were chewing on a name it could not swallow.

Mr. Gryce returned quickly. “The experiment is likely to be a failure,” he murmured. “The old man has gone to his own room and is preparing for bed.”

He led the way into the study, and there—whether from intention or the idle habit of testing mechanisms—touched a button on the table-top. Instantly the room’s illumination shifted, as if some unseen hand had sprinkled the air with new dye. Violet light bloomed over the picture frames, the polished wood, the rigidly placed objects; it altered the color of everything without changing their shapes, like a new language laid over the same meaning, beautiful and imperfectly translating.

Miss Butterworth had just begun to note how the violet made the gilding look older, the shadows deeper, when a faint sound interrupted—steps descending. Mr. Gryce’s hand caught her sleeve; they were back behind the parlor portières in time to see Bartow hurry in from the hall.

This time his busy air remained unchecked. He crossed to the study, paused a breath on the threshold as if a draft had touched him with warning, then went in. He did not look toward the rug that covered the spot where his master had fallen. He began at once the familiar arrangements of the night, as if death had been a rumor and routine the only truth.

He fetched a bottle of wine from the cupboard and set it on the table. Then he placed a glass beside it, wiping the rim with scrupulous care—the quick, circular polishing of one who has been trained that cleanliness is a kind of respect. Miss Butterworth, despite herself, noticed how the cloth moved, how the glass caught the altered light; the action had the mindless precision of kitchen work done by rote, the sort of repetition that outlives the reason for it.

Next he laid out Mr. Adams’s dressing-gown and slippers, placing them within reach as if expecting the dead man’s hand to emerge from the air and claim them. Then he went into the bedroom.

Now the watchers crept near enough to see through the opening without being seen. Bartow stooped over a comb lying on the floor—an object out of place, like salt spilled on a clean board. He picked it up, shook his head with a small, puzzled motion, and set it on the dresser. Then he turned down the bed with the same practiced tug and smoothing palm, as if flattening wrinkles could flatten the world.

“Poor fool,” Miss Butterworth breathed when they had slipped back behind the curtain. “He has forgotten everything but his old routine duties. We shall get nothing from this man.”

She stopped mid-sentence. Mr. Gryce stiffened beside her.

Bartow stood in the middle of the study. His eyes fixed on his master’s empty chair—fixed, questioning, as if the chair itself had spoken in a tongue he half-remembered. Slowly he turned his head to the rug. He stared at it as though his gaze might pull aside the fabric and find the past lying neatly where it had been left.

Something in him woke. The meekness broke like a crust.

He moved with sudden violence—not strength, but passion; a storm in a frail body. He began to re-enact, in a broken pantomime, what he had seen in that room on the day of murder: the start, the recoil, the reaching gesture, the blow mimed with the wrong hand and yet with utter conviction. The performance was clumsy, terrible, and precise in its terror—an untranslatable memory acted out by a body that could not explain.

Then, as abruptly as it had flared, the fit ended. Bartow went softly out and crept upstairs again, leaving the study arranged for a man who would never drink the wine.

Mr. Gryce and Miss Butterworth stepped into the light and looked at each other with frank discouragement. The demonstration had given them motion without meaning—gesture without the words that might have pinned it to motive.

Miss Butterworth’s eyes, however, had taken on a new gleam, the way a cook’s face changes when she suddenly knows what ingredient is missing.

“Turn on another color,” she urged quickly. “Let us see what will happen. I have an idea it will fetch the old man down again.”

Mr. Gryce lifted his brows. “Do you think he can see through the floor?”

He touched the button. The violet yielded to a rich blue, cool and dense as a flame fed with copper. For a moment nothing stirred.

Miss Butterworth’s mouth tightened.

“I have confidence in your theories,” Mr. Gryce began, dryly, “but when they imply the possibility of this man seeing through blank walls and obeying signals which can have no signification to any one on the floor above—”

“Hark!” she cried, one finger raised.

Steps. Descending again—slower, more feeble, as though the old man were being drawn by a thread he did not understand. Bartow entered the study with hesitation, advanced to the table, and held out his hands as if to lift something he expected to find there. Finding nothing, he glanced up at the shelves, then back to the table, bewildered. He shook his head, and suddenly, as if the effort of not understanding had exhausted him, collapsed into a doze on the nearest chair.

Miss Butterworth exhaled, long and satisfied. She looked at Mr. Gryce as if daring him to deny the evidence of his own senses.

“Can you read the meaning of all that? I think I can. Don’t you see he came expecting to find a pile of books on the table which it was probably his business to restore to their shelves?”

“But how can he know what light is burning here?” Mr. Gryce demanded in a low voice. “You can see for yourself there is no possible communication between this room and the one in which he has always been found by any one going above.”

Miss Butterworth’s hesitation was almost naïve; there was apology in her smile, though none in her words. “When I went upstairs—you know I went upstairs when I was here before—I saw a little thing—a very little thing—which you doubtless observed yourself and which may explain, though I do not know how, why Bartow can perceive these lights from the floor above.”

“I shall be very glad to hear about it, madam. I thought I had thoroughly searched those rooms.”

“And the halls?”

“And the halls.”

“Or,” she said, with a quiet insistence, “you did not make it your business to look lower.”

“How?”

“To look lower. To look on the floor, say.”

“On the floor?”

“The floor sometimes reveals much,” she returned. “It shows where a person steps the oftenest, and therefore where he has the most business. You must have noticed how marred the woodwork is at the edge of the carpeting on that little landing above.”

“In the round of the staircase?”

“Yes.”

Mr. Gryce, whether convinced or merely unwilling to argue while time was ripe, moved at once. He left the valet where he sat and went upstairs, and Miss Butterworth—being incapable of being left when her own observation was at stake—followed close.

They paused at the small landing in the curve of the staircase. The carpet edge was worn, the wood scuffed and polished by countless shuffling steps, like a threshold in a farmhouse kitchen where generations have turned from stove to table. Yet there was no obvious reason for anyone to loiter in this corner: the stair was wide, and there was no window.

“No window,” Mr. Gryce murmured, his gaze traveling the wall.

He reached over Miss Butterworth’s shoulder to one of the pictures hanging along the stair, seized it, and pulled it away from the wall. It had been hanging askew—just enough to betray itself. Behind it yawned a round opening, a little loophole. Through it poured a ray of the study’s blue light, clean and unmistakable in the dim hallway.

“No window,” he repeated. “No—but an opening into the study wall which answers the same purpose. Miss Butterworth, your eye is to be trusted every time. I only wonder you did not pull this picture aside yourself.”

“It was not hanging crooked then,” she said. “Besides, I was in a hurry. I had just come from my encounter with this demented man. I noticed the marks on the landing and the worn edges of the carpet on my way upstairs. I was in no condition to observe them on my way down.”

“I see.”

She ran her foot lightly to and fro over the scuffed boards, reading them with the attention of a food historian tracing a culture’s memory in the grease-shine of a well-used pan. “Bartow was evidently in the habit of coming here constantly,” she said, “probably to learn whether his master had need of him. Ingenious in Mr. Adams to contrive signals for communication with this man! He certainly had great use for his deaf-and-dumb servant. So one mystery is solved.”

“And if I am not mistaken,” Mr. Gryce replied, bending to put his eye near the opening, “we can by a glance through this loophole obtain the answer to another. You are wondering, I believe, how Bartow, if he followed the movements of the assailant from the doorway, came to thrust with his left hand instead of with his right. Now if he saw the tragedy from this point, he saw it over the assailant’s shoulder, instead of face to face. What follows? He would imitate literally the movements of the man he saw, turn in the same direction and strike with the same hand.”

“Mr. Gryce,” Miss Butterworth said, her satisfaction sharpened by the click of pieces finding their places, “we are beginning to untangle the threads that looked so complicated.”

A sudden squawk burst up from below, startlingly near in the hollow of the stair, and for a moment the blue light seemed to pulse with it.

“Ah—what is that? Why, it’s that bird! His cage must be very nearly under this hole.”

“A little to one side, madam,” Mr. Gryce answered, “but near enough to give you a start. What was it he cried then?”

“Oh—those sympathetic words about Eva. ‘Poor Eva!’”

“Give a glance to Bartow. You can see him very well from here.”

Miss Butterworth put her eye again to the opening. What she saw drew from her not a word but a grunt—short, emphatic, and in her vocabulary heavy with meaning.

“He is shaking his fist,” she whispered. “He is all alive with passion. He looks as if he would like to kill the bird.”

“Perhaps that is why the creature was strung up so high,” Mr. Gryce returned. “You may be sure Mr. Adams had some basis for his idiosyncrasies.”

“I begin to think so,” she said slowly. “I do not know that I care to go back where that man is. He has a very murderous look.”

“And a very feeble arm, Miss Butterworth. You are safe under my protection. My arm is not feeble.”

Below them, the parrot’s voice rose again—“Remember Evelyn!”—the phrase echoing up the stairwell’s curve, a bright scrap of sound that insisted on being heard, as if names themselves were clues and memory a thing that could be trained, signaled, and—if neglected—lost.

At the foot of the stairs Mr. Gryce paused, murmured an apology, and with a curt gesture summoned two or three of the men he had left outside. They took the valet in hand at once—quietly, efficiently—as if lifting a heavy tray from a crowded table. Only when the house had resumed its muffled, strained stillness did he lead Miss Butterworth back into the study.

The room received them like a larder after a storm: everything in place, and yet the air unsettled, full of the faintest after-sounds—boots on stone, a door’s reluctant click, the thin hiss of a coal fire that would not commit to cheer. Outside, the city’s clatter—car-wheels, a call from the street—pressed like tin against the windows. Miss Butterworth, who had lived long enough to remember quieter neighborhoods and the older cadence of speech, listened as one listens for a kettle beginning to sing: the smallest note could change everything.

Mr. Gryce did not waste words.

“One very important thing has been settled by the experiment we have just made,” he said. “Bartow is acquitted of participation in this crime.”

“Then we can give our full attention to the young people,” she returned, with the practical relish of a cook at last narrowing her recipe to the true ingredients. “You have heard nothing from them, I suppose?”

“No.”

“Nor from the old man who laughed?”

“No.”

Her disappointment showed—sharp as the smell of a sauce that has turned at the last moment. “I thought—it seemed very probable—that the scrap of writing you found would inform you who these were. If it was important enough for the dying man to try to swallow it, it certainly should give some clue to his assailant.”

“Unfortunately, it does not do so.” Mr. Gryce drew a slow breath, as if tasting the bitterness of it. “It was a veritable scrawl, madam, running something like this: ‘I return your daughter to you. She is here. Neither she nor you will ever see me again. Remember Evelyn!’ And signed, ‘Amos’s son.’”

“Amos’s son!” Miss Butterworth repeated, and the words struck her as a forgotten dialect suddenly spoken at the family table—familiar, and yet freighted with meanings the young no longer hear. “That is Mr. Adams himself.”

“So we have every reason to believe.”

“Strange! Unaccountable!” She leaned forward, as if the ink itself might give off a scent. “And the paper inscribed with these words was found clenched between his teeth! Was the handwriting recognized?”

“Yes—as his own, if we can judge from the specimens we have seen of his signature on the fly-leaves of his books.”

“Well, mysteries deepen.” Her fingers tightened on her reticule, the way a housewife’s hand tightens on a jar-lid that refuses. “And the retaining of this paper was so important to him that even in his death-throe he thrust it in this strangest of all hiding-places, as being the only one that could be considered safe from search.” She lifted her chin. “And the girl! Her first words on coming to herself were: ‘You have left that line of writing behind.’ Mr. Gryce, those words, few and inexplicable as they are, contain the key to the whole situation. Will you repeat them again, if you please, sentence by sentence?”

“With pleasure, madam; I have said them often enough to myself. First, then: ‘I return your daughter to you!’”

“So!” Miss Butterworth said, and her mind began laying out possibilities the way she laid out jars on pantry shelves, each label turned outward. “Mr. Adams had some one’s daughter in charge whom he returns. Whose daughter? Not that young man’s daughter, certainly, for that would necessitate her being a small child. Besides, if these words had been meant for his assailant, why make so remarkable an effort to hide them from him?”

“Very true! I have said the same thing to myself.”

“Yet, if not for him, for whom, then? For the old gentleman who came in later?”

“It is possible; since hearing of him I have allowed myself to regard this as among the possibilities,” said Mr. Gryce, “especially as the next words of this strange communication are: ‘She is here.’ Now the only woman who was there a few minutes previous to this old gentleman’s visit was the light-haired girl whom you saw carried out.”

“Very true; but why do you reason as if this paper had just been written?” Miss Butterworth’s voice sharpened with that domestic exactness which cannot tolerate a vague measure where a teaspoon is required. “It might have been an old scrap, referring to past sorrows or secrets.”

“These words were written that afternoon,” he replied. “The paper on which they were scrawled was torn from a sheet of letter paper lying on the desk, and the pen with which they were inscribed—you must have noticed where it lay, quite out of its natural place on the extreme edge of the table.”

“Certainly, sir; but I had little idea of the significance we might come to attach to it.” Her eyes went to the wall where the one large picture hung—its woman’s face pale, mournful, and yet composed, as if grief were a language she had been forced to learn in silence. “These words are connected, then, with the girl I saw. And she is not Evelyn or he would not have repeated in this note the bird’s catch-word, ‘Remember Evelyn!’ I wonder if she is Evelyn?” She pointed, as if naming food on a menu could call it to the table.

“We may call her so for the nonce,” Mr. Gryce said. “So melancholy a face may well suggest some painful family secret. But how explain the violent part played by the young man, who is not mentioned in these abrupt and hastily penned sentences! It is all a mystery, madam, a mystery which we are wasting time to attempt to solve.”

“Yet I hate to give it up without an effort. Those words, now. There were some other words you have not repeated to me.”

“They came before that injunction, ‘Remember Evelyn!’ They bespoke a resolve. ‘Neither she nor you will ever see me again.’”

“Ah! but these few words are very significant, Mr. Gryce.” Her gaze held his, steady as a woman tasting broth for salt. “Could he have dealt that blow himself? May he have been a suicide after all?”

“Madam, you have the right to inquire; but from Bartow’s pantomime, you must have perceived it is not a self-inflicted blow he mimics, but a maddened thrust from an outraged hand.” He paused, and in that pause the study’s sounds came forward: the low tick of a clock, the faint breath of the fire, and—beneath, like a bass note under music—the city’s endless metal din. “Let us keep to our first conclusions; only—to be fair to every possibility—the condition of Mr. Adams’s affairs and the absence of all family papers and such documents as may usually be found in a wealthy man’s desk prove that he had made some preparation for possible death. It may have come sooner than he expected and in another way, but it was a thought he had indulged in, and—madam, I have a confession to make also. I have not been quite fair to my most valued colleague.”

Miss Butterworth’s brows rose; she did not like withheld facts any more than she liked a cook who kept secrets from the kitchen staff.

“The study—that most remarkable of rooms—contains a secret which has not been imparted to you; a very peculiar one, madam, which was revealed to me in a rather startling manner. This room can be, or rather could be, cut off entirely from the rest of the house; made a death-trap of, or rather a tomb, in which this incomprehensible man may have intended to die.” Mr. Gryce stepped toward the doorway and indicated a plate of steel. “Look at this. It is worked by a mechanism which forces it across this open doorway. I was behind that plate of steel the other night, and these holes had to be made to let me out.”

“Ha!” Miss Butterworth exclaimed, not without a shiver that was half excitement, half indignation at so medieval a contrivance in a modern home. “You detectives have your experiences! I should not have enjoyed spending that especial evening with you. But what an old-world tragedy we are unearthing here! I declare—” and she actually rubbed her eyes, as if to clear away soot—“I feel as if transported back into mediæval days. Who says we are living in New York within sound of the cable car and the singing of the telegraph wire?”

“Some men are perfectly capable of bringing the mediæval into Wall Street,” Mr. Gryce answered. “I think Mr. Adams was one of those men. Romanticism tinged all his acts, even the death he died. Nor did it cease with his death. It followed him to the tomb. Witness the cross we found lying on his bosom.”

“That was the act of another’s hand,” she insisted, quick to separate what was cooked by one person from what was plated by another. “The result of another’s superstition. That shows the presence of a priest or a woman at the moment he died.”

“Yet,” Mr. Gryce went on, with a faint, wondering look, “he must have had a grain of hard sense in his make-up. All his contrivances worked. He was a mechanical genius, as well as a lover of mystery.”

“An odd combination.” Miss Butterworth drew in a breath, as if the room held some scent that had not yet declared itself. “Strange that we do not feel his spirit infecting the very air of this study. I could almost wish it did. We might then be led to grasp the key to this mystery.”

“That,” Mr. Gryce said, with the patient firmness of a man who measures facts as precisely as flour, “can be done in only one way. You have already pointed it out. We must trace the young couple who were present at his death struggle. If they cannot be found the case is hopeless.”

“And so,” she said, pushing herself up with a sigh, “we come around to the point from which we started—proof positive that we are lost in the woods.”

For the time being she felt her resources spent, like a household that has given out its last loaf to a neighbor and must now wait for tomorrow’s baking. Mr. Gryce did not detain her; indeed, he looked eager to quit the room himself, as if its silence had become a kind of pressure on the ears.

Yet they lingered long enough to cast one final look about them. As they did so, Miss Butterworth’s finger rose slowly.

“See!” she said. “You can hardly perceive from this side of the wall the opening made by the removal of that picture on the stair landing. Wouldn’t you say that it was in the midst of those folds of dark-colored tapestry up there?”

“Yes,” he replied. “I had already located that spot as the one. With the picture hung up on the other side, it would be quite invisible.”

“One needs to keep one’s eyes moving in a case like this,” she murmured. “That picture must have been drawn aside several times while we were in this room. Yet we failed to notice it.”

“That was from not looking high enough.” Mr. Gryce’s mouth tightened into something that was not quite a smile. “High and low, Miss Butterworth! What goes on at the level of the eye is apparent to every one.”

The smile with which he acknowledged her parting thrust—and prepared to escort her to the door—held less of irony than of sadness. Was he beginning to hear, beneath the case’s clamor and the city’s rasping song, the quieter sound of years accumulating; the sense that a dozen years earlier neither the high places nor the low would have escaped him at all?

“A blonde, you say, sir?”

Sweetwater’s question had the plain tone of a man trying to pin a fluttering thing to a board. Around them the office breathed its usual stale air—ink, damp wool, yesterday’s cigar—yet Mr. Gryce, with the parasol laid before him like an exhibit on a kitchen table, spoke as if he were describing a dish whose perfume refused to be forgotten.

“Yes, Sweetwater; not of the usual type. Frail—one of those light, almost mist-like women it is hard to associate with crime. He, on the contrary—Miss Butterworth’s description, and you may trust her eye—was one of those gentlemanly athletes whose height and breadth draw attention as a bell draws ears. Seen together, you would be apt to know them. But what reason have we for thinking they will be found together?”

Sweetwater’s gaze ran, practical and quick, over the dove-colored parasol and the little scatter of rose leaves beside it, pressed flat as if once trodden into a rug.

“How were they dressed?”

“Like people of fashion and respectability. He wore a brown-checked suit, fresh from the tailor; she, a dove-colored dress with white trimmings. The parasol shows the color of her hat and plumes. Both were young and—again by Miss Butterworth—unused to crime. She was in a faint when carried out, and he, with every reason for self-restraint, showed emotion enough to have brought questions down on him at once, if he had not set her in the vestibule and then swallowed himself and her into the passing crowd. Do you think you can find them, Sweetwater?”

“Have you no clews to their identity beyond this parasol?”

“None—unless you count these few faded rose leaves picked up from the floor of Mr. Adams’s study.”

Sweetwater took the leaves, let them sift through his fingers as carelessly as flour through a cook’s hand when he is judging fineness by touch. The petals had lost their color, but not their suggestion: something sweet, bruised, and faintly peppery still clung to them, a whisper of green stem and bridal ribbon.

“Then you have given me a problem, Mr. Gryce,” he said, doubtful now. “Somehow I don’t feel the same assurance of success I did before. Perhaps I more fully realize the difficulties now that I see how much rests upon chance. If Miss Butterworth had not been so precise, I should have failed in my former attempt, as I am likely to fail in this one. But I’ll make another effort to locate the owner of this parasol—if only to learn my business by failure.”

He held up the rose leaves again, as if he meant to read their veins like a map.

“And now, sir, where do you think I am going first? To a florist’s, with these faded rose leaves. Just because every other young fellow on the force would make a start from the parasol, I’m going to try and effect one from these rose leaves. I may be an egotist, but I can’t help that. I can do nothing with the parasol.”

“And what do you hope to do with the rose leaves? How can a florist help you in finding this young woman by means of them?”

“He may be able to say what kind of rose they fell from. Once I know that, I may discover the particular shop that sold the bouquet to this—more or less conspicuous—couple.”

“You may. I’m not the man to throw cold water on any one’s schemes. Every man has his own methods, and till they are proved valueless I say nothing.”

Sweetwater, all nerve and hope, bowed. He was satisfied to be allowed his own line, to follow scent rather than shape.

“I may be back in an hour,” he threw over his shoulder, “and you may not see me for a week.”

“Luck to your search!” was Gryce’s short reply.

The hour passed; Sweetwater did not return. The day passed, and still no Sweetwater. Another day went by, enlivened only by a passing of notes between the old detective and Miss Butterworth.

Hers, which reached him as briskly as a tray set down with purpose, made him smile—perhaps at its brevity, perhaps at the very sound of her mind in it.

Dear Mr. Gryce: I do not presume to dictate or even to offer a suggestion to the New York police, but have you inquired of the postman in a certain district whether he can recall the postmark on any of the letters he delivered to Mr. Adams?  
A. B.

His answer, dispatched with less warmth than accuracy, drew a frown at Gramercy Park. One could almost hear her disapproval in the silence after reading.

Dear Miss Butterworth: Suggestions are always in order, and even dictation can be endured from you. The postman delivers too many letters on that block to concern himself with postmarks. Sorry to close another thoroughfare.  
E. G.

Meanwhile anxiety worked on both; on Gryce it worked like an ill wind that will not settle—irritating, constant, and made worse by the city’s racket outside: iron wheels, hammers, shrill calls. A man could not think cleanly with such noise always sawing at his nerves. It was a world grown too loud for secrets to keep their shape.

On the third morning Sweetwater stood waiting in the office, plain face lit with satisfaction. He had kept his story like a covered dish for the one he meant to serve first. As soon as the door was shut between them, he turned beaming.

“News, sir—good news! I have found them. I have found them both, and by such a happy stroke! It was a blind trail, but when the florist said those petals might have fallen from a bride rose—well, sir, I know any woman can carry bride roses, but when I remembered the clothes of her companion looked as though they had just come from the tailor, and that she wore gray and white—why, it gave me an idea, and I began my search after this unknown pair at the Bureau of Vital Statistics.”

“Brilliant!” Gryce broke in, the word sharp with pleasure. “That is—if the thing worked.”

“And it did, sir; it did. I may have been born under a lucky star—probably was—but once started on this line, I went straight to the end. Shall I tell you how? Hunting through the list of persons married within the city limits during the last two weeks, I came upon the name of one Eva Poindexter. Eva! That was a name well-known in the house on —— Street. I decided to follow up this Eva.”

“A wise conclusion. And how did you set about it?”

“I went directly to the clergyman who performed the ceremony. He was a kind and affable dominie, sir, and I had no trouble talking with him.”

“And you described the bride?”

“No, I led the conversation so he described her.”

“Good. And what kind of a woman did he make her out to be? Delicate? Pale?”

“Sir, he had not read the service for so lovely a bride in years. Very slight—almost fragile—but beautiful, and with a delicate bloom that showed better health than her figure promised. It was a private wedding, celebrated in a hotel parlor; but her father was with her—”

“Her father?” The words struck Gryce’s thoughts like a stone dropped into still water. A father, present—then that laughing old man, and those lines clenched in a dead man’s teeth, could not sit together as neatly as he had supposed.

“Is there anything wrong about that?” Sweetwater asked, startled by the change he thought he saw.

Gryce made his face a blank again. “No. I had not expected his attendance at the wedding; that is all.”

“Sorry, sir, but there’s no doubt he was there. The bridegroom—”

“Yes; tell me about the bridegroom.”

“Was the very man you described as leaving Mr. Adams’s house with her. Tall, finely developed, with a grand air and gentlemanly manners. Even his clothes correspond: a checked suit, brown, of the latest cut. Oh, he is the man!”

Gryce dropped his eyes to the inkstand as though the lid held an answer. His mind returned, against his will, to the last message Amos’s son had written—words that had the blunt finality of a door shutting on a life:

I return your daughter to you. She is here. Neither she nor you will ever see me again. Remember Evelyn!  
AMOS’S SON.

How could that note—so like the language of a man casting off love with violence—point to a bride of another man?

There must be error. Or the world, which so loved to repeat names and faces across decades as if it enjoyed a refrain, was turning in circles again.

“I’m afraid it’s been a fool chase, Sweetwater,” he said at last, forcing caution into his voice. “The fact of their being married at all tallies little with what certain other evidences led us to expect—”

“Pardon me, sir, but will you hear me out? At the Imperial, where they were married, I learned that the father and daughter had registered as coming from a small place in Pennsylvania; but I could learn nothing about the bridegroom. He had not appeared till the time for the ceremony, and after the marriage was seen to take his bride away in one carriage while the old gentleman departed in another. That concerned me little; it was the young couple I was detailed to find.

“Employing the usual means, I tracked them to the Waldorf. There I learned what makes it certain I have been following the right pair. On the afternoon of the very day of Mr. Adams’s death, this young husband and wife left the hotel on foot and did not come back. Their clothes—all left behind—were taken away two days later by an elderly gentleman who said he was her father, and whose appearance matches the person registering as such at the Imperial. All of which looks favorable to my theory, does it not? Especially when you remember the bridegroom’s name—”

“You have not told it.”

“Is Adams, Thomas Adams. Same family as the murdered man, you see—at least the same name.”

Gryce studied the young detective with unwilling admiration. The facts had a hard, domestic plainness—hotels, registries, clothes left in drawers—yet they braided themselves into the case with the persistence of a repeated tune one cannot stop hearing.

“Humph! I do not wonder you thought it worth your while to follow up the pair, if one is named Adams and the other Eva. But, Sweetwater, the longer you serve on the force the more you will learn that coincidences as strange as these do occur, and must be taken into account. Much as I may regret to throw cold water on your hopes, there are reasons for believing the young man and woman we are seeking are not the ones you’ve been busy about. Certain facts—direct and unimpeachable—would seem to show that if she had a husband at all, his name would not be Thomas Adams, but Felix. And so I fear you will have to start again, on a new tack.”

Sweetwater did not flinch. His mouth held a vague, knowing smile, as if Gryce were tasting only the first spoonful.

“You haven’t asked me, sir, where I’ve spent all the time since I saw you last. The investigations I mentioned didn’t take more than a day.”

“Very true. Where have you been, Sweetwater?”

“To Montgomery, sir—to that small town in Pennsylvania from which Mr. Poindexter and his daughter registered.”

Gryce’s head lifted a fraction. “Ah. And what did you learn there? Something directly to the point?”

“I learned this: that John Poindexter, father of Eva, had for a friend in early life one Amos Cadwalader.”

“Amos,” Gryce repeated, the name landing with weight.

“Yes—and that this Amos had a son, Felix.”

“Ah!”

“You see, sir, we must be on the right track. Coincidences can’t extend through half a dozen names.”

“You are right. It is I who made a mistake in drawing conclusions too readily. Let us hear about this Amos. You gathered something of his history, no doubt.”

“All that was possible, sir. It is closely woven in with Poindexter’s, and presents one feature which may occasion you no surprise, but which came near nonplussing me. Though the father of Felix, his name was not Adams. I say was not, for he has been dead six months. It was Cadwalader. And Felix went by the name of Cadwalader, too, in the early days of which I have to tell—he and a sister whose name—”

“Well?”

“Was Evelyn.”

“Sweetwater,” Gryce said slowly, as if placing a careful finger on a bruised spot in the case, “you are an admirable fellow. So the mystery is ours.”

“The history, not the mystery; that still holds. Shall I relate what I know of those two families?”

“At once. I am as anxious as if I were again twenty-three and had been in your shoes instead of my own for the last three days.”

Sweetwater began, and as he spoke the little office seemed to take on the feel of another place—railroad platforms, a village street, a churchyard on a hill. Gryce, who had been trained to trust what could be measured, found himself forced to attend to what could only be remembered. The past came in on the present like a refrain returning in a song: altered by time, but unmistakable.

“John Poindexter and Amos Cadwalader were, in their early life, bosom friends. They came from Scotland together and settled in Montgomery in the thirties. Both married there, but Poindexter prospered from the first, while Cadwalader had little ability to support a family and was on the verge of bankruptcy when the war broke out and he enlisted.

“Poindexter remained at home, caring for his own affairs and for Cadwalader’s two children, whom he took into his own house. I say his own family, but he had no family, save a wife, up to the spring of ’80. Then a daughter was born to him—the Eva who has just married Thomas Adams.

“Cadwalader, fitted for army life, rose to be a captain; but he was taken prisoner in one of the later battles and confined in Libby Prison, where he suffered like a man flayed alive, till he was released in 1865 by exchange. Broken, old, and crushed, he returned home, and no one in Montgomery ever forgot the day he stepped from the cars and took his way up the main street.

“For not having been fortunate—or unfortunate—enough to receive communication from home, he advanced with cheerful haste, not knowing his only daughter lay dead in his friend’s house, and that it was for her funeral the people were collecting in the green square at the end of the street. He was so pale and decrepit few knew him. But one old neighbor recognized him and led him into a quiet place and there told him he had arrived too late to see his darling alive.

“The shock did not prostrate him. It seemed to nerve him and put vigor into his limbs. He proceeded almost at a run to Poindexter’s house and arrived just as the funeral cortège was issuing from the door.

“Then came a strange thing. The young girl had been laid on an open bier, carried by six sturdy lads. As the father’s eye fell on her young body under its black pall, a cry of mortal anguish escaped him, and he sank on his knees in the line of the procession.

“At the same minute another cry went up—this time from behind the bier—and John Poindexter was seen reeling at the side of Felix Cadwalader, who alone of all present, though the youngest, retained self-possession. Meanwhile the bereaved father threw himself at the bier and began tearing at the pall, desperate to see the face of her he had left in rosy health four years before. But he was stopped—not by Poindexter, who vanished from the scene—but by Felix, cold and severe-looking, who stood like a guard behind his sister.

“Reaching out a hand so white it startled the eye, he laid it in a prohibitory way on the pall—as if saying no. When his father would have struggled on, it was Felix who controlled him and drew him to the side where, a moment before, Poindexter had stood. After that the bearers moved on.

“But the dramatic scene was not over. Near the churchyard another procession issued from an adjoining street. Evelyn’s young lover—Kissam—who had died almost simultaneously, was brought and laid at her side. Not in the same grave: all noticed that. Kissam had shot himself upon hearing of Evelyn’s death.

“Whether Cadwalader grasped then the meaning of the double burial, or whether explanations were deferred till he and Felix walked away together from the grave, has never transpired. From that minute till they both left town next day, no one had any word with him save Poindexter—whom he went once to see—and young Kissam’s mother, who came once to see him. Like a phantom he rose upon the sight of Montgomery, and like a phantom he disappeared, never to be seen again—unless the story is true told twenty years ago by one of the village lads.

“He said that six years after the tragedy, one evening as he hurried by the churchyard, anxious to reach home before dark, he came upon the figure of a man standing beside a grave with a little child in his arms. The man was tall, long-bearded, terrifying. His attitude—as the lad described it—was one of defiance, if not cursing. High in his right hand he held the child, almost as if he would hurl him at the village under the hill.

“The moment passed quickly, but the boy, now a man, never forgot it, nor admitted it was anything but real. The description fit Amos Cadwalader. And the shoe of a little child was found next morning on the grave of Cadwalader’s daughter, Evelyn. Many believed the lad truly saw the old soldier, who for some mysterious reason chose nightfall for this visit. Others called it a freak of imagination fed by romances. For, they argued, had it been Cadwalader, why did he not show himself at John Poindexter’s house—old friend, now with a little daughter and no wife—who could have made him comfortable?

“Among these doubters was Poindexter himself, though some said he looked odd while making the remark, as if he spoke from habit rather than heart. Indeed, since Evelyn died in his house, he never showed the same interest in the Cadwaladers. But then he was occupied with great affairs, while the Cadwaladers, except for their griefs, were considered insignificant—unless we except Felix, who from childhood made himself feared even by grown people, though he never exceeded decorum in speech or gesture.

“A year ago news came to Montgomery of Amos Cadwalader’s death, but no particulars about his family or burial place. That’s all I’ve been able to glean.”

Gryce sat with his hands quiet, as if any motion might disturb the arrangement of these names—Poindexter, Cadwalader, Felix, Evelyn—set now like dishes in a row, each with its own savor, each connected by invisible threads. The past, once spoken aloud, had a sound of its own; and in that sound was something troublingly alive, as if the old griefs were still walking.

“Have you any reason to believe Evelyn’s death was not a natural one?” he asked at last.

“No, sir. I interviewed the old mother of the young man who shot himself out of grief. If any doubt had existed about what drove her son to a violent end, she couldn’t have concealed it. But there seemed none. Evelyn was always delicate, and when quick consumption carried her off no one marvelled. Her lover adored her and could not live without her, so he shot himself. There was no mystery about the tragedy—except that it seemed to sever an old friendship once firm as a rock. I mean that between the Poindexters and Cadwaladers.”

“Yet in this tragedy in —— Street we see them brought together again. Thomas Adams marries Eva Poindexter. But who is Thomas Adams? You have not mentioned him in this history.”

“Not unless he was the child held aloft over Evelyn’s grave.”

“Humph! That seems far-fetched. What did you learn about him in Montgomery? Is he known there?”

“As well as any stranger can be who spends his time courting a young girl. He came to Montgomery a few months ago from some foreign city—Paris, I think—and being gifted with every personal charm to please a cultivated young woman, he speedily won Eva Poindexter’s affections, and her father’s esteem. But not every one shares that opinion. There are those with much to say about his anxious and unsettled eye.”

“Naturally; he could not marry all their daughters,” Gryce returned dryly, then sobered. “But this history—it is meagre. It hints at something deeply tragic, but does not supply the key. A girl dead thirty years. A father vanished. A brother who from Cadwalader has become an Adams. An Eva whose name, as well as that of the long-buried Evelyn, was heard in constant repetition in the place where the murdered Felix lay with those inscrutable lines in his own writing clenched between his teeth! It is a snarl—a perfect snarl—and we have not yet pulled the right thread. But we will. I’m not going to be baffled in my old age by difficulties I would have laughed at a dozen years ago.”

“But this right thread? How shall we know it among the fifty I see entangled?”

“First—find the whereabouts of this young couple. But didn’t you tell me you had done so? That you know where they are?”

“Yes. I learned from the postmaster in Montgomery that a letter addressed to Mrs. Thomas Adams had been sent from his office to Belleville, Long Island.”

“Ah! I know the place.”

“And wishing to be assured the letter was not a pretense, I sent a telegram to the postmaster at Belleville. Here is his answer. It is unequivocal: ‘Mr. Poindexter of Montgomery, Pa. Mr. Thomas Adams and Mrs. Adams of the same place have been at the Bedell House in this place five days.’”

“Very good; then we have them.” Gryce rose, the decision in him hardening like a cooled sauce finally setting. “Be ready to start for Belleville by one o’clock sharp. And mind, Sweetwater—keep your wits alert and your tongue still. Remember, as yet we are feeling our way blindfold, and must continue so till some kind hand tears away the bandage. Go! I have a letter to write; you may send in a boy at the end of five minutes.”

The letter went to Miss Butterworth. It had in it none of the kitchen warmth she might have preferred—no courteous padding, no idle garnish. It was all instruction, like a recipe written by a stern hand: exact, timed, and with signs agreed upon in advance.

Have you sufficient interest in the outcome of a certain matter to take a short journey into the country? I leave town at 1 P.M. for Belleville, Long Island. If you choose to do the same, you will find me at the Bedell House, in that town, early in the afternoon. If you enjoy novels, take one with you, and let me see you reading it on the hotel piazza at five o’clock. I may be reading too; if so, and my choice is a book, all is well, and you may devour your story in peace. But if I lay aside my book and take up a paper, devote but one eye to your story and turn the other on the people who are passing you. If after you have done so, you leave your book open, I shall understand that you fail to recognize these persons. But if you shut the volume, you may expect to see me also fold up my newspaper; for by so doing you will have signaled me that you have identified the young man and woman you saw leaving Mr. Adams’s house on the fatal afternoon of your first entrance.  
E. G.

Late that afternoon the Belleville hotel lay in its accustomed hush between waves: the sea breathing in long blue draughts below the bluff; the beach flickering with parasols like bright fish-scales; the village street clattering now and then with a cart-wheel; and, over it all, the piazza’s wicker-chair creak and the soft percussion of tennis balls on the lawn—small, domestic sounds that made the place feel like an enormous summer kitchen kept in airy order.

In a far corner of that piazza sat a well-dressed lady of uncertain age with a book held too close to her face to be of much use. Her eyes—quick, competent eyes, the sort that could measure out a teaspoon without looking—kept slipping away from print to people. She cared little for children; their shrieks and pattering feet were only noise in the soundscape. But a young pair leaning together, or a tall, imperious old gentleman moving like a metronome through the summer crowd, drew her gaze as surely as the smell of browned butter draws a hungry cook.

At the other end of the piazza a spruce but feeble octogenarian sat as if he belonged to the furniture, absorbed first in a novel and then, midway through a smile, in a newspaper opened wide to the sun. He shifted it a fraction—as if merely to shade his eyes, as if not at all to see.

Two gentlemen had come out through the great doorway between them. The lady’s book snapped shut. She rose, then checked herself, embarrassment tightening her shoulders, and moved out onto the lawn with a tiny dog under her hand. She stroked it with an uncertain touch that betrayed no affection and plenty of fear—like someone forced to handle an ingredient she mistrusted. The dog wriggled, fascinated in spite of itself.

The gentlemen, meanwhile, walked as though indifferent to her existence. One was old—hard old, of a Scotch toughness that holds its line past eighty: upright spine, neat mouth, a calm, cold eye that fell on the world without asking anything of it. The other was young and strong, yet carried his strength like a burden; his gaze shrank from every passer-by as if the very chatter of the piazza were an accusation. They stopped within a few feet of the newspaper reader, close enough that the salt air carried their words the way it carries the smell of tobacco and varnished wood.

“I don’t think it does me any good,” the young man said, and the despair in him made even that placid hotel sound suddenly too loud. “I am wretched when with her, and doubly wretched when I try to forget myself for a moment out of her sight. I think we had better go back. I had rather sit where she can see me than have her wonder—Oh, I will be careful; but you must remember how unnerving is the very silence I am obliged to keep about what is destroying us all. I am nearly as ill as she.”

They drew away. The old man with the paper turned a page with a slow, innocent rustle.

Five minutes later they returned, retracing their steps as mechanically as men pacing a narrow galley. This time the elder spoke lower, his discretion dropping the words into the surrounding sounds so that only fragments surfaced.

“Escape? … South coast—she will forget to watch you for—… a clinging nature—impetuous, but foolishly affectionate—you know that—no danger—found out—time—… a cheerful home—courage—happiness—… all forgotten.”

The young man made a motion of the hand that denied every hope, and they moved on.

Miss Butterworth—for it was Miss Butterworth, unmistakable in her anxious composure—still bent above the little dog. Yet her true attention had turned, sharp as a pin, toward two girls on a bench nearby who chatted with the careless appetite of youth, seasoning their talk with envy and romance as thoughtlessly as they might salt soup.

“But she is! I tell you she is sick enough to have a nurse and a doctor. I caught a glimpse of her as I was going by her room yesterday, and I never saw two such big eyes or such pale cheeks. Then, look at him! He must just adore her, for he won’t speak to another woman, and just moves about in that small, hot room all day. I wonder if they are bride and groom? They are young enough, and if you have noticed her clothes—”

“Oh, don’t talk about clothes. I saw her the first day she came, and was the victim of despair until she suddenly got sick and so couldn’t wear those wonderful waists and jackets. I felt like a dowdy when I saw that pale blue—”

“Oh, well, blue becomes blondes. You would look like a fright in it. I didn’t care about her clothes, but I did feel that it was all up with us if she chose to talk, or even to smile, upon the few men that are good enough to stay out a week in this place. Yet she isn’t a beauty; she has not a good nose, nor a handsome eye, nor even an irreproachable complexion. It must be her mouth, which is lovely, or her walk—did you notice her walk? It was just as if she were floating; that is, before she fell down in that faint. I wonder why she fainted. Nobody was doing anything, not even her husband. But perhaps that was what troubled her. I noticed that for some cause he was looking very serious—and when she had tried to attract his attention two or three times and failed, she just fell from her chair to the floor. That roused him. He has hardly left her since.”

“I don’t think they look very happy, do you, for so rich and handsome a couple?”

“Perhaps he is dissipated. I have noticed that the old gentleman never leaves them.”

“Well, well, he may be dissipated; handsome men are very apt to be. But I wouldn’t care if—”

The dog yelped and bolted across the grass. Miss Butterworth, without meaning to, had pinched him—an involuntary tightening of fingers at the sound of that flippant “dissipated,” that easy word laid over suffering like a garnish meant to distract. The girls laughed and moved away, their voices thinning into the general hum.

For a moment Miss Butterworth had nothing to do with her hands, and no harmless object to pretend at. Then a young man crossed her path and, without breaking stride, bent his head as if the very air here had ears.

“You recognize them? There is no mistake?” he whispered.

“None; the one this way is the young man I saw leave Mr. Adams’s house, and the other is the old gentleman who came in afterward.”

“Mr. Gryce advises you to return home. He is going to arrest the young man.”

And Sweetwater passed on, swallowed by the hotel’s moving tableau.

Miss Butterworth found a seat and sat down as if her knees had been cut from under her. In her mind’s eye she saw, not the beach or sails or bright dresses, but a sick young wife with those “two such big eyes,” sinking under a blow she could not name, in a place full of people and yet without one woman fit to stand beside her. The father—Mr. Poindexter—did not look like a man who could cradle a fainting head or soften a bruised heart. There was something icy under his polish, an old-fashioned correctness that chilled sympathy the way a tin ladle chills hot broth.

An impulse seized her—simple, domestic, almost maternal: to be near the girl when the worst came, if only to steady her own aching conscience at having played her part in this business.

She rose to look for Mr. Gryce. He was gone. Searching the piazza she caught sight of the two gentlemen just vanishing around a corner toward a small smoking-room. She could not follow there. Instead she went upstairs.

In the upper hall she met a maid and asked, carefully, for Mrs. Adams.

“Mrs. Adams is sick, ma’am,” the girl answered, in the soft tone hotels keep for illness, and pointed out the door at the end of a long corridor.

The hall ended in a window, and beneath it a sofa. Miss Butterworth took her seat there with a strange sense that circumstance—like a careful housekeeper—had put what she needed within reach. The air was cooler here, threaded with the sea’s steady hush, a merciful sound that steadied the mind more than any prayer.

Instantly, through the open transom over Mrs. Adams’s door, a sweet voice broke out, thin with fear.

“Where is Tom? Oh, where is Tom? Why does he leave me? I’m afraid of what he may be tempted to do or say down on those great piazzas alone.”

“Mr. Poindexter is with him,” replied another voice—measured, but kind, as if it had been trained to soothe the sick the way one stirs a sauce slowly so it will not break. “Mr. Adams was getting very tired, and your father persuaded him to go down and have a smoke.”

“I must get up; indeed I must get up. Oh! the camphor—the—”

There was a bustle; the faint, helpless scuffle of bodies moving to catch a falling one. The sound of a chair leg scraped once, sharp as a knife on plate. Miss Butterworth stared at the door with misery in her eyes.

Meanwhile, down in that small smoking-room—retired, shuttered against the piazza’s bright life—the truth struck like an axe in a butcher’s stall.

The two gentlemen had lit cigars and sat in forced attitudes, smoking not from pleasure but from habit and the hollow courtesy men offer one another when they have no better language. The tobacco’s sweet-bitter smell thickened the air, laying a fog over the room.

The door opened. An old man came in—strangely serious, strangely at home, and yet as much a guest here as they. He shut the door behind him with a finality that sounded louder than it should have in such a small space.

“Gentlemen,” he announced at once, “I am Detective Gryce of the New York police, and I am here—but I see that one of you at least knows why I am here.”

One? Both. At that authoritative tone—born of years of speaking life-altering words in closed rooms—the young man collapsed as if the muscles had been cut from his spine. His companion did not yield so fully, but the blow landed in him too; a brief tightening about the mouth, a slight withdrawal of the eyes, showed destruction of hope as plainly as smoke shows flame.

Seeing himself understood, Mr. Gryce did not linger.

Directing his attention to Mr. Adams, he said, and there was feeling in him now because the misery before him was naked and unmistakable: “You are wanted in New York by Coroner D——, whose business it is to hold an inquest over the remains of Mr. Felix Adams, of whose astonishing death you are undoubtedly informed. As you and your wife were seen leaving that gentleman’s house a few minutes before he expired, you are naturally regarded as valuable witnesses in determining whether his death was one of suicide or murder.”

The words were not shaped as accusation, yet they fell with the weight of one. A dark flush of shame rose in the young man’s face, displacing the livid terror that had made him look for an instant like a spirit already lost. He could not speak.

Mr. Poindexter drew himself as though to turn aside the blow with dignity—when the hall outside sent in a new sound, urgent and domestic, the kind that pierces any man no matter how grand the crisis: someone calling for Mr. Adams, saying his wife had fainted again and required him.

The young husband started as if stabbed. He cast a look of despair at Mr. Poindexter, then thrust his hand against the door as if to hold it shut against every footfall in the world. He sank on his knees before Mr. Gryce.

“She knows! She suspects! Her nature is so sensitive,” he gasped. “Don’t, don’t disturb her! She is an angel, a saint from heaven. Let me bear the blame—he was my brother—let me go with you, but leave her in ignorance—”

Mr. Gryce, with a vivid sense of justice and a reluctant compassion, laid his hand on the young man’s arm. “Say nothing,” he enjoined. “My memory is good, and I would rather hear nothing from your lips. As for your wife, my warrant does in no way include her; and if you promise to come with me quietly, I will even let you bid her adieu—so that you do it in my presence.”

The change that passed over Adams’s face at this was startling—like a sudden hardening, as when heat is withdrawn from sugar and it turns from syrup to brittle. He rose slowly and took his stand beside Mr. Poindexter, who had scarcely moved in limb or feature since the detective entered.

“What have you against me?” Adams demanded, and his voice rang with a courage that seemed to arrive only because there was no longer any choice. “Of what am I accused? I want you to tell me. I had rather you would tell me in so many words. I cannot leave in peace until you do.”

Mr. Poindexter moved at that and cast his son-in-law a glance—half warning, half suspicion—yet Adams ignored it. He kept his eyes on the detective.

Mr. Gryce quietly took out his warrant.

At that instant the door shook again, as if the corridor itself were pleading. Adams’s mouth twisted. “Lock it!” he commanded hoarsely. “Don’t let any one pass that door, even if it is to bring the tidings of my wife’s death.”

Mr. Gryce reached out and turned the key.

Adams opened the paper the detective had given him. His eyes, blood-shot and strained, roved over the few lines as if they were written in a dialect he could not speak. Beside him Mr. Poindexter drew Mr. Gryce’s attention with a look that asked its question without sound. His lips formed three noiseless words:

“For murder? Him?”

Mr. Gryce’s slight bow answered. At the same moment a long-drawn sigh escaped the young man—an exhalation like steam leaving a pot when the lid is lifted at last.

Mr. Poindexter’s upper lip lifted curiously, showing his teeth for an instant with a hint of something unpleasant, and then he sank back into his previous immobility, as if he could afford to be carved in stone.

“I am indebted to you,” Adams said, and there was a bleak steadiness now in him. “Now I know where I stand. I am quite ready to go with you and stand trial, if such be deemed necessary by the officials in New York.”

He turned abruptly on Mr. Poindexter with almost an air of command. “You will watch over Eva. Not like a father, sir, but like a mother. You will be at her side when she wakes, and, if possible, leave her only when she sleeps. Do not let her suffer—not too much. No newspapers, no gossiping women. Watch! watch! as I would watch, and when I come back—for I will come back, will I not?” he appealed to Mr. Gryce. “My prayers will bless you and—”

A sob caught him. He turned his face aside a moment, mastered himself, then took the detective’s arm with a composure that looked almost unnatural.

“I do not want to say good-by to my wife. I cannot bear it. I had rather go straight from here without another glance at her unconscious face. When I have told my story—for I shall tell it to the first man who asks me—I may find courage to write her. Meanwhile, get me away as quickly as you can. Time enough for the world to know my shame to-morrow.”

Mr. Gryce went to the window that overlooked the piazza and tapped. A young man stepped in at once.

“Here is a gentleman,” Mr. Gryce said, “who finds himself forced to return in great haste to New York. See that he gets to the train in time, without fuss and without raising the least comment. I will follow with his portmanteau. Mr. Poindexter, you are now at liberty to attend your suffering daughter.”

He unlocked the door with a turn of the key. The corridor’s hotel noises—footsteps, a muffled voice, the far-off gull-cry riding above everything—rushed back in, and the painful little room released its prisoners. One of the most bitter scenes of Mr. Gryce’s long life was over; upstairs, behind a transom, the sweet voice that had called for Tom drifted on, unheard by the man being hurried toward the train.

Mr. Gryce had never been squeamish about the small arts that make a confession ripen. He had said, with that blunt honesty which so often cloaked his stratagems, that he meant to follow Mr. Adams. And follow him he did—so close that they rode the same train and breathed the same compartment air, warmed by coal heat and damp wool.

The car had its own domestic life: a woman unwrapped a lunch of hard-boiled eggs whose sulphur rose and fell with the sway of the coach; a man cracked a paper cone of roasted chestnuts, the sweet smoke of them tangling with the soot-laden draft that slid along the floorboards. The wheels stitched their steady, metallic rhythm into every thought, a rough music that could soothe or torment depending on what a man carried in his head. Gryce, who had spent his life listening for the false note in a voice, the hesitation in a footfall, gave his ears to it all: the clack of rails, the hiss at the couplings, the low, tired mutter of city-bound men. Modern noise, he thought—not in words so lofty, but in sensation—had a way of making the mind coarse, as if a delicate palate were forced to live always on tinned fare.

At a distance he watched the young man’s face, handsome still even under the bruise of public suspicion. An hour earlier it had writhed with an agony almost indecent in its openness; now it had settled into a bleak calm, like broth gone cold—clear, still, and somehow more ominous for its quiet. Gryce found himself, against his will, measuring the change, tasting it as one tastes a sauce to find what has been added.

Where did the composure come from? From despair? From some inward decision, set and salted? Or from a mind so far withdrawn that the eyes no longer registered the stares they drew? The young man’s brow brooded; his gaze stared through the car as if the present were a language he could no longer speak.

The detective, seasoned with every variety of criminal, felt an unaccustomed tug—something like conscience, something like age—touch him in the ribs. Not pity exactly. But the sense that he was looking at a man who had been raised, like himself, among certain words and manners, and yet had found those words failing him at the moment they should have saved him.

In Brooklyn Mr. Gryce rose and rejoined his prisoner. The young man scarcely lifted his eyes. The station roared with iron and shouted orders; the air tasted of steam and wet stone. In another hour they were at Police Headquarters, where the clamor changed its shape—less open, more oppressive, the constant door-slam and echoing tread that make even the innocent speak too fast.

The questioning began.

He did not attempt to evade it. If anything, he leaned toward it, as if speech were the only utensil left to him and he must use it before it was taken away. But what he was eager to set on the table was not the dish the police expected.

“I did not kill my brother,” he said, and though his voice held steady, it carried a tremor like a spoon against glass. “But I was—God help me—the incidental cause of his death.”

Then, with the strained deliberation of a man choosing each word because the wrong one could turn his life into something unrecognizable, he began.

“My name is Cadwalader,” he said. “Not Adams. My father was Scotch by birth. He became a citizen in Pennsylvania and settled, when he was young and married, in a place called Montgomery. He had two children then. One died very early. The other was my brother—Felix.”

The name came out as if it caught on his throat.

“I am the child of a later marriage. When I was born he lived in Harrisburg, but we left there before I was old enough to keep anything but shadows. My mother died when I was still an infant. I was sent very early to the Old World—the country my father came from. When I returned—this year, only this year—I found my father dying. And my brother grown, and with money.” His mouth tightened. “A great deal of money. I had been led to believe he would share it with me. But after my father was laid away, Felix”—again that effort—“came to New York, and I was left to wander without any settled promise, without a foundation to build a future on.”

His gaze fixed on some point beyond the interrogators’ shoulders, as if the scene he described were playing there, more vivid than the room.

“While I wandered, I came upon the town where my father had lived in his early youth. I sought out his old friends. In the house of one who had crossed from Scotland with my father, I met a young lady—”

Here his voice betrayed him. It did not break into tears; it simply altered, as broth changes when the first salt hits it, showing what had been in it all along.

“—Evelyn.”

He said it with a poignant exactness, as if any less care would profane her.

“I became interested—too quickly, perhaps, but I cannot pretend it was shallow. I wished to marry her. Yet I had no money, no business, no home. Nothing to offer but what I was. I knew it. And still I could not release the hope. So I wrote to my brother—Felix Cadwalader, or Felix Adams, as he preferred to be called in later years. That change of name had reasons—family reasons—unconnected with what has happened now. I told him I had become attached to a young girl of good family and some wealth. I asked him to settle on me a sum that would let me marry with some self-respect.”

His lips curled, not in humor but in something bitterly akin to it.

“My only answer was what he had given before: a vague promise. Nothing definite. But youth is hopeful; youth can be daring. I decided to make her mine without further parley, in the belief—foolish, perhaps—that when Felix saw her, her beauty, her… endearing qualities, he would be moved to grant what he had denied.”

He drew a breath as if the air itself resisted him.

“And here is the fault I reproach myself with most: I married her without taking her—nor her father—into my confidence. They thought me well off. Possibly rich. Mr. Poindexter is a man of means, but he is ambitious. If he had known I had nothing but the clothes I wore and a mere trifle in my pocket, he would have stopped it. He believes his daughter worth a millionaire’s devotion.” His voice softened on the last words. “As she is.”

He looked down, and for an instant his hands, those hands that had signed a marriage register, seemed foreign to him.

“Felix was notified. He did not come. He did not forbid it. So it was done quietly—in a hotel parlor—with strangers as witnesses. Then, with hopes—vague hopes—and fears that were less vague, I prepared to take my young wife to my brother’s house. I thought that a man hardened by bachelorhood could not be so impervious as to fail to recognize what Evelyn was: a woman deserving every consideration.”

His jaw set. The steady noise of the building—voices in corridors, a distant typewriter’s clatter like hard rain—seemed to press around his words.

“But I had counted without my host. Two days after the ceremony that made us one, I took her to the house—yes, the house you speak of now with such… notoriety. There I found that I had only seen one facet of Felix, and it the least obdurate of his nature.”

His eyes lifted at last, not pleading, simply stating.

“He was brilliant as steel. And hard. Not only did he profess himself unmoved by my wife’s charms, he spoke as if love and marriage were follies—fruit of unoccupied minds, he said—pastimes unworthy of men with talents such as ours. Then he turned his back on us.”

The words came faster, as if the scene had finally begun to run of its own accord.

“I—I was moved by an anger little short of frenzy. I abused him. I said things—things I had no right to say to any man, brother or not. He was so little prepared for it that he crouched as if under blows. And then, minute by minute, he lost what control he had. He caught up a dagger lying close at hand—”

At that, his voice dropped, as if even sound should not be too loud around the memory.

“—and cried, ‘You want my money? Well, then, take it!’ And he stabbed himself to the heart. One desperate blow.”

Silence, thick as congealed fat, filled the room for a moment. Even the building’s noises seemed to draw back, leaving only the faint, relentless throb of the city outside—industry, traffic, iron upon iron—indifferent to whether a family’s name was spoken true or twisted into another shape.

He swallowed, and when he spoke again there was a weary resignation, the knowledge that certain experiences, like certain flavors, cannot be carried into another mouth by any explanation.

“I fear I shall not be believed,” he said. “But that is the story of this crime, gentlemen.”

Despair is a thing men speak of as if it were a clean word, trimmed and sensible—yet in the room where Felix Cadwalader had once contrived his silent tricks of steel and color, despair had a smell: stale lamp-oil, cold iron, paper dust, and that faint, sickly sweetness which clings to rooms where fear has been breathed too long. Outside, the city went on grinding its teeth—wagon-rims, factory whistles, the impatient clatter of hooves on hard street—but in here every sound seemed amplified: the inspector’s chair creaking, a cough swallowed, the soft rasp of a sleeve across a tabletop. Even silence had its own acoustics, and it pressed on the ears like wool.

If such tragedies had not happened before—unpremeditated, violent, absurd in their suddenness—then history had lied since the first hearthfire. And yet no man present, whatever he remembered of Bartow’s strange dumb-show and its stubborn refusal to “tally” with the tale now being poured out, could quite accept Thomas Adams’s account of his brother’s end. Distrust lived in their eyes. The young man felt it as surely as a hand laid on the nape of the neck. His head drooped; the vigor of his frame seemed, for the moment, only a shell carrying a heart that had begun to bruise itself from within.

“I shall add nothing to and take nothing from what I have said,” he muttered, with a doggedness that had in it more fatigue than pride. “Make of it what you will.”

The inspector, conducting the inquiry with a dry steadiness, let his gaze slide—half appeal, half doubt—toward Mr. Gryce. The detective’s face did not change, but his attention sharpened, like a knife lifted to the light.

“We are sorry you have taken such a resolution,” Mr. Gryce observed. “Many things remain unexplained, Mr. Adams. For instance—if your brother slew himself in the manner you describe—why did you leave the house so precipitately? Why no alarm? Why not proclaim at once that he was your brother?”

“You need not answer,” the inspector interposed, as if out of habit as much as fairness. “No man is called upon to incriminate himself in this free and independent country.”

A smile crossed Thomas Adams’s lips—brief, bleak, like the last warmth from coals that have almost gone out. “Incrimination is not what I fear now,” he said, his voice roughened by some inward choke. “From the way you all look at me, I see I am lost. I have no means of proving my story.”

The inspector stared, taken aback by the sound of it—an innocent man’s despair, raw and uncalculated.

“You forget,” he returned at length, “that you had your wife with you. She can corroborate your words. She will prove, no doubt, an invaluable witness in your favor.”

“My wife!” The words broke from him like a sob forced through clenched teeth. “Must she be dragged into this—so sick, so weak? It would kill her, sir. She loves me—she—”

“Was she with you in Mr. Adams’s study? Did she see him lift the dagger against his own breast?”

“No.”

That single syllable fell with an odd effect—like a heavy pan set down in a quiet kitchen, ending a quarrel before it can begin again. And with it, strangely, he seemed to gather a kind of desperate courage, as if truth, even when it ruined him, was still a thing he could hold between his hands.

“She had fainted several moments before,” he went on, hurried now, driven by the need to lay everything out plainly while he still could. “The altercation between my brother and myself—at its height—she could not bear it. She did not see the final act. And—gentlemen—I might as well speak the truth; I have nothing to gain by silence—she finds it as difficult as you do to believe Mr. Adams struck himself. I have tried—God knows how I have tried—to impress it upon her; but what can I hope from the world when the wife of my bosom—an angel, a creature who loves me—cannot be a witness for me? She is too conscientious. Too true to what she believes. I should lose—she would die—”

Mr. Gryce lifted a hand as if to still him, but the flood would not be dammed.

“Spare me, sirs! Spare my wife! Write me down guilty—anything—rather than force that young creature to speak—”

The inspector cut through the appeal with a briskness that was not cruelty so much as the hard need to keep order in a room that was beginning to sway.

“Have you read the newspapers for the last few days?” he asked.

“I? Yes—yes, sir. How could I help it?” Thomas Adams answered, as if the print itself had been a torment he could not stop touching. “Blood is blood; the man was my brother. I left him dying. I was anxious—naturally anxious—and I saw my own danger. I read them, of course.”

“Then you know he was found with a large cross upon his breast—a cross that was once on the wall. How came it to be torn down? Who put it on his bosom?”

“I did,” he said at once. “I am not a Catholic; Felix was. Seeing him dying without absolution, without—without what you call extreme unction—I thought of the holy cross. I tore down the only one I saw and placed it in his arms.”

“A pious act,” the inspector allowed. “Did he recognize it?”

“I cannot say.” Thomas Adams’s eyes flicked away, as though he could not bear to look again at any image of his brother’s last moments. “I had my fainting wife to look after. She occupied all my thoughts.”

“I see. And you carried her out; you were so absorbed in caring for her that you did not observe Mr. Adams’s valet—”

“He’s innocent, sir,” Thomas Adams burst out, quick as a man flinging himself between a blade and another’s skin. “Whatever people may think, he had nothing to do with this crime—”

“You did not observe him,” the inspector persisted, “standing in the doorway and watching you?”

The inspector knew well enough that Bartow had not stood there at all, but in the hidden opening above. Still, there are traps set not with malice but with purpose, and this was one. Thomas Adams went pale in a new way; beads of sweat rose along his forehead like dew on chilled metal. He fought to hold his ground.

“But that man is crazy,” he cried, “and deaf-and-dumb besides—or so the papers say. Surely his testimony is valueless. You would not confront me with him?”

“We confront you with no one,” the inspector said. “We only ask you a question. You did not observe the valet, then?”

“No, sir.”

“Nor understand the mystery of the colored lights?”

“No, sir.”

“Nor of the plate of steel and the other contrivances with which your brother enlivened his solitude?”

“I do not follow you, sir.” But the tone had altered; there was a faint hitch in it now, like a spoon catching on the bottom of a pot.

The inspector leaned forward. His fingers began a soft, irritating tattoo on the table, a domestic sound made harsh by the room’s tension—like someone drumming impatiently while another tries to remember a lost word.

“I see,” he said, “that these complications—which have disturbed us and made necessary this long delay in the collection of testimony—have not entered into the crime as you describe it. That is possible. But there is still a circumstance requiring explanation; a little circumstance, and yet important. Your wife mentioned it to you as soon as she became conscious. I allude to the half dozen or more words written by your brother immediately preceding his death.”

At this, Thomas Adams’s face went blank, as though the very faculty of comprehension had been abruptly taken from him.

“The paper on which they were written has been found,” the inspector continued. “That it was a factor in your quarrel is evident, since she regretted that it had been left behind—and he—Do you know where we found this paper?”

The young man’s eyes lifted, but there was no intelligence in them, only a wide, stricken vacancy. The mere mention of that scrap of writing—those few words, those strokes of ink—seemed to strip him of strength. He shook his head. His hand rose, groping, and failed to grasp the bit of paper the inspector held out.

Then something in him broke with a soundless violence, like a rope snapping in the dark.

“Enough!” he cried. “I cannot hold out—with no other support than a wicked lie. I killed my brother—for reasons as good as any man ever had for killing another. But I shall not impart them. I would rather be tried for murder and hanged.”

It was a complete collapse, made more pitiful by the contrast between the confession and the man who uttered it—herculean shoulders, fine features pinched hard by pain. Mr. Gryce rose slowly and moved nearer, taking his stand at the prisoner’s side as if to steady him against the sudden inward fall. The inspector did not move; he kept beating his fingers upon the table as though the rhythm, however small, helped him keep the world from sliding.

And then, as if the room itself had shifted, Thomas Adams’s head snapped toward the door. His whole frame trembled.

“What is that?” he demanded.

“Nothing,” began the inspector.

But the door flew open, and the word died.

A woman—white as a wraith, and yet lit from within by some fierce, holy resolve—broke free from the grasp of a man who tried to hold her back. She stood before them, palpitating, as if she had run not twenty miles but through fire. It was Mrs. Adams: young, frail, an invalid brought from Belleville, whom her husband had left only three hours earlier. Her delicacy was so exquisite that in any other scene she might have seemed unearthly—except for the unmistakable stamp of human anguish in her face, and the way her breath came in small, painful drafts, like a bird’s wings striking against a cage.

“What is he saying?” she cried, the words trembling from her as steam trembles from a pot just lifted from the flame. “Oh, I know well what he is saying. He is saying that he killed his brother—that he held the dagger which rid the world of a monster of whose wickedness none knew. But you must not heed him. Indeed you must not heed him. He is innocent. I—his wife—have come twenty miles, from a bed of weakness and suffering, to tell you so. He—”

A hand rose—gentle, but firm—and covered her mouth. She looked up and met her husband’s eyes, which were filled with a frantic appeal. For a moment the room heard nothing but the small hiss of breathing, the far-off grind of the city outside, and that relentless industrial whine that seemed to live even in the walls. Then she answered his look with one that went straight through every man who saw it—no melodrama, no bargaining; only love, and the terrible courage it can breed.

She laid her own hand upon his and drew it away, softly, as if moving a utensil from a child’s grip before it cuts.

“It is too late, Tom. I must speak,” she said. Her voice was thin, but it held, like a clear broth made from bones and patience. “My father, my own weakness, your peremptory commands—none could keep me at Belleville when I knew you had been brought here. And shall I stop now, in the presence of these men who have heard your words and may believe them? No. That would be cowardice, unworthy of our love and the true lives we hope to lead together.”

She turned slightly, addressing them all. The men leaned forward without meaning to, as if the faintness of her speech compelled their bodies to become ears.

“Sirs,” she said, and with each word her breath seemed to cost more, “I know my husband to be innocent, because the hand that held the dagger was mine. I killed Felix Cadwalader!”

The horror of such a moment is not fully known until after it has passed. In the instant itself, time feels suspended, like a spoon held above a cup, the drip delayed. Not a man moved—not even Thomas Adams—yet a slow pallor spread across cheeks and brows, visible proof of what those words had done, spoken from lips shaped for gentleness.

Only when a cry came from the doorway—“Catch her! She is falling!”—did the spell break.

Thomas Adams sprang forward, as if life had been poured back into him. He caught her before she struck the floor and bore her to a sofa, laying her down with a tenderness that seemed to contradict the entire ruin of the day. He hovered above her, one hand upon her unconscious head, and when he looked up again he looked altered—no longer the man collapsing under accusation, but a man suddenly given something to protect that was larger than his own body.

Mr. Gryce, watching with the sharp, patient attention that made him what he was, understood in that instant the change he had noted earlier—back at the memorable arrest—when the prisoner first realized the officers had come for him and not for the true guilty one, the idol his heart had set above all self-preservation.

Thomas Adams faced them, his palm still resting on his wife’s hair as if to anchor her to the world.

“Do not think,” he cried, “that because this young girl has steeped her hand in blood, she is a wicked woman. There is no purer heart on earth than hers—none more worthy of the worship of a true man. See! She killed my brother—son of my father, beloved by my mother—yet I can kiss her hand, kiss her forehead, her eyes, her feet—not because I hate him, but because I worship her—the purest—the best—”

His voice broke. He left her side only long enough to stand before them, as if placing his own broad chest between her and their judgment.

“Sirs,” he said, hoarse now, “I must ask you to listen to a strange—a terrible tale.”

“It is like and unlike what I have just related to you,” began young Adams, his voice carrying that thin edge a man gets when he has been forced to chew too long on one mouthful of truth. “In my previous confession I mixed truth and falsehood; and if I am to make you understand my wife’s act, I must begin again—speak as if I had already told you nothing.”

“Wait!” Mr. Gryce cut in, sharp as a knife striking bone. The word did not merely stop Adams; it stilled the whole room, even the faint domestic noises that had been worrying the air—the clock’s industrious tick, the cautious sigh of a register, the far-off clatter of a city wagon scraping iron-rimmed wheels along stone. Gryce leaned over the inspector and murmured a few sentences—low, controlled, like a cook giving directions in the midst of a hot kitchen so the soufflé won’t fall.

Then he took out a pencil and, with the economical movements of a man who wastes nothing—not time, not ink, not inference—he set down a list. He wrote as one might salt in pinches: precisely, never too much, never too little. Tearing the leaf free, he passed it to the inspector, who glanced at it once, then again, and thereafter kept returning to it while Adams—Cadwalader, as he should now rightfully be called—prepared to proceed.

The writing had the look of a memorandum, meant to be consulted in the middle of action; and since it shaped the very air of the examination, I set it down here as it stood on the page, each point like a hook for memory, each question a flavor that would not dissolve.

Points to be made clear by Mr. Adams in his account of this crime:

1. Why a woman who was calm enough to stop and arrange her hair during the beginning of an interview should be wrought up to such a pitch of frenzy and exasperation before it was over as to kill with her own hand a man against whom she had evidently no previous grudge. (Remember the comb found on the floor of Mr. Adams’s bedroom.)

2. What was the meaning of the following words, written just previous to this interview by the man thus killed: “I return you your daughter. Neither you nor she shall ever see me again. Remember Evelyn!”

3. Why was the pronoun “I” used in this communication? What position did Mr. Felix Adams hold toward this young girl qualifying him to make use of such language after her marriage to his brother?

4. And having used it, why did he, upon being attacked by her, attempt to swallow the paper upon which he had written these words, actually dying with it clinched between his teeth?

5. If he was killed in anger and died as monsters do (her own word), why did his face show sorrow rather than hate, and a determination as far as possible removed from the rush of overwhelming emotions likely to follow the reception of a mortal blow from the hand of an unexpected antagonist?

6. Why, if he had strength to seize the above-mentioned paper and convey it to his lips, did he not use that strength in turning on a light calculated to bring him assistance, instead of leaving blazing the crimson glow which, according to the code of signals as now understood by us, means: “Nothing more required just now. Keep away.”

7. What was the meaning of the huge steel plate found between the casings of the doorway, and why did it remain at rest within its socket at this, the culminating moment of his life?

8. An explanation of how old Poindexter came to appear on the scene so soon after the event. His words as overheard were: “It is Amos’s son, not Amos!” Did he not know whom he was to meet in this house? Was the condition of the man lying before him with a cross on his bosom and a dagger in his heart less of a surprise to him than the personality of the victim?

9. Remember the conclusions we have drawn from Bartow’s pantomime. Mr. Adams was killed by a left-handed thrust. Watch for an acknowledgment that the young woman is left-handed, and do not forget that an explanation is due why for so long a time she held her other arm stretched out behind her.

10. Why did the bird whose chief cry is “Remember Evelyn!” sometimes vary it with “Poor Eva! Lovely Eva! Who would strike Eva?” The story of this tragedy, to be true, must show that Mr. Adams knew his brother’s bride both long and well.

11. If Bartow is, as we think, innocent of all connection with this crime save as witness, why does he show such joy at its result? This may not reasonably be expected to fall within the scope of Thomas Adams’s confession, but it should not be ignored by us. This deaf-and-dumb servitor was driven mad by a fact which caused him joy. Why?

12. Notice the following schedule. It has been drawn up after repeated experiments with Bartow and the various slides of the strange lamp which cause so many different lights to shine out in Mr. Adams’s study:

White light—Water wanted.
Green light—Overcoat and hat to be brought.
Blue light—Put back books on shelves.
Violet light—Arrange study for the night.
Yellow light—Watch for next light.
Red light—Nothing wanted; stay away.

The last was on at the final scene. Note if this fact can be explained by Mr. Adams’s account of the same.

With these points in our mind, let us peruse the history of this crime and of the remote and possibly complicated causes which led to it.

BOOK II

REMEMBER EVELYN

Thomas Cadwalader never told a story the way other men did. He let it escape him in fragments, as if every complete sentence might be overheard and used against him. Yet the tragedy that ended with Felix Adams lying dead in ---- Street did not begin there, nor even in the year Evelyn died. It began earlier—before the hand that struck Felix had been born—back when a family name was a thing to be hidden like contraband and a child could be raised on other people’s words.

He had been called Adams as far back as he could remember. His mother was a blank wall: dead before he could form an image, leaving only the faintest scent of lavender in old linen and the rustle of a skirt that might have been memory or wish. His father was worse than absent; he was unspoken. In France, Thomas grew up in the care of an elderly Scotch lawyer—kin on his mother’s side—who kept the boy well fed, well taught, and well fenced-in from any knowledge that might disturb the careful architecture of his upbringing.

The man spoke of his mother at odd moments, usually when the house was quiet enough that the smallest sounds had weight: the sigh of a coal settling in the grate, the far-off hoofbeat in the street, the teaspoon’s thin ring against porcelain. But of Thomas’s father—nothing. Not until Thomas had reached fifteen.

That year, on a morning of thin winter light and damp stone smells rising from the courtyard, his guardian set a small stack of books into his hands.

“Here are romances, Thomas. Read them; but remember—none of them, no matter how they blaze and thunder, will ever equal the story of your father’s bitterly wronged and suffering life.”

“My father!” The word cracked open like a nut, and the taste of it was strange on his tongue. “Tell me about him. I’ve never heard.”

But the old man only looked at him, as one listens to the echo one has made on purpose, and said nothing more.

The books did not matter. After that, nothing mattered but the shape of the person he had never been allowed to name. Thomas began hunting backward through himself, rummaging among half-formed childhood sensations as though they were drawers he had never thought to open. Sometimes he thought he caught a face—hard lines, a brow, a mouth set as if it had learned to endure. But the face never came alone.

Always with it came a scene made of darkness and color: a long stretch of black; two rigid bars of orange light, unwavering, like firelight seen through a crack; black jagged lines against that orange, like distant rooftops or gables cut into the sky. And with these images—an inner thrill that was not joy, a fear that had no object, and the rush of something overhead, as if wind had swooped down to seize him.

He tried to translate these impressions into meaning and failed. They were as persistent as they were inarticulate, like a word on the tip of the tongue in a language one no longer speaks. At last, driven by sheer vexation, he asked his guardian what the images meant.

The old Scotchman could offer no explanation. Yet he did offer hints—carefully placed, as a cook seasons an already simmering pot to make the hunger sharper. There was, he suggested, a duty waiting across the water. Thomas had been kept away for a reason. There were expectations. He must become a man who could enter any room and, by mere competence, bend it.

From that hour, Thomas began to live like someone rehearsing for a role whose title had been withheld. The mystery sharpened him. He studied with a ferocity fed by hope and dread in equal measure. He took to languages, to history, to the intricate and patient discipline of music. He became—too quickly for comfort—an adept. By eighteen he had passed through the Sorbonne with honors, and his hands, trained on the clavier, could make the air itself tremble into order. He fenced well, rode well, excelled in the hard games where mind and muscle must act as one. Among his fellows he was a prodigy.

Only one flaw dogged him, and it was the kind no examination reveals: he could not carry ease on his shoulders. With men he was abrupt, too confident, too edged. With women he was timid, awkwardly deferential, as if the least wrong inflection would be punished. He did not know this until it was pointed out to him in a letter written in a hand he had never seen before.

His guardian came to him with that letter one morning, and the paper itself seemed to change the temperature of the room. It was from his father.

There were commendations—brief, spare as a soldier’s praise—then the criticism, precise and cool: Thomas’s manner was unsatisfactory. He must soften his self-satisfaction with men; he must shed his fear of women. He must become—fully—what was required.

Thomas read, reread, and felt the words like a finger pressed to a bruise. But the letter carried another astonishment: how could his father, so long a shadow, know these intimate defects? The question forced itself out.

The answer overturned what Thomas thought he knew. He had a brother. An older brother—older by twenty years or more—who had recently been in Paris. It was possible, likely, that this brother had seen him, spoken to him, perhaps even touched his hand.

The discovery thickened the air with implications. The secrecy, the long exile, the insistence on perfection—these were not the accidents of a broken household. They were the measures of a plan. Thomas, newly conscious of being watched by love that did not speak its name, obeyed. He moderated his manner, learned grace the way he had learned music: by constant practice and by listening. He began to carry himself like a man who belonged wherever he stood.

A year later his guardian paid him what felt like a final visit. The old man’s voice had a careful steadiness to it, but the house seemed to listen as well—the boards, the window panes, the kettle’s faint murmur on the hob.

“You are now ready,” he said, “for your father’s summons. It will come within weeks. Be careful. Form no ties you cannot break. Once you leave France you are not likely to return. What your father’s purpose may be, I do not know. But it is no ordinary one. You will have money, a proper home, family affection—what you have craved all these years. And in return you will carry solace to a heart that has waited for your healing touch for twenty years. That is all I am ordered to tell you.”

Hope, in Thomas, did not glow; it burned. When the summons came, he sailed at once for America.

Six months before the catastrophe in ---- Street, he stepped onto the wharf in New York with salt still in his hair, the ocean’s rhythm still in his bones. He expected to meet his brother. Instead, a messenger found him—one of those men whose face carries bad news before his mouth forms it.

Thomas learned quickly: his father—whose name, he now discovered, was Cadwalader, not Adams—was ill. Possibly dying. There was no time for explanations, no time even for the settling of travel into rest. He was given exact directions and put on a train heading toward a small village in northern Pennsylvania.

What followed slid through him like a fever dream. The train’s motion, the wet blur of windows, the metallic clack of wheels that seemed to pound one thought into his skull: too late, too late, too late. He arrived at night into storm. Rain streamed, hammered, hissed. The platform reeked of wet wood and coal smoke. Someone called to him to hurry.

Outside waited a panting horse under a lamp whose flame bent sideways in the wind. Even years afterward, Thomas would sometimes wake seeing that flame, hearing the thin, frantic music of rain striking glass. He was urged forward by a hand that seemed both kind and imperative. The horse hauled him along a country road so dark it felt like a tunnel through the world. Wind bent branches to the house and made them lash it like whips. Then, abruptly, the sight of an open door—blackness behind it—and a woman in the threshold holding a candle as if it were a talisman.

She was tall, rawboned, wrapped in old shawls, her face sharp as carved peat. For an instant she stood in a red-gold halo of her own making. Then a gust snuffed the candle and left only her voice, urgent and tremulous.

“Is that you, laddie? Come awa in. Come awa in. Dinna heed the rain. The maister’s been crying on you a’ day. I’m glad you’re no ower late.”

He dismounted and followed that voice into the house. The door was narrow and stubborn; when it swung shut behind him it made a loud, final sound, as if sealing a pact. The hall was bare. The floors were plain boards, scrubbed thin; the walls stained; the staircase pine, common, lit by the candle the woman had relit with shaking hands.

This—this pinched, mean place—was the home he had been led to expect to be well-appointed, perhaps even grand. The revolt rose fast, hot as bile. Years of discipline, of study, of polishing himself into an instrument fit for the world—had it all been for this?

The old woman, smiling and dipping in a kind of senile delight, made it worse. She tugged at his coat, her fingers cold and brisk.

“O the fine laddie! Wad your puir mither could see you the noo! Bonnie and clever! No your faither’s bairn ava! All mither, laddie, all mither!”

“Where is my father?” he asked, and forced authority into the words like a blade.

“Dinna ye hear him? He’s crying on ye. Puir man, he’s wearying to see ye.”

At first Thomas heard only the storm: the furious rain, the swish of heavy boughs striking the house, the rattle of casements, the wind’s shriek in the chimney. The soundscape was brutal, a living thing that pressed in and made speech feel small. Then, under it, threaded through it, he caught a voice—thin, distant, calling with a kind of wild insistence.

“Thomas! Thomas!”

The name, flung into that roaring night, struck him with an almost superstitious force. He moved toward the stairs.

“Na! Na!” the old woman protested, blocking him. “Come in by and eat something first.”

He shook his head. Hunger had been erased by disappointment and by a feeling like grief that had no proper object.

“You will na? Then haste ye—haste ye. But it’s a peety ye wadna ha’e eaten something. Ye’ll need it, laddie; ye’ll need it.”

“Thomas! Thomas!” the voice wailed again.

He went up, drawn as if by a rope. At the top he glanced down once. The woman stood at the foot of the stairs, shading the candle with her trembling hand. Something like fear—more than age—darkened her face. She watched him as if he were walking into a place where words could not follow.

Ahead was a window with small panes that dripped and groaned under the rain. The sight chilled him. Beside it, a door. He found the old-fashioned latch, which rattled with the wind that seeped everywhere, and lifted it softly.

The door fell back at once. In the dimness two eyes blazed from the midst of a huge bed pushed against the far wall—eyes lit by fever and by something fiercer than fever. Two arms lifted, skeletal but urgent. The cry of his name surged into a shout.

Thomas dropped to his knees without deciding to. He seized the wasted hands extended toward him. The face above him was rugged, worn, not the heroic visage his imagination had sketched through years of yearning, but it had power. The hands held life only as a miser holds his last coin, and yet they clung as if to claim what time had withheld.

For a moment, the house and storm vanished. Thomas’s need—his long hunger for recognition—flooded him. He forgot the bare boards, the pinched poverty, the rain’s relentless percussion.

But his father did not embrace him as a man embraces what he loves. He pushed him back—gently, yes, but with purpose—and gestured him into the lamp’s small circle of light on a pine table.

“Stand there,” the old man commanded between gasps, and examined him as if appraising a tool.

Thomas stood. He felt, with a sick clarity, the terrible loneliness of being seen and not held. Outside, the wind rose and fell with animal cries, and he thought how the violence of it echoed the strangled cry in his own chest.

Later in life he would never hear a gale-driven rain without being thrust back into that room: the huge bed like a shipwreck, the lamp’s thin light, his father’s body fighting death while his mind counted advantages in his son’s face.

The scrutiny ended in a rush of words and then a collapse against the pillow. Thomas sprang forward again, grasping his father’s hands, wanting—still wanting—something simple and human. What he received instead was a revelation.

“The grace of God be thanked!” his father cried, voice breaking with triumph and exhaustion. “Our efforts have not failed. Handsome, strong—noble in look and character. We could ask nothing more. My revenge will succeed! John Poindexter will find that he has a heart, and that that heart can be wrung. I do not need to live to see it. For me it exists now; it exists here!” And he struck his own breast with a sudden strength that seemed stored for this one gesture.

John Poindexter. The name meant nothing to Thomas. He said so, half in bewilderment, half in protest.

The look his father gave him was like being scalded.

“You do not know who John Poindexter is, and what he has done to me and mine? They have kept their promise well—too well. But God will accord me strength to tell you what they left unsaid. He would not bring me to this hour only to let me perish before you have heard the story that makes you avenger of innocence upon the enemy of your race. Listen, Thomas. Death has its hand round my heart. If my story finds you cold—But it will not. Your name is Cadwalader, and it will not.”

The word Cadwalader fell into Thomas with weight. It was his true name, and yet it felt like a dialect he had been denied: a sound that belonged to his blood, untranslatable into the life he had lived as Adams.

He sank again to his knees; he could not listen standing.

His father fixed him with hollow eyes where life flickered in erratic flames.

“Thomas,” he said, pausing for breath, “you are not my only child.”

“I know,” Thomas whispered, lips gone pale. “I have a brother. His name is Felix.”

The old man shook his head impatiently.

“Not him! Not him! A sister—your sister—who died before you were born. Beautiful, good, with a voice like an angel’s and a heart—she should be standing by my side to-day, and she would have been if—if he—But none of that. I have no breath to waste. Facts, facts—just facts. Afterward may come emotions and hate, not now. This is my story, Thomas.

“John Poindexter and I were friends. From boyhood we shared bed, food, pleasures. When he came to seek his fortune in America, I accompanied him. He was able—but cold. I was affectionate—but without business capacity. In fifteen years he was rich, esteemed, master of a fine house, owner of horses; while I remained nobody—would have remained nothing—had Providence given me no children and cursed me with the friendship of that prosperous man. When Felix was fourteen and Evelyn three years older, their mother died. Soon after, the little money I had vanished in an unfortunate enterprise. Life began to blacken around me and my growing children.

“John Poindexter, honest enough then—or let me hope so—and childless though long married, offered to take one of mine to educate. I did not consent till the rebellion broke out. Then I sent him both—son and daughter—and went into the army. For four years I fought for the flag, suffered all a man can suffer and live, and at last, released from Libby Prison, came home with a heart full of gratitude and every affection sharpened by unspeakable experiences, to greet my son and clasp once more my daughter—my idol.

“What did I find? A funeral in the streets—hers—and Felix walking like a guard between her speechless corpse and the man under whose protection I had placed her youth and innocence.”

The old man’s voice broke into a shriek as he heaved himself up against the pillow.

“Betrayed! Her innocence! Her sweetness! And he—cold as the stone we laid upon her grave—had seen her perish of anguish and shame without a sign of grief, without a word of contrition!”

“O God,” Thomas breathed, the words torn out of him.

“Ay—God!” his father echoed, shaking his head as if defying heaven itself before he fell back again. “He allowed it—and I—But this does not tell it. I must keep to facts, as Felix did—Felix, who was but fifteen and yet became the only confidant and solace of a young girl betrayed by her protector. It was after her burial—”

“Cease!” said a voice behind Thomas.

It was smooth, fresh, strangely commanding—young in sound yet weighted with a force that made the room contract around it.

Thomas’s father whispered, almost with awe, “Felix!”

“Felix,” Thomas repeated, turning instinctively, trying to rise. But a hand pressed his shoulder—firm, controlling—and the same voice spoke again, softly, peremptorily.

“Wait. Wait till you hear what I have to say. Think not of me—think only of her. It is she you are called upon to avenge. Your sister, Evelyn.”

Thomas, obedient as he had been all his life to voices that claimed authority over his fate, sank back down. His brother stepped forward into the lamplight and took the tale from their father’s failing breath.

“Evelyn had a voice like a bird,” Felix began, and for a moment the storm outside seemed to hush itself to listen. “In those days before father’s return, she filled John Poindexter’s house with melody. I was studious then—more book than song—and I thought she sang too much for a girl whose father was rotting in a Southern prison. But whenever I would have rebuked her, I remembered Edward Kissam, and I held my tongue. It was his love that made her glad, and I wished her happiness, for he was good—honest—and kind to me.

“She was eighteen and beautiful—so I was bound to believe, since every man looked at her. Even old John Poindexter looked, though he looked at no other woman, not even his own wife. She was good, too—pure, I swear it. Her blue eyes never faltered when they met mine—until one day—my God, how well I remember it—they faltered and shrank from me with such terror that, boy though I was, I knew something unprecedented had happened. I thought at once of father. I asked if bad news had come.

“Her answer was a moan that might have been a shriek. ‘Our father! Pray God we may never see him or hear from him again. If you love him—if you love me—pray he may die in prison rather than return here to see me as I am now.’

“I thought she had gone mad. Perhaps she had—for a moment. At my look of startled distress she changed. She remembered my youth. She laughed—or tried to laugh—uttering hurried words I did not understand, and then sank at my knee, laying her head against my side, crying she was not well. She spoke of secret pains, of inward distress she had carried long beneath her songs and merry ways. She said sometimes she feared she would not live long.

“Not live—Evelyn? It was inconceivable. Yet she was pale—mortally pale—as if life had been drained from her in a day. How could death reach her, loved by Edward, by father, by me? Thinking to rouse her, I spoke Edward’s name.

“It was the last word I should have said. She crouched as if struck. She thrust out her hands, faintly shrieking, ‘Not that! Never that! Do not speak his name. Let me never hear of him or see him again. I am dead—do you not understand me?—dead to all the world from this day—except to you!’ And then she sobbed, ‘except to you!’ Still I did not comprehend. But I soon understood this much: no mention was to be made of her illness; her door was to be shut; no one allowed to enter—not even Mrs. Poindexter—least of all her guardian.

“Then I began to grasp the first edge of the horror that ended my youth and filled my whole after life with one thought—revenge. I said nothing. I watched. I waited. Seeing she was truly ill, I became her nurse and sat by her night and day until her symptoms grew so alarming the household woke to them, and we could no longer keep the doctor away. After that I sat at her door. With one ear I listened for her lightest moan. With the other I listened for the step she most dreaded—though it sometimes approached, it never passed the opening of the hall that led to her chamber.

“For a whole week I sat there and watched her life go out like a flame starved of air. Then, when the great shadow fell, and something in me broke with it, I dashed from that place and confronted him—pale, disturbed—walking his own hall. I told him my father was coming. I said I had dreamed it—that in my dream I saw father’s face turned toward this house. Was he prepared to meet him? Had he an answer ready when Amos Cadwalader should ask what had become of his child?”

A sigh rose from Amos Cadwalader’s chest—thin as steam escaping a kettle that has been left too long on the back of the range. Felix heard it and, as if the sound itself were a ladle dipped into old pain, he went on without mercy.

“It was dusk when I told him,” he said; and the room, though it held a bed and two chairs and the heavy curtain of dark-green serge, seemed to Thomas like a kitchen shut up for winter—no draft, no fresh air, only the stale tang of long-kept grievances and lamp oil. “From dark to dawn we sat. No sleep, no rest. Just our eyes—fixed—measuring each other as men do when words have failed and silence becomes a knife.

“Then we sought John Poindexter.

“If he had shunned us we might have found some mercy in it. But he met us openly—quietly—with the indifference of a man who cannot weigh feeling because he has never learned its taste. His first sentence told it.

“‘Spare yourselves, spare me all useless recriminations. The girl is dead; I cannot call her back again. Enjoy your life, your eating and your drinking, your getting and your spending; it is but for a few more years at best. Why harp on old griefs?’

“He said ‘griefs’ as though it were a fashionable word he’d picked up and meant to cheapen with his tongue. And he ended as if he had won.

“‘When a man cares for nothing or nobody, it is useless to curse him.’”

Felix’s voice never rose; that was the terror of it. It moved through the room like a blade drawn slowly from its sheath, without scrape or flourish. Outside, wind worried the eaves, and the rain had a hard, metallic clatter on the panes—the sort of sound that makes a sick man start in his sleep and a well man dream of factories and freight yards.

Amos Cadwalader’s hand, on the coverlet, tightened as if to crush some invisible crust of salt.

“Ah,” Felix murmured, “that was the secret. He cared for nothing—no one—not even himself. We felt the blow and bent under it. Yet before leaving him and the town, your father and I swore we would make that cold heart feel. Someday, in some way, we would cause that impassive nature to suffer as he made us suffer—no matter how bright his prosperity looked, no matter how it clung to him.

“That oath was thirty years ago,” Felix finished. “It has not yet been fulfilled.”

Thomas lifted his head, instinctively, as if a cook roused from the trance of watching a sauce reduce too far. But Amos would not let him speak.

“There are men who forget in a month,” the old man panted. “Others in a year. I have never forgotten—nor has Felix. When you were born—after I married again, thinking to knead new joy into my days—I felt, I know not why, that Evelyn’s avenger had come.

“And when, a year or so after, we heard that God had either forgotten Poindexter’s sins or remembered them—and gave him a child also, after eighteen years of marriage—I looked on your bonny face and saw—thought I saw—a means. A hinge. A handle upon his locked heart.

“You grew. Your ardent nature, your generous temper, your facile mind—everything promised abundance. Then your mother died, and I was widowed again, and the house went quiet in a way no true farmhouse ever should: no morning clatter, no steady talk, only the thin noises that creep in when the living are fewer than the memories. I no longer hesitated to devote you to the purpose for which you seemed born.

“Thomas—do you remember the beginning of that journey that led you far from me? How I carried you on my shoulder along the dusty road—dust like flour thrown in sun—till, within sight of his home, I raised you from among the tombs and showed you those distant gables black against twilight’s gold. There—there I dedicated you to the destruction of whatever happiness might one day grow under his infant’s smile.”

Thomas did remember. He remembered the grit between his small teeth, the rasp of a stone wall under his fingers, the way the evening birds quieted as if some hand had turned down the world’s volume. Even now, the storm’s battering made him ache for those older, cleaner sounds—a creek’s talk, a hinge’s squeak, a kettle’s hum—anything but this harsh, modern rending of air that made thought feel like it had to shout to exist.

“You do,” Amos pressed, mistaking the look on his son’s face for assent and not horror. “I did not think you could forget. And now the time has come for that hour to be fulfilled. I call on you again, Thomas. Avenge our griefs—avenge your sister. Poindexter’s girl has grown to womanhood.”

The words were a hook; they caught under Thomas’s ribs and jerked. He recoiled, sickened by what he heard in them. Yet Amos, fever-driven, read only obedience waiting to be awakened.

Clutching Thomas’s arm, the old man’s voice rose, breaking and re-forming like dough overworked and stubborn.

“Woo her. Win her. They do not know you. You will be Thomas Adams to them—not Thomas Cadwalader. Gather this budding flower into your bosom, and then—oh, he must love his child! Through her we have our hand on his heart. Make her suffer—she’s but a country girl, and you have lived in Paris—make her suffer, and if, in doing so, you cause him to blench, then believe I am looking upon you from the grave I go to, and be happy; for you will not have lived, nor will I have died, in vain.”

He paused to catch breath, lips pale as boiled onion. The room smelled of damp cloth and medicine, and beneath that, the faint, domestic ghost of bread—old crusts, cold hearth, a life where sustenance had once been a language shared without translation. Thomas thought, with a sudden sting, how a dish handed across a table can carry what a mouth cannot say; and how Amos and Felix had chosen instead to speak only in the dominant lexicon of vengeance, grinding every softer word into silence until none remained.

“I shall die,” Amos gasped. “I am dying now. I shall never live to see your triumph; never behold John Poindexter’s eye glaze with sufferings that rend the entrails and make a man question if there is a God in heaven. But I shall know it where I am. No mounded earth can keep my spirit down when John Poindexter feels his doom. I shall be conscious of his anguish and shall rejoice; and when, in the depths of darkness to which I go, he comes faltering along my way—”

His voice shook, then steadied into command.

“Boy, boy—you have been reared for this. God made you handsome; man has made you strong; you have made yourself intelligent and accomplished. You have only to show yourself to this country girl to become master of her will and affection, and these once yours, remember me! Remember Evelyn!”

Never had Thomas witnessed passion like this—unseasoned, undiluted, scorching. It swept him along as a flood carries a man past the familiar landmarks of conscience. He raised his hand—meant to answer, meant to plead, he did not know—and tried to speak. No sound came. Outside, the gusts struck the house in surges, and each impact felt like a door slammed by some unseen foreman of the night. In the pauses, there was no comforting hush, no rural quiet to mend the mind—only the strained, unnatural silence that follows violence.

Amos mistook the dumbness.

“You are dumb!” he cried bitterly. “You do not like the task—are virtuous, perhaps—you who have lived for years alone and unhampered in Paris. Or you have instincts of honor, habits of generosity that blind you to wrongs that for longer than your lifetime have cried aloud to heaven for vengeance. Thomas—Thomas—if you should fail me now—”

“He will not fail you,” Felix said.

The voice was calm, suave, insinuating—like a cordial offered with a smile after the meal has already turned your stomach. Thomas shuddered; he had almost forgotten Felix in the blaze of his father’s fever. Now he turned and looked at him fully.

Felix would draw eyes in any room, under any roof. He was not large, not as large as Thomas, but the will that raved on Amos’s lips lay in Felix’s gray eye as something quiet, fixed, unbendable—the sort of pressure that does not need to shout because it assumes the world will yield. Thomas felt, absurdly, as if a steel band tightened around his heart.

And yet Felix was elegant—perfectly trained. His clothes were good; his bearing belonged as naturally to a Paris salon as to this dim chamber. Poverty—so visible in Amos’s very bedding—did not cling to him. Behind Felix the long curtain of dark-green serge rose from floor to ceiling like a stage’s backdrop. For an instant Thomas had the thought—cold and sharp—that it had been hung for effect, to control what could be seen and what must remain unsaid.

But Felix’s face demanded, and Thomas, as if responding to a stronger hand than his own, bowed his head.

“I am at your command, Felix,” he stammered. “I am at your command, father. I cannot say more. Only remember that I never saw Evelyn—that she died before I was born—and that I—”

Felix cut in, gentle but measured. “Perhaps there is an obstacle we have not reckoned upon. You may already love some woman and desire to marry her. If so, it need be no impediment—”

“No,” Thomas flared, the one honest heat left to him. “No. I am heart-whole—save for a few lingering fancies which are fast becoming vanishing dreams.”

He felt suddenly older than the hour that had begun this conversation, as if years had been poured into him like hot stock into a cold bowl, shocking him into unwanted coherence.

“Your heart will not be disturbed now,” Felix observed. “I have seen the girl. I went there on purpose a year ago. She’s as pale as a snow-drop and as listless. You will not be obliged to recall the gay smiles of Parisian ladies to be proof against her charms.”

Thomas made a small, helpless movement—half shrug, half recoil.

“She must be made to know the full intoxication of hope,” Felix went on, his clear voice cutting through the storm like a whistle that herds men into line. “To realize despair she must first experience every delight that comes with satisfied love. Have you the skill as well as heart to play to the end a rôle that will take patience as well as dissimulation, courage as well as subtlety, and that union of will and implacability which finds its food in tears and is strengthened, rather than lessened, by the suffering of its victim?”

“I have the skill,” Thomas murmured, “but—”

“You lack the incentive,” Felix finished, not unkindly, as one correcting a recipe aloud. “Well. We must have patience with your doubts. Our hate has been fostered by memories of her whom, as you say, you have never seen. Look, then, Thomas. Look at your sister as she was—as she is for us. Look at her, and think of her as despoiled, killed, forgotten by Poindexter. Have you ever gazed upon a more moving countenance, or one in which beauty contends with a keener prophecy of woe?”

Thomas did not know what he expected—some trick, some relic, perhaps a portrait dim as old gravy left in a pan. He almost anticipated a spectre. Yet when he followed Felix’s lifted hand, the curtain that had hung there a moment before was gone from his attention, and instead there was a blaze of light.

In its midst stood a figure—charming, tragic—so vivid that Thomas’s breath caught as though he had stepped into a kitchen where oranges had just been zested and the air was suddenly alive with oils. It did not seem like paint; it seemed like presence. Her eyes—tender, wounded—appealed to something in him that his living relatives had never fed. The room’s harsh sounds receded. Even the storm became a distant drum.

He dropped to his knees as if to an altar.

“Do not look at me so reproachfully, sweet Evelyn,” he cried. “I was born to avenge you, and I will. John Poindexter shall never go down in peace to his tomb.”

From the bed came a sigh of utter contentment—like a man tasting, at last, the dish he has demanded for decades.

“Swear it!” Amos cried, flinging out his arms before him in the form of a cross.

“Yes,” Felix echoed softly, laying his own hand upon those crossed arms.

Thomas rose and drew near. His hand went down beside Felix’s—warm flesh against warm flesh, but his own palm felt numb, as if he had plunged it into snow.

“I swear,” he began, forcing his voice above the tempest, which poured gust after gust against the house, each one whining in the chimney like an animal trapped. “I swear to win the affections of Eva Poindexter, and then, when her heart is all mine, to cast her back in anguish and contumely on the breast of John Poindexter.”

“Good!” came from far away—so far it seemed to Thomas as if it rose from underground.

Then the darkness that had been gathering behind his eyes since the oath settled fully. The room tilted, the light around Evelyn smeared into a white blur, and he sank insensible at the feet of his dying father.

* * *

Amos Cadwalader died that night—but not without one awful scene more.

Near midnight he roused from the heavy sleep that had followed the excitement, and, glancing from Thomas to Felix where they sat on either side of the bed, fixed his eyes with a strange gleam upon the door.

“Ah!” he ejaculated. “A visitor! John Poindexter! He comes to ask my forgiveness before I set out on my dismal journey.”

The sarcasm in his tone, the dreadful courtesy of his manner, made the hair stir on the heads of his sons. That he saw his enemy as plainly as he saw them neither could doubt. The air seemed to tighten; even the rain’s racket sounded momentarily like an audience holding its breath.

“Does he dread my meeting with Evelyn?” Amos went on. “Does he wish to placate me before I am joined to that pathetic shade? He shall not be disappointed. I forgive you, John Poindexter! I forgive you my daughter’s shame—my blighted life. I am dying; but I leave one behind who will not forgive you. I have a son—an avenger of the dead—who yet lives to—to—”

The sentence broke, as if the last word existed only in a tongue he could no longer speak.

He fell back.

With those words—words that sealed Thomas to his task as surely as wax seals a letter—Amos Cadwalader died.

Felix had not taken after his father in the one deficiency that keeps a man poor even when hatred makes him rich—he could make money. In the twenty years since Thomas had been sent abroad, Felix had built a fortune with a quick, cold ingenuity; he could not coax his father into touching it for comfort, but he could easily persuade him to see it poured out for vengeance.

So there was a certain grim weight in the parting order Felix gave his brother at the door, as if he were pressing a purse into a hand that would not feel its heft.

“I have money,” he said. “Spend it. Spend what you will; and when your task is completed there will still be some left—for your amusement.”

Thomas bowed, the old Scriptural phrase moving in his mind like a coin rubbed between fingers. The laborer is worthy of his hire. Aloud he asked, glancing round the scant room whose bare walls had lost their excuse now that their father lay dead: “And you? Will you remain here?”

“No.” Felix’s reply came clipped, final. “I go to New York to-morrow. I have rented a house there, which you may one day wish to share. The name on the lease is Adams—Felix Adams. That is how you will address me. Cadwalader is a name that must not pass your lips in Montgomery. My person is known there; otherwise we should not be dependent on you for the success of our revenge.”

He smiled then—fully aware he was the handsomer of the two, and that beauty in a man can be as useful as a forged signature.

“And those introductions we enjoined you to bring from Paris?” he added.

* * * * *

What happened in the weeks that followed is best read in the double record Thomas kept: the letters he sent Felix—facts with their edges pared smooth—and the private diary in which he allowed his sensations their full, untranslatable violence. On the left, the summary of what he did; on the right, the summary of what he became. Taken together, they show the case as no single statement could.

* * * * *

FIRST LETTER.

DEAR FELIX:

I am here; I have seen her. She is, as you have said, a pale blonde. To-morrow I present my credentials to John Poindexter. From what I have already experienced I anticipate a favorable reception.

Yours aff., THOMAS.

FIRST ENTRY.

I could not tell Felix the true story of this day. Why can I not? And why must I tell it here? To keep my mind from chewing it over like bitter bark? Perhaps. I do not understand my own feelings—only that I begin to dread my task while pressing forward to accomplish it, as if some part of me runs and another part digs in its heels.

I have seen her. That much I wrote. But not where, not how. How could I put into words, for Felix’s iron ear, the sound of a woman’s skirts in a graveyard at sundown, or the way a simple act of remembrance can strike a man like a hand across the mouth?

I went straight from the cars to the cemetery. Before I entered the town; before I let myself taste even the dull comfort of a meal; I sought Evelyn’s grave to renew my oath on the very ground where, nineteen years ago, my father held me up—four years old and terrified—toward John Poindexter’s home like a threat made flesh.

I found the stone at last, old and neglected, its lettering choked with moss. I was bending in the last light to make out her name when I heard it—the faint, intimate rustle of a dress through grass, a sound so domestic it seemed an insult in a place of death. I looked up.

A girl came toward me through a nimbus of rosy sunset that made her seem lifted, as if the air itself carried her. Her figure was slender and sure; her head, pale-gold, was oddly illumined as though the light had chosen it. In her arms she bore a great cluster of lilies—snow-white, waxy, their heavy perfume already thickening the cooling air.

Her eyes were not on me. They were on the grave beside which I stood.

I moved aside into the shadow of bushes and watched her. She knelt without hesitation, as one kneels in a familiar kitchen to lift a pot from the hearth. With careful hands she spread the lilies over the earth—emblems of innocence, yes, but also of something else: of a ritual repeated until it becomes part of the body’s memory. The stems snapped softly; petals brushed stone; the air filled with that clean, almost edible scent that makes the throat ache.

What did it mean? Who was this young girl to bring fresh flowers to the grave of my long-buried sister?

When she rose, I could no longer restrain my curiosity—or the sudden swelling under my ribs that had nothing to do with hatred. I stepped out, greeted her with the respect her bearing demanded, and asked, as indifferently as I could manage, who lay under that stone and why she—so young—brought offerings to a grave dug long before she was born.

Her answer struck me like a door flung open into a room I had thought locked.

“I strew flowers here,” she said, “because the girl buried under this stone had the same birthday as I. I never saw her, of course. But she died in my father’s house when she was no older than I am to-day. And since I have grown up—since I understand what it is to be young and yet to die—I have made it my custom to share with her my birthday flowers. They say she was like a lily, in looks and in nature. So I bring lilies.”

It was Eva Poindexter.

And Eva Poindexter was laying lilies—my sister’s lilies—on Evelyn’s grave.

* * * * *

LETTER II.

DEAR FELIX:

I have touched the hand of John Poindexter. In order to win a place in the good graces of the daughter I must please the father, or at least attract his favorable notice. I have reason to think I have done this.

Very truly, THOMAS.

ENTRY II.

I no longer feel myself a true man.

John Poindexter is cold in appearance, hard in manner, inflexible in opinion; his words strike like a gavel and stop like one. Yet he does not rouse in me the abhorrence I anticipated, nor awaken in me the single pure thought I owe to Evelyn’s memory.

Is it because he is Eva’s father? Has the daughter’s gentleness thrown a kind of mercy over the parent, like lamplight forgiving a harsh room? If so, Felix has a right to execrate me—and my father to—

* * * * *

LETTER III.

DEAR FELIX:

The introductions furnished me have made me received everywhere. There is considerable wealth here and many fine houses. Consequently I find myself in a congenial society, of which she is the star. Did I say that he was, as of old, the chief man of the town?

Yours truly, THOMAS.

ENTRY III.

She is beautiful—yes—but it is not the mere arrangement of feature that undoes me. It is the sweetness of her nature, and the odd strength beneath it, as if the same hands that place flowers on a grave can also lift a heavy pan from coals without flinching.

She has the daintiness of the lily and the flush of the rose. Her presence changes the very air around her. In drawing-rooms crowded with talk, I notice the soundscape first—the high clink of glasses, the scrape of chairs, the forced laughter that rings like tin. Then she speaks, softly, and something settles. Men lower their voices; women stop tapping fans. It is as though her ear is tuned to quiet, and the room obeys.

Could Felix have imagined such a Poindexter? I cannot contemplate such loveliness and tether it to the execrable sin that calls for vengeance upon this house. Under her eye everything in me that is dark, threatening, secret, revengeful slips away. I begin to dream of what is pure and satisfying and ennobling—dream it because of her smile more than her words.

Have I been given an angel to degrade? Or am I blind—seeing a saint where Felix would see only a pretty girl with unexpected attractions?

* * * * *

LETTER IV.

DEAR FELIX:

Rides, dances, games, nonsense generally. My interest in this young girl is beginning to be publicly recognized. She alone seems ignorant of it. Sometimes I wonder if our scheme will fail through her impassibility and more than conventional innocence. I am sometimes afraid she will never love me. Yet I have exerted myself to please her. Indeed, I could not have exerted myself more. To-day I went twenty-five miles on horseback to procure her a trifle she fancied.

Yours aff., THOMAS.

ENTRY IV.

All will not go as easily as Felix imagines.

Eva Poindexter may be a country girl, but she is no simple one. She has standards as real as the rules for bread—what may be rushed, what must be given time, what cannot be cheated without ruining the whole. Grace and attainment do not suffice to win her.

Have I the other qualities she demands? I do not know. I have one quality she cannot suspect; it lies in me like a taint in a well. Will its shadow drown everything else just at the moment I think her mine?

The doubt is a private hell.

Something deeper than revenge—stronger, more imperious—has made the winning of this girl’s heart a necessity. I have forgotten my purpose in the desire. I have forgotten everything except that she is the one woman of my life and I can never rest till her heart is wholly mine.

Good God—have I become a slave where I meant to be master? Have I, Thomas Cadwalader, given my soul into the keeping of an innocent girl? I do not stop to inquire. To win her—that is all I live for now.

* * * * *

LETTER V.

DEAR FELIX:

She may not care for me, but she is interested in no one else. Of this I am assured by John Poindexter, who seems very desirous of aiding me in my attempt to win his daughter's heart. Hard won, close bound. If she ever comes to love me it will be with the force of a very strong nature. The pale blonde has a heart.

Yours aff., THOMAS.

ENTRY V.

If it were only passion, I might hope to bridle it. But it is deeper than the senses. It makes me look with her eyes, listen with her ears, throb with her heart. My soul, rather than my body, is in bondage.

I want to win her—not to satisfy myself, but to make her happy. I want to prove to her that goodness exists in this world— I, who came here to corrode and destroy; I, who am still pledged to do so.

Felix should have chosen an older man for such infamy. Or he should have remembered that one who could be swayed as I was by family affections has a heart too soft to carry poison without spilling it on himself.

* * * * *

ENTRY VI.

Evelyn’s name is never spoken in this house. The silence around it is so complete it has a sound of its own—like a room after a factory whistle stops, when your ears ring with what is absent.

Sometimes I think John Poindexter has forgotten her, and in that thought I find the one remaining spur to revenge. Forgotten her! Strange that his child, born long after the death, should remember the poor girl and he forget.

Yet the blow is planned to fall on the daughter—if it falls at all. Should I pray it never may? That she should loathe instead of love me? Distrust instead of confide?

Who can pray against himself?

Eva Poindexter must love me—even if I am driven to self-destruction by remorse after she has placed her heart in my hands.

* * * * *

LETTER VI.

DEAR FELIX:

Will you send me a few exquisite articles from Tiffany's? I see that her father expects me to give her presents. I think she will accept them. If she does, we may both rest easy as to the state of her affections.

Very truly, THOMAS.

ENTRY VII.

I cannot bring myself to spend a whole day away from her.

If Felix were here, he would commend my assiduity as proof of purpose. He would say, in his cold way: “You are not disappointing us.”

Us—as if our father still hovered near, sharing our hope. If he does hover, he must see deeper than Felix into this matter. For with every day’s advantage— and I begin to believe each day brings one—I shrink farther from the end they planned; the end that alone can justify my advance in her affections.

I am a traitor to my oath, for I know now I shall never disappoint Eva’s faith. I could not. I would rather meet my father’s accusing eyes at the verge of that strange world to which he has gone, or Felix’s recriminations here, or my own contempt—than use her as bait for revenge.

* * * * *

LETTER VII.

DEAR FELIX:

This morning I passed under the window you have described to me as Evelyn's. I did it with a purpose. I wanted to test my own emotions and to see how much feeling it would arouse in me. Enough.

Eva accepted the brooch. It was the simplest thing you sent.

Aff., THOMAS.

ENTRY VIII.

I hate John Poindexter—yes, I hate him. But I can never hate his daughter.

Only Felix could so confuse father with child as to visit his anger on this gentle embodiment of all that is gracious, trustworthy, fascinating in woman.

But am I called upon to hate her? Am I, in a way, required to love her—so that the scheme can work? I will ask Felix—no. I cannot ask him. He would not comprehend her charm, its hold over me. He would doubt—and come at once to Montgomery.

Am I proving such a traitor to my own blood that I cannot bear the thought of Felix contemplating, even in secret, the unsuspecting form of our enemy’s daughter?

* * * * *

LETTER VIII.

DEAR FELIX:

A picnic on the mountains. It fell to me to escort Miss Poindexter down a dangerous slope. Though no words of affection passed between us (she is not yet ready for them), I feel that I have made a decided advance in her good graces.

Yours, THOMAS.

ENTRY IX.

I have touched her hand. I have felt her slight form thrill against mine as we descended the mountain ledges together.

No man was near; no eye—there were moments when we were as alone on those wooded slopes as if the world held no other breath. The pines made their own music—wind moving through needles like a hush laid over speech. Below us the town was a dim cluster, and even its noises seemed far away, softened, made harmless.

Yet I dared no more press her hand or pour my worship into her ears than if all Paris watched. How I love her. How faint and remote seem the years of that dead crime my brother would invoke to punish this sweet soul.

And how remote—yet how sharp—the hour when I knelt under my dying father’s hand and swore—

Ah, that oath. That oath.

* * * * *

ENTRY X.

The thing I dreaded—might have foreseen—has occurred.

Felix has come to Montgomery.

A message reached me to-day: I am to meet him at midnight, at Evelyn’s grave. The witching hour. A stage device, perhaps, to control me. I dread it, and I do not—both at once. I begin to suspect Felix calculates upon shadows and old terrors as other men calculate upon money.

But the day for that has passed. I will show him I can be no more influenced there at that hour than I could be here, in my hotel room, with the sight of her little glove—yes, I stole it; is there sin in such thefts?—lying on the table.

Evelyn is sacred. But the dead must not interfere with the living. Eva shall never be sacrificed to Evelyn’s manes—not if John Poindexter lives in peace to his last hour; not if Felix—

No. I must play the man. Felix is formidable to meet alone in a place of rancorous memories, at an hour when spirits—if spirits exist—are said to haunt the precincts of the tomb. Even the night has its own sound there: the whisper of leaves, the tick of insects, the soft settling of earth, as if the ground itself listened.

* * * * *

ENTRY XI.

I should not have known Felix if I had met him in the street.

In the moonlight at that shaded spot he looked like a stranger who had learned my brother’s face by heart and put it on. His voice seemed altered. In his manner there was a hesitation I had never believed possible.

And his words—he did not ask what I feared, did not utter the recriminations I expected. He told me, coldly, that my courtship must be shortened; that the end for which we had prepared must be hastened. He gave me two weeks.

Then he turned to Evelyn’s grave and bent over the mossy stone, trying to read her name. He was so long about it that I leaned down beside him and laid my hand on his shoulder.

He was trembling. His body was as cold as the stone he braced himself against.

Was it Evelyn’s memory that had done this? If so, it was natural. To all appearance he has never loved anyone as he loved that unhappy sister. The grief has outlived many a man’s lifetime.

I was shrinking back with a kind of respect when he caught my hand. His grip convulsed, though his tone remained strangely measured.

“Do not forget the end,” he cried. “Do not forget John Poindexter—his sin, his indifference to my father’s grief; the accumulated sufferings of years that made Amos Cadwalader a hermit among men. I have seen the girl. She has changed—women do change at her age—and some men— I do not say you, but some men—might think her beautiful. But beauty must not blind eyes fixed upon another goal. Overlook it; overlook her—you have done so, have you not? Pale beauties cannot move one who has sat at the feet of the most dazzling of Parisian women. Keep your eyes on John Poindexter, the debt he owes us, and the suffering we have promised him. That she is sweet, gentle, different from what we thought—only makes the chances of reaching his heart the greater. The worthier she is of affections not indigenous to that hard soul, the surer our grip and the heavier his downfall.”

The old spell fell over me. I could neither answer nor assert myself.

Letting go my hand, he rose. With his back to the village—I noticed he had not turned his face toward it since coming—he said, “I return to New York to-morrow. In two weeks you will telegraph your readiness to take up your abode with me. I have a home that will satisfy you; and it will soon be all your own.”

He gripped his heart then. Dark as the scene was, I caught a strange convulsion cross his face as he turned into the moonlight. It was gone again before we could descend.

“You may hear from me again,” he remarked faintly as he grasped my hand and turned away.

I had not spoken one word during the whole interview.

* * * * *

LETTER IX.

DEAR FELIX:

I do not hear from you. Are you well, or did your journey affect your health? I have no especial advance to report. John Poindexter seems greatly interested in my courtship. Sometimes he gives me very good advice. How does that strike you, Felix?

Aff., THOMAS.

ENTRY XII.

I shall never understand Felix.

He has not left town. He is hiding here—watching me, no doubt, to see whether the weakening he suspects will overthrow the revenge he has planned.

I know it, because I have seen him more than once when he believed himself invisible. Once I caught sight of him in Poindexter’s grounds as Eva and I stood talking at a window. Once I saw him in church, in a dark corner, where he could keep his eye on us in Poindexter’s pew.

He looked thin that day. Suspense is wearing him. There is a kind of hunger in it, like a man starved on noise who can never find quiet enough to sleep.

Is it my duty to cut it short by proclaiming my infidelity to my oath—my determination to marry the girl who has made me forget it?

* * * * *

LETTER X.

DEAR FELIX:

Miss Poindexter has told me unreservedly that she cares for me. Are you satisfied with me now?

In haste, THOMAS.

ENTRY XIII.

She loves me.

Oh, ecstasy of life—Eva Poindexter loves me.

I forced it from her lips to-day. With my arms around her and her head on my shoulder, I urged her to confession, and it came—soft, reluctant, then suddenly steady, like a singer finding the note that fits her native tongue.

Now let Felix do what he will.

What is old John Poindexter to me? Her father.

What are Amos Cadwalader’s hatred, the mortal wrong that cried for revenge? Dead issues. Buried sorrows. God may remember them; men are bound to forget.

Life—life with her. That is the future. That is the only vengeance I will take; the only vengeance Evelyn can demand, if she is the angel we believe her to be.

I will write to Felix to-morrow.

* * * * *

ENTRY XIV.

I have not written Felix.

I had not the courage.

* * * * *

ENTRY XV.

I have dreamed.

I thought I saw my father meeting the white shade of Evelyn in the unimaginable recesses of the world to which both have gone. Strange horrors—stranger glories—lay about them like landscapes seen in lightning. When the two forms greeted and parted, a line of light followed one and a trail of gloom the other.

As their paths divided I heard my father cry:

“There is no spot on your garments, Evelyn. Can it be the wrongs of earth are forgotten here? That mortals remember what angels forget—and that our revenge is late for one so blessed?”

I did not hear her answer; I woke. But the echo has rung in me all day, as if the words had their own resonance the living cannot silence.

Is our revenge late for one so blessed?

ENTRY XVI.

I have summoned courage.

Felix has been here again—and truth has at last passed between us.

I had been pressing Eva to name our wedding day. We stood in the glare of the drawing-room lights—John Poindexter, my dear girl, and I—when I heard a groan too faint for other ears, followed by a light fall from the window overlooking the garden.

It was Felix.

He had been watching; had seen my love; heard marriage on my lips. He must be in the grounds now—in open frenzy or secret satisfaction; it was hard to tell which.

Determined to know—determined to speak—I excused myself on some hurried plea and went out among the paths he knows as well as I.

At last I found him near an old dial where more than once he has watched Eva and me together. He was very pale—deathly pale—in the faint starlight. Yet he greeted me quietly, almost without surprise, as if he had counted on my following him.

“You are playing your rôle well,” he said. “Too well. What was that I heard about your marrying?”

The time had come. I meant to meet it with a man’s courage.

But Felix is not easy to cross, even in trifles; and this is his life, his past, his future.

I do not know which of us truly began. There were broken words. A stammer. Then I heard myself speak distinctly, with a hard emphasis born of restraint.

“I love her. I want to marry her. You must allow this. Then—”

I could not proceed.

The shock he received seemed to pass into me through the air. Something not of earth moved between us, and I raised my hand as if to shield my face. Then—whether branches shifted and let the moon strike clear, or my sight sharpened with emotion—I caught one full glimpse of Felix’s face.

A great suffering was there. For a first instant it felt like the wrenching of my own heart; in the next it impressed itself as his—Felix’s.

I stood appalled.

My weakness had uprooted the one hope of his life, or so I thought. That he answered with silence made my heart yearn toward him for the first time since I had known him as brother rather than stranger. I began to stammer excuses. The darkness returned, mercifully; I could not have borne the sight of his bowed head and set features.

Hidden from his face, I talked—too much. I spoke of Eva’s purity, her goodness, the way she had robbed me of myself in spite of all.

My heart. A strange word between us in reference to a Poindexter. Yet it was the only word that fit.

At last, driven almost mad by his silence—by the weight of it, the way it pressed on the night like the absence of sound after a crash—I cried out:

“You have never loved a woman, Felix. You do not know what passion is when it seizes a man jaded with the hollow pleasures of an irresponsible life. You cannot judge; therefore you cannot excuse. You are made of iron—”

Meanwhile, behind the ordinary ticks and creaks of the house—beneath the respectable hush that fell each evening like flour on a board—another conflict went on, close and airless, in a man built to exclude sympathy as a kitchen door shuts out the clatter of the street.

I can only gesture toward it. Some tragedies do not show their full face. This one was reconstructed from scraps: torn strips of letters addressed to no one, the ink dragged hard as if the nib had been a knife. They were found long afterward, when the accounting of Felix Cadwalader’s death was already considered complete—hidden under the flooring of Bartow’s room, where dust lay in packed drifts and the old wood held smells of damp and stale candle-smoke. How Bartow came by them, or why he hid them there, no one living could say.

“Madness!

“I have seen Eva Poindexter again, and heaven and hell have contended for me ever since—like two cooks fighting over the same pot, each determined to claim the last taste. Eva! Eva! The girl I once allowed myself to name only as prey. The girl I have given to my brother.

“She is too lovely for him. Too lovely for any man—unless it be one who has never yet been startled by a woman’s voice, never yet taken the heat of a glance and felt it go straight through him, as the scent of crushed basil rises through a kitchen and ruins a man for lesser herbs. Is it love I feel? Can I—Felix, who have had but one thought, known but one enthusiasm—retain within this breast of iron a place, however secret, however small, that any woman, least of all his intended, could reach?

“Never. I am the prey of frenzy, or the butt of devils.

“Yet it is as if only the inhabitants of some more celestial sphere brighten around me when I think of those half-raised eyes; those delicately parted lips, so innocent they might have been made only for prayer or for tasting new bread; that bearing devoid of guile; that divine tenderness, mingled with strength, by which she commands admiration and awakens love. I must fly. I must never see her again. Thomas’s purpose is steady; he must never see that mine rocks—like an idol smitten by a thunderbolt.

“If Thomas had not been reared in Paris, he too— But I am the only weak one. Curses on my—”

“Did I say I would fly? I cannot—not yet. One more glimpse of her face, if only to satisfy myself that I have reason for this madness. Perhaps I was but startled yesterday to find a celestial loveliness where I expected pallid inanity, as a man expects thin broth and finds instead a stock so rich it clings to the spoon. If my emotion is due to my own weakness rather than to her superiority, I had better recognize my folly before it proves my destruction.

“I will stay and— Thomas will not, shall not— Poindexter’s daughter— hate, hate for Thom—”

“My self-esteem is restored.

“I have seen her again—him— they were together. There was true love in his eye. How could I expect him not to love her? And I was able to hide my anguish and impose his duty on him, as one sets a lid on a pot to keep the boil from showing. She loves him—or he thinks so—and the work goes on.

“But I will not stay to watch its accomplishment. No, no.”

“I told him my story to-night, under the guise of a past experience. Oh, the devils must laugh at us men! They have reason to. Sometimes I wonder if my father, in the clearness of his new vision, does not join them in their mirth.”

“Home with my unhappy secret! Home, where nothing comes to distract me from my gnawing griefs and almost intolerable thoughts. The house is never truly silent; it has its own acoustic life—the sigh of settling beams, the thin whistle through ill-fitted sash, the far-off groan of carts on the road. Each sound is a spoon scraping a wound.

“I walk the floors. I cry aloud her name. I cry it even under the portrait of Evelyn, where her painted eyes, always composed, seem to measure my disorder with an old reproach. There are moments when I am tempted to write to Thomas—to forbid him—”

“Eva! Eva! Eva!

“Every fibre in my miserable body utters the one word. But no man shall ever know. Thomas shall never know how the thought of her fills my days and nights, making my life a torment and the future—”

“I wait for his letters (scanty they are and cold) as the doomed criminal awaits his executioner. Does she really love him? Or will that exquisite, that soulful nature call for a stronger mate, a more concentrated temperament, a—a—”

“I thought I saw, in one of my dark hours, my father rising up from his grave to curse me. Oh, he might curse on if—”

“What have I said about no man knowing? Bartow knows.

“In his dumbness, his deafness, he has surprised my secret. He shows it by his peering looks, his dissatisfied ways, and a jealousy at which I could shout aloud in mirth if I were not more tempted to shriek aloud in torment. A dumb serving-man—picked up, I have almost forgotten where—jealous of my weakness for John Poindexter’s daughter!

“He was never jealous of my feeling for Evelyn. Yet till the day I dared fate by seeking out and looking for the second time upon the woman whose charms I had scorned, her name often resounded through these rooms; my eyes dwelt upon but one spot, and that was where her picture hangs in the woeful beauty which has become my reproach.”

“I have had a great surprise.

“The starling—which has been taught to murmur Evelyn’s name—to-day shrieked out, ‘Eva! Eva!’ The syllables broke the air like a dropped platter, sharp and clean. My first impulse was to wring its neck; my next, to take it from its cage and hide it in my bosom, as if warmth and darkness could hush what my own mouth could not.

“But I did neither. I am still a man.”

“Bartow will wring that bird’s neck if I do not. This morning I caught him with his hand on the cage and a murderous light in his eye which I had no difficulty in understanding. Yet he cannot hear the word the wretched starling murmurs. He only knows it is a word—a name—and he is determined to suppress it.

“Shall I string the cage up out of this old fellow’s reach?

“His deafness, his inability to communicate with others, the exactness with which he obeys my commands as given him by my colored slides, his attention to my every wish—consequent upon his almost animal love for my person—are necessary to me now, while the bird— Ah! there it goes again: ‘Eva! Eva!’”

“Is it hate or love I feel—abhorrence or passion?

“Love would seek to save; but I have no thought of saving her, since she has acknowledged her love for Thomas, and since he— Oh, it is not now for Evelyn’s sake I plan revenge, but for my own.

“These nights and days of torture—the revelation I have had of my own nature—the consent I was forced to give to a marriage which means bliss to them and anguish beyond measure to me— all this calls for vengeance. They will not escape, these two.

“I have laid my plans deep. I have provided for every contingency. It has taken time, thought, money—like a long simmer that thickens into something dark and inescapable. But the result is good.

“If they cross the threshold of my circular study, they must consent to my will or perish here, and I with them. The very room, turning in upon itself, is a kind of tongue that insists on one grammar only: my grammar.

“Oh, they shall never live and be happy! Thomas need not think it. John Poindexter need not think it! I might have forgotten the oath made on my father’s crossed arms, but I will never forget the immeasurable griefs of these past months, or the humiliation they have brought me.

“My own weakness is to be avenged—my unheard-of, my intolerable weakness.

“Remember Evelyn? Remember Felix!

“Ah, again— Eva! Eva! Eva!”

The rest, the detectives told me afterward, had best be set down in Thomas Adams’s own voice, because it was the marrow of the confession he made when the net at last drew tight.

According to my promise, I took my young wife to Felix’s house at the day and hour appointed. We went on foot; the streets were close with late-day traffic, iron wheels and clanging cars worrying the air, that metallic din which makes a man’s thoughts skid and jar as if his very nerves were rails. Eva—my Eva—kept time to it without knowing, humming under her breath in a way that always made a home wherever she stood. That little thread of song, soft as steam over a pot, was the only mercy in the soundscape.

At the door we were received by an old servant—Bartow—whom I had been warned could neither speak nor hear. He opened to us without a word, his eyes steady, his hands competent, and the first breath from the interior struck my face like a cellar draft: stale velvet, extinguished lamps, old wood that had long ago given up its sap, and beneath it a faint medicinal tang as if metal had been rubbed and oiled in secret.

The hall stretched away in aristocratic gloom, a corridor that swallowed footsteps. At sight of the old man and that dim, old-fashioned passage, Eva turned pale and looked at me as if asking what sort of house asked to be entered in silence. I answered with a smile I did not feel, and offered her my arm. She took it, warm and trusting, and we followed Bartow past an open drawing-room door—where I had expected we should pause—into a plain room that made me frown with disappointment, until Bartow drew aside a heavy portière at one end and let us into my brother’s study.

It would have been fairyland, if Felix—its sole occupant—had not, by the sheer force of his presence, pulled every eye and thought upon himself. The place was a deliberate night, though day still shone outside: a mosque-like chamber, tapestries, dim gold, and above us an odd lantern pouring down a sickly yellow glare that made skin look waxen and blood look thin.

I had said little to Eva of this brother of mine—certainly nothing that would prepare her for a man so handsome, so poised, so imposing in his very stillness. She looked frightened, a little awed; yet she stepped forward bravely, and when I made the introduction she offered Felix a smile so open, so domestic, so like the first taste of bread after hunger, that I—fool that I was—expected it to disarm him.

Perhaps it did. His already pale features went whiter in that yellow light, and the hand he raised in greeting dropped heavily, as if the arm had forgotten its office. He managed a few words of welcome, inadequate, yet his eyes—fixed upon her face—betrayed feeling. Eva, innocent of the nature of it, stood with her roses pressed against her bodice, as if the scent could keep the room humane.

I knew—or believed I knew—and my heart turned over. I looked at him and pleaded without words. Then, saying I had explanations to make, I asked if Mrs. Adams might rest there while we spoke apart.

He answered with a strange look. For a moment he stopped fumbling with some small object on the table before him, and then—bowing with a sarcastic grace that made me feel, for the first time, his superiority even in my own skin—he led the way to a small door I had not noticed.

“Your wife will find it more comfortable here,” he observed, with slow pauses that showed repressed excitement rather than calm. He opened into what appeared a small but elegant sleeping-apartment, arranged with a care that made it feel less like comfort than preparation. “What we have to say cannot take long. Mrs. Adams will not find the wait tedious.”

“No,” Eva said, with a natural little laugh, born—God help me—of her perfect happiness. Yet she could not but have found it strange, and my manner, as well as his, was scarcely that of a bridegroom presenting his bride to his only relative.

“I will call you—” I began; but the vision of her dimpled face above the great cluster of roses she carried cut my sentence in two. The door closed. I stood alone with Felix.

He breathed easier now, as if her presence had been an unendurable scent. His manner grew more natural, yet he did not speak at once. He threw a strange glance about the room, as if taking inventory of its angles and exits; then, seeming satisfied, he moved toward the large table that from the first had seemed to pull him like a magnet.

“Come further away,” he said. “I need air—breathing place—in this close room, and so must you. Besides, why should she hear what we have to say? She will know the worst soon enough. She seems a gentle-hearted woman.”

“An angel,” I began.

He stopped me with a gesture that had always in it something of command and something of contempt.

“We will not discuss your wi—Mrs. Adams. Where is John Poindexter?”

“At the hotel,” I answered. “Or he has returned home. I no longer take account of his existence. Felix—I shall never leave my wife. I had rather prove recreant to the oath I took before I realized the worth of the woman whose happiness I vowed to destroy. This is what I have come to tell you. Make it easy for me. You are a man who has loved and suffered. Let us bury the past; let us—”

Even as I spoke, the set of his features—bronze-hard—told me I was talking into rock. Yet what did that matter? At worst, a struggle; perhaps the loss of the fortune I had begun to consider mine. Petty things. Eva must learn sooner or later my true name and the story of her father’s guilt. Why not now? And if we began poor—at least we began together. Separation from her was death with breath still in it.

Felix’s reply was the last language I expected.

“I anticipated this. From the moment you pleaded for the privilege of marrying her, I have looked forward to this outcome and provided against it. Weakness on the part of her bridegroom was to be expected. I have steeled myself to meet the emergency; for your oath must be kept.”

The tone crushed me: power without haste, a will that did not argue because it did not need to. I found my eyes, against my own intention, copying his earlier glance around the room. There was nothing in sight to alarm a reasonable man. And yet distrust came on me like a taste of iron at the back of the tongue.

It may have been his calm confidence; it may have been the unnaturalness of being penned in Oriental splendor while, beyond these walls, the street-cars clanged and humanity shoved past, ordinary life proceeding with its unthinking noise. Or it may have been the sound of Eva’s voice—plainly audible through the partition—humming a gay tune, a domestic spell against dread.

I forced down my uneasiness. I even smiled, though all the while I felt the eyes of a pictured face upon me—Evelyn’s portrait—hanging where I could not help but sense it, though I stood with my back to it. Felix faced it and did not look up.

He waited for my response. When I faltered, he said again, as if repeating a law rather than a wish:

“That oath must be kept.”

“Impossible!” I burst out. “It was in my nature to take it—worked upon as I was by family affection, the awfulness of our father’s approaching death, and a thousand uncanny influences all carefully measured and prepared for this end. But it is not in my nature to keep it after four months of natural living—after companionship with a man thirty years removed from his guilt, and with his guileless and wholly innocent daughter. You cannot drive me to it. No man can force another to abandon his own wife because of a wicked oath taken long before he knew her. If you think your money—”

“Money?” he cried, and the contempt in his voice did justice to my own disinterestedness as much as his. “I had forgotten I had it. No, Thomas, I should never weigh money against the happiness of living with such a woman as your wife appears to be. But her life I might. Carry out your threat; forget to pay John Poindexter the debt we owe him, and the matter will assume a seriousness for which you are poorly prepared. A daughter dead in her honeymoon will be almost as great a grief to him as a dishonored one. And either dead or dishonored he must find her, when he comes here in search of the child he cannot long forget. Which shall it be? Speak!”

For a moment the room reeled as if the lantern swung. Was this Felix? Was it I hearing such words? The air seemed to thin, as if the very breath had been rationed.

With a spring I reached his side at the table.

“You would not kill her!” I groaned. “You do not meditate a crime of blood—here—on her—the innocent—the good—”

“No,” he said. “It will be you who will do that. You who will not wish to see her languish—suffer—go mad. Thomas, I am not the raving being you take me for. I am merely a keeper of oaths. Nay, I am more: I have talents, skill. The house in which you find yourself is proof of this. This room—see, it has no outlet save those windows, scarcely perceptible to you above our heads, and that opening shielded now by a simple curtain, but which in an instant, without my moving from this place, I can so hermetically seal that no man, save he be armed with crowbar and pickaxe, could enter here—assuming any man knew of our imprisonment—within a house soon to be closed from top to bottom by my departing servant.”

“May God protect us,” fell from my lips.

My eyes went, stiff with horror, to the high windows and then to the doorway—only a few steps away, yet infinitely far from the small room where Eva had been persuaded to wait.

Felix spoke with the calm of a man describing an appliance.

“You are looking for the windows. They are behind those goblin faces outlined on the tapestries under the ceiling. As for the door—if you had looked left when you entered, you would have detected the edge of a huge steel plate hanging flush with the casing. This plate can be made to slide across that opening in an instant—by the touch of my hand on this button. Once done, no power save what I have mentioned can move it back again—not even my own. I have forces at my command for sending it forward, but none for returning it. Do you doubt my mechanical skill or the perfection of the electrical apparatus I have caused to be placed here? You need not. Nor need you doubt the will that has only to exert itself for an instant to—Shall I press the button, brother?”

“No, no!” I shouted, in a frenzy born less of his words than of my knowledge of the man. “Let me think. Let me know more fully what you require—what she must suffer if I consent—and what I—”

He let his smooth white hand slip back from the button. Then he smiled.

“I knew you would not be foolish,” he said. “Life has its charms even for hermits like me; and for a beau garçon such as you are—”

“Hush!” I broke in, maddened into daring his anger. “It is not my life I am buying, but hers—possibly yours, for it seems you have planned to perish with us. Is it not so?”

“Certainly,” he returned coldly. “Am I an assassin? Would you expect me to live, knowing you to be perishing?”

I stared, sick with it. Such resolve—such sacrifice to an idea—was beyond my comprehension.

“Why—what?” I stammered. “Why kill us? Why kill yourself—”

The answer did not come from his lips.

“Remember Evelyn!” shrilled a voice.

It came muffled and strange from above, from the hollow spaces of the high vault, and the yellow glow of the lantern took on for me a meaning it has never lost since—death.

“What is it?” I whispered. “Another of your contrivances?”

He smiled; I would rather he had frowned.

“Not exactly. A favorite bird—a starling. He repeats what he has heard echoed through the solitude of these rooms. I thought I had smothered him sufficiently to insure his silence during this interview. But he is self-willed, and seems disposed to defy the wrappings I have bound around him. It warns me to be speedy. Thomas—this is what I require.

“John Poindexter—you do not know where he is at this hour, but I do—received a telegram but now which, if he is a man at all, will bring him to this house in half an hour. It was sent in your name. In it you informed him that matters had arisen which demanded his immediate attention; that you were on your way to your brother’s—giving him this address—where, if you found entrance, you would await his presence in a room called the study; but that if the possible should occur and your brother should be absent from home, then he was to await a message from you at the Plaza. The appearance of the house will inform him whether he will find you and Eva within; or so I telegraphed him in your name.”

As he said it, I could hear the street again, that constant mechanical roar—sound without conscience—pressing against the walls. The irony of it: outside, the city’s appetite for movement; inside, a man’s appetite for vengeance.

“Thomas,” he went on, “if Bartow fulfils my instructions—and he has never failed me—he will pass down these stairs and out of this house in five minutes. He is bound on a long-promised journey, and he expects me to leave immediately after him. He has drawn every shade and fastened every lock. Consequently, on his exit, the house will become a tomb, to which, just two weeks from today, John Poindexter will be called again in words which will lead to a demolition that will disclose—what? Let us not forestall the future by inquiring.

“But Thomas—shall Bartow go? Shall I not, by signs he comprehends more readily than other men comprehend speech, indicate to him as he goes down that I wish him to wait and open the door to the man we have promised to overwhelm? You have only to write a line. See—I have made a copy of the words you must use, lest your self-command be too severely taxed. These words, left on this table—your handwriting, your signature—will tell him all he needs to know. The rest can come from my lips after he has read it. Have you anything to say against this plan? Anything beyond what you have urged? Anything that will prevent my finger from pressing the button on which it rests?”

I took up the paper. It lay on the table as if it had been waiting there like a blade laid out for supper.

I read the few lines—lines you all know, but which acquire another savor when you understand the purpose for which they were cooked up and set before him:

I return you back your daughter. Neither she nor you will ever see me again. Remember Evelyn!

AMOS’S SON.

“You wish me to sign this,” I said hoarsely—to put it into my own hand and make it mine? Mine?”

“Yes,” he replied. “And to leave it here for him to see when he enters. He might not believe any mere statement from me.”

Something rose in me like nausea and like rage. Love, life, the ordinary decencies that make a table worth sitting at and a name worth speaking—everything swept through my mind in one concentrated flood. I could not set my hand to such words; it would have been a profanity upon the only good thing I had ever earned.

I flung the paper at his face.

“You have gone too far! Better her death, better mine, better the destruction of us all, than such dishonor to the purest thing heaven ever made. I refuse, Felix—I refuse. And may God have mercy on us all!”

His face changed. His finger trembled above the fatal button. I saw, with a mind that ran ahead of sight, the steely edge of that plate sliding out beyond the lintel. I was about to dare everything—to grapple with him, blood or no blood—when a sound came from the other direction.

Eva.

The door of the adjoining room opened, and she advanced into the study.

At that instant a blood-red glow took the place of the sickly yellow that had filled the room. Whether it was a lantern shifted or some mechanism answered to Felix’s hand, I did not know; I only saw how it struck across her face, making her pallor look like snow under sunset, and how it gave her cheeks a color that the roses at her belt did not have.

Fearless and sweet—as in the hour she first told me she loved me—she approached and stood before us.

“What is this?” she cried. “I have heard words that sound more like utterances of some horrid dream than the talk of men and brothers. What does it mean, Thomas? What does it mean, Mr.—”

“Cadwalader,” Felix announced, dropping his eyes from her face but altering neither posture nor expression.

“Cadwalader?” The name was not to her what it was to her father. “Cadwalader—I have heard that name in my father’s house. It was Evelyn’s name—the Evelyn who—”

“Whom you see painted there over your head,” Felix finished. “My sister. Thomas’s sister—the girl whom your father—But I spare you, child though you be of a man who spared nothing. From your husband you may learn why a Cadwalader can never find happiness with a Poindexter. Why, thirty years after that young girl’s death, you—who were not then born—are given at this hour the choice between death and dishonor. I allow you five minutes to listen. After that you will let me know your joint decision. Only you must make your talk where you stand. A step taken by either of you to right or left, and Thomas knows what will follow.”

Five minutes. With such a history to confess and such a decision to reach. My head swam; my tongue failed; I stood dumb until Eva lifted her eyes to mine with a trust so complete it struck fire through my terror. Then words came—hot, broken, falling over one another like a pan tipped too suddenly.

I told her what I could: the oath wrung from me, the name I had hidden, the old hatred that had been kept like a preserving salt for thirty years, and Felix’s plan to shut us in like meat in a tin. I could not make it clean; some things do not translate from one soul to another without loss and ruin. She read more from my face than from my speech.

As the minutes fled and Felix raised a warning hand, she turned toward him.

“You are in earnest? We must separate in shame—or perish in this prison-house with you?”

His answer was repetition—mechanical, but firm.

“You have said it. You have but one minute more, madam.”

She shrank. For an instant all her strength seemed to pour out of her like water spilled. Then something in her—some fierce young instinct to keep life intact—flared up. Where a moment before she had been a delicate woman trembling on the edge of collapse, there stood a creature like flame given a human shape.

She looked at me as if asking whether I had the courage—or the will—to raise my hand against my own flesh and blood. Alas for us both: I did not understand her look, not until afterward.

Her hand moved. She seized an old Turkish dagger lying only too ready, and with one swift sideways thrust drove it into Felix’s side.

“We cannot part, we cannot die,” she cried. “We are too young—too happy!”

It was so sudden, so rapid a birth of purpose, that Felix—the ready, the prepared, the man armed against every contingency—did not press the fatal button.

He pressed his heart.

One impulsive act by a woman—an act made of love and terror—overthrew the fine-spun plans of the subtlest spirit that ever tried to work its will in the face of God and man.

But I did not think of our narrow escape then, nor of the price Eva might pay. My mind, my heart, my whole attention were on Felix.

Though struck to the heart, he did not fall at once. It was as if the will that had held him upright through thirty years of inner torture held him erect still, that he might give Eva one look the like of which I have never seen on any mortal face—and which will never leave my heart or hers until we die.

Then, as he saw her sink shuddering and the delicate woman reappear in her pallid, shrinking figure, he turned his eyes on me and—good God—I saw a tear well up from those stone-like orbs and roll slowly down his cheek. His face was already growing hollow under the stroke of death.

“Eva! Eva! I love Eva!” shrilled the voice from the vault above, the muffled starling insisting on its learned phrase as if the house itself had memory and could not be silenced.

Felix heard. A smile, faint as the failing rush of blood, moved his lips, and in that moment a revelation tore through me.

He, too, loved Eva.

When he saw that I knew, the will that had kept him standing gave way. He sank to the floor, murmuring:

“Take her away. I forgive. Save—save. She did not know I loved her.”

Eva, aghast, staring with set eyes at what she had done, had not moved. When she saw his head fall back and his limbs settle into the stillness that looked like death, a shock went through her so violent I thought her mind would never be whole again.

“I’ve killed him,” she murmured. “I’ve killed him.”

Her eyes flew wildly about and fell on the cross hanging behind us on the wall. Some buried schooling woke: Felix was a Catholic.

“Bring it,” she gasped. “Let him feel it on his breast. It may bring him peace—hope.”

I rushed to do her bidding. As I lifted the cross, she crumpled in a heap on the floor.

“Save,” came again from Felix’s lips—lips we had thought closed forever.

The word snapped me into action. I laid the cross into his arms, and then I caught my wife up—light as hunger, limp as a rag dipped in water—and ran with her out of that house.

The busy street struck my face like a slap: voices, wheels, the hard commerce of sound, a river of people moving with no knowledge of what had just happened in the tomb behind them. In that instant I understood peril anew. Any wrong step, any delay, any officious question—ruin.

I set Eva down only long enough to command life back into her limbs by the force of my own energy, then dragged her down the steps, merged us into the crowd, breathed for her, steadied her, lived in her until I could get her into a carriage. We drove away.

For the silence we kept from that time to this, you must not blame Mrs. Adams. When she came to herself—which was not for days—she wished, with the fierce honesty of her nature, to proclaim her act and take its full responsibility. But I would not have it. I loved her too dearly to see her name flung about in the newspapers like refuse in the street. And when her father was taken into our confidence, he was equally peremptory in enjoining silence and shared with me the watch I felt bound to keep over her.

But his peremptoriness was pride rather than love. John Poindexter has no more heart for his daughter than he had for his wife—or for that long-forgotten child from whose grave this tragedy had sprung. Had Felix triumphed, he would never have wrung this man’s heart. As Felix once said, when a man cares for nothing and nobody—not even for himself—it is useless to curse him.

Silence came down on the room the way a lid settles over a simmering pot—slow at first, then with a final hush that made every small sound too loud. Cloth whispered as people shifted in their chairs; a boot heel worried the floor; somewhere beyond the walls the city kept up its metallic fret, carts and distant hammers, an industrial clatter that had no respect for grief. In that uneasy soundscape Thomas Adams turned toward his wife with a look that was all hunger and fear at once—as if love were a broth he could keep warm only by staring at it.

The inspector, careful as a man returning a knife to its sheath, passed back to Mr. Gryce the memorandum he had earlier received. Gryce unfolded it and held it where the lamplight—steady, domestic, deceptively calm—could show the pages’ numbered lines. The sheet looked like a menu of questions, but each item was a sharp, cold course, served without garnish.

1. Why should a woman calm enough to pause and set her hair in order at the beginning of an interview be driven, before it ended, into such frenzy and exasperation as to kill—by her own hand—a man against whom she had shown no previous grudge? (Remember the comb found on the floor of Mr. Adams’s bedroom.)

2. What was the meaning of the words written just before that fatal interview by the man who was killed: “I return you your daughter. Neither you nor she will ever see me again. Remember Evelyn!”

3. Why was the pronoun “I” used? What place did Mr. Felix Adams hold toward the young woman that he could write so—after her marriage to his brother?

4. And having written it, why, when attacked by her, did he try to swallow the paper—dying with it clenched between his teeth?

5. If he was struck down in anger and “died as monsters do” (her own word), why did his face show sorrow rather than hate—determination rather than the wild, boiling change of passions one expects after a mortal blow from an unexpected hand?

6. Why, if he had strength enough to seize the paper and lift it to his lips, did he not use that strength to turn on a light that would have brought help—why leave instead the crimson glow which, by the understood code of signals, meant: Nothing more required just now. Keep away?

7. What was the meaning of the huge steel plate found between the door-casings, and why did it lie motionless in its socket at that culminating instant of his life?

8. How did old Poindexter come upon the scene so soon? His overheard words had been: “It is Amos’ son, not Amos!” Did he not know whom he was to meet in that house? Was the body before him—cross on the breast, dagger in the heart—less a surprise than the name of the victim?

9. Remember the conclusions drawn from Bartow’s pantomime: Mr. Adams was killed by a left-handed thrust. Watch for acknowledgment that the young woman is left-handed, and do not forget an explanation is due why, for so long, she held her other arm stretched out behind her.

10. Why did the bird whose chief cry is “Remember Evelyn!” sometimes vary it with “Poor Eva! Lovely Eva! Who would strike Eva?” For this tragedy to be true, Mr. Adams must have known his brother’s bride long and well.

11. If Bartow is, as we think, innocent of all connection with the crime save as witness, why did he show such joy at its result? This may fall outside Mr. Thomas Adams’s confession, yet it cannot be ignored. The deaf-and-dumb servitor was driven mad by the fact that caused him joy. Why?

12. Notice the schedule drawn after repeated experiments with Bartow and the sliding shades of the strange lamp in Mr. Adams’s study:

White light—Water wanted.  
Green light—Overcoat and hat to be brought.  
Blue light—Put back books on shelves.  
Violet light—Arrange study for the night.  
Yellow light—Watch for next light.  
Red light—Nothing wanted; stay away.

The last was burning at the final scene. Note whether this can be explained by Mr. Adams’s account.

When Gryce lowered the paper, it was plain that most of the courses had been eaten—explained, digested, accounted for. Two paragraphs alone still sat on the table like untouched food.

Of these, the ninth mattered. Bartow’s silent mimicry—those strained, peculiar gestures that the police had guarded like a family recipe—had insisted on a detail the spoken story had not yet seasoned into place. Gryce, after a brief consultation with the inspector, approached Thomas.

“Have you strength,” he asked, “to show us the blow as you saw it dealt?”

Thomas stared at him with the question he did not dare shape into words. He knew Bartow had made signs—everybody in the room knew that much—but he did not know the exact nature of them. The authorities had kept that knowledge fenced in, as if it were a garden one might trample by looking too closely. So he did not answer at once. He sat, thinking hard, as though he were tasting memory for the hidden spice.

Then, abruptly, he thrust out his left hand, seized some small object from the inspector’s table—anything would serve—and made a lunge across his body into an imaginary man standing at his right. The motion was quick, practiced by terror: shoulder turning, wrist driving, the whole act too bodily to be denied.

He looked up at the faces around him with an anxiety so naked it seemed to change the air.

“You would make your wife out left-handed,” Mr. Gryce observed, his tone dry as a ledger page. “I have watched her since she came here, and I have seen no evidence of it.”

“She is not left-handed,” Thomas broke in, voice roughened by the strain of reliving it. “But she thrust with her left because her right was fast held in mine. I seized her instinctively as she bounded forward for the weapon. Our hands—locked so—did not loosen till the horror of what she had done made her faint. She fell away from me to the floor, crying, ‘Tear down the cross and lay it on your brother’s breast. I would at least see him die the death of a Christian.’”

At that, Mr. Gryce glanced toward the inspector with a relief that did not soften his features but did ease the tightness in his eyes. The strange posture in Bartow’s pantomime—the long-held other arm stretched behind—now found its plain, human cause: a grip of love become, in an instant, a restraint.

Gryce picked up the blue pencil the inspector had laid down. With the crisp finality of a man striking through a completed entry, he wrote across paragraph No. 9 one firm word:

answered.

A few minutes later Mr. Gryce stepped into the outer room and let his gaze travel, with that quiet exactness of his, over the cluster of faces and postures gathered there. He was listening as much as looking. Even in a place meant for official business, the world insisted on its own soundscape: the clink of a chair-leg nudged an inch, the rasp of a boot on worn boards, the thin metallic tick of a radiator that could not decide whether to heat or complain. Beneath it all lay the city’s constant grind—carriage-wheels, distant horns, the hoarse breath of industry—pressing at the windows like a hand that never withdrew. It was in such din, he reflected, that people forgot their own inner language first: the small words of conscience and the older words of custom.

He was seeking an answer to a question that still worried him, and he had come here hoping the room itself would yield it. It did.

In a quiet corner—quiet by comparison, a pocket where the noise fell away as if muffled by drapery—he found Miss Butterworth. She sat as composed as a hostess who has set a roast to rest under its cover and is simply awaiting the moment to carve. Her gloves were folded with domestic precision; her look held the calm of a woman who knew, to the minute, when the kettle would sing.

He approached and, with a rare softening at the mouth, accused her of having had a hand in the commotion.

She met it with a flash of indignation. “I was simply there,” she insisted. “When the crisis came—when the poor young creature learned that her husband had left suddenly for New York in the company of two men—then, why then, it became apparent to every one that a woman should be at her side who understood her case and the extremity in which she found herself. And I was that woman.”

“You are always that woman,” he returned gallantly, “if by the phrase you mean being in the right place at the right time. So you are already acquainted with Mrs. Adams’s story?”

“Yes. The ravings of a moment told me she was the one who had handled the dagger that slew Mr. Adams.” Miss Butterworth’s voice lowered on the word slew, as if even sound might bruise the fact. “Afterward, she was able to explain the cause of what has seemed to us such a horrible crime. When I heard her story, Mr. Gryce, I no longer hesitated either as to her duty or mine.” Then, with the directness of a woman who has measured flour without a scale all her life, she asked the question that mattered. “Do you think she will be called upon to answer for this blow? Will she be tried—convicted?”

“Madam,” he said, “there are not twelve men in the city so devoid of intelligence as to apply the name of crime to an act which was so evidently one of self-defence. No true bill will be found against young Mrs. Adams. Rest easy.”

It was as visible as the easing of a tight lid: the gloom left Miss Butterworth’s eyes. “Then I may return home in peace,” she cried. “It has been a desperate five hours for me, and I feel well shaken up. Will you escort me to my carriage?”

To Mr. Gryce she did not look shaken. Indeed, she had rarely appeared more serene—settled in herself as in a familiar kitchen, where even the sharpest knife had its rightful place. But people were the only evidence of their own tremors, and he did not dispute her. She rose, first satisfying herself—by a glance both appraising and protective—that the object of her care was reviving under the solicitous ministrations of her husband. Then she took the arm he offered and allowed him to conduct her out.

The passage to the door was crowded with incidental noises: a clerk’s pen scratching like a mouse behind a wainscot, the flap of paper, the short laugh of a man trying to sound untroubled. Mr. Gryce, who could read a room the way some read a ledger, found himself thinking how easily a person’s final words could be drowned—not by malice, but by the everyday racket of a world that did not know how to listen.

At the carriage, as he assisted Miss Butterworth in, he asked the question that had been nagging him. “Why is not Mrs. Adams’s father here? Did he allow his daughter to leave him on such an errand as this without offering to accompany her?”

The answer came curt as a snapped string. “Mr. Poindexter is a man without heart. He came with us to New York, but refused to follow us to Police Headquarters. Sir, you will find that the united passions of three burning souls, and a revenge the most deeply cherished of any I ever knew or heard of, have been thrown away on a man who is positively unable to suffer. Do not mention old John Poindexter to me.” She drew a breath, as if shutting a pantry door on a smell she despised. “And now, if you will be so good, tell the coachman to drive me to my home in Gramercy Park. I have put my finger in the police pie for the last time, Mr. Gryce—positively for the last time.”

She sank back upon the cushions with an inexorable look that did not quite succeed in hiding a quiet complacency—as of one who, having intervened in a household crisis, can already taste the relief of returning to her own table, her own familiar receipts, her own rooms where the city’s harshest clamor is kept outside by thick curtains and habit.

Mr. Gryce repressed a smile, bowed lower even than his wont, and under the shelter of that bow the carriage rolled away, its wheels taking up the street’s rhythm until it merged with the larger, ceaseless urban roar.

As he walked slowly back, he sighed. Perhaps he wondered—though he would have denied it if asked—whether a case of similar interest would ever bring them together again in consultation, two minds meeting in the brief hush between one disturbance and the next.
