Romola paced the length of the old library, her steps quiet on the worn stone floor, her eyes fixed on the door as if she could will Tito’s return through sheer longing. Outside, Florence buzzed with the arrival of the French king and his court—windows flung open, crowds gathered, faces bright with anticipation. But Romola had not looked out. Not since her father’s death three months earlier had she taken part in any festivity. That day, when he had fallen suddenly in his chair, still waiting for Tito to come write with him, the silence that followed was more final than any word.

He had asked, just once, “Is not Tito coming to write?”—a question born of hope, not fear. She had answered gently, knowing the truth: Tito had been summoned to a supper at the cardinal’s, one of many such invitations that now crowded his evenings. Bardo had sighed, then rallied with a spark of urgency: “Ah! Then perhaps he will bring news about the library? The cardinal promised last week.” He paused, then flushed, and said, “I must go on without him, Romola. Get the pen. I have no new text—but I must say what I want to say about the New Platonists. I shall die and nothing will be done.”

She brought the pen. He spoke no more.

She remembered that moment later with a pang of shame—not because she mourned too little, but because even in grief, a whisper had stirred within her: *Now my life with Tito may truly begin.*

It was not that she blamed him. Tito had always been tender, attentive, respectful toward her father. But she saw now, with clarity, that no one else could bear the weight of those endless hours—of reading aloud, of copying manuscripts, of enduring the slow erosion of a man’s mind by sorrow and unfulfilled dreams. Even she, who had loved her father with all the passion of youth, had sometimes felt rebellion stir beneath her patience. Before marriage, Tito had seemed stronger in endurance; but then, novelty made effort light. Now, it wore thin.

She told herself she had been foolish once—a girl dreaming of a perfect life where love, duty, and joy would merge into one radiant whole. But reality was less luminous. The sweetness of marriage had been tempered by the quiet ache of absence. Tito was never truly absent from her heart, yet he was often gone—drawn into circles she could not enter, into conversations that left her feeling like a shadow beside him.

She did not resent him. How could she? His caresses were as warm as ever. When she hesitated, timidly asking him to stay, he smiled and lingered, brushing her hair, murmuring words that melted her fears. But she knew—deep down—that this was not enough. It was not his fault. It was simply that their natures were different. Hers was rooted in devotion, in loyalty to memory, in the sacredness of obligation. His was shaped by charm, by ease, by the thrill of the world beyond the walls of their home.

And so she struggled—to understand, to forgive, to hold back the thoughts that might wound him. She refused to let resentment grow. To admit that Tito acted unworthily would be to betray the love she cherished. Instead, she turned inward, seeking strength in endurance. If he could not give her the time she needed, she would make do with the moments he gave. And now, with her father gone, there was no longer any claim dividing them. Their lives could flow together at last.

Yet even this hope carried a weight. The thought of joy in his absence felt like a betrayal. So she clung harder to her purpose—to fulfill her father’s dream. The library, his life’s work, was not merely a collection of texts and relics. It was a testament. A promise kept. And though Bernardo del Nero had saved it from creditors, offering a thousand florins against a lien, the future remained uncertain. The Medici were gone. Their collections, once safe in the Via Larga, now faced dispersal. The French agents already eyed Lorenzo’s gems as rightful spoils. Florence, burdened by war and debt, might sell its treasures for gold.

To Romola, these political shifts mattered only insofar as they threatened her father’s legacy. She had lived apart from the world, raised in books and silence. Heroism, revolution, statecraft—these were distant ideas, like myths from ancient Greece. But now, the fate of the library pulled her into the currents of history. She began to care. She wanted to know what Fra Girolamo preached, what power he wielded over the people, what role he might play in the city’s fate.

That morning, for the first time, she had gone to hear one of his Advent sermons in the Duomo. After visiting her father’s grave in Santa Croce, she walked on, drawn by curiosity and a sense of duty. The memory of Dino still stirred in her—the boy whose voice had once pierced her soul with questions about justice and faith. But that moment had faded under the weight of married life, subsumed by love and routine.

Still, she had expected something from Fra Girolamo. His voice, his presence, had struck her before. She hoped he might move her—not as a fanatic, but as a man of depth. And he did. Not with terror or guilt, but with a strange resonance. When he spoke of martyrdom, she wept. Not from fear, but from a sudden, inexplicable connection—a feeling as if she stood on the edge of something vast, something beyond her own small world.

It was like music remembered, then heard again in full force. But it did not settle in her heart. It passed, leaving only a trace.

Now, she waited. Listened. For three months, each day had felt like a new beginning. She had tried to be gentle, to suppress the criticism that rose when Tito spoke of banquets and politics. She had held back suggestions about frugality, about paying off Bernardo del Nero. She knew Tito valued comfort. He worked hard, yes, but for pleasures that were not hers. And so she resolved to change—no more sharp edges, no more silent judgments. She would become softer, more yielding, so that their union might be whole.

At last, she heard the great door creak open. She hurried to the top of the stairs, heart beating.

Tito ascended slowly, his face tired, his cloak thrown back. The lamp above cast shadows across his features. In eighteen months, Romola had changed more than he. Her expression was softer now, her gaze beseeching. A flush warmed her cheeks as she saw him.

“Tito,” she said, “you are weary. Was it a long day?”

Maso stood nearby, but no further words were spoken until they entered the library. The fire burned brightly in the grate, casting flickering light across the room. Tito kissed her briefly, then sank into his high-backed chair near the flames, tossing aside his cap.

“I wish you would stop sitting here,” he said, his voice heavy with fatigue. “Our rooms are warmer. This weather is cruel.”

Romola blinked. This was not the Tito she knew—the man who always noticed her cold, who brought her wine, who laughed at her smallest worries. Something was wrong.

“You forget,” she said softly, “I am finishing the catalogue—my father’s plan. You cannot help me, so I must work alone.”

Tito closed his eyes, rubbing his temples. He felt alienated—unlike himself. He could not explain why. Some deep fear, some secret dread, had begun to gnaw at him, making him feel distant from her, even afraid of her mind. He had never meant to be cold. But the effort to smile, to speak kindly, felt impossible.

“I am not well,” he said. “Do not be surprised if I seem irritable.”

Romola knelt beside him, brushing his hair from his forehead. Then, suddenly, she froze. “What is that under your tunic, Tito? Something hard… like iron.”

“It is iron,” he said quietly. “Chain-armour.”

She stared. “You bought it? For yourself?”

“For protection,” he replied. “There was danger today. I shall wear it often.”

Her breath caught. “Danger? What threatens you?”

“Everyone who is not a rabid enemy of the Medici,” he said, forcing a smile. “But don’t worry. This armour will keep me safe from ambush.”

She clung to him, trembling. “Then my godfather—what of him? He takes no precautions. Should he not?”

“Precisely because I am less important,” Tito said, “I am more vulnerable. They suspect me of being an envoy. Men like Bernardo are protected by family and influence. I am a Greek—no one will avenge me.”

“But Tito,” she whispered, “is it a specific threat? Or just fear?”

He hesitated. “I have received threats. But I beg you—do not speak of this. Not even to your godfather.”

“I will not,” she said, blushing. “But… this will make you unhappy.”

“What will make me wretched?” he asked, a flicker of pain crossing his face.

“This fear… this heavy metal. It feels like a curse. As if some fiend had turned your skin to steel. It is so unlike you—so unlike my bright, joyful husband.”

Tito smiled faintly. “Then you’d rather have me stabbed or shot? I’ll give up the armour—shall I?”

“No,” she said quickly. “It is fanciful. Do not mind me. But are such crimes common in Florence now?”

“They may become so,” he said. “With hatreds growing daily.”

They sat in silence for a while. Then Romola lifted her head. “Tell me about today. Did everything go well?”

“Excellently,” he said. “First, the rain ended Luca Corsini’s speech—no one wanted to hear it. Then someone—Gaddi, or Melema, or perhaps it was me—gave the Cristianissimo a brief, clumsy welcome in broken French.”

Romola laughed. “It was you, wasn’t it? You never take credit for anything.”

He grinned. “After that, there was a parade of arms and jewels—more than at any Florentine joust. Strutting, prancing, chaos. The crowd roared. The king smiled. Then dinner, flattery, games. I was at Tornabuoni’s. I’ll tell you tomorrow.”

“Not tonight,” she said, leaning closer. “But—do you think peace is possible for Florence? Will the Republic avoid fresh troubles?”

Tito shrugged. “Florence will have no peace unless it pays dearly for it.”

Romola’s smile faltered. But she forced cheerfulness. “You wouldn’t guess where I went today. I went to the Duomo. To hear Fra Girolamo.”

Tito stiffened. He had thought of Baldassarre—had feared he might have entered the church. But Romola mistook his look.

“You’re surprised?” she said, smiling. “I wanted to understand the times. I wanted to hear what the Frate says about the French invasion.”

“And what did you think of the prophet?”

“He has a mysterious power,” she said. “Much of it I expected. But once—I was deeply moved. I wept with the others.”

“Take care,” Tito said lightly, relieved she hadn’t mentioned Baldassarre. “You have a touch of fanaticism. I’ll have you seeing visions like your brother.”

“No,” she said. “It was the same for everyone. Except perhaps Dolfo Spini—he was making faces. There was a man, too—wild-eyed, with a rope around his neck. An escaped prisoner, I think. He wept openly, listening.”

Tito was silent a moment. Then he said, “I saw him. I was outside with Lorenzo Tornabuoni when he ran in. He fled from a French soldier. Did you see him leave?”

“No. Piero di Cosimo took him out. I saw him cut the rope. But you’re ill, Tito? You need rest?”

“Yes,” he said, rising. “I’m tired.”

And as he walked away, the weight of secrecy pressed upon him—the fear that Baldassarre had said something terrible, done something worse—and that every step he took now might lead him deeper into darkness.
