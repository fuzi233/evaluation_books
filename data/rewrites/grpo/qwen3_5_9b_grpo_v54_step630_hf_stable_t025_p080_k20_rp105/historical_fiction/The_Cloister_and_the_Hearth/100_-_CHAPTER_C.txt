The curtain finally falls, yet I remain upon the stage, compelled by a custom I detest but lack the courage to defy. I linger here to gather the scattered fragments of humanity I have left behind. Let it be understood that these minor figures hold no claim upon my affection; they have served their purpose merely to convince you that Gerard traversed the path from Holland to Rome among living souls, and not through a parade of lifeless dolls.

Eli and Catherine lived to a great age, their longevity stretching until both faded into dim memories for Gerard and Margaret. Giles too was long-lived; he journeyed to the court of Bavaria and remained alive there at ninety, though he had somehow withered into nothing more than leather and bone, his voice reduced to a hollow trumpet tone.

Cornelis, freed from all rivals and long forgiven by his mother—who clung to him with increasing desperation as her brood dispersed—waited and waited for his parents' passing. Yet Catherine's shrewd prophecy proved true: before she and her mate could wear themselves out, this worthy man rusted away. At sixty-five, he lay dying of old age in his mother's arms, she herself a hale woman of eighty-six. He had lain unconscious for a time, but returned to consciousness in the very moment of his death. Seeing her near him, he spoke of transforming the shop and premises once they were his. "Yes, my darling," the poor old woman soothed him gently. Within another minute, he was clay, and that clay was followed to the grave by every foot that had ever waited for his shoes.

Denys, broken-hearted by his comrade's death, returned to Burgundy. There, a small pension from the court sustained him until an unexpected inheritance from a distant relative brought him a considerable sum. For many years, he was known in his native land as a crusty old soldier, capable of recounting tales of war when he chose, and a bitter railer against women.

Jerome, disgusted by the northern laxity, retired to Italy. Through high connections, he became a mitred abbot at the age of seventy. He tightened the screw of discipline; his monks revered him and hated him in equal measure. He ruled with an iron rod for ten years. Then, one night, he died alone, for he had never found the way to a single heart. The Vulgate lay upon his pillow, the crucifix in his hand, and upon his lips rested something resembling a smile—a sight never witnessed while he lived. Thus, at that awful hour, he was not quite alone. *Requiescat in pace.* The Master he served has many servants, each with many minds; now and then, a faithful one proves to be a surly one, just as it is in these mortal mansions.

The yellow-haired lad, Gerard Gerardson, belongs not to fiction but to history. She has recorded his birth in terms far grander than mine. Above the tailor's house in the Brede Kirk Straet, she has inscribed: *"Haec est parva domus natus qua magnus Erasmus,"* and she has penned half a dozen lives of him. Yet, there remains something for her yet to do. She has not fully comprehended the *Magnum Erasmus*, nor can any other pigmy comprehend a giant, or a partisan understand a judge.

First scholar and divine of his epoch, he was also the heaven-born dramatist of his century. Some of the finest scenes in this new chronicle originate from his medieval pen, illuminating the pages where they appear. For the words of a genius so lofty as his are not born to die. Their immediate work upon mankind fulfilled, they may seem to lie torpid; yet with each fresh shower of intelligence that Time pours upon their students, they prove their immortal race. They revive, they spring from the dust of great libraries; they bud, they flower, they bear fruit and seed, passing from generation to generation, from age to age.
