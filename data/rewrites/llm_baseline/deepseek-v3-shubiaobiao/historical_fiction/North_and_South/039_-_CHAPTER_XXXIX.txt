Margaret closed the door of her room behind her with a quiet finality, as though shutting out the echoes of Mrs. Thornton’s accusations. The air still hummed with the older woman’s disdain, but Margaret refused to let it settle upon her. She paced once, twice—then stopped, pressing her palms flat against the sides of her skirts. The walls of the house were thin; she would not give Mrs. Thornton the satisfaction of hearing her distress.

When at last the front door clicked shut, signaling the older woman’s departure, Margaret sank into a chair. Methodically, she retraced their conversation, weighing each word, each implication. A bitter smile touched her lips. "She believes the worst of me—yet not the worst I’ve actually done," she murmured. "He never told her about Frederick. Of course he wouldn’t."

The thought brought an unexpected pang. She lifted her chin, as if in defiance of some unseen critic. Then, just as swiftly, her hands clenched. "He must think Frederick is—" The word *lover* scorched her mind unspoken. "That’s why he looks at me so. Not just because I lied, but because he believes I’ve given my heart elsewhere."

The injustice of it stung. She pressed her fingers to her temples. "Why does it matter? Why should I care what he thinks of me?" But the question rang hollow. She *did* care, and the realization was a weight she could scarcely bear.

Restless, she stood abruptly. "Enough. I won’t dwell on this. There’s no use in it." She reached for her bonnet with sharp, deliberate motions. "If I live to be old, perhaps I’ll sit by the fire and mourn the life I might have had. But not today."

Tears threatened—hot, humiliating—but she dashed them away. The open air would steady her. She hurried out, turning toward the fields beyond Milton, as if speed alone could outrun her thoughts.

—

When she returned, her father met her at the door. "Ah, there you are! I hoped you’d gone to see Mrs. Boucher."

Margaret flushed. "I forgot. But I’ll go after dinner."

The visit was inevitable. Mrs. Boucher was dying, the doctor delayed, the children scattered among neighbors. Margaret lingered long enough to feel useless, then turned toward the Higginses’ home, half-hoping for news of Nicholas’s appeal to Thornton.

She found him at the dresser, spinning a penny for wide-eyed children. His grin faded when she asked after the mill master.

"Ay, I saw him. Refused me, as expected." His voice was rough, but his hands gentle as he steadied the wobbling coin. "Told me to mind my own business—and yours."

Margaret stiffened. "He said that?"

A floorboard creaked behind her. She turned—and there stood Thornton himself, his expression unreadable. For a heartbeat, their eyes locked. Then Margaret swept past him without a word, her cheeks burning.

—

Thornton watched her go, irritation warring with something softer. Higgins’s defiance had amused him; the man’s loyalty to his dead foe, Boucher, had moved him. But Margaret Hale’s presence here, meddling again, unsettled him.

"You might’ve named her," he said sharply to Higgins.

The man shrugged. "Would you have spoken fairer of her then?"

Thornton ignored the jab. "The children—Boucher’s?"

Higgins’s jaw tightened. "Aye. And mine now."

A pause. Then Thornton spoke low. "I was wrong this morning. I apologize."

The words hung between them. Higgins studied him, then nodded once. "Work, then?"

"If you want it."

Higgins exhaled. "I’ll take it. And—thank you."

Thornton gripped his hand. "Be on time. No trouble."

A grudging smile. "No promises."

—

Margaret did not see Thornton again until he overtook her on the street. His voice, when he spoke, was cool.

"You were quick to judge me, Miss Hale. Higgins has his work."

She kept her tone even. "I’m glad of it."

He hesitated. "He repeated my words—about women interfering."

"An opinion you’ve every right to hold," she said, too brightly. Then, impulsively: "But he didn’t tell you the full truth—" She broke off, the lie about Frederick choking her.

Thornton’s gaze sharpened. "Truth is rare. I don’t expect it." The unspoken *from you* hung between them. "But if you’ve explanations, I’ll hear them."

She shook her head. "I can’t. It’s not my secret to tell."

His jaw tightened. "Then we’ve nothing more to say." He bowed and strode away.

Margaret stood frozen. *Why must every thought lead back to him?* She forced a breath. *Enough. This ends now.*

But the resolve felt frail as paper.

—

That evening, her father remarked on her unnatural gaiety. She laughed too loud, teased too sharply, until even he grew uneasy. Then Mary Higgins came with news: work secured at Thornton’s mill.

Margaret’s smile faltered. The name was a specter she could not escape.

Days passed in a haze of strain. Letters arrived—Edith’s, full of plans to return to England; Mr. Bell’s, announcing a visit. Margaret grasped at them like lifelines. Harley Street, with its quiet rhythms, beckoned as a refuge.

But Milton held fast. However she turned, Thornton’s shadow stretched long—in her father’s readings, in Higgins’s gratitude, even in Mr. Bell’s offhand mention of lease negotiations.

The past clung; the future loomed. And Margaret, caught between, wondered if she would ever be free.
