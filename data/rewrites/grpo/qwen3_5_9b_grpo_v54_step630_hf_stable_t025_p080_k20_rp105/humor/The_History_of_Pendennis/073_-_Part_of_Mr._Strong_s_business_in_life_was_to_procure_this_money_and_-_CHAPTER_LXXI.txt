The dinner arrived just as Arthur stumbled back into the room, and Lady Rockminster immediately launched into a tirade about his tardiness. But Laura, catching sight of her cousin's ghastly pallor and terrified expression, cut her imperious aunt short. With a voice trembling with concern, she asked what had happened. Was Arthur ill?

Arthur, however, merely drained a massive bumper of sherry. "I've heard the most extraordinary news," he stammered, eyeing the servants nervously. "I'll tell you later." He was visibly shaking, his nerves frayed.

"Don't trample and beat the floor with your feet under the table!" Lady Rockminster snapped. "You've crushed Fido and knocked over his saucer. Look at Mr. Warrington; he keeps his boots perfectly quiet."

By the time dessert arrived, the meal seemed interminable. Finally, Lady Rockminster declared the dinner a dismal failure. "Something must have happened," she muttered, dismissing the possibility of tea. "I'm going to have a nap. Good night, Mr. Warrington. Come again, when there's no business to discuss." With a toss of her head, the old lady marched out with theatrical dignity.

George and the others rose to follow, and Warrington prepared to depart, bidding Laura a farewell that she received with wide, anxious eyes. Then Arthur stopped him. "Stay, George. You must hear this news and advise me. I'm at a loss."

"It concerns Blanche, Arthur," Laura whispered, her heart hammering against her ribs, her cheeks flushing a color she'd never known.

"Yes," Pen added, his voice tight. "And a scandalous story. When I left you to visit my uncle's lodgings, I found his old servant, Morgan, at the door. He said he and his master had parted that morning; my uncle had fled to this very hotel. When I arrived, he was out to dinner. Morgan insisted I come inside, claiming he had something vital to say. Apparently, the scoundrel has hoarded a fortune while serving my uncle and is now a millionaire. He told me this must remain a secret among us. Blanche's father isn't dead. He's alive. The marriage between Clavering and the Begum was never valid."

"So Blanche is her grandfather's heir?" Warrington asked.

"Perhaps," Pen replied grimly. "But what a father! Amory is an escaped convict—Clavering knows it, my uncle knows it. That's the leverage they used to force Clavering to surrender his borough to me."

"Blanche doesn't know," Laura said softly. "Nor does Lady Clavering?"

"No," Pen confirmed. "Blanche believes her father drowned in New South Wales as a ship's captain, not as a convict. Lady Clavering has hinted they weren't happy and that her husband was a bad character. She once told me, tears in her eyes, that it was hard for a woman to admit she was glad to hear her husband dead, adding that she had chosen poorly twice in her life. Now? If the rascal returns, Morgan will blackmail him. Morgan holds the secret and intends to extort money from us all. He thought I knew, asking for an annuity to keep quiet, threatening to drag Clavering down to the hulks. Good heavens, was my uncle mad to conspire in this? To think my mother's son would trade on such treason!"

"It's Colonel Altamont, of course," Warrington realized. "I see it all now."

"If the rascal comes back," Arthur continued, "Morgan will use it. He proposes to rob us all. The devil assumed I was in on it."

"No!" Warrington's deep voice cracked, filled with a mixture of love and agony. He looked at the two young lovers with a pang of despair. "Our boy can't meddle in this filth. Arthur Pendennis can't marry a convict's daughter and sit in Parliament as a member for the hulks. You must wash your hands of this, Pen. Break it off. Give no explanation, just cite family reasons. It's better if those poor women think you false than that they know the truth. Besides, I can get Clavering to acknowledge your reasons are sufficient to void the union. Don't you agree, Laura?" He couldn't bear to meet her gaze, knowing he was casting away his last shred of hope.

"Her father's shame isn't Blanche's fault, dear Arthur," Laura said, her voice rapid and pale. "If you were married, would you desert her because she's innocent? You're pledged to her. If she's unhappy, wouldn't you comfort her? Our mother would have." As she spoke, she wrapped her arms around him, burying her face in his heart.

"Our mother is an angel with God," Pen sobbed. "And you are the dearest, the best of women. Teach me my duty. Pray for me. God bless you, my sister!"

"Amen," Warrington groaned, burying his face in his hands. "She's right. She can't do wrong. That girl looks like an angel." Indeed, she smiled with a radiant innocence that haunted him for days.

"And now," Laura said, resting her small hand on the table, "we shall have some tea."

Before this mundane conclusion could settle, a servant announced that Major Pendennis had returned to the hotel and was waiting for his nephew. Laura, her face writing a silent plea—*Behave, hold the line, be gentle but firm*—took her leave and retreated to her room. Warrington, who rarely drank tea, grumbled as he watched the empty chair. *Why couldn't old Pendennis have come an hour later?* The inevitable moment had struck. The hand was shaken, the door closed, and the friend was gone. Alone in the smoking room of a neighboring club, surrounded by men brawling about politics and opera girls, Warrington lit a cigar. He bore the sacred secret like a lead weight, sinking deeper into the noise, wondering which window in the hotel held the light of the girl he loved.

Meanwhile, Arthur walked the hotel passages, his anger boiling. He was furious that the old man had turned him into a puppet, compromising his honor. When Arthur finally met the Major, the old gentleman's hand was ice-cold and trembling. He coughed, grumbled over the fire, and cursed Morgan's ingratitude with peevish passion.

"The confounded impudent scoundrel! He challenged me to a fight last night! I nearly drove a knife into him! He's made ten thousand pounds and deserves hanging! But he knew all my ways. When I rang the bell, that thief brought the thing I wanted, unlike that stupid German lout."

"What have you been doing in the country? Been with Lady Rockminster? She's of the old school, *vieille ecole*, *bonne ecole*. They don't make gentlemen like that anymore. In fifty years, you won't know a man from another. But they'll last my time. I'm getting old, Pen. I was packing my library today and found a Bible belonging to my poor mother. I'd like you to keep it. You'll open the box when it's your turn, and the old fellow is laid under the sod."

His age and kindness disarmed Arthur's fury, stirring a pang of guilt. Arthur knew the announcement he was about to make would shatter the old man's hopes.

"I'm off, sir," the Major nodded. "But I'd like to read a speech of yours in the Times before I go—'Mr. Pendennis said, Unaccustomed as I am...' Hey, Arthur? You look healthy. My brother Jack brought the family right. Go west, buy the old estate. *Nec tenui penna*. We'll rise again. You'll be a Baronet before you die."

The words struck Arthur like a blow. *And it is I who am about to demolish this poor old man's castle.*

"I went to your lodgings at Bury Street, though I didn't find you," Arthur began slowly. "I spoke with Morgan, Uncle."

"Indeed!" The Major's face flushed. "The cat's out of the bag, begad!"

"He told me a story that shocked and pained me deeply," Arthur said.

The Major feigned nonchalance. "What? The story about... what-d'ye-call-'em?"

"About Miss Amory's father. About Lady Clavering's first husband, who he is, and what."

"Hem. A devilish awkward affair!" The old man rubbed his nose. "I've been aware of that confounded circumstance for some time."

"I wish I'd known sooner, or not at all," Arthur muttered.

*He is safe,* the Major thought, relieved. *I should have kept it from you and those innocent women.*

"You're right. No reason for the women to know. I'll never tell them, though that villain Morgan might. He wants to trade on the secret and has offered me ransom. It's unpleasant to think I'm engaged to a convict's daughter."

"That's exactly why I kept it from you, my dear boy. But Miss Amory isn't a convict's daughter, do you see? She's the daughter of Lady Clavering, with fifty or sixty thousand pounds. Her father-in-law, a Baronet of high reputation, approves the match and gives up his seat to his son-in-law. What could be simpler?"

"Is it true, sir?"

"Begad, yes. Of course. Amory is dead. The first sign of life he shows, he's dead. We have him at a deadlock, like the fellow in the play 'The Critic.' Monstrously witty man, Sheridan. By Gad, when I was at the Cape, I remember—"

The Major's garrulity, a desperate attempt to distract Arthur, failed. Arthur interrupted. "If you'd told me this sooner, you'd have spared us both pain. I wouldn't be tied to an engagement I can't honorably recede from."

"No, begad, we've fixed you. A man fixed to a seat in Parliament and a pretty girl with two thousand a year is fixed to no bad thing."

"Great Heavens, sir!" Arthur cried. "Are you blind? Can't you see?"

"See what, young gentleman?"

"See that rather than trade on this secret of Amory's, I'd join my father-in-law at the hulks! Rather than take a parliamentary seat bought by Clavering's silence, I'd take the spoons off the table! You've given me a felon's daughter for a wife, doomed me to poverty and shame, cursed my career! We've played a guilty game and been overreached. By marrying this girl for her money and advancement, I degraded myself and prostituted my honor!"

"What in Heaven's name do you mean?" the old man shrieked.

"I mean there's a measure of baseness I can't pass. I have no other words. I've felt for months my conduct was wicked, sordid, worldly. I'm rightly punished by losing both."

"How do you mean you lose either?" the Major shrieked. "Who's to take your fortune or seat away? Clavering shall give them to you. You'll have every shilling of eighty thousand pounds."

"I'll keep my promise to Miss Amory, sir."

"And her parents shall keep theirs to you."

"Not so, please God," Arthur answered. "I have sinned, but Heaven help me, I will sin no more. I will let Clavering off from that bargain made without my knowledge. I will take no money with Blanche but her original fortune. I will try to make her happy. You brought this on me, sir. But you knew no better, and I forgive—"

"Arthur—in God's name—in your father's name, who was the proudest man alive—in mine—for the sake of this broken-down old fellow who's fond of you—don't fling this chance away! It's the making of you. You'll be a Baronet; it's three thousand a year. On my knees, I beg of you, don't do this."

The old man actually sank to his knees, seizing Arthur's hand, looking up with pitiful eyes. It was cruel to see the shaking hands, the wrinkled, quivering face, the weeping, winking old eyes. "Ah, sir," Arthur groaned, "you've brought enough pain on me, spare me this. You wished me to marry Blanche. I marry her. For God's sake, rise! I can't bear it."

"You mean to say you'll take her as a beggar and be one yourself?" the Major gasped, rising and coughing violently.

"I look at her as a person to whom a great calamity has befallen, and to whom I am pledged. She cannot help the misfortune. As she had my word when she was prosperous, I shall not withdraw it now she is poor. I will not take Clavering's seat unless he gives it freely. I will not have a shilling more than her original fortune."

"Have the kindness to ring the bell," the Major said, his voice breaking. "I've done my best. And—and Shakespeare was right—and Cardinal Wolsey—'and had I but served my God as I've served you.' Yes, on my knees, by Jove, to my own nephew—I mightn't have been—Good night, sir. You needn't trouble yourself to call again."

Arthur took the passive, clammy hand the old man left him. He looked aged and broken, as if the contest had shattered him completely.

The next day, Arthur kept to his bed and refused to see his uncle.
