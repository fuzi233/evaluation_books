Three months ago—near the end of May in the year 1900—I received a letter that would alter everything. It came from an old friend, Dr. Arthur Lister Browne, a man of sharp intellect and sharper intuition. Our correspondence had lapsed over the years, drowned in the tides of time and distance, but his final missive arrived with the weight of a last confession.

Enclosed were four notebooks, their pages dense with the cryptic swirls of Pitman’s shorthand, scrawled in pencil so faint that deciphering them was like piecing together fragments of a dream. Among them was a letter, written in the same elusive script, its words tinged with morphine and mortality. Browne knew he was dying. And before he departed, he entrusted me with the secrets of a woman named Mary Wilson.

She had been his patient—his *subject*—for fifteen years. A spectral figure, gaunt as a wraith, with eyes like cigarette smoke and hair prematurely white. Hypnotism had been their bond, a bridge between her agony and his control. She suffered from *tic douloureux*, a neuralgia so vicious it clawed at her nerves like a beast. But under trance, she became something else entirely.

Mary Wilson did not merely sleep. She *traveled*.

Through time. Through space.

Her murmurs in trance were not random babble but reports—visions of past and future, spoken in a voice that was not her own. *"Us are in a room, eight hundred and eleven miles above,"* she would say, describing scenes she had never witnessed, words she had never read. Gibbon’s *Decline and Fall* spilled from her lips, though she had never held the book.

Browne, ever the scientist, documented it all. He learned to steer her visions, to anchor her fleeting consciousness to moments beyond the present. The past, she spoke of as if looking down from some celestial height. The future, she described as a descent—*"twenty miles within,"* then thirty, then sixty-three, but never further. Like a diver pressed by the crushing deep, there was a limit to how far she could go.

And then, in one of those trances, she spoke of a book—a book yet unwritten. A record of something terrible.

Browne transcribed it.

And now, with his death, it has fallen into my hands.

Memory fades. Even the sharpest recollections dull with time.

Four years ago, I could have named without hesitation the wild-eyed preacher who stood in Kensington and railed against the *Boreal* expedition. Now, his name eludes me—Mac-something? Mackay? Yes, Mackay. A prophet in a world that had outgrown prophets.

He was a gaunt Scot, draped in rough-spun cloth, his voice a storm rolling over the pews. When the *Boreal* prepared to sail for the North Pole, he denounced it as folly—no, worse than folly. *Blasphemy.*

*"There is a doom upon the Poles,"* he thundered. *"A warning written in ice and failure."*

Thirty-one expeditions in the twentieth century alone, all thwarted. Not by chance, he insisted, but by design. The Poles were forbidden. Like the fruit of Eden, they were not for man’s taking.

And yet—the world laughed.

For there was money in it now. The will of Charles P. Stickney, the Chicago eccentric, had set a bounty of 175 million dollars upon the Pole. Not for a nation, not for a crew—for the *one man* who first set foot upon that frozen axis. The fever had spread. Kings might have sailed as deckhands, if pride had allowed it.

Mackay stood alone, howling into the gale of progress.

*"If you reach it,"* he warned, *"catastrophe will follow. Not for you alone—for all."*

At the time, I dismissed him. A relic. A madman.

Now, I wonder.

Now, I *know*.
