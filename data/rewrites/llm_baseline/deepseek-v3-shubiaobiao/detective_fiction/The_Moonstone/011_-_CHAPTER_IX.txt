The morning of June twenty-first—Miss Rachel’s birthday—dawned gray and uncertain, but by midday the skies cleared, as if in deference to the occasion. Below stairs, we marked the day as tradition dictated: with humble offerings presented to the young mistress, accompanied by my annual speech. Like the Queen’s address to Parliament, my words followed a time-honored script, met each year with the same eager anticipation, and the same mild disappointment when the expected novelty failed to materialize. Still, they endured it with good humor—proof, I mused, that whether governing a nation or a household, consistency breeds contentment.

Breakfast concluded, Mr. Franklin drew me aside to discuss the Moonstone. The time had come to retrieve it from the bank at Frizinghall and place it in Miss Rachel’s hands. Yet Mr. Franklin was uncharacteristically indecisive, torn between conflicting impulses. Had his courtship of his cousin met another rebuff? Or were sleepless nights exacerbating the contradictions in his nature? Whatever the cause, he vacillated like a weathervane in a gale.

For my part, I held firm to the facts. No incident justified alarming Lady Verinder, and the terms of the Colonel’s will left no choice but to surrender the Diamond. After much debate, Mr. Franklin conceded. He would ride to Frizinghall after luncheon, returning with the jewel—and likely in the company of Mr. Godfrey and the two Miss Ablewhites.

With that settled, he rejoined Miss Rachel. The pair devoted the entire morning and half the afternoon to decorating her sitting-room door, aided by my Penelope, who dutifully mixed paints under their direction. Lady Verinder, her handkerchief pressed to her nose (Mr. Franklin’s varnish proving particularly potent), flitted in and out, attempting in vain to lure them away. Not until three o’clock did they relinquish their aprons, liberating my poor daughter—who had suffered most from the fumes—and scrubbing the evidence of their artistry from their hands.

The result, I confess, was striking: a riot of gilded griffins, cupids, and floral wreaths, so exuberantly rendered that one’s head ached for hours afterward from the mere memory of their whirlwind antics. As for Penelope’s subsequent retreat to the back-kitchen, let no one accuse me of disparaging Art. If such sacrifices were required, so be it—though I might wish the victim had been someone else’s child.

Mr. Franklin snatched a hasty meal and rode for Frizinghall, ostensibly to escort his cousins, though our private understanding was otherwise. With the evening’s festivities looming, I had duties enough to occupy me—inspecting the wine, marshaling the servants—before stealing a moment’s respite in my pantry, aided by a restorative pinch of snuff and a familiar volume of *Robinson Crusoe*.

I was roused by the clatter of hooves. Mr. Franklin had returned, accompanied by the Ablewhite party. Mr. Godfrey, I noted, seemed out of sorts—his greeting cordial but subdued, his manner clouded. His sisters, however, more than compensated. Stout, golden-haired, and brimming with vigor, they descended upon the house like a pair of exuberant Valkyries, their laughter ringing through the halls.

Under cover of their clamor, I managed a private word with Mr. Franklin.

“The Diamond is safe?”

A nod, his hand brushing his coat’s breast pocket.

“And the Indians?”

“Not a trace.”

With that, he sought Lady Verinder. Moments later, a chorus of shrieks erupted from the drawing-room. Recognizing the Miss Ablewhites’ signature exclamations, I entered under pretext of consulting her ladyship on dinner arrangements—and beheld a curious scene.

There stood Miss Rachel, the Colonel’s cursed Diamond glinting in her palm, her face alight with fascination. Flanking her, the two sisters knelt in rapture, gasping at each new play of light. Across the room, Mr. Godfrey applauded like an enraptured child, murmuring, “Exquisite!” Near the bookcase, Mr. Franklin tugged at his beard, his gaze fixed on Lady Verinder, who stood at the window, the Colonel’s will clutched in her hand, her back resolutely turned.

At my inquiry, she faced me, her expression stormy. “Attend me in half an hour,” she said curtly, then swept out. Clearly, the Diamond’s legacy troubled her as deeply as it had troubled Mr. Franklin and me at the Shivering Sand. Was it proof of her brother’s vindictiveness—or her own misplaced severity?

Before I could follow, Miss Rachel detained me. “Look, Gabriel!” she exclaimed, tilting the Diamond into a shaft of sunlight.

Heaven help me—what a stone! Nearly the size of a plover’s egg, it held depths like liquid gold, swallowing the gaze into its luminous heart. In darkness, it burned with an eerie lunar glow. No wonder the Miss Ablewhites shrieked; no wonder I myself echoed their “O!” Only Mr. Godfrey remained unmoved, murmuring something about carbon, as if that explained anything at all.

Dinner preparations reclaimed me until, half an hour later, I presented myself to Lady Verinder. Our interview echoed my earlier conference with Mr. Franklin—though I omitted mention of the jugglers, seeing no cause for alarm. Her ladyship’s verdict was plain: the Colonel’s motives were black indeed, and she meant to wrest the Diamond from her daughter’s possession at the first opportunity.

On my return, I crossed paths with Mr. Franklin, who inquired after Miss Rachel and Mr. Godfrey with mounting suspicion. A sharp tug at his beard, a decisive library door slam—his feelings were clear.

I had scarcely donned my waistcoat when Penelope accosted me, her brush attacking my scalp with unwarranted zeal. “News, father!” she whispered. “Miss Rachel has refused him!”

“Him?”

“That philanthropic hypocrite!” she declared, tightening my cravat with vengeful vigor. “I saw them in the rose-garden—arm in arm when they went out, cold as January when they returned. ‘Let us remain cousins,’ she said, and left him grinding his heel into the gravel like a thwarted schoolboy.” She delivered a final, emphatic stroke of the brush. “Mr. Franklin’s the man, mark my words!”

I had no time to reprimand her impropriety. The first carriages had arrived. Adjusting my lobster-hued face in the mirror, I strode to my post just in time to announce Mr. and Mrs. Ablewhite—though I doubt their names stir your interest any more than they did mine.
