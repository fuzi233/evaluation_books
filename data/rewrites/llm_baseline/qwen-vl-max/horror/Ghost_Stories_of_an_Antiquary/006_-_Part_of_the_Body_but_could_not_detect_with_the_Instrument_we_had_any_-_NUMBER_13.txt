Among the towns of Jutland, Viborg holds a place of quiet dignity. It is the seat of a bishopric, crowned by a cathedral—handsome, though largely rebuilt—and adorned with a garden that breathes serenity and a lake so still it seems to hold its breath. Storks nest in the rooftops, as they have for centuries, and near the town lies Hald, one of Denmark’s most picturesque villages. Close by stands Finderup, where Marsk Stig murdered King Erik Glipping on St Cecilia’s Day in 1286. When the king’s tomb was opened in the seventeenth century, fifty-six blows from square-headed iron maces were found carved into his skull. But I am not writing a guidebook.

Viborg has good hotels—Preisler’s and the Phœnix are excellent—but my cousin, whose tale I now recount, chose the Golden Lion on his first visit. He has not returned since. The reason may lie in what follows.

The Golden Lion is one of the few buildings in Viborg untouched by the great fire of 1726, which reduced much of the city—including the cathedral, the Sognekirke, and the Raadhuus—to ashes. The hotel is a massive red-brick structure, its façade marked by corbie steps and a biblical inscription above the door. Yet behind the front, the courtyard is built of black-and-white timber and plaster, a contrast both striking and unsettling.

It was late afternoon when my cousin arrived. The sun slanted across the building, casting long shadows and illuminating the old-world charm of the place. He admired its weathered grandeur, promising himself a peaceful stay in a setting so emblematic of ancient Jutland.

He had not come for business in the usual sense. Mr Anderson was engaged in research into the Church history of Denmark, particularly the final years of Roman Catholicism before the Reformation. He had learned that the Rigsarkiv in Viborg held documents preserved from the fire—letters, decrees, and private correspondence that might shed light on the last days of Catholic rule. He intended to spend two or three weeks copying them, and hoped the Golden Lion could offer him a room large enough to serve as both bedroom and study.

The landlord listened patiently, then suggested he inspect the rooms himself. The top floor was dismissed too soon—the climb would be tiresome after a day’s work. The second floor offered no suitable space. On the first floor, however, several rooms met his needs in size.

The landlord favored Number 17, but Anderson objected: its windows faced only a blank wall, and it would be dark by evening. Number 12 or 14 would be better—both overlooked the street, offering light and a view. After some deliberation, Number 12 was chosen.

It was long and high-ceilinged, with three windows aligned along one wall. There was no fireplace, but a cast-iron stove stood in the corner, decorated with a relief of Abraham sacrificing Isaac and inscribed with “1 Bog Mose, Cap. 22.” Nothing else seemed remarkable—except an old colored print of the town, dated about 1820.

Supper approached, but Anderson lingered in the hall, brushing off the dust of travel. As he descended, he glanced at the guest board—a large blackboard listing names and room numbers. The list was unremarkable: a German advocate, a few merchants from Copenhagen. But something caught his eye: no Number 13.

He had seen this before in Danish hotels. Still, it struck him as curious. Was the superstition so strong that no one would rent a room numbered thirteen? He resolved to ask the landlord.

That night passed quietly. After unpacking, he read a few pages before bed, then remembered his book was in the pocket of his greatcoat, hung outside the dining room. A quick trip down the hall brought him back—only to find he’d entered the wrong room. The handle resisted; within, he heard a sudden movement. He checked the number: 13. His own room was to the left.

Back in bed, he lit his lamp, read a few pages, blew it out, and turned over. Only then did it occur to him: there was no Number 13 on the guest board. Yet here was a door marked 13. Had he imagined it?

He felt a pang of regret. Perhaps he should have taken it. Maybe it was merely a servant’s room. But why had it been hidden? He looked around the dimly lit room. In the glow of the streetlamp, it seemed smaller than before—shorter, taller. An odd sensation. Rooms usually appear larger in shadow, yet this one contracted. Sleep came anyway.

The next day, Anderson visited the Rigsarkiv. He was welcomed warmly. The archives contained more than he expected—official records, personal letters, and a thick bundle of correspondence between Bishop Jörgen Friis and Protestant reformers. Among them, references to a house owned by the Bishop, though not occupied by him. Its tenant, Mag Nicolas Francken, was accused of witchcraft, of selling his soul to the Devil. The reformers called him a *Troldmand*, a sorcerer, a disgrace to the city. The Bishop defended him fiercely, insisting the charges were false and demanding a spiritual court trial.

Anderson skimmed the next letter—Rasmus Nielsen’s reply—before closing time. It declared that Christians were no longer bound by Rome’s authority, and that the Bishop’s court was unfit to judge such matters.

On the way out, he walked with Herr Scavenius, the archivist. They discussed the papers. Scavenius admitted he didn’t know where the Bishop’s house had stood. The original land register from 1560 was incomplete—the section listing urban property missing. “I’ll find it someday,” he said.

Back at the Golden Lion, Anderson decided to inquire about the missing room number. He paused near the corridor, looking for Number 13. There it was—plain as day. And inside, sounds: footsteps, voices, then silence. Then, a sharp, hissing breath—like someone gasping in fear or rage. He moved on.

In his room, the space seemed smaller. More troubling: his portmanteau was gone. He’d left it on a trestle near the far wall. He assumed servants had moved it. But the wardrobe was empty. No theft—he’d never heard of such things in Denmark. Probably just a mistake. He’d wait until morning.

He went to the right-hand window. The street was dark. Opposite, a tall building loomed, its walls bare. He saw the shadow of the man in Room 11—bearded, brushing his hair, later in a nightgown. Then, the shadow of Room 13. A figure leaned on the sill, draped in cloth. A flickering red light played on the opposite wall—steady, then pulsing. A red lampshade? A candle? He leaned forward, but saw only a fold of white fabric.

Then, a distant step. The figure in Room 13 vanished instantly. The red light died. Anderson placed his cigarette end on the sill and went to bed.

Next morning, the maid brought hot water. He asked sharply, “You must not move my portmanteau. Where is it?”

She laughed and left without answering.

He sat up, irritated—then froze. There, on the trestle, was the portmanteau. Exactly where he’d left it. How could he have missed it? He stared. The daylight revealed the true shape of the room—no longer distorted. But when he reached the middle window to check the weather, another shock: the cigarette end rested on the sill of the middle window.

He had sworn he smoked at the right-hand window.

Downstairs, breakfast passed quietly. He noticed Number 13’s boots outside the door—men’s boots. So the occupant was male. He glanced at the door number: 14. He must have passed Number 13 without seeing it. He turned back—only to find the next room was Number 12. No Number 13.

Three errors in twelve hours. Impossible. He began to doubt his senses.

But the day passed normally. He returned to the archives, searching for more on Francken. Only one other letter remained—a response from the Bishop:

“Though we do not accept your judgment of our court, nor shall we yield to you, yet since our trusted Mag Nicolas Francken has been suddenly removed from among us, the matter rests for now. But you also claim that St John describes the Holy Roman Church as the Scarlet Woman—know this…”

No sequel. No mention of how Francken died. Only that he was “removed”—suddenly, unexpectedly. Between the dates of the letters, less than two days passed. A death, perhaps. But how?

That evening, Anderson sat by the window again. The lawyer in Room 14—Sagförer Jensen—was dancing. His shadow crossed the wall repeatedly: arms flung wide, legs kicked high. Barefoot. Silent. A grotesque performance in the quiet hour.

Anderson smiled, then frowned. He thought of Emily in *Udolpho*:
*“When I return to my hotel, At ten o’clock p.m., The waiters think I am unwell; I do not care for them. But when I’ve locked my chamber door, And put my boots outside, I dance all night upon the floor. And even if my neighbours swore, I’d go on dancing all the more, For I’m acquainted with the law, And in despite of all their jaw, Their protests I deride.”*

He laughed softly—then stopped. A chill crept into the air.

At ten, the landlord arrived. Anderson invited him to smoke a cigar. Photographs of English towns served as an excuse. The landlord accepted eagerly.

As they talked, Anderson mentioned the missing Number 13.

“Oh, that?” the landlord chuckled. “I’ve wondered myself. Superstition, you know. People refuse to stay in it. Say it brings bad luck. Lost customers, broken limbs—stories galore.”

“But does it exist?” Anderson asked.

“Exist? No. Not here. I’ve never had a Number 13. You must have imagined it.”

Anderson hesitated. “But I saw it twice. Last night. The night before.”

The landlord laughed. “Impossible. There’s no such room. Next to yours? That’s Number 12. Then 14. No 13.”

Anderson nodded, but unease clung to him like damp.

Later, he retired early. He avoided passing the spot where Number 13 should be, walking past Room 11 instead. He checked the room—nothing unusual. The portmanteau was under the bed, safe. He sat down to write.

Outside, the world was quiet. A cart rolled over cobblestones. A man hurried past. Then silence.

He finished his letters. Ordered whisky. Went to the window.

And saw it again.

A shadow—tall, thin, draped in cloth—leaned on the sill of the room beside his. A red light flickered. Then, abruptly, it vanished. The light died.

Anderson stepped back. His heart beat hard.

He looked at the door.

Number 13.

There was no number.

Yet he had seen it.

Twice.

And the shadow—

It had not danced.

It had watched.
