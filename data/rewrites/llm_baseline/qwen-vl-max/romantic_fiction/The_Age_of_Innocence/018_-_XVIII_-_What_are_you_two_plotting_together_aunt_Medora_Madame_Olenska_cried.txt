XVIII. "What are you two plotting together, Aunt Medora?" Madame Olenska called out as she swept into the room, radiant and poised as if stepping from a dream. She was dressed for an evening of splendor—her gown shimmered like moonlight on water, and her posture carried the quiet defiance of a woman who knew she commanded attention. “We were just saying,” Mrs. Manson replied, rising with a playful gesture toward the bouquet on the table, “that here’s something lovely to surprise you with.”

Madame Olenska paused, her eyes narrowing slightly as they fell upon the flowers. Her expression didn’t shift—no flicker of joy or warmth—but a pale, electric tension passed over her face, like lightning across a still sky. “Ah,” she said sharply, her voice thin and bright, unlike any tone Archer had ever heard from her. “Who is foolish enough to send me a bouquet? Why a bouquet? And why tonight of all nights? I’m not going to a ball. I’m not a girl being betrothed. Some people simply can’t resist making themselves ridiculous.”

She turned abruptly toward the door, flung it open, and called, “Nastasia!” The maid appeared instantly. Madame Olenska spoke in deliberate Italian, clearly meant for Archer to understand: “Here—throw this into the dustbin!” Then, seeing Nastasia hesitate, she added, “No, no—it’s not the flowers’ fault. Tell the boy to take them to the house three doors down—the one belonging to Mr. Winsett, the dark gentleman who dined here. His wife is ill; perhaps it will cheer her. But the boy’s gone? Then you go yourself. Here—take my cloak and run. I want it out of the house at once. And mind—you’re not to say they came from me!”

She draped her velvet opera cloak over the maid’s shoulders and stepped back inside, closing the door firmly behind her. For a moment, Archer thought he saw tears forming beneath her lashes—her chest rose and fell rapidly under the lace of her dress. But then she laughed, low and sudden, and looked from the Marchioness to him. “And you two—have you become friends?”

“It’s for Mr. Archer to say,” Mrs. Manson replied with a teasing smile. “He’s been very patient while you were dressing.”

“Yes, I gave him plenty of time—my hair wouldn’t behave,” Madame Olenska said, lifting a hand to her elaborate chignon. “But that reminds me—Dr. Carver has left, and you’ll be late at the Blenkers’. Mr. Archer, would you see my aunt into the carriage?”

She followed her aunt into the hall, helped her into a chaotic pile of shawls and overshoes, and called from the doorway: “Mind—the carriage must return for me by ten!” Then she returned to the drawing-room, where Archer found her standing before the mirror, studying her reflection.

It wasn’t customary in New York society for a lady to call her maid “my dear one” or send her out wrapped in her own cloak. Yet here was Madame Olenska doing both—her actions swift, decisive, charged with emotion. Archer felt a strange thrill—not of scandal, but of witnessing something real, unfiltered. In this world, feeling moved swiftly to action.

She didn’t turn when he approached. Their eyes met briefly in the glass, then she spun around, sank onto the sofa, and sighed, “There’s time for a cigarette.” He handed her the box and lit the match. As the flame danced up, illuminating her face, she glanced at him with laughter in her eyes. “What do you think of me when I’m angry?”

He hesitated. Then, with unexpected clarity, he said: “It makes me understand what your aunt said about you.”

“I knew she’d been talking about me. Well?”

“She said you were used to grand things—splendors, amusements, excitement—things we could never give you here.”

Madame Olenska smiled faintly, exhaling a ring of smoke. “Medora is terribly romantic. It’s made up for so many things.”

Archer paused again, bracing himself. “Is her romanticism always accurate?”

“You mean—does she speak the truth?”

She considered. “In most things, yes—something true, something false. But why ask? What has she told you?”

He looked into the fire, then back at her—her presence glowing like a beacon. A tightness gripped his chest. This was their last night together, and soon the carriage would come to take her away. “She says—she pretends—that Count Olenski has asked her to persuade you to return to him.”

Madame Olenska didn’t answer. She sat perfectly still, cigarette held aloft, her face unreadable. Archer remembered how she seemed immune to surprise.

“You knew?” he burst out.

She remained silent so long that the ash dropped from her cigarette. She brushed it aside without looking. “She hinted about a letter—poor darling. Medora’s hints…”

“Did your husband send her here?”

She pondered. “Again—one can’t tell. She claimed a ‘spiritual summons’ from Dr. Carver. I fear she may marry him… poor Medora—there’s always someone she wants to marry. Perhaps the people in Cuba finally tired of her. Maybe she was just a paid companion. Honestly, I don’t know why she came.”

“But you believe she has a letter from your husband?”

After a long silence, she said, “After all—it was expected.”

Archer walked to the fireplace, restless, unable to speak. The minutes were slipping away. At any second, he might hear the wheels of the returning carriage.

“You know your aunt believes you’ll go back?”

She raised her head quickly. A flush spread across her cheeks and neck—a rare, painful blush, as though it burned her. “Many cruel things have been said about me,” she murmured.

“Oh, Ellen—forgive me. I’m a fool, a brute.”

She smiled faintly. “You’re terribly nervous. You have your own troubles. I know you think the Wellands are unreasonable about your marriage. Of course, I agree. In Europe, people don’t understand our long American engagements. They’re not as calm as we are.”

She said “we” with a subtle irony. Archer caught it, but dared not respond. Perhaps she’d steered the conversation away from herself deliberately. After the pain his words had caused, he could only follow her lead. But the ticking clock pressed on him. He couldn’t let another barrier fall between them.

“Yes,” he said suddenly. “I went south to ask May to marry me after Easter. There’s no reason we shouldn’t be married then.”

“And May adores you—and yet you couldn’t convince her? I thought she was too intelligent to be ruled by such absurd superstitions.”

“She *is* intelligent—she’s not their slave.”

Madame Olenska studied him. “Then why don’t you understand?”

Archer reddened and rushed on. “We had a frank talk—almost the first. She thinks my impatience is a bad sign.”

“A bad sign?”

“She thinks it means I can’t trust myself to care for her. That I want to marry her quickly to escape someone I care for more.”

Madame Olenska looked at him curiously. “But if she thinks that—why isn’t she in a hurry too?”

“Because she’s not like that. She’s nobler. She insists even more on the long engagement—to give me time—”

“To give her up for the other woman?”

“If I want to.”

She leaned forward, staring into the flames. Down the street, he heard the clip-clop of horses approaching.

“That *is* noble,” she whispered, her voice breaking slightly. “Yes. But it’s ridiculous.”

“Ridiculous because I don’t care for anyone else?”

“Because you don’t mean to marry anyone else.”

“Ah.”

Silence stretched. Then she looked up. “This other woman—does she love you?”

“Oh, there’s no other woman. The person May imagined—was never—”

“So why, after all, are you so eager?”

“There’s your carriage,” he said.

She half-risen, her eyes distant. Her fan and gloves lay beside her. She picked them up mechanically. “Yes. I suppose I must go.”

“You’re going to Mrs. Struthers’s?”

“Yes.” She smiled. “I must go where I’m invited, or I’d be too lonely. Why not come with me?”

Archer felt a desperate need to keep her near. He ignored her question, leaning against the mantel, watching her hands as she held her gloves and fan. As if he could make her drop them.

“May guessed the truth,” he said. “There *is* another woman—but not the one she thinks.”

Ellen Olenska said nothing. Did not move.

After a moment, he sat beside her, took her hand gently, and unclasped it. The gloves and fan fell between them.

She jumped up, freeing herself, and moved to the other side of the hearth. “Ah, don’t make love to me! Too many people have done that,” she said, frowning.

Archer flushed. “I’ve never made love to you—and I never shall. But you are the woman I would have married—if it had been possible for either of us.”

“Possible for either of us?” She stared at him, astonished. “And you say that—when it’s *you* who made it impossible?”

He gazed at her, lost in darkness pierced by a single beam of light. “*I’ve* made it impossible—?”

“You, you, *you*!” she cried, her lip trembling. “Isn’t it you who made me give up divorcing—because you showed me how selfish and wicked it was? How one must sacrifice oneself to preserve marriage’s dignity, to spare the family shame? And because my family was going to be *your* family—for May’s sake, for yours—I did what you told me. What you proved I should do. Ah,” she laughed suddenly, bitterly, “I’ve never hidden that I did it for *you*!”

She sank onto the sofa, huddled among the folds of her dress like a fallen masquerader. Archer stood by the fireplace, staring at her, unmoving.

“Good God,” he groaned. “When I thought—”

“You thought?”

“Ah, don’t ask me what I thought!”

Still watching her, he saw the same flush creep up her neck, color her face. She sat upright, rigid, facing him.

“I do ask you.”

“Well—there were things in that letter you asked me to read—”

“My husband’s letter?”

“Yes.”

“I had nothing to fear from that letter. Nothing at all. All I feared was bringing notoriety, scandal—on the family—on you and May.”

“Good God,” he groaned again, burying his face in his hands.

The silence settled over them—final, irrevocable. To Archer, it felt like a tombstone pressing down. In all the future ahead, he saw nothing that could lift this weight. He didn’t move. Didn’t raise his head. His eyes remained fixed on utter darkness.

“At least I loved you—” he whispered.

From the other side of the hearth, he heard a soft, stifled sob—like a child’s. He leapt up, rushing to her side.

“Ellen! What madness! Why are you crying? Nothing’s done that can’t be undone. I’m still free. You’ll be too.”

He pulled her into his arms, her face like a wet flower against his lips. All their fears dissolved—ghosts vanishing at dawn. The only thing that shocked him now was that he’d stood arguing across the room for five minutes when just touching her made everything clear.

She kissed him back, but then stiffened. Pulled away. Rose.

“Ah, my poor Newland—this had to happen. But it doesn’t change anything.”

“It changes everything for me.”

“No, no—it mustn’t, it can’t. You’re engaged to May. I’m married.”

He stood too, flushed, resolute. “Nonsense. It’s too late for that kind of lie. We won’t talk of your marriage. But do you see me marrying May after this?”

She remained silent, resting her elbows on the mantel, her profile reflected in the glass. One strand of her chignon had slipped, falling across her neck. She looked weary, almost aged.

“I don’t see you asking May that question,” she said at length. “Do you?”

He shrugged recklessly. “It’s too late to do anything else.”

“You say that because it’s easiest right now—not because it’s true. In reality, it’s too late to do anything but what we both decided.”

“Ah, I don’t understand you!”

She forced a smile—painful, not soothing. “You don’t understand because you haven’t seen how you changed things for me—from the beginning. Long before I knew what you’d done.”

“All I’d done?”

“Yes. I didn’t realize at first how people here avoided me—how they thought I was dreadful. I learned later they refused to invite me to dinner. I found out how you made your mother go with you to the van der Luydens’—how you insisted on announcing your engagement at the Beaufort ball, so I’d have two families to stand by me instead of one—”

At that, he laughed.

“Imagine how blind and unaware I was!” she continued. “New York meant peace, freedom—coming home. I was so happy to be among my own people that everyone seemed kind, glad to see me. But from the start, I felt no one was as kind as you. No one gave me reasons I could understand for doing what felt hard, unnecessary. The good people didn’t convince me—they’d never been tempted. But *you* understood. You knew the world outside tugged at us with golden hands—and yet you hated what it demanded. You hated happiness bought with disloyalty, cruelty, indifference. That was new to me—and better than anything I’ve known.”

She spoke quietly, steadily. Each word landed like molten lead in his chest. He bowed his head, staring at the rug, at the tip of her satin shoe peeking from beneath her dress. Suddenly, he knelt and kissed the shoe.

She bent over him, hands on his shoulders, looking down with eyes so deep he froze under her gaze.

“Ah, don’t let us undo what you’ve done!” she pleaded. “I can’t go back to that old way of thinking. I can’t love you unless I give you up.”

His arms reached for her—but she stepped back. They stood apart, separated by the distance her words had created.

Then, abruptly, his anger erupted. “And Beaufort? Is he to replace me?”

He expected fury. He would have welcomed it. But Madame Olenska only paled, her arms hanging limply, her head lowered as if in thought.

“He’s waiting for you at Mrs. Struthers’s. Why don’t you go to him?”

Archer sneered. She turned and rang the bell.

“I won’t go out tonight,” she said when the maid arrived. “Tell the carriage to fetch the Signora Marchesa.”

After the door closed, Archer looked at her with bitter eyes. “Why this sacrifice? If you’re lonely, I have no right to keep you from your friends.”

She smiled faintly, her lashes damp. “I won’t be lonely now. I *was*. I *was* afraid. But the emptiness is gone. When I turn inward now, I feel like a child entering a room where the light is always on.”

Her voice and presence still held a soft, unreachable grace. Archer groaned again. “I don’t understand you!”

“Yet you understand May!”

He reddened, but held her gaze. “May is ready to give me up.”

“What? Three days after you begged her to hurry the wedding?”

“She refused. That gives me the right—”

“Ah,” she said softly, “you’ve taught me what an ugly word that is.”

He turned away, drained. He felt as if he’d climbed a cliff for hours, only to lose his grip at the top and fall into darkness. If he could have held her again, he might have overcome her logic. But she kept him at bay—with her look, her stillness, and his own awe at her sincerity.

At last, he pleaded again. “If we do this now, it will be worse afterward—worse for everyone—”

“No—no—no!” she nearly screamed, as if he frightened her.

Then—the bell chimed through the house. They hadn’t heard a carriage. They stood motionless, eyes locked.

Outside, Nastasia crossed the hall. The outer door opened. A moment later, she entered, holding a telegram.

“The lady was very happy with the flowers,” she said, smoothing her apron. “She thought it was her signor marito who sent them. She cried a little and called it a folly.”

Madame Olenska smiled, took the yellow envelope, tore it open, and walked to the lamp. When the door closed, she handed the telegram to Archer.

It was dated from St. Augustine, addressed to the Countess Olenska:
> *Granny’s telegram successful. Papa and Mamma agree marriage after Easter. Am telegraphing Newland. Am too happy for words and love you dearly. Your grateful May.*

Half an hour later, when Archer unlocked his front door, he found a similar envelope on the hall table. Inside, a message from May Welland:
> *Parents consent wedding Tuesday after Easter at twelve Grace Church eight bridesmaids please see Rector so happy love May.*

Archer crumpled the paper as if that could erase the news. Then he pulled out his pocket diary, flipped through the pages with trembling fingers—but didn’t find what he sought. He stuffed the telegram into his pocket and climbed the stairs.

A light glowed through the door of Janey’s dressing room. He knocked impatiently. The door opened, and his sister stood before him in her familiar purple flannel robe, hair pinned tightly. Her face was pale, anxious.

“Newland! I hope there’s no bad news in that telegram? I waited—just in case—” (No letter escaped Janey.)

He ignored her. “Look—what day is Easter this year?”

She gasped. “Easter? Newland! Why, the first week in April! Why?”

“The first week?” He turned back to his diary, calculating rapidly. “The first week, you say?”

He threw back his head and laughed—a long, wild laugh.

“For mercy’s sake, what’s wrong?”

“Nothing’s wrong—except that I’m getting married in a month.”

Janey threw her arms around him. “Oh, Newland! How wonderful! I’m so glad! But, dearest, why are you laughing? Hush—don’t wake Mamma.”
