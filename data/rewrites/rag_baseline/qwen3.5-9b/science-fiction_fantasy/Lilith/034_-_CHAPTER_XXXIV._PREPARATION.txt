Victory secured, the woman of Bulika turned her gaze toward the city, painting a picture of its defenseless state, the wickedness of its princess, and the cowardice of its inhabitants. Within days, the children's chatter was consumed solely by Bulika, though they possessed no concept whatsoever of what a city entailed. It was then that the design of the woman revealed itself to me, though her ultimate motive remained obscured. The notion of seizing the place resonated powerfully with Lona—and with me. The children were developing faculties with alarming rapidity; I perceived no insurmountable obstacle to the enterprise. I knew the terrible Lilith—whether woman or leopardess—for her one fatal flaw: her doom through her daughter. The weight of the ancient prophecy loomed large over the citizens; surely, whatever risk the venture entailed was justified. If successful—who could doubt their success?—the Little Ones, emerging from a mere crowd of children, would swiftly evolve into a youthful people whose governance and influence would be entirely righteous. Would they not rule the wicked with a rod of iron, becoming the redemption of the nation? Simultaneously, I must confess I harbored ambitions of personal gain. It seemed fitting that Lona should ascend the throne her mother once occupied, and natural that I serve as her consort and minister. I envisioned spending my life in her service; between us, with the Little Ones as our core, what might we not achieve in building a noble state? I even entertained a foolish dream of establishing a gem trade between the two worlds—a project destined to bring only harm to both. Recalling Adam's plea, I suggested to Lona that locating water might accelerate the children's growth. She deemed it prudent to postpone this, uncertain of the initial consequences, noting that eventually, it would impose a new necessity upon them. "They are what they are without it!" she declared. "Once we hold the city, we will search for water!" Thus, we commenced our preparations, constantly reviewing the merry troops and companies. Lona focused primarily on the commissariat, while I drilled the little soldiers, honed their stone-throwing skills, instructed them in various weapons, and strived to forge them into warriors. The primary challenge was ensuring they rallied to the flag the instant the call sounded. Most wielded slings; the older boys carried bows and arrows. The larger girls brandished aloe-spikes, hardened as steel and sharp as needles, mounted on sturdy shafts—formidable instruments. Their sole duty was guarding those too young to fight. Lona herself had grown significantly, yet remained unaware of her transformation. She was, as ever, the tallest. Her hair had lengthened, and she had matured nearly into a woman, retaining none of the fleeting beauties of childhood. When we reunited after our long separation, she laid down her infant, embraced me silently, clinging to me with a face glowing with joy; the child whimpered, and she snatched him into her bosom instantly. To witness her with any thoughtless, obstinate, or irritable toddler was to behold a tender grandmother. I seemed to have known her for eons—from before time began! I barely recalled my mother, yet in my mind's eye, she resembled Lona; whenever I imagined a sister or child, her face was invariably Lona's. Every thought drifted to her; she was my heart's wife! Though she rarely sought me, she was perpetually within earshot. Whatever I did or thought, I referred to her, rejoicing that while working in absolute independence, she felt most at home beside me. She never neglected a single child, and my love only intensified my sense of duty. To love her and fulfill my duty appeared not as separate acts, but as one inseparable imperative. She might suggest actions or inquire about her duties, yet she never seemed to assume that either of us cared for anything beyond what *had* to be done. Her love overflowed onto me—not in caresses, but in a closeness of recognition akin to the devotion of a divine beast. I never confided in her regarding her mother. The forest teemed with birds, their plumage so splendid it seemed to compensate for the absence of flowers, which apparently could not thrive without water. Seeing their glorious feathers scattered throughout the woods, I conceived the idea of crafting a garment for Lona from them. As I gathered and bound them in overlapping rows, she watched with evident appreciation, never questioning my choices but waiting expectantly for the result. Within a week or two, the creation was complete: a long, loose mantle designed to fasten at the throat and waist, with armholes. I rose and draped it over her. She stood, removed it, and placed it at my feet—I presumed out of propriety. I placed it back on her shoulders, demonstrating how to insert her arms. She smiled, glanced at the feathers, stroked them gently, then removed it again, laying it down by her side. When she departed, she carried it with her, and I saw it no more for several days. Eventually, she returned one morning wearing it, accompanied by another garment she had fashioned similarly from the dried leaves of a tough evergreen. Possessing the strength of leather and the appearance of scale armor, I donned it immediately. From that day forth, we wore these garments whenever riding. On the forest's outskirts, a troop of full-grown horses had appeared one day. Unalarmed by creatures of such alien shapes, I quickly befriended them, training two of the finest for Lona and myself. Already accustomed to riding a small steed, her delight was immense when she first looked down from the back of a giant beast; the horse displayed pride in its burden. We exercised them daily until they possessed such confidence in us that they obeyed instantly and feared nothing; thereafter, we rode them at parades and marches. The undertaking occasionally seemed foolhardy, yet the woman of Bulika's confidence—real or feigned—always dispelled my hesitation. The princess's magic, she insisted, would prove powerless against the children; regarding any forces she might muster, our animal allies alone guaranteed superiority. She claimed she was ready, armed with a simple stick, to confront any two men of Bulika. She admitted a significant fear of the leopardess, but I was prepared for her. However, I recoiled from bringing *all* the children along. "Would it not be better," I asked, "if you remained in the forest with your baby and the smallest of the Little Ones?" She replied that she relied heavily on the impact their sight would have on the women, particularly the mothers. "When they see the darlings," she said, "their hearts will be taken by storm! I must be there encouraging them to stand firm! If there remains any hardness in the place, it will be found among the women!" "YOU must not encumber yourself," I urged Lona, "with any of the children; you will be needed everywhere!" For there were two additional babies besides the woman's, and even on horseback, she almost always cradled one. "I do not recall ever being without a child to care for," she answered. "But when we reach the city, it shall be as you wish!" Her confidence in one who had failed so unworthily shamed me. Yet neither had I initiated the movement, nor grounds to oppose it; I had no choice but to lend my utmost support. Personally, I was ready to live or die with Lona. Her humility and trust humbled me, and I threw myself heartily into her purposes. Our path crossed a grassy plain, requiring no fodder for the horses or the two cows accompanying us for the infants; however, the elephants required provision. Although the grass suited them, it was short, and with their one-fingered trunks, they could not gather enough for a single meal. Consequently, we mobilized the entire colony to harvest grass and make hay, allowing the elephants to carry quantities sufficient for several days, supplemented by fresh gathering at each halt. For the bears, we stored nuts; for ourselves, we dried abundant fruit. Having captured and tamed several more large horses, we loaded them and the elephants with these provisions, preparing to depart. Then Lona and I conducted a general review, and I delivered a brief address. I began by stating that I had learned much about them and knew their origins. "We did not come from anywhere," they cried, interrupting me. "We are here!" I explained that each had a mother, similar to the mother of the last baby; I believed they had all been brought from Bulika when so young they could not remember. I recounted how the wicked princess there feared babies so intensely, determined to destroy them, forcing their mothers to flee and abandon them where she could not find them. Now, we marched to Bulika to locate their mothers and liberate them from the bad giantess. "However," I continued, "danger awaits us. As you know, we may have to fight fiercely to capture the city." "We can fight! We are ready!" shouted the boys. "Yes, you can," I returned. "And I know you will: mothers are worth fighting for! Only remember, you must all stay together." "Yes, yes; we'll look out for each other," they answered. "Nobody shall touch one of us but his own mother!" "You must heed every command given by your officers immediately!" "We will, we will!--Now we're quite ready! Let us go!" "One more thing," I added. "When you strike, ensure it is a full, swinging blow; when you release an arrow, pull it to the head; when you sling a stone, throw it strong and straight."
