In the refectory that night—where the long boards shone with the wear of many elbows and the air hung with the warm, yeasty breath of trenchers and broth—Gerard caught, at his own end of the table, a low talk that pricked him sharper than any bell.

The brother who had been set to write out fair new copies of the house’s charter and rule had died suddenly.

A quill laid down in mid-stroke, a hand stilled before the ink dried—so much order depending on one man’s fingers. It made a gap in the monastery’s memory, and the gap had to be bridged with a living hand.

Gerard’s own fingers, stained by travel and hunger, twitched as if they already held a pen. Timidly, as one asks not alms but leave, he offered his service.

There was a moment’s pause among the monks near him, a weighing. Gerard mistook it for suspicion of hire, and hastened, reddening:

“Nay, not for hire, my lords, but for love; and as a small return for many a good night’s lodging your order hath bestowed on me, a poor wayfarer.”

One monk’s mouth softened, approving; yet he shook his head a little, as if to caution the company. The dead brother, he hinted, had been an excellent penman; his work could not be taken up and carried on save by a master.

That word—master—drew from Gerard a quiet courage. From his wallet he brought out, with a tremor he could not wholly hide, a vellum deed. The back of it he had cleaned and scraped, and on that pale, rescued space he had written a specimen: letters laid as carefully as herbs in a mortar, each stroke measured, each flourish obeying its own law.

The monk started as at some rare spice recognized by scent alone. Without another word he rose and went swiftly up the hall to the high table. Gerard, watching, saw him bend his knee so that it just touched, in passing, the fifth step and then the tenth, and then present the vellum to the prior, whispering comments.

At once a dozen knowing eyes fixed on the piece; a buzz ran like bees about a hive. Gerard saw the prior point more than once; then the monk came back with the strut of a man sent with an honour. In his hands were not only praise, but supper: a savoury crustade ryal—a game pie whose lid had been lifted and set back, so that the gravey steamed up, thick with spice and wine—and with it a silver grace-cup brimming with rich pimentum.

The spiced drink sent up a perfume of honey and pepper and warmed herbs; it was like being invited into the kitchen without having scrubbed one’s hands first. Gerard took the cup, and, bowing low first to the distant prior and then to his own company, quaffed and passed it on.

Instantly the whole table hailed him as of their own kin.

“Art convent bred, deny it not?”

He confessed it; and in that moment he thanked Heaven for his cloister-breeding, for he saw clearly how else he might have been as blunt and hungry-minded as his brothers Sybrandt and Cornelis, taking food and shelter as mere filling and roof, and missing the hidden customs that bind men together like a shared loaf.

“But ’tis passing strange how you could know,” said he.

Two monks answered together, as if a rubric had been recited: “You drank with the cup in both hands.”

So slight a gesture, and yet it spoke a whole dialect of the refectory—one of those untranslatable languages that live in the body: how to hold, how to bow, how to receive. A man may travel far and learn many tongues, and yet betray himself by the way he lifts a vessel.

While Gerard was still wondering at the power lodged in such little forms, a rougher sound rose at the bottom of the hall. Voices there had been loudish for some time, but now came a burst of mirth so obstreperous and so long that the prior sent even the sub-prior striding down to check it and lay penance on every monk at that table.

Gerard’s cheek burned with shame; for in the heart of the unruly laughter his ear had caught the word “courage!” and then, clear above the rest, the trumpet tones of Denys of Burgundy.

The monastery’s sound—normally measured as a chant—had been cracked by that soldier’s brass. It jarred the place like a cracked pot in a still dairy. Gerard felt, too keenly, how noise could bruise the mind; yet he could not help loving Denys’s very excess, since it sprang from life.

Soon after, Gerard was installed in the dead Werter’s cell, with wax lights that made the walls swim in gold, and a little writing-frame that could be set at any angle like a cook’s board tilted to catch the best light for trimming, and all the materials of calligraphy: quills cut clean, inks of several strengths, pumice, knife, colours, and the smooth waiting vellum.

Work began at once. Gerard’s hand went steadily, as if the long road had been only a prelude to this stillness. Each letter he laid down was a morsel of order; the strokes joined like stitched seams, binding the monastery’s past so it should not fray.

Yet the labour was too much for one evening; the charter and the rule were not short dishes to be turned in a hurry. Then came a question more difficult than any flourish: how could he ask Denys—the monk-hater—to stay longer?

He spoke to him plainly at last, offering to abide by his decision. And here Denys surprised him. The big man, who had sworn and scoffed at cowls, answered graciously:

“A day’s rest will do neither of us harm. Write thou, and I’ll pass the time as I may.”

Gerard wrote on. When the monks saw what he had done, their admiration was honest and unfeigned; they agreed, with a solemnity that was almost cheerful, that the records had gained by poor Werter’s death. The sub-prior, in the warmth of this discovery, forced a rix-dollar on Gerard and thrust into his hands several brushes and colours from the convent stock, which was very large—pigments like ground berries and burnt bone, all the palette of memory.

Next day Gerard resumed his march, warm at heart; for this was a good omen. It was on the pen he relied to make his fortune, and through that fortune to recover his well-beloved. The road might be harsh, but a man who carries skill carries a hearth with him.

He showed Denys the gifts as they walked.

“Come, Denys. See what the good monks have given me; now do try to be fairer to them. For, to be round with you, it chilled my friendship for a moment to hear even you call my benefactors hypocrites.”

Denys did not snarl, as Gerard expected. Instead he threw up his hand with a soldier’s frankness.

“I recant.”

“Thank you! thank you! Good Denys.”

“I was a scurrilous vagabond.”

“Nay, nay; say not so, neither!”

“But we soldiers are rude and hasty. I give myself the lie, and I offer those I misunderstood all my esteem. ’Tis unjust that thousands should be defamed for the hypocrisy of a few.”

“Now are you reasonable. You have pondered what I said?”

“Nay. It is their own doing.”

Gerard crowed a little—who does not like to be proved in the right?—and then gave him all his attention when Denys offered to tell how his conversion had been wrought.

“Well then,” began Denys, with the relish of a man describing a feast, “at dinner the first day a young monk beside me did open his jaws and laughed right out—most musically.”

Denys’s voice, even telling it, seemed to fill the road like a horn in a narrow lane.

“‘Good,’ said I, ‘at last I have fallen on a man and not a shorn ape.’ So, to sound him further, I slapped his broad back and administered my consigne.”

“‘Heaven forbid!’ says he. I stared—for the dog looked as sad as Solomon; a better mime saw you never, even at a Mystery. ‘I see war is no sharpener of the wits,’ said he. ‘What are the clergy for but to fight the foul fiend? and what else are the monks for? The fiend being dead, The friars are sped. You may plough up the convents, and we poor monks shall have nought to do—but turn soldiers, and so bring him to life again.’”

Denys laughed at the recollection; he could taste again the laughter that had run round the table at his expense.

“Then there was a great laugh at me. ‘Well, you are the monk for me,’ said I. ‘And you are the crossbowman for me,’ quo’ he; ‘and I’ll be bound you could tell us tales of the war should make our hair stand on end.’ ‘Excusez! the barber has put that out of the question,’ quoth I—and then I had the laugh.”

“What wretched ribaldry!” Gerard said, half to himself, his mind still in the quiet cell where letters behaved.

Denys, candid as a child in some matters, admitted at once he had seen merrier jests hatched with less cackle.

“’Twas a great matter to have got rid of hypocrisy,” he went on, satisfied with his own conclusion. “So said I, ‘I can give you the chaire de poule, if that may content ye.’ ‘That we will see,’ was the cry, and a signal went round.”

Then Denys—bursting with glee—told how at bedtime he had been taken, not to the great dortour, but to a cell, and strictly forbidden to sleep; and to aid his vigil, a book had been lent him, full of pictures of a hundred merry adventures of monks pursuing the female laity. How, in due course, he had been taken out barefoot, down to the parlour, where waited a supper fit for a duke, and at it twelve jolly friars, roaring boys, he declared, the merriest he had met in peace or war.

How the story, the toast, the jest, the wine-cup went round. How some played cards with a gorgeous pack in which Saint Theresa and Saint Catherine, bedizened with gold, stood for queens; and black, white, grey, and crutched friars for knaves; and how they staked their very rosaries and swore like troopers when they lost.

And how about midnight a sly monk stole out, and Denys and others followed him into the garden and saw him thrust his hand into the ivy and draw out a rope-ladder. With this he ran up upon the wall—ten feet broad—yet not so nimbly but a russet kirtle popped up from the outer world as quick as he; and then the two fell to billing and cooing.

“That situation,” said Denys, delighted at his own wit, “struck me as rather feline than ecclesiastical, and drew from me the appropriate comment of a ‘mew!’”

The monks joined his mewsical chorus; the visitor shrieked and was sore discomforted. But the monk on the wall—Abelard, Denys named him—only cried:

“What, are ye there, ye jealous miauling knaves? Ye shall caterwaul to some tune to-morrow night. I’ll fit every man-jack of ye with a fardingale.”

That brutal threat, Denys confessed, had reconciled him to stay another day—at Gerard’s request.

Gerard groaned aloud. Denys, unable to disconcert so brazen a monk and the demoiselle beginning to whimper, told how they danced caterwauling in a circle, then bestowed a solemn benediction on the two wall-flowers, and off they went back to the parlour. There they found a pair lying dead drunk, and two others affectionate to tears. They carried off the inanimate, dragged off the loving and lachrymose, and kicked each merrily into his cell.

“And so,” concluded Denys, “shut up in measureless content.”

Gerard’s stomach turned, not with hunger but with disgust. “I am disgusted,” said he. “And I tell you so.”

Denys only chuckled, as if Gerard’s displeasure were a seasoning to his tale, and proceeded to tell how next day he and the young monks drew the fish-ponds and secreted pike, carp, tench, and eel for their own use; and how, in the dead of night, he was taken again, shoeless, by crooked ways into the chapel—a ghost-like place, being dark—and then down steps into a crypt below the floor, where suddenly paradise burst on him.

“’Tis there the holy fathers retire to pray,” Gerard put in quickly, eager to rescue at least the place with a better meaning.

“Not always,” said Denys. “Wax candles by the dozen were lighted, and princely cheer. Fifteen soups maigre—with marvellous twangs of venison, grouse, and hare in them—and twenty different fishes (being Friday), cooked with wondrous art. And each he between two buxom lasses, and each lass between two lads with a cowl—all but me. And think I had to woo by interpreter! I doubt the knave put in three words for himself and one for me; if he didn’t, hang him for a fool.

“And some of the weaker vessels were novices and not wont to hold good wine; had to be coaxed ere they would put it to their white teeth; mais elles s’y faisaient. And the story, and the jest, and the cup went round—by-the-by they had flagons made to simulate breviaries. And a monk touched the cittern and sang ditties with a voice tunable as a lark in spring. The posies did turn the faces of the women folk bright red at first—but elles s’y faisaient.”

Here Gerard could bear no more. He broke out like a man whose native tongue has been mocked and trampled, not with words but with deeds.

“Miserable wretches! Corrupters of youth! Perverters of innocence! But for your being there, Denys—who have been taught no better—oh, would God the church had fallen on the whole gang. Impious, abominable hypocrites!”

“Hypocrites?” cried Denys, honestly amazed. “Why, that is what I clept them ere I knew them—and you withstood me. Nay, they are sinners; all good fellows are that; but by St. Denys his helmeted skull, no hypocrites—right jolly roaring blades.”

“Denys,” said Gerard solemnly, and lowered his voice till the road itself seemed to hush and listen, “you little know the peril you ran that night. That church you defiled amongst you is haunted. I had it from one of the elder monks. The dead walk there; their light feet have been heard to patter o’er the stones.”

“Misericorde!” whispered Denys, and for once the loud man became a child in the dark.

“Ay, more,” Gerard went on, the whisper drawing its own dreadful music from the air, “celestial sounds have issued from the purlieus of that very crypt you turned into a tavern. Voices of the dead holding unearthly communion have chilled the ear of midnight; and at times, Denys, the faithful in their nightly watches have even heard music from dead lips. Chords—made by no mortal finger—swept by no mortal hand—have rung faintly, like echoes, deep among the dead in those sacred vaults.”

Denys’s face altered; the colour left him as if a hand had wiped it away.

“Ugh!” said he. “If I had known, mules and wain-ropes had not hauled me thither.” Then, after a breath, with the stubborn regret of the flesh: “And so I had lost a merry time.”

Whether more might have been drawn from this talk—whether that other, older music of the place could ever be understood by two such men—who can tell? For up came a bearded lay brother from the monastery, spurring his mule, waving a piece of vellum.

It was the deed between Ghysbrecht and Floris Brandt.

Gerard’s heart leapt as if he had been struck. The deed was home in his hand: Sevenbergen in ink, a scrap of the language of his beginnings. He turned pale at first, thinking how near he had been to losing it; and then, to Denys’s infinite amusement, he gave money to the lay brother—and kissed the mule’s nose in gratitude, as if the beast had carried not parchment but a living child back into his arms.

“I’ll read you now,” said Gerard to the vellum, almost tenderly, “were you twice as ill written; and—to make sure of never losing you—”

There, by the roadside, he sat down, drew out needle and thread, and with a dexterity that might have shamed many a housewife, sewed the deed fast to his doublet. The stitches went in and out like steady footfalls; and with each pull of the thread his mind, heart, and soul were away to Sevenbergen—its ovens and fields, its familiar speech, the very household noises that no other country could reproduce.

So they went on, and at last reached the promised land. Denys, in high spirits again, doffed his bonnet to all females; they curtsied and smiled in return. He fired his consigne at most men; some stared, some grinned, some did both.

And so he landed Gerard at one of the long-promised Burgundian inns.

“It is a little one,” said he, “but I know it of old for a good one; Les Trois Poissons.” Then he stopped short. “But what is this writ up? I mind not this.”

He pointed to an inscription that ran across the whole building in a single line of huge letters. Denys, proud of his pains, spelled it out as if it were a hard crust to be chewed.

“Oh, I see. ‘Ici on loge a pied et a cheval,’” said he, going minutely through it and looking bumptious when he had effected it.

Gerard looked too; and the sentence that met his eye was not that at all, but a harsher proverb, written large enough for the blind and the dishonest alike:

ON NE LOGE CEANS A CREDIT; CE BONHOMME EST MORT, LES MAUVAIS PAIEURS L’ONT TUE.
