The Art of the Formal

"If you insist," Trent conceded, though his tone betrayed a distinct reluctance, "I suppose you will have your way. But I had vastly preferred composing this missive in solitude. However, if I am to proceed, ensure I am furnished with a sheet of note-paper so unblemished and white it rivals the stars themselves—or perhaps the parchment of a celestial choir; certainly, nothing bearing your address. Do not mistake this for mere formality; consider the magnitude of the sacrifice I am undertaking. I have never felt less inclined toward correspondence in my entire existence."

She smiled, a warm, rewarding expression.

"What exactly shall I pen?" he asked, his quill trembling slightly above the pristine page. "Shall I invoke Shakespeare and compare him to a summer's day? Or might I simply ask, 'What *shall* I say?'?"

"Write what you intend," she offered gently.

He shook his head vigorously. "My intention, what I have been desperate to articulate to every soul I encounter for the past twenty-four hours, is: 'Mabel and I are betrothed, and all is gas and gaiters.' Alas, that would make a dreadfully poor opening for a letter of strictly formal, indeed sinister, import. I have managed only as far as 'Dear Mr. Marlowe,' yet the silence following remains a chasm. Pray, guide me."

"I am enclosing a manuscript," she prompted, "one I believe you might find of interest."

"Do you perceive," Trent observed, his voice sharpening with a sudden academic fervor, "that within that single sentence reside merely two words exceeding one syllable? This epistle is designed to impress, not to induce comfort. We require a lexicon of gravitas."

"I fail to see the necessity," she replied. "While customary, it strikes me as superfluous. I have received countless missives from legal practitioners and businessmen, all prefaced with phrases like 'with reference to our communication' or similar verbose turgidities, continuing thus throughout. Yet, when I behold them in person, they converse with simplicity. To me, it appears absurd."

"It is far from absurd to them," Trent declared, setting down his pen with an air of profound relief and rising from his seat. "Allow me to elucidate. Our nation, possessing a populace not particularly enamored with mental exertion, advances through the mundane utilizing a vocabulary both narrow and elementary. Long words are, by definition, abnormalities. And like all anomalies, they serve a dual purpose: they are either comically ridiculous or terrifyingly solemn. Consider the phrase 'intelligent anticipation.' Were such a construct uttered in any other European tongue, it would pass unnoticed. Here, it has attained the status of a proverb; we all smirk upon hearing it in a speech or encountering it in a leading article, regarding it as a pinnacle of wit. Why? Solely because it comprises two lengthy terms. The concept expressed is as pedestrian as cold mutton. Then there is 'terminological inexactitude.' We have all roared, and continue to roar, at that monstrosity! The entire jest rests upon the length of the words. Conversely, when we aspire to absolute seriousness, we signal it by turning to these leviathans of syntax. When a solicitor opens a sentence with, 'pursuant to the instructions communicated to our representative,' or some similar gibberish, he feels a tangible sense of professional dignity, as if he has earned his six-and-eightpence. Do not laugh! It is the absolute truth. Now, the Continental mindset differs. They remain perpetually fixated on ideas themselves, resulting in a situation where every shopkeeper or peasant possesses a vernacular that is essentially Greek to the vast majority of Britons. I recall dining some time ago with a friend, a Parisian cabman. We ate at a soot-stained bistro opposite the central post office, surrounded entirely by drivers and porters. The conversation was universal, and it struck me that a London cabman would feel profoundly adrift. Terms like 'functionary,' 'unforgettable,' 'exterminate,' and 'independence' hurled themselves across the table with alarming frequency. And these were merely ordinary, vulgar, jovial, ruddy-cheeked cabmen. Mind you," he hastened to add as the lady moved across the room to reclaim his pen, "I cite this solely to illustrate my point. I am not suggesting cabmen ought to be intellectuals—I do not believe they should; I concur with Keats, that England is happy, sweet are her artless cabmen, and ample their simple loveliness for me. But regarding the collective industrial brainpower of the nation..." He paused, eyes wide. "Why, do you know—"

"Oh, no, no, no!" cried Mrs. Manderson, her voice ringing with urgency. "I know absolutely nothing at this juncture save that your discourse must be halted immediately if we are to progress with this letter to Mr. Marlowe. You shall not escape this task. Come!"

She seized the pen and placed it firmly in his hand.

Trent regarded it with visible distaste. "I warn you not to dampen my spirits further," he murmured dejectedly. "Be assured, gentlemen who refrain from speaking are often more difficult to endure than those who chatter. Oh, guard yourselves against the nature of the mute! I confess I am evading the composition of this document. It borders on indecency. It is a jarring mixture of moods—to draft a letter of this gravity while simultaneously occupying the same space as you."

She guided him back to his vacant chair before the escritoire and gently urged him down. "Very well, but please attempt it. I wish to witness your script, and I require it sent to him forthwith. You understand, I could be content to let matters rest; yet you assert a need for truth. If that necessity exists, I desire it realized without delay. Execute it now—you possess the ability if you apply yourself—and I shall dispatch it the instant it is complete. Have you never felt that peculiar impulse—the urge to deposit the troubling letter into the post and remove it from your hands, rendering it irrevocable, so that no further fussing about it becomes necessary?"

"I shall comply with your wishes," he said, turning to the paper, which he dated from his hotel. Mrs. Manderson gazed down at his bowed head, a soft, benevolent light illuminating her eyes. She gestured as if to smooth the unruly crop of hair atop his brow, but restrained herself. Moving silently to the piano, she began to play with delicate, understated grace. Ten minutes passed in quietude before Trent broke the silence.

"If he elects to respond by stating he will utter nothing?"

Mrs. Manderson glanced over her shoulder. "Naturally, he dares not adopt that stance. He will speak to forestall your denunciation."

"But I harbor no intention of doing so," he countered. "You would not permit it—you explicitly forbade it; furthermore, I shall not do it if you object. The evidence is too dubious at this stage."

"But," she laughed lightly, "does poor Mr. Marlowe suspect you will not?"

Trent sighed heavily. "How extraordinary the codes of honor truly are!" he remarked, lost in abstraction. "I am aware there exist actions I ought to perform, and never hesitate over, which would render you deeply disgraced were I to execute them—such as administering a black eye to anyone who grossly insults me, or swearing violently when I injure my leg in a dimly lit chamber. And now, you calmly recommend I bluff Mr. Marlowe via a tacit threat I do not intend to carry out—a maneuver that not even the most abandoned fiend of Hell, intoxicated by guilt, would ever conceive... Well, regardless, I shall not do it." He resumed his writing, and the lady, wearing an indulgent smile, returned to her silent accompaniment.

A few moments later, Trent announced, "At last, I am his faithfully. Would you care to peruse it?" She darted across the twilight-laden room, switching on a reading lamp beside the desk. Leaning affectionately against his shoulder, she read the ensuing words:

DEAR MR. MARLOWE,—_You will perhaps recall that we encountered one another, under unhappy circumstances, in June of last year at Marlstone._
_On that occasion, it was my professional duty, as a representative of a newspaper, to conduct an independent inquiry into the circumstances surrounding the demise of the late Sigsbee Manderson. I undertook said investigation and arrived at specific conclusions. You may acquaint yourself with these findings via the enclosed manuscript, which was originally drafted as a dispatch for my publication. For reasons deemed unnecessary for immediate articulation, I resolved at the eleventh hour neither to render them public nor to communicate them to you; consequently, they are known to only two individuals besides myself._

At this juncture, Mrs. Manderson lifted her gaze swiftly from the letter. Her dark brows knitted together. "Two persons?" she queried, her voice tinged with inquiry.
