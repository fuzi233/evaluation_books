News of Pen’s dumb little scuffle spread through the neighbourhood faster than a bad cold at a village tea party, and the reactions were perfectly predictable: fusty old Doctor Portman turned purple with huffing rage, like someone had stolen his favourite port decanter, while wily old Major Pendennis just snickered behind his hand and went “oh, good lad, showing some spirit.” As for poor Helen Pendennis? She was this close to draping the whole household in black crepe, convinced her sweet baby boy was one step away from duelling a dozen men at dawn and landing himself in jail for murder. Every little scrap of drama tied to that actress Miss Fotheringay seemed to spit out fresh disaster, and she was more determined than ever to ship Pen off far from Chatteris, anywhere he couldn’t run into the hussy who’d turned his whole life upside down.

When Helen tearfully remonstrated with him and the Doctor yelled at him for his wild, duel-obsessed nonsense, Pen leaned into the whole “wronged gentleman” bit harder than a bad actor in a melodrama, all wide-eyed solemnity and teenaged earnestness. He insisted he’d done absolutely nothing to provoke the fight, that no man had the right to insult his honour and get away with it, then turned to his uncle with the most dignified little stare he could pull off, and demanded to know what else a proper gentleman could possibly do but challenge the lout to a fistfight (or a pistol duel, if we’re being honest about his dramatic daydreams).

“Vous allez trop vite, my good idiot,” the Major said, flustered, seeing as he’d spent the last six months filling the boy’s head with his own old army-era nonsense about honour and duels. “For fancy London men of the world, sure, go shoot each other over a snub if you want. But two overgrown schoolboys? This is ridiculous, my dear boy. Completely daft.”

“It’s wicked, and nothing like my sweet, gentle son,” Helen said, dabbing at her eyes with a handkerchief, bewildered by how stubbornly Pen was clinging to his silly honour code.

Pen gave her a very patronizing kiss on the forehead. “Women, dear mother, simply don’t understand these matters. I put myself entirely in Foker’s hands, there was no other possible honourable path I could take.”

The Major grinned and shrugged. Those two lads were turning out to be far more entertaining than he’d expected, he thought. Helen, meanwhile, declared Foker was a horrid little troublemaker who’d lead her boy straight to the gallows if they went to the same university, and threatened to cancel the whole university plan right then and there. She only held off because she remembered Pen’s late husband had always dreamed of sending their son to his old alma mater, and she couldn’t bear to break that last wish.

Everyone had already agreed Pen would head up for the October term, after all. Foker had promised to introduce him to all the right people, and the Major was dead set on that being a fantastic advantage for the boy. “Young Foker’s on familiar terms with all the very best peers’ sons at the university right now,” the Major blustered to Helen. “The eldest son of the Duke of Saint David’s is there, Lord Magnus Charters, Lord Runnymede’s boy – who’s Foker’s first cousin, by the by, Lady Runnymede was Lady Agatha Milton, you must remember her! Lady Agnes Foker will have you out to their country house Logwood in no time. Far from being worried about Pen being friends with Foker, who’s a bit odd and very funny but totally good-natured and prudent, I think it’s the luckiest thing that ever happened to him! He already bailed us out of that whole mess with the Fotheringay marriage, after all.”

Helen sighed, supposed the Major knew best. Foker had been very kind when Pen was making a fool of himself over that actress, that was true, but she couldn’t shake a bad, squirming premonition that more trouble was coming. All the brawls, the gossip, the fancy worldliness the Major kept gushing about scared the daylights out of her when she thought of her soft, romantic boy wandering off into it alone.

Doctor Portman was all for Pen going off to university too. He hoped the lad would buckle down and study, meet some nice, respectable friends, and at the very least get far away from all the Chatteris drama that was driving Helen half mad. Pen, who was half distracted from his broken heart by all the fights and fuss around him, just mumbled grumpily that he’d do as he was told.

August and September brought Chatteris’ annual assizes, races, and a flood of random out-of-town visitors traipsing through the local theatres, which meant Miss Fotheringay was dragging herself through her farewell performance run night after night. No one except our little circle of Pendennis associates seemed to care one bit she was leaving the stage forever – all the fancy county folks who’d sung her praises to the rafters in London when it was trendy to be her fan, looked at her performing on the rickety little Chatteris stage and went “eh, nothing special about that woman.” It’s a fate a thousand geniuses and a thousand frauds have suffered before and after her time. The actress herself didn’t care a whit, though; she went about eating her three square meals, acting her parts, and sleeping her eight hours a night with the unflappable calm of a person blessed with zero overactive sentiment. Turns out a healthy dose of mild, cheerful dullness is one of life’s greatest superpowers – it skips you right past 90% of the unnecessary grief, drama, and harmful overthinking that sentimental saps like Pen torture themselves over.

Pen wandered in and out of the Chatteris theatre as the mood took him that season, and his moping about over the actress drove his mother half wild with anxiety. She would’ve interfered a dozen times to ban him from going, if the Major hadn’t kept nudging her back and reassuring her the boy was already getting over it. The Major’s biggest clue that Pen’s heartbreak was fading? The kid was spouting terrible poetry everywhere. He’d yell lines of verse at the top of his lungs while stomping through the shrubbery, mutter Latin hexameters through dinner, and when the Major snooped through Pen’s study one day while he was out riding, he found a whole fat notebook full of sappy sonnets, complete with stuffy little footnotes quoting classical authors, just like they taught him at school. “He can’t be that badly heartbroken,” the Major thought, very wisely, and pointed out to Helen that Pen was coming home ravenous for dinner every night, and clearing two plates of breakfast every morning. “Gad, I wish I had that appetite,” the Major said, glumly thinking of the special digestion pills he had to take to force down half a chop. “Mark my words, the boy’s starting to sleep through the night, no more moping till three a.m. It’s cruel, but it’s true – he’s over that actress.”

Pen couldn’t vent all his silly sappy feelings to anyone else: if he told his mom about his broken heart she’d cry for three hours, the Major would tease him in that dry, worldly way that made him squirm, and Foker would just burp and offer him a cigar before changing the subject to oysters. So he latched onto poor Smirke the Curate, and decided Smirke was his Best Friend Forever. What’s a pining teen without a designated confidant to listen to all his moaning? Pen used Smirke like the old shepherd Corydon used his elm tree – he carved Miss Fotheringay’s name (metaphorically) into every conversation, filled Smirke’s ears with endless rants about his lost love. He thought he and Smirke were soulmates, because he could sigh all his griefs right into his tutor’s ears, and Smirke was only too happy to listen for his own secret reasons.

Pen’s gushy affection knew no bounds. He wrote odes to Smirke, full of Latin Sapphics and Alcaics, and gave them to him as gifts. He handed over his fancy bound favourite books, his gold watch chain, even tried to give Smirke his prized double-barrelled shotgun. He ran up to Chatteris and bought an expensive gold pencil case on credit (he still owed Smirke money for half the silly gifts he’d bought Miss Fotheringay, after all) engraved with a dramatic message about their eternal unbreakable friendship. The soft-hearted Smirke ate all the attention right up, he was delighted.

The poor Curate was absolutely devastated at the thought of Pen leaving for university. Once Arthur was gone, his whole reason for popping by Fairoaks every single day vanished. What excuse would he have, to hang around and get a little smile or kind word from Helen Pendennis, that was more precious to him than the cheap little dinner Madame Fribsby cooked up for him every night? No more “popping over to help Pen translate Homer” visits, no more weekly excuse to stop by, no more little Laura needing extra catechism lessons. He’d curled around Fairoaks like ivy on a garden wall, and the thought of getting ripped off made him ache. He debated dropping to one knee and proposing to the widow right that second, going over every little tiny nice thing she’d said to him in the last three months – she’d praised his sermon three weeks back, she’d thanked him profusely for the melon he brought over for her little dinner party, she’d said she’d always be grateful he was so kind to Arthur. But what if he proposed and she said no? He’d be banned from Fairoaks forever, and that little house was his whole entire world.

Which brings us to the universal truth, dear reader: every single person on this planet has their own private little soap opera going on, that they care about a thousand times more than anyone else’s drama. Helen Pendennis is fretting about her baby boy flying the nest for the big wide world, the Major is seething because he’s stuck in this hick village instead of hobnobbing with dukes and duchesses in London, Pen is torn between pining for his old actress crush and secretly dying of excitement to get out and see the world, and Smirke’s got his own little heartache following him around even on his pony rides. We’re all so lonely and secret, aren’t we? You and your spouse have shared the same bed for forty years and think you know each other – psha. Do you feel your partner’s gout pain? Does she lie awake tossing and turning when you have a toothache? Your sweet innocent teenage daughter, who seems to do nothing but practice piano and fawn over her mom? She’s thinking about that lieutenant she danced with at the last ball. Your honest schoolboy son is secretly tallying up the money you’ll give him next term, and all the debts he owes the village tart shop. The little old grandma dozing by the fire, who’s months from the grave? She’s not thinking about any of you – she’s daydreaming about the night she was 19, dancing a cotillon with that handsome captain before your grandpa ever asked her out. We’re all little isolated islands, each with our own whole secret universe under our own hats. But let’s get back to poor lonely Smirke.

Smirke only had one person he could talk to about his secret crush: that most unwise of gossips, his landlady Madame Fribsby. No one had any idea how she’d earned that fancy “Madame” title – she’d left Clavering as a bouncing teenaged seamstress, gone off to work for a milliner in London, popped over to Paris for a bit, and come back calling herself Madame, insisting she’d been awarded the title by the French government. No one was rude enough to point out the French king couldn’t just hand out random Madame titles to every seamstress who walked past his palace. She bought the late Mrs. Harbottle’s millinery shop for a bargain, brought her elderly deaf mother to live with her, was endlessly kind to the poor, went to church every Sunday, and had a perfect reputation. She also read every single cheap sentimental romance novel ever printed, no exceptions, to the point she was so absurdly soft-hearted she saw star-crossed lovers in every single pair of two single people who so much as said hello to each other.

The day after Helen had visited the Curate, Madame Fribsby had decided for certain that Smirke was head over heels in love with the widow, and set about meddling to push them together as hard as she could. Helen barely ever saw her, since she made all her own dresses and never bought fancy millinery, but every rare time Madame Fribsby got to visit Fairoaks, she’d gush over Smirke nonstop – how gentle he was, how studious, how dreadfully lonely, wondering out loud why no nice lady would take pity on him and marry him.

Helen laughed at her silly sentimental remarks, and teased her back: “Why don’t you take pity on him yourself, Madame?” Madame Fribsby would dramatically pat her chest, shake her fancy Madonna-style lace front wig, and spout broken French about how her own poor wounded heart belonged to a lost love in Spain, glancing up at a beat-up print of a French carbineer in a green coat and brass cuirass hanging on her wall. Helen didn’t care to dig into her weird romantic secrets, she thought of Smirke as nothing more than a respectable clergyman who was tutoring her son, no more. But Madame Fribsby was dead set that the widow was secretly pining right back.

Smirke, for his part, was more than happy to ramble on about his crush to his landlady. They’d sit in her parlour for hours, chattering away mostly in terrible French, so her deaf old mother couldn’t understand a word of what they were saying. When Major Pendennis dropped by to tell Smirke that Pen would be off to university in October, and his tutoring services wouldn’t be needed any more, the poor curate almost had a full-on panic attack. The critical moment had come – he either had to propose, or lose Fairoaks forever.

Meanwhile, the entire Clavering gossip mill was already churning out ridiculous stories about the Pendennis household. Madame Fribsby had been gossiping about Pen’s actress drama for months, the whole village of old maids and retired army officers who made up the town’s “genteel” population had heard every exaggerated detail a dozen times. There were rumours Pen had hanged himself in the Fairoaks orchard and his uncle had cut him down just in time, rumours Miss Fotheringay had dumped him, rumours the Pendennis family had paid her thousands and thousands of pounds to keep quiet about the whole affair. When Pen heard the rumours slandering his old crush’s honour, he raged like a tiny furious lion, wishing old Mrs. Pybus (who had more whiskers than most cavalrymen) was a man so he could challenge her to a duel. He glared so menacingly at the local vicar Simcoe one day from horseback that the man ran home and wrote a thundering sermon about Jezebels, bad theatre, and youth going straight to hell, that everyone in the congregation knew was a dig straight at Pen.

Pen got labelled a profligate, a brawler, and almost a murderer in the village, but that wasn’t the end of it. The gossip soon shifted over to Helen, and the new rumour that the widow was planning to marry Smirke the second Pen was safely shipped off to university. “It’s all settled!” Mrs. Pybus cackled to Mrs. Speers over tea. “He’s there every single day, anyone can see it!” The rumours spread fast, and soon even Doctor Portman heard all about it. The good rector flew into such a fit of rage he wanted to fire Smirke on the spot and kick him out of his parish. “That simpering milksop curate, thinking he’s good enough for a wonderful woman like Mrs. Pendennis?” he sputtered. “Where does impudence even end!”
“Besides, he’s far too young for her,” Mrs. Portman added. “Poor dear Helen is old enough to be his mother, for heaven’s sake!”
