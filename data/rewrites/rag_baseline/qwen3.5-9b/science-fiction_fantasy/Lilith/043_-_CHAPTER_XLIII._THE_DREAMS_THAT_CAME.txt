The cold arrived first—not as a sensation, but as a fundamental state of being, absolute and infinite. I became acutely aware of it, and strangely, I felt blessed. More blessed, I realized, than my heart could possibly comprehend in retrospect. Warmth held no allure; I recalled enjoying it once, but the memory was a hollow echo. Here, in this frozen void, the cold performed a surgical function: it soothed every anxiety, dissolved every ache, and numbed every sorrow. Was it comfort? No. Sorrow was simply swallowed by a life force drawing near, poised to restore every good and lovely thing a hundredfold. I lay at peace, brimming with a quiet, terrifying expectation, breathing the damp, rich scent of the earth's bosom. I sensed the souls of primroses, daisies, and snowdrops, dormant and patient within the soil, waiting for spring.

How to articulate the ecstasy of this frozen, lucid slumber? I needed no longer to stand. All that remained was to lie stretched out, motionless. The cold intensified, driving deeper into my marrow, yet I welcomed it with increasing fervor. My consciousness of self eroded, replaced by a swelling awareness of a bliss unimaginable yet undeniably felt. I had neither crafted this state nor prayed for it; it belonged to me solely by virtue of existing, and my existence was sustained by a Will dwelling within me.

Then the dreams descended, crowding in with relentless force. I found myself naked atop a snowy peak, the white mist heaving below like a turbulent ocean. The cold moon hung suspended in the air alongside me, dwarfed by a sky even colder, a realm where the moon and I resided. I was Adam, awaiting the divine breath to animate my nostrils. Then I was a child cradled in the arms of a mother radiating blinding whiteness. Next, a youth astride a white steed, leaping from cloud to cloud across a azure heaven, racing calmly toward a sanctified destination. For centuries—or perhaps millennia, or merely one endless night—I drifted through these visions. Time ceased to matter; I existed in the land of thought, ascending beyond the seven dimensions and ten senses. I believed I stood in the heart of God. I witnessed cycles melting within glaciers, the spectral moon closing in, the wind and the roar of torrents swelling in my ears. I lay there, hearing them: the wind, the water, the moon singing a hymn of peaceful anticipation for a redemption drawing near. These were not mere cycles; they were the solemn, aeonian march of a single second, pregnant with eternity.

Abruptly, amidst this transcendent bliss, the weight of my sins crashed down. Every wrong I had committed, stretching from the furthest reaches of my memory to the present moment, manifested vividly. Within each transgression, the conscious "I" was present—confessing, renouncing, lamenting the dead. I made atonement with every soul I had injured, hurt, or offended. Every human spirit to whom I had caused a ripple of trouble now became unspeakably precious to me. I humbled myself before them, agonizing to sever the clinging offense. I wept at the feet of the mother whose commands I had spurned; with bitter shame, I confessed to my father that I had lied to him twice, lies long forgotten yet meticulously preserved in my mind to crush finally at his feet. I became the eager servant of all whom I had wronged. Countless acts of restitution flooded my imagination: building houses unseen before, training horses never before glimpsed, creating gardens teeming with still pools and running waters, composing songs to swell their hearts and tales to ignite their spirits. I envisioned channeling the world's forces into inventions that would birth laughter born of wonder. Love seized me entirely. Love was my life. Love was, as it was for the Creator, all in all.

Suddenly, I plunged into a solid blackness, so dense that the faint luminescence of the eye's caverns could not pierce it. Yet my heart, fearless and hope-filled, remained tranquil. I lay there envisioning the nature of the light to come, the new creation it would herald. Then, without conscious volition, I sat upright and stared into the void.

Moonlight filtered through the lowest, crypt-like windows of a death-chamber, casting long, diagonal beams across fallen, ripening sheaves of the great husbandman's harvest. But the harvest was gone. Gathered in? Swept away by chaotic storms? Not a single sacred stalk remained. My dead were gone. I was alone. Desolation settled in depths far vaster than I had ever known. Had there never been any ripening dead? Had I merely dreamed them and their beauty? If so, why these walls? Why the empty couches? No—they were all gone! They were all abroad in the new eternal day, having forgotten me. They had abandoned me, leaving me behind. The tomb was ten times more terrible now that its inhabitants were absent. Their presence had once calmed me, permeating my mind with their blissful peace; now I had no friends, and my lovers were distant. Horror-stricken, I sat and stared. I had been alone with the moon on a mountain peak in the sky; now I was alone with her in a vast cenotaph. She too stared about, searching for her dead with a ghastly gaze. I sprang to my feet and staggered from the fearful place.

The cottage was empty. I ran out into the night.

No moon awaited me. Just as I departed the chamber, a cloudy rampart had risen, obscuring her. Yet a broad shimmer emanated from far over the heath, mingled with a ghostly, murmuring music, as if the moon were raining light that splashed upon impact. I stumbled across the moor, drawn to a lovely lake bordered by reeds and rushes. Behind the cloud, the moon gazed upon the monsters' den—a pool of clearest, brightest water, utterly still. But the musical murmur persisted, filling the quiet air and pulling me onward.

I circled the small mere and climbed the range of hills. What a spectacle greeted my eyes! The entire expanse where, with hot, aching feet, I had previously traversed the deep-scored channels and ravines of the dry riverbed was now alive with streams, torrents, and still pools—"a river deep and wide"! The moon flashed upon the water, and the water answered with its own brilliant reflections—white flashes breaking everywhere from its rocky flow. A great, jubilant song erupted from its bosom, the anthem of newly born liberty. I stood transfixed, and my heart began to exult: my life was not a total failure! I had helped set this river free! My dead were not lost! I had only to pursue them, to follow until I reached where they had gone. Our meeting might be thousands of years hence, but at last—at last—I would hold them! Why else did the floods clap their hands?

I hurried down the hill; my pilgrimage had commenced. I knew not which direction to turn, but I must go, go, until I found my living dead. A torrent ran swift and wide at the foot of the range; I rushed in, and it laid no hold upon me. I waded through the next, leaped across the third, swam the fourth, and waded the fifth.

I paused to gaze upon the wondrous loveliness of the ceaseless flash and flow, to listen to the multitudinous, fractured music. Occasionally, a distinct melody seemed ready to emerge from the dulcet confusion, only to merge again into the consorted roar. At moments, the world of waters threatened to overwhelm me—not with the brute force of a seaward rush or the shouting of a liberated throng, but with the greatness of a silence wandering into sound.

Lost in delight, a hand rested on my shoulder. I turned to see a man in the prime of strength, beautiful as if freshly forged from the glad Creator's heart, young like him who cannot age. I recognized him: it was Adam. He stood large and grand, clad in a white robe, the moon woven into his hair.

"Father," I cried, "where is she? Where are the dead? Has the great resurrection already passed? The terror of my loneliness consumed me; I could not sleep without my dead, so I fled the desolate chamber. Whither shall I go to find them?"

"You mistake, my son," he answered, his very breath offering consolation. "You remain in the chamber of death, still upon your couch, asleep and dreaming, with the dead surrounding you."

"But alas!" I protested, "When I merely dream, how can I know it? The dream most beautifully dreamed resembles waking truth most closely!"

"When you are quite dead, you will dream no false dream. The soul that is true generates nothing untrue, nor can the false enter it."

"But, sir," I faltered, "how am I to distinguish between the true and the false when both appear equally real?"

"Do you not understand?" he returned, with a smile capable of slaying all the sorrows of his children. "You *cannot* perfectly distinguish between the true and the false while you are not yet quite dead; nor indeed will you when you are quite dead—that is, quite alive—for then the false will never present itself. At this moment, believe me, you are on your bed in the house of death."

"I am striving to believe you, Father. I do indeed believe you, though I can neither see nor feel the truth of your words."
