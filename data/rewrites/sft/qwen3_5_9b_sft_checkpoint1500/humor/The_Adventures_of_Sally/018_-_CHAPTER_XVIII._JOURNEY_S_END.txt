Night approached with the timid hesitation of a shy guest, as though reluctant to interrupt the perfect, golden afternoon. Beyond the tree line to the west, a faint afterglow lingered like a fading whisper, while above the massive barn, a new moon hung sharp and silver, resembling a slender sickle. Stepping onto the gravel path, Sally bowed three times with solemn gravity—a superstitious charm against bad luck—and then stood still, breathing in the perfume of the evening. Life, she felt, was undeniably sweet.

The shadows, initially coy, soon settled into a determined resolve. They swept across the landscape with efficient speed, turning the sky a uniform, deep indigo speckled with quiet stars. The concrete ribbon leading to Patchogue and Babylon dissolved into obscurity, swallowed whole by the gloom. Yet, lights flickered to life in windows across the meadows, like scattered lanterns guiding travelers home. From the kennel yard, a lone, sleepy bark drifted on the air. Toto, the small white woolly dog who had trotted out at Sally's heels, halted abruptly and let out a challenging squeak.

The silence was so profound that Ginger's heavy footsteps, pounding along the road from his errands in the village, rang out clearly before he even turned the corner at the gate. He had returned to procure provisions, the evening papers, and the yarn for the sweater Sally was currently knitting. Though invisible in the dim light, Sally heard him approach, and a warm, cozy thrill of happiness—one that had become her nightly ritual for the past year—welled up within her chest.

"Ginger!" she called.

"What ho!"

Toto, issuing another authoritative squeak, scampered down the drive to investigate the intruder. He received a frosty greeting; Ginger, despite his professed love for canines, had never managed to extend affection to Toto. The memory of a month prior resurfaced: Sally, having witnessed Mrs. Meecher's distress over her pet's sudden lethargy, had pleaded for hospitality. Ginger had grumbled, but eventually acquiesced.

"It's wonderful what you've done for Toto, angel," Sally remarked as Ginger brushed past, evading the dog's enthusiastic leaps with practiced ease. "He's a different creature entirely."

"A bit of luck for him," Ginger replied, his tone cool.

"During all those years I spent at Mrs. Meecher's, I never saw him move faster than a stately shuffle. Now?" Sally gestured around the yard. "He's running about like the wind."

"The blighter had been overeating since birth," Ginger explained, his face lighting up with satisfaction. "That was the root cause. A little judicious dieting sorted him out completely. Speaking of dieting..." He paused, eyes gleaming. "We'll be shipping him back next week."

"I shall quite miss him."

"I nearly missed him myself this morning—with a shoe!" Ginger admitted cheerfully. "He was perched atop the kitchen table, wolfing down bacon, and I took swift action."

"My prehistoric man!" Sally murmured, shaking her head. "I always suspected you harbored a brutal streak, Ginger. What an exquisite evening, though."

"Good Lord!" Ginger exclaimed suddenly as they stepped through the open kitchen door, illuminated by the soft glow of the lamp.

"Now what?" Sally asked.

He halted, staring at her with intense scrutiny.

"Do you know," he said, his voice dropping an octave, "you are looking prettier than you were when I first descended upon the village!"

Sally threw an arm around his shoulder, laughing softly.

"Beloved!" she cooed. "Did you secure the chops?"

Ginger froze, horror washing over his features.

"Oh, my aunt! I utterly forgot them!"

"Oh, Ginger, you old chump," Sally chided gently. "Well, perhaps you need some judicious dieting yourself, just like poor Toto."

"I say, I'm dreadfully sorry. At least I procured the wool."

"If you imagine I intend to consume wool—"

"But isn't there anything else in the house?"

"Plenty of vegetables and fruit."

Splendid! "But naturally, if you require chops—"

"Not in the least," Ginger declared, adopting a philosophical pose. "I am purely spiritual. Furthermore, the common wisdom suggests vegetables are excellent for blood pressure. Did you also forget to fetch the mail?"

"Absolutes nonsense!" Ginger protested, puffing his chest. "I was on top of it like a razor blade. Two letters arrived from gentlemen seeking Airedale puppies."

"No! Ginger, we are progressing!"

"Quite bloated," Ginger agreed complacently, nodding sagely. "Extremely bloated. If things continue to buzz along at this pace, we shall acquire that two-seater coupe without a second thought. By the way, here is a letter for you."

He handed it over as they entered the kitchen.

"It is from Fillmore," Sally observed, inspecting the envelope. "About time, too. I haven't heard a peep from him for months."

She sat down and unfolded the paper. Ginger, meanwhile, heaved himself onto the table, arranging his limbs with great care before settling into his reading position. After skimming the sports pages, however, he lowered the newspaper. His gaze drifted to Sally's bent head, resting upon the letter, filled with a profound sense of contentment.

Though a husband of nearly a year's duration, Ginger remained suspended in a magical realm of dazed disbelief, struggling to comprehend the reality of his bliss. In his travels, he had encountered many illusions that looked magnificent from afar yet crumbled under close inspection. Marriage, however, with Sally as his partner, stood apart. It seemed to be one of the rarest treasures in existence, devoid of any hidden catch. His honest eyes burned with adoration as he watched her. Sally, breaking into a fit of giggles, drew attention to something specific.

"Ginger, look at this!"

He leaned forward, taking the slip of paper she offered. Bold, block letters stared back at him:

Ginger squinted at the cryptic message, his brow furrowed in confusion.

"What is this?" he asked.

"It's Fillmore," Sally answered, gurgling with delight.

"How do you mean?"

"Silly goose," Sally explained, "Fillmore and Gladys have launched a little restaurant in Pittsburgh."

"A restaurant!" The shock in Ginger's voice was palpable. Even though he knew his brother-in-law, the self-styled 'Man of Destiny,' had previously suffered a catastrophic failure in management, he retained a lingering reverence for his genius. For Fillmore Nicholas, a mastermind of epic proportions, to descend to operating a mere *little* restaurant struck Ginger as almost sacrilegious.

Sally, conversely, radiated pure joy. Sisters often failed to maintain appropriate awe for their brothers' grandeur.

"It is simply splendid," she enthused. "It looks as if Fillmore is finally destined for greatness. They appear to have begun on a modest scale, merely crafting pork-pies..."

"Why 'Popp'?" Ginger interrupted, his curiosity piqued.

"Just a trade name, darling. Gladys is a marvelous cook; she prepared the pies while Fillmore roamed about peddling them. They were so successful that they've expanded into a full-fledged restaurant, which is thriving as well. Listen to this." Sally turned the page. "...sound financial footing. In fact, our success has been so instantaneous that I have decided to launch out on a truly grand scale. It is Big Ideas that forge Big Business. I am contemplating a vast expansion of this venture, and in very short order, I shall establish branches in New York, Chicago, Detroit, and every major city. Each will be overseen by a manager and will feature, as its specialty, our famous Popp's Outstanding Pork-pies. Once these branches are firmly established, I shall sail for England to introduce Popp's Pork-pies there... Isn't he a little marvel?"

"Dashed brilliant fellow. I've always maintained that."

"I confess, I felt a twinge of unease when I read that initial paragraph. I've seen so many of Fillmore's 'Big Ideas.' Usually, he stumbles upon something promising and then, in his haste, overextends himself until he bursts. However, everything seems fine now that he has Gladys to guide him. She appended a postscript. Just four words, yet oh, how they soothe a sister's heart. 'Yes, I don't think!' That is what she writes. I cannot recall ever reading anything more reassuring. Thank heavens, she has poor dear Fillmore firmly in hand."

"Pork-pies!" Ginger mused, his stomach suddenly rumbling with the pangs of genuine hunger. "I wish he had sent us one of those outstanding little chaps. I could certainly use one."

Sally rose and tousled his unruly red hair.

"Poor old Ginger! I knew you couldn't resist. Come on, it's a beautiful night. Let's stroll to the village and feast at the inn. We're going to be millionaires before we know it, so we can easily afford a treat."
