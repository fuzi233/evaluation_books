The moment we were alone, I tried once more to rise from my seat on the sand. Mr. Franklin stopped me with a firm hand.

“There’s one good thing about this cursed place,” he said, “we’ve got it all to ourselves. Stay where you are, Betteredge—I’ve something important to tell you.”

As he spoke, I studied him closely, searching for traces of the boy I once knew in the man before me. But the years had changed him beyond recognition. His cheeks, once rosy and round, were now pale and sharp. A thick, curly brown beard and moustache covered his lower face—something that startled and disappointed me. He still carried himself with a lively, quick-witted charm, pleasant enough to watch, but nothing like the easy, carefree manner of his youth. Worse still, he’d promised to be tall, and he wasn’t. He was neat and well-built, yes—but barely reached middle height. In short, I could find no trace of the old boy except in his eyes: bright, clear, and honest as ever. There, at least, I saw the familiar spark. That was enough.

“Welcome back to the old place, Mr. Franklin,” I said.

“All the more welcome,” he replied, “since you didn’t expect me until later.”

“I have reason to come early,” he answered. “I suspect I’ve been followed and watched in London for the past few days. That’s why I took the morning train instead of the afternoon one—to shake off a certain dark-looking stranger.”

Those words struck me harder than surprise. Instantly, I recalled the three jugglers who’d visited the house earlier, and Penelope’s hunch that they meant harm to Mr. Franklin Blake.

“Who’s watching you—and why?” I asked.

He ignored my question. Instead, he said, “Tell me about the three Indians who came to the house today. It’s possible, Betteredge, that your jugglers and my stranger are pieces of the same puzzle.”

“How do you know about them?” I asked, overlapping questions—a habit I admit is rude. But what can you expect from ordinary folk? Not much, I suppose.

“I saw Penelope at the house,” he said. “She told me everything. Your daughter has turned out prettier than I imagined. Small ears, small feet—charming. Did Mrs. Betteredge possess those qualities?”

“The late Mrs. Betteredge had many flaws,” I replied. “One of them—though I beg your pardon for mentioning it—was her inability to stay on topic. She fluttered about like a fly; never settled on anything.”

“She would have suited me perfectly,” Mr. Franklin said with a smile. “I never settle on anything either. And Betteredge, your edge is sharper than ever. Penelope said so when I asked about the jugglers. ‘Father will tell you, sir,’ she said. ‘He’s wonderful for his age, and expresses himself beautifully.’ Blushing, mind you—not even my respect for you could stop me from noticing that. I knew her as a child, and she’s none the worse for it. Now let’s be serious. What did the jugglers do?”

I felt a pang of irritation—not because Penelope had allowed Mr. Franklin to kiss her (he was welcome to that), but because she’d forced me to recount her tale secondhand. Still, there was no help for it. I began to explain.

As I spoke, Mr. Franklin’s cheerful expression faded. He sat forward, brows knitted, fingers twisting his beard. When I finished, he repeated two of the chief juggler’s questions—apparently to fix them in memory:

“‘Is it on the road to this house, and on no other, that the English gentleman will travel today?’”
“‘Has the English gentleman got It about him?’”

Then, pulling a small sealed parcel from his pocket, he added, “I suspect ‘It’ refers to _this_. And ‘this,’ Betteredge, is my uncle Herncastle’s famous Diamond.”

“Good Lord, sir!” I exclaimed. “How did you come into possession of the wicked Colonel’s Diamond?”

“The Colonel’s will left it to my cousin Rachel as a birthday gift,” he said. “And my father, as executor, entrusted it to me to bring here.”

If the sea had suddenly dried up beneath us, turning the Shivering Sand into solid ground, I wouldn’t have been more astonished.

“The Colonel’s Diamond left to Miss Rachel?” I gasped. “And your father—the Colonel’s executor? Why, I’d have wagered anything, Mr. Franklin, that your father wouldn’t touch the Colonel with a pair of tongs!”

“Strong words, Betteredge,” he replied. “But what was wrong with the Colonel? He belonged to your time, not mine. Tell me what you know about him. Then I’ll explain how my father became executor—and more besides. I’ve made some unsettling discoveries in London about my uncle and the Diamond. I need your confirmation. You called him the ‘wicked Colonel’ just now. Search your memory, my old friend—why?”

I saw he meant business, so I gave him the full account. Here is the substance of what I told him—written down for your benefit. Pay close attention. If you don’t, you’ll be lost when we reach the heart of the mystery. Clear your mind of trivial matters—children, dinner, new hats, politics, horse racing, stock prices, club grievances. I hope you won’t take offense at this blunt appeal. It’s simply my way of urging the gentle reader to focus. Heaven knows I’ve seen you with great authors in hand, and I know how easily attention wanders when a book demands it rather than a person.

Earlier, I mentioned my lady’s father—the old lord, hot-tempered and long-tongued. He had five children: two sons first, then, after a long gap, three daughters in rapid succession. My mistress was the youngest and finest of them.

Of the two sons, Arthur inherited the title and estate. The younger, the Honourable John, received a large fortune from a relative and joined the army. As they say, an ill bird fouls its own nest. I consider the Herncastle family my own nest, and I ask only that you allow me to avoid speaking of the Honourable John if I can.

He was, I believe honestly, one of the worst men ever to live. I can say no more or less. He began in the Guards, but left before twenty—no matter why. The army is strict, and too strict for the Honourable John. He went to India to see if they were equally rigid there, and to try active service.

In bravery—he had that, at least—he was a mix of bulldog and gamecock, with a streak of the savage. He fought in the capture of Seringapatam. Soon after, he transferred to another regiment, then another. In the third, he rose to lieutenant-colonel. But soon after, he suffered a sunstroke and returned to England.

He came back with a reputation that shut every door in his family. My lady—then newly married—led the charge, declaring (with Sir John’s approval) that her brother should never enter her home again.

There were many reasons people avoided him, but the stain of the Diamond is the only one that matters here. It was whispered he acquired his Indian jewel through means too dishonourable even for him to admit. He never sold it—no need for money, and no interest in wealth. He never gave it away. He never showed it to anyone.

Some believed he feared military authorities might act against him. Others—ignorant of his true nature—said he feared showing it would cost him his life. Perhaps there was truth in that last. He wasn’t afraid—but his life had been threatened twice in India. And everyone believed the Moonstone was at the root of it.

When he returned to England and found himself shunned, the Moonstone was blamed again. The mystery of his life became a curse, outlawing him among his own kin. Clubs refused him entry. Women he sought to marry rejected him. Friends and relatives pretended not to see him in the street.

Most men in such a position would have tried to mend their ways. But the Honourable John never gave in—even when he was wrong and society stood against him. He kept the Diamond in defiance of assassination in India. He kept it in defiance of public opinion in England.

That, in a nutshell, is the man: a character who defied everything, and a face—handsome though it was—that looked possessed by the devil.

We heard various rumours over the years. Some said he smoked opium and collected ancient books. Others claimed he dabbled in strange chemistry. Once, he was seen carousing in the slums of London with the lowest company. But whatever the truth, he lived a solitary, vicious, underground life.

Once, and only once, after his return to England, I saw him face to face. About two years before this story begins, and a year and a half before his death, the Colonel arrived unannounced at my lady’s London house. It was Miss Rachel’s birthday—June 21st—and there was a party.

A footman sent word that a gentleman wished to see me. I went to the hall—and there stood the Colonel, thin, worn, shabby, yet as wild and wicked as ever.

“Go to my sister,” he said. “Tell her I’ve come to wish my niece many happy returns.”

He’d written letters before, attempting reconciliation—only, I believe, to provoke her. But this was the first time he’d actually come to the house.

I almost told him my mistress had guests. But the look in his eyes froze me. I took the message upstairs.

My lady had a touch—just a touch—of the family temper.

“Tell Colonel Herncastle,” she said, “that Miss Verinder is engaged, and that _I_ decline to see him.”

I tried to soften the reply, knowing the Colonel’s disregard for social rules. But she cut me off sharply.

“When I want your advice,” she said, “I always ask. I don’t ask now.”

I returned downstairs with my amended version: “My lady and Miss Rachel regret they are engaged, Colonel, and beg to be excused the honour of seeing you.”

I expected a rage. Instead, he remained unnaturally calm. His grey eyes fixed on me for a moment, then he laughed—not outwardly, but inwardly—a soft, chuckling, horribly mischievous sound.

“Thank you, Betteredge,” he said. “I shall remember my niece’s birthday.”

With that, he turned and left.

The next birthday came. We heard he was ill. Six months later—six months before this story begins—a letter arrived from a respected clergyman. It bore two astonishing news items: first, the Colonel had forgiven his sister on his deathbed. Second, he had forgiven everyone else and died in a most edifying manner.

I respect the Church—though I’m not blind to its flaws. But I firmly believe the devil held sway over the Honourable John to the very end. And his final abomination, I swear, was to deceive the clergyman!

That was all I had to tell. I noticed Mr. Franklin listened more intently the longer I spoke. Especially when I described the Colonel being turned away on his niece’s birthday—that seemed to strike him like a bullet hitting its mark. Though he didn’t admit it, I saw unease in his face.

“You’ve said your piece, Betteredge,” he said. “Now it’s my turn. But before I reveal what I’ve discovered in London, and how I became involved with the Diamond, I must ask one thing. Do you understand the purpose of our meeting? Or does your expression betray confusion?”

“No, sir,” I replied. “My expression tells the truth.”

“In that case,” he said, “let me lay out my point of view. Three serious questions arise from the Colonel’s gift to my cousin Rachel. Listen carefully, Betteredge—and count them on your fingers if it helps.”

He paused, clearly enjoying the clarity of his own thinking—reminding me of the boy he once was.

“First: Was the Colonel’s Diamond the target of a conspiracy in India?”
“Second: Has that conspiracy followed the Diamond to England?”
“Third: Did the Colonel know the conspiracy followed it—and did he deliberately leave a legacy of danger to his sister through her innocent child?”

“That,” he said, “is what I’m driving at, Betteredge. Don’t let me frighten you.”

Easy for him to say. He *had* frightened me. If he was right, then our peaceful English home had just been invaded by a cursed Indian diamond—dragging with it a conspiracy of living rogues, set loose by the vengeance of a dead man.

That was our situation, laid bare in his words. Who had ever heard of such a thing—in the nineteenth century, in an age of progress, under the British Constitution? No one. And thus, no one would believe it.

Yet I shall continue—despite that.

When sudden alarm strikes, nine times out of ten, it hits the stomach. When it hits the stomach, attention drifts—and fidgeting begins. I fidgeted silently on the sand.

Mr. Franklin noticed. Just as he was about to begin his part of the story, he broke off sharply.

“What do you want?” he asked.

What did I want? I didn’t tell him. But I’ll tell you, in confidence: I wanted a puff of my pipe—and a chapter of *Robinson Crusoe*.
