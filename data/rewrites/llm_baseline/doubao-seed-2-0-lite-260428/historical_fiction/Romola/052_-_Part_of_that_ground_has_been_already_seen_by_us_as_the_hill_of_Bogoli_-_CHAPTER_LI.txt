When Romola had told the distressed young contadina Tessa that a companion waited for her nearby, that companion was none other than her cousin Monna Brigida — and the elder woman had not the faintest idea how desperately she would soon need Romola there to rescue her. The pair had been walking back toward the main Piazza together when they spotted the roving band of pious Piagnoni youths come to a halt around the frightened Tessa, and when Romola stepped forward to soothe the girl, she called over her shoulder, “Wait for me a moment, cousin.”

But Monna Brigida had no intention of lingering. She had turned pale, and blurted hastily, “I will not stay out here! Go to Boni the druggist’s shop to fetch me — I will retreat there at once.”

The truth was, she had been tangled for weeks in a miserable internal struggle. On one hand, she carried all her usual small vanities on her person that day: rouge, a set of glossy false black braids, and a berretta stitched all over with fine seed pearls. On the other, the fevered fervor of Fra Girolamo’s followers hung over Florence like thick, scented smoke, and every old pleasure of hers tasted dull and guilt-edged now. The Piagnoni insisted rouge, false hair, and ornamental finery were not just silly, but soul-damning — and Romola herself, young, beautiful, and utterly unadorned, led a life that was a quiet, unignorable rebuke to every hour Brigida spent fussing at her dressing table.

At fifty-five, a widow whose whole sense of joy has long rested on how she perceives her own looks, and how she believes others see her, needs a great store of small illusions to keep her spirits high. Every morning now she hesitated over her toilet: if her soul would prosper better without the paint and braids, was it really worth the effort to put them on? But the moment she set down her hand mirror and stared at her bare, sallow face, sagging cheeks, and crow’s feet no smile could erase, her courage failed. If she wore her plain grey hair loose in the new Piagnone fashion, Monna Berta — two years her junior, and long her petty rival — would burst out laughing and call her a dried-up old hag. Every woman who had not converted to the new strict piety would shrug and whisper behind her back, and every man on the street would address her as if she was his grandmother. At fifty-five, she was not so very old, after all — a little touching up was only sensible. So the rouge went on, the braids were pinned in place, the pearl berretta settled on her head, and she draped her finest necklaces over her throat, certain they made it look far smoother and more handsome than Monna Berta’s lined, unadorned neck. That very day, on the eve of the reformed Piagnone Carnival, she had fought that exact battle all over again — and her conflicting fears and longings had made her desperate to run from the youth collectors of the “Anathema” rather than face them alone.

She was not fast enough. Even before she could turn away, the white-robed boys at the back of the cluster that had swarmed Tessa had spotted her, and the ease with which they abandoned Tessa to turn after Brigida was partly because they had caught sight of a far more promising target laden with forbidden finery. One of the smallest, most earnest of the group — a cherubic ten-year-old with olive wreath perched on his curly brown hair, his whole imagination fired with the belief that every small choice for or against vanity carried eternal weight — was the first to dart forward and bar her path.

A fifteen-year-old youth holding one handle of a wide wicker basket spoke first. “Venerable mother! The blessed Lord commands you to surrender the Anathema you carry on your person. That pearl-embroidered cap, the jewels that pin your false hair in place — give them over to be sold for the poor, and cast the false hair away, for it is a lie fit only for burning. No doubt you have other jewels hidden beneath your silk mantle, too.”

“Truly, they are too heavy for you,” the youth on the other handle chimed in, who had memorized every line of Savonarola’s most fiery sermons. “They weigh more than a millstone, dragging your soul down to perdition. Would you deck yourself out in the bread stolen from starving children, and wear God’s own curse upon your head?”

“Buona madre,” the little cherub piped up in a clear, sweet soprano, “you look very ugly with that red on your cheeks and that shiny black false hair and those fine things. Only Satan likes to see you that way. Your guardian angel is sad. He wants you to rub the red off.” He held out a soft silk scarf pulled from the basket.

Monna Brigida’s hot anger curdled at once into unshakable spiritual alarm. Monna Berta and her gossiping circle were nowhere in sight, and the ring of white-robed boys, with their red crosses and unblinking, unflinching candour, felt like a crowd of holy judges. Her usual confessor, the Franciscan Fra Cristoforo of Santa Croce, who always laughed off Savonarola’s rules as silly Dominican excess, was nowhere nearby. Shaking, helpless, and certain this was a divine warning she could not ignore, she took the scarf and scrubbed the rouge clean from her cheeks.

“That is a holy beginning, madonna,” the taller youth said, and reached out to pluck one of the jewelled pins fastening her false braids to her berretta. The heavy black plait slid down across Monna Brigida’s face, breaking the last of her remaining resistance. She fumbled wildly at the rest of the fastenings, tore off her beloved crimson velvet berretta with all its rare pearl embroidery, and dumped the whole lot into the basket. There she stood, unrouged, with her grey hair pushed back from her face, all the deep lines of age that fat and paint had hidden for years now plain to see.

But the boys, their mischief heightened by the thrill of the chase, lifted the berretta up high and held it aloft for every passing citizen to gawk at, the false hair dangling from its edges. “See, venerable mother! What ugly lies you have freed yourself from! Now you look like Saint Anna herself, the mother of the Holy Virgin!”

Monna Brigida’s eyes stung with hot, hard tears that only elderly eyes shed, damp and sharp. She half made up her mind to run straight to a convent and never show her face in the world again. She was certain every onlooker was laughing at her — but perhaps, she thought dimly, it was for the best, that she would be forced to think only of Paradise from now on. A rough, guttural voice boomed over the boys’ shoulders, and a large calloused hand holding a cheap painted red cross was thrust over their heads right in front of her.

“Only four quattrini, madonna! Blessing and all included! Buy it, you’ll find it a great comfort now your wig’s gone! Deh, what do us sinners do all our lives? We make soup in a basket, end up with nothing but scum for our supper. The profit is less than the smell of a single danaro, all the money goes straight to the poor.”

Monna Brigida was fumbling for her coin purse hidden under her mantle, ready to hand over whatever he asked, when the crowd of boys parted to make way for a figure more welcome than an angel sent to loose prison bolts. “Romola! Look at me!” she wailed, holding out both her trembling hands. The white-robed troop was already drifting away, their zeal sated after their uncommonly successful haul of vanities, and they felt no need to press Brigida for more offerings.

“Dear cousin, do not distress yourself,” Romola said, her heart softening with pity even as she fought a smile at the strange, unmasked sight of her kinswoman, stripped of all the adornment she had worn for decades. She pulled the black linen drapery from her own head and wrapped it around Monna Brigida’s shoulders. “There. No one will recognise you now. We will take the narrow Via del Palagio and walk straight home.”

They hurried away, Brigida clinging tight to Romola’s hand as if it was a lifeline to steady her wobbly steps. “Oh my Romola, my dear child! What a frumpy old scarecrow I am! But I will be good, I swear it. I mean to be good, just like you!”

“Buy that cross, madonna!” the guttural voice piped up again. Bratti the peddler had trailed after them, unashamed even in front of the noble, respected Romola, determined not to lose a sure sale. “Only four quattrini, blessing and all — every penny goes to the poor!”

Monna Brigida held up one hand to stop Romola from remonstrating with the peddler, and fished out a large silver grosso worth many times the four quattrini he asked for, pressing it into his palm. “Take it, good man, and begone.”

“You are in the right of it, madonna,” Bratti said, snatching the coin quickly and shoving the cheap cross into her free hand. “I won’t bother giving you change — that would be the same as robbing you of a mass. What! We all get a little singed in this life, but you’ll come off easy: better to fall from a window than a whole roof. A good Easter to you, and a good year!”

Left alone once more, Monna Brigida huffed, but her eyes were bright with earnest resolve. “Well, Romola, if I am to be a proper Piagnone, it does not matter a bit how I look!”

“Dear cousin,” Romola said, smiling warmly at her, “you have no idea how much lovelier you look now than you ever did before. I can see your kind, good nature shining through, where all that rouge and finery used to hide your expression. Ask our Piero, any painter in the city, and he will tell you he would far rather paint your portrait now than the old painted mask. Every line on a human face is either touching or noble, unless it springs from low, petty passions. Old men like my godfather look so grand and dignified — why should old women not look the same?”

“Well, that is all very well when one gets to be sixty, my Romola,” Brigida said, a flicker of her old familiar vanity peeking through. “But I am only fifty-five, and Monna Berta, and all the rest — but no matter. I will be good, like you. Your mother was my own cousin, you know. If she had lived, she would be my exact age now. One has to either die or grow old, after all. But it does not matter a bit that I am old, if I am living right as a Piagnone.”
