Morning broke clear and brisk, and we were up with the first chorus of the island—reef-birds calling across the cliff, the river talking steadily below, and, in the nearer trees, a soft, busy murmur as the pigeons dropped in to feed. After breakfast—still warm breadfruit cakes and a little goat’s milk—there was no loitering. We set ourselves to the bird-lime.

The mixture, worked the evening before, had taken the right temper: caoutchouc oil for body, turpentine for bite, beaten and warmed until it clung with that stubborn, elastic greed that promises success. The boys brought slender rods, and I smeared each one carefully, turning it as I would turn meat before a flame, so the coating lay even and smooth. Then I sent them up among the upper branches where fruit was thickest and the birds gathered as if the trees themselves were granaries.

The multitudes astonished me—far beyond last year’s straggling remnant. Now whole boughs dipped under the weight of feeding bodies; the air was alive with wings and the low, rolling speech of pigeons. Nearby, the evergreen oaks were dropping their sweet acorns, and the ground beneath was a hushed litter of shells and husks—proof enough that at night the birds roosted overhead in great companies. Seeing it, I resolved on a torchlight raid such as the colonists of Virginia practise: light to dazzle, sticks to fell, sacks to gather.

Meanwhile the lime did its work. A pigeon would alight, confident—then the sudden frantic flutter, the feet glued fast, the wings snagging the rod as it struggled. The more it fought, the more the tenacious paste climbed over feather and claw, until, with a piteous cry, bird and stick together tumbled to the ground. Fresh lime, reset the snare, again and again. The boys learned quickly; soon their hands moved with the steady rhythm of a kitchen task—set, wait, lift, clean, smear, return—so I left them to it and went to prepare our torches.

Pinewood split fine and straight, dipped in turpentine, bound firm: torches that would flare bright and sharp, hissing like fat in a hot pan when the resin caught. While I worked, Jack came up cradling a pigeon unlike the rest, its look too familiar, too homely for these wild flocks. He could not bear to kill it. At once I saw it—one of our own European breed, the very kind we meant to keep and multiply. I took the trembling thing, and with oil and a little ashes, rubbed away the sticky filth from feet and wings, gentle as if I were cleaning a child’s hands after berry-picking. We put it into a wicker cage, and I told Jack to bring any others like it.

He did, and we secured several pairs. I felt a plain domestic triumph in that—less the thought of meat than of continuity: a cot of our own, familiar birds in a strange land, and the promise (when numbers allowed) of pigeon-pie, rich and peppered, with a crust that crackles under the knife.

By evening we set out for the oaks, carrying only long bamboo canes, our torches, and canvas sacks. The children had looked at our equipment as if we went to war with kitchen implements; but the island teaches the use of simple things. Darkness fell almost at once in the wood. I struck fire; the torches took with a rush, and their light leapt up into the branches.

Every bough was thick with roosting life—ortolans and wild pigeons, pressed close and drowsy. The sudden glare broke their peace like a cymbal crash in a quiet room. Confusion ran through the canopy: wings beating blind against leaves, bodies dropping in fright. Some fell before we even lifted a cane, and we scooped them into the sacks. Then we began to beat the branches—quick, controlled blows that shook the birds down in showers. My wife and Franz could scarcely gather fast enough. The sacks grew heavy, their canvas sides rounding with warm, feathered weight.

We turned home. At Falconhurst we secured the booty and, tired to the bone, withdrew to rest with the night sounds settling again into their natural order.

The next day belonged entirely to the harvest. There was no idle hour—plucking until the air was full of drifting down, boiling to loosen feathers and sweeten tough flesh, roasting for those we would eat at once, stewing the rest with herbs and a handful of crushed sweet acorns for a faint nutty depth. The kitchen smelled of hot fat and smoke, and under it all the clean, iron scent of blood and broth. Even the work had its music: knives tapping boards, pots bubbling, the crackle of firewood, and, beyond our walls, the sea’s long breathing.

But the morning after, we arranged a greater expedition—to Woodlands—so that the monkey invasion might not be repeated. I hoped, if we could catch the mischievous rascals in the act of ruin, to chastise them so thoroughly they would shun our farm thereafter.

My wife packed us provisions enough for several days, and stayed behind at home with Franz and Turk. I took stronger bird-lime than we had used for pigeons, short posts, plenty of string, and a supply of cocoanut shells and gourds. The buffalo carried the load and one or two of the boys besides. I rode the ass.

Near Woodlands, in a spot well screened by thickets, we made a small encampment—pitched the tent, tethered the animals. The dogs were tied up too; their very eagerness could betray us. The cottage itself stood quiet, deserted, as if the forest held its breath.

We set about our trap. Lightly driven stakes formed an irregular paling around the house; string was wound in and out among them, crossing and recrossing until the space became a labyrinth. No creature could pass without touching stake or cord. Everything—wood, twine, even the bowls—was smeared thick with our adhesive paste. Basins of bird-lime stood about, baited with rice, maize, and other tempting morsels.

Night came without interruption. We lay down under the tent, listening. In such waiting, one notices sound as one notices seasoning—too much ruins the mind, too little leaves it anxious. Here the island gave us a clean silence, broken only by leaf-stir, insect strum, and the distant, steady rush of water. I thought of the world we had left behind—its clang and hammer, its wheels and whistles—and felt, as I often did, how noise can tyrannize a spirit until it forgets the shape of peace.

At earliest dawn, the peace shattered.

A confused rustle and crackle swelled through the forest—creaking boughs, frantic chatter, shrieks that seemed to scrape the air. We armed ourselves with stout clubs and cudgels, held the dogs straining at the leash, and slipped behind the thickets where, unseen, we could command a view.

Out poured the troop—an unruly flood of monkeys, scrambling and springing from tree to tree, racing and tumbling into the open towards the house. They were drawn at once to the bowls and jars. They seemed innumerable, their bodies flashing between shadow and sun, too quick and confused to count.

Some dashed straight through the palings, some scrambled up to the roof and began tugging at pegs to force entrance. But as they swarmed over the place, each in turn touched our smeared cords and stakes—head, paws, back, breast—until the bird-lime marked them like a curse. The predicament deepened with every frantic motion.

Some sat and tried to clean themselves with absurd, earnest gestures, only to spread the paste further. Others became entangled in stake and cord, dragging the whole contrivance behind them, the very picture of bewildered despair. Some tried to help one another and stuck together; the more they pulled and kicked, the worse their plight became.

Many seized the gourds and cocoanut shells baited with grain; their paws were caught, and the shells clattered as they bolted—an ugly, hollow percussion, like pots kicked through a pantry in the dark. Most ridiculous—and, in its way, most pitiable—was an old fellow who found a calabash of palm wine. He drank greedily, and the shell stuck to his forehead and whiskers like a mask, blinding him. He staggered and capered wildly, striking at empty air, the troop shrieking and scattering around him.

Then panic spread. Some fled toward the trees—only to find bird-lime waiting there too. They hung from branches, fixed fast, struggling and shrieking, their cries cutting through the clean morning like a saw.

At that point I loosed the three dogs, whose impatience had been almost uncontrollable. They rushed in as if burning with zeal to execute justice. We were forced to do our part too—clubs rising and falling—until the din of howling, yelping, barking, and screaming in every tone of rage and pain collapsed into an awful silence.

When it was over, the place looked like a battlefield. At least forty apes lay mangled and dead. The boys grew sad and downhearted, and I shared the heaviness with them; necessity is a hard master, and even just punishment leaves a bitter taste. I turned them at once to active work: we removed and buried the slain, and then burned the stakes, cordage, bowls—everything connected with our deadly stratagem—so that the very instruments of that hour would not linger to sour our minds.

Afterward we restored order to the dismantled cottage, and went seeking the scattered sheep, goats, and poultry. By degrees we gathered them, quieted them, and hoped to settle them again in their yards and sheds.

While we worked, we heard repeatedly the heavy thud of something dropping from a tree. We looked and found three splendid birds caught on limed sticks we had placed loosely among the branches. Two were a variety of Blue Molucca pigeon; the third, with resplendent green, bronze, and steely-blue plumage, I took to be the Nicobar pigeon. At once my mind leapt to domestic possibilities: not merely their beauty, but their future as breeding stock, as living wealth, as new tenants near our cave.

“First tenants, father!” Fritz said. “Do you expect to catch more like these?”

“Not exactly catch them,” I replied. “I mean to practise a secret art. Much can be done by magic, Fritz!”

I gave no more explanation.

In a few days Woodlands was set right again—comfortable, ordered—and we returned without further adventure to Falconhurst, where we were welcomed as if we had come back from a campaign.

All agreed we must go at once to Tentholm and make the pigeon-house in the rock. Other matters required attention there too, so we arranged for a prolonged stay.

My plan was to hollow out an ample space in the cliff facing Jackal River, close to our rocky home, then fit it with partitions, perches, nesting-places, and a broad platform before the openings. A wooden front would close it, with entrance-holes and sliding shutters.

After weeks of labour we judged it ready. I set the other children to work at some distance, then summoned Fritz.

“Now, my faithful assistant,” I said, “it is time to conjure the new colonists to their settlement here.” He stared, and I laughed. “A pigeon-dealer’s trick, nothing more. We shall compound a food from soft clay, aniseed, and salt—something our birds will relish. The scent will bring others to share it.”

“I can get those,” Fritz said at once.

“I shall want oil of aniseed besides,” I told him, “to rub about the pigeon-holes, so that the birds brush it as they pass in and out, and their feathers carry the perfume to the wild ones. We’ll make it by pounding aniseed. Bring me the mortar and some oil.”

When the oil was strongly impregnated, I strained it through cloth, pressing it hard. The scent rose sharp and sweet—like a spice chest opened in a quiet room—and I knew it would cling for days. We smeared the entrances, prepared the clay mixture, and installed our pigeons in the new residence, closing the slides.

By then, our European birds were friendly with the three beautiful strangers. When the other boys came home and scrambled up the ladder to peep through a little pane of glass I had fixed, they saw them contentedly picking grain and pecking at the “magic food,” as Fritz called it—though he kept my “secret arts” to himself.

Early on the third morning I roused Fritz and had him ascend the rope ladder, arrange a cord on the sliding door so it could be opened or shut from below, and pour fresh aniseed oil about the entrance. Then we woke the rest of the family and told them, if they made haste, they might see me let the pigeons fly.

Everyone gathered before the dove-cot as if for a festival. I waved a wand with mock solemnity, muttered a ridiculous incantation, and gave Fritz a sign. He drew up the sliding panel.

Pretty heads appeared. Soft eyes blinked into the morning. They withdrew, returned, ventured out upon the platform—“the verandah,” little Franz called it. Then, as if startled by their own freedom, the whole party took wing. Their flight made that shrill, whizzing sound peculiar to pigeons; they circled above us, climbed higher and higher, then darted out of sight.

We stood gazing after them. Presently they reappeared and settled quietly on the dove-cot, and we congratulated ourselves—until the three blue pigeons, the noble foreigners for whom chiefly I had built the house, sprang up and rose in wide circles, winging straight towards Falconhurst. Their departure had a resolute air that made my heart sink; I feared them lost for ever.

We tried to console ourselves by feeding and petting the four that remained, but disappointment sat with us the whole day, and the dove-cot became the centre of every glance and errand.

Nothing more was seen until about the middle of the next day. We were working inside the cavern when Jack burst in, flushed and breathless.

“He is there! He is come! He really is!”

“Who? Who is there?” we cried.

“The blue pigeon, to be sure! Hurrah! Hurrah!”

Ernest scoffed that Jack wished to trick us, but I rose at once. “Why should it be nonsense? I fully believe we shall see them all soon!”

We ran out. There he stood—the handsome blue fellow—and he was not alone. He was billing and cooing to a mate, a stranger of his own breed, bowing and sidling with comical elegance, popping in and out of the door as if to say, See how safe, see how fine, until the shy little lady yielded and stepped daintily within.

“Now shut the door! Pull the cord!” the boys shouted, rushing at the string.

“Stop!” I cried. “Let it alone. Don’t frighten the little darlings. Besides—the others will be coming. Would you shut the door in their faces?”

“Here they come!” Fritz exclaimed, his keen eye catching movement far off.

To our delight the second blue pigeon arrived, also with a mate. After a pretty scene of coaxing and feigned reluctance, he led her in. The third and handsomest was last. Perhaps he had greater difficulty in finding a bride fit for his splendour; but by then we spoke of “Mr. and Mrs. Nicobar” as if the matter were settled by law.

Late in the day, Franz and his mother went out to provide for supper, but the child returned almost at once, crying that we must hasten to the dove-cot to see something beautiful. We rushed out and found the third stranger returned with a lovely mate. Encouraged by the others already housed, they soon made themselves at home.

Nest-building began quickly. Among the gathered materials I noticed a long grey moss or lichen, hanging in beard-like tufts from old trees, and I thought of that West Indian growth used instead of horse-hair for stuffing mattresses. The moment my wife heard of it, her mind—always turning household needs into plans—leapt ahead. If we collected enough, she would clean and sort it; there would be bolsters, pillows, saddle-pads, cushions. Even here, among cliffs and wild birds, her imagination returned to the art of comfort: a home is built as much by what you can rest your head upon as by what you can shelter under.

We had also to thank the pigeons for nutmegs, which we planted carefully in our orchard, and so the days went on without event—until Jack, as usual, got himself into a scrape that set all hearts beating.

He had gone early on one of his private expeditions—his habit, that he might surprise us with some new acquisition. This time he returned in miserable plight: covered with mud and green slime, a great bundle of Spanish canes on his back muddy and green like himself, one shoe missing, and misery on his face so plain we could not laugh, though he looked a perfect caricature of disaster.

“My dear boy! What has happened? Where have you been?”

“Only in the swamp behind the powder magazine, father,” he said, near tears. “I went to get reeds for my wickerwork—baskets and hen-coops—and I saw such beauties farther in, much finer than those at the edge. I tried to get to them. I jumped from one firm spot to another till I slipped and sank over my ankles. I tried to go on—the reeds were close—but I went deeper and deeper till I was above the knees in thick soft mud, and there I stuck!

“I screamed and shouted. Nobody came. I was in a regular fright. At last who should appear but my faithful Fangs! He knew my voice and came right over the swamp, but all he could do was help me make a row; I wonder you didn’t hear us. The very rocks rang, but nothing came of it, so at last I thought of an expedient. I cut down all the reeds I could reach around me and bound them into this bundle. That made a firm place to lean on while I worked and kicked to free my legs. After much struggling I managed to get astride on the reeds.

“There I sat above the slime, while Fangs ran yelping between me and the bank, surprised I didn’t follow. Then I thought of catching his tail. He dragged and pulled, and I sprawled and crawled and waded—sometimes on my reeds like a raft, sometimes lugging them—till we got back to firm ground. But I had a near squeak for it.”

“A fortunate escape indeed,” I said, and thanked God from my heart. “Fangs has acted the hero, and you have shown presence of mind. Now go to your mother and get rid of those slimy traces. But you have brought splendid canes—exactly what I want for a new scheme.”

For I meant to construct a loom for my wife. I knew she understood weaving, and I had long wanted to give her the means to make cloth rather than only mend and patch. I chose two strong reeds, split them carefully, then bound them back together so that when dry they would be straight and equal for a frame. Smaller reeds we cut and sharpened for the teeth of the comb. The boys did this merrily without suspecting the purpose, calling them “father’s monster toothpicks,” and laughing at the idea of anyone with a mouth big enough to use such things.

As the parts were made ready and put together, I kept the secret from my wife. To the children’s questions I replied with mock mystery, “Oh, it is an outlandish sort of musical instrument; mother will know how to play upon it.”

When at last I presented it, her joy was reward enough—joy, and the sight of her hands moving with skill and familiarity over the new contrivance, while the children watched as if she truly were playing an instrument. The loom’s soft clack and steady beat became part of our acoustic world—an honest domestic sound, neither shriek nor clang, but a rhythm that promised warmth for our bodies and continuity for our ways.

About this time a beautiful little foal, son of the onager, was added to our small stud. He promised to grow strong and tractable, and we named him Swift, intending to train him for my riding.

Our cave arrangements were now well advanced, and I applied myself to contriving an aqueduct, for it was a long and troublesome journey to fetch water from Jackal River, especially on washing days. I wanted it finished before the rainy season set in, so I began at once. Hollow bamboo pipes served well, and a large cask became the reservoir. The supply proved good; the comfort of having fresh water close at hand was so great that my wife declared she was as pleased with our engineering as if we had given her a marble basin with dolphins and mermaids—though she valued far more the simple sound of water near her hands.

We pressed forward all work connected with stores for the winter rains. Roots, fruits, grains—potatoes, rice, guavas, sweet acorns, pine-cones—came load after load to the cavern. My wife’s needle never rested, for the demand for sacks and bags was incessant. Casks and barrels of every size were pressed into service, until at last even the raft was knocked to pieces so its tubs might do duty in the store-rooms.

The weather grew unsettled. Heavy clouds banked on the horizon. Storms swept over us with gusting wind, thunder, lightning, and sudden torrents. The sea heaved in frequent commotion; groundswells drove masses of water hissing and foaming against the cliffs. Everything in nature seemed to join in a solemn overture to the grand work of the year—the long season of rain. It was near the beginning of June, and we had twelve weeks of bad weather before us.

We moved some of the animals to the salt-cave with us. The cow, the ass, Lightfoot, Storm, and the dogs were necessary; Knips, Fangs, and the eagle promised amusement in the long evenings when the rain would drum and the wind would prowl round our rock. The boys would ride often to Falconhurst to see all was in order and fetch what we needed.

Much remained to be done to make the cave truly comfortable, and now that we must live indoors, comfort mattered more. The inner darkness annoyed me. After thinking, I called Jack to help. We found a tall, strong bamboo that reached to the vaulted roof. We planted it firmly in the earthen floor, wedging it fast.

Jack climbed it cleverly with hammer and chisel, enlarged a crevice in the roof, and fixed a pulley. When he descended, I drew up a large ship’s lantern well supplied with oil. With its four wicks it gave a fair light, and the cave’s darkness softened into a usable dusk where faces could be seen and work could be done.

Then we set to arranging rooms. Ernest and Franz undertook the library, fixing shelves and setting the books in order—careful as monks preserving a fragile inheritance. Jack and his mother took the sitting-room and kitchen. Fritz and I, better fitted for heavy work, arranged the workshops: the carpenter’s bench, the turning lathe, the great chest of tools placed where hands could find them even when the rain raged outside and the sea threw its endless roar against the rock.
