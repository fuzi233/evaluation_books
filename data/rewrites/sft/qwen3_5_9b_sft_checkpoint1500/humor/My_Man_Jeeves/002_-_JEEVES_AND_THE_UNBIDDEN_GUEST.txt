"It is a curious irony of fate," I remarked, nursing a cup of tea that had grown tepid, "that destiny invariably chooses the precise moment when a chap feels most securely perched upon his high horse to drop a lead pipe behind him. And bless my soul, the man speaks true. Just yesterday, I was floating on a cloud of invincibility, buoyed by a magnificent victory over Jeeves, and today, disaster strikes with the subtlety of a thunderclap."

Let me backtrack a moment to explain the state of affairs. It had been one of those gloriously perfect mornings. I had emerged from a freezing shower feeling as rejuvenated as a newborn colt, specifically pumped up because the previous evening I had finally asserted my independence. For months, I had been treated like a domestic serf by Jeeves. While I could stomach him dictating the color of my socks—after all, his taste in footwear is impeccable, unlike his taste in hats—I drew the line at the Country Gentleman cap versus the Longacre. The Longacre, worn by the legendary John Drew, was the enemy; the Country Gentleman, worn by some other actor, was my beloved. After a dramatic scene involving tears and the assertion of masculine dignity, I purchased the Country Gentleman. I was a free man, a sovereign ruler of my own attire, feeling utterly manly.

And then, the universe decided to intervene.

I was in the bathroom, rubbing my spine with a coarse towel and humming a tuneless melody, when a gentle tap echoed at the door. I froze, opened it a mere fraction, and shouted, "What ho without there!"

"Jeeves," I commanded, "pull yourself together. Bar practical jokes before breakfast. You know perfectly well there is no one waiting in the sitting-room. It is barely ten o'clock!"

"I gathered from Her Ladyship, sir," Jeeves replied, his voice smooth as silk, "that Lady Malvern has landed from an ocean liner at an early hour."

"Lady Malvern?" I stammered. "Who on earth is Lady Malvern?"

"She is accompanied by a Lord Pershore, sir. I suspect he is her son."

"Oh, very well," I muttered, turning away. "Put out some rich raiment, and I shall dress."

"While you are dressing, sir," Jeeves interjected, "our heather-mixture lounge is in readiness."

"Aha!" I cried, climbing into my shirt and reaching for my studs. "I've placed her, Jeeves! She's a pal of my Aunt Agatha. I met her at lunch before I left London. A vicious specimen, writes books on social conditions in India..."

"Not that tie, sir!" Jeeves interrupted, his voice taking on a sharp edge.

"Eh?"

"Not that tie with the heather-mixture lounge, sir!"

The blood drained from my face. I had thought I had quelled him the night before. "What's wrong with it?" I demanded, standing firm. "It's a cheerful pink! Unsuitable? Nonsense! This is the tie I wear!"

"Too ornate, sir."

"Nonsense!"

"Unsuitable, sir."

"Jeeves, this is the tie I wear!"

"Very good, sir."

The silence that followed was deafening. I realized with a sinking heart that the man was wounded. He was trying to get his own back. I tied the tie, buttoned my coat, and marched into the sitting-room, prepared for battle.

"Halloa! Halloa!" I announced, waving my arms. "What?"

"Ah! How do you do, Mr. Wooster? You have never met my son, Wilmot, I think? Motty, darling, this is Mr. Wooster."

Lady Malvern was a force of nature. She was tall, broad, and possessed a vitality that seemed to vibrate off her in waves. She filled my largest armchair as if it had been custom-built around her hips. With bright, bulging eyes and a mane of yellow hair, she radiated an overwhelming, numbing presence. She made me feel ten years old again, dressed in my Sunday best, standing in awe of a queen. Beside her sat Motty, her son. He was twenty-three, tall, thin, and meek-looking, with the same yellow hair plastered down and parted in the middle. His eyes were dull grey with pink rims, and his chin gave up the struggle halfway down. He looked like a sheep that had lost its flock.

"Awfully glad to see you," I stammered. "So you've popped over, eh? Making a long stay in America?"

"About a month," Motty whispered, sucking the knob of his walking stick. "Your aunt gave me your address and told me to be sure and call on you."

"Aunt Agatha?" I breathed. "She's beginning to come round?"

"Yes, sir?" Motty murmured, resuming his stick-sucking.

Suddenly, Lady Malvern launched into a speech about my duties. "I want you to put dear Motty up for a little while," she declared, pouring out the request like warm honey. "Have him to live with you while I am away."

I stared at her, bewildered. "Put him up? For my clubs?"

"No, no!" she insisted. "Darling Motty is essentially a home bird... I should not like him to belong to clubs. I mean put him up here. Live with you."

I looked at Motty. He was blinking at the wall, mouth nuzzling the stick. The thought of housing this creature indefinitely terrified me. "Shot isn't on the board at any price," I muttered internally. "First sign he tries to nestle, I'll yell for the police."

"But I shall have to pick him up on my return," Lady Malvern continued, oblivious to the horror she was conjuring. "He gets so sick when he travels by train. He is a vegetarian and a teetotaller and is devoted to reading. Give him a nice book and he will be quite contented."

With a wave of her hand, she and Motty departed, promising to return early in the afternoon. I howled for Jeeves.

"What about it?" I cried. "You heard it all, didn't you? That pill is coming to stay here."

"Pill, sir?"

"The excrescence!"

"I beg your pardon, sir?"

I glared at Jeeves. He looked guilty. Was he deliberately trying to give me the pip? Then I understood. The man was still upset about the tie. He was seeking revenge.

"Lord Pershore will be staying here from tonight, Jeeves," I said coldly.

"Very good, sir. Breakfast is ready, sir."

I could have sobbed into my bacon and eggs. There was no sympathy to be found. I almost told him to destroy the hat and tie if he didn't like them, but pride held me back. I was not going to let Jeeves treat me like a bally one-man chain-gang!

By midday, Motty's luggage arrived, followed by a massive parcel of books. It looked like enough literature to keep a scholar busy for a decade. Feeling a trifle cheered, I donned my Country Gentleman hat, twisted the pink tie, and escaped to lunch with some friends at a neighboring hostelry. The afternoon passed happily; by dinner time, I had almost forgotten the blight of Motty.

However, the next evening, the nightmare resumed. Motty was nowhere to be seen, and the parcel of books remained untouched. Jeeves entered with the nightly whisky-and-soda, his demeanor still frosty.

"Lord Pershore gone to bed, Jeeves?" I asked with reserved hauteur.

"No, sir. His lordship has not yet returned."

"Not returned? What do you mean?"

"His lordship came in shortly after six-thirty, dressed, and went out again."

Just then, a scrabbling noise erupted outside the front door, followed by a heavy thud. "Better go and see what that is, Jeeves," I urged.

"He is lying on the mat, sir," Jeeves reported, returning moments later. "If you would not mind stepping this way, sir, I think we might be able to carry him in."

I rushed to the door and found Motty huddled on the floor, moaning. "He's had some sort of fit," I said, inspecting him closely. "Jeeves! Someone's been feeding him meat!"

"He is a vegetarian, you know," Jeeves reminded me gently. "Call up a doctor!"

"I hardly think it will be necessary, sir," Jeeves countered smoothly. "If you would take his lordship's legs, while I—"

"Great Scot, Jeeves! You don't think—he can't be—"

"I am inclined to think so, sir."

And there it was. Motty was under the influence. The shock was profound. "You never can tell, Jeeves!" I exclaimed. "Remove the eye of authority and where are you?"

"Precisely, sir."
