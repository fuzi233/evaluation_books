Sally had grand plans for her return to New York. She envisioned herself checking into the St. Regis, sipping champagne from crystal flutes while gazing at a view that cost more than most people’s annual salaries, and generally living like a character in a magazine ad for luxury travel. After all, she’d just come back from abroad with enough money to buy a small island (or at least a very nice apartment), so why not start by pretending she was royalty?

But then, as she stood on the dock giving instructions to the taxi driver—“Take me to the St. Regis, please”—something stopped her cold. It wasn’t the wind, nor the smell of saltwater mixed with diesel fumes. It was the sudden realization that this whole idea felt suspiciously *Fillmorian*. Like something out of a melodrama starring a man who thinks he’s too good for the world but actually just hasn’t learned how to use a fork properly.

No, Sally decided. She wouldn’t abandon Mrs. Meecher’s boarding house quite yet. Not until she’d found a proper place to call home. And besides, it would be rude—nay, *morally questionable*—to sever ties with the only place that had ever treated her like family just because she could now afford a bathroom with two sinks.

So off she went, back to the familiar streets, where even the pigeons seemed to recognize her. The morning was perfect: crisp air, golden sunlight, and the kind of blue sky that makes you believe in miracles—or at least in good coffee. Streetcars were full of cheerful commuters, police officers waved like they were hosting a parade, and the street cleaners—dressed in white, looking like they’d wandered out of a silent film—went about their duties with quiet dignity. It was almost as if the city itself knew Sally was back and wanted to throw her a welcome-home party without any awkward speeches.

Then came Mrs. Meecher.

She greeted Sally with warm hugs and even warmer gossip, which is always dangerous when delivered by someone who once played “Florodora” and still believes she’s part of the theater elite. After confirming Sally wanted her old room, Mrs. Meecher dropped the bombshell:

“Gerald Foster left this morning.”

“Where?” Sally asked, already feeling the first twinge of disappointment.

“Detroit. Miss Doland too. They’re trying out his play Monday. Rehearsing since you left.”

Sally blinked. Her heart sank slightly. But then she remembered: it was a beautiful day. The sun was shining. The city was alive. And she hadn’t seen Gerald in weeks—so maybe this was just a delay, not a disaster.

“Oh,” she said, forcing cheerfulness. “Is Elsa in the cast?”

“Sure. And very good, I hear. Mr. Faucitt saw a rehearsal. Said she was fine. He’s not easy to please, you know.”

That name brought a new concern. “How is Mr. Faucitt?”

Mrs. Meecher sighed dramatically. “Poor old gentleman. Headache last night. Up early this morning, didn’t look well. Might be Spanish flu. Lots of people dying, if you believe what you read in the papers.”

Sally gasped. “You don’t think—?”

“Well, he ain’t turned black yet,” Mrs. Meecher admitted, clearly disappointed. “They say they turn black. If you believe what you see in the papers. Of course, that might come later.” She added this with the confidence of someone who has never missed a single episode of *The Daily Drama*.

Still, Sally couldn’t help but worry. Mr. Faucitt had been a fixture in the boarding house—kind, witty, occasionally dramatic—and now he was sick. And worse: Toto was with him.

Toto was not a dog. Toto was a tiny, woolly menace with a voice like a squeaky toy possessed by a demon. He barked at shadows, chewed books, and once tried to eat Mrs. Meecher’s slippers during dinner. To anyone else, having Toto in the room would be torture. To Mrs. Meecher, it was therapeutic.

“I must go up and see him,” Sally declared.

“Sure,” said Mrs. Meecher, proud. “You know his room. You can hear Toto talking to him now. He wants a cracker. Toto likes a cracker after breakfast.”

Sally nodded and climbed the stairs, clutching a cracker like a peace offering. When she entered, Mr. Faucitt’s eyes lit up—not from joy, but from sheer relief that someone had finally arrived who understood the concept of silence.

“Sally!” he whispered, weakly.

“One moment. Here, Toto!” Sally called.

Toto, momentarily distracted by food, leapt off the bed and stared at the cracker with intense suspicion. But Sally was clever. She lured him into the hallway, tossed the cracker down, and slipped inside, shutting the door just as Toto began yapping furiously—probably thanking her or cursing her, it was hard to tell.

Mr. Faucitt took a deep breath. “Sally, you come, as ever, as an angel of mercy. Mrs. Meecher means well—but she insists on bringing that infernal pup into my room. Each yap feels like a hammer hitting tin. You shine like a good deed in a naughty world. When did you get back?”

“I’ve only just arrived in my hired barouche from the pier.”

“And you came straight here? I’m grateful. Truly.”

“Of course I did. Do you suppose I’d say ‘Oh, really? That’s interesting’ and carry on discussing the weather? Well, what do you mean by it? Frightening everyone. Poor old darling, do you feel very bad?”

“One thousand mice are nibbling my spine,” he said. “And I need cooling refreshment constantly. But your presence is a tonic. Tell me—how was foreign travel?”

“Wonderful. I had the time of my life.”

“Did you visit England?”

“Only passing through.”

“How did it look?”

“Moist. Very moist.”

Mr. Faucitt chuckled. “I confess, though I love America, there are times I miss London—the cozy brown mist, the muddy pavements oozing like sweat, the distant glow of Bodega lamps like harbor lights. Not that I favor Bodega over others. Rule’s and Short’s were equally pleasant. You missed something, Sally.”

“I know I did—pneumonia.”

He shook his head. “Prejudiced. You’d have loved it if you’d braved its gloom. Where did you spend your holiday?”

“Part in Paris, part by the sea. It was glorious. I don’t think I’d have come back if I hadn’t had to.”

“You’re back to see us again?”

“And to be at the opening of Mr. Foster’s play. Mrs. Meecher said you went to a rehearsal.”

“I attended a dog-fight,” Mr. Faucitt said sternly. “Which I was told was a rehearsal. There is no rehearsing nowadays.”

“Oh dear! Was it that bad?”

“The play is excellent. It has fat. But the acting—”

“Mrs. Meecher said you praised Elsa.”

“She has great potential. Reminds me of Matilda Devine. Seeds of greatness. But she’s wasted on a minor role. Only one part matters—the one ruined by Miss Mabel Hobson.”

“Murdered?” Sally whispered.

“Murdered,” Mr. Faucitt confirmed. “She has the face of an angel and the histrionic ability of Mrs. Meecher’s Friday suet pudding. I’ve seen many Lady Friends turned stars, but Miss Hobson eclipses them all. In ’94, a plutocrat tried to launch his beloved onto the stage. I supported her. At the first rehearsal, I said to Arthur Moseby—dead now—‘Arthur, I give it two weeks.’ He replied, ‘Max, you’re an optimist. One consecutive night.’ We bet half a crown. He won. We opened in Wigan, she got the bird, and the show closed next day. Same thing happened today.”

“Oh, poor Ger—poor Mr. Foster!”

“I do not share your sympathy,” Mr. Faucitt said firmly. “I’ve known him. A selfish, self-centered young man. Reminds me of Billy Fothergill—did I ever tell you about Billy and the amateur who—?”

Sally tuned out. The criticism stung. Gerald meant everything to her. But seeing Mr. Faucitt’s pale, tired face, she softened. He didn’t mean harm. He just didn’t know.

She changed the subject. “Have you seen Fillmore?”

“Curiously enough, yes. On Broadway. He seemed different—less stiff, less aloof. Almost… human. He was glad to see me. Most cordial.”

Sally smiled. So her lecture at the party had worked. Maybe Fillmore was changing.

But then Mr. Faucitt added, “I suspect the softening influence may be his fiancée.”

“What? Fillmore engaged?”

“Didn’t he write? Probably waiting to tell you. Yes—he’s betrothed. Miss Winch. In the profession. Introduced me. Charming young woman.”

Sally frowned. “She can’t be. Fillmore wouldn’t marry someone like that. Was her hair crimson?”

“Brown, I think.”

“Very loud? Overdressed?”

“No. Neat. Quiet.”

“That’s impossible,” Sally said firmly. “She can’t have been like that. I’ll have to investigate. It’s hard enough that I can’t go away for a few weeks without everyone getting sick or my brother marrying a vampire.”

A knock. Mrs. Meecher entered, followed by a small man with glasses and a black bag.

“The doctor, Mr. Faucitt.”

Mrs. Meecher eyed the patient closely. “I’ve been telling him you’ve got the Spanish flu. Two more deaths today, if you believe what you read…”

The doctor smiled. “Would you mind fetching me a small glass of water?”

“Why, sure.”

“Not large. Just let the tap run a moment. Don’t spill it coming up. I always ask ladies to bring water—it keeps them busy, gets them out of the way, and they think I’m doing a magic trick. As a matter of fact, I’m going to drink it. Now, let’s examine you.”

After a quick check, the doctor sighed. “Your diagnosis was correct. It’s Spanish influenza. Not severe, but you need rest. Warmth. And nursing. Is this young lady a nurse?”

“No, no, merely—”

“Of course I’m a nurse,” Sally said decisively. “It isn’t difficult, is it, doctor? I know nurses smooth pillows. I can do that. Anything else?”

“Their main job,” said the doctor, “is to keep that excellent, garrulous woman who just left from entering. Also, aim straight with a book or old shoe if that little woolly dog downstairs tries to break in. If you can handle that, I leave the case in your hands.”

“But Sally,” Mr. Faucitt protested, “you mustn’t waste your time on me.”

“There’s nothing I’d rather do,” she said. “I’ll just send a wire, and then I’ll be right back.”

Five minutes later, Sally sat in a Western Union office, typing a message to Gerald:

She pressed send, smiled, and walked out into the sunshine—ready to save a play, a friendship, and possibly the entire future of American theater.
