In the annals of my career—a profession dedicated entirely to prying open the locked doors of other people's lives—I can honestly say that the affair of George Lattaker at Monte Carlo ranks among the most absurd episodes I've ever stumbled into. Naturally, I won't bore you with the gritty details unless you insist, but you really ought to hear the setup.

We had arrived in Monte Carlo aboard the *Circe*, a yacht owned by an old sporting gent named Marshall. Our passenger list included myself, my man Voules, Mrs. Vanderley, her daughter Stella, Mrs. Vanderley's maid Pilbeam, and George.

George was a dear old friend. In fact, I'd personally engineered his inclusion in the party. See, George was scheduled to meet his Uncle Augustus in a few days. Having just hit his twenty-fifth birthday, George was entitled to a legacy left to him by an aunt who'd passed away when he was a toddler. While George technically had an allowance, a steady income is just... an income. A lump sum of cash, however, is a proper pile of "goblins" (money). His uncle was already in Monte Carlo and had promised to visit London, but I figured a smarter move was for George to come to the uncle instead. One trip, two problems solved: settle the estate and enjoy a holiday. So, George tagged along. At the time everything went wrong, we were anchored in Monaco Harbor, waiting for Uncle Augustus to arrive the next day.

Looking back, my involvement began at seven o'clock in the morning. I was woken from a dreamless sleep by the sound of a terrible scuffle outside my cabin door. The soundtrack consisted of a female voice sobbing, "Oh, Harold!" and a male voice, "raised in anger," which I eventually identified as Voules's. I barely recognized it. Officially, Voules speaks like a marble statue carved by a stickler for grammar. Privately, however, he seems to relax enough to actually use his vocal cords. Hearing that level of domestic drama at seven AM was too much.

"Voules!" I bellowed.

The fighting stopped abruptly. Silence followed, then distant sniffles, and finally, a polite knock. Voules entered wearing that specific expression I pay him for—the "my lord, the carriage waits" look. You wouldn't guess he possessed a single drop of human emotion.

"Voules," I said, "do you think I'm the Queen of the May? You've called me early enough. It's only seven."

"I understood you to summon me, sir."

"I summoned you to explain why you were making that infernal racket outside."

"I owe you an apology, sir. In the heat of the moment, I raised my voice."

"It's a miracle you didn't tear the roof off the ship. Who was with you?"

"Miss Pilbeam, sir. Mrs. Vanderley's maid."

"What was the trouble about?"

"I was breaking our engagement, sir."

I gaped. One doesn't usually associate Voules with romantic entanglements. Realizing I had no right to pry into his private heartbreaks, I quickly pivoted.

"I think I'll get up," I said.

"Yes, sir."

"I can't wait to eat with the rest. Can you bring me breakfast immediately?"

"Yes, sir."

So, I ate alone and went on deck to smoke. It was a beautiful morning: blue sea, glittering casino, cloudless sky. Soon, the others trickled up. Stella Vanderley was among the first. She looked pale and tired. "Didn't sleep well," she explained. Fair enough; without eight hours, where are you?

"Seen George?" I asked.

I noticed the name seemed to freeze her slightly. Strange, considering she and George had been inseparable throughout the voyage. I was almost expecting him to appear, slip his hand in mine, and whisper, "I've done it, old scout; she loves me!"

"I have not seen Mr. Lattaker," she replied.

I dropped the subject. George's reputation was clearly in the toilet that morning.

Minutes later, the morning newspapers arrived. Mrs. Vanderley opened hers and screamed. "The poor, dear Prince!" she exclaimed. "What a shocking thing!" added old Marshall. "I knew him in Vienna," Mrs. Vanderley continued. "He waltzed divinely."

I grabbed my paper and saw the headline. Late the previous night, His Serene Highness the Prince of Saxburg-Leignitz (I always wondered why they add "Serene") had been brutally attacked in a dark alley on his way back from the casino. Apparently, he'd gotten into the habit of walking without an escort, allowing a roughneck to ambush and slug him with great vigor. The prince was found unconscious in the street by a passerby and taken back to his yacht, where he remained unresponsive.

"This is going to ruin someone's day," I muttered. "What do you get for punching a Serene Highness? Will they catch the guy?"

"'Later,'" read old Marshall. "'The pedestrian who discovered His Serene Highness proves to be Mr. Denman Sturgis, the eminent private investigator. Mr. Sturgis has offered his services to the police and is understood to possess a most important clue.' That's the same bloke who handled the Chicago kidnapping case. If anyone can track this guy down, it's him."

Five minutes later, just as everyone was heading for breakfast, a boat pulled up alongside. A tall, thin man climbed the gangway, scanned the group, and zeroed in on old Marshall.

"Good morning," he said. "I believe you have a Mr. Lattaker on board—Mr. George Lattaker?"

"Yes," said Marshall. "He's down below. Want to see him? Whom shall I say?"

"He wouldn't know my name. I'd like to see him briefly regarding urgent business."

"Take a seat. He'll be up shortly. Reggie, my boy, go fetch him."

I headed down to George's cabin.

"George, old man!" I shouted.

No answer. I pushed the door open. The room was empty. Worse yet, the bunk hadn't been slept in. I've rarely been more surprised. I rushed back onto deck.

"He isn't there," I announced.

"Not there!" cried old Marshall. "Where is he? Maybe he went for a walk ashore. He'll be back soon for breakfast. Better wait. Have you eaten? No? Join us?"

The stranger agreed, and just then the gong rang, sending everyone below and leaving me alone on deck.

I sat smoking, thinking, smoking some more, when I heard a hoarse whisper calling my name. Looking over my shoulder, there he was at the top of the gangway, dressed in evening wear, dusty to the eyebrows, and hatless. It was dear old George.

"Great Scot!" I cried.

"'Sh!' he whispered. 'Anyone about?'

"They're all down at breakfast."

He sighed with relief, sank into my chair, and closed his eyes. I looked at him with genuine pity. The poor old boy looked like a wreck.

"I say!" I touched his shoulder.

He leaped out of the chair with a stifled yell.

"Did you do that? Why? What's the point? How do you expect to be popular if you touch people on the shoulder? My nerves are sticking out a yard today, Reggie!"

"Yes, old boy?"

"I committed a murder last night."

"What?"

"It's the sort of thing that happens to anyone. As soon as Stella Vanderley broke off our engagement, I—"

"Broke off your engagement? How long were you engaged?"

"About two minutes. Maybe less. I didn't have a stopwatch. I proposed to her at ten last night in the saloon. She said yes. I was about to kiss her when we heard someone approaching. Out came that infernal what's-her-name—Mrs. Vanderley's maid, Pilbeam. Ever been accepted by the girl you love, Reggie?"

"Never. Refused dozens of times..."

"Then you don't understand. I was off my head with joy. I didn't know what I was doing. I just felt compelled to kiss the nearest available object. Couldn't wait. Might have been the ship's cat. It wasn't. It was Pilbeam."

"You kissed her?"

"I kissed her. Right then, the saloon door flew open and Stella walked out."

"Great Scott!"

"Exactly what I said. It flashed across me that to Stella, poor soul, unaware of the context, this might seem peculiar. It did. She ended the engagement, and I grabbed a dinghy and rowed away. I was mad. I didn't care what happened to me. I just wanted to forget. I went ashore. I—It's possible I drowned my sorrows a bit. Anyway, I don't remember anything except a fierce fight in a dark street, someone falling, me falling, and me running for my life. I woke up this morning in the Casino gardens. I've lost my hat."

I dove for the newspaper.

"Read," I commanded. "It's all there."

He read.

"Good heavens!" he gasped.

"You didn't hurt His Serene Nibs, did you?"

"Reggie, this is dreadful."

"Cheer up. They say he'll recover."
