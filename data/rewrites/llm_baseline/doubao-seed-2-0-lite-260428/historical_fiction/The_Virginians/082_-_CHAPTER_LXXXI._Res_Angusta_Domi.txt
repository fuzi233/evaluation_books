Seated in my easy-chair in the soft glow of a winter’s evening, I have often looked back on that rash, headlong jump into matrimony that I persuaded my dear partner to take with me, when we were both scarcely more than lanky, foolish teenagers. As a man, a father, with a proper sense of how critical a well-cooked mutton chop is to household peace, and how dangerous it is to fall behind on the baker’s bill—with a brood of impulsive children clustering around us who threaten to run off for Scotland at any moment, citing the example of their own father and mother as excuse for their impertinence—I know full well I ought to tread softly when narrating this early stretch of George Warrington and his Theo’s wedded life. I ought to beat my breast, cry mea culpa, assume a demure, penitent air, and sit here swaddled in a metaphorical white sheet perched on the repentance stool, holding myself up as a warning to all hot-headed, imprudent young folk who would dare marry before their pockets are full.

But truth be told, that married life, which every one of our prudent, respectable relatives prophesied would end in ruin and starvation, disappointed every last one of their gloomy expectations. It had its trials, to be sure—bitter passionate griefs that God in His mercy softened with the passage of years, days of straitened poverty that we bore so lightly that even our sympathizing onlooking relatives stared in wonder, precious, tender blessings and rewards so great I scarce dare whisper them across the page, too sacred for any ear but His to whom all men lift their prayers and thanks. No one can deny that marrying without a settled competence is wrong, dangerous, a transgression of our social codes—but do not tens of thousands of our fellow men commit that “crime” every year, trusting to nothing but Heaven, their own health, and the labour of their own two hands? Are young couples to never dare set up house until their cottage is fully furnished, their larder and cellar are stocked, their cupboards brim with silver plate, and their strongbox bulges with coin? If the rules our gentry live by were made universal, the human race would soon cease to multiply. Our gentlefolk stand trembling on the bank of matrimony in their silk stockings and polished pumps, waiting years and years for a fancy bridge or gilded barge to carry them across, while the poor do not fear wetting their bare feet, planting them firm in the brook, and trusting to fate and their own strength to carry them over. Who would wish to see his daughter consigned to poverty? Who would counsel his son to drag the woman he loves away from comfort and security, subject her to debt, privation, friendlessness, sickness, and all the hundred grim consequences of a straitened household? I look at my own dear wife and ask her forgiveness for ever imposing such a pain-filled, dangerous lot on one so gentle. I think of all the trials she bore, and I am thankful for them, and for that unfailing love and constancy with which God blessed her, and strengthened her to bear every last one. I am no fair judge of marriage, you see: mine was the rashest match imaginable, and it brought me more happiness than I ever dared hope for, so I have no right to offer young people advice. I lived in poverty, and rarely found it more than tolerable: had I not endured it, I never would have known the unexpected kindness of friends, the quiet thrill of gratitude, the small, surprising joys that creep in beside a scanty meal and a narrow fireside, that lighten the drudgery of a long day’s work. Of one thing I am certain: far too much superfluous pity is lavished on the decent poor. Good-natured wealthy folk who step out of the sunshine of their fortunes into our dim little lodgings are almost blinded by the gloom at first, while we saw everything clear and cheerful; they stumbled over obstacles that did not exist for us, and marveled that we could drink small beer with a smile, and say heartfelt grace over a plate of cold mutton.

My good father-in-law General Lambert had married his Molly when he was no more than a humble foot regiment subaltern, his purse nearly as empty as my own. They had weathered all the ups and downs of fortune together. I suspect—though my wife will never admit it—that they eloped, as young folks sometimes did in those days, without asking their parents’ leave first. [The Editor has pored over Burns’ Registers of Fleet Marriages and found no record of Martin Lambert and Mary Benson’s union, for the curious.] At any rate, they were so well pleased with their own happy match that they bore no grudge against their children for following suit, and were not at all frightened at the thought of any small hardships we might face in our wedded life. When I laid out my pecuniary situation for Mr. Lambert before he sailed for Jamaica, I was so eager to reassure us both that I counted a very doubtful sum as good debt, and he left for the islands fully believing I had two thousand pounds at my disposal, enough to keep his darling daughter comfortable for years at the very least. After paying for his family’s outfit, the good man sailed away scarcely richer than his new son-in-law; and Theo’s whole marriage portion was a handful of trinkets, some fine lace that had belonged to Aunt Lambert, and a purse stitched by her mother and sisters holding twenty bright new guineas.

The sum I had counted as good debt was one my honoured mother never once acknowledged, not even on the day the resolute old lady was called to pay her own final debt. The money I had disbursed on her behalf, she argued, had all been spent to improve and maintain the Virginia estate that would be mine upon her death. Any spare money she had, she declared, was to go to my poor brother Harry, who had nothing—who never would have spent his own means imagining himself sole heir to the Virginian property, as he had every right to suppose, the good lady repeatedly emphasized in her letters, but for that half-hour’s accident of my birth before him. Harry was now distinguishing himself nobly in the King’s service, she insisted, and it was my duty as his older brother to buy him the promotions he deserved. Long before news of my marriage reached her, Madam Esmond had written to me through our housekeeper Mountain more than once to say I was welcome to return home once I finished my legal studies in the Temple and my silly theatrical amusements, to take my rightful place in the colony.

You may imagine the full flood of maternal indignation that poured forth when she learned what I had done. When Canada was pacified after the fall of Quebec, my dear Harry applied for leave and dutifully sailed back to Virginia. His letters described his reception at home: Castlewood, which my mother had not stepped foot in since we sailed for Europe, was thrown wide open to all our old colonial friends, and Wolfe’s comrade, the veteran of Quebec, was greeted with every honour our acquaintances could heap upon him. Naturally, there were frequent stormy quarrels: Harry stubbornly refused to stop singing the praises of his dear friend Colonel Washington of Mount Vernon, whom I hold to be a man of every virtue—one whose glorious constancy and success in the struggle that ended so fatally for England a few years back I can admire as much as his warmest adherents do.

As I read Harry’s accounts of these frequent domestic battles, I could not help wondering why he chose to stay on at home. One obvious reason suggested itself that I scarcely dared mention to my Theo: we had both long discussed our little Hetty’s quiet, romantic attachment for my brother, and wondered how Harry could never have seen it. It was plain, of course, that my gentleman had found some young lady back home far more to his taste than our dear Hester, and that was the reason he lingered on in Virginia.

Presently a letter arrived from him that did not fully confess the fact, but clearly hinted at it: some unnamed being, all perfect beauty and grace, just as smitten young men describe their beloveds. My wife asked to see the letter, and I handed it to her with a very heavy heart, fully expecting to see her grieve for poor Hetty’s disappointment. To my great surprise, she read it without a shadow of sorrow crossing her face.

“I thought of this long ago, my love,” I said. “My heart aches for you over poor Hetty’s disappointment.”

“Ah, poor Hetty,” Theo murmured, her eyes fixed on the carpet.

“It never would have worked out between them,” I said.

“No—they never would have been happy together,” she sighed.

“How strange that Harry never once guessed her secret!” I went on.

She looked full at me with a very odd little expression on her face. “What does that look mean, my love?” I asked.

“Nothing at all, my dear—nothing! I am only not surprised,” she said, blushing bright pink.

“What?” I asked, a strange flutter in my chest. “Do you mean to tell me, Theo, that you ever cared for anyone before me?”

“Oh George!” she whimpered, “When I was at school, there was—there was a boy at Doctor Backhouse’s boys’ school, the one whose loft looked out over ours, and I thought he had the most lovely eyes in the world! Then I went to Mr. Grigg the mercer’s shop to buy a cloak for baby, and there he was, standing behind the counter. I was so mortified I never knew how to tell you!”

I snuck off later to see this wonder with the lovely eyes, after Theo described his clothes to me, and found a little bandy-legged wretch in a blue camlet coat, his foxy red hair tied back with a dirty ribbon. I was too generous to ever reproach my wife over it, and she will never know I laid eyes on the poor fellow until she reads this very page. I thought to myself, if our wives saw us exactly as we are, would they love us half as well? Do we misjudge them as much as they sometimes misjudge us? I look up at my wife’s candid, open face, and know full well it never once deceived me.

Lest I encourage my own children to copy my imprudent example, I will not tell you how tiny the capital was that Theo and I started our wedded life on. My tragedy never earned us a penny, though the critics have since spoken kindly of its merits, and Mr. Kemble himself did me the honour to praise it. Our good friend Lord Wrotham wanted to have it published by subscription, sent me a banknote, and asked for a hundred copies for all his friends, but I never cared for that way of extracting money from acquaintances. I chose to keep my poverty unburdened by favours, and locked the manuscript away, with Theo’s own verses tucked into the very first page. I never could understand why the play gave such offense at court, unless it was because the actor who had run off with an earl’s daughter played the lead role; but I was told that the speeches I put in the mouths of my Indian characters, declaiming against ambition and the greed for British rule, were declared dangerous and unconstitutional, and so any faint hope of royal favour I might have held was entirely extinguished.

What was I to do? A few months after the play failed, I sat down to count the remains of our fortune—an arithmetic that took no time at all—and came to the conclusion I must leave our pretty Bloomsbury apartments. I gave our kind landlady notice, telling her my wife’s health required us to take lodgings in the country. We did not go farther than Lambeth, though, where our faithful servants Gumbo and Molly followed us, so that we lived as people of condition ought, waited on by a footman and maid both in proper livery. You may be sure our kind relatives cried out at our “extravagance”; indeed, is it not always our relatives who are quickest to find fault with our faults, and proclaim them to all the rest of the world?

One day I came back from a trip into London to visit the booksellers, and spotted our family arms and livery blazoned on a grand gilt coach drawn up outside a public house near our lodgings. A crowd of local loiterers had gathered around the splendid vehicle, staring in awe at the footmen standing resplendent in the sun, quaffing foaming tankards of beer. I walked up the stairs to our little apartment to find Lady Castlewood sitting opposite my wife, their sitting room looking out over a bright, pleasant stretch of the Thames dotted with barges and wherries, and the ancient towers and trees of the Archbishop’s palace and gardens beyond. Theo, who has a droll, vivid way of describing people and scenes, told me every detail of the Countess’s conversation after her ladyship left.

“I’ve been gossiping here with cousin Theo this ever-so-long,” the Countess had said, “while you’ve been off at the coffee house, no doubt, making merry with your friends, drinking punch and coffee. I expect she finds it lonely here, nothing to do but sew little baby caps and hem frocks. Never mind, dear, you’ll soon have a little companion to amuse you when cousin George is off at his coffee house! What a nice lodging you have here, I do declare. Our new house we’ve taken is twenty times the size, gilded from top to bottom, but I like this just as well. Bless you, being rich is no better than being poor. When we lived at Albany, I did all the work myself, scrubbed the rooms, boiled the kettle, helped with the wash, and I was just as happy as I am now. We only had one old negro to tend the store. Why don’t you sell Gumbo, cousin George? He’s no use here lazing about and making love to the servant girl! Fogh, these English folks don’t care much for proprieties, do they?” She rattled on in perfect good humour until it was time for her to leave, pulling out a fine repeating watch to say she must pay a call on the Queen at Buckingham House. “And mind you come visit us, George,” she called, waving a gloved hand out of the coach door. “Theo and I have settled all about it.”

“There, at least, is someone who is not afraid of our poverty, nor ashamed to remember her own humble beginnings,” I said, as the laced footmen clambered up behind the carriage and our magnificent little patroness drove away.

“Ashamed!” Theo said, picking up her tiny, fine needlework. “To do her justice, she would make herself at home in any kitchen or any palace in the whole world. She gave me and Molly twenty whole lessons in housekeeping while she was here. She says when she lived in Albany, she roasted, baked, swept the house, and milked the cow.” Theo pronounced the word “cow” in our droll American way, mimicking the Countess’s broad accent so perfectly I laughed out loud.

“And she has no pride at all,” I added. “It was good-natured of her to ask us to dine with her and my lord. When will Uncle Warrington ever think to offer us a crust of bread, or a glass of that famous beer of his?”

“Yes, it was not unkind of her to invite us,” Theo said slyly. “But my dear, you don’t know all the conditions!” Still mimicking the Countess’s manner, she laughingly laid them out for me. “She pulled out her pocketbook and told me exactly what days she was busy,” Theo said. “On Mondays she receives a Duke and Duchess, and half a dozen other members of my lord’s family and their ladies. On Tuesdays there are more earls, two bishops, and an ambassador. ‘Of course you won’t come on those days?’ she said. ‘Now you are so poor, fine company ain’t no good for you. Lord bless you! father never dines on our company days, he don’t like it, he just takes a bit of cold meat in his room.’ I told her Mr. Warrington only cared for the best of company, Theo laughed, “and suggested she invite us on a day when the Archbishop of Canterbury was dining with her, so his Grace could give us a lift home in his coach to Lambeth. She’s a very economical little soul, too, Theo went on. “‘I thought of bringing some of my baby’s old caps and clothes that his lordship has outgrown, but they might be wanted again, you know, my dear,’ she said. So we lost that gift, Theo smiled, “and Molly and I will have to make do without her ladyship’s charity. ‘When people are poor, they are poor,’ she said, plain as day, ‘and must get on the best they can. What we are to do for that poor Maria, goodness only knows! We can’t have her to visit us like we can you, even though you are poor: an earl’s daughter to marry a common play-actor! La, my dear, it’s dreadful! The King and the Princess have both spoken of it! Every noble family in the kingdom who’s heard the story pities us, though I have a plan to help those poor unhappy people, and sent Simons, my groom of the chambers, down to tell them all about it.’ Her plan was that Hagan, who had nearly finished his terms at Dublin College, should go back there to take his degree and enter holy orders, ‘and then we will give him a chaplaincy in our own household, you know,’ Lady Castlewood added.” I may note here that this kind plan was carried out a couple of years later, and I myself was able to do a small service for Mr. Hagan, who was one of the kindest, truest friends we had in our own days of want. Lord Castlewood kept his promise loyally enough, got Hagan ordained and a colonial posting, where he distinguished himself both as a soldier and a preacher, as you shall hear later—though not a single guinea did his lordship ever spare to help either his sister or his kinsman in their trouble. I never once asked him for aid, thank Heaven, though to do him justice, no man ever expressed more genuine, unfeigned joy than he did when my days of poverty finally came to an end.

As for my Uncle Warrington, his pious wife, and their daughters, I will do them the justice to say that their sorrow over my poverty was perfectly consistent and unwavering throughout my whole period of trial. I still had acquaintances who ran into them from time to time, and of course—as friends always will—they brought me back reports of their opinions and conversations. I never once heard that my relatives uttered a single good word on my behalf or my wife’s.
