In the mist-shrouded gardens of Helstone Parsonage, the morning sunlight struggled to penetrate the dense veil of autumnal gloom, casting long shadows across the worn stone paths. Within the parsonage, Mrs. Hale sat at her desk, her fingers drumming a staccato rhythm on the polished wood as she pondered the upcoming Thornton dinner party. Her mind wandered through the anticipated evening, much like a child eager to unwrap a gift, her thoughts dancing with possibilities and speculations. As an invalid, she'd grown accustomed to a life of quiet routine, her perception of the world contracted within the narrow confines of her daily rituals. Yet, amidst the monotony, her vanities – those dormant sparks of youthful pride – still flickered, waiting to be fanned into flame.

As she mused, her daughter Margaret entered the room, a vision of serenity in her white silk gown, the delicate fabric shimmering like moonlight in the dim light. Mrs. Hale's eyes widened with delight, her mind flooded with memories of Margaret's childhood, when the simple pleasures of dressing up had been a cherished indulgence. "Then you think you shall wear your white silk," she began, her voice tinged with a mix of excitement and trepidation.

Margaret smiled, her eyes sparkling with amusement. "Oh, yes, mamma! Mrs. Murray made it, and it's sure to fit." She paused, her brow furrowed in consideration. "It may be a straw's breadth shorter or longer-waisted, depending on whether I've grown fat or thin. But I don't think I've altered in the least."

Mrs. Hale's gaze lingered on her daughter's reflection, her mind conjuring images of the past, when her own vanities had been crushed beneath the weight of poverty and social expectations. Yet, those same vanities now stirred, rekindled by the prospect of seeing Margaret shine at the Thornton dinner party. "Hadn't you better let Dixon see it?" she suggested, her voice tinged with a hint of doubt. "It may have gone yellow with lying by."

Margaret's expression turned thoughtful, her fingers trailing over the delicate folds of her gown. "If you like, mamma. But if the worst comes to the worst, I have a very nice pink gauze which Aunt Shaw gave me, only two or three months before Edith was married. That can't have gone yellow."
