After the departure of her spiritual adviser and chaplain, Madam Esmond found herself once more at peace with her son. Though George never spoke of their quarrel, it lingered in his thoughts like a shadow, heavy and unspoken. Soon after, he fell ill—fever took hold of him swiftly, and during the worst of it, delirium seized his mind. In the hush of midnight, as he tossed upon his bed, he would cry out in anguish: “Broken! Broken! It never, never can be mended!” His mother sat by his side, silent and trembling, her heart pierced by the sound of her child’s torment.

Her remedies proved useless. The medicines she kept in her cabinet—her trusted nostrums—failed to bring relief. At last, she was forced to seek help from a man she had long resented. One winter morning, little Mr. Dempster saw her arrive on horseback, her face pale, her breath fogging in the cold air. She had ridden through snow and wind to beg for his aid. “I shall bury my resentment, madam,” he said gently, “as you buried your pride. Please God, I may have time enough to help my dear young pupil.”

He packed his instruments, called his servant, shut the door of his lonely hut, and returned to Castlewood. That night, death seemed near. For days, George hovered between life and darkness. But under Mr. Dempster’s care, the fever broke. The chills lessened, and slowly, steadily, the boy regained strength.

A change of air was advised—perhaps even a journey to England. But Madam Esmond admitted, with regret, that she had been too quick to turn away from her children’s kin across the sea. Instead, they decided upon a northern and eastern tour. With Mr. Dempster as tutor and two servants in attendance, the two young gentlemen set out for New York. From there, they traveled up the winding Hudson River to Albany, where they were welcomed into the homes of the province’s finest families. Then, guided by letters of introduction, they crossed into the French territories, where they were received with warmth and curiosity by the local gentry.

Harry thrived in the wilderness. He joined Indian camps, hunted bears, and traded furs. But George, whose health remained fragile and whose interests lay elsewhere, became a favorite among the French ladies. They admired his fluency in their language, his elegant manner, and his grace in dance. He perfected his accent so thoroughly that some believed him to be a native-born Frenchman. He played the violin with charm, sang the latest French songs—though his voice began to crack as it changed from boyish treble to deep bass—and even engaged in a duel with a young ensign of the Auvergne regiment, the Chevalier de la Jabotière. He wounded the officer in the shoulder, not from malice, but from honor. Afterward, they swore eternal friendship.

Madame de Mouchy, the superintendent’s wife, wrote to Madam Esmond praising George’s conduct. “What a son you must be blessed with!” she declared. Had Mr. Whitfield heard such news, he might have frowned—yet Madam Esmond rejoiced, proud of her son’s accomplishments.

When the brothers returned home after ten months abroad, their mother was astonished by their transformation. George had grown tall and strong, nearly matching Harry in height. Now, when powdered, the boys looked so much alike that one could scarcely tell them apart. But in daily life, they wore their hair naturally—George’s dark and raven-black, Harry’s light and tied with a ribbon.

Those who have read earlier pages of this tale will recall that George Esmond was a youth of sharp sensibility, generous yet deeply jealous, noble in spirit, and unable to forgive an injury—even if he refused to take revenge. He left Castlewood with no affection for a certain gentleman whose name would one day echo throughout history. And he returned no less firm in his opinion.

Mr. Washington, though barely twenty-one, carried himself with the gravity of a man twice his age. He had managed his family’s affairs since childhood and was trusted by all—more than many older men. Mrs. Mountain, Madam Esmond’s companion and friend, adored both boys and their patroness, despite frequent quarrels. She was also a gifted letter-writer, full of wit and mischief. Her imagination often ran wild, especially when it came to matchmaking.

She was convinced that every unmarried man who visited Castlewood had designs on the widow. She insisted that Mr. Ward meant to court her, and that Madam Esmond secretly favored him. She knew Mr. Washington wanted to marry—after all, such a clever young man would surely seek a wealthy wife. As for age differences? Why, the Major was fifteen years younger than Madam Esmond. But what did that matter? Their family had seen such unions before. “My lady’s mother was how many years older than the Colonel?” she would muse. “And she was so jealous she wouldn’t let him out of her sight!” Poor Colonel, indeed—he’d been henpecked by his daughter too. And she would follow her mother’s example, Mrs. Mountain predicted, marrying again without a doubt.

Madam Esmond stood only five feet tall, even in her highest heels and headdress. Mr. Washington, meanwhile, towered at six feet two. Great men always married small women, Mrs. Mountain argued. “Therefore,” she concluded, “he must be looking after the widow.” What could be clearer?

She shared these theories with George, whom she called “my boy.” “For Heaven’s sake, Mountain,” he begged, “please keep quiet about this.”
“I’ll try,” she replied, “but I can’t close my eyes forever.” And then she recounted a hundred incidents—the way Mr. Washington walked into the house, the way Madam Esmond greeted him, the way she consulted him about every matter, great or small. She pointed out the room at Castlewood now known as “Mr. Washington’s room”—where he left his clothes and portmanteau when he left. “Ah, George, George,” she sighed, “one day he won’t go away.”

George listened, stiffening with each word. Toward his mother’s favorite, he adopted a cold politeness that irritated the honest gentleman, though he never complained. Or worse—sharp sarcasm, which he delivered like a blade through brambles, just as he once cut through thorny thickets while hunting with Harry Warrington. When the others rode off, George retreated to his study, lost in books—mathematics, Latin, French—growing more solitary with each passing day.

Harry was away on a hunting expedition when Mr. Washington arrived at Castlewood. The visit was tender, intimate, marked by a warmth that made George’s jealousy flare almost to bursting. But it was not a stay—it was a farewell. Major Washington was setting off on a dangerous journey to the western frontier, beyond the reach of civilization.

The French had begun to encroach upon British lands. Settlers in Virginia and Pennsylvania had been driven from their farms by armed Frenchmen. The governors of the colonies grew alarmed. Britain claimed all territory inland from its coastal charters, stretching westward to the Pacific. France, meanwhile, aimed to connect its northern and southern colonies through the Mississippi and St. Lawrence rivers, laying claim to the vast interior.

Though peace had been signed in 1748 between the European powers, the colonial dispute simmered. By 1753, it erupted along the Ohio River, where British and French settlers met. Yet neither side acknowledged the true owners of the land—the Native tribes who had lived there for generations. The courts of London and Paris chose to ignore them, treating the conflict as a European affair—while using the Indians’ weapons and alliances as tools of war.

The Ohio Company, granted land by Virginia, found its settlements invaded by French soldiers, who expelled the English settlers. They turned to Lieutenant-Governor Dinwiddie for protection. He decided to send a message to the French commander, demanding they cease their incursions.

Young Mr. Washington volunteered eagerly. With a guide, interpreter, and a few men, he journeyed through forests and frozen rivers in the fall of 1753, traveling from Williamsburg nearly to Lake Erie. He reached Fort le Boeuf and delivered his message. The reply was curt: “We are ordered to hold this place and drive all English from it. We intend to take possession of the Ohio.”

With that, he returned through perilous terrain, navigating by compass, camping in the snow. Harry Warrington cursed his luck—had he been home, he might have joined such a noble adventure. When Washington returned, victorious in purpose if not in outcome, he was more beloved than ever by Madam Esmond. She held him up as a model for her sons. “Ah, Harry,” she would say, “you with your cockfights and races—while the Major is out there in the wilderness, facing the French and the frozen rivers!” To George, she added, “Learning is good, but I wish my eldest son were doing something for his country.”

“I desire nothing more than to serve,” George replied. “But I will not serve under Mr. Washington, nor ask a commission from Mr. Dinwiddie.”

“An Esmond serves only with the king’s commission,” Madam said firmly. “And I would rather beg my bread than ask favors from that man.”

At the time, Washington was raising a regiment with the limited resources of Virginia. He hoped to establish a stronger presence against the French. A smaller force under Colonel Trent had already been sent west, tasked with fortifying a position near what is now Pittsburgh. But outnumbered and outgunned, they were forced to retreat. The French seized the fort, strengthened it, and named it Fort Du Quesne.

No shots had yet been fired—yet war loomed. Then, in 1754, a young Virginian officer, eager to defend his homeland, fired the first shot. It was a simple act, born of duty—not ambition. Yet it sparked a war that would rage for sixty years, sweep across continents, shatter empires, and birth a new nation.

That officer was George Washington. He led a small force to confront the French, met their advance guard, and opened fire—killing their leader. Outnumbered and surrounded, he was forced to surrender. On July 4, 1754, he marched out of Fort Necessity, gave up the fort, and returned home—his command ended, his regiment disbanded.

Defeated and humbled, he returned to Castlewood. He was still young, perhaps too young to bear such a burden. He spoke to George Warrington, holding out his hand. “I was angry when we parted,” he said. “You seemed to scorn me and my regiment. I thought you laughed at us.”

George took his hand. “Nay, you have done your best,” he said. “One hundred fifty starving men, half-starved and low on ammunition, cannot stand against five times their number, fully armed. And everyone knows you would do your duty.”

Harry and I saw the French in Canada last year,” George added. “They obey one will. In our colonies, each governor rules alone. The troops they sent were royal soldiers…”

“Oh, but some of ours should be here!” Madam Esmond interrupted, tossing her head. “A few good English regiments would make those white-coats run!”

“You think little of provincials,” the Colonel said grimly. “And I must admit, we’ve been unlucky. You praised me so highly when I was here before—remember your predictions of victory? Your wine flowed freely. All those dreams are over now. I’m grateful you receive a beaten man so kindly.”

He bowed his head, ashamed.

George, sensitive and moved by the other’s sorrow, was about to speak comfortingly—when his mother stepped in.

“Kind of us to receive you, Colonel Washington?” she said. “I never heard that when men are down, our sex are less their friends.” And she gave him a graceful curtsey.

George watched, his heart tightening. More than ever, he felt the weight of rivalry—of love, of admiration, of belonging—pulled away from him.
