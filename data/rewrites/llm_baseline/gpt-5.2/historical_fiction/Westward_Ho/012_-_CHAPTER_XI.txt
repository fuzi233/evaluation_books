It was the spring of 1582—3, that harsh hinge of the year when March will not yet loosen winter’s grip, but only hardens it into a sharper edge. The sky lay like gray curd above black mountain teeth; the wind came thin and dry over a wide, sour bog, and carried with it the smell of iron water and peat—old decay, old cold, and the faint acrid tang of last year’s heather burned by desperate hands. In the waste, where red and yellow frost-stains still bruised the ground, a single brown knoll rose like a loaf left too long in the ashes. On it a few leafless oaks, clipped by constant weather, stretched mossed arms over a pool that shivered and crisped in the gale; and from beside that pool, now and again, came a cry—mournful, long-drawn—borne away and returned by the wind as if the bog itself were trying to remember a voice and could not hold it.

Along the bog’s rim, choosing their footing between crumbling rock and treacherous green springs that sucked at the boot, pushed a company of English soldiers. Their gear was dulled by weather and service—helmets stained, quilted jerkins dark with old rain, arquebuses steadied on shoulders, pikes trailing and clinking. They were men who, two years before, had worked the guns at Smerwick; men who had learned to eat hard biscuit wet with ditch-water, to sleep with their muskets as if with wives, and to keep marching when the belly made its own complaints.

Two captains rode in front on shaggy ponies. The taller wore armor that had taken on the country’s own complexion—rust and peat and salt; the other sat in a bright inlaid cuirass and plumed helmet, sash clean enough to look like a banner, sword-hilt glittering with gold. It was finery made for torchlight and a court’s close air, a strange thing on a meagre garron picking its way through bog-holes.

Beside them, tethered by a cord to a pikeman’s wrist, trotted a bare-legged Irish kerne. His ragged saffron mantle flapped, his “glib” of uncombed hair fell over his brow, and his eyes slid right and left with fear sharpened into sullenness. He was their guide in the hunt for Baltinglas. If he lied, the bog would not be the worst of his grave.

“A pleasant country, truly, Captain Raleigh,” said the dingier officer to the gay one, glancing at the plume as it dipped and recovered against the wind. “I marvel you had the heart, after escaping it to Whitehall, to come back and spoil that brave suit with bog-water.”

“A pleasant country, my friend Amyas. What you say in jest, I say in earnest.”

Amyas laughed without mirth. “Hillo! Our tastes have changed places. I am sick of it already, as you foretold. Would to Heaven I could hear of some adventure Westward-ho! and find these big bones swinging in a hammock once more. What has made you so suddenly in love with rock and mire, that you come tramping them with us? I thought you had spied out the nakedness of the land long ago.”

“Bog and rock?” Raleigh’s voice warmed as if he were speaking of a dish he meant to perfect. “What is needed here but prudence and skill, justice and law? The soil—see you not?—is fat enough, if men were here to till it. And these rocks—who knows what they hold? I hear of gold and jewels found already; and Daniel, my brother Humphrey’s German assayer, swears these stones are of the very kind that yield silver in Peru. Give me but some few square miles of this wilderness, and in seven years I would make it blossom, by God’s help.”

“Humph!” Amyas eyed him sidelong. “If you can bake bread out of bog, I might be inclined to stay.”

“So you shall, and be my agent,” Raleigh returned lightly. “Get in my mine-rents and corn-rents and fishery-rents. Can you keep accounts, old knight of the bear’s-paw?”

“Well enough,” said Amyas, “for short reckonings like yours will be on the profit side. No, no. I’d rather carry lime all my days from Cauldy to Bideford than pass another twelve-month in the land of Ire. There is a curse upon the face of the earth.”

“There is no curse upon it,” Raleigh answered, with that fearless edge that later would be called irreligion by men who mistook bluntness for blasphemy, “save the old one of man’s sin—‘Thorns and thistles it shall bring forth to thee.’ Root them up, and you plough away nature’s curse and schoolmen’s fables. Till the ground like a man, and wheat comes where the briar stood.”

“It is sword and bullet that are needed here, before plough and harrow,” Amyas muttered. “Clear away some of the curse. Until more of these Irish lords are gone where the Desmonds are, there is no peace for Ireland.”

“Not so far wrong, I fear.” Raleigh’s gaze slid over the bog as if already measuring fields and rents. “And yet—Irish lords? These traitors are better English blood than we who hunt them down. When Yeo here slew Desmond, he let out no more Irish blood than if he had slain the Lord Deputy.”

“His blood be on his own head,” said Yeo, marching behind with the steady anger of a man who has been paid too long in hunger and lead. “He looked as wild as the worst of them—more shame to him. The ancient here had nigh cut off his arm before he told who he was; and then, your worship, there being a price on his head, and he like to bleed to death too—”

“Enough,” Raleigh broke in. “Thou hast done what was given thee to do.”

He fell quiet. The march went on, boots and hoofs making that soft, sucking music peat gives back—a damp applause that never quite becomes a sound. And Raleigh, as often happened when the world offered him a mirror, turned inward.

Strange, he thought, that noble Normans could sink into savages—Hibernis ipsis hiberniores. Was there some uncivilizing venom in the air? Or was it only that a man, set far from law and equal speech, grows used to hearing no “no,” and begins to believe his own desires are statutes?

He glanced at Amyas’s broad shoulders, at the guide’s hungry eyes, at the soldiers’ set faces; and then, with a sudden sting that came not from wind but conscience, he remembered his own craving for Desmond’s lands. What would he be, when he had them? Would his children, fattened on power as on rich meat, sink downwards as those barons sank? Would the genius of tyranny find soil even in him?

And yet—could he not do justice and love mercy? Could he not plant, build, sow—make the desert valleys laugh with corn? He imagined barns sweet with new hay, smoke from hearths, the steady domestic clatter of a country at peace: the grind of quern-stones, the hiss of oats turned on a hot iron, milk foaming in pails, the safe sounds that mean life is being fed. He longed—oddly, fiercely—for those wholesome noises, as if the ear could be nourished like the mouth, and as if this land’s misery were not only in its hunger but in its sound: the sudden scream, the war-cry, the musket’s crack, the thin wail in the wind where there ought to be a child’s laughter and the steady, sane creak of cart-wheels.

Yes—away from courts, among a people who might bless him rather than grin at him—what days might be his! But was that only ambition wearing an angel’s mask?

He broke off, startled. “Leigh—what noise was that?”

“An Irish howl, I fancied,” said Amyas, reining in. “But it came off the bog. It may be only a plover.”

“Something not quite right,” said the ancient, his voice lowering as if the air itself listened. “They tell ugly stories here of pucks and banshees and such. There it is again—wailing like a woman. They say the banshee cried all night before Desmond was slain.”

“Perhaps then,” Raleigh answered, though the hair at his nape prickled against his gorget, “this one is crying for Baltinglas, for his turn may come next—not that I believe old wives’ tales.”

Amyas leaned toward the guide. “Shamus. Do you hear that cry in the bog?”

The kerne made his face a sullen wall. “Shamus hear naught. Perhaps—what you call him?—fishing in ta pool.”

“An otter, he means,” said Amyas; then, as the cry rose again, clearer, with a human break in it: “Stay. Did you not hear it, Shamus? It was a woman’s voice.”

“Shamus is shick in his ears ever since Christmas.”

“Shamus will go after Desmond if he lies,” Amyas said flatly. Then, to the ancient: “Take a few men and see what it is. There may be some poor soul taken by robbers, or starving, as I have seen many.”

“And I too,” the ancient began, with that weary soldier’s philosophy that pins disaster on everybody and nobody. “Poor wretches; and by no fault of their own or ours neither. But if their lords will fall to quarrelling, and then drive each other’s cattle and waste each other’s lands—sir, you know—”

“I know.” Amyas cut him short. “Why dost not take the men and go?”

The old soldier swallowed. “Cry you mercy, noble captain, but—I fear nothing born of woman.”

“Well, what of that?” Amyas’s mouth twitched.

“But these pucks, sir. The wild Irish do say they haunt the pools; and they do no harm when you go toward them; but when you are past, they jump on your back like apes—and who can tackle that manner of fiend?”

“Then by thine own showing,” Raleigh laughed, “thou may’st go safely enough and see. If the puck jumps on thee as thou comest back, run in with him, and I’ll buy him of thee for a noble—or keep him in a cage and make money in London showing him for a monster.”

“Good Heaven forefend, Captain Raleigh! You talk rashly!” The ancient drew a breath, then squared himself with a scrap of bravado.

“‘Where duty calls  
To brazen walls,  
How base the slave who flinches—’

Lads—who’ll follow me?”

“Thou askest for volunteers as if thou wert to lead a forlorn hope,” Amyas said. “Pull away at the usquebaugh, man, and swallow Dutch courage, since thine English is oozed away. Stay. I’ll go myself.”

“And I with you,” Raleigh said, swinging down from his pony. “As the queen’s true knight-errant, I am bound to be behindhand in no adventure. Who knows but we may find a wicked magician just going to cut off the head of some saffron-mantled princess?”

“Oh, sirs, sirs, to endanger your precious—”

“Pooh,” Raleigh interrupted. “I wear an amulet, and have a spell of art-magic at my tongue’s end, whereby neither can a ghost see me, nor I see them. Come with us, Yeo, the Desmond-slayer, and we will shame the devil—or be shamed by him.”

“He may shame me, sir,” Yeo answered, “but he will never frighten me. But the bog, captains—”

“Tut! Devonshire men, and heath-trotters born, and not know our way over a peat moor!”

So the three went off, leaving the troop to watch the guide with hands nearer to knife-hilts than they admitted.

They splashed and scrambled half a mile, boots breaking through the skin of the bog into cold black water that smelled of long-soaked wood and old smoke. The cry grew louder as they neared, not like the call of bird or beast, but with the shaped sorrow of a throat that had learned words and now could only wail.

“That’s neither ghost nor otter,” Yeo said under his breath, “but a true Irish howl, as Captain Leigh said; and I’ll warrant Master Shamus knew it long ago.”

Now they could catch the words—Ochone, Ochonorie—rising and falling like a keening song. They climbed the knoll, slipped between boulders slick with lichen, and in a moment came full upon the sound’s source.

A young girl sat on a stone. She was, as such poor creatures were, sluttish and unkempt; the yellow mantle hung on her in folds that might once have been decent, but now were stained with bog-water and the gray of long want. Yet her face, under the tangles of black hair she tore at, was fair enough—fair, too, with that quick fairness that hunger sharpens rather than softens. She threw her head back and sent out again that long cry, less like speech than like the baying of a hound whose master will not come.

On her knees lay the head of a man in the long soutane of a Romish priest. One look at the set of his limbs told them what they had already tasted in the air: he was dead.

For a heartbeat none spoke. Raleigh, who could turn any scene into a picture and then into a thought, felt the bleakness pierce him—bitter sky, shapeless bog, stunted trees, and this savage young grief crouched over a body in utter desolation. The wind took her wail and scattered it, as if even sorrow here must be thinned to nothing.

Yeo, tougher of nerve and less given to reverence for any man in a cassock, broke the spell. “Shall I go and search the fellow, captain?”

“Better,” Amyas said, and knelt by the body.

Raleigh went to the girl and spoke gently in English. She looked up at armor and plume with wide, stunned eyes, then shook her head as if the language were a locked door, and bent again to her crying.

Raleigh laid a hand on her arm and eased her upright—not roughly, but with a careful firmness, as one lifts a pot from the fire so it will not spill. She did not resist; her grief had made her heavy and empty at once.

Amyas and Yeo bent over the corpse. It was a large man, coarsely featured, but wasted until the skin clung to bone. Hunger had hollowed him like a scooped crust. His hands and legs were cramped, his trunk bowed as if cold had folded him in upon himself. The stench was not of rot—death had come too cleanly for that—but of long privation: sour sweat, stale wool, and that faint sick-sweetness that clings to those who have been without food too long.

Yeo drew back the clothes from the thin breast. The girl screamed and wept, but still made no move to strike him; her sorrow was older than her anger.

“Ask her who it is,” Amyas said. “Yeo, you know a little Irish.”

Yeo asked. The girl’s eyes flashed, then shuttered; she made no answer.

“The stubborn jade won’t tell,” Yeo muttered. “If she were but a man, I’d make her soon enough.”

“Ask her who killed him.”

Yeo questioned again, listening close. “No one, she says. And I believe her, for I find no wound. The man has been starved, sirs, as I am a sinful man. God help him, though he is a priest. Yet—he seems full enough down below. What’s here? A big pouch, stuffed full of somewhat.”

“Hand it hither,” said Amyas.

They opened it. Not a crust, not a grain—only papers. Papers, papers; and then a parchment. They unrolled it with fingers stiffened by wind.

“Latin,” Amyas said. “Construe, Don Scholar.”

Raleigh took it, and his face changed as if the bog’s cold had touched some deeper place. He read a moment—then drew a sharp breath.

“Is it possible?” he said softly. “This is indeed a prize. This is Saunders himself.”

Yeo sprang back from the body as if it had turned to snake. “Nick Saunders, the Legate, sir?”

“Nicholas Saunders,” Raleigh replied, eyes still on the script, “the Pope’s legate.”

“The villain!” Yeo’s voice broke with a soldier’s thwarted fury. “Why did he not wait for me to have the comfort of killing him? Dog!” He kicked the corpse.

“Quiet!” Amyas snapped, half turning toward the girl as she shrieked at the insult. “Remember the poor wench.”

Raleigh, half to himself, went on, not without a strange pity threaded through his contempt. “Yes—Saunders. Misguided fool, and this is the end. To this thou hast come with thy plotting and conspiring, thy boasting, consecrated banners and bulls, Agnus Deis and holy waters, the blessing of all saints and angels, and thy Lady of the Immaculate Conception. Thou hast called on the heavens to judge between thee and us—and here is their answer.”

He glanced at something clenched in the dead man’s hand. “What is that, Amyas? Give it me.”

A pastoral epistle—addressed to Ormond and the nobles of Ireland. Raleigh’s lip curled as he read the opening—“To all who groan beneath the loathsome tyranny of an illegitimate adulteress”—and the usual flood of venom. He murmured, almost with the dryness of a scholar tasting a spoiled sauce: “Bah. And this, forsooth, thy last meditation. Incorrigible pedant. Victrix causa Diis placuit, sed victa Catoni.”

He ran his eye through other sheets—great promises from Pope and Spain, frantic slanders against Elizabeth and the chief servants of the realm, threats of endless fire for those who would not submit, sainting of rebels and traitors newly hanged, arguments for rebellion, for the nullity of English law, for the right to kill a prince deemed heretic. Words upon words—dominant words meant to choke out the small native words of common life: the plain language of bread and milk and hearth, the unrecorded speech by which mothers comfort children. It struck Raleigh—briefly, uncomfortably—that here, too, power hid itself in a lexicon: one tongue declaring another damned, one set of names claiming the right to define all others. And yet what could any ink do against hunger? Saunders had carried a library where he should have carried a loaf.

With a gesture of disgust, Raleigh crammed the papers back into the pouch.

They took it with them and walked back to the company. The wind still tore at their cloaks; the bog still made its sucking, patient sound. Behind them, the girl’s keening rose once more, then dwindled as distance swallowed it, as if the land were trained to eat voices.

They remounted, and marched on toward the Desmond lands; and the girl was left alone with the dead.

An hour passed.

Then another Englishman stood by the same wailing girl, and around him a dozen shock-headed kernes, skeans at thigh and javelins in hand, shifted their tawny rags and joined their lamentation to hers. Their cries rose and fell together, a bitter chorus that made the empty place ring; and in that wild music—so unlike the ordered sounds of a tended farmyard—there was a kind of acoustic rule: grief as a communal duty, sorrow as a language all could speak when other words failed.

The Englishman was Eustace Leigh—still a layman, yet still about the work which had become his penance and his hope. Two years of intrigue and labor from end to end of Ireland had worn him. His brow was more wrinkled, his features harder. The scar upon his face, and the slight distortion that came with it, lay hidden under a bushy beard from all but his own memory; and he never forgot it, nor forgot who had given it.

He had wandered with Desmond in moor and moss for months, hunted like a beast. Now he had been on his way to James Fitz-Eustace, Lord Baltinglas, to bring news of Desmond’s death, and with him a remnant of the clan—men too stout-hearted, or too deeply stained with crime, to seek the English mercy which, as so often happens, is easiest to those who most distrust it.

And now he stood looking down upon what was left of Ireland’s most sacred personage: the man who, Eustace had once hoped, would regenerate the island, bring the West under that gentle yoke in which, he believed, united Christendom labored for the commonweal of the universal Church. There lay Saunders; and with him, in that bog, lay Eustace’s dreams—dreams he had spoken aloud as certainties: that the land would rise as one man, even to the baby at the breast; that the Saxon heretic would be swept into the sea; that Spain and the Pope and the Jesuits’ wealth would stand behind him; that noblemen pledged to die and serfs ready to worship would make victory inevitable.

Starved to death in a bog.

It was a verdict plain enough for any man who could taste reality; but not for Eustace Leigh. Failure was an accident, a momentary clouding of providence in a wicked world whose prince, all knew, was the devil. Indeed—was it not proof of righteousness? When had the true faith not been trampled? If one cleansed one’s eyes of carnal impatience, what was this but martyrdom?

“Blest Saunders,” Eustace murmured, and the words came out with a tremor, half prayer and half hunger—for sanctity, for meaning, for a language that would make suffering intelligible. “Let me die the death of the righteous, and let my last end be like this. Ora pro me, most excellent martyr, while I dig thy grave upon this lonely moor, to wait there for thy translation to one of those stately shrines which, cemented by the blood of such as thee, shall rise restored toward heaven, and make this land once more the Isle of Saints.”

They buried the corpse there in the sour earth. A few prayers were said hastily, words clipped by fear and wind. Then Eustace was gone—not now to find Baltinglas, for it was more than his life was worth. The girl had told him of English soldiers who had passed; he knew they would reach the earl before he did. The game was up; all was lost.

So, as a desperate resource, he turned back toward the last place he would be looked for. After a month of disguises and hiding, he found himself again in his native Devon; while Fitz-Eustace, Viscount Baltinglas, took ship for Spain, having got little by his famous argument to Ormond for joining Rome—“Had not thine ancestor, blessed Thomas of Canterbury, died for the Church of Rome, thou hadst never been Earl of Ormond.” The premises were sound enough, for the Ormond lands had been granted by Henry II to expiate that turbulent murder; but the conclusion was of a piece with the rest of his party’s logic.

And now the road draws us back to Raleigh and Amyas as they jog on, their troop strung out behind them like a dark thread across the pale bog. It is but three days since they met, and they have much to speak of.

Amyas, as the march shows, is sliding fast into Raleigh’s old opinion of Ireland; while Raleigh, under the sudden inspiration of a possible grant of Desmond’s lands, looks on bog and rock transfigured by his hopes as if a rainbow had laid its colors upon them. He looked at all things so—noble fellow—even thirty years after, when he was old, worn, and ruined. Well for him had it been otherwise, had his heart grown old with his head.

Amyas, who knows nothing of Desmond’s lands, cannot make sense of the change.

“Why, what is this, Raleigh?” he said. “You are like children in the market-place, and nothing pleases you. You wanted to get to court, and you have got there; and are lord and master, I hear, or near it already. And as soon as fortune stuffs your mouth full of sweet-meats, do you turn informer on her?”

Raleigh laughed—short, as if the sound itself did not satisfy him—and was silent a moment. The wind made speech costly; it stole consonants and left words ragged.

“And how is your friend Mr. Secretary Spenser,” Amyas asked, “who was with us at Smerwick?”

“Spenser?” Raleigh answered. “He has thriven even as I have. And he has found, as I have, that in making one friend at court you make ten foes. ‘Oderint dum metuant’ is no motto for him—nor for me. I want to be great—great I am already, they say, if a prince’s favor can swell the frog into an ox; but I want to be liked, loved. I want to see people smile when I enter.”

“So they do, I’ll warrant,” Amyas said drily.

“So do hyenas,” Raleigh returned. “They grin because they are hungry, and I may throw them a bone.” He glanced at Amyas with sudden warmth. “I’ll throw you one now, old lad—or rather a good sirloin of beef, for the sake of your smile. That’s honest at least, whosoever else is not. Have you heard of my brother Humphrey’s new project?”

“How should I hear anything,” Amyas answered, sweeping the waste with his hand, “in this howling wilderness?”

“Kiss hands to the wilderness then,” Raleigh said, and in his voice came a note like spice in plain broth—sharp, enlivening, dangerous. “Come with me to Newfoundland.”

“You—to Newfoundland?”

“Yes. I to Newfoundland, unless my little matter here be settled at once. Gloriana does not know it and shall not till I am off. She would send me to the Tower, I think, if she caught me playing truant. I could hardly get leave to come hither; but I must out and try my fortune. I am over ears in debt already, and sick of courts and courtiers. Humphrey must go next spring and take possession of his kingdom beyond seas, or his patent expires. With him I go—and you too, my circumnavigating giant.”

And as they rode—wind hissing through helmet-rims, leather creaking, the bog’s soft suck keeping time—Raleigh poured out to Amyas the whole design of the Newfoundland venture: harbors and fisheries, plantations and patents, the taking of a land by ink and oar and hunger for new beginnings. His words were brisk as a cook’s when the fire is right and the knife is sharp: full of measures and means, of what could be salted down for winter, what could be gathered fresh from the sea, what could be made to feed both purse and pride. Behind them, Ireland’s wail faded into the wind; before them, the road went on, and the world’s larger appetite drew them westward.
