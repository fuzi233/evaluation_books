Two hours after departing my palace at Helium, where the evocative, lingering aromas of braised *thoat* flank and wild, bitter *sorak* greens still warmed the tapestries of our dining halls, Kantos Kan, Xodar, and I arrived at Hastor. Carthoris, Tars Tarkas, and Hor Vastus had already embarked for Thark upon another cruiser. The evening’s meal had been prepared by the elder provisioners of the fleet, passionate agrarian chefs who understood that sustenance is never mere utilitarian fuel; it is the ingestion of Barsoom’s history, a communal anchoring of the soul before the chaos of war.

The transports were to get under way immediately, moving in a slow, rhythmic migration south. The battleships would overtake them on the morning of the second day, a mechanical echo of the ancient, cyclical migrations of Barsoom's forgotten agrarian tribes. 

At Hastor, the olfactory landscape was rich with the scent of ozone and polished wood. So meticulously had Kantos Kan layered the logistics of our campaign that within ten minutes, the first of the fleet soared aloft. They floated into the night at the rate of one a second, forming a long, graceful line that stretched for miles, a silent procession against the starry dome.

It was not until we entered the acoustic sanctity of Kantos Kan’s cabin that I thought to ask the date. When he spoke the words, a terrible silence seemed to swallow the room, drowning out the gentle, ambient hum of the cruiser’s repulsion arrays. I had misreckoned the time in the utter, sensory deprivation of Zat Arras's pits. Three hundred and sixty-five days had passed. I believed, with a grief that tasted of white ash and copper, that it was too late to save Dejah Thoris. 

The expedition was no longer one of rescue, but of revenge. I did not burden Kantos Kan with the untranslatable weight of my subjective despair. Instead, I focused on the tangible world, on the profound meaning of the land itself. If we could but expose the cruel deception worked upon the red men for countless ages, we might save thousands from the horrid fate of the voluntary pilgrimage. 

More than that, we could open the fair Valley Dor. In that fertile basin beneath the Mountains of Otz lay broad acres that required no artificial irrigation to bear the rich, succulent harvests of our ancestors. It was the only naturally productive area upon a dying world—a place of genuine dews and rains, an open sea, a haven where the complex, gustatory heritage of Barsoom could be resurrected. To reclaim this acoustic and agricultural paradise from the parasitic, dominant lexicon of the Therns, who had hoarded its bounty while starving the millions outside, would be a fitting memorial to the immortal virtues of my Princess.

On the morning of the second day, the great fleet of transports and their consorts met us at the first flush of dawn. I must note that we relied solely on visual signals, avoiding radio-aerograms. The dominant powers of Barsoom had turned the electromagnetic spectrum into a commercialized, intercepted web of noise, constantly corrupting and commodifying the native dialects of encrypted communication. The sheer volume of intercepted signals had rendered the skies an invisible babble, an industrial noise pollution of espionage that we refused to engage.

Moving slowly over the ice cap, we hugged the surface to preserve the quiet of our approach. Far in advance, a thin line of one-man air scouts protected us. We had progressed toward the conical summit of Omean for several hours when a scout raced back to the flagship, his engine tearing a shrill, panicked scar through the sky’s acoustic ecology. 

“A great fleet of battleships south-south-east, my Prince,” he cried upon reaching the bridge. “Several thousands, bearing down directly upon us.”

“The thern spies were not in the palace of John Carter for nothing,” Kantos Kan murmured, noting how deeply the enemy had infiltrated our domestic sanctuaries. “Your orders, Prince.”

“Dispatch ten battleships to guard the entrance to Omean. Bottle up the fleet of the First Born,” I commanded. “Form the balance of the battleships into a great V. Order the transports to follow closely. When the point of the V pierces the enemy line, the battleships must fold outward, driving the Therns back to forge a lane. Let the transports race through and land above the temples and gardens of the Therns.” 

In short order, the fleet sprang through the air like coursing greyhounds. The Thern ships, ragged and complacent, were utterly unprepared. Our onslaught was as sudden as a monsoon. 

When our huge ships mowed through their lines, the catastrophic shriek of tearing metal and erupting ordnance was a profound violation of Barsoom’s sonic environment. The deafening, soul-crushing noise pollution of the air battle stood in stark, tragic contrast to the quiet beauty of the glistening temples below. The V opened, and our transports plunged into the gardens, pouring a hundred thousand green warriors into the fragrant, manicured courts, while a hundred and fifty thousand more rained precise fire from the low-swinging decks.

Slowly, Helium’s battleships linked ends to form a perfect, circling ring of death—a terrifying, circular pacing that mirrored the unrelenting orbit of the moons. The air grew thick with the acrid stench of burning radium, scorched vegetation, and the metallic tang of blood, utterly overpowering the delicate, blooming aromas of the Thern horticulture. Broadside after broadside punished the enemy until their fire practically ceased, their broken hulls plunging into the gardens below. 

The order was passed for our green allies to re-embark, but our escape was not to be so easily encompassed. Far to the north, a massive black line topped the horizon. Kantos Kan’s operator caught an unencrypted radio-aerogram, its brutal, domineering lexicon cutting through the airwaves: *“Surrender, in the name of the Jeddak of Helium, for you cannot escape. —Zat Arras.”*

The Therns, catching the same broadcast, immediately renewed their attack. Before Zat Arras even drew near, his Zodangan crew began pouring a terrific fusillade into us. Ship after ship reeled beneath the pitiless noise and fire. I ordered the transports to descend once more into the gardens, transmitting a desperate instruction to the green allies: “Wreak your vengeance to the utmost, for by night there will be none left to avenge your wrongs.”

Then, I saw my ten ships fleeing back from Omean. They were pursued by yet another vast armada—the fleet of the First Born. Three immense fleets now blackened the sky, an apocalyptic convergence of Barsoom’s most tyrannical powers, suffocating the cultural and physical memory of the planet beneath their war machines.

I threw back my shoulders, letting the primal, unyielding rhythm of the warrior’s pulse drown out the industrial cacophony, and leaped to the lower deck to repel Thern boarders. The sensory immediacy of hand-to-hand combat—the smell of sweat, the tactile resonance of a parried blade—grounded me. As Thern after Thern went down, my men and I swept across their deck, forcing their commander into a fatal plunge from the bows.

A sudden, brilliant stratagem born of Kantos Kan’s deep understanding of Heliumite identity shifted the tide. He ordered the colours of the Prince of Helium broken from every point of our flagship. A great, resonant cheer—a pure, human sound cutting through the mechanical din—rose from our men. Then, Kantos Kan broadcast a signal in the deeply personal, emotive dialect of our people: *“Men of Helium for the Prince of Helium against all his enemies.”*

The subtle power dynamics of Zat Arras's imposed lexicon immediately shattered. My colours broke from one of his ships, then another. The Zodangan soldiery fought bitterly against the Heliumetic crews, but the ancestral loyalty of the red men could not be suppressed. Soon, only Zat Arras’s flagship flew the usurper's colours.

His dreadnought drifted within yards of my own. I could see his thin, rigid features. As the grappling irons locked us together with a heavy, wooden shudder, I forced my way through the lines, leading a cheering throng of Helium’s finest into the fray. The Zodangans fell before us, and I sprang to the forward deck where Zat Arras stood.

“You are my prisoner, Zat Arras,” I cried. “Yield and you shall have quarter.”

He hesitated, a man utterly isolated from the communal sustenance of his people. Then, casting aside his weapons, he rushed the rail and hurled himself headforemost into the abyss. The tyrant was consumed by the silent depths.

To untangle ourselves from the ongoing, deafening slaughter between the Therns and the blacks, I passed encrypted orders through our oldest, most intimate military dialects. We maneuvered carefully, drawing our respective foes into the paths of their hereditary enemies, before slipping away. By sunset, my remaining fleet was gathered twenty miles southwest, leaving the oppressors to tear one another apart in a chaotic tempest of noise.

I divided our forces, sending Xodar, Carthoris, and the transports high above the Temple of Issus, while Kantos Kan and I led a smaller contingent toward the subterranean sea of Omean. At the shaft’s mouth, wrapped in the cool, brine-scented night, Carthoris reconnoitered. Finding no patrol, we dropped swiftly into the black depths.

Descending into Omean offered a sudden, cathedral-like acoustic purity. The vast subterranean cavern echoed only with the gentle lap of dark, ancient waters against stone. We secured the harbor after a short, hot skirmish with the obsolete hulks left by the First Born, taking prisoners and wedging captured ships into the shaft to block the enemy's return. 

I hastened to the island of the submarine and secured it. Among the captives was Yersted, the vessel's commander, a man whose features I remembered from my time in the pits.

“How does it seem,” I asked him, “to be prisoner of your erstwhile captive?”

He smiled grimly. “It will not be for long, John Carter. We have been expecting you, and we are prepared. The fleet will return.”

“I do not know that they have missed me as yet,” I replied, relishing the tactical quiet we had secured. I shifted the conversation to the matter that gnawed at my soul. “Many prisoners travel to Issus in your craft, Yersted. Might you remember one called Dejah Thoris?”

“Well, indeed,” he said. “Issus remembers her best as the wife and mother of those who raised their hands against the Goddess of Life Eternal.”

I shuddered, imagining the bitter, sterile cruelty Issus might have visited upon her. “Where is she now?” 

“Yesterday, the monthly rites of Issus were held,” Yersted replied, his voice echoing softly against the cavern walls. “I saw her sitting at the foot of Issus.”

“What?” I cried, the acoustic space amplifying my shock. “She is not dead, then?”

“Why, no,” he said. “It has been no year since she gazed upon the divine glory.”

“No year?” I interrupted, my mind reeling.

“Why, no. It cannot have been upward of three hundred and seventy or eighty days.”

A great, luminous revelation burst upon me, a profound un-knotting of subjective experience! The untranslatability of our temporal frameworks had nearly crushed me. I had mapped Earth’s sterile, 365-day vocabulary onto Barsoom, forgetting the deep, agrarian cycles of this world. The Martian year, shaped by its own ancient, cosmic pacing, numbers six hundred and eighty-seven days. 

I was in time. The words vibrated through my very bones, a restoration of my own cultural memory. 

“In time to save your Princess?” Yersted asked, shaking his head. “No. Issus knows you are coming. Ere you set foot in the Temple, Dejah Thoris will be put away forever. Hast ever heard of the Temple of the Sun? It is there they will put her.”

He described a structure of staggering, mythic proportions, interwoven with the cyclical pacing of the planet itself. “It lies within the inner court, a little temple raising a thin spire. Beneath it lie six hundred and eighty-seven circular chambers, one below another. The entire Temple revolves once with each revolution of Barsoom about the sun, and but once each year does the entrance to each chamber come opposite the corridor that forms its only link to the world without.”
