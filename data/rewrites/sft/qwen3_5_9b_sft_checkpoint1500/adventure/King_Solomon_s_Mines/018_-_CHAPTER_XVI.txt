By the time darkness had fallen on the third night, we had made camp within a cluster of huts nestled at the foot of the "Three Witches"—the jagged trio of peaks that guard Solomon's Great Road. With us were our three companions, the devoted servant Foulata, and Infadoos. Carried aloft in a litter, Gagool remained a source of constant, maddening noise; she muttered curses and gibberish throughout the day, her spirit seemingly as frayed as her tattered robes. Beside us marched a contingent of armed guards and attendants. The mountains themselves were a singular geological anomaly, a solitary upheaval resulting in three distinct summits arranged in a perfect triangle, their bases facing us. One peak stood to our right, one to our left, and the third loomed directly ahead.

I need not describe the scene revealed to us at dawn, for few sights etch themselves so deeply into memory as those twisted, snow-wreathed spires soaring into the azure sky. Below the snowline, the rocks were stained a deep, bruised purple by heather, matching the wild moors that climbed the steep slopes. Before us, stretching upward like a white ribbon, lay Solomon's Great Road. It wound for five miles along the flank of the central peak before abruptly terminating. It was an unfinished journey, a path leading nowhere.

An electric tension, bordering on manic excitement, gripped our party as we began our ascent. For the first time, we stood on the threshold of the legendary mines—the same subterranean wealth that had claimed the lives of the old Portuguese explorer Dom three centuries prior, that haunted the dreams of his tragic descendant, and, worst of all, threatened the life of George Curtis, Sir Henry's beloved brother. Could we escape the curse? As we trudged up the final stretch of the heather-clad road, a superstitious chill seeped into my bones. I felt it shared by Sir Henry and Good. The old fiend Gagool had warned us: "Evil befell them," she would say, her laughter sending a shiver down my spine. Would it now claim us?

"For an hour and a half," I recall thinking, "we plodded upward, driven by a feverish curiosity." The bearers carrying Gagool's hammock struggled to keep pace with our frantic stride. Finally, she could no longer hold back. Projecting her shriveled, grotesque visage through the grass curtains, she commanded us to halt.

"Walk more slowly, white men!" she screeched, her gleaming eyes locking onto ours. "Why will ye run to meet the evil that shall befall you, ye seekers after treasure?" Her horrible laugh, a sound like grinding stones, drained the enthusiasm from our limbs.

Yet, compelled by an insatiable desire, we pressed on. Suddenly, the terrain opened up to reveal a vast, circular abyss. It was a pit three hundred feet deep, spanning half a mile in circumference, with sloping sides that vanished into shadow.

"What could this possibly be?" I asked Sir Henry and Good, who stared in stunned silence.

"They shake their heads," I noted mentally. "Clearly, you have never visited the diamond fields of Kimberley." Pointing to the exposed strata of stiff blue clay clinging to the pit walls, I explained, "This is Solomon's Diamond Mine. Observe the formation; it matches perfectly. And look there," I indicated a series of worn, flat stone slabs arranged gently beneath a dry watercourse, "those are undoubtedly the tables once used to wash the ore. If they are not, I am a Dutchman."

Indeed, the Great Road forked here, bypassing the chasm entirely. In many sections, the path was constructed from massive stone blocks, clearly engineered to support the crumbling edges of the pit and prevent catastrophic collapses. Driven by curiosity, we followed the road around the perimeter, seeking to identify the three towering shapes visible from afar. As we approached, they resolved into colossal figures. Before us sat the "Silent Ones," the idols revered with terror by the Kukuana tribes.

Their majesty was overwhelming. Three giant statues, seated upon pedestals of dark rock carved with crude phallic symbols, faced us. Separated by forty paces, they gazed down the road that stretched sixty miles across the plains toward Loo. Two were male, one female. Each measured roughly thirty feet from head to base. The female figure, though severely damaged by centuries of wind and rain, possessed a fierce, severe beauty. Points resembling a crescent moon rose from her brow. The two males, draped in heavy garments, wore expressions of terrifying intensity. The figure to our right bore the face of a devil, while the one to our left was serene, yet that serenity radiated a dreadful, inhuman cruelty—a calm that suggests the ability to witness suffering without emotion.

"It is a trinity of awe," Sir Henry murmured. "And who, pray tell, chiseled these giants?"

While I pondered the mystery, Infadoos approached. Saluting the Silent Ones with his spear, he asked if we intended to enter the "Place of Death" immediately or wait for midday. Gagool had offered to guide us. Since it was barely eleven, and curiosity burned within us, we decided to proceed instantly. I suggested taking provisions, fearing we might be trapped. Foulata obeyed, storing dried game meat ("biltong") and water in a reed basket.

As Gagool stepped from her litter, casting an evil grin at us, she limped toward the sheer rock face rising eighty feet before us. Following her, we reached a narrow portal, solidly arched like the entrance to a gallery. Here, Gagool awaited us, her smile unchanged.

"Now, white men from the Stars," she piped, "are you ready? Behold, I am here to serve my Lord the King and show you the store of bright stones. Ha! Ha! Ha!"

"We are ready," I replied.

"Good! Make strong your hearts. Comest thou also, Infadoos, thou traitor?"

Infadoos frowned, his hand tightening on his spear. "Nay, I stay behind. It is not for me to enter. Gagool, curb thy tongue, or I will make thee pay for it. Fifty times a witch, thou shalt die if a hair of them is harmed. Hearest thou?"

"I hear thee, Infadoos," she sneered. "Thou hast always loved big words. Even when a babe, thou threatened thy mother. But fear not, for I live only to serve the King. I have served many, and in the end, they served me. Ha! Ha! I go to see their faces once more, and Twala's as well!" She produced a large gourd filled with oil, fitting a rush wick to create a torch.

"Foulata, wilt thou come?" Good asked, adopting a malicious tone learned from his tutelage.

"I fear, my lord," the girl whispered timorously.

"Then give me the basket."

"Nay, my lord, whither thou goest, I go."

"The deuce you will!" I thought, a knot of anxiety forming in my stomach. "If we ever return, this will be a terrible burden."

Gagool plunged into the dark passage, wide enough for two abreast. We followed, the sound of her eerie piping echoing ahead. A sudden rustle of wings startled Good, who cried out as something struck his face.

"Bats," I reassured him. "Press on."

After fifty paces, the tunnel began to glow with a faint, ethereal light. Soon, we emerged into a cavern of such magnitude that it defied description. Imagine the nave of the world's greatest cathedral, windowless yet illuminated from above by shafts piercing the arching ceiling a hundred feet overhead. This natural cathedral was loftier, wider, and infinitely stranger than any built by human hands.

But the true wonder lay in the rows of gigantic pillars lining the length of the hall. They appeared to be made of ice, yet were in truth colossal stalactites of white spar. Some were twenty feet in diameter at the base, rising with delicate, soaring beauty to touch the distant roof. Others were still in formation. On the rocky floor below, the remnants of columns that had failed to reach the ceiling lay like broken pillars of Greek temples. Above, the tips of immense icicles hung, slowly descending.

Standing there, we could hear the slow, rhythmic ticking of creation. *Drip!* A drop fell from a distant icicle to join the column below. On some, drops fell only once every few minutes. Calculating the rate of growth, one could determine that certain pillars, now eighty feet tall, had taken thousands of years to form. In one instance, a crude carving of a mummy with an Egyptian god hovering above it was etched into a pillar only eight feet high. This artwork, likely created by a weary laborer three millennia ago, showed the column growing at a rate of merely an inch per century. Nature worked with a patience that mocked human ambition.

The formations varied wildly. Some took the shape of ornate pulpits, fretted like lace; others resembled strange beasts. Fan-like tracings, resembling frost patterns on glass, adorned the cave walls. Smaller chambers branched off the main aisle, some large, some tiny—one no bigger than a dollhouse yet containing miniature versions of the same crystalline miracles.
