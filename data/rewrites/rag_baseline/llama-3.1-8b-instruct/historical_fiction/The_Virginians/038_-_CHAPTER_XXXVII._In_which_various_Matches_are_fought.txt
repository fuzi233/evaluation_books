As Mr. Warrington navigated the winding streets of London, the cacophony of city life gradually gave way to the din of Marybone Fields. He steered his pair of horses towards the Wooden House, where the anticipated match between Sutton and Figg would soon take place. The young charioteer's inexperience led him astray, and he found himself meandering through the green lanes behind Mr. Whitfield's Tabernacle, eventually losing his bearings near the Middlesex Hospital. Though disoriented, he pressed onward, driven by curiosity and a desire to witness the spectacle.

Upon arriving at the Wooden House, a bustling crowd of onlookers swirled around the entrance. London's underbelly was on full display: beggars and cripples vied for scraps, shoeblack-boys jostled one another for the privilege of polishing his boots, and vendors hawked their wares. A flag flapped in the breeze above the building, and a manager, accompanied by a drummer and a horn-blower, periodically announced the impending commencement of the events.

Warrington paid for his ticket and claimed a seat in the gallery, securing a prime view of the platform. Below him, his valet, Gumbo, settled into a spot in the amphitheater, occasionally rising to join the throngs of liveried attendants – lacqueys, footmen, and coachmen – that seemed to swarm every corner of the establishment. These reminders of a bygone era only added to the air of nostalgia that pervaded the scene.

As the anticipation grew, a series of minor matches and exhibitions preceded the main event. A lackluster boxing match ensued, followed by some half-hearted cudgel-play, which left the audience underwhelmed. The crowd's discontent was palpable, with cries of "The masters, the masters!" echoing through the air. Finally, the champion, Sutton, emerged onto the stage, sword in hand, drawing attention to a private box where a notable figure sat watching with interest.
