I lay beneath a great tree, watching the children drift off one by one—some alone, others in pairs or small clusters—as they climbed into their nests high among the branches. They were always so weary at night and so refreshed come morning that sleep and waking held equal joy for them. I, too, was tired, yet I could not close my eyes. Lona had not said goodnight, and I knew she would come. The moment I’d seen her again, I’d been struck by how much she resembled the princess—so unmistakably the daughter Adam had spoken of. But where Lilith’s beauty was dazzling, sharp, and regal, Lona’s was gentle, softened by innocence, deepened by the quiet strength of motherhood.

“She must be busy,” I murmured to myself, “with the child of the woman I met fleeing.” That woman, Lona had already told me, was not half a mother—too afraid, too lost. Still, I waited.

She came at last, sitting beside me without a word. For a while, there was only silence—the kind that speaks louder than words. She stroked my face, my hands, as if tracing something sacred. Then, softly, she began to tell me everything that had happened since I left.

The moon rose as we spoke, filtering through the leaves in fleeting glimmers, catching her face—thoughtful, burdened, yet radiant with a love that made even sorrow seem holy. How such a creature could have sprung from such a mother—how such a woman could have come from such a princess—was beyond understanding. Yet then, blessedly, she had two parents. Or perhaps three?

She drew me not with grandeur, but with what in me mirrored her own nature. And I loved her—not as a woman, but as one who, no matter how far she grew, would always remain a child at heart. I now knew, when I left her, that the hope of seeing her again had been my only solace. Every word she spoke pierced my soul like truth itself—purifying, illuminating.

She told me that after I departed from the orchard valley, the giants began to believe, just a little more, that their tiny neighbors truly existed. And with belief came hostility. The Little Ones sometimes saw them stomping through the forest, furious over imagined signs of our presence—while we stood nearby, laughing at their blind rage.

But soon, their anger turned practical. They started destroying the trees that fed us—our fruit, our life. This drove the mother of all the Little Ones to act. She sent the sharpest of them to listen at night, and they learned: the giants believed I was hiding near, waiting to strike once I recovered. They thought I’d return under cover of darkness to kill them while they slept.

So the plan formed: make the giants believe we had fled. We would move into the deeper forest—beyond their reach, but still close enough to visit our old trees at night. The only problem? The forest offered little shelter, no thick underbrush to hide in.

Yet she reasoned: where birds lived, so could we. The Little Ones had long shared kinship with all creatures, learning from the wild. Why not live in the treetops? Why not build homes where hollows couldn’t be dug? If birds could nest in the highest branches, why not we?

She spoke to them, and they listened—eagerly. They already climbed trees; they had watched birds weave their nests. The forest trees, though tall, did not look alien. They reached higher than the giants’ trees, arms outstretched, some even bending downward, as if beckoning. Perhaps, in the tallest, they might find the bird who laid baby-eggs and sat on them until they hatched—then dropped them down, letting the little ones burst forth.

Yes, they would build sleep-houses in the trees. No giant would ever look up. And once they believed we were gone, they would stop destroying our trees—and forget us entirely.

That is exactly what happened. Soon, the Little Ones were as comfortable in the canopy as the birds themselves. The giants, convinced we had vanished, returned to their old indifference. They forgot their trees. They stopped believing in us.

Lona asked if I had noticed how many of the children had grown. I said I hadn’t—but I could believe it. She insisted it was true, and added that the real proof was in their minds. Since moving upward, their thoughts had matured—something that eased the fear she first felt upon realizing their physical changes.

At twilight, and later under the moon, they would descend to the valley and gather fruit for the next day. The giants never ventured out then—twilight was darkness to them, and they loathed the moon. If they could, they’d have extinguished it.

But soon, the Little Ones discovered that fruit gathered at night didn’t keep well. So a new idea took root: instead of pretending to leave, why not force the giants to leave?

They had already explored the forest, making friends—or at least acquaintances—with its animals. Knowing some were strong, wise, and docile, others swift and manageable, they set about winning their aid. With playful affection, they called horses and elephants "Brother Horse," "Sister Elephant." Before long, each animal had a name.

It took time to say "Brother Bear"—but that came. And recently, she had heard a child cry, “Ah, Sister Serpent!” as a snake bit him for playing too roughly.

Most avoided caterpillars—except to watch their transformation. But when the chrysalis opened and wings emerged, every child rushed forward to call it "Sister Butterfly," celebrating its change with a word meaning something like *repentance*—as if rebirth were sacred.

One moonlit evening, while gathering fruit, they found a woman seated on the ground, cradling a baby. It was the same woman I had met fleeing toward Bulika. At first, they mistook her for a giantess who had stolen one of their infants—since all babies were theirs, in their eyes.

Furious, they swarmed upon her, striking wildly but carefully, never harming the child. She tried to flee, but a boy threw himself down and held her feet.

Regaining her senses, she recognized them—the children who had offered her shelter—and surrendered the baby.

Lona appeared, taking the infant into her arms. While the woman noted how gently the children struck, avoiding the baby, the Little Ones observed how fiercely the woman clung to her child—just as they would have done. They concluded: this must be a good giantess, like the gentle giant.

Once Lona held the baby, the children brought the woman fruit, showering her with childish affection.

The woman, torn between fear and hope, had nowhere else to go. Returning to the city meant certain exposure—princess Lilith would discover who had crippled her leopardess. But here, among these kind creatures, she found peace. She decided to stay—no need to care for the child, and perhaps a way to return to her husband, rich in jewels and rarely cruel.

Now I must add, from memory and guesswork, what Lona told me of her.

Like all of Bulika, she knew the legend: the princess lived in terror of a child destined to destroy her. But none understood the horror of the means she used to preserve her youth—nor how her failing body forced her to use them more often. So they saw her growing hatred of children not as cruelty, but as a sign of approaching doom. Though no one dared act, hope stirred.

And so, an idea formed in the woman’s mind: either fulfill the prophecy, or use it to regain her place.

What better chance than these children? Marvelously brave, unlike the cowardly Bulikans, who trembled before animals. If she could inspire them to attack the city, she might escape during the chaos, slip unseen into her home, and wait for the outcome.

If the children succeeded in driving out the giants, she would then suggest a greater goal—seizing the city itself.

By nature, they were unfit for war. They rarely quarreled, never fought. They loved all living things, hated pain and suffering. Yet they were easily led. They could be taught anything within their strength.

So she began—teaching the smallest to throw stones at a mark. Soon, all were captivated, growing skillful.

The first result? My rescue.

While gathering fruit, they found me asleep. They ran home, held council, and returned the next day with elephants and horses. They overwhelmed the few giants guarding me and carried me away.

Jubilant, the smaller boys boasted loudly; the older ones were quieter. The girls, though their eyes flashed brighter, were less talkative than usual.

The woman must have felt encouraged.

We talked most of the night—about the children’s growth, what it meant. I had long known Lona’s gift for seeing truth. Now I marveled at her wisdom. Had I been more like a child myself, perhaps I wouldn’t have been so amazed.

Dawn was still far off when I heard faint rustling, scrambling. I propped myself up and saw dozens of Little Ones descending from their nests. They vanished into the dark, and silence fell again.

“What are they doing?” I asked.

“They think,” Lona replied, “that even the stupid giants might search the woods. So they’re gathering stones—to defend themselves.”

Stones were scarce in the forest. They had to scatter far to find enough. They’d carry them back to their nests, ready to hurl from the trees when the giants came.

They expected them at dawn. If they came earlier, it would mean war—expulsion. One side would have to leave. But the outcome was clear.

“We don’t mean to kill them,” Lona said. “Their skulls are too thick—we couldn’t hurt them. And even if we did, it wouldn’t matter. They’re barely alive. If one died, his giantess would forget him in three days.”

“Do they throw well enough that it could happen?” I asked.

“Wait till you see them,” she said, pride warming her voice.

“But I haven’t told you,” she continued, “about what happened the night before last.”

We had returned from gathering fruit and were asleep in our nests when we were woken by terrible sounds—beasts fighting.

The moon shone bright. In seconds, our trees glittered with hundreds of tiny eyes, watching two enormous leopardesses—one pure white, the other black-spotted—ripping at each other with teeth and claws.

From the way the spotted one’s back arched, I guessed she’d been climbing when the white one leapt on her.

When I first saw them, they were rolling beneath my tree. I climbed down to the lowest branch and watched.

The children were enthralled. Some cheered one, some the other. We’d never seen such beasts before—we thought they were only playing.

But slowly, their growls faded. I realized they were fighting to the death. I wished neither survived.

Then the children saw blood streaming from their flanks and throats.

And what do you think they did?

They scrambled down, rushing to comfort them. A crowd gathered around the two beasts, patting, stroking them.

I descended too—but they were too absorbed to hear me. Before I reached them, the white leopardess stopped fighting and roared—a shriek that sent the children flying into the trees like birds.

Before I could climb back, the two were locked in battle again.

This time, the white one won. The spotted one fled. Whitey came and lay at the foot of my tree.

Then, after a minute, she rose and paced slowly, as if searching for the other.

I woke several times. Each time, I saw her still there.

In the morning, she was gone.

“I know both,” I said. “Spotty is evil. She hates the children, wants to kill them. But Whitey loves them. She attacked only to scare them away—so Spotty couldn’t catch any. Don’t fear Whitey.”

By then, the children were returning—noisy, laden with stones. They climbed back to their nests, struggling under their loads. In moments, they were asleep.

Lona retreated to her tree. I stayed where I was, sleeping deeply—knowing the white leopardess might still be somewhere in the woods.

I woke just after sunrise, lying in thought. Two hours passed. Then, truly, the giants began to appear—three or four at a time, straggling in. I counted over a hundred.

The children were still asleep. To wake them would draw attention. I stayed silent.

But one giant stumbled upon me—fell, rose. I thought he’d pass on, but he began searching.

I sprang up and struck him hard in the chest.

His roar woke the children.

Instantly, a storm broke—like hail—of stones. Not one hit me. Every stone found its target.

He fell. Others approached. The storm spread. Each giant, stepping into the range of a tree, became a target—hit from all sides.

Soon, nearly every giant lay prostrate.

A jubilant song, like a chorus of birds, rose from fifty trees.

Elephants charged in. The children descended like monkeys. Within moments, each elephant bore three or four children. They walked over the fallen giants, who lay roaring.

Tired of the noise, the elephants swung their trunks—once, twice—then left.

All day, the giants remained where they fell—silent, motionless.

The next morning, they were gone.

Never again did the children see them.

They moved to the far end of the orchard valley—and never entered the forest again.
