Years afterward, when the great press of time had gone over our house like a tide and left odd shells and fragments in drawers, I found among the old papers a packet, neatly docketed in my mother’s hand: “From London, April, 1760. My son’s dreadful letter.” I burnt it. I would not have that bit of domestic wreckage laid up like a family relic, for inquisitive grandchildren to finger, or for some hot-headed boy of our blood to draw forth and quote when he, too, fancied rebellion a romantic dish. For the same cause I destroyed the answer she sent me in that season of tyranny and revolt, when every line we wrote seemed dipped in gall, and yet was sealed with the old wax of duty.

I was mad—mad with separation from my mistress; and, in my madness, I took it into my head (not wholly unjustly) that my mother was the prime mover of the sharpest misery that had ever cut my life. I wrote to Virginia with what I called respect—respect held between clenched teeth. I said that whatever motives had guided her, I held her answerable for the wreck of my future, which she had chosen, wilfully, to mar. She had forced a separation between me and a virtuous, innocent creature; she had cast down that poor girl’s hopes, health, and happiness; the deed was done; I would make no comment on the doer, who must answer to God; but I did not disguise that the hurt she had done me was mortal, beyond mending by her life or mine; that my allegiance to her was broken; that I never again could be to her what I had been—her dutiful, respectful son.

Madam Esmond replied with a dignity that stung more than anger. Her style was like her needlework—fine, even, and without a loose thread. She gave no reproach, no hard word; she merely told me, coldly, that she had carried our cause before that awful tribunal of God and asked for counsel, and that as a mother she would, in humility, answer for her conduct there. Could I, as a son, answer for mine, and show, when the Great Judge put the question, whether I had honoured my father and my mother?

So the heavens were invoked—as they ever are in our private quarrels, with as much heat as in public wars. In every household dispute, as in every nation’s, we drag Providence into the room, and try to make Him sign on our side. We kneel, we demand, we shriek for sentence according to law; and outside our little chamber the great world goes on: carts rumble, kettles sing, children cry for bread, dogs bark at the milkman, coaches clatter over stones. We hate; we rage; we weep; we reconcile; we pass away; another morning rises that shines on us no more.

My letter, announcing my revolt (and I confess I had a vanity in its composition, as if a smart phrase could heal a wound), I showed to Mr. Lambert in duplicate, because I would have him understand exactly how I stood with my mother, and that, whatever threats might come, I should never consider my separation from Theo other than forced. Whenever I could see her again, I would. My word to her was in secula seculorum—binding at least while I had breath. I spoke as if she were equally bound to me, and her father knew it. He might separate us, as he might put poison before her and the obedient creature would take it and die; but the separation, the death—those would be his doing, and let him answer them.

Yet Martin Lambert was tender to weakness where his children were concerned—tender beyond prudence. He had not the heart to put any of them to torture—this one least of all. We had tried to part: we could not. He had endeavoured to divide us: it was more than lay in his power. The bars were up, but the young couple—maid within, knight without—loved each other all the same. The wall was built; Pyramus and Thisbe whispered on either side. And in the midst of grief and perplexity he still had humour enough to see his own part was sorry: the lime and rough plaster began to show a crack of light; first fingers went through, then hands; presently, had the builders been stubborn, heads would have followed. In truth it was the wall’s best business to retire.

I forget the stages and the days, and it matters little for the instruction of posterity. When my descendants come to their own love-scrapes, they will find their own ladders. I did not go back to Dean Street, I think; the open air was prescribed as the most salutary physic for Miss Lambert. I bought a good horse and rode by her carriage; and the old woman at Tottenham Court grew to know us, nodding and winking with the knowing kindness of a person who has, no doubt, warmed more than one pair of young hearts with a mug and a crust at a roadside door. I can smell the place still in memory—the peat reek and damp hedge-mould, the faint sour of stale small beer, and always the London dust riding on every breath of wind.

Doctor Heberden and the country air worked a prodigious cure. Hetty attended as duenna; and sometimes, on his holiday, Master Charley mounted my horse while I climbed into the carriage. What a world of love-making Hetty had to hear, and with what exemplary patience! If we rode in a closed coach, she would, with a virtue that deserved canonising, take to gazing out of the window at fields and clouds as though hedgerows were her peculiar passion. Then there were the letters—heaps of them—running to and fro as busy as kitchen errand-boys. Gumbo’s bandy legs were for ever trotting between my quarters and Dean Street; and Mrs. Molly, Theo’s maid, was for ever bringing back answers to Bloomsbury, with her apron smelling of starch and lavender-water. When the autumn leaves began to pale, Miss Theo’s roses were in full bloom again, and the doctor pronounced her cure complete.

While love was thus being nursed back to life like a delicate broth—watched, skimmed, warmed, never suffered to boil over—other things stirred in the household pot. I finished my great tragedy of Pocahontas; and this time Mr. Garrick accepted it, Dr. Johnson having spoken not unfavourably. My friend and cousin Hagan was engaged to play Captain Smith. The engagement came none too soon for him; and I had helped him and his family with means perhaps disproportionate to my own—especially considering the feud now blazing between Madam Esmond and me. Her answer to my angry April missive reached me toward autumn: war for war, controlment for controlment. Her thunder frightened me little. Virginia was far; her claws could not reach. And my conscience—if conscience it was, or only casuistry—found interpretations for her Scripture that kept my purpose firm. I did not tell her, nor any of my kin on either side (all bitter against the marriage), how my new loves prospered. What was the use of wrangling? Better to carve the day and its sweetness, and leave railers to grumble, and seniors to advise, over their own dinners.

In that first fever of separation I had also fired off notes—sharp enough—to my Uncle Warrington, to my Aunt Madame de Bernstein, and to my Lord or Lady of Castlewood (I forget which), thanking them for the pains they had taken to prevent the dearest happiness of my life, and promising them a corresponding gratitude from their obliged relative. Business brought the jovial baronet and his family to London earlier than usual; and Madame de Bernstein was never sorry to get back to Clarges Street and her cards. I saw them. They found me perfectly well; they concluded the match was broken; and I did not undeceive them. The Baroness took courage from my cheerful face, and made sly jokes about my philosophy and prudence as a man of the world. She was, as ever, set upon finding a rich match for me; and I fear I paid many compliments in her drawing-room to a young soap-boiler’s daughter from Mile End whom the worthy Baroness wished to thrust into my arms—plump as a Christmas goose, scented like orange-flower water, and frightened of every jest as of a sudden bang of a door.

“You court her with infinite wit and esprit, my dear,” says my pleased kinswoman; “but she does not understand half you say, and the other half frightens her. This ton de persiflage is very well amongst us; but be sparing of it, my dear nephew, among these roturiers.”

At her house, too, there was another person who did not understand my persiflage much better than Miss Badge; and this was a lady who had lived through King James the Second’s reign and was alive and worldly still under King George’s. I loved her company. Her talk was like an old receipt-book, crowded with names and seasons and private flavour: the great folks of the great days came out of her mouth as if she had only just seen them at supper. But my modest muse pipes for boys and virgins. Son Miles—if he ever reads these pages—will have no taste for court scandal, or can get fresh enough from Carlton House. No, my dear wife, you need not shake your powdered locks at me: I shall not bring a blush over our nursery bread-and-butter.

Yet this piece of scandal I cannot wholly pass; my aunt told it with infinite gusto, for she hated would-be good people and sniggered at the faults of the self-styled righteous as over a pie that had burst its crust and shown what lay within. My Lady Warrington—tall and virtuous, goodness for ever on her lips and “Heaven in her eye”—had the world in her other eye, and a shrewd appetite for advancement. When my match was supposed broken off, she tried upon me the same arts she had used on my poor Harry with such ill success—and failed with me likewise. It was not the Beauty, Miss Flora (for her master was of too illustrious a kind for my poor devotion); it was Dora the Muse who was sent to languish and pity and read even my godless tragedy, applauding and consoling. Meanwhile the Beauty—will it be believed?—was paraded before my Lady Yarmouth. My severe aunt gave an entertainment to the royal favourite, presented her boy to her, and placed little Miles under that august protection; and—so Bernstein swore—sent her daughter to stay in the lady’s house which our gracious lord and master daily visited, with designs that would have ended in a countess’s coronet in the family, but for an apoplexy that struck down the schemer. I can see it: my pretty Miles in his little uniform, led forward; the good-natured old Jezebel laying her hand on his curls; the room buzzing like a hive; and outside, in the street, the ceaseless din of London—hawkers, wheels, hammering—always drowning some finer music. Men talk of art, of virtue, of taste; and yet how readily they barter all three at the first loud knock of preferment.

Thus my life sped in the pleasantest occupation; and being so happy, I could afford to grow gentle towards those who had, after all, rather spiced than spoiled my fortune by placing a brief obstacle in my way. No particular plan was formed; but Theo and I knew a day would come when we need say Farewell no more. Should it fall a year hence, ten years hence—we were ready to wait. Day after day we discussed our little schemes, with Hetty for confidante. On our drives we spied cottages that might suit young people of small means; we made childish economies with the solemnity of statesmen: a cot and a brown loaf should content us; Gumbo and Molly should wait upon us (as indeed they have done from that day to this). At twenty, who fears poverty? Our trials would only bake our attachment the firmer, as a slow oven hardens the crust. The sweet sorrow of each day’s parting made the morrow’s meeting more delicious; and when we separated we ran home to write those precious letters which young gentlemen and ladies always write under such circumstances. My wife has them still in a great tin sugar-box in her closet; I have looked once, and found some pretty. Yet I desire my heirs and executors to burn them all, unread, at our demise—specially desiring my son the Captain (to whom manuscripts are no delight) to do this duty. Those secrets carried by the penny-post, or slipped between Molly and Gumbo, were meant for us alone. Let no descendant overhear them.

We had letters, too, telling how dear Harry continued with the army as General Amherst’s aide-de-camp after the death of his own glorious general. By mid-October came news of the capitulation of Montreal and all Canada; and a brief postscript in which Hal said he would ask for leave and must go see the old lady at home, who wrote, he said, as sulky as a bear. I could guess why, though her claws could not reach me. I had written pretty fully to my brother how affairs stood with me in England.

Then, on the 25th of October, news came that his Majesty had fallen down dead at Kensington, and George III reigned over us. I fear we grieved but little. What do those care for the Atridae whose hearts are strung only to love? A modest, handsome, brave new Prince—we took the report gladly that he had every virtue; we cried huzzay with the crowd that hailed his accession; and all the while we were only two simple sweethearts whispering our little story in a corner.

Yet who can tell how great events touch even the smallest lives? Had not our little Charley at the Charterhouse once wished impiously for a new king because, on his gracious Majesty’s accession, Doctor Crusius gave the boys a holiday? Charley and I, and Hetty and Theo (for Theo was strong enough now to walk many a mile), went to hear the Heralds proclaim the new King before Savile House in Leicester Fields. A pickpocket stole a gentleman’s watch and chain close by us; he was caught and carried to Bridewell—this, too, on account of the King’s accession. Had the King not died, the gentleman would not have been in the crowd; the chain would not have been seized; the thief would not have been caught and whipped. So we are all, more or less remotely, tangled in great changes; the drumbeat at court sets even kitchen clocks to a different time.

And so it fell out with us. My Lord Wrotham was a great friend of the august family at Savile House, who knew and esteemed his many virtues. Of all living men, my Lord Wrotham most loved his neighbour and old fellow-soldier, Martin Lambert, declaring the world held few better gentlemen. And my Lord Bute, being at first all potent with the young King, was eager—so it was said at the beginning of his brief and unlucky power—to patronise merit; and through Wrotham he was strongly prejudiced in Mr. Lambert’s favour.

At this time Parson Sampson—our old friend—was occupying poor Hal’s vacant bed at my lodgings, being in truth hunted out of his own by the bailiffs. I liked to have him near: no more amusing Jack-friar ever walked in cassock. He entered into all my rhapsodies about Miss Theo; he swore he never tired of hearing me talk of her; he admired Pocahontas and Carpezan with what I believe was honest enthusiasm; and could repeat whole passages with an emphasis that Barry or cousin Hagan could scarce surpass. He was go-between for Lady Maria and such relations as had not disowned her; and, always in debt himself, was never happier than drinking a pot with friends in similar poverty, mingling tears with them as readily as ale. His knowledge of pawnbrokers’ shops was prodigious; he boasted he could raise more on an article than any gentleman of his cloth. He never paid his own debts, to be sure, but he was always ready to forgive his debtors. Poor as he was, he found means to love and help his needy little sister. A more prodigal, kindly, amiable rogue never grinned behind bars. They say I love parasites: I confess I had a great liking for Sampson, and esteemed him much better than probably much better men.

When he heard my Lord Bute was in the cabinet, Sampson vowed his lordship must give me a place worthy of my birth and merits. I would not go to his levee? The Esmonds were proud as Lucifer; and, demmy, my birth was as good as any man’s in Europe. Where were these great Scots, Sampson roared, when the Esmonds were lords of counties, warriors, Crusaders? Beggarly islanders, by George, tearing raw fish without a rag to their backs! But the times were changed; the Scots were in luck; mum’s the word. “I don’t envy him,” says Sampson, “but he shall provide for you and my dearest, noblest, heroic captain! He SHALL, by George!” And when, the month after the accession, his Majesty ordered Richard III at Drury Lane, my chaplain cursed and swore he would have me to Covent Garden to see Carpezan too.

One morning he burst into my room where I lay rather late, waving a newspaper and singing “Huzza!” with all his might—his voice loud as a street-crier, and, to my irritable ear, as jarring as a hammer on a pot.

“What is it, Sampson?” says I. “Has my brother got his promotion?”

“No, in truth; but some one else has. Huzzay! huzzay! His Majesty has appointed Major-General Martin Lambert to be Governor and Commander-in-Chief of the Island of Jamaica.”

I started up. Here was news indeed. Mr. Lambert would go to his government—and who would go with him?

The night before, I had been supping with some genteel young fellows at the Cocoa-Tree. The rascal Gumbo had had a note for me from my dear mistress, conveying the same intelligence, and had delayed to deliver it. Theo begged me to see her at the old place at midday next day without fail.

There was no little trepidation in our little council when we met. Papa had announced his acceptance and his speedy departure. He would have a frigate and take his family with him. Merciful powers—were we to be torn apart? My Theo’s old deathly paleness returned as if a hand had snuffed a candle. Aunt Lambert thought she would swoon; one of Mrs. Goodison’s girls ran up from the workroom with a bottle of salts. “Going away? Going away in a frigate, Aunt Lambert? Going to tear her away from me? Great God! I shall die!” she cried.

She was better when mamma came up from the workroom with the salts. Those women used to meet me; and knowing Theo’s delicate state, how could they keep from compassion? The house itself seemed to breathe of women’s labour—hot irons hissing, the dry rasp of scissors, the soft thud of cloth laid on the board, and through it all the murmuring of voices kept low as in a sickroom. Mrs. Goodison, who had known and worked for Miss Molly Benson as a schoolgirl, told me she remembered Miss Esmond of Virginia perfectly, and a gown she made for the young lady to be presented at her Majesty’s ball; and even when orders came for an outfit for the three ladies, she said demurely, “why, I thought I could do no harm in completing the order.”

For the General was busy with levees, waiting on ministers, making his outfit, settling affairs; and they never happened to tell him about our little walks and meetings. The world of men was all drums and appointments and official doors; the world of women, all pins and hems and bottles of salts; and between them our love tried to keep its own small music from being drowned.

I need not say in what perturbation I went home that evening after our discussion. No—at least only a very few—more walks; no more rides to dear Hampstead or beloved Islington; no more fetching and carrying of letters between Gumbo and Molly. The former blubbered so that I was touched by his fidelity and gave him a crown-piece to go to supper with the poor girl—his sweetheart, it turned out. What, you too, unhappy Gumbo, torn from the maid you love? I was ready to mingle tear for tear, as if grief were a broth we must season together, each tasting the other’s salt.
