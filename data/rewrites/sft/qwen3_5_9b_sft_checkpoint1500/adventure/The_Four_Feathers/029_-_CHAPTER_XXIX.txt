Three more days," the two men murmured together, sinking into sleep as the words left their lips. Yet the following morning, Trench awoke gasping, a fever seizing him with terrifying speed. By afternoon, the heat had burned through his bones, leaving him light-headed and delirious. Now, the roles reversed: the services Feversham had once rendered to Trench were demanded anew. The thousand nights spent in the House of Stone had finally exacted their toll, but fate played a cruel joke; it was not mere coincidence that Trench fell ill precisely as the iron-bound door of his prison swung open. The overwhelming surge of joy at the prospect of freedom—the sudden, unexpected revelation of escape—had shattered his exhausted frame. The actuality of leaving, however brief, proved a trial his body could not withstand.

"In a few days he will be well," Feversham declared, his voice steady despite the turmoil within. "It is nothing."

"It is *Umm Sabbah*," corrected Ibrahim, shaking his head grimly. He pointed to the terrible typhus that had swept through the infected gaol, claiming countless lives before the seventh day.

Feversham refused to accept the diagnosis, repeating his denial with a stubborn passion. But beneath the surface, another question gnawed at him: *Would the men with the camels wait?* Each day, as he trudged toward the Nile, he spotted Abou Fatma in his blue robes standing guard at his post. Each day, the Arab raised his hand in silent signal, and each day, Feversham offered no reply. Meanwhile, aided by Ibrahim, Feversham nursed Trench back to health. The young boy arrived daily with provisions, venturing out to purchase tamarinds, dates, and bitter roots from which Ibrahim brewed cooling draughts. Together, they dragged the invalid from shade to shade as the sun crawled across the zareeba. Assistance was also extended to the starving family of Idris; consequently, the heavy forty-pound chains binding Trench were removed. He was fed vegetable marrow soaked in brine, his mouth padded with butter, and his skin anointed and swaddled tightly in warm camel-hair cloths.

The fever ran its bloody course. On the seventh day, Ibrahim, weary and solemn, spoke softly: "This is the last. Tonight he will die."

"No," Feversham countered firmly, "that is impossible. 'In his own parish,' he said, 'beneath the trees he knew.' Not here, no." His obstinacy was fueled by a desperate refusal to believe the end had come. For the first time, the looming absence of the third feather, the failure to return it to Ethne, vanished from his mind. Even the grand vision of "the afterwards" was cast aside. He thought only of Trench and the strange, quiet conversation they had shared under the vast sky on their first night together. "No," he insisted again, "he must not die here." Through the long, agonizing night, he fought alongside Trench in the silent, brutal war between life and death. At moments, it seemed the three years of starvation and stone would claim victory; at others, Trench's wiry strength and robust constitution held firm.

For that night, they won. The struggle prolonged, and the perilous seventh day slipped by unnoticed. By the thirteenth day, Trench slept deeply, undisturbed by the madness of his dreams, and when he awoke, his mind was clear. He found himself alone, swaddled so tightly in camel-cloth that he could not stir, yet the heat of the day had broken. The shadow of the House of Stone stretched black across the sand. Lying still, he drifted into a wondering idleness about the duration of his illness. Suddenly, the shouts of the jailers pierced the silence, mingled with the cries of prisoners gathering outside the zareeba near the river. The gate groaned open, and the inmates flooded in. Feversham was among them, walking straight to Trench's corner, collapsing in exhaustion beside him after a day of unloading boats.

"Thank God!" Feversham cried, his voice rough. "I would not have left you, but I was compelled."

"How long have I lain ill?" Trench asked weakly.

"Thirteen days."

"It will be a month before I can travel," Trench rasped. "You must go, Feversham. Leave me here and go while you still can. Perhaps when you reach Assouan you can secure aid. I cannot move now. Will you go tomorrow?"

"No," Feversham replied, shaking his head. "I would not go without you in any case. As it stands, it is too late."

"Too late?" Trench echoed, absorbing the weight of the words slowly. He wished to remain idle, to soak in the cool of the sunset, but gradually the implication forced itself upon him. "Too late? Then the man in the blue gown has gone?"

"Yes," Feversham confirmed. "He spoke to me yesterday by the river. The camel drivers would wait no longer. Fearing detection, they intended to return regardless of whether we went with them."

"You should have gone with them," Trench said, devoid of despair, simply resigned to the quietude of the prison. "So our one chance is lost."

"No," Feversham corrected gently. "Deferred." He revealed the secret gifts the messenger had left: paper, a pen, and wood-soot mixed with water, hidden beneath his *jibbeh*. Under the light of a recent moon, he had written to a Greek merchant running a café at Wadi Halfa. That afternoon, he had handed over the letter. The merchant promised delivery and payment, returning to Omdurman within six months, or perhaps a year at the latest.

"Very likely," Trench mused, knowing the ways of traders. "He will demand another letter for more coin and promise to return in six months or a year. I know these people."

"You do not know Abou Fatma," Feversham interjected warmly. "He served Lord Gordon before Khartum fell; he has been my servant since. He accompanied me to Obak, waited while I traveled to Berber, and risked his life merely to reach Omdurman. Within six months, he will surely be back."

Trench fell silent, letting his gaze wander until it landed on a fresh mound of earth in a corner. "What are they digging?" he asked.

"A well," Feversham answered.

"A well?" Trench frowned irritably. "And so close to the Nile! Why? What is the purpose?"

"I don't know," Feversham admitted, though a deep, cold dread settled in his chest. He suspected the truth but withheld it, fearing the crushing blow it would deal to his companion's spirits. Soon, however, his fears were vindicated. An announcement spread through the prison: a high wall would enclose the House of Stone. Too many prisoners had escaped in their fetters along the riverbank; henceforth, they would be locked inside from New Year's Eve to New Year's Day. The prisoners themselves laid the foundation of mud bricks, baked by the sun. Feversham worked with his full strength, and Trench, as soon as he could stand, joined him.

"Here goes our last hope," Trench muttered, watching the bricks pile higher. Though Feversham tried to mask his own sinking heart, he agreed inwardly.

Brick upon brick, they mortared the wall together. Daily, it rose another foot. Finally, twelve feet high, smooth, and formidable, it stood complete—a fortress that could not be breached in a night. Trench and Feversham stared at it in utter despair. The towering palms of Khartum were now hidden from view. Their entire world shrunk to a square of brilliant blue by day, dark blue by night, jeweled with distant stars and flashes of gold. Trench covered his face with his hands.

"I dare not look at it," he whispered, his voice breaking. "We have been building our own coffin, Feversham. That is the truth." He threw up his arms, a cry rising in his throat. "Will they never send the gunboats and soldiers up the Nile? Have they forgotten us in England? Good God! Have they forgotten us?"

"Hush!" Feversham commanded softly. "We shall find a way of escape, never fear. We must wait six months. Well, we have both waited years. Six months... what are they?"

Though his words were stout, spoken for his comrade's sake, Feversham felt his heart sink like a stone.

The details of their existence during those six months need not be chronicled. In that pestilential enclosure, only the myriad vermin thrived comfortably. News from the outside world filtered in not at all. They fed upon their own thoughts; the sight of a lizard crossing the wall became an event of profound excitement. Scorpions stung them in the dark, and jailers flogged them by day. They lived entirely at the mercy of the whims of Idris-es-Saier and the peculiar spirit Nebbi Khiddr, who invariably reported against them to the Khalifa at the precise moment Idris needed funds for his starving kin. Religious men dispatched by the Khalifa to convert them to Islam turned the enclosure into a theater of theological debate, eagerly anticipated by both men.
