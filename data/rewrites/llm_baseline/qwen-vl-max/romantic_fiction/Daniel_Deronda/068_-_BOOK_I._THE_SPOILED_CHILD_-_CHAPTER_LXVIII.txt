*“All thoughts, all passions, all delights,
Whatever stirs this mortal frame,
All are but ministers of Love,
And feed his sacred flame.”* —COLERIDGE.

Deronda’s heart had never burned with such intensity as it did now—fanned by Hans’s words that Mirah was suffering from jealousy. The knowledge stirred him deeply, and when he returned to Ezra’s home the next day, he carried with him a quiet determination: he would not let this moment pass. He would seek out Mirah, speak plainly, and ask her to be his wife. If she accepted, he felt certain he could face any consequence. And if they were betrothed, he might also offer her a kind of protection—a shield against the turmoil that might come from her father.

He had noticed no outward change in Lapidoth. The man still seemed attentive, dutiful even. But Deronda sensed beneath the surface a growing unrest, an unease that could erupt into something terrible. He didn’t yet know how close the storm was, nor how fragile the peace they’d built truly was.

Lapidoth, meanwhile, was not at ease. To Ezra, his distraction was merely troubling; to Lapidoth, it was a prison. Every moment spent in the house felt like a delay—like being held back from freedom. He had once planned to wait for a grand opportunity, perhaps to extract a large sum from Deronda. But while he waited, he watched—curiously, relentlessly. Where did Mirah keep her money? Her keys? He searched for weaknesses, for moments when he could act without suspicion.

Yet Mirah was careful. She had entrusted all her savings to Mrs. Meyrick, keeping only what she needed. For Lapidoth, this was like being locked in a room where every door was bolted—safe, yes, but infuriating. He could not steal, not openly. Yet the thought lingered: *What if I took something small? Something that belonged to someone who would give me more anyway?*

That morning, Deronda arrived radiant—his eyes bright, his voice warm. He had been thinking of Mirah, of the future, and the joy of confessing his love. His presence stirred Lapidoth’s mind, not with kindness, but with new possibilities. Deronda’s ease made Lapidoth feel the chains tighter. He longed to escape—not just from the house, but from himself.

As Deronda read aloud from a rabbinical text, Lapidoth sat quietly in the background, his gaze drifting. Then—his eyes landed on the ring. It lay on the dark wooden table, gleaming under the light. Deronda had removed it earlier, placing it beside his cravat. It had been there all along, unnoticed, unguarded.

In that instant, Lapidoth saw not just a piece of jewelry—but a key. A way out. A sum of money that could buy passage abroad, freedom from restraint, silence. It wasn’t much—less than what he dreamed of—but it was real. It was within reach. And it belonged to a man who had already shown generosity.

He didn’t consider stealing. Not really. Theft was a crime. But this—this was different. Deronda was not a stranger. He was almost family. And if Deronda had given him more, wouldn’t he have taken it willingly? So why not take this?

The idea came swiftly, like a breath catching fire. He rose, stepped toward the window, then passed the table—his fingers brushing the ring. It slipped into his pocket without sound. He put on his hat and left.

No one noticed. Not Deronda. Not Ezra.

Then Mirah entered, breathless, her hat still on. “I just came to make sure Ezra had his medicine,” she said, looking flustered. “I must go to Mrs. Meyrick’s right away.”

“Let me walk with you,” Deronda said at once. “I can’t stay here—I’m too hot. I want to go to Mrs. Meyrick’s too. May I?”

“Oh, yes,” Mirah whispered, blushing. She turned to pour Ezra’s draught, her hands trembling slightly.

Deronda paused, suddenly remembering—he had left his cravat and ring behind. He went to the table, picked up the cravat, and froze.

“Good heavens,” he murmured. “Where is my ring?”

He began searching—the floor, under the furniture, between the carpet fibers. Ezra looked up from his books, puzzled. Mirah rushed to his side.

“Did you leave it here?” she asked.

“Yes,” he said, still scanning the floor. “It must have fallen.”

But Mirah knew. In that moment, her face drained of color. She moved quickly to Ezra’s ear and whispered, “Was my father here?”

Ezra nodded slowly, meeting her eyes with grim understanding.

She darted back to Deronda, her voice urgent. “You haven’t found it?”

“No,” he said, pretending to check his pockets. “Maybe I put it there.”

She stared at him, her voice sharp, clear: “It’s not there. You left it on the table.”

Then she ran—out of the room, down the stairs, into the bedroom, checking everywhere. She opened the door where his hat usually hung. Nothing. She stood at the window, hands clasped, lips pale, staring into the distance as if seeking a sign.

Deronda followed her silently. When she finally turned, her eyes met his—full of shame, grief, and something deeper: a silent surrender.

He stepped forward, took her hands gently in both of his. “Mirah,” he said, his voice low and reverent, “let me think of him as my father too. Let us share everything—joy and sorrow, honor and disgrace. I would rather carry your pain than feel the happiest joy of another woman. Say you will not reject me. Say you will let me be yours. Say you will be my wife. Say it now—say it always. I’ve waited so long. I’ve hidden my love so long. Say yes.”

Mirah had not known what to expect. At first, she heard only comfort—kindness offered because of Ezra. But as his words sank in, a warmth spread through her. Not just comfort—but hope. A future. A love that could rise above shame, above loss.

Her face glowed under his gaze. Yet she remained still, grave, as if listening to a sacred vow. When he finished, she could not speak. But she lifted her lips—and kissed his softly, as if that single touch was the simplest, most perfect “yes” imaginable.

They stood together, hands clasped, hearts full. No words were needed. They had found each other—through sorrow, through silence, through love.

Then, softly, Mirah whispered, “Let us go and comfort Ezra.”
