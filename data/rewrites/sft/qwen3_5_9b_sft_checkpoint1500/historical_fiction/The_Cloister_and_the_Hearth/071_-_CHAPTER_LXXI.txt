"As frail as the leaf upon the bough, so fleeting is the span of mortal men." Thus spoke the voice of time over Rotterdam, where in this same year, a giant silently budded within a humble tailor's shop, while a colossus of power fell with a thunderous crash.

At Bruges, Philip, the Duke of Burgundy, lay dying. For the poor, sickness came and went as nature whimsically decreed; but for the wealthy, a cruel law held sway: if one physician failed, three might perish under his care. The Duke's ailment, though now known by the clinical name of diphtheria, was then a silent, wasting evil. He was old, and his blood grew thin. So Dr. Remedy, the royal surgeon, turned to the ancient arts of leeching, draining the life from his veins until the great man turned icy cold.

When bleeding proved insufficient, the doctor called upon the arcane secrets of science. "Behold!" cried he, his voice trembling with anticipation. "Flay an ape without delay and bind its skin to the Duke's heart!"

Panic swept through the chambers like a storm. Officers of high estate scrambled in every direction, their voices rising in a frantic chorus as they sought the elusive primate. Yet, perdition awaited them. No ape remained. Buffaloes, lizards, Turks, and leopards filled the halls—beasts wild and unreasoning, save for the specific simian required.

"Why, surely there was an ape here just moments ago!" cried one officer, pointing a shaking finger. "I saw it standing by the door!"

Indeed, it had been there, lively and spirited. But fate, ever jealous, had intervened. A mastiff, hungry for the stolen scraps of dinner, had torn the creature limb from limb, fulfilling the ancient human adage: *Be of your generation*, and face the consequences.

Despair settled heavily upon the shoulders of the seneschal. He gazed around the room, searching for hope, when suddenly, a glimmer of light pierced the gloom. He leaned forward, speaking softly to the chancellor. "Look here. This garment... it bears a resemblance. Doublet and hose aside, the pattern is distinctly apelike."

"Nonsense!" snapped the chancellor, his voice sharp with irritation. "Princess Marie would have us hanged for such a grotesque substitute. She cherishes these things above all else."

It was true; the princess, our friend Giles, strutted through the hall in robes of gold, utterly unaware of the tragedy unfolding, her mind fixed on vanity rather than the dying king.

Impatient with the delays, Dr. Remedy issued a final, desperate command. "Flay a dog!"

"A dog is the nearest kin to the ape," the doctor insisted, "but it must be of a single hue."

A liver-colored canine was seized. With terrible efficiency, it was skinned and pinned to the Duke's chest, still beating wildly against the cold flesh. Within moments, the great Philip the Good ceased to breathe.

Thus ended the reign of a prince who, according to Flemish chronicles, traced his lineage back through Thierry of Aquitaine and the ancient kings of Franconia to the Trojan throne. Yet, in truth, such genealogy meant little. Every ruler of that age, from the mighty to the lowly, claimed descent from Priam, for medieval history often treated Adam himself as merely another Trojan king.

His body was borne back to Burgundy, interred beneath a tomb of black marble in Dijon. The bells of Holland tolled for his passing, unaware that within their borders, in a modest dwelling, a destiny far greater than any duke's was being forged. As the song goes, no one knows where the wise man dies, and few care. Dukes come and go, leaving the world no richer, no wiser, no kinder.

Yet, when Heaven chooses to kindle a torch after centuries of darkness, the light shines from pole to pole, proclaiming *Let there be Light*.
