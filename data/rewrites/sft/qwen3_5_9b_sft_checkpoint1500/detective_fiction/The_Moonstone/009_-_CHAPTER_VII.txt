In this state of mental disarray, acutely requiring solitude to restore my equilibrium, my daughter Penelope intercepted me. Her approach mirrored the interruptions of her late mother on the staircase, instantly summoning me to recount the entirety of the conference between Mr. Franklin and myself. Given the precarious nature of the situation, the optimal strategy was to snuff out Penelope's curiosity immediately. I therefore fabricated a tale, assuring her that Mr. Franklin and I had engaged in a lengthy discourse on foreign affairs until exhaustion compelled us both to succumb to slumber beneath the scorching sun. One must exercise caution when employing such evasions with a woman, relying ultimately on their innate capacity for forgiveness and reconciliation should the truth eventually surface.

As the afternoon waned, Lady Verinder and Miss Rachel returned. Naturally, they were taken aback to learn that Mr. Franklin Blake had arrived and subsequently departed on horseback within the same hour. Equally inevitable was their demand for further particulars, rendering my previous fabrication regarding "foreign politics" and "sun-induced sleep" utterly inadequate. Deprived of plausible deniability, I attributed Mr. Franklin's precipitate return via the early train to one of his eccentric whims. When pressed whether his sudden departure on horseback constituted another of these idiosyncrasies, I admitted it was, thereby extricating myself from the predicament with what I deemed considerable dexterity.

Once the ladies' inquiries were satisfactorily stymied, I retreated to my chamber, only to find further complications awaiting me. Penelope entered, bringing with her the customary female warmth to mend the breach, coupled with an insatiable curiosity that demanded another response. This time, however, her inquiry concerned the condition of our second housemaid, Rosanna Spearman. Since departing for the Shivering Sand with Mr. Franklin, Rosanna had returned in a state of profound bewilderment. According to Penelope, she exhibited a kaleidoscope of emotions, oscillating between unexplained mirth and inexplicable melancholy. She alternated between interrogating the servants about Mr. Franklin Blake with fervor and expressing indignation at Penelope's presumption that a stranger could hold any significance for her. At moments she smiled and scribbled the gentleman's name within her workbox; at others, she wept while gazing at her deformed shoulder in the mirror. Could there have been a prior acquaintance? Utterly impossible. Had they exchanged glances previously? Again, highly unlikely. Yet, the genuineness of Rosanna's stare and the intensity of her questioning were undeniable. The interrogation grew tiresome until Penelope, with a gravity that startled me, proffered what I considered the most preposterous hypothesis I had ever encountered.

"Father!" she declared solemnly, "there is but one explanation. Rosanna has fallen in love with Mr. Franklin Blake at first sight!"

One is accustomed to hearing tales of beautiful maidens falling in love instantaneously, viewing it as a natural, albeit romantic, occurrence. However, the notion of a former reformatory inmate, possessing neither beauty nor physical symmetry, experiencing such a sentiment at first glance toward a visiting gentleman of her mistress's household, defies all known logic. I laughed until tears streamed down my face, finding the absurdity irresistible. Penelope, however, perceived my amusement as an act of cruelty. "I have never known you to be so harsh, Father," she remarked softly, and withdrew.

Her words struck me like a bucket of ice-cold water. A savage self-reproach seized me, for I felt unease ripple through my very soul the moment she spoke, though I could not articulate why. Let us divert the subject, for I am reluctant to dwell upon this thread, yet the necessity will become apparent as the narrative unfolds.

Evening fell, and the summons for dinner rang before Mr. Franklin returned from Frizinghall. I personally delivered his hot water, anticipating news of some calamity following his extraordinary delay. To my profound disappointment—and likely yours as well—nothing untoward had occurred. He had not encountered the trio of Indians, either en route or upon return. He had secured the Moonstone at the bank, describing it merely as a precious stone of immense value, and retained the receipt safely in his pocket. Descending the stairs, I felt a sense of anticlimax, the tension of the morning seemingly evaporated by this mundane resolution.

The details of the reunion between Mr. Franklin, his aunt, and his cousin remain shrouded in mystery to me. I would have preferred to wait upon the table that evening, yet doing so would have compromised my standing among the staff, a dignity I was already struggling to maintain in the eyes of my mistress. The intelligence I gathered later that evening came from Penelope and the footman. Penelope noted that Miss Rachel appeared particularly meticulous regarding her hairstyle and looked radiant and composed as she descended to greet Mr. Franklin. The footman reported that maintaining respectful composure while attending to Mr. Franklin Blake at dinner represented a formidable challenge for his training. Later, the sounds of music drifted through the open windows onto the terrace: Mr. Franklin piping a high melody, Miss Rachel joining with a higher pitch, and my lady accompanying them on the piano, weaving a harmonious tapestry that sounded wonderfully enchanting. Still later, I visited Mr. Franklin in the smoking room, bearing soda water and brandy. I discovered that Miss Rachel had entirely eclipsed the diamond in his thoughts. "She is the most captivating girl I have seen since returning to England," was all I could coax from him when attempting to steer the conversation toward matters of consequence.

Approaching midnight, I began the rounds to secure the house, accompanied as per custom by Samuel, the footman. Once every door was locked save for the side entrance leading to the terrace, I dismissed Samuel to his rest and ventured outside for a breath of fresh air before retiring.

The night was stiflingly quiet, the moon hanging full and luminous in the heavens. So profound was the silence that I could discern, intermittently and faintly, the rhythmic crashing of the surf against the sandbank near the bay's mouth. While the terrace side lay in shadow, the broad moonlight illuminated the gravel path running alongside it. Gazing upward at the sky, then turning my gaze along the path, I observed the distinct shadow of a figure cast forward from behind the corner of the house.

Being elderly and cunning, I refrained from calling out; yet, unfortunately burdened by age and weight, my footsteps betrayed me on the gravel. Before I could swiftly circle the corner as planned, I heard lighter steps retreating in haste—more than a single pair, I judged. By the time I reached the corner, the intruders, whomever they may have been, had fled into the shrubbery at the far side of the walk, disappearing into the dense thicket of trees and bushes. From there, they could easily cross the fence into the road. Had I possessed forty fewer years, I might have intercepted them before they cleared the grounds. As it stood, I returned to mobilize a younger pair of legs. Undisturbed, Samuel and I retrieved a couple of firearms and swept the perimeter of the house and the shrubbery. Finding no evidence of lingering occupants, we returned. Crossing the path where I had spied the shadow, I noticed for the first time a small, glowing object resting on the clean gravel beneath the moonlight. Upon picking it up, I identified it as a tiny vial containing a viscous, sweet-smelling liquid, as dark as ink itself.

I said nothing to Samuel. However, recalling Penelope's account of the jugglers and the ritualistic pouring of a similar ink-like substance into the palm of the boy, suspicion immediately seized me. I concluded that I had inadvertently disturbed three Indians who were lurking about the estate, bent, in their pagan fashion, upon discovering the whereabouts of the Diamond during that very night.
