We weave dreams of what might be,
But life unfolds in the loom of real time—
Where passion threads the shuttle,
And deeds take shape in patterns we cannot deny.

Gwendolen’s note, arriving just as Klesmer was preparing to leave Quetcham, found its way into his hands at the perfect moment—neither too soon nor too late. To honor her plea for kindness, he stayed the night at Wanchester, despite the inconvenience it caused him. There were reasons why he could not remain at Quetcham any longer.

The grand estate, built with no expense spared, had grown too stifling for him. Its owners, much like certain powerful figures in politics, had been caught off guard by a quiet rebellion against the order they believed unshakable—though afterward, from a distance, one can see how the seeds of resistance had long been sown beneath their very feet.

As usual, the house buzzed with guests, among them a man whom Miss Arrowpoint saw as a new contender for her hand: Mr. Bult, a well-connected politician of noble lineage who confidently expected a peerage and felt, on grounds of public standing, that he required a substantial fortune to match the dignity of his future title. Heiresses are never uniform—those who seek them often find fault with their appearance, temperament, or manner; yet there is always one assumption: she will see herself as an appendage to her wealth, marrying whoever others believe her fortune should join. But nature rarely obeys such tidy plans. It does not favor only children who grow up without siblings—and sometimes, even more surprisingly, it endows the daughter of privilege with a mind of her own, sharp and resolute. The Arrowpoints had already begun to fret over Catherine’s independence. She refused to accept the notion that her duty was to marry a poor nobleman or a commoner climbing the social ladder. Their anxiety grew as she continued to reject suitable matches. As for the idea that she might be in love with Klesmer—such a thought did not trouble them at all. A common blindness, indeed. For people often marvel at effects they themselves have helped create, while failing to notice those they’ve merely wished for. Parents are astonished when their sons are ignorant, though they’ve paid for the most expensive tutors. Husbands and wives are surprised when affection fades, though they’ve done nothing to preserve it. And each of us, in turn, wonders why others don’t admire us—forgetting that admiration must be earned, not assumed. In this way, truth becomes improbable. Truth is not shaped by our desires, but by reality. And now, the Arrowpoints’ moment of shock had arrived.

When an heiress falls for a proud, independent man, understanding between them is rare. Yet if that pride doesn’t harden into constant refusal, the connection may still flourish. Brief meetings after long absences can reveal hidden feelings—but nothing is more revealing than sustained companionship, marked by mutual taste and shared values. Especially when one acts as teacher and the other as eager student, delighted in their ability to learn, and equally delighted in teaching. This dynamic has echoed through history and holds no less charm today than it did in the days of Abelard.

Yet such comparisons had never crossed the Arrowpoints’ minds when they first invited Klesmer to Quetcham. Having a master musician in residence was a mark of distinction; Catherine’s talent demanded the best instruction, and she especially wanted to deepen her studies during quieter country days. Klesmer was not yet a Liszt, whose name stirred the hearts of ladies across Europe (except perhaps in Lapland). Even so, it didn’t follow that he would propose to an heiress. No honorable musician would do such a thing. And certainly, Catherine would never give him reason to think she’d consider it. The large check Mr. Arrowpoint intended to issue in his name made Klesmer seem as harmless as a footman. Where marriage was unthinkable, a young woman’s heart was safe.

Klesmer was unquestionably a man of honor. But marriages rarely begin with formal proposals. And Catherine’s sense of what was possible did not quite align with her mother’s.

Others might have thought Klesmer’s position dangerous—if Miss Arrowpoint had been considered beautiful. But they overlooked the deeper truth: the most compelling beauty is not seen at first glance, but revealed slowly, through sympathy and understanding. There is a magic in the eye and lip that light up with every subtle phrase, showing sensitivity and judgment; in the quiet word or gentle smile that reveals a soul attentive to others. No movement of dress or posture is more moving than one that restores confidence—that assures you someone is present, fully aware, and worthy of your trust. How much meaning gathers in frowns and laughter that never misplace themselves! What grace radiates from a body animated by a mind capable of insight and hands skilled in expression! The kind of beauty that dazzles at first—the kind we call "adorable"—often begins as an excuse for folly, and ends, like all excuses, in boredom. That Klesmer, though deeply drawn to such beauty, should fall passionately for Miss Arrowpoint was no paradox, but rather a triumph of complex connection over shallow attraction. We bristle less at being accused of passionate excess than at lacking broader passion. But if truth be told, our supposed intensity often stems not from depth, but from emptiness—having no better use for ourselves. Tannhäuser, we suspect, was a knight of limited imagination, no more eloquent than a bored guardsman. Merlin, once brilliant, had aged into repetition when he fell captive. And Ulysses himself confessed to boredom under Calypso’s spell—so much so that she helped him leave. There is even a rumor he later left Penelope. But since she spent her days absorbed in knitting, and likely passed on her narrow-mindedness to Telemachus—always anxious about property and meat consumption—we can draw no firm conclusions about the link between companionship and fidelity.

Klesmer, on sufficient acquaintance, was as versatile and captivating as a young Ulysses—one whom nature seemed to bless generously, then gifted with music as a dominant force. Like Mendelssohn, he expressed himself not only in flawless execution, but in the fervor of creation and belief that gave his entire life a purposeful glow. His flaws—arrogance, vanity—were no greater than those found in many English families. And Catherine, though naturally kind, was perhaps too cool and self-contained. Yet she was one of those rare souls whose presence invites discovery—whose honesty and clarity stir curiosity about what she’ll say next, how she’ll perform any task. With her, companionship became a rhythm of anticipation and fulfillment—a dance of joy. Over time, the image in the mind came to mirror the person in flesh. Soon both realized they were deeply drawn to each other—but the question of how far remained uncertain. Klesmer didn’t imagine Miss Arrowpoint could ever see him as a lover. And she, accustomed to believing men admired her only for her fortune, never feared stirring anything beyond friendship. Each accepted a quiet ache, a half-acknowledged longing, for the sake of loving the other’s company too deeply. Thus, for a year, Klesmer’s visits—both in town and country—had gone unchecked. He knew full well that if Catherine were poor, he would have pursued her passionately instead of storming the piano or delivering fiery rants about distant places. And she, in turn, understood that if Klesmer had ever desired her hand, she would have given it willingly. Such was the safety of full cups—no more likely to overflow than empty ones, unless disturbed. But silence, like a clock’s unseen hand, moves forward. On this visit to Quetcham, Klesmer began to feel he would not return. Catherine, meanwhile, noticed his growing sharpness—what she saw as unnecessary attempts to assert dominance in every way except convention.

Then entered Mr. Bult, the anticipated peer. A respected political figure, neutral in private life but fervent in debate, he spoke with authority on the Niger, Brazil, and the South Seas. He polished his speeches with care, exuding the solid pinkness of a healthy Englishman in midlife. Catherine, aware of the unspoken agreement that he was the ideal husband for an heiress, had only one objection: he was profoundly tedious. Mr. Bult, amiably confident, couldn’t imagine his ignorance of musical counterpoint being a flaw. He barely regarded Klesmer as a serious human being worthy of a vote, and cared little for Catherine’s passion for music—or her likely spending on antique lace. So when Klesmer erupted after dinner, condemning English politics for lacking idealism and reducing relations between peoples to mere trade, Mr. Bult was taken aback. The crusades, Klesmer argued, at least had a banner of sentiment around which noble feelings could gather—even if scoundrels joined too. But now, everything rallied around slogans like “Buy cheap, sell dear.” His speech, gesticulatory and vivid, burned like stray fireworks before fading into silence. Mr. Bult wasn8t surprised by Klesmer’s opinions—he expected them to be erratic—but was startled by his command of English and his rhetorical flair. Likely, he mused, due to Klesmer’s Polish or Czech roots, a refugee forced into music. Later, in the drawing-room, Mr. Bult approached Klesmer at the piano—Catherine nearby—and said,

“I never knew you were a political man.”

Klesmer replied only by folding his arms, sticking out his lower lip, and staring.

“You must have experience in public speaking,” Mr. Bult continued. “You speak remarkably well, even if I disagree. From your talk of sentiment, I suspect you’re a Panslavist.”

“No,” Klesmer said, flashing a smile at Catherine. “My name is Elijah. I am the Wandering Jew.” Then, with a sudden burst, he played a mysterious, wind-like passage on the piano. Mr. Bult found it offensive—too Polish, too theatrical—but hesitated to leave, seeing Catherine near.

“Herr Klesmer has cosmopolitan views,” Catherine interjected, trying to smooth things over. “He believes in the fusion of races.”

“With all my heart,” Mr. Bult replied, eager to appear gracious. “I knew he had too much talent to be just a musician.”

“Oh, sir,” Klesmer retorted, rising sharply, “you’re mistaken. No man has too much talent to be a musician. Most men have too little. A true artist is no more ‘just’ a musician than a great statesman is just a politician. We are not puppets, sir, confined to a box and brought out only when the world wants amusement. We help shape nations and define eras, as much as any lawmaker. We stand beside legislators. And one who speaks through music faces a challenge harder than parliamentary rhetoric.”

With that, Klesmer turned and walked away.

Catherine flushed. Mr. Bult observed, as usual, with calm detachment, “Your pianist doesn’t think lightly of himself.”

“Herr Klesmer is more than a pianist,” she said, defending him. “He is a great musician—true in every sense. He will stand with Schubert and Mendelssohn.”

“Ah,” Mr. Bult replied, “you ladies understand these things.” Still, he remained convinced music was frivolous—especially after Klesmer’s display of arrogance.

Catherine, always uneasy when Klesmer behaved proudly, found a chance the next day in the music room to say, “Why were you so harsh with Mr. Bult last night? He meant no harm.”

“You wish me to be polite to him?” Klesmer asked, voice tight.

“I think it’s hardly worth your effort to be otherwise.”

“You tolerate him easily, then? You respect a political drone who feels nothing outside political gain? You think his monumental dullness fits the dignity of the English gentleman?”

“I didn’t say that.”

“You mean I acted without dignity—and you’re offended.”

“Now you’re closer,” Catherine smiled.

“Then I should pack my burial clothes and leave at once.”

“I don’t see why. If I endure your criticism of my operetta, you should bear mine of your impatience.”

“But I can’t,” Klesmer insisted. “You want me to ignore his ignorance, to let him insult music and artists without reply. I’m to hear my gods mocked and myself ridiculed. But I apologize—I know you can’t grasp the artist’s anger. To you, we belong to another caste.”

“That’s true,” Catherine admitted, emotion flickering. “You belong to a caste I admire—one above mine.”

Klesmer, seated at a table reviewing scores, rose abruptly and stepped back, saying, “That’s beautifully said—I’m grateful. But I should go anyway. I’ve decided. You’ll manage perfectly without me—your operetta is already moving forward. And Mr. Bult’s company suits me perfectly. I have engagements. I must go to St. Petersburg.”

No answer.

“You agree I should go?” Klesmer asked, irritation creeping in.

“Certainly—if that’s what your duty and heart dictate. I only wonder why you’ve stayed so long. There must be triple the interest elsewhere. I’ve never seen your visits as anything but sacrifice.”

“Why should I sacrifice?” Klesmer asked, walking to the piano and playing softly, delicately, a melody set to Heine’s “Ich hab’ dich geliebet und liebe dich noch.”

“That’s the mystery,” Catherine murmured, trembling. Her hands tore paper into tiny shreds, as if under a cruel fairy’s spell.

“You can’t guess my motive?” Klesmer asked, arms folded.

“None that seems probable.”

“Then I’ll tell you. You are the chief woman in my world—the queen whose colors I carry between my heart and armor.”

Her hands shook too violently to tear paper. Her lips could not form words.

Klesmer continued, “This would be the ultimate impertinence—if I meant to build anything on it. That’s impossible. I don’t intend such a thing. But you once said it was your fate to suspect every suitor of being an adventurer, and what angered you most was men accusing you of foolishly believing they loved you for yourself. Did you not say so?”

“Very likely,” she whispered.

“A bitter truth. Well, at least one man who has seen women as plentiful as May flowers has lingered near you—for your own sake. And since he is one you can never marry, you’ll believe him. That’s proof enough for some other man. But don’t surrender to a minotaur like Bult. I’ll pack now. I’ll explain to Mrs. Arrowpoint.”

He rose and moved toward the door.

“You must take this manuscript,” Catherine said suddenly, gathering courage. She fetched the pile from another table. Klesmer returned. They stood separated only by the sheets.

“Why shouldn’t I marry the man who loves me, if I love him?” she asked. To her, it felt like leaping from a ship into a lifeboat.

“It would be too hard—impossible. You couldn’t endure it. I’m not worth what you’d face. I won’t accept your sacrifice. It would be a _mésalliance_—and I’d be blamed.”

“Are you afraid of gossip?” she asked. “I fear only that we might miss the chance to live together.”

The decisive word was spoken. There was no doubt about their desire—only the path to reach it. Catherine chose the direct route. She went to her parents in the library and announced she had promised to marry Klesmer.

Mrs. Arrowpoint’s reaction was heartbreaking. Imagine Jean-Jacques Rousseau, after writing about the corrupting influence of art, waking to find himself among primitives who offer raw bone for breakfast with a flint knife. Or Saint Just, after denouncing all hierarchy, receiving praise for his mediocrity. Something similar happened to the author of *Tasso*, when her own daughter enacted what she had once demanded of Leonora. It’s hard to live up to our own ideals while grounded in reality. Literature’s proprieties differ from life’s. Mrs. Arrowpoint wanted the best of both worlds—not only to surpass her peers in literary taste, but also in social standing. While Klesmer was a charming musician, tolerated as an eccentric guest, the thought of him as her son-in-law ignited a fierce dread of what society would say. And she had long portrayed Catherine as a model of virtue.

At first, she forgot everything but fury, seizing any words to wound.

“If Klesmer dared propose to you, your father shall horsewhip him off the premises. Speak, Mr. Arrowpoint!”

The father removed his cigar and responded gravely, “This will never do, Cath.”

“Do!” Mrs. Arrowpoint cried. “Who in their right mind ever thought it would? You might as well say poisoning and strangling are acceptable. This is a farce, Catherine. Or you’re mad.”

“I’m sane and serious, mamma. Herr Klesmer isn’t to blame. He never dreamed I’d marry him. I discovered he loved me—and loving him, I told him I would.”

“Don’t say that,” Mrs. Arrowpoint snapped. “Everyone else will say it for you. You’ll be a public joke. People will say you offered yourself to a man paid to come here—who is nobody knows what—a gypsy, a Jew, a bubble on the earth.”

“Never mind, mamma,” Catherine replied, indignant. “We all know he’s a genius—as Tasso was.”

“Those times were different,” Mrs. Arrowpoint insisted. “And Klesmer is no Tasso.”

“The sarcasm stings only because it’s rooted in disrespect.”

“I’m sorry to hurt you, mamma. But I won’t give up my happiness for beliefs I don’t hold or customs I don’t respect.”

“You’ve lost all sense of duty? You forget you’re our only child—our responsibility to place the family fortune in worthy hands?”

“What are ‘worthy hands’? My grandfather earned this land through trade.”

“Mr. Arrowpoint, will you sit silent while this goes on?”

“I’m a gentleman, Cath. We expect you to marry a gentleman,” the father said, straining.

“And a man tied to the institutions of this country,” added the mother. “A woman in your position has serious duties. When duty and desire clash, she must choose duty.”

“I don’t deny that,” Catherine said, growing colder. “But people misuse the word ‘duty’ to justify what they want others to do.”

“Doesn’t your parents’ desire make no duty for you?”

“Yes, within reason. But before I give up my happiness—”

“Catherine, Catherine, it won’t be happiness,” Mrs. Arrowpoint warned, voice darkening.

“Well, what I believe is happiness—before I give it up, I must see a better reason than wanting me to marry a nobleman or a man who votes to become one. I have the right to marry the man I love and respect—unless a higher duty forbids.”

“And it does forbid, Catherine, though you’re blinded. A woman’s duty is not to lower herself. You are lowering yourself. Mr. Arrowpoint, tell your daughter her duty.”

“You must see, Catherine, that Klesmer is not right for you,” the father said. “He won’t manage estates. He looks foreign. He’s impractical.”

“I don’t see how that matters, papa. England’s land has passed to foreigners before—Dutch soldiers, sons of foreign women of questionable reputation. If our land were sold tomorrow, it might go to a foreign merchant on ’Change. Everyone says successful swindlers could buy half the country. How can I stop that tide?”
