The Answer to the Overture

Ethne did not turn toward Durrance. She sat rigid, her violin resting on her knees, staring fixedly at the band of silver moonlight cutting through the trees. She held her posture with deliberate stubbornness, a physical shield against the reality of the speaker. By focusing on the illusion that Harry Feversham himself was standing there, speaking directly to her soul, she could suppress the intrusive knowledge that he was merely using Durrance's mouth. For a fleeting moment, Durrance ceased to exist in her mind; she listened with his own intensity, holding her breath, desperate for the voice to drag out its tale, stretching every second so she could clutch the meaning to her heart like a drowning man grasping driftwood.

"It was the night before I started eastward into the desert—for the last time," Durrance began. Even the profound regret in his tone, dwelling on that final departure, failed to touch Ethne's cold resolve.

"Yes," she murmured, her voice steady. "February. The middle of the month, was it not? Tell me the exact day."

"The fifteenth," Durrance replied. Ethne repeated the date silently, letting it settle in the hollow space where her emotions used to live.

"I was at Glenalla all of February," she said, though the question hung in the air, unanswered. "What was I doing on the fifteenth? It matters little now."

A strange, disorienting shock had seized her throughout Captain Willoughby's storytelling earlier that morning—the realization that she possessed no instinctive knowledge of these specific moments as they happened. Now, waiting for this August night, this garden of moonlight and closed blossoms, to learn of the encounter between Feversham and Durrance, the shock returned with a vengeance. Remorse flared briefly, a hot stab in her chest. *"It was my own fault,"* she chided herself inwardly. *"If I had kept my faith in him, I would have known at once. I am well punished."* Yet, the thought never crossed her mind that this message could bring anything but the finest news. It would carry the good tidings she had already heard, enlarging and completing them, rounding this day to perfection. She was certain of it.

"Well?" she urged softly. "Go on!"

"I had been busy all day in my office, finishing my work," Durrance recounted, his voice weaving a tapestry of distant tragedy. "I turned the key at ten, relieved that for six weeks I wouldn't open it again, and strolled northward along the Nile bank into the little town of Tewfikieh. As I entered the main street, I saw a small crowd—Arabs, Negroes, a Greek or two, and some Egyptian soldiers—gathered outside a café, illuminated by a harsh glare from within. Approaching closer, I heard the sound of a violin and a zither, both played with a vile, jingling incompetence, dragging out a waltz.

I stood behind the crowd, peering over the shoulders of the men ahead into the room. It was a place of four bare, whitewashed walls; a bar stood in one corner, a couple of wooden benches lined the sides, and a single, unshaded paraffin lamp swung and glared from the ceiling. A troupe of itinerant musicians played for a night's lodging and the price of a meal. There were four of them, and so far as I could see, all four were Greeks. Two were evidently man and wife. Both were old, both slatternly, almost in rags. The man was thin and sallow-faced, with grey hair and a black moustache; the woman was fat, coarse of feature, unwieldy in body.

Of the other two, one appeared to be their daughter—a girl of seventeen. She was not beautiful, truly, but she was dressed and turned out with a scrupulous care that lent her an appearance of goodness amidst the sordid surroundings. Indeed, the care with which she was clad assured me she was their daughter. To tell the truth, I was rather touched by the thought that the parents would wear rags so that she might at least appear trim. A clean ribbon bound back her hair; an untorn frock of some white stuff clung neatly to her form; even her shoes were neat. The fourth was a young man, seated in the window with his back to me, bending over his zither. But I could see that he wore a beard.

When I stepped up, the old man was playing the violin, though *playing* was hardly the word. The noise he made was more like the squeaking of a pencil on a slate; it set one's teeth on edge. The violin itself seemed to squeal with pain. While he fiddled and the young man hammered at his zither, the old woman and the girl slowly revolved in a waltz. It sounds comic to recount, but if you could have seen... it fairly plucked at one's heart. I do not think I have ever in my life witnessed anything quite so sad. The little crowd outside, the Negroes laughing at the troupe, passing from one to another with low, untranslatable jests, while inside the four white souls suffered: the old woman, clumsy and heavy-footed, shining with heat, lumbering round slowly, panting; the girl, lissom and young; the two men with their discordant, torturing music; and just above them, the great planets and stars of an African sky, and just below, the great silent, spacious dignity of the moonlit desert. Imagine it! The very ineptness of the entertainment hurt."

He paused, allowing Ethne to picture the scene. She saw Harry Feversham bending over his zither, and immediately the question arose in her mind: *What was he doing with that troupe?* It was intelligible enough that he would not care to return to England. It was certain that he would not come back to her unless she summoned him. From what Captain Willoughby had said, she knew he expected no message from her; Willoughby had not left a name where a letter could reach him. But what was he doing at Wadi Halfa, masquerading with this itinerant group? He had money, Willoughby had told her.

"You spoke to him?" she asked suddenly, breaking the spell.

"To whom? Oh, to Harry?" Durrance returned. "Yes, afterwards, when I found out it was he who was playing the zither."

"Yes, how did you find out?" Ethne pressed.

"The waltz came to an end," Durrance continued. "The old woman sank exhausted onto the bench against the whitewashed wall; the young man raised his head from his zither; the old man scraped a new chord upon his violin, and the girl stepped forward to sing. Her voice had youth and freshness, but no other quality of music. Her singing was as inept as the rest of the entertainment. Yet the old man smiled, the mother beat time with her heavy foot, and nodded at her husband with pride in their daughter's accomplishment. And again in the throng, the ill-conditioned talk, the untranslatable jests of the Arabs and the Negroes, went their rounds. It was horrible, don't you think?"

"Yes," answered Ethne, slowly, in an absent voice. Just as she had felt no sympathy for Durrance when he began, so she spared none for these three outcasts of fortune. She was too absorbed in the mystery of Harry Feversham's presence at Wadi Halfa. She listened too closely for the message he sent. Through the open window, the moon threw a broad panel of silver light onto the floor near her feet. She sat gazing into it, as though it were a window through which, if she looked hard enough, she might see that lighted café blazing upon the street of the little town of Tewfikieh on the frontier of the Sudan.

"Well?" she asked. "And after the song was ended?"

"The young man with his back to me," Durrance resumed, "began to fumble out a solo upon the zither. He struck so many false notes that no tune was to be apprehended at first. Laughter and noise grew amongst the crowd, and I was just turning away, rather sick at heart, when some notes—a succession of notes played correctly by chance—suddenly arrested me. I listened again, and a sort of haunting melody began to emerge. It was a weak, thin thing with no soul in it, a ghost of a melody, and yet familiar. I stood listening in the street of sand, between the hovels fringed by a row of stunted trees, and I was carried away out of the East to Ramelton, to a summer night beneath a melting sky in Donegal, when you sat by the open window as you sit now, playing the Mussolini Overture, which you have played again tonight."

"It was a melody from this overture?" she exclaimed, her composure cracking slightly.

"Yes, and it was Harry Feversham who played the melody. I did not guess it at once. I was not very quick in those days."

"But you are now," said Ethne.

"Quicker, at all events. I should have guessed it now. Then, however, I was only curious. I wondered how it was that an itinerant Greek came to pick up the tune. At all events, I determined to reward him for his diligence. I thought you would like me to."

"Yes," said Ethne, in a whisper.
