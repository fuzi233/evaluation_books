Mrs. Lambert’s little holiday of fancy ended as such holidays commonly do: not with a thunderclap, but with the quiet click of a latch, and the sense of a room returning to its usual proportions. In the space of three days, Miss Theo and her mother had been obliged—tenderly, almost reluctantly—to confess to themselves that the pale young gentleman who had arrived at their gate with blood on his sleeve was not, after all, a creature minted out of romance, but an ordinary mortal with an ordinary appetite, an ordinary awkwardness, and a very real need of clean linen.

They spoke little of it. Love in a household has its own shorthand: a glance over the rim of a teacup, a pause while a ribbon is tied, a finger smoothing a crease in a sleeve that does not need smoothing. Mrs. Lambert, who could weep conscientiously over a printed sorrow, tried to console her daughter without naming the thing that hurt.

“Never mind, my dear,” she whispered in the way a kiss whispers, warm on Theo’s cheek. “Our hero proves human; and none such is good enough for my Theo. Thou shalt have a real husband ere long, if there be one in England. Why, I was scarce fifteen when your father first saw me at Bury Assembly; and while I was yet at school I vowed I’d never have any other man. If Heaven gave me such a husband—the best man in the whole kingdom—sure it will bless my child equally, who deserves a king if she fancies him!”

And indeed, with the same earnestness with which she ordered her still-room to be aired in damp weather, Mrs. Lambert seemed to order Providence to send greatness down her drive. No coach rattled past the gate but, in her mind, it might as well have been rolling straight from a fairy-tale: some young gentleman was sure to alight, ring the bell, and, between the tea-tray and the parlour fire, be gently apprenticed to love. It was even possible—she knew the Prince of Wales’s age, and had heard how handsome and good a young prince was—that she expected royalty itself to come cantering by, tumble obligingly from its horse, be carried in, cured, and afterwards reward Colonel Lambert with a regiment, and fall in love with Theo in the most proper and affecting manner.

Colonel Lambert, for his part, and his second daughter, Miss Hetty, took their stand on the laughing side, the side that pricks sentiment with a pin and then begs pardon for the prick by kissing hard. Mrs. Lambert had her novels; Hetty had her tongue. It ran as briskly as a spit before a clear fire, turning every subject and basting it with mischief.

She made a hundred sly allusions to their guest—so sly that they were not sly at all to the two listeners at whom they were aimed. She brought Prussia and Persia tumbling into talk with abominable pertness and frequency. She asked, with her head on one side, whether the present King of Prussia was called the Shaw or the Sophy; how far it might be from Ispahan to Saxony, which his Majesty was at present invading, as papa might see upon his maps and newspapers; and whether a person could travel thither with no more baggage than a gentleman’s valise and a set shoulder.

She fetched down the Persian Tales from her mamma’s closet and laid them, as if by accident, on the parlour table where the family sat. She declared she would not marry a Persian prince, not for her part: she preferred a gentleman who did not keep more than one wife at a time. She called Mr. Warrington Theo’s prince, and asked her mother, with a saucy bob, whether she wished Hetty to take the other visitor—the black prince—for herself. Then, after she had teased until Theo’s colour rose and Mrs. Lambert’s eyes filled, she would begin to cry too, as if she had been the injured party, and would rush in, kissing them violently one after the other, coaxing them back into good humour with the same hands that had pinched them.

All the while, Harry Warrington—simple Harry—knew nothing. He was the innocent occasion of this domestic play, and as ignorant of its plot as a fish is of the ripple made by a thrown stone. The arrows of “Prussia” and “Persia” whistled by him, and he did not so much as turn his head. A Shaw? A Sophy? He likely imagined a Sophy to be a lady, and would have laughed outright at the notion that a man with a great black beard should be called so. He was not stupid, though Miss Hetty in her impatience thought him so: he was merely full of his own honest affairs, and shy of women in that very common way of young men who have lived hitherto with horses and guns and plain household decencies, and have not yet learned that a look across a room can be a sentence in a language they do not know they are speaking.

So it happened that, three days after his arrival, the hospitable family walked with him in my Lord Wrotham’s park, where Colonel Lambert had leave to wander as a neighbour and friend. The day had the polished clearness that belongs to the end of autumn holidays: a thin sun, a faint bite in the air, and the steady, comforting hush of old trees holding back the wind. On a piece of water, the swans came sailing towards them with that calm authority which makes even a pond seem like court. The young ladies were accustomed to feed the birds with bread, and their fingers broke the crust into small white pieces as if they were preparing a dish rather than bribing a creature.

Hetty, with a queer look at her mother and sister and then a glance at her father—honest and content in a red waistcoat—said suddenly, “Mamma’s swans are something like these, papa.”

“What swans, my dear?” says mamma, puzzled.

“Something like, but not quite. They have shorter necks than these, and are, scores of them, on our common,” continues Miss Hetty with a gravity meant to be outrageous. “I saw Betty plucking one in the kitchen this morning. We shall have it for dinner, with apple-sauce and—”

“Don’t be a little goose!” cried Theo, with a quick, embarrassed laugh.

“—and sage and onions,” Hetty finished, as if she were dictating the very receipt. “Do you love swan, Mr. Warrington?”

“I shot three last winter on our river,” said the Virginian, as simply as if she had asked him whether he liked beef. “Ours are not such white birds as these—they eat very well, though.”

Mrs. Lambert gave a little sound between a protest and a sigh; Theo flushed; Colonel Lambert stared out over the water as if examining the birds’ wings. And Harry, who had answered honestly about the table-worth of a swan, had not the least notion that he himself had been served up in allegory and seasoned with Hetty’s impudence.

Theo’s first tender flutter—her soft shock of admiration when she had seen him at their gate, pale and bleeding—proved, in its own gentle way, like a lark’s note sounded before daybreak. It was not the great passion she had believed her heart capable of feeling. It had wakened too early, sung at a false dawn, and then, sensing the cold in the air, drawn back under the wing. A truer morning might come; but this was not it.

If the young gentleman could walk—and it appeared he could walk perfectly well—what business had he to be walking here at all? His shoulder was set; his health restored; he had not even a change of coats and was obliged to Colonel Lambert for raiment. He ought, by every tie of duty and convenience—by love, by relationship, by a gentle heart waiting for him, by the washerwoman, finally—to be at Tunbridge Wells with Lady Maria Esmond on the Pantiles. Why did he linger at Oakhurst unless he was in love with one of the young ladies (and he was not), or unless some invisible weight, some clinging fate, held him down?

Perhaps his fell aunt’s purpose was answered; perhaps Lady Maria’s late hope had been killed as neatly by poisonous communications as a garden plant is killed by a spoonful of brine. One may imagine the soundscape of Tunbridge without needing to be there: the bright, ceaseless chatter under the colonnade; the scrape of chairs; the fiddles that try too hard; the rattling of wheels on gravel; and, through it all, the harsher note of one dominating voice that makes every other voice smaller. Between her old aunt and her unhappy delusion, Lady Maria could be having no pleasant time. She was poor and hoped for money; she had secrets her aunt knew and brandished; and she stood alone in the crowd, where loneliness is loudest. One might fancy her, for all her faults and false adornments, as an antique Andromeda chained up for the dragon—her rich hair, true or not, falling over her shoulders—while the monster waited and waited, and Perseus loitered elsewhere, warm and comfortable, not in the least hurry.

Harry Warrington, who would not have allowed a mended shoulder to keep him from hunting, remained day after day at Oakhurst, and each day the kindly people who welcomed him grew more to his liking. Perhaps, since his grandfather’s death, he had not been in such good company. He had lived contentedly enough among fox-hunting Virginian squires, riding their horses, drinking their punch, and taking their talk as the measure of the world. The women of his home acquaintance were decorous and pious and well-bred, but narrow in their ways; the place itself had pompous little etiquettes and punctilios, small flatteries, small conversations, small scandals. He had not known, until he left it some time later, how confined his life had been, though he could ride and shoot for miles. The air at home was his mother’s air, and he breathed it whether it suited him or no.

Here, by contrast, was a circle incomparably gayer, brighter, freer; a house where the very sounds were different. The morning began not with a clatter of commands, but with the soft industry of a well-ordered kitchen—the crack of firewood, the steady chop of a knife, the low scold of a kettle coming to boil—sounds that promise sustenance rather than submission. Even the laughter in the parlour had a kind of clean echo, as if the walls had never learned to hoard malice.

Colonel Lambert had seen the world and come unwounded out of it, as he had out of dangers and battles. He had read more books than Harry could pretend to judge of, and had that unmistakable goodness and honesty written on his face and breathed from his lips which brave men instinctively recognise as their own coin. In such company Harry began, to his benefit, to learn that he was profoundly ignorant, and that there were many people far better than he had known how to imagine. He could be arrogant enough with those he thought his equals; in the presence of superiors he became docile with a magnanimity that did him more honour than any loud assurance could have done.

As for the women, they were the kindest, merriest, most agreeable he had yet known. They were pleasanter than Parson Broadbent’s black-eyed daughter at home, whose laugh could carry as far as a gun. They were as well-bred as the Castlewood ladies, save Madam Beatrix, who could be as grand as an empress on occasion. Yet after a talk with Madam Beatrix, for all the amusement and interest of her stories, Harry would come away with a bitter taste in his mouth, as though some bright dish had been flavoured with an herb too sharp, and he would fancy the world wicked round about him. Here there was no such aftertaste. Mrs. Lambert and her girls were not squeamish; they could laugh over pages of Mr. Fielding and cry over volumes of Mr. Richardson, though those books held jokes and incidents that would make Mrs. Grundy’s hair stand on end; yet their merry prattle left no bitterness behind. Their tales of neighbours were droll rather than cruel; their curtseys and salutations in the little town were kindly given and kindly received; their bounties were cordial, like well-made broth carried to a sick-bed without fuss.

Not much happened in those happy hours. It was sweet sleep, pleasant waking, friendly welcome, serene pastime: the kind of peace that later storms make memorable. The gates of the old house seemed to shut the wicked world outside, and the people within to be better and kinder than most. He was not in love—no, not in the least with saucy Hetty nor with generous Theodosia—but when the time came for him to go, he took both their hands and held them fast for a moment, and felt an immense regard for them. He thought he should like to know their brothers, and that they must be fine fellows. And as for Mrs. Lambert, she was as sentimental at his departure as if he had been the last volume of Clarissa Harlowe shut with a sigh.

From the terrace they saw him ride away on the road to Westerham, with Colonel Lambert beside him and servants following.

“He is very kind and honest,” said Theo gravely, watching the small figures diminish, the horses’ hoofbeats softening as the park swallowed sound.

“I don’t think him stupid at all now,” said little Hetty. “And, mamma, I think he is very like a swan indeed.”

“It felt just like one of the boys going to school,” said mamma, pressing her handkerchief to her mouth as if to hold the feeling in.

“Just like it,” Theo answered, and there was a sadness in her tone that was not only about Harry.

“I am glad he has got papa to ride with him to Westerham,” Hetty went on; “and that he bought Farmer Briggs’s horse. I don’t like his going to those Castlewood people. I am sure that Madame Bernstein is a wicked old woman. I expected to see her ride away on her crooked stick.”

“Hush, Hetty!”

“Do you think she would float if they tried her in the pond, as poor old Mother Hely did at Elmhurst? The other old woman seemed fond of him—I mean the one with the fair toupee. She looked very melancholy when she went away; but Madame Bernstein whisked her off with her crutch, and she was obliged to go. I don’t care, Theo. I know she is wicked. You think everybody good, you do, because you never do anything wrong yourself.”

“My Theo is a good girl,” Mrs. Lambert said, looking fondly at both daughters, as if the sight of their faces were a nourishment.

“Then why do we call her a miserable sinner?” Hetty demanded, quick as a sparrow.

“We are all so, my love,” said mamma, with a sigh that had been borrowed from sermons and sweetened by domestic habit.

“What, papa too? You know you don’t think so,” cried Miss Hester; and to allow that even Colonel Lambert might be a “miserable sinner” was almost more than Mrs. Lambert’s heart could afford.

Hetty’s eyes darted with sudden triumph. “What was that you told John to give to Mr. Warrington’s black man?”

Mamma coloured a little, shamefaced, and owned it was a bottle of her cordial water and a cake she had bid Betty make. It was the sort of cake her sons carried to school or college: rich with butter, close with eggs, studded perhaps with currants that had been soaked and dried, flavoured with a rasp of nutmeg from the still-room’s precious store; the kind that, when cut, gives off a warm, honest scent as of wheat and hearth and the hands that laboured for it.

“I feel quite like a mother to him, my dears,” she confessed softly. “I can’t help owning it—and you know both our boys still like one of our cakes to take with them.”
