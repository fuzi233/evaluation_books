Tito's intricate plans had been thwarted by trifling accidents that no calculating mind could foresee. Though he rarely strolled with Romola in the evenings, fate had conspired to place them together precisely on this fateful night, when her presence was utterly inconvenient to his designs. Life proved a labyrinthine game where even the most cunning strategies were undone by random chances, as unpredictable as falling dandelion seeds.

It was not that Tito cared deeply about the collapse of Spini's scheme; rather, he faced a delicate diplomatic dilemma. He needed to warn Savonarola without arousing suspicion among the populace, nor provoke the fierce, unchained wrath of Dolfo Spini. If he rushed to the monastery to alert the friar immediately following the delivery of the forged letters, the timing would inevitably breed unfavorable rumors linking the two events. Conversely, confronting Spini instantly required revealing the truth—that Savonarola had altered his intentions—which Spini would surely interpret as Tito having "changed his mind" and sabotaged the plot himself. By delaying the warning to Savonarola until morning, Tito risked losing the crucial window to inform Spini of the friar's hesitation, leaving the Compagnacci band to return in a fury of disappointed vengeance.

Yet, this was the peril Tito willingly accepted. He trusted his ability to soothe Spini, attributing the failure solely to the Friar's own prudence. Annoyed and irritable, Tito found himself unable to muster a smile—a rare feat for him—and resolved to avoid Romola entirely. Consequently, he did not return home that night.

Inside, Romola remained awake, undressed, staring into the darkness. She listened intently as the rain intensified, finding comfort in the stormy heavens, which seemed to act as a shield against human machinations, forcing men into paralysis. Yet, her peace was shattered by a dual torment: profound doubt regarding her husband's integrity and uncertainty about her own actions. What falsehoods might he have woven? What secret projects lay hidden from her knowledge? To trust Tito was to invite danger; to convince herself otherwise was futile. Was she not acting out of selfish pride by shrinking from the duty to warn others against him?

"If my husband were indeed a criminal, my place would be beside him in prison"—she acknowledged this grim possibility and accepted it. But could a wife stand idly by while her spouse inflicted harm that rendered him a malefactor, when she possessed the power to intervene? Prayer felt impossible; her agitated thoughts refused the quietude necessary for supplication. Her mind raced with morbid intensity, imagining scenarios where Tito would evade her threats. As dawn approached, the rain subsided, the wind scattered the clouds, and a clear morning revealed the world outside. Paradoxically, this clarity made her inner turmoil more unbearable.

Wrapping her mantle tightly, Romola sprinted to the loggia, scanning the vast landscape as if some external sign could dictate her next move. Could she perhaps approach her godfather and urge him to block Fra Girolamo's passage without revealing specific details? But such a measure might arrive too late. Distress gnawed at her as she realized she had gleaned no concrete information from Tito, and the clock had already struck seven. There was no alternative; she must proceed to San Marco.

She descended the stairs rapidly, ignoring the sick patients within, and stepped out onto the street. Moving swiftly toward the Ponte Vecchio via the Via de' Bardi, she cut directly through the heart of the city. The great Piazza offered a slim hope: she might intercept her husband there. Perhaps, by some miracle, he could reassure her of the Friar's safety, rendering her journey to San Marco unnecessary.

Upon reaching the courtyard of the Palazzo Vecchio, she peered eagerly into the pillared space, then scanned the piazza. The familiar figure, once etched in her heart by youthful affection and now scarred by suffering, was absent. Undeterred, she hastened toward the Piazza del Duomo. The square teemed with life: worshippers ascending and descending the marble steps, men pausing for conversation, and vendors laden with goods. Amidst this throng, Romola spotted her husband.

On his way from San Marco, Tito had diverted into Nello's shop and was now leaning casually against the doorpost. As Romola drew nearer, she observed him with shocking nonchalance. He held his cap in one hand, shook back his freshly combed hair, and conversed with an air of effortless ease. The stark contrast between this calm demeanor and the bitter anxieties he had instilled convulsed her with indignation; the fresh vision of his hardness deepened her terror. Standing nearby were Cronaca and two other regulars of San Marco.

A sudden thought seized her: *"I will force him to speak before these witnesses."*

Her light step carried her forward before he could retreat. As Cronaca announced, "Here comes Madonna Romola," Tito felt a sharp jolt as he realized he stood face-to-face with his wife. She appeared haggard from sleepless vigilance, yet her eyes burned with something far more intense than mere worry.

"Has the Frate crossed the gates?" she demanded.

"No," Tito replied, feeling paralyzed before her gaze. He summoned every ounce of self-control to maintain a mask of simple surprise.

"And are you certain he is not departing?" she pressed.

"I am certain he is not going."

"That is sufficient," Romola declared. Turning upward, she ascended the steps to seek refuge within the Duomo, needing solitude to compose herself.

Tito watched her retreat, harboring a sensation of hatred more acute than any he had ever felt. Among the crowd gathered in the square were not only devoted followers of the Friar but also Ser Ceccone, the notary. Like Tito, Ceccone was secretly an agent of the Medici. Unlearned, unattractive, unsuccessful, and devoid of generosity, he was a traitor lacking any redeeming charm. Naturally, he held little affection for Tito Melema.
