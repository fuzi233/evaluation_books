The sun beat down relentlessly on the Wells of Obak, a desolate oasis in the vast expanse of the Nubian desert. Five hundred miles north of Durrance's frontier town, the wells lay hidden among shifting dunes, their location marked only by a cluster of granite hills and a belt of trees that separated the sandy terrain from a stony plain. As May dawned, the blistering heat intensified, and the wind howled through the dunes, sculpting valleys and hills with an otherworldly precision. Vultures and sand-grouse roosted undisturbed, their calls echoing through the stillness.

It was a place where time stood still, where the remnants of past caravans – bleached camel bones and crumbling branch huts – served as a haunting reminder of life's transience. Yet, in this desolate landscape, one man moved secretly, his presence a mere whisper in the wind. Every morning, at the crack of dawn, he emerged from his hiding place, driving two majestic Bisharin camels from the shaded grove to the well's mouth. There, he watered them, feeding them dhoura delicately on a cloth before disappearing into the trees, leaving behind only the faint scent of date palms.

For five mornings, the man repeated this ritual, watching the sand dunes and the distant outline of Berber with an air of quiet anticipation. It was as if he awaited a sign, a harbinger of change in this desolate world. And then, on the sixth morning, his patience was rewarded. A figure, a negro donkey driver, appeared on the horizon, his ebony skin stark against the golden sand. The man by the well, Abou Fatma, looked up, his eyes narrowing as he took in the stranger's appearance. He remained seated, his expression unreadable, as the negro approached, laying aside his spear, shield, and short staff.

"Salam aleikum," the negro said, his voice carrying across the desert expanse. Abou Fatma bowed his head, returning the greeting, and the negro sat beside him. "Is it you, Abou Fatma?" he asked, his eyes scanning the surrounding dunes.

Abou Fatma nodded, his gaze drifting towards the donkey, as if only now noticing its presence. "Tayeeb," he said, his tone detached, "the donkey is mine."
