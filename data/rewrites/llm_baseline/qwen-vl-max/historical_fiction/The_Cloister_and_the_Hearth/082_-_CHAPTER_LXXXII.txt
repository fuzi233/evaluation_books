“Mistress,” said the servant, “they all say he is dead.”

"Not so," she replied, her voice steady despite the tremor in her hands. "They still feed me with hope."

"Ay, to your face," he murmured, "but behind your back—every tongue whispers it. He’s gone."

At that, Margaret’s eyes filled with tears. Luke, standing beside her, whimpered softly, as though the sorrow of another were his own. He had the strength of a man but the heart of a child.

"Prithee, do not weep so, sweet mistress," he said gently. "I would bring him back from the grave itself, rather than see you suffer thus."

She wiped her cheeks and drew a shaky breath. "I think I weep because they are so cruel—so double-tongued. They speak kindly to my face, yet betray me behind my back."

Then, placing her hand on his shoulder, she spoke with quiet intensity: "Luke, he is not dead. Dying men have strange visions. Listen—my father, when he lay dying, was sitting in that very chair where you sit now. Mother stood near, and I was busy at my sleeve. And suddenly, he turned his head and said—'I see him! I see him!' Not weak or fading, but blazing with fire. 'Sore disfigured—on a great river—coming this way.'"

Luke fell silent, absorbing her words.

"Ah, Luke," she whispered, "if you were a woman—if you felt for me even half what you pretend to feel—you would pity me, and go find him. Think of it—the father of my child!"

"I would, mistress," he said, "if I knew how. But how can I?"

"Nay, of course you cannot," she sighed. "I am mad to ask it. Yet—oh, if any one truly cared for me, they would. That is all I know."

Luke sat in thought for long moments, his brow furrowed. "The old folk say dying men see more than living men. Let me ponder—my mind does not race like yours. A great river... Well, the Maas is a great river."

He paused.

"Coming this way? Then if it were the Maas, he would be here by now. So it cannot be the Maas. The Rhine is greater, longer. I believe it must be the Rhine."

"And so do I," she said, her voice rising slightly. "Denys told him to come down the Rhine. But even if it is, he may turn aside before reaching home. He does not pine for me as I pine for him—that is clear. Luke, do you not think he has abandoned me?"

She wanted him to deny it. But he said, quietly, "It looks very much like it. What a fool he must be!"

"What do we know?" she pleaded, her voice breaking. "Let me think again."

"Indeed," Luke said, "I cannot gallop. Let me gather my thoughts."

After a time, he spoke. "There is a station sixty miles up the Rhine where all the public boats stop. I will go there and try to intercept him. True, I do not know him by sight—but I shall mingle with the passengers as each boat arrives and ask if Gerard Eliassoen is among them."

"And mistress," he added, "if you could give me a letter to him—since we are strangers, he might not believe a word I say."

"Good, kind, thoughtful Luke," she said, her voice thick with emotion. "I will—I will write it. Give me until supper-time."

At supper, she handed him a sealed letter, tied with silk in the fashion of the day. It was long, and written with trembling hands.

Luke held it in his palm, weighing it carefully. A shadow crossed his face.

"Say your father was not dreaming," he said slowly, "and say I find this man—and say he proves better than I expect, and returns to you at once—what then becomes of me?"

Margaret flushed deeply. "Oh, Luke, Heaven will reward you. I shall fall on my knees and bless you. I shall love you all my days, as a mother loves her son. I am so old compared to you—sorrow ages the heart. You must not go. It is not fair. Love makes us selfless."

"Humph," he muttered.

"And if," he continued, in the same grave tone, "this runaway should read your letter, and hear me tell him how you pine for him—and yet, being a traitor or a fool, refuse to return—what then becomes of me? Must I die a bachelor, and you go alone to your grave—neither maid, nor wife, nor widow?"

Margaret gasped, her chest tight with fear and emotion. She stammered, "If—oh, if God spare me from such a fate—then—then he would read it, and hear your words. And you, sweet Luke, must tell him—tell him how lovely his son is—fatherless, fatherless. Oh, Luke, can he be so cruel?"

"I doubt it," Luke said. "But I cannot be sure."

"Then," she said, "he will give up my marriage lines. I shall be an honest woman—even if wretched. My boy will no longer be a bastard. And of course, then we could both go into any honest man's house who would take us in. And for your kindness today, I swear—I will never be so ungrateful as to pass your door and enter another man's."

"Ay," he said, "but will you come *in* at mine? Answer me that."

"Oh, do not ask!" she cried. "Some day, perhaps—when the wounds cease to bleed. Alas, I’ll try. If I don’t throw myself and my child into the Maas. Do not go, Luke! Do not even think of going! This is madness from beginning to end."

But Luke moved slowly, as he always did—once a thought took root, it grew firm. His reply showed how love was shaping him into a true man.

"Well," he said, "madness is something, after all. And I am tired of doing nothing for you. I am no great talker. Tomorrow, at first light, I start. But hold—where is my money? My mother keeps it all. I never see it again."

Then Margaret reached into her dress and pulled out Catherine’s gold angel—the coin that had escaped so many times. She gave it to Luke.

And so, with a heart full of fear and hope, he set out on his journey—his mission reckless, perhaps, but not without purpose.

To us, it seems madness. But in those days, faith shaped action. Luke followed a dream—not as fantasy, but as truth. And when Catherine heard of it, she stormed:

"To send the poor lad on such a wild-goose chase! But you are like so many girls. Mark my words—by the time you’ve worn Luke clean out, made him sick of you as a dog is of its master, you’ll turn as fond of him as a cow is of her calf. And then—too late!"

The two friars arrived in Holland from the south just twelve hours after Luke began his journey up the Rhine. Thus, though neither knew it, they were drawing closer—rapidly.

Jerome, unable to preach in Dutch, pressed forward toward the coast, eager to reach England. With the current aiding their progress, the friars would have passed Luke entirely—had it not been for what happened next.

About twenty miles above the station Luke aimed for, Clement landed to preach in a large village. Near the end of his sermon, he noticed a grey nun weeping silently in the back row.

He approached her gently. "My sister, what troubles you?"

"Nay," she replied, "it is not for myself these tears flow. It is for my lost friend. Your words reminded me of who she once was—and who she is now. Poor wretch."

"You are a Franciscan," Clement observed. "I am a Dominican."

"It matters little," she said. "We are both Christians. And if you can help, I will tell you our grief."

She confessed that a young nun—beloved of the convent, her closest friend—had been lured away from her vows. After a slow descent into sin, she now lived in a small inn as chambermaid, in truth a decoy, known to sell herself to wealthy travelers.

"Elsewhere," she said, "we might take her by force. But this innkeeper serves the fierce baron on the hill above. He holds the baron’s ear. Were we to seize her, he would burn our convent to the ground."

"Nor," said Clement, "are souls saved by brute force."

As they spoke, Jerome arrived. Clement urged him to stay the night at the convent. When morning came, Clement told him of the abbess’s sorrow and his promise to try to save the fallen nun.

Jerome objected. "It is not our concern. A waste of time."

But Clement was no longer a mere pupil. He stood firm. At last, they agreed: Jerome would continue onward to secure passage to England, while Clement remained to make his attempt.

By ten o’clock that morning, a figure in a horseman’s cloak, wide boots, and a flapping felt hat stood motionless near the inn where Mary, the fallen nun, lived.

Clement, disguised, was truly wretched. Such hearts, ardent and pure, undertake miracles—but crumble when faced with the raw reality of sin. Yet they do not retreat. They pant, falter, but press on.

He hesitated at the door, prayed, then stepped inside—faint-hearted, sweating cold. But within, he became another man.

He called loudly for wine. It was brought by the landlord. Clement paid with coins from the convent, pretending to drink.

"Landlord," he said, "I hear you have a fair chambermaid."

"Ay, stranger," the man replied. "The buxomest in Holland. But she gives herself only to good customers."

Clement dangled a massive gold chain before the man’s eyes.

The landlord laughed. "Here, Janet! Here is a lover who would bind you in chains of gold—a tall lad, too!"

"Then I am doubly lucky," came a voice. "Send him hither."

Clement rose, shuddered, and followed her into the room. There sat Janet, playing with embroidery, pausing every moment to sing a broken tune. In her life, she had no patience for completion.

After polite greetings, the visitor asked if they could be more private.

"Why not?" she said, smiling. And she led him forward.

He followed, groaning inwardly.

"There," she said. "No one comes here unless they pay."

Clement closed the shutters carefully.

"What is that for?" she asked, uneasy.

"Sweetheart," he whispered, "so that God may not see us."

"Madman!" she laughed. "Do you think a wooden shutter can hide us from His eye?"

"No," he admitted. "But perhaps He has too much to do to notice us. Still, I would not have the saints and angels see us. Would you?"

"My poor soul," she said, "do not hope to escape their gaze. The only way is not to think of them. For if you do, it poisons your joy. For two pins, I’d run and leave you. Are you pleasant company, truly?"

"After all," he said, "so long as men do not see us, what matter if God and the saints do? Feel this chain—it is virgin gold. I shall cut two heavy links off for you."

"Ah!" she exclaimed, greedily touching it. "Why, it is as heavy as the chain around the Virgin’s neck at the conv—"

She stopped short.

"Whisht! Whisht! Whisht!" he hissed. "It is that chain. And you shall have your share. But betray me not."

"Monster!" she shrieked, recoiling. "To rob the Blessed Virgin of her chain—and give it to an—"

"You are none," he declared triumphantly, "or you would not care for that—Mary!"

"Ah! Ah! Ah!"

"Your patron saint—whose chain this is—sends me to greet you."

She ran to the window, trembling, and began to open the shutters.

But Clement had already removed his boots and hat. As the light flooded in, he let his cloak fall, revealing himself—a Dominican friar, calm and majestic as a statue.

He held aloft his crucifix, gazing at her with a look both loving and solemn—one that eased her physical fear, yet crushed her with spiritual terror.

She crouched against the wall.

"Mary," he said gently, "one word: are you happy?"

"As happy as I shall be in hell."

"And they are not happy at the convent. They weep for you."

"For me?"

"Day and night. Especially Sister Ursula."

"Poor Ursula!" she whispered—and wept.

"The angels weep more still. Will you not dry all their tears—here and in heaven—and save yourself?"

"Aye," she sobbed, "if I could. But it is too late."

"Satan avaunt!" he thundered. "That is your favourite lie. Listen not to the enemy, who whispers despair. I, who come to save you, have been a greater sinner than you. See—sin is not so sweet, even in this world, as holiness. Eternity stands at the door."

"How can they ever receive me again?"

"You doubt their worthiness. But in truth, they pine for you. It was their tears that moved me—a Dominican—to break my order’s rule, to wear these clothes, to enter this place. All is forgiven, if you let us rescue your soul from this den of wolves and return you to your vows."

She looked at him, tears in her eyes.

"And you—a Dominican—have done this for a daughter of St. Francis? Why, the orders hate each other!"

"Ay," he said, "but Francis and Dominic love one another."

She seemed struck.

He reminded her how shocked she had been at the thought of stealing the Virgin’s chain.

"But see," he said, "the convent, and the Virgin herself, value their lost nun far more than golden chains. They trusted me, a stranger, with this chain—hoping its sight might remind you of their love."

At last, with terrible simplicity, he showed her the end of her path—and stirred her forgotten memories.

She knelt sobbing at his feet. "I have never known peace since I broke my vows. I will go back—if you go with me. Alone, I dare not. Even if I reach the gate, I cannot enter. How could I face the abbess, the sisters?"

He promised to go with her—as joyfully as a shepherd carries a stray lamb to the fold.

But when he urged her to go at once, petty fears sprang up—like silken nets.

He swept them aside.

"And how can I walk beside you in this habit?"

"I have brought your gown and cowl. Hide your finery. Leave your shoes as I leave mine."

She gathered her jewels.

"What are these for?" Clement asked.

"To give to the convent, father."

"Their source is impure."

"But," she insisted, "to leave them here would be a sin. They can be sold to feed the poor."

"Mary," he said, "fix your eyes on this crucifix. Trample those devilish baubles beneath your feet."

She hesitated—then threw them down and crushed them.

"Now," he said, "open the window and fling them onto that dunghill. Well done. So pass the wages of sin from your hands, the glittering yoke from your neck, the pollution from your soul. Away, daughter of St.6 Francis—we linger too long in this vile place."

She followed.

But danger still waited.

The landlord stared, stunned, as a black friar and a grey nun passed through his kitchen.

"Mummery?" he muttered.

Then he understood.

"Hey! Reuben! Carl! Gavin! A false friar stealing our Janet!"

Men rushed in, threatening.

Clement charged forward, crucifix raised.

"Forbear!" he roared. "She is a holy nun returning to her vows. Any hand that touches her cowl shall wither. His body shall lie unburied. His soul shall burn in eternal fire!"

They recoiled as if struck by flame.

"And thou—miserable pander!" he shouted.

He seized the man by the neck and hurled him across the room—headfirst onto the stone floor.

Then he tore open the door and carried the screaming nun into the road.

"Hush, poor trembler," he gasped. "They dare not molest you on the highroad. Away!"

The landlord lay stunned, bleeding.

Mary glanced back—then fled.

On the road, Clement warned her: "Observe my temper. Hitherto, we have spoken of your faults. Now—mine. My anger is fierce, uncontrollable. By God’s grace, I am not a murderer. I repent—but too late. Had those men touched you, I would have shattered their skulls with this crucifix. I know myself. And I tremble at myself. Beneath this black robe, there lurks a wild beast."

"Alas, father," she said, "had you been otherwise, I would have been lost. To free me from that place required a man both bold as a lion and wary as a fox."

Clement reflected. "This is certain: God chooses well His instruments. With imperfect hearts, He works His perfect will. Glory be to God!"

Near the convent, Mary stopped abruptly, clutching his arm, weeping.

He comforted her. "You have nothing to fear. This will be the happiest day of your life."

He sent her to sit and compose herself while he entered the convent.

"Mother," he said, "give glory to God. Mary is at the gate."

The abbess was overcome with joy. She agreed to soften the path for the penitent.

Clement returned, soothed Mary, and heard her confession.

"The abbess grants you to choose your own penance."

"None the lighter," she vowed.

"Perhaps not," he said. "But today is for joy alone."

He led her to the abbess’s postern. As they walked, music and singing drifted from within.

"It is a feastday," Mary said. "And I come to mar it."

"Hardly," he smiled. "For you are the queen of the festival."

"Me? Father, what mean you?"

"Have you never heard that there is more joy in heaven over one sinner who repents than over ninety-nine righteous who need no repentance? This convent is not heaven—nor the nuns angels. Yet some among them are angelic. And they sing for your return."

A hand trembled at the keyhole.

The postern flew open.

Sister Ursula rushed forward, weeping, clinging to her friend.

The abbess followed, moved beyond words.

Clement bid them farewell. They begged him to stay. But he declined, regretful.

"I have already tried Brother Jerome’s patience. I must hasten to the river. Perhaps sail tomorrow."

So Mary returned to the fold. And Clement strode onward—toward the Rhine, and England.

This was the man for whom Margaret’s boy waited—with her letter.

That letter was simple, tender—written only by a woman’s heart, to one who had wronged her.

She began by telling him of the birth of their son, and how the child had comforted her through the agony of his silence.

She described little Gerard in detail—the mole on his little finger.

"Know anyone with the same? If you saw him, you could not help but be proud. All the mothers envy me. But I envy no one—except the wives whose husbands come home. You come not to us."

"My own Gerard," she wrote, "some say you are dead. But if you were dead, how could I live? Others say you, whom I love so truly, are false. But I will believe that only from your own lips."

"My father loved you well. As he died, he said he saw you on a great river—your face turned toward me, but terribly disfigured. Is it so? Have cruel men scarred your face? Or lost a limb? Then you have greater need of me. And I shall love you not less—alas, do you think a woman’s love is as light as a man’s? No. Better, even than when I shed those few drops from my arm—worthless tears, you said—yet you wept for them. Remember? It was not so long ago, dear Gerard."

She continued in this gentle tone, ending without reproach—without doubt of his love.

Not that she was free from doubt. But she dared not show it. To do so might frighten him away.

And so she entrusted the letter to Luke—her soft, loyal servant.

And so she was not an angel, after all.

Luke mingled with the passengers of two boats—heard nothing of Gerard Eliassoen. Not surprising.

But at the third boat, a black friar stopped him.

"And what would you with this man you call Gerard Eliassoen?"

"Why, father," Luke replied, "if he lives, I have a letter for him."

"Humph," said Jerome. "I am sorry for it. Yet the flesh is weak. Well, my son, he you seek will be here by the next boat—or the one after. And if he answers to that name—well, I am not the keeper of his conscience."

"Good father," Luke said, "one plain word—Heaven’s sake. Is Gerard Eliassoen of Tergou alive?"

"Humph," Jerome mused. "Well, certes—he who went by that name is alive."

"Then it is settled," Luke said, dryly.

But the next moment, he fled from sight—weeping.

"Oh, why did the Lord make women?" he muttered. "I was content with the world till I fell in love. Her little finger means more to her than my whole body. And he is not dead. And now I must deliver this."

He looked at the letter—then flung it to the ground.

But he picked it up again, snatching it with fury, and went to the landlord, tears in his eyes.

"Please," he begged, "give me work. I must keep from breaking my heart over another man’s love."

The landlord refused. "I have my own people."

"Oh, I do not want your money," Luke said. "I only want work—anything—to keep from despair."

"Good lad!" the landlord exclaimed. "Good lad!" And he gave him barrels to mend—on those terms.

And Luke worked with fury—between the boats, as they drifted down the Rhine.
