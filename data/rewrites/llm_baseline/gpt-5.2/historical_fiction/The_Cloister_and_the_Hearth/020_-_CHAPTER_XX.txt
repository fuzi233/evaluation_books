Courage, like any homely craft, often keeps to one narrow rut. A man may face rain, debt, wolves, and a hard winter as steadily as he kneads rye in a trough—because he has done it before. But take him one thumb’s breadth beyond his known labor, and his heart turns strange.

So it was with Martin Wittenhaagen. He had tramped more forests than most men have crossed market-squares; he had watched for poachers, smuggled bales, slept hungry, fought with fist and staff, and borne all the common hazards of a rough soldier’s life with a blunt endurance. Yet he had never been hunted by a bloodhound; never heard, in the deep of a wood, that measured music which was not music but sentence.

A few paces after they fled, he halted as if his joints had rusted. He leaned on his bow; the grain of the yew pressed into his palm, and still the strength ran out of him. The air itself seemed altered—no longer mere damp leaf-and-moss, but a fear-scent, sharp as bruised fern under heel.

Gerard, to whom danger always looked smaller when it stood a field away, urged him on with impatient breath.

“What avails it?” Martin said at last, his voice sunk and heavy. “If we get clear o’ the wood we shall die cheap. Here, hard by, I know a place where we may die dear.”

“Alas, good Martin,” cried Gerard, “despair not so quickly. There must be some way.”

Margaret, who had not once failed them in the day’s sharp turns, came closer, her eyes bright with a desperate kindness. “What if we part company? Gerard’s life alone is forfeit. Is there no way to draw the pursuit on us twain and let him go safe?”

Martin shook his head as if the very thought were childish. “Girl, you know not a bloodhound’s nature. He is not on this man’s track or that. He is on the track of blood. My life on’t they have taken him where Ghysbrecht fell; and from the dead man’s blood to the man that shed it that cursed hound will lead them, though Gerard run through an army or swim the Meuse.”

And again his head sank, and the bow’s horn nock gleamed dull in the green shade.

Then, far off, it came—one long note rolling between trunks as if the wood were a vaulted church. The hound’s bay was mellow, full-throated, almost beautiful; it had the roundness of a horn-call at harvest home, when men bring in sheaves and sing. But here that sweetness was a hook in the blood. It made the skin on Margaret’s arms rise; it made Martin’s stomach go slack as sour beer.

Strange, that what is fair to the ear can be deadly to the flesh. In the same breath that a lullaby calms a child, this sound steadied the hunters and shook the hunted. It seemed to lay its tongue upon Martin’s nerve, to hold him fast. Gerard too felt it—felt, with the sick clarity that comes in terror, the moral weight of his own act. He had struck an old man and shed blood; now blood itself had found a voice, four-footed and unerring, and it was coming.

Was there not something beyond man in it? Was it not as if Heaven had set a living instrument to play him down?

While the men stood thus benumbed, the woman’s wits ran like a quick hand at a hearth, doing three things at once. Her love was in peril; she would not watch him hunted like a hare without trying some cunning of her own.

“Lend me your knife,” she said to Martin.

He gave it, half in scorn, half in stupor. “’Twill be little use in your hands,” he muttered.

Margaret stepped behind Gerard as if only to keep close; and there, in the cover of their bodies and the wood’s shadow, she drew the blade clean across her arm. The cut was quick—no ragged sawing—like the slit a careful cook makes in a skin to drain and dress it. Blood welled at once, warm and startling, and she did not cry out. She stooped, and with deft fingers smeared her hose and shoes, again and again, letting the drops fall where she chose. All the while she kept her face composed; neither Gerard nor Martin saw what she did, though the iron tang rose sharp in the damp air.

Then she caught the soldier’s arm and shook him. “Come—be a man, and let this end. Take us to some thick place, where numbers will not avail our foes.”

“I am going,” Martin said, sulkily, as men go to an appointed whipping. “Hurry avails not. We cannot shun the hound, and the place is hard by.”

He turned left, and led them, not running but moving with the dull obedience of doom. Soon the wood closed into a hazel coppice, a tight-grown tangle of supple stems, as close as wattle in a byre wall—the sort of cover that catches at cloak and hair and keeps sound muffled, so that you hear your own breathing loud as a bell.

“There,” said Martin. “’Tis but a furlong broad, but it will serve.”

“What are we to do?” Gerard demanded.

“Get through, and wait on the other side. Then, as they come straggling through, shoot three, knock two on the head, and the rest will kill us.”

“Is that all you can think of?”

“That is all.”

“Then, Martin Wittenhaagen, I take the lead, for you have lost your head. Come—can you obey so young a man as I am?”

Margaret clutched Martin’s sleeve, pleading him into obedience. “Do not gainsay Gerard. He is wiser than his years.”

Martin gave a sullen nod.

“Do as you see me do,” Gerard whispered, and drew his great knife. At every step he cut a hazel shoot or two close by the ground, clean and low, and then twisted the severed wands breast-high behind him among the standing stems—braiding them so that the path would be easy for a nose that reads the earth, and hard for men who must shove with shoulder and shin. It was work like making a hurdle in haste, each bend and twist with purpose. Martin copied him, grudging and hopeless, the bow dragging in his hand.

They labored through the coppice, sweat mingling with leaf-mould smell. The bay came nearer. What had been mellow now grew louder, sterner, less like music and more like a mallet beating on a door. With it rose another sound: voices—hoops and halloos tossed through the timber, rough as crows. The whole wood’s soundscape shifted. Where earlier there had been only birds and their own footfalls, now the air was full of pursuit: bay, shout, branches snapping, breath.

Margaret trembled, not only from fear but from the slow drain at her arm. Martin went down on his stomach and pressed his ear to the earth.

“I hear a horse’s feet.”

“No,” Gerard whispered. “A mule’s. That cursed Ghysbrecht is still alive. None other would follow me up so bitterly.”

“Never strike your enemy but to slay him,” Martin returned, gloomily, as if quoting an old village proverb that had outlived its language.

“I’ll hit harder this time, if Heaven gives me the chance,” Gerard said between his teeth.

At last the coppice thinned. They forced their way out into an open wood where the trees stood wide apart, tall trunks with clean spaces between—no hiding, no slipping unseen, only long sight-lines and the naked sense of being watched. Behind them the bay and the hooting swelled, and the young hazel seemed to shiver with the passage of men.

“The whole village is out after us,” Martin breathed.

“I care not,” said Gerard. His voice steadied into command. “Listen, Martin. I have made the track smooth to the dog, but rough to the men, that we may deal with them apart. The hound will gain on the men; and as soon as he comes out of the coppice we must kill him.”

“The hound?” Martin’s eyes narrowed. “There is more than one.”

“I hear but one.”

“Ay—but one speaks; the others run mute. Let the leading hound lose scent, and another shall give tongue. There will be two dogs at least—or devils in dog’s hides.”

“Then we kill two instead of one,” Gerard said. “The moment they are dead, into the coppice again, and go right back.”

“That is a good thought,” Martin answered, and heart came back into him like warmth returning to a chilled hand.

“Hush. The men are in the wood.”

Gerard set them in place with quick, whispered orders. “Stand with your bow by the side of the coppice—there, in the ditch. I will go a few yards to yon oak and hide behind it. The dogs will follow me; and, as they come out, shoot as many as you can. The rest I’ll brain as they come round the tree.”

Martin’s eye flashed. He slid into the ditch, the damp earth cold through his hose, and held the bow as steady as if aiming at a deer in rut. Gerard went to the oak and melted behind it; Margaret hovered between, caught by the openness, by the way sound travelled in that space like a thrown stone.

The hooping and hallooing came nearer; the coppice rustled as if it were alive, as if the thicket itself had learned to speak in men’s feet. Now and then, above all, the single bay—like a church bell tolling for one soul.

A trembling hand touched Gerard’s shoulder. He started hard, strung as a drawn bow.

“Martin says,” Margaret whispered, “if we are forced to part company, make for that high ash-tree we came in by.”

“Yes—yes—yes,” Gerard breathed, impatient with tenderness in so sharp an instant. “But go back, for Heaven’s sake. Don’t come here, all out in the open.”

She ran back toward Martin; but before she reached him, a huge dog burst out of the coppice and stood a moment erect, head high, nostrils working as if he drank the world through scent. Margaret crouched instinctively. The brute did not even glance at her; smell was his sight, and her fear was nothing to him.

He lowered his nose. One instant—then, with an awful yell, he sprang straight at Gerard’s oak.

The bow twanged from the ditch like a snapped string on a viol. The hound rolled head-over-heels and fell dead as stone, spitted clean through with Martin’s arrow.

That same heartbeat another hound came out and bent over his dead comrade. Gerard rushed, cudgel up; but before wood met skull, a streak of white flew—another arrow—and the hound grovelled in the dust, desperately hurt, howling thinly, piteously, the sound cutting the ear worse than the bay had done.

Gerard had no time to despatch him. The coppice behind the dogs was already thrashing; it seemed to bulge with bodies. With a wild gesture he pointed Martin back, then ran a few yards to the right and slipped into the hazel just as three men burst out into the open.

Those three had outstripped their fellows. The rest were coming in scattered knots, voices rising and falling like rough chant. Gerard crawled on hands and knees, hazel switches slapping his face; instinct taught Martin and Margaret to do the same, back into the thick. So, within a few yards, the hunters and the hunted passed one another upon opposite tracks—men blundering forward where the fugitives were already sliding back.

A loud cry announced the dead and wounded hounds. Then a babble—arguments, orders, oaths—swelling as more pursuers arrived. Surprise always wastes time; the hunted stole it greedily.

“I hear no more hounds,” Martin whispered to Margaret, and his old soldier’s self came back, brisk as a man who has tasted hope.

It was Margaret’s turn to falter. The blood she had so cleverly spent now took its price; her head swam, and fear for Gerard—separate from her in that twisting brush—gnawed her like hunger.

“Oh, why did we part with Gerard?” she whispered. “They will kill my Gerard, and I not near him.”

“Nay, nay,” Martin breathed. “The head to catch him is not on their shoulders. You bade him meet us at the ash-tree?”

“And so I did. Bless you, Martin, for thinking of that. To the ash-tree!”

“Ay—but with less noise.”

They crept, they slid, they listened. Near the edge of the coppice, hooping and hallooing rose behind them again: the men had satisfied themselves the fugitives were in the thicket and were beating back through it.

“No matter,” Martin whispered, close to Margaret’s ear. “We shall have time to win clear and slip out of sight by hard running.”

He was about to burst from the brush when he stooped suddenly, struck still by what his eye caught through leaves.

A man sat sentinel on a mule.

It was Ghysbrecht Van Swieten. A bloody bandage crossed his face; the bridge of his nose was broken, and beneath the stained cloth his eyes peered keenly, alert as a butcher’s when he weighs a beast and misses no fault. By their very set it was plain he had heard the faint rustle and was searching the green for it.

Martin muttered an oath that seemed to sour the air. Slowly, carefully—careful as a cook lifting a lid lest steam betray him—he strung his bow and fitted his last arrow. Margaret put her hands to her face; she said nothing. She understood without words: this man must die, or Gerard would.

The bow rose. Martin drew steadily—back to the head—holding the aim with the patience of long practice, every breath measured.

And in that very instant, an active figure leaped upon Ghysbrecht from behind, swift as a hawk stooping. A kerchief flashed; in one turn of the hand Ghysbrecht’s head was muffled, his shout smothered. He was whirled from the mule and fell heavily, groaning with terror. Gerard sprang down after him.

“Hist, Martin! Martin!”

Martin and Margaret came out, blinking as if into daylight. Martin’s mouth opened in a great silent cry of relief. “Now fly—fly—while they are all in the thicket. We are saved.”

At the very edge of safety, fate twisted the knife. Margaret, who had held herself up with sheer will, sagged. The blood loss made the world reel; her voice came thin.

“Oh, my beloved—fly! Leave me, for I am faint.”

“No! No!” Gerard cried, fierce and tender together. “Death together, or safety. Ah—the mule! Mount her, you, and I’ll run by your side.”

In a moment Martin was on the mule. Gerard lifted Margaret as if she were a child, set her before the saddle, and took Martin’s bow from him. The mule shifted, uneasy, ears flicking to the noises behind, smelling sweat and fear.

“Help! treason! murder! murder!” shrieked Ghysbrecht, scrambling up on his hams like a man rising out of a nightmare.

“Silence, cur!” roared Gerard, and trod him down again by the throat, crushing him as men crush an adder’s neck.

“Now—have you got her firm? Then fly! For our lives! For our lives!”

But even as the mule, urged by Martin’s heel, scattered flints with his hind hoofs before he found his canter, and even as Gerard lifted his foot from Ghysbrecht’s throat to run—Dierich Brower and five men, returning for orders and drawn by the burgomaster’s cries, burst roaring out of the coppice upon them.
