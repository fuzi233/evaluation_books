Lord Castlewood kept a house in Kensington Square, broad-fronted and dignified, with a gravel sweep before the steps and a thin strip of garden that tried, bravely, to be green in a city whose soot fell like black flour. It was large enough for the whole noble brood when the Court lay near, and for those evenings when her ladyship held her assemblies—cards, compliments, and that measured laughter which is the first music of fashionable life. Kensington, a hundred years ago, was not the easy suburb it has since become: the road out of town had stretches where the wheels fell silent in ruts, and the hedges seemed to listen. A man could hear his own coach springs complain, and, beyond them, the far-off mutter of London like a sea behind walls. George Selwyn would say he feared to go so far lest he be robbed of a night—whether by highwaymen with crepe masks or by ladies in rouge at quadrille, it came to much the same loss.

About noon, the day after Harry Warrington showed his face again at White’s, chance—or that domestic Fate that times its entrances better than any prompter—brought all Lord Castlewood’s virtuous kinsfolk together at breakfast. There was chocolate, thick as varnish, sent up steaming in painted cups; there were rolls split and toasted, their faces browned, and butter softening into them; there were eggs brought to table in their shells and opened with the little silver taps that made a neat, polite sound. Even the room itself had its palate: beeswax on the wainscot, coal-dust in the grate, and the faint medicinal perfume of pomatum lingering where my lord’s wig would presently sit.

The ladies came first and took their chocolate with that air of piety which society assumes towards any morning indulgence. Then Mr. Will clattered in, already dressed for the afternoon’s waiting in his court suit, bright as a toy soldier and twice as restless. At last the master of the house appeared, languid and yawning, in bedgown and nightcap, his wig not yet donned; his Daily Advertiser opened with the indifference of a man who expects to find the world behaving itself.

Here was news, Will said—news he had brought from the Star and Garter, where he had supped last night with men who had heard it at White’s and swore they had seen proof of it at Ranelagh.

“Heard what? seen what?” my lord asked, lifting his paper as though it were a shield.

“Ask Maria,” Lady Fanny said, and turned her bright eyes, trained to mischief, upon her elder sister.

Lord Castlewood looked at Lady Maria. She sat pale as linen, her hands lying in her lap as if they had been placed there by someone else. The chocolate before her had a skin upon it; she had not touched it.

“’Tis one of Will’s usual elegant and polite inventions,” Maria said, and her voice, though steady, had the thinness of a string drawn too tight.

“No,” Will swore—he had oaths ready like small coins in a pocket, and threw them down liberally. “No invention of mine. Tom Claypool of Norfolk saw ’em both at Ranelagh; Jack Morris came out of White’s where he heard it from Harry Warrington’s own lips. Curse him, I’m glad of it!” And he slapped the table so that the spoons rang, that little bright chorus of metal which makes a breakfast sound like battle. “What do you think of your Fortunate Youth, your Virginian—whom your lordship made such a pet of—turning out to be only a second son?”

“The elder brother not dead?” my lord said.

“No more dead than you are. Never was. It’s my belief there was a cross between the two.” And Will leaned back, exulting as though he had tasted a sauce that pleased him: sharp, sour, and triumphantly salted.

“Mr. Warrington is incapable of such duplicity!” Maria cried—too swiftly, as if she feared her own words would be snatched away.

“I never encouraged the fellow,” my lady put in, smoothing her napkin with the exactness of a woman who believes in order as in virtue. “Nor did Fanny. Not we, indeed!”

“Not we, indeed!” echoed Lady Fanny, as obedient in phrase as she was inventive in smile.

“The fellow is only a beggar,” Will went on, “and, I dare say, hasn’t paid for the clothes on his back. I’m glad of it, for, hang him, I hate him!”

“You don’t regard him with favourable eyes—especially since he blacked yours, Will,” my lord said, grinning. “So the poor fellow has found his brother and lost his estate!” He turned to Maria, and though she sat the very picture of woe, something in her rigid propriety—or perhaps the way misery itself can seem absurd to the comfortable—tickled his humour. He gazed at her a moment, then burst into a shrill laugh. At that sound, her face flushed as if scalded; her eyes brimmed and ran. “It’s a shame! it’s a shame!” she sobbed, and hid her face in her handkerchief.

Maria’s stepmother and sister exchanged a glance over their cups. “We never quite understand your lordship’s humour,” her ladyship said gravely, as if humour were a foreign dish badly described.

“I don’t see there is the least reason why you should,” my lord answered coolly; and then, with an easy change to gentleness: “Maria, my dear, pray excuse me if I have said—that is, done—anything to hurt your feelings.”

“Done anything!” Maria rose, her chair scraping—a harsher note in the room’s morning music. “You pillaged the poor lad in his prosperity, and you laugh at him in his ruin!” She glared round at them all as if they were a jury she despised. “Oh, never mind at what or whom else, my lord. You have taken from him all he had to lose. All the world points at you as the man who feeds on his own flesh and blood. And now you have his all, you make merry over his misfortune!” With that she swept out, her silk rustling like dry leaves dragged by wind.

“Tell us what has happened,” Lord Castlewood said, settling his paper again as if it were only a slight inconvenience that tears had fallen on the carpet, “and my sister’s grief will not interrupt us.”

Will obliged, at great length, rolling out the story that now hummed through London like a fiddler’s tune everybody knows: George Warrington’s sudden apparition; the elder brother alive; the estate gone from Harry’s hands as quickly as a dish snatched away at table. Will told it with a relish that belonged less to speech than to eating—he chewed each detail. Lord Castlewood, for all his worldly appetite, felt a certain regret. Harry was brave, open-handed, and pleasant company; my lord liked him as much as certain men like one another when each is useful to the other and neither cares to think too closely of the bargain. To be sure he had played Harry at cards, and taken the advantage of the market upon him; but why not? If another man would pluck the peach, why should not he eat it? If that were all a conscience had to reproach him with, it need not keep him awake.

“Where does Mr. Warrington live?” he asked at last.

Will declared himself ready for reprobation if he knew or cared.

“He shall be invited here, and treated with every respect,” my lord said.

“Including piquet, I suppose!” Will growled.

“Or will you take him to the stables and sell him one of your bargains of horseflesh, Will?” Lord Castlewood asked. “You would have won of Harry Warrington fast enough if you could; but you cheat so clumsily at your game that you got paid with a cudgel. I desire, once more, that every attention be paid to our cousin Warrington.”

“And that you are not to be disturbed when you sit down to play, of course, my lord!” Lady Castlewood said, with that smooth malice which can be poured like cream.

“Madam, I desire fair play—for Mr. Warrington, and for myself, and for every member of this amiable family,” my lord retorted, flashing.

“Heaven help the poor gentleman if your lordship is going to be kind to him,” her ladyship said with a curtsey; and Heaven only knows how far their quarrel might have gone, had not wheels at that instant crunched the gravel without, and a phaeton drawn up.

In it sat the two young Virginians.

It was the carriage Harry had bought in his prosperity, and he still drove it, as if motion itself could argue against misfortune. George sat beside him; their negroes followed behind. Harry had been trying, with a meekness that was new upon him and sat oddly on his lively spirit, to give up whip, reins, and all, to his brother.

“What business has a poor devil like me with horses and carriages, Georgy?” he had said. “Beyond the coat on my back and the purse my aunt gave me, I’ve nothing in the world. You take the driving-seat, brother; it will ease my mind if you’ll take the driving-seat.”

George, laughing, said he did not know the way and Harry did; and as for the carriage, he would claim only half, as he had already done with Harry’s wardrobe. “A bargain’s a bargain; if I share thy coats, thou must divide my breeches’ pocket, Harry; that’s fair dealing!” Harry had sworn again and again there never was such a brother on earth. He rattled the horses over the road with a boy’s pride, delighted to be seen driving so noble a companion, even though that nobility had just stripped him.

Gumbo’s thunder on Lord Castlewood’s door might have belonged to the biggest footman in St. James’s. There is a particular sound to a great house’s knocker struck with conviction: it is not merely noise, but announcement; it enters the body and tells it to stand straighter.

Only Lady Castlewood and her daughter, Lady Fanny, were in the room where the gentlemen were shown. Will had no appetite to face Harry; my lord was not long dressed; Maria had her reasons for being away at least until her eyes were dry. And there is this curious mercy in good breeding—that it can arrange itself in an instant like a table set for guests, napkins folded, crumbs brushed away, as though the knives had not just been drawn.

Lady Castlewood advanced with perfect ease and good humour. “We have heard, Harry,” she said, and gave him a look of particular friendliness, “of this most extraordinary circumstance. My Lord Castlewood said at breakfast he should wait on you this very day, Mr. Warrington; and, cousin Harry, we intend not to love you any the less because you are poor.”

“We shall be able to show now that it is not for your acres that we like you, Harry!” Lady Fanny cried, following her mamma’s lead as neatly as a well-trained echo.

“And I to whom the acres have fallen?” George asked, smiling, and bowed.

“Oh, cousin, we shall like you for being like Harry!” Lady Fanny answered, arch as a pastrycook’s twist of sugar.

They were civil enough to George; but to Harry they were tender—softly familiar, gently kind, affectionately reproachful. Why had he stayed away days and days? Better, Lady Castlewood said, to have had a dish of tea and a game at piquet with them than to have haunted other tables. If they had won enough to buy a paper of pins from him they would have been content; young gentlemen, she sighed, did not know what was for their own good.

“Now you have no more money to play with, you can come and play with us, cousin!” cried fond Lady Fanny, lifting her finger. “So your misfortune will be good fortune to us.”

George watched, puzzled. He had expected London to be all claws and coldness; yet here were caresses offered to the brother who had not a guinea. Perhaps people were not so black as painted. Or perhaps they were—only in a subtler dye that does not run at first washing.

After near twenty minutes of this sweet seasoning, Lord Castlewood entered at last, wig on head and sword at side, composed and shining like a man who has been basted and served. He greeted both young men with equal politeness.

“If you were to come to us—and I, for one, cordially rejoice to see you—what a pity you did not come a few months earlier!” he said to George. “A certain evening at piquet would then most likely never have taken place. A younger son would have been more prudent.”

“Yes, indeed,” Harry said, obediently.

“Or a kinsman more compassionate,” my lord went on, with a fine air of candour. “But I fear the love of play runs in the blood of all of us. I have it from my father, and it has made me the poorest peer in England. Those fair ladies before you are not exempt. My poor brother Will is a martyr to it; and what I win one day, I lose the next. ’Tis shocking—the rage for play in England. All my poor cousin’s bank-notes parted company from me within twenty-four hours after I got them.”

“I have played, like other gentlemen,” George said, “but never to hurt myself, and never indeed caring much for the sport.”

“When we heard that my lord had played with Harry, we did so scold him,” cried the ladies, as if scolding were a ribbon tied over wrongdoing.

“But if it had not been I,” Lord Castlewood said, “some other person would have had your money. ’Tis a poor consolation; but Harry must take it and be glad that friends won his money who wish him well, not strangers who cared nothing for him and fleeced him.”

“Eh! a tooth out is a tooth out, though it be your brother who pulls it, my lord,” George said, laughing. “Harry must bear the penalty of his faults and pay his debts like other men.”

“I am sure I have never said or thought otherwise,” Harry declared. “’Tis not like an Englishman to be sulky because he is beaten.”

“Your hand, cousin! You speak like a man!” cried my lord, delighted; and the ladies smiled to each other, pleased to see virtue seasoned with masculine bravado.

“My sister in Virginia has known how to bring up her sons as gentlemen!” Lady Castlewood exclaimed.

“I protest you must not be growing so amiable now you are poor, cousin Harry!” Lady Fanny cried. “Why, mamma, we did not know half his good qualities when he was only Fortunate Youth and Prince of Virginia! You are exactly like him, cousin George, but I vow you can’t be as amiable as your brother!”

“I am the Prince of Virginia,” George said gravely, “but I fear I am not the Fortunate Youth.”

Harry was beginning, “By Jove, he is the best—” when a harpsichord sounded above: a few cautious notes, then a little run, as if fingers trembled and corrected themselves. The sound fell down the staircase like something private overheard. Harry blushed; the ladies smiled.

“’Tis Maria, above,” Lady Castlewood said. “Let some of us go up to her.”

They rose and moved to the door; and Harry, colouring deeply, followed them. George was about to go too, but Lord Castlewood checked him.

“Nay—if all the ladies follow your brother,” his lordship said, “let me at least have the benefit of your company and conversation. I long to hear the account of your captivity and rescue, cousin George!”

“Oh, we must hear that too!” cried one of the ladies, lingering.

“I am greedy, and should like it all by myself,” Lord Castlewood answered, and gave her such a stern look that she obeyed, smiling. He followed the women to the door, bowed low, and closed it upon them.

“Your brother has no doubt acquainted you with the history of what has happened to him in this house, cousin George?” Lord Castlewood asked, when they were alone.

“Yes, including the quarrel with Mr. Will and the engagement to my Lady Maria,” George replied, and bowed. “I may be pardoned for saying he hath met with ill fortune here, my lord.”

“Which no one can deplore more cordially than myself,” Lord Castlewood said readily. “My brother lives with horse-jockeys and trainers and the wildest bloods of the town; between us there is very little sympathy. We should not all live together were we not so poor. This is the house our grandmother occupied before she went to America and married Colonel Esmond. Much of the furniture belonged to her.”

George looked round with interest at the wainscoted parlour: the old chairs with their faded needlework, the table scarred by candle and spilled wine, the very air seeming to hold the ghost of other breakfasts, other quarrels. It is strange how a room can keep memory better than many a tongue; and yet a tongue can change its word and lose a world.

“Our house has not flourished in the last twenty years,” my lord continued. “We had a promotion of rank a score of years since, through court interest; but the malady of play has been our ruin. I am a miserable victim to it—only too proud to sell myself and title to a roturière, as many noblemen, less scrupulous, have done. Pride is my fault, my dear cousin. I remember how I was born!” And he laid his hand upon his shirt-frill, turned out his toe, and looked nobly into George’s face.

Young George’s disposition was to believe what people said, as a child believes a recipe written in a fair hand. But once undeceived, he could taste deceit in a sentence the way a careful palate detects rancid butter beneath fine spices. He kept his gravity; his scorn stayed inward, like a laugh swallowed.

“We have all our faults, my lord,” he said. “That of play hath been condoned over and over in gentlemen of our rank. Having heartily forgiven my brother, I cannot presume to be your judge; and instead of playing and losing, I wish sincerely you had both played and won!”

“So do I, with all my heart!” my lord sighed. “I augur well for your goodness when you can speak thus; and for your experience too—you seem to possess more of the world than most young men of your age. Your poor Harry hath the best heart in the world; but I doubt whether his head be very strong.”

“Not very strong, indeed,” George said. “But he hath the art to make friends wherever he goes; and in spite of all his imprudences most people love him.”

“I do—we all do, I’m sure! as if he were our brother!” my lord cried.

“He has often described in his letters his welcome at your lordship’s house,” George went on. “My mother keeps them all, you may be sure. Harry’s style is not learned; but his heart is so good that to read him is better than wit.”

“I may be mistaken,” Lord Castlewood said obstinately gracious, “but I fancy his brother possesses a good heart and good wit too!”

“I am as Heaven made me, cousin,” George said, “and perhaps some more experience and sorrow than has fallen to the lot of most young men.”

“This misfortune of your poor brother—I mean this piece of good fortune, your sudden reappearance—has not quite left Harry without resources?” Lord Castlewood asked, very gently, as if he were lifting a lid to see what remained in the pot.

“With nothing but what his mother can leave him, or I, at her death, can spare him. What is the usual portion here of a younger brother, my lord?”

“Eh! a younger brother here is—you know—in fine, everybody knows what a younger brother is,” my lord said, and shrugged.

George continued, with a frankness that cut. “We are the best of friends, but we are flesh and blood. I don’t pretend to do more for him than is usually done for younger brothers. Why give him money—that he should squander it at cards or horse-racing? My lord, we have cards and jockeys in Virginia too; and my poor Harry hath distinguished himself in dissipation at home before he came to yours. He inherits the family failing.”

“Poor fellow, poor fellow—I pity him!”

“Our estate is great, but our income is small,” George said. “We have little more money than what we get from England for our tobacco—and very little of that too—for our tobacco comes back to us in the shape of goods: clothes, leather, groceries, ironmongery—nay, wine and beer for our people and ourselves. Harry may come back and share all these: there is a nag in the stable for him, a piece of venison on the table, a little ready money to keep his pocket warm, and a coat or two every year. This will go on while my mother lives—unless, which is far from improbable, he gets into some quarrel with Madam Esmond. Then, while I live, he will have the run of the house and all it contains; then, if I die leaving children, he will be less and less welcome. His future is dismal unless some strange luck turn up on which we were fools to speculate. Henceforth he is doomed to dependence; and I know no worse lot than to be dependent on a self-willed woman like our mother. The means he had to make himself respected at home he hath squandered away here. He has flung his patrimony to the dogs; poverty and subserviency are now his only portion.”

He spoke with spirit and speed, as if he feared his own pity might rise and spoil the argument. Lord Castlewood listened respectfully, as men listen to a hard truth when it is not their hard truth.

“You speak well, Mr. Warrington. Have you ever thought of public life?” my lord asked.

“Of course,” George replied. “Like every man of my station—every man, that is, who cares for something beyond a dice-box or a stable. I hope to take my own place; my unlucky brother must content himself with his. I say this advisedly, having heard from him of certain engagements he has formed, and which it would be misery to all parties were he to attempt to execute now.”

“Your logic is very strong,” my lord said. “Shall we go up and see the ladies? There is a picture above-stairs which your grandfather is said to have executed. Before you go, my dear cousin, you will please to fix a day when our family may have the honour of receiving you. Castlewood is always your home when we are there. It is something like your Virginian Castlewood, from your account. We have beef and mutton and ale and wood in plenty; but money is woefully scarce amongst us.”

They went up. In the drawing-room they found only Lady Maria, who came out of the embrasure of a window where she and Harry had been talking. The light struck her face and showed plainly what powder and composure could not hide: swollen lids, a mouth held firm by sheer will, the look of a woman whose heart has been pulled at like thread from a seam.

George made his best bow; Maria curtseyed low. “You are indeed wonderfully like your brother,” she said, giving him her hand. “And from what he says, cousin George, I think you are as good as he is.”

At the sight of her tearful face George felt a pang of remorse. Poor thing, he thought: Harry has been vaunting my generosity and virtue to her, and I have been downstairs playing the selfish elder brother. How old she looks! How could he ever have a passion for such a woman as that? How? Because Harry did not see with George’s eyes. He saw with his own—and perhaps he was beginning now, painfully, to see differently.

After the introduction there was a little talk, which Maria managed at first with that trained ease society demands—words laid like a cloth over the bare board. But even ladies, consummate hypocrites of cheer, cannot keep up the mask for long when the face beneath is wet. After a sentence or two she broke into a sob; then, motioning Harry away with her hand—as though she would not even take comfort that might shame her—she fled from the room.

Harry sprang forward, then stopped, checked by that sign. Lord Castlewood said his poor sister was subject to fits of nerves and had already been ill that morning. After this, the young gentlemen judged it needless to prolong their visit. Lord Castlewood went down with them, admired the horses at the door, spoke of their points as if horseflesh were the one honest subject left in the world, waved them off with friendliness.

“And so we have been coaxing and cuddling in the window, and we part good friends, Harry—is it not so?” George said, as Harry gathered the reins.

“Oh, she is a good woman!” Harry cried, and flicked the whip; the harness chimed, that bright, domestic music of leather and brass. “I know you’ll think so when you come to know her.”

“When you take her home to Virginia? A pretty welcome our mother will give her. She will never forgive me for not breaking the match off—nor you for making it.”

“I can’t help it, George!” Harry said, and leaned forward, driving as if speed could carry him out of consequence. “Don’t you be popping your ugly head so close to my ears, Gumbo! After what has passed between us, I am bound in honour to stand by her. If she sees no objection, I must find none. I told her all. I told her Madam would be very rusty at first; but she is very fond of me, and must end by relenting. And when you come to the property, I told her I knew my dearest George so well that I might count upon sharing with him.”

“The deuce you did!” George said sharply. “Let me tell you, my dear, that I have been telling my Lord Castlewood quite a different story—that as an elder brother I intend to have all my rights—there, don’t flog that near horse so—and that you can but look forward to poverty and dependence.”

“What! You won’t help me?” Harry cried, turning quite pale.
