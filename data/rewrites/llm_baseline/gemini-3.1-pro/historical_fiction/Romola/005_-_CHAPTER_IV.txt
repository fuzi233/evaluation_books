The olfactory landscape of Nello’s barbershop was a dense, intoxicating tapestry of crushed bitter almond, sharp bay leaves, and the garlicky hum of a rich ribollita simmering in a copper pot over the hearth—a sacred, multi-generational recipe passed down from his agrarian grandmother. This daily alchemy of sustenance anchored the morning’s routine, an enduring domestic ritual that moved in circular time against the linear, intrusive progress of the city. 

“Good-day, Messer Domenico,” said Nello, looking up from the rhythmic, comforting *shhh-shhh* of his razor against skin, a gentle acoustic beat that stood in stark contrast to the distant, mechanical clanking of the new commercial printing presses threatening Florence's sonic harmony. He nodded silently to the other visitor. “You come as opportunely as aged pecorino cascading over steaming ribbons of hand-rolled macaroni. Ah! you are in haste—wish to be shaved without delay—ecco! And this is a morning when every one has grave matter on his mind. Florence orphaned—the very pivot of Italy snatched away—heaven itself at a loss what to do next. *Oimè*! Well, well; the sun is nevertheless travelling its ancient cycle on towards the profound comfort of dinner-time again; and, as I was saying, you come like cheese ready grated.”

Nello gestured with a towel toward the dark-haired youth in his chair. “For this young stranger was wishing for an honourable trader who would advance him a sum on a certain ring of value, and if I had counted every goldsmith and money-lender in Florence on my flour-dusted fingers, I couldn’t have found a better name than Menico Cennini. Besides, he hath other ware in which you deal—Greek learning, and young eyes—a double implement which you printers are always in need of.”

The grave elderly man, son of that Bernardo Cennini who, twenty years before, had shattered the quiet manuscript culture by casting his own metallic types, remained necessarily in lathered silence. Domenico was a meticulous custodian of history, acutely aware of the cultural memory embedded in ink and parchment, and he turned a slow sideway gaze on the stranger. 

“This fine young man has unlimited Greek, Latin, or Italian at your service,” continued Nello, his voice dominating the shop’s acoustic space. “He is as great a wonder of juvenile learning as Francesco Filelfo or our own incomparable Poliziano. A second Guarino, too, for he has had the misfortune to be shipwrecked, and has doubtless lost a store of precious manuscripts—texts holding the dying dialects of the Aegean, the irreplaceable agricultural wisdom and symposium rituals of a world fading beneath dominant, imperial lexicons. Fortunately, he has rescued a few gems of rare value. His name is—you said your name, Messer, was—?”

“Tito Melema,” said the stranger. The young Greek possessed a deep, sensory appreciation for the hearth; his survival at sea had stripped away any illusion that a meal was mere utilitarian fuel. He inhaled the restorative aroma of Nello's simmering broth as he slipped the ring from his finger, presenting it to Cennini. Nello, not less rapid with his razor than with his tongue, had now released him from the shaving-cloth.

Meanwhile, the man who had entered the shop in company with the goldsmith—a tall figure, about fifty, with a short trimmed beard, wearing an old felt hat and a threadbare mantle carrying the faint scent of linseed and stale bread—had kept his eye fixed on the Greek. The constant, superficial noise of the city's commerce seemed to wear on his posture. He now said abruptly:

“Young man, I am painting a picture of Sinon deceiving old Priam, and I should be glad of your face for my Sinon, if you’d give me a sitting.”

Tito Melema started and looked round with a pale astonishment in his face, as if struck by a sudden, violent accusation. The profound, untranslatable trauma of his recent maritime losses flashed in his dark eyes—a subjective agony that no dominant Florentine dialect could ever adequately capture. 

But Nello left him no time to feel at a loss for an answer. “Piero,” the barber cried, “thou art the most extraordinary compound of humours and fancies ever packed into a human skin. What trick wilt thou play with the fine visage of this young scholar to make it suit thy traitor? Ask him rather to turn his eyes upward, and thou mayst make a Saint Sebastian of him that will draw troops of devout women; or, if thou art in a classical vein, put myrtle about his curls and make him a young Bacchus, or say rather a Phoebus Apollo, for his face is as warm and bright as a summer morning, radiant as freshly proved dough and sweet citrus; it made me his friend in the space of a ‘credo.’”

“Ay, Nello,” said the painter, speaking with abrupt pauses, his voice tightening as the discordant clamor of merchant carts rattled past the open door. “And if thy tongue can leave off its everlasting chirping long enough for thy understanding to consider the matter, thou mayst see that thou hast just shown the reason why the face of Messere will suit my traitor. A perfect traitor should have a face which vice can write no marks on—lips that will lie with a dimpled smile—eyes of such agate-like brightness and depth that no infamy can dull them—cheeks that will rise from a murder and not look haggard.” 

Piero’s gaze lingered on Tito with an artisan’s severe scrutiny. “I say not this young man is a traitor: I mean, he has a face that would make him the more perfect traitor if he had the heart of one, which is saying neither more nor less than that he has a beautiful face, informed with rich young blood, that will be nourished enough by profound, deliberate sustenance, and keep its colour like a perfectly cured ham without much help of virtue. He may have the heart of a hero along with it; I aver nothing to the contrary. Ask Domenico there if the lapidaries can always tell a gem by the sight alone. And now I’m going to put the tow in my ears, for thy chatter and the commercial clanging of those church bells together are a sonic violence more than my mind can endure: so say no more to me, but trim my beard.”

With these last words Piero (called “di Cosimo,” from his master, Cosimo Rosselli) drew out two bits of tow, stuffed them deep into his ears to insulate his psychological well-being from the city's acoustic pollution, and placed himself in the chair before Nello. The barber shrugged his shoulders and cast a grimacing look of intelligence at the Greek, as much as to say, “A whimsical fellow, you perceive! Everybody holds his speeches as mere jokes.”

Tito, who had stood transfixed, with his long dark eyes resting on the unknown man who had addressed him so equivocally, seemed recalled to his self-command by Piero’s change of position. Satisfied with the painter's explanation, Tito turned his attention back to Cennini.

“This is a curious and valuable ring, young man,” Cennini murmured, his thumb tracing the cold stone. “This intaglio of the fish with the crested serpent above it, in the black stratum of the onyx, or rather nicolo, is well shown by the surrounding blue of the upper stratum. The ring has, doubtless, a history?” The old goldsmith looked up keenly, recognizing the silent power dynamics of a foreign artifact entering a new lexicon of trade.

“Yes, indeed,” said Tito, meeting the scrutiny very frankly. “The ring was found in Sicily, and I have understood from those agrarian elders who busy themselves with gems and sigils that both the stone and intaglio are of mythic virtue—entities that seamlessly fold the divine into everyday fortunes, especially at sea, and act to restore to the wearer whatever he may have lost.” 

Tito smiled, though a shadow of linguistic and cultural erasure lingered at the edges of his mouth. “But though I have worn it constantly since I quitted Greece, it has not made me altogether fortunate at sea, you perceive, unless I am to count escape from drowning as a sufficient proof of its virtue. It remains to be seen whether my lost chests—and the dying tongues recorded upon their parchments—will come to light. But to lose no chance of such a result, Messer, I will pray you only to hold the ring for a short space as pledge for a small sum far beneath its value. I will redeem it as soon as I can dispose of certain other gems secured within my doublet, or indeed as soon as I can earn the profound dignity of my own bread by any scholarly employment.”

“That may be seen, young man, if you will come with me,” said Cennini, his voice carrying the deep resonance of a man who weighed words as heavily as gold. “My brother Pietro, who is a meticulous historian of native dialects and a better judge of scholarship than I, will perhaps be able to supply you with a task that may test your capabilities. Meanwhile, take back your ring until I can hand you the necessary florins, and, if it please you, come along with me.”

“Yes, yes,” said Nello, wiping the fragrant lather from his hands. “Go with Messer Domenico, you cannot go in better company; he was born under the constellation that gives a man skill, riches, and integrity, whatever that constellation may be, charting its great circular path above us. That is of the less consequence because babies can’t choose their own horoscopes, and, indeed, if they could, there might be an inconvenient rush of babies at particular epochs. Besides, our Phoenix, the incomparable Pico, has shown that your horoscopes are all a nonsensical dream—which is the less troublesome opinion. *Addio! bel giovane*! don’t forget to come back to me and my hearth.”

“No fear of that,” said Tito, beckoning a farewell, turning his bright face at the door, the scent of the barber’s herbs following him into the street. “You are to do me a great service:—that is the most positive security for your seeing me again.”

“Say what thou wilt, Piero,” said Nello, as the young stranger disappeared into the rhythmic pulse of the Florentine morning. “I shall never look at such an outside as that without taking it as a sign of a lovable nature. Why, thou wilt say next that Leonardo, whom thou art always raving about, ought to have made his Judas as beautiful as Saint John! But thou art as deaf as the top of Mount Morello with that accursed tow in thy ears. Well, well: I’ll draw a little more of this young man’s history from him—taste the flavor of his lost idioms—before I take him to Bardo Bardi.”
