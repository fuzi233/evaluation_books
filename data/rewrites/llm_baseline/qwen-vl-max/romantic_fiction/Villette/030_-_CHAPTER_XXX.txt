The reader should not rush to conclusions—should not assume, with too generous a heart, that from the day of our first encounter, M. Paul transformed into a man gentle and easy to live with, no longer capable of casting shadows of danger or discomfort around him. No. He remained, at his core, a small man governed by moods that defied reason. When overwrought—which was often—he became sharply irritable, his temper flaring like tinder in a storm. And beneath his veins ran a dark current, thick with the poison of jealousy—not merely the tender kind born of love, but a colder, sharper breed rooted in pride and intellect. I used to watch him as he sat, brow furrowed, lip curled, scrutinizing my exercises—always finding fault, though there were fewer than he wished. He liked me to stumble, to make mistakes; a cluster of errors pleased him as much as a handful of nuts might delight a child. I once thought, sitting quietly beside him, that he bore a strange resemblance to Napoleon Bonaparte. I still think so.

In his utter lack of magnanimity, he mirrored the Emperor. M. Paul would have quarreled with twenty learned women without hesitation, carried on petty wars with entire circles of scholars, never caring for dignity or decorum. He would have banished fifty Madame de Staëls had they crossed him. I remember one fierce episode involving Madame Panache—a woman temporarily employed by Madame Beck to teach history. She was intelligent—truly, she knew her subject well—and possessed the rare art of making what she knew shine. Words flowed easily from her, confidence filled her voice. Her appearance was striking; many would have called her “a fine woman.” Yet something about her boldness, her fullness of presence, grated against M. Paul’s delicate, exacting sensibilities. The sound of her voice echoing through the hall would send him into agitation; her long stride down the corridor would prompt him to snatch up his papers and flee.

One day, with deliberate malice, he intruded upon her class. In seconds, he grasped her method—different from his own. Without ceremony or courtesy, he pointed out what he called her errors. Whether he expected submission, I do not know. But he met resistance—sharp, unyielding, and laced with rebuke for his unwarranted interference. Instead of withdrawing gracefully, he threw down the gauntlet. Madame Panache, fierce as any warrior queen, picked it up instantly. She snapped her fingers in his face, launched into a torrent of words. M. Emanuel was eloquent—but Madame Panache was relentless.

A bitter rivalry followed. Rather than laugh at her pride, M. Paul detested her with genuine fury. He pursued her with grim determination, refusing sleep, food, even pleasure, until she was expelled from the school. He won the battle, but I cannot say the victory adorned him with grace. Once, I ventured to suggest this. To my surprise, he admitted I might be right. Yet he insisted that when confronted with people like her—coarse, self-satisfied, proud—he lost control. A deep, active aversion drove him to war, to eradication.

Three months later, hearing she had fallen on hard times and faced real want, he forgot his hatred. With the same energy he had shown in destruction, he now worked tirelessly to secure her another post. When she came to thank him, to mend their rift, her familiar voice—slightly loud, her manner—slightly forward—overwhelmed him. Within ten minutes, he rose abruptly and bowed her out, himself nearly bowing with the effort, consumed by nervous irritation.

In his hunger for power, his need to dominate, M. Paul resembled Bonaparte. He was not always a man to be obeyed. Sometimes, it was necessary to resist—to stand firm, to meet his gaze and tell him plainly that his demands exceeded reason, that his rule approached tyranny.

He watched the emergence of talent under his charge with suspicion, almost hostility. When a new gift began to stir within a student, he scowled. He held back his hand—perhaps saying, “Come on if you can,” but offering no aid. Only after the struggle was over, after life had been drawn into breath, after the heart beat and the eyes sparkled with awareness, did he consider recognition. But even then, he refused to nurture. “Prove yourself true before I cherish you,” he declared. And how difficult he made that proof! Thorns, stones, obstacles—every step was paved with hardship.

He observed trials without emotion, demanding they be endured. He followed the marks left behind—sometimes stained with blood—watching closely, coldly, like a stern guard over a suffering pilgrim. When at last he allowed rest, he opened the eyelids with ruthless fingers, peering into the brain, into the heart, searching for vanity, pride, falsehood—any flaw hidden in the deepest corners of being.

If, finally, he permitted sleep, it was only for a moment. Then he would wake the student suddenly, assign new tasks, send them on exhausting errands when they were weary, test their temper, their mind, their strength. Only when every trial had been faced, when the most corrosive acid had failed to tarnish the metal, did he acknowledge its worth—and then, in silence, stamped it with his approval.

I am not blind to these flaws. Until the end of the last chapter, M. Paul had not been my teacher. He had not given me lessons. But shortly after, overhearing me admit ignorance in arithmetic—one subject where even a charity-school boy would blush—he took me under his wing. He examined me, found me woefully lacking, gave me books, set me tasks.

At first, he did so with pleasure—even triumph. He condescended to say I was “bonne et pas trop faible”—well-intentioned, not entirely without ability—but, he added, “as yet in a state of wretchedly imperfect mental development.” This was true: the beginning of every effort has always brought me a peculiar weakness. Even forming simple acquaintances, I could never prove myself quick-witted. Each new chapter in life began with a heavy, discouraging passage.

While this lasted, M. Paul was kind, patient, gentle. He saw my pain, felt my shame. His eyes grew moist when mine clouded with tears. Burdened as he was, he stole moments of rest to help me. But when the darkness began to lift, when my faculties stirred, when I began to work beyond what he assigned—when I doubled, trebled, quadrupled his tasks to please him—his kindness turned harsh. The light in his eyes dimmed, changed from warmth to fire. He grew restless, resistant, controlling. The more I did, the less he seemed satisfied.

Sarcastic remarks—so sharp they stunned me—began to fill the air. Then came veiled insults about “the pride of intellect.” I was warned, vaguely, of some doom if I ever stepped beyond the bounds meant for my sex, if I dared crave knowledge deemed unfeminine.

Alas, I had no such desire. What I loved, I sought to fulfill. But the grand hunger for science—the divine thirst for discovery—I knew only in fleeting flashes. Yet when M. Paul mocked me, I wanted those feelings more deeply. His injustice sparked ambition—it fueled my spirit, gave wings to my dreams.

At first, his sneer hurt. But soon it burned in my veins, setting my pulse racing. Whatever gifts I had—feminine or otherwise—they were God’s giving. I resolved to be ashamed of none.

The conflict was fierce. It seemed I had lost his affection. He treated me strangely. In his worst moments, he accused me of feigning incompetence when I claimed to be “faible.” Other times, he turned on me suddenly, accusing me of plagiarism—of extracting wisdom from books I had never heard of, books I would surely fall asleep reading.

Once, when he leveled such an accusation, I rose against him. I gathered his books from my desk, filled my apron, and dumped them at his feet. “Take them away, M. Paul,” I said. “Teach me no more. I never asked to be made learned, and you have forced me to see that learning is not happiness.”

I returned to my desk, laid my head on my arms, and refused to speak to him for two days.

He wounded me. His affection had been sweet, precious—something new and rare. Now, it seemed gone. I cared nothing for his lessons.

Yet the books stayed. He restored them carefully. He came as usual to teach. He made peace—too quickly, perhaps. I should have held firm. But when he looked kind, offered his hand, I could not recall the harshness. Reconciliation is always sweet.

One morning, a message arrived from my godmother, inviting me to attend a lecture in the public rooms. Dr. John delivered it himself, speaking to Rosine, who, seeing M. Emanuel passing to the first class, boldly stepped before my desk, hands in pockets, and repeated the invitation aloud—saucily, defiantly—ending with: “Qu’il est vraiment beau, Mademoiselle, ce jeune docteur! Quels yeux—quel regard! Tenez! J’en ai le cœur tout ému!”

When she left, M. Paul demanded why I allowed “cette fille effrontée, cette créature sans pudeur” to address me so.

I had no answer. Rosine spoke as she pleased—her sense of reverence was underdeveloped. And what she said was true: Graham _was_ handsome. He had fine eyes, a compelling glance. I couldn’t help but agree. “Elle ne dit que la vérité,” I said.

“Ah! vous trouvez?” he asked.

“Mais, sans doute.”

Our lesson that day was grueling. We were relieved when it ended. The students rushed out, trembling, exultant. I too prepared to leave—until a command stopped me.

“I want fresh air,” I muttered. “The stove is too hot.”

He said nothing. Just silence. And there he sat—between my desk and the stove, a creature seemingly immune to heat—proceeding to confront me with… a Greek quotation.

M. Paul harbored a deep suspicion that I knew both Greek and Latin. Like monkeys said to possess speech but hide it for fear of misuse, I was believed to conceal a wealth of knowledge—secretly, criminally. It was whispered that I’d enjoyed a classical education, feasted on Hymettus honey, hoarded golden truths in memory, silently feeding my mind.

He tried every trick to expose my secret—placing Latin and Greek texts in my path, watching me like jailors watched Joan of Arc. He quoted passages, rolling out their beauty with musical precision—his voice rich, expressive, commanding. And all the while, he fixed me with a piercing, often malicious eye.

He expected grand reactions. None came. I didn’t understand. So I couldn’t be charmed—or annoyed.

Frustrated, angry, he clung to his belief. My responses were stone. My face, a mask.

He could not accept the truth—that I was simply ordinary. People must believe in illusions. If none are offered, they invent them.

At times, I wished his suspicions were true. There were moments when I would have given anything to possess the knowledge he attributed to me. I longed to shock him, to shatter his doubts with a sudden burst of brilliance. I yearned to stand before him, blazing with learning, and confound his assumptions—his “lunettes”—with a dazzling revelation.

Oh, why had no one taught me while I was young? Why had no one made me clever? That way, I could have crushed the mocking spirit in Paul Carl David Emanuel with one cruel, triumphant act.

But no such feat was possible. Today, as always, his quotations fell flat. He shifted ground.

“Women of intellect,” he said—now he was at home.

To him, such a woman was a freak of nature, a mistake, useless in creation—neither wife nor worker. Beauty fulfilled the first role. He believed, truly, that gentle, passive mediocrity was the only comfort for a man’s thoughts. As for work—only male minds could produce meaningful results—hein?

This “hein?” was meant to provoke contradiction. But I replied: “Cela ne me regarde pas: je ne m’en soucie pas.” Then: “May I go, Monsieur? They’ve rung for the second déjeuner.”

“What of that? You’re not hungry?”

“I was,” I said. “I’ve had nothing since breakfast at seven. I’ll have nothing till dinner at five if I miss this bell.”

“Well, I’m in the same plight. Share with me.”

And he broke his brioche in two, handing me half.

His bark was worse than his bite. But the real attack was yet to come.

As we ate, I couldn’t help expressing my wish that I really did know what he accused me of.

“Do you sincerely feel yourself an ignoramus?” he asked, softening.

Had I answered yes—meekly, fully—I believe he would have reached out, and we would have been friends. But I said: “Not exactly. I am ignorant of the knowledge you claim I have. But I _sometimes_, not _always_, feel a knowledge of my own.”

“What do you mean?” he asked sharply.

Unable to explain, I changed the subject.

He finished his half. Seeing I hadn’t eaten enough—and smelling apples from the refectory—I asked if he too noticed the fragrance.

He admitted he did.

“If you let me out by the garden door, I’ll fetch you a plateful,” I said. “They’re excellent—Goton stews them with spice, sugar, and vin blanc. May I go?”

“Petite gourmande!” he smiled. “I haven’t forgotten how much you liked the pâté à la crème I gave you. And you know very well, fetching apples for me is really getting them for yourself. Go—but return quickly.”

And so, on parole, I was released.

My plan: go swiftly, return with speed, place the plate at the door, and vanish—leaving consequences for later.

But his keen instinct anticipated me. He met me at the threshold, pulled me back in, and seated me immediately.

Taking the plate from my hands, he divided the fruit and ordered me to eat my share.

I complied—reluctantly. Irritated by my hesitation, he unleashed a masked, dangerous assault.

All he had said before, I could dismiss as noise—empty threat. Not this.

It was the same unreasonable demand he had made before: that on the next examination day, I—foreigner, untrained—take my place on the first form, improvise a French composition on any subject dictated by any spectator, without grammar or lexicon.

I knew the outcome. I, whose mind refused impromptu invention; who in public was a cipher; whose creativity only stirred in quiet hours, in solitude, in morning silence or evening peace—how could I be expected to summon inspiration on a platform, between Mathilde and Coralie, under Madame Beck’s gaze, for the amusement of a bourgeois from Labassecour?

We battled over this more than once—loud, fierce, tangled in argument and refusal.

On this day, I was reprimanded harshly.

“The obstinacy of your whole sex,” he said, “is concentrated in you. You have an ‘orgueil de diable.’”

I feared failure? What did it matter? Who was I that I shouldn’t fail? It would do me good.

He wanted to see me humiliated—I knew he did.

Then he paused.

“Would you speak now? Be tractable?”

“Never. Not in this matter. Law itself could not compel me. I’d pay a fine, endure imprisonment, rather than write for show, perched on a platform.”

“Could softer motives move you? Would you yield for friendship’s sake?”

“Not a whit. No form of friendship has the right to demand such a thing. True friendship would never harass me thus.”

Then, with a sneer—M. Paul could sneer with supreme artistry—curling his lip, flaring his nostrils, narrowing his eyes—he said: “Under certain persuasions, from certain quarters, je vous vois d’ici—eagerly accepting the sacrifice, passionately preparing for the effort.”

“Making a fool of myself, a warning, an example, before a hundred and fifty parents of Villette.”

I lost patience.

“I want to be free—to get out into the air! I’m almost feverish.”

“Chut!” he said. “This is just an excuse to run away. _He_ is not hot, with the stove behind him. How can you suffer, safely shielded by his person?”

“I don’t understand your constitution. I know nothing of salamanders. I’m a phlegmatic islander. Sitting in an oven doesn’t agree with me. May I at least go to the well? The apples made me thirsty?”

“If that’s all,” he said, “I’ll fetch the water.”

He went.

And with the door only latched behind me, I did not hesitate.

Before he returned, his half-worried prey had escaped.
