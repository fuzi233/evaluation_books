I hadn’t taken ten steps before something strange caught my eye—a carriage, ancient and crumbling, yet standing upright on its heavy wheels. The skeletons of two horses lay before it, still harnessed to the pole, their bony heads stretching toward the reins held by the skeletal coachman on his tattered seat. The carriage doors had long since fallen away, and inside sat two more skeletons, slumped in opposite corners.

Then, as I watched, they jerked awake. Bones clattered as they lurched upright, each scrambling from its side. One collapsed immediately, a heap of yellowed bones. The other swayed precariously before shuffling around the carriage, gripping it for support, its joints creaking with the effort. It knelt beside the fallen one, fumbling to help it rise—only to nearly topple itself in the attempt.

At last, the first skeleton sat up, its hollow skull swiveling as if taking in its surroundings. Ignoring its companion, it clutched the spokes of the rear wheel and hauled itself upright, hands gripping one knee as if in pain. The second skeleton rose with equal difficulty, its voice thin and distant, like wind through dead branches.

*“Yes, I have,”* the other snapped, voice rough and brittle. *“You did nothing to help, and now this damned knee is out!”*

*“No doubt, madam—your best was poor indeed! I thought I’d never stand again!”* He paused, then let out a rasping laugh. *“But—bless my soul! Are you out in your bones?”*

She glanced down at herself. *“I’ve nothing else to be out in. And you can hardly complain.”* Her tone sharpened. *“What is this? Am I dreaming?”*

*“You may be. I can’t say. But this knee won’t let me pretend.”* He shifted, grimacing. *“And by the devil—I’m bones myself! Are these mine? Every joint aches worse than the last!”*

*“Likely you were too drunk to notice the damp,”* she remarked dryly.

*“How do you know what my fools call me?”* he demanded. *“I don’t recall you!”*

They bickered like long-familiar enemies, each word a barb. She bound his knee with a strand of grass, and he tested it with a cautious stamp.

*“You used to stamp differently,”* she observed.

*“Did I?”* He studied her. *“Now that I look—I think I hated you.”*

*“Ah.”* He gave a rattling chuckle. *“Then we’re damned together.”*

She snatched up a broken branch to steady herself and turned away.

*“Give me that stick!”* he barked.

*“I think I’ll keep it,”* she replied, hobbling off.

He tried to follow, but his knee gave way again. She laughed as he cursed.

*“Now you’ll need splints forever,”* she called back. *“Dry bones don’t mend.”*

I stared, bewildered. *What hell is this?*

Then a voice spoke beside me—the raven’s, though I saw no bird. *“They are in hell. You are not.”*

A moment later, the old librarian stood before me, his coat flapping like wings.

*“They were handsome once,”* he said. *“Now even their bones cling to pride. But hatred cannot last forever. Even they may yet learn.”*

*“Will they?”* I asked.

*“We shall see.”* His eyes gleamed. *“Love runs deeper than hate, in the end.”*

And with that, he was gone—only a raven darting low across the ground, leaving me to wonder at the grotesque tragedy before me.
