"It will hardly be denied that even in this frail and corrupted world, we sometimes meet persons who, in their very mien and aspect, as well as in the whole habit of life, manifest such a signature and stamp of virtue, as to make our judgment of them a matter of intuition rather than the result of continued examination."

Mirah claimed she had slept soundly. When she descended in Mab's black gown, her dark hair drying into fresh curls from its recent bath, she appeared as someone beginning to heal after long sorrow. Her cheeks, previously pale, were regaining color, though faint blue circles lingered beneath her eyes. It was Mab who brought her breakfast and led her down, proud of the tiny felt slippers she had hurried to purchase, as the house lacked shoes small enough for Mirah. Mirah's borrowed dress ended at her ankles, revealing cheap garments that molded to her feet like the protective sheaths of budding flowers. To Mab, the farthing buckles were jewels.

"Oh, if you please, mamma?" cried Mab, clasping her hands and bowing toward Mirah's feet as they entered the parlor. "Look at the slippers, how beautifully they fit! I declare she resembles Queen Budoor—"reciting the verse about delicate feet supported by the Creator."

Mirah glanced down at her own feet with a childlike wonder before smiling at Mrs. Meyrick. The older woman, inwardly thinking, *One could hardly imagine this creature harboring an evil thought,* returned the smile gently. "I fear the feet have sustained a heavy burden recently," she said. "But today, she shall rest and be my companion."

"And she will tell you so many things, and I shall miss them!" grumbled Mab, feeling relegated to the sidelines of a romantic tale she desperately wished to inhabit fully, despite her obligations to pupils.

Kate was already sketching by the river, and Amy was out on business. This solitude, desired by the mother, allowed her to be alone with this stranger, whose story was undoubtedly tragic yet necessary to unfold.

The small front parlor felt like a sanctuary that morning. Sunlight spilled onto the river, drawing in soft air through the open window. The walls, adorned with paintings, seemed to host a silent assembly of witnesses: the Virgin soaring with her cherubs, Grand Melancholia with her solemn universe, the Prophets and Sibyls, the School of Athens, the Last Supper, and various groups from far-off ages rendered in monochrome. There were portraits of grave artists, the Tragic Muse, children musings, and Italian poets. The neat mother, having weathered troubles with a cheerful countenance, sorted colored wools for her embroidery. Hafiz purred on the windowsill; the clock on the mantelpiece ticked steadily, undisturbed by the distant rumble of wheels outside. Mrs. Meyrick believed this profound quiet was the perfect invitation for speech and refrained from disturbing it.

Mirah sat opposite in her accustomed posture, hands clasped on her lap, ankles crossed. Her eyes traveled slowly over the surroundings until they rested on Mrs. Meyrick with a placid reverence. Finally, she began to speak softly.

"I remember my mother's face better than anything else," Mirah said. "Yet I was not seven when I was taken away, and I am nineteen now."

"There are some earliest impressions that endure the longest," Mrs. Meyrick replied.

"Yes, it was the beginning. I think my life started with waking up and loving my mother's face. It was so close to mine; her arms were round me, and she sang. One hymn she repeated endlessly, teaching me to join her. It was the first song I ever knew. They were always Hebrew hymns, and because I did not understand the words, they seemed filled entirely with our love and happiness. Lying in my little bed, with white above me, she would bend over, singing in a sweet, low voice. I can dream myself back to that time when I am awake, and it returns to me in sleep—my hand was so small then, reaching up to her face, and she kissed it. Sometimes in my dreams, I tremble, thinking we are both dead. But then I wake, and my hand lies there, and for a moment, I hardly know myself. Yet, if I could see my mother again, I would know her instantly."

"You must expect changes after twelve years," Mrs. Meyrick said gently, indicating her own grey hair, which had been bright brown a decade prior. "Days and months pass over us like restless little birds, leaving marks of their flight backward and forward. Especially when they are birds with heavy hearts, they tread heavily."

"Ah, I am certain her heart was heavy with want for me. But to feel her joy if we met again, to make her know my love and bring deep comfort after all her mourning! If that were possible, I would mind nothing. I would be glad to have survived my trouble. I did despair. The world seemed miserable and wicked; no one helped me to bear their looks and words. I felt my mother was dead, and death was the only mercy. But yesterday, in the last moment when I longed for the water to close over me, thinking death the best image of mercy, goodness came to me living. I felt trust in the living. And strangely, I began to hope she was living too. Now, here with you this morning, peace and hope have flooded into me. I want nothing; I can wait. Because I hope, I believe, and I am so grateful. You have not thought evil of me; you have not despised me."

Mirah spoke with low-toned fervor, sitting as still as a painting throughout.

"Many others would feel as we do, my dear," Mrs. Meyrick said, a mist rising in her eyes as she looked at her work.

"But I did not meet them—they did not come to me."

"How was it that you were taken from your mother?"

"It takes me a long time to reach that point," Mirah hesitated. "It is dreadful to speak of, yet I must tell you everything. My father took me away. I thought we were going on a short journey, and I was pleased. There was a box containing all my little things. But we boarded a ship and drifted farther from the land. Then I became ill; I thought it would never end. It was the first misery, seeming endless. At last, we landed. I knew nothing then and believed my father. He comforted me, telling me I would return to my mother. But we had reached America, and it was many years before we returned to Europe. At first, I often asked my father when we would go back. I tried to learn writing quickly so I could write to my mother. But one day, when he found me attempting to write a letter, he took me on his knee and told me that my mother and brother were dead—that was why we did not return. I remember my brother slightly; he carried me once, but he was not always home. I believed my father. I visualized them under the earth, eyes forever closed. I never suspected it was untrue. I used to cry every night in my bed for a long time. Then, when she visited me so often in my sleep, I thought she must be living nearby, though I could not always see her. That comforted me. I was never afraid of the dark because of that belief. Often during the day, I would shut my eyes, bury my face, and try to see and hear her singing. Eventually, I learned to do this without closing my eyes."

Mirah paused, her face holding a sweet content, as if viewing a happy vision, while gazing out toward the river.

"Still, your father was not unkind to you, I hope?" Mrs. Meyrick asked after a moment, anxious to redirect the conversation.

"No," Mirah answered. "He petted me and took pains to teach me. He was an actor. Later, I discovered that the 'CoBURG' he mentioned as going to at home was a theater. However, he had more involvement with the theater than merely acting. He had not always been an actor; he had been a teacher and knew many languages. His acting was not particularly good, I think, but he managed the stage, wrote, and translated plays. An Italian lady, a singer, lived with us for a long time. Both she and he taught me, and I also had a master who made me memorize and recite. I worked quite hard, though I was so young. I was not nine when I first stepped onto the stage. I could learn things easily and was not afraid. But from then on, I hated our way of life. My father had money, and we possessed finery in a disorderly fashion. Men and women constantly came and went; there was loud laughter, disputing, strutting, finger-snapping, jeering, and faces I did not like to look at—though many petted and caressed me. Yet, I remembered my mother. Even at first, when I understood little, I shrank away from those external things into a companionship with thoughts unlike them. I gathered thoughts rapidly because I read many things—plays and poetry, Shakespeare and Schiller—and learned of evil and good. My father began to believe I might become a great singer; my voice was considered wonderful for a child. He secured the best teaching for me. But it was painful that he boasted of me, setting me to sing for display at any moment, as if I were a musical box.
