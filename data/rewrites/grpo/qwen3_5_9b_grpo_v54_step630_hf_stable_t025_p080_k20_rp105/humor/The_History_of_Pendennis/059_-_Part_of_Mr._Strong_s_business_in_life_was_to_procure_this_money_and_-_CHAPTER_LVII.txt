Major Pendennis kept his word to Warrington, at least enough to satisfy his own conscience and to offer Helen some relief regarding her son. He assured her that Arthur's connection with the detestable gatekeeper was severed forever, sparing her further anxiety about an imprudent attachment or a humiliating marriage for Pen. For Arthur, the relief came after the initial sting to his vanity faded; he reasoned that Miss Fanny would not die of love for him and that the brief, unfortunate liaison would bear no ugly fruit.

With these matters settled, the party was finally free to execute their planned journey to the Continent. Arthur Pendennis, *rentier* and *voyageant* alongside Madame Pendennis and Mademoiselle Bell, joined George Warrington, *particulier*, age thirty-two, six feet tall (English), ordinary features, black hair and beard, etc. They secured passports from the Belgian consul at Dover and crossed the channel to Ostend. From there, they leisurely toured Bruges and Ghent on their way to Brussels and the Rhine. It is not our purpose to chronicle this well-worn tour, nor Laura's delight in the tranquil, ancient cities she saw for the first time, nor Helen's wonder at the Beguine convents. Yet, the sight of black-veiled nuns kneeling with outstretched arms before illuminated altars, and the strange pomp of Catholic worship, filled her with almost terror. Barefoot friars in the streets, crowned images of saints defying written law, priests in gorgeous robes, and theatres open on Sundays—all these new sights bewildered the simple country lady.

When the young men returned from their evening drives, they found their mother's books of devotion laid out. Upon entering, Laura would often cease reading the psalms, the sacred pages Helen loved most. The recent events had cruelly shaken Helen; Laura watched her friend with intense, hidden anxiety. Poor Pen was most constant and affectionate in his attentions, yet a secret hung between them. Helen's wounded bosom yearned for her son, though she harbored a secret anguish, almost rage, at the thought of being dispossessed of his heart. She missed the sacred days of boyhood when Arthur's heart held no secrets, when she was his all in all, when he poured his hopes and childish vanities into her tender embrace. Now, fate and selfishness had driven him forth on wayward wings to seek his own mate. Watching this devouring care in her friend, Laura once confessed, "If Pen had loved me as you wished, I should have gained him, but I should have lost you, mamma. I like you to love me best. Men do not know what it is to love as we do." Helen sighed, agreeing to this sentiment while protesting the rest. For my part, I suppose Miss Laura was right: love is an hour with us, but all night and all day with a woman. Damon has his taxes and parades; Delia has only Damon. Is it not your nature to creep about his feet and twine round his trunk, while he stands like a British man with his hands in his breeches pockets?

Old Pendennis had seen his friends off to the water's edge, leaving Warrington in charge of the expedition. He himself was bound for a brief visit to a great man and friend, after which he intended to join his sister-in-law at the German watering-place. The Major felt his long attentions to his sick family had earned him relaxation. Though the best partridges were thinned, the pheasants remained at Stillbrook. He betook himself to the hospitable mansion of the Marquis of Steyne, where he disported himself with great comfort. A royal duke, foreign notables, and illustrious statesmen visited; it did the old fellow's heart good to see his name in the *Morning Post* among the distinguished company. He was invaluable in a country-house, entertaining young men with queer anecdotes and grivoise stories in the smoking-room, walking the ladies through the gardens, and pointing out where the timber was to be felled or where old Lord Lynx discovered Sir Phelim O'Neal. He knew the number of domestics in the housekeeper's room and had a word for everybody. Richly meriting his vacation, he transferred the command of the family expedition to Warrington, keeping him in the service of the ladies—a servitude George willingly underwent for his friend's sake and the delightful society he found.

Warrington, a good German scholar, gave Miss Laura lessons, while Pen was too weak or lazy to resume his studies. Warrington acted as courier, interpreter, and manager of money matters, finding the English church and walking decorously with the ladies. He walked by Mrs. Pendennis's donkey during evening excursions, procured *Galignani* for her, and devised comfortable seats under the lime-trees. Many a whiskered Prussian or French dandy cast longing glances toward the pretty, fresh-coloured English girl accompanying the pale widow, wishing to dance with her. But Laura rarely appeared in the ballroom unless Pen vouchsafed to walk with her. As for Warrington, the rough diamond lacked the polish of a dancing-master and could not waltz, though he would have liked to learn if he could have had such a partner. Such a partner! *Psha*, what had a stiff bachelor to do with partners? Yet he stayed on. One might have thought him the widow's son, watching her with constant care, or an adventurer seeking her fortune. Very likely he did seek some benefit, for this is a selfish story, and almost everyone is occupied about Number One. Warrington selfishly devoted himself to Helen, who selfishly devoted herself to Pen, who selfishly devoted himself to himself, save for his mother's health, which caused him serious disquiet. Though they sat together, they spoke little; the cloud remained between them.

Every day Laura looked for Warrington, welcoming him with frank eagerness. He found himself talking to her as he never knew he could, performing acts of gallantry that astounded him afterward. He stared blankly in the glass at the crow's feet around his eyes and the streaks of white in his hair, envying the young bucks, the tight-waisted Germans, and the capering Frenchmen for their youth and good looks. Every night he quitted the little circle with greater reluctance, retreating to his lodging feeling lonelier and unhappier. The widow could not miss his attachment. She understood now why Major Pendennis, always a tacit enemy of her darling project, had been so eager for Warrington to join them. Laura openly owned her enthusiastic regard for him; Arthur made no move to intervene, choosing not to see what was happening or to prevent it. Helen was in torture, secretly feuding with her son, in doubt about Laura, and averse to Warrington, the good and generous. No wonder the healing waters of Rosenbad did her good, nor that Doctor von Glauber found no progress in her recovery. Meanwhile, Pen recovered rapidly, sleeping twelve hours a day and eating huge meals, regaining his strength in a couple of months.

After fifteen days at their resting place, a letter arrived from Major Pendennis announcing his speedy arrival at Rosenbad. Soon after, the Major appeared, accompanied by Morgan, his faithful valet. When traveling, the Major wore a jaunty, juvenile costume; to see his back, one might have taken him for one of the young fellows Warrington was beginning to envy. It was not until he moved that the observer noticed Time had weakened his ancient knees and impeded the action of his natty, varnished boots. Magnates of our own country and foreign nations were present that autumn. The elder Pendennis read the strangers' list with gratification, pleased to find acquaintances among the great folks, and intended to present his nephew to a German Grand Duchess, a Russian Princess, and an English Marquis. That very evening, leaning on his nephew's arm, the old gentleman appeared in the halls of the Kursaal, losing or winning a napoleon or two at *Trente-et-quarante*. He claimed he did not play to win or lose, but simply as other folks did. He pointed out Russians and Spaniards gambling for heaps of gold, denouncing their eagerness as sordid and barbarous, while asserting that an English gentleman should play where the fashion is play but not elate or depress himself at the sport. He recounted how his friend, the Marquis of Steyne, had lost eighteen thousand at a sitting and broken the bank three nights running in Paris without showing emotion. "And that's what I call being an English gentleman, Pen, my dear boy," the old gentleman said, warming as he prattled. "The great manner only remains with us and a few families in France."

As Russian Princesses passed him, whose reputations had long ceased to be doubtful, and damaged English ladies accompanied by their faithful attendants, the old Major, with eager garrulity, told his nephew wonderful particulars regarding their lives, diverting the young man with scandals. Egad, he felt quite young again, remarking to Pen as the Princess Obstropski, rouged and grinning, smiled and recognized him. He remembered her in '14 as an actress of the Paris Boulevard, married to the Emperor Alexander's aide-de-camp. He courteously asked leave to call upon the Princess and present his nephew, pointing out a half-dozen other famous personages. What would poor Helen have thought, could she have heard those tales or known to what kind of people her brother-in-law was presenting her son? Only once, leaning on Arthur's arm, had she passed through the room where the green tables were prepared, shrinking terrified from the pandemonium of the croaking croupiers shouting *Rouge gagne* and *Couleur perd*. She had imploringly begged Pen to promise, on his word of honor, that he would never play at those tables. The scene that frightened the simple widow only amused the worldly old veteran, making him feel young again. He could breathe the air cheerfully which stifled her. Her right was not his right; his food was her poison. To the credit of Mr. Pen, he kept honestly the promise made to his mother and stoutly told his uncle of his intention to abide by it.

When the Major arrived, his presence cast a damp upon at least three members of the party: Laura, who had no respect for him; Warrington, whose manner showed involuntary haughtiness; and the timid widow, who dreaded interference with her darling projects. Indeed, the Major, unknown to himself, was the bearer of tidings destined to bring about a catastrophe. Pen and his two ladies had apartments in Rosenbad; Warrington lodged nearby; the Major, befitting his dignity, took quarters at one of the great hotels, the Roman Emperor or the Four Seasons, where hundreds of gamblers and pleasure-seekers sat daily. On the morning after the Major's arrival, Pen went dutifully to pay his respects, finding the sitting-room arranged by Morgan with hats brushed, coats laid out, and despatch-boxes ready. Everything was trim, from the medicine-bottle to the prayer-book, without which the old fellow never traveled.

The elder had just finished a bath for which Rosenbad is famous. His after-bath toilet was not yet complete when Pen arrived. The elder called out cheerily from the inner apartment, and the valet brought a little packet to Pen's address—letters and papers from London, chiefly numbers of the *Pall Mall Gazette* that Mr. Finucane thought his collaborateur would like. Amongst the letters was a little note addressed to "Arther Pendennis, Esquire," which Arthur opened with a start and a blush, reading it with a keen pang of interest and sorrow. Fanny Bolton had come to his house, found him gone to Germany without a word, and prayed but for one word of kindness or the books he had promised. She said she would not reproach those who had found her at his bedside when he was in the fever and turned her away without a word. She thought she would have died of that, but Doctor Goodenough had kindly tended her. She forgave everybody and would pray for Arthur forever. She had kept one little lock of his hair when they cut it off and asked if she might still keep it, or if his mother would order it given up. She was willing to obey him in all things and remembered how kind he once was.

When Major Pendennis, fresh and smirking from his toilet, came out, he found Arthur with the note before him, his face set in savage anger, which surprised the elder. "What news from London, my boy?" he asked faintly. "Are the duns at you that you look so glum?"

"Do you know anything about this letter, sir?" Arthur asked.

"What letter, my good sir?" the other replied dryly.

"You know what I mean—about Miss... about Fanny Bolton," Arthur broke out. "When she was in my room? Was she there when I was delirious? Who sent her out of my chambers? Who intercepted her letters? Who dared to do it? Did you do it, uncle?"

"It's not my practice to tamper with gentlemen's letters or answer damned impertinent questions," Major Pendennis cried out in a tremor of indignation. "There was a girl in your rooms when I came up at great personal inconvenience, and to meet with a return of this kind for my affection to you is not pleasant, by Gad, sir."

"That's not the question, sir," Arthur said hotly. "Did you say anything harsh to this poor girl? Did you send her away from me?"

"I never spoke a word to the girl," the uncle said testily. "Let's change the subject."

"Then it's my mother that did it," Arthur broke out. "Did my mother send that poor child away?"

"I repeat I know nothing about it, sir," the elder said. "Let's change the subject."

"I'll never forgive the person who did it," Arthur said, bouncing up and seizing his hat.

"Stop, Arthur, for God's sake, stop!" cried the Major, but Arthur had rushed out, and the next minute the Major saw him striding rapidly down the street toward his home.
