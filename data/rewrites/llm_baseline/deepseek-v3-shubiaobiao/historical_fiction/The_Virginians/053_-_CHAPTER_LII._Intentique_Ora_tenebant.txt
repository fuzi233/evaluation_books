For months, life at the fort dragged on in weary monotony. The commandant and I quarreled and made up, played greasy games of cards, and scraped out dismal duets—his wheezing flute against my cracked guitar. The poor Fawn endured her master’s drunken rages and nursed him through gout, me through fever, and her child through whatever ailed it. So the days passed until the first chill of autumn, when a hunter arrived with news that set the little garrison buzzing like a stirred hive.

The Marquis de Montcalm was sending reinforcements—fresh troops, artillery, supplies—up the St. Lawrence to secure French holdings and push further into British territory. Museau, our commandant, would be replaced by a superior officer, one who might trade me as a prisoner or hand me over to the Indians in retaliation for British cruelties. The men cheered at the prospect of driving the *Rosbifs* into the sea, but Museau’s face darkened.

*"Eh, commandant!"* I said, *"All very fine—but what of your farm in Normandy? The cider, the tripe, the apple trees?"*

He scowled. *"And where will you be, my boy, when another has my post? Not all officers are as kind as honest Museau. You’ll rot in a cell, or be handed to the tribes. Remember what they did to Braddock’s men? Eyes burned, nails ripped out—ah, but why frighten you?"*

La Biche, his Seneca woman, listened without flinching. She had seen such horrors before. War had made savages of red and white alike.

*"They may roast me,"* I said coolly, *"but you’ll never taste Normandy cider again."*

Museau called for whisky, and that night, his mood shifted between bravado and melancholy. La Biche sat apart, her dark eyes fixed on me, until the crying of her child drove her away. Then Museau and I spoke plainly.

*"How do you know this hunter will help you?"* he demanded.

*"That’s my secret,"* I said—though I might have told him. The man had lingered at the fort, trading furs for drink, gambling with the soldiers. I had sung for them, strumming my battered guitar, and one evening, I chose an English tune: *"Over the hills and far away."*

The hunter’s eyes gleamed. The next day, as he passed me, he hummed the melody under his breath—in perfect English. *"If you’re ready,"* he murmured, *"I am. Talk to the Fawn."*

She was the key. For weeks, she had tended me, slipping in and out of the fort freely. Now she brought me a pick, hidden in her basket of herbs. *"Hide it,"* she whispered. The next evening, she flung a coil of rope at my feet. *"Tonight,"* she said.

Museau feigned ignorance, grumbling over his soup, playing piquet as though nothing were amiss. But when he left to fetch more whisky, La Biche fell at my knees, weeping. Her devotion shook me.

At last, he dismissed me with a mocking bow. *"Goodnight, my little lord—don’t forget your crutch!"*

The storm outside masked my work. I dug beneath the logs, wriggled through, and lowered myself by rope into the ditch. Rain lashed my face as I crept toward the river, heart pounding. The sentry’s tune faded behind me.

By moonlight, I reached the island, and there, from the shadows, came the hunter’s voice—*"Over the hills and far away."*

(Theo’s fingers flew to the harpsichord, striking up the tune, and the room burst into chorus.)

Through forest and over mountain we traveled, avoiding French patrols and hostile tribes. Frost silvered the leaves; fires burned low at night. We passed maple-sugar camps, where families shared venison and shelter. Then, at last, the towering cliffs of Will’s Creek, eagles circling above, and below—Cumberland, and the flag of England flying once more.

Harry gripped my hand. *"While I was wasting my days at the Wells, you were fighting for your life!"*

Mother’s eyes shone. *"You are your mother’s son."*

And so, after a year of captivity, I stood again on free soil, beneath the colors of home.
