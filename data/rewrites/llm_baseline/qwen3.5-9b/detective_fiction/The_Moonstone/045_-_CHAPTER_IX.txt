The doctor's pretty housemaid stood waiting for me, holding the street door open. As I turned to face her, the bright morning light flooded the hall, illuminating the face of Mr. Candy's assistant.

It was undeniable that Ezra Jennings' appearance worked against him in the eyes of the public. His gypsy-like complexion, hollow cheeks, sharp facial bones, dreamy gaze, and extraordinary multi-colored hair created a confusing contradiction between age and youth. These features were calculated to make a bad first impression on a stranger. Yet, despite my instinctive wariness, Ezra Jennings possessed an inscrutable quality that appealed to my sympathies, making it impossible for me to ignore him. Although my worldly experience urged me to acknowledge Mr. Candy's severe decline and depart, my interest in this strange man pinned me to the spot, granting him the chance to speak privately about his employer—a conversation he had clearly been waiting for.

"Are you walking my way, Mr. Jennings?" I asked, noting he held his hat in his hand. "I am heading to call on my aunt, Mrs. Ablewhite."

Jennings replied that he had a patient to visit and was walking in my direction.

Together we left the house. I noticed that the cheerful maid, who had greeted me with such warmth earlier, now received a modest note from Jennings regarding his return time. She responded with pursed lips and eyes deliberately averted from his face. Outside, according to Betteredge, Jennings was universally unpopular. "What a life!" I thought as we descended the steps.

Jennings seemed eager to let me lead the discussion regarding Mr. Candy's illness. His silence conveyed a clear message: *Your turn.* Having my own reasons to address the topic, I readily accepted the role of initiator.

"Judging by the change I observe," I began, "Mr. Candy's illness must have been far more serious than I imagined?"

"It is almost a miracle," Jennings said, "that he survived it."

"Has his memory ever improved since I visited him? He was trying to speak to me..."

"About something that happened before he fell ill?" Jennings prompted, sensing my hesitation.

"Yes."

"His memory of that past period is hopelessly weakened," Jennings explained. "It is tragic that even the fragments remain. He vaguely recalls plans he made or duties he owed prior to his sickness, yet he is utterly incapable of remembering what those plans were or what those duties entailed. He is painfully aware of this deficit and desperately tries to conceal it. If he could have recovered in a state of total oblivion to the past, he would undoubtedly be happier. Perhaps we all would be," he added with a sad smile, "if we could simply forget!"

"There are surely some events in every man's life," I countered, "whose memory he would be reluctant to lose entirely?"

"That, I hope, applies to most men, Mr. Blake. I fear it cannot be said of *all*. Do you have any reason to believe that the lost memory Mr. Candy struggled to retrieve during our brief conversation was one specifically important to *you* that he needed to recall?"

Those words struck precisely at the point I intended to discuss. My growing fascination with this enigmatic man had compelled me to grant him an audience, reserving my own questions until I was convinced of his discretion and integrity. What little he had revealed thus far confirmed that I was speaking to a gentleman possessing an "unsought self-possession"—a hallmark of true breeding worldwide. Regardless of his motives, I felt justified in answering him fully.

"I believe I have a strong personal stake in uncovering the memory Mr. Candy could not recall," I stated. "May I ask if you can suggest any method to aid his memory?"

Jennings looked at me, a sudden flash of interest lighting his dreamy brown eyes.

"Mr. Candy's memory is beyond the reach of restoration," he declared. "I have attempted to assist it frequently since his recovery, and I can speak with certainty on that point."

Disappointment washed over me, and I admitted it.

"I confess you led me to hope for a less discouraging response," I said.

Jennings smiled. "It may not be a final answer, Mr. Blake. It may be possible to trace Mr. Candy's lost recollection without needing to appeal to Mr. Candy himself."

"Indeed? Is it improper for me to ask how?"

"Not at all. My only challenge is explaining myself. May I rely on your patience if I refer once more to Mr. Candy's illness, and if I share certain professional details?"

"Please, proceed! You have already intrigued me with the details."

My eagerness amused, or perhaps pleased, him. He smiled again. We had passed the last houses of the town. Jennings paused briefly to pick wildflowers from a roadside hedge. "How beautiful they are!" he said simply, displaying his small bouquet. "And how few people in England seem to appreciate them as they deserve!"

"You haven't always lived in England?" I asked.

"No. I was born and partly raised in one of our colonies. My father was English, but my mother... We are drifting away from our subject, Mr. Blake; it is my fault. The truth is, I have deep associations with these humble hedge-side flowers. It doesn't matter; let us return to Mr. Candy."

Connecting his sparse personal remarks with his melancholic view that human happiness lies in forgetting the past, I concluded that the story written on his face was authentic. He had suffered as few men do, and there was a distinct strain of foreign blood in his lineage.

"You have likely heard the original cause of Mr. Candy's illness," he resumed. "The night of Lady Verinder's dinner party was marked by heavy rain. My employer drove home soaked to the skin. Finding an urgent message from a patient, he foolishly proceeded immediately to visit the sick person without changing his clothes. I was professionally detained elsewhere that night. Returning the next morning, I found Mr. Candy's groom waiting in alarm. By then, the damage was done; the illness had begun."

"The illness has only been described generally as a fever," I noted.

"I can offer no more precise description," Jennings answered. "From start to finish, the fever took no specific form. I consulted two of Mr. Candy's physician friends immediately. They agreed the case was serious but strongly disagreed with my assessment of the treatment. We differed entirely on interpreting the patient's pulse. The two doctors, citing its rapidity, insisted on a lowering treatment. I acknowledged the speed but emphasized its alarming feebleness, indicating an exhausted system requiring stimulants. They advocated gruel, lemonade, and barley water; I prescribed champagne, brandy, ammonia, and quinine. A grave disagreement between established local physicians and a mere assistant! For the first few days, I yielded to their authority as the patient sank steadily. I made a second plea based on the pulse's unchecked rapidity and increasing weakness. They took offense at my obstinacy: 'Mr. Jennings, either we manage this case, or you manage it. Which is it?' I replied, 'Gentlemen, give me five minutes to consider.' When the time elapsed, I was ready. 'You positively refuse to try the stimulant treatment?' they asked. 'Yes,' I said. 'I intend to try it at once.' 'Try it, Mr. Jennings, and we withdraw from the case.' I fetched a bottle of champagne and administered half a tumbler-full personally. The two physicians took up their hats in silence and departed."

"You assumed a serious responsibility," I remarked. "In your place, I fear I would have shrank from it."

"In your place, Mr. Blake, you would remember that Mr. Candy employed you under circumstances that made you his debtor for life. You would see him sinking hour by hour and risk anything rather than let the one man on earth who befriended you die before your eyes. Do not suppose I lacked awareness of the terrible position I placed myself in! There were moments when I felt the full weight of my isolation and the peril of my responsibility. Had I been a happy man leading a prosperous life, I believe I would have succumbed to the task. But I had no happy past to contrast with my present anxiety, and I held firm. I took a midday break when his condition was best for necessary rest. For the remaining twenty-four hours, as long as his life hung in the balance, I never left his bedside. Towards sunset, the fever-induced delirium commenced, lasting through the night and intensifying between two and five in the morning—the time when death reaps his greatest harvest. That was when Death and I fought for the man lying there. I pursued my chosen treatment without hesitation. When wine failed, I used brandy; when stimulants lost effect, I doubled the doses. After an interval of suspense unlike any I hope to endure again, a day arrived when the pulse rate slightly diminished and, more importantly, became steady and strong. *Then*, I knew I had saved him. Then, I admit, I broke down. I laid the poor fellow's wasted hand back on the bed and burst into tears. An hysterical release, Mr. Blake—nothing more! Physiology states, and correctly so, that some men are born with female constitutions—and I am one of them!"

He offered this bitter, professional apology for his tears quietly and unaffectedly, demonstrating throughout that he was morbidly anxious not to appear as an object of pity.
