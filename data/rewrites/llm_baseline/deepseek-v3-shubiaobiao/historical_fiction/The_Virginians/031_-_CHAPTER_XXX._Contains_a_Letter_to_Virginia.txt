The White Horse Tavern was alive with laughter when Harry Warrington arrived for his usual dinner, finding none other than the jovial Parson Sampson holding court among the gathered gentlemen. The chaplain, though absent from London for months, regaled the company with the freshest gossip—or at least, what passed for it among country squires. Whether his tales of scandal at court, duels in Marylebone, or the latest toast of Covent Garden were entirely accurate mattered little. A lively story, well told and suitably wicked, was always welcome.

In those days, clergymen like Sampson were not uncommon—men who frequented racecourses, gaming tables, and playhouses as readily as their pulpits. His face flushed deeper—whether from claret or modesty—when he spotted Harry, and he stumbled forward with an eager greeting.

“My dear sir! The conqueror of spades, clubs, and hearts! How splendid you look!”

Harry returned the warmth, pleased to see the chaplain’s rosy cheeks and cheerful demeanor. A place was made for him beside a squire in a drab coat, who demanded Sampson finish his scandalous tale about Lord Ogle—though the narrative was briefly interrupted when the parson trod upon the squire’s gouty toe.

Sampson, undeterred, turned his attention to Harry, delivering news from Castlewood: my lord’s regards, my lady’s retreat to Harrogate, young Will’s lucky streak at Newmarket, and even the housemaid Molly’s tears over Gumbo. All of Hampshire, he declared, buzzed with Harry’s triumphs at Tunbridge—in cards, in society, in all manner of conquests.

As the company dwindled, Harry and Sampson remained, the bottle passing freely between them. The chaplain, though already well-lubricated, saw no reason to stop. “Plenty is no reason to deny oneself plenty more,” he declared, toasting again and again until Harry, equally generous in spirits, insisted Sampson abandon his inn and lodge with him instead. Gumbo was dispatched to fetch the chaplain’s trunk, and in a burst of camaraderie, Harry even restored the horse he had won from Sampson months before. The beast nuzzled its old master fondly, and Sampson, overcome, embraced both steed and benefactor with equal fervor.

Arm in arm, they made their way to Madame Bernstein’s apartments, their laughter and the scent of the stables trailing behind them. The old Baroness received her nephew’s boisterous companion with amused tolerance. She had a fondness for Sampson’s ribald humor—much like a rich dish, best enjoyed in moderation. Cards, however, were her true vice.

“The cards never lie,” she often remarked. “A bad hand tells you the truth to your face. And nothing flatters like a strong suit of trumps.”

Sampson, initially hesitant to join her high-stakes games, soon found himself coaxed into play—thanks to Harry’s open purse. The young Virginian had adapted swiftly to the luxuries of English life: fine lace, French watches, diamond-studded snuffboxes, and a retinue of grooms and tradesmen at his beck and call. He took fencing lessons, mastered the minuet under a French dancing master, and even defended Virginian horsemanship as superior to anything he saw at Tunbridge.

Under Sampson’s jovial guidance, Harry’s education extended beyond the ballroom. They read history, sermons, and Shakespeare together, and the chaplain assured him he was no longer the dunce he had been at home.

All this found its way into a carefully composed letter to his mother, filled with dutiful platitudes, mentions of distinguished acquaintances, and assurances of frugality—though his expenditures on horses, servants, and finery suggested otherwise. A separate, less polished note to the housekeeper, Mrs. Mountain, revealed a freer hand and warmer heart.

* * *

Your letter of June 20th reached me safely, and I thank you for your advice and the news of home. My accident on the road here was trivial, thanks to the kindness of strangers.

Tunbridge proves most diverting. I have been introduced to persons of rank—Lord Chesterfield, the Duke and Duchess of Queensberry, and others. General Wolfe, whom I visited at Westerham, spoke fondly of Grandfather’s service under Marlborough. His son, Colonel James Wolfe, is a gallant officer, soon to marry a lady of beauty here.

Your allowance is most timely, though I endeavor to be prudent. My wardrobe, while not the height of fashion, serves well enough, save for a few finer shirts for grand occasions.

Gumbo thrives on English fare and sends his duty. He has bought trinkets for the maids at home.

I have engaged masters in fencing and dancing, and Mr. Sampson, now lodging with me, assists in my studies. We read history, sermons, and Shakespeare. I met Mr. Richardson, who sends his compliments—his works are as beloved here as in Virginia.

My aunt and cousin Lady Maria send their regards. With respects to Mountain, I remain—

*What do you mean, you silly old dear, sending your dividends to me? Do you think I need your seven pounds? I’ve torn the order to bits! But I thank you all the same. A kiss to Fanny from—*

* * *

The disparity in tone between the two letters was hardly surprising. The first, polished and proper, was surely reviewed by a tutor. The second, dashed off in high spirits, bore the unfiltered warmth of youth.

And if Harry omitted certain revelries from his mother’s account? Well, what son does not, when writing home? There are always corners of life best left unlit by parental scrutiny. The sun shines, the blood dances, and youth will have its day—whether or not it makes the final draft of a letter.
