At the base of the staircase, Mr. Gryce stepped forward to excuse himself. He signaled to two or three men he had stationed outside, instructing them to remove the valet before ushering Miss Butterworth back into the study. Once the room fell silent once more, allowing them a private moment to converse, Mr. Gryce began.

"One crucial matter has been resolved by the experiment we just conducted," he stated. "Bartow is cleared of any involvement in this crime."

Miss Butterworth nodded, though her expression remained somber. "Then we may devote our full attention to the young people. Have you received anything from them?"

"No," Gryce replied. "Nor from the elderly man who arrived shortly afterward."

A shadow of disappointment crossed Miss Butterworth's face. "I had hoped—indeed, it seemed highly probable—that the fragment of writing you discovered would identify these individuals. If it was vital enough for a dying man to attempt swallowing, surely it must hold a clue to his attacker."

"Regrettably, it offers no such lead, madam," Gryce explained gently. "It was merely a hurried scrawl, reading something like this: *'I return your daughter to you. She is here. Neither she nor you will ever see me again. Remember Evelyn!'* Signed simply, *'Amos's son.'*"

"'Amos's son!' That is Mr. Adams himself," she observed.

"Precisely. We have ample reason to believe it is him."

"Extraordinary! Inexplicable! And yet, this paper was found clamped tightly between his teeth! Was the handwriting verified?"

"Yes," Gryce confirmed. "It matches his known signatures found on the fly-leaves of his books."

"Well, the mysteries only deepen," Miss Butterworth murmured. "The fact that he retained this note until his final throes suggests it was the only hiding place he deemed secure from search. And the girl! Upon regaining consciousness, her first words were: *'You have left that line of writing behind.'* Mr. Gryce, those words—few and enigmatic as they are—hold the key to the entire situation. Would you kindly repeat them to me, sentence by sentence?"

"With great pleasure, madam; I have recited them to myself countless times. First: *'I return your daughter to you!'"*

"So," Miss Butterworth analyzed, pacing slightly. "Mr. Adams had someone else's daughter in his care whom he is returning. Whose daughter? Certainly not that young man's, for that would imply she is a small child. Furthermore, if these words were intended for his assailant, why conceal them so meticulously?"

"I have pondered exactly that," Gryce admitted.

"Yet, if not for him, then for whom? Perhaps for the elderly gentleman who entered moments later?"

"It is a distinct possibility," Gryce conceded. "Since learning of his arrival, I have considered this scenario, particularly given the subsequent phrase: *'She is here.'* The only woman present immediately prior to the old man's entrance was the light-haired girl you witnessed being carried out."

"That is logical," Miss Butterworth agreed. "But why treat this document as if it were just written? It could easily be an old scrap referencing past sorrows or secrets."

"These words were composed that very afternoon," Gryce corrected firmly. "The paper was torn from a sheet resting on the desk, and the pen used to write them lay quite out of place on the extreme edge of the table."

"Certainly, sir; but I had not grasped the significance we might attribute to such details. Thus, these words concern the girl I saw. And she is not Evelyn, for otherwise, he would not have invoked the bird's catchphrase, *'Remember Evelyn!'* Do you wonder if she is Evelyn?" she asked, gesturing toward the single large portrait adorning the wall.

"We may provisionally assume she is," Gryce suggested. "Such a melancholy visage naturally hints at some painful family secret. However, how do we account for the violent role played by the young man, who remains unmentioned in these abrupt, hastily penned lines? It is a riddle, madam, and one we seem to be wasting precious time attempting to solve."

"Yet, I detest surrendering without making an earnest effort," Miss Butterworth insisted. "There were other words you omitted from your repetition."

"They preceded the command, *'Remember Evelyn!'* They spoke of a resolve: *'Neither she nor you will ever see me again.'"*

"Ah!" Miss Butterworth exclaimed. "Those few words are profoundly significant, Mr. Gryce. Could he have struck that blow himself? Might this have been a suicide after all?"

"Madam, you are entitled to ask; however, based on Bartow's pantomime, you must have deduced that the wound mimicked a maddened thrust from an enraged hand, not a self-inflicted strike. Let us adhere to our initial conclusion. Nevertheless—to ensure fairness to every possibility—the chaotic state of Mr. Adams's affairs and the conspicuous absence of family papers or standard documents typically found in a wealthy man's desk indicate he had prepared for a potential demise. It may have occurred sooner than anticipated, or in a different manner, but it was a thought he had entertained. Moreover, I must confess a lapse in my own judgment regarding my most valued colleague. The study—that most remarkable chamber—harbors a secret never shared with you. A peculiar revelation, madam, disclosed to me in a startling fashion. This room can be, or rather, could be, completely isolated from the rest of the house; transformed into a death trap, or indeed, a tomb wherein this incomprehensible man may have intended to pass away. Observe this steel plate. It operates via a mechanism designed to slide across this open doorway. Last night, I stood behind that plate, and these holes were forced through specifically to allow my escape."

"Hah! Detectives have their own trials!" Miss Butterworth laughed nervously, rubbing her eyes. "I should not have relished spending that particular evening with you. But what an archaic tragedy we are uncovering here! I declare—I feel as if transported back to medieval times. Who claims we live in New York, within earshot of cable cars and the chirping of telegraph wires?"

"Some men possess the unique ability to bring the medieval era to Wall Street," Gryce replied with a wry smile. "I believe Mr. Adams was one of those men. Romanticism permeated every aspect of his life, even the manner of his death. Nor did it cease with his passing; it followed him to the grave. Consider the cross we discovered lying upon his chest."

"That was the work of another's hand, born of another's superstition. It indicates the presence of a priest or a woman at the moment of his death."

"And yet," Gryce continued, his tone shifting to one of mild wonder, "he must have possessed a measure of hard sense within his constitution. Every contrivance functioned correctly. He was a mechanical genius as much as a lover of mystery."

"A strange amalgamation. It is curious that we do not feel his spirit infecting the very air of this study. One could almost wish it did; perhaps then we might grasp the solution to this puzzle."

"That," Gryce noted, "can be achieved in only one way. You have already indicated the path. We must track down the young couple who were present during his final struggle. If they cannot be found, the case is hopeless."

"And so," Miss Butterworth sighed, "we circle back to the starting point—a definitive proof that we are hopelessly lost in the woods." She rose abruptly. She felt that, for the moment, her own resources had reached their limit. Mr. Gryce made no move to detain her; indeed, he appeared eager to depart the premises himself. Nevertheless, they paused long enough to cast one final glance around the room. As they did so, Miss Butterworth's finger slowly rose.

"Look!" she whispered. "From this angle of the wall, you can barely discern the opening created by removing that picture on the stair landing. Would you not say it lies amidst those folds of dark tapestry up there?"

"Yes," Gryce replied, nodding. "I had already pinpointed that exact location. With the picture hung on the opposite side, it would remain entirely invisible."

"One must keep one's eyes moving in a case like this," she declared triumphantly. "That picture must have been shifted aside several times while we occupied this room, yet we failed to notice. That was because we were not looking high enough. High and low, Mr. Gryce! What occurs at the level of the eye is obvious to everyone."

The smile with which he acknowledged this parting observation and prepared to escort her to the door held less irony than a touch of sadness. Was he beginning to realize that years take their toll even on the most astute minds, and that neither high vantage points nor low perspectives would have escaped his attention a dozen years prior?
