The burgomaster's gaze, a venomous coil of hatred locked upon Gerard, sealed the young man's conviction: Ghysbrecht was not merely an adversary, but a sworn executioner waiting in the shadows. Imprisonment had curdled hope into a singular, driving certainty. As Gerard glided silently around the tower's sharp angle, he expected no miracle, only the cold calculus of malice—a treacherous mechanism devised by a wicked man to ensure his demise within those stone walls, an escape so improbable it would remain unknown even if achieved. Yet, as he moved through the darkness, a hand, soft yet resolute, slipped into his. Margaret stood beside him, sharing the weight of this newly forged peril.

Before the haunted tower could fully reveal itself to their eyes, a vision struck them, numbing their very souls. Halfway up the ascent, a creature mounted the wall with steady, unnatural grace. Its head burned with a fiery luminescence, resembling an enormous glowworm, casting a stark silhouette against the night. The body was shrouded in obscurity, defined only by the glare of that infernal crown, measuring nearly four feet in length. Below, at the tower's base, stood a figure clad in white, indistinguishable from a living woman. Gerard and Margaret stood paralyzed, their hearts hammering against their ribs.

"The rope! The rope!" Gerard gasped, his voice cracking. "It ascends the rope."

As they watched, the glowing entity vanished into the mouth of Gerard's former prison, its light flooding the interior and turning the barred window a sickly crimson. The white figure remained motionless beneath. Those who survive the initial shock of the supernatural often succumb to a peculiar, primal terror—a wild, magnetic urge to throw oneself upon the terrible object, drawn by a fascination akin to a bird fixated on a serpent. Great tragedians knew this well; Macready, portraying Banquo's second appearance, had captured it perfectly, hurling himself backward with averted head at the horror. Now, this strange compulsion seized Margaret. She released Gerard's hand, standing bewildered until, in a single, frantic motion, she cried out and darted toward the specter.

Gerard, unaware of this natural instinct, saw only the devil dragging his friend to perdition. He fell to his knees, screaming the ancient rite: *"Exorcizo vos. In nomine beatae Mariae, exorcizo vos."* While the exorcist shrieked his incantations in the extremity of his terror, a profound relief washed over him when the specter emitted a feeble cry of fear. To discover that hell possessed its own vulnerabilities was an encouragement. Redoubling his efforts, Gerard soon saw the ghastly shape kneeling at Margaret's feet, hearing it pray pitifully for mercy.

Kate and Giles arrived shortly thereafter, finding a new rope dangling from the prisoner's window to the earth. "I see how it is," muttered the lesser intelligence, accepting reality as it unfolded. "Our Gerard descended this rope. He is free. I ascend, and I see."

"No, Giles, no!" commanded the superior intelligence, blinded by prejudice. "Do you not perceive this is glamour? This rope is a line cast by the Evil One to lure thee to destruction. He knows the weaknesses of every heart; he sees how fond thou art of climbing. Where would our Gerard procure such a rope? How fasten it in the sky thus? It defies nature. Holy saints protect us tonight, for hell is abroad."

"Stuff!" retorted the dwarf. "The road to hell is down, and this rope leads up. I have never had the fortune to climb such a long rope. It may be years before I encounter one prepared for me. Better to be knocked on the head at once than to wait forever for happiness." With a cry of delight, he sprang onto the rope, moving with the agility of a cat leaping for a table laden with fish. Every muscle ignited with energy; the only concession Kate secured was permission to fasten a lantern to his neck. "Light frightens ill spirits," she reasoned. Thus, with arms like iron and legs like feathers, Giles climbed upward faster than his brother descended. The lantern at the nape of his neck transformed him into a living glowworm. His sister watched his progress with trembling anxiety.

Suddenly, a female figure burst from the solid masonry, flying at her with superhuman velocity. Kate uttered a weak cry—the limit of her vocal cords under the weight of terror. Dropping her crutches, she sank to her knees, burying her face and moaning, "Take my body, but spare my soul!"

"Why, it is a woman!" Kate quivered.
"Why, it is a woman!" Margaret echoed.
"How you scared me!"
"I am scared enough myself. Oh! Oh! Oh!"

"This is strange," Margaret breathed, recovering her composure. "But the fiery-headed thing? It was with you, and you are harmless! Why are you here at this hour?"
"Nay, why are *you*?"
"Perhaps we share the same errand? Ah! You are his good sister, Kate!"
"And you are Margaret Brandt."
"Yes."
"All the better. You love him; you are here. Then Giles was right. He has won his freedom."

Gerard stepped forward, putting the mystery to rest. But their conversation was abruptly severed by a horrible, unearthly noise, sounding like a sepulcher giving vent to its contents: *"PARCHMENT! PARCHMENT! PARCHMENT!"* Each repetition rose in intensity. Looking up, they beheld the dwarf, clutching bundles of parchments, his face alight with fiendish joy and flushed with diabolical fire. The lantern burning at his neck rendered him a more infernal transparency than any mortal eye had ever witnessed. With the shout, the awful imp hurled the documents at the astonished figures below. Records rained down like wounded wild ducks; some collapsed immediately, others fluttered helplessly, while others spread wide, wheeling slowly in airy circles before settling. Hardly had they landed when the sepulchral roar returned—"Parchment! Parchment!"—and another flock pattered and sailed down, followed by another, blanketing the grass in white.

Finally, the fire-headed imp, his body light and hands horned, slid down the rope like a falling star. Prioritizing business over sentiment, he proposed an immediate settlement for the merchandise he had just delivered.

"Hush!" Gerard warned. "You speak too loudly. Gather them up and follow us to a safer place than this."

"Will you come home with me, Gerard?" asked little Kate.
"I have no home."
"You shall not say so. Who is more welcome than you, after such cruel wrong, to your father's house?"
"Father! I have no father," Gerard replied sternly. "He who was my father has become my gaoler. I have escaped his hands; I will never come within their reach again."
"An enemy did this, not our father." Kate recounted what she had overheard between Cornelis and Sybrandt. Yet the injury was too fresh to be soothed. Gerard displayed a bitterness of indignation he had previously seemed incapable of.

"Cornelis and Sybrandt are two ill curs that have shown me their teeth and their heart for a long while, but they could do no more. My father gave the burgomaster authority, or he dared not lay a finger on me, a free burgher of this town. So be it. I was his son; I am his prisoner. He has played his part; I shall play mine. Farewell to the burgh where I was born, lived honestly, and was imprisoned. While there remains another town in creation, I will never trouble you again, Tergou."

"Oh! Gerard! Gerard!" Margaret whispered urgently. "Do not give way to anger now. Let his choler cool!"

Kate turned quickly toward her. "May I look at your face?" The inspection seemed favorable, prompting her to whisper, "It is a comely face, and no mischief-maker's."
"Fear me not," Margaret said in the same gentle tone. "I could not be happy without your love, as well as Gerard's."
"These are comforting words," Kate sobbed. Then, lifting her eyes, she added, "Little did I think to like you so well. My heart is willing, but my infirmity forbids me to embrace you."

At this hint, Margaret gently wound her arms around Gerard's sister and kissed her lovingly. "Often he has spoken of you to me, Kate; and often I longed for this."
"You, too, Gerard," Kate said, "kiss me ere you go; for my heart lies heavy at parting with you this night."

Gerard kissed her, and she departed on her crutches toward home. The last sound they heard of her was a little, patient sigh. Then tears welled thick in Margaret's eyes. But Gerard was a man, and he noticed nothing of his sister's sorrow. Turning toward Sevenbergen, the dwarf nudged Gerard with his bundle of parchments and extended a concave claw. Margaret dissuaded him. "Why take what is not ours?"
"Oh, spoil an enemy however you can."
"But may they not make this a pretext for fresh violence?"
"How can they? Think you I shall remain in Tergou after this? The burgomaster robbed me of my liberty; I doubt I should take his life for it, if I could."
