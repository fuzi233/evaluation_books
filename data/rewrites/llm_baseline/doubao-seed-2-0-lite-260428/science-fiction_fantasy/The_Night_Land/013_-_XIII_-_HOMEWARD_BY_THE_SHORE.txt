We emerged at last from the sad, shadow-choked mountain pass I had named the Upward Gorge, and paused to catch our breath at the foot of the peaks, far past the gorge’s dark, looming mouth.

Naani spun in giddy half-circles, her eyes darting every direction at once, breath coming in fast little gasps. Her irises glowed bright, lit up by equal parts wonder at all the new sights, and the sharp, dizzy relief of escaping the suffocating dread that had hung over us for days on end. She turned suddenly to stare back up into the gorge’s black depths, spotted its great shadowed opening, shivered, and broke into a run further down the sloping lit expanse of the Sea Country. She skidded to a halt again a short ways later, glancing back with a face that held equal awe and the quiet, loosening weight of released fear, before turning once more to gape at the wide rolling country and the vast stretch of the sea spread out before us. She was caught halfway between laughing and crying, overwhelmed by amazement, joy, and utter astonishment; she had never once in all her life stood in a wide, open stretch of soft, unconfined light, as any reader who knows her origin will already have guessed.

We both realized in that moment we no longer needed to whisper the way we had through the whole dark, narrow length of the gorge. Naani laughed out loud, and shouted as a child does to test an echo, her high clear voice drifting off pretty into the distance before it vanished across the empty country.

But an answer came back a moment later, rolling out of the dark mountains at our backs. We both spun around so fast our necks ached, unsure if the sound was just a natural echo, or some uncanny call luring us back down out of the gorge’s hidden horrors. We did not wait to find out, running further down the slope until we were gasping for air, halting only when we felt certain we had put all trace of the gorge’s strangeness far enough behind us.

We hunted for a flat, open rock to rest on, and soon found a perfect spot, elevated high enough above the low ground to give us a clear view of any approaching threat. We climbed atop it, sat close together to share our rations, glancing around constantly to make sure no Humpt Men or other dangerous creatures snuck up on us unawares.

Naani never stopped asking questions, never stopped staring out across the country at the sea. The vastness of it stirred her so deeply that faint, half-forgotten memories rose in her, vague and disjointed as old half-waking dreams, stinging her with a strange mix of pleasure and quiet grief. She broke suddenly into tears, burying her face in my chest until she had steadied herself, before bouncing back to her usual bright, easy joy.

Again and again she marveled at the clarity of the wide open air, a wonder I shared too, for I had spent my whole life trapped in the enclosed dark of the Night Land just as she had. Sometimes she would slip into speech that felt as old and distant as moonlight that fell ten thousand years prior, words tangled up in memories of a lost sunlit world no living soul in our dark age had any right to remember. Other times she would lean close against me, and we would sit in a soft, solemn silence, our spirits stirred by the great call of the sea that hummed somewhere in the back of both our minds, waking old dream-memories we had carried all our lives without ever knowing their source. I have no words to properly describe the soft, trembling chaos of feeling that wrapped around us in that quiet moment.

Miles away, past the foot of the mountains along the shore to our left, a great cloud of steam billowed up, the same mist I had tramped through on my outward journey. Naani asked about it, and I told her all I knew, explaining we would have to traverse it soon as part of our path home.

She stared awestruck at the glowing volcanoes burning out in the sea and scattered across the wide country, the grand scale of the landscape exalting her even as it made her feel small and soft, humbled by how much of the world existed outside the tiny cell she had been trapped in her whole life. I pulled her into my arms to kiss her, for she was so sweet and alive in that moment I could not help myself. She kissed me back immediately, peppering my face with pecks between questions, half eager to hear more of the world I had seen, half playfully teasing just to make me smile.

Presently she kissed me three slow, warm, deliberate times on the mouth, then set her small hands on my shoulders, their size absurdly precious against the broad metal of my armour, and shook me gently to make me answer her questions faster, full of bright, mischievous naughtiness that demanded I kiss her harder. I answered her between every kiss, and she laughed and slipped her hand between our lips to block me, so I kissed her small, soft palm instead. Before I could react she spread her fingers quick to kiss me through the gaps, then clamped her hand shut again to stop me from returning the gesture.

After that I made her stand up on the rock, brushed her hair loose over her shoulders, and pulled her boots off to show off her small, bare, pretty feet. She feigned protest at first, then stood soft and obedient, blushing so pink it made her even more lovely. I stepped back a pace to look at her, and she gave me a playful, naughty little glance before turning very grave, pretending to be a stiff, unyielding doll. When she turned back to face me, perfectly sedate, she suddenly stretched one foot out, pressing her pink toes to my lips before snatching them back fast, then launched herself gleefully into my arms begging to be hugged. I laughed, soft and tender, for I loved her more than words, and told her what any man in love would tell his beloved: that I wished I had a pocket big enough to tuck her inside, so she could stay pressed next to my heart forever. She laughed mischievously and told me she would tickle me and pinch me the whole time I carried her, and I shook her gently in mock exasperation, only for her to kiss me hard on the mouth halfway through, and I did not have it in me to pretend to be annoyed any longer.

She asked to be set down, then made me turn my back so she could reach the pouch strapped to my spine. She pulled out the small comb I kept there, and began to brush her hair as I sat and talked to her, my heart lighter than it had been for years. Though I still held quiet dread of the Humpt Men and the monstrous creatures that roamed the Sea Country, there was no lingering, crawling horror here like the evil that lurked in the dark of the Night Land. All the wonders here felt natural, untainted by the lurking malice of that old cursed place.

When she had finished brushing her hair, she reached to tie it back, but I asked her to leave it loose over her shoulders, for I thought it was impossibly pretty. She smiled and agreed, happy to indulge me.

We packed up our gear after that, I laced her boots back on for her, and we set off once more heading down the slope toward the shore. We kept a steady, easy pace that would not wear us out, for my plan was to make camp and sleep on this side of the great steam zone near the shore, just as I had done on my journey out. We tramped along the foot of the mountains for six full hours, and when the day’s travel clocked past the eighteenth hour, we were both ready to sleep.

We soon spotted a tall, steep rock that would be almost impossible for any climbing predator to scale, with a flat top twice my height across in every direction, perfect for our camp. We climbed safely to the top, ate our rations, and laid down the cloak to sleep on. The air was warm and soft, we did not need any covering at all.

We woke seven hours later, both sitting bolt upright at the same time to stare at each other, as if we were seeing one another for the first time all over again, flooded with fresh joy just from looking at each other. Naani threw herself into my arms, and we kissed until we were both breathless, hungry for each other even after we had woken pressed close. She laid out our morning rations, the fizzing mineral water bubbling over the edges of the cup to make us laugh, and we ate slowly, chattering about every new sight she spotted. I kept my gaze on the ground nearby, watching for any sign of approaching danger, while she stared up at the mountain range the gorge cut through. Seen in the soft light of day, the peaks loomed monstrous, a vast unbroken wall stretching upward forever until it vanished out of the lit zone, lost to the eternal dark of the cold upper sky. It made me realize how much calmer I was now, traveling with no desperate hurry, than I had been on my frantic outward journey.

We hurried ourselves a little once we noticed we were lingering too long, climbed down from our sleeping rock, and set off again at a good pace. Before long we heard the far-off hissing of steam, the roar of boiling water erupting up from the earth below, a sound Naani had never heard before. I reassured her, and she pressed close to my side as we stepped into the thick billowing mist. I carried her on my back the whole three hours we traversed the steam zone, leading the way so she would never accidentally step into a hidden boiling pool. I guided myself mostly by the feel of the sea shore to our left, since we could see nothing more than an arm’s length in front of us. The water boiled in patches all around us, the ground shook under our feet, and the hissing shrieks and whistles of the geysers sometimes sounded like the cries of great hidden monsters, other times fading to a dead silence broken only by a thin, lonesome piping from some distant steam vent.

By the fourth hour we stumbled out of the thick mist, the roars and hisses fading far behind us until the steam thinned to nothing more than a faint wisp of fog. Clear air opened around us, and Naani spotted the great forests stretching out to our right, while the sea shore stayed to our left. She was utterly awestruck by the trees, running to pluck branches, crumpling the leaves between her fingers and breathing in their scent, overwhelmed by those same faint, dreamlike old memories again; she had never seen a living tree in her whole life trapped in the pyramid.

When the sixth hour of travel after the steam zone came, we stopped at a sheltered spot to rest and eat our rations. Naani asked me to keep watch while she bathed in a shallow warm pool nearby, and I stood guard a short distance away, never far enough that I could not reach her in an instant if something came. When she was done she kept watch for me, and helped me clean up as best she could, soft and nurturing, half motherly in her care, even as her mischievous maidenly peeked through every few moments.

We set off again after that, chatting idly as I kept a sharp eye on our surroundings. After seven hours of walking we came within sight of the bright burning fire-hill volcano that jutted out of the sea, the very same one that cast warm golden light on me while I slept in the great tree during my outward journey. I had planned for us to stop to eat near that very tree, and when I brought Naani there and told her the story of how I had hidden there from the great horned beast, she begged me to climb up to the thick branch I had slept on, to eat our rations there.

I agreed, going below her the whole climb to catch her if she slipped, and we settled on the great wide branch. She made me show her exactly where I had laid, and traced the faint indent my heavy armour had left in the bark. She sat quiet for a long time, staring out at the glowing light of the sea volcano, and I did not disturb her. When she stood to climb back down, she kissed the spot where I had laid, then pulled her small knife out and cut a small chip of bark free, tucking it inside her tunic to keep as a souvenir. When we got back to the ground, she spotted the old tracks of the great horned beast I had outrun that day, the faint mark of its belly brushing the soil as it ran. It struck me then that only seventeen days had passed since I first stopped at that tree, though it felt like a lifetime of terror and joy compressed into that small span of time.

We set off once more, and I lifted Naani onto my back once she had walked twelve full hours, as was our usual custom. She insisted she could walk for six more hours to spare my strength, but I knew her fragile body, weakened after a month trapped alone and running for her life, would wear out far too fast if I let her. She pressed soft against my back as we traveled, marveling at how unshakable my strength and resolve had to be to make the whole journey out to rescue her. I smiled, happy she took such pride in my strength, just as any man in love would be glad to be strong enough to carry his beloved to safety.

I shifted her to make her more comfortable, and suddenly realized my hard metal armour must be digging into her bare shoulders the whole time. I asked her, and when she tried to lie I saw the dark purple bruises blooming where the metal had pressed into her soft skin. I was furious at myself for not noticing, and half-furious at her for hiding the pain from me. I set her down immediately, and she saw how cross I was, teasing me with little playful pouts and kisses to distract me. I pretended I was going to scold her properly, and she finally stopped teasing, tucking her two small fists into the palm of one of my huge hands, too stubborn to apologize properly but soft enough that I could not keep pretending to be stern. I wrapped my other arm around her, pulling her tight against my chest, and all my anger melted away, my whole being swearing to be her shield for the rest of our lives.
