_Aspern._ Pardon, my lord—I speak for Sigismund.
_Fronsberg._ For him? Oh, ay—for him I always hold a pardon safe in bank, sure he will draw sooner or later on me. What his need? Mad project broken? Fine mechanic wings that would not fly? Durance, assault on watch, bill for Epernay, not a crust to eat?
_Aspern._ Oh, none of these, my lord; he has escaped from Circe’s herd, and seeks to win the love of your fair ward Cecilia: but would win first your consent. You frown.
_Fronsberg._ Distinguish words. I said I held a pardon, not consent.

Though Daniel Deronda longed to return to London—his concern for Mirah mingling with a growing curiosity about the mysterious Mordecai—he did not arrive before Sir Hugo, who had gone ahead of his family to prepare for the opening of Parliament on February sixth. Deronda settled into his rooms in Park Lane, knowing full well they were already occupied by Hans Meyrick. That was expected. But what followed was not.

We’ve all seen Retzsch’s drawing of destiny as Mephistopheles playing chess with man for his soul—a game where the devil feigns carelessness, luring the mortal into misplaced defenses while the true danger looms unseen. The fiend mocks preparation, convincing us to leave our waterproofs behind when the sky is clear, knowing full well it will rain. He counts on our folly to turn illusion into prejudice, rather than study the signs. It is a rare test of character when, after carefully preparing for one kind of trial, we discover our foresight was off-target—our best intentions no more than finely cut dovetails built upon a false foundation. We brace ourselves for one kind of failure, only to face an entirely different demand on our spirit. Something like this befell Deronda.

At first, he found nothing but delight in the transformation of his sitting room into a lively studio—scattered with sketches, two chests from Rome unpacked, windows half-shaded with baize, and Hans Meyrick presiding over the chaos like some whimsical muse. His hair longer, his face more deeply creased with expression, his voice still rising with excitement, Hans was as animated as ever. Their friendship, forged during their Cambridge days, had endured through letters, shared travels, and quiet companionship. The original balance—Deronda’s trust, Hans’s indulgence—had deepened into something richer, a mutual exchange of spirit, nurtured over time.

“I knew you’d want to see my casts and antiquities,” Hans said, after the usual greetings and inquiries. “So I didn’t hesitate to bring them here. But I’ve found two rooms in Chelsea—just a few hundred yards from my mother and sisters—and I’ll be moving soon. Just waiting for the walls to be scraped and new lights installed. But I’m not waiting to begin work. You can’t imagine how great I’m going to be. The seed of immortality has sprouted within me.”

“Only a fungoid growth, I dare say—a disease blooming in the lungs,” Deronda replied, used to teasing Hans like a brother. He walked toward the bookshelf, where five rapid sketches leaned against the edge—different angles of the same face. He stood back, silent. Hans, too, paused, picked up his palette, and began touching up the painting on his easel.

“What do you think?” Hans asked at last.

“The full face looks too massive,” Deronda said, colder than usual. “Otherwise, the likenesses are good.”

“No, it’s not too massive,” Hans insisted. “I’ve noted that. There’s always a little surprise when you go from profile to full view. But I’ll enlarge her scale for Berenice. I’m doing a whole series—look along there. And now I realize—you’re just the model I need for Agrippa.” Hans, pencil and palette still in hand, moved beside Deronda, then added quickly, as if catching himself, “No, no—I forgot. You don’t like posing, confound you! But I’ve found a wonderful Titus. Five figures in total. First: Berenice clinging to Gessius Florus’s knees, begging him to spare her people. That’s on the easel. Then, here: she stands on the Xystus with Agrippa, pleading with the crowd not to harm themselves in resistance.”

“Agrippa’s legs won’t work,” Deronda said.

“They’re fine realistically,” Hans replied, grinning. “Public men often wobble. ‘Their legs, the emblem of their various thought,’ as someone says in *The Rehearsal*.”

“But these are as absurd as Raphael’s Alcibiades,” Deronda objected.

“Then they’re ideal,” Hans countered. “Agrippa’s legs may have been bad. I idealize it—make them impossibly bad. Art must intensify. Don’t worry about the legs now. The third sketch: Berenice exulting at the news that Vespasian is Emperor and Titus his successor—her future as Empress of Rome.”

“You should put a scroll in her mouth. Otherwise, no one will understand.”

“That’ll make them feel their ignorance—an excellent aesthetic effect. The fourth: Titus sending Berenice away from Rome after ten years together—both reluctant, both sorrowful—*invitus invitam*, as Suetonius says. I’ve found a model for the Roman brute.”

“Will you make Berenice look fifty? She must have been.”

“No—just a few mature touches. Dark-eyed beauty ages well. Hers especially. Now, the fifth: Berenice seated alone on the ruins of Jerusalem. Pure imagination. What should have been—perhaps was. Here’s where I show a tragic absence. No one knows what became of her—that’s perfectly conveyed by ending the series without a sixth picture.” Hans struck a pose, eyes wide, breath caught, as if awaiting awe. “I break off in the Homeric style. The story is chipped off, jagged, fading into nothing—*le néant*. Can anything be more sublime? Especially in French?”

The vulgar would want to see her corpse, her burial, her will read aloud. But no—this silence is deeper. Come, see the painting on the easel. I’ve made progress.”

“That beseeching gesture is truly strong,” Deronda admitted after a moment. “You’ve been busy over Christmas. I suppose you started this since coming to London.” Neither had mentioned Mirah yet.

“No,” Hans said, adding brushstrokes. “I decided on the theme before. I take this lucky chance as an omen—I’m about to burst onto the world as a great painter. I saw a magnificent woman in Trastevere—the finest women there are half-Jewish—and she inspired me to find a grand scene for a Jewish woman in Rome. Like many learned men, I ended up taking what lay on the surface. I’ll show you a sketch of her head when I can find it.”

“She’d be a better model for Berenice,” Deronda said, unsure how to express his discomfort.

“Not at all. The model should be the most beautiful Jewess in the world—and I’ve found her.”

“Are you certain she wants to be painted in this role? I doubt any woman would find it appealing. Does she even know what you’re doing?”

“Of course. I got her to assume this very pose. Little mother sat for Gessius Florus, and Mirah clasped her knees.” Hans stepped back, studying the effect.

“I suspect she doesn’t know Berenice’s history,” Deronda said, feeling a surge of irritation he couldn’t fully justify.

“Oh, yes, she does—ladies’ edition. Berenice was a passionate patriot, but seduced by love and ambition into siding with her people’s arch-enemy. Hence, Nemesis. Mirah sees it as a tragic parable. She weeps thinking of the penitent Berenice returning to Jerusalem, sitting desolate among ruins. That was her exact phrase. I couldn’t bear to tell her I invented that part.”

“Show me your Trasteverina,” Deronda said, mostly to avoid saying something sharper.

“Mind turning over that folio?” Hans asked. “All my head studies are there. Though they’re in disarray. You might find her next to a crop-eared undergraduate.”

After flipping through a few pages, Deronda said, “These seem to be Cambridge faces and rural scenes. Perhaps I should start from the beginning.”

“No—she’s around the middle. I merged one folio into another.”

“Is this one of your undergraduates?” Deronda asked, holding up a sketch. “It’s a remarkably pleasant face.”

“That? Oh, that’s Rex Gascoigne. A fine fellow—his upper lip is particularly good. I coached him before his scholarship. He should have taken honors last Easter. But he fell ill and had to stay another year. I ought to look him up. Want to know how he’s doing.”

“There she is,” Deronda said, lifting a sketch of the Trasteverina.

“Ah,” Hans replied, glancing at it with mild disdain. “Too coarse. I was unregenerate then.”

Deronda closed the book, leaving the sketch exposed. He turned, gripping his coat collar, and said, “I know my scruples may seem excessive, Meyrick—but I must ask you to abandon this idea.”

Hans threw himself into a dramatic posture and cried, “What! My series—my immortal Berenice series? Think what you’re saying, man—destroying not a life but an immortality, as Milton said. Wait before you answer—let me lay down my tools and prepare to tear out my hair!”

He dropped his brush and palette, collapsed backward into a chair, draped himself limply over the arm, let his long hair fall across his face, raised his hooked fingers beside his head, and looked up with mock horror. Deronda had to smile as he said, “Paint as many Berenices as you like—but I wish you could feel what I do. Perhaps you will, on reflection—choose another model.”

“Why?” Hans asked, standing again, serious now.

“Because she might be recognized. Mrs. Meyrick and I want her known as a gifted singer. It’s right—and she wishes it—that she become independent. She has real chances. One introduction is secured. I’m speaking to Klesmer. Her face may become well-known—and it’s useless to explain unless you feel as I do. I believe that if Mirah understood the situation clearly, she’d strongly object to being portrayed this way—to being used as a model for such a heroine.”

As Hans listened, thumbs in his blouse belt, his face shifted from surprise to amusement—then nearly burst into laughter. But seeing Deronda’s offended look, he stopped himself. “Excuse my laughing, Deronda. You’ve never given me the upper hand before. If it weren’t about my own pictures, I’d swallow every word you said. And so you actually believe I’d get five paintings hung in public, studied closely by everyone? Zounds, man! Cider-cup and conceit never gave me such a dream. My pictures will likely remain as private as hypersensitivity desires.”

He returned to painting, filling awkward silences. Deronda stood still, realizing his concern about publicity was misplaced—but his unease remained. He wasn’t satisfied with himself or Hans, yet silence carried him through. Hans respected his friend so deeply that he felt shy when Deronda seemed wrong—but he didn’t give up easily, especially not on a whim. He continued, brushing paint:

“But suppose I *did* have a public rushing after my art like a railway series—nurses, babies, bonnet-boxes—I still don’t see justice in your objection. Every painter worth remembering has painted the face he admired most. It’s part of his soul, poured into his work. He channels his hatred into caricature, his love into sacred form. If a man could paint the woman he loves a thousand times as *Stella Maris*, inspiring courage in sailors on a thousand ships—how much greater honor to her! Isn’t that nobler than painting a vulgar gaze and calling it divine?”

“Every objection can be answered if you take broad enough ground, Hans,” Deronda said, firm now. “No moral question can be settled that way. I might accept your generalities—and still be right in saying you shouldn’t display Mirah’s face as Berenice. But I admit I was unreasonable about publicity.”

He hesitated. “Still—even privately—there may be good reasons to refrain. You must acknowledge her situation is delicate. Until she’s more independent, she should be treated as fragile as Venetian glass—protected from shocks that might shatter her fragile peace. Are you certain of your own discretion? Excuse me, Hans. Finding her has bound me to guard her. Do you understand?”

“Perfectly,” Hans said, smiling warmly. “You think I’m the kind who breaks everything I touch—and myself along with it. Fair enough. Since birth, everything I’ve loved has turned into trouble—for me or others. Everything I’ve embraced has somehow become a mess. Painting is my latest mess—and I’ll spend my life trying to escape it. You think I’ll cause trouble at home? No—I’m regenerated. You think I’m madly in love with Mirah? Quite right—I am. But you think I’ll scream and ruin everything. There you’re mistaken—excusably, but profoundly so. I’ve undergone baptism by immersion. Awe protects me. Ask the little mother.”

“You don’t count hopeless love among your troubles, then,” Deronda said, his voice deepening as Hans’s rose.

“I don’t mean to call mine hopeless,” Hans replied, coolly setting down his tools, hands in his belt, stepping back as if to assess his work.

“My dear fellow, you’re only setting yourself up for pain,” Deronda said, decisive. “She wouldn’t marry a Christian—even if she loved him. Have you heard her talk about her people, her faith?”

“That can’t last,” Hans said. “She’ll meet no tolerable Jew. Every man of that race is unbearable—‘insupportably advancing’—his nose.”

“She may return to her family. That’s what she longs for. Her mother and brother are probably strict Jews.”

“I’ll convert, if she wants,” Hans said, shrugging and laughing.

“Don’t joke, Hans. I thought you meant this seriously.”

“So I do. You think it’s desperate—but I don’t.”

“I know nothing. I can’t predict what will happen. But I can hardly imagine a greater surprise than that Mirah might have feelings for you worth building hopes on.” Deronda sensed his tone was too harsh.

“I don’t build romantic hopes on a woman’s feelings,” Hans said, amused by seriousness. “I turn to science and philosophy for romance. Nature designed Mirah to fall in love with me. The blending of races demands it—the softening of human ugliness demands it—the harmony of opposites assures it. I am the ultimate contrast—bleached Christian, incapable of singing two notes in tune. Who could stand against me?”

“I see now—it was all *persiflage*. You don’t mean a word, Meyrick,” Deronda said, placing a hand on Hans’s shoulder, relieved. “I was foolish to take you seriously.”

“Upon my honor, I do mean it,” Hans replied, turning sharply, laying his hand on Deronda’s shoulder, their eyes locked. “I’m confessing. I meant to tell you the moment you arrived. My mother says you’re Mirah’s guardian—and she feels responsible to you for every breath she takes under her roof. Well, I love her—I worship her—I won’t despair—I mean to deserve her.”

“My dear fellow, you can’t,” Deronda said quickly.

“I mean to try.”

“You won’t keep your resolve, Hans. You once promised your mother and sisters things—”

“You have a right to reproach me, old friend,” Hans said gently.

“Perhaps I’m ungenerous,” Deronda said, not apologizing. “Yet it can’t be ungenerous to warn you—you’re indulging mad, Quixotic dreams.”

“Who will be hurt but myself?” Hans said, pouting. “I won’t speak to her unless I’m sure of the answer. I won’t consult the oracles—I prefer cheerful uncertainty, as Sir Thomas Browne might say. I’d rather risk losing than be certain of winning elsewhere. And I won’t drink the poison of despair, even if you offer it. I’ve given up wine—so let me get drunk on hope and vanity.”

“With all my heart—if it helps,” Deronda said, easing Hans’s shoulder with a light push. He softened his tone, but his words were hollow. He was silent about his true feeling.

He felt that peculiar irritation that comes when others trust you as a guide—when they assume you’re untouched by desire, temptation, or weakness. We expect our mentors to be sinless—as if those who’ve recently stumbled aren’t often the wisest teachers. Throughout their friendship, Deronda had accepted Hans’s self-absorption, even enjoyed it. But now, he resented it—especially Hans’s assumption that Deronda was as far removed from rivalry for Mirah as an angel. It’s one thing to step aside willingly; another to endure others doing it for you. He’d expected Hans to cause trouble—but not trouble tinged with personal emotion. And he was ashamed that Hans’s hopes unsettled him, despite knowing they’d never come true. They stirred an image of Mirah changing—and though he denied it, the denial kept the image alive. Poor Hans seemed to be entering Deronda’s experience too deeply—going beyond the role of rescued prodigal, stirring something beyond compassion.

When Deronda visited Chelsea, Mrs. Meyrick’s relief at her son’s return was evident—but not enough to make him comfortable. Mirah seemed brighter than before, and for the first time, he saw her laugh. It happened when they spoke of Hans, naturally the mother’s favorite topic.

Mirah wanted to know if Deronda had seen Mr. Hans performing a sort of character piece without changing clothes.

“He shifts from one figure to another like a flame flickering between shapes you barely see,” Mirah said, glowing with the subject. “He’s so quick. I never liked comedy on stage—they dragged on too long. But in one minute, Mr. Hans becomes a blind bard, then Rienzi addressing the Romans, then an opera dancer, then a dejected young man—I pity them all, yet I laugh—all in one.” And then, she gave a little laugh that might have fit into a song.

“We never thought Mirah could laugh until Hans came,” Mrs. Meyrick said, noticing both she and Deronda were watching her.

“Hans seems in high spirits,” Deronda said, pleased. “I’m glad he brings you joy.”

“He’s been perfect since he returned,” Mrs. Meyrick said, keeping to herself the next thought—“if only it lasts.”

“It’s a great happiness,” Mirah said, “to see the son and brother return to this dear home. To hear them talk about their childhood—together. That sounds like heaven. To have a mother and brother who speak that way. I’ve never had it.”

“Nor I,” Deronda said, quietly.
