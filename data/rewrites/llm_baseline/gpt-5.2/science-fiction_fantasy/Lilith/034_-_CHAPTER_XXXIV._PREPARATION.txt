Victory once won, the woman of Bulika let her tongue run as if it had been waiting years for air. By the fire, while Lona tore leaves for bedding and the older girls salted down berries in the little gourds, she talked of the city—how its gates were slack, its walls neglected, its streets full of fear; how the princess was wicked as a blight in seed-time, and how the men of Bulika made a show of stature only to stoop when courage was asked of them.

The words went from mouth to mouth until even the smallest, with sticky fingers and cheeks stained with fruit, chattered “Bulika, Bulika,” as if it were the name of a game. They had no more notion of a city than a chick of the far side of its shell; to them the world was wood and clearing, firelight and sleeping heaps, the smell of crushed fern and warm milk. Yet the name took root, and with it the shape of a desire.

It was then I first saw the woman’s design, though the why of it was still hidden from me like a taste you know in the steam but cannot name on the tongue. To take the place—to go in with our strange troop and lay hold of Bulika—began to seem not merely possible but right. Lona’s eyes had a steady kindling when she listened, as if every word about the city found in her an answering memory. And I, I confess it: I let myself build on it. The Little Ones were growing—daily, hourly—quickening in wit and hand; I could no longer pretend they were helpless. The terrible Lilith—woman or leopardess—had one bare place in her armour, and I knew it: her doom ran through her daughter, and the old prophecy lay on Bulika like a charm half-believed, half-dreaded. The risk, if it was risk, seemed worth the taking.

In my heart I made pictures, and not all of them pure. Successful—how could they fail?—might not this crowd of children become in a breath a young people, and their very innocence be a rod of iron to the wicked? Might they not redeem a nation by being incapable of its cowardice? And with that thought came another, more selfish, sliding in like a spice too strong: that it was just Lona should sit where her mother had sat; that it was natural she should make of me her consort and minister. I even dreamed—shame on me—of opening trade in gems between this world and my own; as if the hunger of one world could ever feed the other without poisoning both.

Adam’s appeal returned to me too—water, the need of it, the mystery of it. I suggested to Lona that if we found them water it might hasten the growth of the Little Ones, as broth strengthens a child where dry crust cannot.

She listened, then shook her head with that quiet firmness which never bruised yet never yielded.

“They are what they are without it,” she said. “If we bring water now, we do not know what it will wake in them. It will make a new need. When we have the city, we will search for water.”

So we left that door unopened, and turned to what we could do with hands and time.

We prepared as people prepare for harvest and for war in the same season: with lists and laughter, with quarrels mended before nightfall, with every small task made part of a greater one. Lona gave herself chiefly to the commissariat, and I, by her order and my own bent, to the drilling. Under the trees I had them form and re-form, break and rally, the hardest part being to get them to come to the flag at the first sound—because each of them, by nature, was a whole little kingdom and did not understand at first the beauty of moving as one body. Most had slings, braided from fibres and softened by long handling; some of the bigger boys had bows and arrows that they had learned to make and mend with the obsession of young craftsmen. The bigger girls carried aloe-spikes—needles of living steel hardened by the sun, fixed into shafts—wickedly sharp, honest weapons that did not lie about what they could do. Their one command was to charge themselves with the too-small-to-fight, and to keep them alive by any means, fierce or tender.

Lona herself had grown, though she seemed not to notice it—she had always been, and remained, the tallest. Her hair fell longer now, heavy as ripened grain; she had become almost a woman, yet not one beauty of childhood had she lost, only gathered them into a new unity. When first we met again after the long separation, she set down her infant, put her arms around my neck, and clung to me without speech, her face lit from within as if the heart were a lamp. The child whimpered; she sprang to him instantly, and he was in her bosom before the sound had fully formed.

To see her with a little one who was obstinate or thoughtless, quick-tempered or sulky from hunger, was to see what a tender grandmother might be if she had the strength of youth too—patient, practical, never sentimental in a way that abandoned the body. I hardly remembered my own mother, yet in my mind’s eye she began to look like Lona; and if I imagined sister or child, it was Lona’s face that would come. My thoughts flew to her as birds to a familiar hedge. She hardly ever sought me, yet she was almost always within sound of my voice; and everything I did or planned I measured by her, not as a judge but as the hearth measures the whole house. She worked in an independence so complete it did not separate her from me; it only made being near her feel like being near a clear stream that does not ask permission to run.

She never neglected the smallest child for my sake; and my love, instead of resenting it, sharpened my duty. To love her and to do what must be done were not one thing, but they could not be pulled apart.

She did not overflow upon me with caresses; it was a closeness of recognition, like the devotion of a faithful animal—divine, not because it spoke great words, but because it never betrayed its nature. And because that nature was humble and true, it humbled me. I never spoke to her of her mother.

The wood in those days was full of birds. Their plumage had a splendour that seemed to pay in colour for the lack of flowers—flowers, it appeared, would not grow without water—yet their song lost nothing for all that brightness. The forest had its own acoustic law: leaf, wing, throat, and distance arranging themselves into a music that did not clatter or grind or drown the small sounds. I had known, in my own world, the hard iron shriek of wheels, the ceaseless commercial noise that pretends to be life; here the very silence had joints, and each bird note sat in its place like a well-chosen herb in broth.

Seeing their dropped feathers everywhere—caught in bark, laid on moss, lying in the little hollows where dew gathered—I began to gather them. It came into my heart, as naturally as putting a blanket on a child, to make Lona a garment. I sorted by length and strength, laid them in overlapping rows as one lays fish-scales or shingles against rain, binding them with fibres softened between my teeth and fingers. The work had the satisfaction of cookery: choosing, cleaning, arranging; the pleasure of making something that would touch the skin kindly and keep off wind.

Lona watched me, her head a little on one side, plainly pleased with my choice and the way the colours lay—never asking what it would become, as if she believed the act itself was enough until it declared its own end.

In a week or two it was finished: a long loose mantle, fastening at throat and waist, open for the arms. I rose and put it on her.

She rose, took it off, and laid it at my feet—out of a modesty that would not let her accept a splendour without first refusing it.

I put it again on her shoulders, showed her where her arms should go through. She smiled, stroked the feathers once or twice as if tasting them with her palm, then took it off again and laid it down, this time by her side.

When she left me she carried it away. For days I saw nothing of it, and thought perhaps she had hidden it as women hide things too dear to use.

Then one morning she came wearing it, and carrying another garment she had fashioned while I thought her busy only with children and stores. It was made of dried leaves of a tough evergreen—worked, pressed, and bound until it had the strength almost of leather and the look of scale-armour. It smelled of sun and sap, a clean resinous scent like a granary newly swept.

I put it on at once. From that day we always wore those garments when on horseback.

For, on the outskirts of the forest, there appeared one day a troop of full-grown horses. They were of the giant kind—great-boned, deep-chested, their breath warm and grassy. Because they were not alarmed at creatures so unlike themselves, I soon made friends with them; and two of the finest I trained for Lona and myself. Lona had been used to riding a smaller one; her delight was great when first she sat high on the back of her new horse and looked down as if the world had lowered itself in homage. The horse, too, showed himself proud of the burden he bore, stepping as though he understood her as more than a rider.

We exercised them daily until they trusted us enough to obey instantly and fear nothing; after that we rode them at parade and on the march.

The undertaking, even in the midst of our bustle, would at times loom before me as foolhardy. But the woman of Bulika’s confidence—whether genuine or worn like a mask—always pushed my hesitation back. The princess’s magic, she insisted, would be powerless against the children; and any force she might muster could not stand against our animal-allies. With a good stick, she said, she was ready to encounter any two men of Bulika.

Of the leopardess she confessed fear; of her I was ready. Yet I shrank from carrying all the children with us. It felt like taking a whole village’s seed-corn and putting it on one cart.

“Would it not be better,” I said to the woman, “that you remained in the forest with your baby and the smallest of the Little Ones?”

She answered that she relied greatly on the impression the sight of them would make on the women, especially the mothers.

“When they see the darlings,” she said, “their hearts will be taken by storm; and I must be there encouraging them to make a stand. If there be a remnant of hardihood in the place, it will be found among the women.”

“And you must not,” I told Lona, “encumber yourself with any of the children. You will be wanted everywhere.”

For there were two babies besides the woman’s, and even on horseback Lona had almost always one in her arms—warm, milky, breathing that soft animal breath which seems itself a kind of prayer.

“I do not remember ever being without a child to take care of,” she answered. “But when we reach the city, it shall be as you wish.”

Her confidence in me—who had failed so unworthily—shamed me. Yet neither had I begun the movement, nor had I clear ground to oppose it. If I drew back now, it would not only be fear in me; it would be moral ruin to the Little Ones, who had begun to believe that courage was simply another sort of play, only truer. I had no choice but to give the enterprise the best help I could. For myself, I was ready to live or die with Lona. Her humility as well as her trust humbled me, and I gave myself heartily to her purposes.

Our way would lie across a grassy plain, so there was no need to carry food for the horses, or for the two cows that would go with us for the infants’ sake. Their milk was not a luxury; it was the difference between peace and wailing, between sleep and a night that could break the nerves of a whole camp. But the elephants—our slow towers—had to be provided for. The grass was good enough, yet it was short; with their long one-fingered noses they could not pick enough for a single meal.

So we set the whole colony to gather grass and make hay. It was a work that made the air smell of sun-warmed green. The children cut and piled, trampling the stalks with bare feet, and the sound of it—rustle, swish, thud—was as wholesome as any hymn. There was no harsh metallic ring, no industrial scream; only the honest noise of bodies moving through plants. The elephants themselves carried loads enough to last several days, with the supplement of what we would gather fresh at every halt.

For the bears we stored nuts, and for ourselves we dried plenty of fruits—slices laid out where the air moved, turned at intervals so they would not sour. Even without salt, there is an art to drying: you must know when the flesh has given up enough water to keep, but not so much that it becomes lifeless on the tongue. The smell of the drying fruit hung about our preparations—sweet, slightly fermented, promising quick strength.

We caught and tamed several more of the big horses. When all was ready we loaded them, and the elephants, with provisions, and stood prepared at last to set out.

Then Lona and I held a general review. The troop gathered—children mounted on whatever would bear them: horses, little bulls that galloped and leaped like deer, the elephants patient as moving hills, bears shambling with a sort of grave amusement. The forest listened. The birds held their places above us, singing as if to test whether our own voices belonged to this soundscape or would tear it.

I made them a little speech.

I began by telling them I had learned a good deal about them, and knew now where they came from.

“We did not come from anywhere!” they cried, interrupting me. “We are here!”

Their certainty struck me—the way a child knows the names in his own dialect and thinks there can be no other. In that cry there was the whole power of the present moment, and also the peril: memory, if it is cut off, cannot teach.

I told them that every one of them had a mother of his own, like the mother of the last baby; that I believed they had all been brought from Bulika when they were so small they could not now remember; that the wicked princess there was so afraid of babies, and so determined to destroy them, that their mothers had to carry them away and leave them where she could not find them. And I told them that now we were going to Bulika to find their mothers and deliver them from the bad giantess.

I could feel, even as I spoke, the pressure of words that did not perfectly fit what they had lived. “Mother” meant something in them already—Lona had fed that meaning with her arms and patience—yet the mothers I spoke of were like a language their tongues had never learned. Some experiences do not translate; the mouth can repeat a word and still not taste what it stands for. But the word worked on them all the same, as smell works on hunger.

“But I must tell you,” I went on, “that there is danger before us; for, as you know, we may have to fight hard to take the city.”

“We can fight! We are ready!” cried the boys, bright-eyed, eager as if I had called for a race.

“Yes, you can,” I returned, “and I know you will. Mothers are worth fighting for. Only mind—you must all keep together.”

“Yes, yes; we’ll take care of each other,” they answered. “Nobody shall touch one of us but his own mother!”

“You must mind, every one, to do immediately what your officers tell you!”

“We will, we will! Now we’re quite ready! Let us go!”

“Another thing you must not forget,” I said, raising my voice so it carried cleanly without shouting. “When you strike, be sure you make it a downright swinging blow. When you shoot an arrow, draw it to the head. When you sling a stone, sling it strong and straight.”

“That we will!” they cried with a jubilant, fearless shout that made the birds startle and then settle again.

“Perhaps you will be hurt,” I said.

“We don’t mind that! Do we, boys?”

“Not a bit!”

“Some of you may very possibly be killed.”

A small boy—one of the finest among them—cried at once, as if he had been offered a sweet: “I don’t mind being killed!” He rode a beautiful little bull that pranced beneath him, tossing its head, proud of its rider’s wild courage.

“I don’t either! I don’t either!” came from all sides.

Then Lona—queen and mother and sister of them all—spoke from her great horse by my side. Her voice was not loud, yet it carried, because everyone wanted it.

“I would give my life,” she said, “to have my mother. She might kill me if she liked. I should just kiss her and die.”

“Come along, boys!” cried a girl, lifting her aloe-spike. “We’re going to our mothers!”

A pang went through my heart—sharp as biting a seed you thought was sweet and finding it bitter. But I could not draw back. To draw back now would be to teach the Little Ones a new word—the coward’s word—and once learned it would cling like taint to everything they did. So I held my place, and the whole living host stood ready, smelling of hay and fruit, leather-leaf and feather, milk and warm animal breath, the forest’s song pressing close around us as we prepared to go and break into the noise of Bulika.
