Denys grabbed Gerard just as he began to fall, slowing his descent—though it’s doubtful that alone would have spared him from a broken neck or shattered limb. His true savior was the dying bear beneath whom his head and shoulders landed. Denys wrenched him free. It was unnecessary. The beast still breathed, her limbs trembling, but she was no longer dangerous. Soon, her breath ceased entirely. Denys propped Gerard up against her soft, lifeless body and fanned him gently. Gradually, Gerard regained consciousness—but confused, and when he felt the bear beside him, he rolled away in terror, screaming.

“Courage,” Denys called. “The devil is dead.”

“Is it truly gone? Completely?” Gerard asked, hiding behind a tree. His courage had been fleeting, and now a chill gripped him—one that had plagued him for some time.

“Behold,” said Denys, pulling the bear’s ear playfully, then opening its jaws and thrusting his head inside, mocking it with crude gestures. Midway through this, Gerard vomited violently.

Denys laughed.

“What now?” he asked. “And why tumble from your perch just when we’d won the day?”

“I fainted, I suppose.”

“But why?”

When no answer came, Denys continued, “Green girls faint at a glance, but they choose their moment and place. What woman ever fainted while climbing a tree?”

“She splashed her foul blood all over me. I think the smell overwhelmed me! Ugh—I loathe blood.”

“I believe you mean it.”

“Look what a mess she made of me!”

“But not with your own blood. I pity any foe who tries to satisfy you.”

“You needn’t boast, Maitre Denys. I saw you under the tree—your shirt was stained.”

“Let us be clear,” said Denys, flushing. “It’s natural to tremble for a friend.”

Gerard responded by throwing his arms around Denys’s neck in silence.

“Look here,” said the soldier, touched by this burst of youthful emotion, “have you ever seen anything so like a woman? I love thee, little milksop—go on. Good heavens! Now he’s on his knees. What new whim is this?”

“Oh, Denys,” said Gerard, “should we not give thanks to Him who saved our lives against such odds?” And he knelt and prayed aloud. Soon, Denys knelt beside him, hands folded across his chest in the manner of his countrymen, face long and solemn. When they rose, Gerard’s expression was radiant.

“Good Denys,” he said, “Heaven will reward your piety.”

“Ah, bah!” replied the Frenchman. “I did it out of politeness—to please you, little one. C’est égal—it was well-prayed, orderly, and edified me thoroughly. A bishop could scarcely have done better. Now that our evening song is sung, and the saints are on our side—marchons!”

Before they took two steps, Denys stopped. “By-the-by—the cub!”

“Oh, no, no!” cried Gerard.

“You’re right. It’s too late. We’ve wasted enough time climbing trees, falling off them, fainting, vomiting, praying. The brute is heavy to carry. And now I recall—we’ll have Papa after it next. Bears make such a fuss over lost cubs. Wait—what’s this? You’re wounded! You’re wounded!”

“Not I.”

“He is wounded—wretched that I am!”

“Be calm, Denys. I feel no pain. I’m unharmed.”

“You? You only feel when someone else suffers,” Denys exclaimed, deeply moved. He dropped to his knees and examined Gerard’s leg with glistening eyes.

“Quick! Before it stiffens!” he urged, helping him forward.

“Who’s making the fuss now?” Gerard asked calmly.

Denys didn’t reply directly.

“Note well,” he said, “that I have a bad heart. You were brave enough to save my life, yet I mocked you—a novice in war. Wasn’t I once a novice myself? Then you fainted from a wound, and I thought you swooned from fear, calling you a milksop. In short, I have a sharp tongue and a cruel heart.”

“Denys!”

“Plait-il?”

“You lie.”

“You’re kind to say so, little one—and I’m eternally grateful,” muttered Denys, humbled.

They walked only a short distance before the muscles in Gerard’s injured leg contracted and hardened. Soon, he could barely lift his foot, doing so only with agony.

At last, he could endure no more.

“Let me lie down and die,” he groaned. “This is unbearable.”

Denys reminded him it was afternoon, and the nights were cold. Hunger and cold were poor companions. To lose heart now, when a great man was clearly dead, would be unreasonable. So Gerard leaned on his axe and limped on—until suddenly, he collapsed helplessly into the road.

Denys pulled him aside into the woods. To Gerard’s surprise, he handed him his crossbow and bolts, instructing him strictly to stay still. If anyone approached and refused to leave, he should shoot them dead at twenty paces. “Honest men walk the path; rogues in the woods—only fools parley with them.” With that, Denys snatched Gerard’s axe and ran—not toward Dusseldorf, but back along the road they’d come.

Gerard lay in pain, smarting. Rome, which had seemed so near at the start, now felt impossibly distant—though he was two hundred miles closer. But soon his thoughts turned to Sevenbergen. How sweet it would be to hold Margaret’s hand and tell her all he’d endured for her! Just thinking of her eased his suffering. Amidst pain and nerve-jarring irritation, he lay resigned, even smiling faintly.

He had lain there over two hours when sudden shouts broke the silence. Then something struck a nearby tree and quivered in the bark.

He looked. An arrow.

He leapt to his feet. More missiles clattered among the branches. The forest echoed with battle cries. Their origin was unclear—sound traveled strangely in these vast woods, disorienting strangers. Yet the noise seemed to fill the air. Then came the thunderous galloping of hooves, louder shouts, shrieks, groans, and strange, terrifying sounds—like furious thunderclaps, roaring and fading into crackling echoes. Red flames flickered between the trees, and clouds of sulfurous smoke drifted overhead. Then—silence.

Gerard stood in awe. “What will become of Denys?” he cried. “Oh, why did you leave me? Oh, Denys, my friend—my friend!”

Just before sunset, Denys returned, nearly crushed beneath a furry bundle. It was the bear’s hide.

Gerard greeted him with joy so intense it stunned him.

“I thought I’d never see you again, dear Denys. Were you in the battle?”

“No. What battle?”

“The bloody clash of men—or devils—that raged in the wood moments ago,” Gerard said, describing it vividly, more fully than I have.

Denys patted him gently.

“Well,” he said, “you’re a fine artist. Fever stirs the imagination. Once, I lay in a cart-shed with a cracked skull and saw two armies fight for an hour on eight square feet. I described it faithfully to my comrade—though not so colorfully as you, lacking book learning.”

“What? You don’t believe me? I heard arrows whiz overhead, heard shouts, and—”

“May the foul fiends take me if I believe a word of it.”

Gerard took his arm and pointed to a nearby tree.

“Why, it looks like—no, it *is*—a broad arrow, as I live!” He stepped close and looked up.

“It came from the battle. I heard it. I saw it.”

“An English arrow.”

“How do you know?”

“By its length. English archers draw their bows to the ear, others only to the breast. Hence their shafts are three feet long—and this one is exactly that, damn them! Well, if this isn’t illusion, then there was a skirmish. And if there was a battle in such a ridiculous place, then it’s none of my concern—my Duke has no quarrel here. Let’s sleep,” said the professional. He gathered leaves and made a bed for Gerard, placing his axe beside him. Then he lay down, hand on his arbalest, and draped the bear-skin over them, fur inward. They were soon warm as toast, fast asleep.

But long before dawn, Gerard woke Denys.

“What shall I do, Denys? I’m dying of hunger!”

“Do? Why, go back to sleep at once: qui dort dine.”

“But I tell you, I’m too hungry to sleep,” Gerard snapped.

“Then let’s march,” Denys replied, indulgent as a father.

After a brief fit of yawns, Denys bundled up several bears’ ears, rolling them in a strip of skin cut for the purpose. Together, they set off.

Gerard leaned on his axe, supported by Denys on the other side, hobbling along, sighing.

“I hate pain,” he muttered bitterly.

“Therein you show wisdom,” Denys replied smoothly.

It was a clear starlit night. Soon, the moon rose, revealing the edge of the wood not far ahead—a welcome sight, since Dusseldorf was only a short league beyond.

At the forest’s edge, they encountered something so mysterious they paused to study it before approaching. Two white pillars stood apart, and between them stood many figures resembling human forms.

“I won’t go farther until I know what this is,” Gerard whispered, agitated. “Are they statues of saints, meant for prayer? Or live robbers waiting to ambush honest travelers? No—they can’t be alive, for they stand on nothing I see. Oh, Denys, let’s turn back till morning—this is no mortal sight!”

Denys halted, peering intently. “They are men,” he said finally.

Gerard wanted to retreat even more.

“But men who will never harm us, nor we them. Don’t look at their feet—look where they stand!”

“Where, in God’s name?”

“Look above their heads,” Denys said gravely.

Following his direction, Gerard soon saw the dark outline of a wooden beam stretching between the pillars. As they approached, tiptoeing carefully, dark cords appeared in the moonlight—each hanging from the beam to a corpse, taut as wire.

As they passed beneath this grim monument of crime and vengeance, a light breeze stirred the bodies, making them sway and creak. Gerard shuddered at the ghastly greeting. So absorbed were they by the gallows that only now did they notice the fire below and the living figure huddled over it. His axe lay beside him, blade glowing red in the flames. He slept.

Gerard jumped, but Denys merely whispered, “Courage, comrade—there’s a fire.”

“Aye, but there’s a man at it.”

“There will soon be three,” Denys said, adding wood to the blaze. The cautious Gerard seized the man’s axe and sat firmly upon it, gripping his own, examining the sleeper. Nothing marked him outwardly. He wore common folk’s clothes and a three-cornered hat known as a Brunswicker—stiff enough to deflect a sword strike, with a thick brass band. Its weight had pressed his ears flat, like those of a fancy rabbit today. Still, such hats were common. What was odd was a man guarding a loaded gallows. For what purpose?

Denys, though curious, showed no sign. He pulled out two bears’ ears, skewered them, and began roasting them. “This will be eating coined money,” he said. “The burgomaster of Dusseldorf offered us a rix-dollar for these ears as proof of the beasts’ deaths. Better a lean purse than a hollow stomach.”

“Unfortunate man!” Gerard exclaimed. “Could you eat here?”

“Where the fire burns, meat must roast. Where it roasts, it must be eaten—nothing travels worse than roasted meat.”

“Eat then, Denys—if you can! But I’m cold and sick. There’s no room for hunger after what I’ve seen,” he shuddered over the fire. “Oh, how they creak! And who is this man? What a coarse fellow!”

Denys studied him like a connoisseur appraising art, then delivered his verdict. “I’d say he’s from the dregs of that gang—these up there (pointing casually) were the cream, and so led themselves into danger.”

“At that rate, why not knock him out before he wakes?” Gerard fidgeted.

Denys opened his eyes with mock surprise. “For one who claims to be a milksop, you have a quick hand. Why stun one when two can handle him? Tush—he wakes. Listen now to what he says, and tell me.”

No sooner were the words spoken than the watcher opened his eyes. Seeing the fire lit and two strangers watching him closely, he stared, forcing himself to appear calm—though a visible tremor ran through him. He quickly regained composure and grumbled, “Good morrow.” But at that instant, he noticed his axe was missing—and saw Gerard sitting on it, his own weapon ready. He lost his nerve instantly. Denys smiled grimly at the scene.

“Good morrow!” Gerard said quietly, eyeing him.

The watcher, now uneasy, said, “You’re using my fire.”

“But you’re welcome,” he added, voice faltering.

Denys whispered to Gerard. The watcher watched them sideways.

“My comrade says, since we share your fire, you’ll share his food.”

“So be it,” the man said warmly. “I have half a kid hanging on a bush nearby—I’ll fetch it.” He rose cheerfully, preparing to leave.

Denys raised his crossbow and aimed at his head. The man fell to his knees.

Denys lowered the weapon and gestured him back. He rose slowly, unsteadily—like a puppet whose strings have been cut—and retreated, sickened, like a mouse released only to be caught again.

“Sit down, friend,” Denys said grimly, in French.

The man obeyed, though he understood no French.

“Tell him the fire isn’t big enough for more than one. He’ll grasp my meaning.”

Gerard translated. The man grinned—ever since Denys spoke, he’d seemed relieved. “I didn’t know you were strangers,” he said to Gerard.

Denys cut a piece of bear’s ear and offered it gracefully to the man he’d just aimed at.

He accepted calmly, drew bread from his pouch, and shared it with both. Then, he winked and plunged his hand into the leaf pile (Gerard tightened his grip on his axe, ready to strike) and pulled out a leather bottle holding two gallons. He drank deeply, then offered it to Gerard. Gerard passed it untouched to Denys.

“Mort de ma vie!” the soldier cried. “Rhenish wine—and fit for an archbishop’s throat! Here’s to you, noble fellow—may you live short and merry! Come, Gerard, drink! Drink! Pshaw, ignore them—they ignore you. Still, if I hung over such a cask of Rhenish and three men sat beneath drinking it without offering me a drop, I’d soon join them.”

“Denys! Denys!”

“My spirit would sever the rope, and down I’d come—body among you, hand on the bottle, one eye winking, the other open.”

Gerard jumped up, screaming, fingers over his ears, running away—until he saw the watcher’s axe. The real threat brought him back. He sat again, hand on the axe, fingers still covering his ears.

“Courage, l’ami, le diable est mort!” Denys shouted cheerfully, offering Gerard a piece of bear’s ear, pressing it under his nose. Gerard turned away in disgust.

“Wine!” he gasped. “Heaven knows I need it, with such company as you and—”

He took a long draught. The wine warmed his veins, strengthened his heart—but couldn’t stop his shivers whenever wind blew. Denys and the other feasted recklessly, passing the bottle endlessly, toasting beneath the creaking gallows and its silent tenants.

“Ask him how they ended up here,” Denys said, mouth full, pointing upward without looking.

The question translated, the watcher replied: treason—diabolical treason and priest-craft. Drunk, he gave a lengthy, dull account. These men, now dangling so pitifully, were strong, honest souls who lived by their wits in the forest. Their independence angered many, and attacks were frequent—but always thwarted by the Virgin, their saints, and their own skill. Yesterday, merchants arrived on mules from Dusseldorf. The honest men let them advance a league, then attacked to force them to surrender ill-gotten gains. But the merchants were soldiers—of multiple nations, hired by the Archbishop of Cologne. Underneath their robes were haubergeons, weapons at hand. The honest men fought bravely, pressing hard—until horsemen, hidden for hours, charged. Armed with deadly engines, they fired leaden bullets, killing many and breaking the rest’s spirits. Captured, the victors—maliciously—had already tied ropes around their waists and swiftly hanged them, including the dead, for dramatic effect. “That one at the end was the captain. He never felt the rope—he was riddled with arrows and bullets before they could seize him: a worthy man, as ever shouted ‘Stand and deliver!’—though perhaps a bit hasty. That one in the buff jerkin was the lieutenant—fine soul, hanged alive. This one here—I never liked him—(not that one; that’s Conrad, my bosom friend)—I mean this one overhead in chicken-toed shoes—you were always spreading lies, thief, causing trouble. You know you were. And I, sir, prefer living in a coppice with honest men rather than a forest full of backbiters. Strangers, I drink to you.” He went down the line, naming each with the bottle-neck like a showman, giving neat but false descriptions—his eye for character nonexistent.

“Enough talk!” Denys barked. “Marchons! Give me his axe. Tell him he must help us.”

The man’s face fell, but seeing Denys’s gaze, he submitted. Gerard objected. “Y pensez-vous? To touch a thief makes my flesh crawl.”

“Childishness! All trades must survive. Besides, I have reasons. Don’t be wiser than your elder.”

“No. But if I must lean on him, I’ll keep my hand in my chest, clutching my knife.”

“A peculiar way to walk—but suit yourself.”

So the trio walked—strange, mixed: tenderness and suspicion intertwined. I wish I could draw them—I wouldn’t trust the pen.

The watchtower’s light at Dusseldorf became visible as they cleared the woods, lifting Gerard’s spirits. After an hour’s march, the black silhouette of the tower and buildings emerged. Their companion stopped, gloomy. “You might as well kill me now as take me closer to Dusseldorf’s gates.”

When Denys heard this, he said simply, “Let him go. His neck is in danger if he goes further with us.” Gerard agreed—his horror of criminals didn’t incline him to aid the law. Yet at this time, no private citizen in Europe meddled with criminals except in self-defense—except England, which, though backward in some things, was centuries ahead in this.

With freedom restored, the man asked for his axe. It was given. To their surprise, he lingered. Was he to get nothing for walking so far with them?

“Here are two batzen, friend.”

“Add the wine—the good Rhenish?”

“Did you pay for it?”

“Yes—the risk to my life.”

“Hmm. What say you, Denys?”

“I say it was worth its weight in gold. Here, lad—silver groshen, one for every acorn on that gallows tree—and one more for you, who’ll likely hang there someday.”

He took the coins, but still lingered.

“Well? What now?” Gerard snapped, thinking him shamefully overpaid. “Seeking our bones?”

“No, good sirs. You’ve seen how perilous my life is. You’re true men—your prayers matter. Give me a small prayer, if you please. I know none.”

Gerard’s temper flared. Since his wound, irritability surged. But he bit his lip. “There go two words to that bargain. First—tell me: is it true what people say about you Rhenish thieves—that you murder innocent travelers as well as rob them?”

The man answered sulkily, “We you call thieves aren’t to blame. The fault lies with the law.”

“Gramercy! So it’s the law’s fault that evil men break it?”

“I don’t mean that. But the law here kills an honest man for stealing. What follows? He feels pity, but is discouraged—pity brings no mercy, only greater danger. Cut a purse, and your life is forfeit. So he cuts the throat too—to save his own neck. Dead men tell no tales. Pray then for the poor soul driven by bloody laws to kill or be slaughtered. If there were less gibbeting on highways, there’d be fewer throats slit in dark woods, masters.”

“Fewer words would have served,” Gerard replied coldly. “I asked a question. I am answered.” Suddenly, he removed his hat—

“Obsecro Deum omnipotentem, ut, qua cruce jam pendent isti quindecim latrones fures et homicidae, in ea homicida fur et latro tu pependeris quam citissime, pro publica salute, in honorem justi Dei cui sit gloria, in aeternum, Amen.”

“And so, good day.”

The greedy outlaw was satisfied. “Latin,” he muttered. “More than I bargained for.” Indeed it was.

He returned to his work, mind at ease. The friends walked in silence, reflecting on the events of the past hours.

At last, Gerard said thoughtfully, “That she-bear saved our lives—by God’s will.”
