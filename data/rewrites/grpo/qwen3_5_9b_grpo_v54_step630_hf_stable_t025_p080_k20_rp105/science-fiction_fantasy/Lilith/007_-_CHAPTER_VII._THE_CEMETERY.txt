The air hit me like stepping into a deep freeze, a sudden, biting chill that stole the warmth from my bones. Behind me, the heavy door slammed shut, sealing us in. The sexton muttered something to his wife, and she turned. The transformation was instantaneous and terrifying. Where there had been ordinary humanity, there was now a brilliance that seemed to burn too brightly for mortal eyes to contain. Her face flashed with a radiance reminiscent of Beatrice, glowing with a purity like a white rose in the light of redemption. It was as if eternal life itself poured from her, a continuous, electric stream. Even her hands seemed to emit a white luminescence, every pearl-shell helmet gleaming like a captured moonstone. Her beauty was so overwhelming, so alien, that I was relieved when she finally turned her gaze away.

Yet, the candlelight our guide held barely pierced the gloom. At first, I saw nothing but darkness. Then, a faint shimmer caught my eye, hovering just above the floor. Was it a bed? Could anything live, let alone sleep, in such a profound cold? And if it couldn't wake of its own accord, was it not a miracle it remained still? Beyond that, other faint glimmers danced in the periphery, uncertain and wavering.

A few steps brought us to the first form: a human shape beneath a sheet, rigid and motionless. I could not tell if it was male or female; the light seemed to recoil from the face as we passed. Soon, I realized we were walking down an aisle lined with these couches. Upon almost every one, facing the passage, lay a figure—sleeping or dead—covered in a sheet as white as fresh snow. My soul fell silent, crushed by a dread that had no name. We moved through aisle after aisle, surrounded by countless couches. I could only glimpse a few at a time, yet they seemed to stretch away in every direction, vanishing into an infinite void. Was this my chosen bed? Was I condemned to sleep among the unwaking, with no one to rouse me? Was this the sexton's library, and were these his books? Truly, this was no halfway house; it was a chamber of the dead.

"One of the cellars I am placed to watch!" the sexton whispered, his voice low, as if fearing to disturb his silent guests. "Much wine is set here to ripen! But it is dark for a stranger!" he added.

"The moon is rising; she will soon be here," his wife replied. Her clear, sweet voice sounded like ancient sorrow that had long been bid farewell.

As she spoke, the moon peered through an opening in the wall, and a thousand white gleams responded to her touch. Yet, I could not yet discern a beginning or an end to the rows of couches. They stretched away endlessly, as if inviting the scattered world to sleep upon them. Along the far, receding narrow ways, each couch stood solitary, occupied by a lone sleeper. At first, I thought their sleep was death, but I quickly realized it was something deeper, something I could not comprehend.

The moon climbed higher, shining through other apertures, but the sheer scale of the place defied my understanding. Sometimes it resembled a long cathedral nave; at others, a vast barn converted into a dwelling of tombs. She looked colder than any moon seen in the frostiest night of Earth. Where her beams struck directly, they cast a bluish, icy sheen on the white sheets and the pale faces below—or perhaps it was the faces themselves that made the moon appear so cold.

Among those I could see, all shared a brotherhood of death, yet none were alike in the histories etched upon their features. Here lay a man who had died in the prime of his strength; though this was not death, I had no other name for it. His dark beard flowed like a liberated stream from the glacier of his frozen countenance. His forehead was smooth as polished marble, and a shadow of pain lingered about his lips, but only a shadow. Beside him lay the form of a girl, passing lovely to behold. The sadness of parting had not yet been absorbed into perfect peace, but absolute submission held her placid features. There was no sign of wasting disease, no trace of killing care or grief of heart; if pain had existed, it was long charmed into sleep, never to wake. Many beautiful ones lay very still—some mere children—but I saw not a single infant.

The most beautiful of all was a lady whose white hair alone suggested her age when she first fell asleep. Upon her stately countenance rested not submission, but a noble acquiescence, an assurance firm as the foundations of the universe that all was as it should be. On some faces lingered the almost obliterated scars of strife, the marring of hopeless loss, the fading shadows of sorrows that had seemed inconsolable. The aurora of the great morning had not yet quite melted them away, but those faces were few. Every one bearing such a brand of pain seemed to plead, "Pardon me: I died only yesterday!" or "Pardon me: I died but a century ago!" That some had been dead for ages, I knew not merely by their unutterable repose, but by something for which I had neither word nor symbol.

We came at last to three empty couches. Immediately beyond them lay the form of a beautiful woman, a little past the prime of life. One of her arms lay outside the sheet, her hand open with the palm upward, centered on a dark spot. Next to her stood the stalwart figure of a middle-aged man. His arm too was exposed, the strong hand almost closed, as if clenched around the grip of a sword. I thought he must be a king who had died fighting for the truth.

"Will you hold the candle nearer, wife?" the sexton whispered, bending down to examine the woman's hand.

"It heals well," he murmured to himself. "The nail found in her nothing to hurt!"

Finally, I ventured to speak.

"Are they not dead?" I asked softly.

"I cannot answer you," he replied in a subdued tone. "I almost forget what they mean by DEAD in the old world. If I said a person was dead, my wife would understand one thing, and you would imagine another. This is but one of my treasure vaults," he continued, "and all my guests are not laid in vaults. Out there on the moor, they lie thick as the leaves of a forest after the first blast of your winter—thick, let me say rather, as if the great white rose of heaven had shed its petals over it. All night the moon reads their faces, and smiles."

"But why leave them in the corrupting moonlight?" I asked.

"Our moon," he answered, "is not like yours—the old cinder of a burnt-out world. Her beams embalm the dead, not corrupt them. You observe that here the sexton lays his dead on the earth; he buries very few under it! In your world, he lays huge stones on them, as if to keep them down. I watch for the hour to ring the resurrection-bell and wake those that are still asleep. Your sexton looks at the clock to know when to ring the dead-alive to church; I hearken for the cock on the spire to crow: 'AWAKE, THOU THAT SLEEPEST, AND ARISE FROM THE DEAD!'"

I began to conclude that the self-styled sexton was in truth an insane parson; the whole thing was too mad! But how was I to escape? I was helpless. In this world of the dead, the raven and his wife were the only living beings I had yet seen. Whither should I turn for help? I was lost in a space larger than imagination; for if here two things, or any parts of them, could occupy the same space, why not twenty or ten thousand? But I dared not think further in that direction.

"You seem in your dead to see differences beyond my perception!" I ventured to remark.

"None of those you see," he answered, "are in truth quite dead yet, and some have but just begun to come alive and die. Others had begun to die, that is, to come alive, long before they came to us; and when such are indeed dead, that instant they will wake and leave us. Almost every night some rise and go. But I will not say more, for I find my words only mislead you. This is the couch that has been waiting for you," he ended, pointing to one of the three.

"Why just this?" I said, beginning to tremble, anxious to delay by parley.

"For reasons which one day you will be glad to know," he answered.

"Why not know them now?"

"That also you will know when you wake."

"But these are all dead, and I am alive!" I objected, shuddering.

"Not much," rejoined the sexton with a smile. "Not nearly enough! Blessed be the true life that the pauses between its throbs are not death!"

"The place is too cold to let one sleep!" I said.

"Do these find it so?" he returned. "They sleep well—or will soon. Of cold they feel not a breath: it heals their wounds. Do not be a coward, Mr. Vane. Turn your back on fear, and your face to whatever may come. Give yourself up to the night, and you will rest indeed. Harm will not come to you, but a good you cannot foreknow."

The sexton and I stood by the side of the couch, his wife, holding the candle, at its foot. Her eyes were full of light, but her face had returned to a still whiteness; it was no longer radiant.

"Would they have me make of a charnel-house my bed-chamber?" I cried aloud. "I will not. I will lie abroad on the heath; it cannot be colder there!"

"I have just told you that the dead are there also," said the librarian.

"I will NOT," I cried again. And in the encompassing dark, the two figures gleamed out like specters waiting on the dead; neither answered me. Each stood still and sad, looking at the other.

"Be of good comfort; we watch the flock of the great shepherd," the sexton said to his wife.

Then he turned to me.

"Didst thou not find the air of the place pure and sweet when thou enteredst it?" he asked.

"Yes; but oh, so cold!" I answered.

"Then know," he returned, and his voice was stern, "that thou who callest thyself alive hast brought into this chamber the odours of death, and its air will not be wholesome for the sleepers until thou art gone from it!"

They walked farther into the great chamber, leaving me alone in the moonlight with the dead.

I turned to escape.

What a long way I found it back through the dead! At first, I was too angry to be afraid, but as I grew calm, the still shapes became terrible. Finally, with loud offense to the gracious silence, I ran, I fled wildly, and, bursting out, flung the door shut behind me. It closed with an awful silence.

I stood in pitch darkness. Feeling about me, I found a door, opened it, and was aware of the dim light of a lamp. I stood in my library, with the handle of the masked door in my hand.

Had I come to myself out of a vision? Or lost myself by going back to one? Which was the real—what I now saw, or what I had just ceased to see? Could both be real, interpenetrating yet unmingling?

I threw myself on a couch and fell asleep.

In the library was one small window to the east, through which, at this time of the year, the first rays of the sun shone upon a mirror whence they were reflected on the masked door. When I woke, there they shone, and thither they drew my eyes. With the feeling that behind it must lie the boundless chamber I had left by that door, I sprang to my feet and opened it. The light, like an eager hound, shot before me into the closet and pounced upon the gilded edges of a large book.

"What idiot," I cried, "has put that book in the shelf the wrong way?"

But the gilded edges, reflecting the light a second time, flung it onto a nest of drawers in a dark corner, and I saw that one of them was half open.

"More meddling!" I cried, and went to close the drawer.

It contained old papers and seemed more than full, for it would not close. Taking the topmost one out, I perceived that it was in my father's writing and of some length. The words on which first my eyes fell instantly made me eager to learn what it contained. I carried it to the library, sat down in one of the western windows, and read what follows.
