When Major Pendennis bid farewell to his nephew Pen on the second day after their arrival at Oxbridge, it’s safe to say that neither man was particularly sorry to see the other go—though for very different reasons. The Major, having spent three months of what he considered precious leisure time tutoring a young man who clearly didn’t need much tutoring, was thrilled to be free of the burden. He’d sacrificed his peace, his privacy, and possibly even his sanity for the sake of “education.” Let us pause a moment and consider: how many men—especially Majors—would willingly surrender such a stretch of life to mentor a relative? A man may lay down his life for honor, but ask him to give up his pipe, his nap, or his favorite armchair, and you’ll see a different kind of resistance. Few can endure that sort of sacrifice. Now, if you happen to have a beard (and let’s face it, many of you do), would you have done the same? I won’t claim women wouldn’t—after all, they’re trained from childhood to put others first. But men? We’re not exactly known for our self-denial. Ask yourself: how many truly saintly men do you know? Ten? Try five. And if you find ten, send me a note—I’ll bring wine.

Anyway, the Major left in high spirits, waving from the coach as Foker and Pen waved back. Foker made sure the coachman knew to treat the Major with special care—“He’s an important gentleman,” he said solemnly, as if the Major were a visiting prince rather than a slightly flustered military man. The Major dashed off to London, then to Cheltenham, where he promptly descended upon several nearby country estates like a hawk spotting pigeons. Shooting, good company, and no lectures—this was the life.

Now, while some authors might spend half a novel chronicling one week of a character’s college career, we shall not. Pen’s academic journey is too rich to cram into endless detail, unless some future historian decides to pick up the pen after my demise and write a sequel for your grandchildren. But let’s be honest: most boys’ college lives are not exactly gripping reading. Yours? Mine? Not really. As long as we keep our honor intact, we’re content. Women remain pure. Men? Well, let’s just say they don’t. Poor Arthur Pendennis wasn’t worse than the rest—he was just part of the pack. And the pack, dear reader, is generally not a model of virtue. Can you name ten spotless men? I can’t. My list includes a few decent fellows, but saints? Hardly.

Pen began his studies with admirable diligence. He attended lectures in classics and mathematics, though he soon realized he had little talent for either. Worse still, he discovered that two particularly unrefined young men—men whose trousers lacked straps, whose shoes were thick and coarse, and whose socks looked like they’d been knitted by a badger—were outperforming him. This was unacceptable. So Pen dropped math and classical science entirely and announced to his mother that he would now focus solely on Greek and Roman literature.

Mrs. Pendennis, ever indulgent, was delighted. “That’s wonderful!” she exclaimed. “But darling, don’t ruin your health. I’ve heard dreadful stories about students who overworked themselves and ended up with brain fever—some even died before finishing their degree.” Pen, though perfectly healthy (or so he claimed), kindly promised never to stay up late reading. And he kept this promise—with more determination than he showed in any other area of his life. That’s the thing about promises: they’re easiest to keep when they involve something you don’t actually want to do.

But soon Pen found the classical lectures duller than a Sunday sermon. His classmates were slow, and the tutor, Mr. Buck, was barely smarter than a fifth-form boy at Grey Friars. He could recite grammar rules about Aeschylus, but couldn’t appreciate poetry any better than Mrs. Binge, the bed-maker. Pen grew weary of watching these amateurs stumble through lines he could read in seconds. Eventually, he concluded that private reading was the only real education. So he told his mother he’d be reading alone far more often—and attending lectures far less. She, bless her, knew nothing about Homer or algebra, but trusted her son completely. “You’ll get the place you deserve,” she said, confident in his destiny.

Pen didn’t return home until after Christmas—slightly earlier than expected, which disappointed both his mother and Laura, who had been counting down the days to build a snow fortress, just like the one he’d built three winters ago. But he was invited to Logwood, Lady Agnes Foker’s estate, where there were private theatricals and a dazzling Christmas party full of fashionable people—people the Major insisted Pen must meet. He stayed home for the last three weeks of vacation, and Laura noticed immediately how many new clothes he’d brought: smart shooting jackets with flashy buttons, velvet waistcoats, embroidered cravats, and linen so fine it seemed to glow in the candlelight.

And then there was the dressing case—silver-mounted, gleaming like a treasure chest. Inside: rings, jewelry, and a French watch with a gold chain. Gone was the old chronometer that had hung from John Pendennis’s fob—the one with the jingling seals, once used by the late doctor to take patients’ pulses. Pen had coveted that watch just months earlier, thinking it the grandest timepiece in existence. Helen had given it to him with a touching speech about his father’s virtues and the importance of time. But now? Pen called it “outdated” and compared it to a warming-pan. Laura thought this disrespectful. He tucked it away in a drawer with old gloves, discarded cravats, and the school watch mentioned earlier.

Rebecca, his old horse, was also deemed insufficient. Pen traded her for a stronger, pricier steed. His father paid for it. Laura cried when Rebecca was led away.

Then came the cigars—boxes of them, labeled *Colorados*, *Afrancesados*, *Telescopios*, and *Fudson Oxford Street*. Pen smoked them everywhere: in the stables, near the greenhouses (where they helped fertilize Helen’s plants), and even in his study. At first, his mother disapproved. But Pen explained he was working on a prize poem and needed his cigar. He quoted Byron: “I smoke because I’m wise.” His mother, seeing him deep in thought, eventually relented. One day, she walked in to find him on the sofa, poring over a novel. Instead of interrupting, she quietly fetched his cigar case and lit it for him. Pen laughed, kissed her hand, and said, “Dear old mother, if I told you to burn the house down, I think you’d do it.” And honestly? He might have been right. She’d probably have done it.

Besides English “light literature,” Pen devoured French novels—books that left Helen wide-eyed when she glanced through them. But Pen defended himself: “It’s not my fault the books exist. I must read the best writers to improve my French.” And so, reluctantly, Mrs. Pendennis allowed it. Miss Laura, however, was strictly forbidden—both by her mother and by Arthur himself, who insisted that while he needed to study everything to become a well-rounded man, a young lady should not be exposed to such material.

During the long vacation, Pen drained the bin of claret his father had stockpiled—“Not a headache in a hogshead,” he’d once declared. When it ran out, he ordered more from Messrs. Binney and Latham of Mark Lane, London, recommended by the rector, Doctor Portman. “You’ll need to entertain your friends,” the rector had advised. “Use a respectable London firm—Oxbridge tradesmen charge exorbitant prices for poor-quality wine.” Pen followed the advice and wrote to Binney and Latham, requesting wine for both Fairoaks and his university account. When the bill arrived, his mother gasped. Pen laughed. “It’s moderate,” he said. “Everyone drinks claret and champagne now.” She paid it, knowing the household expenses were rising—but only briefly. Pen was home for a few weeks. While he was here, shouldn’t they make him happy?

Arthur’s allowance was generous—more than many sons of wealthier families received. Years earlier, John Pendennis had begun saving money for his son’s education, calling it the “Arthur Education Fund.” After his death, his widow added to it. By the time Arthur entered college, it was quite substantial. The Major believed in liberal allowances: “Let him enter society as a gentleman. If he fails later, it’ll be his own fault.” Stinting a boy, he said, was the worst policy. Arthur would face the world soon enough. For now, give him good habits, friends, and a strong start.

So Pen, the only son of a country gentleman, with a good allowance and a gentlemanly bearing, appeared far more important than he actually was. To the university authorities, tradesmen, and undergraduates alike, he was a true young buck—a member of the aristocracy. His manner was bold, cheerful, and perhaps a bit impertinent—just as a spirited youth should be. He was generous with money, which seemed limitless. He loved music, had a good voice, and enjoyed singing. Boat-racing hadn’t yet reached its current frenzy; riding and tandem-driving were the trends. Pen rode well to hounds, wore pink as befitted a dandy, and though not overly extravagant, still managed to rack up impressive bills at Nile’s livery stable and elsewhere.

In fact, Pen had almost every taste in life. He loved books—Doctor Portman had taught him to appreciate rare editions, and he adored beautiful bindings. His shelves became showcases of tall copies, gilded edges, marbled covers, and blind-tooling. He had a refined eye for art—prints by Strange, Rembrandt etchings, Wilkie originals—none of those cheap opera dancers or racing prints that had pleased Mr. Spicer. His rooms were furnished with impeccable taste, earning him respect among his peers.

He also had a weakness for jewelry, rings, and fine clothing. Pen was, by all accounts, a dressy man. He and his friends would prepare for dinner parties with the same care as if they were going to woo a queen. Rumor had it he wore rings over his kid gloves—he denies it, but what absurdities won’t youth commit with perfect seriousness? That he took perfumed baths? True. He claimed it was necessary after encountering certain “low-class” men in hall.

By his second year, when Miss Fotheringay became a sensation in London and dozens of prints were published of her, Pen hung one in his room. He confided to his friends how deeply, wildly, passionately he had loved her. He showed them verses he’d written—his brow darkened, eyes rolled, chest heaved. The verses spread through campus, copied, mocked, admired. There’s nothing that elevates a young man more than being seen as romantically tormented. They said he nearly committed suicide, fought a duel with a baronet. Freshmen pointed him out. When he swaggered out of college at two o’clock, surrounded by cronies, he was a sight to behold. Dressed to the nines, he’d ogle visiting ladies and comment on their beauty with the gravitas of a seasoned critic. People said, “I walked with Pendennis,” as if they’d shared a meal with a duke.

He and the Proctor exchanged nods like rivals—no one knew who held more power.

In short, Arthur Pendennis had become one of the leading figures at Oxbridge. Youth has a peculiar way of idolizing leaders. They follow, admire, imitate. Every generous boy has admired someone else—Pendennis had his followers, his loyal band, his rivals. When word spread at haberdashers that Mr. Pendennis had ordered a crimson satin cravat, within days, dozens appeared in Main Street. Simon the Jeweler sold two gross of “Pendennis pins” based on a design he’d chosen.

Now, if you’re the type who enjoys arithmetic, calculate how much a young man with such tastes would spend in two or three years. It adds up fast. Pen didn’t have a calculating mind, but none of his habits were outrageously wasteful. Still, look at the bills: Paddington’s tailor, Guttlebury’s cook, Dillon Tandy’s print purchases, Wormall’s book orders—each far exceeded Pen’s own modest expenses. Yet Pendennis stood apart. While Lord Paddington cared nothing for art, Guttlebury ignored fashion, Snaffle read only racing guides, and others focused narrowly—Pen embraced it all. He excelled in each, not as a specialist, but as a polymath of pleasure.

And so, with style, flair, and a heart full of romantic nonsense, Pendennis of Boniface ruled the roost—at least until the next season, when the next dashing young man would take his place.
