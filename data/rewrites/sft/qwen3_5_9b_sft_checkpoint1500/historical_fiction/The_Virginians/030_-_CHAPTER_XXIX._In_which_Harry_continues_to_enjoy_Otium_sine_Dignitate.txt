Madame de Bernstein, despite the abundance of card players awaiting her at her lodgings and the assembly rooms, found a peculiar contentment remaining at the Wells. Her days were filled with the dual tasks of chiding her niece and engaging in her rubber. For a man of Harry's age, almost every location becomes a paradise if it offers lively company, fresh air, and a share of sport and diversion. Indeed, at twenty, all pleasure seems exquisite. One rushes forth to meet it with eager anticipation, speculating on its arrival, and when the invitation comes, counting the days until reunion. Yet, how gently and calmly one regards these joys near life's closing season! Does not Lady Merton recall her first ball? Does not her mind wander to that golden past as she sits adorning the walls while her daughters dance? I, too, remember a time when walking three miles through the countryside to dine with Old Captain Jones seemed a delight. To imagine enjoying such a trek today, merely to share Jones's half-pay port! Perhaps it was purchased from a local merchant for a modest sum, yet it arrived wrapped in a kind welcome, flavored by a youth that neither aged wine nor mature men can replicate now. *Viximus nuper.*

I am far less inclined to judge young Harry's conduct and idleness with the stern severity of his friend, the Colonel of the Twentieth Regiment. Oh, blessed idleness! Divine lazy nymph! Reach me a novel as I recline in my dressing gown at three in the afternoon; mix a sherry-cobbler, and bring me a cigar! You, slatternly and smiling enchantress! Though they may assail you with vile names, swear away your character, and brand you the Mother of Evil, you remain the finest company in the world.

Lord March departed for the North, and Lord Chesterfield, finding the Tunbridge waters failed to cure his deafness, returned to his solitude at Blackheath. However, other gentlemen lingered to revel and indulge, and Mr. Warrington quickly discovered ample companionship at his usual haunt, the White Horse. He swiftly mastered the art of ordering a French dinner with the sophistication of any fashionable gentleman from St. James's. He conversed with Monsieur Barbeau in his native tongue with a fluency surpassing many others, cultivated an elegant and discerning taste for wines, and could distinguish between Clos Vougeot and Romande with remarkable acumen. He became the youthful monarch of the Wells, a realm frequented by easygoing men of the world who, rather than being shocked by Harry's reputation for gallantry and extravagance—which had so terrified Mr. Wolfe—embraced it with open arms.

Although our Virginian mingled among the revellers and swam in the same waters as the loose-finned, the boy possessed a natural shrewdness and honesty that shielded him from the common snares laid for the unwary. He rarely entered foolish wagers with the jolly idlers surrounding him, and the seasoned hands found it difficult to deceive him. He participated in games, both indoor and outdoor, driven by a natural aptitude, proving capable of holding his own against any fair competitor. He was scrupulous, playing only with gentlemen he trusted and invariably settling his debts immediately. While he might have fared poorly at a college examination, he possessed prudence, fidelity, keen perception, generous spirit, and dauntless courage.

He frequently found occasion to demonstrate the quality of his make. Consider, for instance, the unfortunate Cattarina, whose entreaties had pushed past the limits of Harry's generosity. With perfect composure and skill, he withdrew from the advances of the Opera-House siren, leaving her free to charm some more gullible soul. In vain did the mermaid's hysterical mother pursue Harry, claiming that a cruel bailiff had seized all her daughter's possessions for debt and that her venerable father lay languishing in a London gaol. Harry firmly declared that no dealings existed between himself and the bailiff. Since he had only gained the fortune of acquainting himself with Mademoiselle Cattarina and satisfying her whims with various trifles and knick-knacks for which she fancied them, he was under no obligation to pay her family's past debts. He refused to act as bail for her father or settle her outstanding accounts at Tunbridge. First, the old woman labeled him a monster and ingrate; then, with a veteran smirk, she questioned why he did not accept payment for the services he had rendered to the young lady. Initially, Mr. Warrington could not comprehend the nature of such compensation. But when the old woman explained, the honest lad rose in horror at the thought of a woman trafficking in her child's dishonor. He told her he hailed from a land where even the savages would recoil from such a bargain. Having bowed the old lady ceremoniously to the door, he ordered Gumbo to note her well and never admit her to his lodgings again. Undoubtedly, she retired breathing vengeance against the Iroquois; indeed, she declared no Turk or Persian would treat a lady thus. Soon after, she and her daughter retreated to London as soon as their anxious landlord permitted.

Then followed Harry's perils of gaming, alongside his dangers regarding gallantry. A man who "plays at bowls" must expect rubbers. After dinner at the ordinary, having declined to play piquet further with Captain Batts, and being roughly asked for his reasons, Harry frankly told the Captain that he played only with gentlemen who honored their debts, like himself. Yet, expressing his readiness to settle any outstanding account with Mr. Batts, the Captain declared himself satisfied beforehand and promptly left the Wells without paying Harry or any other creditor. Furthermore, he demonstrated his spirit by beating a chairman who had been rude to old Miss Whiffler one evening as she headed to the assembly. Upon learning that the calumny regarding himself and the unfortunate opera dancer was being repeated by Mr. Hector Buckler, one of the Wells' fiercest frequenters, Mr. Warrington approached Buckler in the pump room. There, the latter was regaling a crowd of water drinkers with the very slander, and Mr. Warrington publicly informed him that the story was false, warning that he would hold anyone accountable who spread it again. Thus, although our friend, being at Rome, certainly did as Rome did, he proved himself a valorous and worthy Roman. Hurling with the wolves, he was acknowledged by Mr. Wolfe himself to be as brave as the best of them.

Had that officer revealed the stories that caused Colonel Lambert so much pain, we might be certain that when Mr. Wolfe discovered his young friend's innocence, he would have taken the first opportunity to retract the odious charges. Joy rippled through the Lamberts upon the lad's acquittal—a pleasure akin to that felt by higher natures at the recovery of sinners. Never before had the little family been so happy—not even when news arrived of Brother Tom winning his scholarship—as when Colonel Wolfe rode over bearing the account of his conversation with Harry Warrington. "Hadst thou brought me a regiment, James, I think I should not have been better pleased," said Mr. Lambert. Mrs. Lambert summoned her daughters, who were in the garden, kissing them both when they entered, and shouted the good news to them. Hetty leaped for joy, and Theo performed some remarkably brilliant operations upon the harpsichord that night. When Dr. Boyle entered for his game of backgammon, he could not initially account for the illumination in their faces until the three ladies, singing in a happy chorus, told him how right he had been in his sermon and how dreadfully they had wronged that poor, dear, good young Mr. Warrington.

"What shall we do, my dear?" asked the Colonel of his wife. "The hay is in, the corn will not be cut for another fortnight—the horses have nothing to do. Suppose we..." Here he leaned over the table and whispered in her ear.

"My dearest Martin! The very thing!" cried Mrs. Lambert, taking her husband's hand and pressing it.

"What is the very thing, mother?" cried young Charley, home for his Bartholomewtide holidays.

"The very thing is to go to supper. Come, Doctor! We will have a bottle of wine tonight and drink repentance to all who think evil."

"Amen," said the Doctor, "with all my heart!" And with this, the worthy family proceeded to their supper.
