“For never any thing can be amiss
When simpleness and duty tender it.”
— *A Midsummer Night’s Dream*

John Thornton stepped into the next day with the sharp, unswerving focus of a man who has buried personal pain deep under layers of practical purpose. There was a small but steady uptick in demand for finished cotton goods that morning, and he moved fast to seize the advantage, striking shrewd, unyielding bargains that left his trade counterparts nodding in reluctant respect. He arrived exactly on the hour for the magistrates’ meeting, and his quick, unclouded judgement—his gift for spotting the long trail of consequences any choice would leave in its wake before anyone else in the room had finished sorting their notes—made him the de facto leader of the group. Older, wealthier men whose fortunes were locked up in land and property, while his own capital was all tied to the mills, turned to him for direction. He was the one deputized to coordinate with the police, to lay out every necessary step to keep the peace in the wake of the riot.

He noticed none of their quiet deference, no more than he noticed the soft west wind that barely ruffled the straight plume of smoke rising from his mill’s tallest chimney. The only goal he cared about was pushing all necessary business to its swift end. It was his mother, greedy for every scrap of regard paid to her son, who drank in the gossip from the magistrates’ wives and the town’s wealthy households: that if Thornton had not been there, the riot aftermath would have unfolded far worse, a disaster for the whole of Milton.

All day he swept through task after task, the raw mortification of Margaret Hale’s rejection the day before, and the dazed, purposeless hours that followed it, having burned away every last fog of confusion from his mind. He revelled in the sharp, unbreakable power he felt in his work, almost convinced he could lock his heart away entirely, like the old miller by the Dee who sang that he cared for nobody, and nobody cared for him.

The evidence against Boucher and the other riot ringleaders was laid before him that afternoon; charges of conspiracy against three other men fell apart for lack of proof, but he charged the police sternly to keep constant watch, so the long arm of the law would be ready to strike the second they had solid, unassailable proof of wrongdoing. Stepping out of the hot, reeking borough court into the sultry, close air of the street, all that iron resolve seemed to slip away at once. He went languid, unable to rein in his wandering thoughts, that kept drifting back not to his public work, not even to his rejection the day prior—but to that half-hour in the riot, when Margaret had clung to him for safety, her heart beating fast against his chest. He wound his way through the crowded, busy streets mechanically, not seeing a single face around him, sick with a sharp, unnameable longing to relive that one short, bright moment again.

“Why, Mr. Thornton! You’re cutting me dead, aren’t you?” a voice laughed. “How is Mrs. Thornton? Terribly close weather this, and us doctors hate it, I can tell you.”

He snapped out of his trance. “Beg your pardon, Dr. Donaldson—truly didn’t see you. My mother’s quite well, thank you. It’s fine weather for the harvest, at least. If the wheat comes in well, trade’ll bounce next year, no matter how your patients fare.”

“Aye, every man for himself,” the doctor chuckled. “Bad times for trade mean good times for my practice, you mark my words. You Milton millfolk work yourselves thin, undermine your health, and you don’t even notice it till it’s too late.”

“Not me,” Thornton said, half-smiling. “I’m made of iron. The news of the worst bad debt I ever owned never made my pulse skip a beat, and this strike that hits me harder than Hamper, harder than any other man in town? It’s never once spoiled my appetite. You’ll have to look elsewhere for a patient, Doctor.”

“Speaking of patients you recommended, that poor lady in Crampton—Mrs. Hale, you know her? I don’t mind saying, she hasn’t many weeks left. I never held out hope for a cure, as I told you before, but I saw her today, and her fever’s far worse than I thought.”

Thornton went still. That vaunted steady pulse of his stuttered for a full second. “Is there anything I can do?” he asked, his voice rougher than usual. “Money’s no object for anything she might need—any comfort, any dainty at all.”

The doctor shook his head. “She’s got a constant fever, and all she craves is fruit. Jargonelle pears are the best we’ve got in the market right now, but there’s plenty of them around.”

“Send word to me the second she needs anything else, will you?” Thornton said. “I rely on you for that.”

“Oh, never fear, I won’t spare your deep purse,” Donaldson laughed. “I only wish every one of my patients had a patron as generous as you.”

Thornton was no broad philanthropist, no lover of all mankind—few would have guessed he was capable of such fierce, private affection for anyone. But he made straight for the finest fruit shop in Milton, picked out bunches of purple grapes still dusted with their delicate silvery bloom, the richest, sun-ripened peaches, and the freshest green vine leaves to tuck between them. The shopman packed them neatly into a wicker basket, then paused to ask, “Where shall we send this, sir? Back to Marlborough Mills, I suppose?”

“No,” Thornton said, and he hefted the heavy basket into his own two hands. He carried it all the way through the busiest shopping thoroughfare in Milton, where every young lady of his acquaintance turned to stare, shocked to see the stern, formidable mill owner trundling a fruit basket like a common errand boy.

He told himself he was not daunted by the thought of Margaret Hale, not at all. He was doing a kindness for Mr. Hale, his old friend, and he would not be cowed by some haughty girl’s rejection into shirking a simple good turn. This was nothing short of defiance, he told himself, defying her to make him feel small for doing what was right.

He walked fast, reaching Crampton in no time, taking the stairs two at a time, and burst into the Hales’ drawing room before Dixon could even announce him. His face was flushed, his eyes bright with earnest, unselfconscious kindness. Mrs. Hale lay prostrate on the sofa, hot with her fever. Mr. Hale sat beside her reading aloud, and Margaret was perched on a low stool at her mother’s side, stitching quietly. Her heart fluttered fast when she saw him—though he gave no sign he had noticed her at all, barely glancing at Mr. Hale as he walked straight up to the fevered woman on the sofa.

“I ran into Dr. Donaldson, ma’am,” he said, in that soft, gentle tone that feels so striking coming from a big, robust, strong man, addressing a fragile invalid. “He said fruit would do you good, so I took the great liberty of bringing some that looked particularly fine to me.”

Mrs. Hale was almost trembling with delight, so pleased at the thought. Mr. Hale’s gratitude was quieter, far deeper. “Margaret, fetch a plate, a basket, anything to set these out.”

Margaret stood up, half afraid to make a sound, half convinced he had not seen her at all, that her spot on the low stool had hidden her from his notice. She thought a sudden collision between the two of them would be unbearably awkward for both. She had no way of knowing he had been acutely, painfully aware of every inch of her presence from the second he stepped through the door.

“I can’t stay,” he said, already turning for the door. “Forgive my abrupt, rough ways. I’ll try to be gentler next time. If I see any fine, tempting fruit as I go about town, I hope you’ll let me bring you some more. Good afternoon, Mr. Hale. Goodbye, ma’am.”

He was gone. Not one word, not one single glance spared for Margaret. She truly believed he had not seen her at all. She went to fetch a plate silently, lifting the fruit out one by one with her slim, delicate fingers, her heart softening. It was so good of him, she thought—especially after what had happened between them only the day before.

“Oh, these are so delicious!” Mrs. Hale murmured weakly. “How kind of him to think of me! Margaret, love, taste these grapes! Was that not the kindest thing?”

“Yes,” Margaret said, quietly.

“Margaret,” Mrs. Hale said, a little petulant, “you will not admire a single thing Mr. Thornton does. I never saw anyone so stubbornly prejudiced.”

Mr. Hale was peeling a peach for his wife, and he cut off a small sliver for himself to taste, smiling softly. “If I held any prejudices against him, this fruit would melt every last one away. I have not tasted fruit this fine—no, not even in Hampshire—since I was a boy. Back then, I thought all fruit was good, even sour sloes and crabapples I ate out in the hedgerows. Do you remember the matted currant bushes at the corner of the west wall in our old garden, Margaret?”

Of course she remembered. She remembered every weather stain on that old stone wall, the grey and yellow lichen streaked across it like a map, the tiny crane’s-bill weeds growing in every crack. The strain of the last two days, the terror of the riot, the weight of her mother’s worsening illness, all came crashing down on her at the soft, careless mention of their old sunny Hampshire home. She jumped to her feet, dropped her sewing on the floor, and fled to her own small bedroom before anyone could see her tears.

She was just holding back her first choking sob when she spotted Dixon rummaging through her dresser drawers. “Bless me, miss, you startled me half to death!” Dixon said, jumping. “Your mother’s not worse, is she? Nothing’s gone wrong?”

“No, nothing at all,” Margaret said, wiping at her eyes. “I’m just being silly, that’s all. I wanted a glass of water. What are you looking for? I keep my muslin things in that drawer.”

Dixon kept rummaging, the sharp, sweet scent of lavender sachets wafting out of the open drawer. “I didn’t want to tell you,” Dixon said at last, turning to face her, “not when you’ve already got so much to fret over. I was going to keep it from you till nightfall, mayhap. That Higgins girl—Bessy, the one you go visit? She died this morning. Her sister Mary’s downstairs, asking a very strange favour. Bessy took a real fancy to you, and she said she wanted to be buried in something that belonged to you. I was looking for an old nightcap, nothing too fine, that we could give her.”

“Oh, let me find one,” Margaret said, even as her tears started falling again. “Poor Bessy. I never thought I would not see her again.”

“Her sister’s been begging me to ask if you’d go see her laid out,” Dixon said. “You’ve never seen a dead body, miss, you said once. I told her you wouldn’t want to go.”

“I will go down and speak to her,” Margaret said, afraid Dixon’s sharp, gruff manner might hurt the poor grieving girl. She took the soft nightcap in her hand and headed for the kitchen, where Mary sat huddled by the fire, her face puffed and red from crying. She burst into fresh sobs the second she saw Margaret.

“Oh, ma’am, she loved you so, she did! She talked of nothing but you, all the hours before she died. Said you were the prettiest, kindest thing she ever clapped eyes on. Her last words were ‘Give her my affectionate respects, and keep father from drink.’ You’ll come see her, ma’am? She’d have thought it the greatest compliment ever paid her.”

Margaret shrank back for a second, she had never seen a dead person, the thought made her skin prickle. But before Dixon could interject and offer to go in her place, she set her jaw.

“Yes,” she said firmly. “I will come to see you before tea, Mary.” And she turned and left the kitchen fast, before her own cowardice could make her change her mind.
