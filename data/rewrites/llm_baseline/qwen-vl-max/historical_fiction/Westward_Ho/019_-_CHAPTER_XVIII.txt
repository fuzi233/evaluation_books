They had slipped past the southern tip of Grenada under cover of night, and now found themselves within the enchanted circle of islands where nature had poured out all her splendor and man had heaped up all his sin. If Barbados had seemed a marvel to these Englishmen, how much more so must this sea be—calm almost without end, untouched by the hurricanes that roared far to the north. Sky, sea, and land formed one vast, shimmering rainbow, though perhaps little noticed by the practical sailors whose thoughts were fixed on Spanish gold and pearls. And certainly not by Amyas, who, accustomed to tropical landscapes, was already plotting in secret how best to rid the West Indies of the Spaniards and bring them into the realm of Queen Elizabeth. Yet even their hardened eyes could not help but feel awe at the lands they now approached—lands famed throughout Europe, yet still new, wrapped in legend, greed, and sorrow. To sail here was to enter a world nearly unknown to Englishmen, where failure meant not just death, but the agonies of the Inquisition.

Fewer than five times before had English ships dared these waters. But among those aboard were men who knew them well—men who, under Captain John Hawkins, had first attempted to trade along these coasts, only to be barred by Spanish pride. Denied access to life’s necessities, they had seized what they needed with steel and fire, then traded fairly once peace was won. The old mariners of the *Pelican* and the *Minion* were questioned all day long about every island, every cape, every fish and bird. Frank stood silent beside them, listening intently.

A deep reverence had taken hold of him—not sadness, but wonder. His face seemed to drink in the beauty around him, and often he whispered to himself, “This is the gate of heaven.” He watched the ship’s progress all day, forgetting food and rest, as each new turn revealed another miracle. Islands rose high above the water, their reflections inverted below; sandbars melted and twisted in the heat haze; yellow flowers and towering cacti, like giant torches, flickered and danced. The whole world seemed unreal—a blazing vision where nothing was stable, not even the sun, which sank through crimson mists, warped into strange ovals and pear shapes.

While Frank gazed in awe, Yeo rejoiced. To the south of the setting sun, a cluster of tall peaks rose from the sea. They were, unless his reckoning failed him, the mountains of Macanao, at the western end of Margarita—the Isle of Pearls. A name known across Mediterranean ports and German fairs, second only to the pearl-rich island in Panama’s gulf, where fifteen years earlier John Oxenham had lost his life.

The next day they sailed along the northern shore of the island, passing unseen—so far as they could tell—the Spanish fortress built at the eastern end to guard the pearl fisheries.

At last they entered a deep, quiet bay, thickly wooded to the water’s edge. In the roadstead lay a caravel and three boats moored alongside. At the sight, every man rushed to the deck, voices rising in debate over what should be done. Some urged sailing straight in, the breeze strong toward the shore, as it often was in the afternoon. But seeing waves breaking near the mouth of the bay, they decided instead to pass quietly, then send in the pinnace and boat.

Yeo suggested flying Spanish colors to avoid alarming the caravel. But Amyas refused. “It would be base,” he said, “to lie in such fashion, unless we were in mortal danger or serving some great cause of state.”

So they continued their course until the next headland blocked their view, then launched their boats: Cary in the larger, with twenty men; Amyas in the smaller, with fifteen, including John Brimblecombe, who insisted on coming in full cassock and bands, carrying an old sword passed down from his uncle.

As they neared the bay’s entrance, they saw what they expected—coral reefs, too many to navigate easily. They had to follow the edge for some distance before finding a passage. As they did, those unfamiliar with the region gazed in wonder through the clear water at living gardens beneath the surface—fields of coral, schools of fish, and the hidden realms of Nereus and Amphitrite.

Then, suddenly, below them appeared what Yeo called “a school of sharks”—some nearly as long as the boat itself. Their eyes, dark and cruel, stared upward.

“Jack,” Amyas said, sitting beside him, “look how that big one watches you. I’ll wager he’s eyeing your fat hide and thinking you’d make tender eating.”

Jack turned pale, but said nothing.

Just then, that very shark spotted a parrotfish emerging from a crack in the reef and lunged from below—followed by two others. The fish, cornered, leapt clean out of the water, nearly onto the boat. Where it had surfaced, several broad, shaggy-nosed heads crashed together within two yards of Jack, showing rows of jagged teeth, then sank back into the depths.

At that moment, Jack whispered, “In manus tuas, Domine!” and turned his gaze away, no longer wishing to see the creatures.

Once through the reef, they entered the bay under a fair wind. The caravel was now within musket range. Cary rowed straight for her, boarding before the Spaniards could reach their guns. Standing in the stern, he shouted, “Yield!”

The captain demanded, “In whose name?”

“In the name of common sense, ye dogs!” bellowed Will. “Do you not see? You are fifty against our twenty?”

He scrambled up the side. The captain fired a pistol. Cary knocked him down, unwilling to spill unnecessary blood. The crew surrendered—some falling to their knees, others diving overboard. The prize was theirs.

Meanwhile, Amyas circled behind the ship and boarded the second boat, the nearest being already secured. The Spaniards yielded without resistance, crying “Misericordia!” The negroes jumped into the water and swam ashore like sea-dogs.

But the third boat, barely an oar’s length away, began to pull off. Then came a remarkable scene. John Brimblecombe, eager to prove himself, grabbed a boat-hook and latched onto the stern, shouting, “Stay, ye Papists! Stay, Spanish dogs!”

As expected, outnumbered ten to one, he was pulled overboard, landing hard on his face in the sea. The hook remained fastened to the boat.

Now, something seized him—fear, perhaps, or his imagination running wild. He began roaring like a bull, terrified of the sharks he had just seen. In his confusion, he didn’t think to swim back to his own boat. Instead, he struck out wildly after the fleeing Spanish vessel—either hoping to catch the trailing hook, or driven by a madness of courage. No one could say. He swam forward, his cassock floating behind him like a black monk-fish, screaming and gasping, salt water filling his mouth:

“Stay, ye Spanish dogs! Help, good fellows! See you not I am a dead man? They’re nuzzling my toes! He has hold of my leg! My right thigh is bitten clean off! Oh, that I were preaching in Hartland pulpit! Yield, Papist cowards, lest I make mincemeat of you! Take me aboard! Yield, I say, or my blood be on your heads! I am no Jonah—should he swallow me, he will never cast me up again! Better to fall into the hands of men than into the hands of devils with three rows of teeth each. In manus tuas. Orate pro anima—!”

And so on, more frantic than Panurge ever was in his famous bout of seasickness. The English, fearing he would be torn apart by sharks or beaten by oars, opened fire. The Spaniards, seeing this, jumped overboard. Jack scrambled back into the boat, drew his sword with one hand, wiped water from his eyes with the other, and slashed wildly through the air, yelling, “Yield, idolaters! Yield, Spanish dogs!”

Eventually, regaining his senses and finding no one left to fight, he collapsed in the stern, panting, and began removing his hose.

Amyas, thinking he had been overcome by sunstroke or shock, ordered the boat pulled alongside and asked, “What in heaven’s name are you doing with your breeches?”

Jack, amid laughter that shook the boat, swore his right thigh had been bitten clean through to the bone—and that he felt blood soaking his hose. He nearly fainted from imagined loss of blood, until his friends, after much argument and irritation on his part, convinced him he was unharmed.

They then searched the prize. It carried hides and salt pork—but also, in the captain’s cabin and the boat Brimblecombe had boarded, neat bundles of leaves concealing large pearls. Though somewhat brown (the Spaniards had burned the shells to open them quickly, spoiling the color), they were fine pearls nonetheless. The value was uncertain, but the crew returned satisfied—though one mischievous soul set the ship ablaze. Laden with hides, it burned fiercely.

Amyas was furious at the wanton destruction—Drake himself had never done such a thing. But Cary chuckled. “Ah,” he said, “we’re ‘Lutheran devils,’ you know. So we’re bound to vanish with an evil stench, like other fiends.”

No sooner was Amyas back aboard than he turned on Brimblecombe. “You ought to show more backbone,” he said. “Roaring before you’re hurt, wasting breath—what good does that do? If you wish the crew to heed your words, keep your voice for when it matters.”

Frank, overhearing, stepped in. “Leave him to me,” he said. “Come hither, thou recreant Jack, thou lily-livered Jack, thou hysterical Jack. Tell me—have you read Plato’s Dialogues and Aristotle’s Logic?”

“Yes,” Jack replied humbly.

“Then let us reason like ancient sages. Does the greater contain the less?”

“Yes, sure.”

“And that which exceeds a part, contains that part?”

“Yes, sure.”

“Then is not a priest greater than a layman?”

“Of course.”

“So a priest contains a man, and is a man, and more—his priesthood?”

“I suppose so.”

“Then if a priest shows himself no man, does he not show himself all the more no priest?”

“Master Frank,” Jack said, “you may be right in logic, but sharks don’t understand logic.”

“Nay, but Jack,” Frank replied, “is it a man’s duty to howl like a pig in a gate because he fears something that isn’t there?”

Jack had no answer.

“And if it *were* there, would it not be a brave man’s duty to keep his mouth shut—to keep salt water out, and not choke while being eaten?”

“Oh,” Jack sighed, “that’s all very well. But you know as well as I that it wasn’t the Spaniards I feared. They were Heaven’s work, and I knew how to deal with them. But those fiends’ spawn of sharks—when I saw one take the fish alongside, it upset me clean. That’s the end of it!”

“Ah, Jack, Jack,” said Frank, “behold how one sin breeds another! You were a coward, and now you’ve become a Manichee—blaming an evil creator for creatures made for good ends. Sharks exist to devour useless carcasses like yours. And as a brother of the Rose, you were sworn to leap joyfully into any beast’s mouth for the sake of your vow.”

“Ay, likely,” Jack muttered, “if Mistress Rose were in its stomach. But I wanted to fight Spaniards, not be shark-bitten.”

“Jack,” Frank said, “your answer reeks of self-will. If destiny ordains you to suffer shame or torment—shark-bite, flea-bite, or worse—for the sake of the Brotherhood, you must obey. Not choose your death, as Romans do—that is mere self-murder. We therefore declare you a scandal, a rotten branch, and hereby cut you off.”

“Nay, Master Frank,” Jack protested, “how long have you been Bishop of Exeter?”

Frank smiled. “Your wit is blinded by fear, which is a short madness, raising phantoms—sharks and Spaniards alike. Let the Bishop deal with your Manicheeism. For your howling, offensive even in a cur, but disgraceful in a preacher and brother of the Rose—we hereby strip you of your office as chaplain. Unless within seven days you perform a deed worthy of the Seven Champions, or Ruggiero and Orlando, you shall lose sword and dagger. Henceforth, carry no iron but enough to mend your pen.”

“And now, Jack,” Amyas said, “let me tell you something. No wonder young men refuse to listen to the Gospel, when preached by men they must look down upon—soft-handed, unable to dig, ashamed to beg. Even if Popish priests are vowed not to be men, our English clergy must be both good Christians and good men, good gentlemen, good Englishmen. To show the white feather in need is to undo a lifetime of preaching in a single moment.”

“I tell you,” Amyas continued, “had I not thought you a different sort, I would never have entrusted you with the immortal souls of a hundred brave lads.”

Together, they bombarded him with words—starboard and larboard—until Jack clapped his hands to his ears and fled, leaving Frank laughing so hard that Amyas, though stern, was glad the incident had occurred, for it brought a rare smile to Frank’s solemn face.

The next day was Sunday. After service—Jack reluctant to read, and refusing entirely to preach—they gathered to hear the articles of their agreement, as custom dictated. Then, seeing a sloping beach with a clear stream flowing into the sea, they agreed to land, wash clothes, and refill their water casks—scarce since Barbados.

Jack insisted on going, taking sword and arquebuse. He had dreamed the night before that Spaniards attacked him, and believed it would come true. “Besides,” he said, “I don’t care if I die. Better to die than be mocked, ridiculed, badgered with Ramus and Plato, accused of being a Manichee or a Turk or Jew. I’ll go like a man desperate.”

They landed, Amyas giving strict orders not to fire weapons—fearing alarm. On shore, they washed clothes, stretched legs, and admired the beauty of the place. Then they set up their seine.

“We caught many strange fish,” says the chronicler, “and among them, a sea-cow seven feet long, covered in limpets and barnacles, like driftwood. A gentle creature, yet pious: she guards corpses until decay or burial. The Indians call her *manati*. She carries her young under her arm, suckles it like a woman, and when wounded, cries aloud with a human voice. At certain times, she sings sweetly—perhaps the melody Mr. Frank heard and mistook for Sirens and Tritons. I neither affirm nor deny this, for I have seen wonders enough to know that to reject anything for strangeness is folly. Also, one man brought in two great black birds—like capons, but larger than eagles—the Spaniards call *curassos*. With the sea-cow, they gave us excellent fare—roast and boiled. The cow was delicious, tender and rich, like a four-months-old calf.”

Afterward, they filled casks and barrels, placing the boat near the stream’s outflow. Lucky for them, they were all close together—just as well, for moments later, a cry rang out: “The enemy!”

From the woods, not twenty yards from Jack, emerged fifty soldiers, followed by negroes, led by an officer on horseback, plume in hat, sword drawn.

“Stand, for your lives!” shouted Amyas—just in time. Ten minutes were wasted getting men into order.

But Jack, seeing the Spaniards, as if expecting them, closed his Bible, marked the page, placed it down, picked up his arquebuse, and charged like a mad dog. He shot the captain through the chest, killed him instantly, then hurled the arquebuse at the next man and fell upon them with his sword, swinging like a Colbrand. He broke into their ranks, slashing right and left. The Spaniards, believing him a demon or Luther himself risen, retreated in panic, firing wildly—so badly that Jack received only a glancing wound in the thigh, which would leave him lame for weeks.

Yet he pressed forward. The rest joined him, forty strong, well armed. The Spaniards fled as fast as they came.

Cary murmured, “The dogs tasted the parson. They won’t wait for the clerk and congregation.”

“Come back, Jack! Are you mad?” Amyas shouted.

But Jack, silent all this time, pursued them fiercely—until, striking at an arquebusier, he tripped on a root, fell, and struck his head on the ground, knocking out the last of his breath. He lay still.

Amyas, seeing the Spaniards gone, did not pursue. He lifted Jack, who blinked and cried, “Glory be! Glory be! How many have I killed?”

“Nineteen, at least,” said Cary, “and seven with one backstroke.” He showed Jack the dead captain and two wounded arquebusiers—one the man who had tripped him—along with others limping away.

“There!” said Jack, catching his breath. “Will you laugh at me anymore, Mr. Cary? Say I can’t fight, because I’m a poor parson’s son?”

Cary took his hand and apologized for his mockery. “Today, you fought better than any of us.”

Jack, never holding grudges, laughed in return. “Oh, Mr. Cary, we’ve known your ways since you used to put drumble-drones in my desk at Bideford school.”

They returned to the boats, praising God for their deliverance. The crew cheered Jack, who grew faint—he had bled heavily, though unaware. He made as little of his real wound as he had exaggerated the imaginary one the day before.

That evening, Frank asked him, “How did you show such calm courage in such sudden danger?”
