IX. The Doctor

Carmilla refused to allow anyone to sleep in her room, so my father arranged for a servant to stay just outside the door—enough to prevent any midnight escape without alerting us. That night passed uneventfully. But early the next morning, the doctor arrived—someone my father had summoned in secret, without telling me a word.

Madame and I went together to the library, where the small, solemn physician with white hair and spectacles waited, as I had described him before. I told him everything—the dreams, the strange sensations, the paralysis, the fear that gripped me each time I closed my eyes. As I spoke, his expression grew darker, more intense. We stood facing one another in the deep window recess, the silence between us thickening with dread.

When I finished, he leaned back against the wall, his eyes fixed on me—not with pity, but with something deeper: recognition. A flicker of horror beneath careful composure. After a long pause, he turned to Madame and asked if he might speak with my father.

My father was called in, smiling at first. “I suppose you’re about to tell me I’ve made a fool of myself by bringing you here,” he said lightly. “I hope I have.”

But the smile vanished when the doctor motioned him forward. They stepped into the same recess, speaking in hushed tones, their voices swallowed by the heavy stone and glass. I stood with Madame at the far end of the vast room, breathless with curiosity, unable to hear a word. Only glimpses—my father’s shoulder, an arm, the edge of a foot—were visible through the shadowed archway. The conversation seemed urgent, grave.

After some time, my father emerged, pale and tense. He looked at me, then at the doctor. “Laura, dear,” he said, “come here for a moment. Madame, we won’t need you for now.” His voice was quiet, strained.

I approached, suddenly chilled despite my weakness. I didn’t feel sick—only drained, restless. But the doctor’s presence, the way my father looked at me… it sent a shiver down my spine.

He held out his hand, but kept his gaze on the doctor. “It’s very odd,” he said. “I don’t understand it fully. Laura, come here. Now listen carefully to Doctor Spielsberg—and try to stay calm.”

The doctor turned to me. “You mentioned a sensation—like two needles piercing your skin—near your neck, during your first nightmare. Is there any soreness there now?”

“None,” I replied.

“Can you point to exactly where you felt it?”

“Just below my throat—here,” I said, touching the spot under my collar.

He nodded slowly. “Then let me see it. It’s necessary. Just lower your dress slightly—only an inch or two.”

I hesitated, then obeyed. The fabric slipped down just enough to expose the pale skin beneath.

“God bless me,” my father whispered, stepping back. His face had gone ashen.

“You see it now,” the doctor said, voice low but triumphant. “A small blue mark—about the size of your little finger’s tip.”

“What is it?” I gasped, my heart pounding.

“Nothing, my dear,” he said gently. “Just a mark. But it tells us what we need to know.”

He turned to my father. “Now—what shall we do?”

“Is there danger?” I asked, trembling.

“I trust not,” the doctor replied. “If proper care is taken, you may recover quickly. This is the spot where you felt the strangulation? The cold current?”

“Yes,” I whispered.

“And this same place—was it the center of that strange thrill?”

“It may have been… yes.”

“Ay,” the doctor murmured, turning again to my father. “We must act. And soon.”

My father nodded gravely. “Shall I speak to Madame?”

“Certainly.”

He called her over. “I find your young friend unwell,” he said. “Not dangerously, I hope—but we must take precautions. For now, Madame, I ask you to ensure Miss Laura is never alone. Not for a single moment. It is essential.”

“We will do as you wish,” Madame promised eagerly.

“And you, Laura,” my father added, “you’ll follow the doctor’s instructions, won’t you?”

“I will,” I said, though I still did not understand.

The doctor then said, “I would like to examine another patient—our guest. Her symptoms are similar, though milder. I’ll be passing through tonight—I’ll stop for supper and see her.”

“Very well,” my father said. “Come at seven.”

They repeated their warnings, then left. I watched them walk together across the grassy platform before the castle, pacing slowly, deeply absorbed. Then the doctor mounted his horse and rode eastward into the forest.

At nearly the same moment, the man from Dranfield arrived with the mail. My father took the letters, opened one, and read it silently.

Half an hour later, he returned. “This letter was delayed,” he said. “It’s from General Spielsdorf. He might have come yesterday—or tomorrow. Or he could be here today.”

He handed me the letter, but his expression was grim. Not joyful, not even hopeful. He looked as if he wished the General were buried beneath the Red Sea.

“Papa,” I said softly, laying my hand on his arm. “What’s wrong?”

He smoothed my hair, his touch gentle. “Perhaps I’ll tell you soon.”

“Does the doctor think I’m very ill?”

“No, dear,” he said, almost sharply. “He thinks—if we act wisely—you’ll begin to recover within days.”

“But why does he want me watched so closely?”

“Don’t press me,” he said, irritation flashing in his eyes. “You’ll know soon enough. In the meantime—don’t trouble yourself.”

He turned and left. But he returned moments later. “I’m going to Karnstein,” he said. “Carriage ready at twelve. You and Madame will come with me. I have business with the priest near the ruins. Carmilla hasn’t seen them yet—she can join us later with Mademoiselle. She’ll bring supplies for a picnic—something to lay out in the old castle.”

At noon, we set off. Past the drawbridge, along the steep Gothic bridge, westward toward the abandoned village and the crumbling fortress of Karnstein. No road could be more beautiful—winding through hills and hollows draped in wild woods, untouched by human design. The trees rose in natural disorder, the paths twisted around cliffs and valleys, forming a landscape both ancient and alive.

Then, rounding a bend, we saw him—General Spielsdorf—riding toward us, his servant following behind. His luggage came in a hired wagon. He dismounted as we stopped, greeted us warmly, and with no hesitation accepted the empty seat in our carriage. His horse and servant would go on ahead to the castle.

As we drove onward, the wind tugged at the curtains, and the silence between us grew heavier. I stared out at the ruins rising above the trees—broken towers, shattered walls—where shadows clung like memories. And somewhere, beneath the roots of the ancient oaks, something stirred. Something that had once slept. And now, perhaps, remembered.
