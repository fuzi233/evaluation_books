Major Arthur Pendennis finally rolled into Fairoaks, emerging from a night in the mail-coach that could only be described as a trial by torture. He had been squeezed into a corner by a burly passenger inflated by layers of greatcoats, who kept him awake with snoring loud enough to crack glass. Opposite sat a widow who had sealed the carriage against the fresh air, filling the space instead with the potent fumes of Jamaica rum and water, which she sipped perpetually from a flask in her reticule. Every attempt at rest was shattered by the shrill blast of a turnpike horn, the squashing of his neighbor, or the Widow's feet brushing against his toes. It was a chaotic symphony of discomfort that defined travel in the "jolly old times"—a life now vanished, surviving only in nostalgic memory. Yet, despite the eight-hour grind at eight miles per hour, the hard seats, the grumbling coachmen, and the gouty tendencies of the era, everyone managed to remain cheerful.

Among the chaos were two schoolboys heading home for summer break, sitting beside the Major's servant on the roof. At the inn in Bagshot, they devoured a feast of ham, eggs, pies, pickles, tea, coffee, and boiled beef, leaving the Major, sipping weak tea with a yellow complexion and a bristly beard, looking on with tender envy. He imagined Lord Steyne's lavish dinner happening simultaneously, contrasting sharply with his own sparse fare. However, the boys' innocent enthusiasm charmed the Major, especially when he learned one was a lord's son. This young nobleman slept soundly through the noise and crowding, waking up fresh as paint, while the Major descended at Fairoaks Lodge with a rheumatic groan, a wig out of curl, and a face full of suffering.

Helen watched him from her window but held her ground. She knew her brother-in-law hated surprises; he needed preparation, specifically regarding his dentures. Having once stolen the Major's false teeth from his dressing table as a boy, Pen knew exactly how devastating their absence could be. Similarly, Morgan, the servant, treated the Major's wigs with mysterious reverence, curling them in secret corners. Without his hairpiece, the Major refused to show his face to anyone. Once armed with his teeth and wig, the Major descended into the drawing-room, attempting a grave, majestic demeanor suitable for a man of business and fashion.

Inside, however, he found only Helen and little Laura, who was sewing. The Major offered Laura a stiff salute with a single forefinger. She trembled, accepted the gesture, and fled the room. The Major didn't care for her presence anyway, harboring private reasons for his disapproval that would be revealed later. Laura, meanwhile, wandered the grounds searching for Pen, eventually finding him in the orchard deep in conversation with Mr. Smirke.

When Laura sang out, "Uncle Arthur's come!" Pen reacted with sudden ferocity, gripping her small hand tightly. He glanced at Smirke with a look that screamed, *I am ready for anyone*, causing the tutor to sigh gently and step aside. "Lead on, Laura," Pen commanded, masking his internal panic with a half-fierce, half-comic laugh. He was steeling himself for the inevitable confrontation.

Over the past two days, Pen had poured his heart out to Smirke about Miss Emily Fotheringay. With his fiery eloquence, poetic metaphors, and unwavering optimism, he had convinced the tutor that marrying an actress was not only feasible but prudent. Pen had even argued with his own mother, using a logic so absurdly confident it bordered on madness. To him, waiting for wealth or status was cowardice; sharing fate with a beloved woman was the only honorable path. He painted a picture of a perfect union where Emily would be a paragon of virtue, sensitivity, and grace, while dismissing concerns about her background as trivial compared to the joy of love.

That night, unable to sleep, Pen tossed and turned, composing verses inspired by Hamlet, Byron, and the loves of angels. His mother, Helen, crept in softly, finding him surrounded by scattered papers. Seeing his wild-eyed passion, she simply took his hot hand and whispered, "Arthur, why don't you go to sleep?" Overwhelmed, Pen threw his arms around her, declaring, "O mother! I love her, I love her!"

The next morning, when the fully groomed Major presented himself to Helen, he discovered she was far more distressed than he anticipated. Not only did she oppose the marriage, but she feared the blow it would deal to her son's happiness. She pleaded with him to be gentle, citing Pen's high spirit and recent indignation toward Dr. Portman.

"Be very cautious," Helen urged, placing her hand on his sleeve.

The Major, playing the role of the experienced patriarch, kissed her hand gallantly. "Confess," he teased, "that you are trying to clear your conscience by letting the boy have his way." Helen blushed, admitting she worried about his unhappiness.

"To contradict him or let him have his wish?" the Major asked, secretly relieved. "I'm damned if he shall."

"But the match is foolish and cruel," Helen countered, fearing the agony of rejection.

"The issue shan't be marriage, my dear sister," the Major declared firmly. "We aren't going to have a Pendennis, the head of the house, marry a strolling mountebank from a booth. No, no, we won't marry into Greenwich Fair, ma'am."

"If it's broken off suddenly..." Helen warned, trembling at the thought of Pen's volatile temper.

"My dear madam," the Major sympathized, "Arthur will suffer terribly before recovering. But is he the only one who has suffered?"

"No," Helen admitted, recalling her own youthful heartbreak involving a wealthy suitor who left for India.

"I, myself," the Major recounted, "had a similar disappointment. A young woman with fifteen thousand pounds... I was a penniless lieutenant. We exchanged letters and locks of hair, suffered greatly, but recovered. She is now a baronet's wife with thirteen children." He paused, adjusting his wig. "Look at my own brother. He nearly married Miss Balls, daughter of a prominent apothecary, but difficulties arose. I am sure he regrets nothing; it gave him this hand," he said, pressing Helen's fingers again.

"These marriages between different ranks and ages are tragic," Helen murmured, thinking of Laura's father.

"Injudicious," the Major cut in. "Nothing pains a man more than marrying someone vastly older or lower in station. Imagine your house filled with vulgar relatives, or a wife who drops her 'h's! You'll be cut out of society entirely. I've seen men destroyed by the vulgarity of their wives' connections. Take Lady Snapperton, who refused to receive Lady Brouncker because she was a druggist's daughter. Good God, what a trifling pain a temporary separation is compared to the lifelong torment of living among low people!"

"And this woman is ten years older than Arthur," Helen noted, suppressing a laugh despite her distress.

"What happens in such cases?" the Major demanded. "In the highest society, misery is the result. Look at Lord Clodworthy with his mother-like wife, or Lord Willowbank, who cuts down his wife repeatedly for a dancer. You are romantic and sentimental, but I am of the world. Marry this woman! Marry an actress of thirty at eighteen? Bah! I'd sooner he married the cook."

As Helen sighed, lamenting the evils of premature engagement for the third time, the Major realized he needed to address the elephant in the room. "Since you seem so oppressed by the idea of long engagements and unequal matches, and since the circumstances we are discussing require explanation, it would be best to clarify who little Laura is, who has appeared before us several times. We shall reserve that discussion for another chapter."
