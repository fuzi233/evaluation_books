Elinor discovered anew the profound distinction between anticipating a misfortune, however certain reason dictates it must be, and the crushing weight of absolute certainty. Until this moment, despite her better judgment, she had secretly harbored a hope whenever Edward remained unmarried—that fate might intervene to spare him the union with Lucy. She had convinced herself that some personal resolve, the intervention of friends, or a more advantageous prospect for the lady would inevitably emerge to safeguard everyone's happiness. But now, the marriage was done. And she bitterly reproached her own heart for the lingering flattery that had so acerbated the sting of this news.

It startled her initially that he had wed so swiftly, seemingly before he could take holy orders and thus possess the living. Yet, upon reflection, she realized how easily Lucy, driven by her own resourcefulness and eagerness to secure him, might have overlooked everything save the danger of delay. They were married in London and were now hastening toward her uncle's estate. Elinor tried to imagine Edward's feelings as he approached Barton, spotted her mother's servant, and received Lucy's message. Soon, she presumed, they would be settled at Delaford. The name sent a shiver through her; it was a place bound to her by deep ties, one she longed to know intimately yet desperately wished to avoid.

In her mind's eye, she pictured them in their new parsonage. She saw Lucy as the bustling, scheming manager, balancing a desire for fashionable appearance with extreme frugality, yet mortified if suspected of her penny-pinching ways. She envisioned Lucy pursuing her own interests in every thought, courting the favor of Colonel Brandon, Mrs. Jennings, and every wealthy acquaintance. As for Edward, she could discern nothing clearly, nor did she wish to. Whether he appeared happy or wretched mattered not; she turned her gaze away from every image of him.

She consoled herself with the belief that someone among their London connections would soon write to announce the event and provide details. Day after day passed, bringing no letter, no news. Though she knew no specific party was at fault, she found fault with every absent friend, labeling them thoughtless or indolent. "When do you write to Colonel Brandon, ma'am?" the question sprang from her own impatience, needing something tangible to occupy her thoughts. "I wrote to him last week, my dear, and I expect to see him rather than hear from him," her mother replied warmly. "I urged him to come here; I shall not be surprised if he walks in today or tomorrow." This offered a sliver of comfort—a person to anticipate. Colonel Brandon *must* possess information.

Scarcely had she resolved this when a figure on horseback caught her eye at the window. He halted at the gate. It was a gentleman, unmistakably Colonel Brandon. Her heart leaped; now she could learn the truth. But it was not him; neither his bearing nor his stature matched. Could it possibly be Edward? She glanced again. He had just dismounted. There was no mistaking it—it was Edward. She recoiled and sat down heavily. "He has come from Mr. Pratt's specifically to visit us," she whispered to herself. "I will remain calm; I will master myself."

Instantly, she sensed the others shared her confusion. She watched her mother and Marianne flush, exchange glances, and murmur softly. Oh, how she wished to speak, to assure them that no coldness or slight would mar their welcome to him—but her tongue failed her, leaving the matter entirely to their discretion. Silence hung heavy in the room as they awaited his arrival. Footsteps crunched on the gravel path; moments later, he stood before them.

His expression was not joyful, certainly not for Elinor. His face was pale with agitation, his demeanor suggesting fear of rejection and a consciousness that he deserved none. Mrs. Dashwood, trusting implicitly in her daughter's guidance, greeted him with a smile of forced cheerfulness, offering her hand and wishing him joy. Edward blushed deeply and stammered an unintelligible response. Elinor's lips had moved in unison with her mother's, and once the gesture was complete, she regretted not shaking his hand herself. Instead, she sat down, attempting to maintain an open countenance while discussing the weather. Marianne had withdrawn as far as possible to hide her distress, while Margaret, sensing partial knowledge but not the full picture, chose dignity, seating herself as distant as possible and maintaining a rigid silence.

When Elinor finally stopped praising the dryness of the season, an oppressive silence descended. Mrs. Dashwood broke it, hoping he had left Mrs. Ferrars well. In a hurried tone, he affirmed this. Another pause stretched out. Determined to act, though dreading the sound of her own voice, Elinor asked, "Is Mrs. Ferrars at Longstaple?"

"At Longstaple!" he exclaimed, surprised. "No, my mother is in town."

"I meant," Elinor continued, picking up some embroidery from the table, "to inquire for Mrs. *Edward* Ferrars." She dared not lift her eyes, yet both her mother and Marianne turned their gazes sharply upon him. He flushed, looking perplexed and doubtful. After a hesitant moment, he said, "Perhaps you mean... my brother? You mean Mrs.—Mrs. *Robert* Ferrars."

"Mrs. Robert Ferrars!" cried Marianne and her mother in tones of sheer astonishment. Even Elinor, unable to speak, stared at him with impatient wonder. Rising abruptly, he walked to the window, unsure of his next move. Picking up a pair of scissors lying nearby, he began snipping the sheath apart as he spoke in a frantic voice, "Perhaps you do not know; you may not have heard that my brother is lately married—to the youngest—to Miss Lucy Steele."

The words were echoed by everyone except Elinor. While the others reacted with shock, she sat with her head bowed over her work, trembling in an agony of emotion that left her unsure of her surroundings. "Yes," he added, "they were married last week and are now at Dawlish."

Elinor could endure no more. She nearly fled the room. Once the door clicked shut behind her, she collapsed into tears of pure joy, believing they would never cease. Edward, who had been looking anywhere but at her, noticed her rush away. Perhaps he saw, or even heard, her emotion; for immediately afterward, he fell into a deep reverie. No remark, no inquiry, no affectionate plea from Mrs. Dashwood could pierce his trance. Finally, without a word, he left the room and walked out toward the village, leaving the others in a state of bewildered astonishment at the sudden, miraculous turn of events—a confusion they could only attempt to soothe with wild conjecture.
