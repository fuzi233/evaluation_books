That winter, as the fields lay iron-hard and the Miskatonic ran with a thin skin of ice that crackled like old parchment when the wind worried it, there came an event no less strange than Wilbur Whateley’s first venture beyond the Dunwich hills. He had written—clumsy, blotched scrawls in an uncouth hand that seemed to grind against the very rules of spelling—to places whose names were as far away as legends: the Widener Library at Harvard, the Bibliothèque Nationale in Paris, the British Museum, the University of Buenos Aires, and, nearer than any of them and therefore most intolerable to be denied, the Library of Miskatonic University at Arkham. From each came the same polite refusal, the same tidy language of regulations and custody, as bloodless as boiled starch. A man who had spent his life in a valley where one barters eggs for nails and learns history by what grandmothers mutter over bread dough does not take kindly to a “policy.”

So at last he went in person.

He arrived in Arkham like some hungry winter rumor given legs—shabby, dirty, bearded, and raw with dialect; almost eight feet of dark, goatish grotesquerie, with a cheap new valise from Osborn’s general store banging at his knee. Even in a town that had known river fogs thick enough to muffle church bells, he did not belong. The street-sounds rose and fell around him with an urban clangor—iron rims on cobbles, a distant motor’s bark, the thin impatient bleat of a trolley bell—noises that do not soothe the mind the way a barn’s quiet does, with its slow chew of cattle and the hush of stored hay. Yet he paid them no heed. He had never seen a city before, but he moved through it with one purpose, as if dragged by a string tied to the locked cabinets of the college library.

At the university grounds he passed a great white-fanged watchdog that barked with unnatural fury and enmity. The chain snapped taut; the dog tugged and yelped as though it had scented something that did not belong to any ordinary stable, slaughterhouse, or street. Wilbur strode by without so much as a flinch, and the sound of that yelping pursued him like a curse into the quiet corridors where footsteps are softened by stone and habit.

He carried with him what was priceless to him—a copy of Dr. Dee’s English version bequeathed by his grandfather, precious and imperfect, damaged as though some earlier hand had worried certain pages the way rats worry a sack of grain. He had come because that book lacked what he needed. There was, he knew, a passage that would have fallen on the seven hundred and fifty-first page of his defective volume, and he could not be made to accept the notion that an absence on paper must be accepted as an absence in the world.

The dread volume he sought was kept under lock and key at Miskatonic: the hideous *Necronomicon* of the mad Arab Alhazred in Olaus Wormius’ Latin version, printed in Spain in the seventeenth century—an old thing, old as pickled fruit forgotten at the back of a cellar shelf, old as smoke ingrained into rafters. When access was granted under watchful procedure, Wilbur sat down at a table with the stance of a man at a workbench. There was no reverence in him, only appetite and method.

The librarian who received him was Henry Armitage—erudite, careful, the sort of mind that labels and preserves and believes that knowledge, like food, must be handled cleanly lest it poison. He had once called at the Whateley farm and had not forgotten the smell of that place: not manure, not sour milk, not the wholesome funk of pigs, but something else—something that clung to the throat and made breathing feel like swallowing.

Now, as he watched Wilbur open the Dee copy and lay it beside the Latin tome, Armitage plied him with questions as politeness requires, and because he feared what silence might allow. Wilbur, for all his ugliness and his odd, resonant speech that hinted at sound-making organs not arranged like other men’s, could not refrain from telling the truth of his purpose. He was hunting a formula—an incantation—containing the name *Yog-Sothoth*. He spoke of discrepancies, duplications, ambiguities; of how the wording shifted the way a recipe turns treacherous when a single herb is misnamed or a measure misread. It puzzled him, he admitted, to find the matter of determination far from easy.

He began to collate. The pages rustled—dry, crisp, like the flake of aged pepper-dried skin—and he copied with fierce concentration, as if transcribing not words but a method, a set of instructions that must be exact to the grain. In the library’s stillness the scratching of his pen sounded too loud, a tiny industrial rasp that seemed to scrape the air. Outside, a wind moved along the eaves and through bare branches with a thin, keening voice, and those two soundscapes—ink and winter—layered themselves uneasily together.

When Wilbur finally chose a formula and wrote it down, Dr. Armitage looked involuntarily over his shoulder at the open pages. The left-hand page, in Latin, bore matter that translated itself into the mind like rot revealing itself when you lift a lid.

Nor is it to be thought—ran the meaning as Armitage’s learning gave it voice—that man is either the oldest or the last of earth’s masters, or that the common bulk of life and substance walks alone. The Old Ones were, the Old Ones are, and the Old Ones shall be. Not in the spaces we know, but between them. They walk serene and primal, undimensioned and to us unseen. *Yog-Sothoth* knows the gate. *Yog-Sothoth* is the gate. *Yog-Sothoth* is the key and guardian of the gate. Past, present, future, all are one in *Yog-Sothoth*. He knows where the Old Ones broke through of old, and where They shall break through again.

The text went on, and with each line the library’s safe domestic order—the well-oiled ritual of stamped cards, shelved folios, whispered requests—seemed less solid. It spoke of earth’s fields being trod by Things unseen, and of men knowing Them not by sight but by smell. That detail struck Armitage with a physical force, for smell is an argument the body makes without asking the brain’s permission. In winter, in kitchens, smells are comfort: broth bones simmering until they give up their sweetness, onions browning in fat, yeast breathing into dough. But there are other odors—stale, animal, corrupt—whose very presence feels like a violation.

By Their smell can men sometimes know Them near, the passage implied, though of Their semblance no man can know—saving only in the features of those They have begotten on mankind. Many sorts, it said, from likeness near man’s truest image to a shape without sight or substance. It spoke of lonely places where Words had been spoken and Rites howled through at their Seasons; of wind that gibbers with Their voices and earth that mutters with Their consciousness. It spoke, too, of forest bent and city crushed by an unseen hand; of Kadath in cold waste, of sunken isles, of sealed towers garlanded with seaweed and barnacles; of Great Cthulhu as Their cousin who can spy Them only dimly; and then the obscene cry—*Iä Shub-Niggurath!*—like a name that should never have been fitted to human tongues.

And at last: As a foulness shall ye know Them. Their hand is at your throats, yet ye see Them not; and Their habitation is even one with your guarded threshold. *Yog-Sothoth* is the key to the gate, whereby the spheres meet. Man rules now where They ruled once; They shall soon rule where man rules now. After summer is winter, and after winter summer. They wait patient and potent, for here shall They reign again.

It was the seasonal phrasing—the circular inevitability of it—that made Armitage’s unease deepen into something like dread. After winter summer; after summer winter. Such words belonged to farmers and elders, to the old people who can read a year in the taste of water and the sound of wind in the pines. Here, that homely cycle was yoked to an alien patience.

Armitage associated what he read with what he had heard of Dunwich and its brooding presences, and of Wilbur Whateley’s dim hideous aura that stretched from dubious birth to a cloud of probable matricide. A wave of fright came over him as tangible as a draft of tomb-cold damp. The bent, goatish giant at the table seemed less like an unwashed rustic and more like an emissary—spawn of another planet or dimension, only partly of mankind, linked to black gulfs of entity that stretched beyond the neat compartments of force and matter, space and time.

Presently Wilbur raised his head. When he spoke, his voice carried a resonance that did not sit properly in a human chest; it seemed to thrum, as though the air itself were being used like an instrument.

“Mr. Armitage,” he said, “I calc’late I’ve got to take that book home. They’s things in it I’ve got to try under sarten conditions that I can’t git here, an’ it ’ud be a mortal sin to let a red-tape rule hold me up. Let me take it along, sir, an’ I’ll swar they wun’t nobody know the difference. I dun’t need to tell ye I’ll take good keer of it. It wa’n’t me that put this Dee copy in the shape it is….”

He stopped when he saw denial harden on the librarian’s face. In Wilbur’s features—those goatish planes and slants—something crafty tightened, the way a butcher’s hand might tighten when a bargain fails. Armitage, half ready to say that Wilbur could make a copy of what he needed, felt the possible consequences crowd into his mind and checked himself.
