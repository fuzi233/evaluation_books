When Romola reached the entrance of San Marco, a friar stood waiting, as if expecting her. Monna Brigida withdrew into the adjacent church, and Romola was led to the door of the chapter-house in the outer cloister—where the sick brother had been brought. Women were not permitted beyond this threshold. As the door opened, the dim light from outside mingled with the soft glow of two candles placed behind a low bed. There, propped up nearly upright, lay Fra Luca. His face was thin and drawn, his golden hair shaved in the monk’s tonsure, and his deep-set hazel eyes fixed upon a small crucifix held before him. In the quiet stillness, Romola felt a sudden awareness: another monk stood beside the bed, hooded and silent, moving toward the door as she entered. Behind them, on the frescoed wall, loomed the pale figure of Christ on the cross, flanked by sorrowful faces that seemed to watch in silence.

Then, their eyes met—Fra Luca’s rising from the crucifix to meet hers. And in that instant, recognition struck Romola like a wound. This frail, robed figure was Dino—her brother, once so fair and young. “Dino!” she whispered, the word escaping her lips like a cry of pain. Yet she did not move forward. She remained standing, rigid, at arm’s length. A deep repulsion rose within her—a revulsion not just for the monkish garb, but for what it symbolized: the abandonment of duty, the surrender to a faith that called betrayal piety. Her father, a man of proud honesty and simple virtue, had raised her apart from the Church, treating its claims as irrelevant to reason and conscience. To her, religion belonged to the noisy, mixed world they had always avoided. And so, her brother’s choice—this flight into monastic life—had long been a source of cold contempt.

Yet now, though she stood aloof, her gaze searched his face with a hidden longing. Was there any trace of the boy she once knew? Any echo of warmth or regret?

But there was none. He looked at her—not with affection, but with the distant gaze of one who sees only shadows from beyond the veil. “My sister,” he murmured, voice weak yet clear, “it is well you have come. I have a message for you—and time is short.”

Romola took a step closer. Perhaps, she thought, he would speak of their father. Perhaps he would express remorse, beg forgiveness. She had no hope of erasing the years of desertion—but if he acknowledged the wrong, if he spoke of their father’s suffering, then pity might soften her heart. She imagined him asking about his father’s blindness, how he endured the darkness, what life was like without him. She waited for words of tenderness.

Instead, Fra Luca said, “Romola, I have seen a vision. Three times in the past two months—each clearer than the last. I came from Fiesole because I believed it was a message from heaven. And now I know my breath is spared only to deliver it—to you.”

His breathing faltered, cutting off the sentence. Romola’s heart chilled. A *vision*—the same kind her father had mocked so bitterly. Anger surged within her. “Dino,” she said sharply, “I thought you had something to say to my father. You left him when he lost his sight—you made his life lonely and empty. Have you never felt guilt? Never regretted it? What kind of religion makes visions more important than human duty?”

The monk turned his eyes slowly toward her. For a moment, he seemed to weigh whether to answer. Then, softly, almost without emotion, as if speaking through a dying shell, he said: “No. I have never repented fleeing from the poison of sin that choked me. It was thick around me—hot, suffocating. My father could not hear the voice that called me day and night. He did not see the demons trying to pull me back. He lived among sin and suffering, yet denied their reality—as if picking gems in a mine while a plague raged above. I warned him, but he scorned me. I told him the studies he wanted me to pursue—history, poetry—were either childish trifles or else poisoned by worldly ambition. They fed on lust, on pride. All the beauty he admired was shaped by desires that corrupted the soul.”

Romola could not remain silent. “But has not my father lived a pure and noble life? He sought no honors, he lied to no one, he denied himself every luxury. He lived like an ancient sage. He never asked you to chase power or pleasure—he asked you to live as he did, guided by reason and philosophy!”

She spoke with conviction, her cheeks flushed, her body trembling. But Fra Luca remained unmoved. His eyes, calm and distant, studied her passion. “What good were your father’s maxims to me?” he replied. “They told me to be strong when I felt broken. When I longed, like Saint Benedict, to roll in thorns, to welcome pain as freedom. Because the Divine love had found me—had filled me with a hunger no world could satisfy. Like a seed needing space to grow. I had been raised without true faith. I hadn’t studied our doctrine—yet it seized me like a flood. I saw a life of perfect love, where no desire tormented, no fear haunted, no question divided the soul. Before I knew the saints, I sensed their joy. Even pagan philosophy hinted at it: that man can die to earthly needs and live in God’s eternal peace.

“But to reach that, I must leave the world. I must renounce all ties—love, hope, attachment. I must live among men only as souls bound to the unseen. That need consumed me. It came in visions, when my mind grew weary of reading dead men’s passions. It came after I fell into sin and recoiled from the taste of emptiness. And in those visions, I saw the meaning of the Cross.”

He paused, gasping. Romola did not speak. She could not bridge the chasm between their worlds—between her rational soul and his spiritual fire.

When he resumed, his chest was calmer. “I knew whom I must follow. But I saw that even among the servants of Christ, I would be suffocated by hypocrisy, pride, lust. God did not call me to fight evil within the Church, as He called Dominic or Francis. He called me to flee. So I took vows and fled—to lands where danger, scorn, and poverty drove me ever closer to God. I lived as a hermit, served pilgrims. But my task was brief. The veil between me and eternity grows thin. I returned to Florence to—”

“Dino,” Romola interrupted, her voice softer now, touched by the image of her father alone in darkness, “you *did* want to know if he was alive, didn’t you?”

“—To urge my brothers to study the Eastern tongues, to go farther than I did. And I find they are already doing so. But since I came, Romola, I have felt I was sent to you—not to renew earthly bonds, but to deliver a heavenly warning. I have seen the vision three times. And for years, since the first call, I have been guided by visions. For in waking thought, we mix our own errors with truth. But in dreams and visions, we are passive—our souls become instruments in God’s hand. Listen now. Speak no more. Time is short.”

Romola resisted. She did not believe in such things. But the weight of his words, the certainty in his voice, pressed upon her. Still, she refused to listen.

Then, from the shadowed doorway, another monk stepped in. He stood on the other side of the bed, hood drawn over his head. “Kneel, my daughter,” he said, his voice rich and commanding, yet gentle. “The Angel of Death is present. He waits while heaven’s message is delivered. Bend your pride—before it is broken by iron.”

Romola turned. The speaker’s face was hidden beneath the cowl. But her eyes fell on his hands—folded across his chest, delicate, almost translucent, like carved ivory. They carried a strange authority. She wanted to resist—monks had always been figures of mockery in her world. But the hands seemed to plead, not command.

Then, the right hand gently took the crucifix from Fra Luca’s grasp. The left dipped a wet sponge and touched the dying man’s lips. As he bent, the cowl slipped back.

And Romola saw his face.

It was the face of Fra Girolamo Savonarola, Prior of San Marco—the man she had heard of, the one she had judged harshly for his loud sermons, his fanatical zeal. But now, in the candlelight, his features were striking: high nose, firm jaw, dark brows, blue-grey eyes under auburn lashes—eyes that held both fire and gentleness. And again, the voice spoke: “Kneel, my daughter. The pride of the body blocks the gifts that purify the soul.”

Romola hesitated. Then, slowly, she sank to her knees. As she did, a tremor ran through her. Something shifted—not just posture, but spirit. She felt herself yield, not to force, but to a deeper presence.

Fra Luca began again, his voice fading with each breath. “Romola… I saw your father’s room—the library—books, marbles, the leggio where I read. And I saw you—your hair long and fair—sitting before his chair. At the legpto stood a man whose face I could not see. I looked… and it was blank, like a painted face erased. Then he took your hand. And you took your father’s hand. Together, you descended the stone steps into the streets—this man leading. You stood at the altar in Santa Croce. The priest who married you had the face of death. Graves opened. Dead men in shrouds followed you like a bridal train.

“You walked through the city, through the gates, into the valley. The man hurried you faster than you could bear. The dead grew tired and turned back. At last, you reached a barren place—no water, no trees, no grass. Instead of water, parchment unrolled everywhere. Instead of plants, bronze and marble figures rose around you. Your father fell, fainting from thirst. The man released your hand and left. As he walked away, I saw his face—and it was the Great Tempter.

“You wrung your hands, searching for water. None. The statues offered cups—but when you grasped them, they turned to parchment. Then they became demons, snatching your father’s body. The parchments shriveled. Blood flowed instead. Fire burned the blood. Everything vanished. The plain was bare and stony. You were alone.

“And then—night fell. I saw no more.”

He paused. Longer now. Then, barely audible: “Thrice I have seen this, Romola. I believe it is meant for you. A warning against marriage—against the enemy’s temptation. It calls you to dedicate yourself—”

His breath failed. He gasped, eyes fixed on the crucifix as if it were the last light. After a moment, he managed a whisper: “Renounce the vain philosophy of the heathens. Their pride will turn to mockery in sorrow. Their gods—”

The words died.

Romola sat in silence. She had dismissed visions as mere dreams—products of memory and imagination. But this one, though grotesque, stirred her deeply. Its images were cruel, unbearable. And she had never faced death before—not truly. This brother, returning in agony, was like a ghost from another world. The mournful faces on the fresco seemed to lean closer, sharing his fate.

“Frate,” the dying voice whispered.

Fra Girolamo leaned down. No reply came.

“Romola,” he breathed.

She leaned forward. Silence.

“Fra Girolamo… give her—”

“The crucifix,” said Fra Girolamo.

No sound followed.

“Dino!” Romola cried, the name torn from her throat. She knew now—there would be no understanding, no reconciliation.

“Take the crucifix, my daughter,” Fra Girolamo said after a pause. “His eyes see it no longer.”

Romola reached out. Her fingers closed around the cold wood. And in that touch, a great sob burst from her. She bowed her head beside her brother’s body and wept openly. It felt as though the world had changed forever. The light itself seemed altered.

Fra Girolamo moved to the door and summoned a lay brother waiting outside. Then he approached Romola. “Rise, my daughter. Be comforted. Our brother is with the blessed. He leaves you the crucifix—as a reminder of the warning. May it be a beacon in the darkness.”

She rose, trembling, wrapped her veil tightly, and hid the crucifix beneath her cloak. Fra Girolamo led her out into the cloistered court, now lit only by stars and a lantern held near the gate. Several figures in fine robes stood nearby—visitors to the Prior, who had waited upon hearing he was with the dying man.

Romola was half-aware of footsteps, rustling garments. She heard Fra Girolamo murmur, “Our brother is departed.” A hand touched her arm. Then the door opened, and she stepped into the wide piazza of San Marco—alone with Monna Brigida and the servant carrying the lantern.

The open air revived her. The terrible scene receded, though it remained vivid. She quickened her pace, eager to return to her father—and to Tito. Were they together? Had anything changed? The images of the vision clung to her like a nightmare, yet they only deepened her longing for the living voices that would confirm her reality.

Tito, we know, was not with Bardo. His future was being shaped by guilt—the crushing belief that Romola now knew the truth, the truth that would end their bond. And now, the lips that could have spoken it were sealed.

The revelation Fra Luca had offered came not from love or family, but from a realm beyond human feeling. The wisdom that might have come from simple questions—about father, brother, home—was buried in silence. And Romola, standing in the moonlit square, carried with her not just grief, but a haunting uncertainty—whether the world she knew was still safe, or whether the future was already written in blood and parchment.
