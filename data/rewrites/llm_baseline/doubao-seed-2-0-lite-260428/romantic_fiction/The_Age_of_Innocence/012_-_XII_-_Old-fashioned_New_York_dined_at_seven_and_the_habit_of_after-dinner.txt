Old-fashioned New York still dined sharp at seven o’clock, and the old custom of after-dinner calls—dismissed as dreadfully dowdy by Archer’s fast young set—lingered stubbornly across every brownstone thoroughfare. Crisp winter air nipped at Newland Archer’s cheeks as he strolled up Fifth Avenue from Waverley Place: the long, wide street was nearly empty save for a line of glossy carriages idling outside the Reggie Chiverses’ townhouse, waiting to ferry guests home after the grand dinner held in the visiting Duke’s honor, and the occasional elderly gentleman swaddled in heavy wool overcoats and silk mufflers, climbing marble steps to vanish into the warm glow of gas-lit front halls.

He spotted old Mr. du Lac turning in to call on his cousins the Dagonets as he crossed Washington Square, and a few steps further saw Mr. Skipworth, from his own law firm, heading purposefully for the Miss Lannings’ door. Halfway up Fifth, the bold, wealthy Julius Beaufort stepped out of his front doorway, darkly silhouetted against a blaze of parlor gaslight, and climbed into his private brougham to roll off toward a destination no respectable society matron would dare name. It was no opera night, no private party, so his outing was unmistakably clandestine. Archer’s mind drifted at once to the little little clapboard house beyond Lexington Avenue, the one that had lately sprung up with beribboned lace curtains and bright flower boxes on the sills, where Fanny Ring’s famous canary-yellow carriage was always seen lingering outside the freshly painted door.

Outside the tiny, tight-knit pyramid of Mrs. Archer’s social world lay a nearly unmapped quarter, populated by artists, musicians, and “people who wrote.” These ragtag scattered souls had never showed the slightest interest in being folded into old New York’s rigid social structure. Odd as their habits were, most were said to be perfectly respectable, but they preferred to keep to themselves. Medora Manson, back in her prosperous days, had tried to launch a “literary salon” — but it had died almost at once, when the literary crowd refused to step foot in it. Other hosts had tried the same trick, none more successfully than the eccentric Blenker household, ruled by a voluble, fervid matron and her three blowsy, imitative daughters, where you could rub elbows with Edwin Booth, Adelina Patti, the new Shakespearian sensation George Rignold, magazine editors, and leading literary and musical critics.

Mrs. Archer and her circle regarded these people with quiet, fluttery timidity. They were odd, unpredictable, with secrets hidden in the background of their lives and minds that no proper old New Yorker could ever parse. Literature and art were deeply respected in Archer’s set, of course; Mrs. Archer never tired of telling her children how much more gracious cultivated society had been back when it counted Washington Irving, Fitz-Greene Halleck, and the poet of *The Culprit Fay* as regular guests. Those celebrated authors of her youth had all been gentlemen — who knew if the unknown newcomers had gentlemanly hearts? Their origins, their unruly hair, their easy intimacy with the stage and the opera made every one of old New York’s unspoken rules of social labeling useless. “When I was a girl,” Mrs. Archer would sigh, “we knew every soul between the Battery and Canal Street, and only the people you knew had carriages. You could place anyone in five minutes. Now? I don’t even try.”

Only old Catherine Mingott, with her total lack of petty moral prejudices and her parvenu indifference to tiny social distinctions, could have bridged that gap — but she had never opened a book in her life, never looked twice at a painting, and cared for music only as a reminder of gala nights at the Italian opera in the days when she’d been feted at the Tuileries. Beaufort, bold as Mrs. Mingott and just as unbound by petty rules, might have pulled off the fusion too — but his grand marble townhouse and silk-stockinged footmen were too formal for the easy, casual sociability the bohemian crowd preferred. Worse, he was as illiterate as old Mrs. Mingott, and dismissed “fellows who wrote” as nothing more than paid entertainers there to amuse rich men. No one rich enough to change his mind had ever bothered to disagree.

Newland Archer had known all this his whole life, accepted it as a fundamental part of his world’s architecture. He had read travel essays and novels about far more glittering societies, where painters, poets, novelists, scientists, and even great actors were sought after as eagerly as foreign dukes. He had spent hours daydreaming of what it would be like to move through the drawing rooms of Paris and London, where the talk was dominated by Prosper Mérimée — whose *Lettres à une Inconnue* was one of his most tattered, well-loved possessions — Thackeray, Browning, William Morris. But such things were unthinkable in New York, and dwelling on them only made his small, familiar world feel shakier. He knew most of the local “fellows who wrote,” the musicians and the painters; he met them for drinks at the Century Club, or at the small new theatrical and musical cropping up across the city. He enjoyed their company there, but found them tedious at the Blenkers’ parties, where they were passed around between fervid, dowdy hostesses like exotic captured curiosities. Even after his most sparkling, exciting late-night talks with Ned Winsett, he always left feeling that his own world was small, and theirs was equally small — and the only way to make either bigger was to reach a place of easy, unguarded manners where the two groups merged naturally.

He was turning this thought over in his mind as he walked up to Countess Olenska’s door, and remembered the strange, disorienting way she had of turning all his long-held values upside down. He would have to wrap his head around a life far different from anything he’d ever known if he was going to help her through her current mess.

Nastasia, her maid, opened the door with a mysterious little smile. On the hall bench lay a sable-lined overcoat, a folded dull silk opera hat with a gold “J. B.” stitched into its lining, and a soft white silk muffler. There was no mistaking who they belonged to: Julius Beaufort.

Archer burned with sudden hot anger. He almost scribbled a note on his card and stormed right back out into the street — then remembered that in his last letter to Madame Olenska, he had been so careful of proprieties he never actually said he wanted to see her privately. He had no one but himself to blame if she had welcomed other callers. He stepped across the threshold, doggedly determined to make Beaufort feel like an unwanted third wheel, and outstay him.

The banker was leaning against her mantel, which was draped in an ancient piece of hand-embroidered tapestry held in place by brass candelabra stuck full of yellowish old church candles. He puffed out his chest, shoulders braced against the mantel, all his weight resting on one large polished patent leather foot. He was grinning down at his hostess when Archer walked in. Ellen Olenska sat on a sofa placed at a right angle to the chimney, half-reclining, her head propped on one hand, her wide sleeve falling back to leave her arm bare all the way to the elbow. A table heaped with flowers was set behind her like a screen; Archer recognized the orchids and azaleas at once, they were the signature expensive tributes from Beaufort’s own private hot-houses.

Every other proper lady receiving callers in the evening wore a stiff “simple dinner dress” — a whalebone-armored sheath of silk, slightly open at the neck with lace ruffles filling the gap, tight sleeves that flounced just far enough back to show a gold Etruscan bracelet or a velvet band at the wrist. But Madame Olenska ignored every rule of local tradition, dressed in a long clinging robe of deep red velvet, trimmed all around the chin and down the front with glossy black fur. Archer remembered seeing a portrait by the new Parisian painter Carolus Duran on his last trip abroad, the sensation of that year’s Salon, where a lady wore exactly that kind of bold, sheath-like dress, her chin nestled deep in fur. It was perverse, almost provocative, to wear fur in a heated drawing-room in the evening, pairing a swaddled, warm throat with a bare, cool arm — but the effect was breathtakingly lovely.

“Good Lord, three whole days at Skuytercliff!” Beaufort was drawling in his loud, sneering tone as Archer stepped in. “You’d better pack all your furs, and a hot-water bottle to boot.”

“Is the house that cold?” she asked, holding out her left hand to Archer in a way that made it clear she expected him to kiss it.

“Not the house,” Beaufort said, nodding carelessly at the young man in greeting. “The missus.”

“But I thought she was so kind!” Ellen protested. “She came to invite me herself. Granny says I absolutely must go.”

“Granny would say that, of course. And I say it’s a crying shame you’re going to miss the little oyster supper I planned for you next Sunday at Delmonico’s. Private room, Campanini and Scalchi, a whole crowd of jolly people — even a Steinway they’ll play for us all night.”

She glanced doubtfully from the banker to Archer. “Oh, that does tempt me! I haven’t met a single artist since I’ve been here, not even that night at Mrs. Struthers’s.”

“What kind of artists?” Archer cut in boldly. “I know one or two very good painters, decent fellows. I could bring them round to call on you, if you’d like.”

“Painters? Are there even painters in New York?” Beaufort scoffed, his tone implying no painter could possibly exist if he didn’t buy their work.

Madame Olenska smiled her grave, soft smile at Archer. “That would be charming. But I was really thinking of dramatic artists, singers, actors, musicians. My husband’s house was always full of them.”

She said the words “my husband” as if there were no dark, sinister associations clinging to them, her tone almost soft with longing for the lost, glittering delights of that married life. Archer stared at her, perplexed, wondering if this was lightheartedness or careful dissimulation — how could she touch that painful past so easily, when she was risking everything to break free of it?

“I do think,” she went on, speaking to both men, “that the unexpected adds so much to one’s joy. It’s such a mistake to see the same faces every single day.”

“It’s unutterably dull, anyhow,” Beaufort grumbled. “New York is dying of boredom. And just when I try to liven it up for you, you turn me down. Come, think better of it! Sunday’s your last chance. Campanini leaves for Baltimore and Philadelphia next week.”

“How delicious,” she said, amiable but with the faintest sharp hint of dismissal in her voice. “May I think it over, and write you my answer tomorrow morning?”

Beaufort felt it at once. No one ever dismissed him. He stared at her, a stubborn line forming between his brows. “Why not decide right now?”

“It’s too serious a question to settle this late,” she said, meeting his gaze coolly. “Because I still have a little business to discuss with Mr. Archer.”

“Ah,” Beaufort snapped. There was no arguing with that tone. He shrugged, recovered his composure, took her hand and kissed it with a practiced, effortless air, and called over his shoulder from the threshold: “I say, Newland, if you can persuade the Countess to stay in town, you’re welcome at the supper too.” He left the room, his heavy, important footsteps echoing down the hall.

For a second Archer thought Mr. Letterblair must have warned her he was coming — but her next, completely unrelated remark proved him wrong. “You know painters, then? You live in their world?” she asked, eyes bright with interest.

“Not exactly. I don’t think the arts have a proper world of their own here, any of them. It’s more like a very thinly settled outskirt on the edge of society.”

“But you care for those things?”

“More than I can say. Whenever I’m in Paris or London, I never miss an exhibition. I do my best to keep up.”

She looked down at the tip of her little satin boot peeking out from the hem of her long velvet dress. “I used to care immensely, too. My whole life was full of those things. But now I want to try not to.”

“Try not to?”

“Yes. I want to cast off all my old life, to become just like everybody else here.”

Archer’s face flushed hot. “You’ll never be like everybody else.”

She lifted her straight eyebrows a little. “Oh, don’t say that. If you knew how I hate being different!” Her face went sombre, as still and tragic as a carved mask. She leaned forward, thin hands clasped around her knee, gaze drifting past him into some dark, faraway memory. “I want to get away from all of it.”

“I know,” Archer said, clearing his throat. “Mr. Letterblair told me. That’s why I came. He asked me to handle this for you — you see, I’m part of the firm now.”

She looked faintly surprised, then her face lit up. “You mean you can manage it for me? I can talk this through with you instead of stuffy old Mr. Letterblair? Oh, that will be so much easier!”

Her soft tone touched him, and his confidence swelled. He realized she had mentioned “business” to Beaufort purely to get rid of him — routing Beaufort, the boldest man in New York, felt like a quiet, secret triumph. “I’m here to talk it all over,” he repeated.

She sat silent for a minute, her head still propped on the arm draped over the back of the sofa. Her face looked pale, almost dimmed, against the rich glow of her red dress. Suddenly she struck Archer as a terribly pathetic, even pitiful figure. Now we’re getting to the hard facts, he thought, and he felt that same instinctive recoil from messy, uncomfortable situations that he had spent years mocking in his mother and her friends. He had so little practice dealing with anything out of the ordinary! The very words for this kind of situation belonged to novels, to the stage, not to his quiet, predictable life. He felt as awkward and flustered as a schoolboy.

After a long pause, Madame Olenska spoke out with sudden, fierce vehemence: “I want to be free. I want to wipe every last trace of the past away.”

“I understand that.”

Her face softened, warming. “Then you’ll help me?”

“First —” he hesitated. “Perhaps I ought to know a little more.”

She looked surprised. “You know about my husband? About the life I lived with him?”

He nodded.

“Well then — what more is there to know? In this country, are such things even tolerated? I’m a Protestant — our church doesn’t forbid divorce in cases like mine.”

“Of course it doesn’t.”

Silence fell between them, and Archer could almost see the spectre of Count Olenski’s short, vicious letter grinning hideously between their two chairs. It was only half a page long, exactly what he had described it as to Mr. Letterblair: the vague, cruel accusations of an angry blackguard. But how much truth lay behind those words? Only Count Olenski’s wife could say.

“I’ve read all the papers you gave Mr. Letterblair,” he said at last.

“Well — can there be anything more abominable than what I’ve already told you?”

“No.”

She shifted a little, lifting a hand to shade her eyes from the firelight. “Of course you know,” Archer went on, “that if your husband chooses to fight the case — as he’s threatening to —”

“Yes?”

“He can say things — things that might be very unpleasant for you. He can say them publicly, in open court, so the stories spread far and wide, and they will damage you — even if every last word of them is completely untrue.”

“Even if?”

“I mean, no matter how unfounded the accusations are.”

She was quiet for so long that Archer, not wanting to stare at her shaded face, had time to memorize the exact shape of her other hand, the one resting on her knee, every tiny detail of the three rings on her fourth and fifth fingers. He noticed, with a little jolt, that her wedding ring was not among them.

“What harm could those kinds of accusations do to me here?”

He had been about to burst out My poor child — far more harm here than anywhere else in the whole world! But instead, his voice came out as dry and formal as old Mr. Letterblair’s. “New York society is a very small world, compared to the one you came from. And for all appearances, it’s ruled by a tiny handful of people with very, very old-fashioned ideas.”

She said nothing, so he kept going. “Our laws are lenient enough about divorce, but our social customs are not.”

“Never?”

“Not if the woman, no matter how injured, no matter how perfectly blameless she is, has even the faintest hint of unconventional action hanging over her, giving anyone the chance to spin ugly, offensive insinuations.”

She drooped her head a little lower. Archer waited, hoping desperately for a flash of indignation, or even a short, sharp cry of denial. Nothing came. A little travelling clock on the mantel ticked soft and purring by her elbow, and a log in the fire cracked in two, sending a shower of golden sparks up the chimney. The whole hushed, still room seemed to wait silently beside him.

“Yes,” she murmured at last. “That’s what my family has been telling me.”

“It’s not unnatural —” Archer began.

“Our family,” she corrected him gently, and Archer’s face burned. “You’re going to be my cousin soon, after all.”

“I hope so.”

“And you take their side?”

He stood up, wandered across the room, stared blankly at a painting hanging against the old red damask wall, then came back irresolutely to her side. How could he say Yes, if what your husband hints is true, or if you have no way of disproving it?
