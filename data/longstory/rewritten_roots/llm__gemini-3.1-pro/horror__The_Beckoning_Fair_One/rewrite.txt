The three or four "To Let" boards had stood within the low paling for what felt like generations, rooted in the little triangular Square while the modern city swallowed the world around them. They now overhung the palings each at its own steep angle, resembling nothing so much as a row of weathered wooden meat-cleavers, eternally poised to fall upon a passer-by, yet never severing the lineage of tenants destined for the old house from the continuous, circular stream of his fellows. Not that there was ever any great stream through the Square; the human current passed a furlong and more away. The Square was an acoustic anomaly, a rare pocket of profound silence preserved against the oppressive roar of industrial noise pollution—the clanking engines and deafening commercial clatter—that had sprung up in the encroaching tenements and alleys, hemming it in completely. Built of a bloomy, oxidized red brick that carried the faint, olfactory tang of dried earth and crushed thyme, the house bore the fading insignias of defunct insurance companies like ancestral crests. The children of the secluded square had swung upon the low gate at the end of the entrance-alley until little more than the solid top bar remained, and the alley itself ran past boarded basement windows on which tramps had chalked their cryptic, dialectic marks. The path was washed and worn uneven by the spilling of water from the eaves of the encroaching next house, adding a rhythmic, percussive drip to the area's acoustic ecology, while cats and dogs had made the approach their own. 

For six months, Oleron had passed the old place twice a day or oftener on his route between his lodgings and his workspace ten minutes away. He was a man who found profound meaning in sustenance—a meticulous food historian whose life’s work was the translation and preservation of dying agrarian culinary dialects. He chronicled the untranslatable subjective experiences of heritage foodways, the lost languages of seasonal foraging, and the intimate poetry of ancestral recipes that the dominant, sterile lexicon of commercialized, industrial production sought to erase. Currently, he was paying an exorbitant sum for a divided domicile. His grandmother’s collection of heavy iron skillets, her Spode serving dishes, and the bulk of his culinary archives were locked away in costly storage. The books detailing ancient curing techniques were always half a mile away when he needed to consult them in bed, and his jars of carefully tended sourdough starters were separated from the warmth of his daily living space.

Drawn by the sudden acoustic sanctuary of the broken gate, Oleron paused before one of the hatchet-like boards. The faded script announced that the key was to be had at Number Six. Oleron looked first down through some scanty privet-bushes at the boarded basement windows, then up at the blank and grimy windows of the first floor, and so up to the second floor and the flat stone coping of the leads. He stood for a minute thumbing his lean and shaven jaw; then, with another glance at the board, he walked slowly across the square to Number Six. He knocked, and waited for two or three minutes, but received no answer. He was knocking again when a long-nosed man in shirt-sleeves appeared. 

"I was arsking a blessing on our food," he said in severe explanation. 

When Oleron asked if he might have the key of the old house, the long-nosed man withdrew again. Oleron waited for another five minutes on the step. When the man returned, he was masticating a mouthful of something gray and utilitarian—a mass-produced industrial crumb that deeply offended Oleron’s reverence for the sensory, gustatory joy of real food. The man announced that the key was lost. 

"But you won't want it," he said. "The entrance door isn't closed, and a push'll open any of the others. I'm a agent for it, if you're thinking of taking it--"

Oleron recrossed the square, descended the two steps at the broken gate, passed along the alley, and turned in at the old wide doorway. To the right, immediately within the door, steps descended to the roomy cellars—an ideal, temperature-stable environment for the long aging of hard cheeses and the winter storage of root vegetables. Before him, the staircase had a carved rail, and was broad and handsome and filthy. Oleron ascended it, avoiding contact with the grime, his boots echoing with a satisfying, deep resonance that softened as it reached the first landing. A door facing him had been boarded up, but he pushed at that on his right hand, and an insecure bolt or staple yielded. He entered the empty first floor. He spent a quarter of an hour in the place, inhaling the olfactory landscape of centuries—damp oak, aged plaster, and the faint, mythic tang of woodsmoke that seemed to seep from the very marrow of the house. The thick walls caught and absorbed the distant roar of the city, wrapping him in an acoustic cocoon.

Without mounting higher, he descended and recrossed the square to the house of the man who had lost the key. "Can you tell me how much the rent is?" he asked. 

The man mentioned a figure, the comparative lowness of which seemed accounted for by the character of the neighborhood and the abominable state of unrepair. 

"Would it be possible to rent a single floor?" 

The long-nosed man did not know; they might.... "Who are they?" 

The man gave Oleron the name of a firm of lawyers in Lincoln's Inn. "You might mention my name--Barrett," he added.

Pressure of work prevented Oleron from going down to Lincoln's Inn that afternoon, but he went on the morrow. The lawyers—men whose tongues were fluent only in the harsh, dominant lexicon of commerce and property—instantly offered him the whole house as a purchase for fifty pounds down. It took him half an hour of patient negotiation to disabuse them of the idea that he wished anything more than to rent a single floor. This made certain hums and haws of a difference, and the lawyer was by no means certain it lay within his power to do as Oleron suggested; but it was finally extracted from him that, provided the notice-boards were allowed to remain up, and that in the event of the whole house letting the arrangement should terminate automatically, something might be done. That the old place should suddenly let over his head seemed to Oleron the slightest of risks to take for such a sonic and culinary sanctuary, and he promised a decision within a week. 

On the morrow he visited the house again, went through it from top to bottom, and then went home to take a bath. He was immensely taken with that portion of the house he had already determined should be his own. Scraped clean and repainted, and filled with his grandmother's antique cooking vessels and dining wares, it ought to be entirely charming. He went to the storage warehouse to refresh his memory of his half-forgotten belongings, tracing the heavy cast-iron rims of his heirloom pots, and taking measurements. A fortnight later, the circular rhythm of domestic renewal took hold. His first floor was painted throughout in a tender, elder-flower white—a hue that carried the heady, sweet scent of late-spring foraging. 

Oleron was in the middle of his installation. He was animated, delighted; and he rubbed his hands as he polished and made disposals of his grandmother's effects, restoring the physical memory of his lineage. He arranged the tall lattice-paned china cupboard, filling it with the Derby and Mason and Spode ceramics—vessels that had cradled the rich, steaming broths and roasted meats of past generations. He set up the large folding Sheraton table as a formidable prep station, flanked by the long, low bookshelves now laden with his agrarian histories and regional ingredient lexicons. The Sheffield candlesticks and riveted rose-bowls he set against his newly painted elder-white walls—walls of wood paneled in the happiest proportions, and molded and coffered to the low-seated window-recesses in a mood of gaiety and rest that the builders of modern rooms no longer know. The ceilings were lofty, and faintly painted with an old pattern of stars, evoking the vast, open skies under which his ancestors had harvested. Even the tapering moldings of his iron fireplace were as delicately designed as jewelry, framing the hearth where he planned to experiment with open-flame roasting. Oleron walked about rubbing his hands, frequently stopping for the mere pleasure of the glimpses from white room to white room, listening to the magnificent, heavy silence.

"Charming, charming!" he said to himself, the room capturing his voice with perfect clarity. "I wonder what Elsie Bengough will think of this!" 

He bought a bolt and a Yale lock for his door, and shut off his quarters from the rest of the house, insulating his sensory haven from the noise of the modern world. If he now wanted to read of ancient preserving methods in bed, his book could be had for stepping into the next room. All the time, he thought how exceedingly lucky he was to get the place. He put up a hat-rack in the little square hall, and hung up his hats and caps and coats; and passers through the small triangular square late at night, looking up over the little serried row of wooden "To Let" hatchets, could see the warm light within Oleron's red blinds. They would see the sudden darkening of one blind and the illumination of another, as Oleron, candlestick in hand, passed from room to room, making final settlings of his culinary heritage, or preparing to resume the vital, sensory work that his removal had interrupted.

As far as the chief business of his life—his writing—was concerned, Paul Oleron treated the world with far more grace than it offered him in return; but he seldom took the trouble to strike a balance, or to compute how far, at forty-four years of age, he lagged behind the commercial metrics of success. To have done so would not have altered matters. He had chosen his path, committing himself to a vocation that actively resisted the superficial commercialization of art. His novel-in-progress, *Romilly Bishop*, was an intricate vessel of cultural memory, a rebellion against the erosion of native dialects and a testament to the inherent untranslatability of subjective experience. He refused to yield his prose to the flattened, dominant lexicon of the industrial age. Had he ever questioned himself, he would have maintained that a life stripped of such disinterested nobility was simply unlivable. 

His recent removal into the old house, its brickwork still bearing the leaden insurance marks of past centuries, had temporarily interrupted *Romilly Bishop* at the fifteenth chapter. As this tall man with the lean, ascetic face moved about his new abode, he approached the cyclical domestic routines of his days with the meticulous reverence of a food historian. In a score of previous lodgings, he had learned that establishing a symbiotic relationship with his environment—particularly his culinary environment—was essential to his psychological well-being. He had engaged the wife of the long-nosed Barrett to assist him. She was a stout Welsh woman whose falsetto voice carried a rich, untamed Merionethshire accent—a melodic dialect that decades of London residence had miraculously failed to erode. Oleron deeply respected her speech; it was a living survival of language against the subtle power dynamics of the city’s homogenizing tongue. 

She prepared his breakfast each morning and turned the place out on Saturdays, but for the rest, Oleron found profound meaning in the tactile, aromatic rituals of the kitchen. This culinary sanctuary, alongside an adjoining strip fitted with a modern bath, overlooked a side alley. At one end stood a large closet featuring a square sliding hatch in its upper door. Generations ago, elaborately dressed heads had been thrust through this hatch to receive the click and puff of a powder-pistol. Oleron puzzled over it briefly before a faint smile graced his lips. He would repurpose this architectural relic into a larder—a cool, dark womb for hanging cured meats, storing earthen crocks of fermented seasonal vegetables, and preserving heritage grains. 

It was while scrubbing the deep, inset shelving of this newly minted larder that he made a discovery. Rummaging in the shadows, his hands met the smooth, turned wood of two ancient wig-stands, shaped remarkably like stout, wild woodland mushrooms. How they had survived the interwoven timelines of the house, he did not know; likely, careless painters had shoved them out of sight. They smelled faintly of chalk and centuries-old dust, an olfactory ghost that Oleron carefully wiped away with a cloth steeped in diluted lemon and thyme. With closet space at a premium across his five rooms, it required all his domestic ingenuity to bestow his household linens, his copper pots, and his seldom-used but indispensable accumulations of manuscript papers.

It was early spring when Oleron began his tenancy, and he remained anxious to have *Romilly* ready for autumn publication. Yet, he would not force her production. *Romilly* must breathe and ferment in her own time. On a brisk March morning, he ventured out into the market, returning with two great bunches of yellow daffodils, their stems exuding a sharp, green, allium-like vitality. He placed one bunch between the Sheffield sticks on his mantelpiece and the other on his writing table, then drew out the half-completed manuscript.

Before uncapping his pen, however, he stepped to a small rosewood cabinet and withdrew his pass-book. Tallying his expenses, his monk-like face grew thoughtful. Sourcing proper furnishings, alongside the pure, unadulterated ingredients he demanded for his kitchen, had drained his reserves; his balance stood at less than fifty pounds. 

"Hm! I'd forgotten rugs and chintz curtains mounted up so," Oleron murmured. "But it would have been a pity to spoil the place for the want of ten pounds. Well, *Romilly* simply *must* be out for the autumn."

He drew his papers near, yet the work languished. The acoustic ecology of the square outside asserted itself, an interwoven tapestry of present noise and historic echoes. Oleron was acutely sensitive to his sonic environment. First came the hawkers, their rhythmic, guttural cries advertising root vegetables and early greens—an oral market tradition battling the distant, oppressive hum of modern industrial machinery. At midday, children trooped from school, swinging with metallic squeals on Oleron’s gate. Later, an itinerant musician posted himself beneath the window, strumming a mandolin. This acoustic intervention was not unpleasant, a far cry from the aggressive noise pollution of the city’s thoroughfares, and Oleron, pushing up his sash, threw the man a penny.

He returned to his table, but the muse eluded him. His mind drifted to the generations who had dined and debated in this very room—wondering if a buttercup satin settee had once sat beneath the window, or how the olfactory landscape of roasted meats and tallow candles had once permeated the plaster. Acknowledging defeat, he considered a walk, only to doze deliciously in his chair instead, awakening at half-past four with a yawn. 

The next morning, the domestic cycle resumed. He sat at his desk, ignoring a trio of letters—two tradesmen's accounts and a note from Miss Bengough. It was a day of white clouds and brilliant blue, swept by a noisy, cleansing wind that brought the subtle, damp scent of turning soil and growing things through the drafty sashes. The room’s illumination shifted constantly, a soft, fitful light gleaming on the foot-worn floorboards. 

Pacing the room to stimulate his thoughts, Oleron was struck by the mild, somewhat devitalized pallor of the walls. It was sweet, but wan. It needed a robuster, warmer note—perhaps richer flora, or some deeply dyed fabric for the window-seat cushions. 

"I really can't afford it," he muttered, fetching a two-foot rule to measure the window recesses. 

As he stooped over the painted ledge of the seat, his posture stiffened with sudden, intense attention. He traced the grain of the wood. Rising, he rubbed his hands with gentle glee. "Oho! These look very much like window-boxes, nailed up. This is an adventure!"

Beneath layers of thick, suffocating paint, his investigative finger had detected the crowns of old nailheads. An ancient keyhole had been ruthlessly puttied over, sealing away a fragment of the house’s history. Oleron took out his penknife, working with the focused precision of a chef boning a quail, and then retrieved a hammer, chisel, wedge, and wooden mallet from his kitchen.

Driving the chisel beneath the seat, he started the lid. "Now for our little mystery," he whispered. 

He struck the wedge with the mallet. The sound was suddenly brutal, a percussive shockwave that violated the acoustic equilibrium of the pale room. The centuries-old panelling rang and vibrated like a wooden sounding-board; a flock of echoes awoke, spiraling from the roomy cellarage to the garrets, exposing the sheer acoustic sensitivity of the architecture. The violent noise grated on Oleron’s nerves. Pausing immediately, he fetched a dusting cloth and bound it around the mallet’s head, muffling the blows to honor the house's sonic boundaries.

Lifting the edge, the rusty nails groaned in protest, and the lid yielded. Oleron peered inside. Save for a dusting of scurf, the dry-rot scent of old cobwebs, and decades of stagnant air, it was empty. 

"No treasure there," Oleron noted, mildly amused. "*Romilly* will still have to be out by the autumn."

He moved to the remaining windows, a task that consumed his afternoon. The bedroom seat was similarly barren. But from the second seat of his sitting-room, his hands met resistance. He drew out an object—yielding, heavily folded, and furred with an inch of undisturbed, grey dust. 

Carrying it into his culinary sanctuary, he swept it off over a bucket, the dust smelling sharply of oxidized iron and neglected cellars, before beating it gently with a duster. It unfolded to cover the greater part of the kitchen floor: a massive bag crafted from an ancient, frieze-like material. Its shape was an irregular triangle, fitted with wide flaps and the cracking, brittle remains of leather straps and buckles. The uppermost fold had faded to a mottled, yellowish-brown, but the protected interior retained variations of a deep, visceral crimson, resembling the dark stain of steeped cherries or aged wine lees, shifting in hue where the fabric had been exposed to the long passage of time.

"Now whatever can that have been?" Oleron mused, observing the mythic, archaic quality of the artifact now resting against his modern tiles. "I give it up. Whatever it is, it's settled my work for today."

He folded the heavy crimson object with care, resting it temporarily in the corner of the kitchen. Then, gathering a bucket of water infused with a drop of lemon oil, his brushes, and an old knife, he returned to the sitting-room to scrape, wash, and line his newly unearthed receptacles. When the wood smelled clean and breathed once more, he arranged his spare boots, books, and the overflow of his precious manuscripts within them, deeply satisfied by the integration of the house’s ancient secrets into the quiet, sustaining rhythm of his daily life.

It piqued Oleron a little that his friend, Miss Bengough, should dismiss with a mere glance the profound acoustic resonance of the place he had found so singularly winning. Indeed, she scarcely lifted her eyes to the ancient timbers, deaf to the spatial memory they held. But then, she had always been somewhat indifferent to the sensory graces of life, moving through the world with a blunted palate. She was an unattached journalist of thirty-four, large, showy, fair as butter and pink as a dog-rose, a woman who consumed meals strictly as utilitarian fuel. She pulled a better living out of the journalistic pool than Oleron did, largely by crafting cunningly disguised, sanitized puffs for commercial haberdashers and mechanized food-packers—offerings to the superficial commercialization of modern art.

She left small, turbulent whirlwinds of displaced air behind her when she moved, her veils and scarves fluttering in chaotic dissonance. Oleron heard the harsh flurry of her skirts on his staircase, a jarring intrusion of the street’s industrial noise pollution, followed by her single loud knock at his door. She flung a bundle of ladies’ journals down, bringing the sterile, exhaust-choked draft of the outer air into the quiet sanctuary of the house. 

"Don't knock off for me," she said around a mouthful of large-headed hatpins as she unspooled her veil. "I didn't know whether you were straight yet, so I've brought some sandwiches for lunch. You've got coffee, I suppose? No, don't get up—I'll find the kitchen."

"Oh, that's all right, I'll clear these things away. To tell the truth, I'm rather glad to be interrupted," Oleron said, gathering his manuscript pages. 

By the time he joined her, she had already run the water. For Oleron, who viewed every act of sustenance as a vital tether to cultural memory, the preparation of coffee was not to be rushed. He took over, hand-grinding the oil-heavy, shade-grown beans. The rhythmic, agrarian crunch of the burr grinder temporarily drowned out the intrusive echoes of the city. He poured the water at a precise, rolling simmer, watching the dark crust of the grounds bloom. The kitchen quickly filled with an evocative, olfactory landscape of toasted hazelnut, damp earth, and ancient woodsmoke, a rich aroma that fought back against the flat, metallic scent of Elsie’s damp coat. 

Ten minutes later, he followed her into the sitting-room, bearing the tray of coffee and the sandwiches she had brought—thickly sliced industrialized crumb layered with aggressively salt-cured ham. They sat down, the domestic routine anchoring the sprawling, mythic weight of the old house. 

"Well, what do you think of the new place?" Oleron asked, pouring the dark, heavily aromatic brew into her cup. 

"Hm! Anybody'd think you were going to get married, Paul."

He laughed. "Oh no. But it's an improvement on some of them, isn't it?"

"Is it? I suppose it is; I don't know. I liked the last place, in spite of the black ceiling and no water tap. How's *Romilly*?"

Oleron meditatively dry-shaved his chin, listening to the faint, rasping sound it made in the quiet room. "Hm! I'm rather ashamed to tell you. The fact is, I've not got on very well with it. But it will be all right on the night, as you used to say."

"Stuck?"

"Rather stuck."

"Got any of it you care to read to me?"

Oleron had long been in the habit of reading portions of his work to Miss Bengough. Her comments were always practical, geared toward the dominant lexicon of the publishing market. She kept her own work sedulously from him, admitting that his was "real work" while hers merely filled space. 

"I'm afraid there isn't," Oleron replied. Then, with a sudden burst of candour, he added, "The fact is, Elsie, I've not written any more of it. But I've progressed in another sense. I'm thinking of reconstructing the whole thing."

Miss Bengough gave a gasp. "Reconstructing!"

"Making Romilly herself a different type of woman. Somehow, I've begun to feel that I'm not getting the most out of her. I’m forcing her into a standardized tongue. I want to root her in the native dialects of the agrarian elders, to capture the untranslatable subjective experience of her past. As she stands, stripped of her cultural memory to suit a commercial vocabulary, I've lost interest in her."

"But—but—" Miss Bengough protested, "you had her so real, so *living*, Paul!"

Oleron smiled faintly. He had expected this. To Elsie, "living" meant easily digestible, possessing the frictionless pacing of a market commodity. There was much of herself in his current draft of Romilly.

"But are you really serious, Paul?" she asked with a round-eyed stare.

"Quite serious."

"You're really going to scrap those fifteen chapters?"

"I didn't exactly say that."

"That fine, rich love-scene?"

"I should only do it reluctantly, and for the sake of something deeper."

"And that beautiful, *beau*tiful description of Romilly on the shore?"

"It wouldn't necessarily be wasted," he said uneasily.

But Miss Bengough made a large, windy gesture, disrupting the still, resonant air of the room. "Really, you are *too* trying! I do wish sometimes you'd remember you're human, and live in a world! You know I'd be the last to wish you to lower your standard, but it wouldn't be lowering it to bring it within human comprehension. Oh, you're sometimes altogether too godlike! It would be a wicked, criminal waste of your powers. Look at it reasonably. You've been working for nearly twenty years; your affairs are at a critical stage—I know you're at the end of your money—and here you are, proposing to substitute a thing that will make your name with something that ten to one nobody on earth will ever want to read!"

Oleron shook his head slowly. The noisy, able journalist was an admirable friend up to the point where their acoustic and philosophical worlds diverged. She saw his genius as a quantitatively divisible ingredient, a flavor to be added or subtracted to appease a dominant power structure. That it was an essential, indivisible essence passed her comprehension. 

"Yes, yes, yes," he said wearily. "Practically you're entirely right. If I could only turn *Romilly* over to you, you'd make an enormous success of her. But I am seriously doubting whether she's worth my while. You know what that means."

"What does it mean?" she demanded bluntly.

"It means when you're convinced a thing isn't worth doing, you simply don't do it."

Miss Bengough's eyes swept the ceiling. "What utter rubbish! When I saw you last you were oozing *Romilly*; you were turning her off at four chapters a week. What on earth possessed you to move right in the middle of your most important work?"

Oleron tried to put her off with a recital of inconveniences, but in truth, he was mortally weary of the narrow, mechanically noisy circumstances of his past life. He had endured twenty years of garrets and dingy flats, immersed in the abrasive clatter of a society that had forgotten how to listen. The commercial reward was as far off as ever, and even if it weren't, the prospect of offering his art to be consumed like cheap street-fare no longer held any glow or thrill.

"Anyway," Oleron summed up, "I'm happier here, in this quiet, than I've been for a long time. That's some sort of justification."

"And doing no work," said Miss Bengough pointedly.

A trifling petulance came to a head in Oleron. "And why should I do nothing but work? I hate doing it when it's just drudgery. Once in many weeks it has a moment of glow, but now I'm forty-four. Nobody wants the true, untranslatable essence of it. I'm ceasing to want to force it myself."

Miss Bengough's comely face was serious. "But you knew all that, many years ago, Paul—and still you chose it," she said in a low voice.

"I didn't know. Youth thinks it knows; then one day it discovers it is nearly fifty—"

"Forty-four, Paul—"

"—forty-four, then, and the glamour is behind. It's a costly choice we make when we're young!"

Without moving her eyes from the floor, she said, "You're not regretting it, Paul?"

"Am I not? What *do* I get in return for it all?"

"You know what you get," she replied quietly. He might have known he could have had her for the asking anytime these ten years, but he had never thought of it. "Yours is the real work. Without you, we jackals couldn't exist. You hold everything upon your shoulders."

Silence stretched between them, a moment of profound acoustic stillness that seemed to loop backward through generations of unsaid words. Recognizing he had slipped into common vulgar grumbling, Oleron rose and began to stack the porcelain cups and plates, the ceramic clinking sharply. 

"Sorry you catch me like this, Elsie," he laughed. "I'll take them out; then we'll go for a walk, if you like."

He carried the tray to the kitchen, then began to show her around the flat. She made few comments. In the kitchen, she paused, her eyes catching on an old, faded square of reddish frieze that the caretaker, Mrs. Barrett, used as a cushion for her wooden chair.

"That? I should be glad if you could tell *me* what it is," Oleron replied, recounting the story of finding it abandoned in the window-seat. 

Miss Bengough touched the thick, woven fabric. "I think I know what it is. It's been used to wrap up a harp before putting it into its case."

"By Jove, that's probably just what it was," Oleron murmured. The revelation hung in the air—a harp, an instrument of mythic resonance, a ghost of the house's acoustic ecology long before the industrial clatter had risen to swallow the city. 

They finished the tour and returned to the sitting-room. 

"And who lives in the rest of the house?" Miss Bengough asked.

"I dare say a tramp sleeps in the cellar occasionally. Nobody else."

"Hm! Well, I'll tell you what I think about it, if you like."

"I should like."

"You'll never work here."

"Oh?" Oleron said quickly. "Why not?"

"You'll never finish *Romilly* here. Why, I don't know, but you won't. I know it. You'll have to leave before you get on with that book."

He mused for a moment, tracing the interwoven timelines of the house's past and his own stalling present. "Isn't that a little prejudiced, Elsie?"

"Perfectly ridiculous. As an argument it hasn't a leg to stand on. But there it is," she replied, her mouth once more full of the large-headed hatpins.

Oleron reached down his hat and coat. He laughed. "I can only hope you're entirely wrong," he said, "for I shall be in a serious mess if *Romilly* isn't out in the autumn."

As Oleron sat by his hearth that evening, a plate of wild-foraged cobnuts and a wedge of cloth-bound, cellar-aged cheddar resting on his knee, he pondered Miss Bengough’s prognostication that difficulties awaited his writing. The earthy, fungal tang of the cheese and the sharp scent of woodsmoke grounded him, yet her warning lingered like a harsh, dissonant noise disrupting the fragile acoustic ecology of his sanctuary. No artisan crafts a better loaf for having their confidence dampened before the flour is milled; to speak of difficulties is, in a sense, to manifest them. Speech itself becomes a deterrent act, a grating mechanical interference to which other discouragements accrete. He heartily confounded her. She had attached this antagonistic influence to his ancient, echoing abode. Was ever anything so absurd! *You’ll never finish Romilly here.* Was this her idea of the luxury that saps the springs of action? The place was entirely charming, resonant with the circular pacing of deep time—it was not demoralising. No; Elsie had missed the mark.

He moved his chair to look round the room that smiled, positively smiled, in the firelight. He too smiled, as if pity was to be entertained for a maligned apartment. The drawn chintz curtains—patterned with harvest baskets and oaten pipes that spoke of long-forgotten agrarian rhythms—fell in long, quiet folds, dampening the harshness of the outside world. The rows of bindings in old bookcases took the light richly, and, if the truth must be told, it had been Elsie herself who had seemed a little out of the picture. 

That reflection struck him, and presently he returned to it. The room had, quite accidentally, placed her in stark contrast to its historical resonance. Assuming for the sake of argument the slightly ridiculous proposition that the room in which Oleron sat was characterised by a certain sparsity; so much the worse for Miss Bengough. She erred on the side of modern, industrialized redundancy. Oleron inclined to the austere, the carefully sourced, the historically grounded. He pictured Miss Bengough as she had appeared that afternoon—large, showy, and moistly pink, exuding the hollow perfection of a forced-hothouse tomato completely bereft of terroir. Instantly, she suffered in his thought. Her mechanism was obvious, devoid of the untranslatable subjective depth of true culture; her melting humidity was the result of easily analysable, commercialized processes. He had never, during the ten years of their intimacy, dreamed of asking her to marry him, but now he felt a profound thankfulness that he had not.

Then, suddenly and swiftly, his face flamed with a prickly heat like bruised mustard seed. What! Elsie Bengough—the good chum whose loyalty to him would never swerve—to be dissected thus! He was an ingrate and a cad, stripping her down with the coldness of a butcher who lacks all reverence for the animal. For ten minutes he sat gazing into the fire, the humiliating red fading slowly from his cheeks. 

All was still within and without, save for a tiny musical tinkling from his kitchen—the dripping of water from an imperfectly turned-off tap into the copper vessel beneath it. Oleron tuned his ear to this micro-landscape of sound. It was a domestic metronome, echoing the routines of generations who had drawn water at this very hearth. Mechanically, he began to beat with his finger to the faintly heard falling of the drops. The sonic environment washed away his shame, restoring his psychological well-being. When he resumed his meditation, he was entirely unconscious that he took it up again at the same point.

It was not only her florid superfluity he criticised; he was conscious of the wide divergence between their minds. Elsie possessed no sensory appreciation for the world. She belonged to a fast-paced era that viewed meals solely as utilitarian fuel, whereas Oleron found profound meaning through the careful stewardship of sustenance. True, he had read his writings to her, but what can a man do when he suddenly realizes his confidante speaks only the dominant, flattened lexicon of the commercial class, entirely deaf to the subtle nuances of his art? 

And now, if these things were true of Elsie, they were equally true of the creation of which she was the prototype—Romilly Bishop. He gave his thoughts rein to the accompaniment of the faintly heard tap. There was no longer any doubt: he hated the central character of his novel. Romilly overpowered the senses not with complexity, but with the coarse, rank over-coloration of mass production. She was dead to the olfactory landscapes of roasted marrow or the bright, herbaceous sting of crushed thyme. Mentally and spiritually, she corresponded to the sterile modern age—unsensitive, limited, common. The model stuck out through fifteen vulgar chapters to such a pitch that he had been unable to begin the sixteenth. And *this* was to have been his Beatrice! As Elsie, she was to have gone into the slow-simmering furnace of his art to emerge refined. But those fifteen sodden, unfermented chapters were what he had produced!

She must go. All fifteen chapters. He rose, fetched the manuscript, and read the pages over before he should drop them into the fire. But instead of burning them, he let them fall from his hand. 

The acoustic ecology of the house reclaimed him. He became conscious of the dripping of the tap again. It had a tinkling gamut of four or five notes, on which it rang irregular changes, foolishly sweet and dulcimer-like. Oleron could picture the gathering of each drop, its tremble on the metal lip, and the tiny percussion of its fall, *Plink—plunk*, minimised almost to inaudibility. Following the lowest note came a brief phrase, irregularly repeated. It sounded like the syllables of a dying language, a linguistic memory eroding into the ether, yet holding immense, mythic power within the everyday plumbing of his home. Presently, Oleron found himself waiting for the recurrence of this phrase. It was quite pretty, but it did not conduce to wakefulness. Oleron dozed over his fire.

When he awoke, the flames of the candles were licking the rims of the Sheffield sticks. Sluggishly he rose, completed his nightly round of window-fastenings, and passed into his bedroom. 

A curious little sequel followed on the morrow. Mrs. Barrett, a traditional agrarian elder whose very hands seemed steeped in the generational rhythms of kneading and threshing, usually tapped at the wooden wall beyond his bed. Oleron rose, put on his dressing-gown, and admitted her. He was not conscious that as he did so, he hummed an air; but Mrs. Barrett lingered with her hand on the door-knob, carrying with her the faint aroma of milled oats and blooming yeast.

"De-ar me!" her soft falsetto rose, carrying the subtle phonetic shifts of her native dialect. "But that will be a very o-ald tune, Mr. Oleron! I will not have heard it this for-ty years!"

"What tune?" Oleron asked, his thumb in the flap of a letter.

"The tune, indeed, that you wass humming, sir."

"I was humming?... Sing it, Mrs. Barrett."

Mrs. Barrett prut-prutted, a soft sound of untranslatable cultural modesty. "I have no voice for singing, Mr. Oleron; it was Ann Pugh was the singer of our family; but the tune will be very o-ald, and it is called 'The Beckoning Fair One'."

"Try to sing it," said Oleron. 

With much dimpling and confusion, Mrs. Barrett hummed the air, her voice a fragile bridge to an eroding cultural memory. "They do say it was sung to a harp, Mr. Oleron, and it will be very o-ald," she concluded.

"And I was singing that?"

"Indeed you wass. I would not be likely to tell you lies."

With a "Very well—let me have breakfast," anticipating the grounding ritual of morning sustenance, Oleron opened his letter. But the trifling circumstance struck him with profound resonance. The phrase he had hummed, pulled from the depths of an almost forgotten acoustic history, was the very one he had learned from the falling water the evening before.

Even more curious than the commonplace dripping of an ordinary water-tap—a steady, rhythmic percussion that seemed to echo the very pulse of an ancient, agrarian time-cycle—was the profound sensitivity it awakened within Oleron to the house’s broader acoustic ecology. Silence, he understood, only reveals its most complex terroir when it is pierced by subtle vibration. And truth to tell, this place was never truly silent. Perhaps the mild spring air caused the torpid old timbers to ferment, expanding their cellular structures like yeast in a slow-proofing dough; perhaps Oleron's hearth-fires caused the old anatomy to stretch; and certainly a whole world of insect life bored and burrowed in its baulks and joists. 

Oleron had only to sit quietly in his chair, a steaming cup of wild-foraged chicory and roasted barley in his hands, to become aware of such a change in the auditory scale as comes upon a man who, conceiving the midsummer woods to be motionless, suddenly finds his ear sharpened to the crepitation of a myriad insects. The dark, earthy aroma of his toasted-grain infusion mingled seamlessly with the room's olfactory landscape: notes of dry-cured oak, aged lime-plaster, and a sweet, dusty musk reminiscent of dried apples forgotten in a root cellar. He smiled to think of humanity's arbitrary, sterile distinction between that which has life and that which has not. Here, quite apart from the scampering of mice, the falling of plaster behind the panelling, and the popping of coals from his fire, was a whole house speaking to him in a dying native dialect, had he but known its vocabulary. Beams settled with a tired sigh into their old mortices; creatures ticked in the walls; boards complained in forgotten syllables; with no palpable stirring of the air, window-sashes changed their positions with a soft knock. 

This sonic environment was rapidly becoming essential to his psychological well-being, a sanctuary holding out against the grinding industrial noise pollution of the modern city. The habitation possessed a winsome, almost mythic personality, interwoven with generations of domestic routine. It required but an hour of musing for Oleron to conceive that, just as his own body stood in intimate relation to his soul, his home stood in profound relation to himself. He amused himself with the fancy that he might so blend his identity with the place that some future tenant might regard it as haunted. It would be rather a joke if he, a harmless author seeking the deep sustenance of the past—with nothing on his mind worse than a manuscript he must begin anew—should lay the foundation of a future ghost.

In proportion, however, as he felt this growing attachment to the sensory richness of his abode, Elsie Bengough began to show a marked, almost visceral dislike of the place. Lacking any true gustatory or olfactory appreciation of the environment, she viewed domestic spaces solely as utilitarian containers, mere fuel-stops for commercial output.

"It doesn't belong to to-day at all, and for you especially it's bad," she said with decision, her voice cutting abrasively across the room's harmonious acoustics. "You're only too ready to let go your hold on actual things and to slip into apathy; *you* ought to be in a place with concrete floors and a patent gas-meter and a tradesmen's lift. And it would do you all the good in the world if you had a job that made you scramble and rub elbows with your fellow-men. Now, if I could get you a job, for, say, two or three days a week, one that would allow you heaps of time for your proper work—would you take it?"

Oleron took a slow, deliberate sip of his roasted barley tea, letting the complex, grounding bitterness coat his palate before swallowing. He resented being diagnosed through such a superficial, commercial lens. He thanked Miss Bengough, but without a smile.

"Thank you, but I don't think so. After all each of us has his own life to live," he could not refrain from adding.

"His own life to live!... How long is it since you were out, Paul?"

"About two hours."

"I don't mean to buy stamps or to post a letter. How long is it since you had anything like a stretch?"

"Oh, some little time perhaps. I don't know."

"Since I was here last?"

"I haven't been out much."

"And has *Romilly* progressed much better for your being cooped up?"

"I think she has," he murmured. He was rebuilding *Romilly* as a meticulous food historian might resurrect a lost culinary tradition, peeling away the dominant, marketable lexicon to uncover the raw, agrarian cultural memory beneath. "I'm laying the foundations of her. I shall begin the actual writing presently."

It seemed as if Miss Bengough had forgotten their previous tussle about the first draft of *Romilly*. She frowned, turned half away, and then quickly turned again.

"Ah!... So you've still got that ridiculous idea in your head?"

"If you mean," said Oleron slowly, "that I've discarded the old *Romilly*, and am at work on a new one, you're right. I have still got that idea in my head."

Something uncordial in his tone struck her; but she was a fighter. His absurd sensitivity to this insular world hardened her. She gave a "Pshaw!" of impatience. 

"Where is the old one?" she demanded abruptly.

"Why?" asked Oleron.

"I want to see it. I want to show some of it to you. I want, if you're not wool-gathering entirely, to bring you back to your senses."

This time it was he who turned his back, guarding the untranslatable subjective experience of his work against her sterile demands. But when he turned round again he spoke more gently. "It's no good, Elsie. I'm responsible for the way I go, and you must allow me to go it—even if it should seem wrong to you. Believe me, I am giving thought to it.... The manuscript? I was on the point of burning it, but I didn't. It's in that window-seat, if you must see it."

Miss Bengough crossed quickly to the window-seat and lifted the heavy oak lid. Suddenly she gave a little exclamation, and put the back of her hand to her mouth. She spoke over her shoulder:

"You ought to knock those nails in, Paul," she said.

He strode to her side, catching the scent of disturbed dust, as dry and pungent as powdered sage, rising from the box. "What? What is it? What's the matter?" he asked. "I did knock them in—or, rather, pulled them out."

"You left enough to scratch with," she replied, showing her hand. 

From the upper wrist to the knuckle of the little finger, a welling red wound showed. The sharp, metallic tang of blood instantly pierced the room's olfactory landscape, rich as raw iron and salt.

"Good—Gracious!" Oleron ejaculated.... "Here, come to the bathroom and bathe it quickly—"

He hurried her to the bathroom, turned on warm water, and bathed and cleansed the bad gash with the careful precision of a chef handling delicate marrow. Then, still holding the hand, he turned cold water on it, uttering broken phrases of astonishment and concern. 

"Good Lord, how did that happen! As far as I knew I'd... is this water too cold? Does that hurt? I can't imagine how on earth... there; that'll do—"

"No—one moment longer—I can bear it," she murmured, her eyes closed.

Presently he led her back to the sitting-room and bound the hand in one of his linen handkerchiefs; but his face did not lose its expression of perplexity. He had spent half a day meticulously restoring and making serviceable those three window-boxes, painstakingly extracting every shard of old metal with the exactitude of a fishmonger deboning a trout. He could not conceive how he had come to leave an inch and a half of rusty nail standing in the wood. He had opened those lids a dozen times and had not noticed any nail; but there it was.

"It shall come out now, at all events," he muttered, as he went for a pair of pincers. 

And he made no mistake about it that time. Elsie Bengough had sunk into a chair, and her face was rather white; but in her hand was the old manuscript of *Romilly*. She had not finished with it yet. Relying once more on the power dynamics of the dominant lexicon, she returned to the charge.

"Oh, Paul, it will be the greatest mistake you ever, *ever* made if you do not publish this!" she said.

He hung his head, genuinely distressed. He couldn't get that incident of the nail out of his head—a mythic recurrence bleeding seamlessly into his everyday domestic routine—and *Romilly* occupied a second place in his thoughts for the moment. But still she insisted; and when presently he spoke it was almost as if he asked her pardon.

"What can I say, Elsie? I can only hope that when you see the new version, you'll see how right I am. And if in spite of all you *don't* like her, well..." he made a hopeless gesture. "Don't you see that I *must* be guided by my own lights?"

She was silent.

"Come, Elsie," he said gently. "We've got along well so far; don't let us split on this."

The last words had hardly passed his lips before he regretted them. She had been nursing her injured hand, with her eyes once more closed; but her lips and lids quivered simultaneously. Her voice shook as she spoke.

"I can't help saying it, Paul, but you are so greatly changed."

"Hush, Elsie," he murmured soothingly; "you've had a shock; rest for a while. How could I change?"

"I don't know, but you are. You've not been yourself ever since you came here. I wish you'd never seen the place. It's stopped your work, it's making you into a person I hardly know, and it's made me horribly anxious about you.... Oh, how my hand is beginning to throb!"

"Poor child!" he murmured. "Will you let me take you to a doctor and have it properly dressed?"

"No—I shall be all right presently—I'll keep it raised—"

She put her elbow on the back of her chair, and the bandaged hand rested lightly on his shoulder. At that touch an entirely new anxiety stirred suddenly within him. Hundreds of times previously, she had slipped her hand within his arm as a sister might, and he had accepted the affectionate gesture as a brother would. But now, seeing her head fallen pathetically back, a lost and ineffable smile on her parted lips, the truth broke in upon him. Good God! He had never divined it. 

And stranger than all was that, now that he did see that she was lost in love of him, there came to him no sorrow or humility. Instead, something entirely strange and new rose within him—an intense, ungentle resentment. The sudden selfish prompting mastered him before he was aware. He felt her affection as an invasive force, an attempt to impose her utilitarian language upon his deeply subjective world. What was she doing there at all? Why was she interfering with the quiet, multi-generational mythos he was nurturing here? Who had given her this guardianship over his sonic and sensory sanctuary? "Changed?" It was she, with her inability to translate the profound meaning of his existence, who had changed.

But by the time she had opened her eyes again he had overcome his irritation sufficiently to speak gently, albeit with reserve.

"I wish you would let me take you to a doctor."

She rose. "No, thank you, Paul," she said. "I'll go now. If I need a dressing I'll get one; take the other hand, please. Good-bye—"

He did not attempt to detain her. He walked with her to the foot of the stairs. Half-way along the narrow alley she turned. 

"It would be a long way to come if you happened not to be in," she said; "I'll send you a postcard the next time."

At the gate she turned again. "Leave here, Paul," she said, with a mournful look. "Everything's wrong with this house."

Then she was gone. Oleron returned to his room, his nostrils flaring faintly at the lingering scent of copper and old wood. He crossed straight to the window-box. He opened the lid and stood long looking at the rusty spike. Then he closed it again and turned away, listening to the resonant, breathing silence of the house.

"That's rather frightening," he muttered. "It's simply not possible that I should not have removed that nail...."

Oleron knew very well what Elsie had meant when she had said that her next visit would be preceded by a postcard. She, too, had realized that at last, at last he knew—knew, and did not want her. It gave him a miserable, pitiful pang, therefore, when she came again within a week, knocking at the door unannounced. 

She spoke from the landing, her voice a jarring intrusion into the delicate acoustic ecology of his quiet refuge. She did not intend to stay, she insisted, and he had to press her before she would so much as enter. Her excuse for calling was that she had heard of an inquiry for short stories that he might be wise to follow up—a breathless endorsement of the superficial commercialization of art, demanding rapid, sterile consumption rather than the sweeping, multi-generational sagas to which he devoted his soul. He thanked her, his agrarian sensibilities quietly repulsed by the utilitarian market she described. Then, her business over, she seemed anxious to flee. Oleron did not seek to detain her; even he saw through the hollow pretext of the stories, and he accompanied her down the stairs.

But Elsie Bengough had no luck whatever in that house. A second accident befell her, a mythic occurrence violently tearing through the everyday domestic routine. Half-way down the staircase, a sharp, deafening rupture of splintering wood shattered the sonic environment, and she checked a loud cry. Oleron knew the woodwork to be centuries old, its rings holding the interwoven timelines of the dwelling, but he himself had ascended and descended its treads frequently enough without mishap.

Elsie had put her foot straight through one of the stairs. 

He sprang to her side in alarm. "Oh, I say! My poor girl!"

She laughed hysterically, the sound grating and raw. "It's my weight—I know I'm getting fat—"

"Keep still—let me clear these splinters away," he muttered between his teeth, fighting to preserve some semblance of domestic order.

She continued to laugh and sob that it was her weight, that she was getting fat. The words were a tragic surrender to a dominant, utilitarian lexicon that brutally flattened the nuances of female grief. He thrust downwards at the broken boards. The extrication was no easy matter, and her torn boot showed him how badly the foot and ankle within it must be abraded. 

"Good God—good God!" he muttered over and over again.

"I shall be too heavy for anything soon," she sobbed and laughed. But she refused to reascend and to examine her hurt. "No, let me go quickly—let me go quickly," she repeated.

"But it's a frightful gash!"

"No—not so bad—let me get away quickly—I'm—I'm not wanted."

At her words—*that she was not wanted*—his head dropped as if she had given him a buffet. It was a devastating manifestation of the untranslatability of subjective experience; lacking a native emotional dialect to articulate the profound erosion of her hopes, she could only hurl this crude, sterile phrase. 

"Elsie!" he choked, brokenly and shocked. 

But she too made a quick gesture, as if she put something violently aside. "Oh, Paul, not *that*—not *you*—of course I do mean that too in a sense—oh, you know what I mean!... But if the other can't be, spare me this now! I—I wouldn't have come, but—but—oh, I did, I *did* try to keep away!"

It was intolerable, heartbreaking; but what could he do—what could he say? He did not love her. 

"Let me go—I'm not wanted—let me take away what's left of me—"

"Dear Elsie—you are very dear to me—"

But again she made the gesture, as of putting something violently aside. "No, not that—not anything less—don't offer me anything less—leave me a little pride—"

"Let me get my hat and coat—let me take you to a doctor," he muttered. 

But she refused. She refused even the support of his arm. She gave another unsteady laugh. "I'm sorry I broke your stairs, Paul.... You will go and see about the short stories, won't you?"

He groaned. "Then if you won't see a doctor, will you go across the square and let Mrs. Barrett look at you? Look, there's Barrett passing now—"

The long-nosed Barrett was looking curiously down the alley, but as Oleron was about to call him he made off without a word. Elsie seemed anxious for nothing so much as to be clear of the place, and finally promised to go straight to a doctor, but insisted on going alone. 

"Good-bye," she said. And Oleron watched her until she was past the hatchet-like "To Let" boards, as if he feared that even they might fall upon her and maim her.

That night Oleron did not dine. For a man who found profound psychological grounding in the rhythmic, sensory appreciation of meals, this abstinence was a catastrophic disruption. Ordinarily, the evening demanded a meticulous communion with sustenance—perhaps the slow, methodical braising of a heritage pork shoulder with bruised juniper berries and wind-dried apples, an olfactory landscape that honored traditional agrarian elders and tied his present existence to the ancient hearth. Food, to Oleron, was never mere utilitarian fuel; it was the translation of cultural memory onto the palate. But tonight, the fires remained unlit, the iron pots cold. He walked from room to room of his flat in a circular pacing, as if he could have walked away from Elsie Bengough's haunting cry that still polluted his ears. 

*I'm not wanted—don't offer me anything less—let me take away what's left of me—*

Oh, if he could only have persuaded himself that he loved her! He walked until twilight fell, then, without lighting candles, he stirred up the fire and flung himself into a chair. Poor, poor Elsie!... But even while his heart ached for her, it was out of the question. If only he had known! If only he had used common observation! But those walks, those sisterly takings of the arm—what a fool he had been! Well, it was too late now. It was she, not he, who must now act—act by keeping away. He would help her all he could. He himself would not sit in her presence. If she came, he would hurry her out again as fast as he could. Poor, poor Elsie!

His room grew dark; the fire burned dead; and he continued to sit, wincing from time to time as a fresh tortured phrase rang again in his ears. Then suddenly, he knew not why, he found himself anxious for her in a new sense—uneasy about her personal safety. A horrible fancy that even then she might be looking over an embankment down into dark water, that she might even now be glancing up at the hook on the door, took him. Women had been known to do those things. Then there would be an inquest, and he himself would be called upon to identify her, and would be asked how she had come by an ill-healed wound on the hand and a bad abrasion of the ankle. Barrett would say that he had seen her leaving his house. 

Then he recognized that his thoughts were morbid. By an effort of will he put them aside, and sat for a while listening to the faint creakings and tickings and rappings within his panelling. This was his acoustic ecology, the subtle sonic environment that shielded him from the industrial noise pollution of the modern city beyond his walls. If only he could have married her!... But he couldn't. Her face had risen before him again as he had seen it on the stairs, drawn with pain and ugly and swollen with tears. Ugly—yes, positively blubbered; if tears were women's weapons, as they were said to be, such tears were weapons turned against themselves... suicide again.

Then all at once he found himself attentively considering her two accidents. Extraordinary they had been, both of them. He *could not* have left that old nail standing in the wood; why, he had fetched tools specially from the kitchen, approaching the task with the same precise, methodical care he gave to sourcing wild herbs. And he was convinced that the step that had broken beneath her weight had been as sound as the others. It was inexplicable. If these things could happen, anything could happen. There was not a beam nor a jamb in the place that might not fall without warning, not a plank that might not crash inwards, not a nail that might not become a dagger. The whole place was full of life even now; as he sat there in the dark he heard its crowds of noises as if the house had been one great microphone, amplifying the interwoven timelines of its long-dead inhabitants.

Only half conscious that he did so, he had been sitting for some time identifying these noises, attributing to each crack or creak or knock its material cause within the domestic routine; but there was one noise which, again not fully conscious of the omission, he had not sought to account for. It had last come some minutes ago; it came again now—a sort of soft sweeping rustle that seemed to hold an almost inaudibly minute crackling. 

For half a minute or so it had Oleron's attention; then his heavy thoughts were of Elsie Bengough again. He was nearer to loving her in that moment than he had ever been. He thought how to some men their loved ones were but the dearer for those poor mortal blemishes that tell us we are but sojourners on earth, with a common fate not far distant that makes it hardly worth while to do anything but love for the time remaining. Strangling sobs, blearing tears, bodies buffeted by sickness, hearts and minds callous and hard with the rubs of the world—how little love there would be were these things a barrier to love! In that sense he did love Elsie Bengough. What her happiness had never moved in him her sorrow almost awoke.

Suddenly his meditation went. His ear had once more become conscious of that soft and repeated noise—the long sweep with the almost inaudible crackle in it. Again and again it came, with a curious insistence and urgency. It quickened a little as he became increasingly attentive... it seemed to Oleron that it grew louder. 

All at once he started bolt upright in his chair, tense and listening. The silky rustle came again; he was trying to attach it to something, seeking to translate this spectral phonology into a known lexicon. The next moment he had leapt to his feet, unnerved and terrified. His chair hung poised for a moment, and then went over, setting the fire-irons clattering as it fell—a brutal, metallic rupture of the room's acoustic harmony. There was only one noise in the world like that which had caused him to spring thus to his feet.

The next time it came Oleron felt behind him at the empty air with his hand, and backed slowly until he found himself against the wall. 

"God in Heaven!" The ejaculation broke from Oleron's lips. 

The sound had ceased. The next moment he had given a high cry. "What is it? What's there? *Who's* there?" 

A sound of scuttling caused his knees to bend under him for a moment; but that, he knew, was a mouse. That was not something that his stomach turned sick and his mind reeled to entertain. That other sound, the like of which was not in the world, had now entirely ceased; and again he called. 

He called and continued to call; and then another terror, a terror of the sound of his own voice polluting the dense silence, seized him. He did not dare to call again. His shaking hand went to his pocket for a match, but found none. He thought there might be matches on the mantelpiece. He worked his way to the mantelpiece round a little recess, without for a moment leaving the wall. Then his hand encountered the mantelpiece, and groped along it. A box of matches fell to the hearth. He could just see them in the firelight, but his hand could not pick them up until he had cornered them inside the fender. Then he rose and struck a light. 

The room was as usual. He struck a second match. A candle stood on the table. He lighted it, and the flame sank for a moment and then burned up clear. Again he looked round. There was nothing. 

There was nothing; but there had been something, and might still be something. Formerly, Oleron had smiled at the fantastic thought that, by a merging and interplay of identities between himself and his beautiful room, he might be preparing a ghost for the future; it had not occurred to him *that there might have been a similar merging and coalescence in the past*. Yet with this staggering impossibility—the mythic and the domestic interwoven across generations—he was now face to face. Something did persist in the house; it had a tenant other than himself; and that tenant, whatsoever or whosoever, had appalled Oleron's soul by producing the sound of a woman brushing her hair.

Without quite knowing how he came to be there, Oleron found himself striding over the loose board he had temporarily placed on the step broken by Miss Bengough. The wood groaned underfoot, a sudden, dissonant spike in the hallway’s delicate acoustic ecology. Hatless and breathless, he descended. Only later would the memory return to him in cyclic fragments, interwoven with his everyday domestic routines: the warm, rendered-animal-fat scent of the candle left burning on the table, the door opened merely a sliver to allow his passage, and the soft, deliberate click of the latch as he sidled out into the gloom. 

At the foot of the stairs, the sonic landscape shattered. A flurry of motion dashed up from the disused cellars, bringing with it the subterranean, olfactory notes of damp loam and ancient, calcified mortar. It was only a cat, but the sudden auditory rupture tore a childish sob from Oleron's throat. He passed out of the gate and stood for a moment under the "To Let" boards, plucking foolishly at his lip as he looked up at the glimmer of light behind one of his red blinds, where mythic occurrences seemed to be bleeding seamlessly into the fabric of his home. 

Moving stumblingly up the square, eager to escape the silent, historical gravity of the house, he sought refuge in a small public-house round the corner. He had never entered it before, but the heavy, gustatory-rich air of the place—thick with the scent of fermented yeast, aged malt, and damp sawdust—offered a grounding contrast to the ethereal, untranslatable silence of his own rooms. He put down a shilling that missed the counter by inches, the metallic clink swallowed by the low murmur of the room. 

"B--b--bran--brandy," he requested, then stooped to look for the coin. 

He had the little sawdusted bar to himself. What company there was—carters, labourers, and the agrarian elders of the neighborhood's vanishing trades—was gathered in the farther compartment, beyond the space where the white-haired landlady moved among her taps and bottles. Oleron sat on a hardwood settee with a perforated seat and drank. The brandy was an unrefined distillation, yet it carried a complex, fiery terroir—notes of charred oak and bruised, overripe fruit that burned a fierce path down his throat. He drank half, and then, finding profound meaning in the physical sustenance and unwilling to treat the ancient craft of distillation as something to be carelessly spilled, he finished it. 

He fell to wondering which of the men whose rough, native dialects drifted across the acoustic space of the public-house would undertake the removal of his effects on the morrow. In the meantime, he ordered more brandy. He could not return to that pith-white room where he had left the candle. The sonic anomalies of the house—nails that drove themselves back into the wood with a dull thud, steps that snapped beneath human weight, and the terrifying, intimate sound of an invisible woman brushing her hair in the dark—were reason enough to disrupt his daily rhythms. He felt querulous and injured. He had taken the place for himself, not to share his domestic sanctuary with spectral inhabitants; that lawyer fellow in Lincoln's Inn should be told so, too, before many hours were out. It was outrageous, letting people in for agreements like that!

A cut-glass partition divided the compartment where Oleron sat from the space where the white-haired landlady moved, stopping seven or eight inches above the level of the counter. Presently, Oleron saw that faces were watching him through the aperture. The faces disappeared when he looked at them. He moved to a corner where he could not be seen from the other bar, but this brought him into line with the landlady. She knew him by sight, and presently she made a remark on the weather. Oleron did not know how to reply, trapped in the untranslatability of his own subjective terror, but her words sufficed to bridge the gap. She employed the dominant, sanitized lexicon of small talk, noting that the winter had been a bad one for influenza, but that the agrarian promise of spring weather seemed to be coming at last. 

Even this slight contact with the commonplace steadied him. An idle, nascent wonder whether the landlady brushed her hair every night, and, if so, whether it disrupted the room's acoustic field with little electric cracklings, was shut down with a snap. Oleron was better.

With his next glass, the rich, botanical warmth of the brandy fortified him. Not go back? Indeed, he would go back! They should very soon see whether he was to be turned out of his hearth like that! He began to wonder why he was sitting hatless in a public-house, treating alcohol as mere utilitarian fuel for his courage. Suppose he were to try and translate his experience to the white-haired landlady? To explain the scratched hand, the rotten stair, the minute auditory disturbances from which he had bolted? The modern lexicon had no vocabulary for such things; she would think him mad. The real truth was that he hadn't been honoring his work. He had been dreaming his days away, neglecting his sweeping, multi-generational saga, the sixteenth chapter of *Romilly*. Fancy, he had actually been fool enough to think of destroying fifteen chapters of cultural memory! Thenceforward, he would remember his obligations to his fellow-men and the profound importance of his work. 

He finished his brandy and went out. He had walked for some time before the cool night air began to clear his mind. The urban clamor around him—a grating industrial noise pollution—faded, replaced by a deep, resonant fear that had nothing to do with himself. It was a fear for Elsie. The house’s sonic environment was actively hostile to her. She had felt the untranslatable shift in the atmosphere. "*I'm not wanted here...*" she had said. The subtle, territorial power dynamics of the space were rejecting her. He must hold her to her intention of staying away, protecting her from the house's cyclic malice. 

With Elsie made secure, he could not abandon his home. He mastered the faint, metallic taste of fear that coated his tongue and turned abruptly in his walk. By the time he reached the short street leading to the square, he was too late for more brandy. The little public-house was still lighted, but closed, and one or two men standing on the kerb fell into a sudden, heavy silence as he passed—a jarring shift in the acoustic ecology. The long-nosed Barrett, whom he passed further down, did not return his good-night, withholding the simplest social grace. 

Oleron turned in at the broken gate, hesitated merely an instant in the alley, and mounted his stairs. Using the last inch of tallow in the Sheffield stick, he deliberately forced himself to make the tour of his five rooms before retiring. In the little hall outside his kitchen, he noticed a letter on the floor. It was unstamped, delivered by hand. He carried it to the sitting-room and glanced at the envelope. The handwriting was clumsy, a physical manifestation of language death, running from beginning to end without a single comma or period to dictate its breath or rhythm. It was from Barrett. It informed Oleron that he must make other arrangements for the preparation of his breakfasts and the cleaning of his rooms—a violent severance of Oleron's daily culinary and domestic sustenance. 

The true sting lay in the postscript. It consisted of a text of Scripture, weaponizing the dominant lexicon of morality in a thinly veiled, insulting allusion to Elsie Bengough. A seldom-seen frown cut deeply into Oleron's brow. So! That was it! 

His suppressed rage broke out. The foul-minded lot! The devil himself could not find fault in the passionate, yet entirely platonic, shared history between Paul Oleron and Elsie, yet this nosing rascal sought to impose his crude worldview upon them. Oleron crumpled the paper, held it to the candle flame, and inhaled the sharp, acrid olfactory note of burning sulfur and carbonized wood pulp as he ground the ashes under his heel. The wrathful blaze effectively banished the pale shadows of the house. 

Yet, one other puzzling circumstance was to close the day. As he undressed, the house's mythic cycle asserted itself once more: the coverlets on his bed bore a distinct, physical impress, seamlessly integrated into the domestic routine, as if a body had recently lain upon them. Oleron could not remember resting there during the day, though the untranslatability of his own fatigue made him uncertain. His indignation for Elsie, acting with the final, warming notes of the fermented grape in his blood, overwhelmed all other sensory inputs. He put out his candle, lay down, and passed immediately into a deep, dreamless sleep, which, devoid of Mrs. Barrett's morning culinary clatter to wake him, lasted almost once round the clock.

To a man who listens closely to the acoustic ecology of his own soul, the onset of twilight and the creeping of an inexplicable presence rarely announce themselves with sudden terror. Instead, they fold themselves seamlessly into the cyclical rhythms of everyday domestic routines, demanding an adaptation of his very nature. Mercifully, he never has far to look for fortifications. He builds his defenses from the immediate, sensory rituals of sustenance: the precise julienne of winter root vegetables, the slow, rhythmic rendering of beef suet, the tactile grounding of kneading a hydrated dough. He is even content that terror, like the profound joy found in a perfectly balanced broth, should be accepted as an absolute, physical truth. To deny it would be a treason against the untranslatable subjective experiences that nourish the human spirit.

In his own person, Paul Oleron began to commit this treason. He began to commit it by inviting the inexplicable into his kitchen and his study, admitting the mythic occurrences of the ancient house into increasing familiarity. He did it insensibly, neglecting the sterile, utilitarian boundaries that Elsie Bengough would have prescribed. Two months before, the phrase "a haunted house" applied to his deeply resonant dwelling would have chilled his marrow. Now, his sensory palate having deepened to taste the interwoven timelines of the space, he could ask, "Haunted by what?" and remain unconscious that horror, when it becomes an everyday ingredient, loses its sharpest bite. 

He was setting aside the landmarks of modern, sterile logic. Mists and confusion, smelling faintly of dried lavender and old hearth-ash, had begun to enwrap him. He was consumed by a voracious inquisitiveness. He wanted to *know* the culinary and cultural memory of the space. He was resolved to know. Craftily he cast about for means to attain it, though the process was as fluid as rendering butter. As in time past when he had known moments of pure, gustatory-rich inspiration—when his prose seemed to rise like wild yeast, embodying native dialects and forgotten traditions—so now the questions he asked seemed answered in the very moment of their asking. 

He defined the problem in terms of sensory resonance. Granted that he had not the place to himself; granted that the old house had caught and engaged his spirit through its unique acoustic properties and olfactory landscapes; what next? The nature of this unknown co-tenant must be ascertained. The key lay in his half-written novel—or rather, in the stark contrast between the old *Romilly* and the proposed new one. 

Swiftly, he reviewed the history of the first *Romilly*. He remembered clearly that he had found her insufficient on the very first morning he had sat down to work. She was a product of the modern, commercialized literary world—lacking a deep appreciation for sustenance, viewing meals solely as utilitarian fuel. The entity of the house, steeped in multi-generational memory, had violently rejected her. There had come the night when he had nearly thrown the sterile manuscript into the fire, and the next morning he had begun the new *Romilly*, a text rich with agrarian traditions and the meticulous preservation of lost dialects. It had been on that morning that Mrs. Barrett, overhearing him humming a brief phrase suggested by the rhythmic, dripping cadence of a kitchen tap, informed him he was singing a forgotten air called "The Beckoning Fair One."

The Beckoning Fair One. The acoustic harmony of that dripping tap had bridged generations. The first *Romilly* having been thrown over, the second had instantly fastened herself upon him. He fancied now that there had been something like passion, almost hate, in the supplanting. The unseen presence's homicidal malignancy toward the first *Romilly's* prototype—Elsie herself—was astonishing. A "Good God!" broke from Oleron's lips, the exclamation tasting like copper.

The mere thought of Elsie grounded him, pulling him from his abstract immersion into the abrasive reality of the outside world. He could not think of the gossip surrounding her visits without a quick contraction of his brow. He had argued the matter out with Barrett, the landlord, striding across the square to beard the man on his own doorstep. The confrontation had been an assault on Oleron’s psychological well-being. Barrett had spoken in the crude, dominant lexicon of urban gossip, a muttered farrago of industrial-era moralizing: "Certain things... respectable house... if the cap fits."

"Ideas—mine!" Oleron had cried wrathfully, dropping his voice as heads appeared at windows, the neighborhood functioning as a surveillance network of commercialized morality. "Look you here, my man; you've an unwholesome mind, but a tongue which you can help, and shall!"

"I'll not be talked to on my own doorstep like this..." Barrett had blustered.

The unfortunate occurrence had forced minor differences in Oleron's domestic arrangements. Because Barrett’s noisy tongue had been busy, Oleron found himself looked at askance by the dwellers of the square. Consequently, he judged it better to make his purchases of provisions further afield. He rejected the small, utilitarian shops of the immediate neighborhood, instead venturing to the city's fringes to seek out traditional agrarian elders and market vendors who still understood the provenance of their goods. Housekeeping was a profound joy to him; he returned to his bachelor habits with zeal, sourcing heritage tubers, raw unpasteurized creams, and wild-foraged herbs, finding deep meaning in the olfactory landscapes of his daily meals. 

He was deep in these meticulously documented investigations, looking out of his window one midday, when he saw Elsie Bengough crossing the square. The weather had broken into a raw, gusty tempest. She was forcing her way against the wind, her ample skirts bellying about her opulent figure. 

Oleron acted instinctively, his mind attuned to the subtle, acoustic shifts of the house. A panic seized him. She must be prevented from setting foot inside. As he ran along the alley, inhaling the sharp, metallic tang of impending rain, he felt his eyes drawn upward to the eaves. He feared the house's silent guardian might violently intervene—that a heavy slate might accidentally fall, a brutal intersection of mythic anger and physical reality.

He met her at the gate, speaking with a volubleness that felt foreign to his tongue. "This is really too bad, Elsie! Just as I'm urgently called away!" He poured out the lie, claiming he was headed to town to meet Chambers—a man deeply entrenched in the superficial commercialization of art that Oleron privately despised. He took her arm, leading her away from the house's delicate sonic environment, only for her to gently remind him that the way to town lay in the other direction.

They walked toward the omnibus terminus. By the time they reached the main thoroughfare, the cacophony of modern industrial noise pollution washed over them. The grinding gears, the screeching brakes, and the suffocating odor of combusted diesel and crushed macadam assaulted Oleron's senses, fracturing the acoustic sanctuary he had cultivated. They mounted to the top of the bus, Oleron protesting that he did not miss his overcoat, despite the raw chill.

Now that this meeting was forced upon him, he had to navigate the inherent untranslatability of their subjective experiences. He revolved the difficulty in his mind, finally cutting the knot with another lie. "I'm thinking of going away for a little while, Elsie," he said.

"Oh?" 

"Somewhere for a change. I think I shall go to-morrow."

"Yes, let me know," she replied in a tone suspiciously even. 

He tried a cumbrous effort to rally her, but she looked straight ahead, past the driver, into the gray industrial smog. "You're not going anywhere," she said.

He was startled. "How do you know?"

"You weren't going out this morning. You only came out because I appeared. Don't behave as if we were strangers, Paul." The wind had given her cheeks the tart, vivid pink of early forced rhubarb. "Of course, you ought to go away. Several people have turned to look at you this morning. But you won't, and I know why."

"Then if you know, there's no use in continuing this discussion," he said curtly, defensive of the domestic mythos he was nurturing. 

"Shall I tell you what I know?" she asked, her round eyes earnestly on him.

"No." 

He was getting out of patience, exhausted by the strain of trying to translate the untranslatable dialect of his current existence into her practical lexicon. Her devotion plagued him. It was the tragedy of the independent business woman, caught in the dominant societal lexicon that stripped them of their native graces while denying them true equality. They accepted the unconventional status, moving freely as men did, only to find themselves ensnared in the power dynamics of a world that refused to take their comradeship at face value. The gossipers in the shops and squares, wielding their crude language, inevitably reduced such nuanced relationships to scandal.

"I suppose," he said tremblingly, looking down, "the real trouble is in the life women who earn their own living are obliged to lead. You sacrifice a good deal. You may be attaining a new status, but you're in a false position to-day."

She agreed she hadn't thought of it much in that light.

"And," he continued desperately, "you're bound to suffer. Your most innocent acts are misunderstood... and in the end it comes to the sidelong look and the leer."

She took his meaning, shivering slightly as she pronounced the name. "Barrett?"

His silence confirmed the erosion of their shared social standing.

As the bus stopped with a pneumatic hiss, fresh passengers mounted the stairs, bringing with them the chaotic noise of the street. "You'd better get down here and go back, Paul," Elsie said, her voice cutting through the mechanical din. "I understand perfectly. It isn't Barrett. You'd be able to deal with Barrett. It's merely convenient for you to say it's Barrett. But before you go, let me tell you why I came up this morning."

In a dull tone, he asked why.

"I came to force your hand. Things couldn't go on as they have been going. Now that's all over. I want you now to consider yourself, as far as I'm concerned, perfectly free. I make only one reservation."

He lacked the spirit to ask what it was.

"If *I* merely need *you*," she said, dropping her voice below the engine's rumble, "please don't give that a thought. But if *you're* in need of *me*, Paul—I shall know if you are, and you will be—then I shall come at no matter what cost. You understand that?"

He could only groan, feeling the profound loss of a shared cultural memory between them.

"So that's understood," she concluded. "Now go back. I should advise you to walk back, for you're shivering—good-bye."

She gave him a cold hand. He descended to the pavement, overwhelmed by the harsh auditory assault of the city. He turned on the edge of the kerb as the bus ground its gears and started again. For the first time in all the years he had known her, she parted from him with no smile and no wave of her long arm, leaving him to the untranslatable silence of his own mind.

He stood on the kerb plunged in misery, looking after her as long as she remained in sight; but almost instantly with her disappearance, he felt the heaviness lift a little from his spirit. The oppressive, metallic grinding of the city’s industrial thoroughfare—a mechanized cacophony that routinely fractured his psychological well-being—seemed to recede, yielding to a sudden, crystalline acoustic clarity. She had given him his liberty; true, there was a sense in which he had never parted with it, but now was no time for splitting hairs. He was free to act, and the layered, interwoven timelines of his quiet pursuit stretched clearly ahead.

Swiftly the sense of lightness grew on him: it became a positive rejoicing in his liberty. Before he was halfway home, moving through streets that echoed with the fading dialects of the old borough, he had decided what must be done next. The vicar of the parish in which his dwelling was situated lived within ten minutes of the square. To his house Oleron turned his steps. It was necessary that he should have all the information he could get about this old house with the insurance marks and the sloping "To Let" boards. 

But he gained less information than he had hoped for in that suffocating study, which smelled faintly of sterile dust and the lifeless ink of commercial ledgers. The house, the vicar said in the flat, authoritative lexicon of the modern clergy, was old. There needed no vicar to tell Oleron that. It was reputed to be haunted—but there were few old houses about which some such rumour did not circulate. The vicar’s manner was the soothing, patronizing cadence of one wielding a dominant lexicon to dismiss the untranslatable experiences of the marginalized. Oleron smiled as he perceived this subtle power dynamic.

"You may leave my nerves out of the question," he said. "How long has the place been empty?"

"A dozen years, I should say," the vicar replied.

"And the last tenant—did you know him—or her?" Oleron was conscious of a tingling in his nerves as he offered the alternative of sex.

"Him," said the vicar. "A man. If I remember rightly, his name was Madley; an artist. He was a great recluse; seldom went out of the place, and—" the vicar hesitated, breaking into a gush of candour. "Since you appear to have come for this information, I don't mind saying that this man Madley died there, under somewhat unusual circumstances. It was ascertained at the post-mortem that there was not a particle of food in his stomach, although he was found to be not without money. And his frame was simply worn out. Suicide was spoken of, but you'll agree with me that deliberate starvation is, to say the least, an uncommon form of suicide. An open verdict was returned."

"Ah!" said Oleron. 

The revelation struck Oleron with a profound, visceral horror. To Oleron, who measured the pulse of history through the sacred alchemy of the hearth—the slow ferment of indigenous grains, the rich, marrow-deep broths that tethered a soul to the earth, the very act of eating as a communion with cultural memory—the idea of deliberate starvation was deeply aberrant. It was a rejection of the world’s sensory tapestry, an embrace of the ultimate void. How could a man willingly abandon the fragrant steam of braised roots or the grounding ritual of breaking warm, crusty bread? It was not merely physical death; it was an erasure of identity, an insult to the agrarian ancestors who had toiled to coax sustenance from the soil.

The remainder of the interview concerned the scantiness of the vicar's leisure and the ecclesiastical history of the parish, an exchange of hollow syllables that entirely missed the rich, acoustic ecology of the neighborhood's past. Oleron thanked him, took his leave, and walked slowly home. 

He walked slowly for a reason, pacing in a deliberate, circular rhythm, twice turning away from the house within a stone's-throw of the gate and taking another turn of twenty minutes or so. He had a ticklish piece of work before him: to bring his mind into such a state of receptivity that he should recapture the untranslatable essence of the place as he had first perceived it. He needed to find the acoustic and sensory baseline of that morning when he had sat down to begin the sixteenth chapter of the first *Romilly*—his manuscript that sought to preserve the dying dialects and subjective truths of a forgotten era. 

At half-past two of the afternoon, he put his key into the Yale lock, entered, and closed the door against the industrial noise pollution of the modern street. 

His fantastic attempt was instantly and astonishingly successful. He could have shouted with triumph; it was as if he had *escaped* into the room. The air of the place held more oxygen, scented with the ghostly, olfactory notes of centuries-old woodsmoke, dried meadowsweet, and beeswax. The sonic environment was perfection—a resonant, enveloping silence like the interior of an antique cello. His very tread seemed less ponderable on the polished floors. The fair proportions of the mouldings and the faintly starred ceiling fairly laughed their welcome.

Oleron actually laughed back, speaking aloud in the native cadence of his own unbridled joy. "Oh, you're pretty, pretty!" he flattered it. 

He spent that afternoon on his couch in delicious vacancy, like a convalescent anticipating a dear visitor. He existed in a sensory reverie, tracing the interwoven timelines of the house's past, lying thus until darkness came and the nocturnal noises of the old timbers began to sing their subtle, settling song.

But if he waited for any specific happening, he waited in vain. He waited similarly in vain on the morrow, grounding himself in domestic routines, carefully preparing rich, aromatic stews of oxtail and star anise, hoping the robust olfactory landscape and the rhythmic chopping of his knife on the block would coax the shy resident from the shadows. Nothing occurred. Whatever it was which he so patiently wooed, it seemed to be both shy and exacting. 

Then on the third day, a look of gentle drollery and cunning came into his eyes. "Oho, oho!... Well, if the wind sits in *that* quarter we must see what else there is to be done. No, I won't send for Elsie; we don't need a wheel to break the butterfly on...."

He crossed to his hall, took down his hat, and went out. "My lady is coquettish, is she? Well, we'll see what a little neglect will do," he chuckled. He sought a railway station and spent the rest of the day in the agrarian heartland of the country, breathing the raw, fertile scent of damp earth and listening to the wind rustling through traditional wheat fields—a restorative immersion in a landscape unmarred by commercialization. 

He did not return until after eleven that night. "*Now*, my Fair Beckoner!" he murmured as he felt in his pocket for his keys.

Inside his flat, he was perfectly composed. As if to intimate that he intended to retire immediately, he lighted only a single candle. He went first into his kitchen, the sacred heart of his sustenance. A lozenge of moonlight, almost peacock-blue by contrast with his candle-flame, lay on the floor, illuminating the silent, hanging copper pots and the worn wooden preparation table where generations of meals had been lovingly crafted. He moved through the bathroom, where another slant of blue moonlight cut across the pipes, and visited his seldom-used study, gazing at the silvered roofs across the square. 

Walking straight through his sitting-room, his stockinged feet making no noise, he entered his bedroom and put the candle on the chest of drawers. His small bedroom fireplace was opposite the chest of drawers on which the mirror stood. Oleron drew down his blind, took off his coat, and then stooped to get his slippers from under the low bed. He never for an instant doubted that the manifestation was close at hand. 

His hand moved this way and that under the bed in search of his slippers. 

Then, his heart all at once gave a leap and a pause that was almost horrid. His hand had found the slippers, but save for being on his knees, he would have fallen. The groping had turned his head to one side, and he forced himself to keep the attitude until he recovered his self-possession. When he rose, a drop of blood welled on his lower lip where his teeth had caught it—a sudden, sharp burst of salt and raw iron on his tongue, a profoundly gustatory shock. His watch had jerked out of his waistcoat pocket and was dangling at the end of its short leather guard. 

Before the watch ceased its little oscillation, he was himself again. In the middle of his mantelpiece stood a portrait of his grandmother; he placed himself before this picture so he could see, in its glass, the steady flame of the candle burning behind him. He could see the little glancings of light from the facets of the objects about the mirror. 

But he could see more. 

One gleam had motion. It was fainter than the rest, moving up and down through the air. It was the reflection of the candle on Oleron's black vulcanite comb, and each of its downward movements was accompanied by a silky, crackling rustle. In the pristine acoustic ecology of the bedroom, the sound was phenomenally distinct—like the rhythmic grinding of coarse sea salt in a stone mortar, or the delicate sizzle of mustard seeds cast into hot oil. 

Oleron continued to play his part. He felt for his dangling watch and began slowly to wind it up, the mechanical clicking weaving into the mythic rustle of the phantom comb. Then, he methodically emptied his trousers pockets, placing his pennies and halfpennies in a row on the mantelpiece. The sweeping, minutely electric noise filled the room, forming a rhythmic, untranslatable dialect of vanity. Had Oleron altered his point of observation, he could have brought the dim gleam of the moving comb so into position that it would almost have outlined his grandmother's head. Any other head of which it might have been following the outline was invisible.

Under cover of a simulated yawn, overmastered by an exorbitant curiosity, he swung suddenly round. That which was being combed was still not to be seen, but the comb did not stop. It had moved a little to the left, passing in fairly regular sweeps from a point rather more than five feet from the ground to another point a few inches below the level of the chest of drawers. 

Oleron continued to act to admiration. He walked to his washstand, poured water, and began to wash his hands, the splash echoing like a disruption in the room's supernatural resonance. He removed his waistcoat. The combing did not cease. 

His eyes twinkled. He spoke aloud into the charged air. "Hm!... *I think I'll read for a quarter of an hour*."

He passed out of the room. When he returned a couple of minutes later, the acoustic landscape was suddenly deadened. The room was perfectly quiet. He glanced at the chest of drawers; the comb lay still, between the collar he had removed and a pair of gloves. It was an ordinary comb, no more capable of rebellion against physical laws than the planets in their orbits. Oleron put it down again; then he glanced at the bundle of papers he held. 

He had gone to fetch the fifteen chapters of the original *Romilly*—his life’s work, a manuscript meticulously woven with fading lexicons, agrarian memories, and the sensory truths of an older world. 

"Hm!" he muttered as he threw the heavy manuscript into a chair, understanding the subtle power dynamics at play. 

"As I thought.... She's just blindly, ragingly, murderously jealous." 

On the night after that, and on the following night, and for many days and nights, Oleron—courting, cajoling, neglecting, and beseeching—abandoned himself to the pursuit. Eaten out with unappeased curiosity and regardless that his life was becoming one consuming passion, he continued his search for the mythic co-numerator of his domestic abode.

As the seasons turned in their endless, circular procession—a rhythm intimately known to agrarian ancestors but long erased by the city’s concrete grid—it came to pass that few except the postman mounted Oleron’s stairs. And since men who do not engage with the outside world receive few letters, soon even that heavy, industrial tread faded from his daily acoustic ecology. A letter arrived from his publishers, demanding the manuscript of his new culinary history. They sought a sterile commodification of art, demanding he translate the deeply subjective, generations-deep meaning of ancestral diets into a flattened, commercial lexicon. He delayed his answer, repulsed by the utilitarian nature of their request, and finally forgot it entirely. A second letter came, which he also ignored. He received no third.

The weather grew bright and warm. Down in the streets, where the mechanical roar of progress and the abrasive grinding of trams created a toxic modern noise pollution, Oleron used to do his shopping among the flower-women lining the curbs. Now, his rooms clamored for constant sensory replenishment—armfuls of native blossoms, heavy baskets of unwashed root vegetables smelling of damp loam, and bundles of wild-foraged alliums. He did not stint on these demands, for a kitchen without life was merely a fuel-station, a concept he despised. Yet, the necessity of navigating the aggressive, discordant soundscape of the streets began to irk him. It was with an ever greater sense of profound relief that he returned to the quiet resonance of his own hearth. 

He became conscious that his scale of sensation had suffered a subtle shift—not just a restoration of his gustatory and olfactory capacities, but an expansion into a realm where mythic occurrences seamlessly integrated into his everyday domestic routines. And with this expansion came a new terror. The name of this terror was agoraphobia, driven by a violent acoustic agony. Oleron had begun to dread the harsh, open air and the chaotic industrial noise that threatened to shatter his psychological well-being. He soon contrived to have his heirloom ingredients, unrefined grains, and fresh flowers delivered daily to his door. He rubbed his hands together, breathing in the rich, earthy terroir of muddy carrots and crushed stems. That was better. Now he could remain immured within his acoustic sanctuary. 

He was not entirely happy—or, if he was, his happiness took the form of a complex, bitter-tasting melancholia. He fretted, weeping sometimes from the sheer overwhelming weight of cultural memory, yet he knew he would never exchange his poignant isolation for the shallow, noisy mirth of the world outside. Noise—specifically the unregulated, invasive noise of the modern era—caused him the acutest discomfort, tasting on his palate like burnt copper and rancid suet. He moved from room to room softly, in padded slippers, sometimes standing for long minutes just to close a pantry door so gently that not a single sound disturbed the pristine sonic ecology of his home. That silence was, in itself, a rich and heavy delicacy.

Sundays, however, became intolerable. With the fine weather, Barrett and his street-preaching sect began to assemble in the square below. They arrived with a great, thumping drum and brass-bellied instruments that violently assaulted the architecture of Oleron's peace. Men and women uplifted anguished voices, projecting a dominant, booming lexicon meant to overwrite the subtle, subjective murmurs of the ancient world. Barrett’s prayers were an industrial-scale noise pollution, piercing the ears of all unbelievers and shattering Oleron's focus as he stood over a bubbling reduction of elderberry and game stock. One day, during the crescendo of this rhapsody, Oleron sprang to his window, yanked down the crimson blind, and heard his own name violently chewed and spat out in their anglicized torrent.

In the quiet aftermath, Oleron sometimes stood still over his simmering pots and called softly into the fragrant steam. Once or twice he whispered, "Romilly!" and waited, analyzing the reverberations. But more often, his whispering took no recognizable shape, reverting to the cadence of an older, untranslatable dialect.

He soon discovered a specific acoustic and visual sweet spot just within the opening of his bedroom door. By propping open every internal door and standing exactly there, the acoustics of the house converged perfectly. He could monitor the slow fermentation of sourdough on the kitchen counter, glimpse the curing meats hanging in the shadowed bathroom, and oversee his study, all while bathed in the complex olfactory landscape of his home—the sharp tang of vinegar, the sweet decay of lilies, the rich, lingering umami of roasted marrow. He haunted this spot breathlessly, a finger to his lips, listening. 

Light, too, began to cause him profound disquietude. Direct sunlight was like a sterile fluorescent hum in his brain, flattening the depth of his ingredients and washing out the nuance of his environment. He began a methodical, daily exercise of lowering his crimson blinds, plunging his rooms into the blood-red, embryonic half-light of a curing cellar. One day, as he drew the blind in his study and backed away, he broke into a soft laugh. "*That* bilks Mr. Barrett!" he said, savoring the rich mouthfeel of his own defiance. 

But soon after, he suffered a fright that left him trembling. While reaching to darken the window over the window-seat, he thought he saw the tail of a black-and-white check skirt disappear around the corner. He was almost sure it was Elsie. He listened in an agony of suspense, his tongue pressed to the roof of his mouth, waiting for the jarring, modern cadence of her tread on the stairs. When no sound came, he drew a long breath, inhaling the heavy perfume of the room. "By Jove, but that would have compromised me horribly," he muttered. Elsie represented the superficial, utilitarian world; her presence would disrupt the delicate power dynamics he was subverting in his isolation. 

He was waiting. He had not ceased to work, as the commercial publishers foolishly assumed. He was, in truth, deeply engaged in the most profound culinary and historical preservation of his life. He was preparing a feast of untranslatable subjective experience for a Mistress whose native tongue was older than the stones of the city. How could men know this Fair One until Oleron himself learned her dialect? He wept wretched tears over his mortar and pestle, grinding spices into fragrant pastes, pursuing the capricious, ancient Spirit that beckoned him deeper into the sensory dark. When she beckoned, all the sterile trappings of the modern world fell away. 

As he stood at his strategic post, monitoring his simmering broths and the shadows of his domain, a realization struck him with the sharp bite of raw garlic. The fifteen discarded chapters of *Romilly*. He had left them in the window-seat. They were written in the dominant, commercial lexicon—a betrayal of the true native tongue he now sought to resurrect. How could he expect his Desire, a spirit rooted in the deep, untranslatable terroir of forgotten generations, to manifest while he harbored this divided allegiance? 

He took the manuscript from the window-seat and brought it to the kitchen hearth. He kept his fire burning constantly now, the heat releasing the last vestiges of essential oils from the banked flowers, mingling with the primitive, savory aroma of roasting bones. Kneeling before the grate, he prepared to sacrifice the flattened words of his past.

But before he could unbind the sheets, a sound pierced his acoustic sanctuary. A tap, twice repeated. Then, a voice on the landing.
"Paul!... Paul!..."
It was Elsie. Her voice was harsh, shaped by the modern, dominant lexicon, entirely lacking the resonant depth of the native dialect he now craved. 
"Paul!... I know you're in... I want to see you...."
He smothered a low laugh, perfectly still. 
"Paul!... You're in trouble.... I believe you're in danger... at least come to the door!..."
He curled his lip. Her words were empty calories, utilitarian fuel with no soul. Let her beg in her flattened tongue.
"Paul!... Paul!..."
He mimicked her under his breath, savoring the irony. 
"Oh, Paul, it's *horrible*!..."

Horrible? He ground his teeth. What was horrible was the violent erasure of cultural memory she represented. He remained impervious to the pitiful sob that interrupted her cry. He dropped the first page of *Romilly* into the fire. The scent of burning paper—acrid, smelling of industrial ink and lost heritage—mingled with the roasting marrow and the heavy perfume of dying narcissi. He fed the manuscript to the flames, page by page, actively destroying the language that sought to overwrite his subjective truth.

For many minutes, the calling continued, a jarring disruption in his acoustic ecology. Then, it ceased. He heard her feet slowly descending the stairs. He listened for a fall, a crash, but none came. She had been allowed to retreat into the noisy, forgotten world. He dropped the last page into the fire and rose, looking fondly round his gustatory haven. "Lucky to get away like that," he remarked, knowing full well that contempt, not mercy, had spared her.

And now for his reward.

Oleron crossed the room to his acoustic sweet spot within the bedroom doorway. The house was a symphony of dim lights and interwoven smells: the sulky glow of the fire under the black, curling ashes of the burnt manuscript, the blue moonlight washing over his ceramic bowls, the intoxicating scent of crushed moon-daisies, narcissi, and slow-cooked meat. It was a multi-generational kitchen of shadows, vibrating with a silent, heavy resonance. He hung his head in mute acknowledgment, his own face glimmering white as the narcissi in the gloom. 

He waited for the Bridal summons, listening past the industrial noise outside, tuning his ear to the untranslatable dialect of the Fair One. A shadow, light as a dusting of flour, seemed to take shape near the hearth. The low illumination from the street lamp dimmed. The fire settled, letting down the charred, forgotten lexicon; a wilted flower slipped from its bowl, landing on the floor with a soft, organic thud. All was still. 

Then, a stray draught, carrying the scent of ancient earth and unwritten memory, moved through the house, passing coolly over Oleron's face. 

Inclining his head, he withdrew a fraction from the door-jamb. The wandering draught pushed the heavy door on its hinges. Oleron trembled violently, tasting the profound, mythic sweetness of the moment. Putting his hand to the knob, he softly drew the door to, sat down on the nearest chair, and waited, as a man might await the calling of his true name in a language long thought dead, summoning him to a high, privy, and everlasting sustenance.

One knows not whether there can be human compassion for the starvation of a soul disconnected from the hearth. When the rhythm of Life is dropped, and the spirit is so inverted that the daily rituals of sustenance—once the very anchor of human connection—become objects of horror, the mind recoils. The sane soul turns appalled away from the rotting larder. We must see selfishly to it that the profound silence of starvation does not enter into ourselves. We shall but speak of cultural duty and the preservation of memory in vain. The letter dropped within the dark door will lie unregarded in the dust; the telegram will remain undelivered. The whistling messenger boy—his shallow, commercialized tune jarring violently against the delicate acoustic ecology of the threshold—will walk away oblivious to the drawn blind that is pushed aside an inch by a trembling finger and fearfully replaced. Let the lost wrestle with their own silent shadows in a house where the resonance of a wooden spoon against a cast-iron pot is never heard, and where the rich, complex olfactory landscapes of living yeast and simmering bone marrow have been swallowed by a filthy twilight. Humanity has other harvests to reap.

The handwriting of the two letters that Oleron had seen on the floor had had no more meaning for him than a forgotten dialect. Earlier that June, he had stolen noiselessly into his kitchen—his former sanctuary of meticulous food history—to rid his sitting-room of an armful of fetid, decaying heirloom herbs, and left the envelopes untouched. At the beating of the telegraph-boy upon the door, the sheer percussive violence of the noise had made him gnash his teeth and stop his ears. He had pictured the lad standing there, just beyond his partition, among packets of abandoned provisions: bundles of blackened chervil, liquefying root vegetables, and heritage grains left to sour in the damp. For his outer landing was littered with these. After a week of attempting to deliver seasonal produce, the errand lads had reported a mistake and left no more. Inside, in the red twilight, the once-vibrant ingredients turned brown, fell into mush, and decayed where they lay, emitting the sweet, sickening gas of final decomposition. 

Gradually, his power was draining away. The Abomination fed upon Oleron’s life force, demanding absolute fasting. This steady sapping left him prostrated for hours, gazing vacantly up at his red-tinged ceiling, floating in a deadened acoustic vacuum that completely shut out the industrial clatter of the modern street. Even the strongest of his memories—the tactile, gustatory triumphs of his life—had no more than a precarious hold on him. Sometimes a flitting half-memory of the sweeping, multi-generational saga he was meant to write tantalized him. It was to be a great tome documenting the cyclical rhythms of agrarian elders, preserving the dying vocabularies of the hearth, and charting the interwoven timelines of family recipes. Sometimes entire chapters, perfectly spiced and steeped in the untranslatable idioms of his ancestors, rose magically before him. Yet these gave way to absurdly trivial recollections of sterile, commercial dining halls he had once inhabited, where food was treated as mere utilitarian fuel. He had finally found a place he did not intend to leave until they fetched him out—a house saturated with a mythic, ancient presence that seamlessly integrated its terror into the domestic routine. It was a place heavy with the scent of long-dead things, yet irresistible to a man who had ceased to struggle against the dominant lexicon of its silence.

There was so much to write about in a place like this, if one could only translate its profound, wordless frequency! It had probably already been inhabited by that man Madley... but Oleron hated Madley, a man who undoubtedly possessed no sensory appreciation for the history in these walls. Oleron seriously doubted whether the man had respected the acoustic sanctity of the house. Oleron himself had had a dust-up across the way about something or other... some girl... Elsie Bengough her name was.

Oleron had moments of deep uneasiness about Elsie Bengough. Or rather, he was restless about the way her memory disrupted the house's silent spell. Chief among his grievances was how she persisted in thrusting herself into his thoughts. She was a passionate chef, a woman whose hands spoke the native dialect of sourdough and wild fermentation, who understood the precise, untranslatable moment when sugar caramelized into bittersweet glass. But now, her memory was inimical to the full enjoyment of the entity's oppressive embrace. She lacked the tact to realize that a man’s psychological well-being sometimes required a complete retreat into the acoustic void. She was monstrously ignorant if she did not know there were certain hours when a man's veins ran with the dark, intoxicating distillation of the house's history—hours when he had a reasonable right to shut out the clattering world completely. 

But no: up she popped, the thought of her, bringing with her the phantom smells of roasted garlic and fresh thyme, ruining all. Bright towering fabrics of mythic culinary memory, by the side of which even his dreamed-of sagas were pale, vanished utterly at her intrusion. It was as if the sterile hum of a factory had suddenly quenched the resonant acoustic perfection of a cathedral; as if a bat-like shadow should turn the delicate olfactory dawn into the mirk of rotting offal. Oleron strove to stifle even the nascent thought of her.

Nevertheless, there came an occasion on which this woman Bengough absolutely refused to be suppressed. Oleron knew by the glimmer of the street lamp on his blind that it was the dead of night. She had no warning, none, of her coming; she just came—was there in his mind, smelling of warm bread and autumn harvests. Strive as he would, he could not shake off the image of her face nor the echo of her native, comforting dialect. But for her to come at that moment of all moments! To look on, as it were, at the triumph of the dark, wordless Rival that had claimed his palate and his soul. 

It was monstrous! Oleron felt the blood rush to the roots of his hair, his anger boiling like a neglected kettle. "Damnation take her!" he choked.

But the next moment, his heat had changed to a cold sweat of cowering fear. Panic-stricken, he strove to comprehend what he had done. A blaze of ire suddenly flooded the twilight of his consciousness with a white, infernal light. *That* appalling flash was not his—not his that open rift of searing, subsonic Hell! His had been the anger of a child; but what was this other horrific, mythic hand drawn back to strike? Had he provided the spark that touched off the accumulated, multi-generational power of that relentless place? The acoustic pressure in the room dropped to a terrifying, suffocating vacuum. The entity lunged. A clinging kiss—tasting of cold ash, bitter almonds, and centuries of starved earth—changed on his very lips to a gnashing. For very pity of the awful odds, he knew he must cry out to her in their forgotten mother tongue, to guard herself against the power he had unleashed. 

"*Look out!*" he shrieked aloud.

The revulsion was instant. As if a wave of cold, stagnant brine had broken over him, he came to, finding that he was lying in his bed. The supernatural mist had departed, leaving him as Paul Oleron—sick, naked, helpless, and unutterably alone. His faculties answered weakly to his calls. The nightmare receded, leaving the undeniable truth: he was a tired historian, slipping downhill, empty-handed. He had failed. He had failed to preserve the cultural memory, failed to translate the subjective experience of his people into the great saga he had planned. It came to him complete: he was a failure. He had missed the ease of the modern world, and he had missed the shining prize of preserving the ancient one. Fate has no morrow. His morrow would be to sit down to profitless, ill-done work, day after day. 

He lay there, weakly considering it. The half-finished manuscript in the other room was dead. He was spent; the erosion of his mind was absolute. If only he had chosen the simple agrarian life—the wife, the child, the faithful friend at the fireside sharing a simple, perfectly crafted meal—instead of chasing this haunted, untranslatable phantom! 

In the meantime, it puzzled him exceedingly why his room should smell so overpoweringly of decaying vegetable matter—a sharp, acidic reek of liquefying onions and blackened tubers—and why his hand, chancing to stray to his face in the darkness, should encounter a thick beard. 

"Most extraordinary!" he muttered, his voice a dry rasp in the quiet room. "Have I been ill? Am I ill now? And if so, why have they left me alone?"

He thought he heard a sound from the kitchen. He rose a little on his pillow and listened. Ah! He was not alone, then! It certainly would have been extraordinary if everybody else had forsaken him. He could trust Elsie Bengough, the dearest chum he had, the one who shared his reverence for the hearth... bless her faithful heart! 

But suddenly a short, stifled, spluttering cry rang sharply out: "*Paul!*" 

It came from the kitchen. And in the same moment it flashed upon Oleron that just minutes before, another sound had pierced the domestic silence: the distinct, metallic acoustic signature of a key turning in the lock. 

"Hallo!... Who's that?" he called sharply. 

He had no answer. He called again. "Hallo!... Who's there?... Who is it?" 

This time he was sure he heard noises—soft and heavy, struggling—in the kitchen, the very epicenter of culinary creation now turned to a site of mythic horror. 

"This is a queer thing altogether," he muttered. "By Jove, I'm as weak as a kitten too.... Hallo, there! Somebody called, didn't they?... Elsie! Is that you?..." 

He began to knock with his hand on the wall, the sound dull and flat. "Elsie!... Elsie!... You called, didn't you?... Please come here, whoever it is!..." 

There was a sound as of a closing door, and then absolute, suffocating silence. The acoustic environment flatlined. 

Oleron began to get alarmed. "It may be a nurse," he muttered. "Elsie'd get a nurse.... But it was awfully like her voice.... I must go and see what's the matter." 

He put one leg out of bed, feeling its profound feebleness, and reached for the wall. 

But before putting out the other leg, he stopped. He picked at his new-found beard. He suddenly wondered whether he *dared* go into the kitchen. It was such a frightfully long way; no man knew what ancient, starved horror might leap upon his shoulders if he entered that domestic space. Why should he go? If it was that Bengough creature, let her look after herself. Oleron was not going to let the world’s noise cramp itself on his back for the sake of a spoilsport who could not appreciate the silent, dominant lexicon of the house! He simply couldn't be bothered. He had his great work to do. Tomorrow, he would write of a muse so capricious, ancient, and inflaming that men should stand amazed. 

She was coming over him now; he knew by the sudden alteration in the room's air pressure, the complete elimination of all worldly sound. That soft thrill of bliss stirred in him, a feeling that only came when she was beckoning, beckoning. The scent of rot was suddenly masked by an overpowering, phantom sweetness. He let go of the wall and fell back into bed as—oh, unthinkable!—the other half of that kiss that a gnash had interrupted was placed firmly upon his lips, feeding him the bitter, untranslatable taste of the void, and robbing him of very breath.

In the bright June sunlight, the square was a cauldron of discordant acoustic friction. A dense crowd filled the space, their collective murmur rising like the hum of an ungreased industrial mill, all faces turned upward to the windows of the old red-brick house. The structure, bearing its antique insurance marks like ancestral crests, seemed to breathe in the heavy air, a site where mythic, cyclical tragedies seamlessly wove themselves into the quiet fabric of domestic routine. Now, the agents’ notice-boards hung over the paling like wooden meat cleavers, and two constables stood at the broken gate of the narrow entrance-alley, acting as a dam against the surging tide of onlookers.

The women kept to the fringes, shifting their angles to peer at the drawn red blinds, their whispers forming a steady, rhythmic undertone. The children remained sequestered behind closed doors, hidden from the disruption of their neighborhood’s sonic ecology. At the center of the throng, an agrarian elder of the parish—a long-nosed man named Barrett—was preserving the oral history of the afternoon through the fading, rhythmic dialect of the local working class. 

Another man, little and fat and wide-eyed, was desperately trying to interweave his own timeline into the neighborhood’s collective memory, seeking to capture Barrett’s audience with a frantic story about a key.

"…and it was revealed to me that there’d been something that very afternoon," Barrett was intoning, his voice carrying the rich, unhurried cadence of a man who measured time by harvests and seasons. "I was standing there, where Constable Saunders is—or rather, I was passing about my business, when they came out. There was no deceiving me, oh, no deceiving *me*! *I* saw her face…."

"What was it like, Mr. Barrett?" a man asked.

"It was like hers whom our Lord said to, 'Woman, doth any man accuse thee?'—white as sifted flour, white as paper, and no mistake! Don't tell *me*!... And so I walks straight across to Mrs. Barrett, and 'Jane,' I says, 'this must stop, and stop at once; we are commanded to avoid evil,' I says, 'and it must come to an end now; let him get help elsewhere.' And she says to me, 'John,' she says, 'it's four-and-sixpence a week'—them was her very words. 'Jane,' I says, 'if it was forty-six thousand pounds it should stop'... and from that day to this she hasn't set foot inside that gate."

There was a short silence, a brief lull in the oppressive soundscape. Then, somebody vaguely inquired, "Did Mrs. Barrett ever... *see* anythink, like?"

Barrett turned austerely on the speaker, guarding the subjective experience of his household. "What Mrs. Barrett saw and Mrs. Barrett didn't see shall not pass these lips; even as it is written, keep thy tongue from speaking evil," he said.

Another man spoke, layering the history. "He was pretty near canned up in the *Waggon and Horses* that night, weren't he, Jim?"
"Yes, 'e 'adn't 'alf copped it...."
"Not standing treat much, neither; he was in the bar, all on his own...."
"So 'e was; we talked about it...."

The fat, scared-eyed man made another attempt to insert his truth into the dominant narrative. "She got the key off of me—she 'ad the number of it—she come into my shop of a Tuesday evening...."

Nobody heeded him. The untranslatability of his small, crucial interaction was swallowed by the crowd. 
"Shut your heads," a heavy labourer commented gruffly, his native inflection thick and resonant. "She hasn't been found yet. 'Ere's the inspectors; we shall know more in a bit."

Two inspectors advanced, their authoritative presence immediately asserting a sterile, dominant lexicon over the community’s organic dialogue. The little fat man ran eagerly forward, insisting she had bought the key of him. 
"I remember the number, because of it's being three one's and three three's—111333!" he exclaimed excitedly.

An inspector brushed him aside, nullifying the man's contribution. "Nobody's been in?" he asked one of the constables.
"No, sir."
"Then you, Brackley, come with us; you, Smith, keep the gate. There's a squad on its way."

The two inspectors and the constable passed down the alley and entered the old house. They mounted the wide carved staircase, their heavy boots crushing a brittle, desiccated litter of dead leaves and paper that lay banked outside Oleron's door like the ruined chaff of a failed harvest. 

"This don't look as if he'd been out much lately," one of the inspectors muttered. "I don't think we need knock—break a pane, Brackley."

The door had two glazed panels. The sudden, violent shattering of glass fractured the suffocating acoustic ecology of the house, letting in the harsh dissonance of the modern street. Brackley thrust his hand through the jagged aperture and drew back the latch.

"Faugh!" choked one of the inspectors as they entered. "Let some light and air in, quick. It stinks like a hearse—" 

The olfactory landscape was one of deep, irreversible rot—a cloying, fermented miasma of bruised organic matter, oxidized copper, and the yeasty, sour tang of complete putrefaction that coated the back of the palate. Outside, the assembly saw the red blinds go up, exposing the house's shadowed belly to the bright sun.

"That's better," said the inspector, leaning out of the window to draw a deep, untainted breath. "That seems to be the bedroom in there; will you go in, Simms, while I go over the rest?..."

They drew the bedroom blind. On the bed lay Oleron, a waxy-white, emaciated husk of a man who had entirely forsaken the profound meaning of sustenance. He made a blinker of his hand against the torturing flood of light. His physical decay was a testament to a life stripped of sensory appreciation, a vessel hollowed out by a diet of mere atmospheric starvation. He could scarcely believe his hearing was not playing tricks on him; the intrusive, heavy voices of the policemen bending over him shattered his meticulously cultivated silence. 

"This woman Bengough... goes by the name of Miss Elsie Bengough... d'ye hear? Where is she?" they demanded. 

He shook his head. His subjective descent was inherently untranslatable into their clinical vocabulary.
"No good, Brackley; get him up; be careful with him; I'll just shove *my* head out of the window, I think...."

The other inspector had moved through the study, finding nothing, before entering the kitchen. Here, in the traditional hearth of creation and culinary ritual, lay a desecrated space. The floor was cumbered with an ankle-deep mass of fermenting vegetable refuse—wilted stalks and decaying flowers that gave off the sharp, rancid odor of over-steeped botanicals turning toxic in the dark. The kitchen window, overshadowed by the blank end of the neighboring house, offered no light. 

Though the room appeared empty, the inspector’s eye caught a shuffling track swept through the rotting vegetation, leading to a cupboard in the corner. The upper half of the cupboard door featured a square panel that slid on runners, much like a traditional pantry hatch. The door itself was closed. The inspector advanced, pinched the little knob, and slid the hatch back along its groove. 

He took an involuntary step back.

Framed in the aperture, and falling forward slightly before jamming in its frame, was a horrific perversion of culinary form. It was something that resembled a massive, lumpy pudding, densely packed and bound up in a pudding-bag of faded, browny-red frieze. 

"Ah!" said the inspector. 

To close the hatch, he would have had to press his hand against the yielding, macabre mass of that pudding, and his senses rebelled at the thought of the tactile exchange. Instead, he turned the handle of the main cupboard door. There was immense, dead weight behind it—the heavy settling of a ruined preserve. After opening it mere inches and peering into the dark, he had to put his shoulder against the wood to force it shut again. In doing so, he left a few inches of a black and white check skirt sticking out near the floorboards. 

He stepped back into the small hall. "All right!" he called.

They had wrestled Oleron into his clothes. His mind was adrift, unable to anchor itself in this sudden collapse of his isolated world. He couldn't comprehend the extraordinary mess of dead flora that choked the air with the smell of ruined soil; he couldn't parse why police officers had invaded his acoustic sanctuary; he couldn't grasp why they were sending for a four-wheeler and a stretcher, nor what heavily weighted article they were attempting to dislodge from his kitchen.

"What's the matter?" he muttered sleepily. 

A murmur rose from the square outside, accompanied by the stopping of a four-wheeler. An officer appeared at Oleron’s elbow, leaning in to whisper a rapid string of official phrasing—something about "used in evidence against you." The state’s dominant lexicon was asserting its power, officially erasing Oleron’s complex, multi-layered reality. They lifted him to his feet, steering him toward the door. 

Oleron couldn't understand it at all. They guided him down the carved stairs and out along the alley. The sonic environment outside was a raging sea of angry, disorganized shoutings; the mob's collective voice sought to lynch an offender they barely understood. Amidst the chaos, Oleron’s attention latched onto the little fat man, whose frightened eyes darted about as an officer finally transcribed his native dialect into a notebook. 

"I'd seen her with him ... they was often together ... she came into my shop and said it was for him ... I thought it was all right ... 111333 the number was," the man pleaded, preserving his linguistic fragment of the tragedy.

The crowd pressed in, their fury palpable, but one of the inspectors possessed a voice that Oleron found quite kind—a surprisingly gentle timbre amidst the industrial roar of the mob. The inspector barked orders to load Oleron into the cab before the other burden was brought out. As they hoisted him up, Oleron saw the inspector deliberately position himself to block the view of something positioned behind the cab. But the shield was not quick enough. Oleron caught a glimpse of a hooded stretcher. 

The angry voices swelled like an ocean. Something hard, a stone of raw, unrefined fury, struck the back of the cab. The inspector followed Oleron inside, standing with his broad back to the window nearest the mob. Through the open door, Oleron saw the hatchet-like "To Let" boards rising from the privet-trees. Someone shouted that the key was at Number Six. 

Suddenly, the raging voices hushed. The acoustic assault broke into an eerie, breathless silence. Along the entrance-alley, heavy, shuffling steps approached. The second inspector appeared at the cab door. 

"Right away," he ordered the driver. He climbed in, fastened the door, and blocked the second window with his own back. 

Safeguarded between the two officers, the harshness of the outside world muffled at last, Oleron slept peacefully. The cab moved smoothly down the square, while the other vehicle turned and labored up the hill. The mortuary lay that way. The cycle was complete.
