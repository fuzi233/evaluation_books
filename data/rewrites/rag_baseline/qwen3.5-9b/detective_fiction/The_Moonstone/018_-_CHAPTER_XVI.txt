The chamber was shrouded in darkness, save for the solitary, trembling cone of light cast by the reading-lamp. Its shade was screwed down tight, a deliberate act of concealment that swallowed her features in shadow. She did not lift her gaze to greet us with her customary candor; instead, she sat hunched over the table, her eyes locked with an obstinate rigidity upon the open pages of a book, as if the ink itself held the weight of the secrets we carried.

"Officer," she said, her voice steady despite the storm brewing beneath the surface, "does the integrity of your inquiry depend upon knowing beforehand if any soul within these walls wishes to depart?"

"It is paramount, my lady," Cuff replied, his tone devoid of the usual deference that softened his edges elsewhere.

"I must inform you," she continued, never breaking her stare at the text, "that Miss Verinder intends to flee to her aunt, Mrs. Ablewhite, of Frizinghall. She has arranged to leave us at the break of dawn tomorrow."

Cuff's gaze shifted to me, a silent command passing between them. I stepped forward, driven by a reflex of loyalty, only to freeze as a crushing weight settled in my chest—a failure of spirit that made my limbs refuse further motion. I retreated a pace, finding my voice extinguished.

"When," Cuff inquired, turning his sharp eyes back to her, "did Miss Verinder disclose this intention to you?"

"Approximately an hour ago," my mistress answered, her composure a fortress against the crumbling reality outside.

Cuff looked at me again, the scrutiny piercing. They say the hearts of the aged are stone, unmoved by the tremors of youth. Yet, mine hammered against my ribs with a ferocity that would have belied a man of twenty-five, if such a thing were possible.

"I possess no authority, my lady," Cuff stated, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial register, "to dictate Miss Verinder's movements. What I implore of you is to delay her departure until later today. I must travel to Frizinghall myself at first light, returning by two o'clock, or sooner. If Miss Verinder can remain here until that hour, I intend to speak to her—unexpectedly, privately—before she departs."

My mistress signaled to the coachman, issuing orders that the carriage was to stand idle until two. Once the instruction was given, she turned her gaze back to Cuff. "Is there anything else?"

"One final stipulation, your ladyship," Cuff said, leaning slightly forward. "Should Miss Verinder express surprise at this alteration of plans, pray do not attribute the delay to me."

My mistress raised her head abruptly from the book, a flicker of defiance crossing her face before she checked herself with a monumental effort. She returned her eyes to the page, dismissing us with a sharp wave of her hand.

"That is a formidable woman," Cuff murmured as we crossed the threshold into the hall. "Without her ironclad self-command, the enigma that baffles you, Mr. Betteredge, would have unraveled tonight."

And in those words, the dam broke. The truth, long suppressed behind layers of denial and hope, surged into my senile mind. For a fleeting second, I lost my faculties entirely. I lunged forward, seizing Cuff by the lapel of his coat and pinning him against the plaster.

"Damn you!" I roared, my voice cracking with a rage born of betrayal. "Something is rotten concerning Miss Rachel—and you have been concealing it from me this entire time!"

Cuff looked up, his face flat against the wall, unmoving, his melancholy features impassive.

"Aha," he said calmly, "you have deduced it at last."

My hand slid from his collar, and my head bowed onto my breast, defeated. Recall, if you will, that I had served this household for fifty years. Miss Rachel had climbed upon my knees, tugging at my whiskers countless times during her childhood. Despite her flaws, she had been, in my estimation, the most cherished, beautiful, and virtuous young mistress an elderly servant could ever tend. I begged Cuff's forgiveness, though I fear my apology was delivered through watery eyes and with a lack of dignity.

"Do not distress yourself, Mr. Betteredge," Cuff said, his voice softening with a kindness I scarcely expected. "In my profession, swift offense-taking renders one useless. If it brings you solace, seize me again. You lack the technique, but I shall overlook your clumsiness in consideration of your grief."

A faint curl touched the corners of his lips, and in his grim fashion, he seemed to delight in the absurdity of the moment.

I ushered him into my small sitting-room and barred the door.

"Speak plainly, Sergeant," I demanded, my voice trembling. "What do you suspect? It is cruelty to withhold this from me now."

"I do not suspect," Cuff replied, his tone absolute. "I know."

My volatile temper flared anew. "Do you mean to tell me, in clear English," I shouted, "that Miss Rachel has stolen her own diamond?"

"Precisely," Cuff confirmed. "Miss Verinder has held the Moonstone in secret possession from the outset. She enlisted Rosanna Spearman in her confidence solely because she calculated that we would inevitably suspect Rosanna of the theft. That is the entirety of the case, distilled into a single nutshell. Seize me again, Mr. Betteredge. If it vents your anguish, do so freely."

God forgive me, my emotions were not so easily assuaged. "Give me your reasons!" was all I could manage.

"You shall hear my reasoning tomorrow," Cuff said. "Should Miss Verinder refuse to postpone her visit to her aunt—which you will discover she does—I shall be compelled to present the full case to your mistress tomorrow. Since the outcome remains uncertain, I request your presence to witness the proceedings. Let the matter rest for tonight. No, Mr. Betteredge, you shall extract no further words regarding the Moonstone from me. Your supper awaits. That is one of the many human frailties I choose to treat with tenderness. Ring the bell, and I shall offer grace: 'For what we are about to receive...'"

"I wish you a hearty appetite, Sergeant," I muttered, my own hunger extinguished. "As for me, I shall wait to see you served, then beg to withdraw and attempt to conquer this turmoil alone."

I watched him dine on the finest provisions, and I harbored no regret if the feast choked him. Just then, the head gardener, Mr. Begbie, entered with his weekly accounts. Cuff immediately pivoted to a discourse on roses, debating the merits of grass versus gravel paths. I departed, leaving them to their debate, with a heart heavy as lead. This was the first genuine tribulation I recalled in decades that could not be dispelled by a puff of tobacco or the escapism of *Robinson Crusoe*.

Restless and wretched, with nowhere to retreat, I paced the terrace, contemplating the abyss in solitude. The specifics of my thoughts mattered little; I felt ancient, exhausted, and utterly unfit for my station. For the first time in my life, I wondered when God might grant me release. Yet, amidst this despair, I clung tenaciously to my faith in Miss Rachel. Even if Cuff, clad in the wisdom of Solomon, had declared my young lady entangled in a base and guilty conspiracy, my answer would remain unchanged: "You do not know her; I do."

My reverie was shattered by Samuel, who arrived bearing a written missive from my mistress. Entering the house to fetch a light, Samuel noted a shift in the atmosphere. My troubled mind had blinded me to it previously, but now, alerted, I heard the dogs pacing uneasily and the wind moaning low through the eaves. Above, the cloud racks grew darker, racing faster across a watery moon. Wild weather was approaching—Samuel was correct.

The note revealed that the magistrate at Frizinghall had reminded my mistress of the three Indians. By early next week, the rogues must be released to roam free. Should we harbor further queries, time was of the essence. Having overlooked this detail during her previous encounter with Cuff, my mistress now tasked me with rectifying the omission. The Indians had vanished from my mind, as surely they had from yours. I saw little utility in reopening that wound, yet obedience was my nature.

Upon entering the room, I found Cuff and the gardener engaged in a fervent debate over a bottle of Scotch whisky, arguing passionately about horticulture. Cuff was so absorbed he raised a hand to signal me to hold my tongue. The crux of their disagreement concerned whether the white moss rose required budding onto the dog-rose to thrive. Begbie insisted yes; Cuff argued no. They appealed to me with the intensity of schoolboys. Knowing nothing of botany, I adopted the neutral stance of Her Majesty's judges when justice hangs by a thread. "Gentlemen," I remarked, "much merit lies on both sides." In the brief pause created by this impartiality, I placed my mistress's note upon the table, directly before Cuff.

By this juncture, I had nearly reached the point of hatred for the Sergeant. Yet, truth demands acknowledgment: regarding mental agility, he was a prodigy. Within thirty seconds of reading the note, he had retrieved Superintendent Seegrave's report from memory, isolated the section concerning the Indians, and formulated his response.
