I turned and trailed the spotted leopardess, catching only a fleeting glimpse of her as she tore upward over the brow of the hill toward the palace gates. By the time I reached the entrance hall, the princess was just draping the discarded robe around her shoulders. The blood had already ceased to flow from her wounds, drying instantly in the chill wind of her escape.

When she saw me, a flash of irritation crossed her face, and she turned her head away. Then, forcing a smile, she looked back at me. "I've met with a small accident!" she exclaimed. "Hearing that the cat-woman was back in the city, I went down to drive her away. But she had one of those horrid creatures with her—it leaped upon me, driving its claws into my neck before I could strike!"

She shivered violently. I couldn't help but feel pity, even though I knew she was lying. Her wounds were genuine, and the pallor of her face reminded me too sharply of how she had appeared in the cave. My own heart began to reproach me for having allowed her to fight alone, and I suppose my expression betrayed the compassion rising within me.

"Child of folly!" she said, offering another strained smile. "--Not crying, surely?—Wait for me here. I'm going into the Black Hall for a moment. I need you to fetch something for these scratches."

But I followed her closely. Out of my sight, I feared her.

The instant the princess stepped inside, a buzzing sound filled the air, like the murmur of countless low voices. One section after another, the vast assembly began to shift and illuminate, as if struck by a traveling ray of light. Groups would flare brightly for a moment, then sink back into the general gloom, while another part of the crowd grew momentarily luminous.

Some of the scenes unfolding during these flashes were familiar to me; I had been there, or watched from afar, along with the princess. Now, present with every event, I saw her role in each.

In the Skull-Hall, where the headless dancers trod the grass, there she was, peering in through the doorway! In the Evil Wood, where the battle raged, there she was, urging the conflict forward! Yet throughout it all, I remained close behind her, watching her stand motionless, her head bowed against her chest.

The confused murmur continued—the chaotic commotion of colors and shapes—as the shifting ray moved on. Finally, it settled on the hollow in the heath. There, the princess walked up and down, vainly trying to wrap the vapor around herself.

Then, for the first time, I was truly startled. The old librarian approached her, standing for a moment regarding her figure. Suddenly, she collapsed. Her limbs abandoned her, fleeing from her form; her body simply vanished.

A wild shriek rang through the echoing chamber. With the fall of her *eidolon*, the princess herself—who had until that moment stood like a statue before me—fell heavily to the ground and lay still.

I turned immediately and ran outside. Not again would I seek to restore her!

As I trembled beside the cage, I realized with a jolt that within the black ellipsoid, I had been inhabiting the mind of the princess. I saw the tail of the leopardess quiver once more.

While I struggled to compose myself, I heard the princess's voice beside me. "Come now," she said. "I will tell you what I want you to do for me."

She led the way into the courtyard. I followed in a daze, compliant and numb. The moon hung near the zenith, its silver radiance seeming brighter than the gold of the absent sun. She guided me through the trees to the tallest of them, the solitary specimen in the center. It was unlike the others; its branches drew their tips together at the apex, forming a cluster that resembled a fir-cone when viewed from below.

The princess stood close beneath it, gazing upward. "On the summit of that tree grows a tiny blossom," she murmured, as if speaking to herself, "which would heal my scratches at once! I could become a dove for a moment and fetch it... but I see a little snake coiled in the leaves. Its bite would be far worse to a dove than a tiger's bite would be to me! How I hate that cat-woman!"

Turning quickly to me, she offered one of her sweetest smiles. "Can you climb?"

The smile vanished instantly with the question, replaced by a look of profound sadness and suffering. I should have left her to endure her pain, but the way she pressed her hand to her wounded neck pierced my heart.

I studied the tree. Running up the trunk were projections resembling the remnants of fallen palm leaves. "I can climb that tree," I answered.

"Not with bare feet!" she retorted.

In my haste to chase the leopardess earlier, I had forgotten my sandals in my room.

"It does not matter," I said. "I have long gone barefoot!"

Again, I looked up the trunk, my gaze wandering until it was lost among the high branches. The moonlight shimmered like silvery foam across the rugged bark, and a gentle breeze rustled through the treetops with a soft, murmuring sound, like water falling gently into water.

I stepped forward to begin my ascent, but the princess stopped me.

"I cannot let you attempt this with your feet bare!" she insisted. "A fall from that height would kill you!"

"So would a bite from the snake!" I replied—I must admit, I did not believe there was actually a snake.

"It would not hurt *you*!" she countered. "--Wait a moment."

She tore from her garment the two wide borders that met in the front. Kneeling on one knee, she made me place my left foot, then my right, onto the other strip, binding them tightly about my ankles with the thick, embroidered fabric.

"You've left the ends hanging, Princess!" I noted.

"I have nothing to cut them with," she replied calmly. "But they are not long enough to get entangled."

I turned to the tree and began to climb.

Now, in Bulika, the cold after sunset was rarely severe—not as much as in other regions, especially near the sexton's cottage. Yet, as I climbed a few feet higher, I began to feel a sharp chill. I grew colder with every ascent, becoming frigid entirely once I reached the upper branches.

Then came the shivers. I felt as though I had lost my hands and feet. There was hardly any wind, and the branches did not sway, yet as I neared the summit, a peculiar unsteadiness seized me. Every branch I touched or gripped seemed poised to give way.

When my head finally rose above the canopy into the open moonlight, searching for the blossom, I was instantly drenched from head to toe. The next moment, as if plunged into a violent storm, I was flung about wildly, feeling myself sink. Tossed up and down, thrown this way and that, rolling over and over, checked and spun again, I sank lower and lower. Gasping, gurgling, choking, I finally crashed onto a solid bottom.

"I told you so!" a voice croaked in my ear.
