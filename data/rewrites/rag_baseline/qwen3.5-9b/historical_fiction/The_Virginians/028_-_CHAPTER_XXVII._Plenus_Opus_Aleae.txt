The pavement of the common was hard, worn smooth by the tread of countless generations, yet beneath the soles of Harry Warrington's boots, it offered no cushion against the weight of his own momentum. Maria walked beside him, her pace measured, her eyes scanning the horizon for the inevitable decay of the evening.

"Let me hear about those children," she said, her voice carrying the flat, practiced tone of someone reciting a script written by necessity. "Those poor souls running about the house where you were taken in. Oh, Harry, that fall! I thought I should have died watching it. You needn't squeeze my arm so tightly. Do you not care for me?"

Harry pulled his hand free, the motion sharp and dismissive. "The people are the very best, kindest, dearest people I have ever met in the world," he declared, his voice rising to match the cadence of a victory speech. "Mrs. Lambert was a friend of my mother's. Colonel Lambert served in Scotland, in Flanders, at Minorca. No natural parents could have been kinder. How can I repay them? I must give them a present. A token of gratitude." He reached into his pocket, fingers closing around the crisp, metallic weight of the spoils from Morris and March.

"We can go to the toy-shop," Maria suggested, turning her head slightly. "Buy dolls for the children. You would insult them by offering payment for their kindness."

"Dolls for Hester and Theo!" Harry exclaimed, a note of genuine surprise coloring his tone. "Why, do you think a woman ceases to be a woman until she is forty, Maria?" Under his arm, Maria stiffened, a microscopic contraction of muscle betraying the friction between his exuberance and her reality. "Miss Hester does not consider herself a child. Miss Theo is older than her sister. They speak multiple languages. They have consumed mountains of books. They play the harpsichord and sing admirably; Theo composes her own songs."

"I scarcely saw them," Maria replied, her voice dropping. "I thought they were children. They looked quite childish. I had no idea they possessed such perfection, such wonders of the world."

"That is precisely the error of your sex," Harry countered, striding forward with a confidence that bordered on arrogance. "At home, if George or I praise a woman, Mrs. Esmond and Mountain will inevitably find fault with her."

"I would find fault with no one who is kind to you, Mr. Warrington," Maria sighed, falling into step behind him. "Though you are not angry with me for envying them? For having to nurse you when you were wounded and ill, whilst I... whilst I had to leave you?"

"You dear, good Maria!"

"No, Harry! I am not dear and good. There, sir, cease your pressuring attentions." She pointed across the street. "Look! Your black man walks with a dozen others in livery. The horrid creatures are heading to the tea-garden to fuddle, to get tipsy like their masters. That dreadful Mr. Morris was perfectly drunk when I found you, frightening you half to death."

"I had just won great bets from both of them," Harry announced, his spirit buoyant, fueled by the adrenaline of the gamble. "What shall I buy for you, my dear cousin?" He recounted the triumphs of the evening, laughing, braggadocio spilling from his lips. "For the honor of Virginia, I was determined to show them what jumping was. With practice, I believe I could leap two feet farther."

Maria watched him, a faint smile touching her lips despite her weariness. "But you must beware of play, child. It has been the ruin of our family. My brother Castlewood, Will, our poor father, our aunt Lady Castlewood—they were all victims. And my Lord March? He is the most dreadful gambler and the most successful of all the nobility."

"I do not intend to fear him, nor his friend Mr. Jack Morris," Harry said, fingering the notes in his pocket again. "What do you play at Aunt Bernstein's? Cribbage, all-fours, brag, whist, commerce, piquet, quadrille? I am ready for any of them. What clock is that striking? Seven!"

"And you wish to begin now," Maria said, her voice plaintive. "You do not care to walk with your poor cousin. Not long ago, you did."

"Hey! Youth is youth, cousin!" Harry cried, tossing his head back. "And a young fellow must have his fling!" He strutted alongside her, confident, happy, eager for the next sensation. Yesterday he had wanted to be near Theo and Hester, near Mrs. Lambert. But pleasure, life, gaiety, the desire to shine and conquer, held their own seductive pull. The lad seized the cup like any other, refusing to calculate the headache awaiting him in the morning.

While they talked, the fiddles from the open orchestra on the Parade emitted a great tuning and squeaking, preparing for the evening concert. Maria knew her aunt was already awake, already trapped in her cycle. She must return to her slavery. Harry never asked about that slavery, though he must have known it, had he taken the trouble to think. He never pitied his cousin. He was not thinking of her at all. Yet when his misfortune eventually struck, she would be wounded far more cruelly than he. She had scarce ever been out of his thoughts, a fact she buried under smiling hypocrisies, as was the custom of her sex. Perhaps Mrs. Grundy thought her an old fool? Did fools' caps not cover grey hair as well as jet or auburn? Bear gently with our elderly *fredaines*, O Minerva of a woman! Or perhaps you are so wise you do not read novels. I know there are late crops of wild oats, as well as early harvests; and from observation of self and neighbor, I suspect the *avena fatua* grows right to the last day of the year.

Madame Bernstein and her attendants, like worldly parents eager to discard a troublesome child, had put the sun to bed while it was still light. They drew the curtains shut and busied themselves with cards, candles, tea, and negus. One chair after another deposited ladies at the Baroness's door—some painted, some patched, some brocaded. Gentlemen in gala raiment followed. Mr. Poellnitz's star was the largest, his coat the most embroidered.

My Lord March and Ruglen appeared transformed from the ragged figure Harry had met at the White Horse. The tight brown scratch was gone, replaced by a neatly curled feather top, powdered grey, and a rich French suit. Mr. Jack Morris wore a similar wig and a suit of stuff mimicking his lordship's elegance. Mr. Wolfe attended his beautiful mistress, Miss Lowther, and her aunt, who loved cards as the world did.

When Lady Maria Esmond entered, her appearance contradicted Madame Bernstein's earlier accounts. Her shape was fine, her dress revealing much of it. Her complexion was naturally exceeding fair, enhanced by a dark frilled ribbon clasped with a jewel around her neck, accentuating its snowy whiteness. Her cheeks were not redder than other ladies', the roses openly purchased from perfumery shops. An artful patch or two, it was rumored, added to the lustre of her charms. Her hoops were not larger than the iron contrivances of the present day; the costume, absurd in points, was pleasing altogether. Suppose our ladies adopted bangles and nose-rings? We might laugh at the ornaments, yet not dislike them, and lovers would make no difficulty lifting the ring to approach the rosy lips underneath.

As for the Baroness de Bernstein, when she took the pains of a grand toilette, she appeared handsome still, magnificent, yet melancholy, even terrifying. One reads the past in some old faces; others lapse into mere meekness. The fires go out of some eyes, as crow's-feet pucker round them; they flash no longer with scorn, anger, or love. They gaze, and no one is melted by their sapphire glances; they look, and no one is dazzled.

If you are not as perfect a beauty as the peerless Lindamira, Queen of the Ball, and if, at the end of the night, you retire to bed owning that you had but two or three partners while Lindamira had a crowd—console yourself. Think that at fifty, you will look as kind and pleasant as you appear now at eighteen. You will not have to lay down your coach-and-six of beauty and see another step into it, walking yourself through the rest of life. You will forgo no long-accustomed homage; you will not witness the depreciation of your smiles. Fashion will not forsake your quarter. You will not remain all dust, gloom, and cobwebs within your once splendid saloons, with placards in your sad windows, gaunt, lonely, and to let! You may not have known grandeur, but you will not feel desertion. You will not have enjoyed millions, but you will have escaped bankruptcy.

"Our hostess," whispered My Lord Chesterfield to his friend, unaware of the loudness of his voice, "reminds me of Covent Garden in my youth. Then it was the court end of the town, inhabited by the highest fashion. Now, a nobleman's house is a gaming-house, or you may go in with a friend and call for a bottle."
