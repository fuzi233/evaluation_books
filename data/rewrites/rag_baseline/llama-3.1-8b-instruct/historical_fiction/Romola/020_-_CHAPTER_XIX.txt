The Old Man's Hope.

In the warm, golden light of the Tuscan afternoon, Messer Bernardo del Nero strolled through the narrow streets of Florence, the sweet scent of fresh bread wafting from the ovens of the nearby panificio. His footsteps echoed off the stone facades, each step a reminder of the countless times he had walked this very route, offering counsel to those seeking his wisdom. Today, however, he was driven by a sense of urgency, his mind consumed by the weight of his daughter Romola's future. The marriage to Tito, a young Greek scholar, was a prospect both exhilarating and daunting. On one hand, the union promised to elevate the family's social standing, securing their place within the esteemed circles of the Medici. On the other, Bernardo harbored reservations about the young man's intentions, his charm and wit often masking a shallowness that bordered on duplicity.

As he navigated the cobblestone streets, Bernardo couldn't help but think of the countless evenings spent in the company of the Medicis, watching Tito effortlessly weave his way through conversations, effortlessly charming the likes of Cardinal Giovanni de' Medici. The young scholar's reputation as a learned and agreeable companion had indeed opened doors previously closed to the family, and Bernardo couldn't deny the benefits of such connections. Yet, he also remembered the words of the venerable Firenzuola, who spoke of the importance of true nobility, not merely in birth or wealth, but in character and virtue.

Bernardo's thoughts were interrupted by the sound of laughter and music drifting from the nearby Piazza della Signoria. He paused, observing the vibrant scene unfold before him – the young men and women gathered around Tito, their faces aglow with admiration and delight. It was a sight both familiar and strange, a testament to the transformative power of the young scholar's presence in their lives. As he watched, a figure emerged from the crowd, her dark hair shining like polished obsidian in the fading light. Romola, his daughter, moved with a quiet dignity, her eyes fixed on some point beyond the gathering. Bernardo's heart swelled with pride, remembering the words of Firenzuola: "A woman of true nobility sits with gravity, speaks with modesty, and casts an aura of queenliness." In this moment, he knew that Romola's union with Tito would be a union of two worlds, one rooted in tradition, the other in innovation
