Little Gerard was not yet three months old when a messenger, dust in his hair and haste in his limbs, clattered up from Tergou with a summons for Catherine.

“Get thee back,” Catherine cried from the threshold, wiping her hands on her apron as if she could wipe away their need with the same brisk motion. “Tell them I can’t come, and I won’t. They have got Kate.”

The fellow stared—he had ridden for obedience, not argument—and so, with a shrug, went down the stair.

Catherine turned, and the hard words fell from her mouth like a pot set too sharply on a hearthstone. Yet her eyes had already softened as they found the cradle. “There, child, I must go. They are all at sixes and sevens. This is the third time of asking; and to-morrow my man would come himself and take me home by the ear, with a flea in’t.”

Then, as if she were laying by winter stores, she began to lay up counsel in Margaret: how to swaddle and how to loosen; what a fretful cry might mean; what to do if the belly gripped, or if the gums rose; how to warm a cloth without scorching it; how to test milk by smell before offering it, lest sourness creep in unseen. She made domestic science of motherhood, and spoke of it with the authority of a woman who had heard too many midnight whimpers under too many roofs.

And then, because advice is easier given than parting, she pressed money into Margaret’s palm.

Margaret drew her hand back. “Nay, mother. I thank you kindly; but I cannot.”

Catherine thrust it forward again, with that impatient generosity that is half love and half temper. “Take it.”

“I cannot,” Margaret said, and held her hands together as if she were praying them steady.

Catherine’s face tightened; then, oddly, grew calm with contempt—contempt, not for the girl, but for a kind of girlishness she could not endure. “You make excuses like a man draws up reasons. Come—out with your heart. You and me parting; and mayhap shall never see one another’s face again.”

“Oh, mother, say not so.”

“Alack, girl, I have seen it so often; ’twill come into my mind now at each parting. When I was your age I never had such a thought. Nay, we were all to live for ever then. So—out wi’ it.”

Margaret’s eyes went down to the child, as if she would borrow courage from his small breathing. “Well, then, mother—I would rather not have told you—your Cornelis must say to me, ‘So you are come to share with us, eh, mistress?’ Those were his words. I told him I would be very sorry.”

“Beshrew his ill tongue!” Catherine snapped, and her voice was like a spoon rapping a pot. “What signifies it? He will never know.”

“Most like he would sooner or later. But whether or no, I will take no grudged bounty from any family; unless I saw my child starving, and—Heaven only knows what I might do. Nay, mother—give me but thy love. I do prize that above silver; and they grudge me not that, by all I can find. For not a stiver of money will I take out of your house.”

“You are a foolish lass.”

“Why, were it me,” Catherine retorted, and the old spirit in her flashed, “I’d take it just to spite him.”

“No,” said Margaret quietly. “You would not. You and I are apples off one tree.”

Catherine yielded at last, though she did it with a sort of reluctant grace, as one sets down a jug because another hand is steadier. Yet when the actual parting came, both women broke, and clung, and wept; tears running warm as broth down cheeks that had known rough wind and harsher words.

After Catherine was gone, the baby cried and cried: a thin, persistent wail that filled the room, climbed the rafters, and made the hearth’s small cracklings seem to hush in listening. Margaret tried her breast; she walked him; she sang him a fragment of an old Dutch lullaby, the words broken in her mouth as if they had travelled too far from their home. Nothing quieted him.

Then she thought, practical even through sorrow, There is a pin.

She undid him, searching between cloth and skin with careful fingers, and found—no pin—but a gold angel pressing against his little backbone.

“There, now, Gerard,” she whispered, half laughing and half choked, “I thought granny gave in rather sudden.”

She wrapped the coin in linen and laid it deep in her box, under folded cloths that smelled of lye and sun. And she told the baby, with a stern tenderness, that she could be resolute too—at times as resolute as granny herself.

Catherine, once home, poured out the tale to Eli—how Margaret’s foolish pride had stood in the way, and how she had outwitted it. Eli, who had a gentler justice than Catherine’s, said Margaret was right, and she was wrong.

Catherine tossed her head. Eli pondered.

Margaret’s days were a wheel that turned without rest. Two men still sat at her table, and she must feed them as well as the child. She could not work so hard as she had done—her arms were full, her nights broken, her strength spent in little portions like flour measured out too thinly. Yet she swept, and scoured, and made the pot sing; she watched the bread, and knew by smell when it was near ready, and by sound when the crust began to firm. She listened always—listened not only for the baby’s smallest change of cry, but for the stair’s complaint under a familiar footstep.

She had a little money laid by. Often, as she rocked the cradle, she told the child his father would be home to help them before it was spent, as if her words could keep the coins from dwindling. With these bright hopes—and that treasury of bliss, her boy—she lived some happy months.

Time wore on; and no Gerard came. Stranger still—no news.

Then her mind, so used to tasks and tangible things, began to wander into places where there was nothing to grasp. She would stand over her pail, hearing only the drip, drip, drip from a cloth she wrung, and feel the drip inside her heart answering it. She would stir a thin broth and taste it, and it seemed to have lost its savour because it had lost the sound of his voice to season it. Her silence grew heavy. She sighed, not for show, but because the breath must go somewhere.

And while her heart was troubled, her money melted.

One day she opened the cupboard and found it bare. The emptiness stared back—wood and shadow where bread should have been, where a bit of cheese, a heel of meat, even a clove of onion might have waited. She looked into the faces of her dependents, and pity rose in her like hot water. She turned to the baby as if he could judge her. Could she let grandfather and poor old Martin want a meal?

She went to her box and took out Catherine’s angel.

As she unfolded the linen, a tear dropped on it—gentle mortification, not anger: the shame of a proud heart forced to eat its own words. She sent Martin out to change the coin.

While he was gone, a Frenchman came with one of the dealers in illuminated work—the man who once had offered her so poor a price that she had nearly bitten her lip through to keep from crying. The Frenchman spoke with the measured courtesy of one who traffics in beauty for a powerful table. He said he was employed by his sovereign to collect masterpieces for her book of hours.

Margaret brought forth her two best works—those sheets she had guarded as if they were her own memories pressed into pigment. The Frenchman bent close. He breathed in, not scent but colour. And he was charmed most by the page where flowers and raspberries and little creeping things had been shaded by Margaret Van Eyck’s hand: the berries like beads of dark wine, the leaves veined fine as fishbone, the small creatures set among them as naturally as crumbs invite sparrows. It was a garden one could almost smell—sweetness and green bruised stalk—though it lay flat and silent.

He named a price so high it made Margaret blink, as if someone had opened a shutter on full noon.

“Nay,” said she, drawing back, “flout not my need, good stranger. Three mouths there be in this house, and none to fill them but me.”

Curious arithmetic—she had left herself out, as women do when they have grown used to counting others first.

“I’d flout thee not,” he answered quickly. “My princess charged me strictly, ‘Seek the best craftsmen’; but I will no hard bargains. Make them content with me, and me with them.”

The next moment Margaret was on her knees by the cradle, kissing little Gerard, sobbing into his warm baby smell of milk and clean linen, and showering four gold pieces upon him again and again, as if coins could be blessings made visible.

“And oh—what a good princess,” she told him in very broken Dutch, the words coming haltingly, like a woman trying to speak her childhood tongue after living too long among other sounds. “Wasn’t she? We will pray for her, won’t we, my lambkin—when we are old enough?”

Martin burst in then, his face red with wrath, his old pride stung raw. “They will not change it. I trow they think I stole it.”

“I am beholden to thee,” Margaret said hastily, and almost snatched the angel from him. She wrapped it up again, and put it back in its hiding-place, as if shame itself might hear and come peering.

Before those unexpected funds were spent she took to her ironing and starching again. The house filled with the clean steam of boiled linen; the sharp, comforting scent of starch rose like a promise. The iron’s hiss on damp cloth was a small, steady music—work made audible—so different from the mind’s inward roar when it has nothing solid to do. She learned to judge the heat by ear and smell, to cool it by touching it to a wet rag till it gave the right sigh.

In the midst of this, Martin sickened.

He lay nine days, and the hearth seemed to burn lower, as if it grudged warmth to a room where an old man moaned. Margaret made him broths—thin at first, then richened with what she could spare: a bone begged from a neighbour, a pinch of herbs, a crust softened and strained. She listened to his breathing at night; each rattle was a sound that lodged in her chest.

Then he died.

Nearly all her money went to bury him decently. She saw to it herself; she would not let him go out of the world like refuse. Afterward there was an empty chair by her fireside. For Martin had preferred the hearth to the sun as soon as the Busy Body was gone; he had sat there, nodding, hearing the kettle’s murmur and the street’s distant life, content with that small kingdom of warmth.

Margaret would not allow anybody to sit in this chair now. And whenever her eye rested too long on it vacant, it cost her a tear—quick, involuntary, like salt flung on food.

Now there was nobody to carry her linen home. To do it herself she must leave little Gerard in charge of a neighbour. But she dared not trust such a treasure to mortal; besides, she could not bear him out of her sight for hours and hours. So she set inquiries on foot for a boy to carry her basket on Saturday and Monday.

A plump, fresh-coloured youth came in, cap in hand, cheeks pink as if from the cooper’s yard heat. He was called Luke Peterson; he looked fifteen, but was eighteen. He blushed, twiddled his bonnet, and asked, with a shy boldness, whether a man would not serve her turn as well as a boy.

Before he spoke, Margaret had been thinking, This boy will just do. But she took his cue. “Nay; but a man will maybe seek more than I can well pay.”

“Not I,” Luke said warmly. “Why, Mistress Margaret, I am your neighbour, and I do very well at the coopering. I can carry your basket for you before or after my day’s work, and welcome. You have no need to pay me anything. ’Tisn’t as if we were strangers, ye know.”

“Why, Master Luke, I know your face, for that matter; but I cannot call to mind that ever a word passed between us.”

“Oh yes, you did, Mistress Margaret. What, have you forgotten? One day you were trying to carry your baby and eke your pitcher full o’ water; and quo’ I, ‘Give me the baby to carry.’ ‘Nay,’ says you, ‘I’ll give you the pitcher, and keep the bairn myself;’ and I carried the pitcher home, and you took it from me at this door, and you said to me, ‘I am muckle obliged to you, young man,’ with such a sweet voice—not like the folk in this street speak to a body.”

“I do mind now, Master Luke,” said Margaret; and her voice softened, as it always did when remembering kindness. “And methinks it was the least I could say.”

“Well, Mistress Margaret, if you will say as much every time I carry your basket, I care not how often I bear it, nor how far.”

“Nay, nay,” said Margaret, colouring faintly. “I would not put upon good nature. You are young, Master Luke, and kindly. Say I give you your supper on Saturday night, when you bring the linen home, and your dawn-mete o’ Monday; would that make us anywise even?”

“As you please,” Luke answered, but he looked wounded at the thought of being thought greedy. “Only say not I sought a couple o’ diets! for such a trifle as yon.”

So Luke came and went with the basket. Sometimes his feet on the stair were light, a young man’s rhythm; sometimes the basket made its own music, cloth shifting and buckles clinking. The house, for a while, went pretty smoothly. Heaven gave the poor young woman health and strength to balance her many ills; she set her days in order like loaves on a board.

But the heart grew more and more troubled by Gerard’s long, and now most mysterious silence. Work could not drown it. The ear, trained to catch a child’s smallest whimper, could not help catching the absence of another sound: no knock, no tread, no messenger calling his name. The street noises—vendors crying, children shrieking, the hammering from a distant yard—seemed louder when they brought her no meaning. Suspense, that mental torturer, began to tear her heavy heart with hot pincers, till she cried often and vehemently, “Oh, that I could know the worst.”

One day, while she was in this state, she heard a heavy step mount the stair.

She started and trembled. That is no step that I know. Ill tidings?

The door opened, and Eli came in—unexpected—grave and kind. He filled the little room with his presence, as if age itself had entered, bringing the chill of far journeys and the weight of news.

Margaret eyed him in silence, and her agitation grew, as broth grows to a boil when one forgets it for a moment.

“Girl,” said he, and his voice was low, “the skipper is come back.”

“One word,” gasped Margaret, and all the blood in her seemed to rush to her ears. “Is he alive?”

“Surely I hope so. No one has seen him dead.”

“Then they must have seen him alive.”

“No, girl; neither dead nor alive hath he been seen this many months in Rome. My daughter Kate thinks he is gone to some other city. She bade me tell you her thought.”

“Ay,” said Margaret, and her tone fell dark, as if the hearth had suddenly gone out. “Like enough. Like enough. My poor babe!”

Eli’s mouth trembled; then, as if the body will still ask its plain needs even when the soul is shaken, the old man said in a faintish voice that he had come fasting, and asked her for a morsel to eat.

The surface of Margaret’s agitated mind took pity. Her hands moved of themselves—hands trained to feed grief as well as hunger. She set water to heat; she cut what little she had; she fried, she warmed, she poured. She scarcely knew what she did, only that the sound of a knife on board, the simmer of a pot, the small crackle of fire were things she could command when nothing else would obey. The smells rose—salt, fat, warmed bread—and for a moment the room was domestic again, as if the world were not falling through her fingers.

When Eli had eaten, and was ready to go, he laid his hand upon her head—heavy, blessing-like—and said, “Be he alive, or be he dead, I look on thee as my daughter. Can I do nought for thee this day? Bethink thee now.”

“Ay, old man,” she whispered. “Pray for him; and for me!”

Eli sighed, and went sadly and heavily down the stairs. Margaret listened half stupidly to his retiring footsteps until they ceased. The street took him, and the house was hers again—hers and the child’s and the empty chair’s.

Then she sank down by the cradle, moaning, and drew little Gerard tight to her bosom, as if she could make a father out of her own arms.

“Oh, my poor fatherless boy,” she sobbed into his hair. “My fatherless boy!”
