"What was that you said about our appointment at seven-thirty?" Mr. Cupples asked as they emerged from the grand gateway of the apartment block. "Do we actually have such an engagement?"

"Certainly," Trent replied, his voice carrying a lightness that bordered on the unnatural. "You are dining with me. Nothing properly celebrates the conclusion of a year-long obsession quite like a meal I treat. No, forget my club. Tonight is a festival, and the sight of a detective in a London club, visibly buoyed by triumph, would shatter a man's reputation. Besides, the menu there is eternal, the taste identical. Tonight, we shall feast elsewhere. We shall go to Sheppard's."

"Who is Sheppard?" Cupples asked, his tone mild as they ascended Victoria Street.

"That question," Trent echoed, his bitterness sharpening the words, "is characteristic of the aimless curiosity plaguing this restless age. I propose Sheppard's, and instantly you fold your arms, demanding to know who Sheppard is before you will cross the threshold. I refuse to pander to the modern mind's vices. Sheppard's is simply a place where one eats. I do not know Sheppard; he may be a myth of totemic origin. All I know is that their saddle of mutton has caused more American visitors to curse Columbus's birth than any other dish in London. Taxi!"

A cab glided to the curb. As the driver nodded majestically, Trent lit a cigarette feverishly. "There is another reason for Sheppard's: I am about to marry the most wonderful woman in the world. The connection of ideas should be clear."

"You are marrying Mabel!" Cupples cried, seizing Trent's hand. "My dear friend, what glorious news! Shake hands, Trent; this is magnificent! I congratulate you from the depths of my heart. And may I say—I mean no interruption to your high spirits, which are entirely natural, recalling my own youth—but how earnestly I have hoped for this. Mabel has endured so much sorrow, yet she is clearly formed by the great purpose of humanity to be the guiding influence in a good man's life. However, I did not realize her sentiments regarding you. Your mind, I have known for some time. I saw it immediately when you dined at my house, listening to Professor Peppmuller while gazing at her. Some of us older fellows still have our wits about us, my dear boy."

"Mabel claims she knew it before then," Trent replied, his face falling slightly. "I thought I was playing the part of a man not utterly mad for her. Well, I was never good at dissimulation. I shouldn't be surprised if old Peppmuller, through his double-convex lenses, noticed something. But however deranged I may have appeared as an undeclared suitor," he added, regaining his vivacity, "I shall be much worse now. Thank you for your congratulations; I know you mean them. You are the sort of uncomfortable brute who would pull a face three feet long if you suspected we were making a mistake. By the way, I must be an ass tonight; I'm compelled to blither. Perhaps it would be easier if I sang you a song—one of your old favorites. What was that stave you used to sing? Something like this, wasn't it?" He tapped a rhythm on the cab floor.

"There was an old nigger, and he had a wooden leg.
He had no tobacco, no tobacco could he beg.
Another old nigger was as cunning as a fox,
And he always had tobacco in his old tobacco-box."

"Now for the chorus!
Yes, he always had tobacco in his old tobacco-box."

"You're not singing," Cupples noted. "I thought you'd make the sky ring."

"I never sang that song in my life," Cupples protested. "I've never heard it."

"Are you sure?" Trent queried doubtfully. "Well, I suppose I must take your word. It is a beautiful song, anyhow; no warbling grove in concert can beat it. Somehow it expresses my feelings better than anything else; it rises unbidden to the lips. Out of the fullness of the heart the mouth speaketh, as the Bishop of Bath and Wells said when listening to Mr. Balfour."

"When was that?" Cupples asked.

"On the occasion," Trent replied, "of the Compulsory Notification of Diseases of Poultry Bill, that ill-fated measure you of course remember. Hullo!" The cab lurched into a side street and swung onto a broad thoroughfare. "We're there already."

Trent paid the driver and ushered Cupples into a long, panelled room filled with the hum of conversation. "This is the house of fulfilled craving, the bower with roses around it. I see three bookmakers eating pork at my favorite table. We'll take the one in the opposite corner."

While Trent conferred earnestly with a waiter, Cupples warmed himself before the great fire. "The wine here," Trent resumed as they seated themselves, "is almost certainly made from grapes. What shall we drink?"

Cupples emerged from his reverie. "I think," he said, "I will have milk and soda water."

"Speak lower!" Trent urged. "The head-waiter has a weak heart. Milk and soda! Cupples, you may think you have a strong constitution, and I grant you that, but I warn you this habit has killed many a robust man. Be wise. Fill the bowl with Samian wine; leave soda to the Turkish hordes. Here comes our food."

Trent, a respected customer, ordered wine he knew well. "If you have taken a vow, drink the water at your elbow in the name of all teetotal saints, but do not seek cheap notoriety by demanding milk and soda."

"I have never taken a pledge," Cupples said, examining his mutton favorably. "I simply don't care for wine. I bought a bottle once to see what it was like, and it made me ill. But it was likely bad wine. I will taste yours, as it is your dinner. I assure you, Trent, I should like to do something unusual to show how strongly I feel. I haven't been this delighted in years. To think—the Manderson mystery resolved, the innocent exculpated, and your happiness with Mabel crowned—all at once! I drink to you, my dear friend." He took a tiny sip.

"You have a great nature," Trent said, deeply moved. "Your outward semblance belies your soul's immensity. I expected to see an elephant conducting the opera before you drank my health. Dear Cupples! May his beak retain that delicate rose-stain forever!—No, curse it all!" He broke out, surprising a flicker of discomfort on Cupples' face as he tasted the wine again. "I have no business meddling with your tastes. I apologize. You shall have what you want, even if it causes the head-waiter to perish in his pride."

Once Cupples was supplied with his monastic drink and the waiter had retreated, Trent looked across the table with significance. "In this babble of many conversations, we can speak as freely as on a bare hillside. The waiter is whispering to the young woman at the pay-desk. We are alone. What do you think of that interview this afternoon?"

Without pausing to cut his mutton, Cupples replied, "The most curious feature was the irony. We both held the clue to that mad hatred of Manderson's which Marlowe found so mysterious. We knew of his jealous obsession; we withheld that knowledge, properly, for Mabel's sake. Marlowe will never know what he was suspected of. Strange! Nearly all of us move unconsciously through a network of opinions others hold about us. I recall discovering years ago that many believed I had secretly joined the Roman Catholic Church. This absurd fiction stemmed from my advocacy of weekly meat abstinence. Manderson's belief regarding his secretary probably rested on much slighter ground. It was Mr. Bunner, I think, who told you of his hereditary temper of suspicious jealousy. Regarding Marlowe's story, it appeared straightforward to me, not especially remarkable once we admit we are dealing with a disordered mind."

Trent laughed loudly. "I confess the affair struck me as unusual."

"Only in the development of details," Cupples argued. "What is abnormal in the essential facts? A madman conceives a crazy suspicion; he hatches a plot against his fancied injurer involving his own destruction. Put thus, what is remarkable to anyone who knows lunatics? Turn to Marlowe. He finds himself in peril where truth won't save him. He escapes via bold deception. That seems to happen daily." He attacked his mutton, now unrecognizable.

"I should like to know," Trent said after a pause, "if there is anything on earth you couldn't represent as ordinary by such an argument."

A gentle smile touched Cupples' face. "Do not suspect me of empty paradox. My meaning will be clearer if I mention things I find essentially remarkable. Let me see... the life history of the liver-fluke, thanks to Poulton's researches."

"I am unable to argue the point," Trent replied. "Science may have smiled on the liver-fluke's birth, but I never heard it mentioned."

"It is not an appetizing subject," Cupples thoughtfully conceded. "All I mean is that remarkable things happen all around us if we see them; we do our perceptions no credit by regarding only those surrounded by sensational detail as remarkable."

Trent applauded with his knife handle. "I haven't heard you like this for years. You must be almost as above yourself as I am. It is a bad case of the unrest men miscall delight. But I won't sit still and hear the Manderson affair dismissed as commonplace. You may say what you like, but impersonating Manderson was an extraordinarily ingenious idea."

"Ingenious—certainly!" Cupples replied. "Extraordinarily so—no! In those circumstances, it was not strange that a clever man should conceive it. It lay almost on the surface. Marlowe was famous for imitating Manderson's voice; he had a talent for acting, a chess-player's mind, and intimate knowledge of the establishment. I grant the execution was brilliant, but everything favored it. As for the essential idea, I do not place it in the same class of ingenuity as utilizing recoil to eject and reload a firearm. I admit, however, that the details possessed unusual features. It developed a high degree of complexity."

"Did it really strike you that way?" Trent asked with desperate sarcasm.

"The affair became complicated," Cupples continued unmoved, "because after Marlowe's suspicions were awakened, a second subtle mind interfered with the first. Such duels happen in business and politics, but less frequently in crime."

"I should say never," Trent replied. "The reason is that even clever criminals seldom run to strategic subtlety. When they do, they don't get caught, since clever policemen have less strategic subtlety than the ordinary clever criminal. But that deep quality seems rarely to accompany the criminal make-up. Look at Crippen. He was a very clever criminal; he solved the central problem of clandestine murder, the disposal of the body, with extreme neatness. But how far did he see through the game? Criminals and policemen are often swift, bold tacticians, but neither is good for more than a simple plan. It's a rare faculty in any walk of life."

"One disturbing reflection remains," Cupples said, seemingly tired of abstractions. "If Marlowe had suspected nothing and walked into the trap, he would almost certainly have been hanged. How often may not a plan to fix murder on an innocent person have succeeded? There are surely cases where the accused, found guilty on circumstantial evidence, died protesting innocence. I shall never again approve of a death sentence based solely on such evidence."

"I never have," Trent agreed. "To hang in such cases seems to fly in the face of the obvious principle: 'you never can tell.' I agree with the American jurist who says we should not hang a yellow dog for stealing jam on circumstantial evidence, not even if he has jam on his nose. Attempts to fix crimes on innocent men happen constantly. It's a marked feature of coercive rule, whether in Ireland, Russia, India, or Korea; if police cannot catch a man by fair means, they do it by foul. But there is a peculiar case in the State Trials: the Campden Case. The plotter gave up his own life to secure the death of his victims. Probably you've heard of it."

Cupples confessed his ignorance and took another potato.

"John Masefield wrote a remarkable play about it," Trent said. "If it comes to London, you should see it. Women weep undemonstratively at oleo-margarine sentiment in the theatre; imagine their hysterics at that play! The facts were that John Perry accused his mother and brother of murdering a man, swearing he helped them. He told a detailed story with answers to everything, except the curious fact that the body couldn't be found. The judge, probably drunk in Restoration days, made nothing of that. The mother and brother denied it. All three were found guilty and hanged on John's evidence. Two years later, the man they were hanged for murdering returned from the sea, kidnapped by pirates. His disappearance gave John his idea. The point is that John's inclusion of himself in the accusation, amounting to suicide, convinced everyone of its truth. It was so obvious that no man would die to get someone else hanged. That is exactly the answer the prosecution would have made if Marlowe had told the truth. Not one juryman in a million would have believed in the Manderson plot."

We can understand he was capable of such a scheme. But usually, justice breaks down in penetrating spiritual truth. Sometimes it is deliberately concealed, as in Manderson's case.
