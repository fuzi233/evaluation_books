The reports of the British disaster grew heavier with every mile Harry Warrington traveled toward Pennsylvania. The two famous regiments, forged in Scottish and Continental fires, had vanished before an enemy that was barely visible. Their discipline, their valor, had dissolved into silence against a handful of savages and scattered French infantry. The commander had died bravely, his horse shot beneath him four times, twice wounded, finally struck down by a mortal blow three days after the battle. Harry's informants painted the scene with grim detail: the river crossing, the long, futile advance through the wilderness, the firing lines, the artillery struggling to clear a path, and then the ambush. Fire erupted from behind every bush and tree—a murderous fusillade that had cut the expeditionary force in half. Yet, not everyone was lost. One aide-de-camp, a Virginian gentleman, lay ill with fever and exhaustion at Dunbar's camp.

Which one? Harry hurried to the camp, arriving eventually to find George Washington stretched in a tent. It was not his brother. A sharper pain than the fever gripped Washington when he saw Harry, offering no news of George. Washington dared not speak fully. For three days after the fight, his duty had bound him to the General's side. On the fatal ninth of July, he had seen George move forward with orders from the chief, never to return. After Braddock died, the aide had retraced his steps to the field. The remaining corpses were stripped and horribly mutilated. One body he had buried, convinced it was George Warrington's. His own illness deepened, perhaps caused by the anguish of that desperate search.

"Ah, George! If you had loved him, you would have found him dead or alive," Harry cried. Nothing satisfied him but to go to the ground himself, to examine the earth. With money, he procured guides. He forded the river where the army had crossed, walking from one end to the other of the dreadful field. The Indians were gone, but birds of prey fed on mangled, festering carcasses. Save for his grandfather, lying calm with a sweet smile, Harry had never seen the face of Death. The horror of the mutilation made him turn away, shuddering. What news could the vacant woods or those rotting bodies offer the lad of his lost brother? He considered going unarmed, with a white flag, to the French fort where the victors had retired, but his guides refused. "Keep your hair for your lady mother, my young gentleman," the guide said. "'Tis enough that she loses one son in this campaign."

When Harry returned to the English encampment at Dunbar's, it was his turn to succumb to the fever. Delirium seized him; he lay in tents and beds while his friend recovered. For days, he did not know who watched him. Poor Dempster, who had tended him through previous illnesses, feared the widow would lose both her children. But the fever subsided enough for the boy to mount a horse. Mr. Washington and Dempster escorted him home. With heavy hearts, the three beheld the gates of Castlewood once more.

A servant announced their arrival. First came Mrs. Mountain and her little daughter, welcoming Harry with tears and embraces, though she offered no nod to Mr. Washington. The little girl approached the young officer, hands clasped behind her back, asking why he had not brought George back too. Harry did not hear; the sobs and caresses of his nurse kept him from listening to Fanny.

Dempster was received graciously. "Whatever could be done, we know you would do, Mr. Dempster," Mrs. Mountain said, taking his hand. "Make a curtsey to Mr. Dempster, Fanny, and remember to be grateful to all who have been friendly to our benefactors. Will it please you to take any refreshment before you ride, Colonel Washington?"

Washington had ridden enough. He counted on Castlewood's hospitality as surely as he counted on the shelter of his own roof. "The time to feed my horse, and a glass of water for myself, and I will trouble Castlewood hospitality no further," he said.

"Sure, George, you have your room here, and my mother is above-stairs getting it ready!" Harry cried. "That poor horse of yours stumbled with you, and can't go farther this evening."

"Hush! Your mother won't see him, child," Mrs. Mountain whispered.

"Not see George? Why, he is like a son of the house," Harry insisted.

"She had best not see him. I don't meddle any more in family matters, child: but when the Colonel's servant rode in and said you were coming, Madam Esmond left this room, my dear, where she was sitting reading Drelincourt, and said she felt she could not see Mr. Washington. Will you go to her?" Harry took his friend's arm, excused himself to the Colonel, promising to return shortly, and left the parlor for the upper rooms where Madam Esmond waited.

He hurried across the corridor, averting his head from one special door he disliked looking at—the room of his brother—but as he reached it, Madam Esmond emerged, folding him to her heart and leading him inside. A settee stood by the bed; a book of psalms lay on the coverlet. Everything else in the room remained exactly as George had left it.

"My poor child! How thin thou art grown—how haggard you look! Never mind. A mother's care will make thee well again. 'Twas nobly done to go and brave sickness and danger in search of your brother. Had others been as faithful, he might be here now. Never mind, my Harry; our hero will come back to us—I know he is not dead. One so good, and so brave, and so gentle, and so clever as he was, I know is not lost to us altogether." Perhaps Harry thought within himself that his mother had not always spoken of her eldest son thus. "Dry up thy tears, my dear! He will come back to us, I know he will." When Harry pressed her for a reason, she said she had seen her father two nights running in a dream, and he had told her her boy was a prisoner among the Indians.

Madam Esmond's grief had not prostrated her as Harry's had; instead, it had stirred and animated her. Her eyes were eager, her countenance angry and revengeful. The lad wondered almost at the condition in which he found his mother.

But when he besought her to go downstairs and welcome George Washington, who had accompanied him, the lady's excitement painfully increased. "I should shudder at touching his hand," she said. "He declared Mr. Washington had taken her son from her; she could not sleep under the same roof with him."

"He gave me his bed when I was ill, mother; and if our George is alive, how has George Washington a hand in his death? Ah! please God it be only as you say," Harry cried, bewildered.

"If your brother returns, as return he will, it will not be through Mr. Washington's help," Madam Esmond said. "He neither defended George on the field, nor would he bring him out of it."

"But he tended me most kindly in my fever," Harry interposed. "He was yet ill when he gave up his bed to me, and was thinking only of his friend, when any other man would have thought only of himself."

"A friend! A pretty friend!" the lady sneered. "Of all his Excellency's aides-de-camp, my gentleman is the only one who comes back unwounded. The brave and noble fall, but he, to be sure, is unhurt. I confide my boy to him, the pride of my life, whom he will defend with his, forsooth! And he leaves my George in the forest, and brings me back himself! Oh, a pretty welcome I must give him!"

"No gentleman," Harry cried warmly, "was ever refused shelter under my grandfather's roof."

"Oh no—no gentleman!" the little widow exclaimed. "Let us go down, if you like, son, and pay our respects to this one. Will you please to give me your arm?" Taking an arm that was little able to give her support, she walked down the broad stairs into the apartment where the Colonel sat.

She made a ceremonious curtsey, extending one of the little hands, allowing it to rest in his for a moment. "I wish that our meeting had been happier, Colonel Washington," she said.

"You do not grieve more than I do that it is otherwise, madam," the Colonel replied.

"I might have wished that the meeting had been spared, that I might not have kept you from friends whom you are naturally anxious to see—that my boy's indisposition had not detained you. Home and his good nurse Mountain, and his mother and our good Doctor Dempster, will soon restore him. 'Twas scarce necessary, Colonel, that you, who have so many affairs on your hands, military and domestic, should turn doctor too."

"Harry was ill and weak, and I thought it was my duty to ride by him," the Colonel faltered.

"You yourself, sir, have gone through the fatigues and dangers of the campaign in the most wonderful manner," the widow said, curtseying again, looking at him with her impenetrable black eyes.

"I wish to Heaven, madam, some one else had come back in my place!"
