# CHAPTER XXXIX: In Which Colonel Altamont Crashes The Dinner Party, Makes A Spectacle Of Himself, Then Bails Before Anyone Can Even Offer Him A Proper Slice Of Pineapple
The pre-arranged dinner at Sir Francis Clavering’s found Major Pendennis turning up only because every far more impressive boring snob engagement he’d bet on fell through, and Arthur Pen tagging along because he couldn’t think of a single better way to waste the evening. The drawing room was nearly empty when they trundled in: only the Claverings, plus their old mate Captain Strong. Pen was chuffed to see him, but the Major pulled a face so sour it could’ve curdled the nearby lemonade, dead annoyed at the thought of dining next to what he rudely called “Clavering’s blasted glorified house steward.”

His mood perked up considerably when Welbore Welbore, Clavering’s stuffy country neighbor and fellow MP, wandered in. Though perfectly dull, and so quiet at dinner he contributed less conversation than the footman looming behind his chair, Welbore was an ancient-family country gentleman with seven thousand a year, and the Major always relaxed instantly around people that rich and unthreatening. The rest of the party trickled in after that: the sharp old Dowager Lady Rockminster, who had very specific scheming reasons to stay in the Claverings’ good graces, plus Lady Agnes Foker and her doofus son Harry, our old acquaintance. Mr. Pynsent bailed last minute for parliamentary duties – duties the other two MPs at the party were actively ignoring so hard they’d already eaten half the bread rolls.

Last to show was Blanche Amory, decked out in a devastating white silk gown that showed off her pearly shoulders so well they could’ve been on a fancy pastry platter. Harry Foker leaned over to Pen, who was staring at her with obvious heart eyes, and stage whispered that she was a total stunner. Blanche was on her absolute best “simple country ingénue” behavior that night, greeting Pen like they’d been separated at birth, gushing about dear Fairoaks, asking after Laura and his mom, and carrying on about how she was dying to get back to the quiet countryside. Harry Foker, who usually clammed up so hard around ladies he could barely order a drink, suddenly found himself chattering away like a magpie, even before the dinner bell rang.

He was dead gutted he didn’t get to escort Blanche down the sweeping carpeted stairs, but as an earl’s grandson he got stuck with Mrs. Welbore Welbore instead – turns out that stuffy rank thing has real downsides. But he whooped internally when he got seated right next to Blanche at the dinner table, convinced he’d pulled off the greatest dining table heist in history. Spoiler: it was Blanche who’d moved the place cards, duh. She had Pen on one side and Harry on the other, and both boys were tripping over themselves to be charming.

Harry’s mom, from across the table, stared at her son like he’d grown a second head he was being that lively. He rambled about Taglioni dancing in *The Sylphide*, begged the footman for more volaille soup, pondered out loud where all the chicken legs go when they serve that soup, and hummed the ballet’s little tune off-key. Pen purred that Blanche was basically a sylph herself. Blanche gushed that Harry had the most wonderful tenor voice she’d ever heard, that she sang a little too, and they absolutely should do a duet. Pen remembered Blanche had said the exact same line to him a couple years back, and snickered to himself wondering how many other guys she’d tricked into singing duets with her, but obviously he didn’t say that out loud – instead he oozed that her voice was still his absolute favorite in the whole world, way better than Laura’s annoying contralto that was always going hoarse anyway, the little bitter gremlin.

Harry loudly declared he liked English music best, and could the footman pass him the saddle of mutton please? Blanche instantly agreed that English ballads were the only real music worth listening to. Pen begged her to sing one after dinner, and Blanche turned to Harry, giving him the full force of her big flirty eyes, and said she’d sing an English song for him if he promised to come right upstairs after the men finished their wine. Harry promised he’d be up fast, said he didn’t care about fancy expensive wine anyway, he was just here for the dinner, and then rambled on about how the hell did they get cherries inside jelly? That was the kind of question no one had ever asked at a fancy London dinner before, but Blanche listened to every single word like he was reciting poetry. When the ladies left, she dropped her gloves by Harry’s chair and her handkerchief by Pen’s, so both of them had a little souvenir to moon over. Her last bright, dazzling glance went straight to Harry, who sighed so loud his wine glass rattled, then chugged a full bumper of claret.

This dinner was way earlier than the usual 9pm London season fancy banquets, so it was still full daylight when the ladies trundled upstairs to the drawing room. The windows were propped open to let in the summer air, little urchins on the sidewalk jumped up at the area railings to peek in and gawk at the spread of pineapples and decanters, dandies trotted their horses past on the way to their fancy parties, the gas man lit the street lamps, and soon the chandelier inside the dining room was glowing bright, while outside the sky was soft blue, with a couple of stars just starting to pop on. The footman Jeames was leaning against the portico pillar staring at the sky, when he noticed a very flashily dressed bloke hanging off the railings, covered in chains and jewelry, wearing shiny boots and a slipping black wig, staring right into the dining room and cackling every time Strong laughed at a joke.

The guy was clearly three sheets to the wind, and when Strong laughed extra loud, he slapped his thigh and winked at Jeames like they were old mates. Jeames hated the look of him, so he softly called over the passing Policeman X, pointed, and didn’t say a single other word. Policeman X told the guy to move along, but he just kept cackling and staring at Strong. When X tapped his shoulder, the bloke squared up like he was ready to fight, slurring that he knew everyone inside, that was Sir Francis Clavering the MP, that was Strong, that was the kid who caused a fight at the Baymouth ball. He yelled “Strong! Strong!” so loud everyone inside heard him. “Bloody Altamont!” Sir Francis yelped, jumping out of his chair like he’d sat on a pin. Strong groaned, got up, and ran outside to drag him away, calling for a cab.

But Altamont was furious the copper had tried to move him on, and he fought his way past Strong right through the front door, slurring that he was going in the dining room to get a bottle of the good yellow seal wine that cost 100 rupees a dozen. Sir Francis, panicking that the very boring, very wealthy Mr. Welbore would think he was a total weirdo, caved and said fine, come on in. Colonel Altamont – the “ambassador” for the Nawaub of Lucknow everyone had met once at that messy Baymouth ball, if you don’t remember – stumbled into the dining room, plopped down in the first empty chair, and immediately started guzzling wine.

Strong, never one to lose his cool, deadpanned that the man had lived for ages in an Indian native court, where they all had very eccentric habits. Major Pendennis stared, wondering what on earth sort of low-rent dinner he’d accidentally been invited to. Harry Foker perked up, recognized him as the guy who sang that terrible Malay song at the Back Kitchen pub, and offered him some pineapple. Altamont snorted and said the Lucknow Nawaub fed pineapples to his pigs. Then he looked over at Pen, cackled and said he remembered him from the Baymouth ball, trying to fight that French guy. Then he locked eyes with Major Pendennis, and the second their gazes met, he went stone cold sober for two seconds, his wig slipped sideways off his head, he yelped “Captain Beak! Captain Beak!” and bolted right out the door, Strong hot on his heels. He was there for all of 15 minutes tops.

Sir Francis Clavering went white as a sheet, staring at the Major like he’d just seen a ghost. The Major squinted, pretended to think for a minute, then went “Oh! Wait a second, that’s that ex-Horse Artillery deserter who got a job with the Lucknow court! I recognize his face perfectly.” Clavering exhaled so hard he almost collapsed with relief. The cab Strong had called pulled up, they shoved Altamont inside, and the rest of the guys trundled upstairs to the ladies. Harry Foker told Pen it was the weirdest, rummest event he’d ever witnessed in his entire life, which Pen agreed was a very well thought out observation.

Blanche sang her heart out on the piano, even busted out a little tune she’d set to Pen’s own old poetry, so Pen was completely wrapped around her finger the whole time, turning pages for her like a loyal little lapdog. Harry sighed so loud the curtains fluttered, saying he wished he could write poetry like Pen, he’d make Blanche fall for him in two seconds flat. No one mentioned the drunk chaos downstairs to the ladies; Pen almost spilled the beans but caught sight of Clavering’s terrified face and kept his mouth shut. The evening was pretty dull after that: Welbore fell asleep in his chair, the Major didn’t tell any of his usual scandalous little stories, just sat quiet watching Blanche sing.

When it was time to leave, the Major gushed to Blanche that she was a perfect nightingale, the best singer in all of London, and that every fancy drawing room in the city would be begging to invite her. Pen was shocked, because his uncle had been calling Blanche a useless little sylph five minutes earlier that very morning. On the walk home, Harry stuck with the two of them, smoking a giant cigar, chattering nonstop about Blanche Amory the whole way back to Pen’s rooms in the Temple, gushing about her singing and how wonderful she was, and barely even noticed when the Major ducked into his own house and scurried inside, clearly very deep in some scheming, snobbish thought. The Major called Pen back for a second, whispered at him to make sure he went and called at Grosvenor Place the next day, they’d been extremely civil to him, very civil indeed. Pen promised, all confused, and then Harry dragged him off to ramble more about his new crush for the rest of the walk.
