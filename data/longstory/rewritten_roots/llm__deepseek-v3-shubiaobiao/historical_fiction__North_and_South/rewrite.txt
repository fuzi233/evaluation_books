The drawing-room in Harley Street was bathed in the soft glow of evening, the chandelier casting delicate patterns of light upon the walls. Margaret Hale stood by the sofa, gazing down at her cousin Edith, who lay curled in slumber like some fairy-tale princess. The white muslin of Edith’s dress pooled around her, the blue ribbons at her waist fluttering faintly with each breath.

*How lovely she is,* Margaret thought, not for the first time. They had grown up side by side, yet only now, with Edith’s wedding looming near, did Margaret truly see her cousin’s beauty—the kind that turned heads and inspired sighs. It was strange how impending absence sharpened one’s eyes.

They had been speaking of bridal finery—Corfu, where Captain Lennox’s regiment was stationed, the impracticality of pianos in tropical climates—before Edith’s words had dwindled into drowsy murmurs and then silence. Margaret had been on the verge of sharing her own dreams of returning to Helstone, to the quiet parsonage where her father preached and her mother kept house. But Edith slept, and so Margaret’s thoughts remained unspoken, folded away like the lace Newton was repairing upstairs.

From the adjoining room, voices drifted in—her aunt Shaw holding court among the ladies, their conversation a blend of congratulations and veiled comparisons.

*"I spared no expense for her trousseau,"* Mrs. Shaw declared. *"She has all my Indian shawls—the General’s gifts—though I shall never wear them again."*

*"She is fortunate indeed,"* came the reply, ripe with the unspoken envy of mothers whose daughters had married with less fanfare.

Margaret exhaled softly, half-amused, half-weary. How quickly admiration soured into competition.

A stir at the door announced Henry Lennox’s arrival, and the room shifted—shawls abandoned, postures straightened. Margaret, still draped in one of the embroidered silks, met his gaze with a quiet laugh, as if to say, *Look at us, playing at finery.*

Edith, roused by the commotion, blinked like a child awakened from a dream. Her face brightened at the sight of Captain Lennox’s brother, and she was instantly alight with questions about the bridal party, her future sister-in-law, Scotland—anything to prolong the thrill of her own romance.

Margaret retreated a step, content to observe. Henry Lennox, though less striking than his brother, had a keenness about him, a way of listening that made one feel studied. Presently, he claimed the seat beside her.

"Tell me of Helstone," he said. "I’ve heard nothing but London bustle for weeks. Describe the place you’re so eager to return to."

Margaret hesitated. How could she explain the scent of roses climbing cottage walls, the silence of the forest, the way the church spire cut the dawn sky? Words flattened such things.

"It’s home," she said at last. "That’s all."

He smiled, as if her reluctance told him more than any description could.

Edith’s sudden gasp interrupted them. Captain Lennox himself stood in the doorway, early—or was it terribly late? The guests dispersed in flurried apologies, leaving the family to their reunion.

Margaret watched as Edith, glowing, led her fiancé inside. Mrs. Shaw welcomed him with her usual air of gentle martyrdom, already sighing over her impending winter in Italy—a trial, she implied, not a pleasure.

Henry Lennox remained by the hearth, his expression unreadable. Margaret wondered what he saw in this scene: the bride-to-be fluttering over teacups, the mother sighing over imagined ailments, the cousin standing just apart, her thoughts already wandering to a village neither grand nor fashionable, but wholly her own.

The evening melted into the quiet hum of preparation, the last calm before the whirlwind of vows and farewells.

The train rattled southward, carrying Margaret Hale back to Helstone, her father dozing beside her. The weight of the last few days—the hurried farewells, the flurry of her cousin’s wedding—still pressed upon her, a dull ache beneath her ribs. She had longed for home, yet now, as the fields blurred past, an unexpected melancholy settled over her.

Her father stirred, his face softening briefly before the familiar lines of worry reclaimed it. He had aged since she last saw him properly, his once-dark hair now threaded with silver, his features sharper. Margaret wondered, not for the first time, what burdens he carried that etched such weariness into his expression.

*If only Frederick were here,* she thought. Her brother’s absence was a hollow space in their family, a wound no one dared name too loudly.

Helstone welcomed her in a rush of green—the dense woods, the sunlit ferns, the wide commons alive with darting insects and the hum of summer. She walked with her father, crushing fragrant bracken beneath her boots, laughing as the wind tugged at her skirts. Here, she was free.

But home was not without its shadows. Her mother fretted over small grievances—the dampness of the parsonage, the bishop’s neglect, the quiet isolation of their life. "If only we lived nearer to the Stansfields," she sighed, as though a mile’s distance might mend all discontent.

Margaret bit back impatience. She loved the roughness of the land, the honest faces of the villagers, the simplicity that others might call poverty. Yet evenings were long. Her father retreated to his books; her mother’s conversation circled restlessly between nostalgia and complaint.

One rainy afternoon, as they sat by the fire, Margaret nearly asked about Frederick—where he was, when they might hear from him—but the words stuck in her throat. Some sorrows were too deeply buried to disturb.

Then October came, bright and golden, and the world seemed to mend. Margaret took to sketching, determined to capture the forest’s fleeting beauty before winter stripped it bare.

She was bent over her drawing board when the maid announced, with a flourish, *"Mr. Henry Lennox."*

The name startled her. London—and all its complications—had followed her home.

*"To win a lady’s faith, be noble in thy aim;
Speak as befits a sacred thing—
Not lightly, nor for game.
Lead her from hollow mirth,
Point to the steadfast stars above,
And prove thy love not only worth
Her smile, but her respect and love."*

—After Mrs. Browning

Margaret was not surprised when Sarah announced Mr. Henry Lennox. She had just been thinking of him—how odd it was that the mind could summon a presence so effortlessly. The sunlight seemed to brighten as she rose, setting aside her sketching board to greet him.

"Tell Mamma, Sarah," she said, then turned to Lennox with a smile. "We’ve so many questions about Edith. It’s kind of you to come."

"Did you doubt I would?" His voice was lower now, almost intimate. "Though I confess, after chasing your cousins through the Highlands, I scarcely imagined Hampshire would be my next destination."

He laughed then, the lightness returning. "Those two were incorrigible—climbing cliffs, sailing lochs, sending their poor father into apoplexies. I couldn’t in good conscience abandon them until I saw their ship safely off at Plymouth."

"Plymouth! Edith never mentioned—but then, her letters have been so hurried. Did they truly sail on Tuesday?"

"Gone, and left me to my own devices at last." He produced a small, folded note. "Edith tasked me with delivering this."

Margaret took it, fingers brushing his, and excused herself—ostensibly to alert her mother again (Sarah must have forgotten), but in truth to read Edith’s words without his observant eyes upon her.

Left alone, Lennox surveyed the room. The morning sun gilded the worn chintz and faded carpet, revealing shabbiness at odds with Margaret’s quiet elegance. On the table lay Dante’s *Paradiso* in gilt-edged vellum, beside vocabulary lists in her precise hand. The contrast struck him: this was no frivolous girl.

When Margaret returned, her mother fluttered in after, full of apologies about their meager dinner—cold meat, owing to the servants’ ironing—though secretly pleased by the attention. Mr. Hale, she fretted, was out of spirits, having buried his face in his hands earlier when she criticized Helstone’s damp air.

Margaret felt a chill at the words. *One place he loved on earth.* She steered the conversation back to practicalities: "Papa likes Mr. Lennox. They talked for hours at the wedding. And cold meat will do perfectly—he’ll think it a luncheon."

To spare her mother, she proposed sketching. Lennox agreed with alacrity, selecting brushes with care. They wandered to the forest’s edge, where cottages sagged under ivy.

"These haunted me all last rain," Margaret said. "I ought to have drawn them sooner, before they crumble."

"Then we must seize the moment." He gestured to a felled oak. "Your throne, my queen—if you don’t mind a puddle for a footstool."

An old man hobbled from one cottage, and Margaret went to him, speaking gently while Lennox captured them both in quick strokes—she, tall and graceful; the man, bent like a wind-twisted tree.

"You’ve made us part of your landscape!" she accused later, laughing.

"I couldn’t resist. You’ve no idea how this sketch will torment me." The words slipped out, uncalculated. She pretended not to hear, rinsing her palette in the brook with pink-tipped ears.

Dinner was salvaged by a neighbor’s gift of carp and Mr. Hale’s return. Though threadbare, his bearing was unmistakably gentlemanly. Margaret watched him anxiously; something lurked beneath his courteous interest in their sketches.

"Pears!" he exclaimed suddenly. "Margaret, fetch the beurrés from the south wall."

Lennox proposed eating them outdoors, and they adjourned to the garden. As Mr. Hale meticulously peeled a pear, Margaret and Lennox strolled the terrace.

"You live an idyll here," he murmured. "I begin to scorn my own city-bound life."

"You mocked Helstone once—called it a ‘village in a tale.’"

"Scorned? No. Though I might have been... dismissive."

They rounded a bend, bees humming in the wall’s warmth. Abruptly, he stopped.

"Margaret." He seized her hand. "I wish you didn’t love this place so much—that you missed London just enough to—" She tried to pull away, but he held firm. "I’ve wanted to say this for months. I love you. Have I startled you too much?"

Her lips trembled. "I *am* startled. I’ve only ever thought of you as a friend. Please, let’s forget this."

His grip slackened. "Of course. If it’s unpleasant, by all means, forget." The bitterness leaked through. "Though that’s easier said than done."

She winced. "I don’t mean to hurt you."

"A struggling barrister dreaming of marriage—how absurd." His tone turned flippant, mocking himself. Margaret’s mouth curled faintly in disdain.

They rejoined Mr. Hale, who was still savoring his pear, oblivious. The conversation limped along until Lennox took his leave. At the door, his mask cracked.

"Margaret, don’t despise me. However I prattle, my heart is in earnest. Goodbye."

Then he was gone, leaving Margaret to the quiet and the weight of what had passed.

The evening had closed in, the house quieted, and Margaret ascended the stairs to dress for tea, her mind still adrift on the day’s events—the golden hours of sketching, the warmth of shared conversation, and then the cold, wrenching tension of the garden walk. How strange men were! Here she sat, heartsick over the necessity of refusal, while he, scarcely moments after the rejection of what should have been his most earnest hope, could speak as though ambition and society’s trifles were all he desired.

*If only he had been different—* The thought pierced her, sharp and regretful.

Dixon, irritable from the disruption of a visitor’s call, tugged at Margaret’s hair with rough impatience, muttering about the evening’s duties. Yet when Margaret descended, she found herself waiting alone in the dim drawing-room, the firelight flickering over unlit candles, her thoughts churning.

Her mother’s entrance scattered them. There was talk of burnt ironing blankets, of Susan Lightfoot’s vanity in adorning her bonnet with false flowers—petty concerns that Margaret answered mechanically, her mind elsewhere. Her father, silent over his tea, seemed lost in some private struggle.

At last, he stirred.

"Margaret," he said abruptly, startling her. "Leave your work. I must speak with you."

The gravity in his voice sent a chill through her. Had Mr. Lennox spoken to him? She followed, bracing herself—but nothing could have prepared her for his next words.

"I am leaving Helstone," he said, the words dropping like stones.

Her breath caught. *Leave?* But why?

He would not meet her eyes, fidgeting with papers, his voice thick with something she had never heard before—shame, perhaps, or fear.

"Papa, tell me why!"

When he looked up at last, his face was pale, resolved. "Because I can no longer serve as a clergyman in the Church of England."

The words struck like a blow. Not a promotion, not a change of parish—an abandonment. *Why?* Her mind raced—had Frederick’s troubles ensnared him? Had he—

"Not Frederick," he said quickly, as if reading her terror. "This is my own doing. My own... doubts."

*Doubts?* Her world tilted. Not of faith, he assured her—but of his place within the Church. He spoke of silenced ministers, of conscience, of a choice that had weighed upon him for years. He read to her from an old text, words meant to fortify, but they only made her weep.

She could not reconcile it—her father, steadfast and sure, now adrift.

"And Mama?" she whispered.

His face crumpled. "She does not yet know."

Margaret felt the weight of it settle upon her—the secrecy, the impending upheaval. Where would they go?

"Milton-Northern," he said dully.

A manufacturing town—grim, unknown. But there, he said, he could work. Tutor the sons of tradesmen, men who hungered for learning they had never had. There, he would not be haunted by the ghost of Helstone.

A fortnight. Only a fortnight.

She swallowed the rising protest. If it must be, then let it be swift. But to tell her mother—that task fell to her, and the dread of it was worse than anything.

Before she left, he laid his hands upon her head, murmuring a blessing. She answered instinctively—"May He restore you to His Church"—then feared she had wounded him. But he only held her close, whispering of martyrs, of pain endured.

When her mother’s voice called from the hall, they parted as if the sound were a blade between them.

"Tell her by tomorrow," he said, and Margaret nodded, numb.

The drawing-room air was stifling. She sat, staring at the fire, her future upended, her heart aching.

Helstone was lost to them. And what lay ahead, she could not yet see.

*"Grant me a love both thoughtful and wise,
To see the joy in laughter’s bright eyes,
To wipe the tears from sorrow’s frail face,
And walk beside each soul with tender grace."*

Margaret listened patiently as her mother spoke of small kindnesses for the poorest of their parish—a warm shawl for old Simon, broth for Mary’s ailing boy. Each word was a needle pressed to her heart. Soon, the frost would come, and they would be gone from Helstone. Who would read to Simon when his eyes failed? Who would notice the crippled child waiting in vain at the cottage door?

“We must do all we can,” Margaret said quickly, her voice trembling. “Our time here is short.”

Mrs. Hale frowned. “Are you unwell? You look pale.”

“Only tired,” Margaret lied, forcing a smile. “The air is sweet after London’s smoke. But I should rest.”

She retreated upstairs, feigning weariness. Yet once alone, she paced, restless, until the groan of a floorboard silenced her. She curled into the window-seat, staring at the church tower, its sharp lines cutting into the dusk. Sixteen hours ago, she had watched the sunrise with hope. Now, her heart lay heavy, drained of its lightness.

Henry Lennox’s proposal felt distant, unreal. The truth was harder: her father, doubting, had stepped away from the Church. An outcast. A schismatic. The weight of it pressed upon her like stone.

Beyond the window, the sky stretched endless, indifferent. Where was God in such vastness? If prayers vanished into that void, unheard—what then?

A touch startled her. Her father stood in the moonlight, his face weary. “Margaret,” he said softly, “pray with me.”

They knelt together—he whispering the Lord’s Prayer, she bowed in silent shame. She had doubted more deeply than he. When he left, she crept to bed, but sleep brought no peace. Dreams twisted with visions of Henry falling, dying, yet alive again in London’s drawing rooms.

Dawn came too soon.

At breakfast, her mother chattered brightly, oblivious. Mr. Hale stood abruptly. “I’ll be home by seven,” he said, avoiding their eyes.

Margaret understood. By evening, her mother must know.

She waited until they walked the garden, then spoke quickly, as if ripping off a bandage.

“Mamma—Papa is leaving Helstone. He’s given up the Church. We’re to live in Milton-Northern.”

Mrs. Hale stared. “Nonsense.”

“It’s true. He wrote to the bishop. It’s done.”

Her mother sank onto a bench, trembling. “He never told me.”

Margaret swallowed guilt. She should not have been the messenger. “He feared to pain you,” she murmured, drawing her close.

“Doubts? What doubts?” Mrs. Hale’s voice sharpened. “Does he think himself wiser than the Church?”

Margaret shook her head, tears spilling.

“And in a fortnight?” her mother cried. “No consultation—no warning—”

Margaret bit back a defense of her father. “Perhaps you’ll be glad to leave. The air here never suited you.”

“Glad? For factories and smoke? For disgrace?”

The day stretched long. Margaret stayed at her mother’s side, steering her from anger to practical worries—the furniture, the move—anything to blunt the edge of grief.

By evening, Mrs. Hale lay exhausted in bed. When Mr. Hale returned, his face ashen, his wife ran to him. “Richard! Why did you not tell me?”

Margaret fled upstairs, muffling sobs in her pillow.

Later, Dixon found her, red-eyed. The maid clucked disapproval. “Turning Dissenter at his age! What would Sir John say?”

Margaret rose, fire in her gaze. “You forget yourself, Dixon.”

The maid faltered, then bowed to Margaret’s steel.

Time raced. Mrs. Hale took ill, overwhelmed. Margaret shouldered the burden—packing, planning, while her father wandered, hollow-eyed.

“Where do we go?” she pressed him one night.

“Lodgings first, then a house.” He sighed. “We’ll have less, Margaret.”

She nodded. Once, her choices had been ribbons and dinner guests. Now, the weight of their future rested on her shoulders.

And each day asked more of her than the last.

The final day at Helstone dawned gray and heavy, as if the very sky mourned their departure. The once-tidy parsonage was now a maze of crates and straw, the air thick with the scent of dust and memories. The floors, stripped bare, echoed with each footstep, a hollow sound that seemed to whisper of endings.

Upstairs, Mrs. Hale and Dixon moved slowly through the remnants of their life, pausing over tiny relics—a child’s worn shoe, a lock of hair tied with ribbon—each object a tender wound. Downstairs, Margaret stood motionless in the chaos, her face pale but composed, directing the workmen with quiet efficiency. To the servants, she seemed untouched by sorrow, but beneath her calm, her heart ached with a weight no sigh could lift.

The garden outside bore the marks of autumn’s decay. A mist, soft and lilac-tinted, blurred the edges of the world, and the last leaves clung stubbornly to the trees, soon to be felled by frost. A robin sang—perhaps the very one her father had loved, the one for whom he’d built a shelter by his study window. Margaret walked the familiar path beneath the pear trees, her fingers brushing the thyme where Henry Lennox had once spoken words she could no longer dwell upon.

*Where is he now?* she wondered. In London, no doubt, amid the clamor of courts and dinners, strolling through Temple Gardens as he’d described to her, the city’s roar a constant hum around him. Here, there was only silence, broken by the distant creak of a cottage door or the stealthy rustle of a poacher in the woods.

A sudden crack—a falling branch—sent her fleeing back to the house, her pulse racing. Charlotte, startled, let her in, and Margaret stood in the dismantled drawing room, its emptiness pressing in around her.

By evening, exhaustion weighed upon them all. Mr. Hale, worn and withdrawn, could scarcely speak. Margaret, fighting tears, forced herself to steady her voice. "We cannot talk of it tonight," she murmured, turning away before her composure broke.

The next morning, the carriage bore them away, Helstone shrinking behind them—the roses, the church tower, the life they had known. Margaret’s throat tightened as she closed her eyes against the sight, but the tears came anyway, silent and unchecked.

London, when they reached it, was a whirl of noise and strangers. Mrs. Hale, briefly animated, pointed out familiar shops, exclaiming at the changes. Then, by chance, Margaret glimpsed Henry Lennox in the crowd—just a flash of his profile before the carriage rolled on. Her breath caught, but she did not call out.

That night, in the hushed gloom of the hotel, the weight of their isolation settled over them. Outside, the city pulsed with life; inside, they were adrift, their sorrow too deep for words.

And so, with silent tears and unspoken grief, they left Helstone behind.

*"Mist clogs the sunshine,
Smoky dwarf houses
Hem me round everywhere."*

The following afternoon, the train carried them twenty miles from Milton-Northern, branching onto the narrow line that led to Heston. The town sprawled in a single, meandering street, parallel to the seashore, its character distinct from the genteel bathing resorts of southern England—sturdier, more purposeful. The carts bore iron fittings rather than wood, the people moved with brisk intent even in leisure, and the colors of the world seemed muted, enduring.

Margaret noted the absence of smock-frocks among the country folk—too cumbersome for machinery, she supposed. In southern towns, shopkeepers lounged at their thresholds, savoring the air; here, idle hands found occupation in needless tasks, ribbons unrolled and re-rolled. The contrasts pressed upon her as she and her mother searched for lodgings. Two nights at hotels had strained their purse, and they took the first clean rooms available.

For the first time in days, Margaret felt rest—a dreamy repose by the sea, the rhythmic lap of waves, the cries of donkey-boys, the salt-kissed air warm even in late November. She let the scenes drift past, unwilling to anchor them in thought, content to exist in the present. But the future loomed, inevitable.

Milton-Northern announced itself miles before they arrived—a leaden smudge on the horizon, stark against the pale winter sky. As they neared, the air soured with smoke, the grassy sweetness of Heston replaced by soot. The streets unfurled in rigid lines, rows of brick houses interrupted by the hulking factories, chimneys belching black plumes. The thoroughfares clogged with wagons, all laden with cotton—raw in sacks, woven into bales. The crowds were well-dressed but slovenly, lacking the threadbare polish of London’s poor.

Mr. Hale pointed out New Street, Milton’s bustling artery, its growth having enriched their friend Mr. Bell. Margaret scarcely listened, her attention snagged by the oppressive energy of the place. They alighted at a hotel, where a message awaited: Mr. Thornton, a local manufacturer, had called.

When Margaret entered the sitting room, she found not the clergyman her father expected, but a young woman of quiet command—her straw bonnet plain, her shawl draped like an empress’s mantle. Thornton, unprepared for her presence, faltered. She met his gaze without fluster, her poise unshaken by his rough Milton bearing.

Their exchange was brief, strained. He spoke in clipped sentences; she replied with courteous detachment, her pride mistaken for disdain. By the time Mr. Hale returned, Thornton’s pride had hardened into resentment. Only the elder Hale’s warmth thawed the tension, their talk turning to mutual acquaintances and the house in Crampton—a practical choice, though its gaudy wallpaper dismayed Margaret.

Thornton left with a stiff bow, his departure a relief. Margaret, exhausted, spared little thought for him—a man of thirty, unremarkable in feature, yet formidable in presence. "Not a gentleman," she admitted, "but not common." Her father bristled at the implied slight to his friend.

Later, when they moved into the Crampton house, the garish paper was gone. The landlord claimed no credit, but Margaret suspected Thornton’s intervention—a small mercy, unspoken. Milton’s grime and clamor awaited them, but for now, the walls were bare, and the future, though stern, was theirs to shape.

The yellow fog of November clung thick to Milton, swallowing the world beyond the windows. It seeped under doors, pressed against the glass like a suffocating hand, and left the Hale family stranded in their half-unpacked house, their spirits as damp as the air.

Margaret set down the crate she had been carrying—*Helstone* still scrawled across its side in faded ink—and turned to her mother, who stood pale and trembling in the doorway.

"Are we to live *here*?" Mrs. Hale whispered.

The words struck Margaret like a blow. She forced a steadiness into her voice she did not feel. "London’s fogs are worse, sometimes."

"But London had friends behind it," her mother murmured. "Here, we are alone."

Dixon, ever loyal, clucked her tongue. "A grim place indeed, ma’am. I’ve half a mind it’ll be the death of you."

Margaret stiffened. "Then we must make it livable. Come, Dixon—let’s finish Mother’s room before she takes ill from standing in this chill."

Her father, too, was restless. He paced to the window, though there was nothing to see but the shifting grey. "Margaret, I fear this air is poison. If your mother sickens—if *you* do—"

She could offer no comfort. Milton was their lot now, its smoke and grit inescapable. The weight of it pressed upon her that night as she sat in her narrow bedroom, the fog pressing close outside, the blank wall of the neighboring house looming like a prison gate.

A letter from Edith lay half-read in her lap—pages brimming with sunlight and blue Mediterranean waves, dances on ship decks, a life untouched by care. Margaret traced the words, remembering Harley Street’s polished floors, the shimmer of crystal, the ease of a world where nothing was ever out of place. That life had closed over her absence without a ripple.

*Would Henry Lennox think of me?* she wondered. But no—he would will himself to forget, just as she must.

She undressed slowly, savoring the quiet, and slept at last, dreaming of green hills.

———

Reality offered no such mercy. Winter deepened, and with it, illness. Mrs. Hale took to her bed with a cough that rattled ominously. Dixon, though she would never admit weakness, moved stiffly, her face pinched.

Servants were impossible. Factory wages lured every able-bodied girl, and those who did apply met Dixon’s scorn. "Cheeky things," she grumbled. "Asking *me* if we’ve the means to keep staff!"

Margaret took the task upon herself, venturing into streets alive with the clatter of mill-workers. The boldness of the girls unnerved her—their loud laughter, their fingers plucking at her shawl to judge its quality. But it was the men’s frank admiration that set her cheeks aflame.

"Bonny face like yours brightens the day," one called.

She ought to have been insulted. Yet when a careworn laborer added, "You may well smile—many would, to look as you do," she found herself returning his tired grin.

———

They met often after that, she and the gaunt man with his hollow-cheeked daughter. One afternoon, as Margaret gathered early violets from the roadside, the girl’s wistful glance moved her to offer them.

"Thank you," the man said, his voice rough with emotion. "Bessy’ll treasure these."

Margaret walked with them, matching their slow pace. "You’re not strong?" she asked the girl gently.

Bessy shook her head. "Nor ever will be."

"Spring will help," Margaret ventured.

The girl’s eyes were calm. "Not for me. My spring’s coming elsewhere."

Her father did not contradict her, only murmured, "Aye, lass," with a sorrow that twisted Margaret’s heart.

"Where do you live?" she asked abruptly.

"Frances Street," the man—Nicholas Higgins—said, wary. "But we’ve no need of charity."

"It’s not charity," Margaret said, stung. "I only wished to call."

He studied her, then relented. "You may come, if you like. You’ve a kind heart—I see that."

Bessy, as they parted, caught her sleeve. "You’ll not forget?"

Margaret smiled. "No."

And with that, Milton grew a little less grey.

*"Let porcelain, painted with azure and gold,
Receive the sweet leaf that the East does hold,
Or Mocha’s dark berry, kissed by the sun—
Let china and brew in due union be one."*

The morning after her encounter with Higgins and his daughter, Margaret was startled by her father’s unusual presence in the drawing-room at such an early hour. He moved about restlessly, touching a vase here, adjusting a book there—a nervous habit she recognized at once. At last, he spoke.

"My dear, I’ve invited Mr. Thornton to tea this evening."

Mrs. Hale, reclining in her chair with closed eyes—her face now perpetually shadowed by discomfort—stirred with immediate displeasure.

"Mr. Thornton? Tonight? Whatever for? Dixon is laundering my muslins, and there’s no soft water to be had with these wretched east winds. I suppose Milton’s air will never soften."

"The wind is shifting, my love," Mr. Hale offered, though his knowledge of the compass was dubious at best.

"Nonsense," Mrs. Hale replied, drawing her shawl tighter. "But wind or no wind, I suppose the man will come."

Margaret smiled. "Mamma, you’ve never seen Mr. Thornton. He strikes me as the sort who would relish a challenge—storms, adversaries, anything to test his mettle. The worse the weather, the more certain his arrival. Still, I’ll help Dixon. I’ve become quite adept at starching. And he’ll likely be content with Papa’s conversation." She turned to her father with playful curiosity. "I do long to meet this Pythias to your Damon. Our first meeting was awkward enough—I wonder if we’ll fare better this time."

Mr. Hale shook his head. "I doubt you’d find him agreeable, Margaret. He’s not what one would call a ladies’ man."

She arched a brow. "I’ve little admiration for such creatures. But he comes as your friend—as one who respects you."

"The only man in Milton who does," Mrs. Hale muttered.

"Well, then we must welcome him properly," Margaret said briskly. "Cocoa-nut cakes, perhaps. Dixon will be pleased if we ask. And I’ll see to your caps, Mamma."

Yet as the morning wore on, Margaret found herself resenting the intrusion. She had meant to write to Edith, to lose herself in Dante, to visit the Higginses again. Instead, she stood ironing, listening to Dixon’s grievances, her own weariness settling into the beginnings of a headache.

By the time she sat down at last, she could barely speak. "I am no longer Peggy the laundry-maid," she announced faintly, "but Margaret Hale, the lady." It was meant lightly, but her mother took it to heart.

"To think a daughter of mine—a Beresford, once the belle of Hampshire—should labor like a servant to receive a tradesman!"

Margaret straightened, stung. "Mamma, don’t punish me for a careless jest. I don’t mind work, not for you or Papa. I’m still a lady, whether I’m ironing or scrubbing floors. I’m only tired now—but I’d do it all again. And as for Mr. Thornton’s trade, it’s hardly his fault. What else could a man of his breeding do?"

She retreated to her room, the weight of the day pressing upon her.

---

Meanwhile, in the Thornton household, another scene unfolded. Mrs. Thornton, a woman of formidable presence, sat mending a fine linen cloth in the dining room, her strong hands moving with precision. The room was austere in its elegance—no frivolous books, only Matthew Henry’s commentaries solemnly displayed.

From elsewhere in the house came the sound of a piano—a rushed, stumbling rendition of some drawing-room piece, half the notes lost in haste.

Her son’s firm step passed the door.

"John? You’re home early."

He appeared, already turning toward the stairs. "I’m dressing for tea at the Hales’."

"Dressing? In my day, young men didn’t primp twice for a parson’s invitation."

"Mr. Hale is a gentleman. His wife and daughter are ladies."

"Wife and daughter?" Her needle paused. "What do they do? You’ve never spoken of them."

"I’ve only met Miss Hale briefly."

Her lips thinned. "Mind you don’t get caught by some penniless girl."

His expression darkened. "I’m not so easily trapped. And I’ll not hear Miss Hale spoken of that way. No woman has ever tried to snare me—nor would I tolerate it."

Mrs. Thornton sniffed. "Our Milton girls may have too much pride for such games, but southern ladies? They’re bred to hunt fortunes."

A bitter laugh escaped him. "If Miss Hale hunted anything, it certainly wasn’t my favor. She treated me with all the warmth of a queen dismissing a peasant."

His mother’s eyes flashed. "A clergyman’s daughter, looking down on you? I wouldn’t dress for the likes of her!"

As he left, he tossed back, "Mr. Hale is a good man. I’ll tell you of his wife tonight—if you care to hear."

The door closed sharply behind him.

Mrs. Thornton’s grip tightened on her needle. *Despise my son?* she thought savagely. *Let her try to find his equal. I know his worth—better than anyone. And I know contempt when I see it.*

Her stitching grew fiercer with every pull of the thread.

—George Herbert

Mr. Thornton left the house swiftly, his mind still churning from the earlier conversation. He walked briskly toward Crampton, the evening air cool against his skin. The church bell tolled half-past seven as he reached the Hale residence, where Dixon, with her usual reluctance, admitted him after a delay that tried his patience.

The drawing-room welcomed him with warmth, a stark contrast to the austere grandeur of his own home. Here, the lamplight pooled softly in the center of the room, casting a glow over the worn but comfortable furnishings—Helstone chintz drapes, a scattering of books, and baskets of needlework. Margaret stood by the tea-table, her movements graceful as she poured, her ivory hands flashing with the occasional glint of a bracelet that slipped down her wrist. She pushed it back absently, unaware of how his gaze followed the motion, how the simple act held his attention more than her father’s words.

Mr. Hale greeted him warmly, steering him toward Mrs. Hale, whose pallor spoke of lingering illness. Margaret’s silence and the shadows beneath her eyes betrayed her own fatigue, yet she remained poised, ready to interject should the conversation falter.

The contrast between the two men was striking. Mr. Hale, slight and delicate in frame, his expressive face alive with shifting emotions, seemed almost ethereal beside Thornton’s solid presence. The mill owner’s features were carved with purpose—brows straight and heavy over eyes that missed nothing, lips firm until a rare smile transformed him, bright as sudden sunlight. Margaret had admired that smile before; it was the first thing that had softened her toward him.

Their talk soon turned to Milton, its industry and its struggles. Thornton spoke with fierce pride of the city’s innovations, the raw power of progress.

*"I would rather be a man toiling—even failing—here, than idle in the complacent South,"* he declared.

Margaret stiffened. *"You do not know the South,"* she retorted, color rising in her cheeks. *"If there is less frenzy of trade, there is also less suffering. Our poor do not wear that look of bitterness I see here."*

Thornton’s voice gentled. *"And may I say, you do not know the North?"*

She fell silent, her throat tight with homesickness.

Mrs. Hale interjected with a complaint of Milton’s grime, and Thornton conceded with a wry smile. *"We are commanded by Parliament to burn our smoke—like obedient children, we’ll comply… eventually."*

The discussion veered into the clashes between masters and men, the brutal rise of industry. Thornton defended the system, the possibility of a workingman’s ascent through discipline.

*"Then those who fail are their own enemies?"* Margaret challenged, her tone icy.

*"Yes,"* he shot back, stung by her disdain. But honesty compelled him to explain. He spoke of his father’s ruin, his own abrupt plunge into labor, his mother’s iron resolve. *"Hardship taught me contempt for indulgence, not hatred for those trapped by it."*

Mr. Hale remarked on his education, but Thornton scoffed. *"What good was Homer when I was fighting for bread?"* Only now, with security, could he appreciate the past.

As he departed, Thornton extended his hand to Margaret in the local custom. Unprepared, she offered only a curt bow. The rebuff stung. He left, muttering under his breath—

Yet for all his irritation, the memory of her—the defiant tilt of her chin, the fleeting warmth of that forgotten smile—lingered.

*"There’s iron, they say, in all our blood,
And a grain or two perhaps is good;
But his, he makes me harshly feel,
Has got a little too much of steel."*

Margaret sat in silence after Mr. Thornton’s departure, her fingers tracing idle patterns on the armrest of her chair. The firelight flickered, casting shadows that mirrored the unease in her thoughts.

“Margaret,” her father said as he returned from seeing their guest out, “I watched your face when Mr. Thornton spoke of his past. I confess, I feared you might rise and leave the room.”

She looked up, startled. “Papa! Did you truly think me so petty? Of all he said, that was the part I admired most. The rest was hard, unfeeling—boasting of Milton as if it were the center of the world, or dismissing the poor as if their struggles were mere laziness. But when he spoke of his beginnings—so plainly, without shame—and of his mother with such devotion…” She shook her head. “No, that was the only moment I found him tolerable.”

Mrs. Hale sighed. “I cannot understand you, Margaret. You, who once scorned anything resembling trade at Helstone! Mr. Hale, you ought to have warned us of his history before inviting him here. I nearly betrayed my shock when he mentioned his father’s miserable end. It might as well have been the workhouse!”

“Perhaps worse,” Mr. Hale said quietly. “Mr. Bell told me the full tale. His father gambled recklessly, failed, and took his own life. No one stood by his widow and children—no one, until Mrs. Thornton scraped together what she could and left Milton. They lived on next to nothing for years. And yet, when young Thornton returned, he repaid every debt, silently, without fanfare. That is no small thing.”

Margaret’s expression softened. “It *is* admirable. But must such strength of character be wasted on the pursuit of wealth? He speaks of progress as if its only purpose is profit, and of the poor as if their suffering were their own fault.”

“He said ‘improvident,’ not ‘vicious,’” her father corrected gently.

She gathered her mother’s sewing with a sigh. At the door, she hesitated. “Papa… I do think Mr. Thornton remarkable. But I cannot like him.”

Her father laughed. “Well, I can. Not as a hero—but as a man. Now, goodnight. Your mother is weary.”

Margaret glanced at Mrs. Hale, her heart tightening at the sight of her pallor. Milton’s sooty air and unrelenting grind had drained her. Worse still were the hushed conversations between her mother and Dixon, the stifled tears, the murmured prayers for endurance. Margaret longed to ask, to share the burden—but the wall of secrecy held firm.

Sleep eluded her that night. She resolved to hire more help, to spare her mother the strain of their new life. Days slipped by in fruitless searches until, by chance, she met Bessy Higgins in the street.

The girl’s feverish gaze and hollow cheeks spoke louder than words. “Better and not better,” Bessy whispered. “The cough eases, but my heart’s weary. I’m tired of this life, Miss Hale.”

Margaret walked beside her, a knot in her throat. “Do you truly wish to die?”

Bessy’s answer was a quiet devastation of words—years of toil, of longing for a rest that never came. When they reached the Higgins’ home, the chaos within mirrored Bessy’s exhaustion. Margaret bathed her brow, her own hands trembling.

Nicholas Higgins returned, his gruffness a shield for his fear. “No preaching,” he warned when Margaret spoke of patience, of faith. “I believe in what I see—and what I see is work left undone while folk fret over heaven.”

But Bessy clutched Margaret’s sleeve. “He’s a good man,” she pleaded. “Only—only lost.”

When the coughing fit subsided, Margaret promised to return. As she left, Higgins’ voice followed her: “If there *is* a God, may He bless thee.”

The words haunted her. At home, tea waited, cold. Her father’s attempt at cheer—news of Mrs. Thornton’s impending visit—fell flat. Margaret could only think of Bessy’s labored breath, of the shadow over her own mother.

“Perhaps Mrs. Thornton knows of a servant,” Mrs. Hale mused.

Margaret barely heard. Tomorrow would bring another call, another delay in seeing Bessy. And beneath it all, the unspoken dread: that time was slipping away, for Bessy, for her mother—for all of them.

Mr. Thornton had spent considerable effort persuading his mother to extend civility to the Hales. Mrs. Thornton was not one for casual visits, and when she did call upon others, she did so with solemnity, as though performing a duty. Her son had gifted her a carriage, but she refused to keep horses for it, preferring to hire them only for formal occasions. She had exhausted her social obligations not a fortnight prior, having paid visits to every acquaintance in Milton—leaving them now to reciprocate at their own expense.

Yet Crampton was too distant to walk, and she had repeatedly questioned whether her son’s insistence on calling upon the Hales justified the cost of a cab. Privately, she hoped it did not. "I see no sense in befriending every instructor and tradesman in Milton," she had remarked. "Next, you’ll expect me to call on Fanny’s dancing-master’s wife!"

"And so I would," John replied, unyielding, "if they were as friendless and displaced as the Hales."

"Oh, there’s no need for haste. I shall go tomorrow. I only wished you to understand the matter fully."

"If you go tomorrow, I shall order the horses."

"John, don’t be absurd. One would think you had money to burn."

"Not yet. But I will not have you jolted about in a cab again. The last time left you with a headache."

"I never complained."

"No, you never do," he said, pride softening his tone. "Which is why I must watch over you. Fanny, on the other hand, could do with a little hardship."

"She is not made of your stern material, John. She could not bear it."

Mrs. Thornton fell silent. The remark touched upon an old wound—Fanny’s fragility, so unlike the resilience of her mother and brother. Mrs. Thornton was not given to introspection, but she knew, instinctively, that Fanny would never endure adversity with fortitude. The knowledge pained her, yet it bred a gentler manner toward her daughter—the sort reserved for the sickly or weak.

A stranger might have mistaken her indulgence for greater affection toward Fanny than John. But such an observer would have been profoundly wrong. The blunt honesty between mother and son revealed a mutual trust, a bedrock of understanding. Her tenderness toward Fanny, laced with unease, betrayed no such security. She never called John by endearments—those were reserved for Fanny. Yet her pride in him was boundless, her gratitude silent but constant.

---

"Fanny, dear," Mrs. Thornton announced, "we shall take the carriage today to call on the Hales. You might visit Nurse while I am with Mrs. Hale—it is in the same direction."

"Oh, Mamma, it’s such a long way, and I am dreadfully tired."

"From what?" Mrs. Thornton’s brow furrowed.

"The weather, I think. It’s so enervating. Couldn’t Nurse come here instead? The carriage could fetch her."

Mrs. Thornton set down her sewing, considering. "It is a long walk back for her in the evening."

"I’ll send her home in a cab," Fanny said airily.

At that moment, Mr. Thornton entered, pausing before departing for the mill.

"Mother," he said, "if there is anything that might comfort Mrs. Hale in her illness, you will offer it, I trust?"

"If I can discern what she needs. I’ve little experience with invalids."

"Fanny might advise—she is seldom without some complaint. Isn’t that so, Fan?"

"I am not *always* ill," Fanny retorted. "And I shan’t be going. I have a headache."

John’s expression darkened. His mother remained bent over her stitching.

"Fanny," he said, in a tone that brooked no argument, "you will go. It will do you good. Consider it a favor to me."

He left before she could protest further. Had he stayed, she would have dissolved into tears at his severity. As it was, she muttered, "John always speaks as though I invent my ailments. Who *are* these Hales, that he makes such a fuss over them?"

"Fanny, do not speak so of your brother. He has his reasons. Now ready yourself."

Yet the exchange did little to endear the Hales to Mrs. Thornton. A quiet resentment simmered—*Who are they, that we must pay them such attention?* The question lingered long after Fanny had forgotten it, preoccupied with the effect of her new bonnet in the mirror.

---

Mrs. Thornton was not at ease in unfamiliar company. Society, to her, was a matter of dinners given and judged—not idle calls upon strangers. As she entered the Hales’ modest drawing room, her bearing was more rigid than usual, her expression forbidding.

Margaret sat embroidering cambric for Edith’s unborn child—work Mrs. Thornton privately deemed frivolous. The room was cluttered with trinkets that surely demanded excessive dusting. Time, to a woman of limited means, was money.

Her conversation with Mrs. Hale was stilted, a recitation of pleasantries. Yet when Mrs. Hale admired the antique lace at Mrs. Thornton’s collar—a rare heirloom—the visit warmed slightly.

Meanwhile, Margaret struggled to converse with Fanny.

"You have no piano," Fanny observed. "I suppose you are not musical."

"I enjoy listening, but I play poorly. My parents care little for music, so we sold ours when we moved."

"How can you bear it? A piano is nearly a necessity."

*Fifteen shillings a week, and three saved,* Margaret thought wryly. Aloud, she said, "We have concerts here, I hear."

"Oh, yes! Though too crowded. One hears the newest music, at least. I always order the latest pieces afterward."

"Do you like new music simply because it is new?"

"If it is fashionable in London, it must be worth hearing. You’ve lived there, I suppose?"

"Yes."

"How I long to see London! And the Alhambra!"

"The Alhambra?"

"From *Tales of the Alhambra*. You’ve not read it?"

Margaret admitted she had not.

Fanny sighed. "Mamma has never been to London herself. She adores Milton, smoky and grim as it is."

Margaret replied evenly, "If it has long been her home, it is natural she should love it."

Mrs. Thornton’s voice cut in. "What are you saying of me, Miss Hale?"

Caught off guard, Margaret hesitated. Fanny answered instead: "Only wondering why you are so fond of Milton, Mamma."

"It requires no explanation," Mrs. Thornton said coolly. "One does not apologize for affection toward one’s birthplace."

Margaret flushed, vexed. The exchange had been misconstrued, yet Mrs. Thornton’s pride was needlessly sharp.

"You have seen little of Milton’s industry, I gather?" Mrs. Thornton pressed.

"No," Margaret admitted. Then, truthfully: "I have no great interest in factories."

Mrs. Hale interjected with a memory of a ruined lilac gown during a candle-making tour.

Mrs. Thornton’s lips thinned. "I merely thought, as newcomers, you might wish to understand what elevates Milton. My son’s mill, for instance, showcases the finest machinery in the kingdom. Should you ever condescend to visit, I would arrange it."

As they departed, Fanny whispered, "I’m glad you dislike mills. They are so dreary."

Margaret answered quietly, "I think I should like to understand them, if I were you."

In the carriage, Mrs. Thornton spoke firmly. "We shall be civil to the Hales, Fanny, but do not grow attached to the daughter. She will do you no good. The mother is ill and seems harmless enough."

Fanny pouted. "I’ve no desire to befriend her. I only tried to amuse her."

"At least John will be satisfied," Mrs. Thornton murmured, though her thoughts remained uneasy.

*"That doubt and trouble, fear and pain,
And anguish, all, are shadows vain,
That death itself shall not remain..."*

—R.C. Trench

Margaret hurried upstairs the moment their guests departed, fetching her bonnet and shawl to visit Betsy Higgins before dinner. The cramped, bustling streets of Milton no longer felt oppressive—not now that she had come to care for one who lived among them.

The Higgins’ home was stifling, the heat from the roaring fire unbearable, though Margaret did not yet understand it was Mary’s way of offering warmth in welcome. Bessy lay on a short couch beneath the window, weaker than before, her eyes brightening as Margaret entered.

"You look so fine," Bessy murmured, fingers brushing the fabric of Margaret’s sleeve. "Like the soft raiment in the Bible. Where’d you get this?"

"London," Margaret said.

"London!" Bessy’s voice was awed. "Tell me of the country. Trees, and quiet places—I’ve a hunger for them."

Margaret hesitated. She had not spoken of Helstone since leaving it, yet now the memories poured forth—the great trees, the whispering leaves, the golden ferns like waves upon the earth.

Bessy listened, eyes closed, as if drinking in each word. "I’ve never seen the sea," she whispered. "Nor the wide commons you speak of. Here, the air’s thick with mill dust, and the noise... it never stops."

A shadow crossed her face. "Sometimes I think if this is all there is—work and coughing and no rest, even in death—I could go mad with it." Her grip on Margaret’s hand tightened, desperate.

Margaret knelt beside her. "There is more, Bessy. A Father who wipes every tear."

Bessy turned away, restless. "I know it. I do. But at night, when the fever comes..."

Margaret spoke gently of lighter things—Bessy’s sister Mary, the girl’s future. Yet even as she promised friendship, Bessy’s words hung between them: *In a week or a fortnight, I may be dead and buried.*

When Margaret returned home, she found her mother’s health weighing on her mind. Mr. Hale dismissed her worries, brushing aside the flush in his wife’s cheeks as vigor rather than pain.

"You fret too much," he said, though his pacing long into the night betrayed his own unease.

Margaret lay awake, listening to his restless steps, the echoes of Bessy’s labored breath still fresh in her ears. The year had brought sorrows she could never have imagined—yet amid them, small sparks of light still lingered.

*"I was used
To sleep at nights as sweetly as a child,—
Now if the wind blew rough, it made me start,
And think of my poor boy tossing about
Upon the roaring seas. And then I seemed
To feel that it was hard to take him from me
For such a little fault."*

In the quiet of the parlor, Margaret found an unexpected solace—her mother, Mrs. Hale, had begun to lean upon her as though she were a trusted confidante, the role Margaret had always longed to fill. No longer was she envious of Dixon’s closeness to her mother; now, it was Margaret whose ear was sought for every whispered worry, every fleeting sorrow. She listened with patience, offering kindness even for the smallest of grievances—things she herself might have overlooked, had they been her own.

And then, one evening, with Mr. Hale absent, her mother spoke of Frederick.

Margaret had yearned to ask about her brother, yet fear had kept her silent. Now, the words came unbidden from her mother’s lips, as if the wind that rattled the windows had stirred the memory of him.

*"Oh, Margaret, it was so windy last night!"* Mrs. Hale began, her voice trembling. *"It howled down the chimney—I couldn’t sleep. I never can, when the wind is like that. I used to lie awake when Frederick was at sea, and now, even if I doze, I dream of him—trapped in some terrible storm, great waves like glass walls curling over the ship, white foam hissing like a monstrous serpent..."* She shuddered. *"But he’s on land now, thank God. Though I do fear the wind might topple those factory chimneys."*

*"Where is Frederick now?"* Margaret ventured. *"Our letters go to Cadiz, but where is he truly?"*

Mrs. Hale hesitated. *"I can’t recall the place, but he does not go by Hale—you must remember that. His letters are signed ‘F.D.’—he took the name Dickenson. I wished he’d chosen Beresford, a name he had some claim to, but your father thought it too dangerous."*

Margaret hesitated, then pressed gently, *"Mamma, I was at Aunt Shaw’s when it all happened. I was too young to understand. Will you tell me now?"*

*"Pain?"* Mrs. Hale’s cheeks flushed. *"No. But it pains me to think I may never see my boy again. Yet I know he did right, Margaret. No court-martial can make me doubt him—not when I have his letters."* She gestured weakly toward the japanned cabinet. *"The second drawer—there’s a packet tied with silk."*

Margaret retrieved the bundle—yellowed, salt-stained pages that still carried the faint scent of the sea. Her mother’s hands trembled as she untied them, her words tumbling out before Margaret could even read.

*"You see, from the very first, he disliked Captain Reid. He was only a midshipman then, so proud in his uniform, slashing open newspapers with his dirk as if it were a letter-knife! But Reid took against him, and when Frederick was assigned to the Russell—with Reid in command—he vowed to endure it. Here, read this..."*

Margaret’s fingers traced the faded ink, the words of a young man steeling himself for tyranny. *"‘My father may rely upon me,’"* she read aloud, *"‘to bear with all proper patience what one officer may inflict upon another. But knowing my captain as I do, I fear a long trial ahead.’"*

*"And he did bear it,"* Mrs. Hale insisted. *"He was ever sweet-tempered, unless provoked. See here—where he writes of Reid’s cruelty, lashing men for mere slowness in drills, when half the crew were green hands!"*

Margaret read on—of a sailor, driven by fear of the lash, leaping to his death from the rigging. The crew’s fury had simmered; Frederick’s letters burned with outrage.

*"But we didn’t know,"* Mrs. Hale whispered. *"Not until the news came—of mutiny, of the Russell stolen away, Reid cast adrift... Oh, Margaret! When we saw the list of those spared—and Frederick’s name was not there—we prayed it was a misprint. Your father walked to Southampton for the papers, and when I met him..."* Her voice broke. *"He could not speak. Only held me, shaking, as if the world had ended."*

Margaret pressed her mother’s hand, but the words poured out—of the mutineers hanged, their deaths branded as treason, their loyalty to Frederick held against them.

*"And yet,"* Mrs. Hale murmured, *"I am proud. Prouder of him for defying cruelty than if he had been merely obedient."*

*"As am I,"* Margaret said fiercely. *"To bow to injustice is no virtue."*

A silence fell. Then, softly: *"Do you think... if he returned now...?"*

*"No."* Mrs. Hale’s voice was hollow. *"They would hang him. He can never come home."*

There was no comfort to give. Her mother turned her face to the wall, withdrawing even from Margaret’s touch.

When Mr. Hale returned, Margaret slipped away, the weight of the night heavy upon her, the future bleak and unchanging.

The following afternoon found Margaret and her father preparing to return Mrs. Thornton’s call. Mrs. Hale, weakened by persistent ill health, had remained at home, leaving her daughter to accompany Mr. Hale with quiet concern.

“Did you send for the doctor, Margaret?” her father asked abruptly, his voice edged with a worry he could no longer suppress.

“No,” she admitted. “But if you could recommend someone reliable, I would fetch him at once. Mamma is not well—I am sure of it.”

Her words were firm, for she had seen how readily her father dismissed such fears before. Now, his face darkened. “You don’t think—surely our move to Milton hasn’t…” His voice faltered.

“Papa, don’t imagine the worst,” Margaret said quickly. “She only needs rest and care.”

Yet his anxiety lingered, unspoken but heavy between them as they walked.

---

Marlborough Street was not what Margaret had expected. The Thornton name carried weight in Milton, yet the address led them not to a grand residence but to a plain lane lined with modest houses. Confused, Mr. Hale hesitated until Margaret stopped a passerby.

“Mr. Thornton? The mill owner? His house is by the factory gates,” the man said, pointing toward a high-walled yard.

They were admitted through a narrow lodge-door into a vast courtyard, alive with the relentless clamor of machinery. Smoke darkened the air, but the house itself stood apart—a solid, well-kept structure, its stone façade weathered but immaculate. Margaret marveled at the contrast: this was a home of substance, yet it stood amidst industry’s unceasing roar.

Inside, the drawing room was a study in oppressive neatness. Every surface gleamed, every chair shrouded in protective covers. The effect was sterile—a place preserved rather than lived in.

Mrs. Thornton arrived with her usual imposing grace, her black silk rustling like a challenge. Margaret explained her mother’s absence, but the words stumbled. She saw the flicker of disapproval in Mrs. Thornton’s eyes—the unspoken judgment that Mrs. Hale’s frailty was an indulgence.

“And how is Mr. Thornton?” Mr. Hale interjected, shifting the subject.

“My son is never ill,” Mrs. Thornton replied, pride sharpening her tone. “Even when he is, he does not let it hinder his duties.”

“His dedication is admirable,” Mr. Hale said warmly. “His appreciation for literature is a rare pleasure.”

Mrs. Thornton’s lips thinned. “Classics are a luxury for idle men. Milton demands action, not philosophy.”

Margaret could not remain silent. “But surely the mind must engage with more than one pursuit, or it grows rigid.”

“A manufacturer has no need for such distractions,” Mrs. Thornton countered. “His purpose is clear: to stand firm among the leaders of trade—men who shape the world with their labor, not their musings.”

Margaret felt the sting of unintended condescension. “I meant no disrespect. Since coming to Milton, I’ve heard much to admire in your son.”

“From whom?” The question was swift, possessive.

Margaret hesitated, unwilling to be interrogated. “From Mr. Thornton himself,” Mr. Hale supplied.

The older woman’s gaze sharpened. “My son does not boast. Who else has spoken of him?”

Margaret relented. “Mr. Bell mentioned his character. But it was Mr. Thornton’s own restraint in speaking of his past that revealed more than words could.”

A grudging satisfaction softened Mrs. Thornton’s face. “You are frank, Miss Hale. Most young women would flatter to win favor.”

Margaret laughed, startled. “I assure you, I have no designs on your son’s heart.”

The older woman’s smile was thin. “We shall see.”

---

That evening, Mr. Thornton appeared at the Hales’ door, bearing a physician’s address. Margaret took it swiftly, relieved her mother had not overheard their concerns.

The conversation turned to the looming strike. Mr. Thornton’s expression hardened. “Let them strike. They’ll learn their error soon enough.”

“But why not explain the state of trade?” Margaret pressed. “If they understood—”

“Must a man justify how he spends his own capital?” he interrupted.

“It is a human right,” she said quietly.

He leaned closer, his voice low. “Do you deny me principles because they differ from yours?”

She drew back. “I speak only of stewardship. Waste is not wisdom.”

The tension between them was palpable. Margaret turned the subject, speaking of the divide between masters and workers—how each viewed the other with suspicion.

“You mistake me,” Thornton said stiffly. “Our interests are the same.”

“Yet they see yours as opposed to theirs.”

“Then they are misled.” His frustration was clear.

Margaret exhaled. “Perhaps in some future time, harmony will come.”

“A utopian dream,” he said, but his voice had softened.

As she left the room, Thornton watched her go, the unspoken words between them lingering like the factory smoke outside—inescapable, and yet, for now, unresolved.

*"Trust in that veiled hand, which leads
None by the path that he would go;
And always be for change prepared,
For the world’s law is ebb and flow."*

Dr. Donaldson arrived the following afternoon to examine Mrs. Hale, but Margaret found herself shut out—excluded while Dixon was permitted to stay. The old distance, which she had hoped was bridged, had returned. She paced her mother’s adjoining bedroom, her ears straining for any sound. A stifled moan sent her hands clenching, her breath catching. Then silence again—until the scrape of chairs, murmured voices, the doctor’s departure.

She intercepted him in the hall.

"Father is away just now. Would you speak with me in his study?"

Dixon protested, but Margaret swept past, assuming an authority she did not feel. The brief amusement at her own severity faded as she faced the doctor, her voice steady but her pulse wild.

"Tell me plainly—what is wrong with my mother?"

Dr. Donaldson hesitated, then sighed. "Your mother wished you kept ignorant, but you are not a child to be sheltered from truth."

Margaret whitened, her grip on the chair tightening. "Then say it."

Two sentences. That was all it took. Her vision darkened at the edges, her lungs empty of air. When she could speak again, her voice was low.

"Thank you for your honesty. I have feared this for weeks." A tremor ran through her. "Will she suffer?"

"Science grants us some relief," he said gently. "But your father—"

"I will tell him when he is ready. Not yet."

The doctor nodded. "Then I leave her in your hands. You are stronger than you know."

Margaret did not weep until he was gone. Then, alone in the study, she pressed her hands to her face. *Oh, God, let her pain be brief. Let me bear it for her.*

Upstairs, Mrs. Hale sat wrapped in shawls, her weariness lending her an unnatural calm.

"Margaret," she chided softly, seeing her daughter’s face, "you’ve spoken to him."

Margaret fell to her knees, clutching her mother’s hands. "Let me care for you. Let me try."

Mrs. Hale shuddered. "You don’t know what you ask."

"I know enough. And I will learn the rest."

A faint pressure returned Margaret’s grasp. "Then you may stay. Dixon thought—"

"Dixon underestimates me," Margaret said fiercely. "Do not let her doubts stand between us."

They spoke of Helstone then—its makeshift comforts, its lost joys. But when Frederick’s name arose, Mrs. Hale shattered.

"Frederick! Come to me—" Her cries were raw, frantic. Margaret called for Dixon, who scolded but relented, bringing coffee afterward with grudging care.

"You’ll not tell your father?" Dixon muttered.

"No. He could not endure it." Yet even as she said it, Margaret’s composure broke.

Dixon softened. "Ah, child. You’ve her spirit, even if you lack her beauty."

Margaret kissed the old servant’s cheek before stepping outside, the weight of the future heavy upon her.

Dixon watched her go. "Three people I’ve loved in this world," she murmured. "Missus, Master Frederick, and her. The rest can hang."

Margaret stepped into the Milton afternoon with a heart like lead, but the sharp northern air soon worked its alchemy. By the time she reached the first corner, her blood had quickened, her lips reddened without her notice. The streets themselves seemed charged with restless energy—clusters of girls laughing too loudly, men loitering with hands in pockets, their gazes tracking passersby with a boldness that made her spine stiffen. The sour tang of beerhouses hung in the air, and she altered her course abruptly. A country walk would not do today. She turned instead toward the Higgins’ home.

Nicholas Higgins lounged by the hearth, pipe in hand, when she entered. Bessy rocked steadily in her chair, her face drawn.

"Her spirits are low," Higgins said by way of greeting, nodding at his daughter. "This strike sits ill with her. She’d rather have peace at any cost."

Bessy sighed. "Third strike I’ve lived through. That’s answer enough."

Higgins knocked his pipe against the hearth. "Third time’s the charm. This time, we’ll break the masters. They’ll come begging us back at our price—see if they don’t."

Margaret frowned. "What is a strike, exactly? Where I come from, the idea is foreign."

"Leaving work till they pay what’s fair," Higgins said. "But down your way, I reckon folk bow their heads and take what’s given."

Margaret’s chin lifted. "If laborers struck in the South, crops would rot in the fields. What then?"

"Then the farmers would pay fair wages or lose their land."

"And if they couldn’t? If there were no harvest to sell, how would wages be paid the next year?"

Higgins exhaled a sharp plume of smoke. "Southern folk must be half-starved already, to bend so easy. Here, we’ve spine enough to stand. We’ll not be cheated."

Bessy murmured, "I wish I lived where there’s quiet."

Margaret softened. "There’s hardship everywhere. Men labor in rain and cold till their bones ache. No place is free of suffering."

"You defend the South now?" Higgins eyed her.

"I defend truth. There’s good and bad in all places."

"And no strikes in your green fields?"

"No. They’ve too much sense."

Higgins snapped his pipe stem with a crack. "Or too little heart."

Bessy pressed a hand to her temple. "What good came of the last strike? We near starved. Some never recovered."

"Aye, that was bungled," Higgins admitted. "This time, we stand united. No man breaks ranks."

Margaret leaned forward. "But what do you strike *for*?"

"Five masters cut wages after years of profit. We’ll not take less. Let them choke on their own greed."

"You’d starve yourselves to spite them?"

Higgins’s grin was wolfish. "I’d die at my post before I yield. They call that honor in soldiers—why not in weavers?"

"A soldier dies for his country. For others."

"And who d’you think I fight for?" He jabbed a finger toward the wall. "John Boucher next door, with eight children and a sick wife. This is justice, lass."

Margaret held his gaze. "Perhaps the masters have reasons. Trade may be poor."

"Trade!" He spat. "A master’s trick to scare fools. They’d grind us to dust and call it business."

Bessy moaned softly. "Must we talk of nothing but wages and work till I’m in my grave?"

Higgins’s face gentled. He stubbed out his pipe. "No more smoke inside, if it troubles you."

Margaret watched the exchange, struck by the tenderness beneath his roughness. When Higgins stepped outside, Bessy clutched her arm.

"I drove him out—now he’ll drink with the others. Strike-time makes men reckless."

"Does he drink often?"

"Not to drunkenness. But a man needs *something* to break the grind." Bessy’s voice frayed. "You don’t know strikes. Hope turns to rage, then despair. And then—"

Margaret squeezed her hand. "There must be brighter sides."

"Easy for you to say, with your soft life."

Margaret’s grip tightened. "My mother lies dying. The one man who could comfort her is exiled under threat of death. Judge less quickly, Bessy."

Bessy flushed. "I beg pardon. Only—sometimes I think my sorrow was written long ago. Like the star called Wormwood in Revelations, bitter and fated."

Margaret sighed. "God does not delight in pain. Read clearer passages."

"But Revelations *speaks* to me. The sound of it, like an organ—"

"Then I’ll come read to you. Perhaps your father will listen too."

Bessy’s eyes brightened. "Mary’s out cutting fustian. We must live, strike or no."

Margaret rose. "You’ve done me good today."

"*I* helped *you*?" Bessy laughed weakly. "Gentlefolk don’t take help from the likes of me."

"Then don’t think on it too hard."

As Margaret left, Bessy stared after her.

"Like country air, she is," she murmured. "Who’d think such a face knew sorrow? I wonder how she’ll sin. All do, sooner or later."

The door creaked. Higgins returned, sober, and sat heavily. For once, he did not reach for his pipe.

*"My heart revolts within me, and two voices
Make themselves audible within my bosom."*

Margaret returned home to find two letters awaiting her—one a note for her mother, the other, postmarked from abroad, thin and crisp, unmistakably from Aunt Shaw. As she turned it over in her hands, her father entered abruptly.

"Your mother has retired early—the heat must have fatigued her. What did the doctor say? Dixon mentioned he spoke with you."

Margaret hesitated. The gravity in her father’s expression deepened.

"He doesn’t believe her condition is serious?"

"Not presently. She requires care, he said. He was kind—promised to call again to assess her progress."

"Only care? He didn’t suggest a change of air? Nothing about the city’s smoke affecting her?"

"No, not a word." Her voice was quiet. "Though he seemed… concerned."

"Doctors always do. It’s their manner."

Yet Margaret saw the unease beneath his dismissive tone. The specter of fear had taken root, though he fought it, circling back to the subject restlessly throughout the evening, as if repetition might dispel the dread.

"Aunt Shaw writes from Naples," Margaret ventured, lifting the letter. "She’s moved to Sorrento—the heat was intolerable. Though I doubt Italy pleases her."

"Did the doctor mention anything about diet?"

"Nourishing and digestible. Mamma’s appetite is fair."

"Strange he should mention it at all."

"I asked him."

A pause. Then Margaret added, half-smiling, "Aunt Shaw sends coral ornaments, but fears Milton’s dissenters won’t appreciate them. She confuses all dissenters with Quakers, doesn’t she?"

"If your mother expresses any wish—anything at all—you must tell me. I fear she keeps silent." His voice tightened. "Engage that servant Mrs. Thornton recommended. A competent maid would free Dixon to attend her constantly. With proper care, we can restore her. This heat, the strain of managing without help—rest will mend her. Don’t you think?"

"I hope so," Margaret murmured, her sorrow so palpable her father pressed her cheek.

"You’re too pale. Take care, or the doctor will be needed for you next."

But he could not settle. All evening, he stole upstairs on tiptoe, returning each time with fragile reassurance.

"She’s awake now—smiled at me, just as she used to. She feels refreshed, wants tea. Where’s that note? I’ll read it to her while you prepare the tray."

The note was an invitation from Mrs. Thornton—a dinner on the twenty-first. Margaret was surprised when her parents resolved to accept, despite the day’s grim tidings. Yet her mother clung to the prospect with frail determination, brightening at the thought of an outing.

"If she wishes it, we’ll go gladly," Mr. Hale insisted the next morning, watching Margaret pen the acceptance. "She wouldn’t insist unless she felt stronger—would she?" His hands trembled. "Margaret?"

To deny him hope felt cruel. His own stubborn refusal to entertain fear kindled a spark in her.

"She does seem better since last night. Her eyes are clearer."

"God bless you," he breathed. "But was it the sultry air yesterday that made everyone ill? An ill-chosen day for the doctor’s visit."

He left for his lectures—an engagement at the Lyceum, where he would speak on Ecclesiastical Architecture, a subject dear to him, if not to Milton’s laborers. The struggling institution was grateful for his unpaid expertise.

***

That evening, the Thorntons convened.

"Who has accepted for the twenty-first?" John inquired.

Fanny produced the replies. "The Slicksons, Collingbrooks, Stephenses—Browns decline. Hales will attend, though the mother is indisposed. Macphersons, Mr. Horsfall, Mr. Young."

"Strange they’d accept if she were truly unwell," Fanny remarked.

"I said *far from well*, not *dying*," John snapped. "They may not realize." Then he recalled—Margaret, at least, knew the truth.

"Perhaps they appreciate the advantage of meeting the Stephenses and Collingbrooks," Mrs. Thornton suggested.

John stiffened. "That’s not their motive."

Fanny laughed nervously. "How you claim to understand these Hales, yet deny *us* any insight. Are they so unlike anyone else?"

He ignored her, though the jab rankled.

Mrs. Thornton mused, "He seems a decent man, though unsuited for trade. She’s a trifle delicate, and the girl—" She frowned. "Proud, somehow. Not rich, yet carries herself as if above her company."

"She can’t even play the piano," Fanny added.

"Must she meet *your* standards?" John retorted.

His mother intervened. "Fanny meant no harm. If you’d cease defending Miss Hale, we might find reason to like her."

Fanny muttered, "*I* never could."

John paced, willing the candles to be lit, the conversation ended. But his mother kept to her frugal habits.

At last he halted. "Mother—I wish you *would* like her."

"Why?" Her eyes sharpened. "You’re not thinking of *marrying* her? A penniless girl?"

"She wouldn’t have me," he laughed shortly.

"No, I think not," his mother agreed. "She laughed outright when I praised her for defending you. Saucy creature! Where would she find better?"

"Since we agree, trust my motives. I foresee hardship for her—lack of guidance. If the time comes, be her friend." He turned to Fanny. "And you—understand that any hint of my attachment would offend her as much as me. More, perhaps."

Mrs. Thornton snorted. "I’ll tolerate her for your sake. But her pride galls me. One moment I near like her; the next, I detest her."

"If she scorns me," John said coldly, "I’m no boy to cringe. I can laugh at it."

"Then laugh at *her* and her airs!"

Fanny yawned. "Must we talk of her? I’m weary of it."

John’s voice turned bitter. "Shall we discuss something cheerier? A strike, perhaps?"

Mrs. Thornton leaned forward. "Have the hands walked out?"

"Hamper’s men have. Mine stay for fear of prosecution. I’d have punished every one who broke contract."

"The law costs would outweigh their worth," his mother scoffed.

"Yet I’d have shown them my word is iron. They know me. Slickson’s men are out—he won’t spend to punish them. We’re in for a fight."

"Are there many orders?"

"Plenty. They know it—but not the full truth."

"What do you mean?"

As candles were lit, Fanny took up her endless needlework, gazing vacantly.

"The Americans flood the market. Our only hope is to produce cheaper. Fail, and we all starve—masters and men. Yet these fools demand wages from three years past, blind to the fines Dickinson extracts. If the old combination laws still stood—" He broke off, exasperated. "Must we beg the Spinners’ Union for labor at *their* price? Drive us away, and they’ll find no work at all."

"Bring in Irish hands," his mother said flatly. "Teach them who’s master."

"I will, if this lasts. But it’s risk and expense."

"Then why host a dinner now?"

"Necessary. Horsfall won’t stay long; the others are owed."

He resumed pacing, jaw clenched. Fanny prattled inconsequentially, earning terse replies. At ten, the servants filed in for prayers—a chapter from the Old Testament, read sternly by Mrs. Thornton.

When she bid him goodnight, her gaze held no softness, yet the intensity of her silent blessing steadied him. Alone, he paced still, the strike upending his plans. Fools, led by Slickson’s cunning—playing at yielding to embolden them.

At two o’clock, the guttering candles mirrored his smoldering resolve.

"They’ll have a fortnight. No more. If sense doesn’t prevail, I’ll bring in Irish hands. Let them learn whom they defy."

The strike would break them before it broke him.

Mrs. Hale’s spirits had been lifted by the prospect of the Thorntons’ dinner party. Like a child promised a rare treat, she dwelled on every detail, her curiosity piqued by the novelty of an event so removed from the quiet rhythm of her invalid’s life. The walls of her sickroom had long narrowed her world, making the outside seem distant and small—yet now, the thought of Margaret stepping into Milton’s society stirred something forgotten: the flicker of vanity she had once known as a lively girl, before poverty and duty had subdued it.

*“Then you’ll wear the white silk?”* she fretted. *“It’s been a year since Edith’s wedding—are you certain it still fits?”*

Margaret suppressed a smile. *“Mrs. Murray made it. It may be a hair’s breadth shorter or longer, but I’ve not changed.”*

*“Then I’ve the pink gauze Aunt Shaw gave me, or the green silk,”* Margaret teased. *“I’m burdened with choices.”*

Mrs. Hale sighed. *“I wish I knew what was proper.”*

Seeing her mother’s distress, Margaret turned earnest. *“Shall I try them on? Then you can decide.”*

And so she did, though the impulse to twirl in her finery—to make her skirts billow like a cheese or retreat as if she were royalty—was checked by her mother’s solemnity. *Why must the world fuss so over dresses?* she wondered.

Yet the world did fuss. When she mentioned the dinner to Bessy Higgins later that afternoon, the girl’s hollow eyes widened.

*“Does it surprise you?”* Margaret asked lightly.

Bessy flushed. *“They keep company with Milton’s finest.”*

*“It’s just—they measure folk here by their coffers,”* Bessy muttered. *“And yours aren’t deep.”*

Margaret laughed. *“True enough. But we’ve education and breeding. Is it so strange that Mr. Thornton, who admits his own lack by seeking my father’s tutelage, would invite us?”*

*“But can you return the favor?”* Bessy pressed. *“Thornton’s house is thrice the size of yours.”*

*“We could manage a modest dinner,”* Margaret said, amused. *“Though I confess, we’ve not given it thought.”*

*“I never pictured you among their sort,”* Bessy murmured. *“The mayor dines there. Members of Parliament. And the ladies dress so grand.”* Her glance flicked to Margaret’s humble print gown.

Margaret’s laughter rang clear. *“Oh, I’ve finer things! A week ago, I’d have called them too fine for Milton. But for Mr. Thornton’s table? I’ll wear my best, never fear.”*

*“What will it be?”* Bessy asked, appeased.

Bessy sagged in relief. *“That’ll do. I’d hate to see you slighted.”*

*“I’ll dazzle them enough to avoid contempt,”* Margaret promised wryly.

A strange intensity lit Bessy’s face. *“I wish I could see you in it. I’ve dreamt of you, you know—long before we met.”*

*“It’s true.”* Bessy’s voice dropped to a whisper. *“Your face, just so—clear-eyed, your hair like rays about your brow. You came to me in the dark, dressed in shining white, just as you will be. You gave me strength.”*

Margaret touched her hand gently. *“A dream, Bessy. Only a dream.”*

*“Dream or not,”* Bessy insisted, *“you’re here now, and when you’re near, I feel eased, like warmth on a bitter day. Let me come see you in that gown.”*

Margaret hesitated, then nodded. *“If you’re well enough.”*

But Bessy’s mind was already slipping elsewhere, her breath shallow. *“Father’s been turned out. Him and all Hamper’s men. The women wail of hungry children. If only the masters sent food instead of fine dinners—”*

*“Don’t,”* Margaret pleaded. *“You’ll make me ashamed to go.”*

Bessy’s fever-bright eyes fixed on her. *“Some are meant for feasts, others for toil. But if you asked, I’d cross any gulf to reach you.”*

Alarmed by her heat, Margaret dampened a cloth and pressed it to Bessy’s brow. The girl quieted, but her words lingered like a shadow.

When Nicholas Higgins stormed in, his rage was palpable. *“Aye, we’ll fight!”* he declared, though Margaret suspected drink fueled his fervor. *“The masters’ll yield when their contracts bleed. Tell Thornton that when his workers march in, he’ll rue his stubbornness!”*

Margaret fled the brewing storm, but the weight of Bessy’s visions and Nicholas’s fury followed her home.

Dr. Donaldson’s treatments had brought Mrs. Hale fleeting relief, enough to kindle fragile hope. Mr. Hale, oblivious to the graver fears, rejoiced in the reprieve—while Dixon, ever dour, muttered warnings Margaret chose to ignore.

Yet outside their walls, Milton simmered with discontent. The men who sought Mr. Hale’s ear spoke of hunger and despair, their pride worn thin by suffering. And when Mr. Thornton came that evening, bearing offers of aid for Mrs. Hale’s comfort, Margaret could scarcely meet his eyes.

How could he speak so coldly of trade’s inevitable cruelties—of masters and men alike ground beneath its wheel—then turn with such gentleness to her private grief? The dissonance gnawed at her.

The next day, she found Higgins locked in argument with Boucher, a broken man whose children starved while the Union held firm. *“If my wife dies,”* Boucher rasped, *“I’ll fling their wages back and curse them all.”*

Higgins, fierce with conviction, pressed coins into Boucher’s hand. *“Hold fast! One more week, and the masters will beg us back.”*

But Boucher’s face was a mask of hollow resignation. *“The Union’s worse than the masters,”* he said. *“It demands we watch our families clem before yielding.”*

Margaret left money with Bessy, her heart heavy. The girl, frail as a moth, whispered, *“Father won’t let them starve. He believes the strike will break soon.”*

But as Bessy’s eyelids fluttered shut, exhaustion claiming her, Margaret wondered: *What then?*

The air was thick with coming storm—not just of labor’s strife, but of lives balanced on a knife’s edge. And Margaret, poised between worlds, felt the tremors deep in her bones.

Margaret returned home burdened by the weight of what she had witnessed—the suffering in the Higginses' cottage lingered in her mind, making the thought of dressing for a dinner at the Thorntons' seem frivolous. Yet duty called; her mother, confined to her bed, would expect every detail upon her return.

"Did your factory friend agree to see you in your finery?" Mrs. Hale asked, her voice tinged with the fretfulness of prolonged illness.

"I scarcely thought to ask," Margaret admitted, her voice heavy. "She was too ill for such trifles."

Mrs. Hale sighed, a flicker of guilt crossing her face. "Perhaps some of Mr. Thornton’s port wine might help?"

Margaret shook her head. "Consumption does not yield to wine, Mama. Though I might take her some of our Helstone preserves—if only to offer comfort."

The contrast between her evening’s obligations and the poverty she had just seen gnawed at her. How could she adorn herself for a dinner while others starved? Yet her mother, in a rare burst of energy, insisted on packing a basket for the Higgins family—only to fret afterward whether such charity prolonged the workers' defiance.

Mr. Hale, caught between his wife’s impulsive kindness and Mr. Thornton’s stern practicality, wavered. He would visit Boucher in the morning, he decided, though Milton’s hardships still felt foreign to his pastoral sensibilities.

---

Margaret dressed mechanically, the ritual of pinning up her hair and fastening coral beads feeling hollow. Her reflection—pale against the black silk, her coiled hair like a crown—drew admiration from Dixon, but her mother’s wistful praise only sharpened her melancholy.

"I wish I were going with you," Mrs. Hale murmured, her eyes bright with memory. "You look every inch the lady."

Margaret kissed her, swallowing the urge to stay.

---

The Thorntons' home was a spectacle of opulence. The drawing room blazed with yellow damask and floral carpets, a deliberate assertion of wealth that bordered on ostentation. Mrs. Thornton presided with pride, her disdain for the striking workers barely veiled.

"Does the mill’s proximity trouble you?" Mr. Hale ventured.

"Not at all," she replied coolly. "We do not forget the source of our prosperity."

Fanny Thornton wrinkled her nose. "The noise is dreadful."

Margaret, silent, noted the irony—the masters dined in splendor while the workers starved.

When Mr. Thornton entered, his presence commanded the room. Margaret observed the ease with which he moved among his peers, his authority unchallenged. Yet his gaze, when it flickered to her, held an intensity that unsettled her.

---

Dinner was a lavish affair, the excess almost vulgar to Margaret’s London-trained eye. The conversation, dominated by the men, buzzed with trade disputes and market strategies. She listened, surprised by her own interest in their fierce debates—until Mr. Thornton’s voice cut through, decisive and calm.

Later, he approached her. "You sided with us tonight," he remarked.

"On principle," she said. "Though I wonder at the morality of some tactics."

"A ‘gentleman’ might hesitate," he countered. "A *man* acts."

The distinction intrigued her. Before she could reply, he was summoned away, leaving her to ponder the unyielding resolve in his words—and the cost of such conviction.

---

As the evening waned, Margaret overheard fragments of talk about the strike—Thornton’s unwavering stance, the threats of violence. His voice, steady as a drumbeat, dismissed concerns: *"I take the risk."*

She caught curious glances from the other guests, their whispers branding her an outsider. Fanny’s flustered explanations only deepened her isolation.

Yet amid the stifling grandeur, Margaret found herself reluctantly admiring the Milton men’s vigor—their unapologetic ambition, their vision of a future they might never see. It was a far cry from London’s polished ennui.

And when Mr. Thornton’s eyes met hers across the room, she sensed a challenge—one that unsettled and exhilarated her in equal measure.

*"On earth is known to none
The smile that is not sister to a tear."*
—Elliott

The night air was crisp against Margaret’s cheeks as she walked home with her father, her white silk gown lifted just enough to keep it from the dust of the streets. The excitement of the evening still hummed in her veins, though her thoughts lingered on Mr. Thornton’s unspoken unease.

"You noticed it too, then?" Mr. Hale murmured. "He tried to mask it, but there was tension in his bearing."

Margaret nodded. "He is not a man to betray alarm easily. But his workers’ bitterness festers—they see him as unyielding, unmoved by their struggles."

Her father sighed. "And yet, I wonder if they see the whole of him. Pride can conceal as much as it reveals."

Margaret frowned. "Boucher’s despair was raw. How could Thornton remain so composed?"

"Perhaps because he feels more deeply than he shows," Mr. Hale suggested. "Not all men wear their hearts where others can see."

Margaret fell silent. She had admired Thornton’s intellect, his strength—yet she could not reconcile the man who spoke so coolly of strikes with one capable of hidden tenderness.

Their conversation faltered as they reached home. The moment Dixon flung open the door, her face ashen, the night’s earlier concerns shattered like glass.

"Thank God you’ve come!" Dixon gasped. "The doctor’s here—your mother—"

Margaret’s breath seized. Her father swayed, his fingers digging into her arm. She steadied him, her own dread a cold, leaden weight, yet her voice was steady. "Take us to her."

Upstairs, Dr. Donaldson met them on the landing. "The worst has passed for now," he said softly. "The medicine has eased her."

*This time.* The words hung unspoken.

Inside the room, Margaret’s knees nearly buckled. Her mother lay still, her face already touched by the distant shadow Margaret had prayed never to see. Her father staggered, his strength crumbling.

"Speak to him," the doctor urged.

"Papa—" Margaret’s voice broke.

He turned on her, anguish sharpening his words. "You knew. Why did you not tell me?"

Dr. Donaldson stepped forward. "It was my doing. I did not want to burden you prematurely. She may rally yet—but the disease is beyond us."

The truth settled between them, merciless and final.

—

The hours blurred. Margaret moved through them like a ghost, her senses sharpened to every sound, every shift in her mother’s breath. Dixon dozed in a chair; her father, at last, succumbed to exhaustion on the drawing-room sofa. Alone, Margaret watched the slow creep of dawn, her mind adrift in a sea of regrets.

How trivial the worries of yesterday seemed now—Bessy Higgins, the strikes, even Thornton’s stoic frown. All paled against the looming void.

By morning, her mother woke unaware of the night’s terror, puzzled by their strained faces. For a day, hope flickered—she sat up, spoke, even laughed weakly. But by evening, the fever returned.

"There must be something more we can do," Margaret pleaded with Dr. Donaldson.

"A water-bed might ease her," he said. "Mrs. Thornton has one. If you could ask—"

Margaret did not hesitate.

—

The walk to Marlborough Street was a trance. The August sun pressed down, but the air thrummed with something stranger than heat—a restlessness in the crowd, a murmur like distant thunder. Men stood clustered, their faces taut with anger or fear.

Margaret barely registered it. Her errand was a lifeline, a fragile chance to stave off the inevitable.

At the mill gates, the porter’s grip on the door was tense. "They’re stirring," he muttered, eyeing the empty street as if it were a calm before a storm.

Inside, the yard was eerily quiet. No machinery, no shouts—only the far-off growl of unrest.

But Margaret heard none of it. Her heart was already miles away, in a quiet room where her mother slept, and time slipped like sand through her fingers.

The words tolled in her soul as she raised her hand to knock.

The drawing-room was shrouded in its usual protective covers, the blinds drawn against the heat, casting a dull, sickly light that made even Margaret’s reflection in the mirror seem pallid and ghostly. She waited, the silence broken only by distant murmurs—not of wind, but of something far more ominous.

Fanny Thornton burst in, breathless.

“Mamma will be down shortly,” she said, her voice unsteady. “You must forgive the delay—everything is in chaos. The workers are furious. My brother brought in Irish laborers, and now the Milton men are threatening violence. The poor creatures are barricaded in the mill, terrified for their lives!”

When Mrs. Thornton entered, her face was like stone. Margaret hesitated, then spoke of her mother’s need for a water-bed, as the doctor had advised. But before Mrs. Thornton could reply, a thunderous crash echoed outside.

“They’re at the gates!” Mrs. Thornton cried. “Fanny, call John—now!”

The pounding intensified, the wooden gates shuddering under the assault of bodies driven to fury. Margaret stood frozen as the Thornton women clustered at the windows, watching the mob below. Fanny collapsed onto the sofa, sobbing.

John Thornton emerged from the mill, locking the factory doors behind him. He looked up at them, his expression calm, then moved to unbar the house door. The moment his voice rang out, the crowd erupted into a roar of hatred.

Margaret’s pulse raced. This was no longer an abstract conflict—it was real, visceral danger. She had always feared her own cowardice, but now, faced with the raw violence outside, she felt only a desperate need to act.

Thornton turned to her. “You shouldn’t be here,” he said grimly. “This is no place for you.”

But Margaret barely heard him. Below, the gates gave way with a splintering crash. The mob surged forward, a tide of wrath.

Then she saw him—Boucher, his face twisted with rage, fighting his way to the front.

“That’s Boucher!” she gasped. “He’s leading them!”

Thornton’s jaw tightened. “Let them come.”

But Margaret could not stand idle. “Go down!” she urged, her voice shaking. “Face them—speak to them! They’re not beasts, they’re desperate men!”

He turned, his eyes dark. “You ask me to walk into that?”

“I ask you to try!”

For a heartbeat, they stared at one another. Then he moved, unbarring the door. “Stay behind me,” he ordered.

But Margaret did not stay. As he stepped out, she followed, barring the door behind him before rushing back to the window. Below, Thornton stood alone before the seething crowd, his arms folded, unmoved by their threats.

Then she saw the raised clogs—the glint of a stone—

Without thinking, she flung open the window and ran downstairs. The door crashed open under her hands, and suddenly she was there, between Thornton and the mob, her arms outstretched.

“Stop!” Her voice was raw, barely audible. “He is one man—you are many!”

For a breath, the crowd hesitated. Then a stone flew. Pain exploded across her temple. The world blurred.

She felt Thornton catch her as she fell.

Behind her, his voice cut through the chaos. “Look what you’ve done! You attack a woman now?”

The mob wavered. Shame flickered across Boucher’s face. The fury began to drain away, replaced by uneasy murmurs.

By the time the soldiers arrived, the crowd had scattered.

Margaret awoke to the sting of eau de Cologne on her wound, Mrs. Thornton’s stern face hovering above her.

“Foolish girl,” the older woman muttered, but her hands were gentle.

Fanny wept nearby. “How did she get hurt? Was she with John?”

Jane, the maid, exchanged a glance with Sarah. “She was,” she said quietly. “Right at his side.”

And in the shadows, John Thornton stood, his fists clenched, his heart in turmoil.

She had thrown herself into danger—for him.

The realization struck like lightning.

But he said nothing.

Not yet.

Margaret had scarcely departed five minutes when Mr. Thornton burst into the house, his face alight with restless energy.

“I couldn’t come sooner—the superintendent insisted on— Where is she?” His gaze swept the dining room before fixing, almost accusingly, upon his mother, who was calmly restoring order to the disarranged furniture.

“Miss Hale?” he demanded when she did not immediately answer.

“Gone home,” she replied tersely.

“Gone?” The word was sharp with disbelief.

When John Thornton left the house that morning, the world seemed too bright, too loud, too cruel. Her attempt was clumsy, and his response was colder still. He addressed only her mother, his promise to bring Mrs. Thornton delivered with stiff courtesy before he departed once more.

Left alone, Margaret burned with frustration. His avoidance was deliberate—each glance averted, each word unspoken, a testament to his wounded pride. And yet, she could not hate him for it. Instead, she felt regret, a quiet longing to bridge the rift between them.

Not love. Never that. But a sorrow for the friendship lost, and a patient hope that time might mend what words had broken.

As for Mr. Thornton, he told himself he despised the sight of her. But the truth was far more dangerous—a sting of pleasure at her presence, a pride in his own restraint, and a heart that, despite all reason, refused to forget her.

Mrs. Thornton arrived the next morning, steeled against sympathy. She had come at her son’s insistence, her pride bristling at the thought of yielding tenderness to the Hales—especially to Margaret. Yet the sight of Mrs. Hale struck her like a blow.

The woman before her was scarcely recognizable—her face ashen, her breath shallow. The vibrant wife and mother had been reduced to a specter, clinging to the edges of life. Mrs. Thornton’s defenses wavered.

She approached the bed, her usual firmness faltering. Mrs. Hale’s glassy eyes fixed on her with desperate intensity.

The words hung between them, fragile as the woman who uttered them. Mrs. Thornton’s face remained stern, but something within her fractured. A memory surfaced—tiny fingers, a cradle left empty long ago. The ice in her chest thawed, just enough.

*"You wish me to be a friend to Miss Hale,"* she said, her voice measured, betraying nothing of the reluctant ache beneath.

Mrs. Hale’s trembling fingers pressed hers in silent gratitude.

*"I will be a true friend,"* Mrs. Thornton continued, each syllable deliberate. *"Not a tender one—that is not my nature. But if she comes to me, I will help her as my own."*

A pause. Then, with grim resolve: *"And if I see her in error, I will tell her plainly—as I would my own child."*

It was not kindness. Mrs. Thornton would not lie, even now. But the promise was given, and it was enough.

---

Meanwhile, Margaret moved through the house like a shadow, her mind divided between grief and the urgent secrecy of Frederick’s arrival. Every creak of the floorboards set her nerves alight. When the doorbell finally rang, she nearly wept with relief.

There he stood—taller, weathered by years at sea, but unmistakably her brother.

*"Frederick!"* She seized his hands, pulling him inside.

*"Mother—?"* His voice cracked.

*"Alive,"* she whispered. *"But only just."*

Upstairs, their father crumpled at the news, his sobs muffled against the table. Margaret steadied him, her own heart breaking even as she guided him toward the study where Frederick waited.

The reunion was quieter than she’d imagined. No dramatic cries, only the silent grip of hands and the weight of shared dread. Frederick’s presence was a buoy in the storm—practical, irreverent, alive. He stoked the fire, teased her clumsiness, and filled the oppressive silence with stories of distant ports.

For a few precious hours, the house breathed again.

But reality loomed. Dr. Donaldson’s prognosis was grim.

*"She may rally briefly,"* Margaret told Frederick later, her voice hollow. *"But not long."*

He scoffed. *"London doctors—there must be someone better!"*

*"We can’t afford it,"* she said softly. *"And even if we could... I don’t think it would change anything."*

Frederick paced, frustration rolling off him like a tide. *"Then we hope,"* he said fiercely, gripping her shoulders. *"We hope, Margaret. And we act."*

She nodded, clinging to the stubborn light in his eyes. For now, that would have to be enough.

---

That night, as Mrs. Hale slept with Frederick’s hand clasped in hers, Margaret watched them from the doorway. The peace was fragile, borrowed. But for this moment, at least, they were together.

And that, she thought as the candle flickered, was a mercy.

*"Show not that manner, and these features all,
The serpent’s cunning, and the sinner’s fall?"*

A damp October morning settled over Milton, its fog clinging to the streets like a shroud. There were no golden autumn hues here—only a dull haze that the sun, when it dared appear, could scarcely pierce. Margaret moved through the house with heavy steps, assisting Dixon in the grim task of setting things in order. Her eyes burned with unshed tears, but there was no time for surrender. Her father and brother leaned upon her; while they grieved, she must act. Even the funeral arrangements seemed to fall to her hands.

By the time the fire crackled to life and the kettle hummed on the hearth, the room was as warm and bright as she could make it. Yet the cheerfulness felt cruel against the weight in her chest. Kneeling by the sofa, she pressed her face into the cushions, stifling her sobs—until Dixon’s hand touched her shoulder.

"Come, Miss Hale—you mustn’t give way now. Who else is to manage things? There’s the funeral to see to, and who’s to come, and where it’s to be held—and Master Frederick’s beside himself, and your father wandering like a ghost. I know it’s hard, my dear, but death comes for us all."

Margaret swallowed the lump in her throat. No platitude could measure this loss. Still, Dixon’s rare tenderness moved her. She forced a smile and went to summon her father and brother.

Mr. Hale drifted into the room as if half-asleep, his gaze hollow. Frederick entered with false energy, but the moment he met Margaret’s eyes, his composure shattered. Through breakfast, she filled the silence with trivial chatter, desperate to distract them from the memory of their last meal—when every sound had been a potential summons from the sickroom.

Later, she approached her father about the funeral. He agreed to every suggestion, though his nods were vacant. Only when she turned to leave did he stir.

"Ask Mr. Bell," he murmured.

"Mr. Bell of Oxford?"

"He was my groomsman."

The association struck her. "I’ll write today," she promised.

The hours blurred into a haze of tasks. By evening, Dixon confessed her fears: Mr. Hale’s stunned grief was dangerous. She had roused him with a warning—Frederick was no longer safe in Milton.

"I met a man from Southampton," Dixon explained. "George Leonards, a scoundrel if ever there was one. Sailed with Frederick on the *Orion*—though whether he was there during the mutiny, I can’t say."

Margaret tensed. "Did he recognize you?"

"Only because I was fool enough to say his name first. He had the gall to mock Frederick’s troubles—claimed there’s a hundred-pound reward for his capture. As if that wretch could wash his own sins clean by betraying another!"

Margaret’s pulse quickened. "You didn’t tell him anything—about us?"

"Not a word. But he’s in Milton, and he’d sell his own mother for coin."

Frederick must go. The thought was a knife to Margaret’s heart. He had become their anchor—his laughter, his care for their mother, his shared sorrow binding them tighter. Now he would vanish again, leaving silence behind.

She found him by the fire, his usual brightness dimmed but his grief less raw. He kissed her forehead.

"You look exhausted," he said softly. "Rest awhile."

"The worst of it is having nothing to do but think," she whispered, but she let him tuck a shawl around her.

As they talked in hushed tones, she relayed Dixon’s news. Frederick’s lips thinned.

"I’d like to thrash that snake. He was a coward aboard ship—currying favor with the officers. If he knew I was here—"

"You must leave," Mr. Hale interrupted, his voice frayed. "It’s not safe."

Margaret bit her lip. "But is there no way to clear your name? For Miss Barbour’s sake, shouldn’t you try?"

Frederick scoffed. "Courts-martial weigh authority over truth. And who’d read a pamphlet defending a mutineer?"

"Then consult a lawyer," she pressed. "Someone honorable—Mr. Henry Lennox. He’d help."

Frederick considered it. "If I leave for London tomorrow, I could meet him before sailing."

Mr. Hale shuddered. "Every hour you stay risks discovery."

Margaret scribbled a note to Lennox, Frederick peering over her shoulder. When a lock of dark hair slipped from his pocketbook, his grin was boyish.

"You’ll see her whole someday," he said. "No snippet could do her justice."

As the firelight flickered, Margaret clung to the fragile hope that justice—for Frederick, for them all—might yet be found. But first, he must vanish into the shadows once more.

The following day passed in heavy silence, the three of them bound together by grief. Mr. Hale spoke little, responding only when pressed by his children, as if drawn reluctantly into the present. Frederick, whose initial anguish had been violent, now bore his sorrow with a quiet dignity, ashamed of his earlier outpouring of emotion. His grief was deep, enduring—but it would remain unspoken from this moment onward. Margaret, though less demonstrative at first, now carried the weight of their shared loss more visibly. Tears came often, and even in conversation, her voice held a mournful softness, especially when her gaze lingered on Frederick, his departure looming like a shadow.

She was glad he was leaving, for their father’s sake. Mr. Hale’s fear of Frederick’s discovery had become a consuming dread, worse now that his wife was gone. Every unexpected sound made him start; he could not rest unless Frederick sat out of sight from the door.

As evening fell, Mr. Hale turned to Margaret. *"You will see Frederick to the station, won’t you? I must know he has left Milton safely. Promise you’ll bring me word."*

*"Of course,"* Margaret said. *"I’d like to go—unless you’d be too lonely without me?"*

*"No, no. I’ll only imagine the worst if I don’t hear he’s gone. Take him to Outwood station—it’s quieter. And hire a cab. Less chance of him being recognized. What time is your train, Fred?"*

The farewells were agony. Margaret hurried Frederick into the cab, sparing her father the prolonged torment of watching his son take a final look at his mother’s still form.

Yet when they reached Outwood, they found they had misjudged the time—nearly twenty minutes remained before the train’s arrival. The ticket office was still closed, so they descended to the cinder-path beside the tracks, walking slowly in the fading light. Margaret’s hand rested on Frederick’s arm, and he clasped it warmly.

*"Margaret,"* he said suddenly, *"I mean to consult Mr. Lennox about clearing my name. Not just for my sake—for yours. If anything should happen to Father… I can’t bear to think of you alone. He looks so altered. Promise me you’ll consider Cadiz."*

Her throat tightened. The same fear had gnawed at her for months. *"Life has taught me not to plan too far ahead,"* she said, forcing steadiness into her voice. *"I try to think only of today."*

They paused near the stile, the sunset casting gold across their faces. Frederick searched her expression, seeing more than she would say.

*"We’ll write often,"* she promised. *"And I’ll tell you every worry—if it eases your mind."*

Then she stiffened—just slightly—but Frederick felt it. A horseman was passing. Margaret bowed; the acknowledgment was returned stiffly.

*"Who was that?"* Frederick asked as the rider moved away.

*"Something must have upset him,"* Margaret said, too quickly.

Soon, it was time to return to the station. Inside, under the harsh gaslight, Margaret purchased Frederick’s ticket, aware of the idle stares of loitering men. One face struck her as familiar—and she met his brazen admiration with cold disdain before hastening back to Frederick.

*"Let’s walk the platform,"* she urged, her courage fraying.

A footstep echoed behind them. It stopped when they did. The approaching train’s whistle sounded in the distance. Their silence was thick with unspoken dread.

Then—movement. A rough hand shoved Margaret aside, seizing Frederick by the collar.

Margaret’s vision swam. In a blur, Frederick twisted free, throwing the man—Leonards—down onto the gravel below.

*"Run!"* she gasped. *"The train—go now!"*

He leapt into a carriage just as it began to move. *"God bless you, Margaret!"* he called—and then he was gone.

She staggered to the ladies’ waiting room, trembling. Had Leonards risen? Was he hurt? Steeling herself, she peered over the platform’s edge. No one. Relief came—but not enough to steady her legs for the walk home. She would wait for the next train.

Yet fear prickled again. What if Leonards had recognized her? She listened to the stationmen’s chatter:

Margaret exhaled. Safe—for now. When the train arrived, she boarded quickly, avoiding the porter’s face until they were moving. Not Leonards.

But the night’s terror lingered, clinging like smoke.

*"Sleep on, my love, in thy cold bed,
Never to be disquieted!
My last Good Night—thou wilt not wake
Till I thy fate shall overtake."*
—Dr. King

The house was too quiet now, the echoes of grief and clamor faded into oppressive silence. Margaret returned to find her father seated in his usual chair, lost in one of his waking dreams, while Dixon and Mary Higgins moved through the kitchen in hushed agitation. Even Dixon’s scolding was subdued, as though raising her voice would profane the solemn stillness of death.

Margaret resolved not to burden her father with the terror of Leonards’ pursuit. What good would it do? The danger had passed—for now. Frederick would be cautious, and in a matter of days, he would be safely beyond England’s reach.

“Mr. Bell should write tomorrow,” Margaret said, more to fill the silence than anything else.

“Yes,” her father murmured. “I suppose so.”

“If he comes, it will be by evening.”

“If he does not,” Mr. Hale said, voice trembling, “I must ask someone to accompany me. I cannot face the funeral alone.”

“Then let me go with you,” Margaret urged, sudden and fierce.

“You? My dear, women do not—”

“Women do not go because they fear their grief will shame them. But I am not ashamed. If you will not have Mr. Thornton, then let it be me.”

Mr. Hale looked at her, bewildered by her intensity. But Margaret would not be swayed. If Mr. Bell could not come, she would stand by her father’s side, no matter the custom.

The letter arrived the next day—Mr. Bell was indisposed, gout keeping him from travel. His words were tender, full of regret, but Margaret scarcely needed to read them aloud to know what they contained. Her father’s face crumpled further.

Then came Mrs. Thornton’s note—a cold, formal offer of the family carriage for the funeral. Margaret nearly crumpled it in her hand.

“An empty carriage,” she said bitterly. “How generous.”

Her father frowned. “I thought you did not want him to attend.”

“I don’t. But this—this is a mockery.” To her horror, tears spilled over. She had been so controlled, so careful, but now the weight of it all crashed upon her.

The night was restless, and worse still was Frederick’s letter the next morning—he was delayed in London, waiting for Mr. Lennox’s return. Worse, he hinted at the near-discovery at the station, the lingering threat of pursuit. Margaret’s blood ran cold. She thanked God her father was not present to hear it.

The funeral was a blur of crape and murmured prayers. Mr. Hale trembled, his voice breaking as he whispered, *"Pray for me, Margaret. I have no strength left."* She held him upright, reciting verses of comfort, her own voice steady even as her heart threatened to fracture.

In the crowd, unnoticed, stood Higgins and his daughter, their presence a silent testament to shared sorrow. But Mr. Hale saw nothing. He moved as if blind, clinging to Margaret’s arm.

Dixon wept openly, only stirring when a voice spoke beside her.

Mr. Thornton stood there, his face grave.

Dixon wiped her eyes. “Master is broken, sir. Miss Hale bears it better than expected.”

*Better than expected.* The words struck Thornton like a blow. He had wanted—foolishly—to believe Margaret might need comfort, that his love might somehow ease her pain. But now, the memory of that stranger at the station, the ease between them, twisted in his chest.

“May I call?” he asked, voice clipped. “On Mr. Hale, in a day or so.”

Dixon nodded. “I’m sure he’ll see you, sir.”

But she never spoke of this meeting to Margaret. And so, Margaret never knew Thornton had been there—that he had watched, that he had ached—just as he never knew how deeply her own heart was wounded.

The silence between them stretched, vast and unspoken, as the dead lay quiet in the ground.

*“Truth will fail thee never, never!
Though thy bark be tempest-driven,
Though each plank be rent and riven,
Truth will bear thee on for ever!”*

Margaret bore the strain of her grief with a quiet fortitude that belied the storm within. The pretense of composure before her father was a daily torment, each cheerful word a dagger twisting in her heart. The absence of Frederick’s letter gnawed at her—each silent post a fresh weight upon her dread. Her father, too, was restless, his newfound stillness shattered. He paced, wandered, opened doors without purpose, a man adrift in his own home.

She read aloud to soothe him, though his mind would not settle. When Mr. Thornton was announced, she felt a flicker of relief—his presence would divert her father’s thoughts. Thornton entered with no words, only a firm clasp of Mr. Hale’s hands, his face alight with unspoken sympathy. Then he turned to Margaret.

Her beauty was shadowed now—eyes dulled from sleepless nights, cheeks pale from weeping. She stood apart, uncertain of his manner, but when he spoke, his voice was unexpectedly tender. The kindness in it nearly undid her. She turned away, blinking back tears, and took refuge in her needlework.

Thornton’s pulse quickened. The memory of Outwood Lane faded as he watched her, drawn despite himself. He spoke to Mr. Hale, who, as ever, found comfort in the mill owner’s steady certainty. Their conversation flowed easily, a rare moment of calm.

Then Dixon appeared, flustered, summoning Margaret. The maid’s agitation sent a chill through her. *Frederick—something has happened to Frederick.* She excused herself, heart pounding.

“What is it, Dixon?” she demanded the moment the door closed.

Dixon led her to her room—once her mother’s—and hesitated. “Only a police inspector, miss. He wishes to speak with you.”

Margaret’s breath caught. “Did he say—”

“No names, miss. Only asked for you.”

Margaret’s fingers tightened on the door handle. “Keep Papa away. Mr. Thornton is with him.”

The inspector was taken aback by her cold dignity as she entered. She stood like marble, her gaze unwavering, betraying nothing. No questions, no fear—only disdain.

He cleared his throat. “A man has died at the Infirmary from injuries sustained at Outwood station. Witnesses suggest a scuffle with a gentleman and a lady—identified by some as yourself.”

Her dark eyes fixed on him, unblinking. “I was not there.”

The inspector hesitated. The porter’s account was vague—a glimpse of a struggle, Leonards’ drunken fury, his later ramblings about lost money. No certainty, only suspicion.

“You deny being the lady present?”

The words struck like a blade. *Frederick—where are you?* A flash of agony crossed her face, gone as swiftly as it came.

“I was not there,” she repeated, voice hollow.

The inspector studied her. There was no tremor, no guilt—only that unnerving stillness. He closed his notebook slowly.

“I may need to summon you to the inquest. To disprove the witness.”

She did not flinch.

He retreated, unnerved by her composure. Perhaps it was a mistake. Yet as he left, she followed, holding the door wide, her gaze locked on him until he was gone.

Then she turned, locked the door behind her, and walked—only to falter, sway, and collapse to the floor in a dead faint.

John Thornton remained long in the quiet study, though he had no expectation of seeing Margaret return. Her father’s pleading—soft, insistent—kept him anchored there. The older man’s grief was a palpable thing, a raw wound that needed the balm of companionship, and Thornton, despite the storm in his own heart, could not deny him.

It was strange, this unburdening. Mr. Hale, who had always been so reserved, even with his daughter, now spoke freely of death, of doubt, of the creeping numbness of a mind too long wearied by the world. And Thornton, of all people, was the one who drew the words from him. Perhaps it was because Margaret’s sympathy would have been too keen, too sharp—her love too bright a mirror for his fears. Or perhaps it was because Thornton, a man of action, of iron will, carried within him a faith deeper than Hale had ever guessed. He spoke little, but each word was a steadying hand on the older man’s trembling thoughts.

And all the while, Margaret lay insensible on the floor of the empty study, her face as pale as the pages of an unwritten letter.

When life crept back into her, it came first as a flicker at her lips—a silent plea. Then, with effort, she pushed herself upright, her limbs unsteady, her hair loosened from its careful arrangement. She groped for her fallen comb, her movements slow, deliberate, as if reassembling the fragments of herself.

Her mind circled back to the terror of the night before—Frederick’s danger, her own lie. She had sworn falsely to protect him, and the weight of it pressed upon her like a stone. If the inspector returned before she had word of Frederick’s safety, she would lie again, though the words would choke her. But if she had confirmation of his escape—then she would stand before any tribunal, bear any shame, to undo the falsehood.

Dixon’s entrance startled her.

*“Mr. Thornton has just left,”* the servant said, her voice sharp with concern. *“You ought to be in bed.”*

Margaret scarcely heard her. The world had narrowed to a single point: survival, endurance, the next breath.

---

Thornton had not gone far when the inspector intercepted him—a man he had once helped, now a figure of unwelcome authority.

Thornton stiffened. *“What of it?”*

The words struck like a blow. *“She denies it?”*

Thornton’s pulse roared in his ears. *“Come to my warehouse at four. Take no further steps until then.”*

---

Alone in his office, he let the fury rise—not at her, but at himself. How had he been so blind? That night at the station—her presence, her secrecy—now it made a terrible sense. And yet, the lie galled him. Margaret Hale, who held truth like a banner, had lowered it to shield another.

He would spare her the inquest. Whatever her reasons, whatever the cost to himself, he would not see her dragged through public shame.

The note he sent was terse, final:

---

When the inspector returned to the Hale household that evening, Margaret was waiting, her composure brittle as ice.

*“There will be no further inquiry,”* he told her.

She barely heard the rest—his apologies, his explanations. Only one thing mattered: it was over. And Thornton had ensured it.

The knowledge was a knife twisting in her ribs. He knew. He had seen her lie, and still, he had acted to shield her. Why? Contempt? Pity? Or something worse—some lingering regard she did not deserve?

She took Frederick’s letter with numb fingers, reading his hurried scrawl with dull relief. He was safe. The danger had passed. But the weight of her falsehood remained, and the man who knew it stood between her and any peace.

That night, sleep brought no respite. In her dreams, she stood before a crowded courtroom, Thornton’s eyes upon her, silent, unreadable—judging.

And she could not say which was worse: his condemnation, or his mercy.

*"The steps of the bearers, heavy and slow,
The sobs of the mourners, deep and low."*

At the appointed hour, Margaret and her father set out to visit Nicholas Higgins and his daughter. The weight of their recent loss clung to them, unspoken but palpable, in the stiffness of their new mourning clothes and the hesitant way they walked side by side—closer than usual, as if seeking solace in shared silence.

They found Higgins slumped in his usual corner by the fire, pipe absent, his head cradled in his hands. He did not rise when they entered, though his eyes flickered with recognition.

"Sit ye down," he muttered, jabbing at the embers. "Fire’s near out."

He looked unkempt—days of stubble shadowing his pallid face, his jacket frayed and patched. Margaret noted the disarray but said nothing.

"We thought we’d find you after dinner," she ventured.

"Aye, well. Sorrows don’t keep hours," Higgins replied bitterly. "My dinner stretches all day now. Ye’d always find me here."

"Are you out of work?" Margaret asked gently.

"Aye." He exhaled sharply, then added, "Not for lack o’ brass. Bess left a bit tucked under her pillow—Mary’s cutting fustian. But work? None to be had."

Mr. Hale spoke up. "We owe Mary thanks for her kindness—"

Higgins cut him off. "If she takes a penny from ye, I’ll turn her out. She’ll not profit from your charity."

"But her service—" Mr. Hale persisted.

"I’ve not thanked your daughter yet for tending my Bess," Higgins growled. "Don’t make me start with Mary."

Margaret steered the conversation back. "Is it the strike keeping you from work?"

"Strike’s over. I’m out ’cause I won’t beg. And I won’t beg ’cause good words—asking honest for work—are scarce. Bad words? Plenty. Refusals, sneers, threats." His voice turned sardonic. "‘Loyalty’ now means pledging never to help the Union or starving turn-outs. A man’s meant to starve his conscience along with his belly."

Mr. Hale hesitated. "Could you not appeal to your old master?"

Higgins laughed, a harsh, mirthless sound. "Master? Let me ask *you*—does your wage come with strings? Do they dictate what you do with your own coin?"

"Certainly not!"

"Then you see my point. Hampers Mill demands oaths—swear never to aid the Union, or starve. I’ll not forswear myself, not for all the work in Milton."

Margaret’s voice trembled. "Boucher once called the Union a tyrant. I pitied him then. Now I wonder—did you not make him one?"

Higgins’s face darkened. "The Union’s our only strength. Boucher was a fool who rioted and ruined the strike. We *had* to rein him in."

"But at what cost?" Margaret pressed. "You hounded him till he broke."

Before Higgins could retort, a murmur swelled outside—a slow, shuffling tread, voices hushed. A crowd gathered, parting as six men bore a makeshift stretcher: a door, its burden draped in a sodden sheet. Droplets pattered on the stones.

"Found him in the brook," a bearer muttered. "Face down. Dead weight."

Higgins reeled. "Not Boucher—surely not—"

The sheet shifted. Margaret glimpsed the bloated face, the waterlogged hair. Her stomach lurched. Without thinking, she stepped forward and covered the corpse with her handkerchief.

The crowd stirred. "Higgins, thou knew him best. Go tell his wife."

Higgins backed away. "I can’t. I *can’t*."

Margaret’s father paled. "Papa, let me."

She knocked at the Bouchers’ door. Inside, chaos—children wailing, the stench of unwashed linen. Mrs. Boucher slumped in a chair, hollow-eyed.

"John’s gone tramping," she rasped. "Four days now. Might’ve sent word..."

Margaret knelt. "Mrs. Boucher... he’s not coming back."

The woman’s hands clenched. "What d’ye mean?"

"He’s drowned."

A silence. Then a guttural cry. "No—no, he loved us! He *loved* us!" She clawed at Margaret. "Bring him back! Bring my John back!"

The children’s wails crescendoed. Margaret gathered the youngest, pressing him into his mother’s arms. "He *was* loved," she whispered.

The door creaked open. Neighbors filed in, bearing the body. Mrs. Boucher howled, collapsing onto the floor. Margaret motioned the crowd away. "Give her air—leave just one or two."

Outside, Higgins had vanished. At his bolted door, Margaret called softly: "Nicholas?"

A book thudded inside. "Go away."

Mr. Hale made to protest, but Margaret shook her head. "Let him be. Some griefs need solitude."

They walked home beneath a leaden sky, the echoes of weeping trailing behind them.

The following morning found Margaret and her father at the locked door of Higgins’s dwelling, their intended visit to the widow Boucher thwarted once more. A neighbor, eager to share the latest gossip, informed them Higgins had indeed been home—briefly—before setting out on his day’s uncertain labors.

Their encounter with Mrs. Boucher left them disheartened. Grief had twisted her sorrow into resentment—against the world, against the masters, even against the lifeless man who had abandoned her. She wore her widowhood like a martyr’s cloak, casting blame upon all who might have driven her husband to despair: the mill owners, the Union, even her own children, whose needs she alternately coddled and cursed.

Margaret, kneeling to speak with the little ones, found their memories of their father purer, untainted by bitterness. *"Daddy was kind,"* one whispered, *"but that thing upstairs isn’t him. I’m scared of it."* The words struck Margaret like a blow. To force these children to gaze upon their father’s distorted remains—what cruelty masked as mourning! She coaxed them into tidying the cramped room, turning their thoughts to small, tangible duties. *What would Father have wanted?* she asked, and their small hands moved with purpose.

Mr. Hale, meanwhile, faltered in his attempts to lift the widow’s spirit. She could not—or would not—grasp the magnitude of her husband’s suffering, nor the mercy of a God who had not intervened. Her grief was a closed circle, centered solely on herself.

---

*"It’s the city,"* Margaret mused later, as they walked home through Milton’s soot-choked streets. *"The noise, the confinement—it frays the nerves. In the country, even in winter, there’s air to breathe, space to think."*

*"Yet each place breeds its own trials,"* her father countered. *"Rustic stagnation can be as deadly as urban frenzy."*

Margaret nodded. *"The townsman must fight for patience; the countryman, for vigor. Neither finds it easy to plan beyond the present—one caught in the rush of the moment, the other lulled by the rhythm of the seasons."*

But philosophy offered scant comfort. *"How little we’ve done for Mrs. Boucher,"* Mr. Hale sighed.

*"Yet we must keep trying,"* Margaret said softly. *"Oh, Papa—it’s a hard world."*

A shadow crossed her face. Frederick’s visit—so bright a spot in their sorrow—now tasted bittersweet. Her own cowardice, her lie to the police, gnawed at her. Worse still was the knowledge that Mr. Thornton had seen through her deception. Shame prickled her skin at the memory of her past disdain for trade, her haughty judgments. How he had schooled her then—how justly!—on honor in commerce. And now? She burned to know his thoughts, even as she dreaded facing him.

---

That evening, her father grew restless, pacing as he awaited Thornton’s promised visit. *"He surely meant to come,"* Mr. Hale fretted. *"Perhaps a note was lost?"*

Mr. Bell, studying Thornton’s face, suddenly asked, "Did you ever care for Margaret?"

No answer.

"I once thought you did. So did Hale, after I put the notion in his head."

Mr. Thornton’s jaw tightened. "I admired her. Anyone would. She is a beautiful creature."

Mr. Bell scoffed. "Is that all? ‘A beautiful creature’—as if she were a painting or a horse! I thought you had more heart."

Thornton’s eyes burned, but he would not be drawn. "Some men are not free to speak as you are. Let us talk of something else."

And so they did—of business, of the new dining-room Thornton was building for his workers, of the shared meals that had bridged the gap between master and men.

Mr. Bell, amused, offered funds for a feast, but Thornton refused. "I want no charity in it. Let it stand on its own."

"People will talk regardless."

"Let them. But you, as a friend, will oblige me by keeping silent."

Mr. Bell agreed, and so the matter rested—while upstairs, Margaret wept in her aunt’s arms, and the weight of her losses pressed ever heavier upon her.

*“The meanest thing to which we bid adieu,
Loses its meanness in the parting hour.”*
—Elliott

Milton had become intolerable to Mrs. Shaw. The clamor of machinery, the soot-laden air, the ragged workers and overdressed ladies—all grated on her nerves. Worse still, no man, high or low, wore a well-fitted coat! She was certain Margaret would never recover her health in such a place, and she herself feared a return of her nervous attacks. Margaret must return to London at once.

Weak and heartsick, Margaret at last yielded. Once Wednesday—the day of her father’s burial—had passed, she would prepare to leave, entrusting Dixon with the dismal tasks of settling accounts and closing the house.

Before that sorrowful day arrived, a letter came from Mr. Bell.

---

I had intended to return to Milton by Thursday, but duty calls me to remain at Oxford. Captain Lennox and Mr. Thornton are here. The former, a capable man, insists on traveling to assist in the search for your father’s will—though I suspect none exists, else you’d have found it by now.

He is determined to escort you and Mrs. Shaw back to London, his wife’s condition permitting no delay beyond Friday. Still, your Dixon is reliable; she can manage until I arrive. Should no will surface, my attorney in Milton will handle affairs—Captain Lennox, for all his fine mustaches, strikes me as no man of business.

Now, two matters:

First, my estate is yours when I die (though I’ve no plans to depart just yet). To ensure your independence, I propose an arrangement: you shall pay the Lennoxes £250 annually for your shared lodgings, Dixon included. This way, should circumstances change, you’ve means to establish yourself elsewhere—unless I claim you as my housekeeper first!

Second, for your personal expenses, I’ll consult a lady of my acquaintance to determine a suitable allowance from your father’s estate.

You may bristle at my presumptions, but an old man who loved your father for thirty-five years—who stood by him in joy and in death—has earned the right. I’ve no kin to mourn me; let me do this small good for you.

Reply at once, but *no thanks.*

---

Margaret’s hand trembled as she scrawled her response:

Exhausted, she sank back, vexed at her own lack of eloquence.

---

“Dearest, did that letter upset you?” Mrs. Shaw fretted.

“No,” Margaret murmured. “I shall feel better when tomorrow is past.”

“You won’t recover until we’ve left this dreadful air! How you’ve endured it so long, I cannot fathom.”

“Where else could I have gone? I could not leave Papa and Mamma.”

“Well, don’t distress yourself. Our butler’s wife lives better than this!”

“It was pleasant in summer,” Margaret said softly, closing her eyes to end the discussion.

---

The house now brimmed with comforts—fires in every room, delicacies piled high—but Margaret moved through it like a ghost. She drifted from room to room, selecting keepsakes, her mind numb to all but the looming farewells.

Dixon shadowed her, under orders to coax her into rest.

“These books go to Mr. Bell,” Margaret instructed. “And this—” She paused, then took up a volume. “This must go to Mr. Thornton.”

With hurried strokes, she penned a note:

---

The next morning, she insisted on bidding farewell to a few friends. Mrs. Shaw protested but relented, bundling herself into the carriage with a maid and cushions in tow.

First, the Higginses’ home. Nicholas was out; only Mary and the Boucher children were there. Mary wept so openly that Margaret’s prepared words dissolved into helpless comfort.

“Take something to remember Bessy by,” Mary urged when Margaret hesitated at the door.

Margaret chose a humble cup—the one that had so often held Bessy’s water. Mary protested, but Margaret clasped it tightly and left before her own tears fell.

---

Next, the Thorntons’.

The drawing-room was icy. Mrs. Shaw shivered, muttering about the chill, while Margaret steeled herself.

When Mrs. Thornton entered, her manner was softer than Margaret had ever seen.

“I wished to apologize,” Margaret said quietly. “I believe you meant kindly, even when we misunderstood one another.”

Mrs. Thornton inclined her head. “You do me justice. I have always wished to be your friend.”

Margaret flushed. “And I hope you’ll believe that my conduct, though unexplained, was never what you feared.”

For once, Mrs. Thornton’s sternness wavered. “I do believe you. Where will you go?”

“To London, with my aunt.”

Mrs. Shaw effused empty courtesies, unaware that the “Mr. Thornton” she referenced was the very man now standing in the room—his presence sudden, his mourning clothes a silent testament to where he had been.

“You are leaving,” he said, his voice low.

“Yes. Tomorrow.”

He turned away sharply, as if struck. When the carriage arrived, he handed Mrs. Shaw in, then stood beside Margaret on the step. The memory of the riot—of her desperate words that day—hung between them.

His jaw tightened. *Let her go,* he thought. *Beauty and wealth will not buy her a truer heart than mine.*

Their farewell was cool, his hand clasping hers with deliberate indifference.

---

That evening, Higgins came.

“Eh, lass! To think of the master gone so sudden. I told Thornton I’d chase you to London if I had to!”

Margaret smiled faintly. “You’d have found me.”

She pressed her father’s Bible into his hands. “Keep this. Study it, for his sake.”

When she tried to give him money for the Boucher children, he balked—until she laughed. “It’s not for *you*, you stubborn man!”

His voice thickened. “Bless you, lass. Bless you.”

And with that, the last thread to Milton was cut.

Margaret found the quiet of Harley Street a welcome reprieve after the turbulence of Milton. The house moved with effortless grace, its rhythms smooth as silk, untouched by the worries that had once filled her days. Yet, in this very comfort, she felt an odd disquiet. The absence of struggle left her restless, as though she were drifting in a world too polished to feel real.

Edith, now recovered, showered her with affection, eager to drape her in the same luxuries she herself enjoyed. Mrs. Shaw fussed over Margaret’s wardrobe, as if fine fabrics could mend the quiet ache in her heart. Captain Lennox was kind, though his attentions to Edith’s appearance—as though her beauty were a duty—sometimes grated on Margaret’s patience.

The days passed in a soft monotony: late breakfasts, endless notes penned on Edith’s behalf, the fleeting company of little Sholto, and the hollow cheer of social calls. The evenings, when the house emptied into the whirl of London’s season, left Margaret alone with her thoughts—thoughts that wandered inevitably back to Milton. She missed the rawness of it, the purpose, even the hardships. Here, life was a performance, all surface and no depth.

Dixon’s letters were her only tether to that past. Brief, practical, they carried faint echoes of Milton—a mention of Mr. Thornton’s advice on furniture, a passing reference to the house in Crampton Terrace. But they were sparse, and Margaret often found herself staring at them, wondering if the world she had left behind moved on without her, as though she had never been there at all.

Then, unexpectedly, Mr. Bell arrived.

His presence was a jolt of warmth in the stifling stillness. He teased her, coaxed her into conversation, and with him, she felt she could breathe again. Yet even he brought reminders of Milton—of the life she had lost.

“You never told me Frederick was in England,” he chided gently when the subject arose.

Margaret flushed. “I thought you knew.”

Mr. Lennox, ever the lawyer, was cautious, measuring his words. But Mr. Bell, blunt as ever, dismissed secrecy. “I’m no gossip,” he said, though his eyes twinkled with curiosity.

The conversation turned, inevitably, to Mr. Hale’s choices—his resignation, his move to Milton. Mr. Lennox, with detached politeness, called it quixotic. Mr. Bell bristled. Margaret listened, torn between loyalty and the quiet sting of truth.

When Mr. Lennox departed, Mr. Bell lingered, watching her. “You look pale,” he remarked.

“I am well enough,” she said, too quickly.

He studied her, then sighed. “Milton changes a person. Even those who leave it.”

She had no answer.

That night, as the house settled into silence, Margaret sat by the window, the letters still in her lap. Outside, London glittered, indifferent. Somewhere beyond it, the smoke of Milton still rose, the machines still turned, the people still labored. And she—she was here, caught between two worlds, belonging to neither.

Sleep did not come easily. When it did, her dreams were of the north.

*"Where are the sounds that swam along
The buoyant air when I was young?
The last vibration now is o’er,
And they who listened are no more;
Ah! let me close my eyes and dream."*
—W. S. Landor

The name *Helstone* had lodged itself in Mr. Bell’s thoughts after his conversation with Mr. Lennox, and that night, it wove itself through his dreams. He was young again—no longer the aging Fellow of a college, but the eager tutor of years past, spending the long vacation with his dear friend, the proud and happy vicar of Helstone. They leaped across brooks that seemed to hold them suspended in the air for hours, where time and space melted away, yet every sensation was vivid. The trees blazed in autumn gold, the air was thick with the scent of flowers and herbs, and the young vicar’s wife moved through the house with that same mingling of pride in her husband and faint discontent with their modest means—just as Mr. Bell had observed in life so many years ago.

The dream was so real that when he woke, the present felt like the illusion. Where was he now? In a stifling London hotel room, surrounded by fine furnishings but empty of life. Where were the voices, the faces, the touches of those who had seemed so near in his sleep? Gone—buried, lost beyond recall. He was an old man, though his heart still remembered the vigor of youth. The loneliness of his existence pressed upon him like a weight.

He rose quickly, dressing with haste to escape the ache of memory, and hurried to the breakfast in Harley Street. There, he could scarcely focus on Mr. Lennox’s careful legal explanations, watching instead as Margaret’s eyes widened, her lips paling with each fresh blow—each piece of evidence that might have cleared Frederick now slipping irrevocably away. Even Mr. Lennox’s voice softened as he neared the final, crushing conclusion.

Margaret had known the outcome already, yet the relentless march of disappointment, laid out in such exact detail, was too much. At last, the tears came.

Mr. Lennox paused. "I should stop," he said gently. "This was unkind of me."

*Lieutenant Hale*—the title itself, long denied Frederick, was a small comfort to Margaret.

"He is safe now," Mr. Lennox continued. "Happier, perhaps, than he might have been in the navy. He has his wife’s country, his own prospects..."

"That’s true," Margaret murmured, attempting a smile. "It’s selfish to grieve. But he is lost to me, and I am so very alone."

Mr. Lennox shuffled his papers, wishing fiercely for the wealth and stability he did not yet possess. Mr. Bell blew his nose loudly but said nothing. Margaret, after a moment, steadied herself and thanked Mr. Lennox with deliberate grace—more gracious than necessary, as if to assure him she did not blame him for the pain. Yet it was pain she would not have spared.

Later, Mr. Bell approached her, fumbling with his gloves. "Margaret," he said abruptly, "I’m going to Helstone tomorrow. To see the old place. Will you come? Or would it hurt too much? Be honest."

"Oh, Mr. Bell—" Her voice broke. She caught his gnarled hand and pressed it to her lips.

"Now, now, none of that," he grumbled, flushing. "Your aunt Shaw will let you go, I suppose? We’ll leave in the morning, reach Helstone by two. Have a bite at the Lennard Arms—if it’s still there—then wander the woods till we’ve worked up an appetite. Can you bear it? It’ll be hard, I know, but I’d like it. And after, you can visit whoever’s left while I nap. I’ll have you back by Friday, safe as houses—barring train wrecks, of course. Well? Yes or no?"

"I can’t tell you how much I want to go," she whispered, tears trembling again.

"Then save the weeping for another time. If you start, I will too, and that’s a sight nobody needs."

Margaret laughed, blinking the wetness from her lashes. "Not a single tear."

"Good girl. Then let’s settle it with your aunt."

Her hands shook with suppressed emotion as Mr. Bell laid out the plan to Aunt Shaw, who wavered between doubt and reluctant agreement, persuaded more by his stubborn insistence than her own certainty. Only when Margaret returned unharmed, the journey completed, would she declare it had been *just the thing*—exactly the respite Margaret needed after so much strain.

But for now, Margaret could think only of Helstone—the past, the ghosts, and the bittersweet joy of seeing it once more.

Margaret stood ready long before the hour appointed, stealing quiet moments to weep when no one noticed, and brightening her face with a smile whenever eyes turned her way. Her final fear was that they might miss the train, but they arrived with time to spare. Settled in the carriage opposite Mr. Bell, she exhaled at last, watching the familiar stations blur past—the drowsy southern villages bathed in golden light, their russet roofs glowing warmly under the sun, so unlike the cold northern slate. Pigeons circled the peaked gables, alighting with slow, deliberate grace, feathers ruffled to catch every whisper of warmth.

The stations were nearly empty, as though the locals were too content to stir. None of the clamor Margaret remembered from her journeys north. Later in the season, pleasure-seekers might crowd these platforms, but the constant rush of trade would never mark this line as it did the industrial routes of the North. Here, a lone spectator often lounged at each stop, hands in pockets, staring idly as if the train’s departure would leave him with nothing but empty tracks and distant fields to ponder. The heat shimmered over the land, each passing farm a vignette from some pastoral tale—Hermann and Dorothea, Evangeline—until the jolt of arrival roused her.

This was where they must alight, where the fly would carry them to Helstone.

Now the ache sharpened in her chest, indistinguishable between joy and sorrow. Every mile was thick with memories she would not have spared for the world, yet each one made her whisper, *"The days that are no more,"* with a longing too deep for words. The last time she had traveled this road, her father and mother had been beside her—the day grey, her heart heavy, but they had been there. Now she was alone. An orphan. They had vanished from the earth, and the road to Helstone lay sun-drenched, unchanged, as though the world had no need to mourn.

Mr. Bell, sensing her thoughts, held his silence. They pulled up at the Lennard Arms, its whitewashed front half-hidden behind the spreading lime-tree that dominated the village green. The door stood open, but no eager host hurried forth. When the landlady appeared—after ample time for theft, had they been so inclined—she greeted them with the unhurried warmth of an expected guest, apologizing for her delay. Haymaking had demanded her attention; the men’s baskets had needed packing.

Then the sunlight caught Margaret’s face.

"Bless me—Miss Hale!" The woman rushed to the door, calling for her daughter. "Jenny, come quick! It’s Miss Hale!" She clasped Margaret’s hands, her voice softening. "And how are you all? How’s the Vicar? God bless him—we’ve never stopped missing him."

Margaret’s throat tightened. She touched her mourning dress, whispered, *"Papa,"* and could say no more.

Mrs. Purkis turned to Mr. Bell, eyes widening. "Surely not—"

He nodded. "In Oxford, with me. Peacefully, as he deserved." Gently, he steered Margaret forward. "We’ve come to see the old place. And I’ve a fond memory of your dinners."

Recognition flickered in the landlady’s face. "Mr. Bell! Forgive me—I was that taken with Miss Hale. Come, dear, I’ve a room for you with roses in the water-jug. Nothing sweeter than spring-water scented with musk-rose." She sighed. "To think of the Vicar gone. But we must all go, mustn’t we?"

Margaret pushed aside the rose-laden casement in her room and glimpsed the parsonage chimneys through the trees.

"Times are changed," Mrs. Purkis murmured, smoothing the bed. "The new Vicar’s got seven children—building a nursery where the old arbor stood. Plate-glass windows, new grates. Stirring folk, mind you, but they do turn things upside down. Teetotaler, magistrate, his wife with her yeastless bread—" She broke off. "But I mustn’t chatter. I’ll be back."

Mr. Bell had laid out strawberries, cream, and brown bread for Margaret. After eating, they walked, directionless, pulled by old haunts.

"Past the vicarage?" he asked.

"Not yet."

Here and there, trees had been felled, cottages replaced. Margaret grieved each absence like a lost friend. They passed the spot where she and Mr. Lennox had once sketched—the lightning-scarred beech was gone, the old man who lived in the ruinous cottage dead, his home replaced by something tidy and dull.

"I did not think I had grown so old," Margaret said softly.

Mr. Bell nodded. "The first changes make time a mystery to the young. Later, we expect it."

They sought out little Susan, but she was at school. Her mother sighed. "She’s a deal cleverer than me now."

Mr. Bell muttered, "I was wrong about schooling. Better she were home, learning simple things."

Margaret changed the subject. "How is old Betty Barnes?"

The woman’s face darkened. "We’re not friends. She stole my cat."

"What?"

"Roasted it. For a charm."

Margaret listened in horror as the tale unfolded—superstition, fear, cruelty. She tried to reason, but the woman only repeated, *"It were cruel, but it works."*

Margaret left, sick at heart.

"You don’t gloat over my mistake," Mr. Bell observed.

"About schooling? No. But I must see Susan."

The school buzzed like a hive. The Vicar’s wife—bright, bustling—mistook Mr. Bell for Margaret’s father. Margaret corrected her, voice unsteady. The woman’s manner shifted, kindly yet patronizing.

"Would you like to take a class?" she asked, and Margaret found herself seated before eager faces, some still familiar. When a girl stumbled over the word *"a,"* Margaret supplied, *"An indefinite article,"* only to be gently corrected by the students: *"An adjective absolute."*

Mr. Bell hid a smile.

After, Margaret spoke quietly to old favorites, their faces half-remembered, half-strange. The Vicar’s wife invited them to see the *"alterations"* at the parsonage. Margaret dreaded it.

Inside, nothing was the same. The garden, once immaculate, was strewn with children’s toys. A straw hat crushed a rose branch. The house echoed with loud, cheerful voices.

"Seven children," Mrs. Hepworth said proudly. "We’ve made improvements—hardly habitable before." Every room bore the mark of change, even the study where her father had once pondered. Now a window overlooked the road, so the Vicar might spy parishioners sneaking to the pub.

Margaret endured the tour, aching for the past.

On their return, Mr. Bell teased, "Don’t scold me for mocking her. She was insufferably proud of those *improvements.*"

Margaret sighed. Even Helstone was not as she remembered. Trees gone, paths enclosed, light where shadow had been. *Progress,* they called it.

As dusk fell, she sat by the window, watching the dark gather. Mr. Bell dozed, worn from the day. When tea came, brought by a flushed girl fresh from the hayfield, Margaret let the quiet wrap around her, heavy with the weight of time.

Dixon returned from Milton, slipping back into her role as Margaret’s maid with the ease of habit. She carried with her a trove of gossip from the north—tidbits of Milton’s daily rhythms, now distant yet still vivid in Margaret’s mind.

Martha, she reported, had gone to live with Miss Thornton after her marriage—an event Dixon recounted with relish, down to the bridesmaids’ finery and the lavish breakfast. There were murmurs, she added, that Mr. Thornton had been too extravagant, given his recent losses from the strike and broken contracts. The sale of the Hales’ belongings had been a sore point; Dixon lamented the paltry sums fetched by cherished pieces, though she brightened when recounting how Mrs. Thornton had snapped up bargains one day, only for Mr. Thornton to overpay the next, as if balancing the scales.

Mr. Bell’s erratic orders about the books had vexed her. Letters, she declared, were more trouble than they were worth—had he come himself, it might have made sense. Of the Higginses, she recalled little. Nicholas, she believed, was well; he had asked after Margaret often. Mary, she dismissed with a sniff—some rumor had reached her about the girl working at Thornton’s mill to learn cooking, which she found absurd. Margaret, though doubtful of the tale, was glad to have someone with whom to speak of Milton, even if Dixon preferred to leave the past behind.

Dixon was far more interested in hinting at Mr. Bell’s possible intentions—his murmured words suggesting Margaret might be his heiress. But Margaret would not indulge such speculations, no matter how artfully Dixon wove them.

Yet Margaret harbored a quiet hope: that Mr. Bell might visit Milton. They had agreed, that day in Helstone, that any explanation for Mr. Thornton must come from his own lips, unprompted. But Mr. Bell’s letters, infrequent and terse, held no mention of such plans. They were unlike him—short, peevish, tinged with an unfamiliar bitterness. When Margaret inquired after his health, he brushed her off, claiming it was nothing but old-fashioned spleen. She let the matter drop, though unease lingered.

Then, a slip from Edith—a fragment of conversation with Mr. Bell—set Margaret’s imagination alight. He had half-spoken of a trip to Spain, to see Frederick and his new wife. Margaret pressed Edith for details, but there were none to be had. The idea, however, took root, glowing like a promise. Spain! A reprieve from the quiet ache of her days.

Dixon, when Margaret tentatively raised the prospect, balked at the thought of Papist influences. "My soul is dearer to me than even Master Frederick," she declared. Margaret laughed, assuring her she need not come—but Dixon, despite her protests, was not so easily resigned. The lure of the unknown tugged at her, even through her fears.

Margaret tried not to dwell on the possibility, but it was a welcome distraction from her other restless longing—the unresolved matter with Mr. Thornton. Mr. Bell remained at Oxford, silent on Milton, and Margaret felt an unspoken restraint, as though pressing the subject would break some fragile balance.

Edith’s child, at least, was a joy. Wilful and bright, he was the darling of the household—until his tempers flared. Then Edith would sigh, summoning the nurse, but Margaret relished these battles of will. She would whisk him away, firm yet playful, until his storm passed into drowsy affection, his face pressed against hers. In those moments, she tasted a tenderness she feared might never be hers.

Mr. Henry Lennox, ever present, added a sharper flavor to the household’s rhythm. Brilliant but cool, he carried an air of faint disdain for his brother’s idle comfort. Once, Margaret caught his dry remark: "And is that all you live for?" Yet the brothers’ bond was steady—the elder leading, the younger content to follow.

Henry had grown more reserved with Margaret since that single earnest conversation about Frederick. They met often, but always in company, as if by unspoken agreement. Still, she noticed how his gaze flickered to her face when he spoke well, seeking her reaction first. And though they had drifted apart in opinions, his attention—when given—held a reluctant weight, as though her judgment mattered more than he would admit.

The dinner parties at the Lennox household were a carefully curated affair: Mrs. Lennox’s circle supplied elegance, Captain Lennox the effortless discourse on current affairs, and Mr. Henry Lennox, along with his circle of promising acquaintances, brought sharp wit and erudition—never heavy-handed, never dull. The evenings sparkled, yet even here, Margaret felt a quiet unease. Every virtue, every talent, was polished for display, like fireworks spent in fleeting bursts of light—bright, dazzling, but leaving only smoke behind. Art was admired for its surface, never its soul; lofty ideals were championed in company, then forgotten in solitude. Enthusiasm became a performance, and sincerity dissolved into clever phrases.

One evening, as the gentlemen rejoined the ladies in the drawing-room, Mr. Lennox approached Margaret—the first words he had freely offered since her return to Harley Street.

"You seemed displeased with Shirley’s remarks at dinner."

"Did I? My face must betray me too easily," Margaret replied.

"It always did. You were never one for masks."

"I disliked his jesting over something he knew to be wrong—blatantly wrong," she said swiftly.

"Yet it was undeniably clever. Those well-placed barbs—you remember them?"

"I do."

"And despised them, you mean to add. Don’t restrain yourself on my account, though he *is* my friend."

"There! That is precisely the tone in you that—" She broke off, flushing.

He waited, but when she turned away, he spoke low and clear:

"If my manner displeases you, grant me the courtesy of saying so. Let me learn how to mend it."

Weeks passed with no word of Mr. Bell’s promised visit to Milton. He had spoken of it at Helstone as imminent, yet Margaret suspected he had settled matters by letter—avoiding a place he disliked, unaware of how urgently she needed to speak with him in person. August arrived, and still no mention of Spain, no hint of travel. She resigned herself to disappointment—until a letter came.

He would soon be in London, he wrote, to discuss a plan he had in mind. And, he admitted, he might seek a physician’s counsel; he had begun to think her right—perhaps his irritability owed more to ill health than temperament. The letter’s forced cheer did not escape her notice, but Edith’s outcry distracted her.

"Another dinner? In this heat? And half of London’s already fled! Who could we even invite?"

"He would prefer quiet, surely. If he’s unwell, he won’t want company. I knew he wasn’t himself—his letters have been strained for weeks."

"But if he were truly ill, he wouldn’t speak of Spain."

"He doesn’t mention Spain."

"No, but this *plan* must concern it. Would you truly travel in such weather?"

"It will cool. Yes—if it comes to pass. Though I fear I’ve wished too fiercely. Such longing often brings either bitter denial or hollow fulfillment."

"That’s superstition!"

"No—a warning. To temper my desires before they consume me. Like Rachel crying, *Give me children, or I die.* My plea is, *Let me go to Cadiz, or I perish.*"

"Margaret! What if you stayed? What would I do? Oh, if only I could find you a husband here!"

"I will never marry."

"Nonsense! Sholto says half his acquaintances will flock here next season just to see you."

Margaret stiffened. "Edith, sometimes I think Corfu left you with—"

"Well?"

"A trace of coarseness."

Edith dissolved into wounded tears, accusing Margaret of lost affection, until Margaret, remorseful, spent the day appeasing her—while Edith, the aggrieved martyr, sighed dramatically on the sofa before succumbing to a nap.

Mr. Bell did not come. The next morning brought a letter from his servant, Wallis: his master had been unwell for days, had postponed his journey, and then—just as he ought to have departed—had been struck by an apoplexy. The doctors held little hope; by the time Margaret read these words, he might already be gone.

She paled, handed the letter to Edith, and left without a word. Edith wept in shock, while Captain Lennox struggled to soothe her—death, so near and sudden, was beyond her experience. Mrs. Shaw, breakfasting in her room, was spared the first outburst. Only later did Edith think of Margaret and hurry upstairs.

She found Margaret fastening her bonnet with trembling hands, Dixon packing hastily beside her.

"Margaret! You’re not going out?"

"To Oxford. There’s a train in half an hour. Dixon will accompany me."

"But—mamma will object! You don’t even know where you’re going—a Fellow’s rooms! At least consult her!"

Margaret yielded. Mrs. Shaw, flustered and tearful, delayed them until the next train. After heated debate, it was settled: Captain Lennox would escort her. Margaret’s resolve was unshaken—she would go, with or without approval.

She was glad, later, that she had gone—even if only to learn he had died in the night. She stood in his rooms, breathing the air he had last breathed, and thought of her father—of the two friends, now both gone. She traced the memory of his laughter, his wit, the kindness that had sheltered her.

The return journey was quiet. Captain Lennox slept; Margaret wept, reflecting on this cruel year and its relentless losses—each fresh grief tearing open wounds barely healed. Yet when she arrived home—to Edith’s embrace, Sholto’s eager welcome, the warmth of lighted rooms—she roused herself from despair. Seated in Edith’s place on the sofa, watching Sholto carry her tea with solemn care, she whispered thanks: her old friend had been spared long suffering. That, at least, was mercy.

*"And down the sunny beach she paces slowly,
With many doubtful pauses by the way;
Grief hath an influence so hushed and holy."*

Margaret Hale sat motionless on the shore, her gaze fixed on the restless waves. The salt-laced wind tugged at her hair, unheeded. Behind her, Cromer bustled with the trivialities of holiday-makers—nurses with their charges, ladies with their parasols—but she remained apart, lost in thought.

The sea had become her confessor. In its endless rhythm, she found a quietude that eluded her elsewhere. Here, she could unravel the tangled threads of her past without interruption. The weight of Frederick’s flight, her father’s death, the misunderstandings with Mr. Thornton—all of it pressed upon her, yet the vastness of the ocean seemed to put them into perspective.

Edith, ever effervescent, had declared Margaret "moped" and insisted on filling her days with distractions. Captain Lennox, ever indulgent, had obliged by inviting Henry Lennox to join them. Margaret had not objected. There was a steadiness in Henry, a sharp intellect that did not demand her constant attention. He spoke of Milton with a curiosity that surprised her, his lawyer’s mind dissecting its industry and vigor.

"You admire them," she observed one evening, as the sun dipped below the horizon.

"They are a force unto themselves," he replied. "Unyielding, yes—but there is something magnificent in their determination."

She smiled faintly. "You sound almost envious."

"Perhaps I am." His gaze lingered on her. "But not of their mills. Of their purpose."

Margaret turned back to the sea. Purpose. It was what she sought, what she had always sought. Not in the idle comforts of Harley Street, nor in the rigid expectations of society, but in something deeper, truer.

When they returned to London, she made her decision.

"I will manage my own affairs," she told her aunt, her voice quiet but firm.

Mrs. Shaw protested, Edith laughed, but Margaret held fast. She had spent too long yielding to the whims of others. Now, she would carve her own path—whether they approved or not.

Henry watched her with keen interest. He saw not just the heiress, but the woman beneath—the quiet strength, the sharp mind, the sorrow she carried with grace. He was patient. He could afford to be.

And Margaret? She breathed in the sea air one last time, then turned toward home. There was work to be done.

The tides would always return. So would she.

Milton throbbed like a restless beast, its chimneys belching smoke, its factories roaring with the ceaseless pulse of machinery. Iron and steam labored without thought, yet their relentless rhythm was matched only by the tireless workers—men driven by purpose, though what they sought remained uncertain. The streets bore no idlers; every face was etched with haste or worry. News was snatched up with a hunger near-violent, and men jostled in the Exchange as they did in life, each guarding his own interest.

A shadow hung over the town. Buyers were few, and sellers eyed them with suspicion, for trust had grown brittle. The tremors of financial ruin in distant ports had reached Milton, and though no house had yet fallen, whispers coiled through the streets like smoke—*Who next? How deep will the wound cut?*

"Thornton stands firm," some murmured. "His mind is shrewd, his risks calculated."

Yet in hushed corners, dissent crept in. "Aye, but his capital is sunk in new machines, in cotton bought dear. A man may stretch too far."

John Thornton himself felt the vise tightening. Pride had been his armor—pride in the name he had built from nothing, in the belief that commerce rewarded the bold and upright. His mother’s voice echoed in memory: *"Her merchants be like princes."* But now, the world he had commanded seemed to slip from his grasp.

Strange, how perspective shifted. Once, he had dreamed of influence in far-flung markets, his word as good as gold in foreign ports. Yet now, it was the faces of his own workers—men like Higgins—that haunted him. The strike had been a wound, but from it had come something unexpected: recognition. Master and man had glimpsed the humanity in one another, and Thornton could no longer dismiss their struggles as abstract.

But sentiment did not pay debts. Orders had dwindled; his stock’s value halved. Bills loomed, money grew scarce, and creditors circled. Still, he refused despair. By day, he moved through the mill with grim focus; by night, he pored over ledgers, seeking escape from the snare.

Higgins watched him. "Th’ measter’s burdened," he observed one evening, hearing Thornton’s sharp question to an overlooker, the weary sigh that followed. Later, Higgins and another stayed unseen, mending what had been neglected. Let the master think it the overlooker’s work. Small kindnesses cost little.

Yet kindness could not stem the tide. When Thornton confessed his straits to his mother, her face hardened—not at the loss of wealth, but at the injustice. "My son, just and true, yet fortune turns her back," she said, her voice trembling. "Where is the right in it?"

He shook his head. "I must bear what comes."

The final blow fell softly. His brother-in-law, Watson, had gambled on a risky venture—one Thornton had refused—and won spectacularly. Now Watson’s name rang through the Exchange, while Thornton faced the unthinkable: surrendering his mill, his home, the life he had built.

"Will you take a partner’s offer?" his mother pressed.

"No." His jaw set. "I will not kneel to a man who sees only profit."

And so the fall came. The machinery stilled; the great doors of Marlborough Mills closed. Thornton walked away, his head high, his heart heavy. The world had turned, and he with it—down, down, downee.

Yet in the wreckage, one truth remained: he would not risk another’s livelihood to save himself. Let others chase the specter of wealth. He would find his own path, though it led through shadow.

And somewhere, far from Milton, Margaret Hale moved through her own life, untouched by his ruin. The thought of her—unmarried still, as Higgins had revealed—was a fragile comfort, a golden thread in the dark.

But threads, however bright, could not mend what was broken.

*"Steady now, brave soul—let not the eyes betray,
Nor voice, nor cheek confess what lips dare not say.
Yet deep within, unchanged through fleeting years,
She dwells, beloved beyond all doubts and fears."*

The evening air hung heavy with summer’s warmth as Edith swept into Margaret’s room, first in her riding habit, then later in her evening finery. The first time, the chamber was empty; the second, she found Dixon arranging Margaret’s gown—but no sign of Margaret herself.

Edith hovered impatiently. "Dixon, surely not those dull blue flowers against that gold? Have you no sense of harmony? Wait—I’ll fetch something better."

"It’s straw-coloured, ma’am," Dixon protested, "and blue suits it well enough."

But Edith had already returned with a cluster of vibrant pomegranate blossoms. "There—now that gives it life. Where *is* Margaret?" she fretted. "I cannot fathom how Aunt Shaw permitted her to roam Milton’s wretched streets alone. Some of those alleys are no place for a lady!"

Dixon sniffed. "If fine ladies spent less time shuddering at shadows, perhaps there’d be more saints left in the world."

Just then, Margaret appeared, her cheeks flushed from the heat.

"At last!" Edith exclaimed. "But you look quite worn. Oh, and Henry has vexed me—he insists on bringing that Mr. Thornton of yours to dinner, utterly spoiling my seating arrangement!"

Margaret’s fingers tightened on the back of a chair. "I—I needn’t join you. Dixon can bring me tea here."

"Nonsense! You’re the only one who can speak sensibly of Milton to Mr. Colthurst. And now this Thornton is coming—why, it’s providential! They say he’s fallen on hard times, but Henry insists he’s respectable. You must help me be civil."

Margaret scarcely heard her. "Did Mr. Lennox say why he’s in London?"

"Oh, some legal matter about the mill leases. Henry will explain—ah, here he is now!"

Henry Lennox arrived early, and Margaret, with unsteady voice, pressed him for details. "He’s relinquishing Marlborough Mills. There are agreements to settle. Edith was cross at my inviting him, but I thought you’d wish him shown courtesy."

Then Mr. Thornton entered.

A year had altered him. Though still commanding in stature, his face bore the marks of strain—yet there was a quiet dignity in his bearing that silenced pity. His gaze swept the room, lingering nowhere, yet Margaret felt the weight of his presence like a touch.

He greeted her with the ease of an old acquaintance, though her replies were measured, careful. Others soon claimed his attention, and he retreated into conversation with Henry Lennox.

"Margaret thrives in London," Henry remarked. "Last autumn, a mile exhausted her. Now she walks to Hampstead and back without fatigue."

*We.* The word struck Thornton like a blow.

At dinner, Mr. Colthurst, the astute MP, took note of Thornton’s remarks. "Your name is known in my circles," he said. "There’s curiosity about your methods."

Thornton’s answer was measured. "I lack the knack for popularity. Yet I believed in the path I took—building not just industry, but understanding between master and men."

"And you’ve abandoned it?"

Henry deftly redirected the talk, but Thornton, unflinching, resumed later. "I’ve lost my position. I seek only employment now—and the chance to bridge the divide between classes. Not through hollow charity, but *shared* endeavor."

His passion stirred the room. Even Colthurst leaned in. "You think this could temper the strife of strikes?"

"Not end them. But perhaps make them less bitter. I am no optimist."

Then, as if drawn by an unseen thread, Thornton crossed to Margaret. "Miss Hale, my men sent word—they’d work for me again, if ever I rise. Higgins’ doing, I suspect."

Her eyes met his, bright with approval. "It’s what you deserve."

For a breath, they stood suspended in that glance—until he turned away, his voice formal once more.

Later, as Henry departed, Margaret hesitated. "Might I consult you tomorrow? I... need your assistance."

His face lit. "Name the hour. There’s nothing I’d value more."

*Eleven*, she whispered. And in his heart, hope flared anew.

*"For joy or grief, for hope or fear,
For all hereafter, as for here,
In peace or strife, in storm or shine."*

Edith moved quietly through the house that morning, her usual vivacity softened into cautious silence. Even Sholto’s boisterous energy was hushed at her insistence, as though a single loud sound might shatter the delicate negotiations unfolding behind the closed doors of the drawing-room.

By two o’clock, the meeting had yet to conclude. At last, rapid footsteps descended the stairs, and Edith, unable to contain her curiosity, peered out.

“Well, Henry?” she asked, her eyes alight with expectation.

He exhaled sharply. “Well.”

“Will you stay for lunch?”

“No, I’ve wasted enough time as it is.”

Her face fell. “Then it’s not settled?”

“If you mean what I think you mean—no. And it never will be. Put it out of your mind, Edith.”

“But it would be so perfect,” she pressed. “Margaret near us always—I wouldn’t have to worry about the children, or her sailing off to Cadiz—”

Henry cut her off with a wry smile. “When I marry, I’ll find someone skilled with children. That’s the best I can offer. Miss Hale has refused me, and I won’t ask again.”

Edith’s brows knit in confusion. “Then what *have* you been discussing all this time?”

“Investments. Leases. Land values.”

She waved a dismissive hand. “How tedious. No wonder you both looked so grim. If that’s all, you may go.”

He turned to leave, then paused. “I’m returning tomorrow with Mr. Thornton. We’ll need the back drawing-room again—no interruptions.”

“Mr. Thornton? What has he to do with it?”

“He’s Miss Hale’s tenant. And he wishes to end his lease.”

Edith sighed. “Very well. Spare me the details.”

***

The next day, Mr. Thornton arrived promptly, but Mr. Lennox did not. After an hour’s wait, Margaret entered, her pallor betraying her unease.

“I’m sorry,” she began hurriedly, “Mr. Lennox isn’t here—he would have explained this far better than I can. He’s been advising me—”

Thornton’s voice was quiet. “If my presence troubles you, I’ll go.”

“No, please.” She steadied herself. “I wanted to say—I’m grieved you’re giving up the mill. But Mr. Lennox believes prospects will improve—”

“Mr. Lennox knows little of it,” Thornton interrupted, his tone edged with bitterness. “A man blessed in all things cannot fathom what it is to face ruin when half his life is already spent—to see time wasted, with nothing left but regret.”

Margaret’s gaze softened. “You misjudge him. He spoke only of your chances to recover—more than recover—what you’ve lost.” She fumbled with papers, her hands unsteady. “Here—he drafted a proposal. If you were to borrow funds of mine, lying idle at the bank, you could repay me generously and keep Marlborough Mills.”

Her voice grew firmer, but Thornton did not reply. She searched for the terms, desperate to frame it as mere business—until his voice, rough with emotion, stopped her heart.

Her eyes flicked up, then she bowed her head, her fingers shielding her face. He stepped closer, his voice trembling.

Still she did not look up. He knelt beside her, his whisper urgent.

“If you stay silent, I’ll claim you as mine by sheer will. Send me away now—if I must go. *Margaret.*”

At the third plea, she turned her face—still veiled by her hands—and pressed it against his shoulder. He held her tightly, neither daring to speak. At last, her voice broke.

“I’m not worthy of you.”

He laughed softly. “Not worthy? Don’t mock my own unworthiness.”

Gently, he drew her hands from her face and guided her arms around him, as she had once shielded him from the rioters.

“Do you remember, love?” he murmured. “And how I repaid you with arrogance the next day?”

“I remember only my own harsh words.”

He cupped her chin. “Look at me.” When she did, blushing, he drew a worn pocket-book from his coat. “Do you know these?”

Pressed within were faded roses. She studied them, puzzled, then recognition dawned.

“From Helstone? The leaves—you’ve been there?”

“I had to see the place that shaped you,” he admitted. “Even when I believed you’d never be mine.”

Her fingers brushed the petals. “Give them to me.”

He smiled. “Only if you pay for them.”

A long, contented silence followed. Then she whispered, “How shall I tell Aunt Shaw?”

“Let me.”

“No—but what will she say?”

He chuckled. “Her first words will be, *‘That man!’*”

Margaret’s laughter was muffled against his shoulder. “Hush, or I’ll mimic your mother: *‘That woman!’*”
