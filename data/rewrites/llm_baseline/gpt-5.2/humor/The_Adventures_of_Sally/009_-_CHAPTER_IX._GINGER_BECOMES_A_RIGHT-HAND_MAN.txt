She had imagined him dead with a novelist’s thoroughness: Ginger Kemp, buoyed up like an unclaimed parcel on the Thames, pale as yesterday’s haddock, drifting past the Houses of Parliament while indifferent ducks reviewed him. In the small boarding-house bedroom, the myth collapsed in an instant, replaced by a more immediate spectacle—Ginger standing alive in her doorway with his hair in a haystack condition and a black smudge on the very tip of his nose, like a cook who has tasted the gravy with his face.

Relief hit Sally so hard it came out of her as laughter. She dropped into a chair and let loose a sound that startled even herself, as if some hidden bird had been trapped in her throat and had finally found the open window.

“I say, you know!” Ginger began, hovering in the middle of the room in the tense way men do when they feel the furniture might accuse them.

Sally tried to speak, failed, and laughed again, wiping her eyes with the back of her hand as if she had been chopping onions instead of catastrophes.

“Oh, I am glad to see you,” she got out at last.

“No, really?” Ginger’s face brightened, pleased in the simple, earnest fashion of a dog who has just been told he isn’t entirely useless. “That’s fine.”

It seemed to occur to him that there were manners to be observed, even in burglar-like situations.

“I say, you know, awfully sorry. About barging in here, I mean. Never dreamed it was your room. Unoccupied, I thought.”

“Don’t mention it. I ought not to have disturbed you. You were having a nice sleep, of course. Do you always sleep on the floor?”

“It was like this—”

“Because if you’re wearing it for ornament,” Sally continued, her eyes narrowing with the clinical kindness of a woman inspecting a cake that’s sunk, “as a sort of beauty-spot, all right. But in case you don’t know, you’ve a smut on your nose.”

Ginger’s hand flew up.

“Oh, my aunt! Not really?”

“Now would I deceive you on an important point like that?”

“Do you mind if I have a look in the glass?”

“Certainly, if you can stand it.”

He hurried to the dressing-table, peered, and gave a little yelp of wounded dignity, dabbing at the spot with his handkerchief.

“You’re perfectly right,” he announced, as if commending her judgment in a matter of law. “I thought I was. I’m very quick at noticing things.”

“My hair’s a bit rumpled, too,” he added, still staring at himself, combing air into order with his fingers.

“Very much so.”

“You take my tip,” Ginger said earnestly, turning from the mirror like a man delivering a sermon to the newly fallen, “and never lie about under beds. There’s nothing in it.”

“That reminds me. You won’t be offended if I ask you something?”

“No, no. Go ahead.”

“It’s rather an impertinent question. You may resent it.”

“No, no.”

“Well, then—what were you doing under my bed?”

“Oh, under your bed?”

“Yes. Under my bed. This. It’s a bed, you know. Mine. My bed. You were under it. Why? Or putting it another way, why were you under my bed?”

“I was hiding.”

“Playing hide-and-seek? That explains it.”

“Mrs. What’s-her-name—Beecher—Meecher—was after me.”

Sally shook her head with grave disapproval, as though reprimanding a village elder for taking up jazz.

“You mustn’t encourage Mrs. Meecher in these childish pastimes. It unsettles her.”

Ginger passed an agitated hand over his forehead.

“It’s like this—”

“I hate to keep criticizing your appearance,” said Sally, “and personally I like it; but when you clutched your brow just then, you put about a pound of dust on it. Your hands are probably grubby.”

Ginger inspected them, holding them out as if offering them for seasoning.

“They are!”

“Why not make a really good job of it and have a wash?”

“Do you mind?”

“I’d prefer it.”

“Thanks awfully. I mean to say it’s your basin, you know, and all that. What I mean is, seem to be making myself pretty well at home.”

“Oh, no.”

“Touching the matter of soap—”

“Use mine. We Americans are famous for our hospitality.”

“Thanks awfully.”

“The towel is on your right.”

“Thanks awfully.”

“And I’ve a clothes brush in my bag.”

“Thanks awfully.”

Sally sat listening while he attacked the water with the zeal of a man trying to drown his past. Splashing filled the room—wet, slapping, domestic thunder—like a sea-lion taking a dip in a tin bath. The boarding-house itself contributed its usual thin accompaniment: a far-off phonograph wheezing something that had once been a song, the elevator clanking like a man chewing nails, and somewhere a door banging with that modern, joyless insistence which says the building would like to be a factory when it grows up. Sally, who always heard rooms as much as she saw them, felt her nerves loosen as the clean, honest noise of washing replaced the secretive hush of hiding.

“Now, then,” she said when the final splash subsided, “why were you hiding from Mrs. Meecher?”

Ginger emerged with his face wet and his expression that of a man who has been threatened with public speaking.

“I say, you know, that woman is rather by way of being one of the lads, what! Scares me! Word was brought that she was on the prowl, so it seemed to me a judicious move to take cover till she sort of blew over. If she’d found me, she’d have made me take that dog of hers for a walk.”

“Toto?”

“Toto.” Ginger spoke the name as if it were a harsh spice best used sparingly. “You know,” he went on, with a strong sense of having been wronged by nature, “no dog’s got a right to be a dog like that. I don’t suppose there’s anyone keener on dogs than I am, but a thing like a woolly rat—” He shuddered slightly. “Well, one hates to be seen about with it in the public streets.”

“Why couldn’t you have refused in a firm but gentlemanly manner to take Toto out?”

“Ah! There you rather touch the spot.” Ginger’s eyes slid away. “You see, the fact of the matter is, I’m a bit behind with the rent, and that makes it rather hard to take what you might call a firm stand.”

“But how can you be behind with the rent? I only left here the Saturday before last and you weren’t in the place then. You can’t have been here more than a week.”

“I’ve been here just a week. That’s the week I’m behind with.”

“But why? You were a millionaire when I left you at Roville.”

“Well, the fact of the matter is, I went back to the tables that night and lost a goodish bit of what I’d won. And, somehow or another, when I got to America, the stuff seemed to slip away.”

“What made you come to America at all?” Sally asked, politely laying down the question as one lays a knife on a table—flat, clean, unavoidable.

One of Ginger’s familiar blushes started up, as if his skin were trying to apologise on his behalf.

“Oh, I thought I would. Land of opportunity, you know.”

“Have you managed to find any of the opportunities yet?”

“Well, I have got a job of sorts. I’m a waiter at a rummy little place on Second Avenue. The salary isn’t big, but I’d have wangled enough out of it to pay last week’s rent, only they docked me a goodish bit for breaking plates and what not. The fact is, I’m making rather a hash of it.”

“Oh, Ginger! You oughtn’t to be a waiter!”

“That’s what the boss seems to think.”

“I mean, you ought to be doing something ever so much better.”

“But what? You’ve no notion how well all these blighters here seem to be able to get along without my help. I’ve tramped all over the place, offering my services, but they all say they’ll try to carry on as they are.”

Sally, who had grown up in a world where a kitchen could be run like a small kingdom and every pair of hands mattered, found this American talent for self-sufficiency almost offensive. She sat forward, struck by a thought as crisp as a snapped bean.

“I know!”

“What?”

“I’ll make Fillmore give you a job. I wonder I didn’t think of it before.”

“Fillmore?”

“My brother. Yes, he’ll be able to use you.”

“What as?”

Sally hesitated, searching for a title with the seriousness of someone deciding what to label a jar whose contents might explode.

“As a—as a—oh, as his right-hand man.”

“Does he want a right-hand man?”

“Sure to. He’s a young fellow trying to get along. Sure to want a right-hand man.”

“’M yes,” said Ginger reflectively. “Of course, I’ve never been a right-hand man, you know.”

“Oh, you’d pick it up. I’ll take you round to him now. He’s staying at the Astor.”

“There’s just one thing,” said Ginger.

“What’s that?”

“I might make a hash of it.”

“Heavens, Ginger! There must be something in this world that you wouldn’t make a hash of. Don’t stand arguing any longer. Are you dry? and clean? Very well, then. Let’s be off.”

“Right ho.”

He took one step toward the door and froze with one leg suspended, as if struck by a spell. From the passage came a shrill yapping—thin, sharp, metallic, like a badly tuned piccolo being murdered.

Ginger stared at Sally. Then he looked—longingly—at the bed, which in his mind had become a sacred cave of refuge.

“Don’t be such a coward,” said Sally severely.

“Yes, but—”

“How much do you owe Mrs. Meecher?”

“Round about twelve dollars, I think it is.”

“I’ll pay her.”

Ginger flushed awkwardly, the colour rising like shame to the top of a stew.

“No, I’m hanged if you will! I mean,” he stammered, “it’s frightfully good of you and all that, and I can’t tell you how grateful I am, but honestly, I couldn’t—”

Sally didn’t press it. In fact, she liked him better for refusing. Fillmore, in his penniless days, had never been too proud to accept help; he had accepted it as if it were a tax imposed by society on the comfortable.

“Very well,” she said. “Have it your own way. Proud. That’s me all over, Mabel. Ginger!” She broke off sharply as the yapping renewed itself, nearer now, a sound with teeth in it. “Pull yourself together. Where is your manly spirit? I’d be ashamed to be such a coward.”

“Awfully sorry, but honestly, that woolly dog—”

“Never mind the dog. I’ll see you through.”

They stepped into the passage almost on top of Toto, who was stalking phantom rats with the intense concentration of a creature whose mind contained nothing but vengeance and fluff. Behind him, Mrs. Meecher manoeuvred, ready to pounce like a general who has located the enemy’s pantry. Her face lit grimly when she saw Ginger.

“Mister Kemp! I been looking for you.”

Sally, bright as a bell and twice as carrying in that narrow corridor, slipped into the space between predator and prey.

“Oh, Mrs. Meecher,” she said, shepherding Ginger forward with the brisk competence of a woman steering a cart through geese, “I was so surprised to meet Mr. Kemp here. He is a great friend of mine. We met in France. We’re going off now to have a long talk about old times, and then I’m taking him to see my brother—”

“Toto—” began Mrs. Meecher, her voice taking on the particular tone of an employer loading a burden onto someone else’s shoulders.

“Dear little thing!” Sally exclaimed, as if Toto were a soufflé that had risen perfectly instead of a nervous mop with a bark like ripping cloth. “You ought to take him for a walk. It’s a lovely day. Mr. Kemp was saying just now that he would have liked to take him, but we’re rather in a hurry and shall probably have to get into a taxi. You’ve no idea how busy my brother is just now. If we’re late, he’ll never forgive us.”

She flowed down the stairs as she spoke, leaving Mrs. Meecher dissatisfied but uncertain, caught in the net of Sally’s tone. There had always been something about Sally—even before money—that made Mrs. Meecher feel as if she were being politely disciplined in a language she didn’t quite understand. Now, with Sally rich and independent, that bafflement edged into awe. By the time Mrs. Meecher had gathered her faculties, the front door had closed.

On the sidewalk Ginger stopped and drew a long breath, the city air tasting faintly of coal smoke and hot metal and somebody’s lunch.

“You know,” he said, looking at Sally as if she had just pulled a rabbit out of a hat and then convinced it to pay rent, “you’re wonderful!”

Sally accepted the compliment as if it were simply the correct seasoning for the moment.

“Now we’ll go and hunt up Fillmore,” she said. “But there’s no need to hurry, of course, really. We’ll go for a walk first, and then call at the Astor and make him give us lunch.”

Even the word lunch put a steadier rhythm into the street for Sally, as if the day had acquired a proper structure: talking, walking, eating—life arranged the way it ought to be, with nourishment as the hinge.

“I want to hear all about you. I’ve heard something already. I met your cousin, Mr. Carmyle. He was on the train coming from Detroit. Did you know that he was in America?”

“No, I’ve—er—rather lost touch with the Family.”

“So I gathered from Mr. Carmyle. And I feel hideously responsible. It was all through me that all this happened.”

“Oh, no.”

“Of course it was. I made you what you are to-day—I hope I’m satisfied—I dragged and dragged you down until the soul within you died, so to speak.” She spoke quickly, like someone beating egg whites before they collapse. “I know perfectly well that you wouldn’t have dreamed of savaging the Family as you seem to have done if it hadn’t been for what I said to you at Roville. Ginger, tell me—what did happen? I’m dying to know. Mr. Carmyle said you insulted your uncle!”

“Donald. Yes, we did have a bit of a scrap, as a matter of fact. He made me go out to dinner with him and we—er—sort of disagreed. To start with, he wanted me to apologize to old Scrymgeour, and I rather gave it a miss.”

“Noble fellow!”

“Scrymgeour?”

“No, silly! You.”

“Oh, ah!” Ginger blushed again, the reliable blush of a man whose skin has better manners than his circumstances. “And then there was all that about the soup, you know.”

“How do you mean, ‘all that about the soup’? What about the soup? What soup?”

“Well, things sort of hotted up a bit when the soup arrived.”

“I don’t understand.”

“I mean, the trouble seemed to start, as it were, when the waiter had finished ladling out the mulligatawny. Thick soup, you know.”

“I know mulligatawny is a thick soup. Yes?”

“Well, my old uncle—I’m not blaming him, don’t you know—more his misfortune than his fault—I can see that now—but he’s got a heavy moustache. Like a walrus, rather, and he’s a bit apt to inhale the stuff through it. And I—well, I asked him not to. It was just a suggestion, you know. He cut up fairly rough, and by the time the fish came round we were more or less down on the mat chewing holes in one another.”

Sally could see it with humiliating clarity: the gleam of the soup, the steam carrying curry and onion and that faint sharpness of apple that good mulligatawny ought to have; the heavy moustache acting like a sieve, catching broth and dignity alike. Some experiences, she thought, were untranslatable—how could one describe to a person who hadn’t been there the exact sound a moustache makes when it meets hot soup? Language had no proper word for that particular wet slurp of authority.

“My fault, probably,” Ginger continued. “I wasn’t feeling particularly well-disposed towards the Family that night. I’d just had a talk with Bruce—my cousin, you know—in Piccadilly, and that had rather got the wind up me. Bruce always seems to get on my nerves a bit somehow and—Uncle Donald asking me to dinner and all that. By the way, did you get the books?”

“What books?”

“Bruce said he wanted to send you some books. That was why I gave him your address.”

Sally stared.

“He never sent me any books.”

“Well, he said he was going to, and I had to tell him where to send them.”

They walked on. The city kept up its constant racket—horns barking, wheels grinding, voices rising and falling like badly rehearsed choruses—and Sally felt, under it all, the way modern noise tries to flatten every private thought into the same thin coin. She didn’t like the idea of Bruce Carmyle following her across an ocean, and she liked even less the idea that he could do it while pretending it was something else—books, culture, a little gift wrapped in respectable words. Words were powerful that way: put the right label on desire and it passed through customs.

“Go on telling me about your uncle,” she said, briskly, as if steering the conversation back to safer ground were like steering a pot away from boiling over.

“Well, there’s not much more to tell. I’d happened to get that wireless of yours just before I started out to dinner with him, and I was more or less feeling that I wasn’t going to stand any rot from the Family. I’d got to the fish course, hadn’t I? Well, we managed to get through that somehow, but we didn’t survive the fillet steak. One thing seemed to lead to another, and the show sort of bust up. He called me a good many things, and I got a bit fed-up, and finally I told him I hadn’t any more use for the Family and was going to start out on my own. And—well, I did, don’t you know. And here I am.”

Sally listened, breathless, as if he were describing a storm that had uprooted an old orchard. The story settled something in her. Whatever faint doubts she had had about handing her money over to Fillmore had dissolved. Ginger, with his rent arrears and his terror of small dogs and his tragic aptitude for making a hash of perfectly reasonable employment, was plainly a creature who needed to be set carefully on his feet, the way one sets a slightly cracked teacup on the safest shelf.

“We’ll go to the Astor now,” she said, “and I’ll introduce you to Fillmore. He’s a theatrical manager and he’s sure to have something for you.”

“It’s awfully good of you to bother about me.”

“Ginger,” said Sally, “I regard you as a grandson. Hail that cab, will you?”
