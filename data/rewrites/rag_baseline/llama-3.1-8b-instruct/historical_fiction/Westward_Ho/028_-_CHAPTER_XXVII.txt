As Ayacanora's tenure aboard the vessel continued, a peculiar crisis began to unfold, one that would pose a formidable challenge to the collective well-being of both the crew and the indigenous populations that interacted with them. This phenomenon, though not unique to the encounter between Western colonizers and Native Americans, had, in the centuries that followed, contributed significantly to the decline and eventual disappearance of various Native American tribes. The crisis stemmed from the inherent dissonance between the cultural values and social norms of these two vastly different groups, precipitating a profound loss of self-respect among the indigenous individuals, particularly those who had come into contact with the Western world.

Their exposure to the superior technological prowess, material wealth, and cognitive abilities of the white settlers led to a precipitous erosion of their self-reliance, as they grew accustomed to acquiring the necessities of life with minimal effort. Consequently, the virtues that were once characteristic of their society—though fragile and limited in scope—began to fade, replaced by a downward trajectory of dependency, idleness, and moral decay.

Ayacanora, the Omagua warrior-prophetess, exemplified this trajectory all too well. Initially, she appeared to be an embodiment of the mythical figure of the Virginian princess, Pocahontas, who had navigated the complexities of cross-cultural exchange with relative success. However, as time passed, Ayacanora's behavior became increasingly erratic, reflecting the destructive tendencies that often accompany the loss of self-respect and self-sufficiency. She developed a penchant for pilfering small items that caught her fancy, often taking them without permission, and exhibited a marked propensity for sulking and self-pity when rebuked by Amyas, the captain.

Despite her propensity for mischief, Ayacanora's actions were not entirely devoid of a deeper logic. Her affinity for sweet treats and intoxicants, for instance, may have been an expression of her longing for the familiar flavors and substances of her native forest environment. Furthermore, her defiance of Amyas's authority, including her repeated threats of self-harm, could be seen as a manifestation of her struggle to adapt to the restrictive social norms of the Western world.
