Tito returned from Siena with swift efficiency, yet his departure marked the beginning of a longer absence; he did not return until the seventeenth of August. Nearly two weeks had elapsed since the arrest of the accused, and the shadow of uncertainty still clung to them within the prison walls. For Romola, these days had felt like a suspension of existence, her entire world narrowed to the precarious balance of probabilities regarding their fate. The mood in the city was a volatile mixture of fear and calculation. On one hand, the clamor grew louder that the Republic teetered on the brink of ruin, and that mercy shown to the prisoners would invite immediate attack from their enemies. On the other, the certainty loomed that condemning five citizens of such distinguished standing to death and confiscating their estates would breed a rancorous hatred from their families that no authority could withstand.

The power to deliver the final judgment rested with the Eight, the magistrates overseeing criminal justice, whose sentence required a majority of six votes. Yet, the Eight recoiled from this heavy burden, requesting that the responsibility be shared by the Signoria—the Gonfaloniere and the eight Priors. The Signoria, in turn, shrugged off the weight, proposing an appeal to the Great Council. According to a law championed by Savonarola three years prior, any citizen condemned to death by the "six beans" (*set fave*) possessed the right to appeal to the Great Council. However, at this critical juncture, the friends of the accused resisted the formal appeal, driven primarily by a desperate need for delay. Since strict legality demanded a sentence be passed before an appeal could be lodged, their resistance forced a compromise: the case was referred to a massive assembly convened on the seventeenth, comprising all higher magistracies, the Senate of Eighty, and a select body of citizens.

On this fateful day, Romola, her anxiety sharpened by the fear that her godfather's destiny might be sealed before the proceedings concluded, had secured leave to visit him for the second time, though only in the presence of witnesses. She returned to the Via de' Bardi accompanied by her cousin, Monna Brigida, who remained unaware of whether the council had reached a decisive verdict. Brigida departed again to await news at a friend's house within one of the magistracies, promising to return with authentic tidings the moment they were available.

Exhausted and sick at heart, Romola collapsed onto the first seat in the bright saloon, too agitated to care for her surroundings or notice the discordant arrangement of the room. She sat with her back to the door, resting her head in her hands, waiting for Brigida's return. When the door finally opened, she knew instantly that it was not her cousin who entered.

Since her parting from Tito on that memorable night, Romola had possessed no external proof that he had secured his safety through treachery. To the contrary, she had evidence that he was still trusted by the Mediceans, believed to be carrying out their errands in Romagna under the guise of a government commission. The obscurity surrounding the conspirators allowed the comforting illusion that Tito had escaped any implication. Yet, Romola's suspicion was insurmountable. Her horror at his conduct toward Baldassarre projected itself over every interpretation of his actions; it was as if she had witnessed a murder and carried a permanent, diseased impression that his hands were stained with fresh blood.

As she heard his step on the stone floor, a chill shudder ran through her. She could not turn, could not rise to offer greeting. He did not speak but, after a brief pause, took a seat opposite her across the table. When she finally raised her eyes to meet his gaze, she found herself mute. He showed no irritation, only a cool composure.

"This meeting corresponds with our parting, Romola," he said softly. "But I understand it is a moment of terrible suspense. I have come, if you will listen, to bring you the relief of hope."

Romola started, shifting her position but regarding him with deep doubt.

"It will not be unwelcome to hear—even though it is I who tell it—that the council is prorogued until the twenty-first. The Eight have finally been frightened into passing a sentence of condemnation, but a demand has now been made on behalf of the condemned for an Appeal to the Great Council."

Romola's doubtful expression vanished; she asked eagerly, "And when is it to be made?"

"It has not yet been granted, but it *may* be. The Special Council meets again on the twenty-first to deliberate whether the Appeal shall be allowed. In the meantime, there is an interval of three days—a window in which chances may arise in favor of the prisoners, where interest may be used on their behalf."

Romola sprang from her seat. Color rushed to her face like a visible thought, and her hands trembled. In that instant, her feelings toward Tito were forgotten.

"Possibly," Tito said, rising as well, "your own intention anticipated what I was about to say. You are thinking of the Frate."

"I am," Romola replied, looking at him with surprise. "Has he done something? Is there anything to tell me?"

"Only this: it was Messer Francesco Valori's bitterness and violence that chiefly determined the council's course today. Half the men who voted against the prisoners were frightened into it. There are numerous friends of Fra Girolamo, both inside and outside this Special Council, who strongly oppose the death sentence—Piero Guicciardini, for instance, a member of the Signoria who offered stoutest resistance; and Giovan Battista Ridolfi, who, as a Piagnone, will not lightly forgive the death of his brother Niccolò."

"But how can the Appeal be denied?" Romola cried indignantly. "When it is the law! Was it not one of the chief glories of our popular government to enact such a law?"

"They call this an exceptional case. Of course, there are ingenious arguments, but there is much more loud bluster about the danger to the Republic. Yet, no opposition could prevent the assembly from being prorogued. A certain powerful influence, rightly applied during these next three days, might determine the wavering courage of those who wish the Appeal granted, and perhaps check the headlong enmity of Francesco Valori. It has come to my knowledge that the Frate has already interfered by sending a message to Valori in favor of Lorenzo Tornabuoni. I know you sometimes have access to the Frate; it would certainly be worthwhile to use your privilege now."

"It is true," Romola said, her voice abstracted. "I cannot believe the Frate would approve of denying the Appeal."

"I heard it said by many in the Palazzo court before I left that it would be to Fra Girolamo's everlasting discredit to allow a government composed almost entirely of his party to deny the Appeal without protest, especially when he boasts in his books and sermons that he secured the law's passage. But between ourselves, with all respect for your Frate's ability, my Romola, he has fallen into the habit of preaching that form of human sacrifice called killing tyrants and wicked malcontents—a stance some of his followers might find inconsistent with lenity in this specific case."

*I know, I know,* Romola thought, pain etching her features. *He is driven into those excesses of speech. It used to be different.* "I *will* ask for an interview. I cannot rest without it. I trust in the greatness of his heart."

She did not look at Tito; her eyes were bent in a vague gaze toward the ground, lacking distinct consciousness of the words issuing from her husband.

"Better lose no time, then," Tito said, his tone unmixt with suavity, moving his cap in his hands as if preparing to depart. "And now, Romola, you will perhaps be able to see, despite prejudice, that my wishes align with yours in this matter. You will not regard the misfortune of my safety as an offense."

An electric shock passed through Romola: the full, crushing consciousness of her husband's presence returned to her. She looked at him without speaking.

"At least," he added, his tone hardening slightly, "you will endeavor to base our intercourse on reasonings other than the fact that an evil deed is possible, *I* have done it. Am I alone to be cast beyond the pale of your extensive charity?"

The feeling that had been suppressed from Romola's lips two weeks prior rose again with the gathered force of a tidal wave. She spoke with a decision that signaled her disregard for consequences.

"It is too late, Tito. There is no killing the suspicion that deceit has once begotten. And now I know everything. I know who that old man was: he was your father, to whom you owe everything—to whom you owe more than if you had been his own child. By comparison, it is a small thing that you broke my trust and my father's. As long as you deny the truth about that old man, a horror rises between us; the law that should make us one can never be obeyed. I too am a human being. I have a soul of my own that abhors your actions. Our union is a pretense—as if a perpetual lie could be a sacred marriage."

Tito did not answer immediately. When he finally spoke, it was with calculated caution, stimulated by alarm.

"And you mean to carry out that independence by quitting me, I presume?"

"I desire to quit you," Romola said impetuously.

"And suppose I do not submit to part with what the law gives me some security for retaining? You will then, of course, proclaim your reasons in the ear of all Florence. You will bring forward your mad assassin, who is doubtless ready to obey your call, and tell the world that you believe his testimony because he is so rational as to desire to assassinate me. You will first inform the Signoria that I am a Medicean conspirator, and then inform the Mediceans that I have betrayed them. In both cases, you will offer the excellent proof that you believe me capable in general of everything bad. It will certainly be a striking position for a wife to adopt. And if, on such evidence, you succeed in holding me up to infamy, you will have surpassed all the heroines of the Greek drama."

He paused, but she stood mute. He continued with a sense of mastery.

"I believe you have no other grievance against me—except that I failed in fulfilling some lofty, indefinite conditions on which you gave me your wifely affection, so that, by withdrawing it, you have gradually reduced me to the careful supply of your wants as a fair Piagnone of high condition and liberal charities. I think your success in gibbeting me is not certain. But doubtless you would begin by winning the ear of Messer Bernardo del Nero?"

"Why do I speak of anything?" Romola cried in anguish, sinking back onto her chair. "It is hateful in me to be thinking of myself."

She did not notice when Tito left the room, nor did she know how long it was before the door opened to admit Monna Brigida. But in that instant, she sprang up and declared, "Cousin, we must go to San Marco directly. I must see my confessor, Fra Salvestro."
