In the dimly lit chamber, where shadows danced upon the stone walls, the alderman's words hung heavy with the weight of judgment. "She is a witness, and may be a necessary one," he declared, his voice dripping with a detached curiosity. Denys, standing tall beside him, spoke up, his voice laced with skepticism. "Why, Messire Bailiff, you lay not all your witnesses by the heels I trow."

The alderman's eyes lit up at being addressed as bailiff, and he leaned forward, his thick fingers drumming a staccato beat on the wooden table. "In a case of blood we detain all testimony that is like to give us leg bail, and so defeat justice, and that is why we still keep the women folk. For a man at odd times hides a week in one mind, but a woman, if she do her duty to the realm o' Friday, she shall undo it afore Sunday, or try." He paused, his gaze sweeping across the room, as if daring anyone to contradict him. "Could you see yon wench now, you should find her a-blubbering at having betrayed five males to the gallows. Had they been females, we might have trusted to a subpoena. For they despise one another. And there they show some sense. But now I think on't, there were other reasons for laying this one by the heels."

Denys and Gerard exchanged a look, their faces set in identical lines of dismay. "No need to deave me," the alderman growled, his tone a low rumble of thunder. "Here 'tis in black and white." He pushed a parchment across the table, his massive frame looming over Gerard as he reached for it. "Jean Hardy, that is one of the thieves, being questioned, confessed that... Ah, humph? Ay, here 'tis. 'And that the girl Manon was the decoy, and her sweetheart was Georges Vipont, one of the band; and hanged last month: and that she had been deject ever since, and had openly blamed the band for his death, saying if they had not been rank cowards, he had never been taken, and it is his opinion she did but betray them out of very spite, and—"
