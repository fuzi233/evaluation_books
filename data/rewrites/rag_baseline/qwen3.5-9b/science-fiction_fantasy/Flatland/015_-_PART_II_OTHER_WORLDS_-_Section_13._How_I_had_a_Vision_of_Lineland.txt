It was the penultimate day of the 1999th year, the eve of the Great Long Vacation. Exhausted from my favorite pastime—Geometry—I had retired to sleep carrying an unsolved theorem in my mind. But dreams possess a logic of their own, one that defies Euclidean proof.

In my slumber, I found myself standing amidst a vast, chaotic throng. Before me stretched a multitude of tiny, jagged lines—figures I instinctively categorized as women—interspersed with entities even smaller, mere lustrous points. All moved back and forth along a single, infinite axis, vibrating with a uniform, hypnotic velocity. From this congregation erupted a cacophony: a confused, multitudinous chirping, a twittering static that filled the void between movements. Occasionally, the motion ceased; the line froze, and absolute silence descended, heavy and suffocating.

Driven by curiosity, I approached one of the larger lines, intending to address her. But she offered no response. A second attempt yielded the same mute indifference. A third was met with identical coldness. My patience, already frayed by the absurdity of the scene, snapped. This was not merely rudeness; it was an ontological insult. I stepped directly into her path, blocking her trajectory, and shouted my query into her face: "Woman, what signifies this concourse? What drives this strange, confused chirping and this monotonous oscillation within a single Straight Line?"

"I am no Woman," came the reply, sharp and clear. "I am the Monarch of this world. But tell me, whence do you intrude into my realm of Lineland?"

Startled by the sudden shift in tone, I apologized profusely for startling His Royal Highness. Describing myself as a bewildered traveler, I implored him to explain the nature of his dominion. Yet, extracting truth proved difficult. The Monarch operated under a delusion of shared omniscience; convinced that familiarity bred understanding, he assumed my silence stemmed from playful feigned ignorance rather than genuine confusion. Through persistent questioning, however, I pieced together the grim architecture of his existence.

This poor, ignorant sovereign believed that the Straight Line he inhabited *was* the entirety of space, the totality of the universe. Unable to perceive beyond his dimension, incapable of movement perpendicular to his axis, he knew nothing of what lay outside. When I first spoke, his auditory organs registered the sound, but his mind rejected it. As he later explained, the vibrations struck him not as external speech, but as internal rumblings—"from my own intestines." Without visual confirmation, he saw no man; without spatial context, he heard only noise beating against his stomach. To him, everything outside his line was not empty space, but non-existence—a terrifying void where nothing could possibly be.

His subjects, the small lines and the luminous points, were trapped in this one-dimensional prison. Their entire horizon collapsed to a single point. To a Linelander, a man, a woman, a child, or a stone was indistinguishable from a dot. The only variable capable of differentiation was pitch: the timbre of the voice revealed sex and age. Furthermore, because every individual occupied the entire width of their universe, no passing was possible. Once neighbors, forever neighbors. Neighborhood in Lineland was akin to marriage in Flatland: an eternal, unbreakable bond until death severed the connection.

Such a life, stripped of lateral vision and linear mobility, seemed to me a nightmare of claustrophobic monotony. Yet, I was struck by the King's inexplicable cheerfulness. Wondering if conjugal bliss could survive such constraints, I hesitated before asking, but eventually plunged into the topic by inquiring about the health of his family.

"My wives and children," he declared, "are well and happy."

Staggered by this assertion—and noting the absence of any females in his immediate vicinity—I challenged him. "Pardon me, Your Majesty, but I cannot conceive how you can see or approach them when at least six individuals stand between you and them. Is proximity truly unnecessary for marriage and procreation in Lineland?"

"How dare you ask such an absurdity!" the King retorted, his voice rising in indignation. "If the universe functioned as you describe, we would long ago be extinct. No, no! Proximity is irrelevant to the union of souls. The birth of children is too sacred to rely on the chance accident of physical closeness. Since you choose to remain in ignorance, I shall instruct you as if you were the youngest infant in Lineland."

He paused, composing his royal dignity. "Know this: Marriages are consummated solely through the faculty of sound and the sense of hearing. Every Man possesses two mouths—or voices, as you might say—just as he has two eyes. At one extremity lies the Bass; at the other, the Tenor. I failed to distinguish your tenor earlier, hence my initial silence."

I admitted I possessed only one voice and was unaware of his dual apparatus.

"That confirms my suspicion," the King sneered. "You are not a Man, but a feminine monstrosity endowed with a bass voice and an utterly uneducated ear. Nevertheless, let us proceed."

"Nature dictates that every Man must wed two wives," he continued.

"Why two?" I asked.

"You push your feigned simplicity too far," he cried. "How can a union achieve complete harmonic perfection without the synthesis of the Four-in-One: the Bass and Tenor of the Man, combined with the Soprano and Contralto of the two Women?"

"But what if a man prefers one wife, or three?" I pressed.

"It is impossible," he stated dogmatically. "As inconceivable as claiming that two plus one equals five, or that the human eye can perceive a Straight Line."

Before I could interject, he launched into the mechanics of their reproduction. "Once per week, a Law of Nature compels us to engage in a violent, rhythmic oscillation lasting roughly a hundred beats. During this Universal Marriage Chorus, at the precise fifty-first pulsation, all inhabitants halt their frantic motion. Each individual emits their richest, purest vocal strain. It is in this suspended moment that destiny unfolds. The adaptation of Bass to Treble, Tenor to Contralto, is so exquisite that lovers separated by twenty thousand leagues can instantly recognize the responsive note of their soulmate. Penetrating the barriers of distance, Love binds the trio. The resulting marriage produces a triplet offspring—one male and two females—to maintain the cosmic balance of the sexes. Two girls for every boy; otherwise, the Alphabet of Nature would be broken."

"What! Always triplets?" I exclaimed. "Does every wife bear twins?"

"Bass-voiced monstrosity! Yes!" the King roared, momentarily silenced by his own fury. "How else could equilibrium be preserved?"

After a tense silence, he resumed, his tone softening slightly. "Do not assume that every bachelor finds his match immediately during this grand chorus. For most, the process is iterative. Few hearts are blessed with the immediate recognition of their destined partners. Courtship is often prolonged. The Wooer's voice may align with one potential wife but clash with the other, or fail to harmonize with either initially. Perhaps the Soprano and Contralto lack cohesion. In such cases, Nature provides a mechanism for refinement. Each weekly chorus brings the three parties closer. Every trial of voice, every detected discord, subtly forces the imperfect party to modulate their pitch, inching toward the ideal frequency. After countless iterations and microscopic adjustments, harmony is finally achieved. Then, amidst the universal chorus, the distant lovers discover perfect resonance. Before they are fully conscious, the wedded Triplet is drawn into a vocal embrace, and Nature rejoices in another marriage and three new lives."
