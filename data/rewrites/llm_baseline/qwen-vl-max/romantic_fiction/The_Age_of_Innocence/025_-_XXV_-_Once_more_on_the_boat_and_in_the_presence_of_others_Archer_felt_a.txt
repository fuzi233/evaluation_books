XXV. Once more on the boat, and in the presence of others, Archer felt a quietness within him that both surprised and sustained him. The day, by any ordinary measure, had been a failure—no kiss, no whispered word, no promise of what might come. Yet for a man consumed by unfulfilled longing, parting from the woman he loved with no certainty of ever seeing her again, this calm was almost shameful in its depth. It wasn’t triumph, nor even relief—it was something deeper: a peace born of knowing she had held fast to both duty and truth, not through calculation, but through honesty so pure it trembled in her eyes and faltered in her voice. Now that the moment had passed, he found himself filled with tenderness and awe. He thanked fate that he hadn’t yielded to pride or vanity, that he hadn’t risked their fragile bond for the sake of a dramatic gesture before strangers.

Even after they’d said farewell at the Fall River station, and he walked away alone, the conviction remained: he had gained far more than he had lost. He returned to the club, wandered into the empty library, and sat there, reliving every second of their time together. In his mind’s eye, he saw her face, heard her words, felt the weight of her silence. And as he reflected, one truth became clearer: if she chose to return to Europe, to her husband, it wouldn’t be because she longed for her old life—even under the new terms offered. No. She would go only if she feared becoming a danger to him, a temptation too strong to resist. Her decision would depend on whether she believed he could remain true to the standard they had both quietly set. And that standard rested entirely on him. If he could hold her at a distance—close enough to care, yet far enough to protect—then she might stay. But it was up to him to keep her there.

On the train, these thoughts wrapped around him like a golden veil. The faces around him blurred, distant, unreal. He felt as though speaking to them would be meaningless—they wouldn’t grasp what stirred in his heart. That same hazy clarity followed him into the next morning, when he woke to a stifling September day in New York. The train windows revealed a stream of weary travelers, their faces sunken and drawn; he watched them through the same dreamlike haze. Then, suddenly, one face broke through—the young man he’d seen leaving the Parker House the day before, the one who didn’t look like any American traveler. He remembered now: the sharp eyes, the foreign air, the way he seemed out of place among the bustling crowds.

The man stood still, gazing about him with the bewildered expression of someone cast adrift in a strange world. Then he stepped forward, lifted his hat, and said in English, “Surely, Monsieur, we met in London?”
“Ah, yes—London!” Archer replied, grasping his hand with warmth and curiosity. “So you *did* make it here, after all?” He looked closely at the thin, haggard face of M. Rivière, Carfry’s French tutor.
“Oh, I arrived,” the Frenchman smiled faintly, lips tight. “But not for long—I leave the day after tomorrow.” He held his valise loosely, gloved hands clenched, eyes searching Archer’s face. “I wonder, Monsieur… since I’ve found you, might I—”
“I was just going to invite you,” Archer interrupted. “Come to lunch? Downtown? If you’ll look me up at my office, I’ll take you to a decent place.”
M. Rivière looked touched, startled. “You’re too kind. But I was only asking how to find transport. There are no porters, and no one listens—”
“I know,” Archer said. “Our stations can be shocking. They give you chewing gum when you ask for help. But come along—I’ll get you sorted. And you *must* lunch with me.”
After a brief hesitation, the young man declined, saying he was already engaged. But once outside, in the open street, he asked if he might call later that afternoon. Archer, relaxed in the summer slowness of the office, agreed and scribbled down his address. The Frenchman pocketed it with thanks, tipped his hat grandly, and vanished into a passing horse-car.

At the appointed hour, M. Rivière arrived—shaved, composed, but still pale and serious. Archer was alone in his office. Before accepting the offered chair, the young man began abruptly: “I believe I saw you yesterday in Boston.”
It was a trivial remark—Archer was about to nod, then stopped. There was something in the man’s gaze—intense, probing, charged with meaning.
“It is extraordinary,” M. Rivière continued, “that we should meet under these circumstances.”
“What circumstances?” Archer asked, wondering if the man needed money.
M. Rivière studied him carefully. “I have come—not to seek employment, as I mentioned last time—but on a special mission.”
“A special mission,” Archer repeated, catching the thread instantly. The two encounters now connected in his mind. He paused, letting the revelation settle. M. Rivière waited silently, as if aware that his words were enough.
“A special mission,” Archer echoed.
The young Frenchman raised his palms slightly, as if offering nothing but truth. The two men stared across the desk, neither moving. Finally, Archer said, “Do sit down.”
M. Rivière bowed, took a distant chair, and waited.
“It was about this mission you wished to speak?” Archer asked.
M. Rivière nodded. “Not for myself. On that score, I have already decided. But I wish—may I?—to speak to you about the Countess Olenska.”

Archer had known the words were coming. When they did, they struck him like a blow—a sudden surge of blood to his temples, as if caught in a hidden thicket.
“And on whose behalf?” he asked, voice steady.
M. Rivière met his gaze firmly. “Well… I might say hers, though that sounds presumptuous. Shall I say—on behalf of justice?”
Archer smiled ironically. “In other words—you’re Count Olenski’s messenger?”
He saw the flush deepen on M. Rivière’s sallow face. “Not to *you*, Monsieur. If I come to you, it is for another reason.”
“What right do you have,” Archer snapped, “to stand on any ground but his?”
The young man considered. “My mission is over. As far as the Countess is concerned, it has failed.”
“I can’t help that,” Archer retorted.
“No—but you *can* help,” M. Rivière said, pausing. He turned his hat slowly in his gloves, looked inside it, then back at Archer. “You can help, Monsieur, I am convinced, to make it equally a failure with her family.”

Archer pushed back his chair and stood. “Well—and by God, I will!”
He stood with hands in pockets, glaring down at the small figure before him. M. Rivière rose too, but his face was still lower, pale as ash.
“Why the devil,” Archer exploded, “should you think—since you’re appealing to me because of my connection to Madame Olenska—that I’d take a different view from the rest of her family?”
For a moment, M. Rivière said nothing. His expression shifted—from timidity to deep distress. He looked utterly defenseless.
“Oh, Monsieur—”
“I can’t imagine,” Archer continued, “why you came to me when there are others closer to her. Or why you thought I’d be more open to your arguments.”
M. Rivière accepted this storm with quiet humility. “The arguments I want to present, Monsieur, are mine—my own, not those I was sent with.”
“Then I see no reason to listen.”
M. Rivière looked into his hat again, as if deciding whether to leave. Then he spoke with sudden force: “Monsieur—will you tell me one thing? Is it my right to be here that you question? Or do you believe the matter is already closed?”

That quiet insistence made Archer feel the heat of his own anger recede. M. Rivière had claimed space. Archer reddened slightly, sat down, and gestured for him to do the same.
“I beg your pardon. But why isn’t it closed?”
M. Rivière looked at him with anguish. “You do agree, then, with the rest of the family—that in light of the proposals I brought, it’s hardly possible for Madame Olenska not to return to her husband?”
“Good God!” Archer exclaimed.
A low murmur confirmed it.
“Before seeing her, I saw Mr. Lovell Mingott—at Count Olenski’s request. We spoke several times before Boston. I understand he represents his mother’s view—and Mrs. Manson Mingott’s influence is great throughout the family.”

Archer sat silent, feeling the ground shift beneath him. To be excluded from negotiations, even unaware they were happening—this stunned him. And then he understood: they had stopped consulting him because they knew he was no longer theirs. He recalled May’s careless remark during their drive home from Mrs. Mingott’s: “Perhaps Ellen would be happier with her husband.”
He remembered his sharp protest—and realized she had never spoken of Ellen since. That offhand comment had been a test. The wind had blown in their direction. The family had acted. He had been cut out.

And he admired them for it. May, especially—she would have resisted if her conscience rebelled. But she likely shared the belief that Ellen was better off as an unhappy wife than as a separated woman. And she probably thought it useless to discuss it with him—he had a habit of questioning the most sacred assumptions.

Archer looked up. “Don’t you know, Monsieur—is it possible you don’t know—that the family now doubts whether they have the right to advise the Countess to refuse her husband’s offer?”
“The proposals I brought?”
“The proposals I brought.”

Archer opened his mouth to snap that none of this was M. Rivière’s concern—but something in the young man’s humble courage stayed him. Instead, he asked: “What is your purpose in telling me this?”

The answer came without delay. “To beg you, Monsieur—to beg you with all I have—don’t let her go back. Oh, please, don’t let her!”

Archer stared. There was no doubt—this was real. The man had sacrificed everything to speak this truth.
“May I ask,” Archer said slowly, “if this was the line you took with the Countess?”
M. Rivière flushed, but his eyes held firm. “No, Monsieur. I accepted the mission in good faith. I truly believed—reasons I needn’t explain—that it would be better for her to return, to reclaim her position, her fortune, the respect her husband’s name gives her.”
“I supposed so,” Archer said. “One couldn’t accept such a task otherwise.”
“I wouldn’t have.”
“Well, then—?”
They locked eyes again, in silence.
“Ah, Monsieur,” M. Rivière said at last, “after I saw her, after I listened to her… I knew she was better off here.”
“You knew—?”
“She was good enough to hear me twice. She listened patiently, impartially. And in the course of those talks, I changed. I saw things differently.”
“What led to this change?”
“Simply seeing the change in *her*.”
“The change in her? You knew her before?”
The young man’s color rose. “I used to see her in her husband’s house. I’ve known Count Olenski for years. You can imagine—he would not send a stranger on such a task.”

Archer’s gaze drifted to the calendar on the wall, with the stern face of the President of the United States above it. That such a conversation could happen anywhere within his vast domain felt surreal.
“The change—what kind?”
“Ah, Monsieur—if I could tell you! _Tenez_—the discovery, perhaps, of what I’d never thought of before: that she’s an American. And that if you’re an American of *her* kind—of *your* kind—things that are accepted elsewhere, or tolerated as part of a convenient compromise, become unthinkable. Unbearable. If Madame Olenska’s relations truly understood what these things meant, their opposition to her return would be as absolute as her own. But they seem to see her husband’s desire to have her back as proof of a longing for domesticity.”
He paused. “Whereas it’s far from being that simple.”

Archer looked back at the President’s face, then down at his desk, scattered with papers. For a moment, he couldn’t speak. Then he heard the chair scrape, and saw M. Rivière rise.
When he looked up, the young man was as moved as he.
“Thank you,” Archer said simply.
“There’s nothing to thank you for, Monsieur. It is I, rather—”
M. Rivière broke off. Then, in a firmer voice: “I should like to add one thing. You asked if I’m in Count Olenski’s employ. I am—for reasons of necessity, like anyone who must care for older, ill relatives. But from the moment I came here to speak to you, I consider myself discharged. I will tell him so on my return—and give him my reasons.”
“That’s all, Monsieur.”
M. Rivière bowed, stepped back.
“Thank you,” Archer said again, as their hands met.
