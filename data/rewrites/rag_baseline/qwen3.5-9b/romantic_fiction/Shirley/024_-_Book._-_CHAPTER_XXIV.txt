To perceive the architecture of this approaching doom, one must first understand the absolute fragility of the human vessel when subjected to the invisible pressures of fate. The future sometimes sobs a low, prophetic warning, like a gathering storm whose winds tear the firmament and whose clouds announce a blast capable of scattering the sea with wreckage; or it arrives as a creeping fog, carrying the yellow taint of pestilence from distant lands to poison the white isles of the West, dimming the lattices of English homes with the breath of foreign contagion. Yet, at other times, this catastrophe does not whisper; it erupts with the sudden violence of a rock splitting open to reveal a grave, issuing forth the corpse of one who slept—a new Lazarus, risen only to confirm the end.

Caroline Helstone returned from Hollow's Cottage believing herself in perfect health, a delusion shattered the moment she awoke the following morning. An oppressive, unwonted languor seized her, draining the vitality from her limbs. By breakfast, and at every subsequent meal, the fundamental instinct of appetite vanished. Food, once palatable, transformed in her mouth into mere ashes and sawdust, devoid of sustenance or flavor.

"Am I ill?" she asked, turning her gaze to the glass. Her reflection offered a cruel contradiction: her eyes burned with a bright, unnatural luster, their pupils dilated; her cheeks flushed with a rosy fullness that seemed excessive, vibrant even. "I look well," she murmured, confusion clouding her brow, "yet why can I not eat?"

Beneath the surface of this deceptive calm, a frantic pulse hammered against her temples. Her brain engaged in a strange, hyper-active frenzy. Her spirits, paradoxically, were elevated, occupied by hundreds of busy, fragmented, yet brilliantly sharp thoughts that danced across her mind, casting a feverish glow upon her complexion. But this elevation was not joy; it was the prelude to collapse.

What followed was a night of scorching heat, parched thirst, and restless agitation. Toward dawn, a terrible dream seized her with the ferocity of a tiger. When consciousness returned, the illusion of health evaporated. She felt it, knew it with terrifying clarity: she was ill.

The mechanism of her infection remained a mystery to her. Perhaps, during her solitary walk home, a sweet, poisoned breeze—redolent of honey-dew and miasma—had invaded her lungs and veins. Finding there already a fever of mental excitement, compounded by the languor of long conflict and habitual sadness, this insidious agent had fanned a dormant spark into a roaring fire, leaving behind a blaze that consumed her from within.

Yet, initially, the fire seemed gentle. For two days of burning heat and tormented nights, the symptoms showed no violence. Neither her uncle, nor Fanny, nor the doctor, nor Miss Keeldar, who visited briefly, harbored any genuine fear. The consensus among them was optimistic: a few days would suffice to restore her. Every one believed in the efficacy of time.

But the few days passed, and the anticipated revival failed to materialize. Though it was whispered that recovery could not long be delayed, Caroline sank deeper into the mire. Mrs. Pryor, who had visited daily, entered her chamber one morning, having been absent for a fortnight. She watched the young woman with narrowed, scrutinizing eyes for several minutes. Taking Caroline's hand, she pressed her finger firmly against the wrist, feeling the erratic rhythm beneath the skin. Then, silently, she withdrew.

She retreated to Mr. Helstone's study, remaining cloistered with him for nearly half the morning. Upon her return to the bedside, she removed her shawl and bonnet, discarding the barriers of public propriety. Standing by the bed, one hand resting upon the other, she rocked gently to and fro, a motion habitual to her, a silent prayer enacted in physical form. Finally, she spoke.

"I have sent Fanny to Fieldhead to fetch a few necessities for my short stay here," she announced, her voice steady. "It is my wish to remain with you until you are better. Your uncle kindly permits my attendance. Will it be acceptable to you, Caroline?"

"I am sorry you should take such needless trouble," Caroline replied, her voice thin. "I do not feel very ill, but I cannot refuse resolutely. It will be such comfort to know you are in the house, to see you sometimes in the room; but please, do not confine yourself on my account, dear Mrs. Pryor. Fanny nurses me very well."

Mrs. Pryor leaned over the pale sufferer, smoothing the hair beneath her cap and gently adjusting the pillow. As she performed these intimate offices, Caroline smiled, lifting her face to press a kiss against the nurse's cheek.

"Are you free from pain? Are you tolerably at ease?" Mrs. Pryor inquired in a low, earnest tone, yielding to the caress.

"I think I am almost happy," Caroline whispered.

"You wish to drink? Your lips are parched."

She held a glass filled with a cooling beverage to Caroline's mouth.

"Have you eaten anything today?"

"I cannot eat."

"But soon your appetite will return; it *must* return—that is, I pray God it may."

As she laid the girl back upon the couch, she encircled her in her arms. With a movement that seemed scarcely voluntary, she drew Caroline to her heart, holding her close for an instant, a gesture of profound intimacy.

"I shall hardly wish to get well," Caroline confessed, her voice trembling, "so that I may keep you always."

Mrs. Pryor did not smile at this speech. A tremor swept over her features, which she struggled in vain to suppress for several minutes.

"You are more used to Fanny than to me," she remarked eventually. "I should think my attendance must seem strange, perhaps officious?"

"No; quite natural, and very soothing," Caroline insisted. "You must have been accustomed to waiting on sick people, ma'am. You move about the room so softly, and you speak so quietly, and touch me so gently."

"I am dexterous in nothing, my dear. You will often find me awkward, but never negligent."

Negligent, indeed, she was not. From that hour, Fanny and Eliza became ciphers in the sickroom. Mrs. Pryor claimed the domain entirely; she performed all its duties, living in the room day and night. The patient remonstrated—faintly at first, and not at all thereafter. Loneliness and gloom were banished from the bedside; protection and solace took their place. Patient and nurse coalesced in a wondrous union. Caroline, usually pained by the necessity of receiving such constant attendance, found herself willing to be cherished as the nurse was bent on cherishing. No sign of weariness in the latter ever reminded the former that she ought to be anxious. There was, in fact, no very hard duty to perform; but a mere hireling might have found it unbearable.

Despite this relentless care, the sick girl did not recover. It seemed a strange defiance of nature, yet such was the case. She wasted like a snow-wreath in thaw, fading like a flower in drought. Miss Keeldar, whose thoughts rarely intruded upon danger or death, had initially entertained no fears for her friend. But witnessing the gradual change and sinking of Caroline during her visits, alarm clutched her heart. She approached Mr. Helstone with such energetic urgency that the gentleman was finally obliged, however unwillingly, to admit the possibility that his niece was afflicted with something more grievous than a simple migraine. When Mrs. Pryor came and quietly demanded a physician, he conceded, saying she might send for two if she wished.

One came, but he proved to be an oracle rather than a healer. He delivered a dark prophecy of which the future alone would solve the mystery, scribbled some prescriptions, issued vague directions with an air of crushing authority, pocketed his fee, and departed. Probably he knew well enough that he could do no good, but he lacked the courage to say so.

Still, no rumor of serious illness spread through the neighborhood. At Hollow's Cottage, it was maintained that Caroline suffered merely from a severe cold; she had even written a note to Hortense confirming this diagnosis. Mademoiselle contented herself with sending two pots of currant jam, a recipe for a tisane, and a note of advice.

Mrs. Yorke, upon learning that a physician had been summoned, sneered at the hypochondriac fancies of the rich and idle. She argued that those with nothing but themselves to think about must inevitably summon a doctor if only their little finger ached.

The "rich and idle," represented in the person of Caroline, meanwhile, fell rapidly into a condition of prostration. Their quickly consummated debility puzzled all who witnessed it, save for one; for that single observer reflected how liable an undermined structure is to sink into sudden ruin.

Sick people often harbor fancies inscrutable to ordinary attendants, and Caroline possessed one that even her tender nurse could not immediately explain. On a specific day of the week, at a precise hour, regardless of whether she felt worse or better, she would entreat to be taken up, dressed, and allowed to sit in her chair near the window. She retained this station until noon had passed. Whatever degree of exhaustion or debility her wan aspect betrayed, she softly resisted all persuasion to seek repose until the church clock duly tolled midday.
