By six o'clock that evening, most of Florence breathed a sigh of relief that the arrival of the new "Charlemagne" had finally concluded. When the rolling of drums, the blaring of trumpets, and the heavy tramp of hooves along the Pisan road merged with the ringing of excited church bells, it was indeed a magnificent spectacle for those perched on turreted rooftops. From their vantage points, overlooking the winding procession against the backdrop of green hills and valleys, they watched a terrifying yet splendid parade unfold. Though the lack of sunlight dimmed the brilliance of banners, spears, and plumed helmets, the absence of choking dust allowed the details to stand out with stark clarity.

Tall and sturdy Scottish archers marched alongside ponderous Swiss halberdiers, while nimble Gascon troopers seemed ready to scale walls at a moment's notice. The cavalrymen appeared as true knights-errant, mounted on chargers and wielding indomitable lances. It was a comforting sight to behold such an array of strength and splendor, filled with nobles, knights, and pages of noble birth, adorned with jeweled sword hilts, embroidered satin scarves, gold chains, and the transcendent canopy borne aloft by select youths above the head of the Most Christian King. As an old diarist noted, simply halting his spelling just behind the sheer wonder of the event: *"Fù gran magnificenza"* (It was great magnificence).

For the Signoria, however, who waited on their platform near the gates before marching out to greet the mighty guest, the grandeur was obscured by uneasy feelings. Had Messer Luca Corsini been able to deliver his prepared Latin welcome without interruption, the scene might have been less chaotic. Instead, a sudden rainstorm caused impatience among both men and horses, breaking off his carefully studied speech and forcing the scholarly representatives of the city to offer a clumsy greeting in impromptu French. This sudden confusion proved to be a stroke of luck for Tito Melema. Stationed behind the Signoria as a secretary, he found himself thrust into the mix when the crowd was pressed by the approaching horses.

"Somebody step forward and say a few words in French," urged Soderini. No one of high rank dared risk another public failure. "You, Francesco Gaddi—you can speak." But Gaddi, doubting his own quick wit, hesitated. Pushing Tito forward, he declared, "You, Melema."

Tito stepped forward instantly. With the natural grace of a man accustomed to deference, he delivered the necessary few words in French on behalf of the Signoria, then bowed gracefully and yielded the path to the King. The presence of mind that had deserted him during the terrifying crisis of the morning returned with full force. It was a reliable servant that never abandoned him unless danger was visibly present. Yet, when later complimented on his timely intervention, Tito merely laughed it off as trivial, allowing Gaddi to claim the credit for the improvised welcome. After all, vanity is often the truest test of character.

Other aspects of the day, however, did not go as planned. The Florentine procession of clergy and laypeople had become hopelessly congested, forced to detour through back streets to reach the Cathedral. Furthermore, the young monarch, seated beneath his canopy with a lance resting on his thigh, bore little resemblance to the legendary Charlemagne; he looked more like a hastily modeled grotesque. One might have wished that the "Champion of the honor of women" possessed legs of normal length rather than a miserable stump, a mouth less like a reptile's slit, and features less exaggerated. Still, his thin leg rested upon cloth of gold and pearls, and his face was merely a small interruption amidst the sea of black velvet, gold, rubies, and the blazing embroidery of the canopy. *"Fù gran magnificenza."*

With cries of *Francia, Francia!* echoing through the streets, the people tore the canopy to shreds as an ancient custom dictated, claiming it as their spoil. Royal lips touched the altar, and despite the various mishaps, the King and his retinue were lodged in the Palace of the Via Larga. The nobility scattered to their great homes, and the formidable army camped in the fields of Prato. The official business of the day was done.

Yet, the streets retained a startling appearance unseen by Florentines under the November stars. Gone was the usual gloom, broken only by feeble lamps before saints' images or the red glow of open doorways. Now, lamps hung from every window, illuminating the thoroughfares so brightly that pedestrians walked as securely by night as by day. *"Fù gran magnificenza."*

At approximately eight o'clock, Tito Melema walked alone along these illuminated streets, heading home from the Piazza di Santa Croce via the Via dei Benci. Having spent the entire day under the weight of hidden anxieties, he had managed to slip away unnoticed from the post-dinner festivities. Now, free to confront his situation calmly, he hoped to adjust his mindset to the probable outcomes and shed his childish fears. He regretted only that he had not recognized Baldassarre earlier; had he done so, perhaps the outcome would have been happier for everyone, sparing him the guilt of knowingly inflicting suffering on his father. Yet, there was no turning back now. Conflict was impossible; the only path remaining was self-preservation.

Lost in these thoughts, Tito moved toward the angle where the Via dei Benci met the Borgo Santa Croce. Suddenly, his ears were pierced not by the music of revelry, but by the rhythmic clang of labor—the sound of the anvil.

"What keeps the giant working so late?" Tito wondered, quickening his pace. The noise sparked a welcome idea. "Much better for me; I can handle that small errand tonight instead of tomorrow morning."

Preoccupied though he was, he paused briefly in admiration as he approached the workshop. The wide doorway, situated at the corner of a massive block of houses, was topped by a loggia with fluted tile roofing and supported by stone columns bearing rough capitals. Framed in the warm red light spilled from within, the silhouette of Niccolò Caparra stood out in stark black relief. His huge arms rose and fell rhythmically, alternating between hiding and revealing the profile of his firm mouth and powerful brow. Beside him, two smaller figures—one at the anvil, the other pumping the bellows—accentuated his imposing massiveness.

Tito cast a shadow across the threshold, standing silent, knowing it was futile to speak until Niccolò took notice. This did not happen until the smith had sharpened the head of an axe to perfection and tossed it aside. Then, Niccolò nodded with an unceremonious but good-natured gesture, resting his hammer on his hip.

"What is it, Messer Tito? Business?"

"Assuredly, Niccolò; otherwise, I would not dare interrupt you while you work past hours, taking that as a sign your work is urgent," Tito replied smoothly.

"I've been at the same task all day—forging axes and spearheads. Every fool who passes my shop sticks his head in to ask, 'Niccolò, will you not come see the King of France and his soldiers?' And I answer, 'No: I do not wish to see their faces; I wish to see their backs.'"

"Are you making arms for the citizens, then, Niccolò, so they may have something better than rusty scythes and pitchforks in case of trouble?"

"We shall see. Arms are good, and Florence may soon need them. The Frate tells us we shall regain Pisa, and I agree with him. But I would like to know how that promise will be kept if we do not secure plenty of quality weapons. The Frate sees far ahead; I believe that. But he understands that birds cannot be caught by blinking at them, as some of our people pretend. He sees sense, not nonsense. However, you are a bit of a Medicean, Messer Tito Melema. Well, so was I once, before the wine began to turn sour. What brings you here?"

"Simply to inquire about the price of that fine coat of mail I noticed hanging here recently. I intend to purchase it for someone who requires protection beneath their doublet."

"Let him come and buy it himself," Niccolò replied bluntly. "I am particular about what I sell and to whom. I prefer to know my customers."

"I understand your scruples, Niccolò. But this is defensive armor; it cannot hurt anyone."

"True: but it may give the wearer a false sense of security if they decide to cause harm. No, no; this is not my own work. It is fine craftsmanship by Maso of Brescia. I would be reluctant to see it cover the heart of a scoundrel. I must know who will wear it."

"Well, then, to be perfectly honest, Niccolò mio, I want it myself," Tito admitted, sensing further persuasion was pointless. "In fact, I am likely to undertake a journey soon—and you know what traveling entails in these turbulent times. Do you suspect me of treason against the Republic?"

"No, I know nothing harmful of you," Niccolò said, returning to his blunt manner. "But do you have the funds to pay? You frequent my shop often enough to recognize my signs; you have seen the burning account books. I trust no one. The price is twenty florins, discounted because it is second-hand.
