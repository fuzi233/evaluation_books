The dawn of 1492 arrived with a terrifying uniformity. Three and a half centuries prior, the angel of the morning had traversed the globe—from the Levant to the Pillars of Hercules, across the snow-capped Alps to the bare coasts of the West—seeing the exact same topography: the great mountain shadows, the olive groves, the pine forests, and the verdant plains. The river courses that carved the lives of men remained unchanged; the currents within the human heart pulsed to the identical rhythms of hunger, labor, seed-time, harvest, love, and death. Even the skyline of Florence, a world-famous city seemingly untouched by the flux of time, stood as an unviolated monument, reminding the living that they resembled their ancestors more than they differed from them. The mechanical principles governing those domes and towers ensured a structural continuity in human building that transcended political upheaval.

If the spirit of a Florentine citizen, whose eyes had closed during the era when Christopher Columbus argued for his three frail vessels at Palos, could return from the shades to witness this golden morning, he would find a profound sense of fellowship among the inheritors of his birthright. Let us imagine such a Shade standing once more upon the hill of San Miniato, overlooking the city from the south.

Clothed in the habit of his life, the Spirit wears his well-lined black silk *lucco*, the folds hanging in grave, unbroken lines from neck to ankle. A plain cloth cap, adorned with the *becchetto*—a long strip of drapery serving as a scarf in emergencies—surmounts a penetrating face. It is not conventionally handsome, yet it possesses a firm, well-cut mouth, kept distinctly human by a close-shaven lip and chin. This visage is charged with the memories of a keen and varied existence spent along the banks of the gleaming Arno. As he surveys the scene, the sense of familiarity overwhelms the perception of change; he feels he could descend instantly into the streets and resume the busy life he left behind.

He recognizes the mountains and the westward-bending river, the dark flank of Mount Morello opposite him, and the long valley stretching toward the ridges of Carrara. He identifies the steep height of Fiesole, crowned with monastic walls and cypresses, and the green-grey slopes dotted with villas he knows by name. Though he notes the absence of the seventy or more towers that once circled the city like a regal diadem, his gaze refuses to dwell on that blank. Instead, he is irresistibly drawn to the unique tower springing from the square, turreted mass of the Old Palace in the city's heart—the tower that looks no worse for the four centuries elapsed since he walked beneath it. The great dome, once merely a daring thought in the mind of a small, quick-eyed man in his boyhood, rises in massive curves, eclipsing the hills. Giotto's campanile hints at rich color in the distance, joined by the graceful spire of the Badia, structures he viewed from the shoulder of his nurse.

"Surely," he muses, "Florence can still ring her bells with the solemn hammer-sound that once beat on the hearts of her citizens, striking out the fire within. And here, to the right, stands the long dark mass of Santa Croce, where we laid the laurel on the brows of our famous dead, fanning them with the breath of praise and banners." He recalls that Santa Croce lacked a spire in his day; the Florentines were too consumed by grand building projects to execute them all in stone and marble. Their resources were diverted to frescoes, shrines, and the demands of rapacious condottieri, bribed royalty, and purchased territories. Consequently, their façades and spires had to wait. Yet, he wonders: what architect did the Frati Minori employ to construct that spire? If it had been built in his era, Filippo Brunelleschi or Michelozzo would have devised something entirely different—something worthy to crown the church of Arnolfo.

With a sigh, the Spirit turns his attention to the city walls, marveling at the changes wrought by modern times. Five of the eleven convenient gates have been sealed shut. Most jarringly, the towers that once served as both glory and defense have been leveled. Has the world become so pacified, have the Florentines achieved such harmony, that conspiracies to bring ambitious exiles home with armed bands are no longer a threat? These are difficult questions; it is far easier and pleasanter to recognize the old than to account for the new.

Yet, the Arno flows on, its bridges remaining exactly where they were. The Ponte Vecchio, unlike any other bridge, remains laden with its quaint shops. The Spirit remembers lingering there, perhaps to inspect the progress of the great palace Messer Luca Pitti was constructing with massive stones quarried from the Hill of Bogoli (now Boboli), or to conduct business with the cloth-dressers in Oltrarno. While the exorbitant line of the Pitti roof is hidden from San Miniato, the old Florentine's yearning is not to behold Messer Luca's overly ambitious private edifice. Rather, he longs to be down among the narrow streets and the busy, humming piazzae where he inherited the eager life of his fathers. Does the anxious voting with black and white beans still occur there? Who holds the office of Priori in these months, dining on soberly regulated meals of tripe and boiled partridge in the Palazzo Vecchio, seasoned by practical jokes aimed at the unfortunate butt among the potent signors? Do the significant banners still hang from the windows, distributed with decent pomp under Orcagna's Loggia every two months?

Life possessed a distinct zest for the old Florentine when he trod those marble steps and shared in such dignities. His politics spanned as wide an area as his trade, reaching from Syria to Britain, yet they retained a passionate intensity and detailed practical interest belonging only to a narrow scene of corporate action. Only members of a community shut in by hills and walls of six miles' circuit could possess such intimacy; men who knew one another as they passed in the street, who set their eyes daily on the memorials of their commonwealth, and who were conscious not merely of the right to vote, but of the chance of being voted for. He loved his honors and his gains—the business of his counting-house, his guild, and the public council chamber. He loved his enmities as well, fingering the white bean intended to keep a hated name out of the *borsa* with more complacency than if it had been a golden florin. He cherished strengthening his family through advantageous alliances, returning home with a triumphant light in his eyes after securing a satisfactory marriage for his son or daughter under his favorite loggia in the evening cool. He relished his games of chess under that same loggia, his biting jests, and even his coarse jokes, considering them beneath no dignity of a man eligible for the highest magistracy.

His insights extended to affairs at home and abroad. He had served in the "Ten" managing the war department, the "Eight" attending to domestic discipline, and as a Prior or Signore heading the executive government. He had even ascended to the supreme office of Gonfaloniere. He had participated in embassies to the Pope and the Venetians and served as commissary to the Republic's hired army, directing inglorious, bloodless battles where no man died of brave breast wounds—*virtuosi colpi*—but only of casual falls and tramplings. Through these experiences, he learned to distrust men without bitterness, viewing life primarily as a game of skill, yet never dead to traditions of heroism and clean-handed honor. The human soul is hospitable, entertaining conflicting sentiments and contradictory opinions with remarkable impartiality. Furthermore, he took pride in being duly tinctured with the learning of his age, judging not with the vulgar but in harmony with the ancients. In his prime, he had eagerly sought the most correct manuscripts and paid many florins for antique vases and disinterred busts of the immortal ancients—some, perhaps, *truncis naribus*, lacking noses, yet no less authentic. In his old age, he hastened to view the first sheets of that fine Homer among the early glories of the Florentine press.

Nevertheless, he had not neglected to hang a waxen image or double of himself under the protection of the Madonna Annunziata, nor to perform penance through large gifts to the shrines of saints whose lives were not modeled on classical studies. He had not even failed to make liberal bequests toward buildings for the Frati, against whom he had leveled many a jest.

For the Unseen Powers were mighty. Who knew—or was truly sure—that there existed any name behind which there was no angry force to be appeased, no intercessory pity to be won? Were not gems medicinal, merely pressing the finger? Were not all things charged with occult virtues? Lucretius might be right—an ancient and great poet—but Luigi Pulci, suspected of disbelief from the roof upward (*dal tetto in su*), appeared equally correct over the supper table when wine and jests circulated freely, though he was merely a poet in the vulgar tongue. Even learned personages maintained that Aristotle, the wisest of men (or perhaps Plato?), was a thoroughly irreligious philosopher; a liberal scholar must entertain all speculations. Yet, the negatives might ultimately prove false. Indeed, they seemed manifestly false as the circling hours swept past him, turning with graver faces. Had not the world become Christian?
