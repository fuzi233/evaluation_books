"He cast me upon some naked shore,
Where I may track
Only the print of some sad wreck,
If thou be there though the seas roar,
I shall no gentler calm implore."

Gone. The house stood silent against the evening, the doors locked, the windows shuttered against the fading light. Outside, the sky had lost its deep blues and crimsons, yielding to a grey twilight. Inside, Margaret ascended to prepare for the early tea, finding Dixon in a foul mood. The interruption of a visitor on a busy day had evidently rankled, and he brushed away her hair with vicious, impatient strokes under the guise of haste to visit Mrs. Hale. Yet, despite his urgency, Margaret found herself waiting a long, interminable time in the drawing-room before her mother descended. Alone by the fire, surrounded by unlit candles on the table behind her, she replayed the day: the joyous walk, the pleasant sketching, the cheerful dinner, and finally, that terrible, miserable stroll through the garden.

"How different men are to women!" she thought, a bitter chill settling within her. She was distressed, her instincts screaming that refusal was impossible, leaving her shaken and unhappy. He, conversely, mere minutes after receiving what ought to have been the deepest, holiest rejection of his life, spoke as if briefs, success, and the superficial trappings of a good house and clever company were the sole objectives of his desire. *Oh dear!* How she could have loved him if he had been different, if only he lacked this specific flaw that she now sensed ran deep down into his soul. Perhaps, she reasoned, his lightness was merely a mask covering a bitterness of disappointment, a wound that would have branded her own heart if she had loved and failed.

Her mother entered before Margaret could untangle this whirlwind of speculation. She had to shake off the heavy recollections of the day's events to become a sympathetic listener to Dixon's complaints about a burnt ironing blanket and Susan Lightfoot's artificial flowers, evidence of vanity and giddiness. Mr. Hale sipped his tea in abstracted silence; Margaret carried the weight of the conversation alone. She marveled at how her parents could be so forgetful, so careless of their companion throughout the day, failing to mention his name. For a fleeting moment, she forgot that he had not made her an offer.

After tea, Mr. Hale rose, leaning his elbow on the chimney-piece, his head resting on his hand. He murmured deeply, sighing with a frequency that betrayed profound turmoil. Mrs. Hale stepped out to consult with Dixon regarding winter clothing for the poor. Margaret prepared her mother's worsted work, shrinking from the prospect of a long, empty evening and wishing desperately for bedtime, hoping to revisit the day's events in solitude.

"Margaret!" Mr. Hale called out, suddenly, in a voice that sounded desperate, causing her to start. "Is that tapestry of immediate consequence? Can you leave it and come into my study? I wish to speak to you about something very serious to us all."

*Serious to us all,* she thought. Mr. Lennox had never had the chance for private conversation with her father since her refusal, or else matters would indeed be grave. First, she felt guilty, ashamed of growing into a woman enough to be courted. Second, she feared her father might be displeased that she had independently declined Mr. Lennox's proposal. But soon, logic settled in; nothing recent and sudden could require such complicated thoughts. He gestured for her to sit by him, stirring the fire, snuffing candles, and sighing twice before he could summon the courage to speak. Finally, the words came out with a jarring abruptness: "Margaret! I am going to leave Helstone."

"Leave Helstone, Papa! But why?"

For a minute or two, Mr. Hale said nothing. He fumbled with papers on the table, opening his lips several times only to close them again, lacking the fortitude to utter a syllable. Margaret could not endure the suspense, which gnawed at her father as much as it tore at her heart.

"But why, dear Papa? Do tell me!"

He looked up at her suddenly, then spoke with a slow, forced calmness. "Because I must no longer be a minister in the Church of England."

Margaret had imagined nothing less than the fulfillment of her mother's desires: a preferment forcing him from beautiful Helstone to some stately, silent Close in a cathedral town. Those places were grand, but leaving Helstone forever would have been a lingering, sad pain. Yet, the shock of his actual words was far greater. What could he mean? It was worse for being so mysterious. The piteous distress on his face, almost imploring a merciful judgment from his child, sent a wave of sickness through her. Could he be implicated in anything Frederick had done? Frederick was an outlaw. Had her father, driven by a natural love for his son, connived at any transgression?

"Oh! What is it? Speak, Papa! Tell me all! Why can you no longer be a clergyman? Surely, if the Bishop were told all we know about Frederick, and the hard injustice—"

"It is nothing about Frederick; the Bishop would have nothing to do with that. It is all myself. Margaret, I will tell you about it. I will answer any question this once, but after tonight, let us never speak of it again. I can meet the consequences of my painful, miserable doubts; but it is an effort beyond me to speak of what has caused me so much suffering."

"Doubts, Papa! Doubts as to religion?" Margaret asked, more shocked than ever.

"No! Not doubts as to religion; not the slightest injury to that."

He paused. Margaret sighed, feeling as if she stood on the verge of some new horror. He began again, speaking rapidly, as if rushing to complete a daunting task:

"You could not understand it all if I told you—the anxiety I have harbored for years past, knowing whether I had any right to hold my living; my efforts to quench my smoldering doubts by the authority of the Church. Oh! Margaret, how I love the holy Church from which I am to be shut out!" He could not continue for a moment or two. Margaret could not speak; it seemed to her as terrifying and mysterious as if her father were about to turn Mahometan.

"I have been reading today of the two thousand who were ejected from their churches," continued Mr. Hale, with a faint smile, "trying to steal some of their bravery; but it is of no use—no use—I cannot help feeling it acutely."

"But, Papa, have you well considered? Oh! It seems so terrible, so shocking!" Margaret suddenly burst into tears. The one staid foundation of her home, of her cherished image of her beloved father, seemed to reel and rock. What could she say? What was to be done? The sight of her distress spurred Mr. Hale to steel himself, attempting to comfort her. He swallowed down the dry, choking sobs that had been heaving up from his heart, went to his bookcase, and retrieved a volume he had recently read, believing it to have given him strength for this course.

"Listen, dear Margaret," he said, wrapping one arm around her waist. She took his hand in hers, gripping it tightly, but she could not lift her head; nor could she attend to what he read, so great was her internal agitation.

"This is the soliloquy of one who was once a clergyman in a country parish, like me; it was written by Mr. Oldfield, minister of Carsington, in Derbyshire, a hundred and sixty years ago, or more. His trials are over. He fought the good fight." These final two sentences he whispered, as if to himself. Then he read aloud:

"When thou canst no longer continue in thy work without dishonour to God, discredit to religion, foregoing thy integrity, wounding conscience, spoiling thy peace, and hazarding the loss of thy salvation; in a word, when the conditions upon which thou must continue (if thou wilt continue) in thy employments are sinful, and unwarranted by the word of God, thou mayest, yea, thou must believe that God will turn thy very silence, suspension, deprivation, and laying aside, to His glory, and the advancement of the Gospel's interest. When God will not use thee in one kind, yet He will in another. A soul that desires to serve and honour Him shall never want opportunity to do it; nor must thou so limit the Holy One of Israel, as to think He hath but one way in which He can glorify Himself by thee. He can do it by thy silence as well as by thy preaching; the laying aside as well as thy continuance in thy work. It is not pretence of doing God the greatest service, or performing the weightiest duty, that will excuse the least sin, though that sin capacitated or gave us the opportunity for doing that duty. Thou wilt have little thanks, O my soul! if, when thou art charged with corrupting God's worship, falsifying thy vows, thou pretendest a necessity for it in order to a continuance in the ministry."

As he read, glancing at much more which he chose not to utter, he gained resolution for himself, feeling as if he too could be brave and firm in doing what he believed to be right. But as he ceased, he heard Margaret's low, convulsive sob, and his courage sank beneath the keen sense of suffering
