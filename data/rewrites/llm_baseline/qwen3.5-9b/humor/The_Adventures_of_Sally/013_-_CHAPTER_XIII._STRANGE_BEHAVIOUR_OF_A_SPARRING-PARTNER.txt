Sally sat in her apartment on the morning of her return to New York, wrestling with emotions akin to a swimmer hesitating on a freezing dawn before plunging into a chilly pool. Her muscles ached, yet she knew she had fought well. Happiness required combat, and for months she had been dodging the battle. Now, she was done with hovering at the edge; she stood in the deep water, ready for whatever struck. It hurt—exactly as she'd feared—but it was a stimulating sting, not a dull melancholy. She felt alive and defiant.

With packing complete, her next move was clear: see Ginger. Suddenly, the need to see him was overwhelming. His steady, stolid friendliness would be her anchor. She regretted not sending a telegram so he could greet her at the docks. The journey back had been dreadful; the echoing customs sheds had drained her courage, leaving her feeling utterly alone.

Glancing at her watch, she was shocked by how early it was. She could catch him at the office and force him to take her to lunch. Donning her hat, she stepped out into the city.

New York's relentless engine of change had already churned over the outer offices of Fillmore Nicholas Theatrical Enterprises Ltd. during her absence. When she arrived, she wasn't greeted by the pimpled, genial clerk she'd known before, but by a brand-new stripling. While he shared the predecessor's generous crop of pimples, his demeanor was worlds apart. This was a grim-faced boy, stern and suspicious. He eyed Sally narrowly, as if catching her stealing the office blotting-paper, then demanded her business with sharp acidity.

"I want Mr. Kemp," Sally said.

The office boy scratched his cheek dourly with a ruler. No one would have suspected, given his austere expression, that seconds prior he had been attempting to balance the ruler on his chin while juggling two paper weights. Impervious to human frailty as he appeared, his secret ambition was to enter vaudeville.

"What name?" he asked coldly.

"Nicholas," Sally replied. "I am Mr. Nicholas' sister."

Previously, this declaration had led to disaster, but today it worked perfectly. It struck the boy like a bullet. He convulsed, dropped the ruler, and scrambled to recover it. In that brief moment of stooping, he pulled himself together. He hadn't cared about her name; he'd wanted the name of the person she sought. Realizing he'd stumbled onto a stroke of luck—a visit from the boss's sister—he instantly switched tactics. He returned upright, wearing a smile so winning it was like the sun breaking through a London fog.

"Will you take a seat, lady?" he asked, polishing his coat sleeve to dust hers. He added that the morning was fine.

"Thank you," Sally said. "Tell him I'm here."

"Mr. Nicholas is out, miss," the boy said with regretful gentility. "He's back in New York, but he's gone out."

"I don't want Mr. Nicholas. I want Mr. Kemp."

"Mr. Kemp?"

"Yes, Mr. Kemp."

Sorrow washed over the boy's face.

"Don't know of anyone of that name around here," he apologized.

"But surely..." Sally trailed off. A grim premonition seized her. "How long have you been here?"

"All day, ma'am," he answered with the cool detachment of a Casablanca resident.

"I mean, how long have you been *employed* here?"

"Just over a month, miss."

"Hasn't Mr. Kemp been in the office all that time?"

"Name's new to me, lady. Does he look like anything? I mean, what's he look like?"

"He has very red hair."

"Never seen him in here," the boy stated flatly. The truth hit Sally cold. She blamed herself for leaving. She should have known. Left to his own devices, Ginger had once again made a mess of things. And this mess must be unprecedented; surely Fillmore wouldn't have dismissed someone under her special protection so easily.

"Where is Mr. Nicholas?" she asked, realizing Fillmore was her only lead. "Did you say he was out?"

"Really out, miss," the boy confirmed with engaging honesty. "He drove off to White Plains in his car half an hour ago."

"White Plains? Why?"

The pimpled stripling now surrendered completely to social chatter. Usually, he preferred solitude to practice his juggling, but Sally's charm had melted his defenses. He delighted in conversing as long as she wished.

"I guess what's happened is, he's gone up to check on Bugs Butler," he explained.

"Whose butler?" Sally asked, bewildered.

The boy smiled tolerantly, assuming women weren't "hep" to the finer points of life.

"Bugs Butler is training up at White Plains, miss."

"Who is Bugs Butler?"

Something of his earlier gloom returned. Sally's question touched a nerve.

"Ah!" he exclaimed, dropping his deferential mask. "Who is he! That's what everyone's saying. Who has Bugs Butler ever licked?"

"I don't know," Sally admitted, sensing the trap.

"Nor nobody else," the boy insisted vehemently. "Just a bunch of stiff names from the coast. Except Cyclone Mullins, where it took a fake-out fifteen-round fight to get a referee's decision. The boss wants to give him a shot at the champ, but I'd tell him the real contender is K-leg Binns. K-leg knocked Cyclone out in the fifth. Well," he sighed dramatically, "if anyone thinks Bugs Butler can last six rounds with Lew Lucas, I'll bet my two dollars in my vest pocket that he won't."

Sally began to see the light.

"Oh, Bugs—Mr. Butler—is one of the boxers in the fight my brother is involved in?"

"That's right. He's fighting the lightweight champ. Lew Lucas. He's a bird!"

"Yes?" Sally asked. The boy had a habit of cocking his head expectantly.

"Yes, sir!" he emphasized. "Lew Lucas is a hot sketch. He lived right next door to me. I've seen his old mother closer than I am to you. Say, I've seen her a hundred times. Is any stiff like Bugs Butler going to beat a guy like that?"

"It doesn't seem likely."

"You spoke it!" the lad cried, striking futilely at a fly on the blotting paper.

After a pause, Sally began to rise.

"And there's another thing," the boy added, reluctant to end the topic. "Can Bugs Butler weigh in at one hundred and thirty-five pounds without being weak?"

"It sounds awfully difficult."

"They say he's clever," the expert laughed sarcastically. "But what's that worth? The poor fish can't punch a hole in a nut sundae."

"You don't seem to like Mr. Butler."

"Oh, I've nothing against him," the boy declared magnanimously. "I'm just saying he's no license to mix it with Lew Lucas."

Sally stood up. While absorbing this commentary on current form, more urgent matters called.

"How will I find my brother when I get to White Plains?" she asked.

"Oh, anyone will show you the way to the camp. If you hurry, there's a train you can catch now."

"Thank you very much."

"You're welcome."

He opened the door with an old-fashioned politeness that rusted slightly from disuse. Then, as if returning to business after a frivolous interlude, he picked up the paper weights again and carefully balanced the ruler on his chin.

Fillmore heaved a sigh of relief and slipped toward the exit. The room was vast, half barn, half gymnasium, filled with athletic gear hanging on the walls. In the center, a roped-off ring surrounded by an expectant crowd buzzed with anticipation. In this commercial age, a champion's training was fair game for the public; if you had fifty cents, you could stare your fill. Today, about forty plutocrats had gathered, though not all had paid admission. Many were press representatives eager to polish Bugs Butler's crude predictions about dismantling Lew Lucas, or artists ready to draw him in semi-nudity with impossibly large feet.

Fillmore's relief stemmed from Mr. Lester Burrowes, Bugs' manager, finally distracting himself to handle other business, leaving Fillmore free to escape to the hotel for a desperately needed meal. His enthusiasm for inspecting the final day of preparation (the fight was tomorrow) had caused him to skip lunch entirely since leaving New York.

As Fillmore reached the door, he collided with Sally. He was looking over his shoulder and didn't hear her until she spoke.

"Hallo, Fillmore!"

Her voice was soft, yet it shattered his composure like a dynamite blast. In a leaping twist, he rose three inches off the floor. His nervous system felt as if it had been prodded with a pole. He gasped for breath, moistening his lips, staring at her unblinkingly.
