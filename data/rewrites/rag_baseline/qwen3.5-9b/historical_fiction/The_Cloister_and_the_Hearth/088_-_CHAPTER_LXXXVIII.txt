Supper-time descended upon the household, casting a warm, domestic glow over the gathered family. The board was laden, the air thick with the savory perfume of cooking meats, yet a profound silence hung over the scene. Margaret was absent. To Catherine's astonishment, Eli declared he would wait for her return.

"Why, I told her you would not wait for the duke," someone remarked, the irony sharp in the quiet room.

"She is not the duke," Eli replied, his voice steady despite the tension coiling in his chest. "She is a poor, good lass who has waited not minutes, but years, for a graceless son of mine. You may set the meat upon the board; we shall eat without further loss of time once she arrives."

The aroma of the smoking dishes proved irresistible. Relenting to the sensory pull of hunger, Eli gestured. "She will come if we begin," he insisted. "They always do." He motioned for Mistress Joan to take her seat. "You are not here as a slave, I trust, but as a guest." Just then, a quick, urgent step echoed outside, signaling arrival. "There, I hear her," Eli announced, pulling out his chair. "Sit, and fall to."

The covers were drawn back, and the knives were raised in anticipation of the feast. But the figure that burst through the doorway was neither the beloved daughter nor the feared Duke. Instead, a Dominican friar stood framed in the light, his face a mask of livid rage. In a single, fluid motion, he crossed the threshold and slammed himself against the narrow table, positioning his towering form directly between Cornelis and Sybrandt. With both hands hovering inches above their shrinking heads, like raptors circling helpless prey, he unleashed a torrent of invective.

It was an era eloquent in its cursing, and this particular diatribe was so dense, so minutely detailed, so blighting and terrible that the weight of it threatens to crush the page itself.

"Cursed be the lips," he shrieked, his voice cracking with supernatural intensity, "that spoke the lie declaring Margaret dead! May they rot before the grave and kiss white-hot iron in hell thereafter! Doubly cursed be the hands that forged those fraudulent letters; may they be severed by the hangman's blade and forced to handle the fires of damnation forever! Thrice accursed be the cruel hearts that conceived this damnable falsehood, designed to sever true love eternally! May they sicken and wither on earth, joyless, loveless, and hopeless, reduced to dust before their natural time, consumed by eternal flame!"

His wrath expanded, consuming everything. He cursed the very meat awaiting their consumption and every atom of their physical being, from the crown of their hair to the soles of their feet. Turning then to the cowering pair, who had nearly hidden themselves beneath the tablecloth, he reached into his bosom, tore forth a letter, and hurled it down before his father.

"Read this, thou hard old man who imprisoned thy son!" he commanded, his voice trembling with righteous fury. "Read, and behold the monsters thou hast spawned into the world! The memory of my wrongs and hers shall dwell with you all forever! I shall meet you again at the Judgment Day; on this earth, thou shalt never see me more."

With the speed of a ghost, he vanished as abruptly as he had arrived, leaving behind a tableau of stunned horror. The family sat rigid, cold, and pale as statues encircling the untouched, steaming meal.

Then, the door opened again. Margaret entered, her eyes meeting the scene of petrified men and women surrounding the unbroken feast. She gasped, relief washing over her features. "Oh, joy! All are here; no blood hath been shed. Oh, you cruel, cruel men! I thank God he hath not slain you."

At the sight of her, Catherine emitted an eloquent scream before turning her head away, unable to bear the emotional surge. But Eli, having just scanned the false letter and beginning to grasp the magnitude of the deception, saw the other victim enter at that precise moment, her own sorrows mirrored in her sweet, pale countenance. A transport of rage seized him. He sprang to his feet, shouting, "Stand clear! Let me reach these traitors! I will hang for them!"

In an instant, he drew his short sword and lunged forward.

"Flee!" screamed Margaret. "Flee!"

They scrambled, howling, slipping under the table and crawling out the opposite side. Yet, before they could reach the door, the furious old man swept around, intercepting their path. Catherine merely screamed and wrung her hands—a futile gesture, for notable figures are often paralyzed at such critical junctures. Blood would surely have flowed had not Margaret and Jorian seized the elderly man's arms, holding him fast with all their might until the fugitives escaped the house. Only then did they release him, and he went vainly raging after them into the street.

They were a furlong distant, fleeing like hares. Inside, the old man hacked down the board bearing their names, dragged it indoors, and flung it into the fireplace. Catherine sat rocking herself, her apron pulled over her head in despair. Joan had rushed to her husband. Margaret embraced Catherine, her arms tight around the grieving woman's neck, pale and panting yet striving to offer comfort.

But it was impossible. "Oh, my poor children!" Catherine wailed. "Oh, miserable mother! 'Tis a mercy Kate was ill upstairs. There, I have lived to thank God for that!" she cried, bursting anew into sobs. "It would have killed her. He had better have stayed in Italy, coming home only to curse his own flesh and blood and set us all by the ears."

"Oh, hold your chatter, woman," Eli snapped angrily. "You remain on the side of the wrongdoer. You are cheaply served; your weakness made these rogues what they are. I intended to correct them in their youth; for sore ills, sharp remedies. But you sided with their faults and undermined me, baffling wise severity." He turned to Margaret. "And you, Margaret, cease comforting her when you ought to comfort yourself. What is her hurt compared to yours? But she never possessed a grain of justice under her skin, and never will. So come to me, that am thy father henceforth."

It was a command. Margaret kissed Catherine goodbye and tottered toward him. He placed her on a chair beside him, and she rested her feeble head upon his honest breast, though not a tear fell—it was sorrow too deep for tears. "Poor lamb," he murmured.

After a pause, true Eli spoke in a broken voice to Jorian and Joan. "Come, good folks, we are in a little trouble, as you see; but that is no reason you should starve. For our Lady's sake, eat. Add not to my grief the reputation of a churl. What the dickens!" he added, attempting a sudden, ghastly display of stout-heartedness. "The more knaves I have the luck to rid myself of, the greater my need of true men and women to help me clear the dish and cheer my eye with honest faces where else there are gaps. Eat, I entreat you."

Catherine, sobbing, backed his request. Poor, simple, antique, hospitable souls! Jorian, whose appetite, especially since his illness, was keen, prepared to act on this invitation. But Joan whispered a word in his ear, and he instantly recoiled. "Nay, I'll touch no meat that Holy Church hath cursed."

"In sooth, I forgot," Eli apologized humbly. "My son, who was reared at my table, hath cursed my victuals. That seems strange. Well, what God wills, man must bow to."

The supper was carried out into the yard. Heavy sadness reigned in Eli's house that night.

Meanwhile, where was Clement? He lay stretched full-length upon the floor of the convent church, his lips pressed against the lowest step of the altar. He existed in an indescribable state of terror, misery, penitence, and self-abasement, through which struggled faint gleams of joy that Margaret was alive. Night fell, finding him there weeping and praying; morning would have found him there too. But suddenly, he remembered that, absorbed in his own wrongs and Margaret's, he had committed another sin besides intemperate rage: he had neglected a dying man.

He rose instantly, groaning at his accumulated wickedness, determined to repair the omission. The weather had shifted; rain poured down heavily. As he cleared the town, he heard wolves bay—they were on his trail. Yet Clement was himself again, or nearly so. He thought little of danger or discomfort, driven solely by the shameful omission of religious duty he sought to rectify. He moved stoutly forward through the rain and darkness. As he walked, he often beat his breast and cried, "Mea culpa! Mea culpa!"
