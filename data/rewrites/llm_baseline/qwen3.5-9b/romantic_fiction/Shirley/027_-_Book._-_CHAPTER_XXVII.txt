Miss Keeldar and her uncle were a study in contrasts that had never yielded harmony. He was irritable; she, spirited. He ruled with a despotic hand; she craved absolute freedom. He was thoroughly worldly; she, perhaps, retained a spark of romance.

Mr. Sympson had descended to Yorkshire with a singular purpose: to secure a suitable marriage for his niece. His plan was clear—he intended to find her a proper husband, install her safely within his grasp, and finally wash his hands of her forever. Unfortunately, from infancy onward, Shirley and her guardian had fundamentally disagreed on the definitions of "suitable" and "proper." While Mr. Sympson held rigid standards, Shirley had never accepted his version, and it remained uncertain whether she would ever consent to his definition during the most critical decision of her life.

The test arrived swiftly.

Mr. Sympson formally proposed his son, Samuel Fawthrop Wynne.

"Decidedly suitable! Most proper!" declared Mr. Sympson, convinced. "A substantial estate, impeccable connections, real fortune. It must be done!"

Summoning his niece to the oak-paneled parlour, he locked the door, presented the proposal, voiced his approval, and demanded her consent.

It was flatly denied.

"No," Shirley replied firmly. "I shall not marry Samuel Fawthrop Wynne."

"Why?" Mr. Sympson demanded, requiring a justification. "In every respect, he is more than worthy of you."

Shirley stood by the hearth, pale as the white marble slab behind her, her eyes flashing with a large, dilated intensity.

"And I ask," she countered, "in what sense is that young man worthy of *me*?"

"He possesses twice your fortune, twice your common sense, equal connections, and equal respectability."

"Even if he held my money fifty times over, I would take no vow to love him."

"State your objections clearly."

"He has lived a course of despicable, commonplace profligacy. That alone sinks him in a gulf of immeasurable inferiority. Furthermore, his intellect fails to reach any standard I esteem. His views are narrow, his feelings blunt, his tastes coarse, and his manners vulgar."

"The man is a respectable, wealthy gentleman! To refuse him is sheer presumption."

"I refuse point-blank! Cease to annoy me with this subject; I forbid it!"

"Then, is it your intention to remain unmarried? Do you prefer celibacy?"

"I deny your right to claim an answer to that question."

"May I inquire if you await a man of title—a Peer of the Realm—to demand your hand?"

"I doubt if there is a peer breathing whom I would honor with my affection."

"Then you must be mad. Your eccentricity and conceit border on frenzy."

"Perhaps, before I finish, you will realize I have leaped over it."

"I anticipate no less. You are frantic and impracticable! Take warning! Dare not sully our name with a *mésalliance*!"

"Our name! Am I called Sympson?"

"God be thanked that you are not! But beware; I will not be trifled with!"

"What, in the name of common law and common sense, would you—or could you—do if my choice led me to someone you disapprove?"

"Take care! Take care!" he warned, his voice and hand trembling with agitation.

"Why? What shadow of power do you hold over me? Why should I fear you?"

"Take care, madam!"

"Scrupulous care I shall take, Mr. Sympson. Before I marry, I am resolved to esteem, to admire, and to *love*."

"Preposterous! Indecorous! Unwomanly!"

"To love with my whole heart. I may speak in an unknown tongue, but I am indifferent to whether I am understood."

"And if this love falls upon a beggar?"

"On a beggar, it will never fall. Mendicancy is not estimable."

"On a low clerk, a play-actor, a playwright..."

"Take courage, Mr. Sympson! Or what?"

"A literary scrub, or a shabby, whining artist."

"For the scruffy, shabby, whining type, I have no taste. For literature and the arts, I have plenty. And therein lies my wonder regarding your Fawthrop Wynne. He cannot write a note without orthographic errors; he reads only sporting papers; he was the booby of Stilbro' Grammar School!"

"Unladylike language! Great God! Where does this end?" He threw up his hands and eyes in despair.

"Never to the altar of Hymen with Sam Wynne."

"To what end will she come? Why are the laws not more stringent so I might compel her to listen to reason?"

"Console yourself, Uncle. Even if Britain were a serfdom and you the Czar, you could not *force* me into this step. I shall write to Mr. Wynne myself. Give yourself no further trouble on the subject."

Fortune is proverbially changeful, yet her caprice often repeats the same stroke of luck in the same direction. By this time, Miss Keeldar—or rather, her fortune—had created a sensation in the district, generating impressions in quarters she had not foreseen. Following Mr. Wynne's rejection, no fewer than three other offers arrived, all varying degrees of eligibility. Each was pressed upon her by her uncle, and each was subsequently refused. Amongst them were several gentlemen of unexceptionable character and ample wealth. Neighbors and relatives alike wondered what she meant, asking who she hoped to entrap with such insolent fastidiousness.

Eventually, the gossipers believed they had unlocked the key to her conduct, and her uncle was certain of it. More importantly, this discovery cast his niece in a completely new light, prompting him to alter his entire demeanor toward her.

Fieldhead had recently grown too hot to contain them both. The suave aunt could not reconcile their differences; the daughters froze at the sight of their quarrels. Gertrude and Isabella spent hours whispering in their dressing room, becoming chilled with decorous dread whenever left alone with their audacious cousin. However, a change soon supervened. Mr. Sympson was appeased, and the household returned to tranquility.

The village of Nunnely, known for its ancient church, forests, and monastic ruins, also boasted a hall called the Priory—an older, larger, and more lordly residence than any owned by Briarfield or Whinbury. Moreover, it possessed a Baronet, a title neither neighboring estate could claim. This possession, its proudest asset, had for years been merely nominal. The current Baronet, a young man residing in a distant province, was virtually unknown on his Yorkshire estate.

During Miss Keeldar's stay at the fashionable resort of Cliffbridge, she and her companions met and were introduced to Sir Philip Nunnely. They encountered him repeatedly on the sands, the cliffs, and in various walks, occasionally at public balls. He appeared solitary. His manner was remarkably unpretentious—too simple to be called affable, more timid than proud. He did not condescend to their company; rather, he seemed genuinely glad to be included.

With any unaffected individual, Shirley could quickly cement an acquaintance. She walked and talked with Sir Philip; she, her aunt, and her cousins sometimes sailed in his yacht. She liked him for his kindness and modesty, charmed by the power she felt she had to amuse him.

There was one slight drawback, inevitable in any friendship: Sir Philip possessed a literary turn. He wrote poetry—sonnets, stanzas, ballads. Perhaps Miss Keeldar found him slightly too fond of reciting these compositions; perhaps she wished the rhymes possessed greater accuracy, the meter more music, and the inspiration more fire. Regardless, she always winced when he reverted to the topic of his poems and usually diverted the conversation elsewhere.

He would coax her onto moonlit walks across the bridge solely to pour the longest of his ballads into her ears. He would lead her to secluded rustic seats where the sound of the surf against the sands was soft and soothing. When he had her entirely to himself, with the sea before them and the scented shade of gardens surrounding them, he would produce his latest batch of sonnets and read them in a voice tremulous with emotion. He seemed unaware that while they contained rhyme, they lacked true poetry. From Shirley's downcast eyes and disturbed expression, it was evident she perceived this flaw and felt heartily mortified by this single foible of such a good and amiable gentleman.

She often tried, as gently as possible, to wean him from this fanatical worship of the Muses. It was his monomania; on all ordinary subjects, he was sensible enough, and she eagerly engaged him in mundane topics. He questioned her about his estate at Nunnely, and she was too happy to answer his inquiries at length. She never tired of describing the antique priory, the wild woodland park, the hoary church and hamlet, nor did she fail to counsel him to descend and gather his tenants around him in his ancestral halls.

To her surprise, Sir Philip followed her advice to the letter. Towards the close of September, he arrived at the Priory.
