The legal sanctuaries of London—those ancient inns of court where barristers congregate like hermit crabs in venerable shells—cling stubbornly to the customs of bygone days, as if time itself had taken the bench and ruled against progress. While modern prisons boast ventilation and workhouses scrub their floors with zeal, the Temple remains a glorious relic of medieval discomfort. Here, men pay a small fortune to sleep in chambers barely fit for a scullery maid, where the only running water arrives via a bedmaker’s grudging jug.

Consider old Grump of the Norfolk Circuit, a man who had dwelled beneath Warrington and Pendennis for three decades, cursing the innovative shower-baths that dripped through his ceiling like an indoor weather system. Grump, who hadn’t voluntarily touched water since the reign of George III, declared the practice a dandy’s frivolity. And who could blame him? His forefathers had thrived in glorious filth—why break tradition now?

Yet for all its archaic inconveniences, the Temple holds a peculiar charm. Walk its halls, and you tread the same stones as legal titans of yore—Coke, Eldon, Chitty—ghosts who whisper precedent from the shadows. The place teems with life of every variety: the penniless barrister gnawing his nails in a garret one year, dining in Belgravia the next; the aged scholar hoarding books and solitude like a miser; the relentless workhorse Paley, who traded poetry for pleadings and never looked back.

And then there was Pen.

Young Arthur Pendennis had arrived with vague ambitions of legal grandeur but found himself far more enchanted by the Temple’s social symphony. His studies were punctuated by strolls in the park, rowing excursions, and evenings of modest revelry—always with a pipe in hand and Warrington, his newfound comrade, at his side. Warrington, a baronet’s son with a taste for shilling taverns and a disdain for pretension, was the perfect antidote to Pen’s occasional bouts of vanity.

Dining in Hall became a spectacle in itself. Pen, seated beside the opportunistic Lowton—a man who eyed the benchers’ leftovers with the intensity of a starving bloodhound—observed the ecosystem of aspiring lawyers: the Irish braggarts, the dandies who never actually ate, the political orators, and the bookish drones murmuring case law into their soup.

“That’s Boosey the bencher,” Lowton whispered, pointing at a bald man attacking a bowl of dubious liquid. “Probably turtle. They *love* turtle.”

“Fascinating,” Pen replied, suppressing a laugh.

But the true highlight was Warrington’s arrival—an unkempt figure in a frayed gown who slid onto the bench beside Pen with the ease of an old friend. Their bond was instant, forged over college tales and shared port (Pen, abandoning his earlier pretensions, drank heartily). By night’s end, Pen had resolved to abandon his lonely lodgings and move into Lamb Court, where tobacco and camaraderie awaited.

Thus, our hero settled into the rhythms of Temple life—a world where ambition and idleness waltzed in equal measure, and where the only certainty was that someone, somewhere, would grumble about the plumbing.
