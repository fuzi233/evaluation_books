"My heart revolts within me, and two voices Make themselves audible within my bosom." — Wallenstein.

Upon returning home, Margaret discovered two letters waiting on the table. One was a brief note for her mother; the other, bearing fresh foreign postmarks, was clearly from Aunt Shaw. It was thin, silvery paper that rustled softly as she lifted it. She began to examine it when her father entered abruptly.

"So your mother is already tired and retired!" Mr. Hale exclaimed, his voice tinged with concern. "I feared such a thunderous day was hardly suitable for the doctor's visit. What did he say? Dixon mentioned he spoke to you regarding her condition."

Margaret hesitated. Her father's expression grew increasingly grave and anxious. "Does he not consider her seriously ill?"

"Not at present," Margaret replied cautiously. "He believes she requires careful nursing. He was very kind and promised to return later to assess how the prescribed remedies are taking effect."

"Only care?" Mr. Hale pressed, his tone sharpening. "Did he not suggest a change of air? Did he not imply that this smoky town is harming her health, Margaret?"

"No," she answered solemnly. "He said nothing of the sort."

"He seemed anxious, I thought," her father noted.

"Doctors often possess that anxious demeanor; it is merely professional," he dismissed lightly, though his eyes betrayed him. Margaret recognized the signs in her father's nervous agitation. Despite his attempts to minimize the situation, the initial shadow of dread had taken root in his mind. He could not dismiss the topic, unable to transition to other subjects. Throughout the evening, he returned to it repeatedly, refusing even the faintest suggestion of unfavorable news, leaving Margaret deeply sorrowful.

"This letter is from Aunt Shaw, Papa," Margaret interrupted gently. "She has arrived in Naples but found it too hot, so she has secured lodgings in Sorrento. However, I doubt she enjoys Italy."

"Did he mention anything regarding her diet?" her father asked immediately.

"It should be nourishing and easy to digest," Margaret explained. "Mamma's appetite seems reasonably good, I believe."

"Yes! And that makes it all the stranger that he considered diet at all," her father mused.

"I asked him, Papa."

Another pause stretched between them. Then Margaret continued, "Aunt Shaw mentions sending me some coral ornaments, Papa. But," she added with a tentative smile, "she fears the Milton dissenters may not appreciate them. Has she formed all her impressions of Dissenters solely from the Quakers?"

"If your mother expresses a wish for anything, ensure you inform me immediately. I am terrified she does not always tell me what she desires. Please, also attend to that young woman Mrs. Thornton mentioned. Had we a competent housekeeper, Dixon could accompany her constantly, and I guarantee we would quickly integrate her into our family if rest alone can heal her. She has been exhausted lately due to the heat and the scarcity of servants. A period of repose will surely restore her completely—will it not, Margaret?"

"I hope so," Margaret replied, but her sadness was palpable. Her father noticed instantly. Pinching her cheek affectionately, he said, "Come, if you appear so pale, I must apply a touch of rouge. Take care of yourself, child, or you'll require the doctor next."

Yet, he remained unsettled throughout the evening. He moved restlessly back and forth on the tips of his toes, peering into the bedroom to confirm whether his wife was still asleep. Margaret's heart ached witnessing his turmoil—his desperate attempt to suppress and suffocate the terrifying fear rising from the depths of his soul. Finally, he returned, slightly reassured.

"She is awake now, Margaret. She smiled warmly upon seeing me stand by her side. It was her old, familiar smile. She stated she feels refreshed and eager for tea. Where is the note for her? She wishes to see it. I shall read it aloud while you prepare the tea."

The note contained a formal invitation from Mrs. Thornton, extending an invitation to Mr., Mrs., and Miss Hale to dine on the twenty-first. Margaret was surprised to find an acceptance being considered, especially after learning of the grim possibilities earlier that day. Nevertheless, the prospect of her husband and daughter attending captivated Mrs. Hale's imagination, even before Margaret revealed the contents of the letter. It represented a welcome diversion from the monotony of her invalid confinement. Clinging to the idea with determined insistence, Mrs. Hale resisted any objections Margaret raised.

"Nonsense, Margaret," Mr. Hale insisted anxiously as he prepared to draft the acceptance the following morning. "If she desires it, I am certain we shall both go willingly. She would never express such a wish unless she genuinely believed herself stronger, truly better than we suspected, would she not, Margaret?"

"Eh, Margaret?" he queried, making a nervous gesture with his hands.

It seemed cruel to deny him the solace he craved. Moreover, his passionate denial of fear nearly inspired hope within Margaret herself.

"I do believe she is improving since last night," she said softly. "Her eyes appear brighter, and her complexion clearer."

"God bless you," her father said earnestly. "But is it true? Yesterday was so stiflingly hot that everyone felt unwell. It was a particularly unfortunate day for Mr. Donaldson to visit."

With that, he departed to his daily duties, which had recently expanded to include preparing lectures for a neighboring Lyceum. He had selected Ecclesiastical Architecture as his topic, aligning more closely with his personal tastes and expertise than with the specific interests of his audience or the nature of the venue. Given that the institution was heavily indebted, they were only too grateful to secure a free series of lectures from an educated and accomplished man like Mr. Hale, regardless of the subject matter.

"Well, Mother," Mr. Thornton asked that evening, "who have accepted your invitations for the twenty-first?"

"Fanny, where are the notes?" Mrs. Thornton called out. "The Slicksons accept, Collingbrooks accept, the Stephenses accept, and the Browns decline. The Hales—both father and daughter—are coming, though Mother is too ill to travel. The Macphersons accept, along with Mr. Horsfall and Mr. Young. I was considering inviting the Porters, as the Browns cannot come."

"Very well," Mr. Thornton replied. "Do you realize, Fanny, that I am genuinely concerned Mrs. Hale is far from well, based on what Dr. Donaldson reported?"

"It is peculiar of them to accept a dinner invitation if she is so severely ill," Fanny remarked.

"I did not say 'severely ill,'" her brother corrected sharply. "I merely said 'far from well.' Perhaps they are unaware of the extent of her condition."

Then, he suddenly recalled that, according to Dr. Donaldson's account, Margaret must certainly be cognizant of the precise state of affairs.

"Very likely they are fully aware of what you said yesterday, John—of the significant advantage it would offer them, specifically Mr. Hale, to be introduced to individuals such as the Stephenses and Collingbrooks."

"I am certain that motive would not influence them," Fanny declared confidently. "No! I believe I understand the situation now."

"John!" Fanny laughed in her characteristic weak, nervous manner. "How you claim to comprehend these Hales, and how you steadfastly refuse to acknowledge that we can understand anything about them. Are they truly so distinct from the majority of people one encounters?"

She did not intend to provoke him, yet if she had, she could scarcely have succeeded more effectively. He bristled in silence, refusing to respond to her query.

"They do not seem extraordinary to me," Mrs. Thornton observed. "He appears to be a decent, worthy man, albeit perhaps too simplistic for commerce—which explains why becoming a clergyman and subsequently a teacher suited him. She is somewhat of a fine lady, bolstered by her illness. As for the girl... she is the only one who perplexes me when I reflect upon her, which I rarely do. She seems possessed of a grandiose notion of elevating herself, and I cannot fathom why. I almost suspect she considers herself superior to her current companions at times. Yet, they are not wealthy; from everything I gather, they have never been affluent."

"And she lacks accomplishments, Mother," Fanny added. "She cannot play music."

"Proceed, Fanny. What further criteria do you demand to elevate her to your standards?"

"Nay, John," his mother interjected, "Fanny's remark caused no offense. I myself heard Miss Hale admit she could not play. If you would leave us in peace, we might eventually learn to appreciate her and recognize her virtues."

"I am certain I never could!" Fanny murmured, shielded by her mother.

Mr. Thornton heard her but chose not to reply. He paced the length of the dining room, wishing fervently that his mother would command the candles be lit so he could engage in reading or writing, thereby halting the conversation. However, he never considered interfering with the minor domestic regulations Mrs. Thornton meticulously maintained, born of her lifelong habits of economy.

"Mother," he stopped abruptly, speaking the truth with brave sincerity, "I wish you would like Miss Hale."

"Why?" she asked, startled by his earnest yet gentle tone. "Are you not contemplating marriage to her? A girl without a penny."

"She would never have me," he replied with a brief laugh.

"No, I do not believe she would," his mother agreed.
