Mr. Home remained for two days, a guest who preferred the sanctuary of the hearth to the uncertainties of the outdoors. He sat by the fire, a figure of quiet melancholy, engaging only when Mrs. Bretton offered her conversation—a discourse of sensible, moderate sympathy, seasoned with a maternal warmth appropriate to her seniority. While Mr. Home absorbed the warmth, Paulina existed in a state of suspended animation: happy yet silent, industrious yet watchful. Her father often lifted her onto his knee, where she would remain until she sensed his growing restlessness, at which point she would declare, "Papa, put me down; I shall tire you with my weight," and slide to the rug. There, amidst her white work-box and a scarlet-speckled handkerchief destined to be a parting gift, she began her stitching, a tiny, frantic industry driven by the impending departure.

The evenings, however, brought a vibrant shift in the household's atmosphere, fueled by the reunion of Graham with his maternal roof. The dynamic between the boy and the child was immediate and fraught with the tension of their disparate natures. Paulina, recently humiliated by his arrival, maintained a haughty distance, responding to his advances with a curt, "I can't attend to you; I have other things to think about." When pressed, she would merely mutter "Business." Graham, undeterred, attempted to seduce her attention with the treasures of his desk—wax seals, pen-knives, and colorful engravings. His efforts yielded only furtive glances from her work. Eventually, an etching of a child and a spaniel fluttered to the floor.

"Pretty little dog!" she exclaimed, her dignity momentarily breached by delight.

Graham, seizing the opportunity, offered the picture. Paulina hesitated, torn between the desire to possess and the fear of compromising her pride. She rejected it, turning away. When Graham threatened to cut the image into strips for tapers, her resistance crumbled. "No! _No!_ NO!" she cried, rushing to him.

"Now, will you have it?" he asked.

"Please," she conceded.

"But I shall want payment," Graham countered.

"A kiss," she demanded.

"Give the picture first," he insisted.

Reluctantly, she handed it over, only to dart to her father's knee, declaring, "Papa—send him away!" When Mr. Home refused, Graham, feigning injury, collapsed onto a sofa, covering his face with his hands. Paulina, sensing his distress, peered anxiously at her foe. When Graham groaned and feigned blindness, she was beside him in an instant, her face working with contrition. "I am sorry; I am sorry!" she wept.

Mrs. Bretton intervened, calling the incident nonsense, and Mr. Home lifted his daughter, who resumed her punishment of the boy, pulling his hair and labeling him the naughtiest person alive.

On the morning of Mr. Home's departure, a private conversation unfolded in a window recess. Paulina begged to accompany him, asking if she were a trouble. "Yes, Polly," her father admitted gently, explaining that only the strong and great should travel. He promised to return soon, urging her to be cheerful and not to fret. Though she claimed she was not sad, her quivering lips betrayed her. As he departed, she collapsed to her knees, crying out a long, agonized "Papa!" that echoed the despair of abandonment. For a brief, intense interval, she endured an agony of separation that seemed to compress years of feeling into moments. While Mrs. Bretton wept and Graham watched in silence, I, Lucy Snowe, remained calm.

Over the following days, Paulina learned to suppress her grief, growing passive and withdrawn. Yet, a new bond began to form with Graham. He would pick her up gently, and she would nestle against him, finding solace in his presence. She became his constant attendant, carrying his tea, selecting his food, and ensuring his comfort with an officious zeal that made her forget her own needs. She adapted herself to his interests with startling speed, learning the names and characters of his schoolfellows and mimicking their peculiarities. Their relationship deepened into a strange intimacy; she would sit beside him during meals, offering cakes and sweets, treating him as a sovereign figure whose every whim required immediate gratification.

One evening, however, the equilibrium shattered. Graham had invited friends to dinner, and after the guests arrived, Paulina sat alone on the staircase steps, watching the dining room door with anxious eyes. Encouraged by my suggestion, she knocked timidly. Graham, impatient and loud, dismissed her with a sharp command to go to bed, his face flushed with the energy of his new companions. The door slammed shut, leaving Paulina stunned. She retreated to the mat, burying her face in the floor, refusing to be comforted by anyone until she chose to rise. The next day, she withdrew entirely, becoming like marble to his teasing. It was only after he coaxed her with tears and pets that they reconciled, though she never again solicited his notice with such eagerness.

Their interactions were marked by these sudden shifts. She would reject a ride on his pony with icy coolness, dismissing his offer as if it were beneath her, yet later admit to me that she longed for it. She confessed to me that she found him most excellent on Sundays, when he was quiet and kind, contrasting sharply with his boisterous weekday self. In quieter moments, by the fireside, they would engage in solemn conversations, reciting hymns and reading Bible stories. She would read of Joseph in the pit or Daniel in the lions' den with remarkable pathos, eventually gathering Graham in her arms and declaring, "As much... as I love you: if you were to die, I should refuse to be comforted." I observed this display with a mixture of awe and unease, noting the rashness of her affection, yet Graham bore it with a passive, sometimes amused tolerance.

The tranquility of their arrangement was abruptly ended by a letter from Mr. Home, announcing his permanent settlement on the Continent and requesting Paulina's immediate departure. The news struck Paulina with devastating force. Sitting alone in the drawing-room, rocking her doll Candace, she spoke of distant lands and wild men with a detached curiosity, only to recoil violently when I mentioned traveling with her father. "Where is the good of talking in that silly way?" she snapped, her lip trembling. "Why do you mention papa? What is papa to you?"

When I finally revealed the letter, she fell silent, her gravity heavy with the weight of the decision. That evening, as Graham entered, she whispered to me, "Tell him by-and-by, tell him I am going." At tea, Graham, preoccupied with a school prize, barely registered the news, dismissing it with a sorrowful "Dear little Mousie." Paulina, however, lay motionless at his feet, her face to the floor, until she softly caressed his foot with a hidden hand.

Unable to sleep, Paulina perched vigilantly on the edge of my bed, her eyes wide and restless. "I cannot—_cannot_ sleep; and in this way I cannot—_cannot_ live!" she cried, her voice filled with dread. I carried her to the drawing-room to see Graham one last time. He took her, kissing her hot face, and remarked that she cared for him more than for her father. "I _do_ care for you, but you care nothing for me," she whispered bitterly.

I attempted to soothe her, explaining the natural differences between a sixteen-year-old boy and a six-year-old girl, assuring her that she was his favorite. "But," she insisted, "he _should_ love me a little." I warned her not to expect too much, lest she become troublesome. "All over!" she echoed softly, promising to be good. Yet, as I tucked her in, she asked if he would forgive her, and whether she would ever see him again.

In the dark, her small voice returned, questioning my affection for Graham. "Do you like him as I do?" she asked. "Only a little," I admitted. "Where is the use of caring for him so very much: he is full of faults," she reasoned, displaying a wisdom beyond her years. "Are you a wise person?" she asked. "I mean to try to be so," I replied. "Go to sleep."

"I _cannot_ go to sleep," she persisted, placing her hand on her chest. "Have you no pain just here... when you think _you_ shall have to leave Graham?"

I reminded her of her father, but she fell silent. Seeing her shiver in the cold, I called her to me. She came instantly, like a small ghost, and I warmed her in my arms until she finally slumbered. Watching her sleeping face by the moonlight, I reflected on the unique and fragile nature of this child, wondering how she would ever endure the shocks and desolations of life. The next day, she departed, trembling like a leaf yet exercising a strange, painful self-command.
