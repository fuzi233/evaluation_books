*"Sleep on, my love, in thy cold bed,
Never to be disquieted!
My last Good Night—thou wilt not wake
Till I thy fate shall overtake."*
—Dr. King

The house was too quiet now, the echoes of grief and clamor faded into oppressive silence. Margaret returned to find her father seated in his usual chair, lost in one of his waking dreams, while Dixon and Mary Higgins moved through the kitchen in hushed agitation. Even Dixon’s scolding was subdued, as though raising her voice would profane the solemn stillness of death.

Margaret resolved not to burden her father with the terror of Leonards’ pursuit. What good would it do? The danger had passed—for now. Frederick would be cautious, and in a matter of days, he would be safely beyond England’s reach.

“Mr. Bell should write tomorrow,” Margaret said, more to fill the silence than anything else.

“Yes,” her father murmured. “I suppose so.”

“If he comes, it will be by evening.”

“If he does not,” Mr. Hale said, voice trembling, “I must ask someone to accompany me. I cannot face the funeral alone.”

“Then let me go with you,” Margaret urged, sudden and fierce.

“You? My dear, women do not—”

“Women do not go because they fear their grief will shame them. But I am not ashamed. If you will not have Mr. Thornton, then let it be me.”

Mr. Hale looked at her, bewildered by her intensity. But Margaret would not be swayed. If Mr. Bell could not come, she would stand by her father’s side, no matter the custom.

The letter arrived the next day—Mr. Bell was indisposed, gout keeping him from travel. His words were tender, full of regret, but Margaret scarcely needed to read them aloud to know what they contained. Her father’s face crumpled further.

Then came Mrs. Thornton’s note—a cold, formal offer of the family carriage for the funeral. Margaret nearly crumpled it in her hand.

“An empty carriage,” she said bitterly. “How generous.”

Her father frowned. “I thought you did not want him to attend.”

“I don’t. But this—this is a mockery.” To her horror, tears spilled over. She had been so controlled, so careful, but now the weight of it all crashed upon her.

The night was restless, and worse still was Frederick’s letter the next morning—he was delayed in London, waiting for Mr. Lennox’s return. Worse, he hinted at the near-discovery at the station, the lingering threat of pursuit. Margaret’s blood ran cold. She thanked God her father was not present to hear it.

The funeral was a blur of crape and murmured prayers. Mr. Hale trembled, his voice breaking as he whispered, *"Pray for me, Margaret. I have no strength left."* She held him upright, reciting verses of comfort, her own voice steady even as her heart threatened to fracture.

In the crowd, unnoticed, stood Higgins and his daughter, their presence a silent testament to shared sorrow. But Mr. Hale saw nothing. He moved as if blind, clinging to Margaret’s arm.

Dixon wept openly, only stirring when a voice spoke beside her.

Mr. Thornton stood there, his face grave.

Dixon wiped her eyes. “Master is broken, sir. Miss Hale bears it better than expected.”

*Better than expected.* The words struck Thornton like a blow. He had wanted—foolishly—to believe Margaret might need comfort, that his love might somehow ease her pain. But now, the memory of that stranger at the station, the ease between them, twisted in his chest.

“May I call?” he asked, voice clipped. “On Mr. Hale, in a day or so.”

Dixon nodded. “I’m sure he’ll see you, sir.”

But she never spoke of this meeting to Margaret. And so, Margaret never knew Thornton had been there—that he had watched, that he had ached—just as he never knew how deeply her own heart was wounded.

The silence between them stretched, vast and unspoken, as the dead lay quiet in the ground.
