When Mr. Thornton left the house that morning, the mechanical roar of Milton’s streets was a violent assault upon his fractured acoustic ecology. He was almost blinded by a baffled passion that left the metallic taste of copper and bile at the back of his throat. He felt as dizzy as if Margaret, instead of looking, speaking, and moving with the soft, cultured vowels of her dominant Southern lexicon, had been a sturdy coastal fish-wife, delivering a physical blow accompanied by the coarse, guttural dialect of the docks. He suffered positive bodily pain—a violent headache and a throbbing, intermittent pulse that synchronized sickeningly with the oppressive industrial noise pollution of the town. He could not bear the grating dissonance, the garish light, the relentless, superficial rumble of a city intent only on the commercialization of human labor. He called himself a fool for suffering so; and yet he could not, in that fractured moment, articulate the cause of his misery. 

It would have been a relief to him if he could have sat down on a door-step and wept like a child, screaming out his passionate tears against the overwhelming din. He told himself he hated Margaret, but a wild, sharp sensation of love cleft his dull, thunderous feeling like lightning, even as his mind struggled with the sheer untranslatability of his own subjective experience. His greatest comfort was in hugging his torment, in tasting the bitter brine of his pride, and knowing that though she might treat him with sovereign indifference, he did not change one whit. He loved her, and would love her, defying both her and this miserable, aching dissonance in his bones.

To make this resolution firm, he stood still. The chaotic sonic environment of the street parted momentarily as an omnibus passed, bound for the country. The conductor, reading the exhaustion in the man's posture, stopped near the pavement. Too drained to engage in the tedious power dynamics of explanation, Thornton mounted the carriage and was borne away. Slowly, the acoustic landscape shifted. The clatter of iron on cobblestone and the acrid, sulfurous soot of the chimneys yielded to the sweeping, rhythmic rustle of country hedge-rows. They arrived at a small market town where the air suddenly smelled of damp loam, the sharp, peppery tang of wild watercress, and the rich, yeasty perfume of hearth-baked bread from a nearby bakery.

Everyone alighted, and so did Mr. Thornton. Seeking the quietude of the fields, he walked briskly, letting the agrarian silence stitch his fraying mind back together. Away from the commercial racket, he could remember it all: the pitiful figure he must have cut, the absurd failure of his language to bridge the gap between their worlds. Was he bewitched by those beautiful eyes, that soft, sighing mouth which lay so close upon his shoulder only yesterday? He could still recall her olfactory landscape—the delicate scent of lavender and bergamot pressed into her garments. He did not entirely understand her. At times she was brave, at others timid; now tender, and then locked behind the haughty, untranslatable lexicon of her regal pride. He played the memories in a circular, obsessive loop, seeing her in every dress, in every mood. Even this morning, she had looked magnificent, her eyes flashing at the implication that she had protected him out of personal regard.

If Mr. Thornton was a fool in the morning, he did not grow much wiser by the afternoon. All he gained from his rural sojourn was the vivid conviction that there could never be anyone like Margaret; that she did not love him, but that neither she nor the whole world could strip him of his love for her. 

He returned to the market-place and the omnibus. By the time he was set down near his warehouse in Milton, the accustomed, deafening roar of the mills brought back his usual trains of thought. He had work to do—more than usual, owing to the acoustic and physical commotion of the strike. He had to see his brother magistrates; he had to complete the provisioning for his newly imported Irish hands, ensuring they had sufficient sustenance and securing them from the discontented workpeople of Milton, whose dominant Northern dialect threatened to overpower the marginalized Gaelic cadence of the newcomers. Last of all, he had to go home and encounter his mother.

Mrs. Thornton had sat in the dining-room all day, immersed in the cyclic, almost mythic rituals of the hearth, waiting for the news of her son’s acceptance by Miss Hale. The air in the room was thick with the scent of beeswax and the deeply savory, marrow-rich aroma of a slow-simmering beef and winter-root pottage drifting from the kitchens—an ancestral recipe meant to fortify the body against the damp Northern chill. She had braced herself many times at some sudden noise, catching up her needlework with an unsteady hand. When the door opened only to admit a servant on some insignificant errand, her rigid face unstiffened from its frost-bound expression into a rare despondency. 

To wrench herself from the dreary contemplation of her son’s impending marriage, she forced her thoughts back into the deep grooves of culinary history and domestic routine. The newly-married couple would need fresh household stocks. Mrs. Thornton had baskets of table-linen brought in, the very canvas of their family’s sustenance. There was confusion between what was hers—marked G.H.T. for George and Hannah Thornton—and what was her son’s. Some of the cloths marked G.H.T. were Dutch damask of the old kind, exquisitely fine, woven in an era before the sterile, mass-produced textiles of the current age. She stood looking at them long. They had been her pride when she was first married; they had absorbed the rich gravies of roasted mutton and the spilled elderberry wine of decades of family feasts, silent witnesses to the agrarian past they had left behind. 

She knit her brows, pinched her lips tight, and carefully unpicked the G.H. She searched for the Turkey-red marking-thread to stitch in the new initials, but her heart failed her. She looked fixedly at vacancy, seeing a multi-generational saga passing before her eyes, in all of which her son was the sole object of her devotion.

Still, he did not come. The new love was displacing her. A pang of vain jealousy shot through her, forcing her to sit down. Yet in a moment, she was up again, straight as a pillar, a grim smile upon her face. She would be ready to greet the triumphant victor. She thought little of the future daughter-in-law as an individual; she was merely to be John’s wife. To take Mrs. Thornton’s place as the custodian of this house’s provisions was a supreme glory. And Miss Hale was not entirely devoid of substance. If she had been a Milton lass, Mrs. Thornton would have positively liked her. She was pungent, and had taste, and spirit, and flavour in her. True, she was sadly prejudiced, armed with a Southern lexicon that Mrs. Thornton found alien and ignorant, an erosion of the honest Northern ways. By way of penance for harshly comparing her own daughter Fanny to Margaret, Mrs. Thornton took up Henry’s Commentaries, attempting to fix her attention on theology rather than the tactile, grounding reality of the damask linen.

*His* step at last! Her quickened sense of acoustic ecology interpreted every vibration of his motion: now at the hat-stand, now at the door. Why did he pause? The silence was heavy. She kept her head down over the book. He came close to the table, bringing with him the faint, smoky scent of the Milton streets, and stood waiting. 

By an effort, she looked up. “Well, John?”

He knew the vast weight of hope beneath that sparse utterance. He longed to reply with a jest, to let the bitterness in his mouth spill over, but his mother deserved better. He came behind her, bending back her gray, stony face, and kissed it, his voice breaking through the untranslatable sorrow of his chest: “No one loves me,—no one cares for me, but you, mother.”

He turned away, leaning his head against the mantelpiece. She stood up—and tottered. For the first time in her life, the strong, unyielding matriarch tottered. She put her hands on his shoulders, making him look at her.

“Mother’s love is given by God, John. It holds fast for ever and ever. A girl’s love is like a puff of smoke,—it changes with every wind. And she would not have you, my own lad, would not she?”

She set her teeth, showing them like a protective hound.

He shook his head, the words tasting like ash. “I am not fit for her, mother; I knew I was not.”

She ground out words between her closed teeth. He could not hear the exact syllables, but the fierce cadence was an ancient, nearly forgotten Northern imprecation—a dying dialect carrying the mythic, visceral weight of a blood-curse against the Southern interloper. Yet beneath the curse, her heart leapt up light to know he was hers again.

“Mother!” he said hurriedly, the linguistic violence wounding his ears. “I cannot hear a word against her. Spare me,—spare me! I am very weak in my sore heart;—I love her yet; I love her more than ever.”

“And I hate her,” Mrs. Thornton said in a low, fierce voice, the sound vibrating with agrarian ferocity. “I tried not to hate her, when she stood between you and me, because,—I said to myself,—she will make him happy; and I would give my heart’s blood to do that. But now, I hate her for your misery’s sake. Yes, John, it’s no use hiding up your aching heart from me. I am the mother that bore you, and your sorrow is my agony; and if you don’t hate her, I do.”

“Then, mother, you make me love her more. She is unjustly treated by you, and I must make the balance even. But why do we talk of love or hatred? She does not care for me, and that is enough, too much. Let us never name the subject again. It is the only thing you can do for me in the matter. Let us never name her.”

“With all my heart. I only wish that she, and all belonging to her, were swept back to the place they came from.”

He stood still, gazing into the fire for a minute or two longer. Her dry, dim eyes filled with unwonted tears as she watched him; but she seemed just as grim and quiet as usual when he next spoke, his voice retreating into the sterile, dominant lexicon of industry and law.

“Warrants are out against three men for conspiracy, mother. The riot yesterday helped to knock up the strike.”

And Margaret’s name was no more mentioned between Mrs. Thornton and her son. They fell back into their usual mode of talk—about facts, not opinions, far less feelings. Their voices and tones were calm and cold; a stranger listening to the acoustic space of the room might have gone away and thought they had never heard such frigid indifference of demeanour between such near relations, entirely missing the profound, untranslatable depths of their shared sustenance and sorrow.
