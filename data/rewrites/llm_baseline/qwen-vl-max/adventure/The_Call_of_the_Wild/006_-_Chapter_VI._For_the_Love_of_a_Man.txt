When John Thornton froze his feet in December, his partners had seen to his comfort and left him behind while they pushed upriver to harvest a raft of logs for Dawson. He was still limping slightly when he found Buck half-dead by the riverbank, but as the weather stayed warm, even that limp faded away. Lying there through the long spring days, watching the water flow and listening to birdsong and the hum of nature, Buck slowly regained his strength.

After traveling three thousand miles, rest was a gift. And as his wounds healed, his muscles swelled, and flesh returned to cover his bones, Buck grew lazy—content, even. The same could be said of John Thornton, Skeet, and Nig. They waited together for the raft that would carry them down to Dawson, drifting into a quiet rhythm of stillness and ease.

Skeet was a small Irish setter who quickly befriended Buck, though he was too weak then to resist her affection. She possessed a gentle instinct, like a mother cat cleaning her young—she washed and tended to Buck’s wounds each morning after breakfast, until he came to expect her care as much as he did Thornton’s. Nig, larger and quieter, was a massive black dog, part bloodhound, part deerhound, with kind eyes and an endless patience. He never challenged Buck, nor did he seek attention. He simply existed in harmony.

To Buck’s surprise, neither dog showed jealousy. They shared the warmth and generosity of their master. As Buck grew stronger, they pulled him into playful games—games so joyful that even Thornton couldn’t help joining in. Through these moments, Buck passed from recovery into a new life. For the first time, he knew love—not just loyalty or duty, but something deeper, fiercer, more alive. It was not the polite companionship he’d known at Judge Miller’s estate in the sun-drenched Santa Clara Valley. There, with the Judge’s sons, it had been work; with the grandsons, a sort of solemn guardianship; with the Judge himself, a dignified friendship. But this—this was devotion without measure. This was madness born of trust. And only John Thornton could awaken it.

Thornton had saved his life—that alone meant everything. But more than that, he was the perfect master. While others treated dogs as tools or responsibilities, Thornton cared for them as if they were children—because he couldn’t help it. He remembered every kindness, offered words of encouragement, and loved nothing more than sitting quietly with them, talking in low tones (“gas,” he called it), sharing thoughts and stories. He had a way of gripping Buck’s head in both hands, pressing his own forehead against Buck’s, shaking him gently back and forth, muttering curses that to Buck were songs of affection. Each motion sent waves of ecstasy through him, as if his heart might burst from joy. When released, Buck would leap up, mouth open in silent laughter, eyes bright, throat humming with unspoken sound. Then Thornton would look at him reverently and whisper, “God, you can almost speak.”

Buck had his own way of expressing love—one that seemed almost painful. He would grab Thornton’s hand in his jaws and clamp down hard enough to leave marks, teeth sinking deep. But Thornton understood. He saw it not as aggression, but as a tender gesture, a bond sealed in bite.

Mostly, though, Buck’s love was silent. Unlike Skeet, who nudged insistently for pets, or Nig, who rested his great head on Thornton’s knee, Buck preferred to watch. He would lie at Thornton’s feet for hours, gazing up, studying every flicker of expression, every shift in posture. Or he’d rest farther off, observing the man’s silhouette, the rise and fall of his chest, the quiet movements of his body. Sometimes, their mutual gaze would draw Thornton’s head around, and they would lock eyes—no words needed, no need for speech. In those moments, their hearts shone through, speaking plainly across silence.

For weeks after being rescued, Buck couldn’t bear to let Thornton out of sight. From the moment the man stepped outside the tent until he returned, Buck followed closely behind. He feared what he had seen before: masters passing into memory, slipping away like shadows. Perrault, François, the Scotch half-breed—all gone. Even in sleep, the fear haunted him. He’d wake, trembling, and crawl to the edge of the tent flap, listening for the steady breaths of his master.

Yet despite this fierce love, the wild remained within him. Civilization had touched him, but not tamed him. Faithfulness and devotion were now part of him—gifts shaped by fire and shelter—but the ancient instincts lived on. He was not a domesticated dog of the Southland, bred soft by generations of comfort. He was a creature of the wild, drawn in by firelight, not forged by it. Because of his love for Thornton, he would never steal from him. But from any other man, in any other camp, he would take what he wanted—and vanish unseen, clever and swift.

His face bore scars from many fights, and he fought as fiercely and wisely as ever. Skeet and Nig were too gentle to quarrel, and anyway, they belonged to Thornton. But any strange dog—regardless of breed or courage—either acknowledged Buck’s dominance or faced a fight to the death. And Buck was merciless. He had learned the law of club and fang: there was no mercy, no hesitation. To show weakness was to invite death. Mercy didn’t exist in the old world. It was mistaken for fear—and fear meant failure. Kill or be killed. Eat or be eaten. That was the law, and Buck obeyed it without question.

He was older than his years, older than the breaths he’d drawn. He carried the echoes of ancestors—half-wolves, wolves—within him. They stirred in his blood, urging him, guiding him. They tasted the meat he ate, drank the water he sipped, smelled the wind with him, heard the forest’s secrets, and dreamed through him. When he slept, they walked beside him, shaping his dreams.

And so, day by day, the call of the wild grew louder. Deep in the forest, a voice echoed—mysterious, powerful, impossible to ignore. Whenever he heard it, he felt the urge to turn from the fire, to flee into the trees, to run without knowing where or why. Yet each time he reached the green shade and soft earth, the pull of Thornton brought him back. Only Thornton could hold him.

No one else mattered. Travelers might praise or pet him, but he remained cold. If someone grew too familiar, he would rise and walk away. When Hans and Pete arrived on the long-awaited raft, Buck ignored them until he saw they stood near Thornton. Then, reluctantly, he accepted their presence, taking their offerings as if granting them a favor.

Hans and Pete were men of the earth—simple, clear-sighted, strong. Before the raft reached the mill at Dawson, they understood Buck. They respected his ways, and never forced closeness.

But for Thornton, Buck’s love grew deeper every day. No one else could command him as Thornton could. Only Thornton could put a pack on his back during summer travels. Nothing was too difficult when Thornton asked.

One afternoon, they sat atop a cliff overlooking a sheer drop of three hundred feet. Thornton leaned near the edge, Buck beside him. A sudden impulse seized Thornton—he turned to Hans and Pete. “Jump, Buck!” he shouted, sweeping his arm over the chasm.

The next instant, Buck lunged forward, leaping toward the void. Thornton grabbed him just in time, wrestling him back from the edge as Hans and Pete dragged them both to safety.

“It’s uncanny,” Pete said, catching his breath.

Thornton shook his head. “No. It’s splendid. And terrible. Sometimes… it frightens me.”

“I wouldn’t want to be the man who tried to touch you while he’s near,” Pete declared, nodding at Buck.

“Py Jingo!” Hans added. “Not me either.”

That fear became reality later, at Circle City. “Black” Burton, a cruel and angry man, was arguing with a newcomer at the bar. Thornton stepped in, trying to calm things. Buck lay nearby, watching every move his master made. Without warning, Burton swung a fist—straight from the shoulder. Thornton spun backward, clutching the bar rail to keep from falling.

Then came a sound—not a bark, not a growl, but a roar. Buck rose from the floor, launching himself at Burton’s throat. The man threw up his arm instinctively, blocking the first strike, but Buck drove in again—this time, tearing open his throat. The crowd rushed in, driving Buck back. He prowled the room, snarling, refusing to stop, until surgeons bound Burton’s wound. A miners’ meeting decided Buck had cause to act—and he was cleared. But his name spread across Alaska.

Later, in autumn, Buck saved Thornton’s life in another way. The three men were guiding a narrow poling boat through treacherous rapids on Forty-Mile Creek. Hans and Pete worked along the bank, using a rope to control the boat, while Thornton stayed aboard, pole in hand, shouting directions. Buck ran alongside, eyes fixed on his master.

At one point, a submerged rock jutted into the river. Hans cast off the rope, letting the boat drift past, then ran down the bank to catch it again. But he pulled too sharply—the boat flipped over, and Thornton was thrown into the current, swept downstream toward the worst stretch of water.

Buck jumped in instantly. Three hundred yards downstream, amid swirling chaos, he caught up to Thornton. When the man grabbed his tail, Buck turned toward shore, swimming with all his strength. But progress was slow—the current was too fast. Below, the water roared over jagged rocks like teeth.

Thornton scraped over rocks, bruised, battered. He clung to one slippery stone, releasing Buck. Above the thunder, he shouted: “Go, Buck! Go!”

Buck couldn’t stay. He was swept downstream, fighting hard but unable to turn back. When he heard the command again, he reared up, then obeyed—turning toward shore. He swam powerfully, and Pete and Hans hauled him ashore just before the water became deadly.

They knew Thornton couldn’t last long. They ran upstream, attached a rope to Buck’s neck, and launched him again. He struck out boldly—but not far enough into the current. By the time he reached Thornton, he was already being carried past.

Hans tightened the rope, jerking Buck under the surface. He stayed submerged until he hit the bank, half-drowned. Hans and Pete pounded air into him, bringing him back. He staggered, fell. Then, faintly, Thornton’s voice reached them—weak, desperate. Hearing it, Buck sprang up, racing ahead of the men to the starting point.

Again, the rope was fastened. Again, Buck plunged into the water—but this time, straight into the current. He had learned from his mistake. Hans paid out the line carefully, keeping it taut. Buck swam to a point directly above Thornton, then turned and charged downward like a train.

Thornton saw him coming. When Buck slammed into him with the full force of the current, he wrapped his arms around the dog’s thick neck. Hans snubbed the rope around a tree. Together, Buck and Thornton were yanked under—dragged, choked, smashed against rocks, scraping along the bottom—until they veered to shore.

Thornton woke up belly-down, being shaken violently across a log by Hans and Pete. His first glance was for Buck. Nig howled over his limp body. Skeet licked his wet face, closed eyes. Thornton checked Buck’s injuries—three broken ribs.

“That settles it,” he said. “We camp here.” And they did, until Buck recovered.

That winter, in Dawson, Buck earned another legend. Not as heroic, perhaps—but one that raised his fame even higher. It began in the Eldorado Saloon, where men bragged about their dogs. Buck, because of his record, became the target. Thornton defended him fiercely.

One man claimed his dog could start a sled with five hundred pounds. Another said six hundred. A third, seven hundred.

“Pooh! Buck can start a thousand,” Thornton said calmly.

“And break it out? Walk off with it for a hundred yards?” Matthewson, a Bonanza King, challenged.

“And break it out. And walk off a hundred yards,” Thornton replied.

“Well,” Matthewson said slowly, “I’ve got a thousand dollars that says he can’t.” He slammed a sack of gold dust onto the bar.

Silence. Thornton had been trapped. He didn’t know if Buck could do it. Half a ton—impossible. And he had no money. Neither did Hans or Pete.

“I’ve got a sled outside with twenty fifty-pound sacks of flour,” Matthewson added bluntly.

Thornton looked around, lost. Then his eyes met Jim O’Brien’s—a friend, a Mastodon King. “Can you lend me a thousand?” he whispered.

“Sure,” O’Brien said, slamming down his own sack. “Though I don’t believe it’ll happen.”

The saloon emptied into the street. Men gathered around the sled, frozen solid in the sixty-below-zero cold. Odds were two to one against Buck. A dispute arose over “break out”—did it mean freeing the runners or pulling from a dead stop? Most ruled in Matthewson’s favor. Odds climbed to three to1.

No one believed Buck could do it. Thornton doubted. But the challenge lit a fire in him—the warrior spirit that ignores odds, sees no limits, hears only battle cries.

He gathered Hans and Pete. Their combined savings totaled two hundred dollars. They laid it against Matthewson’s six hundred.

The team was unhitched. Buck was harnessed. He sensed the moment—something great must be done for Thornton. Murmurs rose: “Look at him!” “Magnificent!” He was lean, powerful, 150 pounds of muscle and vitality. His coat gleamed like silk. His mane bristled with energy. His chest and legs were massive, muscles coiled beneath skin like iron.

A man offered eight hundred dollars for him before the test.

Thornton shook his head. He knelt beside Buck, took his head in his hands, cheek to cheek. He didn’t shake him or curse softly. He whispered: “As you love me, Buck. As you love me.”

Buck whined—eager, controlled.

The crowd watched. It felt like magic.

Thornton stood. Buck gripped his mittened hand between his jaws, pressing in, then releasing slowly—like a promise.

“Now, Buck,” Thornton said.

Buck tightened the traces, then let them slacken. He knew the ritual.

“Gee!” Thornton commanded.

Buck swung right, then lunged—taking up the slack, jerking his weight forward. The sled trembled. Cracks snapped under the runners.

“Haw!”

Buck turned left. The sled moved. Then, slowly, steadily, it began to roll.

The crowd gasped. Then roared. Buck pulled it forward—fifty yards, then a hundred. The impossible had happened.

And the legend grew.
