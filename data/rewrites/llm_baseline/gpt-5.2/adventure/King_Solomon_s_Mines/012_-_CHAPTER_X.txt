When we gained our hut again I beckoned Infadoos inside, for the air out of doors was thick with the breath of many fires and the restless shuffle of feet, and I wanted the old captain where words could be weighed.

“Now, Infadoos,” I said, “we would speak with thee.”

“Let my lords say on,” he answered, standing very straight, yet with a look as though he listened not only with his ears but with his skin.

“It seems to us,” I went on, “that Twala the king is a cruel man.”

“It is so, my lords.” His voice dropped, and through the thatch came the far-off pulse of stamping, like a drum laid on the earth. “Alas! the land cries out because of his cruelties. To-night ye shall see. It is the great witch-hunt, and many will be smelt out as wizards and slain. No man’s life is safe. If the king covets a man’s cattle, or a man’s wife, or if he fears a man that he should excite rebellion, then Gagool—she whom ye saw—or some of the witch-finding women whom she has taught, will smell that man out as a wizard, and he will be killed. Many must die before the moon grows pale to-night. It is ever so.”

As he spoke, the village sounds seemed to press nearer: the dry clack of spears striking shield rims, the scrape of sandals, the occasional sharp call—each noise carrying clean through the cool evening like a knife through soft fruit. There was something in that ordered harshness that set the teeth on edge; it was no wholesome music of field and home, but a drilled sound, as if men had been trained to drown their own thoughts.

“Perhaps I too shall be killed,” Infadoos added, not dramatically, but as a farmer might remark that hail could come before harvest. “As yet I have been spared because I am skilled in war, and am beloved by the soldiers; but I know not how long I have to live. The land groans at the cruelties of Twala the king; it is wearied of him and his red ways.”

“Then why is it,” I asked, “that the people do not cast him down?”

“Nay, my lords, he is the king; and if he were killed, Scragga would reign in his place, and the heart of Scragga is blacker than the heart of Twala his father. If Scragga were king his yoke upon our neck would be heavier than the yoke of Twala. If Imotu had never been slain, or if Ignosi his son had lived, it might have been otherwise; but they are both dead.”

“How knowest thou that Ignosi is dead?” said a voice behind us.

We turned, startled. Umbopa stood in the doorway. The firelight caught the planes of his face and the line of his shoulders; he looked as calm as a man who has been long carrying a secret and at last feels its weight shift.

“What meanest thou, boy?” Infadoos demanded. “Who told thee to speak?”

“Listen, Infadoos,” Umbopa answered, and his voice had the measured steadiness of a story told in winter to keep memory from freezing. “Years ago the king Imotu was killed in this country and his wife fled with the boy Ignosi. Is it not so?”

“It is so.”

“It was said that the woman and her son died upon the mountains. Is it not so?”

“It is even so.”

“Well, it came to pass that the mother and the boy Ignosi did not die. They crossed the mountains and were led by a tribe of wandering desert men across the sands beyond, till at last they came to water and grass and trees again.”

“How knowest thou this?” Infadoos broke in, as though the tale rubbed him raw where old scars lay.

“Listen. They travelled on and on, many months’ journey, till they reached a land where a people called the Amazulu, who also are of the Kukuana stock, live by war, and with them they tarried many years, till at length the mother died. Then the son Ignosi became a wanderer again, and journeyed into a land of wonders, where white people live, and for many more years he learned the wisdom of the white people.”

“It is a pretty story,” Infadoos said, but there was hunger in his incredulity.

“For years he lived there working as a servant and a soldier,” Umbopa went on, “but holding in his heart all that his mother had told him of his own place, and casting about in his mind to find how he might journey thither to see his people and his father’s house before he died. For long years he lived and waited, and at last the time came—as it ever comes to him who can wait for it—and he met some white men who would seek this unknown land, and joined himself to them. The white men started and travelled on and on, seeking for one who is lost. They crossed the burning desert, they crossed the snow-clad mountains, and at last reached the land of the Kukuanas, and there they found thee, O Infadoos.”

“Surely thou art mad to talk thus,” Infadoos said, but his hands had begun to tremble.

“Thou thinkest so; see, I will show thee, O my uncle.”

With one swift motion Umbopa slipped off his girdle and stood naked before us, the fire making copper of his skin. He pointed to the blue tattoo about his middle: a great snake biting its own tail.

“Look,” he said. “What is this?”

Infadoos stared as though the air had suddenly turned to water and he could not breathe.

“Koom! Koom!” he burst out, and fell on his knees. “It is my brother’s son; it is the king.”

“Did I not tell thee so, my uncle?” Umbopa—Ignosi—said gently. “Rise; I am not yet the king, but with thy help, and with the help of these brave white men, who are my friends, I shall be. Yet the old witch Gagool was right: the land shall run with blood first, and hers shall run with it, if she has any and can die, for she killed my father with her words, and drove my mother forth.

“And now, Infadoos, choose thou. Wilt thou put thy hands between my hands and be my man? Wilt thou share the dangers that lie before me, and help me to overthrow this tyrant and murderer, or wilt thou not? Choose thou.”

The old soldier bowed his head, thinking hard, as men do who have lived long enough to have seen promises rot and names change their taste. Then he rose, stepped forward, and knelt again—this time not out of shock but out of allegiance. He took Ignosi’s hand between his own.

“Ignosi, rightful king of the Kukuanas, I put my hand between thy hands, and am thy man till death. When thou wast a babe I dandled thee upon my knees; now shall my old arm strike for thee and freedom.”

“It is well, Infadoos,” Ignosi answered. “If I conquer, thou shalt be the greatest man in the kingdom after its king. If I fail, thou canst only die, and death is not far off from thee. Rise, my uncle.

“And ye, white men, will ye help me? What have I to offer you? The white stones! If I conquer and can find them, ye shall have as many as ye can carry hence. Will that suffice you?”

I translated.

“Tell him,” said Sir Henry, “that he mistakes an Englishman. Wealth is good, and if it comes in our way we will take it; but a gentleman does not sell himself for wealth. Still, speaking for myself, I say this: I have always liked Umbopa, and so far as lies in me I will stand by him in this business. It will be very pleasant to me to try to square matters with that cruel devil Twala.”

“What do you say, Good, and you, Quatermain?” he added.

“Well,” said Good, “to adopt the language of hyperbole, in which all these people seem to indulge, you can tell him that a row is surely good, and warms the cockles of the heart, and that so far as I am concerned I’m his boy. My only stipulation is that he allows me to wear trousers.”

I gave the sense of this to Ignosi, who smiled a little, though his eyes were set far away.

“And what sayest thou, Macumazahn,” he asked me, “art thou also with me, old hunter, cleverer than a wounded buffalo?”

I scratched my head, for it is no light thing to set your hand to another man’s storm.

“Ignosi,” I said, “I don’t like revolutions. I am a man of peace and a bit of a coward”—at which he smiled more openly—“but I stick up for my friends. You have stuck to us and played the part of a man, and I will stick by you. Only, mind you, I am a trader, and have to make my living, so I accept your offer about those diamonds in case we should ever be in a position to avail ourselves of it. Another thing: we came to look for Incubu’s lost brother. You must help us to find him.”

“That I will do,” Ignosi said. Then his face hardened again as he turned to Infadoos. “By the sign of the snake about my middle, tell me the truth. Has any white man to thy knowledge set his foot within the land?”

“None, O Ignosi.”

“If any white man had been seen or heard of, wouldst thou have known?”

“I should certainly have known.”

“Thou hearest, Incubu,” Ignosi said to Sir Henry. “He has not been here.”

Sir Henry’s mouth worked; his eyes went away from us to the packed darkness under the eaves.

“Well, well,” he said at last, with a sigh that seemed to empty him. “There it is. I suppose that he never got so far. Poor fellow, poor fellow! So it has all been for nothing. God’s will be done.”

I broke in, for the grief in his voice was worse than any loud crying. “Now for business. It is very well to be a king by right divine, Ignosi, but how dost thou propose to become a king indeed?”

“Nay, I know not,” he answered. “Infadoos, hast thou a plan?”

“Ignosi, Son of the Lightning,” said Infadoos, “to-night is the great dance and witch-hunt. Many shall be smelt out and perish, and in the hearts of many others there will be grief and anguish and fury against the king Twala. When the dance is over, then I will speak to some of the great chiefs, who in turn—if I can win them—will speak to their regiments. I shall speak softly at first, and bring them to see that thou art indeed the king, and I think that by to-morrow’s light thou shalt have twenty thousand spears at thy command. And now I must go and think, and hear, and make ready. After the dance is done, if I am yet alive, and we are all alive, I will meet thee here, and we can talk. At the best there must be war.”

At that very moment a cry outside cut across our plotting: messengers from the king.

We went to the doorway and ordered them in. Three men entered, each bearing a shining shirt of chain armour, and a magnificent battle-axe. The links of the mail caught the firelight as fish-scales do when you turn them in your palm; there was a cold, clean smell to the metal, like stone after rain.

“The gifts of my lord the king to the white men from the Stars!” said the herald.

“We thank the king,” I answered. “Withdraw.”

When they had gone we handled the armour with a sort of unwilling admiration. The chain-work was so fine and close that a whole coat lay in a heavy, supple heap, scarcely too big to cover with both hands.

“Do you make these things in this country, Infadoos?” I asked.

“Nay, my lord. They came down to us from our forefathers. We know not who made them, and there are but few left. None but those of royal blood may be clad in them. They are magic coats through which no spear can pass, and those who wear them are well-nigh safe in the battle. The king is well pleased, or much afraid, or he would not have sent these garments of steel. Clothe yourselves in them to-night, my lords.”

We spent what remained of the day as quietly as men can who feel a whole country shifting beneath them. Outside, the sounds of preparation thickened: the repeated metallic kiss of spear to shield, the short barking orders, the measured trotting of runners. The very air seemed tuned to harsh instruments, as if the village had forgotten the softer noises of cattle and pestles and women’s talk.

At last the sun went down. A thousand watch-fires bloomed and steadied into red eyes across the dark, and the full moon rose, washing everything in pale silver and making the smoke drift like ghost-cloth.

Infadoos came for us clad in war-dress, with twenty men to escort us. We had already put on the chain shirts under our ordinary clothing. To our surprise they were neither very heavy nor very uncomfortable. They hung loose on Good and on me, but on Sir Henry they fitted as though made for him. We strapped our revolvers about our waists, took up the battle-axes, and went out.

The great kraal was crammed—some twenty thousand men arranged in regiments around the open space. Between each company ran narrow paths, left clear as channels, for the witch-finders to pass up and down. The order of it was terrifying: so many bodies held in such exact shape, like a field planted with spears instead of grain. In the moonlight the raised points shimmered in ranks; the shields made broad patterns of colour; the plumes trembled. Yet over it all lay a silence so deep it felt unnatural, like a forest where all birds have flown.

“Surely,” I murmured to Infadoos, “the whole army is here?”

“Nay, Macumazahn,” he answered. “But a third of it. One third is present at this dance each year, another third is mustered outside in case there should be trouble when the killing begins, ten thousand more garrison the outposts round Loo, and the rest watch at the kraals in the country. Thou seest it is a great people.”

“They are very silent,” said Good, and his attempt at lightness fell flat.

“What says Bougwan?” asked Infadoos.

I translated.

“Those over whom the shadow of Death is hovering are silent,” Infadoos returned, and there was no mistaking the relish of grim knowledge in his tone.

“Will many be killed?” I asked, though I knew the answer by the emptiness in my own stomach.

“Very many.”

“It seems,” I said to the others, “that we are going to assist at a gladiatorial show arranged regardless of expense.”

Sir Henry shivered. Good muttered that he wished we could slip away like smoke.

“Tell me,” I asked Infadoos, “are we in danger?”

“I know not, my lords. I trust not; but do not seem afraid. If ye live through the night all may go well with you. The soldiers murmur against the king.”

We advanced toward the centre where stools had been set. From the royal hut another small party approached.

“It is the king Twala,” Infadoos said, “Scragga his son, and Gagool the old; and see, with them are those who slay.”

He pointed to a dozen great, savage-looking men—executioners—spears in one hand, heavy kerries in the other. Their faces shone with sweat and anticipation.

Twala seated himself on the middle stool. Gagool crouched at his feet, a bent bundle of age and malice, her skull-like head thrust forward as though scenting the air. The others stood behind.

“Greeting, white lords,” Twala cried when we came up. “Be seated. Waste not precious time—the night is all too short for the deeds that must be done. Ye come in a good hour, and shall see a glorious show. Look round, white lords; look round.” His single eye rolled from regiment to regiment. “Can the Stars show you such a sight as this? See how they shake in their wickedness, all those who have evil in their hearts and fear the judgment of ‘Heaven above.’”

“Begin! begin!” piped Gagool, her voice thin as a reed whistle. “The hyænas are hungry, they howl for food. Begin! begin!”

A stillness fell that was not peace but a held breath before a blow.

Twala lifted his spear. Instantly twenty thousand feet rose and stamped together, three times, so that the earth itself seemed to answer with a dull groan. From far round the circle a lone voice began a wailing chant.

“What is the lot of man born of woman?”

Back came the answer from every throat in that vast ring:

“Death!”

Company after company took up the song. The words shifted and slid; the same mouths that had given “Death” like a judgment now shaped sounds that seemed—by turns—love, pride, battle, lament. It was as though the whole life of the people was being sung out in one great coil, returning always to the same cold centre. It ended in a sudden wail that ran along the ring and away into the night like an animal driven under the knife.

Again silence.

Then, at another motion of the king’s hand, we heard the patter of running feet. Out of the masses came strange figures—women, most of them old, with white hair streaming and ornamented with small fish bladders. Their faces were striped in yellow and white; snake-skins hung down their backs; circlets of human bones rattled at their waists with each step. Each carried a small forked wand.

Ten of them halted before us. One pointed her wand at Gagool and cried:

“Mother, old mother, we are here.”

“Good! good! good!” the hag answered. “Are your eyes keen, Isanusis, ye seers in dark places?”

“Mother, they are keen.”

“Good! good! good! Are your ears open, Isanusis, ye who hear words that come not from the tongue?”

“Mother, they are open.”

“Good! good! good! Are your senses awake—can ye smell blood, can ye purge the land of the wicked ones who compass evil against the king and against their neighbours? Are ye ready to do the justice of ‘Heaven above,’ ye whom I have taught, who have eaten of the bread of my wisdom, and drunk of the water of my magic?”

“Mother, we can.”

“Then go!” Gagool shrieked. “Tarry not, ye vultures; see, the slayers make sharp their spears; the white men from afar are hungry to see. Go!”

With a wild yell they scattered, the bones at their waists clattering like dry pods in a gourd rattle. We fixed our eyes on the nearest. She halted before a company and began to dance, faster and faster, turning like a top, shrieking:

“I smell him, the evil-doer!”
“He is near, he who poisoned his mother!”
“I hear the thoughts of him who thought evil of the king!”

She whipped herself into frenzy till foam flecked her mouth and her eyes looked ready to burst. Then she froze, stiff as a hunting dog, and began to creep forward, wand outstretched.

The warriors before her—men who had stood unmoving like carved posts—seemed to shrink. It was not fear of death alone; it was fear of being named, of having your life rewritten by another’s tongue. And there is a kind of power in that: to speak a word that cannot be answered in the same language.

Suddenly she sprang and touched a tall man.

At once two comrades seized him by the arms and marched him toward the king. He did not struggle; his limbs dragged as if the spirit had already been knocked loose, and his spear fell from fingers that had gone limp.

Two executioners came forward and looked to Twala.

“Kill,” said the king.

“Kill,” squeaked Gagool.

“Kill,” echoed Scragga, with a hollow chuckle.

Before the syllable had died, the spear was driven home and the heavy club followed. The body was hauled aside and laid out.

“One,” said Twala, counting as calmly as a man tallying cattle.

Another was brought—this one wearing a leopard-skin cloak that told of rank. Again the word. Again the fall.

“Two.”

So it went on—one after another—till near a hundred corpses lay in grim rows behind us, their dark blood turning almost black in the moonlight. I have heard much of Roman shows and Spanish bull-fights, but those at least were called amusement; here there was no joy, only a great public terror, and a tyrant feeding on it. The circle of men watched as people watch a fire that may leap to their own roofs.

Once we rose and tried to protest, but Twala checked us with a glare.

“Let the law take its course, white men. These dogs are magicians and evil-doers; it is well that they should die.”

About half-past ten there came a pause. The witch-finders gathered, panting and wiped-out, and for a moment we hoped the madness had spent itself. But then Gagool herself rose from her crouch. She tottered into the open space, leaning on a stick; and it was a sight to see that bent, vulture-headed thing draw strength as she moved, until she was darting about, crooked as a hook and yet swift.

To and fro she went, muttering and chanting, until she dashed at a tall man in front of a regiment and touched him. A groan rose from that regiment—deep, collective, helpless—yet two officers stepped out, seized him, and dragged him forward all the same. We learned later he was a rich and important man, cousin to the king.

He died like the rest.

“One hundred and three,” counted Twala.

Then Gagool began to drift closer, circling, circling—like a carrion bird that has chosen the next carcass but enjoys the tightening spiral. The whole assembly watched her with the strained attention of men listening for a twig-snap in the dark.

“Hang me if I don’t believe she is going to try her games on us,” Good whispered, his face grey.

“Nonsense,” Sir Henry muttered, though his jaw was set.

My own heart sank like a stone in cold water. Behind us those rows of bodies lay like a harvest of evil, and I thought how quickly any of us might be added—one touch, one word, and the knives were out.

Nearer she came, eyes gleaming. Then she stopped and pointed.

For one beat of time none of us breathed. Then she rushed in and laid her hand on Umbopa—Ignosi—on the shoulder.

“I smell him out!” she shrieked. “Kill him, kill him, he is full of evil; kill him, the stranger, before blood flows from him. Slay him, O king!”

A hush—so complete it seemed the very fires outside had died.

I sprang up before Twala could speak.

“O king,” I cried, “this man is the servant of thy guests, he is their dog. Whosoever sheds the blood of our dog sheds our blood. By the sacred law of hospitality I claim protection for him.”

“Gagool, mother of the witch-finders, has smelt him out,” Twala answered sullenly. “He must die, white men.”

“Nay, he shall not die,” I said. “He who tries to touch him shall die indeed.”

Twala roared to the executioners. They moved forward—then hesitated, for even their courage had edges, and those edges met the unknown.

Ignosi gripped his spear and lifted it, ready to sell himself dearly.

“Stand back, ye dogs!” I shouted. “If ye would see to-morrow’s light, touch one hair of his head and your king dies.” I covered Twala with my revolver. Sir Henry and Good drew too—Sir Henry aimed at the foremost executioner; Good, cool as a man sighting a buck, levelled at Gagool.

Twala flinched when my barrel steadied on his chest.

“Well,” I said, “what is it to be, Twala?”

He held my eyes a moment—his one eye full of hate and calculation—then spat out his decision as though it tasted bitter.

“Put away your magic tubes,” he said. “Ye have adjured me in the name of hospitality, and for that reason, but not from fear of what ye can do, I spare him. Go in peace.”

“It is well,” I answered, making myself sound unconcerned though my hands were damp. “We are weary of slaughter, and would sleep. Is the dance ended?”

“It is ended,” Twala said, sulkier than before. He lifted his spear and gestured toward the corpses. “Let these dead dogs be flung out to the hyænas and the vultures.”

Instantly the regiments began to file away through the gateway in perfect silence, the only sound that remained the dull dragging as a fatigue party started on the work of hauling the bodies out into the night.
