It was the first of October; one of those mornings which seem to hush themselves, as though the year, tired of thunder and blood, had drawn a cool cloth across its brow. The air lay bright and still upon the Taw, and the sky, from east to west, was gently mottled with pale, gray autumn cloud—as if heaven had folded its hands and listened.

Silently, almost with shame, the Vengeance slid over the bar and past the sleeping sand-hills, and dropped her anchor off Appledore. Her flag hung half-mast; for Salvation Yeo lay dead aboard.

A boat put off, heading for the western end of the strand; and Cary and Brimblecombe helped Amyas Leigh ashore and led him slowly up the hill toward his home. The people clustered round with cheers and blessings, with sobs too from women whose hearts were quicker than their tongues. All Appledore and Bideford knew by now what had befallen him.

“Spare me, my good friends,” Amyas said. “I landed here that I might go quietly home, without passing through the town and being made a gazing-stock. Think not of me, good folks, nor talk of me; but come behind me decently, as Christian men, and follow to the grave the body of a better man than I.”

As he spoke, another boat came off; and in it, covered with the flag of England, was the body of Salvation Yeo. The folk took Amyas at his word. A man was sent on to Burrough to tell Mrs. Leigh her son had come.

When the coffin was lifted, Amyas and his friends took their place behind it as chief mourners; the crew followed in order; and the crowd fell in after, swelling every moment, until before they had reached Northam town the train was near five hundred souls. The sexton had been warned the day before; and at the churchyard gate the parson waited ready.

Mrs. Leigh stayed quietly at home. She had no heart for a crowd; and though her heart yearned for her son, she was well content—when was she not content?—that he should do honor to the old servant who had been faithful through youth and war and wandering. So she sat in the bay-window with Ayacanora beside her; and when the tolling ceased she opened her Prayer-book and began the Burial-service.

“Ayacanora,” she said softly, “they are burying old Master Yeo, who loved you, and sought you over the wide, wide world, and saved you from the teeth of the crocodile. Are you not sorry for him, child, that you look so gay to-day?”

Ayacanora blushed and hung down her head. Her thoughts were not on Yeo—nor on death, nor on England’s flag—but only on Amyas.

The Burial-service was read; the blessing said. The parson drew back; yet the people lingered, crowding to look down at the coffin. Amyas stood at the head of the grave, very still.

By his command it had been dug at the west end of the church, near the foot of the tall gray tower that stood wind-swept, watching like a sentinel for beacon far and wide over land and sea. Perhaps, Amyas thought, the old man would like to lie where he might still be near the water—to “look,” as it were, on the ships coming in and out across the bar, and to hear the wind in winter nights roaring through the belfry overhead. It was but a fancy; and yet it clung to him, because it was homely and seamanlike, and because now—blind—he too felt what it was to need the world’s sounds to make a place real.

The crowd waited, looking first at the grave, then at the great blind giant who stood above it, as though by instinct they knew something more was due.

And something more came.

Amyas drew himself up to his full height and lifted his hand. Twice he tried to begin, and twice the words were choked upon his lips; and then at last he spoke.

“Good people all, and seamen, among whom I was bred, and to whom I come home blind this day, to dwell with you till death—here lieth the flower and pattern of all bold mariners; the truest of friends, and the most terrible of foes; unchangeable of purpose, crafty of council, and swift of execution; in triumph most sober, in failure—God knows I have found it—of endurance beyond mortal man.

“Who first of all Britons helped to humble the pride of the Spaniard at Rio de la Hacha and Nombre, and first of all sailed upon those South Seas, which shall be hereafter, by God’s grace, as free to English keels as is the bay outside.

“Who, having afterwards been purged from his youthful sins by strange afflictions and torments unspeakable, suffered at the hands of the Popish enemy; learned therefrom, my masters, to fear God and to fear naught else; and having acquitted himself worthily in his place and calling as a righteous scourge of the Spaniard, and a faithful soldier of the Lord Jesus Christ, is now exalted to his reward, as Elijah was of old, in a chariot of fire unto heaven: letting fall, I trust and pray, upon you who are left behind the mantle of his valor and his godliness; that so these shores may never be without brave and pious mariners, who will count their lives as worthless in the cause of their Country, their Bible, and their Queen. Amen.”

Then, feeling for his companions’ hands, he walked slowly from the churchyard, across the village street, and up the lane to Burrough gates. The crowd made way for him in solemn silence, as for something awful and solitary—strength and fame shut in, all at once, within a dark prison-house. He seemed to know each turn. At the gates he found the lock with his own hands and opened it, and went boldly forward up the gravel path; while Cary and Brimblecombe followed trembling, for they feared some violent breaking loose of grief, from him or from his mother. Their own tender hearts fluttered like girls’.

Up to the door he went as if he saw it. He felt for the entrance, stood in it, and called quietly, “Mother!”

In a moment his mother was on his bosom. Neither spoke awhile. She sobbed inwardly with tearless eyes; he stood firm and cheerful, his great arms clasping her.

“Mother,” he said at last, “I am come home, you see, because I needs must come. Will you take me in, and look after this useless carcase? I shall not be so very troublesome, mother—shall I?” And he smiled down toward where her face must be, and kissed her brow.

She answered nothing, but slipped her arm gently round his waist and led him in.

“Take care of your head, dear child,” she whispered; “the doors are low.”

They went in together.

“Will! Jack!” Amyas called, turning; but the two good fellows had already walked briskly off.

“I’m glad we are away,” said Cary. “I should have made a baby of myself in another minute, watching that angel of a woman. How her face worked and how she kept it in!”

“Ah, well!” said Jack. “There goes a brave servant of the Queen’s cut off before his work was a quarter done. Heigho! I must home now, and see my old father, and then—”

“And then home with me,” said Cary. “You and I never part again! We have pulled in the same boat too long, Jack; and you must not go spending your prize-money in riotous living. I must see after you, old Jack ashore, or we shall have you treating half the town in taverns for a week to come.”

“Oh, Mr. Cary!” said Jack, scandalized.

“Come home with me, and we’ll poison the parson, and my father shall give you the rectory.”

“Oh, Mr. Cary!” said Jack again; and so the two went off to Clovelly together that very day.

And Amyas sat alone.

His mother had gone out for a little to speak with the seamen who had brought up his luggage, and to set them to eat and drink; and he sat in the old bay-window where he had sat when he was a tiny boy, reading his old books—King Arthur, Fox’s Martyrs, and the Cruelties of the Spaniards. He put out his hand and felt for them: there they lay side by side, as they had lain twenty years before. The window stood open. Cool air came in, bearing the scents that made the house itself seem to have a memory—four-season roses and rosemary, and the sharp clove-sweetness of autumn gilliflowers.

On the table stood a dish of apples. He knew them before he touched them; their smell told him—round, clean, with that bruised-green, sun-warmed perfume that belongs to orchards and childhood. He took one and turned it in his hands, feeling the waxy skin and the little dent where the stem had been. Another followed; he weighed them, rolled them, set them down and drew them back again, as though, by their small honest shapes, he could persuade time to fold itself and come full circle.

As he fingered them, his whole past rose up before him—as men say it flashes before the drowning: places, voices, blows, blessings; the clank of arms, the hiss of arrows, the sea’s long grind against ship’s sides; and then, suddenly, that fairy island on the Meta. The cataract roared again in his mind; he saw palm-tops sleeping in sunlight far above the spray; he felt the smooth trunks beneath his hands as he stepped among boulders fringed with flowers; he leaped down to the gravel beach beside the pool—and there, from fern-grown rock, rose Ayacanora’s vision.

Where was she?

He had not thought of her till now. How he had wronged her. Let be; he had been punished. The account was squared. Perhaps she did not care for him any longer. Who would care for a blind ox like him, who must be fed and tended like a baby for the rest of his lazy life?

He listened: the house was full of small sounds—the faint mutter of voices outside, a creak of timber, the garden’s leaves stirring. Yet in that hush, his own helplessness rang loudest. How long his mother was away.

To steady himself, he began playing again with the apples, thinking of nothing but them and of climbs with Frank in the orchard long ago—hands sticky with juice, pockets heavy, the chatter of boys, the bees’ steady music in the grass. One apple slipped through his fingers and fell to the floor. He stooped and groped; his hands swept boards and found only emptiness.

Vexatious! He turned hastily to search another way and struck his head sharply against the table.

Was it pain? A child’s disappointment? Or the sudden, ridiculous way in which blindness will mock a man—by an apple on a floor and a blow from a table, as though the world had turned clown to show him his doom? Whatever it was, it broke through the last tight holding of his courage. He stamped once, pettishly, and then, checking himself, burst into a violent flood of tears.

A quick rustle passed close to him; the apple was set back into his hand.

“There! there it is!” Ayacanora’s voice sobbed out. “Do not weep! Oh, do not weep! I cannot bear it! I will get you all you want! Only let me fetch and carry for you, tend you, feed you, lead you, like your slave—your dog! Say that I may be your slave!”

She fell on her knees at his feet, seized both his hands, and covered them with kisses.

“Yes!” she cried. “I will be your slave! I must be! You cannot help it! You cannot escape from me now! You cannot go to sea! You cannot turn your back upon wretched me. I have you safe now—safe!”

She clutched his hands with a triumph that turned on itself in an instant.

“Ah! and what a wretch I am,” she sobbed, “to rejoice in that! to taunt him with his blindness! Oh, forgive me! I am but a poor wild girl—a wild Indian savage, you know: but—but—” And she broke down into tears.

A great spasm shook Amyas—body and soul. He sat silent a full minute; then said, very solemnly, “And is this still possible? Then God have mercy upon me a sinner!”

Ayacanora looked up into his face, searching it—trying, as always, to read in English what her own heart could not translate cleanly into any tongue. Before she could speak, he bent down; and lifting her as a lion lifts a lamb, he pressed her to his bosom and covered her face with kisses.

The door opened. There was the rustle of a gown. Ayacanora sprang from him with a little cry and stood half trembling, half defiant, as though to say: He is mine now; no one shall part him from me.

“Who is it?” Amyas asked.

“Your mother.”

“You see that I am bringing forth fruits meet for repentance, mother,” said he, smiling.

He heard her come nearer. Then there was a kiss and a sob between the women; and he felt Ayacanora sink once more upon his bosom.

“Amyas, my son,” said Mrs. Leigh’s silver voice—low, dreamy, like far-off chimes heard across a still moor—“fear not to take her to your heart again; for it is your mother who has laid her there.”

“It is true, after all,” Amyas said within himself. “What God has joined together, man cannot put asunder.”

From that hour Ayacanora’s power of song returned to her. Day by day, year after year, her voice rose within that happy home—sometimes soft as rosemary brushed between the fingers, sometimes keen as the sea-wind in the tower, always living—and it carried the blind giant’s thoughts back toward the West, along the wake of those heroes who from that time forth sailed out to plant another and vaster England, to the heaven-prospered cry of Westward-Ho!
