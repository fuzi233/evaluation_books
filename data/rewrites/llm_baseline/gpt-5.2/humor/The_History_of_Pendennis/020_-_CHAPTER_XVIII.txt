Every man who has ever worn a cap too large for his wisdom, and a gown too long for his discretion, carries somewhere about him—like a nutmeg in his pocket, or a remembered smell of toast in a childhood passage—the tenderness of his University days. Youth begins there with a clink of keys and bells: the leading-strings are cut, and a fellow fancies he is a free agent because nobody tells him, this hour, not to put his elbows on the table.

And he drinks his first draught of liberty as if it were new cider—sharp, sparkling, unwholesome in quantity, and delicious beyond all reason. Old epicures, who are reduced by time and circumstance to nibbling poached eggs and pretending that water has a bouquet, can still take a keen pleasure in seeing young jaws work honestly; and I hope there is no age so crabbed, no experience so sour, that a man cannot be amused by the sound of a boy laughing at the world as though it were made for his entertainment.

Only a few weeks ago, coming back from a visit to the old University of Oxbridge (where my friend Mr. Arthur Pendennis once went to be improved), I was packed in a railroad carriage beside a young gentleman now studying at Saint Boniface. He had acquired an exeat by some miracle known only to college authorities and their victims, and was bound for a day’s riot in London. He talked from the first jolt to the last—cracking his jokes like walnuts, spilling his laughter as freely as a cook spills flour—till, when we reached the terminus, nothing would content him but a hansom, that he might be pitched into town the quicker and begin at once to waste his time with the most industrious zeal.

Off he went, whipped away into smoke and hurry, his face lit up as if London were a pie just taken from the oven. As for your humble servant—being encumbered only with a small carpet-bag and no appetite whatever for haste—I climbed to the outside of an omnibus and sat contentedly between a Jew-pedlar smoking a cigar that had clearly been steeped in old boots, and a gentleman’s servant who guarded a poodle as if it were the heir to an earldom. We waited until Fate filled the seats and lashed on the boxes; then the coachman rumbled away at a pace suited to digestion. We were not in a hurry to plunge into that near smoking Babylon. The pedlar did not dream of dining at a club; the servant did not long to dance at a casino. A few years more, and my young railroad companion will be no more eager either; he will have learned that pleasure, like porter, loses something when it is kept too long.

There were no railroads when Arthur Pendennis went up to Oxbridge. He travelled thither in a handsome coach, thick with dons, gownsmen, freshmen, and the guardians who conveyed them like parcels labelled FRAGILE. Major Pendennis sat within beside a fat City gentleman in grey stockings, whose pale-faced son—destined, no doubt, for great things in account-books—looked as if he had already been lectured into an early grave. The old citizen was frightened out of his propriety when he learned that the coach had been driven for a couple of stages by young Mr. Foker of Saint Boniface: a friend of all mankind (coachmen included), able to handle reins with the air of a man who has been born in a box-seat and christened with a whip.

Pen sat on the roof, peering at everything—coach, passengers, hedges, clouds—with the delighted hunger of a boy who has never yet been overfed with sights. When Oxbridge came in view, his heart gave such a jump you might have heard it above the rattle of wheels: venerable towers and pinnacles, tall elms, a shining river laid out like silver sauce beside the old brown city roofs. It looked to him like a dish long promised and at last set upon the table.

He had spent a few days in London first at his uncle’s lodgings in Bury Street. Major Pendennis declared the lad’s wardrobe wanted renewal; and Arthur, who could appreciate a good coat as readily as a good compliment, was by no means averse. The self-denying uncle made sacrifices in the boy’s behalf with that touching heroism which costs the martyr nothing but other people’s money.

London, in those days, was awfully lonely in the season. Pall Mall’s pavement seemed deserted; the very red jackets had migrated; faces in club windows were as scarce as pheasants in a poulterer’s shop on a bad day. The Major took his nephew into one or two of those quiet mansions, wrote down his name in a candidate-book, and thereby caused Pen a thrill of gratification so intense that it might almost have served as a dinner.

There, in cold parchment dignity, he saw himself set down as “Arthur Pendennis, Esquire, of Fairoaks Lodge, ——shire, and Saint Boniface College, Oxbridge; proposed by Major Pendennis; seconded by Viscount Colchicum.” Pen’s imagination instantly furnished the stucco-halls with his own future ease; he walked through libraries as if they were already his larders, and surveyed drawing-rooms with the proprietary eye of a young gentleman who had not yet learned that a door may be shut upon him. The Major laughed slyly at the boy’s strut; vanity in a comely lad always pleases an old man who has spent his life cultivating it as an art.

Pen and Foker drove down in the latter’s cab to Grey Friars to renew acquaintance with old comrades. The boys came crowding to the gates to admire the chestnut horse, the livery, the tights, and the grave countenance of Stoopid the tiger, who sat behind like a small footman nailed to a plank. The afternoon bell rang as they swaggered in the playground. The awful Doctor passed into school, grammar in hand. Foker shrank away, uneasy as a boy caught near a jam-closet; but Pen, blushing, went up and shook the dignitary’s hand, laughing inwardly to remember how that same Latin Grammar had once boxed his ears. He was generous, good-natured, and, like most generous good-natured youths, perfectly satisfied with himself.

Then to the parental brew-house: Foker’s Entire—an empire of vats and brick, with its gilded name shining from innumerable public-house signs like a lord’s arms on his tenants’ doors. The venerable junior partner and manager did honour to the young lord of the mash-tuns and his friend; and served them with silver flagons of brown stout so potent that you would have thought the very horse drank by sympathy. In truth, the animal rushed home westward at a pace which endangered pie-stalls and crossing-sweepers, brought cab-steps into alarming intimacy with corner-posts, and caused Stoopid to swing and clutch like a man clinging to a ship’s yard in a gale.

The Major was delighted to see Pen in such company. He listened to Foker’s artless stories with solemn approval; gave the two boys a fine dinner at a Covent Garden coffee-house—where the smell of hot joints and chopped herbs rose like incense, and the knives made their pleasant music on plates—and then took them to the play. But his chief bliss was obtained when Mr. and Lady Agnes Foker, being in London, requested the pleasure of Major Pendennis and Mr. Arthur Pendennis at dinner in Grosvenor Street.

“Having obtained the entrée into Lady Agnes Foker’s house,” the Major said to Arthur with affectionate solemnity, as if he were presenting him with a sacrament, “it behoves you, my dear boy, to keep it. Never neglect to call in Grosvenor Street when you come to London. Read up carefully, in Debrett, the alliances and genealogy of the Earls of Rosherville; and if you can, make some trifling allusions to the family—something historical, neat, complimentary: the sort of thing you, with a poetic fancy, can manage. Mr. Foker himself is worthy enough, though not of high extraction nor much education. He always makes a point of having some of the family porter served round after dinner; you will on no account refuse it—which I shall drink myself, though all beer disagrees with me confoundedly.”

And the heroic martyr did indeed sacrifice himself, and swallowed the porter with a face arranged for suffering, while old Mr. Foker, at the head of the table, made his time-honoured joke about Foker’s Entire. One would have given a guinea to see Major Pendennis’s grin at that venerable witticism—so sleek, so thankful, so calculated.

Lady Agnes, wrapped up in her son as a joint is wrapped in bacon, received Pen with cordiality, and astonished him by accounts of the severe studies her darling was pursuing, and which she feared might injure his precious health. Mr. Foker the elder burst into a horse-laugh at some of these speeches; and the heir winked knowingly at his friend over the tureen. Then her ladyship, beginning at Harry’s infancy and continuing without drawing breath, related his miraculous sufferings in measles and hooping-cough, his escape from drowning, the shocking tyrannies practised upon him at that horrid school (to which Mr. Foker would send him because he had been brought up there himself), and how she never would forgive that disagreeable Doctor—no, never. For an hour she ladled out maternal narrative as steadily as if she were serving soup.

When pheasants came with the second course—hot, rich, scented with their own wildness and the butter that had kissed them in the pan—the Major praised them as the finest birds he ever saw; and Lady Agnes said they came from Logwood (as the Major knew perfectly well), and begged both gentlemen would visit there—at Christmas, or whenever dear Harry was at home.

“God bless you, my dear boy,” said Major Pendennis to Arthur afterwards in Bury Street, as they lit their candles to go to bed. “You made that little allusion to Agincourt, where one of the Roshervilles distinguished himself, very neatly and well; though Lady Agnes did not quite understand it. Exceedingly well for a beginner—though you ought not to blush so, by the way. And remember through life: with an entrée—with a good entrée, mind—it is just as easy to have good society as bad. It costs a man, when properly introduced, no more trouble to keep his footing in the best houses than to dine with a lawyer in Bedford Square. Mind this at Oxbridge, pursuing your studies; and for Heaven’s sake be very particular in the acquaintances you make. The premier pas in life is the most important of all—did you write to your mother to-day?—No?—well, do, before you go, and call and ask Mr. Foker for a frank—They like it—Good night. God bless you.”

Pen wrote a droll account to his dearest mother: of London’s emptiness, the play, the visit to the old Friars, the brewery stout with its black froth, the Grosvenor Street dinner with its pheasants and its porter. At home, in the lonely house at Fairoaks, his mother said her prayers with a heart full of love that had no words for itself; and she and Laura read that letter, and those which followed, many times, and brooded over them as women do—turning each line as though it were a slice of bread to see where the sweetness lay.

It was Pen’s first step. Ah, what a dangerous journey begins with a first step, and how often the brave stumble where they meant to stride. Brother wayfarer, may you have a kind arm linked in yours; may truth guide, mercy forgive at the end, and love go always with you. Without that lamp, the road is blind indeed, and the night very black.

So the coach drove up to the ancient and comfortable inn called the Trencher, in Main Street, Oxbridge. Pen, with eagerness, remarked for the first time gownsmen going about; chapel bells clinking—for in Oxbridge the bells ring from morning-tide till even-song, a continual silver rain of sound that freshens the spirit if one is not too fashionable to hear it. How different from London’s iron uproar, its cart-rattle and hammer-clang and shouting—noise without meaning, as if the city had forgotten its own language and spoke only in wheels. Here the sound had lineage; it belonged to the place as yeast belongs to old dough, and made the air itself seem alive.

Towers and pinnacles rose calm over gables and antique roofs, above a homely, busy city that smelt of river damp, old stone warmed by sun, ink, leather, and dinners. Communications had passed between Dr. Portman (on Pen’s part) and Mr. Buck, Tutor of Boniface, with whom Pen was entered. As soon as Major Pendennis had arranged his personal appearance into that expression which might convey at once gentility and authority, he and his nephew walked down Main Street, passed Saint George’s great gate and belfry-tower, and came to Saint Boniface.

Again Pen’s heart beat as they entered at the wicket of the venerable ivy-mantled gate. Above it rose an ancient dome almost hidden by creepers; the effigy of the Saint looked down with a stone serenity, while coats-of-arms of royal and noble benefactors told, in painted heraldry, stories that many a modern tongue could no longer pronounce. The porter pointed out a queer old tower at the quadrangle corner by which Mr. Buck’s rooms were approached. They crossed the square, and Pen’s eyes drank in everything with the thirst of first impressions: the little fountain playing in the centre, making its own cool music; the tall chapel windows and buttresses to the right; the hall with its tapering lantern and oriel window; the lodge doors, from which the Master issued with rustling silks; the carved chimneys, grey turrets, quaint gables. The Major surveyed all with calmness, having a gentleman’s indifference to picturesqueness and eyes somewhat dimmed by the perpetual glare of Pall Mall.

Saint George’s, huge and consecrated, with its four vast quadrangles, its beautiful hall and gardens, lay near and gave its men airs. The Georgians wore gowns of a peculiar cut and superiority of a still more peculiar. Little Saint Boniface was but a hermitage beside that great pile; yet, for its size, it kept an excellent name. Its ton was good; the best families of certain counties had time out of mind sent their young men there; the college livings were remarkably comfortable; fellowships easy; Boniface men had a generous share of honours; their boat was third on the river; their chapel-choir not inferior to Saint George’s; and, best point of all, their ale the best in Oxbridge.

In the wainscoted College Hall, and about Roubilliac’s statue of Saint Boniface (who stood in seraphic benediction over uncommonly good cheer), portraits of eminent Bonifacians looked down at dinner with painted gravity. There was learned Doctor Griddle, who suffered in Henry VIII.’s time, and Archbishop Bush who roasted him; Lord Chief Justice Hicks; the Duke of St. David’s, K.G., Chancellor of the University; Sprott the Poet, of whose fame the college was proud; Doctor Blogg, late Master and friend of Doctor Johnson. Their coats-of-arms flashed emerald and ruby, gold and azure in the tall windows; a whole vocabulary of rank and memory—much of it, like forgotten dialects, meaningful only to the initiated, and yet powerful enough to make a freshman sit up straighter as if grammar and genealogy were one.

The venerable cook of the college was one of the best artists in Oxbridge (his son had taken highest honours at Camford), and the wine in the fellows’ room was famed for excellence and abundance. Into this snug arbour among the groves of Academe Pen now found his way, leaning on his uncle’s arm, and they reached Mr. Buck’s rooms.

Mr. Buck, having received previous information from Dr. Portman, was cordial to the young freshman and his guardian. Portman had described Arthur as “a young gentleman of some fortune and landed estate, of one of the most ancient families in the kingdom, and possessing such a character and genius as were sure, under proper guidance, to make him a credit to the college and the university.” Under such recommendations, the tutor invited the Major to dine in hall, that he might see his nephew wear gown and eat commons for the first time; requested them to take wine afterwards; and—upon the strength of Arthur’s reported merits—offered him the best set of rooms in college: a gentleman-pensioner’s set, luckily vacant.

They parted till dinner, which was near at hand. Major Pendennis pronounced Mr. Buck uncommonly civil indeed; and when a college magnate chooses to be polite, no man can be more splendidly courteous. These reverend gentlemen, immersed in books and excluded from the world, have a solemn magnificence of compliment; when they rustle and swell in their state manners, you feel you are being bowed to by the University itself.

On returning into Mr. Buck’s anteroom—handsome, turkey-carpeted, hung with excellent prints and richly framed pictures—they found the tutor’s servant waiting, with a man bearing a bag full of caps and a number of gowns. Pen was in a tremor of pleasure as the bustling tailor tried on a gown and pronounced it an excellent fit. Then Arthur set the pretty college cap on his head with a dandified touch, a little on one side, as he had seen Fiddicombe, the youngest master at Grey Friars, wear it. He inspected himself in one of the great gilt mirrors. Some college divines, it appears, are no more above a looking-glass than ladies are, and consider the set of a gown with an anxiety almost devotional.

The Major smiled to see the boy dandifying. He was not displeased with the comely lad’s appearance. Then Davis, the skip, led the way across the quadrangle, keys jingling like a small bell-chorus, and brought them to the rooms destined for the freshman, vacated by the retreat of the gentleman-pensioner Mr. Spicer.

They were comfortable rooms, with large cross-beams, high wainscots, and small windows sunk in deep embrasures. Mr. Spicer’s furniture remained, to be sold at a valuation. Major Pendennis agreed to take what was available, though he and Pen both laughingly declined six sporting prints and four groups of opera-dancers with gauze draperies that formed the late occupant’s pictorial collection. A man can be a gentleman-pensioner, it seems, without pensioning his taste.

Then they went to hall. Pen sat down with his brother freshmen and ate his commons—bread with honest crust, meat properly browned, the steam smelling of pepper and fat and that indefinable comfort which is called dinner when one is young. Above, the Major took his place at high table among dignitaries and other fathers or guardians who had brought their sons up to Oxbridge and now watched them begin to feed as if watching them begin to live.

After hall they went to Mr. Buck’s to take wine; after wine to chapel, where the Major sat with gravity in the upper place, with a fine view of the Master in his carved throne under the organ-loft. Doctor Donne sat magnificent with the great prayer-book before him—statuesque piety in silk and solemnity. The freshmen behaved with decorum; but Pen was shocked to see that atrocious little Foker, arriving very late, and half a dozen comrades in the gentleman-pensioners’ seats giggling and talking as if they were in stalls at the Opera. These things, remember, happened some years back when William the Fourth was king. Young men are much better now—besides, Saint Boniface had the name of being rather fast.

Pen could hardly sleep that night at the Trencher, so eager was he to begin his college life and get into his own apartments. What did he think of as he tossed? His mother at home—pious soul whose life was bound up in his? Yes, let us hope he thought of her a little. Miss Fotheringay and his eternal passion? That passion which had kept him awake so many nights and made him wretched with longing? He had a trick of blushing; and had you been there (the candle not out), you might have seen his face redden more than once as he broke into incoherent exclamations regarding that luckless episode.

His uncle’s lessons had not been thrown away upon him: the mist of passion had lifted, and he saw her as she was. To think he—Pendennis—had been enslaved by such a woman, and then jilted by her! That he should have stooped so low to be trampled in the mire! That there was a time—only months back—when he was willing to take Costigan for his father-in-law! “Poor old Smirke!” Pen presently laughed out. “Well, I’ll write and try to console the poor old boy. He won’t die of his passion—ha, ha!”

The Major, had he been awake, might have heard a score of such ejaculations. Perhaps it would have been better, on the eve of the battle of his life, to have kept a different vigil; but the world had got hold of Pen in the shape of his selfish old Mentor. Those who care for his character must already have seen that he was weak as well as impetuous, vain as well as frank; generous, yet a little selfish amid his profuseness; and fickle too, as eager pursuers of self-gratification often are. Six months’ passion had aged him; there was a gulf between Pen the victim of love and Pen the innocent boy of eighteen sighing after it. He came to Oxbridge with a sense of superiority already, an experience that was not wisdom but had the insolent air of it; and that conceit and imperiousness, which afterwards grew upon him, gave him a kind of command over the young men with whom he now began to live.

In the morning he and his uncle passed hours with great satisfaction making purchases to render the rooms more comfortable. Mr. Spicer’s china and glass were in a dreadfully dismantled condition; his lamps smashed; his bookcases too small for the shelves that would be required to receive the contents of the boxes lying at Fairoaks, addressed to Arthur in the hand of poor Helen.

Those boxes arrived in a few days—packed by his mother with loving care. Pen was touched as he read the superscriptions in that dear well-known hand. He set everything in its place: books—old friends—linen and table-cloths chosen from the family stock; jam-pots which little Laura had bound in straw; a hundred simple gifts of home, smelling faintly of pantry and lavender and the quiet dialect of Fairoaks that no Oxbridge man could ever speak quite right.

Pen had another Alma Mater now.

But it is not all children who take to her kindly.
