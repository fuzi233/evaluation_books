Nearly a year has slipped by since the last time we saw Arthur Pendennis in a state of sartorial distress. Now, the black coat is retired to make way for a dashing blue frockcoat, and the wig—a heavy burden of the past—is finally laid aside. Though his natural hair, having suffered a slight thinning, has been allowed to re-enter the public sphere, it looks far less like a broom-brush than it did previously. Most significantly, Arthur has earned the honor of appearing at Court in the uniform of a Cornet in the Clavering troop of the Yeomanry Cavalry, presented to the Sovereign by the formidable Marquis of Steyne.

This elevation was a measure vigorously, and somewhat pathetically, pushed by Arthur's uncle. The Major simply refused to let a single year go by without this ceremony of "gentlemanhood." To the old gentleman, it seemed imperative that his nephew abandon the rather unsavory Megatherium Club for something far more select. Indeed, the Major has loudly proclaimed his disappointment across the globe regarding the financial prospects of his nephew, noting dryly that the estate has failed to materialize into the windfall he had hoped for, currently yielding a modest income of just under fifteen hundred a year.

And so, this is the figure by which the world measures Pendennis. Where once publishers treated him with suspicion, they now regard him with a newfound, if slightly condescending, respect. Even mothers have ceased to be outright rude. The logic is sound: if the prettier daughters are destined to marry men of vastly superior expectations, then at least the plain ones remain available. Since the dazzling Myra is rumored to be snaring an Earl, it follows that poor little Beatrice—who possesses a distinct asymmetry in her shoulders—must settle for a boor throughout her life. Why, then, should not Mr. Pendennis serve as her support?

In the first winter following his mother's inheritance, Mrs. Hawxby, residing in a country house, arranged for Beatrice to learn billiards from Arthur. Furthermore, she insisted on driving exclusively in his pony carriage, reasoning that both she and Beatrice shared a "literary" sensibility. She publicly declared that the young man, allegedly instigated by his "horrid old uncle," had behaved with infamous treachery toward Beatrice's affections. The reality, however, was that the elderly Major, possessing a keen understanding of Mrs. Hawxby's predatory nature and her habit of preying on unwary youths, had personally intervened. He had rescued Arthur from the immediate clutches of the lady at the country house, saving him from her claws, if not from the sharp sting of her tongue. While the elder Pendennis had wished for his nephew to spend Christmas at Clavering, Arthur lacked the fortitude. Clavering was simply too close to the desolate ruins of poor old Fairoaks, a place saturated with memories too sad to revisit.

Our view of the Claverings has waned until their sudden reappearance at the Epsom racecourse, necessitating a brief retrospective on their tumultuous year. The world, it seems, had not treated any member of the family with particular kindness. Lady Clavering, historically renowned for her excellent digestion and impeccable grammar, found her appetites and temperaments severely tested by relentless domestic strife. Her grievances were so profound that they rendered the finest French cuisine unpalatable and the softest sofa cushions intolerably hard. "I'd sooner eat a turnup for dessert," she lamented to her faithful companion, staring vacantly at a table laden with pineapples and Muscatel grapes, "than endure that pineapple. If only I could enjoy my meal in silence. How infinitely happier I was when I was a widow, before all this money descended upon me!"

The Clavering family had, in essence, made a disastrous start. They offered hospitality without guidance, position, or gratitude, receiving no return of kindness from those they entertained. Their initial London season was questionable, followed swiftly by a notorious failure. Gossip ran rampant: "Human patience was not sufficient to tolerate Sir Francis Clavering." He was deemed hopelessly low, dull, and disreputable. Whispers abounded regarding the Begum, a woman of immense wealth lacking the ability to pronounce 'h', and her origins remained a mystery. Her daughter was criticized for a bizarre blend of Gallic affectation and conceit, unfit for association with well-bred English girls. The circle surrounding them was filled with strangers. Sir Francis, a confirmed gambler, was seen in the company of blacklegs and profligates. Even Hely Clinker, serving in his regiment, was accused of cheating at cards and displaying the "white feather." When Lady Rockminster eventually abandoned Lady Clavering, the great ladies refused to bring their daughters to her parties. The young men attending these gatherings behaved with an odious freedom and scornful familiarity. Consequently, Lady Clavering was forced to admit she had opened the door to "the canal"—the lower sort of society—in her drawing-room, as the elite simply would not visit.

Yet, Lady Clavering harbored no malice toward "the canal," nor did she suffer from excessive pride. She had blindly followed the social dictates given to her by her godmother upon entering the world: to know whom they knew, and ask whom they asked. While "the canal" was often pleasanter than high society, leaving a mistress is easy, whereas being cast off by her is cruel. One can forsake society with a sense of relief, but if society casts you out, the resulting mortification is severe.

Among the fashionable youth, Harry Foker, Esquire, stood out as potentially faithful amidst the faithless. However, he had mishandled his affair with Priscilla Pen. What began as a secret passion became notorious, reaching the ears of his weak and doting mother, and ultimately landing him before the stern gaze of the bald, inflexible Mr. Foker Senior. When the father learned of this scandal, a violent confrontation ensued, culminating in the younger gentleman's banishment from England for a year. He was ordered to return and either marry his cousin or retire to private life with an allowance of three hundred a year, forever barred from seeing his parents or the brewery. Mr. Henry Foker departed, carrying a sorrow and anxiety that passes freely through customs and is proverbial among exiles. To him, even the Parisian Boulevards appeared gloomy, and the Italian skies turned black.

For Sir Francis Clavering, that same year proved calamitous. As our sporting acquaintances recall, Lord Harrowhill's horse, a classical nobleman named Podasokus, won the Derby, to the dismay of the wise who had backed the non-existent favorite, Borax. Sir Francis, intimately connected with the rascally characters of the turf and possessing "valuable information," had heavily wagered against the winner and freely supported the favorite. The result, as his son later admitted to the stricken Lady Clavering, was a staggering loss of seven thousand pounds.

This was a devastating blow to the lady, who had already discharged her husband's debts countless times. Having received numerous oaths of reform, paid his money-lenders and horse-dealers, and furnished both town and country residences, she was now summoned to meet this colossal sum, the penalty for her husband's cowardice and extravagance.

Earlier, the Major Pendennis had acted as an advisor to the Claverings, inspecting every room of their home, including the dreaded closet where, per the proverb, the family skeleton is locked away. Regarding the Baronet's finances, the Major admitted ignorance, not due to lack of inquiry, but because Sir Francis himself hid them in a hopeless labyrinth of lies, making it impossible for any attorney or adviser to discern the truth. However, concerning Lady Clavering, the Major was well-informed. Upon the misfortune of the Derby, he had meticulously investigated her means, learning of the vast sacrifices the widow Amory had made for her current husband.

He openly expressed, winning considerable favor from Miss Blanche in doing so, his belief that Lady Clavering's daughter had been unfairly treated financially by her second marriage. He gently suggested to the lady that Miss Blanche deserved a better provision. The Major had previously indicated to the widow that he was fully aware of her early, tragic history in India, specifically the circumstances leading to her separation from her first husband. He could direct her to the Calcutta newspaper detailing Amory's trial. Moreover, he demonstrated to the Begum his forbearance in keeping this knowledge silent, maintaining friendship with the family throughout.

"My dear Lady Clavering," he remarked, "interest may drive me, and surely we all act from interest. My motive, which I need not hide, is to arrange a marriage between my nephew and your daughter." To this, the surprised Lady Clavering readily agreed.

Frankly, however, the Major continued, "My dear lady, my boy earns merely five hundred a year. A wife with a fortune of ten thousand pounds would barely improve his situation. I am convinced we could do far better for him. He is a shrewd, cautious young man who has sown his wild oats; he possesses good parts and ample ambition. His objective in marrying is clearly self-improvement. If you and Sir Francis agree—and trust me, Sir Francis will not refuse anything—you could place Arthur in a position to advance considerably.
