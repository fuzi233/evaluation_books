Margaret Brandt had always kept herself apart from the close-knit, gossiping circle of Sevenbergen’s villagers, and her quiet reserve had long been misread as cold, haughty pride. Word of that judgment had reached her ears months before, and she knew full well how many hearts swelled with petty jealousy and hidden ill will toward her. What triumph they would take, she thought, when her condition could no longer be hidden from even the most casual passerby! That thought gnawed at her waking hours and haunted her sleep, driving her to shut herself inside their small cottage for days on end, shunning even the daylight on the rare occasions she dared step outdoors.

She did not mistake her own moral position, as many unlearned folk around her might have. Though she did not know the fine points of contemporary ecclesiastical law, she knew betrothal was a binding marriage contract, no more breakable by either party than a witnessed, signed legal deed. She knew that in past times, a marriage to a third party after a formal betrothal had been annulled by both Church and State, and that many betrothed couples had come together without further ceremony, their children recognized as fully legitimate. What weighed on her simple, earnest medieval mind was this: the physical paper of that betrothal contract was not in her keeping. Gerard had carried it away with him, and Gerard was leagues and leagues off, somewhere in distant Italy. She hated herself for that careless, fatal oversight that left her entirely at the mercy of cruel, false local opinion. Though she had never read Horace’s famous line about how firsthand visible harms strike deeper than words far away, her sharp, unclouded good sense saw the world exactly as it was. Her “marriage lines” hidden out of sight across the Alps would never balance the visible, unmissable proof of her pregnancy, and the child that would soon be born. What flimsy tale was that to still slanderous tongues? “I have my marriage contract, but I cannot show it to you.” What woman in all Sevenbergen would believe her, even pretend to? And as one of the most modest, soft-spoken girls in all Holland, it was the good opinion of other women she craved far more than that of men. Slander of that sort always strikes women with a cruel advantage, but here the slander would carry the whole visible weight of apparent truth behind it.

The “strong-minded woman” had not yet been invented in that age, and Margaret, though resolute and capable from having run her father’s household single-handed since she was sixteen, was soft as water in other matters, weakest of all when facing the threat of public shame. Like so many gentle, virtuous women of her station, she was a fragile little leaf trembling at every gust of public opinion, true or false. No pages of a man’s descriptive prose could ever fully paint the quiet, searing anguish of a virtuous woman who sees months of good reputation crumbling before her, and foresees herself paraded through the village as a fallen, frail thing. Had she been frail at heart, she might have brazened the gossip out. But she was pure as new-fallen snow, and she could feel the dark pitch of public shaming creeping closer, day by day.

She would sit listless for hours at a time, quiet moans of anguish catching in her throat, and when her old father spoke to her, she would answer mechanically, her mind miles away. More than once her cheeks would suddenly burn bright as fire mid-conversation, and Peter would stare, confused, wondering what harmless thing he had said to upset her. Nothing he said mattered at all. It was the ever-lurking dread of shame that sent heat flooding to her face, no matter what she pretended to talk or think about.

The fear and shame peaked the night she came home from Margaret Van Eyck’s. Her condition had been discovered, and by other women. The old reclusive artist might keep her secret, but Catherine — Gerard’s own mother — a lifelong gossip at the centre of a sprawling family and a crowded neighbourhood? Only one small spark of hope remained: Catherine had spoken to her that day with kindness, even warmth. There was no middle path for the woman who had raised the man she was betrothed to: she would either be her staunchest ally, or her worst enemy. Margaret waited in racking anxiety for days for word from her. No message came. She gave up all hope. Catherine would not stand by her. She would expose her, surely, when the natural love of chattering about a neighbour’s secret outweighed any faint kind feeling she might once have held.

That was when the wild, hungry urge to flee the neighbourhood took root in her, growing till it was a feverish, all-consuming passion. But how could she persuade her old, infirm father, who had lived in Sevenbergen his whole life, to pull up all his roots and move? The only choice left was to confide in him. It was far better than running away and leaving him behind. Trapped between her overwhelming need to hide, and her utter inability to speak of her condition out loud to any man, even her own father, she swung back and forth in torturous, endless suspense.

Then one fatal day, the terrible thought came to her: end it all. Many of the canals that laced the Low Countries were deep, dark, and still, that bogeyman Catherine had warned her of a hundred times, and for a moment they tempted her sorely. She stood on the bank one soft afternoon, staring down at the calm dark water that looked like perfect, endless rest. No more trouble. No more fear of shame. If Gerard had not loved her, she would surely have stepped into that water and ended it all there. As it was, she dropped to her knees on the bank, and prayed fervently to God to chase the wicked thought from her. “Oh, selfish wretch,” she whispered to herself, “to leave your poor old father all alone. Oh, wicked wretch, to kill your unborn child, and make Gerard lose every bit of that pain and peril he crossed the Alps through to come back to you. I will tell father all, ay, before this sun sets.”

She hurried home as fast as her feet could carry her, terrified her brave resolution would leak away before she got there. Wise old Peter, the scholar and healer, was as simple as a child in all domestic matters, and Margaret had ruled their household gently but absolutely since she was sixteen. It was a strange, unheard of sight in that little cottage, to see its steady, usually self-assured young mistress faltering, stammering, irresolute as she approached the quiet old man who held far less sway over the running of their home than the local blacksmith Martin Wittenhaagen, or even the little scullery maid who came each morning to wash their dishes for a pittance.

“Father,” she said, twisting her hands together, “I would speak to thee.”
“Speak on, girl,” he said, not looking up from his book of herbs.
“Wilt listen to me? And — and — not be angry? Wilt thou try to excuse my faults?”
“We all have our faults, Margaret. Thou hast no more than the rest of us, fewer still, unless a father’s fondness blinds me.”
“Alas, no, father,” she wept. “I am a poor foolish girl, that wanted only to do well, but have done ill — very ill, most unwisely — and now I must bear the shame. But father, I love you, in spite of all my faults. Will you not forgive my folly, and still love your motherless girl?”
“Thou mayest count on that, lass,” Peter said cheerfully.
“Oh, do not smile! How can I speak and make you sad if you smile at me so?”
“Why, what is all this coil about?” he asked, setting his book aside.
“Father, disgrace is coming to this house. It is at the very door. And I am the one at fault. Turn your head away, please. I — I — father, I let Gerard take away our marriage lines.”
“Is that all? ‘Twas only a silly oversight,” he said, relieved.
“‘Twas the act of a madwoman. But woe is me — that is not the worst.”
“The lad is honest, and loves you dearly,” Peter interrupted. “What is a year or two apart for two young folk? Gerard will come back, you may be sure, and keep his troth.”
“And meantime do you know what is coming?” Margaret wailed.
“Not I, except that I will be dead and gone long before any harm comes to you, at my age,” he said.
“It is worse than death. There is worse pain than dying. For pity’s sake, turn your head away, father.”
“Silly wench,” Peter muttered, but he turned his face away as she asked.

Margaret trembled so hard she could barely stand, her cheeks burning bright as embers. “I looked on Gerard as my husband, we being betrothed — and he was in such sore danger, and I thought I had killed him, and I — oh, if only you were my mother, I might find the courage to speak it out! You would ask me gentle questions, but you say not a word.”
“Why, Margaret, what is all this fuss about? Why are your cheeks bright crimson, speaking only to your own old father, no stranger in sight?”
“Why are my cheeks on fire? Because — because — father, kill me! Send me to heaven! Bid Martin shoot me through the heart with his arrow! Then the gossips will come and tell you why I blush so today. And when I am dead, I hope you will love your girl again for her mother’s sake.”
“Give me thy hand, mistress,” Peter said, his tone a little stern.

She put her trembling hand out to him. He took it gently, his face softening with quiet concern, and felt her racing, fluttering pulse.
“Alas, no,” she said, pulling away. “It is my soul that burns, not my body. I cannot, will not, stay in Sevenbergen another day.” She wrung her hands, impatient and distraught.
“Calm thyself, child,” the old man soothed her. “Do not torment thyself for nothing. You do not want to stay in Sevenbergen? There is no need to stay even a single day more if it vexes thee so and puts thee in a fever, for fevered thou art, deny it not.”
“What!” Margaret cried, staring at him in shock. “You would agree to leave this place, and — and ask no reason but that I long to be gone?” She threw herself on her knees beside him, clutching his sleeve, then his arm, then his shoulder in a fervour of desperate supplication, begging him to leave the village and never ask her why. “Alas, what need to tell you? You will see it soon enough. And I could never speak the words out loud. I would rather die.”
“Foolish child, who would pry into your girlish secrets? Not me. I have spent my whole life searching for the secrets of nature, not pestering my own daughter for hers. And as for leaving Sevenbergen? What is there to keep me here, if it makes thee miserable? Do the villagers respect me? Thank me? They call me quacksalver behind my back, and wizard to my face when they need me to heal their sick, and give all their praise and coin to the charlatans that kill them with their bad potions. Besides, what does it matter to me where we make our home? Choose wherever you wish, just as your mother did before you.”

Margaret hugged him tight, and wept against his shoulder. She was respited. Even as she cried in relief, she almost wished she had found the courage to tell him the full shameful secret. After a little while, he insisted she take a calming draught of poppy and lavender simples he had brewed. She took it submissively, to please him, and though he gave it in the mistaken belief that her upset was nothing but a nervous fever, it did her far more good than harm. She woke after a long, deep, restful sleep, calm at last, and began her preparations to leave that very day.

The next week, they left Sevenbergen bag and baggage, and took lodgings above a tailor’s shop on Brede-Kirk Straet in Rotterdam. Only one man in Tergou knew exactly where they had gone: the Burgomaster. He locked that secret tight in his own breast. The use he would make of it before long, not even he could guess yet. But time will show all things in the end.
