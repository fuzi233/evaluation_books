“Where be the true men?” The cry reverberated through the shattered timbers of the inn, a harsh but welcome polyphony that cut through the oppressive, blood-heavy silence of the room. 

“Here be we. God bless you all! God bless you!” 

A rush of heavy leather boots thundered against the stairs, generating a resonant acoustic warmth as half-a-dozen calloused, flour-dusted and earth-stained hands reached out in the flaring torchlight. The glittering breastplates of the archers and their deeply bronzed faces, weathered by seasons of sun and agrarian toil, stood in stark contrast to the pale, bloodless cheeks of the bound thieves. Nearby lay the bleeding giant, his massive form resting in a pooling glaze of his own gore, a grim harvest now still. 

Gerard moved among the archers, his eyes glistening as he took each man by the hand, reading the native dialects of their regions in the rough texture of their palms. They kissed him, a traditional, deeply rooted gesture of life affirming itself against the void, and he kissed them in return. Then, turning to a handsome archer of his own age, he murmured, “Prithee, good soldier, have an eye to me. A strange drowsiness overcomes me. Let no one cut my throat while I sleep—for pity’s sake.” The archer laughed, a hearty, baritone sound, assuming it a jest. Yet Gerard, retreating from the sensory overload and the harsh dissonance of the night’s violence, immediately slipped into a profound, anchoring slumber, his breathing adding a rhythmic, cyclical bass note to the room. 

Denys observed this retreat but did not interfere, turning instead to the immediate, visceral needs of the body. Two archers were currently inspecting the fallen giant, nudging him with their boots, their voices echoing with practical curiosity as they asked which of the two had flung such an enormous rogue from the upper storey, eager to learn the mechanics of the feat. Denys initially scoffed, wary of boasting lest Gerard wake and attribute the survival to the mythic intervention of Heaven, diminishing Denys’s own physical grounding in the act. But seeing Gerard firmly tethered to the dream-world, Denys spoke with the unvarnished precision of a seasoned butcher explaining a necessary slaughter.

“Well, then, you see, comrades, I had run my sword through this one up to the hilt, and one or two more of 'em came buzzing about me; so it behoved me have my sword or die. I just put my foot against his stomach, gave a tug with my hand and a spring with my foot, and sent him flying to kingdom come! He died in the air, and his carrion rolled in amongst you without ceremony. Made you jump, I warrant me. But pikestaves and pillage! What avails prattling of these trifles once they are gone by? *Buvons, camarades, buvons.*” 

To Denys, survival demanded sustenance; wine was not mere fuel, but a profound liquid memory of the earth, a necessary fermentation to wash away the metallic tang of adrenaline. The archers grumbled that declaring “*buvons*” was easy where no vintage flowed. 

“Nay, I'll soon find you liquor,” Denys countered, his olfactory senses already mapping the environment. “My nose hath a natural alacrity at scenting out the wine. You follow me, and I my nose. Bring a torch!”

They descended a short flight of stone steps into a large, low-ceilinged cellar. The acoustic ecology of the space shifted instantly; the chaotic echoes of the upper floors were swallowed by a damp, subterranean hush. The air was thick with the scent of geosmin, wet limestone, and the sharp, curing-salt tang of saltpetre that had oozed from the damp stones, crystallizing on the walls like the delicate white flora on aging charcuterie. 

“Oh! The fine mouldy smell,” Denys breathed, deeply appreciating the subterranean terroir. “In such places still lurks the good wine; advance thy torch. *Diable!* What is that in the corner? A pile of rags? No: 'tis a man.”

The torchlight washed over a figure crouched on a heap in the corner, pale as wood ash and shivering violently. 

“Why, it is the landlord,” said Denys.

“Get up, thou craven heart!” shouted one of the archers, his voice booming against the low vaults. “Why, man, the thieves are bound, and we are dry that bound them. Up! And show us thy wine; for no bottles see I here.”

“What, be the rascals bound?” the pale landlord stammered. His voice was a thin, reedy squeal, entirely lacking the rich, hospitable resonance of a true keeper of the hearth. “Good news. W-w-wine? That will I, honest sirs.” He rose on unsteady joints, offering to lead them further into the dark.

But Denys interposed, his instincts attuned to the discord in the man's tone. “You are all in the dark, comrades. He is in league with the thieves.”

“Alack, good soldier, me in league with the accursed robbers! Is that reasonable?”

“The girl said so anyway.”

“The girl! What girl? Ah! Curse her, traitress!”

“Well,” another archer rumbled, “the girl is not here, but gone on to the bailiff. So let the burghers settle whether this craven be guilty or no, for we caught him not in the act: and let him draw us our wine.”

“One moment,” Denys said shrewdly, his ear catching the unnatural cadence of the man's curse. “Why cursed he the girl? If he be a true man, he should bless her as we do.”

“Alas, sir!” the landlord whined, adopting a superficial, commercial tone that grossly commercialized his own survival. “I have but my good name to live by, and I cursed her to you, because you said she had belied me.”

“Humph! I trow thou art a thief, and where is the thief that cannot lie with a smooth face? Therefore hold him, comrades: a prisoner can draw wine an if his hands be not bound.”

The landlord offered no objection, insisting with obsequious falsehood that he would gladly reveal his meager stock of wine, though he hoped they would pay, as his rent was two months in arrears. The archers smiled grimly at his transactional simplicity. One laid a heavy hand on his shoulder, while Philippe, bearing the torch, led the way. They reached the threshold when Denys barked, “Halt!”

“What is't?”

“Here be bottles in this corner; advance thy light.” Denys had unbelted his scabbard and was probing the very heap of earth upon which the landlord had been crouching.

“Nay, nay,” the landlord cried, his voice tightening. “The wine is in the next cellar. There is nothing there.”

“Nothing is mighty hard, then,” Denys observed. He reached into the dark soil. He knew the tactile feedback of the culinary arts—the density of root vegetables, the distinct architecture of butchered livestock. What he pulled from the earth felt profoundly wrong. It was a bone. He tossed it onto the stone floor; it landed with a hollow, unnatural clatter that disrupted the cellar’s ancient quiet.

“There is nought there but the bones of the house,” the landlord babbled. 

“Just now 'twas nothing. Now that we have found something 'tis nothing but bones. Here's another. Humph? Look at this one, comrade; and you come too and look at it, and bring yon smooth knave along.”

Philippe held the bone to the flame, turning it slowly. “Well, if this was a field of battle, I should say 'twas the shankbone of a man; no more, no less. But 'tisn't a battlefield, nor a churchyard; 'tis an inn.”

“True, mate,” Denys said softly, the weight of generational tragedy settling over his words. “But yon knave's ashy face is as good a light to me as a field of battle. I read the bone by it. Bring yon face nearer, I say. When the chine is amissing, and the house dog can't look at you without his tail creeping between his legs, who was the thief? Good brothers mine, my mind it doth misgive me. The deeper I thrust the more there be. Mayhap if these bones could tell their tale they would make true men's flesh creep that heard it.”

“Alas! Young man, what hideous fancies are these!” the landlord cried, trying to assert a false culinary reality. “The bones are bones of beeves, and sheep, and kids, and not, as you think, of men and women. Holy saints preserve us!”

“Hold thy peace! Thy words are air. Thou hast not got burghers by the ear, that know not a veal knuckle from their grandsire's ribs; but soldiers—men that have gone to look for their dear comrades, and found their bones picked as clean by the crows as these I doubt have been by thee and thy mates. Men and women, saidst thou? And prithee, when spake I a word of women's bones? Wouldst make a child suspect thee. Field of battle, comrade! Was not this house a field of battle half an hour agone? Drag him close to me, let me read his face: now then, what is this, thou knave?” Denys thrust a small, earth-crusted object into the flickering light.

“Alas! I know not.”

“Well, I would not swear neither: but it is too like the thumb bone of a man's hand; mates, my flesh it creeps. Churchyard! How know I this is not one?” 

Drawing his sword, Denys began to rake the heap, excavating a macabre anti-kitchen of broken crockery and discarded humanity. The landlord protested, attempting to shroud his sins in the language of mere marketplace survival. “We poor innkeepers are sinners; we give short measure and baptize the wine: we are fain to do these things; the laws are so unjust to us; but we are not assassins. How could we afford to kill our customers? May Heaven's lightning strike me dead if there be any bones there but such as have been used for meat. 'Tis the kitchen wench flings them here: I swear by God's holy mother, by holy Paul, by holy Dominic, and Denys my patron saint—ah!”

Denys held a bone directly under the man's eye in a sudden, suffocating silence. It was a remnant no agrarian worker, no chef, no man however ignorant could confound with the honest butchery of sheep or oxen. It was a human skull. The stark truth of it shattered the landlord’s lying lexicon. His hair rose in stiff spikes, and his knees collapsed. The archers hauled him upright, forcing him to stare into the cavernous, untranslatable void of the eye sockets.

“Ah!” Denys intoned, trembling with a rage born of a deep reverence for life. “Look on the sockets out of which thou hast picked the eyes, and let them blast thine eyes, that crows shall pick out ere this week shall end. Now, hold thou that while I search on. Hold it, I say, or here I rob the gallows—” He threatened the quaking wretch with his naked blade until, moaning, the landlord cradled the cold skull.

As Denys scattered the earth, a cyclical nightmare unfolded; not one, but many skulls surfaced, each a lost dialect, an erased personal history subsumed into the landlord’s commercial greed. 

Suddenly, Denys uttered a profound, agonizing cry. From the damp earth, he hoisted a mass of human hair to the torchlight. It was long, glossy, and golden. In its silken threads lay the echo of a woman’s domestic routines, her unwritten lineage, her intimate sensory world, all brutally silenced. The archers instinctively shook the wretch, who could only whine.

“I have a little sister with hair just so fair and shining as this,” Denys gulped, the visceral pain of cultural and familial loss tearing at his throat. “Jesu! If it should be hers! There, quick, take my sword and dagger, and keep them from my hand, lest I strike him dead and wrong the gibbet. And thou, poor innocent victim, on whose head this most lovely hair did grow, hear me swear this, on bended knee, never to leave this man till I see him broken to pieces on the wheel even for thy sake.” 

He rose, tucking the untranslatable grief of the golden hair into his bosom. In a sudden fury, he seized the landlord by the neck, forced him to his knees, and ground his face savagely into the dense, rotting marrow of his victims. The assassin yelled, then whimpered, forced to inhale the olfactory landscape of his own profound desecration, like a dog pushed into the carcass of the innocent leveret it had senselessly killed.

“Now lend me thy bowstring, Philippe!” 

Weaving the string through the eye sockets, Denys hung the ghastly relic round the man's neck, then kicked him industriously up the stone steps toward the kitchen. 

There, the mundane world of dominant power structures had arrived. An alderman of the burgh stood with constables, listening to a little pettifogging clerk taking an archer's deposition. The clerk’s voice was a peevish, abrasive noise—like the shrill cry of a guinea-fowl—polluting the solemn acoustic space of the tragedy. 

The grave burgher started at the sight of the bleeding landlord wearing his horrible collar, but Denys panted out the fiery truth, translating the horror of the cellar into undeniable fact.

“Bind him like the rest,” the alderman commanded sternly. “I count him the blackest of them all.”

The wretched landlord begged that the skull be removed.

“Humph!” said the alderman. “Certes I had not ordered such a thing to be put on mortal man. Yet being there, I will not lift voice nor finger to doff it. Methinks it fits thee truly, thou bloody dog. 'Tis thy ensign, and hangs well above a heart so foul as thine. Have we secured the whole gang, or but a part?”

“Your worship,” said Denys, “there are but seven of them, and this landlord. One we slew upstairs, one we trundled down dead, the rest are bound before you.”

“Good! Go fetch the dead one from upstairs, and lay him beside him I caused to be removed.”

Here, the clerk’s dissonant squawk intruded again. “Now, now, now, where is the hand? That is what I want to see.” He whispered to his master, his words dripping with the sterile commercialization of justice, “What a goodly show will the *pieces de conviction* make!” With scratching, thoughtless ink, he penned the remnants of mythic suffering into a bloodless list, reducing silenced lives to mere inventory: *Skulls—Bones—A woman's hair—A thief's hands—1 axe—2 carcasses—1 crossbow bolt.*

Itching to commodify further evidence—an ear, perhaps, or an earring—the clerk caught up a torch and hurried toward the cellar. But the inn, steeped in its own cyclical, mythic interventions, stopped him. 

The constables had ascended the stairs in single file. The head constable, a ponderous man, reached the top only to be confronted by the phosphorescent corpse seated by the bedside—a radiant, otherworldly disruption of the everyday. Stupefied, he began to shake. The silence was shattered by his terrified howl as he recoiled swiftly, missing the step. He tumbled backward onto his companion, who, catching a glimpse of the glowing horror, was already staggering. In a rhythmic, cascading percussion of bodies, the two carried away the third, and the three the fourth. They streamed down into the kitchen, a chaotic avalanche of armored flesh that slammed into the bustling clerk. 

The impact knocked the clerk flat on his back, the breath and the squeak driven out of him simultaneously. He lay there waving his torch in silent, absurd convulsion, his sterile noise finally extinguished by the physical weight of the constables. 

“What is to do now, in Heaven's name?” cried the alderman, starting up in alarm. 

Denys quickly explained the mythic glow upstairs and offered to accompany his worship. The bruised constables ruefully collected themselves, and the alderman led them to examine the premises above and below, weaving through the remnants of domestic life and unspeakable death. 

The prisoners’ interrogatory was postponed until they could face the servant. And so, as the cyclical turn of the earth brought the pale hues of dawn, the thieves, the dead, and the tragic, physical memories of their crimes were swept away into the sprawling, unfeeling net of the law. The inn returned to a hollow, echoing silence, abandoned save for one lingering constable, Denys, and the heavily sleeping Gerard, anchored in his dreams while the world around them slowly readied itself to demand sustenance once more.
