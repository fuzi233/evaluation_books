"Brass within, and steel without, with beams on his topcastle strong; And eighteen pieces of ordinance He carries on either side along."

Thus sang Sir Andrew Barton of the *Pelican*. Let us take a boat at Whitehall Stairs, slipping down ahead of Amyas beneath the shadow of Old London Bridge, until we reach Deptford Creek. There lies the famous vessel, preserved as if embalmed—the *Pelican*, in which Drake circumnavigated the globe. Drawn high and dry upon the sedge-lined bank of the Thames, she stands like an ancient warrior resting after a lifetime of toil. Epigrams and verses honoring her and her captain are nailed to her mainmast; three of these, penned by the Winchester scholar Camden, survive in his history. Queen Elizabeth herself had consecrated the ship solemnly, feasted aboard, and there and then conferred knighthood upon Drake. "At which time," the chroniclers note, "a bridge of planks, by which they came on board, broke under the press of people, and fell down with a hundred men upon it, who, notwithstanding, had none of them any harm. So as that ship may seem to have been built under a lucky planet."

She has remained there ever since, a spectacle and a sort of dining hall for jovial gatherings from the city. One such gathering appears to be underway this afternoon, judging by the flags bedizening the masts, the sounds of revelry, and the savory steam issuing from windows that were once portholes. White-aproned waiters rush to and fro along the riverbank and across that lucky bridge, accompanied by gay serving men bearing the city badge on their shoulders. A great feast is clearly in progress. The Lord Mayor is hosting a dinner for distinguished gentlemen of the Leicester household, men deeply interested in foreign discoveries. What venue could be more fitting for such a feast than the *Pelican* itself?

Observe the company gathered around; a nobler assembly is rarely seen. Indeed, if you are American, cast a reverent gaze upon their faces, for to their wisdom and courage you owe the existence of your mighty nation.

At the head of the table sits the Lord Mayor, instantly recognizable as the famous Sir Edward Osborne, clothworker and ancestor of the modern Dukes of Leeds—a romance known to everyone today. Though aged, he shows no sign of change since he leapt from a window onto London Bridge into the roaring tide below to rescue the infant who is now his wife. The chivalry and promptitude of the apprentice boy have hardened into the thoughtful daring of a wealthy merchant adventurer. He sits like a king, flanked on his right by the Earl of Cumberland and on his left by Walter Raleigh. The three converse in low tones regarding the possibility of vast, rich countries remaining undiscovered between Florida and the River of Canada. Raleigh's half-scientific declamations and frequent citations of Doctor Dee the conjurer have less effect on Osborne than on the Earl of Cumberland—who, having attempted many adventures abroad, failed in all of them, apparently because he preferred sending others rather than going himself. Consequently, Raleigh turns to the quiet student seated beside him, Richard Hakluyt of Oxford, for support. Yet, Raleigh is deeply engaged in discourse with a reverend elder whose long white beard flows nearly to his waist and whose face is furrowed by a thousand storms. This is Anthony Jenkinson, the great Asiatic traveler, discoursing to the Christ-Church virtuoso of reindeer sledges, Siberian steppes, and the fossil ivory dug by the Tungus from the ice-cliffs of the Arctic Sea—a plain proof, he claims, of Noah's Flood.

Next to him sits Christopher Carlile, Walsingham's son-in-law (Sidney is now likewise connected), a valiant captain who later became General of the soldiery in Drake's triumphant West Indian raid of 1585. He is currently chatting with Alderman Hart the grocer, Sheriff Spencer the clothworker, and Charles Leigh—Amyas's merchant cousin—as well as Aldworth, Mayor of Bristol, and William Salterne, Alderman thereof and cousin of our friend at Bideford. Carlile and Secretary Walsingham have been tirelessly aiding Humphrey and Adrian Gilbert in collecting funds for their great North-West expeditions over the last two years. On one of these ventures, Carlile was indeed intended to sail, though he ultimately did not go; I could never discover the reason.

Opposite them lies a group scarcely less fascinating. Martin Frobisher and John Davis, the pioneers of the North-West Passage, discuss matters with Alderman Sanderson, the great geographer and "setter forth of globes"; with Mr. Towerson, Sir Gilbert Peckham, our old acquaintance Captain John Winter; and, last but not least, with Philip Sidney himself. With his customary courtesy, Sidney has relinquished his rightful place near the head of the table to create a knot of virtuosi for himself, bringing with him his two special intimates, Mr. Edward Dyer and Mr. Francis Leigh. They, too, speak of the North-West Passage. Sidney laments his bondage to diplomacy and court life, expressing envy of old Martin Frobisher in a shower of pretty compliments. To this, Frobisher replies:

"It's all very fine to talk of here, sailing on dry land with a good glass of wine before you; but you'd find it another guess sort of business, knocking about among the icebergs with your beard frozen fast to your ruff, Sir Philip, specially if you were a bit squeamish about the stomach."

"That were a slight matter to endure, my dear sir, if by it I could win the honor which Her Majesty bestowed on you, when her own ivory hand waved a farewell kerchief to your ship from the windows of Greenwich Palace."

"Well, sir, folks say you have no reason to complain of lack of favors, as you have no reason to deserve lack; and if you can get them by staying ashore, don't you go to sea to look for more, say I. Eh, Master Towerson?"

Towerson's gray beard, weathered by many foreign voyages both fair and foul, wags in grim assent. Just then, a waiter enters.

"Please my Lord Mayor's Worship, there is a tall gentleman outside who would speak with the Right Honorable Sir Walter Raleigh."

"Show him in, man. Sir Walter's friends are ours."

Amyas enters, standing hesitantly in the doorway.

"Captain Leigh!" cries half a dozen voices.

"Why did you not walk in, sir?" asks Osborne. "You should know your way well enough between these decks."

"Well enough, my lords and gentlemen. But, Sir Walter—you will excuse me"—he offers Raleigh a glance sufficient to test even his quick wit. Turning pale as death, Raleigh rises and follows Amyas into an adjoining cabin. They remain together for five minutes, during which Amyas delivers the sad story we already know. Before it ends, noble tears glisten on several of those stern faces.

"The old Egyptians," says Sir Edward Osborne, "when they banqueted, set a corpse among their guests, for a memorial of human vanity. Have we forgotten God and our own weakness in this our feast, that He Himself has sent us thus a message from the dead?"

"Nay, my Lord Mayor," replies Sidney, "not from the dead, but from the realm of everlasting life."

"Amen!" answers Osborne. "But, gentlemen, our feast is at an end. There are those here who would drink merrily, as brave men should, in spite of the private losses of which they have just received news; but none here who can drink with the loss of so great a man still ringing in his ears."

The truth is undeniable. Though many guests had suffered financially due to the expedition's failure, they had utterly forgotten that fact in the face of the awful news of Sir Humphrey's death. The feast breaks up sadly and hurriedly, while each man asks his neighbor, "What will the Queen say?"

Raleigh re-enters a few moments later, silent, yet pressing many an honest hand as he passes, before departing to call a wherry, beckoning Amyas to follow. Sidney, Cumberland, and Frank depart in another boat, leaving the two brothers to discuss the tragic details.

They disembark at Whitehall Stairs; Raleigh, Sidney, and Cumberland proceed to the Palace, while the two brothers head for their mother's lodgings.

Amyas had prepared a speech for Frank regarding Rose Salterne, but now that the moment had arrived, he lacked the courage to begin, longing instead for Frank to open the matter. Frank, too, shrank from what he knew must come, especially since he was unaware that Amyas had visited Bideford or knew anything of the *Rose*'s disappearance.

They ascend the stairs, finding relief in discovering that their mother is at the Abbey. It was for her sake that both dreaded the impending conversation. Standing in the bay-window overlooking the river, they talk of indifferent things, gazing earnestly at each other's faces by the fading light, for it has been three years since they last met.

Years and events have deepened the contrast between the two brothers. Frank smiles with affectionate pride as he looks up into Amyas's face, seeing that he is no longer merely the rollicking, handy sailor-lad, but a self-confident, stately warrior embodying "reason firm, the temperate will, endurance, foresight, strength, and skill."
