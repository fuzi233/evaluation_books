It was not long after the birth of the Prince of Wales that our own little son made his entrance into the world—though, unlike the royal infant, his arrival passed without fireworks or fanfare. No golden rails or noble nurses stood guard at his cradle; only Gumbo, steadfast and loyal, and Molly, kind-hearted and gentle, watched over him with all the devotion they could muster. Our child came into a humble life, yet born under skies no less bright for their simplicity.

Why did we not name him George, like the prince, or like his father? Because in those days, when affection from my own blood was scarce, one small soul had touched me deeply—a cousin, a boy of my family’s line, named Miles. He had been a wild, spirited lad, but full of warmth and mischief, and though he’d passed on, his memory lived in my heart. Theo and I agreed: our son should carry that name—not as a jest, but as an echo of love, of kinship, of a bond that mattered more than rank or title.

We wrote to inform my parents of the joyous news, and boldly announced the birth in the *Daily Advertiser*, noting the place—Church Street, Lambeth—as plainly as if it were a palace courtyard. Aunt Bernstein replied with a note both sharp and tender: “How could you expose your dwelling so publicly?” she wrote. “But I kiss the little mamma, and send a remembrance for the child.” That remembrance arrived in the form of a rich silk coverlid, edged with lace fit for a prince. It was beautiful, though scarcely practical—the cost of the lace alone might have fed us for weeks. Still, Theo and Molly rejoiced in its finery, and our son’s cradle gleamed with a dignity that matched any nobleman’s.

Dr. Heberden, kind and wise, visited often, ensuring my wife’s recovery. He recommended a local surgeon who proved attentive and skilled. The care needed was minimal—Theo was strong, and our landlady, along with Molly, tended her with quiet competence.

Then came Lady Castlewood again, her chariot flashing through the narrow lane as if it belonged to some grand procession. She brought a pot of jelly—no doubt once served at her own table—and spoke of court ceremonies, of the splendour surrounding the new heir to the throne. Mr. Johnson happened to be visiting that day, and he bowed low before her, awkwardly but respectfully. She called me “cousin” with gracious ease, and helped transfer the jelly from her silver dish to our crockery with a smile. Dr. Heberden tasted it and declared it exquisite.

“The great,” he said, “are fortunate in every way. They gather the finest cooks, the most witty companions. If this jelly has indeed graced his lordship’s table, then it has done so in company worthy of its sweetness. It has trembled beneath the gaze of famed beauties, kissed by ruby lips, shared among lords and ladies whose conversation must have sparkled like wine.”

And so he ate a generous portion—though I must confess, Theo found it unpalatable, and Molly, sniffing it sharply, declared it mouldy.

Yet our boy had one distinction few could claim: an earl’s daughter as his godmother. Lady Maria, our dear cousin, took the role with heartfelt devotion. Having known no children of her own, she poured her tenderness into ours, watching over mother and child with a love that stirred even the hardest heart.

Captain Miles—our own gallant son—now wears the fine uniform of the Prince’s Hussars, resplendent in gold braid and polished buttons. But let us hope he never forgets that his first garments were once meant for a poor player’s child. Sampson, the same man who married us in Southwark’s chapel, baptized him there too. Never had I heard the words of the Prayer-book spoken with such grace—until, at the final blessing, his voice broke, and all present wiped away tears. “Mr. Garrick himself,” Hagan remarked, “could not have read them more nobly. And never did a child enter the world with such sincere prayers.”

Now, how did he come by the name Miles?

A few days before the christening, while we still debated whether to name him George, the sound of hooves clattered up our street. At the gate stood young Miles—our cousin—dismounted, breathless, his little boots dusted with road-grime. I fear he had ridden off without permission, driven by restless spirit and loyalty.

“You know,” he said, “Cousin Harry gave me my horse. And I can’t help liking you—you’re just like Harry, and people always talk about you at home, and it’s not fair. So I’ve brought my whistle and coral—my godmamma Lady Suckling gave them to me—for your baby. And if you’re poor, Cousin George, here’s my gold moidore. It’s worth a lot, and I can’t spend it anyway. It’s no use to me.”

We lifted him to Theo’s room—proud in his tramping boots, cheeks flushed with effort. She kissed him, thanked him, and held the coin close. From that day, the moidore stayed in her purse, a token of innocence and generosity.

My mother, writing through her usual envoy, expressed her dismay upon learning my son bore the name Miles—one unknown in the Esmond line. I did not explain then. But years later, when I told Madam Esmond the truth, I saw a tear trace her wrinkled cheek. And afterward, I learned she had questioned Gumbo many times about the boy who gave his name to our Miles—our Miles Gloriosus, who would one day ride through Pall Mall, Valenciennes, Almack’s, and Brighton, remembered not for wealth or title, but for the kindness that began with a single gold coin.
