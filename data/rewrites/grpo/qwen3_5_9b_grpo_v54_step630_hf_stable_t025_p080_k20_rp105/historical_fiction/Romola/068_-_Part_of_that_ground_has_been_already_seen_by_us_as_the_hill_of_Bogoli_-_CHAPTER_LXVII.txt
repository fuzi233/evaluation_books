On the far bank of the Arno, where the twilight had settled into a chill, slate-grey hue, another figure emerged from the shadows, moving with a purpose entirely disconnected from the armed bands crossing the river nearby. This man sought not the city's heart, but its neglected margins. He made his way to a stretch of the riverbank hidden from view by towering warehouses and granaries, a place where the silence was heavy and the air damp with the scent of rotting rushes and stagnant gutters. Here, amidst the long, sodden grass, he sat down, bending his weary frame over the swift, dark current.

He had come for the river's bounty. In times past, a kind-hearted beggar had dropped a crust of bread, a raw carrot, or apple parings into his lap. These small, anonymous gifts were the only reason he endured the cold vigil. To wait here, unseen, was preferable to the silent, humiliating supplication required on the church steps beyond the Porta San Frediano.

Baldassarre loathed begging with a visceral hatred; he would have preferred death to the act of asking for charity. Yet, he lived for a single, consuming reason. It was no longer a hope, but a delusion that had taken root deep in his mind—a possibility so persistent it defied logic. It was the kind of obsession that drives a woman to watch a headland for a ship long known to be a wreck, refusing to accept the neighbors' certainty of doom. Since fleeing the convent hospital of San Miniato, where the monks had tended him only until he became helpless, Baldassarre had searched in vain for the Wife who was to be his salvation. She had vanished, likely claimed by the pestilence that seemed to swallow Florence whole. Without her, the idea of sacred vengeance through his own hand had crumbled. His knife was lost, and his body and mind were too shattered to forge another weapon or devise a plan. He was a broken, bewildered old man, yet he clung to life, waiting for something formless and dim that pulsed within him with the intensity of a waking dream. He crept out into the grey light, seated himself in the dank grass, and watched the waters, searching for a faint, elusive promise.

While Baldassarre waited in solitude, the Compagnacci were already in motion. The formidable bands of armed men, left largely unchecked by a distracted Signoria, had split into two streams, both converging on the Arno. One mass headed for the Ponte Rubaconte, the other for the Ponte Vecchio, yet their destination was identical: the Via de' Bardi. A whispered command had spread through the rabble, promising plunder and the sack of a specific house. The leaders understood the true intent: to ambush the master of that house in his morning slumber.

But the master, Tito, was neither asleep nor in bed. The events of the previous day had driven him to flee Florence. Investigations were imminent, and delay meant danger. Fear, once a tangible armor he had worn, had died, only to be replaced by its ghost—the undying habit of dread. He felt unsafe within the city's fierce, turbid walls. His house was being handed over to a new tenant, his horses awaited in San Gallo, and his family, Tessa and the children, were lodged safely in the Borgo. Tito descended the stone steps into his courtyard, passing through the great doorway. He was no longer the man who had once fallen in love with Romola; that life was cast off, and he was ready to depart.

He turned swiftly toward the Piazza dei Mozzi, intending to cross the Ponte Rubaconte, but sounds caught his ear, forcing him to reverse course. Was the mob approaching from the Oltrarno? He hurried toward the Ponte Vecchio, drawing his mantle tight around him. Before he could reach the end of the Via de' Bardi, the sounds grew louder, unmistakable now. The mob was coming over the bridge. Driven by an impulse stronger than reason, he turned to confirm it, only to find the street filled with the throng entering from the opposite end. He did not retreat to his house; he reasoned they would not attack him personally, yet he carried valuables, and chaos made all things possible. Necessity forged his courage. He marched toward the bridge, the roar of trampling feet and confused shouts drowning out everything else.

At the end of the street, the crowd pouring from the bridge hemmed him in. Before he could process the sudden shout rising around him, he felt himself surrounded not by unarmed rioters, but by armed Compagnacci. His cap was knocked from his head, and he was shoved violently forward into the crush of the mob along the narrow bridge passage. The cries erupted instantly: "Piagnone! Medicean! Throw him over the bridge!"

Strong hands tore at his mantle, threatening to strangle him until his belt buckle gave way. They snatched at his money pouch, the *scarsella*, but he was already being dragged and hustled. Shouts of execration rang in his ears, spreading panic even to those who had not yet recognized him. Tito's horror was that he would be trampled or struck down before reaching the open arches of the bridge. His only hope was that they would throw him into the river before inflicting fatal wounds. His entire soul focused on this desperate gamble.

They reached the central arches. In that moment of extremity, Tito summoned a final reserve of strength. With a frantic effort, he unclasped his belt and flung the pouch forward onto a patch of clear space near the parapet, shouting in a ringing voice, "There are diamonds! There is gold!"

In the instant the mob's attention shifted to the pouch, Tito seized his chance. He threw himself onto the parapet and leaped with all his might, plunging into the dark river below with a great splash. It was his salvation. He had survived drowning before, and as he broke the surface, gasping for air, he felt a surge of deliverance. He struck out with the energy of his youth, aided by the current. If he could reach the Ponte alla Carrara, he might land in a remote district and make his way to San Gallo. Life was still possible. The idiot mob on the bridge, shouting and bellowing, would assume he was drowned.

They believed him. Peering over the parapet into the darkness, they saw nothing but the moving blackness of floating hair and velvet sleeves.

From the other side, however, a pale olive face emerged from the dark water, white and strained against the current. It was a face unforgettable in its features: a square forehead, long low arches of eyebrows, and lustrous, agate-like eyes. The face drifted onward, nostrils inflated, blue veins distended on the temples. He passed the bridge of Santa Trinita. Should he risk landing now? No. He heard, or imagined he heard, cries pursuing him. Terror pressed hardest from the side of his fellow men; he was less afraid of fate than of humanity, and he swam on, panting and straining, though exhaustion began to cloud his senses.

The last bridge passed. He felt vaguely that he was safe, but the current carried him blindly, exhaustion creeping into a dreamy state bordering on unconsciousness.

Then, eyes discerned him—aged, strong eyes fixed on the distance. Baldassarre, looking up blankly from his vigil by the runlet, saw a white object drifting along the broader stream. Could this be fortune? He watched until the object took form, then leaned forward with a start, his eyes filling with a new, fierce light. Yet he remained motionless. Something was being brought to him.

The next instant, a man's body was cast violently onto the grass just two yards from him. Baldassarre sprang forward like a panther, clutching the velvet tunic as he fell upon the body, flashing a look into the man's face.

Dead? The eyes were rigid. But no, it could not be. Justice often brought men back from the brink. Baldassarre did not feel feeble now; he knew exactly what he could do. He inserted his large fingers into the neck of the tunic, holding them tight, kneeling on one knee beside the body and watching the face. A fierce hope burned in his heart, mixed with trembling. In his eyes, there was only ferocity; the slow-burning remnant of life within him seemed to leap into flame.

Rigid. Still rigid. Those eyes with half-fallen lids were locked against vengeance. Could he truly be dead? Time was hard to measure, but it seemed long enough for hope to freeze into despair.

Surely now the eyelids were quivering. The eyes were no longer rigid. A vibrating light flickered within them; they opened wide.

"Ah, yes! You see me—you know me!"

Tito knew him, but he did not know if it was life or death that had brought him into the presence of his injured father. It might be death—and death might mean this chill gloom with the hideous past hanging over him forever.

Now, Baldassarre's only dread was that the young limbs should escape him. He pressed his knuckles against the round throat and knelt upon the chest with all the force of his aged frame. Let death come now!

He kept his watch on the face. When the eyes went rigid again, he dared not trust them. He would never release his hold until someone came. Justice would send a witness, and then he, Baldassarre, would declare that he had killed this traitor, to whom he had once been a father. They would perhaps believe him now, and then he would be content with the struggle of justice on earth—then he would desire to die with his hold on this body, following the traitor to hell to clutch him there.

So he knelt, pressing his knuckles against the throat, refusing to trust the seeming death, until the light grew strong and he could kneel no longer. Then he sat on the body, still clutching the neck of the tunic. But the hours passed, and no witness came. No eyes descried the two human bodies lying among the tall grass by the riverside. Florence was busy with greater affairs, preparing for a deeper tragedy.

Not long after those two bodies lay in the grass, Savonarola was being tortured, crying out in agony, "I will confess!"

It was not until the sun had sunk westward that a wagon drawn by a mild grey ox arrived at the edge of the grassy margin. As the driver leaned to gather the round stones heaped nearby, ready for removal, he detected a startling object in the grass. The aged man had fallen forward, his dead clutch still gripping the garment of the other. It was impossible to separate them; indeed, it was better to place them in the wagon and carry them as they were into the great Piazza, so notice might be given to the Eight.

As the wagon entered the frequented streets, a growing crowd escorted it with its strange burden. No one recognized the bodies for a long while, for the aged face had fallen forward, half hiding the younger. But before they were moved out of sight, recognition came.

"I know that old man," Piero di Cosimo testified. "I painted his likeness once. He is the prisoner who clutched Melema on the steps of the Duomo."

"He is perhaps the same old man who appeared at supper in my gardens," said Bernardo Rucellai, one of the Eight. "I had forgotten him. I thought he had died in prison. But there is no knowing the truth now."

Who can point to the work of justice and say, "It is there"? Justice is like the Kingdom of God—it is not merely a fact without us, but a great yearning within us.
