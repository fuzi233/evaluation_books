Any fool who’s ever popped into the Wheel of Fortune public-house—where you’ll recall Mr. James Morgan’s “Confidentials” valet club met, and where Sir Francis Clavering had that delightfully awkward chat with Major Pendennis—knows its ground floor has three guest rooms besides the landlady’s snug little bar. One’s a regular parlour open to all and sundry; another is reserved for gents in livery who spend hours swapping the most embarrassing secrets of their posh employers; the third, with a chipped “PRIVATE” sign tacked to the door, belongs exclusively to the Confidentials, where Morgan and his mate Lightfoot were core members.

That quiet little shadow Morgan had crept along and listened to every word Strong and Major Pendennis had chattered about in the Major’s own lodgings, stockpiling gossip to chew over for weeks. His curiosity piqued so bad he followed the Major back to the Wheel of Fortune later, tucked himself into a little nook in the Confidentials room while Pendennis and Clavering yelled at each other in the next parlour. That nook had acoustics so good you could hear someone crack a nut in the next apartment, so every shouty, heated line of the conversation reached his ears perfectly, confirming every sneaking suspicion he’d already formed.

“Wait a minute, he recognised Altamont straight away back in Sydney, did he? Clavering’s no more properly married to his Lady than I am the Archbishop of Canterbury! Altamont’s the real husband, and he’s a convict to boot! Young Arthur’s gonna waltz into Parliament, and that old rascal Pendennis promised not to snitch. Good grief, that old fox is slipperier than an eel in a rain barrel! No wonder he’s dead set on marrying Blanche off to Arthur—she’s got a hundred thousand quid easy, and she’ll hand her new hubby a Parliament seat on a silver platter too!” A physiognomist would’ve paid good money to gawk at Morgan’s face when this bombshell clicked fully into place. “If society didn’t have these daft hang-ups about age and valets, dammit James Morgan, you could marry her yourself,” he muttered, winking at his reflection in the pub mirror.

He couldn’t marry Blanche and her fortune, sure—but he could line his own pockets with this secret easy. Everyone tangled up in it would pay through the nose to keep it quiet. Sir Francis would cough up to avoid total ruin, Altamont would pay to not get dragged back to the colonies and hanged, that stuck-up prat Arthur would pay to stop the world knowing he’d bought a Parliament seat by trafficking a convict’s secret. Lady Clavering? She’d pay if she wanted to dump Clavering, she’d pay double to protect her son Arthur. And Blanche would definitely fork over cash to get the fortune she was cheated out of. “Dammit,” Morgan grinned to himself, “these cards are so good I’m set for life. It’ll be a regular annuity. Every last one of them will have to keep paying me off. With the cash I’ve already saved I can quit valeting, fire my old boss, turn proper gentleman, and hire my own damn servant.” For a man planning such grand schemes, he showed incredible self-restraint: he still polished the Major’s boots to a mirror shine, brought his early morning tea up exactly at 7 a.m., and took all the old gent’s swears and snide remarks with a perfectly blank, obsequious face. No one looking at him hefting the Major’s trunks or helping serve at posh country house dinners would guess he was richer than his own employer, and holding half the neighbourhood’s most explosive secrets in his waistcoat pocket. He was a legend among valets: the younger blokes called him a boring old fogey with no ideas, but every last one of them prayed to die as well off as Morgan Pendennis.

Major Pendennis, for his part, spent the autumn traipsing between the country houses of his remaining friends. If the Duke was off in France and the Marquis up in Scotland, he’d condescend to crash with plain old Sir John or the local squire. Truth be told, his reputation was on the wane: most of his old Regency mates were dead, their kids and grandkids had never heard of his wild Prince and Poins anecdotes, and didn’t care a fig for them if they had. He’d walk past old familiar London doors and realise no one was inviting him in for banquets anymore, and get all melancholy, convinced the young folks were laughing at him behind his back. “The breed’s gone!” he grumbled to himself. “No more grand old courtly manners. Old Lord Castlewood used to roll into London with four post-chaises and sixteen horses, the whole North Road lined up to gawk at his procession! The new Lord Castlewood crams five bagmen into a railway carriage and sneaks off from the station smoking a cheap cigar in a beat-up brougham. The old Lady Lorraine wore diamonds the size of walnuts and rouge so bright it outshone a streetlamp, and all the wits bowed at her feet. The new one dresses like a governess in a plain black gown, won’t stop yammering about astronomy and labouring classes, and creeps to church at 8 a.m. like she’s hiding from the law. The guardsmen these days sprawl over sofas, smoke tobacco that stinks up the whole turnip field, and don’t respect nothing—they’re all useless cotton spinners and prissy young parsons with their hair plastered down their foreheads. My day’s over,” the old man sighed, and he wasn’t half wrong.

After a pretty rubbish autumn full of bad shooting, grumpy young hosts, and no chance to play his beloved whist, Pendennis trundled back to London at the miserable end of October, when the fog rolls in and all the lawyers drag themselves back to town. Everyone knows that vibe: the overloaded cabs rattling through dun grey evenings, dumping sleepy kids and sunburnt holidaymakers back into cold dark houses, the stack of bills waiting on the study desk, the printer’s boy loitering in the passage, that bloke Smith who turns up out of nowhere to collect that one tiny bill you forgot all about. Pendennis didn’t have any bills to stress over, and no wife or kids waiting to soothe him when he got home, but he was still in a foul mood. He’d had two bouts of gout at his last country house, walked for miles over ploughed fields that ached his old bones to bits, left his umbrella on the train, and he’d been taking all that frustration out on Morgan for days.

Bays’s Club wasn’t any better: it had just been repainted, reeking of varnish so strong it made his eyes water, and some careless painter had streaked a thick line of white paint right down the back of his favourite fur-collared coat. The dinner was terrible, and the three most boring old gits in London had decided to sit right near him: old Hawkshaw hacking up a lung nonstop, old Colonel Gripley hogging every single newspaper in the room, and Jawkins the bore who wouldn’t stop reciting every single inn bill he’d paid on his recent European tour. Then the club waiter trod right on his gouty toe while bringing his coffee. The Furies really do hunt in packs: they chased Pendennis all the way from home to the club, and all the way back.

While he was out, Morgan had planted himself in the landlady Mrs. Brixham’s parlour, guzzling hot brandy and water and ranting about how badly the Major had treated him all week. Mrs. Brixham, a poor clergyman’s widow, was his total slave: he’d bought the lease on her house, held all her furniture as collateral for a tiny loan she’d taken out five years prior, and could throw her son, an insurance office clerk, in jail whenever he felt like it. Morgan made her fetch his bootjack, his slippers, and refill his glass over and over, drinking her own liquor for free, three full tumblers deep, when the Major trundled back home and rang the bell for his valet. Morgan swore savagely under his breath, finished his last full glass, and dragged himself up to answer.

The Major yelled at him for being slow, none the wiser that the red-faced valet was three sheets to the wind. He knelt down to pull off Pendennis’s boots, and as the old gent yelled “Confound you, mind that strap! Don’t wrench my foot off,” Morgan was muttering under his breath that he’d love to wring the old man’s neck and drown him in the foot bath by the fire. When he got the boots off, he leaned close to help Pendennis out of his coat, and the old man caught a whiff of brandy strong enough to knock over a horse. “You drunken beast,” he snapped, “you reek of liquor.”

That was the final straw. Morgan snapped, throwing every last bit of obsequious behaviour out the window. “Drunk am I? A beast am I? You infernal old miscreant! D’you think I’m gonna put up with your snooty old arrogance forever, you grinning old baboon? Come on, if you’re such a man, fight me!”

The Major grabbed a knife off the side table. “Step one inch closer and I’ll stick this in you. Get out of my sight, send for your wages in the morning, and never show your face here again. You’ve been getting too big for your boots for months, you’re too rich to be a valet now. Get out.”

Morgan laughed a nasty, sneering laugh, and plopped himself down in a chair like he owned the place. “And where d’you think you are, old man? This house is mine. Every stick of furniture in it belongs to me, except your old rags, your shower bath, and your silly wig box. I worked hard and bought the whole lease. I’ve served you loyally for twelve years, and all you ever do is call me a beast and a dog? No more of that. I quit. You don’t get to boss me around anymore. We ain’t done talking, you and me, not me, not your stuck-up nephew Arthur, and not the whole Clavering family. You’ll see.”

The Major was so shocked he cooled off instantly, like someone had dumped a bucket of freezing water over his head. He tried to shoo Morgan out of the room, but the valet sneered, “You’ll get sick of seeing me long before this is over,” and trundled out, leaving the old gent to sit by the fire fuming. He ranted to himself about ungrateful servants, and then grumbled that for all his cheek, Morgan was actually the best valet he’d ever had: he made a boot polish better than any sold in shops, whipped up perfect mutton broth, and looked after him like a nurse when he was sick. How on earth was he gonna replace the man?

He’d just taken off his wig, popped on his little night handkerchief, and settled in by the fire to grumble some more, when there was a tiny, pitiful knock at the door. It was Mrs. Brixham the landlady, looking half in tears. “God bless my soul, Mrs. Brixham!” the Major yelped, embarrassed that the widow was seeing him in his silly bedtime getup. “It’s very late. About Morgan, I suppose? I can’t take him back, I’m sorry. He’s gone too far.”

“Oh sir,” the poor woman wailed, “I know. He’s ruined me. I borrowed a tiny little sum off him five years ago, I’ve paid it back ten times over, but every single thing I own is his now. My son’s at his mercy. He’s a dreadful man.”

The Major blinked, and sighed. “Dash it, that’s terrible news. But there’s no help for it. I’ve got to leave your house tomorrow too, can’t stay under the same roof as that villain. No way around it.”
