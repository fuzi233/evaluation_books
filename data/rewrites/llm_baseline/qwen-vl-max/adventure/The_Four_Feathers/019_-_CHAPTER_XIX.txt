Ethne had meant to slip away unseen. But Mrs. Adair, seated in the shadow of the house on the terrace, watched her from a vantage point near the open drawing-room window. The moonlight spilled across the glass, illuminating the room’s sudden shift from light to darkness as Ethne extinguished the lamp. It was a quiet act—yet it carried weight. Mrs. Adair saw Ethne cross the terrace swiftly, descend the steps, and vanish into the garden. Her movements were hurried, almost frantic. She looked not like one fleeing danger, but like someone escaping herself.

Mrs. Adair sat still, her eyes fixed on the empty chair where Ethne had been moments before. The silence that followed the lamp’s snuffing out was thick with unspoken things. Then came music—soft, tender, full of longing—and after it, hushed conversation, low and intimate, drifting through the night air. It stirred something deep within Mrs. Adair—a memory, a pang, a jealousy that flared like fire in the dark. She had endured weeks of silent torment, watching, listening, knowing she was never truly part of this world Ethne and Durrance shared. Now, this moment—this private exchange—was too much. She did not move. She listened.

And then—Ethne fled.

Mrs. Adair blinked, startled. There was no sign of anger or conflict. Ethne did not storm out; she slipped away like smoke. Yet the man inside remained. Durrance continued speaking, his voice clear now, though his words were lost to her. He spoke with ease, with relief, as if he had finally laid a burden down. And yet—he believed Ethne was still there.

She rose slowly, stepping silently toward the window. She could hear him now: laughing lightly, recounting something that had once troubled him, now resolved.

“I began to see,” he said. “Even on that first afternoon at Hill Street, two months ago, I sensed there was only friendship on your side. My blindness helped me. With your face and eyes in view, I would have believed anything you wanted me to believe. But without those defenses… I began to see. For the first time in my life, I began to see.”

Mrs. Adair stood motionless, breath held. He spoke with confidence, even joy—like a man who had won a battle he didn’t know he was fighting. He confessed his own hesitation, his selfishness, how he had delayed their marriage, lied about hope for recovery, all because he clung to the faint possibility that Ethne’s affection might deepen. He had waited, hoping—praying—for signs: a new warmth in her voice, a lift in her laughter, a depth in her music.

“And tonight,” he said, “I’ve heard them. The softness, the pride—the restraint is gone. When you played, it was as though your heart sang through the notes. I’ve waited so long… Ethne, Ethne!”

At that moment, Ethne was far from the house, in the little enclosed garden by the creek—the place she had led Captain Willoughby earlier that day. Here, beneath the silver moonlight, she was alone. Her collie dog had found her, drawn by her distress. The garden offered sanctuary. She sank onto the bench, trying to breathe in the peace of the night, to find solace in the slow rhythm of nature—the lapping tide, the whisper of leaves, the stars above. But her strength had failed. The events of the day had shattered her composure.

Only that morning, news had come—good news. Harry Feversham was alive. She had thought she had found him again. But now, the truth had struck: he had chosen to go south, to Omdurman, to join Colonel Trench. To redeem himself—not just for her, but for the three men whose honor he had dishonored. And he would pay every last price.

“Surely,” she whispered, “he could have stopped. One redemption—done right—would have been enough.” But no. He had gone, and she could not stop him. And now, she feared, he would not return.

She remembered Captain Willoughby’s warning—how cruel Omdurman could be. She had refused to listen then. Now, she could not help imagining the horrors: the prison, the suffering, the silence. Was he already enduring it? At this very hour? She shut her eyes tight, refusing to let the thought take root.

The tide sighed against the bank. It reminded her of another river—of Donegal, of summer nights spent watching dawn break over hills and streams. That land had always felt sacred to her, untouched, pure. Now, she longed for it more than ever. And with that longing came shame—a fierce, burning shame—for the lies she had told, the masks she wore, the love she kept hidden. She had built walls around herself, pretending to care only for Durrance, when all along, another name lived in her heart.

Yet she would not falter. Two lives—hers and Durrance’s—were at stake. She could not let her feelings destroy them both. Tomorrow, she would gather herself. Until then, she must remain hidden.

Inside the house, Durrance finished his confession.

“So you see,” he said, “I couldn’t speak of Harry Feversham until tonight. I feared hurting you. I feared you still remembered him. But tonight—I could tell you freely. Because I’ve seen the change in you. You’re not the same woman. You’re alive again.”

He waited. Called her name. No answer.

Then he moved. His hands touched the back of the chair. He leaned forward, searching for her presence. “Ethne?” he said again, voice softer now, tinged with doubt.

And then—twang.

A violin string snapped sharply. She had left it behind. His fingers had brushed it.

He froze. A shock passed through him. He stepped back, hand to his forehead, breathing shallow. Then, slowly, he walked to the window.

Mrs. Adair stood beside it, unmoving. The moonlight caught his face. He turned—his blind eyes searching the air, sensing something near.

“Ethne?” he called, tentatively.

“It is not Ethne,” Mrs. Adair said quickly.

Durrance stepped back. “Where has she gone?”

“She ran across the terrace. I saw her. Then I heard you speaking alone. She went into the garden—toward the trees.”

“Can you see her now?”

“No. Only the moonlight.”

He stepped out onto the sill, standing beside her. The silence stretched between them.

He knew now. The flight, the sudden silence, the broken string—it all pointed to one thing. He had spoken of Feversham, of his peril, of his journey into the wilderness. He had assumed she had forgotten. But she hadn’t. She had run because she could not bear to lie anymore.

“She ran as though she was in great trouble,” Mrs. Adair said.

“Did I cause that?”

“Yes.”

She watched his face. He was composed—but not unshaken. She had hurt him. And she took pleasure in it. Not because she loved him, but because she had suffered long enough. The irony of it—him confessing to an empty room, while she stood outside, yearning for what he would never give her—cut deep. So she gave pain in return.

“There’s one thing I don’t understand,” Durrance said. “The change in Ethne tonight. I mistook its cause. But there must have been a reason. Her laughter returned. Her gravity vanished. She became… young again.”

“Exactly,” Mrs. Adair replied. “Just as she was before Mr. Feversham disappeared from Ramelton. You’re quick, Colonel Durrance. Ethne received good news of him this morning.”

He turned sharply toward her. “Are you sure?”

“As sure as you gave her bad news tonight.”

But Durrance didn’t need confirmation. He recalled something else—Ethne mentioning that Feversham still received money from his father. “I heard it only today,” she had said.

Now it made sense. “So she misjudged him five years ago?” he murmured. “Was there really wrongdoing—or only misunderstanding? Has she learned the truth now?”

Mrs. Adair hesitated. Then: “I’ll tell you what I know. It’s not much. But you deserve to know.”

“Wait,” Durrance said sharply. He walked away, leaning on the balustrade, staring into the garden. He didn’t want to hear it. Not yet. He didn’t want to betray Ethne’s secret. But he also knew why she kept it. She didn’t want him to suspect that her heart still belonged to another.

Still, he turned back. “I’m no casuist,” he said. “But here—disloyalty feels like the truest loyalty. Tell me what you know.”

She did.

Captain Willoughby had arrived that morning from Kingsbridge. He had once served in Feversham’s regiment. He brought news—and a token.

“Ethne met him,” Mrs. Adair said. “They walked together into the little garden. I saw them. She led him there without looking back—as though she had forgotten.”

“As though she had forgotten me,” Durrance said quietly.

“No doubt she had.”

They stayed there for a long time. Durrance had left before they emerged. Mrs. Adair had been in the garden, near the water’s edge.

“She introduced him,” she said. “But wouldn’t let him stay. She sent him back immediately.”

“How do you know the news was good?”

“Her manner. Her laugh. She was radiant. And she carried a token—something precious.”

“A token?”

“A white feather,” Mrs. Adair said. “All soiled and speckled with dust. Can you read the riddle of that feather?”

Durrance paused. Then: “Not yet.”

He walked back and forth on the terrace, lost in thought. Finally, he returned to the hall, fetched his cap, and came back.

“It was kind of you to tell me,” he said. “Now—when I was in the room alone, and you came to the window—how much did you hear? What were the first words?”

Mrs. Adair smiled faintly. “You spoke of your blindness. Of your hope being gone. Of using it as an excuse to delay our marriage. She didn’t hear any of it.”

Relief washed over him. “Yes. She escaped the moment she read the letter. She didn’t know I’d given up hope. I’m glad. Very glad.”

He shook her hand. “Goodnight.”

“You see,” he added, almost to himself, “if I hear that Feversham is in Omdurman—something might be done. From Suakin. Or Assouan. Something might be done.”

“Which way did she go?”

“Over to the water.”

“She had her dog with her, I hope.”

“The dog followed her.”

“I’m glad,” Durrance said.

He knew what comfort the dog would bring her. And perhaps, in that moment, he envied it.

Mrs. Adair watched him walk across the garden, steady, purposeful—like a man who sees. There was nothing in his stride to betray the fact that the one thing he had left—his sight, his hope, his belief in the future—had just been taken from him.

And yet, he walked forward.
