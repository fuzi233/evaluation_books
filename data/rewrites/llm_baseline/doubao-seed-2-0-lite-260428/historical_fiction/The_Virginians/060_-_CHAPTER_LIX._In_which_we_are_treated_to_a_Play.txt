The earnest, humdrum business of ordinary life, I think, makes up very little of a novelist’s stock-in-trade. We may turn to tales of military service, where courage and cowardice, devotion and sacrifice, heroic death and narrow escape, naturally lend themselves to high, stirring narrative; but the rest of daily toil scarcely belongs between the pages of a story. What use is there in lingering over Ficulnus haggling over sacks of sugar, spices, and cheese at his wharf? Or Causidicus poring for twelve hours straight over mildewed black-letter law volumes? Or Sartorius cross-legged on his workbench, chalk in hand, taking the measure of a gentleman for a new pair of breeches? Would anyone sit through a tedious, blow-by-blow chronicle of a farm labourer’s hobnail boots tramping mud, his wages of eighteen pence a day? Even the poets of our own time dress their shepherds in pink silk breeches and chintz waistcoats, dancing before their flocks and playing a flageolet tied up with blue satin ribbon, rather than show them mucking out pens and threshing barley. I say this in reply to the gentle, potent critics who chide me for skipping over the finer details of professional life: saving for the theatre of war, there is no room for full descriptions of stockbroking, polemical theology, linen-drapery, or the daily grind of the apothecary’s shop. All a writer can do is depict his characters out of their working hours—ruled by their passions, their loves, their laughs, their hatreds—taking their daily labour for granted, leaving it as a quiet, unspoken background to the moments we share with them.

So it is that we only ever find our friends lingering in theatre lobbies, coffee houses, ridotto halls, fair booths, and feasting rooms, while the rest of the world plods on unseen in counting houses, weaves at looms, and tends to its endless, uncelebrated labours. Corydon the shepherd does not only make love to Phillis; he carts manure and stacks hay too. Ancillula the maid scrubs the nursery, waits at breakfast, and drags fractious children out for walks before she can snatch her five minutes of stolen courtship behind the area railings with her policeman sweetheart. We only ever stumble across our characters in the small, precious gaps between their work—and that is where we meet our equally disengaged readers, too.

The macaronis and fine gentlemen who haunted White’s and Arthur’s had turned so cold a shoulder to poor Harry Warrington that he soon stopped seeking their company at all. The Ring, the Mall, and the gaming tables knew his face no more. His aunt Madame de Bernstein bade him defy the town’s indifference, vowing that the world would soon relent if he only faced its snubs with a bold front. But Harry was far too honest to wear a false smile when he was stung, to hide his mortification under a mask of impudent wit, or flatter his way back into favour as so many men and women of fashion learned to do to get ahead.

“Fiddlededee, child!” the old lady snapped at him when he pulled a long face and complained of his treatment. “Everyone eats impertinence in this world. If you get a box on the ear when you are poor and fallen, you bite your lip, smile it off, and if you are clever, pay the insult back ten years later. Do you suppose I never had to swallow humble pie in my time? All of us do, when our luck runs out. Now that you are no longer the Fortunate Youth, be the Clever Youth, and win back the place you lost to bad luck. Go out more than ever. Attend every rout and party you are invited to, and seek out more still. Be civil to everyone, especially every woman you meet—only never forget to show the spirit you have in plenty. With economy, and your brother George’s admirable generosity, you can still cut a genteel figure. With that handsome face and frame of yours, you cannot fail to catch a rich heiress. Tenez! Go down to the City and mingle with the merchants there. They will never have heard that you are out of fashion at the Court end of town. A little management, and you can build a fine position for yourself again. And when last did you wait on Lady Yarmouth? She took a great fancy to you, you fool. I order you to attend her evenings regularly, and lose no opportunity of paying court to her.”

The old woman who had doted on Harry when he first stepped off the ship from Virginia, who had hung on his artless stories and lavished him with kisses and favours, was now siding with the world against him. She greeted him with coldness now, grew peevish and patronising, and would cast gibes and cutting jests at him in front of her guests until his honest face burned bright with humiliation, a dull ache of bewildered grief settling heavy in his manly, gentle heart. Even her servants, who had once fawned on him and bowed so deep their wigs nearly touched the floor, scarcely paid him any mind. More than once the footman told him her ladyship was indisposed or engaged when he called; no one pressed him to wait, no one invited him to stay for dinner as they had done in the days when he was the wealthy Fortunate Youth, companion to dukes and earls.

Harry brought all his woes to the Lamberts’ little Soho parlour, and poured out his heart to Mrs. Lambert in a passion of sorrow, reeling from the shock of how quickly the whole world could turn heartless on a man the moment his luck failed. While the kind ladies of the house bustled about their domestic chores, poor Harry would sit glum in the window seat, heart-sick and silent.

“I know you are the best, kindest people alive,” he would say to them. “And I know I must be the dullest company in all the world—yes, that I am.”

“Well, you are not very lively just now, Harry,” said Hetty, who had begun to take a sort of gentle command over him, and who secretly wondered, for a moment, was this the dashing hero she had once dreamed him to be?

“Why should he be lively when he is hurt?” Theo asked softly. “He has a good heart, and it pains him to see friends desert him. There is no harm in that at all.”

“I would have too much spirit to show I was hurt, no matter what they did to me!” Hetty cried, clenching her little fists. “I would smile even if that horrible old painted woman boxed my ears. She is dreadful, mamma. You think so too, don’t you, Theo? You said so last night, and mimicked her hobbling in on her crutch, grinning round at all her guests!”

“I may not like her,” Theo said, blushing bright red. “But there is no call to speak rude names of Harry’s own aunt right to his face.”

“You provoking thing, you are always right!” Hetty laughed. “And that is what makes me so cross. Harry, I beg your pardon, truly, for speaking ill of your relation.”

“I do not care about the rest,” Harry said. “But it is hard that she of all people should turn on me. I grew very fond of her. And somehow, it makes me mad when people I care for deeply turn away and treat me unkindly.”

“Suppose George were to do that?” Hetty asked, teasing. The little quiet web of affection was already being spun between them: George and Hetty, Theo and Harry, all tangled together in the soft, unspoken way of young people.

“You are clever and lively, Hetty, and you may fancy all sorts of things—but not that!” Harry cried, standing up straight, his face flushing dark. “You do not know my brother as I do. You would never dare to suppose my George could do anything unkind or unworthy.” He looked so fierce and loyal, his eyes bright, that Hetty turned pale as a sheet. She looked up at him, and said not a single word.

A little later, before he took his leave, Harry said in his simple, earnest way, “I am very sorry, Hetty, I beg your pardon if I spoke too roughly. But I will always fight anyone who says a word against my brother George.”

Hetty said nothing at all, just gave him her hand and dropped a tiny, prim curtsey. That night, when she and Theo were sitting up late, twisting their hair into curl papers and whispering all their secrets, Hetty sighed. “I thought it would be such pure happiness to see him every day, and I was so glad when papa said we would stay in London. But now I do see him, I keep offending him, over and over. I know he is not clever, Theo. But oh! is he not so good, and kind, and brave? Did he not look perfectly handsome when he was angry just now?”

“Silly little thing,” Theo laughed. “You are always making him out to be handsome, no matter what he does.”

General Lambert, for his part, had long ago taken to calling his wife “Bo” as a gentle rebuke for that all-consuming love of sentiment and match-making that lives in every good woman whose heart is worth a fig. He teased her that she was a fool if she fancied the two Virginian twins would fall in love with his girls. “Little Hetty may have her little fancies,” he said, “and little girls do, but they get over them. You know perfectly well, Molly, you fancied a dozen other young men before you set eyes on me,” he said, and his soft-hearted wife could not deny it. Harry, after all, was no great catch now: penniless, overshadowed by his elder returned brother, not a single acre he could call his own.

“Oh, of course, now he is poor we will turn him out just like all the rest of the world, I suppose!” Mrs. Lambert protested, wiping her eyes with her pocket handkerchief.

“Do I ever turn my back on a friend in distress, Molly?” the General asked, gently.

“No, my dear, I am a perfect goose, I own it!” she laughed.

“Then let the poor boy come to us whenever he likes, and welcome. This selfish town has scarcely another house that will take him in. He is unhappy, and being with us eases his mind. For God’s sake, let him stay as long as he likes!” the kind officer said. And so Harry always had a place at their dinner table, a chair by their fire whenever he wanted it. George, too, was welcome. At first he did not come with the easy, warm affection Harry felt for the Lamberts, for he was colder by nature, more mistrustful of himself and other people. But the family’s unfeigned kindness soon won him over, and he learned to love them for their own sake, as well as for their tender care of his brother. He could not help but see how low-spirited Harry was, and pity his brother’s quiet despair. He would joke dryly with him over their pipes, saying, “My dear Harry, I am George the Unlucky, now that you have ceased to be Harry the Fortunate. That rogue Florac would have done far better to leave that Indian brave to take my scalp in the forest, instead of killing him. Then everyone at White’s would still treat you like a prince. Our mother would be weeping over me as a poor defunct angel, instead of being angry that I have come back to supplant her favourite. You are her favourite, and you deserve to be. The whole world’s favourite—though if I had stayed dead, your cousin Maria would have insisted on marrying you, and that is how the gods would have revenged themselves on you for all your good luck.”

“I never can tell if you are laughing at me, or at yourself, George,” Harry said. “I never know when you are jesting and when you are serious.”

“Precisely my own situation, my dear Harry!” George laughed.

“But this I know: there never was a better brother in the world, nor better people than the Lamberts.”

“Never a truer word was spoken!” George said, squeezing his brother’s hand.

“If I am miserable, it is not your fault, nor their fault, nor even mine, perhaps,” Harry went on, in a low voice. “It is fate, you see. It is having nothing to do. I must work, but how, George? That is the question.”

“We will wait to hear what our mother writes us. We will plan once we have her letter,” George said.

“George?” Harry said, quiet and a little alarmed. “Do you know, I do not think I could bear to go back to Virginia.”

“What! Are you in love with one of the lasses here?”

“I love them like my own sisters, every bit! The dearest, best girls that ever lived!” Harry said quickly. “That is not why. After all that happened, and you saved me, I do not want to go back to that life of hunting, duck-shooting, planting tobacco, playing whist, and sitting through three hour sermons, over and over again, for the rest of my days, George. What else is there for me there? What is there for me to do at all? That is what makes me miserable. You could make your way anywhere, you are so clever. But for a poor fool like me, what chance is there? Until I find something worthwhile to do, I will never be happy.”

“Have I not always said the same thing? Are you finally coming round to my way of thinking?”

“What way of thinking, George? You know I always agree with whatever you think, you know that!” Harry said, the dutiful younger twin.

“That Florac should have let that Indian take my scalp, my dear!”

Harry burst out in an angry exclamation, and then the two lapsed into quiet, friendly silence, puffing on their pipes side by side.

They lived together, following their own separate routines, bound only by their deep, abiding affection for each other. Harry would never so much as touch one of George’s books, and would sit as quiet as a mouse in their lodgings while his brother pored over his studies. Soon they moved out of the Court end of town, much to Madame de Bernstein’s disgust, who fussed and pshawed at their “low” new address. George had taken a great liking to Sir Hans Sloane’s new reading room and museum at Montagu House, so they rented cheerful lodgings in Southampton Row, Bloomsbury, overlooking the green fields that stretched all the way to Hampstead, behind the Duke of Bedford’s gardens. Soon after, Lord Wrotham’s family returned to their Mayfair house, and General Lambert, whose work kept him in London, moved his household into furnished apartments in Soho, scarcely a ten minute walk from the twins’ new home. Night after night, Harry found refuge in their little parlour.

George was there often, too. As their acquaintance ripened, he visited more and more frequently, finding the Lamberts’ company far more pleasant than his aunt’s gambling circle, or Sir Miles Warrington’s heavy port and overdone mutton, or the noisy chatter of the coffee houses. Since the Warrington twins and the Lambert ladies were all relative strangers to London’s pleasures, they began to explore them together, visiting Vauxhall and Ranelagh Gardens, Marylebone Pleasure Grounds, the Tower of London, and, of course, the playhouse. General Lambert loved nothing more than to give his girls every innocent amusement he could afford, and George, who was open-handed and generous by nature, loved nothing better than to treat the friends who had been so kind to his brother.

George Warrington loved the theatre with every fibre of his heart. He had never once seen a proper stage play in Virginia, and had only enjoyed a handful of performances in Quebec, when he had visited Canada. Now that he was in London, with both Drury Lane and Covent Garden operating at their full glory, he thought he could never see enough plays to sate his appetite. Everything he loved he wanted to share with his friends, so it was no surprise he was eager to arrange a party to the theatre. “Drury Lane, ladies?” he asked. “You can see Garrick himself playing Shakespeare there.” But much to his surprise, the ladies declared they wanted to see the famous new tragedy showing at Covent Garden, written by a celebrated new Scottish author.

A brilliant new star had blazed across London’s theatrical sky: the great Mr. John Home, a Scottish Presbyterian clergyman, had written a tragedy called Douglas that everyone agreed was more classic, more elegant than anything written since the days of ancient Greece. The wits in the coffee houses all asked why on earth Mr. Garrick had refused to stage such a masterpiece at Drury Lane. Everyone said that while Shakespeare was great (and had grown far more popular lately, ever since Monsieur Voltaire had dared to criticise him), his plays were full of rough barbarisms that would shock a polite modern audience. Mr. Home, by contrast, knew how to be graceful even in the midst of grief and passion, how to depict death not just as awful, but delicate and pathetic, and he never degraded the Tragic Muse by larding it with crude buffoonery and cheap puns, as the old Elizabethan playwright had done. No less a person than the poet Mr. Gray of Cambridge declared there had not been dramatic dialogue of such true, noble style for a hundred years, and back in Edinburgh, where the play had first been staged, the triumphant Scots had shouted out from the pit in their broad dialect, “Where’s Wully Shakespeare noo?”

“I should like to meet the man who could beat Willy Shakespeare!” the General laughed.

“Pure national prejudice, no doubt,” George said.

“Beat Shakespeare indeed!” Mrs. Lambert huffed.

“Pooh, pooh, Molly!” the General teased. “You have cried far more over Mr. Richardson’s novels than you ever did over Shakespeare. Very few women truly love reading Shakespeare, no matter how much they claim to.”

“Oh, papa!” all three of the Lambert ladies cried, throwing their three pairs of hands up in protest.

“Well, then, why do all three of you insist we go see Douglas? And you two young Tories—will you go watch a play written by a Whig Scots minister who was actually captured fighting for the rebels at Falkirk?”

“*Relicta non bene parmula*,” George quoted, the scholar.
