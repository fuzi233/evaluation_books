Three weeks elapsed before the library's contents were finally secured and transported. Instead of retreating into silence, Romola remained vigilant, observing the dismantling of her world. The exhaustion born of violent emotional upheaval often breeds a dreamlike denial of reality's causes; thus, as the workmen departed each evening, she descended upon the chaos of straw and wooden crates with a hand-lamp. She paused before every vacant pedestal, every toppled artifact, driven by a bitter compulsion to verify that her love's absence and the world's sterility possessed a tangible, logical foundation. Yet, as the nights wore on, her visits shifted from verification to necessity. This vivification of pain regarding her father's memory became the sole vitality remaining in her affections. By December 23rd, knowing the final shipments were imminent, she sprinted to the upper loggia. She could not afford to miss the visceral sting of witnessing the slow wheels traverse the bridge and vanish.

The sky was overcast, pressing toward dusk. The Arno ran dark and shivering; the hills stood mournful. Florence, encircled by its stone towers, bore the silent, sepulchral aspect of a city viewed from above under unbroken shadow. Santa Croce, where her father lay interred, was swallowed by the gloom. Crawling slowly across the bridge and disappearing up the narrow street was the white cargo, resembling a cruel, deliberate fate intent on consigning her father's lifelong hopes to an unmarked grave. Romola felt less as the observer than as a participant in a shared hallucination: her father, helpless beneath the imprisoning stones, conscious of this removal, unable to reach out to tell her he was not alone.

She remained motionless even after the load vanished, indifferent to the biting cold, soothed only by the gloom that draped her like a mourning vestment, silencing the discordant joy of the city. Suddenly, the great bell in the palace tower shattered the silence with a mighty peal—not the hammer-stroke of alarm, but an agitated clamor of triumph. One by one, every other bell in every other tower seized the vibration, joining the chorus. As the sound swelled until the air itself seemed composed of noise, little flames, vibrating in sympathy, erupted between the palace turrets and the girdling towers.

That sudden clang, that leaping light, struck Romola like sharp wounds. These were the triumphs of demons celebrating her husband's treachery and the desolation of her existence. Less than three weeks prior, she had been intoxicated by the very sound of these bells; in the gladness of Florence, she had heard a prophecy of her own joy. Now, the universal celebration appeared cruel. She stood aloof from that common life—the Florence flinging its loud exultation to deafen the ears of sorrow and isolation. She could never again clasp hands with gladness, only with those whom the hard nature of joy inevitably forgot. In her bitterness, she perceived all rejoicing as mockery. Men shouted paeans with souls heavy with grief, then scanned their neighbors' faces to confirm whether joy truly existed. Romola had lost her faith in the happiness she once thirsted for; it was a hateful, smiling, soft-handed construct with a narrow, selfish heart.

Covering her ears, she fled down from the loggia, hurrying across the antechamber when she collided unexpectedly with her husband, who had come seeking her. His step was elastic, radiating a satisfaction unusual for him.

"Was the noise too much for you?" he asked, noting how she pressed her hands tighter against her auditory organs. He took her gently by the wrist, drawing her arm within his, guiding her into the salon adorned with dancing nymphs and fauns. "Florence has gone quite mad with acquiring its Great Council, designed to eradicate all evils under the sun, particularly the vice of merriment. You appear stunned, my Romola, and chilled. Do not linger late in that windy loggia without protection. I am summoned abruptly to Rome for learned business concerning Bernardo Rucellai. I depart immediately to join my party at San Gaggio tonight, ensuring an early start tomorrow. I require no trouble from you; my packages are prepared. I shall return shortly."

He anticipated nothing but her quiet endurance. He dared not kiss her brow, merely pressing her hand to his lips before departing. Tito sensed Romola was a more unforgiving woman than he had imagined; her love lacked that sweet, clinging instinct stronger than judgment, which he now recognized as the essence of a wife's charm. Nevertheless, this petrified coldness proved preferable to passionate, futile opposition. Her pride and her ability to discern where resistance was useless held a distinct utility.

Once the door sealed behind Tito, Romola shed the look of cold immobility that froze her whenever he approached. Internally, however, she was far from tranquil endurance. The days following the rupture with Tito had been consumed by active planning and preparation for the fulfillment of her purpose.

Her first command was directed at old Maso.

"Maso," she stated in a decisive tone, "we depart tomorrow morning. We can now overtake the first convoy of cloth while they await us at San Piero. Prepare the two mules tonight; be ready to set off at dawn and meet me at Trespiano."

She intended to accompany Maso as far as Bologna, then dispatch him with letters to her godfather and Tito, declaring her departure permanent. She engineered her exit for absolute secrecy, intending to hide her broken love and life from vulgar scrutiny. Bernardo del Nero was absent at his villa, escaping political suspicion to tend his lands. She had settled his debts without a personal interview. He remained unaware that the library was sold, left to conjecture that some sudden windfall enabled Tito to raise the necessary funds. Maso knew only that her journey was a secret; performing exactly as instructed was the greatest desire of his withered, wintry age.

Romola did not intend to sleep that night. After securing the door, she lit a taper and moved to the carved, painted chest containing her wedding garments. White silk and gold lay within, along with the long white veil and the pearl circlet. A great sob rose in her throat as she beheld them; they resembled the shroud of her dead happiness. Nestled in a tiny gold loop of the circlet was a sugar-plum—a pink hailstone from a shower of sweets. Tito had discovered it first, decreeing it should remain forever. At intervals like this, Romola was swept by a sudden wave of memory back to the era of perfect trust. She felt again the presence of the husband whose love rendered the world as fresh and wondrous to her as to a child sitting in stillness among sunny flowers. She heard his gentle tones, saw his soft, truthful eyes, and breathed that expansive freedom of the soul granted by faith in a being greater than oneself. In those fleeting moments, tears invariably rose: the woman's lovingness felt akin to a bereaved mother sensing tiny fingers warm on her bosom, yet finding them marble to her lips as she bent over the silent bed.

However, the chest held something else besides the wedding attire: a dark, coarse bundle wrapped tightly. Turning her gaze from the white and gold to the dark mass, her tears subsided as her hands touched the serge. That coarse roughness anchored her firmly in the present, stripping away love and delight. Unfastening the thick white cord, she spread the bundle on the table. It was the grey serge habit of a sister of the Third Order of Saint Francis, a *Pinzochera* living in the world yet devoted to piety. Romola resolved to don this garb as a disguise, putting it on immediately so that if she required rest before dawn, she would awaken perfectly prepared. Removing her black garment, she thrust her soft white arms into the harsh sleeves of the serge mantle. Feeling the hard girdle of rope bite her fingers as she tied it, she welcomed those rude sensations. They aligned with her new scorn for pleasure—the dexterous contrivance for selfish ease, the shrinking from endurance when others bowed beneath burdens too heavy for them, a trait that now united her with her husband. Gathering her long hair, she pulled it tight from her face, binding it in a severe knot at the nape of her neck. Taking a square of black silk, she tied it close across her forehead and under her chin, then drew the cowl over it. Lifting the candle to the mirror, she assessed her transformation. To any stranger, the disguise would be complete. To herself, she resembled her brother Dino: the full oval of her cheek required only wasting; her eyes, already sad, needed only to sink further. Did she resemble him in anything else? Only in understanding now how men could be prompted to flee earthly delights forever, dwelling on images of sorrow rather than beauty and joy.

She did not linger before the mirror. Setting aside vanity, she began collecting and packing the relics of her parents too large for her traveling wallet. All were destined for the chest, to be entrusted to her godfather upon her safe departure. First, the portraits; then, item by item, every object bearing a sacred memory found its place in the wallet or chest.

She paused. Something else remained to be stripped away from her, belonging to a past she intended to turn her back on permanently. She placed her thumb and forefinger on her betrothal ring, but they rested there, failing to draw it off. Romola's mind had rushed impetuously toward this act—the severing of a husband who betrayed all trust, the breaking of an outward tie that no longer mirrored the inward bond of love.
