I had not gone ten paces through the moss-choked, half-shadowed wood when a lopsided, half-buried shape caught my eye, and I drifted closer to make sense of it. It was a mouldering carriage of forgotten vintage, its gilded flaking, its frame warped with rot but still held upright on pitted, heavy wooden wheels. Flanking the rotting pole, still harnessed in rusted traces, lay the skeletons of two horses; from their grinning, lichen-dusted white skulls, shrivelled leather reins stretched taut up to the bony hand of a skeleton coachman perched stiff on his tattered, frayed hammer-cloth. Both carriage doors had fallen away to splinters long ago, and within the empty frame, two skeletons slouched back in their corners as if frozen mid-sulking journey.

Even as I stared, they stirred awake. There was a sharp, rattling crack of grinding bone, and each launched itself out the empty doorway nearest their perch. One slipped at once and crumpled in a heap of dead leaves. The other wobbled for a long, perilous moment, its entire frame shaking like a stack of dry twigs, then crept with stiff, grinding joints, one hand clamped to the back of the carriage for purchase, over to its fallen companion. Its thin leg bones seemed far too thin to bear its weight, and it nearly toppled twice as it knelt, bony hands fumbling to haul the other upright.

At last the prostrate skeleton heaved itself up to a sitting posture by its own effort, yellowed skull turning side to side as if testing its neck joints. Ignoring the other for a moment, it dragged itself to its feet by clinging to the spokes of the rear carriage wheel, half-hunched, both hands clamped around one loose, wobbly knee joint. The other, still kneeling, contorted its way upright a moment later, and turned its empty eye sockets on its companion.
“Have you hurt yourself, my lord?” Its voice was thin, distant, whistling through the hollows of its ribcage as if blown off course by some spectral wind.
“Yes, I have,” the other rasped back, rougher, his teeth clicking when he snarled. “You did nothing to help me, and this cursed knee is popped clean out of its socket!”
“I did my best, my lord.”
“No doubt, my lady, for it was terrible! I thought I’d never get my feet under me again—But bless my soul, madam! Are you out in your bones?”
She glanced down at her own ribcage, the movement of her skull making a small clinking rattle. “I have nothing else to be out in,” she returned dryly. “And you at least cannot complain! What in all the world does this mean? Am I dreaming?”
“You may be, madam—I won’t argue that—but this throbbing knee of mine forbids me that grateful illusion. Ha! I see now I’ve got nothing to walk on but bones too! Not so unbecoming for a man, I suppose. Though I do hope to goodness these aren’t *my* bones—every single one aches worse than the last, and this loose knee worst of all! The bed must have been damp, and I far too drunk to notice it when I collapsed.”
“Probably, my lord of Cokayne!”
“What? What? You make me wonder if I *am* dreaming, aches and all! How do you know the taunting title my roistering drinking buddies gave me? I don’t remember you! Anyhow, you’ve no right to take such liberties. My name is—am I lord—tut, tut. What do you call me when I’m—I mean, when you’re sober? I can’t think of it right this moment. Why, what *is* my name? I must have been very drunk when I went to bed last! I often am.”
“You came to my bedchambers so rarely, I scarcely know it myself, my lord—but I’ll take your word for that much.”
“I should hope so!”
“—If for nothing else, that is.”
“Hoity toity! I never told you a lie in my life!”
“You never told me anything *but* lies.”
“Upon my honour! Why, I’ve never seen this woman before in my life!”
“You knew me well enough to lie to, my lord.”
“I do seem to half-dream I’ve met you somewhere before, but on my oath, there’s nothing here to tell who you are! Stripped of your fine clothes, who could say who you might not be? One thing I can swear for certain—I never saw you this undressed before! By heaven, I have no recollection of you at all.”
“I’m glad to hear it. My few memories of you are all the less distasteful for that. Good morning, my lord.”
She turned away, hobbling across the leaf litter with a steady clack of bone on stone, then paused a few paces off.
“You are just as heartless as—as—any other woman, madam! Where in this hellish place am I supposed to find my valet? What was the cursed fool’s name I always called him?” He twisted his bare skull side to side on its creaking neck vertebra, still clutching his wobbly knee with both hands.
“I’ll be your valet for once, my lord,” the lady said, turning back to him. “What is it you need? It’s not easy to guess.”
“Tie my leg on properly, of course, you fool! Can’t you see it’s all but falling off? Heigho, my dancing days are done!”
Her empty sockets scanned the underbrush until she found a long, tough strand of fibrous grass, and she knelt to bind the displaced knee joint back into place. When she finished, he tested his weight with two careful, tentative stamps.
“You used to stamp your feet far harder than that, my lord,” she said as she stood.
“Eh? What? Now I look at you again, it seems to me I used to *hate* you—eh?”
“Naturally, my lord. You hated a great many people. Your wife, of course, was chief among them.”
“Ah, I begin, I be-gin to recall. But I must have been somewhere a very long time. I really can’t remember all of it. There! Your damned miserable bit of grass is already stretching thin! We used to get on pretty well together, didn’t we?”
“Not that I recall, my lord. The only happy moments I ever had in your company were scattered across the first week of our marriage.”
“Was that how it was? Ha! Ha! Well, it’s over now, thank goodness!”
“I wish I could believe it! Why were we both sitting in that carriage together? It wakes the worst of fears.”
“I thought we got divorced, my lady!”
“Hardly enough of one. We are still trapped together.”
“A sad truth, but easy enough to fix. This forest looks large enough to lose each other in.”
“I doubt it. I very much doubt it.”
“I’m sorry I can’t think of a compliment to pay you without lying. Judging by your frame and the yellow of your bones, you’ve lived very hard since I last saw you. I can’t surely be quite as naked as your ladyship—beg your pardon, madam! I do hope you’ll take it as a jest in a dream. It doesn’t matter anyhow. Dreaming or waking, it’s all the same, all just mere appearance. You can’t be certain of anything, and that’s as good as knowing nothing at all. Any fool learns that much from life.”
“It taught me what a fool I was to ever love you.”
“You weren’t the only fool to do that! Women used to have a trick of falling in love with me. I’d forgotten you were one of them.”
“I did love you, my lord, a little, once.”
“Ah! That was your mistake, my lady. You should have loved me much, loved me devotedly, loved me savagely, loved me eternally. Then I would have tired of you far sooner, and not hated you so bitterly afterward. But let bygones be bygones! Where *are* we? Locality is the only real question. To be or not to be is not the question at all!”
“We are in the afterlife, I presume.”
“Granted. But which sort, what kind of afterlife? This can’t be hell.”
“It must be hell. There’s still marriage in it. You and I are damned to each other.”
“Then I’m not like Othello, damned to a fair wife! Oh, I still remember my Shakespeare, madam!”
She picked up a sharp, broken fallen branch half-buried in a bush, steadied herself on it, and began to walk away, her skull tossing with every step.
“Give me that stick!” he cried after her. “I want it far more than you do.”
She did not answer.
“You mean to make me beg for it?”
“Not at all, my lord. I mean to keep it,” she replied, picking up her pace, the clack of her joints growing sharper.
He lurched to follow her, but nearly toppled at once: the grass around his knee had snapped. He swore, the sound thin and piping through his empty jaw, and clutched his loose leg again. “Come and tie this up properly right now!”
She turned, her empty sockets fixed on him for a long quiet moment, then took a few steps further away.
“I swear I will not harm you if you come back!” he shouted.
“Swear on, my lord. There’s no one here to believe you. But pray do not lose your temper, or you will shake yourself all to pieces, and I do not know where you will find enough string to tie all your crazy joints back together again.”
She walked back, knelt down at his side—but not before she set the disputed branch out of his reach, but close enough for her to grab. The second she finished retying his knee, he lashed out to grab for her hair, only for his bony fingers to slip uselessly over her smooth, bare skull.
“Disgusting,” he muttered, then clamped his hand hard around her upper arm bone.
“You will break it!” she said, looking up from her knees.
“Then I will break it,” he snarled, and began to twist.
“I will not tie your leg back together the next time it comes loose,” she threatened.
He wrenched at her arm harder, but her bones were in far better condition than his rotting ones. She stretched her other hand out for the branch.
“That’s right, reach me the stick,” he grinned, teeth clicking.
She swung it around in a wide, hard arc, and the bone of his good leg snapped clean through. He fell in a heap, choking on curses that only came out as thin whistling puffs of air. The lady laughed, a dry rattling sound like pebbles shifting in a bag.
“Now you will have to wear splints forever!” she said. “Such old dry bones never knit back together.”
“You devil!” he cried.
“At your service, my lord. Shall I fetch you two wheel spokes for splints? Neat, but heavy, I fear.”
He turned his bone-white face away, did not answer, and lay groaning in the leaves. I marveled he had not shattered entirely when he fell. The lady stood, adjusted her grip on the branch, and walked away—not entirely ungracefully, I thought.
“What can come of this?” I murmured to myself. “These two are far too wretched for any world I could imagine. But this can’t be hell. The Little Ones are here, and the gentle sleepers I met earlier, too. What can this all mean? Can things ever come right for a pair of skeletons?”
“There are words far too big for you and me. *All* is one of them, and *Ever* is another,” said a familiar voice somewhere close by. I looked all around, but could not see the speaker.
“You are not in hell,” it went on. “Neither am I in hell. But those two skeletons *are* in hell.”
Even as the voice finished, I spotted the raven perched on the bough of a beech tree right over my head, feathers black and glossy in the faint filtered sun. The next instant it launched itself from the branch, landed softly on the moss, and shifted at once into that thin old man I had seen in the library, long nose, trailing black coat and all.
“That man was never a gentleman,” he went on, nodding at the two figures in the distance. “Now, in this bony stage of their retrogression, all their pretense of skin and fine manners has fallen away, and their true characters are laid bare for all to see. The woman is less vulgar, and still has a little heart left beating somewhere in that empty ribcage of hers. Now that all the restraints of society are gone, you see them exactly as they are, exactly as they always were.”
“Tell me, Mr. Raven, what will become of them?” I asked.
“We shall see,” he replied. “In their day, they were the handsomest couple at the royal court. Now, even in their dry bones, they still carry the ghost of that old fame as if it were some inalienable possession. Back when they had flesh and fine clothes, they thought themselves rich beyond measure, but now they are already beginning to feel pinched for even a scrap of grass to tie a joint. My lord used to think my lady a useless encumbrance, when he had tired of her beauty and spent every penny of her fortune. Now he needs her to cobble his broken joints back together for him. All these small shifts have roots of hope in them. Besides, they cannot wander far from each other now, and they will meet no other souls like themselves for a very long time. At last they must grow weary of their mutual loathing, and begin to love one another—for love, not hate, is the deepest thing in everything that Love once loved into being.”
“I saw many more souls like them in the great hall an hour ago,” I said.
“Of their kind, but not of their sort,” he answered. “These two are centuries behind the souls you saw last night. The ones you saw are far further along. They can even dress themselves in faint, soft fabric for parts of the night now. By and by they will grow faces, and flesh, and lives of their own. Every grain of truthfulness in them adds a new fibre to their humanity. Nothing but the truth can appear here. And whatever is, must show itself.”
“Are they upheld by this hope?” I asked.
“They are upheld by hope, but they have no idea what that hope is. To understand it is still immeasurably far beyond them,” answered Mr. Raven. His sudden shift from bird to man did not surprise me. I had become like a small child, full of endless wonder at everything, but no longer shocked by anything.
“Did you come here to find me, sir?” I asked.
“Not at all,” he replied. “I have no anxiety about you. Souls like you always find their way back to us.”
“Tell me, please, what am I such as?” I said.
“I cannot make my friend the subject of idle conversation,” he said, with a faint smile.
“But the friend is right here, present with you!” I urged.
“Then I decline even more strongly,” he rejoined.
“But the friend is asking you directly!” I persisted.
“Then most positively I refuse,” he returned.
“Why?”
“Because he and I would be talking of two very different people as if they were one and the same. Your own idea of who you are, and my knowledge of who you really are, are worlds apart.”
The lapels of his coat flared out wide, the long tails lifting, and I thought I was about to watch the full metamorphosis of man to raven unfold before my eyes. But the coat fell closed again around him, and he added, with strange seeming inconsequence: “In this world, never trust a person who has once deceived you. Above all, never do a single thing that such a person asks you to do.”
“I will try to remember,” I answered. “Though I might forget.”
“Then some evil that is good for you will follow, all the same.”
“And if I do remember?”
“Some evil that would not have been good for you will not touch you at all.”
The old man seemed to sink a little into the moss, and the very next instant I saw the raven several yards away, flying low and fast over the underbrush, vanishing into the green dark of the trees.
