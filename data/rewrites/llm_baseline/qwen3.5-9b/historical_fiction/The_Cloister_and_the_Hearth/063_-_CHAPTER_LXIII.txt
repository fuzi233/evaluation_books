A profound darkness descended upon a noble mind. For years, his pure and unrivalled love for Margaret had served as his guiding star, illuminating his path through the storms of life. Now, that light was extinguished. He drifted aimlessly across a grey, hopeless sea. Yet, his torment was not merely despair; it was compounded by a searing exasperation. Every act of self-denial, every display of fortitude, every peril endured, and every sacrifice of virtue felt like salt rubbed into a fresh wound. The bitter irony gnawed at him: had he remained lazy and selfish at home, guarding his own comfort, he might have saved Margaret's life.

These twin poisons—despair and rage—raging together in his youthful blood, drove him mad. He rushed headlong into pleasure, seeking escape. In those days, as always, pleasure was indistinguishable from vice. Wine, women, gambling, and anything capable of procuring an hour of excitement or a moment of oblivion became his obsession. He plunged into these vices with the desperate frenzy of a man fleeing toward enemy bullets because he has grown weary of living. The substantial savings he had accumulated for Margaret now funded his debauchery. Soon, he became the leader of a circle of loose companions he had previously shunned. His heart rotted alongside his morals. He sulked violently against his old landlady for offering gentle advice, eventually moving to a different quarter of the city simply to escape her remonstrance and the painful memories associated with her home.

As the weeks wore on, his hand grew unsteady; he could no longer write with the precision required to satisfy his artistic standards. Furthermore, his patience eroded as the habits of indulgence took root. Abandoning drawing, he attempted portraiture in color, but even this craft was neglected whenever his idle cronies summoned him. Thus, he dove deeper into foul waters, searching desperately for that hollow shell known as "Oblivion."

It is not my purpose to paint a graphic picture of the coarse vices in which this unfortunate young man now immersed himself. However, it is essential to state the undeniable truth: he was a rake, a debauchee, and a drunkard—one of the most reckless and wicked youths in Rome. Those who wish to conceal or soften the wickedness of the virtuous, thereby robbing despairing sinners of hope, are not lovers of truth or humanity. Suffice it to say, the man was not born to do things halfway. Neither was his vice.

His former humble female friends began to gossip about him. His old landlady confided in Teresa that Gerard was going to ruin, praying Teresa to locate him. Teresa relayed the sad news to her husband, Lodovico, instructing him to search for the young man's current whereabouts.

"Shall I recognize his face, Lodovico mio?" Teresa asked.

"Teresa, a man in my line of work never forgets a face, especially that of a benefactor," Lodovico replied. "But you know I rarely venture abroad during the daylight hours."

Teresa sighed. "How long must this continue, Lodovico?"

"Until some cavalier drives his sword through me," Lodovico answered grimly. "They will not allow a poor fellow like me to pursue any honest trade."

Pietro Vanucci, who bore prosperity far worse than adversity, was one such figure. After being ignominiously ejected from their lodging by the landlady for keeping late hours, he encountered Gerard in the street. He greeted him warmly and shortly thereafter secured quarters in the same house. He arrived accompanied by a lad named Andrea, who ground his colors, served as his pupil, and acted as his model—a youth of striking beauty and sharp wit. Pietro had not entirely forgotten the old days and professed a fervent friendship for Gerard. Gerard, however, in whom all warmth of feeling seemed extinguished, accepted the other's friendship with icy reserve. It was a strange alliance: Pietro was a libertine, half-misanthrope, and an open infidel, while Gerard was a broken spirit. They walked together, bound by little in common.

One day, having undermined his health, stripped the bloom from his face, and squandered most of his fortune, Vanucci organized a boisterous party to ascend the Tiber in a boat pulled by buffaloes. Lorenzo de' Medici had introduced these beasts to Florence roughly three years prior, but they were novelties in Rome. Nothing satisfied Vanucci, the "beggar on horseback," except to be drawn upstream by the brutes. The plan was simple: each libertine was to bring a lady, who must be handsome, or face a fine. The guest contributing the most beautiful partner would be crowned with laurel and declared a public benefactor—a twisted interpretation of *vir bonus est quis?* ("Which man is good?").

They secured a magnificent galley and twelve buffaloes. Gradually, the libertines and their female companions assembled at the embarkation point. But Gerard was absent. They waited, first patiently, then with growing impatience. Vanucci offered an excuse. "I heard him say he forgot to procure a fardingale [a protective skirt]. Comrades, the good lad is hunting for a beauty worthy to stand among these peerless dames. Consider the difficulty, ladies, and be patient!"

Finally, Gerard appeared in the distance, holding a woman's hand.

"She is long enough," one woman critiqued from afar.
"Gemini! Look at her stride," said another.
"Oh! It is wise to hurry into good company," Pietro muttered as an excuse.

When the pair approached, all pretense of satire vanished. Gerard's companion was a peerless beauty; she eclipsed the entire boatload like the rising sun extinguishes the stars. She was tall but not towering, straight as a dart yet supple as a young panther. Her face was a perfect oval, her forehead pale, her cheeks a rich olive tone flushed with eloquent blood, and her glorious eyes framed by long, thick silken lashes seemingly designed to sweep away sensitive hearts. Her lips were saucy red, her teeth the whitest ivory.

The women were visibly depressed by this wretched sight; the men were in ecstasy. They welcomed her with loud shouts and waving caps. One enthusiast even knelt on the boat's gunwale, hailing her of divine origin. But as his mistress pulled his hair and the goddess delivered a playful kick, he collapsed supine. The peerless creature skipped over his body without deigning him a glance, taking her seat at the prow.

Pietro Vanucci sat in a sort of collapse, glaring at her with his mouth agape like a dying fish. The drover cracked the reins, the ropes tightened, and the buffaloes moved upstream.

"What do you think of this new beef, mesdames?" Vanucci asked.

"We have never seen such vile monsters," a lady retorted. "With their nasty horns that make one afraid, and their foul nostrils held high to the sky. They are holes, not nostrils."

"Signorina, the cattle are a gift from Florence the Beautiful. Would you look a gift horse—or beef—in the mouth?"

"They are so dull," objected a lively lady. "Last time I ascended the Tiber twice as fast with just five mules and an ass."

"Nay, that can easily be remedied," cried a gallant. Jumping ashore, he drew his sword. Despite the drivers' protests, he charged down at the dozen buffaloes, prodding them. The animals snorted and whipped their tails but refused to move faster. The passengers laughed loudly. Finally, he goaded a patriarchal bull, which instantly turned on the sword. With a furious jerk of its prodigious neck, it sent the man flying over his head. He described a bold parabola and fell sitting, unconscious, waving his glittering blade, directly into the yellow Tiber.

The laughing ladies screamed and wrung their hands, except for Gerard's companion. She uttered something resembling an oath, seized the helm, and steered the boat away. The gallant surfaced, sputtering, gripping the gunwale, and was dragged aboard dripping wet. He glared around him, confused and peevish.

"I do not understand that," he said, puzzled and clearly discontented. Realizing he had survived, though his doublet was perforated rather than his body, they began to laugh even louder.

"What are you cackling at?" the spark demanded. "I demand to know how it is that one moment a gentleman is out there pricking African cattle, and the next—"

Gerard's lady interjected. "Disporting in his native stream."

"Tell him not," cried Vanucci. "Let him solve his own riddle."

"Confound you all! I might puzzle my brains until Doomsday and never find it out. Also, where is my sword?"

"Ask the Tiber!" she replied. "Your best course, Signor, is to do it again. In short, keep pricking Africa's cattle until your mind receives enlightenment. Only then will you comprehend the matter, degree by degree, as lawyers climb to heaven and buffaloes cross the Tiber."

At this, a chevalier remarked that the speaker surpassed the sons of Adam in wit as much as she exceeded the daughters of Eve in beauty. At these compliments—and indeed at all of them—Pietro Vanucci's behavior became peculiar.
