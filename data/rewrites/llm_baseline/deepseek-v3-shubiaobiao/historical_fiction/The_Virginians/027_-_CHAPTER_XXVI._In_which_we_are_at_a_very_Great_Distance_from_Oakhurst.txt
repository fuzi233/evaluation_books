The White Horse Tavern’s garden stretched before them, its bowling-green bathed in golden afternoon light. Beneath the open windows of the dining room, guests lounged at scattered tables, sipping punch or tea in the warmth of the day. Having finished their meal, the three gentlemen—Lord March, Jack Morris, and Harry Warrington—strolled onto the green, where Lord March proposed a bottle of cool wine.

“Jack Morris would drag us to the Dust Hole if it meant another drink,” Lord March remarked dryly.

“Every man has his own road to ruin,” Jack retorted with a grin. “Mine happens to be a bottle.”

“And Lord Chesterfield’s is dice,” added Lord March. “Cards, horses, wagers—his lordship can’t resist a gamble.”

“Nor can you,” Jack shot back, nudging Harry. “Take care, Mr. Warrington. His lordship will play you for every acre in Virginia.”

“With pleasure,” Lord March interjected smoothly.

“For your tobacco, your slaves, your oxen—everything,” Jack continued with mock solemnity.

Harry laughed. “Fortunately, those are not yet mine to lose. I’ve only just come of age, and my mother, God bless her, is in far better health than I.”

“I’ll wager you outlive her,” Lord March said. “Name your sum.”

Harry shook his head. “You’d have to settle with her first. The estate isn’t mine to pledge.”

“Castlewood, isn’t it?” Lord March mused. “A fine prize, if half the tales are true. How did your family come by the name?”

“Through my mother’s line,” Harry replied. “An ancestor chose the wrong side in ’15.”

“Ah, yes. My grandmother knew of him. Friend of Addison and Steele, wasn’t he? Pity he didn’t stay in England—might have spared your branch the plantations.”

“I’ve just visited Castlewood with my cousin,” Harry said.

“Did you play cards with him?” Lord March asked sharply.

“Only for sixpence with the ladies.”

“But you played with Will Esmond,” Lord March pressed. “I’d wager ten to one you did.”

Harry flushed slightly but admitted to a few evening games.

“And the chaplain, Tom Sampson—was he there?” Jack Morris cut in. “God help you if he was.”

“As it happens, I won against both,” Harry said with a grin.

“And they paid you?” Jack feigned astonishment. “Miracles never cease!”

Harry merely smiled into his wine.

“You don’t betray confidences,” Lord March observed approvingly.

“I don’t speak French, my lord,” Harry replied, earning a laugh.

“Lord March learned his Italian at the opera,” Jack said. “Cost him a fortune.”

“Did it?” Lord March’s tone cooled slightly.

The afternoon wore on with bets and banter, the young men’s camaraderie as effervescent as their wine. A wager was struck over who could leap the farthest—Harry, with Virginian vigor, bested Lord March by nearly three feet, much to the company’s delight.

As evening approached, they joined the promenade along the public walk, where society’s finest mingled. Jack Morris’s loud jests drew glances, while Lord March exchanged nods with passing dignitaries.

“That’s Dr. Johnson,” Lord March murmured as a towering, scowling figure passed. “The dictionary man. Chesterfield patronized him once—until Johnson scorned the favor.”

“And the stout fellow beside him?” Harry asked.

“Richardson. Wrote *Clarissa*.”

Harry’s eyes widened. “The author himself?” He nearly bolted forward, but decorum held him back.

The walk was a parade of notables: bishops, duchesses, and the occasional infamous beauty. Lord March pointed out a garishly dressed woman.

“The Duchess of Queensberry,” he said. “A beauty in her day—now just a relic with a taste for poets.”

Harry barely listened. His attention was seized by a familiar figure—Lady Maria, gliding toward him with a smile.

“Have you forgotten me so soon, Mr. Warrington?” she asked, offering her hand.

Harry’s face burned as he bowed. “Impossible, my lady.”

She took his arm, her touch light but deliberate. “Shall we walk? Or do you prefer the music?”

“I prefer nothing but your company,” he said, too quickly.

She smiled, though her eyes searched his. The past week had altered him—she saw it in his hesitation. Once, he would have flushed with joy at her presence. Now, he was merely polite.

As they strolled, Harry’s thoughts strayed from her entirely. The thrill of the wager, the spectacle of society, even the shadow of his cousin’s betrayal—all eclipsed the woman beside him.

Lady Maria, ever the actress, kept her voice light. “Tell me of your adventures. I hear you’ve been winning fortunes at cards.”

Harry laughed. “Only a few guineas. But the day isn’t over yet.”

She pressed his arm gently, but his mind was already elsewhere—on the next wager, the next laugh, the next distraction.

The distance between them, once so perilously narrow, now stretched as wide as the ocean.
