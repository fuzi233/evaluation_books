XIII. It was a crowded night at Wallack’s Theatre. The play, *The Shaughraun*, starring Dion Boucicault in the title role and featuring Harry Montague and Ada Dyas as the lovers, had drawn a full house. The English company was at the height of its fame, and this particular production always filled every seat. In the galleries, audiences were swept up in fervent delight; in the stalls and boxes, people smiled gently at the familiar sentiments and theatrical clichés—yet still found themselves captivated by the performance.

One scene, in particular, held the entire theatre spellbound. After a quiet, almost wordless farewell between Montague and Miss Dyas, he turned to leave. She stood near the mantelpiece, gazing into the fire, dressed in a simple gray cashmere gown that clung to her tall figure and fell in long, graceful lines to the floor. Around her neck was a narrow black velvet ribbon, its ends trailing down her back. As he stepped away, she leaned her arms on the mantel and buried her face in her hands. He paused at the threshold, then quietly returned, lifted one end of the ribbon, kissed it softly—and slipped out without a sound, leaving her motionless and unaware.

The curtain fell just as the silence deepened. That moment—so understated, so full of unspoken sorrow—was the reason Newland Archer came to see *The Shaughraun*. He considered the parting of Montague and Dyas among the most moving scenes he had ever witnessed, rivaling even the finest performances he’d seen in Paris or London. Its restraint, its quiet ache, touched him more deeply than any grand gesture or dramatic cry ever could.

On this evening, however, the scene stirred something deeper than usual. Without knowing why, it reminded him of his own recent farewell with Madame Olenska—a conversation they’d shared a week or two before, after their private talk in her drawing room. There was no real similarity between the two moments: Montague was young, handsome, and heartbroken; Archer was neither. Miss Dyas was a striking red-haired woman of imposing presence, while Ellen Olenska was delicate, radiant, and profoundly different in every way. And yet, as the curtain closed, Archer felt a strange tremor in his chest. Why did this silent departure echo so vividly in his mind?

It was not the situation, but the feeling—the sense of something hidden, something tragic just beneath the surface. Madame Olenska had an uncanny way of making ordinary moments feel charged with drama, of suggesting depths beyond everyday life. She never spoke of them directly, but they were there, woven into her very being—perhaps from her mysterious past, perhaps from some inner intensity that made her seem destined for extraordinary things, even when she tried hardest to avoid them.

Archer had always believed that people weren’t shaped so much by chance as by their own nature—their innate capacity to draw events toward them. From the first moment he met her, he’d sensed that about Ellen Olenska. She seemed quiet, passive, even fragile—but somehow, she carried within her a storm that refused to be contained. Her calm demeanor only heightened the impression that she had lived through tempests others couldn’t imagine.

And now, after their last meeting, he was certain of what he’d suspected: Count Olenski’s accusations were not entirely baseless. The man who had once been known only as “the secretary” in her husband’s past—someone who had helped her escape—had likely earned her gratitude. She had fled a life so unbearable, so cruel, that it was natural she would be thankful to anyone who saved her. But that same gratitude, in the eyes of society and the law, made her appear complicit. It placed her on equal footing with her despicable husband.

He had told her this, as he had to. He had also made her understand that kind-hearted, conventional New York—where she might have hoped for mercy—would be the least forgiving place of all. To explain this to her, and to see her accept it with such quiet resignation, had wounded him deeply. A strange mix of pity and jealousy stirred within him—like a secret burden she had laid upon him, humbling her, yet making her more compelling.

He was glad it had been him she confided in, not Mr. Letterblair’s cold judgment, or the awkward glances of her family. Immediately, he took it upon himself to reassure them all that she had abandoned her plan to seek a divorce. He framed it as her own realization of its futility, and with great relief, everyone moved on—turning away from the "unpleasantness" she had spared them.

“I knew Newland would handle it,” Mrs. Welland said proudly of her future son-in-law. And old Mrs. Mingott, summoning him for a private chat, had praised his cleverness, then added impatiently, “Silly goose! I told her myself it was nonsense. Wanting to pass herself off as Ellen Mingott, a plain old maid, when she’s lucky enough to be a married woman and a countess!”

These words, these gestures—everything that followed—had made the memory of that final conversation with Ellen burn brighter in Archer’s mind. So when the curtain fell on the stage, he found himself blinking back tears. He rose to leave, turning slightly toward the side of the house—and froze.

There, in a box, sat Madame Olenska, surrounded by the Beauforts, Lawrence Lefferts, and several others. He hadn’t spoken with her alone since that evening, and had deliberately avoided being near her in public. But now their eyes met.

Mrs. Beaufort recognized him at once and gave her languid little wave—an invitation he could not refuse. Beaufort and Lefferts made room, and after a few polite exchanges with Mrs. Beaufort—whose chief concern was maintaining her flawless appearance—Archer seated himself behind Madame Olenska.

Only Mr. Sillerton Jackson remained in the box, murmuring to Mrs. Beaufort about Mrs. Lemuel Struthers’s recent Sunday reception, where rumors claimed dancing had taken place. Under the cover of this trivial gossip, Madame Olenska turned to him and whispered, her voice barely audible.

“Do you think,” she asked, glancing toward the stage, “he’ll send her yellow roses tomorrow morning?”

Archer flushed. His heart leapt. He had visited her twice since their talk—each time sending a box of yellow roses, each time without a note. He had assumed she didn’t know who sent them. Now, here she was, mentioning them—not with suspicion, but with a quiet recognition that stirred something fierce and tender inside him.

“I was thinking of that too,” he replied, his voice low. “I was going to leave just to take the picture with me.”

To his surprise, her cheeks darkened slightly, as if she were embarrassed by her own boldness. She looked down at the mother-of-pearl opera glass in her gloved hands and said, after a pause, “What do you do while May is away?”

He frowned faintly. “I stick to my work,” he answered, annoyed by the question.

It was true. The Wellands had left the previous week for St. Augustine, where Mr. Welland always spent the winter—out of concern for his bronchial health. He was a gentle man, soft-spoken, deeply rooted in routine. His wife and daughter always traveled with him, not out of obligation, but out of love. The family adored him, and he was their anchor. His sons, both lawyers like Archer, joined him for Easter and returned together. It was unthinkable for him to go alone.

Archer had wanted to go too—to spend a few weeks in sunshine, rowing boats with May, enjoying the warmth of southern skies. But professional duty kept him bound. To ask for leave in mid-winter would have been seen as frivolous by the Mingott clan. He accepted May’s absence with the resignation he now saw would be a constant companion in marriage.

He felt Madame Olenska watching him, her gaze soft beneath lowered lashes.

“I’ve done what you advised,” she said suddenly.

He blinked. “Ah—I’m glad,” he managed, uncomfortable that she brought it up now.

She went on, breathless: “I understand… you were right. But sometimes life is difficult… perplexing…”

“I know,” he said quietly.

“And I wanted to tell you,” she continued, lifting her opera glass quickly as the box door opened and Beaufort’s voice boomed in, “that I *do* feel you were right—and I’m grateful to you.”

Archer stood up. He left the box, and soon the theatre.

Earlier that day, he had received a letter from May. With her characteristic honesty, she had written: “Please be kind to Ellen while we’re gone. She likes you and admires you so much. And though she doesn’t show it, she’s still very lonely and unhappy. Granny and Uncle Lovell don’t understand her—they think she’s more worldly, more fond of society than she really is. I can see that New York must seem dull to her, even if the family won’t admit it. She’s used to wonderful music, art, brilliant people—artists, authors, the kinds of things you care about. Granny only sees dinners and dresses, but I can see that you’re almost the only one who can talk to her about what matters.”

Her wisdom—her insight—had touched him deeply. He had loved her for that letter. But he hadn’t meant to act on it. He was busy. And as an engaged man, he didn’t want to be seen as overly involved in Ellen’s affairs. He thought she could protect herself better than May imagined—she had Beaufort’s devotion, van der Luyden’s protection, and countless others waiting in the wings.

Yet every time he saw her, every time he spoke to her—even briefly—he felt a quiet certainty: May’s simplicity wasn’t foolishness. It was intuition. And she was right.

Ellen Olenska was lonely. And she was unhappy.
