The Miss Dashwoods had now been in town for more than two months, and Marianne’s longing to return home grew stronger with each passing day. She sighed for the open air, the peace of the countryside, and the quiet simplicity of Barton—where she believed true comfort might still be found. Elinor, though more composed, shared her sister’s desire to leave, though she was careful not to rush into plans that could strain their strength or cause discomfort. She understood the burden of such a journey, especially on one so unwell as Marianne, who refused to acknowledge any such limitations. Still, Elinor began to seriously consider how best to make their departure possible. She had already spoken to Mrs. Jennings about their wish to go, and the kind hostess responded with heartfelt resistance, pleading every reason to keep them longer.

But then came a proposal that, though it would delay their return by a few weeks, seemed far more suitable than any other option. The Palmers were planning to move to Cleveland at the end of March for the Easter holidays, and Charlotte Palmer had extended a warm invitation to both Miss Dashwoods and Mrs. Jennings to join them. While this arrangement might have seemed too familiar for Elinor’s delicate sense of propriety, Mr. Palmer himself spoke with such genuine courtesy and kindness—especially since his behavior toward them had improved greatly since learning of his sister-in-law’s unhappiness—that Elinor felt no hesitation in accepting.

When she told Marianne of her decision, however, her sister reacted with immediate distress. “Cleveland!” she exclaimed, her voice trembling. “No, I cannot go to Cleveland.”
“You forget,” Elinor said gently, “that its location is not near—”
“But it is in Somersetshire,” Marianne interrupted, her eyes glistening. “I cannot go into Somersetshire. There—where I once hoped to go… No, Elinor, you cannot expect me to set foot there.”

Elinor did not argue against the sentiment; instead, she sought to redirect Marianne’s thoughts. She reminded her sister that going to Cleveland would allow them to return to Barton sooner than they otherwise could—within just over three weeks. From Cleveland, only a few miles from Bristol, the journey back to Barton would take but a single long day. Their mother’s servant could meet them there, and they need not stay longer than a week. With such practical advantages, and knowing how deeply Marianne loved their mother, Elinor trusted that affection would outweigh sorrow.

Mrs. Jennings, far from wanting to part with her guests, pressed them to return with her from Cleveland. Elinor appreciated the gesture, but it did not change her mind. With their mother’s approval secured, all arrangements were made as thoroughly as possible. And slowly, Marianne began to find comfort in counting down the hours until they could finally leave London behind.

“Ah, Colonel,” Mrs. Jennings remarked when he first called after their departure was settled, “I do not know what you and I shall do without the Miss Dashwoods. They’re determined to go home with the Palmers, and I shan’t know what to do when I come back! Lord! We’ll sit and stare at each other like two bored cats.”

She may have meant this as a playful exaggeration—but perhaps also as a subtle hint. For soon after, when Elinor moved to the window to measure a print she intended to copy for a friend, the Colonel followed her there, his expression thoughtful, and they conversed quietly for several minutes. Mrs. Jennings, pretending not to listen, had changed her seat to sit beside the piano where Marianne played, yet she could not help noticing Elinor’s flushed face and agitated manner. Her attention was fully absorbed by what the Colonel said, and her copying paused entirely.

Then, as Marianne transitioned between pieces, a few words drifted clearly to Mrs. Jennings: “I am afraid it cannot take place very soon.”
Startled, she nearly cried out—then stopped herself, muttering under her breath, “This is strange! Surely he need not wait to grow older?”

Yet despite the apparent delay, Elinor showed no sign of disappointment. When they parted, Mrs. Jennings distinctly heard her say, in a voice full of sincerity, “I shall always think myself very much obliged to you.”
Delighted by the warmth of her gratitude, Mrs. Jennings marveled that the Colonel could leave so calmly, without even acknowledging it. She had never imagined him so indifferent a suitor.

But what truly passed between them was far different from what she suspected.

“I have heard,” the Colonel said, with deep compassion, “of the injustice your friend Mr. Ferrars has suffered at the hands of his family. If I understand correctly, he has been cast aside for standing firm in his engagement to a most worthy young woman. Is this true?”
Elinor confirmed it.

“The cruelty of it,” he replied, “the sheer imprudence of tearing apart two people so long devoted to one another, is heartbreaking. Mrs. Ferrars does not know what she may be driving her son to. I’ve seen Mr. Ferrars a few times in Harley Street, and I find him admirable—a man of quiet dignity and good character. Though one cannot become intimately acquainted with him quickly, I’ve seen enough to wish him well—for his own sake, and because he is dear to you. I understand he intends to enter the clergy. Would you do me the favor of telling him that the living of Delaford, recently vacated as I learned today, is his if he chooses to accept it? Of course, given his present circumstances, it may seem a poor offering. But I hope it may still be of some use. It is a small rectory—the late incumbent earned no more than two hundred pounds a year. Though it could be improved, I fear not enough to provide a comfortable income. Still, I would take great pleasure in giving it to him. Please convey my offer to him.”

Elinor was stunned—her astonishment equal to what she might have felt had he proposed marriage. A position she had thought utterly beyond Edward’s reach was now offered to him, and she—of all people—was chosen to deliver it. Her emotion was profound, though not born of romantic hope. It was gratitude, respect, and admiration for the Colonel’s generosity and kindness. She thanked him sincerely, praising Edward’s integrity and devotion, and promised to carry the message if he truly wished her to.

Yet she could not help adding, “No one could perform this task better than yourself.”
And indeed, she would have preferred to spare Edward the burden of receiving a favor through her. But the Colonel, equally sensitive to delicacy, declined the role himself, insisting she should act as his messenger. So she agreed, grateful to be able to serve.

Edward, she believed, was still in town—and fortunately, she had learned his address from Miss Steele. She would inform him of the offer before the day ended.

Afterward, the Colonel spoke of his own happiness at having such a respectable neighbor, and then, with gentle regret, mentioned that his house was small and modest.
Elinor, seeing the concern, reassured him: “The size of the house matters little. It will suit their family and means perfectly.”

The Colonel was surprised—this response revealed that she already saw Edward’s marriage as inevitable. He had assumed the living was too insignificant to support a wife. “It can barely sustain a bachelor,” he said. “It cannot enable him to marry. My influence ends here. But if fortune ever allows me to assist him further, I would do so gladly—though I must confess, I see no way forward now. What I offer today seems almost nothing, since it brings him so little closer to the true joy he seeks. His marriage remains distant—at least, I fear it cannot happen soon.”

That was the sentence Mrs. Jennings had misheard, thinking it a refusal of love. But now, understanding the truth of their conversation, Elinor’s gratitude appears not less sincere, nor less rightly expressed—whether the gift was a hand in marriage or a future in life.
