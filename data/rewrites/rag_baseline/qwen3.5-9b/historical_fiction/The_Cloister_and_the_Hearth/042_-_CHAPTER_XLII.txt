The letters of fire scrawled upon the church wall had interrogated the silence with a genuine, burning curiosity, demanding to know why no Mass was held for the Duke during this season of turmoil. This supernatural exclamation had been witnessed by many, lingering briefly before fading into the gray mist, leaving the spectators staring with mouths agape at the empty space where divine favor should have burned. Yet, the Corporation, unwilling to lag behind celestial powers in their fealty to a temporal sovereign, generously funded the services. From this transaction, an old friend of ours, the Cure, derived a substantial profit—a sum he possessed a distinct and refined taste for. However, I shall not detain you further with such trivialities as miracles, nor with the mundane economics of piety.

Denys pleaded for his weapons. "Why disgrace him as well as shatter his heart?" he demanded, his voice tight with the friction of conflicting loyalties.

"Then swear upon the cross of thy sword," the commander replied, his tone leaving no room for negotiation, "not to abandon the bastard's service until the sedition is extinguished." Necessity, that cruel architect, compelled Denys to yield. He delivered three volleys of oaths, binding his soul to the cause, and in exchange, recovered his arms and his liberty.

The troops halted at an inn known as "The Three Fish." Upon seeing Denys, Marion cried out, "I am out of luck; who would have thought to see you again?" Her greeting was blunt, lacking the polish of courtly romance. Seeing that Denys wore a mask of sorrow rather than amusement at her jest, she pressed him gently. "What troubles thee?"

He confessed his burden. Marion, however, possessed a bright, unyielding optimism regarding the trajectory of fate. "Gerard is too handsome and too well-behaved to come to harm," she reasoned, her logic as sturdy as the stone walls of the city. "Moreover, the women will always stand by his side. Things must either go well or ill with him. If fortune smiles, he joins the noble companies marching to Rome; if ill, he returns home, perhaps arriving before his friend, for you have a trifling campaign in Flanders awaiting you by all accounts."

She produced her purse, offering gold pieces to ease his journey. Denys steadfastly refused to accept a single coin, despite her repeated urging. Yet, Denys found himself swayed by her argument, not merely because she aligned with his own desires, but because her honesty warmed him. It made him regard the world in which his poor, solitary friend wandered with a newfound respect.

Scattered foot soldiers, reduced to small bands of two or three, were already striding lazily toward Flanders. Many were penniless, drifting from town to town, relying on bailiffs to secure food and lodging from innkeepers along the way. Anthony of Burgundy intercepted these wandering groups, gathering them beneath his banner until he led a formidable host of six hundred men into the province. Crossing the frontier, he was greeted by his brother Baldwyn, who arrived laden with men, arms, and provisions. Anthony organized his entire force, marching in battle array through several towns. Their passage was marked not only by a lack of impediment but by great acclamations from the populace. Such overt loyalty, however, drew comments that were far from gracious.

"This rebellion of ours is a bite," growled a soldier named Simon, who had elected himself Denys's comrade, his voice dripping with cynicism.

Denys said nothing, but he made a silent vow to Saint Mars: should this uprising, which had cost him Gerard, prove to be anything less than a true rebellion, he would shoot Anthony of Burgundy dead.

That afternoon, the army came into sight of a strongly fortified town. A whisper rippled through the little army, identifying the place as disaffected. Yet, when they approached, the great gate stood wide open, and the flanking towers were manned by a single sentinel each. Confident and careless, the advancing force broke their formation.

Within a furlong, the drawbridge across the moat rose slowly, creaking with the groan of timber, until it stood vertical against the fortifications. The very moment it settled into this warlike posture, the portcullis rumbled downward at the gate, and the towers and curtains bristled with lances and crossbows.

A stern hum vibrated through the bastard's front rank and spread to the rear.

"Halt!" cried Denys. The command traveled down the line, and they froze. "Herald to the gate!" A pursuivant spurred out of the ranks, halting twenty yards from the entrance. Raising his bugle with his herald's flag hanging low beside him, he blew a summons. A tall figure in brazen armor appeared over the gate. Fiery words exchanged between the herald and the gatekeeper were inaudible, yet their intent was clear; the herald blew a single, keen, threatening note at the walls and galloped back, war written plainly upon his face.

The bastard moved out of the line to meet him. Within two seconds of their heads coming together, he turned in his saddle and shouted, "Pioneers, to the van!" Instantly, hedges were leveled, the force took the field, and encamped just out of range of the walls. Mounted officers flew south, east, and west to friendly towns, scouring for catapults, palisades, mantelets, raw hides, tar-barrels, carpenters, provisions, and every material necessary for a siege.

This bright prospect greatly cheered the drooping spirits of the soldiers. At the first clang of the portcullis, eyes brightened and temples flushed. When the herald returned with battle in his gaze, the realization hit them in a moment, and for the first time in many days, a cry rang out: "Courage, *tout le monde*, le diable est mort."

If that great warrior heard, how he must have grinned!

The besiegers established their camp a furlong from the walls and began constructing roads. Pikemen remained in camp, ready for assault when practicable, while sappers, pioneers, catapultiers, and crossbowmen moved forward. These engineers initiated the siege by filling the moat, mining, and preparing to breach the walls. Since much of this work required execution under the close fire of arrows, quarrels, bolts, stones, and rocks, these artisans needed a hundred eyes. They operated with a concert of vigilance, individual intelligence, daring, and skill that rendered the siege not only fascinating but, to the observers, almost amusing.

First, they advanced their carpenters behind rolling mantelets to erect a high, strong stockade right on the edge of the moat. Some lives were lost, but not many. A strong force of crossbowmen, including Denys, rolled their mantelets up and fired over the workmen's heads, targeting every besieged who exposed himself and every loophole, arrow-slit, or aperture commanding the specific spot where the carpenters labored. Shielded by this condensed fire, they soon raised a high palisade between themselves and the ordinary missiles raining down from the pierced masonry.

But the besieged anticipated this move. At night, they ran out boards or wooden penthouses atop the curtains. The curtains were constructed with square holes near the top designed to receive beams supporting these structures—the true defense of medieval forts. From these positions, the defenders delivered missiles with far greater freedom and variety of range than the besiegers could achieve through the oblique, immovable loopholes of the curtain or the sloping crenelets of the higher towers. In response, the besiegers brought up mangonels, setting them to hurl huge stones at these woodworks, battering them to pieces. Simultaneously, they erected a triangular wooden tower as high as the curtain, keeping it ready for use just out of immediate shot.

To the besieged, this was a terrible sight. Wooden towers had taken many a town. They began mining underneath the section of the moat where the tower stood frowning, making other preparations to give it a warm reception. The besiegers also mined, aiming to undermine the square barbican and bring it crashing down. Throughout this chaos, Denys remained behind his mantelet alongside another arbalestrier, protecting the workmen and delivering excellent shots. These actions eventually earned him the esteem of an unseen archer, who periodically sent a winged compliment quivering into his mantelet. One bolt struck within an inch of the narrow slit through which Denys was squinting at that precise moment.

"Peste!" cried Denys. "You shoot well, my friend. Come forth and receive my congratulations! Shall merit such as thine hide its head? Comrade, it is one of those cursed Englishmen, with his half-ell shaft. I will not die until I have had a shot at London Wall."

On the side of the besieged, a figure soon attracted great notice by promenading under fire. It was a tall knight, clad in complete brass, carrying a light but prodigiously long lance with which he directed the movements of the defenders. Whenever disaster befell the besiegers, this tall knight and his long lance were invariably implicated.

"My young reader" might ask, "Why did not Denys shoot him?" Denys did shoot him; every day of his life. Other arbalestriers shot him; archers shot him. Everyone shot him. He was there to be shot, seemingly by design. But the abomination was that he did not mind being shot. Worse still, he grew so demoralized by the barrage that he seemed unaware when he was struck.
