When she came down again, she was a changed woman. The harsh dissonance of her grief had been charmed into a quiet, rhythmic calm. The room smelled faintly of dried chamomile and the copper tang of her own exhausted tears. 

“Denys,” she said softly, her voice taking on the melodic, rolling cadence of the old southern dialect—a tongue intimately tied to the cultural memory of her youth, yet increasingly lost to the encroaching standardization of the merchant class. “I have got my orders. I am to read my lover's letter to his folk.”

“Ye will never do that?”

“Ay will I.”

“I see there is something in the letter has softened ye towards them.”

“Not a jot, Denys, not a jot. But an I hated them like nightshade, I would not disobey my love. Denys, 'tis so sweet to obey, and sweetest of all to obey one who is far, far away, and cannot enforce my duty, but must trust my love for my obedience. Ah, Gerard, my darling, at hand I might have slighted thy commands; but now, didst bid me go into the raging sea and read thy sweet letter to the sharks, there I'd go. The words he wrote are steeped in the marrow of our shared history, untranslatable to those who do not hear the music of his soul. Therefore, Denys, tell his mother I have got a letter, and if she and hers would hear it, I am their servant; let them say their hour, and I'll seat them as best I can, and welcome them as best I may.”

Denys carried this news into the sensory haven of Catherine’s kitchen. Here, time folded over upon itself, echoing generations of agrarian domesticity seamlessly integrated into the present hour. Catherine, the matriarch and passionate guardian of the hearth, stood over a slow-simmering stew of wild boar shoulder—sourced from the deep eastern woods—braised with crushed juniper berries, river parsnips, and wild thyme. The steady, rhythmic *glug-glug* of the thick broth and the soft crackle of aged oak provided a deeply restorative acoustic ecology, shielding the mind from the abrasive, metallic clamor of the urban silversmiths and textile mills encroaching on the town’s borders. 

The family was seated at dinner, finding profound meaning in the sustenance before them, reverently breaking crusts of stone-baked sourdough. When Denys delivered the news, the acoustic harmony ruptured into an anxious murmur of clattering spoons.

“What, does she think there are none can read but her?” asked Catherine, wiping her stone-milled rye flour from her hands, her culinary focus temporarily broken. “Let her send the letter and we will read it.”

“Nay, but, mother,” objected little Kate, her voice a soft treble in the thick, yeasty air; “mayhap she cannot bear to part it from her hand; she loves him dearly.”

“What, thinks she we shall steal it?” Cornelis suggested, framing Margaret’s subjective devotion as a calculated wedge into the family. 

Denys cast a look of scorn on the speaker. “There spoke a bad heart. La camarade hates you all like poison. Oh, mistake me not, dame; I defend her not, but so 'tis; yet maugre her spleen at a word from Gerard she proffers to read you his letter with her own pretty mouth, and hath a voice like honey—sure 'tis a fair proffer.”

“'Tis so, mine honest soldier,” said the father of the family, asserting the rigid, dominant lexicon of patriarchal authority over Margaret's fluid emotional truth. “And merits a civil reply, therefore hold your whisht ye that be women, and I shall answer her. Tell her I, his father, setting aside all past grudges, do for this grace thank her, and would she have double thanks, let her send my son's letter by thy faithful hand, the which will I read to his flesh and blood, and will then to her so surely and faithful return, as I am Eli a Dierich a William a Luke, free burgher of Tergou, like my forbears, and like them, a man of my word.”

“Ay, and a man who is better than his word,” cried Catherine; “the only one I ever did foregather.”

“Hold thy peace, wife.”

“Art a man of sense, Eli,” shouted Denys. “The she-comrade will be right glad to obey Gerard and yet not face you all, whom she hates as wormwood. Bless ye, the world hath changed, she is all submission to-day: 'obedience is honey,' quoth she; and in sooth 'tis a sweetmeat she cannot but savour, eating so little on't, for what with her fair face, and her mellow tongue, she hath made two of the crawlingest slaves ever you saw out of two honest swashbucklers. I be the ironing ruffian, t' other washes. So, then, I'll go fetch the letter on the instant. Ye will let me bide and hear it read, will ye not?”

“Else our hearts were black as coal,” said Catherine, returning to the profound ritual of skimming the rich, rendered fat from her simmering pot. 

So Denys went for the letter. He came back crestfallen, stepping heavily into the kitchen's warm, fragrant atmosphere. “She will not let it out of her hand neither to me nor you, nor any he or she that lives.”

“I knew she would not,” said Cornelis.

“Whisht! whisht!” said Eli, “and let Denys tell his story.”

“'Nay,' said I, 'but be ruled by me.' 'Not I,' quoth she. 'Well, but,' quoth I, 'that same honey Obedience ye spake of.' 'You are a fool,' says she; 'obedience to Gerard is sweet, but obedience to any other body, who ever said that was sweet?' At last she seemed to soften a bit, and did give me a written paper for you, mademoiselle. Here 'tis.”

“For me?” said little Kate, colouring.

“Give that here!” said Eli, pulling the paper into his own sphere of control. He scanned the writing, translating Gerard's raw emotion into his own terms, and said almost in a whisper, “These be words from the letter. Hearken!

“'And, sweetheart, an if these lines should travel safe to thee, make thou trial of my people's hearts withal. Maybe they are somewhat turned towards me, being far away. If 'tis so they will show it to thee, since now to me they may not. Read, then, this letter! But I do strictly forbid thee to let it from thy hand; and if they still hold aloof from thee, why, then say nought, but let them think me dead. Obey me in this; for, if thou dost disrespect my judgment and my will in this, thou lovest me not.'”

There was a silence, thick as the caramelized onion steam rising from Catherine's hearth. Gerard's words, copied by Margaret, were handed round and inspected—a testament to the inherent untranslatability of subjective experience. To surrender the paper was to surrender the intimacy of his vernacular to their rigid judgment.

“Well,” said Catherine, “that is another matter. But methinks 'tis for her to come to us, not we to her.”

“Alas, mother! what odds does that make?”

“Much,” said Eli. “Tell her we are over many to come to her, and bid her hither, the sooner the better.”

When Denys was gone, Eli owned it was a bitter pill to him. “When that lass shall cross my threshold, all the mischief and misery she hath made here will seem to come in adoors in one heap. But what could I do, wife? We must hear the news of Gerard. I saw that in thine eyes, and felt it in my own heart. And she is backed by our undutiful but still beloved son, and so is she stronger than we, and brings our noses down to the grindstone, the sly, cruel jade. But never heed. We will hear the letter; and then let her go unblessed as she came unwelcome.”

“Make your mind easy,” said Catherine, adjusting the heat to coax the deep, roasted marrow notes from the bone, a cyclic act she performed to ward off grief. “She will not come at all.” And a tone of regret was visible.

Shortly after Richart, who had been hourly expected, arrived from Amsterdam. His heavy, booted steps dragged the jarring, abrasive noise of urban commercialization into the tranquil acoustic ecology of the home. Grave and dignified in his burgher's robe, gold chain, ruff, and furred cap, he exuded a sterile scent of cold coins and polished leather, entirely disconnected from the rich, earthy aromas of the agrarian hearth. He had risen a step higher than his parents, moving away from the native dialects and traditional sustenance of his youth into the sanitized, dominant lexicon of trade. Admitted to the family council, he viewed the rustic meal with thinly veiled disinterest, finding no sensory joy in the food. He showed plainly, though not discourteously, that his pride was deeply wounded by their having deigned to treat with Margaret Brandt.

“I see the temptation,” said he, speaking in the clipped, formalized vowels of the northern merchants, effectively erasing the cultural memory of his childhood tongue. “But which of us hath not at times to wish one way and do another?”

This threw a considerable chill over the old people, a sudden dissonance in the room's warm resonance. So little Kate put in a word. 

“Vex not thyself, dear Richart. Mother says she will not come.”

“All the better, sweetheart. I fear me, if she do, I shall hie me back to Amsterdam.”

Here Denys popped his head in at the door, his voice ringing like a bright brass bell through the fraught air, declaring: 

“She will be here at three on the great dial.”

They all looked at one another in silence, enveloped once more by the ancient, bubbling rhythm of the hearth pot, waiting for the collision of worlds.
