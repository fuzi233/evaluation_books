"That doubt and trouble, fear and pain,
And anguish, all, are shadows vain,
That death itself shall not remain;

That weary deserts we may tread,
A dreary labyrinth may thread.
Thro' dark ways underground be led;

Yet, if we will one Guide obey,
The dreariest path, the darkest way
Shall issue out in heavenly day;

And we, on divers shores now cast,
Shall meet, our perilous voyage past,
All in our Father's house at last!"

Margaret ascended the stairs with hurried steps the moment her visitors departed, donning her bonnet and shawl to hasten to the Higgins household. Her purpose was twofold: to inquire after Betsy's condition and to offer her companionship until dinner. As she navigated the crowded, narrow thoroughfares, she felt a profound shift within herself; the mere act of caring for a resident of these streets had imbued the environment with a new depth of significance.

Inside the Higgins home, Mary, the younger sister whose appearance was often described as slatternly, had attempted to prepare for the arrival with whatever dignity she could muster. Rough stoning covered the center of the floor, yet the flags beneath the chairs and table remained dark and unwashed. Despite the sweltering heat outside, a large fire burned brightly in the grate, transforming the interior into an oven. Margaret, however, did not interpret this lavish consumption of coal as a gesture of warm hospitality; rather, she suspected the stifling warmth was a necessity for Bessy's fragile state. Bessy lay upon a small sofa positioned beneath the window, appearing far more debilitated than the previous day. Exhausted from repeatedly rising to peer out the window for Margaret's approach, she now sank back into silence, her gaze fixed on Margaret's face with a childlike wonder as she traced the fine textures of Margaret's clothing.

"I never understood why the scriptures spoke so highly of soft garments," Bessy remarked, her voice weak. "It must be delightful to be dressed as you are. It is unlike the common attire. Most fine clothes tire my eyes with their vibrant colors, yet yours possess a quality that soothes me. Where did you acquire this frock?"

"In London," Margaret replied, amused by the question.

"London! Have you actually been there?"

"Yes. I resided there for several years. Though my true home lies in the countryside, amidst the forests."

"Tell me of it," Bessy urged, leaning back and closing her eyes, crossing her hands over her chest as if to receive the incoming imagery. "I crave to hear of the country, of trees, and such things."

Margaret, who had rarely spoken of Helstone since leaving it—mentioning it only incidentally—found her heart suddenly opened to this young girl. "Oh, Bessy, I loved the home we left so dearly! I wish you could see it. I cannot describe half its beauty. Great trees stand everywhere, their branches stretching long and level to create a deep, resting shade even at noon. Yet, though every leaf appears still, there is a constant rushing sound of movement all around—not close at hand. Sometimes the turf is as soft and fine as velvet; at other times, it is lush with the perpetual moisture of a hidden, tinkling brook nearby. In other sections, there are billowy ferns—vast stretches of them, some in green shadow, others streaked with golden sunlight, resembling the sea."

"I have never seen the sea," Bessy murmured. "But continue."

"And here and there lie wide commons, elevated as if above the very treetops—"

"That pleases me," Bessy interjected. "I felt suffocated down below. Whenever I ventured out, I always sought to ascend high, to gaze far away and draw a deep breath of fullness from the air. I become suffocated enough in Milton, and I imagine the eternal sound you describe among the trees would render me dazed; it was precisely that which caused my head to ache in the mill. On these commons, I suppose, there is little noise?"

"No," Margaret assured her. "Nothing but occasionally a lark high in the sky. Sometimes I would hear a farmer speaking sharply to his servants, but it was so distant that it merely reminded me pleasantly that others were laboring hard in some remote place while I sat on the heather, doing nothing."

"I once thought that if I could possess a day of pure idleness, resting in a quiet place like the one you describe, it might restore me. But now, after many days of idleness, I am just as weary of them as I was of my work. Sometimes I am so utterly exhausted that I fear I cannot enjoy heaven without a period of rest first. I am somewhat afraid of going straight there without first receiving a good sleep in the grave to strengthen me."

"Do not be afraid, Bessy," Margaret said, placing her hand gently on the girl's. "God can grant you a far more perfect rest than either earthly idleness or the dead sleep of the grave can provide."

Bessy shifted uneasily. "I wish my father would not speak to me as he does. He means well, as I told you yesterday, and I tell you again. But you see, though I do not believe him during the day, by night—when I am feverish, half-asleep and half-awake—it returns upon me. Oh! so terribly! And I think, if this is truly the end of all, and if all I was born for was to wear out my heart and life, to sicken in this dreary place with those mill-noises ringing in my ears forever until I scream for them to stop and let me have a little quiet—and with the fluff filling my lungs until I thirst to death for one long, deep breath of the clear air you speak of—and my mother gone, unable to tell her again how I loved her, and all my troubles... I think, if this life is the end, and there is no God to wipe away all tears from all eyes—you wench, you!" She sat up abruptly, clutching Margaret's hand violently, almost fiercely. "I could go mad, and kill you, I could!" She collapsed back, completely drained by her outburst.

Margaret knelt beside her. "Bessy, we have a Father in Heaven."

"I know it! I know it," Bessy moaned, turning her head restlessly from side to side. "I am very wicked. I have spoken very wickedly. Oh! Do not be frightened by me and never come again. I would not harm a hair of your head. And," opening her eyes to look earnestly at Margaret, "I believe, perhaps, more than you do, of what is to come. I have read the Book of Revelations until I know it by heart, and I never doubt, when I am awake and in my senses, of all the glory I am destined to reach."

"Let us not discuss the fancies that arise in your feverish mind," Margaret suggested gently. "I would rather hear of what you used to do when you were well."

"I think I was well when my mother died, but I have never been truly strong since that time. I began working in a carding-room shortly thereafter, and the fluff entered my lungs, poisoning me."

"Fluff?" Margaret asked, inquiringly.

"Fluff," Bessy repeated. "Tiny bits that fly off from the cotton when it is being carded, filling the air until it looks like fine white dust. They say it winds around the lungs and tightens them up. Anyway, many who work in a carding-room fall into a waste state, coughing and spitting blood, because they are simply poisoned by the fluff."

"But can it not be prevented?" Margaret asked.

"I do not know. Some factories have a great wheel at one end to create a draft and carry the dust away, but that wheel costs a great deal of money—perhaps five or six hundred pounds—and yields no profit. Consequently, only a few masters will install them. I have heard of men who refused to work in places equipped with such wheels, claiming that after becoming accustomed to swallowing fluff, they found it difficult to go without it, and that their wages should be raised if they were forced to work in cleaner conditions. Thus, between masters and men, the wheels often fail to be installed. I know I wished there had been one in our place, though."

"Did your father know about it?" Margaret asked.

"Yes. And he was sorry. But our factory was generally a good one, employing a steady and reliable group of people. My father feared sending me to a strange place; though you might not think it now, many considered me a respectable girl then. I did not wish to be regarded as weak and soft, and Mary's education needed to continue, as my mother insisted. Furthermore, my father always desired to purchase books and attend various lectures, all of which required money. So, I simply continued working until I shall never get the whirring out of my ears or the fluff out of my throat in this world. That is all."

"How old are you?" Margaret asked.

"Nineteen, coming July."

"And I too am nineteen." Margaret thought more sorrowfully than Bessy did of the contrast between them. She could not speak for a moment or two, struggling to contain the rising emotion.

"Regarding Mary," Bessy said. "I wanted to ask you to be a friend to her. She is seventeen, but she is the last of us. I do not want her to go to the mill, yet I do not know what she is fit for."

"She could not," Margaret replied, glancing unconsciously at the uncleaned corners of the room. "She could hardly undertake a servant's position, could she? We have an old, faithful servant, almost a friend, who requires assistance, but she is very particular. It would not be right to burden her with help that would truly become an annoyance and an irritation."

"No, I see. I reckon you are right. Our Mary is a good girl; but who has taught her how to manage a house? No mother, and me at the mill until I was good for nothing but scolding her for doing poorly what I did not know how to do myself. But I wish she could have lived with you, despite everything."

"Even though she may not be exactly suited to come and live with us as a servant—and I am uncertain about that—I will always strive to be a friend to her for your sake, Bessy. And now I must depart. I will return as soon as possible; but if it is not tomorrow, or the next day, or even a week or a fortnight hence, do not think I have forgotten you. I may be occupied."

"I will know you will not forget me again. I will not mistrust you anymore. But remember, in a week or a fortnight, I may be dead and buried!"

"I will come again as soon as I can, Bessy," Margaret said, squeezing her hand tightly. "But you will let me know if you grow worse."

"Ay, that I will," Bessy replied, returning the pressure.

From that day forward, Mrs. Hale became increasingly a suffering invalid. It was approaching the anniversary of Edith's marriage, and looking back upon the year's accumulated heap of troubles, Margaret wondered how they had been endured. If she could have anticipated them, how she would have shrunk away and hidden herself from the coming time! Yet, day by day, existence had been surprisingly endurable in itself, small, keen, bright spots of positive enjoyment sparkling into the very midst of sorrows. A year prior, or when she first arrived in Helstone and became silently aware of the querulousness in her mother's temper, she would have groaned bitterly at the prospect of enduring a long illness in a strange, desolate, noisy, and busy place, with diminished comforts surrounding every aspect of home life. But with the increase of serious and just grounds for complaint, a new kind of patience had sprung up in her mother's mind. She was gentle and quiet in intense bodily suffering, almost in proportion to how restless and depressed she had been when there was no real cause for grief.

Mr. Hale, however, was in exactly that stage of apprehension which, in men of his stamp, takes the form of willful blindness. He was more irritated than Margaret had ever known him to be regarding his daughter's expressed anxiety.

"Indeed, Margaret, you are growing fanciful! God knows I should be the first to take the alarm if your mother were really ill; we always knew when she had her headaches at Helstone, even without her telling us. She looks quite pale and white when she is ill; and now she has a bright, healthy color in her cheeks, just as she used to have when I first knew her."

"But papa," Margaret said with hesitation, "do you know I think that is the flush of pain?"

"Nonsense, Margaret. I tell you, you are the one not well, I think. Send for the doctor tomorrow for yourself; and then, if it will make your mind easier, he can see your mother."

"Thank you, dear papa. It will make me happier, indeed." She rose to kiss him. But he pushed her away—gently enough, yet as if she had suggested unpleasant ideas which he would be glad to dispel as readily as he could her presence. He paced uneasily up and down the room.

"Poor Maria!" he muttered, half to himself. "I wish one could do right without sacrificing others. I shall hate this town, and myself too, if she..." He turned to Margaret. "Pray, Margaret, does your mother often talk to you of the old places: of Helstone, I mean?"

"No, papa," Margaret said sadly.
