"If anyone should demand a reason for my love, I could offer none other than the simple truth: 'Because it was he, because it was I.' There exists a power beyond explanation that drew us together." —Montaigne, *On Friendship*.

The moment had arrived to prepare Mordecai for the revelation of his restored sister and the impending change of residence, a necessity before Mirah could reunite with her brother. Mrs. Meyrick, to whom Daniel Deronda had entrusted every detail save Mordecai's unique bond with himself, had tirelessly assisted in securing a charming lodging in Brompton, mere minutes from her own home. This proximity ensured that the siblings would remain within the comforting reach of her maternal care. Her delightful blend of Scottish fervor and Gaelic vivacity had kept the secret tight, shielding the girls and Hans from any betrayal that might spark suspicion and ruin the delicate groundwork laid for Mirah's independence—a dignified form of dependence that required careful nurturing. Both Mrs. Meyrick and Deronda possessed profound, unspoken reasons for wanting Mirah to stand on her own feet. Perhaps the "little mother" was aided in her secrecy by a certain hesitation regarding the remarkable brother described to her; certainly, any joy she felt stemmed from her faith in Deronda's judgment. While the consumption that plagued Mirah appealed to her tender heart, she struggled to embrace an enthusiasm she perceived merely as Jewish pertinacity, fearing the introduction of a man whose conversation lacked the modern encouragement she valued. Her mind, though capable of romanticizing Mirah's story, recognized that real-life romances require adaptation. One might admire figures like Sakya-Mouni or Oliver Cromwell from the safety of a night's reading, but welcoming such a soul as a new relation the following morning was a different matter entirely. Furthermore, Mrs. Meyrick had secretly hoped that Mirah's intense devotion to Judaism would eventually soften, merging into the deepening currents of love with her new friends. Her true favorite continuation of the romance had been the discovery of something favorable for Hans, not a Jewish brother. Now, however, here was a sibling who would plunge Mirah's mind once more into the deepest dye of Jewish sentiment. She could not help confiding to Deronda, "I am as glad as you are that the pawnbroker is not her brother. There are Ezras and Ezras in the world; it is a comfort to know not all Jews are like those shopkeepers who refuse to let you leave. Besides, his words about his mother and sister make me bless him. I am sure he is good. But I never did like anything fanatical. I suppose I heard too much preaching in my youth and lost my palate for it."

"I do not think you will find Mordecai obtruding any preaching," Deronda replied gently. "He is not what I would call fanatical. I reserve that word for those whose enthusiasm is narrow and hoodwinked, lacking a sense of proportion, becoming unjust and unsympathetic to those outside their circle. Mordecai is an enthusiast, but I wish to keep that title for the highest order of minds—those who care supremely for the grand and general benefits of mankind. He is not a strictly orthodox Jew and is full of allowances for others; his conformity in many ways is an accommodation to the condition of fellow Jews. Those he lives with adore him, yet they cannot comprehend his ideas."

"Oh, well, I can live up to the level of the pawnbroker's mother and appreciate what I see as good in him; for what I cannot see, I will take your word. By your definition, one might even be fanatical in worshipping common-sense, for my poor husband used to say the world would be a poor place if it contained nothing but that. However, Mirah's brother will have excellent bedding—I have taken care of that; and I shall paste up this extra window to prevent drafts." The conversation took place in the destined lodging. "It is a comfort to know the people of the house are no strangers to me, no hypocritical harpies. And when the children know, we shall be able to make the rooms much prettier."

"The next stage is to tell everything to Mordecai and get him to move, which may prove more difficult," Deronda noted.

"And will you tell Mirah before I say anything to the children?" Mrs. Meyrick asked. Deronda hesitated, and she continued in a tone of persuasive deliberation, "No, I think not. Let me tell Hans and the girls the evening before, and they will be away the next morning?"

"Yes, that will be best. But do justice to my account of Mordecai—or Ezra, as I suppose Mirah will wish to call him. Do not assist their imagination by referring to Habakkuk Mucklewrath," Deronda smiled, noting that Mrs. Meyrick herself had previously compared the Covenanters to the family.

"Trust me, trust me," the little mother assured him. "I shall have to persuade them so hard to be glad that I shall convert myself. When I am frightened, I find it a good thing to have somebody to be angry with for not being brave; it warms the blood."

Deronda might have been more argumentative regarding the view of Mirah's brother had he not been anxiously preoccupied with the more important task immediately before him, one he desired to accomplish without wounding the Cohens. Mordecai, through a memorable answer, had made it clear he would be keenly alive to any inadvertence concerning their feelings. In the interval, Deronda had met Mordecai at the *Hand and Banner*, but after due reflection, he wrote to invite him to his own home the following evening, asking to sit in his workroom for an hour if the Cohens did not regard it as an intrusion. He called with the understanding that if there were any objection, Mordecai would accompany him elsewhere. Deronda hoped this would create a little expectation with a preparatory effect.

He was received with the usual friendliness, some additional costumes in the women and children, and in all the elders a slight air of wondering which even in Cohen was not allowed to pass the bounds of silence—the guest's transactions with Mordecai being a sort of mystery he was rather proud to think lay outside the sphere of light enclosing his own understanding. But when Deronda said, "I suppose Mordecai is at home and expecting me," Jacob, who had profited by the family remarks, went up to his knee and asked, "What do you want to talk to Mordecai about?"

"Something that is very interesting to him," Deronda replied, pinching the lad's ear, "but that you can't understand."

"Can you say this?" Jacob immediately gave forth a string of his rote-learned Hebrew verses with a wonderful mixture of the throaty and the nasal, nodding his small head at his hearer with a sense of giving formidable evidence that might alter their mutual position.

"No, really," Deronda kept grave, "I can't say anything like it."

"I thought not," Jacob performed a dance of triumph with his small scarlet legs, taking various objects out of the deep pockets of his knickerbockers and returning them, a slight hint of his resources. After which, running to the door of the workroom, he opened it wide, set his back against it, and said, "Mordecai, here's the young swell"—a copying of his father's phrase, which seemed to him well fitted to cap the recitation of Hebrew.

He was called back with hushes by mother and grandmother. Deronda, entering and closing the door behind him, saw that a bit of carpet had been laid down, a chair placed, and the fire and lights attended to, in sign of the Cohens' respect. As Mordecai rose to greet him, Deronda was struck by the air of solemn expectation in his face, such as would have seemed perfectly natural if his letter had declared that some revelation was to be made about the lost sister. Neither spoke until Deronda, with his usual tenderness, drew the vacant chair from the opposite side of the hearth and seated himself near Mordecai, who then said in a tone of fervid certainty, "You are coming to tell me something that my soul longs for."

"It is true I have something very weighty to tell you—something I trust that you will rejoice in," Deronda said, on his guard against the probability that Mordecai had been preparing himself for something quite different.

"It is all revealed—it is made clear to you," Mordecai said more eagerly, leaning forward with clasped hands. "You are even as my brother that sucked the breasts of my mother—the heritage is yours—there is no doubt to divide us."

"I have learned nothing new about myself," Deronda replied. The disappointment was inevitable; it was better not to let the feeling be strained longer in a mistaken hope.

Mordecai sank back in his chair, unable for the moment to care what was really coming. The whole day his mind had been in a state of tension toward one fulfillment. The reaction was sickening, and he closed his eyes.

"Except," Deronda went on gently, after a pause, "except that I had really some time ago come into another sort of hidden connection with you, besides what you have spoken of as existing in your own feeling."

The eyes were not opened, but there was a fluttering in the lids.

"I had made the acquaintance of one in whom you are interested."

"One who is closely related to your departed mother," Deronda continued, wishing to make the disclosure gradual; but noticing a shrinking movement in Mordecai, he added, "whom she and you held dear above all others."

Mordecai, with a sudden start, laid a spasmodic grasp on Deronda's wrist; there was a great terror in him. Deronda divined it. A tremor was perceptible in his clear tones as he said, "What was prayed for has come to pass: Mirah has been delivered from evil."

Mordecai's grasp relaxed a little, but he was panting with a tearless sob.

Deronda went on: "Your sister is worthy of the mother you honored."

He waited there, and Mordecai, throwing himself backward in his chair, again closed his eyes, uttering himself almost inaudibly for some minutes in Hebrew, and then subsiding into a happy-looking silence. Deronda, watching the expression in his uplifted face, could have imagined he was speaking with some beloved object; there was a new suffused sweetness, something like that on the faces of the beautiful dead. For the first time, Deronda thought he discerned a family resemblance to Mirah.

Presently, when Mordecai was ready to listen, the rest was told. But in accounting for Mirah's flight, Deronda made the statement about the father's conduct as vague as possible, throwing the emphasis on her yearning to come to England as the place where she might find her mother. He also kept back the fact of Mirah's intention to drown herself and his own part in rescuing her, merely describing the home she had found with friends of his, whose interest in her and efforts for her he had shared. What he dwelt on finally was Mirah's feeling about her mother and brother; in relation to this, he tried to give every detail.

"It was in search of them," Deronda said, smiling, "that I turned into this house: the name Ezra Cohen was just then the most interesting name in the world to me. I confess I had fear for a long while. Perhaps you will forgive me now for having asked you that question about the elder Mrs. Cohen's daughter. I cared very much what I should find Mirah's friends to be. But I had found a brother worthy of her when I knew that her Ezra was disguised under the name of Mordecai."

"Mordecai is really my name—Ezra Mordecai Cohen."

"Is there any kinship between this family and yours?" Deronda asked.

"Only the kinship of Israel. My soul clings to these people, who have sheltered me and given me succor out of the affection that abides in Jewish hearts, as sweet odor in things long crushed and hidden from the outer air. It is good for me to bear with their ignorance and be bound to them in gratitude, that I may keep in mind the spiritual poverty of the Jewish million, and not put impatient knowledge in the stead of loving wisdom."

"But you don't feel bound to continue with them now there is a closer tie to draw you?" Deronda asked, not without fear that he might find an obstacle to overcome. "It seems to me right now—is it not?—that you should live with your sister; and I have prepared a home to take you to in the neighborhood of her friends, that she may join you there. Pray grant me this wish. It will enable me to be with you often in the hours when Mirah is obliged to leave you. That is my selfish reason. But the chief reason is, that Mirah will desire to watch over you, and that you ought to give her the guardianship of a brother's presence. You shall have books about you. I shall want to learn of you, and to take you out to see the river and trees. And you will have the rest and comfort that you will be more and more in need of—nay, that I need for you. This is the claim I make on you, now that we have found each other."

Deronda spoke in a tone of earnest, affectionate pleading, such as he might have used to a venerated elder brother. Mordecai's eyes were fixed on him with a listening contemplation, and he was silent for a little while after Deronda had ceased to speak. Then he said, with an almost reproachful emphasis, "And you would have me hold it doubtful whether you were born a Jew! Have we not from the first touched each other with invisible fibres—have we not quivered together like the leaves from a common stem with stirring from a common root? I know what I am outwardly, I am one among the crowd of poor—I am stricken, I am dying. But our souls know each other. They gazed in silence as those who have long been parted and meet again, but when they found voice they were assured, and all their speech is understanding. The life of Israel is in your veins."
