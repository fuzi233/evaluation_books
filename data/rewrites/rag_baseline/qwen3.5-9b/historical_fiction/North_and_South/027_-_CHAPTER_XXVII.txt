Mr. Thornton moved through the affairs of the day with a clarity that cut through the fog of his previous despair. He seized the slight demand for finished goods to drive hard bargains, sharpening his focus until the meeting of the magistrates. There, amidst older men whose wealth was sunk in land and whose influence was rooted in tradition, he stood as the arbiter of consequence. They looked to him for prompt wisdom; he was the one tasked with coordinating the police, leading the necessary steps against disorder. Yet, he cared for their unconscious deference no more than the south wind cares for the smoke rising from the great chimneys—it neither swerved nor lingered. He remained oblivious to the silent respect that surrounded him, viewing it only as potential friction against his singular objective. While the womenfolk whispered among themselves about how highly Mr. Thornton was held, how things would have faltered without his intervention, he swept aside these trivialities. The deep mortification of the prior day had burned away, clearing the mists from his intellect. He felt his power, reveling in it with a ferocity that bordered on defiance. If he had known it, he could have sung the Miller's song: *"I care for nobody—Nobody cares for me."*

Justice was swift in the borough court. The evidence against Boucher and his riotous followers was secured, though the charge of conspiracy against three others collapsed under scrutiny. Still, Mr. Thornton issued a stern charge to the constabulary: the law's right arm must remain poised, ready to strike the moment a new fault was proven. Stepping out of the hot, reeking courtroom, he emerged into the sultry street, feeling a sudden, overwhelming languor. His thoughts, unmoored, drifted inevitably to her—not the cold rejection of yesterday, but the fleeting warmth of the day before. He walked mechanically through the throng, a ghost in his own city, haunted by the memory of her heart beating against his chest.

"Why, Mr. Thornton! you're cutting me very coolly, I must say. And how is Mrs. Thornton? Brave weather this!" Dr. Donaldson hailed him, his voice bright against the gloom.

"I beg your pardon, Doctor. I didn't see you. My mother is well, thank you. It is a fine day, good for the harvest. If the wheat gets in well, we shall have a brisk trade next year, regardless of your medical prognostications."

"Ay, ay. Each man for himself. Your bad weather breeds sickness and prepares for death among you Milton men, unaware of the rot beneath the surface."

"Not with me, Doctor. I am made of iron. Even the news of my worst debt never varied my pulse. This strike, which weighs heavier on me than on anyone else in Milton—even more than on Hamper—does not touch my appetite. Go seek another patient."

Dr. Donaldson paused, his expression shifting. "By the way, you recommended a poor lady to me. Mrs. Hale, in Crampton House. I feared the worst, but today confirmed it. She has but a few weeks left."

Mr. Thornton fell silent. The vaunted steadiness of his composure cracked for an instant. "Can I do anything?" he asked, his voice altered.

"You know money is scarce. Are there comforts she requires?"

"No," the Doctor replied, shaking his head. "She craves fruit. A constant fever burns her, but jargonelle pears will suffice. There are plenty in the market."

"You will tell me if there is anything else," Mr. Thornton insisted. "I rely on you."

"Oh! Never fear! I shall not spare your purse; I know it is deep enough. Give me a *carte-blanche* for all my patients' wants."

But Mr. Thornton possessed no universal philanthropy, nor did he offer broad charity. Yet, driven by a specific, stubborn impulse, he marched straight to the first fruit shop in Milton. He selected a cluster of purple grapes with the most delicate bloom, the richest-hued peaches, and vines fresh with green leaves. Packed into a basket, the shopman waited for his destination.

"To Marlborough Mills, I suppose, sir?"

"No!" Mr. Thornton snapped. "Give the basket to me—I'll take it."

The weight filled both his hands, forcing him through the busiest thoroughfare, navigating the sea of feminine shoppers. Young ladies turned to stare, bewildered to see the formidable manufacturer carrying a load like a porter or an errand-boy. Inside his mind, he constructed a fortress of justification: *"I will not be daunted from doing as I choose by the thought of her. It is simply right to bring this fruit to the poor mother. She shall never scorn me for doing what pleases me. A pretty joke, indeed, if fear of a haughty girl prevents me from kindness to a man I like! I do it for Mr. Hale; I do it in defiance of her."*

At an unusual pace, he reached Crampton House. Ascending two steps at a time, he bypassed Dixon's announcement and entered the drawing-room. His face flushed, his eyes burning with a kindly earnestness, he found Mrs. Hale lying on the sofa, heated by fever. Mr. Hale read aloud nearby, while Margaret worked on a low stool beside her mother. Her heart fluttered, sensing the tension, though Mr. Thornton barely acknowledged either of them. With his basket in hand, he approached Mrs. Hale directly. In that subdued, gentle tone so striking when spoken by a robust man to a fragile invalid, he said, "I met Dr. Donaldson, ma'am. Since he said fruit would aid you, I took the liberty—the great liberty—of bringing you some that seemed fine."

Mrs. Hale trembled with eager delight. Mr. Hale, with fewer words, expressed a deeper gratitude. "Fetch a plate, Margaret—a basket—anything."

Margaret rose, paralyzed by the fear of movement, terrified of a conscious collision between her father and this stranger. She assumed that, having started on a low seat and now standing behind her father, he had overlooked her in his haste. Yet, she felt his consciousness of her presence radiating from him, even as his eyes never truly rested upon her.

"I must go," he declared abruptly. "I cannot stay. If you will forgive this liberty—and my rough ways, which seem too abrupt—I promise gentleness next time. You will allow me the pleasure of bringing fruit again if I find anything tempting. Good afternoon, Mr. Hale. Good-bye, ma'am."

And he was gone. Not a word, not a glance directed at Margaret. She believed he had not seen her. In silence, she fetched a plate, lifting the fruit tenderly with her delicate fingers. It was kind of him, especially after yesterday.

"Oh! It is so delicious!" Mrs. Hale whispered, her voice weak. "How kind of him to think of me! Margaret, love, taste these grapes! Was it not good of him?"

"Yes!" Margaret answered quietly.

"Margaret!" Mrs. Hale queried, her tone querulous. "You won't like anything Mr. Thornton does. I never saw anyone so prejudiced."

Mr. Hale peeled a peach, cutting a small slice for himself. "If I had any prejudices, the gift of such fruit would melt them away. I have not tasted such delicacies since I was a boy. Boys, I fancy, find all fruit good. Remember the matted currant bushes at the corner of the west wall at our home?"

Did she not? Did she not recall every weather-stain on the old stone wall, the gray and yellow lichens mapping its age, the tiny crane's-bill growing in the crevices? Shaken by the tumult of the last forty-eight hours, her entire existence a strain upon her fortitude, these casual words triggered a visceral reaction. The memory of sunny times shattered her composure. She dropped her sewing, stumbled to her feet, and fled hastily into her own chamber.

Hardly had she stifled her first choking sob when she sensed Dixon standing by her drawers, searching frantically.

"Bless me, miss! How you startled me! Is Missus worse? Is anything the matter?"

"No, nothing. Only I'm silly, Dixon, and need water. What are you looking for? I keep my muslins in that drawer."

Dixon ignored her, continuing to rummage. The scent of lavender drifted out, perfuming the stagnant air. Finally, she found what she sought, though Margaret could not see it. Turning sharply, Dixon spoke.

"Now, I dislike telling you what I wanted, for you are fretting enough already, and I know you'll fret more. I intended to wait until tonight, or some such time."

"What is it? Tell me at once."

"That young woman you visit—Higgins, I mean."

"Well?"

"Well! She died this morning. Her sister is here, begging a strange favor. Apparently, the deceased had a fancy to be buried in something of yours, so the sister has come to ask for it. I was looking for a nightcap fit to give away."

"Oh! Let me find one," Margaret said, tears welling instantly. "Poor Bessy! I never thought I'd see her again."

"Why, that's another thing. The girl downstairs asked me if you wished to see her."

"But she's dead!" Margaret turned pale. "I've never seen a corpse. No! I'd rather not."

"I shouldn't have asked if you hadn't come in. I told her you wouldn't."
