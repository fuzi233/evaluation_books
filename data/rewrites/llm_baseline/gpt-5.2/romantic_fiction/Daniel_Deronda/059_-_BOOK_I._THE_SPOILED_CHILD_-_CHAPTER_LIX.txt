Sir Hugo was never a man to be hustled by other people’s urgency; and Daniel, for his part, would not let himself leave Genoa while the thought of his oldest friend—his first shelter and habit of home—was still only a promise on paper. Grandcourt’s drowning lay between them like a cold stone in the house, and, heavier still, that sudden opening of Daniel’s own life—the mother who had come and gone as if the air had shaped itself into a figure and then forgotten the trick—was not a thing to be told in letters without leaving the heart misheard.

So it was not until the fifth evening that Daniel stood waiting at the station, the telegram’s hour narrowing toward eight, then nine. The place had its own harsh orchestra—iron wheels clanging, porters calling, luggage thudded down with the blunt impatience of travel—yet beyond, the sea kept sending in its low breath, and a faint bell from somewhere in the town threaded through the racket like a remembered tune. Daniel found himself listening with that inward habit he had of taking the world in by its sounds as well as its sights: the difference between a noise that only bruised the mind and a sound that allowed it to be whole.

When Sir Hugo stepped down from the carriage at last, his familiar figure seeming to bring with it the smell of English cloth and old rooms and well-kept leather, Daniel felt the warmth of long gratitude rise with a force that for a moment drowned every new judgment. He had been wronged—yes, by a falsity, by a concealment that had made his boyhood a kind of hunger with no name for what it lacked. Yet the hand that had held him up had meant kindness; and Daniel’s nature, which could be unbending in principle, was tender in blame. Ignorant kindness might cut like cruelty; still, to rage at it as if it had been deliberate was only another form of ignorance. And after the last ten days—after the curtain had lifted on souls cramped by secrecy and power—he was less than ever willing to indulge that resentful appetite which looks so much like a love of punishment.

Sir Hugo’s hand closed on his.

“Well, Dan,” he said—nothing more, because the rest was crowded into the clasp and into their faces.

They gave brief orders to the courier, and Sir Hugo, who looked travel-worn but sound, proposed they should walk; there was no need to hurry to the hotel. The evening was mild, starlight still thin with the last sheen of day, and the air carried the briny dampness of the port. Carts rattled over the stones; a distant singer somewhere tried a phrase and let it drop; even the water made a small, ceaseless chafing against wood and rope that steadied the nerves more than silence could have done.

“I have taken my journey easily, and am in excellent condition,” Sir Hugo said. “I didn’t hurry in setting off, because I wanted to inquire into things a little; and so I got sight of your letter to Lady Mallinger before I started. But now—how is the widow?”

“Getting calmer,” Daniel answered. “She seems to be escaping the bodily illness one might have feared after her plunge and terrible excitement. Her uncle and mother came two days ago, and she is being well taken care of.”

“Any prospect of an heir being born?”

“From what Mr. Gascoigne said to me, I conclude not. He spoke as if it were a question whether the widow would have the estates for her life.”

Sir Hugo looked round at Daniel as they walked. “It will not be much of a wrench to her affections, I fancy, this loss of the husband?”

“The suddenness of the death has been a great blow to her,” Daniel said, quietly, stepping away from what Sir Hugo was trying to pull into light.

“I wonder whether Grandcourt gave her any notion what were the provisions of his will?”

“Do you know what they are, sir?” Daniel returned, unwilling to take up the role of interpreter of another person’s inwardness.

“Yes, I do,” Sir Hugo said sharply. “Gad! if there’s no prospect of a legitimate heir, he’s left everything to a boy he had by a Mrs. Glasher. You know nothing about the affair, I suppose; but she was a sort of wife to him for a good many years, and there are three older children—girls. The boy is to take his father’s name; he is Henleigh already, and he is to be Henleigh Mallinger Grandcourt. The Mallinger will be of no use to him, I am happy to say; but the young dog will have more than enough with his fourteen years’ minority—no need to have had holes filled up with my fifty thousand for Diplow that he had no right to. And meanwhile my beauty, the young widow, is to put up with a poor two thousand a year and the house at Gadsmere—a nice kind of banishment for her if she chose to shut herself up there, which I don’t think she will. The boy’s mother has been living there of late years. I’m perfectly disgusted with Grandcourt. I don’t know that I’m obliged to think the better of him because he’s drowned; though, so far as my affairs are concerned, nothing in his life became him like the leaving it.”

“In my opinion he did wrong when he married this wife—not in leaving his estates to the son,” Daniel said, the dryness in his tone like the edge of a knife used with restraint.

“I say nothing against his leaving the land to the lad,” Sir Hugo answered. “But since he had married this girl he ought to have given her a handsome provision, such as she could live on in a style fitted to the rank he had raised her to. She ought to have had four or five thousand a year and the London house for her life; that’s what I should have done for her. I suppose, as she was penniless, her friends couldn’t stand out for a settlement. It’s ill trusting to the will a man may make after he’s married. Even a wise man generally lets some folly ooze out of him in his will—my father did, I know; and if a fellow has any spite or tyranny in him, he’s likely to bottle off a good deal for keeping in that sort of document. It’s quite clear Grandcourt meant that his death should put an extinguisher on his wife, if she bore him no heir.”

“And, in the other case, I suppose everything would have been reversed—illegitimacy would have had the extinguisher?” Daniel said, and this time the contempt showed through.

“Precisely—Gadsmere and the two thousand. It’s queer. One nuisance is that Grandcourt has made me an executor; but seeing he was the son of my only brother, I can’t refuse to act. And I shall mind it less if I can be of any use to the widow. Lush thinks she was not in ignorance about the family under the rose, and the purport of the will. He hints that there was no very good understanding between the couple. But I fancy you are the man who knew most about what Mrs. Grandcourt felt or did not feel—eh, Dan?”

There was no jocoseness in Sir Hugo now; his voice dropped, as if the clang and shout around them ought not to overhear. Daniel felt that if he dodged, he would only set Sir Hugo’s imagination to work in a wrong direction.

“She was certainly not happy,” Daniel said gravely. “They were unsuited to each other. But as to the disposal of the property—from all I have seen of her, I should predict that she will be quite contented with it.”

“Then she is not much like the rest of her sex; that’s all I can say,” Sir Hugo answered with a small shrug. “However, she ought to be something extraordinary, for there must be an entanglement between your horoscope and hers—eh? When that tremendous telegram came, the first thing Lady Mallinger said was, ‘How very strange that it should be Daniel who sends it!’ But I’ve had something of the same sort in my own life. I was once at a foreign hotel where a lady had been left by her husband without money. When I heard of it, and came forward to help her, who should she be but an early flame of mine—who had been fool enough to marry an Austrian baron with a long mustache and short affection? But it was an affair of my own that called me there—nothing to do with knight-errantry, any more than you coming to Genoa had to do with the Grandcourts.”

The talk ran on the safer rail for a while—other people’s fates, the decent distance of scandal and law and property. Yet both of them were waiting, with an inward reluctance, for the subject that lay like a sealed packet between them. Daniel’s earlier letter—written after his first meeting with his mother—had been more like mist than sunlight; he knew it. He had been unable then to set down in ink what had shaken his blood and altered the language of his own being. Better to wait for Sir Hugo’s first opening.

They had nearly lost sight of the port when Sir Hugo turned, pausing as if for a last look at the masts and the dim play of lights over the water. The sea’s hush met the street’s receding clatter; it was as if the world offered a quieter chamber for what must now be spoken.

“And about the main business of your coming to Genoa, Dan?” he said, and there was a new gravity under the words. “You have not been deeply pained by anything you have learned, I hope? There is nothing that you feel need change your position in any way? You know, whatever happens to you must always be of importance to me.”

“I desire to meet your goodness by perfect confidence, sir,” Daniel answered. “But I can’t answer those questions truly by a simple yes or no. Much that I have heard about the past has pained me. And it has been a pain to meet and part with my mother in her suffering state, as I have been compelled to do. But it is no pain—it is rather a clearing up of doubts for which I am thankful, to know my parentage. As to the effect on my position, there will be no change in my gratitude to you, sir, for the fatherly care and affection you have always shown me. But to know that I was born a Jew may have a momentous influence on my life, which I am hardly able to tell you of at present.”

He forced the last words out with steadiness; not because he wished to wound Sir Hugo, but because he felt the future already pressing at him—asking not only what he would do, but in what tongue he would name himself. There are truths which, once spoken, change the room’s air; and Daniel felt Sir Hugo’s quick glance like a hand testing that altered atmosphere.

Sir Hugo turned and walked on again. For a few moments he was silent, and Daniel could almost hear the older man’s mind sorting its stored impressions, picking up fragments of half-remarked facts and trying to fit them into a shape that would not disturb his settled sense of the world.

At last Sir Hugo said, “I have long expected something remarkable from you, Dan; but, for God’s sake, don’t go into any eccentricities! I can tolerate any man’s difference of opinion, but let him tell it me without getting himself up as a lunatic. At this stage of the world, if a man wants to be taken seriously, he must keep clear of melodrama. Don’t misunderstand me. I’m not suspecting you of setting up any lunacy on your own account. I only think you might easily be led arm in arm with a lunatic, especially if he wanted defending. You have a passion for people who are pelted, Dan. I’m sorry for them too; but so far as company goes, it’s a bad ground of selection. However, I don’t ask you to anticipate your inclination in anything you have to tell me. When you make up your mind to a course that requires money, I have some sixteen thousand pounds that have been accumulating for you over and above what you have been having the interest of as income. And now I am come, I suppose you want to get back to England as soon as you can?”

“I must go first to Mainz,” Daniel said, catching at the practical, as if it were a rung of a ladder when the heart is dizzy. “To get away a chest of my grandfather’s, and perhaps to see a friend of his. Although the chest has been lying there these twenty years, I have an unreasonable sort of nervous eagerness to get it away under my care, as if it were more likely now than before that something might happen to it. And perhaps I am the more uneasy because I lingered after my mother left, instead of setting out immediately. Yet I can’t regret that I was here—else Mrs. Grandcourt would have had none but servants to act for her.”

“Yes, yes,” Sir Hugo said, and the flippancy that came back into his voice had the uneasy note of a man covering vexation with a thin cloth. “I hope you are not going to set a dead Jew above a living Christian.”

Color rose in Daniel’s face; it was as if, with that careless phrase, the power of a dominant vocabulary had tried to press down the truth he had only just dared to utter. He held back the retort that sprang to his lips—held it with the same discipline with which he had learned to bear the world’s clamor without letting it ruin the inner hearing.

They were turning in under the sign of the Italia, the hotel doors yawning bright and noisy, letting out a gust of warmed air thick with tobacco and roasted meat, and a burst of laughter that jarred after the sea’s steadier breath. Daniel went in beside Sir Hugo, carrying with him, under all the ordinary sounds of travel and society, the newly named fact of himself—still strange on the tongue, still untranslatable into the easy idioms of those he loved, yet irrevocable as blood.
