There is no compassion for the wasting of the soul. When life’s vigor fades, when the spirit twists inward until sweetness rots into horror, then humanity withdraws. The sane recoil, not out of cruelty, but self-preservation—for we are no exorcists. Devils must not be invited in, even by love’s trembling hand. Pleas of honor, duty, mercy—all fall on deaf ears. A letter slips beneath a darkened door, ignored. A telegram goes unanswered, its messenger unaware of the pale finger that parts the blinds for only a second before retreating.

No. Let the damned wrestle their phantoms alone. Let them, in their madness, embrace the succubus in the fetid dark where neither sun nor heaven’s breath may reach. The lost belong to the shadows. The living have their own burdens.

Oleron had long ceased to care for the world outside. The letters that lay just inside his door might as well have been written in a forgotten tongue. The telegraph boy’s knocking sent him into a frenzy, fingers clawing at his ears. He imagined the boy standing there, amid the withered flowers and spoiled provisions that littered his threshold—offerings he no longer had the strength to retrieve. The stench of decay thickened the air.

His strength bled away, siphoned by the thing that had fastened onto him. Hours slipped by in a red-tinged haze, his mind adrift. Flickers of memory taunted him: half-formed novels, grand visions of stories that would never be written. Sometimes he recalled past lodgings, garrets he had known—trivial fragments of a life now severed. None of it mattered. He had found his final dwelling, a place that suited him perfectly, a place that *wanted* him.

A novel? Oh, someone should write of such a place! There were depths here, if one dared plumb them. A man named Madley had tried—Oleron despised him on instinct. (A joke, that. A bitter laugh in the dark.)

But one thought refused to be banished: Elsie Bengough. She forced her way into his mind, unwelcome, disruptive. Her presence shattered his fragile ecstasies, tore down the glittering palaces of his imagination. She was a fog over a star, a pit yawning beneath his feet. He fought her, strangled her memory before it could take root—but then, without warning, she returned.

Not as memory. *As presence.*

In the black heart of night, she was *there*. No warning. No moment of transition. She simply *existed* in his mind, unshakable, undeniable. And worse—she *watched*.

The realization ignited fury in him. How *dare* she? To intrude now, of all times—to witness *this*—

"Damn you!" he snarled—

And then, terror.

The rage was not his own.

Some other wrath, vast and infernal, surged through him, white-hot, annihilating. His petty anger was a child’s slap compared to the force that now coiled to strike. Had *he* summoned this? Had his weak spark ignited something beyond reckoning?

Before he could scream a warning, the vision shattered.

He woke gasping, drenched in sweat, the nightmare receding. He was Paul Oleron again—just a man, sick and hollow, a failed writer past his prime. The truth settled over him like a shroud: he had grasped for greatness and missed. Twice over. The comforts of ordinary life, and the glory of art—both lost.

And now, only exhaustion remained.

He lay there, piecing together the strangeness around him. The stench of rot. The beard on his face. How long had he been ill? Why was he alone?

Then—a sound. A whisper.

"*Paul.*"

From the kitchen.

And another memory, sharp as a blade: the click of a key in the lock. Minutes ago? Hours?

"Who’s there?" he called. Silence. Then movement—soft, shuffling.

He should rise. Investigate.

But the dark stretched between him and the kitchen like an abyss. What waited there? Better not to know. Better to stay, to let the whispers fade.

Besides, she was near again—*his* muse, his torment, his obsession. He felt her in the air, the electric promise of a story yet unwritten. A tale of a woman wicked and wondrous, a creature to make men tremble.

She was beckoning.

And Oleron, weak as he was, could not resist.

The last thing he felt, before surrender, was the ghost of lips upon his—not Elsie’s, never hers—but something colder, hungrier, stealing his breath away.
