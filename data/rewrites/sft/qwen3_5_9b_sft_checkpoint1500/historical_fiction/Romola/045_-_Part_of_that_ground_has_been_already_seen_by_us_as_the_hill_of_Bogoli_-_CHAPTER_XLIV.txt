As the throng swept past, Romola slipped down into the street and hurried toward San Stefano. Cecco had drifted with the crowd to the Piazza, leaving Baldassarre standing solitary by the church doors. The priest held a horn-cup, waiting, yet a profound transformation had overtaken him. Where once lay the vacant, dreamy gaze of a half-conscious mind, there now burned a fierce, concentrated intensity. As Romola approached, offering a word, that look flared upon her with the sudden, terrifying recognition of a caged beast spotting prey slipping safely beyond the bars.

Romola started at the glare, her immediate thought leaping to Tito. *Has he seen him?* she wondered. As the hatred in Baldassarre's eyes scraped against her skin, a fragile hope ignited within her: perhaps this man was the criminal, and her husband innocent. If she could confront Baldassarre with Tito, if she could see the truth laid bare, her mind would finally rest.

"If you will come with me," she said gently, "I can offer you shelter and food until you regain your strength. Will you?"

"Yes," Baldassarre replied, the word sounding like a muttered prayer rather than a response to her. "I must get my strength. I need it."

"Then come!" She extended her hand, guiding him away from the noisy square toward the quieter path by the Arno, leading to the Ponte Rubaconte.

"I suspect you are not of Florence," she ventured as they crossed the bridge.

He glanced around, silent and wary. His suspicion was heightened by the physical frailty that clouded his mind, making the fog of his confusion thicker. Yet, something in her gentle, steady gaze compelled him to answer.

"No," he said cautiously. "I am no Florentine. I am a lonely man."

Noticing his reticence, Romola held her tongue, fearing to push him away if he desired solitude. As they walked, her heart heavy, the faint hope that he was merely a victim of circumstance began to dim. If an old man like him harbored malice, what terrible secret did it conceal?

Silence stretched between them until they reached the entrance of the Via de' Bardi. Suddenly, Baldassarre stopped, turning with a jerky motion as if struck by a blow. Moments later, Romola halted before the open courtyard, where straw littered the ground and four or five ill folk lay prostrate. Tiny children crawled among them, pale and weak, gnawing at straws.

"If you will enter," Romola whispered, her voice trembling, "I will find you a corner and bring you more food."

"No," Baldassarre refused, though he stood frozen, paralyzed by the weight of his confused thoughts. "I cannot go in."

"What can I do for you?" she asked, desperation edging her tone. "Take this money so you may buy sustenance. It will be abundant soon."

She drew coins from her *scarsella*, holding out a palm full of *grossi*. She intended to give him more than she ever would to any stranger. He stared at the metal, then nodded slowly.

"Yes," he murmured.

She poured the coins into his hand, and he clutched them tight. "Tell me," she pleaded, her voice breaking, "what will you do next?"

Baldassarre had already turned, walking back toward the bridge. Crossing it, he marched straight up the Via del Fosso, pausing before the shop of Niccolò Caparra. Without hesitation, he pushed inside. Niccolò was absent, having joined a procession of armorers, leaving only an apprentice behind. Weapons hung everywhere, gleaming in the dim light. Baldassarre's eyes, starving for bread, fixated on steel.

Though Niccolò might have refused to arm a prisoner, the boy sold a sharp hunting-knife for three *grossi* without a second thought. It was small, easily concealed within Baldassarre's tunic. As he walked on, feeling a phantom surge of power, he realized the edge could deliver a fatal thrust even from an aged arm. Perhaps the armor his enemies wore would not always be present; perhaps, in those long months of imprisonment, their baseness had grown complacent. The knife had been purchased with the traitor's own coin. Justice balanced.

And if he secured enough food, he could think clearly again. First, he needed to think of where to hide, lest Tito drag him back to prison. With that resolve, he navigated the narrowest alleys, buying meat and bread, then sat beneath a loggia to eat. The bells rang out in joyful, swelling peals, shaking the air with celebration. To Baldassarre, they sounded only like the roar of a mighty tide rising against him.

Romola watched him vanish around the corner of the Piazza de' Mozzi. A mixture of relief and self-reproach swirled within her. Had she sought the truth more vigorously? Could she have relieved his suffering? Yet, if the truth involved a grievous sin committed by her husband, what choice remained to a wife? Was it not her duty to remain silent, to shield him? These intricate threads of feeling tangled in her weary mind, untangled for now.

Instead, she turned to the court to share the good news. Closing the door to muffle the sound of the bells, she threw back the black drapery covering her head so the women might see her face. Standing amidst the straw beds, she announced that corn was coming and that the bells sang for joy. The sick lifted themselves, the children crawled forward, pulling at her skirts, eager to touch her. Exhausted as she was, she sat down on the straw, letting the pale faces cluster around her basket.

"The Holy Virgin has pity on us!" the feeble voices cried.

"It was the procession!"

Romola rose eventually, too drained to force a smile, promising to return with dinner. "Bless you, Madam!" the chorus echoed, a soft sound indistinguishable from their earlier thanks to the invisible Madonna.

Romola listened to the fading echoes of their gratitude with deep affection. Unlike those who found comfort in the mere routine of charity, her early training had kept her distant from such labors. Only the inspiration of her deepest sorrows had drawn her near. Now, however, this work of sympathy had become her anchor, the single clear path through the darkness. Even as the chasm between her and Tito widened, fueled by her futile attempts at reconciliation, doubts gnawed at her regarding the validity of their bond, and a revulsion rose against the narrow superstitions of Savonarola's followers, she found stability here.

Whatever cast doubt on her faith, her service to the citizens grounded her. She remembered the words of Fra Girolamo: her place had never been empty; it was filled with her love and labor. Florence needed her. As her own grief pressed harder, she found a deeper gladness in the memory of easing burdens for others. All the ardor of her nature, which could no longer flow solely into the tenderness of a wife and daughter, had transformed into a fervent empathy for the collective life of humanity. She had ceased to seek happiness for herself; the sole aim of her existence appeared to be the alleviation of sorrow.

Her spirit, constantly renewed, drew strength from Savonarola. Though she recoiled from the stale, wearisome visions and allegories repeated by lesser imitators, her affinity for his passionate sympathy and lofty aims remained undimmed. His burning indignation against the corruption of the Church and State kindled a fire within her. His focus on the liberty and purity of Florence, linked to a universal regeneration, awakened in her a new awareness of history as a vast, unfolding drama. Through her daily contact with the poor, this abstract consciousness hardened into a concrete, self-denying purpose. She thought little of dogmas, shunning close reflection on the Friar's prophecies of imminent scourge. Instead, she had submitted her mind to his, entering communion with the Church to satisfy a moral hunger that her previous education had left unquenched.

Fra Girolamo's voice had given her a reason to live, distinct from personal pleasure or affection—a reason demanding fuel far greater than she possessed internally. Her obedience to every office of the Church was a form of vigilance, a waiting to see if fresh strength might arrive. The pressing question before her was not theological debate, but the preservation of that unselfish flame that allowed a life of sadness to remain a life of active love.

Her trust in Savonarola's superior nature formed the bedrock of her courage. It was not intellectual force that blinded her to his eccentricities, nor visionary acumen that ignored his flaws; it was simply the negation of high sensibilities. Moved profoundly by the grandeur of his energy, she listened patiently to every prophecy and doctrine delivered in the vehicle of his ardent faith. No soul remains truly desolate so long as there exists a human being worthy of trust and reverence. For Romola, this trust in Savonarola was like a rope securely fastened to her path, rendering her steps elastic and sure. Should that rope snap, no firmness of the earth beneath her feet could prevent her from stumbling, or falling.
