Supper time had fallen, and the household gathered round the board, save for one. Margaret was absent, and though Catherine expressed surprise, Eli declared he would wait. "I told her you'd not wait for the Duke," Catherine remarked. Eli shook his head gently. "She is no Duke; she is a poor, gentle lass who has waited not minutes, but many long years, for a graceless son of mine. Lay the meats forth, then, Mistress Joan; we shall eat without further loss when she arrives."

The savory scent of the smoking dishes proved irresistible, and Eli relented. "She will come if we begin," he assured them. "They always do." He beckoned Mistress Joan to sit, assuring her she was a guest, not a servant. As the covers were drawn away and the knives raised, a frantic footstep echoed across the threshold—not Margaret's return, but the entrance of a Dominican friar, his countenance drained of all color, twisted in a mask of livid fury.

In a heartbeat, the monk stood between the table and his enemies, looming over Cornelis and Sybrandt like an eagle over trembling prey. With both hands hovering above their shrinking forms, he unleashed a torrent of invective, cursing them by name, soul, and body in this life and the next. His words were a blight, a devastation, terrible enough to freeze the blood. "Cursed be the lips!" he shrieked, his voice cracking with agony, "that spoke the lie of Margaret's death! May they rot before the grave and kiss white-hot iron in hell thereafter! Doubly cursed be the hands that forged those deceitful letters, may they be severed by the hangman's blade and handle the fires of damnation forever! Thrice accursed be the cruel hearts that conceived this damnable falsehood to rend true love asunder! May they sicken and wither on earth, joyless, loveless, hopeless, and turn to dust before their time, burning in eternal flame!"

His wrath descended upon the meat before them and every atom of their existence, from hair to sole. Turning from the cowering pair, who had hidden beneath the table, he plucked a letter from his bosom and hurled it before his father. "Read this, thou hard old man, that imprisoned thy son! Read, and behold the monsters thou hast spawned! Let the memory of these wrongs dwell with thee forever! I shall meet thee again at the Judgment Day; on earth, thou shalt never see me more."

With the speed of lightning, he vanished, leaving the room silent, the figures round the smoking board stiff, cold, and white as statues.

Just then, the scene unfolded before Margaret and Jorian. They saw the petrified men and women frozen around the untouched feast, a tableau akin to those feigned by Eastern poets. Margaret's gaze swept the room, and she gasped, "Oh, joy! All are here; no blood hath been spilled. Oh, you cruel, cruel men! I thank God he hath not slain you."

At the sight of her, Catherine let out an eloquent scream before burying her face away. But Eli, who had just scanned the false letter and begun to comprehend its horror, saw the other victim enter with her sorrows mirrored in her pale, sweet face. A transport of rage seized him. He sprang to his feet, shouting, "Stand clear, and let me lay hands on the traitors! I will hang for them!" In an instant, he drew his short sword and lunged at them.

"Fly!" Margaret screamed. "Fly!"

Cornelis and Sybrandt slipped howling under the table, crawling out the far side. Before they could reach the door, the furious old man circled back to intercept them. Catherine merely screamed and wrung her hands; her notables were useless in such a crisis, and surely blood would have flowed. Yet, Margaret and Jorian seized the fiery old man's arms, holding him fast with all their might until the fugitives gained the street. Only then did they release him, and he went vainly raging after them into the night.

They fled a furlong, running like hares. Behind them, Eli hacked down the board bearing their names, dragged it within, and threw it into the chimney. Catherine sat rocking herself, her apron pulled over her head. Joan had rushed to her husband. Margaret clung to Catherine, her arms tight around the weeping woman, panting yet striving to offer comfort.

"It cannot be," Eli murmured, breaking the spell. "Oh, my poor children! Oh, miserable mother! 'Tis a mercy Kate was ill upstairs. There, I have lived to thank God for that!" A fresh burst of sobs shook her. "It would have killed her. He had better have remained in Italy than return to curse his own flesh and blood and set us all by the ears."

"Oh, hold your chatter, woman," Eli cried angrily. "Thou art still on the side of the evildoer. Thou art cheaply served; thy weakness made the rogues what they are. I sought to correct them in their youth with sore ills requiring sharp remedies, yet thou sidedst with their faults and undermined me, baffling wise severity." He turned to Margaret. "And thou, Margaret, leave comforting her who ought rather to comfort thee. For what is her hurt to thine? She never possessed a grain of justice under her skin, nor will she."

He extended his hand. "So come thou to me, that am thy father from this hour."

It was a command. She kissed Catherine farewell and tottered to him. He seated her beside him, and she laid her feeble head upon his honest breast. Yet not a tear fell; the sorrow was too deep for tears.

"Poor lamb," he whispered. After a pause, his voice broke, "Come, good folks," he addressed Jorian and Joan. "We are in a little trouble, as ye see; but that is no cause for starvation. For our Lady's sake, fall to, and add not to my grief the reputation of a churl. What the dickens!" He forced a sudden, ghastly attempt at stout-heartedness. "The more knaves I have the luck to shake off, the greater my need of true men and women to help me clear the dish and cheer mine eye with honest faces where else there are gaps. Eat, I do entreat ye."

Catherine, sobbing, backed his request. Poor, simple, antique, hospitable souls! Jorian, whose appetite, especially following his illness, was keen, moved to accept this invitation. But Joan whispered a word in his ear, and he instantly recoiled. "Nay, I'll touch no meat that Holy Church hath cursed."

"In sooth, I forgot," Eli said apologetically. "My son, who was reared at my table, hath cursed my victuals. That seems strange. Well, what God wills, man must bow to."

The supper was flung out into the yard.

Jorian took his wife home, and a heavy sadness reigned in Eli's house that night.

Meanwhile, where was Clement?

He lay prostrate upon the floor of the convent church, his lips pressed against the lowest step of the altar. He existed in a state of indescribable terror, misery, penitence, and self-abasement, through which glimmered fleeting rays of joy that Margaret lived. Night fell, finding him there weeping and praying; dawn would have found him there still. But suddenly, absorbed in his own wrongs and Margaret's, he realized he had committed another sin besides intemperate rage. He had neglected a dying man.

He rose instantly, groaning under the weight of his accumulated wickedness, and set out to make amends. The weather had shifted; it rained hard. When he cleared the town, he heard the wolves bay, close upon his heels. Yet Clement was himself again, or nearly so. He thought little of danger or discomfort, driven by a shameful omission of religious duty to repair. He strode stoutly forward through rain and darkness, beating his breast and crying, "ME A CULPA! ME A CULPA!"
