To a soldier, the entrenchments of Breed's Hill were perhaps the single most significant defensive feature of the entire Revolutionary War. The carnage inflicted upon us from behind those lines was so terrible, and the enemy's behavior so stubborn, that British commanders thereafter held the American barricades in profound, superstitious awe. Even if the defenders were merely hiding behind rows of blankets, certain of our generals hesitated to force them. At White Plains, when Washington's army stood literally at the mercy of victory, we later learned that our triumphant troops were halted before a makeshift barrier composed entirely of cornstalks and straw.

Another chance for decisive action presented itself, lasting through a long, bitter winter. During this time, the dwindling, disheartened forces of Congress starved and remained unarmed beneath our grasp, while the magnanimous Lord Howe allowed the infamous Camp at Valley Forge to remain untouched. Meanwhile, his own great, brave, and impeccably equipped army fiddled, gambled, and feasted in Philadelphia. Triumphal arches were raised by his countrymen; medieval-style tournaments were held in mocking celebration; and beautiful ladies presented wreaths and garlands to this "clement" chief, adorned with fantastical mottos declaring his laurels would be immortal. Yet, why have my ungrateful countrymen never erected a statue to this general? For within their entire army, there was no officer who fought their battles better, who retrieved their errors with such dexterity, or who ensured their defeats caused them so little harm. Above all, when fortune inevitably turned against them, who displayed such tireless tenderness, patience, and calm in lifting the battered opponent back onto his feet? One cannot help but recall the fiery young warrior England sent to fight across the American continent—a man forever pacing the defenses behind which the foe hid, sleepless and eager by day and night, consumed by a fierce wrath and longing that denied him rest. He wound his tracks with an unwearying scent, panting and hungry for blood and battle, prowling through midnight forests or climbing silent precipices before dawn. He watched until his great heart was nearly worn out, until finally the foe revealed himself, at which moment he sprang upon him, grappled, and, dying, slew him. Think of Wolfe at Quebec, and then listen to Howe's fiddles as he sat smiling among the dancers in Philadelphia!

A favored strategy among our ministers at home and some of our generals in America was to establish a communication between Canada and New York, thereby cutting off New England from the neighboring colonies, overwhelming them in detail, and forcing submission. Burgoyne was entrusted with this plan and departed from Quebec, confidently promising success. His march commenced in full military state; the trumpets of his proclamations blared ahead, summoning the colonists to remember the immense power of England and ordering the misguided rebels to lay down their arms. He brought with him a formidable English force, an army of German veterans of equal power, a terrifying band of Indian warriors, and a brilliant train of artillery. It was hoped the populace along his route would rally to the Royal cause. Initially, the Continental force opposing him was small, and Washington's army had been further weakened by the withdrawal of troops hurried forward to intercept this Canadian invasion. A British detachment from New York was to sweep up the Hudson, clearing the route, and join Burgoyne at Albany. This was the moment when Washington's weakened army should have been crushed; but a greater Power willed otherwise. Nor do I, for one, regret the termination of the war. Looking back now, how clear the blunders of the losing side appear! From start to finish, we were perpetually too late. Our supplies and reinforcements from home arrived too late. Our troops struggled, and aid reached them too late. Our fleet appeared off York Town just in time—only too late—to witness Cornwallis surrender. A way of escape was offered to Burgoyne, but he resolved upon retreat too late. I have heard discomfited officers in later years prove infallibly how a different wind would have saved America for us; how we must have destroyed the French fleet but for a tempest or two; how, once, twice, thrice, but for nightfall, Mr. Washington and his army would have fallen into our power. Who has not, in reading history, speculated upon the "Has Beens" and "Might Have Beens"? I take my tattered old map-book from the shelf and examine the board upon which the great contest was played. I marvel at the curious chances that lost it. Putting aside any idle talk about the relative bravery of the two nations, I cannot but see that we held the best cards, and yet we lost the game.

I confess the sport possessed a considerable fascination for me, stirring my languid blood. My brother Hal, settled on his plantation in Virginia, was perfectly content with the pleasures and occupations there. The company of country neighbors sufficed him; he never tired of tending his crops and people, catching fish, shooting ducks, hunting in the woods, or enjoying his rubber and supper. Happy Hal, in his great barn-like house, under his roomy porches, with his dogs lying at his feet; his friends, the Virginian Will Wimble, staying at free quarters in his mansion; his negroes fat, lazy, and ragged; his shrewd little wife ruling over them and her husband—who always obeyed her implicitly while she lived, and who was consoled with surprising speed when she died! I call him happy, though his lot would have been intolerable to me. My income was five times as great, my house in England larger and built of brick faced with freestone. My wife... would I have changed her for any other woman in the world? My children... well, I am content with Lady Warrington's opinion of them. But with all these plums, peaches, and rich fruits pouring from Plenty's horn into my lap, I fear I have been something of an ingrate. Hodge, my gatekeeper, who shares his bread and scrap of bacon with a family as large as my own, seems to me to enjoy his meal as much as I do, despite Mrs. Molly's best dishes and sweetmeats, and Mr. Gumbo's finest wines. Ah, me! Sweetmeats have lost their savor for me, however much they may delight my young ones in the nursery, and the perfume of claret grows pallid upon old noses!

Our parson has preached many a sermon to me, and perhaps I did not care for them much when he first began. Dost thou remember, honest friend? (Sure he does, for he has repeated the story over the bottle as often as his sermons, and my Lady Warrington pretends she has never heard it)—I say, Joe Blake, thou rememberest full well, with advantage, that October evening when we scrambled up an embrasure at Fort Clinton, and a clubbed musket would have dashed these valuable brains out, had not Joe's sword whipped my rebellious countryman through the gizzard. Joe wore a red coat in those days—the uniform of the brave Sixty-Third, whose leader, the bold Colonel Sill, fell pierced with many wounds beside him. He exchanged his red coat for black and my pulpit. His doctrines are sound, and his sermons short. We read the newspapers together over our wine. Not two months ago, we read of our old friend Howe's glorious deeds of the first of June. We were told how the noble Rawdon, who fought with us at Fort Clinton, had joined the Duke of York; and today, that Royal Highness is in full retreat before Pichegru, while he and my son Miles have taken Valenciennes for nothing! Ah, parson! Would you not like to don your old Sixty-Third coat? (Though I doubt Mrs. Blake could ever make the buttons and buttonholes meet again over your broad frame). The boys were acting a play with my militia sword. Oh, that I were young again, Mr. Blake! That I had not the gout in my toe! And I would saddle Rocinante and ride back into the world, feeling the pulses beat again, and play a little of life's glorious game!

The last "hit" I witnessed was gallantly won by our side, though truth be told, even in this party, the Americans won the rubber. Our people gained only the ground they stood upon, capturing and destroying the guns, stores, and ships, while our efforts at rescue proved too late to prevent the catastrophe impending over Burgoyne's unfortunate army. Following one of those inevitable delays that retarded our plans and weakened the blows our chiefs intended to deliver, an expedition was launched from New York at the close of September, 1777. Had it advanced but a fortnight earlier, it might have saved the doomed force of Burgoyne. *Sed Dis aliter visum.* This delay was not Sir Henry Clinton's fault, who could not leave his city unprotected, but rather the winds and weather that delayed the arrival of reinforcements we had long awaited from England. The fleet bringing them also delivered long, fond letters from home, containing the very latest news of the children under the care of their good Aunt Hetty and their grandfather. The mother's heart yearned toward the absent young ones. She offered me no reproaches, but I could read her importunities in her anxious eyes, her terror for me, and her longing for her children. "Why stay longer?" she seemed to say. "You who have no calling to this war, or to draw the sword against your countrymen—why continue to imperil your life and my happiness?" I understood her appeal. We were to engage in no immediate service of danger.
