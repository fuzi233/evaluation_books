Gerard went beside Teresa through the Roman streets as if they were moving along the margin of a great, warm sea. He did not question her; he watched her instead, the way a draughtsman watches a face before he dares a first line—waiting to learn what she meant to make of him, and what she meant to make of the city.

She spared him the asking.

“A friend,” she said at last, “told me of a goldsmith’s wife that would have a writer. Her shop is hard by; thou wilt not have far to go.”

So they turned into a lane where metal rang under hammers in quick bright strokes, and the air tasted of charcoal, hot filings, and the sharp, clean bite of vinegar used to brighten dull gold. The goldsmith’s wife stood in her doorway among glittering trifles and little scales, her hands white with polishing-powder.

“Madama,” said Teresa, with the grave courtesy that made her seem taller than she was, “Leonora telleth me you want a writer. I have brought you a beautiful one; he saved my child at sea. Prithee look on him with favour.”

The woman looked—looked as women look at comely youth with idle leisure—and could scarce take her eyes away; but her tongue gave small comfort.

“Nay. I have no use for a writer. Ah! I mind now—’tis my gossip Claelia, the sausage-maker, wanteth one. She told me, and I told Leonora.”

Teresa answered smoothly, as if refusal were but another door to be opened with patience, and drew Gerard away.

Claelia lived farther; the city’s sounds changed by degrees as they walked—less of jewellers’ tinkling, more of carts and donkey-bells, and the long hum of voices that makes Rome like a hive. When they reached the sausage-maker’s house, the door was shut and the place smelled—though even from the street—of smoke and pepper, of rendered fat that had clung to rafters for years. But Claelia herself was out.

“I will await her return,” said Teresa.

She seated herself with such stillness, such composed weight, as if she had been carved for a niche. Gerard, who lived by the hand and the eye, found his fingers itching to steal her outline on paper. He had already begun, furtively, in his mind, when footsteps and a burst of warm spiced air announced the woman’s return.

Claelia came in with her sleeves pushed up, a red string of sausages over one arm like a rosary of meat. Her hands were stained with wine and garlic, and her hair carried the scent of smoke.

“Madama,” Teresa began again, “I hear from the goldsmith’s wife, the excellent Olympia, that you need a writer.” Here she took Gerard’s hand and led him forward as one presents a chosen knife to a cook. “I have brought you a beautiful one; he saved my child from the cruel waves. For our Lady’s sake look with favour on him.”

“My good dame, my fair Ser,” cried Claelia, laughing with the easy mirth of women who have vats to stir and no time for courtly fears, “I have no use for a writer. But now you remind me—my friend Appia Claudia asked me for one but the other day. She is a tailor; liveth in the Via Lepida.”

Teresa withdrew as calmly as if she had gained a point.

“Madama,” said Gerard, when they were in the street again, “this is like to be a tedious business for you.”

Teresa opened her eyes slowly, as if he had spoken a childish thing. “What was ever done without a little patience?” Then, with that mild iron in her voice, she added, “We will knock at every door at Rome, but thou shalt have justice.”

He hesitated, and lowered his voice. “But, madama, I think we are dogged. I noticed a man that followeth us—now afar, now close.”

“I have seen it,” said Teresa coldly; but a faint colour stirred under her cheek like a blush hidden under marble. “It is my poor Lodovico.”

She stopped, turned, and beckoned with one finger.

A figure that had been hovering like a shadow in the bustle approached unwillingly. When he came up, Teresa looked full into his face. He wore the sheepishness of a man caught doing what he has no right to do.

“Lodovico mio,” she said, “know this young Ser, of whom I have so often spoken to thee. Know him and love him; for he it was who saved thy wife and child.”

At the last words Lodovico’s face broke, and what had been false bowing and grinning fell away. He embraced Gerard warmly, with a gratitude that seemed honest enough. Yet something—his first manner, and the stealth of his following—made Gerard’s skin tighten beneath the friendly arm, as if a dish too richly spiced had left a bitterness on the tongue.

Gerard drew back and kept his courtesy. “We shall have your company, Ser Lodovico?”

“No, signor,” answered Lodovico quickly. “I go not on that side Tiber.”

“Addio, then,” said Teresa, and there was meaning in the word, as in a lid set down on a pot.

“When wilt thou return home, Teresa mia?” he asked.

“When I have done mine errand, Lodovico.”

They went on. Teresa’s face had altered; the calm remained, but it was now the calm of a shut door. The sounds of Rome—fiddles from a courtyard, boys crying fruit, the slap of sandals on stone—seemed to leave her untouched. Gerard, who could not keep the world out of his senses, felt the city’s noise press closer, and wished, in spite of himself, for the simple hush of a Dutch field where wind and birds do the speaking.

Appia Claudia, the tailor, proved at least merciful: she did not send them over Tiber again, only a hundred yards down the street to Lucretia of the glove-shop.

“She it is,” said Appia, half amused, half puzzled, “that wanteth a writer; but what for, I cannot conceive.”

Lucretia was a little merry dame, with quick eyes and quicker hands. The shop was hung with gloves like pale fruit—kid as soft as cream, tanned leather smelling of oak-bark and smoke, and new-dyed pairs whose colour still breathed pungently: madder-red, walnut-brown, saffron, and the bluish black that catches the light like a raven’s wing. The air within was thick with that intimate perfume of leather, oil, and the faint sweetness of starch.

She received them heartily enough and laughed at the notion.

“A writer? I want no writer. I keep all my accounts in my head. ’Twas for my confessor, Father Colonna. He is mad after them.”

“I have heard of his excellency,” said Teresa.

“Who hath not?” Lucretia tossed her chin. “But, good dame, he is a friar; he hath made vow of poverty. I cannot let the young man write and not be paid. He saved my child at sea.”

“Did he now?” Lucretia gave Gerard an approving glance, as if measuring a roast before buying. “Well, make thy mind easy. A Colonna never wanteth for money. The good father hath but to say the word, and the princes of his race will pour a thousand crowns into his lap. And such a confessor! the best in Rome. His head is leagues and leagues away all the while; he never heedeth what thou sayest. I think no more of confessing my sins to him than of telling them to that wall.”

Teresa waited, patient as a woman who has watched bread rise slowly and knows haste ruins it. Lucretia, delighted with her own talk, went on, seasoning her tale with relish.

“Once, to try him, I confessed—along with the rest—how I had killed my lodger’s little girl and baked her in a pie. A fine pie too, mind you—crust browned, edges pinched fair as a collar, steam smelling o’ pepper and sweet herbs. Well, when my voice left off, he started out of his dream and mustered up a gloom. ‘My erring sister,’ saith he, ‘say three Paternosters and three Ave Marias kneeling, and eat no butter nor eggs next Wednesday, and pax vobiscum!’ And off he went with his hands behind him, looking as if there were no such thing as me in the world.”

Teresa, without a change of brow, brought her back as one brings a pot to the fire again.

“Would you be so kind as go with this good youth to the friar and speak for him?”

“Alack! how can I leave my shop?” cried Lucretia. “And what need? His door is aye open to writers and painters and scholars—and all such cattle. Why, one day he would not receive the Duke d’Urbino, because a learned Greek was closeted with him; the friar’s head and his so close together over a dusty parchment new come from Greece, as you could put one cowl over the pair. His wench Onesta told me. She mostly looks in here for a chat when she goeth an errand.”

“This is the man for thee, my friend,” said Teresa, and there was a warmth under the gravity.

“All thou hast to do,” continued Lucretia, “is go to his lodgings—my boy shall show you—and tell Onesta you come from me, and you are a writer, and she will take you up to him. If you put a piece of silver in the wench’s hand, ’twill do you no harm: that standeth to reason.”

“I have silver,” said Teresa promptly.

“But stay,” said Lucretia, and her tone sharpened like a knife finding bone. “Mind one thing. What the young man saith he can do, that he must be able to do, or let him shun the good friar like poison. He is a very wild beast against all bunglers. ’Twas but t’other day one brought him an ill-carved crucifix. ‘Is this how you present Salvator Mundi?’ saith he, ‘who died for you in mortal agony; and you go and grudge him careful work. This slovenly gimcrack—a crucifix? But that it is a crucifix of some sort, and I am a holy man, I’d dust thy jacket with thy crucifix.’ Onesta heard every word through the key-hole; so mind.”

“Have no fears, madama,” said Teresa loftily, as if the matter were already judged. “I will answer for his ability; he saved my child.”

Gerard, who could not follow her leap from courage at sea to neatness of hand, felt his confidence ebb.

He begged a respite. He would rather not go to the friar to-day: would not to-morrow do as well?

“Here is a coward for ye,” cried Lucretia.

“No, he is not a coward,” said Teresa, and for the first time her quiet caught fire; “he is modest.”

“I am afraid of this high-born, fastidious friar,” Gerard confessed, ashamed and honest together. “Consider he hath seen the handiwork of all the writers in Italy, dear dame Teresa. If you would but let me prepare a better piece of work than yet I have done, then to-morrow I will face him with it.”

“I consent,” said Teresa, and the flame went out as quickly as it had risen.

They walked home together. The city smelled now of supper beginning—onion frying in oil, fish newly gutted by the river, sour wine in cups, bread cracked open with warm hands. Voices rose and fell; a lute was tried and tuned; a quarrel started and ended in laughter. Gerard heard it all as one hears water running, and it soothed him; yet beneath it he thought of the friar’s “wild beast” temper and of his own trembling hand.

Not far from his lodging was a shop that sold vellum. In the window lay a skin, white as milk, with a faint sheen like the inside of a shell. It seemed to drink the late light and give it back. Gerard’s eye rested on it; his throat tightened; he sighed and hurried on, knowing he could not pay.

But as he went, his mind—always hungry, always contriving—chose another way. He parted from Teresa at his own door, then ran upstairs and drew from his bundle the bond he had carried from Sevenbergen as if it were a relic and a burden both. He laid it on the table with a sigh.

He set out his little chemicals to erase old writing—materials that smelled acrid and sour, like sharp fruit turning. Yet before he destroyed anything, he forced himself—once, and for the last time—to look hard at the crabbed hand upon the parchment. He loathed bad writing as some men loathe rancid butter; it offended his whole sense of order. Still he deciphered, slowly, wincing at each detestable contraction.

It appeared Ghysbrecht Van Swieten was to advance money to Floris Brandt on a piece of land, and repay himself out of the rent.

At that Gerard stopped, as if the parchment had spoken. It would be imprudent—improper—to destroy such a deed. Instead he vowed to decipher every word at leisure, and keep it safe.

He went downstairs resolved to buy a small piece of vellum with his half of the card-money, and so do his sample honorably. But at the bottom of the stairs he found his landlady and Teresa in talk, their heads close as if over a bowl.

At sight of him the landlady cried, triumphantly, “Here he is. You are caught, donna mia. See what she hath bought you?” And she whipped from under her apron the very skin of vellum Gerard had coveted.

His breath left him.

“Why, dame! why, donna Teresa!” He could not find words enough for the pleasure that flooded him, warm as broth after fasting. “Dear donna Teresa, there is not a skin in all Rome like it. However came you to hit on this one? ’Tis glamour.”

“Alas, dear boy,” said Teresa simply, “did not thine eye rest on it with desire? and didst thou not sigh in turning away from it? And was it for Teresa to let thee want the thing after that?”

“What sagacity! what goodness!” He took it as if it were bread in a famine. “Oh, dame, I never thought I should possess this. What did you pay for it?”

“I forget.” And already she was withdrawing, as if gifts were nothing and gratitude a burden. “Addio, Fiammina. Addio, Ser Gerard. Be happy; be prosperous, as you are good.”

She glided away while Gerard stood hesitating—half longing to press money on her, half knowing money would insult the stateliness of her charity.

The next day, in the afternoon, he went to Lucretia, and her boy led him to Fra Colonna’s lodgings. Gerard had his vellum page safe, like a fresh loaf wrapped against dust. He fed Onesta with the silver as Lucretia had advised; the coin clicked softly in her palm, and she smiled, practical as any market-woman weighing figs.

She took him up to the friar.

Gerard entered with a heart that beat so loudly he feared it would be heard above the house’s own sounds—the faint scrape of quills, the shifting of paper, the creak of a chest-lid, a distant chant passing like wind through a cloister.

The room was large, and strewed and heaped with objects of art, antiquity, and learning lying about in rich profusion and confusion: manuscripts piled like loaves cooling after baking, pictures leaned face to wall, carvings in wood and ivory, musical instruments with their bellies catching light. The place had its own smell too—dust, ink, old glue, the dry sweetness of parchment, and a faint musk from stored fabrics and leather bindings—like a pantry of memory where everything is kept but nothing is labelled in a tongue the living speak.

In the midst of this glorious chaos sat the friar, poring intently over an Arabian manuscript. He looked up a little peevishly at the interruption.

Onesta whispered in his ear.

“Very well,” said he. “Let him be seated. Stay; young man, show me how you write?” And he threw Gerard a piece of paper and pointed to an inkhorn.

“So please you, reverend father,” Gerard said, his mouth dry, “my hand it trembleth too much at this moment; but last night I wrote a vellum page of Greek, and the Latin version by its side, to show the various character.”

“Show it me.”

Gerard brought the work to him in fear and trembling, and then stood heart-sick, awaiting sentence as a cook waits while a great lord tastes.

When the verdict came, it staggered him; for the verdict was not words at all, but a Dominican falling on his neck.

The next day an event took place in Holland, the effect of which on Gerard’s destiny no mortal then—nor even my intelligent reader now—could, I think, foresee.

Marched up to Eli’s door a pageant brave to the eye of sense, and to the vulgar judgment noble, but to the philosophic pitiable more or less. It looked one animal, a centaur; but on severe analysis proved two. The human half was sadly bedizened with those two metals to clothe his carcass with which and line his pouch man has now and then disposed of his soul. Yet the horse was the vainer brute of the two, far worse beflounced, bebonneted, and bemantled than any fair lady regnante crinolina; for the man, under the colour of a warming-pan, retained Nature’s outline—subaudi equum! Scarce a pennyweight of honest horse-flesh was to be seen. Great sheaths of white linen tipped with silver and blue swallowed the ears; stiff gorgeous cloths swaddled the body down to the ground, save where room was left in front to mince; the tail—dear to memory, no doubt—was lost to sight, tucked in Heaven knows how. Only the eyes shone out like goggles through holes in that wall of haberdashery, and the little front hoofs peeped in and out like rats.

Yet did this compound, gorgeous and irrational, represent power—absolute power. It came straight from a tournament at the Duke’s court, which being on a progress lay last night at a neighbouring town—to execute the behests of royalty.

“What ho!” cried the upper half; and on Eli emerging with his wife behind him, saluted them. “Peace be with you, good people. Rejoice! I am come for your dwarf.”

Eli looked amazed and said nothing. But Catherine screamed over his shoulder, “You have mistook your road, good man; here abides no dwarf.”

“Nay, wife,” said Eli, slow and grave, “he means our Giles, who is somewhat small of stature. Why gainsay what gainsayed may not be?”

“Ay!” cried the pageant. “That is he, and discourseth like the big taber.”

“His breast is sound for that matter,” said Catherine sharply.

“And prompt with his fists though at long odds.”

“Else how would the poor thing keep his head in such a world as this?”

“’Tis well said, dame,” returned the emissary. “Art as ready with thy weapon as he; art his mother, likely. So bring him forth—and that presently. See, they lead a stunted mule for him. The Duke hath need of him, sore need. We are clean out o’ dwarven and tiger-cats, which may not be, whiles earth them yieldeth. Our last hop o’ my thumb tumbled down the well t’other day.”

“And think you I’ll let my darling go to such an ill-guided house as you,” cried Catherine, “where the reckless trollops o’ servants close not the well mouth, but leave it open to trap innocents, like wolven?”

The representative of autocracy lost patience at this unwonted opposition. He set his stern look on her and bade her bethink which was better: “to have your abortion at court fed like a bishop and put on like a prince, or to have all your heads stricken off and borne on poles, with the bellman crying, ‘Behold the heads of hardy rebels, which having by good luck a misbegotten son did traitorously grudge him to the Duke, who is the true father of all his folk, little or mickle!’”

“Nay,” said Eli sadly, “miscall us not. We be true folk, and neither rebels nor traitors. But ’tis sudden, and the poor lad is our true flesh and blood, and hath of late given proof of more sense than heretofore.”

“Avails not threatening our lives,” whimpered Catherine; “we grudge him not to the Duke; but in sooth he cannot go—his linen is all in holes. So there is an end.”

But the male mind resisted the crusher. “Think you the Duke will not find linen—and cloth of gold to boot? None so brave, none so affected, at court as our monsters, big or wee.”

How long the dispute might have lasted, before the iron arguments of despotism achieved the inevitable victory, I know not; but it was cut short by a party neither disputant had deigned to consult. The bone of contention walked out of the house and sided with monarchy.

“If my folk are mad, I am not!” Giles roared. “I’ll go with you—and on the instant.”

At this Catherine set up a piteous cry. She saw another of her brood escaping from under her wing into some unknown element. Giles was not quite insensible to her distress, so simple yet so eloquent.

“Nay, take not on, mother!” he said. “Why, ’tis a godsend. And I am sick of this, ever since Gerard left it.”

“Ah, cruel Giles!” she sobbed. “Should ye not rather say she is bereaved of Gerard: the more need of you to stay aside her and comfort her.”

“Oh! I am not going to Rome. Not such a fool. I shall never be farther than Rotterdam; and I’ll often come and see you; and if I like not the place, who shall keep me there? Not all the dukes in Christendom.”

“Good sense lies in little bulk,” said the emissary approvingly. “Therefore, Master Giles, buss the old folk, and thank them for misbegetting of thee; and ho! you—bring hither his mule.”

One of the retinue brought up the dwarf mule. Giles refused it with scorn; and being asked the reason, said it was not just.

“What! would ye throw all into one scale? Put muckle to muckle, and little to wee! Besides, I hate and scorn small things. I’ll go on the highest horse here—or not at all.”

The pursuivant eyed him attentively a moment, then adopted a courteous manner. “I shall study your will in all things reasonable. (Dismount, Eric; yours is the highest horse.) And if you would halt in the town an hour or so while you bid them farewell, say but the word, and your pleasure shall be my delight.”

Giles reflected. “Master,” said he, “if we wait a month, ’twill be still the same. My mother is a good soul, but her body is bigger than her spirit. We shall not part without a tear or two, and the quicker ’tis done the fewer; so bring yon horse to me.”

Catherine threw her apron over her face and sobbed.

The high horse was brought, and Giles was for swarming up his tail like a rope; but one of the servants cried out hastily, “Forbear, for he kicketh.”

“I’ll kick him,” said Giles. “Bring him close beneath this window, and I’ll learn you all how to mount a horse which kicketh, and will not be clomb by the tail, the staircase of a horse.”

He dashed into the house, and almost immediately reappeared at an upper window with a rope in his hand. He fastened an end somehow, and holding the other descended as swift and smooth as an oiled thunderbolt in a groove, and lighted astride the high horse as unperceived by the animal as a fly settling on him.

The official lifted his hands to heaven in mawkish admiration. “I have gotten a pearl,” thought he. “And wow but this will be a good day’s work for me.”

“Come, father, come, mother—buss me and bless me—and off I go.”

Eli gave him his blessing and bade him be honest and true, and a credit to his folk. Catherine could not speak, but clung to him with many sobs and embraces; and even through the mist of tears her eye detected in a moment the little rent in his sleeve he had made getting out of window. She whipped out her needle and mended it then and there; her tears fell on his arm the while, unheeded—except by those unfleshly eyes, with which they say the very air is thronged.

So the dwarf mounted the high horse and rode away complacent, while the old hand laid the court butter on his back with a trowel. Little recked Perpusillus of two poor silly females that sat by the bereaved hearth, rocking themselves and weeping and discussing all his virtues and how his mind had opened lately—blind as two beetles to his faults—who rode away from them jocund and bold:

Ingentes animos angusto pectore versans.

Arrived at court he speedily became a great favourite. One strange propensity of his electrified the palace; but on account of his small size, and for variety’s sake, and as a monster, he was indulged on it. In a word, he was let speak the truth.

It is an unpopular thing.

He made it an intolerable one.
