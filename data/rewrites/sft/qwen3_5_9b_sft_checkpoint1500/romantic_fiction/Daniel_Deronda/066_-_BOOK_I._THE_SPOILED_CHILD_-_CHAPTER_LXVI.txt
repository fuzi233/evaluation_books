"One day still fierce 'mid many a day struck calm."
—BROWNING: *The King and the Book*.

While Gwendolen's thoughts remained fixed elsewhere, a quieter, more intimate current flowed between Ezra, Mirah, and the man who claimed them both. Their connection, previously obscured, was now drawn taut and illuminated.

Lapidoth had stepped onto the threshold, his mind already consumed by the thrill of a gamble larger than mere survival. Having surrendered the thirty shillings from his daughter's purse, his world narrowed to the mechanics of the game and the hope of doubling back. Hunger, a constant companion, paled before the insatiable need for the next roll, the next chance. To him, the act of wagering was a spiritual necessity, a way to suspend reality, to dance on the burning marl of fate rather than face the grim prospect of tomorrow's bread. Yet, beneath this fever dream, a primal need for sustenance lingered. He had slipped into a shabby existence where money was the only key to warmth, and now, holding Mirah's empty purse, he balanced two conflicting impulses: pawn it for immediate relief, or return it with a lie, claiming a debt paid, to preserve his fragile dignity. A lingering pride, the belief that his children owed him their loyalty, weighed heavily against the justice of Ezra's anger. After all, wasn't staying with his offspring the safest harbor? And why shrink from facing Ezra when luck might yet smile, offering supplies through Mirah's "good friends"? Lapidoth clung to the illusion of his past cleverness, convinced he could feign vitality despite the erosion of time.

His plan, however, unraveled as evening deepened. Walking the streets of Brompton, hoping to spot Mirah, he grew weary, then resigned. When he approached the house, the soft hum of a melody confirmed her presence. Inside, Mirah played "Herz, mein Herz" with a sorrowful grace, while Ezra listened, eyes closed, lost in a silent storm. Mrs. Adam's arrival shattered the moment. A knock followed by her hesitant announcement—"A gentleman below says he is your father, miss"—sent a jolt through the room.

"I will go down to him," Mirah offered, rising abruptly.

"No, Mirah," Ezra stopped her, his voice firm. "Let him come up, Mrs. Adam."

Mirah stood frozen, her fingers twisting together, sickness rising in her stomach. She watched Ezra, who had risen too, his face transformed. There was a severity in his knitted brow, hardened lips, and piercing gaze that she had never seen before. As Mrs. Adam ushered Lapidoth inside, casting a glance at the siblings, she murmured, "Father, surely enough." The resemblance was striking in outline, yet the spirit within was a stark contrast, forged by invisible fires into something formidable.

Lapidoth, masking a deep wince with a melancholy mask, spoke the words that tore the air apart. "Well, Ezra, my boy, you hardly know me after so many years."

"I know you—too well, father," Ezra replied, his tone slow, biting, rendering the title a reproach.

"You are not pleased?" Lapidoth rushed forward, his voice regaining a glib, defensive rhythm. "Appearances have been against me! When a man falls, he cannot choose his path... I've suffered enough!" He turned to Mirah, thrusting the purse toward her. "Here, my dear. I thought you'd worry about that note. I emptied it, you see—I had scores to pay. Here I stand, without a farthing, at your mercy. Turn me out, if you like, without a policeman. Just say the word, Mirah: 'Father, I'm done with you,' and I'll vanish like a spark." Tears welled instantly, a familiar weapon.

"You know I could never say it, father," Mirah whispered, her voice thick with anguish, knowing the truth behind his performance.

"Mirah, my sister, leave us!" Ezra commanded, his authority absolute.

Mirah hesitated, her eyes wide with fear and awe. She moved close to her brother, placing her hand in his, her voice barely audible above the silence. "Remember, Ezra—you said my mother would not have shut him out."

"Trust me, and go," Ezra said gently.

Mirah retreated, her heart pounding like a drum. As she ascended the stairs, she sat down, terrified of what might follow. Lapidoth, meanwhile, sensed the trap closing but adjusted his stance, preparing a shield of superior calm. He saw the tragedy in Ezra's pale face, interpreting it through the lens of his own self-preservation. To him, Ezra's grief was merely a dramatic performance, a religious howl in an open cathedral that offered little shelter from the hail of his own conscience.

"This home," Ezra began, his voice steady but heavy, "is sustained by the generosity of a friend and the labor of my sister. So long as we have a roof, we will not cast you out to the mercy of your vices. You are our father, and though you have broken your bond, we acknowledge ours. But I will never trust you. You fled with money, leaving debts unpaid; you abandoned my mother, stealing her child and breaking her heart; you became a gambler, where shame once lived, now sits an insatiable void. You were ready to sell my sister—you had the buyer, but the price was denied you. A man who has done these things cannot ever expect trust again. We will feed you, clothe you, give you a bed, because you are our father. But you will never be trusted. You are an evil man, and you made the misery of our mother. That fact brands our flesh, a wound that will never heal. But the Eternal has laid this burden on us. Even if human justice dragged you through the mud, if your body fell helpless before the scorn of the crowd, we would still say, 'This is our father.' Make way, that we may carry him out of our sight."

Lapidoth, expecting a softer rejection, was blindsided by the lightning of Ezra's truth. He could not predict the depth of the son's soul, nor the raw, hysterical spring it tapped. As Ezra finished, Lapidoth collapsed into a chair, crying like a woman, burying his face in his hands. It was a reaction inevitable to the shock, yet also a calculated resource, a tool he had honed since youth to deflect resentment.

Ezra sat in silence, exhausted by the eruption of feelings he had carried alone for years. His hands trembled on the chair's arms; he felt as though he had just stepped toward death. Suddenly, Mirah returned, her ear catching a sound that chilled her blood. Rushing to Ezra's side, she took his trembling hand, finding a sliver of strength in his grip. He did not look at her, his eyes fixed on the floor.

Lapidoth, sensing her approach, raised his head. With a plaintive hoarseness, he reached for her hand. "Good-bye, Mirah. Your father will not trouble you again. He deserves to die like a dog by the roadside, and he will. If your mother had lived, she would have forgiven me—thirty-four years ago I put the ring on her finger under the *Chuppa*, and we were made one. She would have forgiven me, and we should have spent our old age together. But I haven't deserved it. Good-bye."

As he rose, Mirah seized his arm, her voice shaking. "No, father, no!" Turning to Ezra, she pleaded, "Ezra, you have not forbidden him? Stay, father, and leave off wrong things. Ezra, I cannot bear it. How can I say to my father, 'Go and die!'?"

"I have not said it," Ezra answered, the effort tearing at him. "I have said, stay and be sheltered."

"And you will stay, father—and be taken care of—and come with me," Mirah insisted, guiding him toward the door.

It was exactly what Lapidoth desired. For a fleeting moment, the comfort of his daughter's dutiful attendance made the idea of changing his ways seem plausible. She led him downstairs to the parlor, indicating a bedroom behind it. "You will stay and be good, father. Think that you are come back to my mother, and that she has forgiven you—she speaks to you through me." Her tone was imploring, yet devoid of the caresses she once gave.

Lapidoth quickly composed himself, chattering easily about Mirah's improved voice and trivialities, trying to impress Mrs. Adam when she served supper, proving he was no ordinary beggar despite his ragged clothes. But as night fell, the illusions dissolved. Lying awake, his mind raced with calculations of Mirah's funds, replaying scenes from Continental casinos, visualizing the roulette wheels and the chances that had cheated him. These were the stronger visions haunting him, overshadowed by the worn frame of his irate son, whose terrible judgment echoed in the darkness. Ezra had passed the gaming tables like a ghost, his words hollowed out by the crushing weight of numbers and movements that seemed to weave the very fabric of Lapidoth's consciousness, eroding his resolve stone by stone.
