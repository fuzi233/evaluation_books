That morning, Mademoiselle Moore had a pupil who seemed lost in thought. Caroline repeatedly forgot the lessons being taught her, yet she bore each gentle reprimand with a quiet smile, as if no fault had been made. Seated near the window, bathed in sunlight, she appeared to draw warmth not just from the rays but from something deeper—something that softened her heart and lifted her spirit. In this mood, she was at her most radiant, and her beauty shone with a quiet grace.

She was blessed with natural charm. One need not know her well to feel drawn to her. Her presence was pleasing even at first glance. Her figure was light and graceful, perfectly suited to her youth—slender, poised, and balanced. Her face was delicate, expressive, and kind; her eyes held a warmth that could melt reserve, and at times sparkled with a softness that touched the soul. Her lips were gently shaped, her skin fair and smooth, and her chestnut hair—abundant and wavy—fell in lovely curls about her shoulders. She wore her clothes with taste: modest, unostentatious, yet harmonious in color and cut. Her winter dress was of soft merino, the same rich brown as her hair, trimmed with a pink ribbon at the collar and fastened with a small bow. No jewelry adorned her—only simplicity, elegance, and quiet dignity.

This was Caroline Helstone. As for her mind or character—if they existed—they would reveal themselves in time.

Her family history was brief. Her parents had separated soon after her birth, their union undone by irreconcilable differences. Her mother was the half-sister of Mr. Moore’s father, making Caroline, though without blood relation, a distant cousin to Robert, Louis, and Hortense. Her father, brother to the Reverend Mr. Helstone, was a man whose name was best left unsaid after death had settled all accounts. He had brought sorrow to his wife, and rumors of his misconduct had cast doubt upon the reputation of his more upright brother. Caroline never knew her mother—taken from her in infancy, she’d seen her only once, long ago. Her father died young, leaving her under the care of her uncle, the rector. But he was ill-suited to guide a child’s life. He took little interest in her education, and had it not been for Caroline’s own quiet determination—her occasional pleas for books, for instruction, for learning—she might have grown up entirely untaught. Still, she carried within her a quiet shame, feeling herself behind others of her age and station. So when Hortense offered to teach her French and embroidery shortly after arriving at Hollow’s Mill, Caroline accepted eagerly.

Hortense delighted in the role. It gave her authority. She saw Caroline not as an equal, but as a student—perhaps even a bit backward. When Caroline progressed quickly, Hortense attributed it not to talent or effort, but to her own superior method. Even when Caroline revealed knowledge on topics where Hortense was ignorant, she believed the girl had simply absorbed wisdom from conversation, unaware of its source. The idea was illogical—but Hortense clung to it with firm conviction.

Proud of her “esprit positif,” and devoted to practical studies, Hortense kept Caroline focused on grammar and logic. She assigned endless “analyses logiques”—exercises that required dissecting sentences into principal and subordinate clauses, identifying determinative and applicative incidents, determining whether a proposition was complete, elliptical, or implicit. To Caroline, these tasks felt like puzzles without purpose. She believed she could learn French without them—and resented the hours spent wrestling with abstract terms. When she grew confused, she sometimes slipped her book to Robert in the counting-house while Hortense was upstairs rummaging through drawers—an odd habit that consumed much of her day. Robert, calm and clear-minded, could unravel any difficulty in moments. With two words, he would unlock what had stumped her for hours. She often wished Hortense could teach like him. Grateful and admiring, she would return to the cottage, her spirits lifted, wishing—just once—that she were a boy so she might sit beside Robert, be his clerk, and learn not from rules, but from reason.

Occasionally, she visited Hollow’s Cottage in the evening. These visits were rare. Sometimes Robert was away at market or visiting Mr. Yorke. Other times, he was engaged with guests. But when he was home and free, the evening passed swiftly—too swiftly. There was no room in England more pleasant than that small parlour when the three cousins gathered. Hortense, when not teaching or scolding, was not cruel. By nightfall, she softened. If coaxed to play her guitar, she became almost charming—her voice clear, her fingers sure. Her music was soothing, though tinged with formality, as if every note were measured by duty rather than joy.

Robert, freed from business, listened with quiet pleasure. He was not lively, but he was attentive. He smiled at Caroline’s stories, answered her questions with patience, and sat beside her with a presence that felt comforting. At times, he seemed almost warm—gentle, even tender. But come morning, he was once again reserved, distant. Though he enjoyed the evenings, he rarely invited them. This puzzled Caroline. “If I had a source of happiness,” she thought, “I would use it often. I would keep it bright, not let it grow cold.”

Yet she never acted on this thought. Though she loved visiting the cottage, she never came uninvited. Often, when Hortense urged her, she refused—because Robert did not encourage it. That morning, however, he had spoken first, inviting her with such kindness that the simple words filled her with joy, lasting through the entire day.

The morning unfolded as usual. Hortense bustled between kitchen and parlour—scolding Sarah, checking Caroline’s work, listening to her recitation. No matter how well the lesson was done, she never praised. Praise, she believed, undermined a teacher’s dignity. Blame, in some form, was essential. If there was no flaw in the work, then it was the pupil’s posture, expression, or attire that needed correction.

Dinner arrived with the usual drama. Sarah placed the tray on the table with a look that said plainly, “I’ve never seen worse food in my life.” Yet the meal was far from terrible. A thick pea soup, prepared with great lamentation over the absence of haricot beans in England. Then a dish of meat—unidentifiable, chopped with bread crumbs, seasoned uniquely, and baked in a mold. Odd greens, roughly chopped, followed, and a fruit pâté made with a recipe from Madame Gérard Moore’s grandmother—so sweet it likely used molasses instead of sugar.

Caroline found nothing wrong with the meal. In fact, she liked it—especially as a change. Had she shown disapproval, it would have ruined her standing with Hortense. Dislike of foreign food was a greater sin than any other.

After dinner, Caroline managed to coax Hortense upstairs to dress. It required skill. To suggest that the jupon, camisole, or curl-papers were absurd would have been disastrous. Instead, she pretended to crave a change of scene. Once in the bedroom, she persuaded her cousin to stay and finish dressing there. While Hortense delivered a sermon on her own moral superiority—on ignoring fashion—Caroline quietly removed the camisole, dressed her in a decent gown, arranged her collar and hair, and made her presentable. But Hortense insisted on finishing touches: a thick handkerchief around her neck and a large black apron—both symbols of domestic virtue. She would never appear without them. The fichu was a matter of morality; the apron, a sign of thrift. She had made similar outfits for Caroline, and their one serious quarrel—still fresh in her mind—had begun when Caroline refused to wear them.

“I wear a high dress and collar,” Caroline said. “I’d suffocate with a handkerchief. My short aprons are enough. I’d rather make no change.”

But Hortense persisted—until Robert overheard the argument and declared Caroline’s aprons sufficient. Since she was still a child, he added, she could go without the fichu—especially since her curls nearly reached her shoulders.

There was no arguing with Robert. Hortense reluctantly yielded, though she disapproved deeply of Caroline’s neatness and grace. She preferred something simpler, more homely—“beaucoup plus convenable.”

The afternoon was spent sewing. Hortense, like many Belgian women, excelled at needlework. She saw no waste in spending hours on embroidery, lace, knitting, or especially mending stockings—each stitch meant to imitate the original fabric exactly. It was a sacred duty, she believed, passed down through generations. She had been given a torn stocking as a child, and by six, she’d already demonstrated her skill to guests. When she discovered Caroline knew nothing of darning, she wept inwardly at the neglect.

Without delay, she found a pair of hose with holes in both heels and set Caroline to mending them. Two years had passed, and the stockings still lay in her workbag. She stitched a few rows daily—as penance for her ignorance. They were a burden. She wanted to burn them. Once, Robert, seeing her sighing over them, suggested a secret incineration in the counting-house. But Caroline knew better—such defiance would only bring another, worse pair. So she endured.

All afternoon, the two sat sewing until fatigue crept into hands and eyes. The sky darkened after dinner. Rain fell hard, steady and heavy. Caroline began to fear Robert wouldn’t return—he might stay in Whinbury until the storm passed. Five o’clock struck. Clouds streamed. A wind sighed through the roof. Daylight faded. The fire in the parlour glowed like twilight.

“It won’t clear until moonrise,” Hortense declared. “So I’m certain Robert won’t return till then. Indeed, I’d be sorry if he did. We’ll have coffee. Waiting is useless.”

“I’m tired. May I stop now, cousin?”

“You may. It’s too dark to see. Fold your work, put it in your bag. Then go to the kitchen and ask Sarah to bring in the *goûter*.”

“But it’s not yet six. He might still come.”

“He will not. I know his ways. I understand my brother.”

Suspense is wearying. Disappointment, bitter. Everyone knows this. Obedient, Caroline entered the kitchen. Sarah was stitching a dress at the table.

“You’re to bring in coffee,” she said listlessly, leaning against the mantelpiece, staring into the fire.

“How low you seem, miss! But it’s because your cousin keeps you working so hard. It’s a shame!”

“Nothing of the kind, Sarah.”

“Oh, but I know it is. You’re fit to cry just now—nothing but sitting still all day. It would dull a kitten.”

“Sarah, does your master often come home early when it rains?”

“Never, hardly. But today, for some reason, he’s different.”

“What do you mean?”

“He’s here. I saw Murgatroyd lead his horse into the yard five minutes ago. He’s in the counting-house with Joe Scott.”

“You’re mistaken.”

“What should I be mistaken for? I know his horse.”

“But you didn’t see him?”

“I heard him speak. He was saying something about settling things, new frames coming in the mill, and four soldiers from Stilbro’ barracks guarding the wagon.”

“Sarah, are you making a gown?”

“Yes. Is it handsome?”

“Beautiful! Get the coffee ready. I’ll finish cutting out that sleeve and give you some trimming. I have narrow satin ribbon that matches perfectly.”

“You’re very kind, miss.”

“Be quick. And first—put your master’s shoes on the hearth. He’ll take off his boots when he comes in. I hear him. He’s coming.”

“Miss, you’re cutting the cloth wrong.”

“So I am. But it’s just a snip. No harm done.”

The kitchen door opened. Mr. Moore stepped in, wet and cold. Caroline turned slightly from her task, then returned to it—for a moment, as if to gain time. Bent over the dress, her face hidden, she tried to compose herself. When she finally looked up, her expression lit with joy.

“We stopped expecting you. They said you wouldn’t come.”

“But I promised to return soon. _You_ expected me?”

“No, Robert. I dared not when it rained so hard. And you’re wet and chilled. Change everything. If you caught cold, we’d blame ourselves.”

“I’m not soaked. My coat is waterproof. Just dry shoes. There—the fire feels good after miles of wind and rain.”

He stood on the hearth. Caroline stood beside him. Robert gazed at the brasses above the shelf, then glanced down—into a face flushed, smiling, full of warmth, framed by soft curls, glowing with life. Sarah had gone to the parlour with the tray, detained by her mistress.

Robert placed a hand on Caroline’s shoulder, leaned down, and kissed her forehead.

“Oh!” she breathed, as if the gesture unlocked her heart. “I was miserable when I thought you wouldn’t come. I’m almost too happy now. Are you happy, Robert? Do you like coming home?”

“I think I do—tonight, at least.”

“Are you certain you’re not fretting about your frames, your business, the war?”

“Not now.”

“Are you sure you don’t find Hollow’s Cottage too small, too narrow, too dull?”

“At this moment, no.”

“Can you truly say you’re not bitter that rich and powerful people forget you?”

“No more questions. You’re wrong if you think I seek favor from them. I only want a chance—a position—a path.”

“Which your talent and goodness will win you. You were made to be great. You _shall_ be great.”

“I wonder what recipe you’d give me for greatness—if you spoke honestly. But I know it. Would it work? Yes—poverty, misery, bankruptcy. Oh, life is not what you imagine, Lina.”

“But you are what I imagine.”

“I am not.”

“You are better, then?”

“Far worse.”

“No—far better. I know you are good.”

“How do you know?”

“You look so, and I feel you _are_ so.”

“Where do you feel it?”

“In my heart.”

“Ah! You judge me with your heart, Lina. You should judge me with your head.”

“I do—and then I’m proud of you. Robert, you cannot tell all my thoughts about you.”

Robert’s dark face warmed. His lips curved, yet stayed tight. His eyes sparkled, yet his brow remained knit.

“Think poorly of me, Lina,” he said. “Men are generally scum—very different from anything you can imagine. I claim no better.”

“If you did, I wouldn’t esteem you as much. It’s because you’re modest that I trust your worth.”

“Are you flattering me?” he asked sharply, turning to study her face.

“No,” she replied softly, laughing at his sudden intensity. She seemed to think no defense necessary.

“You don’t care if I think you flatter me?”

“No.”

“You’re so sure of your intentions?”

“I suppose so.”

“What are they, Caroline?”

“Only to ease my mind by speaking what I feel—and then to make you feel better about yourself.”

“By telling me my kinswoman is sincere?”

“Exactly. I am your sincere friend, Robert.”

“And I am—what chance and change shall make me, Lina.”

“Not my enemy, then?”

Before she could answer, Sarah and Hortense burst into the kitchen—both agitated. They had argued over the coffee—Sarah called it a waste, a “queer mess”; Hortense called it “a royal drink,” too fine for anyone who objected.

The others withdrew to the parlour. Before Hortense followed, Caroline whispered, “Not my enemy, Robert?”
And Robert, with quiet gravity, replied, “Could I be?”
Then he sat at the table, and pulled her beside him.
