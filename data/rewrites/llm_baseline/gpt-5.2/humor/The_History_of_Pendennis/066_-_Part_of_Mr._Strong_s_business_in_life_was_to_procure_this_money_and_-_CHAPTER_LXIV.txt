On a bit of well-behaved common near Tunbridge Wells—where the grass seems to have been trained in a genteel seminary—Lady Clavering discovered a villa exactly suited to a lady who has lately quarrelled with her husband and now desires Nature to take her part. Thither she retired after the unlucky season in London, dragging behind her, as inevitably as a snail its shell, Miss Amory; and Master Clavering came down for the holidays, bringing with him that tender spirit of domestic concord which shows itself, in boys of his years, by perpetual skirmishes with his sister.

Frank’s chief occupation at home was to fight Blanche; but this was merely an indoor game, and he liked his outdoor games better. Tunbridge offered cricket and horses and that choicest delicacy of boyhood—plenty of other boys to make a riot with. The Begum’s house presently swarmed with young gentlemen of thirteen, who came in gusty flocks, ate as if they were paid by the mouthful, and drank as if champagne were lemonade and lemonade a moral weakness. There were tarts—great shining, sticky, jammy tarts—vanishing in wedges; there were crusts shattered like battlements; there were buttery crumbs marched into carpets; there were bottles popped with a sound like a pistol-shot in a drawing-room—and all this juvenile feasting was garnished with lawn-races on horseback, cigars furtively nibbled and then heroically swallowed, and a general air of small men trying to be big men and only succeeding in being ill.

The dining-room, in consequence, became unbearable to Miss Blanche. She did not like the society of young gentlemen of thirteen; their talk was noisy, their laughter cracked, their forks clattered like ironmongery, and the very act of their eating seemed conducted with a species of percussion. To be sure Blanche could endure almost anything—if it was a change. For a week she would have liked poverty and a cottage and bread-and-cheese; and for a night she might have entertained a dungeon and bread-and-water; the point was not the fare, but the novelty. Tunbridge, therefore, was welcome. She wandered in woods and sketched trees and farmhouses; she read French novels as regularly as a person takes physic; she drove into Tunbridge Wells whenever there was the faintest promise of a play, a ball, a conjurer, or any musician likely to be applauded on credit. She slept a great deal. She quarrelled with Mamma and Frank before luncheon. She found the village school and attended it—first petting the girls and thwarting the mistress with smiles, then scolding the girls and laughing at the teacher with the same smiles sharpened. She was constant at church, of course.

It was a pretty little church—one of those ancient bijous which look as if they had been built in the reign of King Somebody, repaired in the reign of King Anybody, and redecorated last week by a committee. There were painted windows that threw red and blue patches on the prayer-books like spilled wine; carved saints’ heads peeped down as if listening; gilt texts shone with a hard metallic piety; and the pews were open, so that no one could hide from the eyes of neighbours or Heaven. Blanche began at once a most correct High-Church altar-cover, stitching little crosses with the patience of a seraph and the temper—well, with the temper of Blanche. For a while she passed for a saint with the clergyman, whom she took in with consummate art; coaxed him, wheedled him, fondled him—an entire liturgy of flattery—so that poor Mrs. Smirke, who had at first been charmed, then borne, then become chilly, at last grew almost mad with jealousy.

Mrs. Smirke was the wife of our old acquaintance Smirke—Pen’s former tutor and poor Helen’s former suitor. When Helen refused him, he had consoled himself (under maternal direction) with a young lady from Clapham. When his mamma died, his opinions, like his hair, began to grow. Every day they developed—his views in church matters, his locks down his back. He cut off his coat-collar as if it were a snare of the Evil One; he gave up the curl on his forehead (once as dear to him as doctrine); he gave up the neckcloth tie, and at last gave up any tie at all, as though the smallest knot were a compromise with Rome. On Fridays he went without dinner; he read the Roman Hours; he intimated, gently but distinctly, that he was ready to receive confessions in the vestry, as if the parish were full of secret Borgias. The most harmless creature alive, he was denounced as a black and dangerous Jesuit by Muffin of the Dissenting chapel and Mr. Simeon Knight at the old church—men who could smell Popery in a candlestick at twenty paces.

Mr. Smirke had built his chapel-of-ease with the money left him by his mother at Clapham. Lord! what would that excellent lady have said could she have heard a table called an altar!—seen candlesticks on it!—received letters dated on the Feast of Saint So-and-so or the Vigil of Saint What’s-his-name! Yet all these things the Clapham boy practised with conscientious simplicity, and his faithful wife followed him, step by step, as if holiness consisted chiefly in keeping pace.

But when Blanche held a conference of near two hours in the vestry with Mr. Smirke, Belinda paced up and down on the grass among the two little gravestones already there, wishing—oh, with what fervour—that there were a third, only so it need not be hers. She fancied her husband, in his long hair and loose collar, offered up—himself—to that creature who had infatuated him in a fortnight. She pictured the convent, the veil, the renunciation, and all the rest of the melodrama; and her neighbours, meanwhile, pictured Smirke in direct correspondence with the Bishop of Rome, and Belinda bewailing errors to her even more odious than death. Yet Smirke meant no earthly harm. The post never brought him any letters from the Pope. He thought Blanche, at first, the most pious, gifted, right-thinking, fascinating person he had ever met; her manner of singing the chants delighted him into open-mouthed rapture—tea-less and bread-and-butter-less, as inspiration commonly is.

He had heard that the opera was fascinating, but had only once attended any exhibition of that kind—an event he mentioned with a blush and a sigh, as though he had been caught in a tambourine. It was on the day he had accompanied Helen and her son to the play at Chatteris. He could not conceive anything more delicious—more celestial, he had almost said—than Miss Amory’s music. She had a precious soul; remarkable talents; to all outward seeming the most heavenly disposition—etcetera, etcetera. Such was the kind of talk with which, being at the height of his own fever and bewitchment, Smirke discoursed to Arthur Pendennis when Arthur came down to pass a few days at Tunbridge Wells with the Begum.

The meeting of the old acquaintances was cordial. Arthur loved anybody who loved his mother; and Smirke could speak on that theme with real feeling, in a tone at once kind and melancholy, with his eyelids lowered and his fair head bowed slightly as if to a perpetual hymn. They told each other a hundred things—those little biographies we hand round like family recipes, each man seasoning his own with what he can bear to taste. Smirke observed, with modest emphasis, that Arthur would perceive his—his views on Church matters had developed since their former acquaintance. Mrs. Smirke, exemplary in everything, seconded these views with all her endeavours, as if opinions were joints to be basted.

Arthur was immensely amused—at Smirke’s airs, follies, and simplicity; at the blank stock, the long hair; at the real goodness and friendliness that made the whole absurdity innocent. And Smirke’s praises of Blanche both pleased and surprised him, and made him regard her with eyes of particular favour.

The truth is, Blanche was very glad to see Arthur; as one is glad, in the country, to see a man who brings down the last London news like a hamper of delicate provisions. He could talk better than most country-folks; at least he could talk that darling London jargon—so dear to London people, so indispensable to them, and so little understood by persons “out of the world,” who have the misfortune to speak plainly.

The first day Pen came down he kept Blanche laughing for hours after dinner. She sang with redoubled spirit. She did not scold her mother; she fondled and kissed her, to the Begum’s honest surprise, as though affection were a new dish she had just learned to dress. When bedtime came she said “Déjà!” with the prettiest regret in the world, and squeezed Arthur’s hand quite fondly. Arthur, who was of that turn that eyes very moderately bright can dazzle, gave her little palm a cordial pressure in return.

Later, standing at his window, he looked out into the night and took his thoughts, as men do, with their smoke. The air had the clean coolness that makes a cigar taste more itself; the summer hush was broken only by far-off hoofs on a road and the soft, living stir of leaves—an honest soundscape, not the clanking, brazen, coach-and-cry racket of London. The little Saxon church lay in the moonlight, white and tidy, as if Heaven kept it under glass.

“She is very much improved,” thought Pen. “Very much. I suppose the Begum won’t mind my smoking with the window open. She’s a jolly good old woman, and Blanche is immensely improved. I liked her manner with her mother to-night. I liked her laughing way with that stupid young cub of a boy, whom they oughtn’t to allow to get tipsy. She sang those little verses very prettily; they were devilish pretty verses too, though I say it who shouldn’t say it.”

And he hummed the tune Blanche had put to some verses of his own.

“Ah! what a fine night! How jolly a cigar is at night! What a comfort it is to hear nothing but the world breathing, instead of London grinding its teeth! I wonder what old Warrington’s doing? Yes, she’s a dayvlish nice little thing, as my uncle says.”

“Oh, heavenly!” broke out a voice from a clematis-covered casement near by—a girl’s voice: the voice of the author of “Mes Larmes.”

Pen burst into a laugh, and leaned out. “Don’t tell about my smoking,” he said.

“Oh! go on! I adore it,” cried the lady of “Mes Larmes,” with the same lavish enthusiasm that made her distribute adjectives as if they were comfits. “Heavenly night! heavenly, heavenly moon! but I must shut my window, and not talk to you on account of les moeurs. How droll they are, les moeurs! Adieu.”

And Pen began to sing the goodnight to Don Basilio, as if the moon were an audience and the village hedges a row of boxes.

Next day they walked in the fields together, laughing and chattering—the gayest pair of friends. They talked about the days of their youth, and Blanche became prettily sentimental, as if she were tasting an old sauce and finding it agreeably sharp. They talked about Laura—dearest Laura. Blanche had loved her as a sister. Was she happy with that odd Lady Rockminster? Wouldn’t she come and stay with them at Tunbridge? Oh, what walks they would take together! what songs they would sing—the old, old songs! Laura’s voice was splendid. Did Arthur—she must call him Arthur—remember the songs they sang in the happy old days, now he was grown such a great man and had such a succès?

And the day after, enlivened by a ramble through woods to Penshurst, with a sight of that pleasant park and hall—where the rooks’ cawing and the distant thud of an axe in timber seemed to belong to the place as naturally as the scent of bruised fern—came that conversation with the curate which has been narrated, and which set Arthur thinking more and more.

“Is she all this perfection?” he asked himself. “Has she become serious and religious? Does she tend schools and visit the poor? Is she kind to her mother and brother? Yes, I am sure of that; I have seen her.”

And walking with his old tutor over the little parish, and going to see the school, it was with inexpressible delight that Pen found Blanche seated instructing the children—her voice lowered into an industrious softness, her fingers busy with slates and spelling-books; and he fancied how patient she must be, how good-natured, how ingenuous, how really simple in her tastes, and unspoiled by the world.

“And do you really like the country?” he asked, as they walked together.

“I should like never to see that odious city again,” Blanche cried. “O Arthur—that is, Mr.—well, Arthur then—one’s good thoughts grow up in these sweet woods and calm solitudes, like those flowers which won’t bloom in London, you know. The gardener comes and changes our balconies once a week. I don’t think I shall bear to look London in the face again—its odious, smoky, brazen face! But—heigho!”

“Why that sigh, Blanche?”

“Never mind why.”

“Yes, I do mind why. Tell me—tell me everything.”

“I wish you hadn’t come down;” and out came a second edition of “Mes Soupirs,” freshly printed and very damp.

“You don’t want me, Blanche?”

“I don’t want you to go away. I don’t think this house will be very happy without you, and that’s why I wish you never had come.”

Here “Mes Soupirs” were laid aside and “Mes Larmes” began in good earnest. Ah! what answer is given to those in the eyes of a young woman? What is the method employed for drying them? What took place?

O ringdoves and roses, O dews and wildflowers, O waving greenwoods and balmy airs of summer! Here were two battered London rakes, for a moment taking themselves in, and fancying they were in love with each other like Phyllis and Corydon. When one thinks of country houses and country walks, and the way a quiet field can muffle the world’s harsh noises until a sigh sounds like fate, one wonders that any man is left unmarried.
