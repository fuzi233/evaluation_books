The Wife of Dives

Rain lashed against the windowpane of Mrs. Manderson's sitting room at White Gables, turning the garden outside into a blurred wash of grey and green. It was a rare break in the June gloom, yet the atmosphere inside was heavier than the storm. The landscape beyond was a sullen expanse of mist drifting from the sea, the sky a dead, unbroken slate shedding moisture that tapped against the glass like a persistent, despairing insect. Mrs. Manderson stood before the glass, her face a mask of sorrow, staring at a world that offered no comfort to a woman bereaved, isolated, and stripped of purpose.

A knock shattered the silence. She straightened, a reflex born of the sudden realization that the crushing weight of the world had momentarily overwhelmed her spirit. "Come in," she called.

Her maid announced that Mr. Trent had arrived, apologizing for the early hour but insisting on a matter of urgent importance. Mrs. Manderson nodded, her resolve hardening. She walked to the mirror, studying the olive-toned face reflected back at her, shaking her head at the grimace that threatened to form, before turning to greet her visitor.

Trent entered, and she noted immediately that the man she knew was gone. In his place stood a figure drained of sleep, his usual fixed good humor replaced by a reserved, unreadable tension that sent a chill through her.

"May I come to the point at once?" he asked, taking her hand. "I have a train to catch at Bishopsbridge at noon, but I cannot leave until I settle this. It concerns you alone, Mrs. Manderson. I have worked half the night and thought the rest, and I know what I must do."

"You look wretchedly tired," she said, her kindness a shield against the storm in his eyes. "Won't you sit? This chair is restful. Of course, it is about this terrible business and your work as a correspondent. Ask me anything you think I can properly answer. I trust you won't make my situation worse than duty requires. If you say you must see me, I know it is because you ought to."

"Mrs. Manderson," Trent said, measuring his words with deliberate care, "I promise I won't make it worse than I can help. But I am bound to make it bad for you—though, between ourselves, I hope only in private. Whether you can tell me what I ask is up to you, but on my word of honor, I will ask only enough to decide whether to publish or suppress certain grave discoveries regarding your husband's death. Things unknown to anyone else, and unlikely to be suspected. What I have found—what I believe I have practically proven—will shock you regardless. But it could be worse. If you give me reason to believe publication would destroy you further, I will burn this manuscript," he laid a long envelope on the small table beside him, "and nothing of it will ever see print. It contains a private note to my editor and a dispatch for the *Record*."

He paused, his gaze steady. "You may refuse to speak. If you do, my duty compels me to take this to London today and leave it to my editor's discretion. I am not entitled to suppress it merely on a hunch. But if I gather from you—and no one else—that there is substance to the danger I fear, then as a gentleman and as one who wishes you well, I will not publish. I decline to assist the police in some directions. Do you follow?"

"I understand perfectly," Mrs. Manderson whispered. She drew a deep breath. "I don't know what dreadful thing you've found, but it was honorable of you to come to me. Will you please tell me?"

"I cannot," Trent replied firmly. "The secret belongs to my newspaper unless it is yours. If it is yours, you may read and destroy my manuscript. Believe me, I detest such mystery-making from the bottom of my soul; but I did not create this enigma. This is the most painful hour of my life, and you make it harder by refusing to treat me like a hound. The first thing I ask is this: Is it true, as you stated at the inquest, that you had no idea why your late husband changed his attitude toward you, becoming mistrustful and reserved in his final months?"

Mrs. Manderson's dark brows lifted, her eyes flaring with sudden fire. She rose quickly. Trent stood up simultaneously, retrieving his envelope, signaling the end of the interview. But she raised a hand, color rushing to her cheeks, her voice quickening. "Do you know what you ask, Mr. Trent? You ask if I perjured myself."

"I do," he answered, unmoved. "You knew already I had not come to preserve polite fictions, Mrs. Manderson. The notion that no reputable person could withhold the truth under oath is a fiction itself."

He waited, but she was silent. She walked to the window, and he watched miserably as her shoulders moved slightly before settling. Turning her face away to the dismal weather, she finally spoke.

"Mr. Trent," she said, "people trust you. I feel that things I wish to keep hidden are safe with you. I know you have a serious reason for this, though I do not know what it is. Perhaps telling you the truth would assist justice. To understand it, you must know about my marriage. Many could tell you as well as I that it was not... a successful union. I was only twenty. I admired his force, his courage, his certainty; he was the only strong man I had ever known. But I soon learned he cared more for his business than for me. I think I realized even sooner that I had deceived myself, blinding myself with impossible promises because I was dazzled by the prospect of spending money beyond the dreams of any English girl. I have despised myself for that for five years."

She paused, her voice trembling slightly. "My husband's feelings for me... I cannot speak of that. But alongside that, he believed I was destined for a great place in society. He thought I would throw myself into it with enjoyment, become a personage, and bring him credit. That was his idea, and it remained even as other delusions faded. I was a part of his ambition. His bitter disappointment was that I failed him socially. I think he was too shrewd not to know the risk of marrying a girl brought up for music and books, unpractical and self-indulgent, yet he reckoned on me to do the honors of his position. I found I could not."

Mrs. Manderson had spoken herself into a fervor unseen before. Her words flowed freely, her voice ringing with a natural expressiveness dulled by recent shock and restraint. She turned swiftly from the window, facing him now, her beautiful face flushed, her eyes gleaming, her hands gesturing emphatically as she surrendered to the impulse to release pent-up truths.

"The people!" she exclaimed. "Can you imagine what it is to step from a world of creative work, of dignity and ideals, into a world where one must be shamefully rich to exist? Where money is the only currency, where the millionaires are jaded and sport is their only refuge, and the idle are duller and vicerous than the workers? And the women? They live for display, silly amusements, and immorality. Do you know how awful that life is? There are clever people, certainly, but they are swamped and spoiled. It is all empty, empty! Oh, I suppose I exaggerate, and I did make friends and have happy times; but that is how I feel now. The seasons in New York and London, the house-parties, the yacht cruises—all the same people, all the same emptiness."

"And you see," she continued, her voice rising, "my husband could not have an idea of all this. His life was never empty. He lived in business plans and difficulties. He had no suspicion of my inner turmoil, and I never let him know; it would not have been fair. I felt I must do something to justify myself as his wife, sharing his fortune and position. The only way was to try, and try, to live up to his idea of my social qualities. I acted my best. It became harder year by year. I was never a popular hostess; how could I be? I was a failure, yet I persisted. I stole holidays now and then. I felt I was breaking a bargain when I took an old school friend, who couldn't afford to travel, to Italy for a month, or when I stayed in London with quiet people who knew me from childhood. We lived as in the old days, worrying about theater seats and cheap dressmakers. Those were my best times, my escapes. But I felt my husband would hate to know how much I enjoyed returning to that old life."

"In the end, despite everything, he came to know. He could see through anything once his attention was turned to it. He had always seen that I was not fulfilling his vision of a social figure, and perhaps he thought it my misfortune rather than my fault. But the moment he realized I wasn't playing my part with spirit, he knew the whole story. He divined how I loathed the luxury and the brilliance simply because of the people who surrounded them. It happened last year. I don't know how or when. Perhaps some woman suggested it to him—they all understood, of course. He said nothing to me, and I think he tried not to change his manner at first, but such things hurt. It worked in both of us. I knew he knew. Eventually, we were merely polite and considerate. Before he found me out, we had been on a footing of intelligent companionship; we talked freely about things we agreed or disagreed upon without it going deep. Then that ended. I felt the foundation of our coexistence slipping away. At last, it was gone."

"It had been like that," she concluded simply, "for months before he died."

She sank into the corner of a sofa by the window, relaxing her body as if after a great exertion. Silence fell between them. Trent hastily sorted through a tangle of impressions, amazed by the frankness of her story and the vigorous expressiveness of her delivery. In this vivid being, carried away by an impulse to speak, he saw the real woman in a state of activity, unlike the pale, self-disciplined creature of majesty she presented to the world. With this amazement came a terror of her dark beauty, kindled by excitement into an appearance scarcely mortal. Incongruously, a knot of ideas rushed into his mind: she was unique not because of her beauty, but because it was united with an intensity of nature. In England, the very beautiful were placid, and the fiery women seemed to have burned up their beauty. No beautiful woman had ever cast this spell on him before. When it came to wit, he had preferred the brighter flame to the duller, without much regard for the lamp. "All this is very disputable," his reason argued, but instinct answered, "Yes, except that I am under a spell"; and a deeper instinct cried out, "Away with it!" He forced his mind back to her story, finding growing within him an irrepressible conviction. It was all very fine, but it would not do.

"I feel as if I have led you into saying more than you meant, or than I wanted to learn," he said slowly. "But there is one brutal question which is the whole point of my inquiry." He braced his frame as if preparing for a plunge into cold water. "Mrs. Manderson, will you assure me that your husband's change toward you had nothing to do with John Marlowe?"

And what he dreaded came. "Oh!" she cried, a sound of pure anguish, her face thrown up, open hands stretched out as if for pity. Then the hands covered her burning face, and she flung herself aside among the cushions at her elbow. He saw nothing but her heavy crown of black hair, her body moving with sobs that stabbed his heart, and a foot turned inward gracelessly in an abandonment of misery. Like a tall tower suddenly breaking apart, she had fallen in ruins, weeping helplessly.

Trent stood up, his face white and calm. With a senseless particularity, he placed his envelope exactly in the center of the little polished table. He walked to the door, closing it noiselessly as he went out. Within minutes, he was tramping through the rain, out of sight of White Gables, going nowhere, seeing nothing, his soul shaken by the fierce effort to kill and trample the raving impulse that had seized him in the presence of her shame. That impulse clamored to drag himself before her feet, to pray for pardon, to pour out words he knew not what, but knew were straining at his lips—to wreck his self-respect forever, and hopelessly defeat even the crazy purpose that had almost possessed him, by drowning her wretchedness in disgust, by babbling with the tongue of infatuation to a woman with a husband not yet buried, to a woman who loved another man.

Such was the magic of her tears, quickening in a moment the thing which, as his heart had known, he must not let come to life. For Philip Trent was a young man, younger in nature even than his years, and a way of life that kept his edge keen and his spirit volcanic had prepared him very ill for the meeting that comes once in the early manhood of most of us, usually—as in his case, he told himself harshly—to no purpose but the testing of virtue and the power of the will.
