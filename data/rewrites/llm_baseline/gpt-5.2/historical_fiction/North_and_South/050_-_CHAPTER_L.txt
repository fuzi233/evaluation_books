All the while, Milton went on as it had always gone on—up and down, up and down—only now the fall seemed longer, the lift harder bought.

The chimneys wrote their sooty scripture across a low, wintry sky. The mills’ breath came in harsh gusts; steam hissed, iron clashed, belts slapped and worried at their wheels. The town’s very air tasted of coal-dust and damp cotton-fibre; it furred the tongue, it dried the throat, it clung to hair and sleeve as if Milton would mark a man for its own. And under that roof of noise—loom-thunder, engine-beat, the shriek of some ungreased axle crying for mercy—men moved with faces tightened into one question.

In the streets there were no saunterers. Even the costermongers, usually ready with a jest and a bell-like cry, pitched their voices lower, as if the town itself required quiet to listen for news. In the Mart, in the Exchange, talk did not bloom and meander; it snapped and struck like flint. Credit was thin as skim-milk; one splash of bad tidings and it curdled. A failure in a great shipping-house far away could be heard here as plainly as a crack in ice heard across a pond: one fissure, and all the frozen surface began to web with danger.

“Hitherto none in Milton,” men said, as if naming a charm; and yet they spoke with that caution that makes a rumour the more terrifying—dwelling on who was safe, and leaving who was not to be understood in the pause between words. Idle breath could topple a house in such weather; one firm going down dragged others after it, as a sinking cart draws the traces and the load.

“Thornton is safe,” they repeated—some with conviction, some with envy. His business had been large, and he had grown it, season by season, with a boldness that looked like prudence because it had been fortunate.

But there was always someone to stoop closer, to turn his head so his words fell into an ear, not the room.

“Large, ay,” murmured Mr. Harrison, who had inherited his father’s trade-made money and lived as if change were a sin. “But he’s spent his profits in stretching it. He’s no capital laid by. His machinery’s new within two years, and cost him—well!—a word to the wise.”

And folk called Harrison a croaker and went their way, because a croaker is useful only when he is wrong.

Yet John Thornton was hard pressed.

What touched him most sharply was not the mere ache of figures—though they ached like toothache, always returning—but the bruise upon that pride which had been the spine of him. He had built himself, stone by stone, out of poverty and discipline, out of a mother’s stern affection and a boy’s early knowledge that the world does not nurse the weak. Commerce, to him, had once seemed a grand, clean engine: put in courage, honesty, endurance, and it yielded power; a man’s name, spoken in distant ports where his face would never be known, could pass like good coin. He had begun with the hunger to be such a name—printed, repeated, trusted—head of a firm that would run on into generations.

His mother had fed that hunger with Scripture spoken like a trumpet: “Her merchants be like princes.” It had sounded to her like a promise addressed to her boy.

For years he had been alive to the far-off and dead to the near. He had desired influence abroad, and had scarcely perceived, close at hand, the lives that moved beside his own—parallel, near enough to touch, yet never touching—until, by what looked like accident, Higgins had come into his path, not at first as workman and master, but as man and man. That was the fine point of a wedge driven into an old timber. Once you hear another human heart beating close to your own, you cannot go back to imagining the world is made of classes and contracts only.

Lately, and too late, Thornton had felt something new: not merely the pride of a manufacturer, but an interest in his very place in Milton because it brought him into contact—direct, daily—with people sharp, stubborn, ignorant sometimes, but vivid with character and feeling. He had begun to plan—small experiments, tender things to his mind, the kind of changes that do not show in a ledger at once, but might alter the air between men. And now the dread of losing that—of having those plans cut off like green shoots by a late frost—gave a keener edge to his fear.

For the frost had come.

The strike a year and a half ago—long enough ago that it ought to have been folded away as the past, and yet it lived still, like a debt of time—had stopped his work in the middle of large contracts. He had locked capital into new machinery, bright steel and cunning gearing that had eaten money as a hungry horse eats oats. He had bought cotton heavily to fulfil orders taken under bond. When labour failed him, he had brought in Irish hands; but their want of skill had spoiled much work, damaging goods that his house could not, for pride’s sake, send out as first-rate.

For months the strike had been a stone in his shoe. When his glance fell on Higgins he sometimes felt a quick flare of resentment rise, hot and useless, without any present cause, merely because Higgins’s face called up the remembered injury. Thornton, ashamed of that, had curbed himself. It was not enough to avoid the man; he must prove mastery over himself by admitting him—by letting Higgins come when rules allowed, as if to say, I will not be ruled by a grudge.

And gradually resentment fell away, replaced by a kind of astonishment: how could two men—both living by the same trade, both labouring toward the same object—see duty and right in such utterly different lights? Out of that wonder had grown a rough intercourse that did not promise the end of all conflict, but promised something better: charity in the midst of conflict, patience where there had been hard contempt.

They had learned facts from each other too—the sort of facts no one tells you until you care.

Then trade turned, as it turns, with a cruel quietness.

The market fell and the value of Thornton’s stocks fell with it to near half. Orders dried up. The new machinery stood, not earning interest but demanding it; money went out in wages and coal and oil while money did not come in. Payments for completed orders were slow, evasive; he had to press, and hated pressing. Bills fell due for the cotton he had bought when the world looked steadier. Money was scarce, and what could be borrowed came at an interest like a bite.

He could not realise property; he could not sell without losing; he could not hold without sinking. Yet he did not despair. He watched, calculated, foresaw, provided—day and night, as if he could outwork misfortune. At home he was as gentle as ever with the women—his mother, his sister—keeping his voice even, his manners unaltered, as if manners could keep a roof from leaking. In the mill he spoke few words; but the men knew him now. A curt command did not sting as it once would have stung; they heard behind it the press of care, and even sympathy began to grow where antagonism had smouldered.

One day Higgins heard a sharp inquiry—why had such an order not been obeyed?—and, passing near, caught the sound of a sigh, held down and yet unmistakable, as if the master’s breath had to push through iron.

“Th’ measter’s a deal to potter him,” Higgins muttered.

That night he and another man stayed beyond hours, unknown to anyone, to finish the neglected piece of work. Thornton never knew; he thought the overlooker had done it. The mill’s noise swallowed gratitude before it could be spoken; but there are kindnesses that do not need a name.

Another day Higgins, walking along Marlborough Street, saw Thornton coming in his quick, resolved stride, eyes set as if on figures no one else could see.

“Eh,” Higgins thought, “I reckon I know who’d ha’ been sorry for to see our measter sittin’ so like a piece o’ grey calico! Th’ oud parson would ha’ fretted his woman’s heart out, if he’d seen the woeful looks I’ve seen.”

He stopped Thornton with the blunt courage of one man to another.

“Measter.”

Thornton started, annoyed at being pulled from inward work, and looked up sharply.

“Have yo’ heerd aught of Miss Marget lately?”

“Miss—who?” Thornton asked; the question was almost harsh from habit.

“Miss Marget—Miss Hale—th’ oud parson’s daughter—yo’ known who I mean well enough—if yo’ll only think a bit—”

There was no insolence in the dialect; it was simply Higgins’s way of pushing through fog.

“Oh—yes!” Thornton’s face changed as if a door had opened and a warmer air had come in. The frost-bound look of care loosened; though his mouth stayed firm, his eyes softened and smiled out, unguarded, on the questioner.

“She’s my landlord now, you know, Higgins. I hear of her through her agent here, every now and then. She’s well—and among friends—thank you, Higgins.”

That “thank you” lingered with a heat that surprised even Higgins. It lit something in him—a will-o’-the-wisp of understanding, perhaps, but bright enough to follow.

“And she’s not getten married, measter?”

“Not yet.” The cloud returned as quickly as it had lifted. “There is some talk of it, as I understand, with a connection of the family.”

“Then she’ll not be for coming to Milton again, I reckon.”

“No,” said Thornton, and the single syllable had in it the sound of a door shutting.

“Stop a minute, measter.” Higgins stepped closer, dropping his voice into a confidential undertone. “Is th’ young gentleman cleared?” He added a knowing wink, which only made the matter more puzzling to Thornton.

“The young gentleman? What young gentleman?”

“Th’ young gentleman—I mean—Master Frederick, they ca’ed him—her brother as was over here, yo’ know.”

“Over here?” Thornton repeated, as if the phrase itself were foreign.

“Ay, to be sure—at th’ missus’s death. Yo’ need na be feared o’ my telling; for Mary and me, we knowed it all along, only we held our peace, for we got it through Mary workin’ i’ th’ house.”

Thornton’s heart gave a strange leap; his whole attention came, quick as a knife, to that one point.

“And he was over. It was her brother.”

“Sure enough. And I reckoned yo’ knowed it, or I’d never ha’ let on. Yo’ knowed she had a brother?”

“Yes,” Thornton said; his voice was controlled, but his face had a tense pallor under it. “I know all about him. And he was over at Mrs. Hale’s death?”

“Nay! I’m not goin’ for to tell more,” Higgins replied, suddenly cautious. “I’ve maybe getten them into mischief already, for they kept it very close. I nobbut wanted to know if they’d getten him cleared?”

“Not that I know of.” Thornton drew back into reserve, the old wall rising. “I know nothing. I only hear of Miss Hale now, as my landlord, and through her lawyer.”

With that he turned away—back to the business that had been driving him when Higgins first called—leaving Higgins baffled, the bright thread of his discovery snapping in his fingers.

But within Thornton the words kept sounding, steady as a footstep: It was her brother.

“I am glad,” he said to himself, alone in his own mind. “I may never see her again; but it is a comfort—a relief—to know that much. I knew she could not be unmaidenly; and yet I yearned for conviction. Now I am glad.”

It was a little golden thread laid across a dark cloth. He held it in thought as one might hold, in a hungry season, the memory of a dish that once warmed the house: not merely the taste, but the clatter of plates, the murmur of voices, the peace of familiar sounds. In Milton now there was little peace in any sound. The mills’ roar pressed into the brain; even when a man lay down, the engines seemed still to beat in his blood.

And the web darkened.

His agent had trusted largely a house engaged in American trade. That house went down, and others followed, tumbling like a pack of cards: the fall of one compelling the fall of the next. Each morning brought new names, new gaps, new tightening of lips. Thornton sat late—night after night—with books and papers spread before him in his private room, adding, subtracting, reckoning, as if the act of calculation might bend fate. He thought no one knew. He heard the household go to bed; he heard the house settle; he heard the wind probe at shutters and eaves; and still he sat, staring at figures until the figures blurred and seemed to swim like dark fish in a grey stream.

One morning, when daylight stole in thin and cold through the crevices of his shutters, he realised he had not been in bed at all. A hopeless indifference came over him: he could do without an hour’s rest. There would be no real rest anywhere.

Then the door opened.

His mother stood there, dressed as she had been the day before. She too had never lain down. Her face was cold, rigid, wan from watching—like a statue cut out of grief.

“Mother! why are you not in bed?”

“Son John,” she said, “do you think I can sleep with an easy mind while you keep awake full of care? You have not told me what your trouble is; but sore trouble you have had these many days past.”

“Trade is bad.”

“And you dread—” Her steady voice trembled in an unwonted way.

“I dread nothing,” he replied, drawing his head up erect, as if the very posture might ward off humiliation. “I know that no man will suffer by me. That was my anxiety.”

“But how do you stand? Shall you—will it be a failure?”

“Not a failure. I must give up business, but I pay all men. I might redeem myself—I am sorely tempted—”

“How?” she cried, advancing; her hand closed on his arm. Her eyes had an eager, almost fierce light. She held her breath to catch his answer. “How redeem it?”

“By a speculation offered to me—full of risk; but, if successful, placing me high above water-mark, so that no one need ever know the strait I am in. Still, if it fails—”

“And if it fails,” she said, leaning nearer, voice hushed with intensity.

“Honest men are ruined by a rogue,” he answered gloomily. “As I stand now, my creditors’ money is safe—every farthing of it; but I don’t know where to find my own—it may be all gone, and I penniless at this moment. Therefore it is my creditors’ money that I should risk.”

“But if it succeeded, they need never know,” she urged. “Is it so desperate a speculation? I am sure it is not, or you would never have thought of it. If it succeeded—”

“I should be a rich man,” he said, and there was iron in the words, “and my peace of conscience would be gone.”

“Why! You would have injured no one.”

“No; but I should have run the risk of ruining many for my own paltry aggrandisement.” He paused; then the decision, long shaped in him, came out with a strange gentleness. “Mother, I have decided. You won’t much grieve over our leaving this house, shall you, dear mother?”

“No!” she cried. “But to have you other than what you are will break my heart. What can you do?”

“Be always the same John Thornton in whatever circumstances,” he answered, and his voice for once let the tiredness show. “Endeavouring to do right, and making great blunders; and then trying to be brave in setting to afresh. But it is hard, mother. I have worked and planned. I have discovered new powers in my situation too late—and now all is over. I am too old to begin again with the same heart. It is hard, mother.”

He turned away and covered his face with his hands, as if to shut out dawn, shame, the whole world.

She stood, rigid with love and anger—anger not at him, but at the shape of things.

“I can’t think,” she said, the defiance in her tone like a clash of metal, “how it comes about. Here is my boy—good son, just man, tender heart—and he fails in all he sets his mind upon. He finds a woman to love, and she cares no more for his affection than if he had been a common man. He labours, and his labour comes to nought. Other people grow rich, and hold their paltry names high and dry above shame.”

“Shame never touched me,” he said low; but she went on as if she could not stop.

“I sometimes have wondered where justice was gone to, and now I don’t believe there is such a thing in the world—now you are come to this; you, my own John Thornton—though you and I may be beggars together—my own dear son!”

She fell upon his neck and kissed him through her tears. Her tears were hot and salt against his cheek; he held her gently, as if she were suddenly older than he had ever allowed himself to see.

“Mother,” he said, when she had wept herself into a quieter breath, “who has sent me my lot in life, both of good and evil?”

She shook her head. She would have nothing to do with religion just then; grief had made her proud again.

“Mother,” he persisted, and his voice had the strange simplicity of a boy asking for the old story he once loved, “I too have been rebellious; but I’m striving to be so no longer. Help me, as you helped me when I was a child. Then you said many good words—when my father died, and we were sometimes sorely short of comforts—which we shall never be now; you said brave, noble, trustful words then, mother, which I have never forgotten, though they may have lain dormant. Speak to me again in the old way, mother. Do not let us have to think that the world has too much hardened our hearts.”

“I have had a many,” she sobbed, “but none so sore as this. To see you cast down from your rightful place! I could say it for myself, John, but not for you. Not for you! God has seen fit to be very hard on you, very.”

Her old body shook with the convulsive sobs that come when age weeps—when tears are no longer a quick shower but a storm that threatens to break the heart’s roof. At last the stillness in the room struck her. She looked and saw her son collapsed by the table, his arms flung half across it, his head bent downwards.

“Oh, John!” she cried, and lifted his face.

The pallor on it was so strange, the gloom so set, that for an instant she thought death had come quietly to claim him. Then the rigidity melted; colour returned; his eyelids stirred; and he was himself again. In that moment, all mortification—houses, names, rank, money—fell away like husks. The great blessing was that he existed; that he was hers.

She thanked God for that, and for that alone, with a fervour that washed rebellion out of her like soot from a stone.

He did not speak much after. He went and opened the shutters, letting in the ruddy flood of dawn. It showed the room as it was—papers, ink, the hard corners of furniture; it showed, too, the thin grey outside and the east wind cutting like a knife. The cold had lasted for weeks. No one would buy light summer goods in such weather. Even hope of a quick revival of trade must be given up; the year itself seemed against them.

Yet there was comfort—stern comfort—in having spoken. They might keep silence henceforward about anxieties, but they understood each other now; if not in harmony, at least in one shared discord against misfortune.

Fanny’s husband was vexed at Thornton’s refusal to take a share in the speculative venture he had offered—vexed as men are who cannot forgive another for being honest in a way that reflects on themselves. He withdrew from any possibility of being supposed able to assist Thornton with ready money—money he indeed needed for his own venture.

And at last the thing Thornton had dreaded, and prepared for, came like the closing of a gate.

He must give up the business in which he had been so long engaged with honour and success. Marlborough Mills and the adjacent dwelling were held under a long lease; they must, if possible, be relet. He must look out for a subordinate situation—a word that tasted bitter, like burnt crust, in his mouth.

There were offers almost at once. Mr. Hamper would have been only too glad to secure Thornton as steady, experienced partner for his son, whom he was setting up with a large capital in a neighbouring town. But the young man was half-educated in information and wholly uneducated in any responsibility beyond getting money—brutal in pleasures and brutal in pains. Thornton saw at once that such a partnership would strangle what few plans survived the wreck—those plans not of profit but of governance, of better relation between master and man. He would sooner be only a manager, with a certain degree of power beyond mere money-getting, than share authority with a tyrannical moneyed partner whose humours would breed quarrel within months.

So he waited. He stood aside—profoundly humble now—as news swept through the Exchange of the enormous fortune his brother-in-law had made by his daring speculation. It ran through Milton for nine days like a tune everyone whistles, half admiring, half resentful. Success brought its worldly consequence: extreme admiration. Men who had sneered yesterday called the speculator wise and far-seeing today.

No one was considered so wise, so far-seeing, as Mr. Watson.
