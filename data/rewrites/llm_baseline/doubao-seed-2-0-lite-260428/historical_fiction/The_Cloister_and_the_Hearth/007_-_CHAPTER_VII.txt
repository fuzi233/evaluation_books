One bright spring morning in Tergou, the cobblestones glinted under a parade of sights the townsfolk rarely saw: rich velvet liveries, plumes of exotic bobbing over riding helmets, and horse hooves striking sparks as the French ambassador rode through the streets bound for a hunt in the neighbouring royal forest. Every window and balcony was crammed with curious faces: old women set their spindles aside, children scrambled atop railings, and even the most dour blacksmith paused mid-blow at his anvil to gawk. Alongside the ambassador’s own retinue, the Duke of Burgundy had lent a cohort of his most peculiar household staff to honour the guest and amuse him.

First in the procession rode the Duke’s tumbler, sitting his horse with such absurdly grave, almost regal dignity that all the actual noble lords beside him seemed frivolous and flighty by comparison. But the second the crowd’s awe threatened to turn uncomfortable, he rolled clean off his mount in a silly, undignified somersault that even the stony-faced ambassador burst out laughing at. He then proceeded to scramble back up over the horse’s tail in a further fit of buffoonery, and the crowd roared.

Far more eyes turned to the figure riding further back: the Duke’s own hunting leopard. A giant huntsman, mounted on a Flemish dray-horse so large his boots nearly brushed the ground, had a long wooden box strapped to his saddle, and on it crouched the sleek, spotted beast, chained fast to the man’s wrist. Villagers pressed close to marvel at her glossy pelt, more than one bold soul reaching out to stroke her, even pluck at her tail, until the huntsman bellowed so loud the crowd jumped back: “Beware! In Antwerp last month a fool threw a handful of dust at her, and the Duke turned him to dust for it. Locked him in a sunken cell where the rats stripped his bones bare in a single night. Serves any man right who molests the poor creature.” A murmur of fear rippled through the onlookers, and no one dared tease their sovereign’s leopard after that.

Bringing up the very rear was the Duke’s Hungarian giant, seven feet four inches tall, with a soft, fluting treble voice so small it was absurd coming from such a mountain of flesh. Vain and completely unaware of his ridiculous speaking voice, he spotted little Giles perched right on the top rail of a front balcony, and called out in that high squeak: “Hallo, brother! I almost rode past without seeing thee!”
“You’re big enough for a blind mole to spot,” Giles boomed back in his deep, rumbling bass.
“Come up on my shoulder, little brother,” the giant piped up, holding out a fist the size of a leg of mutton to help him down.
“If I do I’ll box your ears so hard you’ll hear bells in Brussels for a month,” roared the dwarf.

The crowd shrieked with laughter, not at the giant’s terrible jokes, but at the sublime absurdity of the pair: the giant, all bulk, emitting a tiny squeak like a sparrow, and the tiny dwarf, all fire, spitting thunder like a storm cloud. The giant mistook the mirth for approval, and teased Giles further, until the little man snapped entirely. Fearless, and blazing with rage, he leaped clean off the balcony right onto the giant’s neck, one hand fisting his hair, the other pummeling his skull as fast and hard as he could. The giant started laughing at first, but the force of the blows quickly sobered him.
“Hey! Stop! Help! Holy saints, the imp will knock my brains out! I’ll dash his skull against that balcony–” he charged backwards full tilt at the stone balustrade. Giles saw the crack coming, grabbed the balcony top with both hands, and flung himself over the edge just as the giant’s head slammed into the stone with a resounding, echoing crack. The crowd cheered so loud the windowpanes rattled. Furious, the giant grabbed two random bystanders, knocked their heads together like wooden dumbbells, and flung them flat on the cobblestones. Catherine shrieked and yanked her apron over Giles to hide him, and the giant, nursing his throbbing head, stomped off to catch up with the rest of the hunting party.

The incident had unforeseen soft consequences: overnight the whole town was proud of their plucky little Giles, and everyone happily gave him the scraps of parchment he begged for to trade to his brother Gerard for copper pennies, running his tiny side trade smoothly for weeks.

Later that same afternoon, Catherine and her daughter Kate sat gossiping by the hearth, their favourite topic of conversation the bright future ahead for their family, now that Gerard was set to take the prestigious benefice the Countess Charolois herself had promised him. They thrilled at the thought of having a holy priest in their own home: he would officiate Cornelis and Sybrandt’s weddings, hear their confessions instead of a stranger priest, anoint them with sacred oil, and close their eyes when their time came. Kate shivered and begged her mother not to speak of dying any time soon; her chronic leg pain was gone that day, she felt light as air, and she had a secret to show her, something beautiful Gerard had been hiding to surprise them. She hobbled over on her crutches, fetched a cloth-wrapped wooden panel, and unwrapped it to reveal a stunning painting of the Virgin, a jeweled tiara sitting atop great cascades of burnished red-gold hair spilling loose over her shoulders, her eyes lifted soft and serene to heaven. Catherine crossed herself in awe, swearing it looked like the Queen of Heaven had stepped right onto the wood.

Before they had finished exclaiming over it, the cottage door creaked open. In stepped Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, the burgomaster, who had not set foot in their home for ten years, since the old land dispute that pitted his interests against Eli’s. The two women stared, stupefied. He claimed to have come to atone for that old unfriendly visit, for their sake, not his own, and that his news concerned their son Gerard. Catherine assumed he wanted Gerard to paint for the town for free, but he shook his head and dropped his bombshell: Gerard was falling into bad hands, spending every waking hour with Margaret Brandt, Peter the magician’s red-haired daughter, so deep in love he would never take holy orders if they did not part the two soon.

Catherine and Kate burst out laughing at first, certain he was lying, that their pious Gerard would never look at a woman that way. But doubt crept over Catherine’s face; she remembered how foolish and headstrong she had been in her own youth. Indignant, Kate held the painted Virgin high, declaring that if Gerard’s heart was tangled up in earthly love, he could never have rendered the Holy Mother with such pure, unworldly grace.

Ghysbrecht gaped at the panel, his eyes going wide. “That is no Virgin!” he gasped. “That is Margaret Brandt! Every painter paints his sweetheart’s face that beautifully, man has done since paint was invented.” The women reeled in horror, arguing that the figure wore a crown, she could not be a mortal girl. The burgomaster snorted: “Crown? Look at that bright red hair! The Holy Virgin would never be painted with red hair, that is Margaret, sure as the sun rises.”

Just then their neighbour Peter Buyskens poked his head in the door, saying the whole street could hear them shouting, and asked what the ruckus was about. Ghysbrecht dragged him over to the panel and demanded he name the woman in the painting. Peter squinted, grinned, and said it was Margaret Brandt, as natural as life, though he had never seen her wearing a crown before. Catherine and Kate immediately flung their aprons over their faces, rocking back and forth in silent, heartbroken sobs.

Eli walked in from his shop moments later, and went white as a sheet, convinced someone was dead. When the story was laid out before him, he huffed in relief at first, saying boys will be boys, he would grow out of the silly infatuation. But when Ghysbrecht pointed out the two youngsters were clearly already talking marriage, his face hardened. “Marriage? That shall never be,” he swore. “I will stop it, even by force if need be, as I would stop his hand raised to cut his own throat. I will do what old John Koestein did not long ago.” The burgomaster filled in the rest: Koestein had made him lock his son Albert in the Stadthouse prison for forty-eight hours, on bread and water, until the boy forgot all about his girl and begged to see the sun again. Catherine sighed at the cruelty, but Eli insisted a father had every right by law to keep his son from throwing his whole life away.

Supper passed in gloomy silence. Evening fell, eight o'clock struck, and Gerard still had not come home. Eli sent everyone else to bed, took Catherine’s hand, and said they would walk out to the road to Sevenbergen to meet him, talk this through that very night, no putting it off till tomorrow. The night was clear and balmy, the moon bright over the fields, and the old couple walked hand in hand – the custom of linking elbows while walking would not reach Europe for centuries yet – reminiscing about their own courting days, and how happy they had been together through every hardship, the old proverb proving true: shared joy is doubled, shared trouble is halved.

And then around the curve of the road, they saw him. Gerard was walking home, singing soft under his breath in the moonlight, his face glowing bright, like he trod on air.
