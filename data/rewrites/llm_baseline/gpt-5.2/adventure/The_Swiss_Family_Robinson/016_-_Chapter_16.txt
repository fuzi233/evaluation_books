“We spend our years as a tale that is told.” The line came back to me with the steady insistence of surf against stone as I turned page after page of my journal and found, almost with surprise, that ten full years had slipped past us on this island—years not marched out like soldiers, but poured, day by day, like milk into a pail: warm, ordinary, nourishing, and gone before one thinks to measure them.

There were moments, in that looking back, when a shadow lay across my mind—less grief than the quiet awe of time’s passing—yet gratitude quickly drove it away. God had kept our household in health and peace; the boys, trained early to labour and self-command, had grown into men. If any young reader should ever open these pages, let him understand that there is an adventure more enduring than storms or shipwrecks: the patient, cheerful industry of a united family, where duty is learned at the table as much as in the field, and where faith is not shouted but practiced, like daily bread.

It would weary even the kindest listener were I to recount, year by year, every hunt, every exploring party, every contrivance hammered together and improved upon—enough, indeed, to fill an encyclopaedia. Yet before I reach the events that ended this long interval, I must set down what our “New Switzerland” had become; for the place itself, shaped by our hands, shaped us in return.

Rockburg and Falconhurst remained our winter and summer homes. Rockburg, once so bare and scorching that my wife could scarcely endure it, had been softened into a dwelling that seemed to breathe. We coaxed fountains from the rock; we trained verandahs under trellises until they were hung with flowering creepers that dripped scent in the heat—honeysuckle sweetness, lemon blossom sharpness, and the resinous breath of young European saplings brought to strange soil and persuaded, by care, to remember home. Shrubs and trees, both native and from our old world, stood in planted groves; the air moved through them like a slow hymn, and with it moved sound—the clean chatter of leaves, the steady trickle of water, the gentler music that heals the mind more surely than any medicine. I often thought, hearing that living choir, how cruel to the spirit must be the ceaseless clang of cities, where iron screams over stone and even art is sold in noise. Here, our peace was measured in the island’s own language: wind, wing, and running water.

Out beyond, Shark Island—once a harsh sentinel of rock—was now feathered with palms. It guarded the entrance to Safety Bay; the battery and flagstaff were plain on its crest, as if the island itself wore our signal as a crown. The drained swamp had become a lake, leaving only enough reeds and marsh to shelter the waterfowl who had accepted us as masters without fear. On the blue surface moved black swans with solemn grace, snow-white geese that scolded and conversed like village wives, and richly coloured ducks that flashed as they turned. Among the rushes, at intervals—like bright words in a half-forgotten dialect—appeared the brilliant Sultan, marsh-fowl, crimson flamingos, soft blue-grey demoiselle cranes, and crested herons. Their calls wove together into a soundscape that never repeated itself yet never jarred: a living order that no conductor could commercialize.

Jack’s giant frogs, Grace and Beauty, had grown until they were, in truth, almost the size of small rabbits. They knew their names—names given with the authority boys take in such things—and at his call they waddled from the marsh, solemn as old servants, to swallow grasshoppers or the daintiest fly. Under the spreading trees, old Hurry the ostrich paced with grave dignity, as if he carried in his long legs the memory of kingdoms. Pigeons of every beautiful kind nested in the rocks and dove-cots, and their soft cooing was often the undertone to our evenings, a domestic music as necessary as firelight.

Rockburg could now be approached only by the bridge. Above the river, near the cascade where once it might be forded, we had planted a living barrier: orange and lemon trees, Indian figs, prickly pears, and thorny shrubs interlocked so densely that it was a wall of leaf and spine—fragrant, formidable, and, when in bloom, perfuming the air so richly that even the bees seemed drowsy with it.

Shark Island’s rabbit-warren supplied us with food and fur. The antelopes, failing on Whale Isle, were moved there among the shaded groves, and Whale Isle itself we gave over to necessary work that offended the senses—candle-making, tanning, wool-cleaning—so that the rest of our dominion might keep its sweetness. At Woodlands the farm flourished; our flocks and herds gave mutton, beef, and veal, and my wife’s dairy, with its crocks and pans and clean, sour-sweet smell, was so abundant that it often seemed to command her more than she commanded it.

The boys had never outgrown their delight in naming animals, as if language itself were another form of husbandry. A creamy-white cow was Blanche; a bull with a voice like thunder was Stentor. Two young onagers, fleet as arrows, were named Arrow and Dart. Jack had a descendant of his old favourite Fangs the jackal, and insisted on calling him Coco, maintaining—loudly, and with relentless demonstrations—that no word could be heard at a distance unless it contained an “o.” He would shout his examples until our ears rang, and then laugh as if he had discovered a new law of nature. I sometimes wondered, watching the boys coin names so freely, how many words, in the wider world, were dying unlamented—how dialects, like neglected gardens, go to weeds until the old meanings cannot be recovered. Here, we preserved memory in syllables, even when the syllables were foolish.

We enjoyed excellent health. My wife suffered now and then slight fever; the boys had their small accidents, inevitable in such a life. Fritz, at twenty-four, was moderately tall, uncommonly strong and active, high-spirited and ready. Ernest, two years younger, was tall and slight, mild, calm, studious; the indolence and selfishness of his earlier youth had been largely overcome by necessity and affection. Jack, twenty, resembled Fritz but more lightly built—swift, agile, made for climbing and leaping rather than sheer strength. Franz, seventeen, had something of each brother: wit and shrewdness, though not Jack’s arch drollery. All were honourable, God-fearing, dutiful to their mother and me, warmly attached to each other.

Though so many years had passed in seclusion, my conviction remained that we should not always be alone. Yet time that carried our sons toward manhood carried their parents toward age; and sometimes, when the island’s evening sounds softened into silence, anxious thoughts pressed on me—what if we were never restored? what if the boys, one day, stood alone here? I did not speak of these shadows. I took them to God in prayer, and always found strength renewed, like a fire coaxed back to life by careful breath.

My elder sons would often make expeditions and return only after many hours. Any reproof I had prepared would vanish at the sight of their joyous faces.

One day Fritz was absent from Rockburg the whole day. Only toward evening did we notice his cajack was gone and that he must have ventured out to sea. Uneasiness drove us to Shark Island. Ernest, Jack, and I climbed to the watch-tower, hoisted the signal flag, and loaded the gun.

We gazed long across the ocean, glittering under the level beams of the setting sun, until at last we discerned a black speck. Through the telescope it resolved into the returning cajack. Even at that distance I saw it moved more slowly than usual. We fired the cannon so he might know we watched, then hurried back to the harbour to meet him.

As he came in, the reason for his delay was plain: he towed a large sack, and the skiff itself was heavily laden.

“Welcome, Fritz!” I called. “Welcome back—wherever you come from, and whatever you bring. You have quite a cargo.”

“Yes—and my trip has led to discoveries as well as booty,” he answered, pushing in. “Discoveries that will tempt us again in the same direction. Come, boys—help carry these things up. When I have rested, I will tell you all.”

We gathered around him as soon as the goods were secured. He began with the frankness that always belonged to him.

“My absence without leave deserves reproach, father, more than this reception. I must apologize. But ever since I possessed the cajack, it has been my ambition to make a voyage along the coast beyond the point where I killed the walrus. I prepared beforehand—provisions, compass fixed in front of my seat, rifle, harpoon, axe, boat-hook, fishing-net—so that when a good morning came, I could go at once. And I resolved to take Pounce, my eagle. I mean to do so always.”

He described the dawn—calm sea, gentle breeze—and how the current bore him out. He rounded the point and passed above the spot where our ship’s wreck lay under water. Through glass-clear depths he had seen the scattered ironwork, cannon balls, and other indestructible remnants lying on the flat rocky bottom like bones of a great animal picked clean.

Then he spoke of the coast: rugged cliffs, rocks thrusting from shore or rising in jagged masses from the sea. Sea-fowl crowded the inaccessible ledges. On lower ridges seals, sea-bears, and walruses basked, plunged, or hauled their heavy bodies up by tusks and awkward strength. He admitted he had not felt comfortable among such monsters, and had worked hard to pass quickly and unnoticed.

After more than an hour and a half he reached a high, precipitous cape that ran far out to sea. In the rock face he saw a magnificent archway—at first glance like the entrance to an immense vaulted cavern. He passed beneath this noble portal and examined the interior.

“It was tenanted by numbers of a small swallow, scarcely larger than a wren,” he said. “The walls were covered with thousands of their nests. They were rudely built, each resting on a kind of platform like a spoon without a handle. I detached a number. They looked fibrous and gelatinous—more like sponges or fungi than nests. I brought them home in my fishing-net.”

“If we had trade with the Chinese,” I told him, “your discovery would be valuable. These are edible birds’ nests, made by the esculent swallow. New, nearly white nests are valued even at their weight in silver. In Java and elsewhere they are gathered at great risk. When soaked they soften and swell, and are made into a strengthening soup. I think you might try them, wife—only for curiosity.”

My wife regarded the queer trophies with a doubtful expression. “I do not fancy the look of them,” she said, “but I can try, if they truly turn to jelly. Boiling birds’ nests is cookery quite out of my line.”

“Oh do, mother!” Jack cried. “Let us taste birds’ nests. Though the thought makes me imagine my mouth full of feathers.”

Fritz, curious as ever, asked from where the swallows obtained such substance.

“It is not exactly known,” I said, “whether they find it or produce it. But it is clear much of it is furnished by certain glands which pour out a viscid secretion.”

Fritz continued. The cavern, he said, was not an end but a corridor; it turned and opened into a lonely bay so calm and lake-like that he judged it nearly land-locked. Its shores, beyond the rocky boundary, spread into a fertile plain toward what seemed the mouth of a river. Beyond that lay rough, likely marshy ground, and a dense cedar forest that shut off the view.

The water beneath his skiff had been clear as crystal, and in the shallows he saw beds of shellfish—like large oysters—attached to rocks and to one another by tufts of hairy filaments.

“If these are oysters,” he thought, “they must be worth eating.” He hooked up clusters with the boat-hook, landed, flung them on the sand, and went back for more, intending to tow them in the net. But the hot sun made the shells gape. He began to “examine” them with his knife—and found resistance in places. Closer examination produced pearly balls, like peas, various sizes. He had brought a number home in a box.

The boys clamoured to see them. In their palms the pearls caught the light with a soft gleam, not the harsh glitter of glass but something deeper, as if the sea’s own muted colours had condensed into spheres.

“You have discovered treasure indeed,” I said. “Beautiful pearls. Valueless here, perhaps, but wealth if ever we return to the civilized world. We must visit your oyster beds as soon as we can.”

He told how he named the place Pearl Bay. The bay, he explained, was sheltered by reefs and sandbanks with only a single channel to the open sea. The tide had set strongly inshore, so he could not pass out then. He examined the headland crags, hoping for a second archway, but found nothing remarkable—only innumerable sea-fowl, from gull and sea-swallow to mighty albatross.

“They took my approach as trespass,” he said, with a half-laugh. “They beset me, screaming and wheeling, until I lost patience and struck about me with the boat-hook. One blow stunned an albatross; it fell into the water.”

He then attempted the narrow channel, succeeded, and returned home, seeing our flag and hearing our salute.

His narrative ended there, and for the household it was enough: edible nests, pearl oysters, a new bay—new work, new supplies, new names to give. The boys were already dreaming of huts and fisheries; my wife was considering, with a cook’s wary courage, what could be made of swallows’ nests and how they might taste when coaxed into broth.

But the next morning Fritz drew me aside and, in a voice lowered not by fear but by the gravity of wonder, told me what he had not spoken before.

“There was something extraordinary about that albatross, father. I allowed you to think I left it. I did not. I lifted it onto the cajack and saw a rag wound round one leg. I removed it and—God knows how—English words were written on it. I read them plainly: ‘Save an unfortunate Englishwoman from the smoking rock!’”

For a moment I could not answer him. The words, so simple and so impossible in our isolation, struck me like a sudden cry heard in a forest that one believed empty. My mind spun between hope and caution.

“Is this reality?” Fritz went on. “Or delusion? Can it be true—a fellow creature breathes in this lonely region?”

He told me how he had sat stupefied, then, as the bird stirred, compelled himself to act. He tore a strip from his handkerchief and wrote, “Do not despair! Help is near!” He bound his reply carefully round one leg, replaced the original rag on the other, gave the albatross water, and worked until it revived. Then it rose—unsteady at first—then steadied and flew swiftly away westward.

“Now one thought occupies me,” Fritz said. “Will my note reach her? Shall I find her—shall I save her?”

I listened with astonishment and deep interest. Yet I forced myself to speak soberly.

“My dear son, you have done wisely to confide this to me alone. Until we know more, we must not unsettle the others. It is possible those words were written long ago, perhaps on some distant shore, and the unhappy stranger may by this time have perished. By ‘the smoking rock’ one would think of a volcano. There are none here.”

Fritz could not accept such gloom. He argued the rag did not seem very old; that smoke could rise from rock not volcanic—perhaps from a fire, perhaps from some strange natural cause. Above all, he clung to the hope that an appeal so direct had been meant to be answered.

In truth I was as eager as he; but I judged it prudent to lessen rather than kindle hopes that might end in bitterness.

We consulted earnestly and resolved that Fritz should go in search of the writer of the message—but not until his canoe was altered to carry two persons and provisions enough for a long absence. Impatient as he was, he saw the wisdom of delay.

When we returned to the house, we found the boys already at the oysters Fritz had brought. They prised shells open with the eagerness of children cracking nuts at a winter hearth. Each time a pearl rolled out, there was a shout, a chorus of astonished exclamations; the small hard globes gleamed among wet flesh and brine.

“May we not establish a pearl-fishery at once, father?” they cried. “We could build a hut by the bay and work there regularly!”

An excursion to Pearl Bay became the subject of every conversation and every plan. Preparations were made on a grand scale, and the outing would serve, as Fritz intended, as the foundation for his more secret voyage—secret only because he would not yet burden the household with the uncertain hope of a stranger’s rescue.

One day, when all were present, I spoke with deliberation.

“I have been considering, dear wife, that our eldest son is now of an age to be dependent on himself. Henceforth I shall leave him at liberty to act according to his own judgement. Especially in voyages or excursions, he must not be hampered by fear of alarming us should he remain absent longer than we expect. I have entire confidence in his prudence—and in his affection. He will never needlessly cause us anxiety.”

Fritz looked at me with gratitude. His mother embraced him, her voice trembling as she said, “God bless and preserve thee, my boy!”

It took time to make several scraping machines—rakes of my own invention—for detaching and lifting oysters from their rocks. Meanwhile Fritz altered the fittings of his canoe so it had a spare seat. His brothers, of course, assumed he meant to take one of them as shipmate; he allowed their mistake to stand.

They busied themselves making what they thought useful—ropes, bags, small tools—bearing the delay with tolerable patience. At last the day came.

We took leave of my wife and Franz and went aboard our yacht with some of the dogs. Jack, proudly installed in the new seat beside Fritz in the canoe, shared with him the honour of leading the way as pilot.

We passed safely through the rocks and shoals near Walrus Island into calm water sheltered by jutting cliffs. The sea lay like polished metal, and for the first time we saw paper-nautiluses—the fairy shells—sailing lightly over the dazzling surface, each like a living boat with a fragile white sail.

We could not look upon them without longing to keep them. The canoe gave chase and soon secured half a dozen. They were handed aboard to be carefully preserved for our little museum, and we named the place Nautilus Creek. Even as I spoke the name aloud, I felt that naming is a kind of claiming—yet also a kind of remembering—and I wondered what words, in what tongues, had once named these waters before we came, and whether those tongues had vanished as completely as a tide-mark washed from stone.

Further on we rounded a short, flat promontory ending in an abrupt rock, which we called Cape Pug-Nose; then, at some distance, appeared the grand cliffs of a headland running far out to sea. I supposed we should have to weather it, but our pilots held their course, and soon we saw the majestic archway that offered a short passage to Pearl Bay.

The architectural splendour of pillars, arches, and pinnacles around that entrance struck me with admiration. It resembled a gothic cathedral carved by waves rather than masons, and I proposed the name Cape Minster.

As we approached, a cloud of little swallows darted out, divided into flocks, soared and wheeled, then returned as swiftly as they had come, vanishing into the long dark tunnel whose sides were festooned with nests. The air there was cool and damp, tasting of salt and stone; every sound was altered—oar-dips became hollow taps, voices fell into reverent echoes, wings rustled like pages turning in a vast, dim book.

We detached nests as we passed, careful to leave those with eggs or young. The best were high, but broken shelving rocks gave footing for climbers like Fritz and Jack. They moved with the fearless grace of youth, returning with as many as we could reasonably carry—odd pale trophies, light as dried seafoam.

The tide assisted us, bearing us onward along the nave of this natural cathedral. Aisles and side-chapels seemed to open between columns and arches. In that dim religious light the world looked older than any human story; yet our oars, our breathing, our ordinary labour belonged there too, as if domestic life could inhabit even the grandest mystery without profaning it.

When we emerged into dazzling sunshine, we floated upon the calm expanse of Pearl Bay. For some minutes we could scarcely look around, the light was so sudden and the scene so bright.

Fritz had not overstated its beauty. Romantic islets studded the waters, giving a smile to features already perfect. Meadows and groves lay along the shore; hills rose gently; brooks murmured as if the land itself spoke in a soft local tongue. Birds called from reed and tree; the bay answered with its own hush and lapping, and I felt again that our well-being depended not only on food and shelter but on this acoustic mercy—the island’s refusal to rage in needless noise.

We cruised, surveying for a landing place near the shallows where the oyster beds lay. At last we found a convenient spot close to a sparkling streamlet. As the day declined, we made quick arrangements for a watch-fire. Then we ate a hasty supper—plain fare, yet rendered precious by salt air and labour—and left the dogs, with Coco the jackal, to sleep on shore as sentinels.

We returned to the yacht for the night and anchored within gunshot of land, the bay quiet around us. Above, the swallows’ distant murmuring and the soft pulse of water against the hull braided together into a lullaby, and in that sound I felt both the comfort of our settled routine and the faint, unsettling pull of Fritz’s secret: a human voice, perhaps, calling from some “smoking rock” beyond our known shore.
