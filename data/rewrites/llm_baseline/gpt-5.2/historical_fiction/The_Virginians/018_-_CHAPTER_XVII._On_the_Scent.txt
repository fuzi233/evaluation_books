Harry Warrington’s refusal—sharp as a knife turned in a hand that had grown used to wielding it—fell upon Madame de Bernstein so suddenly that rage was her only seasoning for the moment. She flashed two eyes like struck flint at Lady Fanny and Lady Castlewood as she swept out. Lady Maria, behind her tambour-frame, scarcely lifted her sandy lashes; her needle went on pricking silk with the calm persistence of a kitchen clock that keeps time whether there’s a storm outside or none.

Between mother and daughter there passed those looks that speak louder than a viol in a drawing-room when the house has gone quiet. Fanny tried on puzzlement as if it were a borrowed lace cap; it sat ill. Had she been guilty, she would have made innocence as neat and finished as a trussed bird. Lady Castlewood’s glare promised a private reckoning later—smiles first, claws afterwards, as in households where public breeding is kept like silver: polished for company, locked away for use.

“So you don’t fancy going with the old lady to Tunbridge Wells?” Lady Castlewood said at last to Harry, her mouth simpering as if it had been taught good manners by a dancing-master, though the eyes behind it were all accounts and reckonings.

“And small blame to our cousin!” my lord broke in, and for one instant Maria’s head rose from the tambour, as if a familiar sound—some phrase, some cadence of power—had crossed her ear like the distant cry of hounds. “A young fellow must not have it all idling and holiday. Let him mix up something useful with his pleasures. Mr. Warrington has to conduct a great estate in America: let him see how ours in England are carried on. Will hath shown him the kennel and the stables; and the games in vogue, which, I think, cousin, you seem to play as well as your teachers. After harvest we will show him a little English fowling and shooting; in winter we will take him out a-hunting. Though there has been a coolness between us and our aunt-kinswoman in Virginia, we are of the same blood. Ere we send our cousin back to his mother, let us show him what an English gentleman’s life at home is. I should like to read with him as well as sport with him; and that is why I have been pressing him of late to stay and bear me company.”

He spoke with a frankness so well-cooked—clear, unclouded, carrying no visible spice of guile—that his mother-in-law and his half-brother and sister could not tell what dish lay hidden beneath. In that house they were used to little cabals, like servants whispering over the range when the master had stepped out; but when Castlewood chose this open tone, his meaning might not be tasted until months had passed. He did not say, This is true. He said, This shall be believed in my family. So it became, at once, a thing convenu: my Lord Castlewood had a laudable desire to cultivate domestic affections, to educate and amuse and improve his young relative; he had taken a great fancy to the lad; he wished Harry to stay.

Down a corridor hung with tapestries that muffled footfalls and swallowed laughter as bread swallows gravy, William drew his mother and sister aside.

“What is Castlewood’s game now?” he asked; and then, with a click as of a latch lifted, “Stop! By George, I have it!”

“What, William?”

“He intends to get him to play, and to win the Virginia estate back from him. That’s what it is!”

“But the lad has not got the Virginia estate to pay, if he loses,” Lady Castlewood said, her voice thin with calculation.

“If my brother has not some scheme in view, may I be—”

“Hush! Of course he has a scheme in view. But what is it?”

“He can’t mean Maria—Maria is as old as Harry’s mother,” Will mused; then, with the cruelty of a man who knows where to tap the bruise, “Pooh! with her old face and sandy hair and freckled skin! Impossible!” Lady Fanny sighed, though she tried to make it sound like scorn.

“Of course, your ladyship had a fancy for the Iroquois, too!” mamma snapped, in that tone which could sour milk at ten paces.

“I trust I know my station and duty better, madam! If I had liked him, that is no reason why I should marry him. Your ladyship hath taught me as much as that.”

“My Lady Fanny!”

“I am sure you married our papa without liking him. You have told me so a thousand times!”

“And if you did not love our father before marriage, you certainly did not fall in love with him afterwards,” Will broke in, laughing with the easy brutality of a man who never has to pay the reckoning for the household’s peace. “Fan and I remember how our honoured parents used to fight. Don’t us, Fan? And our brother Esmond kept the peace.”

“Don’t recall those dreadful low scenes, William!” cried Lady Castlewood. “When your father took too much drink, he was like a madman; and his conduct should be a warning to you, sir, who are fond of the same horrid practice.”

“I am sure, madam, you were not much the happier for marrying the man you did not like, and your ladyship’s title hath brought very little along with it,” Lady Fanny whimpered, stung into boldness. “What is the use of a coronet with the jointure of a tradesman’s wife?—how many of them are richer than we are? There is come lately to live in our Square, at Kensington, a grocer’s widow from London Bridge, whose daughters have three gowns where I have one; and who, though they are waited on but by a man and a couple of maids, I know eat and drink a thousand times better than we do with our scraps of cold meat on our plate, and our great flaunting, trapesing, impudent, lazy lacqueys!”

The complaint—so oddly domestic, so sharply gustatory—made the gallery itself seem to smell of yesterday’s beef, of congealed fat and vinegar too thin to wake it. One could almost hear the scrape of dull knives on pewter, the sigh of the household when a joint is carried out more bone than blessing. The acoustics of poverty were there: not silence, but the wrong kinds of sounds—lacqueys’ insolent heels, empty plates set down too hard to seem full, the rustle of gowns that could not afford to be careless.

“He! he! glad I dine at the palace, and not at home!” said Mr. Will. Through his aunt’s interest with Count Puffendorff, Groom of the Royal (and Serene Electoral) Powder-Closet, he had one of the many small court-crumbs—Deputy Powder—so that even his vanity smelled faintly of starch, pomatum, and the powdered air of a prince’s antechamber.

“Why should I not be happy without any title except my own?” Lady Frances persisted. “Many people are. I dare say they are even happy in America.”

“Yes!—with a mother-in-law who is a perfect Turk and Tartar, for all I hear—with Indian war-whoops howling all around you, and with a danger of losing your scalp, or of being eat up by a wild beast every time you went to church.”

“I wouldn’t go to church,” said Lady Fanny, pettishly.

“You’d go with anybody who asked you, Fan!” roared Will. “And so would old Maria, and so would any woman, that’s the fact.” He laughed at his own wit, loud enough to be heard through the hangings.

It was then that Madame de Bernstein’s head and shoulders appeared at the tapestried door, as if she had been listening all along and now chose to step into the very steam of their quarrel. The door fell back behind her with a soft hush, as cloth shuts on secrets.

“Pray, good folks, what is all your merriment about?” she asked.

Will told her, grinning, that his mother and sister had been having a fight—no novelty—because Fanny wanted to marry their cousin, the wild Indian, and Lady Castlewood would not let her. Fanny protested, coloring, that since the very first day her mother had warned her not to speak to the young gentleman, she had scarcely exchanged two words with him. She knew her station better. She did not want to be scalped by wild Indians, or eaten by bears.

Madame de Bernstein looked from one to the other, and the look was that of a woman tasting a sauce and finding in it a spice she had not put there herself.

“If he is not staying for you, for whom is he staying?” she asked. “At the houses to which he has been carried, you have taken care not to show him a woman that is not a fright or in the nursery; and I think the boy is too proud to fall in love with a dairymaid, Will.”

“Humph! That is a matter of taste, ma’am,” Mr. William said, with a shrug.

“Of Mr. William Esmond’s taste, as you say; but not of yonder boy’s. The Esmonds of his grandfather’s nurture, sir, would not go a-courting in the kitchen.”

“Well, ma’am, every man to his taste. A fellow might go farther and fare worse than my brother’s servants’ hall; and besides Fan, there’s only the maids or old Maria to choose from.”

“Maria! Impossible!”

Yet as she said it, the very word seemed to open a drawer in Madame de Bernstein’s mind where old recollections lay folded, scented, and dangerous. She had seen boys made fools by women old enough to be their aunts; she had heard the town’s whispered talk in doorways where the sound carries, in salons where laughter is arranged like a dessert course and the truth served under sugar. She remembered how often Harry had been absent of late—absences she had lazily salted down as field-sports. She remembered how often, when he was away, Lady Maria too had vanished from her tambour and her quiet corner. Walks under clipped hedges; pauses in cool avenues where leaves make a small sea-sound; garden temples where whispers gather and do not fly; hands that meet in twilight corridors, as accidentally as spoons touch in a crowded dish; glances exchanged on stairs like stolen sweetmeats. Madame’s fancy—trained by experience, sharpened by a lifetime of being spoken of—served all these images at once.

“Impossible, ma’am! I don’t know,” Will went on. “My mother warned Fan off him.”

“Oh, your mother did warn Fanny off?” Madame asked, as if she were innocently stirring a pot.

“Certainly, my dear Baroness!”

“Didn’t she? Didn’t she pinch Fanny’s arm black-and-blue? Didn’t they fight about it?”

“Nonsense, William! For shame, William!” cried both ladies together, as if the same whip had cracked over them.

“And now, since we have heard how rich he is, perhaps it is sour grapes, that is all. And now, since he is warned off the young bird, perhaps he is hunting the old one, that’s all. Impossible—why impossible? You know old Lady Suffolk, ma’am?”

“William, how can you speak about Lady Suffolk to your aunt?” Lady Castlewood cried.

A grin—half impudence, half a boy’s delight in naming forbidden things—passed over Will’s face. “Because Lady Suffolk was a special favourite at Court? Well, other folks have succeeded her.”

“Sir!” Madame de Bernstein cried. She may have had reasons, private and exquisitely sharp, to dislike such a hint.

“So they have, I say; or who, pray, is my Lady Yarmouth now? And didn’t old Lady Suffolk go and fall in love with George Berkeley, and marry him when she was ever so old? Nay, ma’am—if I remember right, and we hear a deal of town-talk at our table—Harry Estridge went mad about your ladyship when you were somewhat rising twenty; and would have changed your name a third time if you would but have let him.”

That last allusion—so notorious that it had long since passed from scandal into common vocabulary—did not anger Madame as the former had done. On the contrary, it warmed her, as a remembered cordial warms the chest on a cold day. She played her pretty little hand upon the table, and her mind, no doubt, went visiting mad young Harry Estridge.

“Au fait,” she said, musing, “’tis not impossible, William, that old folks, and young folks too, should play the fool.”

“But I can’t understand a young fellow being in love with Maria,” Will persisted, “however he might be with you, ma’am. That’s oter shose, as our French tutor used to say. You remember the Count, ma’am; he! he!—and so does Maria!”

“William!”

“And I dare say the Count remembers the bastinado Castlewood had given him. A confounded French dancing-master calling himself a count, and daring to fall in love in our family! Whenever I want to make myself uncommonly agreeable to old Maria, I just say a few words of parly voo to her. She knows what I mean.”

The words—parly voo—were like a counterfeit coin, passed with a wink. In a house where English titles sat on debts and foreign phrases served as both ornament and weapon, language itself became a kind of power: who may speak, who may mock, who may name. Maria’s French was a private country she had once lived in; Will used it as a stick to beat her with, as if stealing a woman’s dialect were the same as stealing her keys.

“Have you abused her to your cousin, Harry Warrington?” Madame asked quickly.

“Well—I know she is always abusing me—and I have said my mind about her,” Will replied.

“Oh, you idiot!” Madame cried, with sudden sincerity. “Who but a gaby ever spoke ill of a woman to her sweetheart? He will tell her everything, and they both will hate you.”

“The very thing, ma’am!” Will burst out laughing. “I had a sort of suspicion, you see, and two days ago, as we were riding together, I told Harry Warrington a bit of my mind about Maria—why shouldn’t I? She is always abusing me, ain’t she, Fan? And your favourite turned as red as my plush waistcoat—wondered how a gentleman could malign his own flesh and blood, and, trembling all over with rage, said I was no true Esmond.”

“Why didn’t you chastise him, sir, as my lord did the dancing-master?” Lady Castlewood demanded, the memory of old blows rising in her like smoke.

“Well, mother—you see that at quarter-staff there’s two sticks used,” Will answered. “And my opinion is, Harry Warrington can guard his own head uncommonly well. Perhaps that is one of the reasons why I did not offer to treat my cousin to a caning. And now you say so, ma’am, I know he has told Maria. She has been looking battle, murder, and sudden death at me ever since. All which shows—” He turned towards his aunt, triumphant, like a huntsman hearing the first cry in cover. “All which shows that I think we are on the right scent; and that we’ve found Maria—the old fox!” And he clapped his hand to his mouth and hallooed, loud and foolish, as if the hangings were hedgerows and the gallery a field.

How far had this pretty intrigue gone? That was the question now, and it lay in the house like a smell you cannot place at first—smoke, or singed sugar, or the faint sourness that warns you a cask has turned. Will said that at Maria’s age she would conduct matters as rapidly as possible, not having much time to lose. There was not a great deal of love lost between Will and his half-sister; their quarrels were old bones, gnawed down and still offered up at every meal.

Who was to sift the matter to the bottom? Scolding only hardens a will. Threats, in such cases, are like banging a pot-lid: they make more noise than change. Madame de Bernstein, who had lived long enough to know how easily a family mistakes clatter for control, spoke more quietly.

“I never was in danger but once, young people,” she said; “and I think that was because my poor mother contradicted me. If this boy is like others of his family, the more we oppose him, the more entêté he will be; and we shall never get him out of his scrape.”

“Faith, ma’am, suppose we leave him in it?” Will grumbled. “Old Maria and I don’t love each other too much, I grant you; but an English earl’s daughter is good enough for an American tobacco-planter, when all is said and done.”

His mother and sister broke out as if he had flung a coal among dry herbs. They would not hear of such a union.

“You are like the dog in the manger,” Will retorted. “You don’t want the man yourself, Fanny.”

“I want him, indeed!” Lady Fanny cried, tossing her head, and the denial sounded too quick—like a cook protesting she never tasted the sauce she made.

“Then why grudge him to Maria? I think Castlewood wants her to have him.”

“Why grudge him to Maria, sir?” Madame de Bernstein cried, with sudden fire that made them all look at her. “Do you remember who the poor boy is, and what your house owes to his family? His grandfather was the best friend your father ever had, and gave up this estate, this title, this very castle in which you are conspiring against the friendless Virginian lad, that you and yours might profit by it. And the reward for all this kindness is, that you all but shut the door on the child when he knocks at it, and talk of marrying him to a silly elderly creature who might be his mother! He shan’t marry her.”

“The very thing we were saying and thinking, my dear Baroness!” Lady Castlewood interposed at once, swift to wrap herself in the moral cloak when it suited. “Our part of the family is not eager about the match, though my lord and Maria may be.”

“You would like him for yourself, now that you hear he is rich—and may be richer, young people, mind you that,” Madame de Bernstein said, turning on the other women with the clean, cutting accuracy of a knife that knows the grain.

“Mr. Warrington may be ever so rich, madam, but there is no need why your ladyship should perpetually remind us that we are poor,” Lady Castlewood answered with more spirit than tenderness. “At least there is very little disparity in Fanny’s age and Mr. Harry’s; and you surely will be the last to say that a lady of our name and family is not good enough for any gentleman born in Virginia or elsewhere.”

“Let Fanny take an English gentleman, Countess, not an American,” Madame replied. “With such a name and such a mother to help her, and with all her good looks and accomplishments, sure she can’t fail of finding a man worthy of her. But from what I know about the daughters of this house, and what I imagine about our young cousin, I am certain that no happy match could be made between them.”

“What does my aunt know about me?” Lady Fanny asked, turning very red, as if the blood itself protested to be so read aloud.

“Only your temper, my dear,” Madame said. “You don’t suppose I believe all the tittle-tattle and scandal which one cannot help hearing in town. But the temper and early education are sufficient. Only fancy one of you condemned to leave St. James’s and the Mall, and live in a plantation surrounded by savages! You would die of ennui, or worry your husband’s life out with your ill-humour. You are born, ladies, to ornament courts—not wigwams. Let this lad go back to his wilderness with a wife who is suited to him.”

The other two declared at once—too quickly, too neatly—that for their parts they desired no better; and after a few more words, they went away down the corridor, their silks making that dry, expensive whisper which, in that house, so often took the place of kindness. Madame de Bernstein lifted the tapestried door and retired to her own chamber, where the air smelled faintly of past perfumes and closed drawers, and the only sound was the gentler, truer kind: the hush of cloth, the far-off tick of a clock, the faint murmur of a household living around her like a sea she had learned to navigate.

Now she saw the whole scheme; and she admired, with something like bitter amusement, the ways of women, calling back a score of little circumstances—glances on staircases, absences that had been explained by sport, Maria’s quiet vanishings, Harry’s sudden heat when her name was touched. She wondered at her own blindness. How far had it gone? Was Harry’s passion serious and tragical, or a mere fire of straw that burns bright and dies by morning? How deeply was he committed?

She dreaded the strength of Harry’s feeling and the weakness of Maria’s. A woman of Maria’s age may be desperate, she thought—desperate enough to make any efforts to secure a lover. Scandal? Bah. Maria would retire and be a princess in Virginia, and leave England to talk itself hoarse. There is always, is there not, some one thing women do not tell one another—some secret kept not in ink but in blood and breath—and about which they agree, without speaking, to deceive? Is it deceit, or modesty, or that deeper untranslatability of experience which makes even sisters speak different dialects when the heart is concerned? Men, as soon as inclination stirs, go seeking a friend to whom they can pour the delicious news. Women, with more or less art, bury it from their kind; and for days and weeks past had not this old Maria—Maria, the butt of the family—made fools of the whole house?

Madame de Bernstein did not care to examine Lady Maria’s antecedents too curiously; she had her own opinions about her own. A hundred years ago, people of the great world were not so straitlaced as they now pretend to be, when everybody is good, pure, moral, modest—when there is no skeleton in any closet; no scheming; no slurring over old stories; when no girl tries to sell herself for wealth, and no mother abets her. Suppose Lady Maria tries to play her little game: wherein is her great eccentricity? Thus the Baroness de Bernstein thought, alone with the muffled sounds of Castlewood, and the knowledge that in every great house there are languages spoken plainly at table, and others—older, darker, more intimate—whispered behind hangings where even truth must learn to lower its voice.
