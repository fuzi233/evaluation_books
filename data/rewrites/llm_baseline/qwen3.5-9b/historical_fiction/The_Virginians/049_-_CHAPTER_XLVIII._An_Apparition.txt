Driven from his morning's discourse with Harry by sheer wrath, Mr. Draper believed he heard the young prisoner muttering behind him. In truth, Harry had risen and let out a half-choked cry to recall the lawyer, but pride held him fast while the other remained offended. Reluctant to humble himself before a man he despised, and unwilling to yield merely due to a lack of funds, Harry checked his words, though Draper showed no sign of stopping.

*"An hour hence will suffice,"* Harry thought, sinking sulkingly back onto the bed. *No, he cared nothing for Maria Esmond!* Shame burned within him for the manner in which he had been ensnared into that engagement. She was a cunning, seasoned woman who had exploited his boyish ardor, taking unfair advantage of him just as her brother had at cards. They were his own flesh and blood, yet instead of sparing him, both had made prey of him, using him for their selfish ends. He recalled how they had violated the sanctity of hospitality, turning the trusting young kinsman who entered their gates into a victim. His heart felt sorely wounded; his head fell back against the pillow, and bitter tears soaked the linen.

*"Had they come to Virginia,"* he murmured, *"I would have offered them a far different welcome!"*

Gumbo's grinning face suddenly appeared at the doorway, announcing that a lady had arrived to see Master Harry. Following the lad came the lady in the capuchin, previously mentioned. Harry sat up, pale and haggard. Before the servant could fully withdraw, the lady rushed toward the young prisoner, overcome with sobs. With genuine emotion and maternal tenderness, she threw her arms around his neck, weeping over his pallid cheek and kissing it amidst a flood of tears.

"Oh, my Harry! Did I ever, ever think to see thee here?"

Harry recoiled, startled by her sudden presence, but she sank down beside the bed, seizing his feverish hand and embracing his knees. There was a profound regard and tenderness in her gaze. The wretched state in which she found him, his miserable appearance, filled her heart with sincere love and pity.

"I—I thought none of you would come!" poor Harry groaned.

More tears, more kisses upon his hot young hand, more tight embraces formed her reply for a moment or two. "Oh, my dear! My dear! I cannot bear to think of thee in misery," she sobbed. Though hardened by circumstance, her heart was not entirely marble; her dreary life was not a barren desert. Harry's mother could not have been fonder, nor her tones more tender than those of his kinswoman now kneeling at his feet.

"Some of the debts, I fear, were owing to my extravagance!" she confessed—and this was true. "You bought trinkets and jewels solely to please me. Oh, how I hate them now! I little thought I ever could! I have brought them all with me, and more trinkets—here! and here! and every coin I possess in the world!"

She poured brooches, rings, a watch, and a score of guineas into Harry's lap. The sight strangely agitated and deeply moved the young man.

"Dearest, kindest cousin!" he sobbed. Words failed him, yet his expression conveyed his gratitude, his affection, and his overwhelming emotion. Soon, however, he grew cheerful, smiling as he tucked away some of the gifts intended for Maria, recounting the dangers he had faced by selling goods purchased on credit and detailing how a lawyer had insulted him moments prior regarding these very matters. He refused Maria's money—he had enough for the present—but he valued her twenty guineas as highly as if they were twenty thousand. He vowed never to forget her love and kindness; by all that was sacred, he would not! He must tell his mother of her goodness. It had buoyed him when he stood on the brink of breaking under disgrace and misery. May Heaven bless her for it!

There is no need to prolong the cousins' conversation. After Maria's visit, the dark day seemed brighter to Harry; the imprisonment felt less burdensome. The world was not wholly selfish and cold. Here was a loving soul who truly cherished him. Even Castlewood was not as terrible as he had imagined. The Baroness had expressed the deepest grief at her inability to aid her kinsman, noting she was hopelessly in debt, having lost every shilling she had won from Harry to others the following day. Yet, anything within her power, she would do. She promised to visit Mr. Warrington soon; he was currently in waiting, imprisoned alongside Harry. Thus, the pair conversed cheerfully and affectionately until twilight descended. Then, with a sigh, Maria bid Harry farewell.

Scarcely had the door closed upon her when it swung open again to admit Draper.
"Your humble servant, sir," the attorney said.

His voice grated on Harry's ears, and his presence offended the young man.
"I had expected you some hours ago, sir," Harry replied curtly.

"A lawyer's time is not always his own, sir," Mr. Draper retorted, having just consulted with a bottle of port at the Grecian. "Never mind, I am at your orders now. Presume it is settled, Mr. Warrington? You are still in your bedgown. Let me descend to finalize arrangements while you summon your black man to freshen up. I have a coach at the door, and we shall depart to dine with the old lady."

"Are you going to dine with the Baroness de Bernstein, pray?"

"Not me—no such honor. I have already dined. It is you who are dining with your aunt, I suppose?"

"Mr. Draper, you suppose far more than you know," Mr. Warrington said fiercely, folding his brocade dressing gown tightly around himself.

"Goodness gracious, sir, what do you mean?" Draper asked.

"I mean, sir, that I have reconsidered. Having given my word to a faithful and honorable lady, it becomes me not to withdraw it."

"Confound it, sir!" shrieked the lawyer. "I tell you she has lost the paper! There is nothing binding you—absolutely nothing. Besides, she is old enough to be—"

"Enough, sir!" Mr. Warrington stamped his foot. "You seem to believe you are speaking to some other pettifogger. I take it, Mr. Draper, you are not accustomed to dealing with men of honor."

"Pettifogger, indeed!" Draper cried in a fury. "Men of honor, indeed! Let me inform you, Mr. Warrington, that I am as honorable a man as you. Perhaps I do not know so many gamblers and horse-jockeys. I have not gambled away my patrimony and lived like a nobleman on two hundred a year. I have not bought watches on credit and pawned—" Touch me if you dare, sir!" The lawyer sprang toward the door.

"That is the exit, sir. You cannot pass through the window, for it is barred," Mr. Warrington stated calmly.

"And the answer I give to my client is 'No,' then!" Draper screamed.

Harry stepped forward, fists clenched. "If you utter another word, I'll—"

The door slammed shut rapidly; the sentence was never finished. Draper departed, furious, heading to Madame de Bernstein. Though he delivered the most damning version of his story, he received even harsher language from the Baroness than he had from Mr. Warrington himself.

"What? Shall she trust me, and I desert her?" Harry paced his room, his flowing brocade rustling loudly. "Dear, faithful, generous woman! If I lie in prison for years, I shall remain true to her."

Once the lawyer left after the stormy interview, the desolate old woman was forced to sit down to the meal she had hoped to share with her nephew. The chair before her, meant for him, remained empty. Glasses gleamed beside the silverware. Dish after dish was placed before her by the silent major-domo, tasted, and pushed aside. Finally, the man pressed his mistress.

"It is eight o'clock," he said. "You have eaten nothing all day. It is good for you to eat."

She could not eat. She desired only coffee. "Let Case fetch her coffee."

The lacquies removed the dishes, leaving their mistress seated before the vacant chair. Shortly thereafter, the old servant re-entered without the coffee, his face etched with strange terror.

"MR. WARRINGTON!" he shouted.

The old woman gasped, rising from her armchair only to sink back down, trembling violently. "So you are come, sir, are you?" she whispered, her voice shaking. "Bring back the—Ah!" Here she screamed, "Gracious God, who is it?"

Her eyes widened wildly; her white face looked ghastly beneath her rouge. Clinging to the arms of her chair for support, she watched as the visitor approached. A gentleman whose face and figure mirrored Harry Warrington exactly, and whose voice possessed strangely similar tones, had followed the servant into the room. He bowed toward the Baroness.
