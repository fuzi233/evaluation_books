On the eighth day following that unforgettable night, Romola stood upon the precipice of the Mediterranean, gazing at the gentle, rhythmic pulse of the summer sea just above the tiny fishing hamlet of Viareggio. Once more, she had fled from Florence, and this time, no commanding voice had summoned her return. Clad once again in the grey habit of the religious order, she felt no shame in the disguise; indeed, in the depths of her heart-sickness, she cared nothing for the label she bore. A fresh rebellion had ignited within her, fueled by a profound despair. Why should she cling to one title over another, or insist on being addressed by her own name? She had lost faith in the existence of any consistent duty attached to that identity. What power remained to forge within her that sacred motive men call duty, a force that exists only through the vessel of believing love?

Every bond of strong affection had been severed. In her marriage, the highest of all ties, she could no longer perceive the mystical union that guarantees indissolubility, nor even the obligation of a voluntary pledge. Had she not demonstrated that the things to which she had bound herself were impossible? The urge to liberate herself surged with overwhelming force, yet she knew this freedom would merely be an exchange of one calamity for another. There is no recompense for the woman who realizes that the central relationship of her life was nothing more than a mistake. She had lost her crown. The deepest secret of human blessedness had half-whispered its truth to her, only to slip past her forever.

Now, Romola's greatest support in her supreme sorrow had vanished. The vision of any grand purpose, any end to existence capable of ennobling endurance and elevating the mundane dust of daily life with divine ardor, was utterly eclipsed by a sense of confusion in human affairs. Every effort felt like dragging at tangled threads; every fellowship, whether for resistance or advocacy, appeared as mere unfairness and exclusivity. Who, after all, was the man who had embodied for her the highest heroism—the heroism not of stoic, self-contained endurance, but of willing, self-offering love? What cause was he truly fighting for? Romola had lost her trust in Savonarola, extinguishing the fervor of admiration that had previously made her blind to his aberrations, allowing her to focus solely on the grand arc of his destiny. Now that her deep affection for her godfather had cast her into opposition with the Friar, she viewed the repulsive and inconsistent details of his teachings with a painful, exaggerated clarity. In the bitterness of her disappointment, she declared that his striving to renovate the Church and the world was a pursuit of a mere name—a title that signified nothing more than the measures designed to strengthen his own position in Florence, or perhaps questionable deeds performed merely to shield his influence from the consequences of his own errors. The political reform that had once ignited a new interest in her life now seemed reduced to narrow schemes for the safety of Florence, a contemptible contradiction to his alternating professions of blind trust in Divine care.

It was inevitable that she should judge the Friar unfairly regarding a question of individual suffering, viewing it through the lens of personal tenderness, while he viewed it through the eyes of theoretic conviction. In his declaration that the cause of his party was the cause of God's kingdom, she heard only the ring of egoism. Perhaps such words are rarely spoken without that meaner undertone; yet they remain the implicit formula of all energetic belief. If such belief, pursuing a grand and remote end, often risks becoming a form of demon-worship where the votary sends his children through the fire with a readiness that hardly resembles sacrifice, then tender fellow-feeling for the near and dear has its own dangers too. It is apt to become timid and skeptical toward the larger aims without which life cannot rise into religion. Thus, poor Romola was being blinded by her tears.

No one who has ever known the agony of losing faith in a fellow-man whom he has profoundly loved and revered will lightly claim that such a shock can leave the faith in the Invisible Goodness unshaken. With the sinking of high human trust, the dignity of life sinks as well; we cease to believe in our own better selves, for that too is part of the common nature degraded in our thoughts, and all the finer impulses of the soul grow dull. Romola felt even the springs of her once active pity drying up, leaving her to barren, egoistic complaining. Had not she suffered her own sorrows? And while she had cared for many, few had cared for her. She had done enough; she had striven for the impossible and was weary of this stifling, crowded existence. She longed for that repose in mere sensation, a dream she had sometimes entertained during the sultry afternoons of her early girlhood, when she had fancied herself floating naively in the waters.

The clear waves seemed to beckon her; she wished she could lie down upon them and pass from sleep into death. Yet Romola could not directly seek death; the fullness of young life within her forbade such a direct act. She could only wish that death would come to her.

At the spot where she paused, the shore curved deeply, and a small boat with a sail was moored there. In her longing to glide over the waters turning golden under the level sun-rays, she recalled a story from Boccaccio that she had loved to dwell upon when her father slept and she crept from her stool to the floor to read the *Decamerone*. It was the tale of the fair Gostanza, who, in her lovesick despair, desired no longer to live but lacked the courage to attack her young life. She had placed herself in a boat, pushed off to sea, and lain down, wrapping her mantle around her head, hoping to be wrecked so that her fear would be helpless to flee from death. The memory had remained a mere thought in Romola's mind, without blossoming into a distinct wish; but now, as she paused again in her pacing to and fro, she saw another boat gliding black against the red gold, carrying a single man toward the bend where the first, smaller boat was moored. Walking on again, she finally saw the man land, pull his boat ashore, and begin to unload cargo. He was perhaps the owner of the smaller boat as well; he would be departing soon, and her opportunity would vanish with him—her chance to purchase that small vessel. She had not yet admitted to herself that she intended to use it, but she felt a sudden eagerness to secure the possibility of using it, revealing the half-unconscious growth of a thought into a desire.

"Is that little boat yours also?" she asked the fisherman, who had looked up, slightly startled by the tall grey figure, and made a reverence to this holy Sister wandering thus mysteriously in the evening solitude.

It was indeed his boat; an old one, hardly seaworthy, yet worth repairing to any man who would buy it. By the blessing of San Antonio, whose chapel stood in the village yonder, his fishing had prospered, and he now possessed a better boat, once belonging to Gianni, who had died. But he had not yet sold the old one. Romola asked him its value, and then, while he was busy, thrust the price into a small satchel lying on the ground, which contained the remnants of his dinner. Afterward, she watched him furl his sail and asked him how he should set it if he wished to go out to sea. Pacing up and down again, she waited to see him depart.

The imagination of herself gliding away in that boat upon the darkening waters grew into a longing, as the thought of a cool brook in sultriness becomes a painful thirst. To be freed from the burden of choice when all motive was bruised, to commit herself, sleeping, to destiny which would either bring death or else new necessities that might rouse a new life in her—it was a thought that beckoned her the more because the soft evening air made her long to rest in the still solitude, rather than return to the noise and heat of the village.

At last, the slow fisherman had gathered up all his belongings and was walking away. Soon the gold was shrinking and growing dusky in the sea and sky, and there was no living thing in sight, no sound but the lulling monotony of the lapping waves. In this sea, there was no tide that would carry her away if she waited for its ebb; but Romola thought the breeze from the land was rising slightly. She stepped into the boat, unfurled the sail, and fastened it as she had learned in that first brief lesson. She saw that it caught the light breeze, and this was all she cared for. Then she loosed the boat from its moorings and tried to urge it with an oar until she was far out from the land, until the sea was dark even to the west, and the stars were disclosing themselves like a palpitating life over the wide heavens. Resting at last, she threw back her cowl and, removing the kerchief underneath that confined her hair, doubled them both under her head for a pillow on one of the boat's ribs. Her fair head was still very young and could bear a hard pillow.

Thus she lay, with the soft night air breathing upon her as she glided on the water, watching the deepening quiet of the sky. She was alone now; she had freed herself from all claims, and she had freed herself even from that burden of choice which presses with heavier and heavier weight when claims have loosened their guiding hold.

Had she found anything like the dream of her girlhood? No. Memories hung upon her like the weight of broken wings that could never be lifted—memories of human sympathy which, even in its pains, leaves a thirst that the Great Mother has no milk to still. Romola felt orphaned in those vast spaces of sea and sky. She read no message of love for her in that far-off symbolic writing of the heavens, and with a great sob, she wished that she might be gliding into death.

She drew the cowl over her head again and covered her face, choosing darkness rather than the light of the stars, which seemed to her like the hard light of eyes looking at her without seeing her. Presently, she felt that she was in the grave, yet not resting there; she was touching the hands of the beloved dead beside her, trying to wake them.
