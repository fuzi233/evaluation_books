Our young Virginian, having won a fair sum from his cousin and the chaplain at cards, felt it only right to give them a chance to reclaim their losses. Yet, had his mother or any of the more upright minds in Virginia seen him thus engaged, they might have frowned upon such a way of life. For Harry Warrington played cards far too often—more than was wise, more than was proper. Beyond the daily rounds of whist and quadrille, which began promptly after dinner at three o’clock and continued until supper, there were private games where fortunes changed hands swiftly and dramatically. All the gentlemen—including the Earl himself—joined in these contests. Since their conversation on Sunday, Lord Castlewood had grown more relaxed and open with his kinsman, betting freely and sharing in games of backgammon and piquet. Mr. William and the pious chaplain, though discreet about it, enjoyed a little hazard now and then—though this pastime remained a secret from the ladies, who had repeatedly demanded promises from Cousin Will that he would neither lead the Virginian into mischief nor involve himself in such matters. And so Will gave his word: he would never play—never. But as soon as the household retired for the night, he would slip away with a dice box and a bottle of rum to Cousin Harry’s quarters, where he, Hal, and the reverend gentleman would sit up through the hours, playing until dawn.

When Harry described his mother’s estate in America to Lord Castlewood, he meant no deception. He was not one to boast, nor to fabricate; his nature was honest and straightforward. But truth be told, life in Virginia had exposed him to all manner of rough company—horsemen, tavern idlers, gamblers, and sporting men, many of whom thrived in his native colony. With a landed aristocracy relying on enslaved laborers to cultivate tobacco and corn, entertainment was limited to hunting or games of chance around the punch bowl. Hospitality in the province knew no bounds—every man’s house was open to his neighbor—and idle gentlemen rode from one great mansion to another, finding much the same pleasures, welcomes, and coarse comforts wherever they went. The Virginian squire might ride a cobbled saddle and keep a barefoot valet, but within his ramshackle fences there was always plenty of corn for the horses, venison for the table, and liquor enough to last through the longest night. Harry had slept on straw mattresses more than once, and spent countless nights drinking claret and punch from cracked bowls until the hounds called them out again. His younger brother, by contrast, was sober and reserved—a fact Harry acknowledged with quiet regret. Such is nature’s way: some are drawn to books and tea, others to wine and wild rides across the countryside. It was clear from the start that Harry’s tastes were not those of the Puritan mind. None of his new English companions found fault with him for lacking refinement; indeed, they admired him all the more for not being a milksop. Manners, after all, were looser a century ago—tongues ran free, names were freely spoken, and deeds were done that we today would find shocking. Yes, madam, we are not like our ancestors. Should we not be grateful that time has made us so much more virtuous?

Keenly aware of those around him, and sensing rightly that his cousins sought to probe him, Harry chose to remain cautious about his own affairs. In every game of chance and every contest of sport, he proved himself no less capable than the three men he had come to know. Even in the noble game of billiards, after only a few days, he could hold his own against his cousins and their reverend pastor. His grandfather had loved the game, and had brought over from Europe one of the rare tables in the King’s province of Virginia. Though Mr. Will could best him at first, he could not gain unfair odds. After their initial wager, Harry became more guarded, and Will admitted—though reluctantly—that the American was his equal in most things, and perhaps even superior in some. Yet despite Harry’s skill—so sharp that he could beat the parson and soon match Will, who naturally outplayed both girls—why was it that in matches against the ladies, especially one among them, Mr. Warrington often came off second-best? He was profoundly courteous to every woman who wore a petticoat—an old-fashioned politeness still alive in his homeland. All the women of Castlewood adored the young man. The stern housekeeper softened in his presence; the plump cook greeted him with warm smiles; the maids, whether French or English, giggled and blushed when he passed; even the pretty daughter of the lodge keeper always had a kind word for him. Madame de Bernstein observed all this closely, though she said nothing. She watched the boy’s behavior, noting every nuance of his character.

Who can say how old Lady Maria Esmond truly was? Books of the Peerage were scarce in those days, and I cannot fix her age within a few years, let alone a decade. When Will claimed she was thirty-five, he was exaggerating—though he was known for such excesses. Maria was Will’s half-sister, born of the late Lord Castlewood’s first wife, a German lady whom he married during Queen Anne’s wars. Baron Bernstein, who wedded Maria’s aunt Beatrix—the widow of Bishop Tusher—was also a German nobleman, a Hanoverian relative of the first Lady Castlewood. If Maria was born under George I., and if George II. had reigned thirty years, how could she possibly be twenty-seven, as she told Harry? “I am old, child,” she used to say, calling him “child” when they were alone. “I am a hundred years old. I am twenty-seven. I might almost be your mother.” To which Harry would reply, “Your ladyship might be the mother of all the cupids, I’m sure. You don’t look twenty, on my word you do not!”

Lady Maria looked any age you pleased. Fair of face, with a dazzling complexion of white and red, she had a cascade of golden hair flowing over her shoulders and arms so round and lovely that they drew admiration whenever she played billiards with cousin Harry. When she stretched across the table to take a shot, Harry caught sight of a delicate ankle, a clocked stocking, and a small black satin slipper with a red heel—sights that filled him with unspoken delight. He swore there was no such foot, ankle, or slipper in the world. Yet, foolish boy! Your mother’s foot was slimmer, half an inch shorter than Lady Maria’s. But boys do not gaze upon their mothers’ ankles with such rapture.

No doubt Lady Maria was kind to Harry when they were alone. Before her sister, aunt, or stepmother, however, she mocked him—calling him a simpleton, a chit, and worse. Behind his back, and sometimes even to his face, she imitated his provincial accent, which carried the faintest trace of Virginia. Harry blushed and tried to correct his speech under the guidance of his English tutors. His aunt declared they would soon make him a fine gentleman.

Lord Castlewood, as we’ve said, grew increasingly familiar and friendly with his guest. Until the harvest was complete, there was little sport save occasional cockfights in Winchester and bull-baiting at Hexton Fair. Harry and Will rode together to many jolly fairs and races nearby. The young Virginian was introduced to several county families—the Henleys of the Grange, the Crawleys of Queen’s Crawley, the Redmaynes of Lionsden. Neighbors arrived in their heavy coaches, staying two or three days in country fashion. More would have come, had it not been for the fear the Castlewood family held of offending Madame de Bernstein. She disliked rural society; the rough talk and manners annoyed her. “We shall be merrier when my aunt leaves us,” the young people whispered. “We have cause, as you may imagine, for being very civil to her. You know what a favorite she was with our father? And with reason. She helped secure his earldom, being well connected at Court with the King and Queen. She commands here naturally—perhaps too much. We are all afraid of her. Even my elder brother fears her, and my stepmother obeys her more than she ever did my father, whom she ruled with an iron rod. But Castlewood is merrier when our aunt is not here. At least, we have more company. You will come to us in our gay days, Harry, won’t you? Of course you will: this is your home, sir. I was so pleased—oh, so pleased—when my brother said he considered it your home!”

A soft hand reached out after this tender speech. A pair of bright blue eyes, remarkably preserved, gazed warmly at him. Harry seized his cousin’s hand with eagerness. I cannot say what privileges of kinship he might have wished to claim, except that he was too shy. They call the English selfish and cold. Harry had thought so at first—but how mistaken he was! How kind and affectionate they were, especially the Earl—and dear, dear Maria! He longed to recall the letter he had written to Mrs. Mountain and his mother, in which he hinted that his welcome had been chilly. The Earl, his cousin, was everything kind—promising to introduce him to London society, present him at Court, and take him to White’s. He was to regard Castlewood as his English home. He had been rash in judging his relatives in Hampshire. All this, along with many expressions of remorse, he wrote in his second letter to Virginia. And he added—since it has been noted that the young gentleman did not yet spell with perfect accuracy—“My cousin, the Lady Maria, is a perfect Angle.”

“Ille praeter omnes angulus ridet,” muttered little Mr. Dempster, back in Virginia.

“The child can’t be falling in love with his angle, as he calls her!” exclaimed Mountain.

“Pooh, pooh! My niece Maria is forty!” said Madam Esmond. “I perfectly remember her when I was at home—a tall, awkward, carrot-haired creature, with feet like bellows.” Where, indeed, is truth? And who knows it? Is beauty truly beautiful, or is it only our eyes that make it so? Does Venus squint? Does she have a splay foot, red hair, a crooked back? Anoint my eyes, good Fairy Puck, so that I may always see the Beloved Object as a paragon! Above all, keep anointing my mistress’s dainty peepers with the strongest ointment, so that my head may always appear lovely to her, and she may continue to crown my honest ears with fresh roses!

Not only was Harry Warrington beloved by some in the drawing room and all the ladies of the servants’ hall, but his valet, Gumbo, was equally admired and respected by many in the domestic circle. Gumbo possessed a hundred talents. He was renowned as a fisherman, hunter, and blacksmith. He could style hair beautifully, learning the art under the Earl’s Swiss valet. He excelled in cooking Virginian dishes and picked up new culinary skills from the Earl’s French chef. We have heard how melodiously he sang at church; he sang sacred music as well as secular, often composing rude lyrics in the manner of his people. He played the fiddle so enchantingly that he set all the girls dancing in Castlewood Hall, and was always welcomed with a free mug of ale at the Three Castles inn, provided he brought his instrument. He was good-natured and delighted in playing for village children. Thus, Mr. Warrington’s negro servant was a universal favorite throughout the Castlewood domain.

Yet it was not hard for the servants’ hall to recognize that Mr. Gumbo was a liar—though this flaw did not diminish his charm. For example, at church, when he pretended to read from Molly’s psalm book, he sang entirely different words—words he could not possibly have read, since he could not decipher a single syllable. He claimed to understand music, so the Swiss valet handed him a score, and Master Gumbo turned the page upside down. Such instances of exaggeration occurred daily and were plain to all in the Castlewood household. They knew Gumbo lied, though they did not seem to disapprove—perhaps because they liked to believe him.

Whatever wonder or envy Gumbo may have felt upon seeing the splendor of Castlewood, he kept silent. Instead, he examined the place with cool detachment—the park, the appointments, the stables. The horses, he said, were decent, though there were fewer than at Castlewood in Virginia, where they had six times as many and fourteen or eighteen grooms to care for them. Madam Esmond’s carriages were far finer than the Earl’s—much more gold on the panels. Her gardens covered acres and grew every kind of flower and fruit under the sun. Pineapples and peaches? Those were so common they were fed to pigs in his country. They had twenty or forty gardeners—all black gentlemen, like himself. Inside the house were twenty or forty men in livery, besides countless women-servants—too many to count. “Tink dere were fifty women-servants,” he said. “All Madam Esmond’s property, worth ever so many hundred pieces of eight apiece.” How much was a piece of eight? Bigger than a guinea, he said. “Tink Madam Esmond have twenty or thirty thousand guineas a year—whole rooms full of gold and plate. Came to England in one of her ships—have ever so many ships, Gumbo can’t count how many. And estates, covered all over with tobacco and negroes, reaching out for a week’s journey.” Was Master Harry heir to all this? Of course—he was now, since Master George was killed and scalped by the Indians. Gumbo had killed many Indians and tried to save Master George, but he was Master Harry’s man. And Master Harry was as rich—oh, as rich as he liked. He wore black now because Master George was dead, but you should see his chests full of gold clothes, lace, and jewels at Bristol. Of course, Master Harry was the richest man in Virginia, and might have twenty or sixty servants—though he preferred traveling with just one, and that one, need scarcely be said, was Gumbo.

This tale was not invented at once, but slowly pieced together from Mr. Gumbo, who may have contradicted himself at first. But by the time he had repeated it twice or thrice in the servants’ hall or the butler’s private room, he had mastered his story—knowing exactly how many slaves Madam Esmond owned and how much income she earned. The truth was, four or five blacks could do the work of one white man, so American households employed far more servants than English ones. Like most Virginian landowners, Madam Esmond’s estate teemed with negroes.

Gumbo’s account of his mistress’s wealth reached the Earl through his man, and Madame de Bernstein and the ladies through their waiting-women. And, we may be certain, the story lost nothing in translation. A young gentleman in England is not less liked because he is believed to inherit vast riches. When Lady Castlewood heard of Harry’s enormous expectations, she regretted her initial coldness toward him—and her harsh pinch of her daughter’s arm when Fanny reached out to greet him too warmly. Was it too late to welcome him back into those fair arms? Lady Fanny was invited to try, and resumed her dancing lessons. The Countess offered to play the piano with all her heart. But how irritating that sentimental Maria always insisted on being present! As sure as Fanny walked in the gardens, so sure would her sister follow. As for Madame de Bernstein, she laughed and enjoyed the tales of her Virginian relatives’ immense fortune. She knew her half-sister’s banker in London and likely understood the true state of Madam Esmond’s finances. But she did not deny the rumors—nor did she suppress the amusement she took in watching how the reports affected the family’s treatment of their young kinsman.

“Hang him! Is he so rich, Molly?” asked the Earl of his elder sister. “Then farewell to our chances with your aunt. The Baroness will leave him all her money out of spite, knowing he doesn’t need it. Still, the lad is a good fellow enough. It’s not his fault he’s rich, you know.”

“He is very modest and simple in his habits for one so wealthy,” remarked Maria.

“Rich men often are,” replied the Earl. “If I were rich, I’d be the greatest miser, living in rags and eating bread. There’s no pleasure so lasting as making money. It grows on you, increases with age. But since I’m as poor as Lazarus, I dress in purple and fine linen, and feast every day.”

Maria went to the library and found *The History of Virginia* by R. B. Gent. She read about the province’s excellent climate, its abundance of fruit and grain, and the noble rivers—Potomac and Rappahannock—teeming with fish. She wondered if the climate would suit her, and whether her aunt would approve. And Harry was certain his mother would adore her—as would Mountain. When asked about the number of his mother’s servants, he said they certainly had more than in England—he didn’t know how many. But the negroes did not work as efficiently as English servants, hence the need for so many. As for other details from Gumbo, Harry laughed and said the boy was a wonderful storyteller—probably trying to speak for the honor of the family.

So Harry was modest as well as rich! His denials only strengthened his relatives’ belief in his vast inheritance. The Countess and the ladies grew ever more affectionate toward him. Mr. Will bet with him more frequently and tried to sell him bargains. Harry’s plain clothing and modest equipage only deepened the impression of his wealth. To see a young man of his rank traveling with only one servant, without horses or a carriage of his own—what humility! When he went to London, he would surely cut a better figure. Of course he would. Castlewood would introduce him to the finest society in the capital, and he would appear as he ought at St.7 James’s. No one could be more pleasant, wicked, lively, or attentive than the chaplain, Mr. Sampson. How proud he would be to show his young friend a bit of London life—to warn off rogues and keep him safe from harm! Mr. Sampson was kind; everyone was kind. Harry liked the respect shown him. As Madam Esmond’s son, he thought it perhaps his due. He accepted the image others had of him as the personage they imagined him to be. How could he know otherwise? He had never seen beyond his own province. Why should he not respect his condition when others respected it so highly? So the small circle at Castlewood House, and then the villagers, and finally the whole county, came to believe that Mr. Harry Esmond Warrington was heir to immense wealth and a man of great importance—because his negro valet had told lies in the servants’ hall.

After a fortnight, Harry’s aunt, Madame de Bernstein, began to tire of Castlewood and its inhabitants. This clever woman tired of most things and people sooner or later. She started nodding off during the chaplain’s stories, dozing at whist and over dinner, and speaking sharply to her Esmond nephews and nieces. She launched barbs at the Earl and his brother the jockey, at the widowed and unmarried ladies, who winced under her scornful remarks and endured them as best they could. The cook, once praised, now failed her; the wine was corked; the house was damp, dreary, full of drafts; doors wouldn’t shut, chimneys smoked. She began to think Tunbridge waters essential for her health and ordered the doctor from Hexton to prescribe them.

“I wish to heaven she would go!” growled the Earl, the most independent member of the family. “She may go to Tunbridge, Bath, or Jericho—for me.”

“Shall Fanny and I come with you to Tunbridge, dear Baroness?” asked Lady Castlewood.

“Not for worlds, my dear! The doctor orders absolute quiet. If you came, I’d have the knocker ringing all day, and Fanny’s lovers would never leave the house,” answered the Baroness, thoroughly weary of her sister-in-law’s company.

“I wish I could be of service to my aunt,” said the sentimental Lady Maria, demurely.

“My good child, what can you do for me? You cannot play piquet as well as my maid, and I’ve heard all your songs till I’m tired of them! One of the gentlemen might accompany me—at least make the journey and see me safely from highwaymen.”

“I’m sure I’ll be glad to ride with you,” said Mr. Will.

“Oh, not you! I don’t want you, William,” cried the young man’s aunt. “Why don’t you offer? Where are your American manners, you ungracious Harry Warrington? Don’t swear, Will—Harry is much better company than you are, and much better ton too, sir.”

“Ton, indeed! Confound his ton,” muttered envious Will to himself.

“I dare say I’ll be tired of him, as I am of others,” continued the Baroness. “I’ve hardly seen Harry at all these past days. You shall ride with me to Tunbridge, Harry!”

At this direct appeal—and to no one’s greater surprise than her own—Mr. Harry Warrington blushed, hemmed and hawed, and finally said, “I’ve promised my cousin Castlewood to go to Hexton Petty Sessions with him tomorrow. He thinks I should see how the courts operate here—and—and—the partridge-shooting will soon begin, and I’ve promised to be here for that, ma'am.” Saying this, Harry turned as red as a poppy, while Lady Maria lowered her eyes and busied herself with her needlework.

“You actually refuse to go with me to Tunbridge Wells?” exclaimed Madame Bernstein, her eyes flashing, her face flushing with anger.

“Not to ride with you, ma'am—I’d be honored to do that. But to stay there—I’ve promised…”
