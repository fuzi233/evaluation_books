Pen had been bumbling through his tiny, cheap, delightfully cozy little streak of revels for months now, completely oblivious that a giant metaphorical guillotine was hanging over his last crumbs of cash, waiting to chop all his frolics short at any second. His funds were down to the dregs. His club subscription had already eaten a full third of his remaining money; he’d splurged on cute little knickknacks to make his dingy rented bedsit feel fancy, and now all that was left in his pocketbook was a single crumpled five-pound note, with absolutely zero plan to replace it. The poor fool had been raised like a coddled royal toddler, never once having to fend for himself or wonder where next week’s grocery money came from.

Warrington had no clue his best mate was basically one pint of porter away from being fully skint. Pen was an only kid, after all, with a widowed mom in the country and a dandy old uncle who dined with lords every other night, so for all Warrington knew the guy had a secret fortune stashed under his mattress. He carried a gold chain shiny enough for a duke, owned a dressing case fit for a peer, and while he’d happily chow down on a plate of cheap cookshop beef and a pint of porter with zero complaints, he couldn’t be arsed with the tiny penny-pinching bits of real life. He’d never tip a waiter twopence if his life depended on it, would hail a cab the second a single raindrop hit his shoulder, and overpaid every driver by sixpence out of sheer laziness. He scoffed at people who rewashed their gloves to make them last, and couldn’t say no to any beggar with a half-sad story or a pair of big piteous urchin eyes. If he’d been born to ten thousand a year he couldn’t have been more free-handed, and half the time he suspected he was doing it just to get a little extra praise from the waiters and cabbies. None of us are actually as in touch with our own dumb motives as we like to pretend, after all.

Warrington had no idea Pen’s finances were circling the drain, and Pen had been too embarrassed to spill the tea. His friend knew Pen had been a wild, overspending idiot at Oxbridge like every other college lad, but he had no clue just how much cash the kid had blown, or how little his mom actually had to spare.

The truth finally tumbled out one evening, when Pen was glumly prodding that last five-pound note across the pub tray next to Warrington’s tankard of ale.

“It’s the last rose of summer,” Pen moaned. “All its blooming mates are long gone, and this last one’s about to shed its leaves for good.” And then he spilled the whole sad saga: his mom’s tiny income, all his stupid college follies, how Laura had bailed him out last time he was broke. Warrington just puffed his pipe the whole time, listening intently, and knocked the ash out when Pen finished talking.

“Being skint’ll do you a world of good, mate,” he said. “There’s no better tonic for an honest bloke than owing people a few quid. It’s like a workout for your morals! Suddenly you’re staring down your problems head on like you’re about to jump a fence on horseback, and it forces you to dig up whatever pluck you’ve got hiding under that soft coddled skin. You’ll learn real quick how much stupid crap you don’t actually need. No more new patent boots, no overpriced cologne, no cabs every time it drizzles. You’ve been spoiled rotten by women your whole life, Pen. Any healthy guy with half a brain who can’t earn a crust in this world deserves to throw himself off Waterloo Bridge, or steal a leg of mutton and get transported out of the country. I’ve spoken. Pass the ale.”

“Easy for you to say!” Pen huffed. “Beef and bread don’t grow on trees, you know, you have to pay for ’em with work or cash. And who the hell’s gonna hire me? What work do I even know how to do?”

Warrington cackled so hard he spilled a little ale down his shirt. “We could stick an ad in the Times for a tutor job at some stuffy boarding school! *Gentleman, B.A. of St Boniface’s, who spectacularly flunked his degree, offers lessons in classics, math, and rudimentary French. Can also cut pupils’ hair and play second piano for the headmaster’s daughters.*”

“Go to hell,” Pen grumbled.

“People make a living out of way dumber stuff!” Warrington went on. “Your mate Bloundell-Bloundell’s a professional card cheat who wanders the continent fleeces rich young idiots. My old school pal Bob O’Toole drives the Ballynafad mail coach now. I knew a doctor’s son once who got so broke he grew a silly mustache, moved to a small town, introduced himself as Professor Spineto, chiropodist to the Emperor of All the Russias, and cured the local newspaper editor’s corn to build up a client base. He lived like a respectable man for three years until he made up with his dad and inherited all his medical supplies.”

“Corns, card tricks, driving a mail coach? None of that’s for me!” Pen said, kicking the leg of the table.

“Then there’s writing,” Warrington shrugged. “Before you told me you were broke I fully assumed you were a rich aristocrat. Your whole fancy vibe makes everyone think you’ve got a fortune stashed away. And for the record? I’ve been writing for cash for years. I don’t tell anyone, because I’m a stubborn idiot who doesn’t want people saying George Warrington scribbles articles for bread. But I write legal reviews, and columns for a friend’s newspaper, *The Dawn*.” He dragged Pen to their club later that week, showed him the file of the paper, and pointed out his sharp, sarcastic, brilliantly written pieces that Pen immediately loved.

Pen shook his head, starry-eyed. “I could never write that. I don’t know jack about politics or history. All I’ve got is a little smattering of poetry.”

“Nah, your stuff’s better,” Warrington said, clapping him on the shoulder. “Those little verses and scraps of yours I’ve read? You’ve got a real natural poetic gift, not the boring plodding stuff I churn out. You’re a poet, you dork.”

Pen got so happy he almost cried. “Wait—you loved *Ariadne in Naxos*, right? I wrote that when I was 18! And the college prize poem?”

Warrington burst out laughing so loud the whole pub turned to look. “That garbage? *Ariadne in Naxos* is the mushiest, most overwrought slop I’ve ever read. Your prize poem is so pompous and dull it’s a miracle they didn’t give you the medal for how boring it was. You’re not gonna out-write Milton, you silly little wren, but you *can* churn out a pretty magazine article and a half-decent set of verses. That’s a perfect living for you.”

Pen jumped up, stamped his foot. “I’ll show you I’m way better than you think!” Warrington just snickered and blew 24 rapid puffs out of his pipe as a reply.

Opportunity knocked faster than Pen expected. Big-shot publisher Mr. Bacon (formerly Bacon and Bungay) of Paternoster Row put out a fancy gilt-edged book every spring called the *Spring Annual*, edited by the posh Lady Violet Lebas, full of verses from the fanciest poets and rich young lords of the day. Plates for the illustrations were engraved months in advance, so the poets had to write to fit the pictures, not the other way around. One day Warrington popped into Bacon’s office to see his manager Hack, and found him panicking over a half-finished page: Percy Popjoy had turned in the absolute worst set of verses ever written by a nobleman, for a new illustration called *The Church Porch*. It was so bad Lady Violet refused to put her name next to it, and rival publisher Bungay was putting out a competing annual that was eating up all the hype. They’d already spent 60 pounds on the plate, they couldn’t scrap it.

“I know a guy who can write you decent verses,” Warrington said, tucking the plate in his pocket. “I’ll drop them off tomorrow, you pay him properly, yeah?”

He went straight home, shoved the plate at Pen, and said “Go earn your dinner, idiot.” Pen stared at the picture: a pretty young girl hurrying into a church with her prayer book, a boy hidden in a niche watching her with all the pining in the world. He sat up all night cranking out verses, and handed them to Warrington at two in the morning. They were perfect. Pen dug up another old ballad from his notebook, and the two of them marched down to Paternoster Row. Pen was too nervous to go inside the shop, so he paced up and down the street staring at all the wild book displays: ancient black letter tomes next to penny true crime rags next to dissenting preacher pamphlets next to cheap Catholic saint prints next to anti-Catholic sermons. Every opinion under the sun had its little shop under the bells of St Paul’s.

Inside, Warrington pulled off the greatest bluff of his life. He perched on the edge of the table, left his hat on (something Bacon, who loved being treated rudely by upper class guys, ate right up), and told the publisher that Arthur Pendennis was a rich aristocrat, cousin to Lord Pontypool, who dined at Gaunt House every single week. He threatened to take Pen’s verses straight to Bungay if Bacon tried to pay him with a free copy of the book. Bacon caved instantly, wrote Pen a handsome check no questions asked.

When Warrington walked out grinning and handed him the check, Pen screamed so loud passersby stared. He immediately wanted to treat Warrington to a fancy dinner at Richmond, buy his mom and Laura fancy gifts, and yelled that he’d never have to sponge off his family ever again. “I’m gonna make my own way! I’m gonna be famous!”

“Sure, you are,” Warrington laughed, clapping him on the back. “Soon you’ll have a mansion in Belgrave Square and a castle in the air, no doubt. But for what it’s worth? Bread you earn yourself always tastes better than free bread your mom gives you.” They headed back to the club, cracked open a giant magnum of claret, and toasted the *Spring Annual*.

It never rains but it pours, as the saying goes. A few days later, a tiny ragged printer’s devil banged on their chambers door, handed Pen a letter, mumbled “From Captain Shandon, sir,” and then immediately curled up on the bench in the passage and fell fast asleep, like he’d done a hundred times before. The letter read:

MY DEAR SIR,—Bungay will be here to-day, about the Pall Mall Gazette. You would be the very man to help us with a genuine West-end article,—you understand—dashing, trenchant, and damned aristocratic. Lady Hipshaw will write; but she’s not much you know, and we’ve two lords; but the less they do the better. We must have you. We’ll give you your own terms, and we’ll make a hit with the Gazette.

Shall B. come and see you, or can you look in upon me here?—Ever yours,
