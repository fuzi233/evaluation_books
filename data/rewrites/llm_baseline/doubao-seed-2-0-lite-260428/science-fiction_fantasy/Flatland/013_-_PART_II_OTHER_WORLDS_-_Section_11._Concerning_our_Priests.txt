It is high time I set aside these scattered, casual notes on daily life in Flatland, to turn to the central event of this whole book: my initiation into the mind-bending mysteries of Space. That is the story I was born to tell, and every word that came before it is nothing more than preface.

That is why I must pass over a hundred small oddities of our 2D existence that I flatter myself would fascinate you: the way we propel ourselves to move, and grind to a full stop, with no feet to speak of; the trick that lets us anchor structures of wood, stone, or brick firmly in place, even though we have no hands, cannot dig deep foundations as you do in Spaceland, nor leverage the sideways weight of earth to hold our constructions steady; the quirk of our atmosphere that makes rain fall only in the gaps between our north-south zones, so the northernmost regions never hoard all the moisture and leave the south high and dry. I will not linger on the shape of our hills and mines, our trees and crop cycles, our short growing seasons and annual harvests, or our strange line-based alphabet, built for writing on narrow linear tablets. I name all these details now only to make clear that their omission is not from any forgetfulness on my part: I simply respect your time, and would not pad this account with trivialities when the great truth of Space waits to be told.

Yet before I dive into that subject, I owe you a few final remarks on the pillars and mainstays of Flatland’s entire constitution: the forces that govern our conduct, shape every part of our destiny, and are objects of near-universal adoration. Need I spell it out? I speak of our Circles—our Priests.

Do not mistake our Priests for the mere religious clerics you know in your 3D world. Among us, the Priests are administrators of every sector of business, art, and science. They direct all trade, all military strategy, all architecture and civil engineering, every school curriculum, every law passed by our legislature, all social morality, and all theological doctrine. They do no tedious manual labor themselves, of course—they are the source, the guiding cause, of every single worthwhile achievement that every other class of Flatlander ever undertakes.

The common folk of Flatland will call any high-sided ruling figure a perfect Circle, but our educated elite know the truth: no shape among us is a perfect Circle. Every so-called Circle is nothing more than a polygon with an extraordinarily large number of extraordinarily tiny sides. As side count climbs higher and higher, a polygon grows closer and closer to a true curve. When the number of sides runs into the hundreds, even the most sensitive touch would struggle to pick out any sharp polygonal angles at all. I say “would”—for as I explained earlier, the act of Recognition by Touch is considered unforgivably vulgar in high society, and to dare to reach out and Feel a Circle to identify his shape is regarded as an act of unforgivable audacity. This widespread custom of never touching a member of the priestly class lets every Circle wrap the exact count of his sides in a thick, enduring veil of mystery from the day he is born.

With the average perimeter of a grown Circle sitting at three full feet, that means a 300-sided polygon would have each side no longer than one hundredth of a foot, barely a tenth of an inch long. A polygon with 600 or 700 sides would have individual sides no bigger than the head of a common Spaceland pin. By universal, unchallenged courtesy, the reigning Chief Circle of our nation is always assumed to have no less than ten thousand sides.

The steady climb of priestly descendants up the social ladder does not follow the same Law of Nature that restricts all lower regular classes to gaining no more than one new side per generation. If that rule applied here, a Circle’s side count would be nothing more than a matter of simple arithmetic, and the 497th grandchild of an equilateral triangle would automatically be a perfect 500-sided polygon. That is not the case. Nature has laid down two conflicting rules that govern the propagation of the near-Circle ranks: first, the higher a bloodline climbs on the scale of side-count nobility, the faster their rate of side growth speeds up with each new generation; second, the higher that same bloodline rises, the more infertile they become. That is why a 400 or 500-sided noble almost never births a son, let alone two sons. But the rare son that a 500-sided polygon does sire has been recorded to enter the world already boasting 550, even 600 sides, skipping centuries of slow, incremental generational growth in a single leap.

Art steps in to accelerate this higher evolution even further. Our top physicians have discovered that the tiny, soft, still-growing sides of a newborn high-class polygon, no more than a month old, can be carefully fractured and precisely reset to double or triple their side count in one single procedure, letting a child leap over hundreds of generations of slow inheritance, effectively doubling the nobility of his bloodline overnight.

This process is far from safe, of course. Barely one in ten of these infants survive the procedure. Hundreds of promising, high-born children are lost to complications every year. Yet the ambition of those near-Circle nobles perched right on the cusp of the priestly class is so fierce that you will almost never find one of them who has not signed their firstborn up for treatment at the renowned Circular Neo-Therapeutic Gymnasium, before the baby is even a month old.

One year after the procedure, success or failure is final. More often than not, the child will be laid to rest in one of the overcrowded tombs that fill the Neo-Therapeutic Cemetery. But on very rare occasions, a cheering, jubilant procession will carry the tiny survivor back to his overjoyed parents: no longer a mere 200 or 300 sided noble, but a full, courtesy-recognized Circle, raised to the highest possible rank of Flatland society. Even one such miraculous success is enough to convince thousands of other polygonal parents to gamble their own firstborn’s life for the same glorious, almost impossible prize.
