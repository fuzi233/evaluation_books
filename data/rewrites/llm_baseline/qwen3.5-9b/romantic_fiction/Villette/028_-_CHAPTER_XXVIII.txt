M. Paul Emanuel possessed an acute sensitivity to interruptions during his lessons. To traverse the classroom under such circumstances was, in the estimation of both teachers and students, tantamount to risking one's life. Madame Beck, if compelled to enter, would "skurry" through, retreating her skirts and carefully navigating the formidable platform like a ship fearing breaking waves. As for Rosine, the portress—who bore the terrifying duty every half-hour of fetching pupils from the depths of various divisions to attend music lessons elsewhere—she would, after her second or third attempt, frequently become tongue-tied with sheer consternation. This panic was fueled by the unspeakable glares directed at her through a pair of dart-like spectacles.

One morning, I sat in the *carré*, working on a piece of embroidery begun but unfinished by a pupil. While my fingers moved across the frame, my ears were treated to the crescendo of a voice haranguing in the neighboring class. The tone grew increasingly agitated, ominously varied. Though a sturdy partition wall separated me from the gathering storm, and a glass door offered a swift escape route to the courtyard, I found myself deriving more amusement than alarm from the thickening atmosphere. Poor Rosine was not safe; that blessed morning she had already made the perilous crossing four times. Now, for the fifth time, it fell to her to snatch a brand from the burning—a pupil from directly under M. Paul's nose.

"Mon Dieu! Mon Dieu!" she cried. "What shall I become? Monsieur will surely kill me; he is truly furious!"

Driven by desperate courage, she opened the door.

"Mademoiselle La Malle au piano!" she shouted.

Before she could retreat or fully close the door, the voice erupted:

"Dès ce moment! The class is forbidden. The first to open this door or pass through this division shall be hanged—even if it be Madame Beck herself!"

Less than ten minutes after this decree, Rosine's French slippers shuffled again along the corridor.

"Mademoiselle," she said, "I would not, for a five-franc piece, enter that class again just now. Monsieur's spectacles are truly terrible. Here is a commissionaire with a message from the Athénée. I have told Madame Beck I dare not deliver it, and she says I must charge you with it."

"Me? No, that is too bad! It is not within my duties. Come, Rosine, bear your own burden. Be brave—go once more!"

"I, Mademoiselle? Impossible! Five times today I have crossed him. Madame must hire a gendarme for this service. Oh, I cannot endure it anymore!"

"Bah! You are simply a coward. What is the message?"

"Precisely the sort Monsieur dislikes most: an urgent summons to go directly to the Athénée, as an official visitor—an inspector, I know not what—has arrived, and Monsieur *must* meet him. You know how he hates a 'must'."

Indeed, I knew well enough. That restive little man detested any spur or curb; against anything urgent or obligatory, he was bound to revolt. Nevertheless, I accepted the responsibility—not without fear, though fear was blended with curiosity. I opened the door, entered, and closed it behind me as quickly and quietly as my somewhat unsteady hand would allow. To be slow, bustling, rattling a latch, or leaving a door gaping wide were aggravations of crime often more disastrous than the act itself.

There I stood, and there he sat. His humor was visibly poor, bordering on its worst. He had been giving a lesson in arithmetic—a dry subject that invariably disagreed with him; no pupil trembled more when he spoke of figures. Bent over his desk, he refused to look up at the sound of my entrance, a direct breach of his will and law, finding the effort impossible for the moment. This was fortunate; it gave me time to walk up the long aisle. It suited my nature far better to confront the near-burst of anger up close than to bear its menace from a distance.

Pausing at his *estrade*, just in front, I waited. Naturally, I was not worthy of immediate attention, so he continued his lesson. Disdain would not suffice; he must hear and answer my message.

Not being tall enough to peer over his elevated desk, I ventured to peek around, intending merely to get a better view of his face. Upon entering, it had struck me as bearing a close, picturesque resemblance to a black and sallow tiger. Twice I enjoyed this side view with impunity, advancing and receding unseen. On the third attempt, as my eye barely cleared the obscuration of the desk, it was caught and transfixed through its very pupil—pierced by the "lunettes." Rosine was right; these instruments held a blank, immutable terror beyond the mobile wrath of the wearer's unglazed eyes.

However, I now found the advantage of proximity. These short-sighted spectacles were useless for inspecting a criminal standing right under Monsieur's nose. Accordingly, he doffed them, and we stood on more equal terms.

I am glad I was not truly terrified of him. Indeed, close in his presence, I felt no terror at all. When he demanded the cord and gibbet to execute the sentence recently pronounced, I was able to furnish him with a needleful of embroidery thread with such accommodating civility that it could not but allay some portion of his surplus irritation. Of course, I did not parade this courtesy publicly; I merely handed the thread around the corner of the desk and attached it, ready-noosed, to the barred back of the Professor's chair.

"Que me voulez-vous?" he grumbled, the sound confined entirely to his chest and throat, his teeth clenched. He seemed registering an inward vow that nothing earthly should wring a smile from him.

My answer began uncompromisingly: "Monsieur," I said, "I want the impossible, unheard-of things." Thinking it best not to mince matters, but to administer the "shock" with decision, I delivered the Athenian message in a low but quick voice, floridly exaggerating its urgency.

Of course, he would not hear a word. He would not go; he would not leave his present class, regardless of what all the officials of Villette sent for him. He would not move an inch at the bidding of kings, cabinets, and chambers combined.

I knew, however, that he *must* go. Both duty and interest commanded immediate compliance. Standing silently, as if he had not yet spoken, I waited. He asked what more I wanted.

"Only Monsieur's answer to deliver to the commissionaire."

He waved an impatient negative.

Venturing to stretch my hand toward the *bonnet-grec* lying in grim repose on the windowsill, he followed this daring movement with his eye, no doubt in mixed pity and amazement at its presumption.

"Ah!" he muttered. "If it came to that—if Miss Lucy meddled with his *bonnet-grec*—she might just put it on herself, turn *garçon* for the occasion, and benevolently go to the Athénée in his stead."

With great respect, I laid the bonnet on the desk, where its tassel seemed to give me an awful nod.

"I'll write a note of apology—that will do!" he insisted, still bent on evasion.

Knowing well it would *not* do, I gently pushed the bonnet toward his hand. Thus impelled, it slid down the polished slope of the varnished, unbaized desk, carrying before it the light steel-framed "lunettes." Fearful to relate, they fell onto the *estrade*. A dozen times before I had seen them fall without damage—but this time, as Lucy Snowe's hapless luck would have it, they fell so that each clear pebble became a shattered, shapeless star.

Now, indeed, dismay seized me—dismay and regret. I knew the value of these spectacles; M. Paul's sight was peculiar, difficult to fit, and these glasses suited him perfectly. He had called them his treasures. As I picked them up, cracked and worthless, my hand trembled. Frightened through all my nerves at the mischief I had caused, I think I was even more sorry than afraid. For several seconds, I dared not look the bereaved Professor in the face. He was the first to speak.

"Là!" he said. "Behold, I am widowed of my spectacles! I think Mademoiselle Lucy will now confess that the cord and gallows are amply earned; she trembles in anticipation of her doom. Ah, traitress! traitress! Are you resolved to leave me quite blind and helpless in your hands?"

I lifted my eyes. His face, instead of being irate, lowering, and furrowed, was overflowing with a smile colored by the bloom I had seen brightening it that evening at the Hôtel Crécy. He was not angry—not even grieved. Regarding the real injury, he showed himself full of clemency; under the real provocation, patient as a saint. This event, which seemed so untoward and which I thought had ruined my chances of successful persuasion, proved my greatest aid. Difficult to manage so long as I had done him no harm, he became graciously pliant as soon as I stood in his presence a conscious and contrite offender.
