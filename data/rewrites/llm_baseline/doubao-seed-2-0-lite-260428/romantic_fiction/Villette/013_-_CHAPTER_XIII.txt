I found myself smiling—no, outright laughing—at Madame Beck again, not twenty-four hours after that little charged scene in the garden. Villette’s weather is as fickle as a coquette’s fancy: not as damp as London’s skies, but every bit as unpredictable. The soft, honeyed sunset we’d watched the night before gave way to a gale that howled through the hours of dark, and the whole next day blew dry and stormy: heavy clouds blotted out the sun, but not a drop of rain fell, the streets hazed fine with sand and dust torn up from the broad boulevards.

Even if the day had been bright and lovely, I never would have gone back to that garden nook I’d once thought of as my private, quiet refuge. My little flagged alley, every walk and shrub in the whole school grounds, had gained a new, unpleasant kind of notoriety. Their seclusion no longer felt safe, their calm felt fragile. That window that had rained down a little lead casket of billets had turned the soft, beloved spot vulgar, somehow. I half swore the rose bushes were peeking over their leaves, the gnarled knots in the old tree trunks leaning in like listening spies. I’d gotten up before dawn that day, before the household stirred, to prop up the flowerbeds Dr. John had trodden flat rushing after that fallen casket, to brush away the footprints he’d left sunk into the soft soil before anyone else could spot them, all while that strong gale was still whipping the bare branches. I settled down to my desk with my German textbook after that, soft, half-content and half-pensive, as the pupils filed in for evening lessons and the other teachers pulled out their needlework.

The *étude du soir* was always held in the small refectory, far smaller than any of the school classrooms, for only the boarding students were allowed there, a scant twenty of them. Two oil lamps hung from the ceiling over the long tables, lit as soon as dusk crept in. Lighting those lamps was the signal to close all the schoolbooks, hush all chatter, and begin *la lecture pieuse*—the pious reading. I soon learned that this reading was designed, it seemed, to do nothing but humiliate the human intellect, grind common sense down to dust under its heel. The book they used never changed: when they finished it, they just started it over again, a tattered old volume as grey and weathered as the spires of the Hôtel de Ville, stuffed full of wild, impossible saints’ legends. I would have paid two whole francs just to get my hands on it once, to turn its yellowed, crumbling pages and see the absurd tales I’d only ever listened to, wide-eyed, from my seat at the table.

The stories were outrageous: tales of priests who’d trampled high-born ladies and princesses into abject slavery under the name of piety, tales of cruelty and tyranny that made my ears burn hot, that made me stab my scissors point-first into the worm-eaten tabletop without noticing, snapping the tip clean off. I sat through those readings for as many nights as I could bear, until my temples throbbed so hard my head ached, until my nights were full of fevered, restless dreams. Then I decided I would slip away the very second that old tattered book was pulled out, vanishing in the small bustle before the enforced silence settled, before anyone could think to call me back. I’d flee to the unlit hall, or the empty classrooms, pacing fast to keep warm through the cold winter nights, and in the long summer twilight I’d slip up to my dormitory room, lean my head out the wide casement, listen to the band music drifting from the park in the square, and drift away into my own quiet, unshared thoughts.

That stormy evening, I fled the refectory as usual, and slipped up the stairs to the dormitory. Before I even saw a thing, I could feel the room was not empty, though no one stirred, no sound whispered. All the white *lits d’ange* stood empty, every one. I heard a soft, careful drawer slide open, stepped back behind the curtain, and there stood Madame Beck, neat as a pin in her shawl and her crisp white nightcap, rummaging through my toilet table. She’d opened my locked workbox, every drawer one by one, unfolding every scrap of paper, lifting every small trinket, tidying everything back into perfect, neat place, unhasting and unresting, like a star moving across the sky.

I stood there watching, half-amused, half-fascinated. It would have been so easy to step out then, to blow every pretense between us to smithereens, but I had no wish to leave this place. My life held no secret lover, no hidden assignation, nothing for her to find, and I had no desire to lose this easy, light employment. So I turned and fled, down the stairs as swift and silent as the spider that skittered down the banister right beside me. I laughed until my sides ached once I was safe in the empty classroom, tickled half to death at the sight of her so caught up in her own suspicions of a secret romance between me and Dr. John. When the laugh faded, though, a sharp little ache pricked at me, hot tears stung my eyes—not because I cared a farthing for her distrust, but because it reminded me just how far I was from ever having the luxury of a secret, soft romance to hide. The tumult settled after an hour, though, and by the time I climbed back up to my room, I was Lucy Snowe again, calm as ever. Every drawer was locked tight, not a single thing out of place. Even the little bunch of dried white violets a stranger had pressed into my hand once, that I’d tucked between the folds of my best dress, was untouched. I forgave her, right then and there.

The one question that still puzzled me was how Dr. John had known that casket had been thrown into the garden at all, to rush there so fast to look for it. I found myself daring to wonder if I could ask him, outright, the next time I saw him.

Little Georgette was almost fully recovered now, and Dr. John’s visits had grown few and far between. That evening, I’d just finished tucking her in after her lisped little bedtime prayer, when Madame walked into the nursery, took the child’s warm hand, and said Georgette still had a little fever. She glanced at me sharp and quick, said she was going out on errands in a fiacre, that she would call on Dr. John and send him over to see the child, that I should receive him while she was out. It was so obvious a ploy I could have laughed out loud: Georgette was only warm from the July heat, no fever at all, and Madame never went out on errands in the evening, never left the house when Dr. John was due to call. Her little pale green bonnet was perched at a very smart angle as she left, and I smiled to myself at how thoroughly her clever plot was misdirected.

Georgette curled up in my lap, wrapping her small warm arms around my neck, pressing her soft cheek to mine, and I felt tears prickle again at the sweet, uncomplicated affection, so rare in this cold, formal house. I promised her she could sleep as soon as she wanted, if the doctor didn’t turn up in ten more minutes.

Then the doorbell rang, and there was Dr. John, taking the stairs two at a time, Rosine the parlour maid trailing after him, bold as brass, hands stuffed in the pockets of her frilled apron. She chattered away to him without a scrap of shyness, asking right out if he’d found that dropped box the other night, if there was a secret romance the whole house was whispering about. Dr. John laughed, told her he’d been attending a patient in the college right across the street, had seen the box drop out a student’s window, so he’d run over to pick it up. No romance at all, just that. He tipped Rosine a gold coin, and she danced off to answer the door for the day students being picked up.

That whole mystery cleared up in ten seconds flat, and I felt my shoulders relax. But then, all at once, I saw that lattice window across the garden swing open, a white handkerchief waving out of it, and then a little folded white billet fluttered down, landing on a shrub branch right below the window.

I cried out before I could stop myself. Dr. John rushed to the window, told me to go pick it up—no one would notice me slipping out, but if he went, everyone would see. I ran, grabbed the note, brought it straight back, and he tore it to tiny pieces without even reading it. He told me it was no fault of the sweet, innocent girl he was trying to protect, that the man throwing the notes was a worthless, persistent fool, and that the girl was far too refined and unsuspecting to know how to fend off his impertinence. He looked at me, earnest, asking if I would help him watch over her, keep her safe from harm. I nodded, eager to return the small kindnesses he’d done for me before, even as I chuckled to myself, half-convinced he was talking about Madame Beck, the only woman in the whole house refined enough to qualify as his “spotless, unspeakably beautiful” paragon.

He leaned in, voice soft, about to tell me her name. “So spotless, so good, so unspeakably beautiful! Impossible that one house should contain two like her. I allude, of course—”

The door to Madame Beck’s private chamber, opening right off the nursery, clicked. Somebody had been leaning against the keyhole listening for ten minutes straight, and their attempt to hold back a sneeze had backfired spectacularly. Dr. John jumped, startled, and out stepped Madame, perfectly composed, pretending she’d just walked in the door, sneezing again and declaring she’d caught a cold on her errands. She rattled off a long story about the fiacre rides, and the prayer bell rang before Dr. John could ever finish that sentence. I left them there together, half-laughing to myself, that meddlesome, perfectly timed sneeze having spoiled the secret that was almost mine to hear.
