The morning after that chaotic dinner at Grosvenor Place (where uninvited guest Colonel Altamont made quite the unforgettable entrance) found the Colonel himself stumbling blearily out of his garret room in Shepherd’s Inn, to find his roommate Captain Strong already fully settled in his favorite armchair, cigar in one hand, newspaper in the other. This was a man who could turn any temporary lodgings into a five-star campsite, you see—long before Altamont had even blinked awake, Strong had already polished off a massive breakfast of fried eggs and crispy broiled rashers that their man Grady had cooked to absolute perfection.

He was the sort who hated silence more than he hated even the most annoying company, and while he didn’t particularly care for his fellow lodger (he wouldn’t have shed a single tear if Altamont had met with the very “unfortunate accident” Sir Francis Clavering was fervently daydreaming about), he kept things perfectly friendly between them. He’d even tucked Altamont into bed like a fussy nurse the night before, swiped his candle away to avoid burning the house down, and when he found the nightcap bottle of spirits he’d been saving empty, he’d just shrugged, drank a glass of water, smoked his last pipe, and conked out like a baby. Nothing ruffled this guy: whether he was marching into battle the next morning or getting tossed in the Fleet prison, he always had perfect digestion, rosy cheeks, and a snore so loud it could rattle the rafters, waking up the next day ravenous and ready for whatever chaos came his way.

Altamont’s very first move was to roar for Grady to bring him a full pint of pale ale, which he chugged straight out of the pewter tankard in one go, wiped his mouth on his ratty dressing gown (Strong had long since noticed that his dyed jet-black whiskers were a completely different color than the fuzzy grayish-brown beard growing on his chin, not to mention the tuft of fair hair sticking out under his obviously fake black wig, but he was far too polite to mention any of it), and sighed so loud the windows rattled. “Nothing touches this beer when your insides are roasting,” he announced. “Drank a dozen of Bass a day back in Calcutta, me, and—”
“And Lucknow, I’d bet,” Strong snickered. “Grabbed this beer just for you, knew you’d be gasping for it after last night.”
That sent Altamont off on a ramble about the mess he’d made the evening before. “I can’t help it, mate,” he banged his big fist against his forehead. “The second liquor hits my system I turn into a total lunatic, shouldn’t be left alone within a mile of a spirit bottle. Once I start pouring, I can’t stop till the whole thing’s empty, and after that? Lord knows what garbage comes out of my mouth. I was supposed to be so sober the parson would have asked me for tips! Grady only gave me two tumblers of spirits at home, I was gonna spend the whole night at the Black and Red gambling den totally teetotal. But no—some idiot left that damn sample bottle of Hollands out on the table, right? Then Grady ran off to run errands, left the kettle boiling for my tea, and I couldn’t resist. Washed the whole bottle down, and then I swear I had even more swill later at that thieving little dive.”
“Wait, you were already wasted before you showed up at Clavering’s dinner?” Strong blinked. “Starting the party awfully early, aren’t you?”
“Drunk and done with it by nine o’clock, what a talent, eh?” Altamont cackled. “Yep, waltzed right over there like a total berk, found Blackland, young Moss, and a whole crew of their lowlife mates tucking into dinner. If we’d stuck to roulette I definitely would have cleaned them out—can’t lose at that game, me, never! But they were too sneaky, the lot of ’em. That rascal Blackland dragged out a set of dice, we started playing hazard right on the dining table, and I lost every last penny you’d given me that morning. That’s what made me so hot under the collar! Next thing I know I’m tramping over to Clavering’s place begging for more cash, and I don’t remember a single other thing till I woke up at 7 a.m. to that old guy Bows from number 4 murdering a piano tune next door.”
Strong lit a fresh cigar, puffed thoughtfully. “Curious how you always manage to squeeze money out of Clavering, Colonel. Dude’s famous for not paying anyone back, even people he actually likes.”
Altamont burst out cackling. “Ha! He owes me, that’s why.”
“Pfft, owing people money has never once made Frank Clavering want to pay them,” Strong pointed out. “Half the people in London are chasing him for cash.”
“Aw, he just loves me to bits, like a long-lost brother,” Altamont sneered, grinning. “You know that, Captain. No? He don’t? Alright then, ask no questions, I’ll tell no lies—stick that in your pipe and smoke it, old boy.”
“Gonna give up the brandy, though,” he added after a dramatic pause. “Haven’t got a choice, it’s gonna ruin me one day.”
“Makes you say some real wild stuff,” Strong said, staring him dead in the eye. “You remember all the nonsense you spouted at Clavering’s dinner table last night?”
“Say what?” Altamont jolted upright, panicking. “Did I spill the beans? Dammit Strong, did I blow my whole secret?”
“Ask no questions, tell no lies, remember?” Strong teased. He’d heard every weird little line Altamont had slurred last night, and seen him bolt the second he spotted Major Pendennis (who he’d mistaken for some old cop named Beak), but he wasn’t about to jog that particular memory. “Nah, you didn’t blab anything coherent. You were so far gone no one could make head nor tail of a single word you said.”
Altamont let out a huge, relieved sigh. That went down a treat, he thought. He was in a very talkative mood now, and kept rambling. “Yeah, I got my flaws, no denying. Three things I can never resist: a bottle of good brandy, a box of dice, and a pretty woman. No bloke with any spark in him ever could, far as I know. Every country I’ve ever visited, all three of those have landed me in hot water at some point.”
“Go on then, spill,” Strong said, amused.
“Started when I was 15, ran away from home, worked as a cabin boy on an Indiaman,” Altamont said. “Been chasing trouble ever since. You ever hear about that thousand quid I was carrying in Paris? Got swept right away by one woman—black eyes, neck full of jewels, draped in ermine like an actual duchess, I swear. I was playing it real careful at first, staying at Meurice’s hotel, calling myself Colonel Altamont, never gambled except at the public tables, and I won more than I lost. Then I met this fancy bloke called Bloundell-Bloundell, white kid gloves, little tuft of hair on his chin, total poser. He dragged me off to dinners, took me to soirees at the Countess de Foljambe’s place—blimey, that woman! Could sit down at the piano and sing so sweet you’d think she was singing your soul right out of your body. She invited me over every Tuesday, I bought her opera boxes, took her out for fancy dinners, I was having the time of my life. Then one night after supper, we had stacks of champagne, then the brandy started flowing, she mixed me tumbler after tumbler of punch, next thing I know I’m playing cards till I pass out. Woke up the next day with a headache so bad I thought my skull was splitting, and there’s the Honourable Deuceace—earl’s son, super fancy—hanging out in my sitting room, saying I lent him 30 louis the night before. Handed me the cash no questions asked, what a gentleman!
“Ten minutes later Bloundell and the Marquis de la Tour de Force turn up. They’re all grinning, waving bits of paper in my face. Turns out I’d apparently signed IOUs to the Marquis for 340 louis, and Bloundell for 210 more! I was raging, told them I wasn’t paying a farthing, they couldn’t fool me with their fake bits of paper. Then they started yelling at me about ‘affairs of honour’, saying they’d duel me if I didn’t pay. Just as we were about to come to blows, a mate of mine walked in, the one I’d met in Boulogne, who’d warned me about those crooks before. And what does he do? Sides with them! Says I was the one insisting on huge stakes, the Countess even begged me to stop playing. Told me the Countess was upstairs crying her eyes out, hysterical, scared the Marquis would shoot me dead. Said she was so in love with me she’d gotten on her knees and begged him to save my life!
“Course I ran right over to her place the second I got dressed. She shrieked when she saw me, almost fainted, called me Ferdinand! I swear that’s what she called me. Cried so hard her eyes were all puffed up, said she couldn’t stand to see me lose all that money in her house, begged me to take all her diamonds and pay the debts. I told her I’d never touch a penny of her stuff, and I wasn’t paying those con artists a single cent. Then she went all cold on me, said her dad was a Grand Army general, real men of honour pay their gambling debts, no exceptions. She was gonna march out the door and never speak to me again.
“What was I supposed to do? I didn’t wanna upset my sweet Caroline! So I paid every last penny, 560 gold Napoleons, no questions asked. I still can’t tell if they rooked me or not,” he mused, looking genuinely confused. “Sometimes I think they did, but then that Caroline was so crazy about me! She’d never let anyone trick me, I’m sure of it. Women don’t lie about that sort of thing, do they?”
Before he could spill any more of his chaotic life story, there was a loud bang on the front door. Their servant Grady opened it, and who should storm in but Sir Francis Clavering himself, looking so purple in the face he was one step away from bursting a blood vessel.
“The big boss is here!” Strong yelped, completely shocked.
“What the hell is he doing here?” Altamont growled, scowling under his bushy brows. “Can’t be anything good.”
He was right. Clavering never turned up at Shepherd’s Inn unless he was chasing or being chased for money, and today he was spitting nails. “You!” he screamed at Altamont. “You’ve pulled your stupid old tricks again, you absolute scoundrel!”
“Which tricks, exactly?” Altamont said, unphased, grinning.
“You got wasted at the Rouge et Noir gambling den last night, didn’t you?” Clavering yelled. “Used my name to draw a 15-pound bill on me, lost every penny of it at the dice table! And then you had the audacity to march uninvited into my fancy dinner party in Grosvenor Place, drunk off your arse, yelling for more booze like you owned my house! What the hell are all my fancy posh friends gonna think of me, letting a bloke like you wander in off the street?”
“Fair, that’s a bit awkward,” Altamont said, still totally calm. “I apologize, on my honour! 15 quid is nothing to a big shot MP baronet like you, is it? I was so wasted I completely forgot I even did it, that’s all.”
“15 pounds isn’t the point!” Clavering shrieked, swearing so loud the neighbors probably banged on the ceiling. “I will not have you dragging my good name through the mud, you hear me? If you ever so much as dare show your face near my house again, or mention my name in any gambling den ever again, or even speak to me on the street before I speak to you first, I swear I’ll disown you completely, I won’t give you another single shilling!”
“Don’t get your knickers in a twist, Governor,” Altamont grumbled, finally looking a bit annoyed. “You keep taunting me like that, and I’ll go make a scene at your next dinner party just to spite you. I know I shouldn’t have turned up last night, but I was absolutely plastered! That’s the sort of mistake gentlemanly blokes forgive each other for, right?”
“YOU a gentleman?” Clavering spluttered, purple with rage. “How dare a lowlife like you even call yourself that?”
