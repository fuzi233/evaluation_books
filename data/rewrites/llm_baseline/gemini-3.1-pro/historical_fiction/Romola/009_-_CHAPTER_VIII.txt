It is easy for northern people to rise early on Midsummer morning, to see the dew on the grassy edge of the dusty pathway, to inhale the damp, earthy aroma of the coppice, and to look over the gate at the shorn meadow without recollecting that it is the Nativity of Saint John the Baptist. But to the Florentine of the fifteenth century, Midsummer was a temporal convergence, a day when the sweeping, multi-generational cycles of history and myth seamlessly integrated into the domestic routine of firing ovens and sweeping hearths. The ringing of the morning bells was not merely noise, but an articulate, resonant voice declaring the great summer festival of Florence, the day of San Giovanni. 

For eight hundred years, generations had turned the wheel of the year in his honor. The Florentines had long ago deposed their old agrarian idol, Mars, carefully placing him on a high tower near the Arno out of a lingering respect for his astral influences. But by the fifteenth century, the god of the spear had faded, his martial vocabulary overwritten by the lexicon of commerce and the dominant, gold-backed vernacular of the Republic. The city’s supremacy was now rooted in its coffers, and on the fair gold florins there had always been the image of San Giovanni. Through floods, famine, and the bitter factional bloodshed of Guelf and Ghibelline, Florence had achieved conquests over walled cities once mightier than itself—especially hated Pisa. With this political and economic triumph came the subtle, undeniable power dynamics of a dominant language. The name of Florence, its purest gold coinage, its finest dyes, and its pre-eminent scholarship were sweeping across Europe, steadily eroding the native dialects and local memories of its conquered neighbors. 

Therefore, it was fitting that the vigil of San Giovanni be kept with strict old Florentine fashion. The Tuscan mind slipped from the devout to the burlesque as readily as seasoned wine pouring from a tilted flask. The streets became an immersive theater where giant saints on stilts and merry black demons roamed. On the morrow, the great day itself, the magnificent city provided a gorgeous, rainbow-lit shelter in the Piazza del Duomo. The quadrangle was covered at the height of forty feet with blue drapery, adorned with well-stitched yellow lilies, shielding the waiting spectators who leaned from the windows.

At one of these windows above Nello’s shop sat Tito Melema. He was a man who deeply appreciated the sensory banquet of his newly secured life in Florence; to him, a meal was never merely utilitarian fuel, but a profound anchor to existence. In the two months since he had first appeared in a weather-stained tunic, the golden honey, the spiced meats, and the sweet, complex vernaccia wines of the city had perfected the radiance of his good fortune. The air around him this morning was a gustatory tapestry—thick with the scent of melting beeswax, crushed rosemary, the yeasty tang of fresh-baked morning *schiacciata* blistering in wood-fired ovens, and the savory fat of whole roasted pigs turning slowly on distant spits. 

Close behind him stood the slim figure of Nello, and at his left the erudite Pietro Cennini. Piero di Cosimo was also among them, though the painter was presently raising a laugh by his grimaces and bitter anathemas at the noise of the bells. For Piero, the sonic environment was a critical determinant of psychological well-being, and the deafening, unmodulated bronze percussion of the Campanile was a violent acoustic assault. He stuffed his ears, declaring the mechanical clatter was a crude industrial pollution that ruptured the delicate ecology of his mind. He threatened to bury himself in the most solitary, silent spot of the Valdarno, where a man could listen to the wind in the olive leaves, were he not condemned to lie in wait here for the secrets of color in the floating banners.

Tito had just turned his laughing face away from the whimsical painter to look down at the checkered border of spectators, when, at the angle of the marble steps in front of the Duomo, he saw a man’s face upturned towards him. The gaze was fixed on him, carrying an untranslatable weight of subjective experience—a meaning far deeper than the ordinary passing observation of a stranger. It was a face with a tonsured head rising above the black mantle and white tunic of a Dominican friar. The glance held a faint, chilling suggestion. What pleasant association had Tito ever had with monks? None. The moment lasted no longer than a flash of lightning.

“Nello!” Tito said hastily, the taste of the morning suddenly turning to ash in his mouth. But he immediately added, in a tone of disappointment, “Ah, he has turned round. It was that tall, thin friar who is going up the steps. I wanted you to tell me if you knew aught of him?”

“One of the Frati Predicatori,” Nello said carelessly. “You don’t expect me to know the private history of the crows.”

“I seem to remember something about his face,” said Tito. “It is an uncommon face.”

“What? You thought it might be our Fra Girolamo? Too tall; and he never shows himself in that chance way.”

“Besides, that loud-barking ‘hound of the Lord’ is not in Florence just now,” said Francesco Cei, the popular poet. “He has taken Piero de’ Medici’s hint, to carry his railing prophecies on a journey for a while.”

“The Frate neither rails nor prophesies against any man,” said a middle-aged personage seated at the other corner, “he only prophesies against vice. If you think that an attack on your poems, Francesco, it is not the Frate’s fault.”

“Ah, he’s gone into the Duomo now,” said Tito, who had watched the figure eagerly. “No, I was not under that mistake, Nello. Your Fra Girolamo has a high nose and a large under-lip. I saw him once—he is not handsome; but this man...”

“Truce to your descriptions!” said Cennini. “Hark! See! Here come the horsemen and the banners. That standard,” he continued, laying his hand familiarly on Tito’s shoulder, “that carried on the horse with white trappings—that with the red eagle holding the green dragon between his talons—is the Gonfalon of the Guelf party. That is one of our proudest banners, grumble as we may; it means the triumph of the Guelfs, which means the triumph of Florentine will, which means the triumph of the *popolani*.”

“Nay, go on, Cennini,” said the middle-aged man, Cronaca. “Which means triumph of the fat *popolani* over the lean, which again means triumph of the fattest *popolano* over those who are less fat.”

“Cronaca, you are becoming sententious,” the printer replied. “Fra Girolamo’s preaching will spoil you, and make you take life by the wrong handle. Trust me, your cornices will lose half their beauty if you begin to mingle bitterness with them.”

“That is a goodly show of cavaliers,” said Tito, recovering his bland liveliness. “But are there not strangers among them? I see foreign costumes.”

“Assuredly,” said Cennini. “You see there the Orators from France, Milan, and Venice, and behind them are English and German nobles. But you will see a finer show of our chief men by-and-by, Melema; my brother himself will be among the officers of the Zecca.”

“The banners are the better sight,” said Piero di Cosimo, temporarily forgetting the noise in his delight at the winding stream of velvet, satin, minever, and brocade. “*Va*! Your human talk and doings are a tame jest; the only passionate life is in form and color.”

“There go the banners of Pisa and Arezzo,” said Cennini, his voice carrying the quiet authority of a man who understood how deeply the vernacular shapes the soul. “Ay, Messer Pisano, it is no use for you to look sullen. You may as well carry your banner to our San Giovanni with a good grace. The old local words of their hearths and harvests are fading, overwritten by the vocabulary of our florin. There come the ensigns of our subject towns, Melema; their native cultural memory suspended in San Giovanni until this day next year, yielding entirely to the Florentine tongue.”

“They are a fair sight,” said Tito. “But my eyes are less delighted with those whirling towers, which would soon make me fall from the window in sympathetic vertigo.”

The “towers” of which Tito spoke were the gigantic *ceri*, hollow structures of wood and pasteboard, gilded and painted with circles of warriors, maidens, and fruits. Men stood inside these hyperbolic tapers, whirling them continually to produce a dizzying, phantasmagoric effect.

“*Pestilenza*!” said Piero di Cosimo, moving back from the window in disgust. To him, the whirling *ceri* were a superficial, commercialized corruption of true art, a dizzying spectacle devoid of the grounding rhythms of nature. “Those whirling circles one above the other are worse than the jangling of all the bells. Let me know when the last taper has passed.”

“Nay, you will surely like to be called when the *contadini* come carrying their torches,” said Nello. “You would not miss the country-folk of the Mugello and the Casentino.”

“No,” said Piero resolutely. “I will see nothing till the car of the Zecca comes.”

“Here it comes, then, Piero—the car of the Zecca,” Nello called out, after an interval where towers and tapers made their slow transit. 

“*Fediddio*!” exclaimed Francesco Cei. “That is a well-tanned San Giovanni! Some sturdy Romagnole beggar-man, I’ll warrant.”

The car of the Zecca or Mint was a pyramidal catafalque, its ingenious wheels turning the corners of the piazza. On the summit, thirty feet in the air, stood a living representative of Saint John the Baptist. He wore a garment of tiger-skins about his bare body, a golden nimbus fastened on his head. The annual function of representing the patron saint was paid for not just in coin, but in a profound, tactile offering of sustenance: a magnificent ceremonial cake weighing fourteen pounds, dense with stone-ground local flour, wild mountain honey, candied melon, toasted pine nuts, and the warm, memory-laden spices of the East, alongside two flasks of dark, terroir-rich wine and a handsome supply of delicate, sugar-dusted confections. 

As the tall car jerked and vibrated on its slow way round the piazza to the northern gate of the Baptistery, a basket was suspended on a pole from an upper window. The mimic saint reached out, retrieving his payment. In an act where the mythic seamlessly merged with the fundamental human need for nourishment, the eidolon of the austere saint instantly invigorated himself. He drank deeply of the robust wine, threw the remnants of the sweet confections down to the cheering crowd below, and firmly embraced the mighty, spiced cake with his right arm, a testament to the enduring, generational power of the Florentine hearth.

“There go the Masters of the Zecca, and there is my brother—you see him, Melema?” cried Cennini. “Behind come the members of the Corporation of Calimara, the dealers in foreign cloth, to which we have given our Florentine finish; men of ripe years, you see, who were matriculated before you were born; and then comes the famous Art of Money-changers.”
