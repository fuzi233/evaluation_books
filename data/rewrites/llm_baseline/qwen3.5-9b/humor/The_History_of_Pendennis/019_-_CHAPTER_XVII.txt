The afternoon sun was shining with uncommon brilliance, prompting Mrs. Portman to extend her usual stroll from the Rectory all the way to Fairoaks. After settling her affairs with Mrs. Pybus, she decided the weather was simply too fine to waste indoors, eager to visit her dear friend, Mrs. Pendennis. Upon arrival, a revelation nearly caused a scandal among the ladies. Mrs. Portman confessed a "very great secret" concerning the Reverend Mr. Smirke: he harbored an ancient, deeply hidden attachment.

"And on whom has Mr. Smirke bestowed his heart?" Mrs. Pendennis asked, feigning a superior air while internally panicking.

"Why, my dear," replied Mrs. Portman, "when he first arrived to dine at the Rectory, everyone whispered we wanted him for Myra, forcing us to withdraw our inquiries. Later, rumors suggested he was smitten elsewhere, but I always denied it, insisting it was you—"

"That I!" cried Mrs. Pendennis, indignant. "People are dreadfully impertinent. Mr. Smirke came merely as Arthur's tutor; surely no one dares speak thus..."

"Pon my soul, it is a trifle too much," interjected the Major, lowering his newspaper and double eyeglasses.

"I have no patience with Mrs. Pybus," Helen added, joining the chorus of indignation.

Mrs. Portman quickly clarified the situation. "I told her there was no truth to it. Now, however, it is confirmed: my demure gentleman has been secretly engaged to Miss Thompson of Clapham Common for years. I am delighted, especially for Myra's sake, as an unmarried curate can be quite awkward in a household. Naturally, this remains strictly private, but I felt duty-bound to inform you to prevent future misunderstandings. Please, keep this between ourselves."

Mrs. Pendennis expressed sincere relief and wished Mr. Smirke a happy marriage with a deserving wife. Once the visitor departed, Helen and her brother discussed the news with great satisfaction. The kindly lady immediately chastised herself for her recent haughty behavior toward the Curate, realizing she should have been grateful for his constant devotion to Arthur.

"Gratitude to such people is commendable," the Major remarked dryly, "but familiarity is out of the question. This gentleman teaches lessons and collects fees like any other master. Distinctions in rank must be maintained. I warned you previously that you were too kind to Mr. Smirke."

Helen, however, disagreed. With Arthur preparing to depart, memories flooded her mind of Mr. Smirke's politeness: running errands, copying music, teaching Laura, and offering thoughtful gifts. Her conscience pricked her sharply regarding her past ingratitude. Consequently, when the Curate emerged from the study, looking eager to linger in the hall before leaving, Helen stepped forward. Blushing slightly, she invited him into her drawing-room—a space where he had rarely been admitted—and extended a warm invitation to dinner. Eager to accept such a delightful summons, Mr. Smirke agreed instantly.

Feeling relieved of her earlier suspicions, Helen treated Mr. Smirke with exceptional grace during the meal, perhaps doubling her attentiveness because the Major remained aloof and reserved toward his nephew's tutor. When Pendennis attempted to toast the Curate, he addressed him with the condescension of a sovereign speaking to a lowly retainer, a display so absurd that even Pen chuckled, despite his own youthful vanity.

Mr. Smirke, however, cared little for the Major's impertinence so long as his hostess remained kind. He spent a delightful evening beside her, deploying all his conversational skills to charm her. He spoke fluently of the Fancy Bazaar, the Great Missionary Meeting, the latest novels, and the Bishop's sermon on fashionable London society—all gleaned from the newspapers. In short, he employed every tactic available to a college divine possessing sprightly wit, serious talent, impeccable manners, and a susceptible heart, all aimed at winning the affection of his chosen object.

Shortly after Helen and little Laura retired, the Major yawned out of the dining room. "What an insufferable bore that man is, and how he talked!" he grumbled.

"He has been very good to Arthur, who adores him," Mrs. Pendennis noted. "I wonder who Miss Thompson is?"

"I always suspected the fellow was looking elsewhere," the Major muttered.

"And where?" asked Mrs. Pendennis, innocent as a child. "Toward Myra Portman?"

"Toward Helen Pendennis, if you must know," her brother-in-law replied bluntly.

"Himself? Impossible!" Helen exclaimed, knowing full well the truth. "His marriage will undoubtedly be happy. I only hope Arthur does not drink too much."

Indeed, Arthur, buoyed by the privilege of managing the cellar and anticipating shared meals with his favorite companion, had procured a generous supply of claret. As the elders and Laura departed, Pen and the Curate began to pour the wine with reckless abandon. One bottle vanished completely; another lost half its contents within thirty minutes. Pen, laughing with a hollow voice, toasted the falsehood of women, declaring that wine was a mistress who never deceived and always welcomed her lover.

Smirke countered gently that he knew women of truth and tenderness. Casting his eyes upward and sighing as if invoking a beloved, unmentionable presence, he drained his glass, his face flushing pink. Pen then recited verses he had composed that morning, lamenting the woman who had slighted his passion. He declared himself waking from love's fever, ready to leave a heartless deceiver, yet hoping to prove his worthiness to his race. Though death seemed preferable to life except for the sake of one kind being, he vowed that the false one would eventually learn the value of the treasure she had discarded.

Sentimental and emotional, Pen rolled out these lines in a rich, trembling voice, his honest gray eyes betraying a genuine sensitivity that would have melted anyone, even Miss Costigan. Smirke, equally moved, seized Pen's hand over the dessert dishes. "These verses are beautiful," he cried. "You are a poet, a great poet, destined for greatness by Heaven's permission. Go on and prosper, dear Arthur. Your current wounds are temporary; this grief will only cleanse and strengthen your heart. I have always prophesied your success once you correct certain character flaws. But you will overcome them. When you become famous, as I know you will, will you remember your old tutor and these happy early days?"

"I shall never forget," Pen swore, shaking hands across the glasses and apricots. "I don't know what I'd do without you. You are my best friend."

"Am I, really, Arthur?" Smirke asked, peering through his spectacles, his heart pounding audibly against his ribs.

"My best friend, my friend forever," Pen affirmed, draining the final glass of the renowned vintage imported by Lord Levant. "God bless you, old boy."

"We'll have another bottle!" Pen shouted, singing the drinking song from *Der Freischütz*. The dining room windows were open, and his mother paced softly on the lawn below, hearing the sweet, fresh notes of her son's voice. It cheered her heart immensely.

"You're taking too much wine, Arthur," Smirke warned softly.

"No," Pen insisted. "Women give headaches, but this doesn't. Fill your glass, old fellow. Let's drink—to her. Your 'her,' not mine, for whom I swear I'll care no more—not a penny, not a fig, not a glass of wine. Tell us about the lady, Smirke. I've seen you sighing for her."

"Oh!" Smirke gasped, his white shirtfront and glistening studs heaving with emotion.

"Oh, what a sigh!" Pen cried, becoming hilariously boisterous. "Fill, my boy, and drink the toast. No gentleman refuses a toast. Here's her health, good luck to you, and may she soon be Mrs. Smirke."

"Do you say so?" Smirke trembled. "Do you really say so, Arthur?"

"Of course! Down with it. Here's Mrs. Smirke's good health: Hip, hip, hurrah!"

Smirke convulsively gulped down his wine while Pen waved his glass overhead, cheering loudly enough to make his mother and Laura wonder on the lawn, and his uncle, dozing in the drawing room, start awake muttering, "That boy is drinking too much." Smirke set his glass down.

"I accept the omen," gasped the blushing Curate. "Oh, my dear Arthur, you—you know her..."

"What? Myra Portman? I wish you joy; she has a devilish large waist. But I wish you joy, old fellow."

"Oh, Arthur!" groaned the Curate, nodding speechlessly.

"Beg your pardon—sorry I offended you—but she has a large waist, you know—devilish large," Pen continued, the third bottle clearly affecting him.

"It's not Miss Portman," the other said in a voice of agony.

"Is it someone from Chatteris or Clapham? Someone here? Not old Pybus? Not Miss Rolt at the factory? She's only fourteen."

"It's somebody rather older than I am, Pen," the Curate cried, looking up guiltily before casting his eyes down into his plate.
