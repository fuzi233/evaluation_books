# BOOK II CHAPTER I: Paul Lands In A Strange New World, Learns A Bunch Of Dumb Truths, And Heads Off To Meet The Mysterious Man In Grey
Fate had fully intended me to be one of those infuriatingly lucky people everyone else resents. By all rights I should have been born in June – a month every old wives’ tale swears is the luckiest possible window to pop out a baby, and one thoughtful parents really ought to prioritize like booking a summer holiday rental before the peak season. How I ended up dropped unceremoniously in May, statistically the absolute unluckiest month on the entire calendar, I leave to people who make a career out of arguing with astrologers to explain. My very first distinct human memory, a bony, knobbly-nosed nurse named Mrs. Fursey, wasted no time blaming it on my own impatience, and helpfully predicted that same reckless lack of patience would land me in far deeper trouble later – a prophecy that every subsequent year of my life has stamped with a giant, annoying "verified" sticker.

That same sharp-elbowed dame was the one who filled me in on the chaotic circumstances of my arrival. Turns out I showed up completely unannounced, exactly two hours after a messenger galloped up to the house with news that my father’s mines had flooded and ruined every penny he owned. Misfortunes don’t just travel in pairs around my family, apparently – they bring a whole picnic squad. To make the whole mess perfectly on-brand, my poor mother, trying to reach the bedroom bell to call for help, stumbled, smacked straight into the cheval glass, and shattered it into a hundred shards. Her Welsh blood ran thick with superstition no amount of logic could scrub out, so she immediately convinced herself she’d cursed my first seven years of life to be nonstop disaster.

"And I do hate to say it," Mrs. Fursey sighed, stabbing her needle through a scrap of linen like it personally owed her money, "but it sure does look like the old saying might be true after all."

"Wait – so I’m not a lucky little boy?" I asked, completely bewildered. Up until that exact second, Mrs. Fursey’s whole parenting schtick had been drilling into me how unbelievably favoured I was by the universe. I got sent to bed at six sharp, while all the less fortunate village brats were kept up torturously late till eight or nine, and she’d framed that like winning the toddler lottery. Some kids didn’t even have beds to sleep in! A fact I’d spent many rowdy, overtired evenings bitterly envying. Also, every time I so much as sneezed, I got a mandatory lunch of linseed gruel and a supper of brimstone and treacle – a confection named to deliberately fool innocent children, because the treacle part was basically a marketing lie, the entire thing tasting like you’d licked a dirty fireplace poker. That, too, was supposed to be proof Fortune adored me: other kids were so unlucky they got left alone to suffer their colds with no weird toxic snacks. And the biggest proof of all? I had Mrs. Fursey as my personal nurse. The idea I wasn’t the luckiest kid within ten miles was absolute news to me.

The good lady realized she’d messed up, and scrambled to backtrack.
"Oh, you! You’re plenty lucky. I was thinking about your poor mother, that’s all."
"Isn’t mamma lucky?"
"She hasn’t exactly been drowning in good fortune since you showed up, no."
"Wasn’t it lucky for her, having me?"
"Can’t say it was, not at that exact second."
"Didn’t she want me?"

Mrs. Fursey was one of those well-meaning busybodies who think every child’s default attitude to life should be apologizing for daring to be born. "Oh, I daresay she could have done without you just fine," she said like it was the most obvious thing in the world.

I can still picture the scene perfectly: I’m perched on a tiny low chair right up against the nursery fire, both hands clasped around one bare knee, while Mrs. Fursey’s needle scrapes against her thimble in the same monotonous click-click rhythm that could hypnotize a parrot. That was the very first time the Big Question of Life knocked at my tiny, three-foot-tall soul.

Without moving a muscle, I blurted out: "Then why did she take me in?"

The clicking stopped dead. "Took you in? What nonsense are you spouting, child? Who’s talking about taking you in?"
"Mamma. If she didn’t want me, why did she let me in?" Even as I asked the very logical, unanswerable (or so I thought) question, full of dignified toddler outrage, I was secretly very glad she had. My imagination immediately conjured the stork, standing on our windowsill looking flustered and annoyed, the exact same face Tom Pinfold the gardener’s boy got when Anna the cook had sniffed the fish he was holding out for her and slammed the kitchen door in his face. I pictured the stork scratching his head with one of those long, compass-shaped legs, muttering to himself about ungrateful customers. Then I started panicking: where would he have taken me next? I’d watched blackbirds carry worms around the garden a hundred times, and the worms always looked like they were having the worst day of their entire lives, wiggling around all nervous. Had I squirmed like that on the stork’s beak? If mom had turned me away, where would he have dumped me? Probably Mrs. Fursey’s cottage first, but I knew for a fact she’d have sent me right back. The next house down the road was Mr. Chumdley the cobbler, a lame man who sat all day in a little cave half under the ground banging boots with hands so dirty you could barely see the skin, looking exactly like a low-budget ogre who ate small boys for breakfast. I’d have hated to be his kid. After that, who? Nobody else on the village road seemed like they’d take a random stray baby. I grew very pensive, imagining myself unwanted by the entire village, left holding the stork’s metaphorical baby parcel. "Nurse," I said, "where did I come from, anyway?"
"I’ve told you a hundred times. The stork brought you."
"Yeah, I know that part. But where did the stork get me from?"

Mrs. Fursey paused for so long I thought she’d fallen asleep, probably weighing if the answer would make me so conceited she’d have to smack me. Eventually she decided to risk it: "Oh, from Heaven."
"But I thought Heaven was a place you went to when you died," I said. "Not a place you comed from." I know I said "comed" – that was my very own custom irregular verb I’d invented, along with "goned", and my poor mom was tearing her hair out over how bad my grammar was. Mrs. Fursey didn’t even bother correcting me, she’d been told by mom not to mess with my grammar lessons so we wouldn’t confuse the kid, which was very smart of her.
"You came from Heaven," she repeated, "and you’ll go back there – if you’re very good."
"Do all little boys and girls come from Heaven?"
"So they say." Her tone made it very clear she thought this was a very suspect popular myth, and she took zero personal responsibility for it.
"Did you come from Heaven too, Mrs. Fursey?"
"Of course I did. Where on earth do you think I came from?"

And that, friends, was the exact second Heaven lost 90% of its magic for me. I’d already been confused why literally everyone I knew was guaranteed a spot there, but finding out the lady who forced brimstone treacle down my throat on the regular was also a native resident? All the sparkle vanished immediately. But that wasn’t even the worst part. My tiny, self-absorbed kid brain didn’t stop to think this was just the standard origin story for everyone. I zeroed straight in on my own sad little situation.
"Didn’t they want me in Heaven either?" I asked, my voice all wobbly and miserable. "Weren’t they fond of me up there?"

The despair in my voice must have even poked a hole in Mrs. Fursey’s bony chest, because she answered softer than usual. "Oh, they liked you fine, I expect. I like you, but I still want to get rid of you sometimes, don’t I?" There was no arguing that fact. Even back then I suspected my "6pm bedtime" was sometimes 5:30 if she was in a bad mood.

That didn’t comfort me at all. It was crystal clear now: no one wanted me. Not Heaven, not my mom, not anybody. God was glad he’d shipped me off, my mom could have lived without me, what was I even doing here? And then, like someone flipping on the light in a dark closet, a thought popped into my tiny brain: there must be something, somewhere that needs me, right? You can’t just exist for no reason. I belonged to some big Something, and it belonged to me, as much a part of me as my own nose. That feeling popped back a few more times when I was little, but I never could put it into words, till years later the son of that Portuguese Jew I knew managed to say the quiet part out loud for me. But back then all I knew was, for a split second, I realized I was alive, I was *me*. And then the light flipped off again, and I was just a confused little kid sitting by the fire pestering a village nurse for life answers.

Then a new thought hit me. "Nurse, why don’t we have a husband?"
Mrs. Fursey dropped her sewing and stared at me like I’d grown a second head. "What weird little maggot got into your brain now? Who doesn’t have a husband?"
"Mamma."
"Don’t talk rubbish, Master Paul. Your mamma *does* have a husband."
"No she doesn’t."
"Don’t you dare contradict me. Her husband’s your papa, who lives in London, working hard."
"What’s the good of him all the way out there?"

Mrs. Fursey blew up at that like I’d just swore in church. "You wicked ungrateful little brat! Where are your commandments? Your pa’s slaving away in London to keep you fed and spoiled, and you sit there saying ‘what’s the good of him’? I’d be mortified to be that rude."

I hadn’t meant to be rude at all. I’d just parroted a conversation I’d overheard the day before, between my mom and my aunt. My aunt was the kind of woman who had every right to lecture people about moping, because she grumbled about absolutely everything all day long, but she did it cheerfully. She’d caught my mom staring out the French window at the crocuses, hands clasped behind her back looking all sad. "There she goes moping again," my aunt had said. "Drat me if I ever saw anyone waste more time looking glum."
"I want a husband," my mom had pouted, in such a silly little kid voice I’d looked up from my fairy story half expecting her to shrink down to seven years old. "I hate not having a husband who’s never around."
"Help us all," my aunt had scoffed. "How many more does a girl need? You *have* one."
"What’s the point of him if he’s hundreds of miles away," my mom had whined. "I want him here where I can get at him."

I’d heard about this London-based father of mine my whole life, supposedly the source of all our good fortune, but my kid brain had written him off as a purely spiritual being, like Santa Claus but less fun. I totally agreed with my mom: a husband you couldn’t grab to give you piggyback rides around the garden, or take you sailing on the lake, was basically useless. "You don’t get it, nurse," I explained. "I mean a husband you can actually reach."
"Well, you’ll get at him soon enough, poor man," Mrs. Fursey said. "When he’s all sorted, he’ll send for you, and you and your ma and your aunt will go live in London. And thank the good Lord I’ll finally be rid of you."
"Do you have to die first to get to London?" I asked.

Mrs. Fursey just sighed so deep her whole bony frame shook. "I swear, you are without a shadow of a doubt the silliest little boy I have ever met in my whole life. I’ve no patience left for you. London is a big, loud town, and you get there on a train. Soon your pa will send a letter saying everything’s ready, you’ll all leave this village, and you’ll never come back. Ever."
"Never? I’ll never play in the garden again? Never go down to the pebble beach for tea, or climb up to Jacob’s Tower?"
"Never again." She said the phrase like it was straight out of the prayer book, like she was really savouring the drama of it.
"And I’ll never see Anna, or Tom Pinfold, or old Yeo the fisherman, or Pincher the dog, or *you* ever again?" Right then, when every single thing I knew was crumbling out under my feet, I’d have happily clung even to dry, grumpy Mrs. Fursey.
"Never any more. You’ll go start a whole new life, and I do pray Master Paul that it’s a better one than the mischievous one you’ve had here so far."

Her pious little speech went in one ear and straight out the other. I was staring into the fire, reeling from the news that Life, apparently, was just getting ripped away from all the people and places you love, dumped into a whole strange new world, and expected to be fine with it. What a weird, messed up thing life was.

That night when my mom came in to kiss me goodnight, I turned my face to the wall and pretended to be asleep, because even little kids get stupid pride sometimes. But when I felt her soft curls brush my cheek, my pride melted, I threw my arms around her neck, and blurted the question that had been bouncing around my head all evening. "You couldn’t send me back now, could you? You’ve had me for ages, I’m way too big for the stork to carry, aren’t I?"
"Send you back where?" she asked, confused.
"Back to Heaven. The stork can’t pick me up now, right?"

She knelt down beside the bed so her face was level with mine, and all the stupid fear I’d been carrying fell right off me. "Who’s been filling my silly little boy’s head with nonsense?" she said, not letting go of my arms around her neck.
"Nurse and I were talking," I said. "And she said you could have done without me." It didn’t feel scary to say it any more. It was obviously just Mrs. Fursey’s weird mean joke.
My mom pulled me tight against her. "And where did she get that silly idea?"
"Well, I showed up at a really bad time, right? When you already had all the bad news about the mines."
She laughed, but then went all soft and serious. "I never realized you thought about all that stuff. We’re going to spend way more time together, you and I, Paul, you can tell me every single thing that pops into your head. Nurse doesn’t quite get how your brain works. It’s true we had a whole heap of trouble right when you arrived. But I could never have done without you. I was so sad, and you were sent to make it better, to help me get through it."

That was a way better story than Mrs. Fursey’s. "So it was lucky, having me?" I asked. She laughed again, then got that quiet serious look on her face, like she was about to tell me the most important secret in the world. "Will you remember what I’m about to say, Paul? When you’re a good boy, and then later when you grow up to be a good man, then I’m the luckiest little mother in the whole world. And every time you mess up, that’s bad luck for me. Promise me you’ll remember that even when I’m gone, and you’re all big?"

I promised, even though I knew my memory was terrible. And I’ll laugh at it now, thinking back at the two of us, two earnest silly faces huddled together over the bed, but I swear to you, even on the days I’ve messed up real bad, I never quite forgot that promise.

From that day on, Mrs. Fursey faded into the background, and my mom became the whole centre of my world. We spent sunny mornings in the overgrown garden chasing leaves around, reading books, and twilight evenings tucked into the window seat behind the dark red curtains whispering about fairies, ogres, saints and pirates, for no reason at all. Our "school lessons" were very unstructured, to put it mildly. Fact and fiction got so jumbled up in my brain I would have failed every history exam ever written. I fully believed Aphrodite stepped out of the sea and walked straight over to meet King Canute, who sat on his throne on the beach yelling at her not to get his feet wet. I thought Robin Hood had accidentally shot King Rufus with a poisoned arrow in the New Forest, but Queen Eleanor had sucked all the poison out to save him. I was 100% convinced Oliver Cromwell beheaded King Charles, married his widow, and then got stabbed by Hamlet. Ulysses had sailed the Argo to discover America, Romulus and Remus had killed the big bad wolf and rescued Little Red Riding Hood, King Arthur got murdered by his uncle in the Tower of London because he burned the cakes, Prometheus got saved by Saint George, and Paris gave the golden apple to William Tell. Who cares if it was all wrong? It just needed rearranging a little bit later, that’s all.

Lots of afternoons we’d climb the winding steep path through the woods, past precipices local legend said were full of ghosts, past patches of grass where fairies danced at midnight, until we got to the high plateau above the sea where Jacob’s ruined tower stood. Locals called it Jacob’s Folly, some even called it the Devil’s Tower – the story went old Jacob and the Devil used to meet there on stormy nights to hang fake lights and wreck passing ships for their cargo. No one ever told me who old Jacob even was, or why he built the stupid tower, but all the fishermen swore you could see strange lights glowing in its ivy-covered windows on dark storm nights.
