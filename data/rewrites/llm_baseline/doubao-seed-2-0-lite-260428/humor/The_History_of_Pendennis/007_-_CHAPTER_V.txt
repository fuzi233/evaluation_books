Rebecca the mare didn’t slacken pace for a second, galloping full tilt all the way to Baymouth like she had a personal grudge against the cobblestones, until Pen dumped her at the inn stables and bolted straight for Mr. Foker’s digs— the address he’d scribbled down the other day, above a chemist’s shop that didn’t do half as much business selling cough syrup as it did peddling cheap cigars and lukewarm soda water to the horde of rowdy young university students living on the floor above.

When Pen burst through the door, the only person home was Mr. Spavin, Foker’s old mate and co-owner of that tandem they’d careened into Chatteris with the day before, who was half-asleep smoking a cigar and trying to teach a scruffy little terrier to do tricks with a crumb of biscuit. Pen’s bright pink, wind-burned gallop-fresh face looked ridiculous next to Spavin’s waxy, overindulged, three-nights-no-sleep features, and Spavin noted the contrast immediately. “Who’s that bloke?” he thought. “Looks as fresh as a newly picked pea. I’d lay five to one his hand doesn’t shake even before his first morning beer.”

Foker was nowhere to be found. Disaster! Spavin couldn’t say when he’d roll up— sometimes he vanished for a day, sometimes a whole week, no questions asked. Which college was Pen from? Did he want a pint of perfectly decent ale? He fished Pen’s calling card off the table (Pen was absurdly proud of having a proper calling card back then, for the record), the two exchanged a couple of awkward pleasantries, and Pen beat a hasty retreat.

He trundled down the cliff to the beach, pacing up and down the sand gnawing his nails while the sea roared and crashed in the background, bright and endless, blue waves foaming and thundering at the shore. Pen stared right through them, too wrapped up in his own whirring, overemotional teen brain to notice a single bit of the view. There was a whole tidal wave of nonsense and infatuation flooding through his head, and he hadn’t a hope in hell of stopping it. He chucked stone after stone into the surf, but it didn’t make the smallest dent in his mood. He was furious he couldn’t find Foker. He needed to see Foker. He had to see Foker. “What if I just ride a little ways down the Chatteris road, just to chance running into him?” he thought. Half an hour later, Rebecca was saddled up again, galloping along the grass verge.

Four miles out of Baymouth, the Clavering turnoff branched off, as every local knows, and Rebecca naturally tried to swerve for it (she knew the way home to Pen’s stables, where the hay was far better than anything she’d get on the Chatteris road) but Pen whacked her over the shoulder and kept going all the way to the turnpike, not a single sign of Foker’s black tandem or bright red wheels anywhere.

Well, he was already at the turnpike, so he might as well keep going, right? That was perfectly logical. So he rode straight to the George, where the hostler cackled and confirmed Foker was definitely inside. “He were making a right racket last night, sir, drinking and singing and threatening to knock Tom the postboy’s block off! Though I reckon Tom would’ve wiped the floor with him, if it came to it.” He winked super sarcastically at Foker’s footman Stoopid, who trundled out of the yard carrying his master’s immaculately brushed clothes. “Go on then, show Mr. Pendennis up to his lordship,” and Pen trailed after him up the stairs to the bedroom, where Harry Foker lay buried under a mountain of featherbeds.

The pillows and duvets swelled up all around him so all you could see of the little bloke was his sallow face and pointy red silk nightcap peeking over the top edge.

“Oy, who goes there? Halt and give the countersign, brother!” Foker yelled out groggily from the pile of bedding. “Wait, Pendennis? Did your mum know you snuck out last night? Did you join us for supper? Wait no— who *did* we have at supper last night, Stoopid?”

“Three officers, Mr. Bingley, and Mr. Costigan, sir,” Stoopid said, completely deadpan, not cracking a single smile.

“Ah right! The cup went round, the jokes were flying, we all chanted songs. I remember I tried to fight a postboy. Did I win, Stoopid?”

“No sir. Fight never happened, sir.” Stoopid replied, still utterly straight-faced, while he arranged Foker’s fancy dressing case — a huge silver-mounted trunk his doting mum had gifted him, that he never travelled anywhere without. It was stuffed full of fancy silver trinkets: a silver dish, silver mug, little silver jars for every possible fancy perfume, and a whole set of razors all sharp and ready, for the hypothetical future day Foker finally grew a beard.

“Ah well, save it for another day,” Foker yawned, throwing his lanky little arms over his head. “No fight, but *plenty* of singing. Bingley sang, I sang, the General — that’s Costigan — sang. Have you ever heard him do *The Little Pig Under The Bed*, Pen? It’s a masterpiece.”

“That man we met yesterday,” Pen stammered, already trembling, “the father of —”

“Of the Fotheringay! Exactly! Ain’t she a straight-up Venus, Pen?”

“Beg pardon, sir,” Stoopid cut in. “Mr. Costigan’s in the sitting room, sir. You invited him for breakfast, sir. He’s been knocking five times, but we weren’t to wake you, and he’s been sat there since eleven o’clock, sir.”

“What time is it now?”

“One, sir.”

“Can you imagine what my dear old mum would say,” Foker groaned, “if she saw me lazing in bed at one in the afternoon? She sent me down here with a tutor, you know. She wants me to ‘nurture my neglected genius’ — snort — this is a damn sight better than seven o’clock Latin lessons, isn’t it, old pal?” he cackled, then waved Pen off. “Go on in and chat to the General while I get dressed. And for God’s sake ask him to sing *The Little Pig Under The Bed*, it’s hilarious.” Pen, all jittery and flustered, trundled off to meet Costigan while Foker started his two hour toilet routine.

For context: one of Foker’s grandfathers was a self-made brewer who left him a massive fortune, the other was an earl who gave him the world’s most doting, overprotective mum. The Fokers had gone to the same Cistercian school for generations, where young Harry’s name was painted right over the playground pub sign, under the words “Foker’s Entire”. He got bullied unmercifully for that, for his funny face, for being useless at schoolwork, for being a total slob, for gorging himself on tuck. Anyone who’s ever seen a quiet, bullied kid skulk around school doing everything he can not to draw attention to himself knows exactly what happens the second he walks out the school gates: within months, shy little Harry bloomed into the sarcastic, quick-witted, absurdly dandyish Foker we all know. He was still a total dunce, of course — you don’t suddenly learn Latin just cause you leave school and sign up as a wealthy fellow-commoner at university — but by the time he walked into the sitting room to join his guests, he was doused in cologne, decked out in immaculate linen, and looking positively splendid.

Captain Costigan — he preferred “Captain” to “General”, for the record — was perched on the windowsill, holding the newspaper at full arm’s length. His eyes were a bit wonky and bloodshot, so he mouthed every word he read, like most gents who don’t get much practice reading. His hat was tilted so far over one ear it was practically slipping off, and anyone paying attention would’ve noticed his boots were so big and scuffed and falling apart it was clear times were not exactly flush for him. Poverty always attacks the extremities first, right? Your hat, your boots, your gloves are the first things to go when you’re skint, and every single one of Costigan’s was extra ragged and shabby. The second he saw Pen he hopped off the windowsill, saluted him with two fingers sticking out of his broken black glove, then whipped his hat right off his head to bow. He was half bald, but he’d combed a whole mess of lank iron-grey hair over his scalp, with two wispy little tufts hanging down on either side of his face. Decades of whisky had turned what was once a handsome complexion a rich coppery red. He wore a high stiff neckerchief covered in stains, and a dress coat buttoned tight across his chest where the remaining buttons hadn’t fallen off.

“The young gentleman I had the honour of being introjuiced to yesterday in the cathedral yard!” he boomed, doing an over-the-top dramatic bow. “Hope I see you well, sir! I spotted ye in the theatre last night during my daughter’s perfawrumance, and ye were gone by the time I walked her home — Jack Costigan’s poor, but he’s a gentleman, after all! I came back to the house to drink with our young friend Mr. Foker, and we had such a night of it, sir — him, three gallant dragoons, and your humble servant. Gad, it reminded me of the old days, when I was serving His Majesty in the Foighting Hundred and Third!” He pulled out a beat-up old snuff box and offered it to Pen with the utmost stately dignity.

Pen was so flustered he could barely speak. This shabby, ragged old buck was her father! The Captain reeked of last night’s cigars, and twisted the little tuft of hair on his chin as jauntily as any 20 year old dandy about town.

“I — I hope Miss Fotheringay, Miss Costigan is well, sir!” Pen blurted out, bright red in the face. “She — she gave me more pleasure than I’ve ever had at a play. I think, sir, I think she’s the finest actress in the whole world!”

“Shake my hand, young man! Ye speak from the heart!” Costigan yelled, clapping him on the shoulder. “Thank ye, sir, an old soldier and fond father thanks ye. She *is* the finest actress in the world. I’ve seen the great Siddons, I’ve seen O’Neill — they were grand, but what were they next to my daughter? I don’t let her use our real family name on the stage, ye see. The Costigans of Costiganstown are proud folk, and they’d say an old soldier who served the crown was disgracing himself letting his daughter earn his daily bread.”

“Surely there’s no more honourable work in the world,” Pen stammered.

“Honourable? Bedad, sir, I’d like to see the man who’d say Jack Costigan would do anything dishonourable! I’ve got a heart, sir, even if I’m poor. I can spot a man with a heart the second I see him, and you’ve got one — I read it in your honest face and your steady eye. And would ye believe it,” he leaned in to whisper, all tragic and mournful, “that that rogue Bingley, who’s made a fortune off my child, only pays her two guineas a week? She’s got to pay for all her own costumes out of that, and that, plus my own tiny little income, is all we’ve got to live on!”

Costigan’s income was so small it was basically non-existent, but nobody knows how shabby Irish gents manage to stay afloat, somehow, scraping together whisky and water every single day even when they haven’t got a penny to their name. If he’d ever written his life story it would’ve been a wildly entertaining moral fable, but he never would’ve told it even if he could, and he couldn’t have told it straight anyway — fact and fiction were all muddled together in his whisky-softened old brain. He’d started life pretty grand, with a commission, a handsome face, and one of the best singing voices in the whole army. Right up until the day he died he sang those wild, sad, hilarious Irish ballads that made everyone cry, even himself. Poor old Cos! Brave and soppy, funny and daft, endlessly good-natured, almost honest half the time. He’d drink with anyone, he’d back anyone’s bill, and he died in a spunging house where even the sheriff’s officer that locked him up was fond of him.

Within half an hour of chatting to Pen, Costigan had smoothly relieved him of his last two sovereigns, in exchange for tickets for his daughter’s upcoming benefit night. It wasn’t the dodgy old arrangement from the year before, when Miss Fotheringay had lost fifteen shillings on the whole show — this was a deal with the theatre manager where she got to sell a set number of tickets and keep most of the cash herself. Pen only had two pounds in his whole purse, but he handed it straight over. He was too scared to offer more, in case he offended the old captain’s delicate honour. Costigan scrawled him a ticket order for a box, slipped the two sovereigns into his waistcoat pocket, patted the spot with a big grin like the coins were warming his chest.

“Faith, sir, gold’s a damn sight scarcer for me these days than it used to be, same as many a good fellow,” he said. “I won six hundred of ’em in one single night, sir, when my great friend the Duke of Kent was stationed in Gibraltar!” And off he went, reeling off endless stories about the garrison, the races, the bets, the mountains of claret they all drank, keeping Pen completely entertained until Foker finally trundled in ready for breakfast.

Breakfast was a glorious spread of devilled turkey and mutton chops, and Costigan’s spirits rose higher and higher the more he ate. He chattered nonstop about his old army days, the lords and generals and lord lieutenants he’d rubbed shoulders with, the tragic death of his dear late wife Bessie, the duel he’d challenged a Captain Shanty Clancy to for looking rude at his daughter when she was out driving in Phoenix Park, and how Clancy apologized, bought them all a dinner at Kildare Street, and the six of them drank 21 bottles of claret between them. By the time he’d finished his second glass of curaçao, he was so happy and sentimental he started crying, saying he couldn’t think of a greater joy than hanging out with two such noble, generous young gentlemen. He wasn’t exactly the most upstanding role model for impressionable young lads, but there are far worse, richer, more dishonest men walking around in fine positions in life that never got up to half the harmless silly roguery old Cos did.

The three of them trundled out onto the street, Costigan linking an arm through each of theirs, grinning and winking at every shopkeeper they passed that he owed money to, as if to say “look at the fancy rich company I’m with, you’ll definitely get your money soon mate”. They parted ways with Foker outside a billiards hall, where he had a pre-planned game with some of Colonel Swallowtail’s regiment’s officers.

Pen and the shabby old captain wandered on together, Costigan slyly grilling him about how rich Foker was, what his family was like. Pen told him Foker’s dad was the famous brewer, and his mum was Lady Agnes Milton, daughter of Lord Rosherville. Costigan immediately burst into the most over-the-top stream of compliments about Foker, gushing about his “native aristocracy you can spot at a hundred paces, paired with a brilliant mind and a heart made of pure gold” — not a single word of which he actually believed.

Pen just wandered along, listening to him ramble, amused and confused and starry-eyed. He was far too earnest and honest a kid to ever assume anyone would lie to him, so he took every single one of Costigan’s tall tales at face value. Costigan had never had a more grateful, attentive listener, and he was chuffed to bits with the boy.

He liked Pen so much — he seemed so sweet, artless, cheerful — that he finally offered him an invitation he almost never gave to random young men: “Would ye do me the honour, sir, of popping back to my little humble lodgings just around the corner? I’d love to introjuice my young friend to my daughther, Miss Fotheringay.”

Pen was so shocked and over the moon he almost went limp on Costigan’s arm. He thought he was going to collapse right there on the pavement, and trembled so hard he was sure the old captain would notice. He stammered out a bunch of incoherent, flustered words saying he’d be more than delighted, he was already her biggest fan, and he followed Costigan up the street barely even noticing where he was going. He was going to see her! He was going to see her at home! She was the entire centre of the universe as far as he was concerned. The day before he met her felt like a lifetime ago. A whole new world was about to start.

Costigan led him down to quiet little Prior’s Lane in Chatteris, tucked in the church quarter right next to Dean’s Green and the canons’ houses, overshadowed by the huge looming cathedral towers. He lived on the first floor of a low old gabled house, with a brass plate on the door reading “Creed, Tailor and Robe-maker”. Old Mr. Creed was long dead, his widow worked as a pew-opener in the cathedral, their eldest son was a little scamp choirboy who spent all day playing toss-halfpenny and leading his little brothers into mischief, with a voice so sweet it could’ve been an angel’s. Two of the smaller Creed boys were sat on the front doorstep, and they jumped up the second they spotted the captain, lunging straight for the tails of his dress coat.
