The loggia crowning Bardo’s Florentine house rose higher than all the neighbouring rooftops, forming a sun-warmed gallery wrapping around the quadrangular stone walls. On the street-facing side, its sloped roof rested on slim carved columns; the remaining three sides were lined with walls pierced by graceful arched openings, so that the back walk, looking out over a jumble of half-erected, weathered dwellings toward the soft green slope of the hill of Bogoli, gave Romola a secluded, sheltered path where she could wander unseen by passersby.

Close to one of those arched openings, just a few steps from the door that led up from the main house, Tito waited for her. A sick, twisting weight sat in his stomach as slanted golden Tuscan sunlight pooled at his feet, weaving through the shards of all the plans he had thought were broken. He had never for a second foolishly presumed Romola’s love for him would overpower the revulsion she would feel if she learned the truth of his secrets: he knew full well her affection for him was rooted in her unshakable faith in his goodness, a faith he had no right to count on surviving exposure. But as he pictured her walking toward him, luminous and lovely, her face softened by that warm hazel gaze that had first stolen his breath, he found himself aching for something small, something selfish: that she had been less unyieldingly pure, just for a moment, so that he could pull her tight and kiss her deeply before their world crumbled apart. They had never shared more than fleeting, chaste touches: a lingering glance across the library, a quick brush of lips, a squeeze of hands as they parted. He had dreamed for months of being alone with her at last, and now that the moment had come, he had been certain she would stand far off, cold and unforgiving.

His heart jolted so hard it ached when the loggia door creaked open. There she was. It hit him like a flash of summer lightning: he felt rather than saw the glow of her golden hair lit by the sun, the tears swimming in her wide, imploring eyes, he felt rather than heard the soft, breaking cry of his name on her lips: “Tito!” In the same instant she was in his arms, sobbing hard, her face pressed into his shoulder.

Romola had lain awake through every hour of the previous night, yearning for the comfort of his bright, familiar face. The shock of seeing her long-lost brother Dino on his deathbed, the bewildering, unanswerable new questions his strange, fevered testimony had planted in her mind, the haunting vision that felt less like a story she had heard than a nightmare she herself had lived through—all of it had driven home just how completely Tito was the first person who had ever flooded her quiet, library-bound life with hope and gladness, who had softened the sharp old grief of losing her brother years before. She could not tell any other living soul what she had witnessed that day at San Marco, not even her grieving, blind father. She had held her sobs in all morning while tending to Bardo, but now that Tito was here, the pent-up sorrow burst free. Proud and reserved with every other person in Florence, Romola was as unguarded and simple as a child in her love for him. She had been happy for months just sharing quiet looks across Bardo’s shelves, but now that she ached to cling to him, no old shyness held her back.

“My Romola, my golden goddess,” Tito murmured, his voice thick with sudden, fierce fondness as he held her gently, pressing soft kisses to the thick, sun-bleached curls at the nape of her neck. In that moment he was in paradise. Disgrace, shame, the fear of being found out—all of it vanished. This happiness was too bright to be spoiled by the quiet awareness that Romola still did not know his secrets. He found himself rejoicing in her innocence; concealment, he told himself, had only ever been wisdom. The only thing he regretted now was how unnecessary all his prior dread had been, if that dread had not been worth enduring for the sake of this sudden, overwhelming rapture.

The last of her ragged sobs faded, and Romola lifted her head. Neither spoke for a long while. They stood still, staring at each other’s faces with that soft, wondering sweetness only young love can bring: her long, white scholar’s hands tangled in his dark brown curls, his dark, slim fingers sifting through the streams of her loose golden hair. They had never felt so completely seen by another person. The cold, heavy weight of her new grief pressed at Romola’s chest, making her cling all the longer to that quiet, soothing sense of nearness. Tito did not even dare to lean in to kiss her lips, afraid to break the perfect, wordless peace of the moment.

“Tito,” she said at last, “all of it was so terrible, but I have to tell you everything. Your strength will help me shake off these impressions that reason alone cannot chase away.”

“I know, Romola,” Tito said, his dark, lustrous eyes giving away nothing of the hundred sharp wishes he had held for Dino’s death long before that day. “I know he is gone.” Romola saw nothing but her own pure, loving thoughts reflected in their dark depths, the way we read warm, familiar letters in a happy dream.

“He was so changed, Tito! It pierced me to the heart to realize that wasted monk was the brother I remembered. And it was so strange, so hard: he would not say a single word to our father, nothing but a vision he insisted he had to share with me alone. And yet it was so piteous—his gasping, fighting breath, his eyes fixed on the crucifix over the altar, that he could not even see clearly. I will never forget it. It feels like that memory will stand between me and every other thing I ever look at for the rest of my life.”

Romola’s throat swelled with fresh tears, and she had to stop speaking. But the desperate urge to unburden herself to Tito pushed her to push down the rising anguish, and when she spoke again, her thoughts had shifted a little. “It was odd, Tito. That vision he spoke of was about our marriage—and he had never even heard your name.”

“What was it, my love? Sit down, tell me all of it,” Tito said, leading her to the weathered stone bench set near the arch. A sudden sharp fear pricked at him, terrified that the dying man’s vision might somehow reference Baldassarre, the secret he could never let Romola learn, and the unease made him crave the small change of position.

Romola recounted every second of the scene at San Marco, almost word for word, Dino’s lines burned into her memory the second they had left the dying monk’s lips. But when she finished describing the vision, she paused, the rest of the memory rising so vividly before her eyes she could not bring herself to speak it, staring out across the distant Bogoli hill, almost forgetting Tito was there beside her. His mind was completely at ease now: the vague, rambling vision had washed over him like thin white mist, leaving no trace at all. He held his silence, waiting for her to go on.

“I took the crucifix from his hands,” she went on, as if he had been reading her unspoken thoughts. “It is down in my bedroom, on my bedside table.”

“And now, my Romola,” Tito said, his tone soft and pleading, “you must banish all these ghastly fancies from your mind. That vision was nothing but a common monkish hallucination, bred of weeks of fasting and fanatical delusion. It cannot possibly hold any real weight for you.”

“No, Tito, not logically. But poor Dino believed it was a divine message. It is so strange,” she went on, her voice distant and meditative, “this life of men seized by such ferocious, unshakable beliefs that look like madness to everyone around them. Dino was no vulgar, uneducated fanatic. And that Fra Girolamo—his very voice seemed to seep into my bones, convincing me there is some hidden truth to the things that move him, some truth I have never once understood.”

“That was only because you were overwrought, my Romola,” Tito soothed her. “Your brother’s state of mind was no more than that old brand of theosophy that has plagued excitable, dreamy men for centuries—the very same ideas your father’s old rival Marsilio Ficino pores over in the New Platonist texts. The only difference is that Dino’s passionate nature drove him to act out what other men only write about and debate over wine. As for Fra Girolamo, he is nothing but a narrow-minded monk with a gift for preaching and scaring the easily swayed crowds. Any loud, urgent voice would have shaken you, after all you had been through. Once your mind has a little rest, you will judge all these things exactly as you always have.”

“I do not think I will ever be angry with poor Dino again,” Romola said. “I was furious with him when he first started speaking, my heart felt closed off to him. But since then I have thought far less of my own hurt, and far more of what he was suffering. Oh Tito, it was so piteous to watch his young life burn out that way. That yearning, straining look he gave the crucifix as he gasped for his last breath—I will never forget it. Last night I sat staring at that crucifix for hours, trying to imagine it giving him comfort, until by lamplight it felt as if the carved suffering face was actually shining with pity.”

“My Romola, promise me you will resist these dark, lingering thoughts,” Tito said. “They are fancies fit for sickly shut-in nuns, not for my golden-haired Aurora, who looks like she was made to chase away all these twilight nightmares. Try not to think of them now. We will not get many more chances to be alone like this.” He turned her face gently toward him with a light touch of his right hand, his tone soft and beseeching.

Romola had been staring absently through the arched opening, not seeing the distant hill, still stuck back in that cold San Marco chapter house, watching the pale faces of sorrow and death play out before her. Tito’s touch and his warm voice pulled her back to the sunlit present, and suddenly she was looking at his rich, dark, vivid beauty—the kind of beauty that seemed to hold every image of joy the world had to offer: purple grapevines strung between gnarled elms, golden corn ripening under the hot summer sun, bright-winged darting bees and butterflies drifting over clover, dancers’ feet pounding the earth in celebration as they held cymbals aloft, light lilting melodies sung over the thrumming rhythm of stringed lutes. Every sight and sound of Nature revelling in her own strength was wrapped up in him. The transition felt bewildering, dizzying: how could that worn, anguished face of her dying brother, straining for an invisible peace he would never reach, exist in the same world as this bright, satisfied youthfulness, this sun-god who seemed to have never known a single dark night? For a split second, Romola felt that sharp, unanswerable question as a dizzy lurch, a craving for something solid to hold onto. It was over as quickly as a sigh, that seed of all the ages’ great philosophical doubt compressed into a single moment of uncertainty, and it vanished fast, washed away by the rush of her young, bright love for the man before her.

“Your mind is wandering far away from our love, my Romola,” Tito murmured, soft and gently reproachful. “It feels like I am a forgotten thing to you.”

She stared back at his beseeching eyes in silence, until every last trace of sadness melted out of her own. “My joy,” she said, in her full, clear, warm voice.

“Do you really care for me enough to banish all those cold fancies, or are you always going to suspect me of being the Great Tempter?” Tito teased, his bright, familiar smile spreading across his face.

“How could I not care for you more than everything else in the world?” Romola laughed softly. “Everything I ever felt before—my love for my father, my loneliness growing up in that library—all of it was just preparation for loving you. You would laugh at me, Tito, if you knew what kind of husband I once imagined I would have married: some old scholar with deep lines carved into his face, like Alamanno Rinuccini, streaked with grey hair, who would agree with my father and argue for the Aristotelians, and be perfectly happy to live shut up in our library with him. I used to read about grand love in the old poets, but I never once dreamed anything like that could happen to me here, in our dusty old Florentine library. And then you came. You were so kind to my father, and slowly I started to believe that life could be happy for me, too.”

“My goddess—there is no other woman in the world like you,” Tito breathed, equal parts fondness and wonder at the strange, perfect mix of quiet majesty and sweet simplicity that made her Romola. “But dearest,” he went on, a little timidly, “if you cared half as much about our marriage as you ought, you would persuade your father and Messer Bernardo not to put it off any longer. You act like you do not mind the delays at all.”

“I do mind, Tito, more than anything. But I am sure my godfather will insist on even more delay now, because of Dino’s death. He never once agreed with my father disowning Dino, you know that. He has always said we ought to wait until you have spent at least a full year in Florence. Do not judge him too harshly. I know he is narrow and set in his ways, but he is so deeply noble. He often says my father is a fool for wanting to hoard his library to keep it with his name, and yet he would do everything in his power to make sure my father’s wish is carried out. That seems so great and noble to me—the ability to respect a feeling you do not share, or even understand.”

“I bear no grudge against Messer Bernardo for thinking you are far too precious for me, my Romola,” Tito said, and that was entirely true. “But your father knows his son is dead, then?”

“Yes, I told him. I could not keep it from him. I told him where I had been, that I had seen Dino die, but nothing else. He has forbidden me to speak of it ever again. But he has been so quiet all morning, making those restless, fidgety movements that always break my heart—the ones that look like he is trying to claw his way out of the prison of his own blindness. Let us go down to him now. I convinced him to try to rest, because he barely slept a wink last night. Your voice will soothe him, Tito, it always does.”

“Not one kiss? I have not gotten a single one yet,” Tito said, his soft, reproachful tone giving him an air of sweet, vulnerable dependence that felt utterly charming on a man so gifted he usually seemed to have every right to presumption. A bright pink blush spread across Romola’s face and neck as fast as light, as she leaned in toward him. It felt impossible that their kisses could ever grow commonplace.

“Let us walk all the way around the loggia once first,” Romola said, “before we go down.”

“There is always something grim and grave about Florence to me,” Tito said, as they paused at the street-facing edge of the loggia, looking out over the red rooftops to the far bank of the Arno. “Even its merriment feels sharp, shrill and hard—biting, not warm and joyful. I wish we lived down in Southern Italy, where thought does not get worn down by heavy study, but by delicious, drowsy languor that never touches the sharp, fiery Florentine wits. I should love to see you under that soft southern sun, laying among wild flowers, no heavy books or sad memories, nothing but sweet enjoyment, while I lean over you and pluck at the lute, singing you a little tune that feels as much a part of the warm light as the breeze. You have never known that careless, happy life of the nymphs, my Romola.”

“No, but I have dreamed of it ever since you came,” Romola said. “I am so thirsty for that deep draught of joy, for a life as bright and warm as you are. But let us not think of it right now, Tito. It feels to me as if there would still be those pale, sad faces lingering in the flowers, those eyes that strain for something they can never reach. Come. Let us go down to my father.”
