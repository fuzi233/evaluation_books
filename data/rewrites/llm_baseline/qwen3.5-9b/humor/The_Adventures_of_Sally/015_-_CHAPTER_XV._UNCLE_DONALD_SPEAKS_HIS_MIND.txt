There exists a specific breed of man—Bruce Carmyle was a prime specimen—who possesses a stubborn resilience, a gritty refusal to admit defeat that serves them equally well in matters of romance as it does in the ring. As a suitor, Bruce resembled that particular type of boxer who delivers his finest punch only after absorbing a solid blow to a tender spot. Though Sally had flatly rejected his proposal at Monk's Crofton, Bruce never considered the matter settled. Having spent his entire life acquiring whatever he desired, he intended to secure her now. He was convinced he loved her. While fleeting doubts had occasionally surfaced, they evaporated instantly in the face of temporary rejection. The bohemian spark in her personality, which had previously grated against his orderly sensibilities, was now conveniently forgotten. All that remained in Bruce's memory were the brilliance of her eyes, the confident tilt of her chin, and her dashing figure. Her cheerful charm seemed to lash at him like a whip in the dark hours of sleepless nights, driving him relentlessly forward. Quietly and methodically, much like a respectable wolf tracking Little Red Riding Hood, he began his pursuit. Delicacy or imagination might have held him back, but he lacked both.

His preparations for departure, though executed with speed and secrecy, failed to escape the watchful eyes of the Family. Within many English households, a system of communication akin to African tribal telepathy ensures that news travels faster than sound. On his final night in London, the Family's designated emissary arrived at Bruce's apartment on South Audley Street: Uncle Donald, in person. Two hundred and forty pounds of pure flesh descended upon the room, causing the chair to groan ominously under the weight. Years ago, at Monk's Crofton, Sally had ruined a morning for her brother Fillmore by declaring Uncle Donald the living embodiment of what he would become as an adult. A childhood superstition regarding his "weak heart" had prevented the Family's managing director from exercising for nearly half a century. Combined with a profound aversion to movement and one of the most hearty appetites in the southwestern postal district, Uncle Donald, at sixty-two, was certainly not a man one wished to see lounging in armchairs.

Bruce's usual deference was tinged with barely concealed disdain as he surveyed the intruder. Uncle Donald's walrus mustache heaved rhythmically with his labored breathing, resembling seaweed tossed by a rising tide. Stairs had clearly been involved in his ascent.

"What's this?" Uncle Donald managed to gasp finally. "What's this?"

"Yes," replied Mr. Carmyle, curtly. For the first time in his life, he felt that familiar pang of guilty stealth usually reserved for his cousin Ginger when caught in Uncle Donald's presence.

"You're leaving?"

"Yes."

"Where to?"

"America."

"When?"

"To-morrow morning."

"Why?"

This exchange was recorded here as if it were a rapid-fire routine between vaudeville comedians, yet in truth, Uncle Donald's unique conversational style stretched it over nearly three minutes. After every answer and before every question, he would pant, sigh, and inhale deeply through his mustache with such agonizing deliberation that his host found his nerves fraying under the strain.

"You're chasing that girl," accused Uncle Donald.

Bruce flushed darkly. At that precise moment, a thought flitted through his mind that Ginger's behavior at Bleke's Coffee House on a notorious occasion had perhaps not been entirely indefensible. It was undeniable that the Family's Chosen One could be trying.

"Would you care for a whisky and soda, Uncle Donald?" Bruce offered, attempting to change the subject.

"Yes," his relative agreed, fulfilling a vow made in the early eighteen-eighties never to decline such an offering. "Gimme!"

One might have expected this gesture to soften the atmosphere. Instead, having consumed the restorative, Uncle Donald launched his assault with renewed vigor.

"I never thought you were a fool before," he stated severely.

Bruce's proud spirit bristled. Such interrogations, common enough with his cousin Ginger, were foreign territory for him. Until now, his actions had escaped both praise and critique.

"I'm not a fool."

"You are a fool. A damn fool," Uncle Donald insisted, narrowing his gaze. "I didn't like the girl. Never did. She wasn't nice. Didn't like her. From the very start."

"Need we discuss this?" Bruce asked, slipping effortlessly into his grand manner.

The head of the Family took a sip of his drink, blew air through his mustache, and exhaled.

"Need we discuss it?" he retorted sharply. "We *are* discussing it! Do you realize I climbed these blasted stairs with my weak heart just to hear this? Gimme another!"

Mr. Carmyle obliged. "'Tis a terrible affair," Uncle Donald moaned, repeating the motion. "Shockingly bad. If your poor father were alive, what do you suppose he'd say about you tearing across the globe after this girl? Let me tell you what he'd say. He'd say... What sort of whisky is this?"

"O'Rafferty Special."

"New to me. Not bad. Quite decent. Sound. Mellow. Where did you get it?"

"Bilby's in Oxford Street."

"Must order some. Mellow. He'd say... well, heaven knows what he'd say. Why are you doing it? What drives you? That's what I can't grasp. None of us can. It puzzles your uncle George. It baffles your aunt Geraldine. No one understands it. The girl is simply after your money. Anyone can see that."

"Pardon me, Uncle Donald," Bruce said stiffly, "but that is surely absurd. If that were true, why would she have refused me at Monk's Crofton?"

"Drawing you on," Uncle Donald declared immediately. "Luring you in. A well-known tactic. Back in 1881, when I was at Oxford, a girl tried to lure me in. Had I possessed less sense and a stronger heart..." He paused. "What do you know of this girl? What do you know of her? That's the crux of the matter. Who is she? Where did you meet her?"

"I met her at Roville, in France."

"Traveling with her family?"

"Alone," admitted Bruce, reluctantly.

"Not even with that brother of hers?"

"Bad!" Uncle Donald exclaimed. "Very bad!"

"American women are accustomed to greater independence than English ones."

"That young man," Uncle Donald continued, following a thread of thought, "will be fat as a pig within a year if he doesn't watch his diet. Traveling alone, was she? How did you manage? Did you catch her eye on the pier?"

"Really, Uncle Donald!"

"Well, you must have gotten to know her somehow."

"I was introduced to her by Lancelot. She was a friend of his."

"Lancelot!" Uncle Donald erupted, trembling violently like a struck jellyfish at the mere mention of the hated name. "Well, that tells you everything about the sort of girl she is. Any girl who associates with... Unpack!"

"I beg your pardon?"

"Unpack! We cannot proceed with this nonsense. Absolutely not. Find yourself a girl who makes a suitable wife. Your Aunt Mary has been meeting some people named Bassington-Bassington, relatives of the Kent Bassington-Bassingtons... the eldest daughter is a charming girl, perfect for you."

Outside the pages of old-fashioned fiction, few people truly grind their teeth, but Bruce Carmyle came closer than anyone ever had. He scowled blackly, and the last vestige of politeness vanished.

"I shall do nothing of the kind," he said briefly. "I sail to-morrow."

Uncle Donald had faced defiance from a nephew before, but it had not prepared him for the sensation. He felt a distinct sense of powerlessness. Nothing is more difficult than knowing what to do next when someone defies you.

"Eh?" he sputtered.

Realizing that Bruce had begun to defy him, Uncle Donald apparently decided to make a proper show of it.

"I am over twenty-one," Bruce announced. "I am financially independent. I shall do as I please."

"But, consider!" Uncle Donald pleaded, painfully aware of the hollowness of his words. "Reflect!"

"I have reflected."

"Your position in the county..."

"I've thought of that."

"You could marry anyone you pleased."

"I'm going to."

"You are determined to run off to God-knows-where after this Miss-I-can't-even-remember-her-damned-name?"

"Yes."

"Have you considered," Uncle Donald intoned portentously, "that you owe a duty to the Family?"

Bruce's patience snapped, and he plummeted to absolute Ginger-levels of blunt honesty.

"Oh, damn the Family!" he shouted.

A painful silence ensued, broken only by the relieved sigh of the armchair as Uncle Donald heaved himself out of it.

"After that," Uncle Donald muttered, "I have nothing more to say."

"Good!" Mr. Carmyle said rudely, shedding all remaining shame. "Except this: If you return home married to that girl, I'll cut you in Piccadilly. By George, I will!"

He moved toward the door. Bruce looked down his nose without speaking. A tense moment hung in the air.
