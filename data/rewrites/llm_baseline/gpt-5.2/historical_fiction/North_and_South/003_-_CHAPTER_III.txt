“The More Haste the Worse Speed.”

Margaret had been thinking of Henry Lennox only an instant before—of his quick, half-amused questions in Harley Street, and the way he had asked what she did with her hours now that London was laid aside. It was as if speaking of sun had brought its warmth at once: when Sarah announced him, a brightness ran over Margaret’s face; she set down her work and went forward with both hands held out.

“Tell mamma, Sarah,” she said. “Mamma and I want to ask you so many questions about Edith; I am so much obliged to you for coming.”

“Did not I say that I should?” he answered; and though his words were simple enough, his voice dropped into a lower key, as if they were suddenly alone in the room.

“But we heard of you so far away in the Highlands that I never thought Hampshire could come in.”

“Oh,” he replied lightly, “our young couple were playing such foolish pranks—running all sorts of risks, climbing this mountain, sailing on that lake—that I really thought they needed a Mentor. Indeed they did. They were quite beyond my uncle’s management, and kept the old gentleman in a panic for sixteen hours out of the twenty-four. When I once saw how unfit they were to be trusted alone, I thought it my duty not to leave them till I had seen them safely embarked at Plymouth.”

“Have you been at Plymouth? Oh! Edith never named that. To be sure, she has written in such a hurry lately. Did they really sail on Tuesday?”

“Really sailed, and relieved me from many responsibilities. Edith gave me all sorts of messages for you. I believe I have a little diminutive note somewhere—yes, here it is.”

Margaret’s fingers closed over the scrap as if it were something alive.

“Oh! thank you,” she exclaimed; and then, half wishing to read it unwatched, she made her excuse of going—again—to tell her mother (for Sarah must surely have mistaken) that Mr. Lennox was there.

When she left him, Henry Lennox did what was habitual with him: he took the measure of his surroundings as swiftly as he took the measure of men. The little drawing-room was at its best under the morning sun that streamed through the bow-window. One casement stood open; roses and scarlet honeysuckle leaned in as if they belonged indoors, and the small lawn beyond burned with bright geraniums and verbenas. Yet the very glory without made the colours within seem gentler, older—faded chintz that had been washed often, a carpet that had lived long past its first youth. It was a poorer frame than he had expected for Margaret—Margaret, with her queenly carriage and clear, steadfast eyes.

He picked up a book from the table. Dante—Paradiso—in old Italian vellum and gold; beside it a dictionary, and a list of words in Margaret’s handwriting. Dull words in themselves; and yet he lingered over them, liking the look of her careful strokes, the patient, almost reverent way she had netted meaning. He set them down with a sigh.

“The living is evidently as small as she said,” he thought. “Strange, for the Beresfords belong to a good family.”

Margaret had found her mother in one of Mrs. Hale’s fitful moods, when every common arrangement turned into an oppression. The very fact of a visitor—Edith’s brother-in-law, almost a relation—took the shape of a hardship, though under it a shy gratification trembled.

“It is most unfortunate!” Mrs. Hale said. “We are dining early, and having nothing but cold meat, in order that the servants may get on with their ironing; and yet, of course, we must ask him to dinner. And your papa is in such low spirits this morning about something—I don’t know what. I went into the study just now, and he had his face on the table, covering it with his hands. I told him I was sure Helstone air did not agree with him any more than with me, and he suddenly lifted up his head and begged me not to speak a word more against Helstone—he could not bear it; if there was one place he loved on earth it was Helstone. But I am sure, for all that, it is the damp and relaxing air.”

Margaret listened with the patience she always kept ready for her mother’s complaints; but as she heard of her father’s bowed head, it was as if a thin cold cloud had slid between her and the sun. Still she drew her mother gently back to what was needful.

“Papa likes Mr. Lennox; they got on together famously at the wedding breakfast. I daresay his coming will do papa good. And never mind the dinner, dear mamma. Cold meat will do capitally for a lunch—which is the light in which Mr. Lennox will most likely look upon a two o’clock dinner.”

“But what are we to do with him till then? It is only half-past ten now.”

“I’ll ask him to go out sketching with me. I know he draws, and that will take him out of your way, mamma. Only do come in now; he will think it so strange if you don’t.”

Mrs. Hale took off her black silk apron and smoothed her face into the pretty, lady-like composure that sat on her as naturally as a well-folded shawl. She greeted Mr. Lennox with cordiality due to one who was almost family. He evidently expected to be asked to spend the day, and accepted with a readiness so frank that it made Mrs. Hale wish—absurdly, remorsefully—that she could add something to the cold beef.

He was pleased with everything; delighted with Margaret’s idea of going out sketching; would not have Mr. Hale disturbed for the world, with the prospect of meeting him at dinner. Margaret brought out her drawing materials for him to choose from—paper, brushes, a little box of colours, the clean sharp scent of pencil-wood when she opened the case. After due selection, they set out with the merriest spirits in the world.

“Now, please, just stop here for a minute or two,” said Margaret. “These are the cottages that haunted me so during the rainy fortnight, reproaching me for not having sketched them.”

“Before they tumbled down and were no more seen,” he said. “Truly, if they are to be sketched—and they are very picturesque—we had better not put it off till next year. But where shall we sit?”

“Oh! you might have come straight from chambers in the Temple, instead of having been two months in the Highlands! Look at this beautiful trunk of a tree which the woodcutters have left just in the right place for the light. I will put my plaid over it, and it will be a regular forest throne.”

“With your feet in that puddle for a regal footstool! Stay—I will move, and then you can come nearer this way. Who lives in these cottages?”

“They were built by squatters fifty or sixty years ago. One is uninhabited; the foresters are going to take it down, as soon as the other is dead—poor old fellow! Look—there he is—I must go and speak to him. He is so deaf you will hear all our secrets.”

The old man stood bareheaded in the sun, leaning on his stick at the front of his cottage. His stiff features softened into a slow smile as Margaret went up and spoke to him—her voice pitched a little higher, not from condescension but from the instinct to send kindness across a failing sense. Even her steps altered; she moved as if she belonged to the place, as if these cottages and this old Isaac were part of her own history and would be part of it still when roofs had sunk and names been forgotten.

Henry Lennox, with the quick economy of a practised draughtsman, set the two figures into his sketch—lightly at first, as a subordinate incident—then with a subtle emphasis that betrayed itself only when they compared their work.

When at last Margaret came back—putting away the little slips of wet paper, tipping out the grey water from her brush-cup—she saw what he had done. She laughed, and blushed in the same breath.

“Now, I call that treacherous. I little thought you were making old Isaac and me into subjects when you told me to ask him the history of these cottages.”

“It was irresistible,” he answered. “You can’t know how strong a temptation it was. I hardly dare tell you how much I shall like this sketch.”

He was not quite sure whether she heard that last sentence before she went down to the brook to wash her palette; the water took the faint stains from her fingers, and the air smelt of wet earth and crushed fern. She came back rather flushed, but with a look perfectly innocent, unconscious—unaware of what her presence could do to another person’s mind. He was glad of that; the words had slipped from him unawares, a rare thing for one who so often premeditated his actions.

When they reached home, the aspect of it was all right and bright. The clouds on Mrs. Hale’s brow had cleared under the propitious influence of a brace of carp, most opportunely presented by a neighbour. The fish lay on a dish like two silvered torpedoes, their clean river-smell rising sharp and fresh; already Dixon’s footsteps sounded in brisk purpose, as if the kitchen had awakened into ceremony. Even the house seemed to breathe more freely for the promise of something hot, something properly cooked, instead of cold slices eaten in apology.

Mr. Hale had returned from his morning’s round and was awaiting his visitor just outside the wicket-gate. He looked a complete gentleman in his rather threadbare coat and well-worn hat. Margaret’s pride in him sprang up, fresh and tender as always; yet her quick eye travelled over his face and found traces of unusual disturbance—put aside, not cleared away.

Mr. Hale asked to look at their sketches.

“I think you have made the tints on the thatch too dark, have you not?” he said, returning Margaret’s and holding out his hand for Mr. Lennox’s, which was withheld from him one moment—no more.

“No, papa! I don’t think I have. The house-leek and stone-crop have grown so much darker in the rain. Is it not like, papa?” She peeped over his shoulder as he looked at the figures in Mr. Lennox’s drawing.

“Yes, very like. Your figure and way of holding yourself is capital. And it is just poor old Isaac’s stiff way of stooping his long rheumatic back. What is this hanging from the branch of the tree? Not a bird’s nest, surely.”

“Oh no! that is my bonnet. I never can draw with my bonnet on; it makes my head so hot. I wonder if I could manage figures. There are so many people about here whom I should like to sketch.”

“I should say that a likeness you very much wish to take you would always succeed in,” said Mr. Lennox. “I have great faith in the power of will. I think myself I have succeeded pretty well in yours.”

Mr. Hale had preceded them into the house while Margaret lingered to pluck some roses, meaning to fasten them at her throat and waist for dinner. The flowers were warm from the sun; their scent rose thick and sweet when her fingers bruised a leaf.

“A regular London girl would understand the implied meaning of that speech,” thought Henry Lennox. “She would be up to looking through every sentence a young man made her for the arrière-pensée of a compliment. But I don’t believe Margaret—Stay!” he exclaimed, suddenly. “Let me help you.”

He gathered for her some velvety cramoisy roses above her reach; then dividing the spoil, he placed two in his own button-hole, and sent her in pleased and happy to arrange her flowers.

At dinner, talk flowed quietly and agreeably. There were plenty of questions—news of Mrs. Shaw’s movements in Italy to exchange, scraps of travel, hints of Edith’s letters. In the interest of what was said, in the unpretending simplicity of the parsonage ways, and above all in the neighbourhood of Margaret, Mr. Lennox forgot the little disappointment of that morning—forgot the shabbiness he had noted, the smallness of the income she had spoken of so simply, as if money were merely another kind of weather.

“Margaret, my child,” said Mr. Hale, as a freshly-decanted bottle of wine was placed on the table with something of hospitable pride, “you might have gathered us some pears for our dessert.”

Mrs. Hale started, hurried. Desserts had the air of being impromptu and unusual things at the parsonage, though if Mr. Hale had only looked behind him he would have seen biscuits and marmalade and other little stored comforts arranged with formal care upon the sideboard. But the idea of pears had seized him and would not be dislodged.

“There are a few brown beurrés against the south wall,” he said, with the pleased insistence of a man clinging to the zest and perfection of an hour stolen from anxiety. “They are worth all foreign fruits and preserves. Run, Margaret, and gather some.”

“I propose that we adjourn into the garden, and eat them there,” said Mr. Lennox. “Nothing is so delicious as to set one’s teeth into the crisp, juicy fruit, warm and scented by the sun. The worst is, the wasps are impudent enough to dispute it with one, even at the very crisis and summit of enjoyment.”

He rose as if to follow Margaret, awaiting Mrs. Hale’s permission. She would rather have wound up the meal properly, with all the little ceremonies that had gone on so smoothly hitherto—especially as she and Dixon had fetched the finger-glasses from the store-room on purpose, to be as correct as became General Shaw’s widow’s sister. But Mr. Hale got up directly, prepared to accompany his guest, and she could only submit.

“I shall arm myself with a knife,” said Mr. Hale. “The days of eating fruit so primitively as you describe are over with me. I must pare it and quarter it before I can enjoy it.”

The garden received them with a softness of sound as palpable as scent. Bees hummed busily in the hives along the south wall; their small, steady labour made a kind of domestic music, a kindly underpinning to the hush. The leaves overhead held still; now and then a bird gave one clear call that seemed less a note than a mark of place. It was not silence, but a living quiet—each sound in proportion, no harsh clangour to startle the mind.

Margaret brought back the pears in her apron, and made a plate of a broad beet-root leaf, which threw up the brown-gold colour admirably. Their skins looked russeted with sun; their fragrance, when she lifted one, was honeyed and faintly spicy.

Mr. Lennox looked more at her than at the fruit. Mr. Hale, absorbed, chose fastidiously the ripest, sat down on the garden bench, and began to enjoy it at leisure, peeling it in one long strip of silver-paper thinness.

Margaret and Mr. Lennox strolled along the little terrace-walk under the south wall, where the bees still worked, and where heat lay stored in the bricks like a gentle secret.

“What a perfect life you seem to live here!” he said. “I have always felt rather contemptuously towards the poets before, with their wishes—‘Mine be a cot beside a hill’—and that sort of thing; but now I am afraid the truth is I have been nothing better than a Cockney. Just now I feel as if twenty years’ hard study of law would be amply rewarded by one year of such an exquisite serene life as this—such skies!” He looked up. “Such crimson and amber foliage, so perfectly motionless as that!”

“You must please to remember that our skies are not always as deep a blue as they are now,” Margaret replied. “We have rain, and our leaves do fall, and get sodden. Though I think Helstone is about as perfect a place as any in the world. Recollect how you rather scorned my description one evening in Harley Street—‘a village in a tale.’”

“Scorned, Margaret! That is rather a hard word.”

“Perhaps it is. Only I know I should have liked to have talked to you of what I was very full at the time, and you—what must I call it then?—spoke disrespectfully of Helstone as a mere village in a tale.”

“I will never do so again,” he said warmly.

They turned the corner of the walk. He hesitated—so unusual a pause in the fluent lawyer that Margaret looked up in questioning wonder. In that instant, from something about him she could not name—his altered breathing, the way his voice was about to change—she wished herself back with her mother, with her father, anywhere away from him; for she knew he was going to say something to which she would not know how to reply.

Then her pride rose to meet her fear. She would not shrink. She could answer—and answer rightly. It was poor and despicable to tremble before words, as if she had not the power to end them with her high maidenly dignity.

“Margaret,” he said, taking her by surprise, getting sudden possession of her hand so that she was forced to stand still and listen—despising herself for the fluttering at her heart all the time—“Margaret, I wish you did not like Helstone so much—did not seem so perfectly calm and happy here. I have been hoping for these three months past to find you regretting London—and London friends—a little—enough to make you listen more kindly” (for she was quietly, firmly striving to extricate her hand from his grasp) “to one who has not much to offer, it is true—nothing but prospects in the future—but who does love you, Margaret, almost in spite of himself. Margaret, have I startled you too much? Speak!”

He saw her lips quiver as if she were going to cry. The steady garden sounds—the bees’ hum, the soft scrape of his boot on gravel—seemed suddenly too loud, too near, as if the world had narrowed to this one stretch of walk and the pressure of his hand.

She made a strong effort to be calm. She would not speak till she mastered her voice; and then she said:

“I was startled. I did not know that you cared for me in that way. I have always thought of you as a friend; and, please, I would rather go on thinking of you so. I don’t like to be spoken to as you have been doing. I cannot answer you as you want me to do, and yet I should feel so sorry if I vexed you.”

“Margaret,” he said, looking into her eyes—eyes so open and straight, so full of good faith and reluctance to wound—that what he was going to ask died on his lips. “Forgive me! I have been too abrupt. I am punished. Only let me hope. Give me the poor comfort of telling me you have never seen any one whom you could—” Again he paused; he could not end the sentence.

Margaret reproached herself acutely as the cause of his distress.

“Ah! if you had but never got this fancy into your head! It was such a pleasure to think of you as a friend.”

“But I may hope—may I not, Margaret—that some time you will think of me as a lover? Not yet, I see—there is no hurry—but some time—”

She was silent for a minute or two, trying to discover the truth as it was in her own heart before replying; and then she said slowly:

“I have never thought of—you—but as a friend. I like to think of you so; but I am sure I could never think of you as anything else. Pray let us both forget that all this—” She had been going to say “disagreeable,” but stopped short—“conversation has taken place.”

He paused before replying. Then, in his habitual coldness of tone, he answered:

“Of course, as your feelings are so decided, and as this conversation has been so evidently unpleasant to you, it had better not be remembered. That is all very fine in theory, that plan of forgetting whatever is painful; but it will be somewhat difficult for me, at least, to carry it into execution.”

“You are vexed,” she said sadly. “Yet how can I help it?”

She looked so truly grieved that he struggled a moment with his real disappointment, and then answered more cheerfully—yet with a little hardness that cut even through his attempt at ease.

“You should make allowances for the mortification—not only of a lover, Margaret, but of a man not given to romance in general—prudent, worldly, as some people call me—who has been carried out of his usual habits by the force of a passion. Well—we will say no more of that. But in the one outlet which he has formed for the deeper and better feelings of his nature, he meets with rejection and repulse. I shall have to console myself with scorning my own folly. A struggling barrister to think of matrimony!”

Margaret could not answer. The whole tone annoyed her. It called out, as if with a needle’s point, the very differences that had often repelled her in him; and yet he was the pleasantest man, the most sympathising friend, the person who in Harley Street had seemed to understand her better than any other. Pain was there—true pain at having refused him—yet a tinge of contempt mixed with it. Her beautiful lip curled in slight disdain.

It was well that, having made the round of the garden, they came suddenly upon Mr. Hale. His whereabouts had been forgotten by them both. He had not yet finished the pear he had peeled so delicately; he still ate it with deliberate enjoyment, as if time were his obedient servant. It was like the tale of the eastern king who dipped his head into a basin at the magician’s command, and in the instant it took to lift it out had gone through the experience of a lifetime.

Margaret felt stunned, unable to recover her self-possession enough to join in the trivial talk that followed between her father and Mr. Lennox. She was grave and little disposed to speak, full of wonder when Mr. Lennox would go and allow her to relax into thought on the events of the last quarter of an hour.

He was almost as anxious to depart as she was for him to leave; but a few minutes of light, careless talking—carried on at whatever effort—was a sacrifice he owed to his mortified vanity or his self-respect. He glanced from time to time at her sad, pensive face.

“I am not so indifferent to her as she believes,” he thought. “I do not give up hope.”

Before a quarter of an hour was over, he had fallen into a way of conversing with quiet sarcasm—speaking of life in London and life in the country as if he were conscious of his own second, mocking self, and afraid of his satire. Mr. Hale was puzzled. His visitor was different from the man he had seen at the wedding breakfast, and at dinner to-day—a lighter, cleverer, more worldly man; and, as such, dissonant to Mr. Hale, as a sudden harsh note jars in the midst of a gentle piece.

It was a relief to all three when Mr. Lennox said he must go directly if he meant to catch the five o’clock train. They proceeded to the house to find Mrs. Hale and wish her good-bye.

At the last moment Henry Lennox’s real self broke through the crust.

“Margaret, don’t despise me. I have a heart, notwithstanding all this good-for-nothing way of talking. As a proof of it, I believe I love you more than ever—if I do not hate you—for the disdain with which you have listened to me during this last half-hour. Good-bye, Margaret—Margaret!”
