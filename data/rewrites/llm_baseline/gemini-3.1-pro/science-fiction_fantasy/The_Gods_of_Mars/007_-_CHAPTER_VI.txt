“What is it?” I asked, turning to the girl as the delicate acoustic ecology of the gardens was instantly shattered.

For answer, she pointed to the sky. I looked, and there, slicing through the upper atmosphere, I saw shadowy bodies flitting hither and thither high over temple, court, and garden. Almost immediately, the harsh, grating mechanical dissonance of their engines violated the carefully curated sonic environment of the Valley Dor. Flashes of light broke from these strange objects, followed by a deafening, industrial roar of musketry that obliterated the organic symphony of rustling leaves and distant waters. Answering flashes and roars erupted from the temple and rampart, turning the sanctuary into a cacophony of noise pollution that physically battered the eardrums.

“The black pirates of Barsoom, O Prince,” said Thuvia, her voice a breathy, ancient dialect that felt increasingly fragile against the booming, hegemonic thunder of the assault.

In great, cyclical circles that spoke of a multi-generational choreography of violence, the aircraft of the marauders swept lower toward the defending forces of the therns. Volley after volley they vomited upon the temple guards; volley on volley crashed through the thin, ozone-scented air toward the fleeting and illusive fliers. As the pirates swooped closer to the ground, thern soldiery poured from the temples into the gardens and courts. Their emergence into the open brought a score of fliers darting toward us from all directions. The therns fired upon them through shields affixed to their rifles, but on, steadily on, came the grim, black craft. 

They were small fliers for the most part, built for two to three men, emitting a shrill, commercialized whine that spoke of mass-produced warfare rather than the bespoke craftsmanship of agrarian life. A few larger ones kept high aloft, dropping bombs that burst with the astringent, chemical tang of combusted propulsion-powder, drowning out the gentle olfactory landscape of the valley. 

At length, with a concerted rush—evidently in response to a signal of command barked in a harsh, dominant lexicon—the pirates in our immediate vicinity dashed recklessly to the ground in the very midst of the thern soldiery. Scarcely waiting for their craft to touch, the creatures manning them leaped among the therns with the fury of demons. 

Such fighting! Never had I witnessed its like before. I had thought the green Martians the most ferocious warriors in the universe, but the awful abandon with which the black pirates threw themselves upon their foes transcended everything. Beneath the brilliant light of Mars’ two glorious moons, the whole scene presented itself in vivid distinctness. The golden-haired, white-skinned therns battling with desperate courage in hand-to-hand conflict with their ebony-skinned foemen. 

The tragedy of it lay heavily upon my senses, a profound disruption of domestic and agrarian routine. Here, a little knot of struggling warriors trampled a bed of gorgeous *pimalia*—blossoms meticulously cultivated by generations of agrarian elders to yield the saffron-hued, bitter-almond-scented pistils essential to Barsoomian festival broths. Now, their delicate oils were crushed into the dirt, mixing with the coppery, hot-iron tang of fresh blood. There, the curved sword of a black man found the heart of a thern, leaving its dead foeman to bleed out at the foot of a wondrous statue carved from a living ruby; yonder, a dozen therns pressed a single pirate back upon a bench of emerald. 

A little to one side stood Thuvia, the Thark, and I. The tide of battle had not reached us, but the fighters swung close enough that we might distinctly note them. The black pirates interested me immensely. I had heard vague rumours of them during my former life on Mars; tales passed down like dying languages around the cooking fires of the red men. They were popularly supposed to inhabit the lesser moon, from which they descended upon Barsoom at long intervals. Where they visited, they wrought the most horrible atrocities, carrying away firearms, ammunition, and young girls. These latter, rumour had it, they sacrificed to some terrible god in an orgy of consumption, a dark inversion of the sacred, communal act of sharing sustenance.

I had an excellent opportunity to examine them. They were large men, possibly six feet and over in height. Their features were clear-cut and handsome; their eyes were well set and large, though a slight narrowness lent them a crafty appearance. The iris was of extreme blackness, while the eyeball itself was quite white and clear. The physical structure of their bodies seemed identical with those of the therns, the red men, and my own. They moved with the fluid, caloric efficiency of men sustained by a fiercely rich, perhaps iron-dense culinary tradition. Only in the colour of their skin did they differ materially from us; it possessed the appearance of polished ebony, which added to rather than detracted from their marvellous beauty. 

But if their bodies were divine, their hearts were quite the reverse. Never did I witness such a malign lust for blood. All about us in the garden lay their sinister craft, which the therns, for some reason then unaccountable to me, made no effort to injure. Now and again a black warrior would rush from a nearby temple bearing a young woman in his arms. Straight for his flier he would leap while his comrades rushed to cover his escape. The therns would hasten to rescue the girl, and in an instant the two would be swallowed in the vortex of a maelstrom of yelling devils, hacking and hewing like fiends incarnate. But always, it seemed, the black pirates of Barsoom were victorious, and the girl, brought miraculously unharmed through the conflict, was borne away into the outer darkness.

Fighting similar to that which surrounded us could be heard in both directions as far as sound carried. As the noise of the battle receded from our position for a moment, leaving a temporary, bruised silence in its wake, Thuvia turned toward me. 

“Do you understand now, O Prince,” she whispered, her native phrasing carrying the poignant melancholy of an endangered cultural memory, “why a million warriors guard the domains of the Holy Therns by day and by night? The scene you are witnessing now is but a repetition of what I have seen enacted a score of times during the fifteen years I have been a prisoner here. From time immemorial, the black pirates of Barsoom have preyed upon the Holy Therns.”

She described a sweeping, multi-generational cycle of harvesting, where the pirates utilized the race as playthings to satisfy their ferocious lust for fighting, collecting a toll in arms, ammunition, and lives. 

“Why don’t they jump in and destroy these fliers?” I asked, gesturing to the unguarded machines. “That would soon put a stop to the attacks.”

“The therns do not dare,” Thuvia replied, her tone reflecting the deep, untranslatable sorrow of subjective subjugation. “They tried it once, ages ago. But the next night, and for a whole moon thereafter, a thousand great black battleships circled the Mountains of Otz, pouring tons of projectiles upon the temples, the gardens, and the courts. The therns know that they live at all only by the sufferance of the black men. They were near to extermination that once, and they will not venture risking it again.”

As she ceased talking, a new element was instilled into the conflict. The great banths which we had liberated in the garden had evidently been awed at first by the overwhelming noise pollution of the battle. But now, driven mad by the continuous, grating acoustic assault and deeply stimulated by the rich, olfactory burst of spilled life-fluids, a great form shot from a clump of low shrubbery into the midst of a struggling mass of humanity. 

A horrid scream of bestial rage broke from the banth as he felt warm flesh beneath his powerful talons. As though his cry was but a signal to the others, the entire great pack hurled themselves among the fighters. Panic reigned in an instant. Thern and black man turned alike against the common enemy. The awful beasts bore down a hundred men by the mere weight of their great bodies. Leaping and clawing, they mowed down the warriors with their powerful paws, turning for an instant to rend their victims with frightful fangs, reducing the complex, cultured bodies of their prey to mere utilitarian protein. 

The scene was fascinating in its terribleness, but suddenly it came to me that we were wasting valuable time. The therns were so engaged with their terrible assailants that now, if ever, escape should be comparatively easy. As my eyes wandered about the garden, the sight of the hundreds of aircraft lying unguarded around us suggested the simplest avenue to freedom. 

I was thoroughly familiar with the mechanism of every known make of flier on Barsoom. For nine years I had sailed and fought with the navy of Helium. I appreciated these vessels not just as conveyances, but as intricate ecosystems of energetic exchange. Grasping Thuvia by the arm, I whispered to Tars Tarkas to follow me. Quickly we glided toward a small flier which lay furthest from the battling warriors. 

Another instant found us huddled on the tiny deck. My hand was on the starting lever. I pressed my thumb upon the button which controls the ray of repulsion, that splendid discovery of the Martians which permits them to navigate the thin atmosphere of their planet. The craft swayed slightly, its engine vibrating with a low, localized hum that briefly harmonized with the natural resonance of the earth below, but she did not move. 

Then a new cry of warning broke upon our ears. Turning, I saw a dozen black pirates dashing toward us from the melee. We had been discovered. With shrieks of rage rendered in their harsh, guttural tongue, the demons sprang for us. With frenzied insistence, I continued to press the little button, but still the vessel refused to budge. 

Then it came to me. We had stumbled upon a two-man flier. Its ray tanks were charged only with sufficient repulsive energy to lift two ordinary men. The Thark’s immense, dense weight was anchoring us to our doom. 

The blacks were nearly upon us. There was not an instant to be lost. I pressed the button far in and locked it. Then I set the lever at high speed, and as the blacks came yelling upon us, I slipped from the craft’s deck and with drawn long-sword met the attack. At the same moment a girl’s shriek rang out behind me, and an instant later, as the blacks fell upon me, I heard far above my head, faintly in Thuvia’s dialect: 

“My Prince, O my Prince; I would rather remain and die with—”

But the rest was lost in the industrial noise of my assailants. I knew though that my ruse had worked; the vessel carrying Thuvia and Tars Tarkas—and with them, the living repositories of their dying languages and deep cultural histories—was safe. 

For a moment it seemed that I could not withstand the weight of numbers. But again, I found that my earthly strength, fueled by the memory of vital, sun-drenched harvests and the deep marrow-warmth of my Virginian sires, so far transcended that of my opponents that the odds were not so greatly against me. My seething blade wove a net of death. I fought with the measured, passionate precision of a master chef managing the fierce heat of a roasting hearth, parrying and striking with an intimate understanding of physical anatomy. 

For an instant the blacks pressed close, but presently they gave back, and the esteem in which they suddenly had learned to hold my sword arm was writ large upon each countenance. I knew though that it was but a question of minutes before their greater numbers would wear me down. I shuddered at the thought of dying thus, my own personal history and subjective experience rendered forever untranslatable, far from my Dejah Thoris. 

Then the fierce blood lust and the joy of battle surged over me. I put the thought of death out of my mind and fell upon my antagonists with a fury that those who escaped will remember to their dying day. As I fought, I kept my wits at work, searching for an avenue of escape. 

It came from an unexpected quarter out of the black night behind me. I had just disarmed a huge fellow, and for a moment the blacks stood back for a breathing spell. They eyed me with malignant fury, yet withal there was a touch of respect in their demeanour. 

“Thern,” said one, attempting to force my identity into his dominant lexicon, “you fight like a Dator. But for your detestable yellow hair and your white skin you would be an honour to the First Born of Barsoom.”

“I am no thern,” I said, and was about to explain the untranslatable truth of my origins from another world, thinking I might patch a truce. But just at that moment, a heavy object smote me a resounding whack between my shoulders that nearly felled me to the bruised earth. 

As I turned to meet this new enemy, an object passed over my shoulder, striking one of my assailants squarely in the face and knocking him senseless. The air filled with the sharp scent of oxidized iron. I saw that the thing that had struck us was the trailing anchor of a rather fair-sized air vessel, floating slowly above us, not more than fifty feet over our heads. 

Instantly the one chance for escape presented itself. The vessel was slowly rising, the anchor now beyond the blacks and several feet above their heads. With a bound that left them gaping in wide-eyed astonishment, I sprang completely over them. A second leap carried me just high enough to grasp the rapidly receding anchor. 

I hung by one hand, dragging through the branches of the higher vegetation, stripping leaves that released a sudden, bright burst of citrus and crushed pine into the night air, while my late foemen shrieked and howled their untranslatable frustrations beneath me. 

Presently the vessel veered toward the west and swung gracefully to the south. In another instant I was carried beyond the crest of the Golden Cliffs, out over the Valley Dor, where, six thousand feet below me, the Lost Sea of Korus lay shimmering. The deafening cacophony of the gardens fell away, replaced by the pure, rushing acoustic clarity of the high atmosphere. 

Carefully I climbed to a sitting posture across the anchor’s arms. I wondered if by chance the vessel might be deserted, or possibly belong to a friendly people. But I decided to know positively, and at once. With the greatest caution, I commenced to climb slowly up the cold metal of the anchor chain toward the deck above me, savoring the kinetic expenditure of energy. 

One hand had just reached for the vessel’s rail and found it when a fierce black face was thrust over the side, and eyes filled with triumphant hate looked into mine.
