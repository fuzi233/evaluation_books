Loves of my youth—where have you gone to hide yourselves? Tubby, with hair like buttered corn and the courage to suggest we could reach the North Pole by taking the No. 9 omnibus to Baker Street; Langley, whose nose bore the permanent impression of somebody else’s knuckles; stout Shamus, all heart and no legs, who could be “downed” with a sigh and yet could not be persuaded, by any science known to man, to say he was beaten; Neal the thin; Dicky—“dicky Dick”—the fat; Ballett of the tearful eye; Beau Bunnie, lord of many neckties, who fought solemnly in black kid gloves as though a bout were a tea-party that had turned regrettable; and the rest of you, unnamed now but once as necessary to me as bread and jam—where do your creeping ghosts keep school?

Not long ago—unless time is lying to me again—I’m almost certain I saw you, Tubby. It was you, in spite of the side-whiskers and the greyness and the thinning of the once magnificent thatch. You were hurrying down Throgmorton Street, chained to a small black bag as if it were a can of soup you feared might escape. I should have stopped you; but I had a train at Liverpool Street to catch and a shave to procure on the way—two errands that, taken together, form the modern man’s idea of romance. I wonder if you recognised me. You looked hard, as one looks at a menu one suspects has been altered since last Tuesday.

And you, gallant Shamus, who once fought for half an hour to save a frog from being skinned—someone told me you are now an Income Tax assessor. I can picture you, grimly heroic, rescuing no frogs, believing nothing, taxing everything; a man with a power of disbelief unusual even among those whose business is to assume that all other men are liars. May Providence, if it has a sense of humour, assign you to districts far from mine.

So Time, Nature’s tireless handyman, bustles about the rooms of the world, sweeping away our volcanoes, smoothing down our ruins, and putting a nice respectable covering of earth over every embarrassment. Even a lover’s initials carved on beech bark get healed, as though the tree itself were ashamed to remember.

In the beginning I was not a favourite with my schoolmates, and it was then trouble first took lodgings with me and paid no rent. A grown person can wrap himself in a dozen warm delusions—one of which is that people rather like him, or would if they knew him, or are on the point of liking him as soon as their digestion improves. A child has no such overcoat. He feels the draught.

My ostracism was painfully plain, and—being made in the silent brooding pattern—I went about with it inside me as with a hot potato, afraid to drop it and unable to swallow it.

“Can you run?” asked me one day an important personage whose name I have lost, though I remember his authority with a clearness that makes up for it. He was head of the Lower Fourth: tall, beak-nosed, and possessing the air of a boy who had been born to sign documents. His father, I later learned, kept a draper’s shop in the Edgware Road, and when the shop failed the future conqueror of Europe was packed off into a clerkship in the Civil Service. But to us he was a young Duke of Wellington in short trousers, and under other circumstances might, perhaps, have become something equally disastrous.

“Yes,” I answered. In truth it was my one accomplishment. Rumour of it—school rumours travel like the smell of suet pudding—must have reached him.

“Run round the playground twice at your fastest,” he commanded. “Let me see you.”

I clenched my fists and charged off. If gratitude could be measured in perspiration, I was a model son. How thankful I was that he, the crowned head of the Lower Fourth, should speak to me publicly—the outcast!—I could only show by running as though pursued by the Devil and the French.

When I drew up before him I was panting hard, but I could see he approved.

“Why don’t the fellows like you?” he asked bluntly.

If only I could have stepped out of my shyness and spoken the hunger that was eating me! If only I could have cried: O Lord of the Lower Fourth, you upon whom success—success, that steaming, irresistible thing, like a pie just taken from the oven—has fallen without your so much as ordering it! Tell me the secret recipe. Is it Tommy’s swagger? Then I will swagger too, though my heart shakes like jelly. But why, when Tommy struts, do they admire it—and when I do, do they crow like roosters and sidle after me like pouter pigeons? Is it Dicky’s playfulness—Dicky who steals caps, runs away with balls, springs on backs, and is rewarded with laughter—while I, doing no more and being if anything lighter, am repaid with a bloody nose and a widening ring of dislike?

Should I fling my arms about their necks as Billy does? They wrench themselves from me as from a wet apron. Should I be indifferent—walk apart, practising carelessness? No friendly arm hooks itself through mine. Should I win them by kind offices? Oh, if I could. I would do their sums (I was good at sums), write their impositions, take their punishments, scrub their boots with devotion—if only they would pay me in the small coin of love and the larger coin of admiration.

But all I could find to say—sulkily, like a boy pretending not to bleed—was: “They do like me, some of them.”

“Don’t tell lies,” he answered. “You know they don’t—none of them.” And I hung my head.

“I’ll tell you what I’ll do,” he continued, in the grand manner. “I’ll give you a chance. We’re starting hare and hounds next Saturday; you can be a hare. You needn’t tell anybody. Just turn up and I’ll see to it. Mind, you’ll have to run like the devil.”

He walked away without waiting for my answer, leaving me to meet Joy running towards me with outstretched hands—Joy, who always arrives breathless, without asking whether there’s room.

I obeyed orders and gave no hint in school of the glory preparing for me. But the moment I reached home I burst in like a kettle boiling over.

“I’m to be a hare because I run so fast! Anybody can be a hound, but there’s only two hares, and they all want me. And can I have a jersey? We begin next Saturday. He saw me run. I ran twice round the playground. He said I was splendid! Of course it’s a great honour to be a hare. We start from Hampstead Heath. And may I have a pair of shoes?”

The jersey and the shoes my mother and I bought that very day, because the fear was upon me that unless we hurried the last blue-and-white striped jersey in London would be snapped up by some other aspiring hero and I should be forced to run in something plain and therefore doomed.

That evening, before bed, I dressed myself in full costume and admired myself in the glass, as a cook admires a cake before it collapses. From then till the end of the week I practised leaping over chairs; my way of descending stairs became perilous, circuitous, and—if I may say so—innovative. My mother watched me with the look with which one watches a saucepan one suspects is about to boil over.

“The credit of the Lower Fourth is at stake,” I explained, with the solemnity of a statesman. “Banisters and legs are of small account compared with fame and honour.”

My father nodded and supported me with manly argument; my mother supported herself by adding another line to her prayers.

Saturday came. We were to meet at half-past two by the turnpike gate outside the Spaniards. I took my lunch and ate it in Regent’s Park—what it was I no longer remember, but I recall the damp taste of anticipation, which is always the chief ingredient in a boy’s meal. Then I took the ’bus to the Heath.

One by one the others came up. Beyond glances none took any notice of me. I wore my ordinary clothes over my jersey. I knew they thought I’d come merely to watch them start, and I hugged to myself the surprise I meant to serve up—hot, with myself as the dish.

He came at last, our leader—one of the last, because commanders arrive when it suits them. I sidled behind him and waited while he organised and constructed, as though building a railway.

“But we’ve only got one hare,” cried one of them. “We ought to have two, you know, in case one gets blown.”

“We’ve got two,” answered the Duke. “Think I don’t know what I’m about? Young Kelver’s going to be the other one.”

Silence fell—thick, sudden silence, like flour thrown over a mess.

“Oh, I say, we don’t want him,” broke in a voice at last. “He’s a muff.”

“He can run,” explained the Duke.

“Let him run home,” said another voice; and laughter came out, sharp and eager, like the snap of a biscuit.

“You’ll run home in a minute yourself,” threatened the Duke, “if I have any of your cheek. Who’s captain here—you or me? Now, young ’un, are you ready?”

I had begun unbuttoning my jacket. My hands stopped and fell to my side as if they’d been told to. The heat in my chest turned instantly to cold.

“I don’t want to come,” I said, “if they don’t want me.”

“He’ll get his feet wet,” suggested the first boy. “Don’t spoil him—he’s his mother’s pet.”

“Are you coming or are you not?” shouted the Duke. He could not endure hesitation; authoritative people prefer even disaster, provided it is prompt.

But the tears were rising and would not go back. I turned my face away, without speaking.

“All right, stop then,” cried the Duke. “Here, Keefe, you take the bag and be off. It’ll be dark before we start.”

My substitute snatched at the chance as one snatches at a new bun. Away went the hares, while I, keeping my face hid, moved slowly off.

“Cry-baby!” shouted a sharp-eyed youngster.

“Let him alone,” growled the Duke; and I went on until I found the cedars.

A few minutes later I heard them start, whooping. Their sound went across the Heath and seemed to press me down into the wet earth. How could I go home and confess—confess the shame that I had been offered a place and refused it because it came with contempt? My father would be waiting with questions; my mother with hot water and blankets, as though I’d been out in a storm of triumph. What explanation could I give that did not betray the miserable secret—that I was unwanted?

It was a chill, dismal afternoon. The Heath lay empty; a thin rain began, the sort of rain that insinuates itself into everything as gossip does. I slipped off my shirt and jacket and rolled them under my arm. Then I trotted off alone—hare and hounds combined into one small carcass—to chase myself sadly by myself.

I see it still: that pathetically ridiculous little figure, jogging doggedly over the dank fields. Mile after mile it runs, the little idiot—jumping, sometimes falling into muddy ditches as though anxious to season itself; scrambling through dripping hedges; swarming over tarry fence and slimy paling. On it pants—through Bishop’s Wood, by tangled Churchyard Bottom, where now the railway shrieks; down sloppy lanes bordering Muswell Hill, where now stand rows of prim villas, looking as though they have never smelt anything more pungent than respectability.

At intervals it stops to dab its eyes with a dingy rag of a handkerchief, to rearrange the bundle under its arm; its chief anxiety to keep out of sight of chance wanderers, to dodge farmhouses, to dart across high roads when nobody is looking. And so, tear-smeared and mud-bespattered, up the long rise of darkening Crouch End Lane—where to-night the electric light will blaze from a hundred shops—and dead beat into the Seven Sisters Road station, there to tear off its soaked jersey; and then home to Poplar with a shameless account of the jolly afternoon it has spent, of the admiration and praise it has won.

You poor little brat. Popularity? It is a shadow. Turn your eyes toward it and it will run before you. Turn your back and walk toward the living sun and it will follow—so they say, and I, who have tried both, can only tell you the proverb tastes better than the fact. Why, then, do you look at me with that quizzical grin, as if you already know you will spend years proving the proverb wrong?

When one takes service with Deceit, one signs a contract one may not break without penalty. Perhaps those lonely runs were good for my lungs. But oh, they were dreary. I persuaded myself—by a sort of logic that grows easily in the mind of a boy—that truth was merely a matter of words; that so long as I had actually gone over the ground I described, I was not lying. To further satisfy my conscience, I bought a big satchel and scattered torn-up paper as I ran, making myself, in a reduced and melancholy way, my own hare.

“And they never catch you?” my mother would ask.

“Oh, no, never,” I answered. “They never even get within sight of me.”

“Be careful, dear,” she would advise. “Don’t overstrain yourself.” But I could see she was proud—proud as a woman is when she thinks her child has been invited into the world.

After a while imagination came to my help. Often I could hear behind me the sound of pursuing feet, catch through gaps in the trees a sight of a merry host upon my trail, and I would redouble my speed. The rain would patter on leaves with that soft, busy whisper that makes even loneliness feel occupied, while far off some cart would rattle along a road like an argument that will not end. In those days the earth still had room for quiet; now the shriek of rails and the clatter of building have moved in like vulgar neighbours who keep a piano.

But for Dan my loneliness would have been unbearable. His friendship was there for me to creep to like shade on a scorching day. To this day you can always know Dan’s politics: they are those of the party out of power. You can always know the cause he will champion: the unpopular one. The man he will defend: the man who is down.

“You are such an un-understandable chap,” complained a fellow-clubman to him once in my hearing. “I sometimes ask myself if you have any opinions at all.”

“I hate a crowd,” was Dan’s only confession of faith.

He never claimed anything from me in return for his affection. He was there—solid, quiet, not asking me to perform. When I returned to him for comfort, baffled in attempts to win others, he gave it without even troubling himself to add advice. When childish success came to me at last and I needed him less, he was neither hurt nor surprised. Other people—what they thought, what they did—even when it concerned himself—never seemed to trouble him. He loved to bestow; response bored him. He was like a fountain: content to give, unable to receive.

My popularity came to me unexpectedly, after I had given up hoping—surprising me and, in a way, annoying me. Gradually it dawned that my company was being sought.

“Come along, Kelver,” the spokesman of one group would say; “we’re going part of your way home. You can walk with us.”

Perhaps I would go with them; more often, before we reached the gate, the delight of my society would be claimed by a rival troop.

“He’s coming with us this afternoon. He promised.”

“No, he didn’t.”

“Yes, he did.”

“Well, he ain’t, anyhow. See?”

“Oh, isn’t he? Who says he isn’t?”

“I do.”

“Punch his head, Dick!”

“Yes, you do, Jimmy Blake, and I’ll punch yours. Come, Kelver.”

I might have been some Queen of Beauty offered as a prize for knightly combat. Indeed, more than once the argument ended in the simplest manner: a fight, and I was carried off, half laughing and half ashamed, by the victorious party.

For a while it was a mystery to me, until I asked Norval—George Grampian—whom we called “Norval” because we were witty. Norval, who had taken particular delight in teasing me, had abruptly become one of my greatest admirers. He was in the second eleven and, after Dan, the best fighter in the lower school. If I could understand Norval, all would be plain.

One afternoon he bounded on me in the cloakroom and slipped his arm into mine, clamouring for my company to Camden Town. I stopped and put it bluntly.

“Why should I walk home with you? Why do you want me?”

“Because we like you.”

“But why do you like me?”

“Why!” He stared, as though I’d asked why jam sticks to fingers. “Because you’re such a funny chap. You say such funny things.”

It struck me like a slap. I had meant to win popularity on a ladder of heroic qualities. In the school books I read, Leonard or Marmaduke won love and admiration by integrity, nobility, goodness, intellect—perhaps with a little bowling or jumping thrown in by the way. Not one of them had ever said anything funny, either by accident or design. And if a Marmaduke existed among us in the Lower Fifth, we called him Marmalade; the school books never mention that.

“Don’t be disagreeable, Kelver,” Norval complained. “Come with us and we’ll let you into the team as an extra. I’ll teach you batting.”

So I was to be their Fool. I, dreamer of knightly dreams, aspirant to laurels, had won not wonder but laughter. I had prayed for popularity and it had been granted—in this form. Had the gods, who once played tricks on Midas, grown no kinder?

Had my vanity been less I might have flung their gift back at them. But my thirst for approbation was too keen. I had to choose: cut capers and be followed, or walk in dignity and be ignored. I chose the capers.

As time wore on I strove to cut them quicker, quainter. I thought out funny stories, prepared ingenuous impromptus, twisted every idea into odd expression. And I had my reward. Before long my company was desired by all the school.

But I was never content. I would rather have been captain of their football club—aye, even deputy vice—than the king of their laughter. I would have given all my meed of chuckles for stuttering Jerry’s one round of applause when, in our match against Highbury, he knocked up his century and won us the victory by three.

Till the end I never quite abandoned hope of exchanging my vine leaves for laurels. I rose an hour earlier to practise throwing at broomsticks set up in waste places. Later, when stilts came temporarily into fashion, I wearied body and mind for weeks trying to acquire skill upon them. That fat Tubby could out-distance me saddened my life for months.

There was a Sixth Form boy—Wakeham, if I remember rightly—who envied me my gift of amusing. He was at the age when the other sex becomes important and the desire had come to him to be regarded as a star of wit among the social circles of Gospel Oak. Need I say that by nature he was ponderously dull?

One afternoon I happened to be the centre of a small group in the playground. I had been holding forth and they had been laughing. Whether I had said anything truly entertaining I cannot tell. It made little difference; they had got into the habit of laughing when I talked. Sometimes I would say something serious on purpose; they laughed just the same, as though seriousness were my best joke.

Wakeham was among them, watching me with the fixed stare boys reserve for conjurers when they’re trying to find the hidden rabbit. Later he slipped his arm through mine and drew me into an empty corner.

“I say, Kelver,” he broke out the moment we were beyond hearing, “you really are funny!”

It gave me no pleasure. If he’d told me he admired my bowling I might not have believed him, but I should have loved him for it.

“So are you,” I answered savagely. “Only you don’t know it.”

“No, I’m not,” he said. “Wish I was. I say, Kelver”—he glanced round to make sure no one was near—“do you think you could teach me to be funny?”

I was about to reply with conviction that it could not be done, when an idea occurred.

Wakeham was famous for one thing. He could insert two fingers in his mouth and produce a whistle capable of confusing dogs a quarter of a mile off and causing people nearby to jump from six to eighteen inches into the air—an accomplishment like a bad smell: powerful, immediate, and seldom welcomed.

I envied him it as keenly as he envied me my supposed wit. I could not see the use of it; it generally called forth irritation rather than affection. Once he performed close to a purple-faced old gentleman’s ear; the old gentleman promptly cuffed his head, and for so doing was commended by the whole street as a public benefactor. Drivers flicked at him with whips. Even youth, from whom sympathy might have been expected, was moved to throw whatever came handy. My own social circle regarded the whistle as vulgar; Wakeham dared not perform it within hearing of his own classmates. That any human being should desire to acquire it seems incomprehensible.

And yet for weeks in secret I had wrestled to produce it. Why? Because it drew attention; because someone else could do it and I could not; and because I had no natural aptitude for it—and therefore my soul wanted it all the more. Had a boy appeared who could walk on his hands with his heels in the air, I should probably have broken my neck trying to copy and excel. I make no apologies for the brat; I present him merely as a study for the amusement of a world of wiser boys—and men.

I struck a bargain with Wakeham. I would teach him to be funny; he would teach me the costermonger’s whistle.

Each of us strove conscientiously to impart knowledge. Neither succeeded.

Wakeham tried hard to be funny. I tried hard to whistle. He did all I told him; I followed his instructions implicitly. The result was the feeblest of wit and the feeblest of whistles.

“Do you think anybody would laugh at that?” Wakeham would enquire pathetically at the end of his supremest effort.

And honestly I had to confess: “I don’t think any living being would.”

“How far off do you think anyone could hear that?” I would demand anxiously, on recovering breath enough to speak at all.

“Well,” Wakeham would reply kindly, not wishing to discourage me, “it would depend on whether you knew it was coming.”

We abandoned the scheme by mutual consent at about the end of a fortnight.

“I suppose it’s something you’ve got to have inside you,” I suggested by way of consolation.

“I don’t think the roof of your mouth can be quite the right shape for it,” concluded Wakeham.

And thus ended the only formal education I ever received in the arts of humour and noise.
