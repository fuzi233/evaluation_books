CHAPTER IX. A Man’s Ransom.

Tito moved quickly through the crowd, his steps light despite the weight of thoughts beneath his surface ease. Though he had brushed off Nello’s casual inquiry about the girl he’d seen earlier—her bright blue eyes peering from beneath the white linen hood typical of a contadina at festival time—he could not entirely dismiss the fleeting wish that he might meet her again. He knew full well it was Tessa, yet he had chosen silence. What concern was it of Nello’s? Tito possessed an almost instinctive gift for concealment, a talent that operated without conscious intent, like breath or heartbeat. It was as natural to him to withhold a truth as it was to others to speak plainly. And so, even when the matter bore no real secrecy—like the sight of a flock of crows passing overhead—he would sometimes hide it, simply because the act felt right.

But this thought of Tessa soon faded, overtaken by the persistent image of the friar whose face had stirred something deep and inexplicable within him. Why should a frail, fasting man with sunken eyes have fixed his gaze on Tito in particular? Where had he seen that face before? The memory clung like a cobweb, irritating and vague. Foolishness, surely—such impressions were best swept aside. Tito had more pressing matters to occupy his mind.

By the time he turned into a narrow side street from the Corso degli Adimari, his thoughts had settled upon the sun high in the sky and the delay the procession had caused. He was late for his visit to the room in the Via de’ Bardi, where he knew Romola and her father waited anxiously for him. He could already picture the moment: the blind man’s face lighting up with joy, like a lamp glowing softly behind translucent glass; Romola’s cheeks flushing pink, a brief warmth that only enhanced her quiet dignity, her expression both tender and startlingly open, like that of a child who has just discovered a secret but feels no need to hide it.

They were companions in quiet harmony during those hours spent around the blind man’s chair. Romola often turned to Tito for answers, and he gladly shared what he knew—but still, he found himself strangely subject to her presence, drawn by a simplicity that seemed to elevate him without pride. For the first time, he felt a kind of reverence—not worship, perhaps, but something akin to it—before a woman whose nature was noble, serene, and deeply rooted in truth. She was not one to be approached lightly, nor could he imagine them alone together, sharing words that might cross into love. He could only dream wildly—knowing it was impossible—that she might one day say to him, “I love you.”

Once, in Greece, while leaning over a sunlit wall, a small dark-eyed peasant girl had crept near, resting her water jug beside him. With shy innocence, she had asked him to kiss her, lifting her smooth olive cheek. Tito had been accustomed to such unbidden affections. But Romola’s love would never come so easily. Would it come at all?

He was young, not inflamed with passion but deeply impressionable. His feelings for Romola were as inevitable as sunlight reflected in clear water—natural, pure, undeniable. Yet he carried no vanity. He understood, with a quiet certainty, that Romola stood far above him. Many men have felt such humility before a woman whose soul seems larger than her years. Still, Tito had enjoyed swift success—success that might have made another man arrogant. But not him. He had earned favor from Bartolommeo Scala, the secretary to the Signory, and their growing friendship had opened doors. It had led to a decision that, even apart from other reasons, would have drawn him permanently to Florence.

Politian, the renowned scholar, taught both Greek and Latin in the city, though the university itself had long since moved to Pisa. For years, Demetrio Calcondila, a distinguished Greek exile, had held a parallel chair in Greek, balancing Politian’s influence. But Calcondila had gone to Milan, leaving Politian without a rival. And now, thanks to Scala and others who valued balance in learning, it was decided that Tito should take up the Greek chair—supported, as Calcondila once had been, by the patronage of Lorenzo himself. This was no mere honor; it was a mark of recognition, a promise of place and purpose.

Tito sailed under favorable winds. He had proven his worth to discerning minds, and he wore his fortune with modest grace. No one resented his success. He was likely to enter the highest circles of Florentine society—where wealth gleamed brighter than silver on brass dishes, and where the Signory did not forbid the wearing of the richest brocades. Who could refuse a handsome young scholar who played the lute and sang with charm? His face, his smile, his voice—each seemed to bring a holiday mood to life, like music and colors raised in defiance of sorrow. Here was a professor who might make the ancient Greeks beloved among noble sons.

And yet, Tito’s good fortune ran deeper. He had sold all his jewels except one ring he refused to part with, and now he held five hundred gold florins—more than most men ever saw in a lifetime.

But the moment he received that sum, it became the turning point in his life—the first time his easy nature faced a true test.

A thought, long shadowed, now seized him with sudden force. He could no longer ignore it. He must choose: surrender to duty, or reject it—and bear the consequences forever.

It happened in the room above Nello’s shop, now Tito’s own lodging. There, the elder Cennini handed him the final installment from Bernardo Rucellai, the buyer of the two most valuable gems. “_Ecco, giovane mio_,” said the printer and goldsmith, “you have now a fine fortune. If you’ll take my advice, let me place your florins safely, where they may grow instead of slipping away in banquets and follies common among our youth. Scholars, especially those like Pietro Crinito, who think their learning needs perfume and embroidery, often squander one hand only to beg with the other. I’ve brought you the money. You’re free to choose wisely or not. I shall see which way the scale tips. We Florentines do not admit a man to any art until he has shown his skill and been enrolled. And no man is truly enrolled in the art of life until he has been well tempted. If you decide to lend your florins to usury, tell me tomorrow. A scholar may marry, and should have something ready for the _morgen-cap_. Addio.”

As Cennini closed the door, Tito turned, his smile fading. He stared at the coins lying on the table, motionless, thumbs tucked into his belt, transfixed by the weight of what lay before him.

“A man’s ransom!”—who had said that five hundred florins was more than enough to buy a man’s freedom?

What if, somewhere under this same midday sun, on some distant coast, a man—older now, worn but still noble-hearted—was toiling as a slave? A man who once rescued a starving boy from filth and cruelty, raised him with care, treated him as a son? That man—Baldassarre Calvo—might now be hewing wood, drawing water, beaten for being slow. And what if he whispered to himself: *Tito will find me. He had only to carry the manuscripts and gems to Venice. He will raise money. He will not rest until he finds me.*

If that were certain, could Tito stand there, look at the gold, and say: *I will stay in Florence, where soft airs whisper of love and prosperity, and risk nothing for him?* No—surely not, if it were certain.

But it was not certain.

The galley had been captured by a Turkish ship en route to Delos—this much was known, from the survivor’s report. But there had been fighting, bloodshed. A man had fallen overboard. Who lived? Who died? Had Tito not himself nearly drowned in a shipwreck? He knew how fragile plans were, how easily fate could unravel everything. Rumors spoke of pirates settling in Delos—but such tales were unreliable. And what if he left Florence, went to Venice, obtained official letters, and set out across the Archipelago? The likely result? He might be seized himself, spend all his money on useless preliminaries, and end once more as a penniless wanderer—with no gems left to sell.

Tito saw that outcome more clearly than the possibility of finding his father alive. It would be unfair, wouldn’t it? To turn his back now, in the bloom of youth, on love and promise, only to lose them forever?

“And yet,” he murmured aloud, “if I were certain Baldassarre was alive—and that I could free him, no matter the danger—I would go now. Now I have the money. Before, it was useless to debate. I would go to Bardo, to Scala, and tell the whole truth.”

He did not say to himself—though he knew it—that if Scala and Bartolommeo had known the truth, there would have been no choice: he would have had to search. He did not say aloud what he knew well—that many Greeks of distinction had crossed seas again and again, begged help from kings and bishops, to free family from Ottoman slavery. Such deeds were not seen as extraordinary, but as duties.

This was his first true conversation with himself. Until now, he had followed impulses, often concealing half-truths. He had never paused long enough to confront why he hid. What use was telling all? True, the thought had crossed his mind since leaving Nauplia: *After all, it’s a relief to be rid of Baldassarre.* He had even wondered, with a pang of curiosity, *Who fell overboard?*

Such thoughts arise naturally from burdensome relationships. Baldassarre had been demanding, growing more so with age. He scrutinized Tito constantly, measuring his thoughts against his own grand expectations. Age had hardened him—a thickset man beyond sixty, heavy-browed, bald, whose intellectual fervor had become monotony. Among strangers, such a man would not command admiration. Youth, beauty, rank—they would not kneel at his feet. Novelty does not warm hearts for the dimmed and weary. Only love rooted in memory, in fidelity, in enduring tenderness, can keep such souls from loneliness.

But surely such memories were not absent in Tito?

Far back in time, when Tito was seven, Baldassarre had pulled him from abuse, taken him to a home that felt like paradise—food, comfort, gentle hands. From then until their parting, Tito had been the center of his benefactor’s world. He had been obedient, quick, eager to learn—bright, lovely, graceful. A youth without vice, as if his very form embodied perfect balance. When silent, he did not seem sullen; when refusing labor, he would fall to the ground with a half-smile, half-plea, so charming that the pleasure of seeing him outweighed the disappointment. His mouth curved with effortless humor. And his mind—how swiftly it grasped philosophy, poetry, even street songs caught in passing!

Would anyone have said Tito had failed his benefactor? That gratitude had waned?

He did not believe it. But the truth remained: it was not certain Baldassarre was alive. Not certain he was enslaved.

“Do I not owe something to myself?” Tito asked, shifting slightly—the first movement he’d made since looking at the gold. “Before I abandon everything, before I face again the risks I am already weary of, I must have a reasonable hope. Am I to spend my life searching? _I believe he is dead._” Cennini was right—his florins deserved protection. “I will give them to him tomorrow.”

And so, the next morning, when Tito acted on his decision, he had chosen his path. He had sealed his future. He had made it impossible to desire otherwise—to want, deep down, that his father was dead. Impossible not to be tempted toward falsehood rather than expose the truth of his actions.

Under every hidden secret lies a nest of guilty wishes, nurtured in darkness. The harm of sin often lies less in the deed than in the way it distorts desire—how self-interest aligns itself with deceit. Conversely, confession purifies, not by removing guilt, but by destroying the illusion that lies can save us. The soul regains its honesty.

Now, in this first clear reckoning with himself, scattered thoughts had coalesced. The small streams of selfishness had merged into a single current, strong enough to overcome resistance. Before, Tito had merely hesitated—uncertain whether he would return to seek his father’s fate. Now he had made a firm excuse. He had admitted to himself a choice he would never confess to others, a choice that would shame him if Baldassarre ever returned.

Yet the inner shame, the echo of a moral law written deep in human conscience—even without sympathy, without impulse—was showing its blushes in Tito’s quiet assertion: *He is dead. Or, at least, hope is gone.*
