A sharp, northern wind cut through the desert dark, stinging cheeks and livening the camels, which broke into a hard, ground-eating trot.
“Faster,” Trench ground out between clenched teeth. “Idris must have noticed we’re gone by now.”
“Even if he has,” Feversham called back, keeping his tone even, “it will take hours to rouse patrols, round up men, and fetch their mounts. And it’s full dark already.”
His words were steady, but he craned his neck again and again to stare at the faint, blood-orange glow pooling above Omdurman’s skyline. The distant thud of Ansar war drums had faded at last, small mercy—but the desert here was deadly silent. Galloping hooves would make no noise in the soft sand, no warning hum rising to alert them pursuit was close. For all he knew, armed riders could be thirty paces behind, and he would only spot the glint of a white turban when it was too late.
Trench never turned his head at all. He rode stiff-backed, jaws locked, eyes fixed dead ahead. His fear burned no less hot than Feversham’s—hotter, in fact. He dared not look back, too afraid the vision would seize him of Omdurman’s narrow fire-lit streets, of sentries shouting as they poured through the corridors of the House of Stone, hunting the two escaped prisoners.
Feversham’s quiet voice pulled him out of his trance. “I can’t see Omdurman’s fires anymore.”
“The golden blot, eh? Sunk right low?” Trench replied, half dazed. He did not explain the reference, and Feversham did not ask. The phrase had sprung unbidden to Trench’s mind: that very same golden glow had been the last thing Feversham saw when he left Omdurman at the start of his long, dangerous mission, and now it was vanishing the moment that mission was done.
They spoke no more until two shadowy forms loomed directly in the path of their camels, and Abou Fatma’s low, sharp voice cut the dark: “Instanna!”
They reined in, making their mounts kneel. “The fresh camels?” Abou Fatma asked, and two of his men vanished for a minute before leading four strong beasts out from behind a sand dune. Saddles were stripped from the camels they had ridden out of Omdurman, and slung tight on the new mounts.
“These are good?” Feversham asked, tugging a strap firm.
“Anafi breed, the fastest in the Sudan,” Abou Fatma said, glancing anxiously east and cupping a hand to his ear. “Quick, quick.”
“The arms?” Trench’s voice was feverish. “You have them? Where are they?” He was already sliding off his camel, scrabbling at the sand with his boots.
“A moment,” Abou Fatma said—but Trench could hardly wait. He was more eager to lay hands on a rifle than he had ever been terrified of being dragged back to Omdurman. “Ammunition too?” he gasped.
“All of it, rifles, revolvers, rounds enough for a dozen patrols.” Abou Fatma led them twenty paces off, where long desert grass hissed around their calves, and dug into the soft sand with bare hands. “Here.”
Trench threw himself to his knees, digging furiously with both hands, letting out a low, whining keen that sounded exactly like a foxhound baying at a fox’s cover. Feversham watched, unsettled: the man was half beast in that moment, scrabbling like a dog for a bone, his face tight with raw, hungry need. Then Trench’s fingers brushed the cold metal of a rifle lock, and the humanity snapped back. He stood slow, hefting the weapon, and laughed—a wild, bright sound of pure triumph.
“Now I don’t care who follows!” he whooped. “Let every Ansar soldier in Omdurman chase us. I’ll never go back to that stone prison, not even if they ride us down.” He stroked the rifle’s stock like it was a living thing, a friend he’d thought he’d lost forever.
Two of Abou Fatma’s men took the old, ridden-out camels and trotted back towards Omdurman to throw any trailing trackers off the scent. The rest mounted the fresh Anafi beasts and struck northeast. Less than fifteen minutes had passed since they’d first halted in the dark.
All that night they picked their way slowly through tall halfa grass and spiny mimosa, but by sunrise they were out on flat, bare sand dotted with low hillocks. “Shall we stop and eat?” Abou Fatma asked. “There is bread and dates strapped to each saddle.”
“No,” Feversham said. “We eat in the saddle.” They passed a water skin between them, cramming dates into their mouths as the camels loped on at full speed. By sunset they paused for an hour’s rest, then rode straight through the next night and all the next day, pushing themselves and their mounts to breaking point across rocky uplands, winding valleys, and honey-colored sand plains. Even at that gallop, the world crawled. A mountain would peek over the horizon at dawn, and hang there all day, never an inch closer, never a foot taller. Twice they had to detour wide around villagers tending scraggly sorghum patches that made their hearts leap into their throats. Once Abou Fatma spotted a distant caravan, and they all made their camels kneel, hunkered behind a rock with loaded rifles in their hands until the procession was out of sight.
Ten miles outside Abu Klea, a second relay of fresh camels was waiting. They mounted and rode a full day’s march west of the Nile, through the wild desert of the Ababdeh people, until a wide grey track stretched across their path.
“The Berber to Merowi trade road,” Abou Fatma said. “We turn east for the river once we cross it. If God wills, we reach the far bank tomorrow evening.”
“If God wills,” Trench echoed, his eyes darting to every shadow. The closer they got to safety, the sharper his paranoia burned. This was caravan country, far too busy to travel by day. They dismounted and hid all day behind a thick belt of shrubs on high ground, watching the tiny specks of traders and travelers crawl along the road far below. They crossed it after full dark, riding hard for the Nile until dawn broke, when Abou Fatma called another halt.
This was flat, empty desert, no cover higher than your ankle. Feversham and Trench stared east, straining to spot the Nile glint—but it was nowhere in sight. “We build a low circle of stones,” Abou Fatma said. “Lie flat inside it, no movement. I will ride ahead to the river, make sure our boat is waiting, then come back for you after dark. The soldiers patrol the banks day and night.”
They stacked the stones fast, a wall barely a foot high, then laid down inside it with their water skin and rifles at their sides. Abou Fatma took their camels with him, promising to bring fresh mounts back at sundown.
That day was the longest both men had ever lived. They were a few hours from freedom, and yet still in mortal danger. Trench swore it felt longer than any night locked in the House of Stone. To Feversham, it dragged worse than those six years before, when he’d hidden in his London rooms overlooking St James’s Park, too ashamed to step out into the streets after dark. The sun blazed down unforgiving, no shade, no room to stretch their legs. They barely spoke a word.
At last the sun set, cool wind rustling the grass, and Trench’s hand clamped down on Feversham’s arm. “Listen.” They heard the soft padding of camel feet, coming fast. A low whistle, and they scrambled out of their stone hiding place.
“God be thanked,” Abou Fatma said, when they called out. “Good news and bad. The boat is ready, and there are camels waiting on the far bank to take you to Abu Hamed. But your escape is public. Every ferry, every road, every path is staked out. We must cross the Nile before sunrise, and strike straight inland from the far shore.”
They slipped across the river around one in the morning, scuttling the boat on the far bank so no one could trace their crossing, then mounted the waiting camels. The ground was littered with boulders, and they could barely move faster than a walk, fraying every last thread of their patience. They hid another full day behind a ring of stones while the camels grazed out of sight, then rode hard through the night, reaching the date groves outside Abu Hamed two days later. They rested for twelve hours, then climbed onto a new relay of mounts to cross the Nubian Desert.
Today, a traveler rides that 240 miles of waterless black rock and yellow sand by train, sleeps in their berth, pulls into stations marked by painted signboards, the telegraph poles strung out ahead and behind, the whole journey done in 12 hours. But when Feversham and Trench rode that stretch, no tracks cut the sand. They followed the old hidden line of hill wells, staying east of the modern railway. On their second night out, Trench shook Feversham hard to wake him, pointing south at the starry sky.
“Look. There’s no Southern Cross.” His voice cracked. “Six years. Every single night for six years I’ve stared up at those four slanted stars from the floor of that prison cell, wondering if I’d ever live to see a night where it wasn’t there. This is the first second I’ve actually let myself believe we made it.”
They sat together long after that, staring up at the dark southern sky, hearts full of quiet thanks. They woke over and over through the rest of the night, half convinced they would see that familiar constellation burning low above the horizon after all, before drifting back to sleep, sure at last their escape was real.
Seven days later, they rode into Shof-el-Ain: a tiny well tucked between bare, featureless ridges, in the land of the Amrab Arabs. The chase was over.
“We are safe!” Abou Fatma cheered, spreading a cloth out before the kneeling camels and heaping grain for them. He was so giddy with relief he patted one grumpy camel on the neck, and the beast snapped at him, baring its teeth.
Trench laughed, holding out a hand to Feversham. “Thank you.”
“No thanks needed,” Feversham said, not taking it at once. “I did this for myself, first and last.”
“You’ve learned the nature of a camel, then,” Trench chuckled. “It’ll carry you all the way across the desert till it drops dead, but bite your head off if you try to thank it. That’s us, isn’t it? No flowery words. Here, my hand.”
Feversham untied the knot at the breast of his ragged jibbeh, and pulled out three white feathers: two small heron plumes, one large broken ostrich feather plucked from a fan. “Will you take yours back?”
“Of course.”
“You know what to do with it.”
“Burn it, the second I get somewhere I can light a match. No delays.”
Feversham tucked the two remaining feathers carefully back inside his tunic, tying the knot tight. “We shake hands, then.” As their palms met, he added, “Tomorrow morning we part ways.”
“Part ways? After a year locked in Omdurman together, all these weeks of running?” Trench blurted. “Why come home separately?”
“People will line the docks to welcome you when you reach Assouan, Cairo. You are the war hero who escaped the Mahdi’s prison. I don’t want to be there, when you go to tell Ethne what I did. She deserves to hear it from you first, no awkwardness.”
Trench nodded. He understood. “None of the regiment ever breathed a word about why you resigned, or the feathers we sent you. We swore an oath never to speak of it, for the honour of the regiment. Every man kept it.”
“Find Durrance, if you can,” Feversham said. “Tell him the next time he invites me to visit, anywhere in the world, England or Wadi Halfa, I’ll say yes.”
“Which way are you going?”
“West, to Wadi Halfa,” Feversham said. “I’ll take Abou Fatma with me, ride slow down the Nile. The other Arab will guide you north to Assouan.”
They slept the night through without a single watch, no fear of pursuit. At dawn, Trench was first to mount his camel. He leaned down to take the reins Feversham passed him. “Ramelton, wasn’t it? I won’t forget.”
“Ramelton. There’s a ferry across Lough Swilly to Rathmullen. You have to drive twelve miles inland to get there. She might not be there, though.”
“Then I’ll find her wherever she is,” Trench promised, and rode off with his guide. He glanced back over his shoulder more than once, and saw Feversham standing motionless by the well, but Feversham never waved back.
Feversham’s six long years of trial were over that morning, and he felt no joy, only a hollow, empty space inside him. For six years, his mission to redeem himself had been the only thing that kept him going. Now it was done, and he had no idea what came next. Ethne was no doubt married long since, he thought, and a sharp, bitter despair welled up in him for that stupid, reckless mistake he had made six years before, that had cost him everything. He saw again his London room overlooking St James’s Park, the knock at the door, the servant handing him the telegram.
He roused himself at last. There was still work to do. He had to go see his father, who was no doubt reading his Times over breakfast on the terrace of Broad Place, under the Surrey pine trees. He still had to take the last feather back to Ramelton. He still had to send a telegram to Lieutenant Sutch at Suakin.
He climbed onto his own camel, and rode slow west towards Wadi Halfa beside Abou Fatma. The green, wooded slopes of Ramelton glimmered vividly through the shimmering desert haze. For the first time in all his years of struggle, Harry Feversham doubted the quiet faith he had always held that everything would make sense in the afterwards.
