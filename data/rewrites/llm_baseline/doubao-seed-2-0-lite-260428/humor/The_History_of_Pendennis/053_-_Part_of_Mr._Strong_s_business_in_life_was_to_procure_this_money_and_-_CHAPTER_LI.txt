Way back near the start of this chaotic little story, we rambled about Clavering — that drowsy market town a stone’s throw from Pen’s childhood home Fairoaks — and its extremely unimpressive roster of local personalities. The place’s tea-party society was so deathly dull you could use a stack of their calling cards as tinder, so we never bothered droning on about it for long.

Readers will remember Mr. Samuel Huxter, the over-caffeinated medical student we bumped into at Vauxhall a few chapters back. Sam was Clavering’s self-appointed “London sophisticate” every time he came home for vacation, holding court at every dinner table to share the very select gossip of Bartholomew’s Hospital and the seedy Covent Garden music halls he snuck off to, acting like he’d just had high tea with the Prime Minister instead of eating pickled eggs off a pub table at 2 a.m.

You’ll also recall Mr. Hobnell, the young man Pen thrashed mid-Fotheringay drama? Back when he was a grammar school pupil in Clavering, he was a permanent fixture at Mrs. Huxter’s (Sam’s mum) tea table, snuck into the surgery to raid the tamarind sweet jars, and doused his handkerchief in rosewater so heavily he made the apothecary’s cat sneeze for three hours. Before long he fell hard for Sam’s sister Sophy, married her the second his old man kicked the bucket, and carted her off to The Warren, his family’s farm a few miles outside town. The Hobnells had been humble yeomen farmers for generations, but the late patriarch tore down their drafty old cottage, built a blindingly whitewashed new mansion with stables big enough for a racehorse and a piano nobody in the family knew how to play, bought a pack of hounds that couldn’t track a lost rabbit, and unilaterally declared himself “Squire Hobnell” no county vote required. Sam bragged about his brother-in-law’s fancy estate, his “elite” hounds, and his never-ending supply of home-brewed cider to all his wide-eyed med school mates back in London, and no one can blame him a bit. Every single year, right when Sophy was up to her elbows in nappies and couldn’t possibly leave the nursery, Hobnell trundled up to London for a lark, booked a room at the Tavistock, and he and Sam went gallivanting around the town. They hit Ascot, fell asleep mid-theatre performance, got thrown out of Vauxhall for singing too loud, and sampled every tavern within reeling distance of Covent Garden. As Hobnell himself put it, when he was in London he liked to “do as London does and go it a bit.” He’d come home bearing a bonnet two sizes too small for Sophy, then hang up his London party boots for 11 whole months of hunting and haymaking.

It was a very mutually beneficial arrangement between Sam and the Hobnells: Sam sent them all the hottest London nonsense gossip he could dig up, and in return they shipped him wicker baskets stuffed full of hares, partridges, and clouted cream so thick you could stand a spoon up in it. To the Hobnell household, Sam was a walking delight: he sang rude songs, pulled pranks on the vicar, kept the whole manor cackling for days, and even fished a stray fishbone out of their eldest toddler’s throat once — a feat they still bring up at every family dinner five years later. He was the undisputed favorite.

As rotten luck would have it, Pen ran right into Sam Huxter a mere three days after their messy Vauxhall run-in. Pen had been obeying his dumb, self-inflicted vow not to go see little Fanny, trying to scrub her from his brain by any means necessary. He slaved away in his tiny London rooms (accomplishing almost nothing), and in his role as critic for the *Pall Mall Gazette*, wrote so vicious a takedown of a newly published poem and a turgid romance that both authors probably cried into their porridge. Having metaphorically murdered two writers for the greater good, he dragged himself off to eat alone at the Polyanthus Club, a place so vast and empty the echo of his own chewing made him even more glum. He trundled off to a theatre after that to cheer up, where the whole audience was rolling in the aisles at a silly farce — but Pen’s face was so doleful the lead comic almost forgot his entire monologue just staring at him. The whole show blurred past him like a weird fever dream.

He decided to mosey over to the Back Kitchen, his old regular spot with Warrington, since he wasn’t the slightest bit sleepy. He’d already tramped 20 miles over Hampstead Common the day before trying to walk off his mood, and still hadn’t slept a wink. At least Bows, the quiet old piano man who ran the place, would be there. The pub was packed with people screaming off-color comic songs loud enough to rattle the windows, but Pen could only see Bows. He liked to brag his chest was an extinct volcano — so it was very annoying to find himself burning with this stupid, all-consuming crush after just two days of avoiding the object of it. He was chugging whisky and water one after another, lost in his own moping thoughts, when in walked Sam Huxter and a crew of his med school mates, fresh from the theatre.

Huxter immediately whispered to his friends, and Pen’s jaw tightened. Huxter weaseled his way through the crowd, plopped down right across from Pen, nodded like nothing had ever happened between them, and shoved a very grubby hand out to shake. Pen shook it. He even felt a little guilty for laying into the guy so hard at Vauxhall. Huxter, for his part, was so perfectly pleased with himself it never crossed his mind he could ever be rude to anyone, and he’d written off their Vauxhall scuffle as a silly bit of harmless “chaff” that wasn’t worth remembering.

Huxter called for four stouts for his crew, cast around for the most fun possible topic to bring up with old Pen, and landed straight on the single most hurtful thing he could possibly say.

“Jolly night at Vauxhall, wasn’t it?” he winked, extremely proud of himself. “Devilishly drunk that night, I was — dined with some blokes at Greenwich. That pretty little bit of muslin hanging off your arm — who was she, then?”

That was the last straw. “Did I ask you for any details about my personal life, Mr. Huxter?” Pen said, ice cold.

“Whoa, no offence, mate, sorry — hang on, you’re cutting up pretty savage,” Huxter spluttered, completely bewildered.

“Do you remember what we agreed on the other night?” Pen snapped, his temper rising. “You were too tipsy to remember, I suppose — you were extremely rude, and I very politely asked you to not treat me as an acquaintance from that point onward, and skip the public small talk. Do try to remember that for the future, won’t you? That new song’s starting, so I’ll leave you to enjoy it.”

He grabbed his hat, bowed to the completely stunned Huxter, and walked right out the door. Huxter’s friends burst out laughing so loud the pub host had to yell for silence, announcing “Quiet, gents! Show some respect for the Body Snatcher!” just as that notoriously gory ballad started up. Pen was weirdly proud he hadn’t fully lost his temper — but he also kind of wished Huxter had taken a swing at him, just so he’d have an excuse to punch something. He trundled home, still miserable, and slept just as badly as he had the night before.

A few days later, Sam Huxter sat down and wrote a very dramatic letter back to the Hobnells, and Arthur Pendennis was the star villain of the whole thing. Sam painted Pen as a full-blown reprobate Don Juan, a stuck-up humbug who was deliberately ruining the life of a poor innocent working-class girl he’d spotted with at Vauxhall. He claimed he’d found out exactly who the girl was from an ex-army bloke at his London club, and they all ought to warn the poor thing’s family to save her. At the bottom of the letter, he remembered to thank them for the last batch of rabbits they’d sent, and very unsubtly hinted that another consignment of that famous clouted cream would make him their grateful servant forever.

As luck would have it, the Hobnells were throwing a christening for their new baby girl the very day the letter arrived. They named the little mite Myra Lucretia, after her two godmothers: Clavering’s Miss Portman and Mrs. Pybus. Naturally, Mr. Hobnell shared Sam’s gossipy letter with his wife, who spilled every single lurid detail to the two godmothers right after the ceremony. By the end of the afternoon, the story had spread through Clavering faster than a cold at a village bake sale. Mrs. Pybus chattered about it to the Portmans, the Simcoes, and Mrs. Glanders — who immediately ordered all her teenage daughters out of the drawing room so they wouldn’t hear the sordid details. Madame Fribsby, the local dressmaker, stared at her framed portrait of her long-lost dragoon lover and sighed that all men are lying deceivers, quoting lines from *Marmion* dramatically while wondering where all those cheating lovers get to end up. Mrs. Pybus didn’t have words strong enough to call Pen a villain, ranting that this is exactly what happens when a boy gets uppity, refuses to drink tea with his perfectly respectable neighbours, and gallivants off to the den of sin that is London. The local Captain of Dragoons whistled and cracked a rude joke about the story over dinner, which earned him a sharp “You brute!” from his wife, who booted the girls out of the room for the second time that day. Mr. Simcoe, the local busybody magistrate, pretended to be calm and impartial — but he was secretly absolutely chuffed, because he’d hated Pen for years for no discernible reason, and this confirmed every dumb opinion he’d ever held. The story even got its own full Sunday evening sermon at the village chapel-of-ease, where the parson ranted for an hour about the evils of London, and the terrible sin of reading or writing novels, to a huge, very enthusiastic congregation. No one stopped to ask if the story was even true. Everyone just assumed Pen was a monster, and they were all racing to be the first to throw a stone at him.

The next day, poor Helen Pendennis ran all the way to Dr. Portman’s house, almost fainting with shock. She’d received an anonymous hate letter, sent by some “good Christian” who thought it was their duty to stab a kind, harmless old mother in the back, full of Bible verses about the fate of sinners and extremely lurid, specific details of Pen’s supposed crime. She was so distraught the letter slipped out of her hand, and Laura read the whole thing. Laura’s face burned, she trembled all over — but not with grief, with rage. “Cowards,” she spat. “It isn’t true. Not a word of it, mother.”

But Helen snapped, half out of her mind with terror. “It IS true, and it’s your fault, Laura!” she screamed. “You turned him down when he asked you to marry him! You broke my heart and refused him, so you’re the one who drove him straight into this other woman’s arms! Don’t talk to me, don’t argue. Martha, get me my bonnet and shawl. You’re not coming with me, Laura. Go away, cruel girl — this shame is all your fault.” She stormed out the door before anyone could stop her.

Dr. Portman glanced at the letter, immediately recognized Sam Huxter’s scrawly handwriting, and did his best to console Helen, pointing out that anonymous letters are always written by cowards and liars, that Pen deserved a chance to defend himself before everyone called him a villain, that a boy raised by such a kind mother could never do something so terrible. Helen saw right through his attempts to be reassuring. “You think he did it,” she wept. “I know you do. But if it *is* true? Then that girl will be my daughter. She’ll be welcome here. Pen will leave that awful London den, come home to me, and we’ll all forgive him. Let’s go get him right now — let’s leave today —”

She didn’t finish the sentence. She fainted dead away, and the doctor’s family carried her to a bed, called the local surgeon, and she lay unconscious all night, refusing to let Laura anywhere near her. Portman, now fully convinced Pen was innocent if only to calm the distraught mother, sat down to write an urgent letter to London, warning Pen about the wild rumours tearing through his hometown, and begging him to come home immediately, to fix this mess before it ruined his whole life.

And poor Laura? Left standing there, shunned by the woman she loved like a mother, the whole village calling the man she’d adored her whole life a monster, her heart ached so bad she could barely breathe. It felt like she’d just lost every bit of love that mattered to her in the whole world.
