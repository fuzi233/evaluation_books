While the good people at Fairoaks were nursing their days along as one nurses a weak broth—covered, dutiful, and a little drowsy—the great house beyond the Brawl, that old stone stomach on the hill which had digested two generations of silence, began to growl again with appetite.

It was, in fact, at about the date of Mr. Arthur Pendennis’s private calamity (which, like a burn on one’s own tongue, makes all other people’s dinners seem unimportant), that a notice crept into the provincial journals and set the whole county clattering. It ran from mouth to mouth with the cheerful certainty of a recipe: Clavering Park was to be inhabited again.

They discussed it everywhere that England permits herself a remark. At Clavering Market, among barrows of onions and the wet gleam of mackerel; at Cackleby Fair, where the showman’s drum bullied the fiddler; at Chatteris Sessions, where the lawyers’ voices rasped like knives on pewter. It was repeated at Gooseberry Green, when the squire’s carriage met the vicar’s one-horse contrivance and both parties paused, as if the road itself demanded a bit of gossip. It was whispered at Tinkleton Church gate while the bell tolled in the sunshine, and the smocks and scarlet cloaks came over the grass with that soft shuffling sound—country feet making their old music on the earth. Even the rooks above the elms seemed to take the matter up, and the river, busy as ever, carried it down in a rippling treble: the Park, long shut like a larder nobody had the key to, was to be opened; the great hearth was to be lit; the plates—those ancestral plates, one hoped—would clink again.

Five years earlier, the county papers had recorded, with a delicacy proper to print and a relish proper to a county, the marriage at Florence, at the British Legation, of Francis Clavering, Esquire, only son of Sir Francis Clavering, Baronet, of Clavering Park, to Jemima Augusta—daughter of Samuel Snell, of Calcutta, Esquire—and widow of the late J. Amory, Esquire. At that time the local legend, simmered and skimmed by all the idle tongues, was that young Clavering had been ruined for a good while and had done what ruined young men do: he had taken, with the widow, a portion.

People had caught glimpses of this couple abroad as one catches glimpses of strange dishes through the open door of an inn-kitchen: a waft of perfume, a flash of silver, then the door shuts. The Kickleburys, touring in Italy, had seen them in Florence, where Clavering occupied the Poggi Palace, gave parties, and lived comfortably—comfortably, that is, so long as England remained out of reach. Another year, young Peregrine of Cackleby, doing the Long Vacation grand tour, stumbled upon them at Schloss Schinkenstein by the Mummel See; and thereafter, now at Rome, now at Lucca, now at Nice, now at the baths and gambling places of the Rhine and Belgium, you heard of them at intervals—rumours arriving like gusts from far off, tasting of foreign sauces and bad morals.

Their last kitchen, so to speak, had been Paris; and there, after the news came that Samuel Snell of Calcutta was dead, the widow Amory—now Lady Clavering by a stroke of mortality and law—appears to have boiled up into a very handsome splendour indeed.

As for Sir Francis Clavering’s antecedents, they were not of that kind which a family Bible likes to keep pressed between its leaves. Son of an outlaw who lived goutily and gloomily in a dismal old château near Bruges, this gentleman had made a feeble attempt at respectability with a commission in a dragoon regiment, and had broken down almost before the first polish had taken on his boots. The gaming-table finished what the army began. After a couple of years he sold out, passed some time in Her Majesty’s prison of the Fleet (a sort of national pantry where imprudent gentlemen are stored when they cannot pay for their suppers), and then shipped to Ostend to join his exile father. For years thereafter he might be found, like a fly on a sugar-bowl, wherever there were billiard-rooms and watering-places: punting at gambling-houses, dancing at boarding-house balls, riding steeple-chases on other men’s horses, always hungry for the next mouthful of luck and always chewing the same old disappointment.

It was at a boarding-house at Lausanne that Francis Clavering made what he called his lucky coup: he married the widow Amory, lately returned from Calcutta. His father died soon after, and by that demise the lady acquired her title. Mr. Snell, the father, so delighted to have a baronet in the family stew-pot, doubled his daughter’s allowance; and, dying himself not long after, left a fortune to her and her children which rumour served out in portions of ten, twenty thousand a year—according to the appetite of the teller.

Before this, there had not been scandals of the downright sort about Lady Clavering so much as a general sense—an unpleasant flavour, if you will—that she was not of the finest ingredients. The best English people abroad, those who like their society strained clear and served cold, were shy. Her manners were not the most refined; her origin was lamentably low and doubtful. The retired East Indians, a tribe to be found thick as raisins in continental towns, spoke with scorn of her father as a disreputable old lawyer and indigo-smuggler; and of Amory, her first husband, as the mate of the Indiaman in which Miss Snell came out to Calcutta. Neither father nor daughter had been in society at Calcutta; Government House had never tasted them. Old Sir Jasper Rogers, Chief Justice of Calcutta, had once said to his wife that he could tell a queer story about Lady Clavering’s first husband; but like a cook who promises a wonderful dish and then “can’t be got” to produce it, the old Judge never would reveal the mystery, greatly to Lady Rogers’s disappointment and that of the young ladies his daughters, who loved a secret as other girls love sugar.

Yet Paris is a city that will swallow many flavours if they are rich enough; and when Lady Clavering took the Hotel Bouilli in the Rue Grenelle and blazed out in the polite world in the winter of 183—, the very people who had held their noses before were suddenly willing to take a seat at her table. The Faubourg St. Germain took her up. Viscount Bagwig, our excellent ambassador, paid her marked attention. Princes frequented her salons. Those notable English ladies resident in the French capital—so rigid, so severe, so venerable, so sparkling with moral purity that one fancied they might fizz if shaken—acknowledged and countenanced her: Lady Elderbury, Lady Rockminster, the Countess of Southdown. Ten (some said twenty) thousand a year had a very beneficent influence upon Lady Clavering’s character and reputation; it sweetened her, like sugar in a bitter draught, until all the world declared the draught wholesome.

Her munificence, too, was unbounded. Anybody in society with a scheme of charity found her purse ready, like a pantry-door that opens at a touch for the right kind of hand. French ladies of piety got money for schools and convents; she subscribed indifferently for the Armenian patriarch; for Father Barbarossa collecting for his monastery on Mount Athos; for the Baptist Mission to Quashyboo; for the Orthodox Settlement in Feefawfoo, the largest and most savage of the Cannibal Islands. It is recorded of her that on the same day Madame de Cricri got five Napoleons from her for the persecuted Jesuits, Lady Budelight put her down for the Reverend J. Ramshorn, who had had a vision ordering him to convert the Pope of Rome. And, for the benefit of the worldly, she gave the best dinners and the grandest balls and suppers known at Paris that season; so that while the saints had their alms, the sinners had their soufflés.

During this time, too, the good-natured lady must have arranged matters with her husband’s creditors in England; for Sir Francis reappeared in his native country without fear of arrest, was announced in the Morning Post and the county paper as having taken up his residence at Mivart’s Hotel; and one day the anxious old housekeeper at Clavering House beheld a carriage and four drive up the long avenue and stop before the moss-grown steps of the vast melancholy portico.

Three gentlemen were in the open carriage. On the back seat sat our old acquaintance Mr. Tatham of Chatteris. In the places of honour were a handsome portly gentleman wrapped in moustachios, whiskers, fur collars, and braiding—as if he had dressed himself from the trimmings of an entire cavalry regiment—and by him a pale, languid man who descended feebly when the little lawyer and the furred gentleman jumped out with briskness enough for both.

They mounted the mossy steps to the hall-door. A foreign attendant with earrings and a gold-laced cap tugged strenuously at the great bell-handle. The cracked bell clanged through the vast gloom as if the house itself were surprised at being addressed. Presently steps resounded on the marble pavement within, and the doors opened; and finally appeared Mrs. Blenkinsop the housekeeper, Polly her aide-de-camp, and Smart the keeper, bowing humbly as if the very air in the hall demanded a curtsey.

Smart pulled the wisp of hay-coloured hair that decorated his sunburnt forehead, kicked out his left heel as though a dog snapped at his calves, and brought down his head. Old Mrs. Blenkinsop dropped a curtsey full of tremble. Little Polly made a curtsey and several rapid bows, like a toy wound too tight. And Mrs. Blenkinsop, with emotion enough to salt a whole ham, quavered, “Welcome to Clavering, Sir Francis. It du my poor eyes good to see one of the family once more.”

The greeting was addressed to the grand gentleman in fur and braiding, who wore his hat magnificently on one side and twirled his moustachios with royal leisure. He burst out laughing.

“You’ve saddled the wrong horse, old lady—I’m not Sir Francis Clavering what’s come to revisit the halls of my ancestors. Friends and vassals! behold your rightful lord!” And he pointed towards the pale languid gentleman, who said with faint irritation, “Don’t be an ass, Ned.”

“Yes, Mrs. Blenkinsop,” said the pale one, “I’m Sir Francis Clavering; I recollect you quite well. Forgot me, I suppose?—How dy do?” He took the old lady’s trembling hand and nodded in her astonished face with a not unkind vacancy.

Mrs. Blenkinsop protested upon her conscience she would have known Sir Francis anywhere; that he was the very image of Sir Francis his father and of Sir John before him—those old portraits whose eyes had stared down for so long at dust.

“O yes—thanky—of course—very much obliged—and that sort of thing,” said the Baronet, looking about the hall as though searching for a door out of his own inheritance. “Dismal old place, ain’t it, Ned? Never saw it but once, when my governor quarrelled with gwandfather in the year twenty-thwee.”

“Dismal?—beautiful!—the Castle of Otranto!—the Mysteries of Udolpho, by Jove!” cried Ned, with the delight of a man who smells old stone and imagines romance rising like steam from it. “What a fireplace! You might roast an elephant in it. Splendid carved gallery! Inigo Jones, by Jove! I’d lay five to two it’s Inigo Jones.”

“The upper part by Inigo Jones; the lower was altered by the eminent Dutch architect, Vanderputty, in George the First his time, by Sir Richard, fourth baronet,” said Mrs. Blenkinsop, proud as any cook displaying the pedigree of her sauce.

“O indeed,” said the Baronet. “Gad, Ned, you know everything.”

“I know a few things, Frank,” Ned answered, marching his eyes over the walls as if tasting each panel. “I know that’s not a Snyders over the mantelpiece—bet you three to one it’s a copy. We’ll restore it, my boy. A lick of varnish, and it will come out wonderfully, sir. That old fellow in the red gown, I suppose, is Sir Richard.”

“Sheriff of the county, and sate in parliament in the reign of Queen Anne,” said the housekeeper, astonished at the stranger’s knowledge and eager to serve it up. “That on the right is Theodosia, wife of Harbottle, second baronet, by Lely, represented in the character of Venus, the Goddess of Beauty,—her son Gregory, the third baronet, by her side, as Cupid, God of Love—”

“Thank you—needn’t go on, Mrs. Blenkinsop,” said the Baronet, cutting her catalogue as one cuts a too-long string of sausages. “We’ll walk about the place ourselves. Frosch, give me a cigar. Have a cigar, Mr. Tatham?”

Little Mr. Tatham tried a cigar handed by Sir Francis’s courier and spluttered over it with the frantic distress of a man whose mouth has been invaded by foreign policy. “Needn’t come with us, Mrs. Blenkinsop. What’s—his—name—you—Smart—feed the horses and wash their mouths. Shan’t stay long. Come along, Strong,—I know the way: I was here in twenty-thwee, at the end of my gwandfather’s time.”

And Sir Francis and Captain Strong—for such was the style and title of the furred gentleman—passed out of the hall into the reception-rooms, leaving the discomfited Mrs. Blenkinsop to vanish by a side-door to her quarters, now the only habitable rooms in that long-uninhabited belly of a mansion.

It was a house so large that no tenant could afford to keep it warm; its rooms lay like empty dishes after a banquet: splendid shapes, cold surfaces, a grandeur that made one a little hungry and a little melancholy. Sir Francis and his friend walked through room after room, admiring vastness and dreary deserted state. On one side of the hall were the saloons and drawing-rooms; on the other, the oak room, parlour, grand dining-room, library—where Pen had once found books in old days. Around three sides of the hall ran a gallery, and beyond it passages to bedrooms of stately proportions, still showing marks of splendour as a silver tureen shows tarnish. Above, on the second story, was a labyrinth of little discomfortable garrets meant for servants in the days when my lord and my lady slept under gold canopies and their people lay overhead in quarters not so airy nor so clean as stables are now. The house had its own acoustics too: their footsteps went away from them in long echoes, and the draughts sighed in chimneys that had not tasted fire for years, as if the building remembered old voices but could not translate them into any speech the present could understand.

Up and down they wandered; and the owner of the mansion was silent and resigned about the pleasure of possessing it, as a man might be resigned to inheriting a cold roast when he has no hunger. The Captain, however, examined everything with such eagerness you would have thought him master and Sir Francis a visitor dragged along.

“I see capabilities in it—capabilities in it, sir,” cried Captain Strong, his voice enlivening the halls more than any candle could. “Gad, sir, leave it to me, and I’ll make it the pride of the country, at a small expense. What a theatre we can have in the library here, the curtains between the columns! What a famous room for a galop!—it will hold the whole shire. We’ll hang the morning parlour with the tapestry in your second salon in the Rue de Grenelle, and furnish the oak room with the Moyen-age cabinets and the armour. Armour looks splendid against black oak, and there’s a Venice glass in the Quai Voltaire will suit that high mantelpiece to an inch, sir. The long saloon, white and crimson of course; the drawing-room yellow satin; and the little drawing-room light blue, with lace over—hay?”

“I recollect my old governor caning me in that little room,” Sir Francis said, with the calm of a man recalling a bad dinner from childhood. “He always hated me, my old governor.”

“Chintz is the dodge, I suppose, for my lady’s rooms—the suite in the landing, to the south, the bedroom, the sitting-room, and the dressing-room. We’ll throw a conservatory out, over the balcony. Where will you have your rooms?”

“Put mine in the north wing,” said the Baronet with a yawn, “and out of the reach of Miss Amory’s confounded piano. I can’t bear it. She’s scweeching from morning till night.”

Captain Strong burst out laughing, and settled the whole future of the place as easily as a man laying out a supper-table on paper. When their promenade ended, they went into the steward’s room—now Mrs. Blenkinsop’s domain—where Mr. Tatham sat poring over a plan of the estate, and the old housekeeper had prepared a collation in honour of her lord and master. It was not one of Lady Clavering’s Parisian triumphs; but it smelt honest: cold meats, bread, cheese, and that very English fragrance of strong tea and pride. If the great house had been dumb, this little table spoke in a homely dialect.

They inspected the kitchen and stables, about both of which Sir Francis took some interest—there is a straightforward truth in bricks, straw, and a horse’s breath which even a languid baronet can comprehend. Captain Strong wished to examine gardens; but Sir Francis said, “D—— the gardens, and that sort of thing!” and finally drove away from the house as unconcernedly as he had entered it. That night Clavering learned, with the satisfaction of people who like a new noise in an old silence, that Sir Francis had visited the Park and was coming to live in the county.

When the news reached Chatteris, it set all the little place in commotion: High Church and Low Church, half-pay captains and old maids and dowagers, sporting squireens, farmers, tradesmen, factory people—all buzzing as if a hive had been tapped. The tidings came to Fairoaks too, and were received by the ladies there and by Mr. Pen with some excitement.

“Mrs. Pybus says there is a very pretty girl in the family, Arthur,” Laura said, with the calm cruelty of women who think a romance is as necessary as a pudding. “A Miss Amory, Lady Clavering’s daughter by her first marriage. Of course, you will fall in love with her as soon as she arrives.”

Helen cried out, “Don’t talk nonsense, Laura.” Pen laughed. “Well, there is the young Sir Francis for you.”

“He is but four years old,” Miss Laura replied. “But I shall console myself with that handsome officer, Sir Francis’s friend. He was at church last Sunday, in the Clavering pew, and his mustachios were beautiful.”

Indeed, the number of Sir Francis’s household—every member already paraded by rumour like dishes at a banquet—was soon known in the town; and everything else as nearly as human industry could calculate. In summer evenings the Park avenue and grounds were dotted with townsfolk strolling up to peep and criticise improvements. Loads of furniture arrived in vans from Chatteris and London, and numerous as the vans were, there was not one whose contents Captain Glanders did not know, and escort up to the Park House as if each chair and sideboard were a regiment under his command.

By this time Captain Glanders and Captain Edward Strong had formed an intimate acquaintance. The younger Captain took the lodgings at Clavering once occupied by the peaceful Smirke, and was deep in the good graces of Madame Fribsby his landlady, and of the whole town besides. He was splendid in person and raiment; fresh-coloured, blue-eyed, black-whiskered, broad-chested, athletic—though with a slight tendency to fulness that did not lessen the comeliness of his jolly figure. As he strode down Clavering High Street, hat on one side, cane clanking or wheeling in soldierly flourishes, his jolly laughter ringing through the otherwise quiet place, he was as welcome as sunshine and as noisy as a fair. He brought his own soundscape with him: the click of boot-heels, the tap of cane, the burst of laugh; and you could see townsfolk brighten as if their ears had been hungry.

On the first market-day he knew every pretty girl in the market as quickly as if he had been cataloguing fruit. He joked with the women; he had a word with farmers about stock; and he dined at the Agricultural Ordinary at the Clavering Arms, where he set the gentlemen in top-boots dying with laughter by his fun. “Tu be sure he be a vine veller, tu be sure that he be,” was the universal verdict, given in the local dialect with the local relish—as if the very vowels belonged to the soil. He shook hands with a score of them as they rode out on old nags, and waved his hat to them splendidly, smoking his cigar in the inn-gate. Before evening was done he was free of Madame Fribsby’s bar, knew what rent the landlord paid, how many acres he farmed, how much malt went into the strong beer; and whether a little brandy, unexcised by kings, ever slipped inland from Baymouth or the fishing villages along the coast.

He had tried living at the great house at first, but found it too dull.

“I am a creature born for society,” he told Captain Glanders. “I’m down here to see Clavering’s house set in order; for between ourselves, Frank has no energy, sir, no energy; he’s not the chest for it, sir”—and he threw out his own trunk as he spoke, broad enough for any campaign—“but I must have social intercourse. Old Mrs. Blenkinsop goes to bed at seven, and takes Polly with her. There was nobody but me and the Ghost for the first two nights at the great house, and I own it, sir, I like company. Most old soldiers do.”

Glanders asked where he had served.

Captain Strong curled his moustache and laughed. The other might almost ask where he had not served. “I began, sir, as cadet of Hungarian Uhlans, and when the war of Greek independence broke out, quitted that service in consequence of a quarrel with my governor, and was one of seven who escaped from Missolonghi, and was blown up in one of Botzaris’s fireships, at the age of seventeen. I’ll show you my Cross of the Redeemer, if you’ll come over to my lodgings and take a glass of grog with me this evening. I’ve a few of those baubles in my desk. I’ve the White Eagle of Poland; Skrzynecki gave it me”—he pronounced Skrzynecki with a gusto as if it were something you could taste—“upon the field of Ostrolenka. I was a lieutenant of the fourth regiment, sir, and we marched through Diebitsch’s lines—bang thro’ ’em into Prussia, sir, without firing a shot. Ah, Captain, that was a mismanaged business. I received this wound by the side of the King before Oporto,—where he would have pounded the stock-jobbing Pedroites, had Bourmont followed my advice; and I served in Spain with the King’s troops, until the death of my dear friend, Zumalacarreguy, when I saw the game was over, and hung up my toasting iron, Captain. Alava offered me a regiment, the Queen’s Muleteros; but I couldn’t—damme, I couldn’t—and now, sir, you know Ned Strong—the Chevalier Strong they call me abroad—as well as he knows himself.”

Thus almost everybody in Clavering came to know Ned Strong. He told Madame Fribsby; he told the landlord of the George; he told Baker at the reading-rooms; he told Mrs. Glanders and the young ones at dinner; and finally he told Mr. Arthur Pendennis, who, yawning into Clavering one day, found the Chevalier in company with Captain Glanders and was delighted with his new acquaintance.

Before many days were over, Captain Strong was as much at home in Helen’s drawing-room as in Madame Fribsby’s first floor, and made the lonely house very gay with good-humour and ceaseless talk. The two women had never before seen such a man. He had a thousand stories of battles and dangers; of Greek captives, Polish beauties, Spanish nuns. He could sing scores of songs in half a dozen languages, and would sit down to the piano and troll them off in a rich manly voice. His notes filled the room and pushed back its quiet like a curtain; and yet—when the song ended—the old country hush flowed in again, and you heard the clock and the faint sounds of the night outside, as if the house insisted on its own native tongue.

Both ladies pronounced him delightful—and so he was; though, in truth, they had not had much choice of men’s society, having seen in their lives but few persons besides old Portman, the Major, and Mr. Pen, who was a genius to be sure; but geniuses, like sauces too much reduced, can be intense and somewhat moody at home.

Captain Strong, who never came to a house without bringing a whole baggage-train of information, acquainted his friends at Fairoaks not only with his own biography but with the history of the family now coming to Clavering. It was he, he said, who had made the marriage between his friend Frank and the widow Amory. She wanted rank; he wanted money. What match could be more suitable? He organised it; he made them happy. There was no romance—Lady Clavering was not of an age or a person for romance, and Sir Francis, if he had his billiards and his dinner, cared for little besides. But they were as happy as people could be. Clavering would return to his native place; his wife’s fortune would pay the encumbrances; his son and heir would be one of the first men in the county.

“And Miss Amory?” Laura asked. Laura was uncommonly curious about Miss Amory, as though a new girl were a new spice.

Strong laughed. “Oh, Miss Amory is a muse—Miss Amory is a mystery—Miss Amory is a femme incomprise.”

“What is that?” asked simple Mrs. Pendennis; but the Chevalier gave her no answer—perhaps because the phrase was the kind of foreign condiment one sprinkles on conversation when one has no solid meaning to serve.

“Miss Amory paints, Miss Amory writes poems, Miss Amory composes music, Miss Amory rides like Diana Vernon. Miss Amory is a paragon, in a word.”

“I hate clever women,” said Pen.

“Thank you,” said Laura; and for her part she was quite sure she should be charmed with Miss Amory, and longed for such a friend. With which she looked Pen full in the face, as if every word the little hypocrite spoke were Gospel truth, and as if she could already hear—through the still country air—the first thin approach of that “confounded piano” which Sir Francis dreaded, like a modern noise beginning to creep into an old, quiet world.
