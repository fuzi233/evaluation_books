Fresh off his transatlantic crossing, Harry Esmond Warrington stood ringing the knocker of Castlewood, his grandfather’s rightful ancestral home, for long minutes before any soul inside bothered to answer. The footman who finally wrenched open the oak door couldn’t have cared less that the visitor was a blood relation to the family inside: the master and mistress were still away, and John the footman was in a tearing hurry to get back to his card game with Thomas in the window seat. The housekeeper, harried and breathless as she prepped for the lord and lady’s arrival that very evening, only let Harry beg his way into the lady’s parlour and the portrait gallery after much pleading. There, exactly as he’d known he would find them, hung the familiar faces: his grandfather in periwig and polished breastplate, the perfect twin to the portrait above the mantel at their Virginia plantation, and his grandmother as the young Lady Castlewood, dressed in the loose, low-necked finery of Charles II’s court, her golden hair cascading over her shoulders in soft ringlets Harry still remembered as snow-white from his childhood. The scowling housekeeper chased him off before he could linger, reeling off the names of the party due to arrive: the Countess, Lord Castlewood and his younger brother, the two young ladies, and the Baroness Bernstein, the girls’ aunt, who was to be given the grand state bedroom. Harry scribbled his full name on a scrap of paper from his pocketbook and left it on the hall table: *Henry Esmond Warrington, of Castlewood, in Virginia, arrived in England yesterday--staying at the Three Castles in the village*. The lackeys scrambled up from their card game to hold the door for him, grinning expectantly for tips, and his own servant Gumbo trundled over from the gate bench where he’d been chatting with old porter Lockwood, who accepted Harry’s guinea half confused what the gift even meant. The only unselfish, kind face Harry saw during his entire visit was that of little scullery maid Polly, and he walked away refusing to admit how deeply disappointed he was. Back home in Virginia, any traveller who rode up to their door would be welcomed even if the master was miles away out hunting—but here, at the gates of his ancestors’ hall, he was forced to trudge to a roadside alehouse to beg a plate of bacon and eggs for his dinner.

After he finished his meal, Harry wandered down to the stone bridge over the brook and sat perched on its parapet, staring back at the great house as the sun dipped behind its gables, rooks cawing home to their nests in the ancient elms. His young imagination ran wild, conjuring the ancestors his mother and grandfather had told him tales of: knights fording the stream, Cavalier gentlemen of King Charles’s court, the old Lord Castlewood, his grandmother’s first husband, riding out with hawk and hound. The memory of his lost twin brother hit him then, sharp with tender grief, and he hung his head, the old familiar sorrow welling back up for the companion he had shared every joy and hardship with since birth. Lost in his own thoughts, half listening to the distant clink of the blacksmith’s forge, the chatter of rooks and evening birdsong, two young men on horseback came thundering over the bridge. One snarled a foul oath, called Harry a fool and shouted for him to get out of the way. The other nearly jostled him straight over the parapet into the water, kicked his horse into a gallop on the far side, and yelled for his companion to keep up. Before Harry could even react to their rudeness, the pair were halfway up the drive to the great house. Two liveried grooms on horseback rode past next, snorting the very British welcome of “Curse you, who are you?” at the lone traveller. Then a great, heavy coach and six rumbled over the bridge, stuffed with three ladies, two maids, and an armed footman perched on the rear seat. Three pale, pretty faces peeked out at Harry, and when he tipped his hat in greeting—recognizing the Castlewood coat of arms blazoned on the coach door—not one of them acknowledged him. The armed footman on the back glared at him as if he were a wandering vagrant. Harry suddenly felt overwhelmingly, terribly alone. The tiny, tossing cabin of the ship *Rachel* he had sailed over on felt warmer and more welcoming than the spot he was standing on. The inn staff told him later that the ladies in the coach were the Countess, her stepdaughter Lady Maria, and her own daughter Lady Fanny; the young man in the grey frock coat who nearly rode him down was Mr. William Esmond, and the man who had yelled the loudest oath was Lord Castlewood himself.

The landlord of the Three Castles had shown Harry a bedroom earlier that day, but he had refused to let his trunks be unpacked, fully certain the great house would send an invitation at any moment. One hour passed, two, three—no note arrived. Finally, he gave in, let the host unpack his bags, and called for his slippers and dressing gown. Just before dark, a second chariot and four rumbled over the bridge, and a stout, sharp-eyed lady with very dark eyes stared hard at Harry from the window. That was the Baroness Bernstein, the landlady explained, my lord’s German-born aunt. By then, the entire party had vanished behind Castlewood’s great iron gates, and Harry trundled off to bed heavy with the sting of neglect and raw loneliness. He could not sleep, and soon he heard raucous shouts, curses, giggles and screams coming from the inn’s taproom, loud enough to rouse even the heaviest sleeper.

Presently he heard Gumbo’s voice outside his door, protesting, “You cannot go in, sar--my master asleep, sar!” before a shrill, swear-laced voice Harry now recognized cursed Gumbo for a stupid woolly-headed negro, shoved him aside, and burst into the room. It was Will Esmond, blind drunk. “Beg your pardon, Cousin Warrington!” he slurred, weaving unsteadily across the room. “Are you asleep? Beg your pardon for riding you over on the bridge. Didn’t know it was you—thought you was a lawyer come with a writ, all dressed in black! Gad, I thought Nathan the bailiff was here to nab me, I was gonna heave you straight in the river. But I ask your pardon. I was over at the Bell at Hexton, their punch is the best in three counties. Hullo, Davis! Bring a bowl of punch up here, now!” Harry sat bolt upright in bed, tall nightcap on his head, every inch the dignified Virginian. “You do me great honour to mistake me for a sheriff’s officer, cousin,” he said coolly. “I have had more than enough drink tonight, and I should think you have too.” Will pouted, rambling incoherently and lurching from side to side across the room. “You want me gone, don’t you? They made me come! I said I’d be hanged before I traipsed down here to look after some fellow I don’t care a pin about. That’s exactly what Castlewood said too! Why the devil should we bother? But the Baroness insisted—if she says a thing, it gets done, so you have to get up and come back to the house with me right now.” Harry’s temper snapped. “I tell you what, cousin, I won’t move an inch for the Countess, or the Baroness, or any of the rest of the Castlewood cousins.” When the landlord trundled in carrying the bowl of punch Will had ordered, Harry shouted at him to throw that sot out of his room. Will went ballistic, screaming, “Sot, you little tobacco-selling colonial! Sot, you Cherokee savage! Jump out of bed and I’ll run my sword right through you! Why didn’t I do it today when I thought you were a pettifogging bailiff!” He carried on screeching oaths and nonsense until the landlord, the taproom drawer, the hostler and half the kitchen staff all piled into the room to drag him away. Harry yanked his bed curtains shut, fuming, and eventually fell asleep.

The next morning, the landlord greeted Harry with obsequious bows and scrapes, now fully aware of his guest’s name and status. Other servants had come down to the inn late the night before to fetch Will home, it turned out, and they’d had to wheel him back to Castlewood in a wheelbarrow—a mode of transport he was hardly unaccustomed to. “He never remembers a thing the next day, poor Mr. William,” the landlord swore. “He’s got a real good, kind heart, sober. The stable boys earn crowns off him just by saying he thrashed them the night before when he was drunk. He’s the devil when he’s in his cups, but the sweetest of young fellows when he’s himself.”

While Harry had waited outside the house the day before, the Castlewood family had held a hasty, quiet council after finding his scrap of paper on the hall table. Lord Castlewood admitted the lanky lad they had seen loitering on the bridge must have been the young Virginian, and since they hadn’t actually drowned him, they supposed they ought to send a servant with an invite. Young Lady Fanny thought it would be far more polite to send one of the brothers, especially after how rudely they had treated him on the bridge. Lord Castlewood agreed Will could go, and Will immediately swore he’d be hanged before he traipsed down to the inn after some colonial stranger. Lady Maria thought the young man on the bridge was quite handsome, and Castlewood was so dreadfully dull, it would be fun to have a new novelty to amuse them, even if he was vulgar. The Countess, her stepmother, insisted they wait for the Baroness to arrive, to see what she thought before extending any welcome—no use making a fuss before consulting the woman whose vast fortune the whole family was angling to inherit. So their hospitality to Harry hung completely in limbo.

When the Baroness Bernstein’s coach finally pulled up, the entire household fawned over her like royalty. The state bedroom had been prepped for days, the cook had spent all the previous day making her favourite dishes, the oak dining table glinted with old silver plate, set beneath walls lined with family portraits: the late Viscount, his father, his mother, his sister, the old Vandyck of an earlier Earl, his Viscountess, and the portrait of Colonel Henry Esmond—Harry’s grandfather, the very man whose grandson they had treated like a stray vagrant. The Baroness tucked into her supper with gusto, and the whole family hovered over her, pouring her champagne, laughing at every one of her famously wicked anecdotes about the scandals of European courts, even the strait-laced, propriety-obsessed Countess snickering at her most outrageous stories. “That was my father’s place,” she said, pointing to the Countess’s seat at the head of the table. “All the old silver that used to sit here was melted down to pay his gambling debts. I do hope you boys don’t play cards for money, eh?” “Never, on my word,” Lord Castlewood said solemnly. “Never, pon honour,” Will echoed, winking at his brother behind her back. She pointed a plump, still elegant arm at the portrait of Colonel Esmond in his red coat and cuirass. “That man there was the only real man this whole Esmond family ever produced. Every other male Esmond before or since has had no more sense than a silly goose, except him. The world was too wicked for him, so he did the sensible thing and went off to live in Virginia. Where would your father have ended up, if not for him?” “Was he that kind to our papa?” Lady Maria asked. The Countess sniffed. “Old stories, my dear! I’m sure the Earl was very kind to him in return, giving him that great big Virginia estate.” “Since his twin brother died,” the Baroness said, “that boy who was here today is heir to all that land. Peste, I don’t know why my father ever gave such a property away.” “Who was here today?” the Baroness asked, sitting up sharp. “Harry Esmond Warrington, from Virginia,” Lord Castlewood replied. “The boy Will nearly rode into the river. I tried to get my lady to invite him to stay.” “You mean a grandson of Henry Esmond, the rightful master of this house, showed up here yesterday, and none of you asked him to come inside?” the Baroness thundered, slamming her hand on the table so all the glasses rattled. The whole family hung their heads. “Call a servant! Get me my hood! I’ll go get that boy myself, right this instant. Come on, Castlewood.” Lord Castlewood stood up, red-faced and angry. “Your ladyship can do as you please, but I won’t. I won’t have you calling my conduct shameful. I’m not going crawling after this American boy just to curry favour with you for your money. You all want her fortune more than I do—you can cringe for it, I won’t.” He flopped back into his chair. The Baroness looked at him, and laughed a little, muttering in German: “I was wrong to say Colonel Esmond was the only man in the family. You have it in you, Eugene, if you choose to show it.” She turned back to the rest. “Fine. If his lordship won’t go, Will will. You boys go fetch him.” Will, already half drunk, jumped up. “Great! And we’ll have another bowl of punch when he gets here!” And that was how he ended up bursting into Harry’s bedroom at the Three Castles, as Harry had already experienced.

The Baroness knew perfectly well why the Countess had refused to welcome Harry: no wealthy aristocrat mother is eager to host a handsome nineteen year old bachelor with a big estate across the ocean, when there are unmarried young ladies in the house, and Harry’s Virginia lands don’t seem nearly as real or desirable as Norfolk acres would. She understood the whole family’s greedy little games, how they all fawned over her for her money, and she found their petty humiliations and greed quite amusing. But by then, warmed by punch and champagne, a good old-fashioned generous fit came over her, furious that the grandson of the man she had loved like a father was stuck sleeping in an inn outside the gates of his own ancestral home.

Long before the languid Castlewood girls had dragged themselves out of their feather beds after a long season of London routs, and long before Will had slept off his night of punch, the Baroness was up, pacing the dewy green terraces, watching the sun rise over the trim flower beds, the fountains splashing, the rooks cawing in the great wood. She sent a note down to the Three Castles by young Lockwood, the porter’s son, summoning Harry up to the house. Soon Harry came trundling up the drive, through the old Gothic arch, hat in hand, his fair hair blowing in the morning breeze, his slim figure dressed in black mourning for his brother. He gave her a deep, elegant bow that would have done credit to Versailles. She held out her hand to him, and he took it, blushing bright pink. “I knew your grandfather very well, Harry,” she said gently. “You came yesterday to see his picture, and they turned you away, even though this house was his of right, wasn’t it?” Harry blushed. “The servants didn’t know who I was. The young gentleman who came to my room last night was very drunk, and I spoke rudely to him. I should like to apologize to my cousin. In Virginia, we welcome every stranger who comes to our door. I confess I expected a different kind of welcome here.” “That was me who sent Will to fetch you last night,” the Baroness said, smiling. “You’ll find all your cousins far more friendly today. You’re going to stay here, in this house. Lord Castlewood would have come to meet you himself this morning, but I was far too eager to see you first. Breakfast will be ready in an hour. Send someone down to the inn to get your servant Gumbo and your trunks. Give me your arm—my cane fell over there, and you shall be my cane to lean on.” Harry grinned. “My grandfather used to call us boys his crutches, when he was old.” “You look just like him,” she said softly, “even with that fair hair.”
