Mr. Home lingered with us for two days, though he scarcely ventured beyond the fireside. There he sat, wrapped in his own thoughts, while Mrs. Bretton’s measured kindness—neither too tender nor too distant—filled the silence when he wished for company. She understood his melancholy well enough to offer comfort without intrusion.

Paulina, meanwhile, was a quiet storm of devotion—content in his presence, yet fiercely attentive. She would perch on his knee until she sensed his weariness, then slip away with a murmured, *“Papa, let me down; I mustn’t tire you.”* Settled at his feet, she would ply her needle with solemn diligence, stitching at a handkerchief she meant to send with him—a labor of love, though her small fingers managed only twenty stitches in half an hour.

The evenings brightened when Graham returned from school. From the first, he and Paulina had struck sparks off one another. After his initial teasing—which she met with icy disdain—he learned to lure her with treasures from his desk: sealing wax, penknives, engravings of dogs and children in gaudy colors. Once, an etching of a boy playing with a spaniel fluttered to the floor.

*“Pretty little dog!”* she exclaimed, eyes alight.

Graham, wisely, said nothing. Soon enough, curiosity drew her nearer.

*“Nice picture,”* she conceded.

*“You may have it,”* he offered.

She hesitated, pride warring with desire. *“I would rather not, thank you.”*

She did—but when he demanded a kiss as payment, she snatched the etching and fled to her father’s lap. Graham gave chase, laughing, until she turned on him with a tiny, furious fist. His retaliation was theatrical: collapsing onto the sofa, groaning as if mortally wounded. Paulina, stricken, crept close.

*“Is he hurt?”* she whispered.

*“Ask him yourself,”* Mr. Home suggested.

Graham moaned about blindness. Her resolve crumbled. *“Let me see! I didn’t mean to—I’m sorry!”* Tears followed, and Graham, triumphant, swept her into another romp.

---

The morning of Mr. Home’s departure, I overheard them by the window.

*“Couldn’t I go with you, Papa?”* Her voice was a thread of hope.

He refused gently. *“You’re too little for such journeys.”*

*“Then I’ll be good till you return,”* she promised, dry-eyed—until the door closed behind him. Then she dropped to her knees, a single cry wrenching from her: *“Papa!”* It was the sound of a heart breaking. Mrs. Bretton wept quietly; Graham watched, puzzled; I alone remained unmoved.

---

Paulina’s grief was fierce but brief. By the third evening, she sat listless on the floor until Graham gathered her up without a word. She nestled into his arms, asleep before he carried her to bed.

The next morning, her first question was, *“Where is Mr. Graham?”*

He was shut in his study, skipping breakfast for his lessons. She insisted on carrying his tea, setting it solemnly at his feet like a jailor’s offering. When he praised her, she became his eager servant, selecting the choicest treats—though she refused even marmalade, lest she seem greedy.

Their bond deepened. With others, Paulina was quiet, even dull; but Graham’s return each evening set her alight. She’d perch on the stairs, scolding him for muddy shoes or taunting him from the banister.

*“My dear boy!”* she’d call—a mimicry of his mother’s endearments.

Once, when he feigned exhaustion, she descended warily. *“Promise not to whirl me about.”*

Of course, he did.

---

Yet not all was harmony. On Graham’s birthday, his laughter with friends spilled from the dining room. Paulina sat alone on the stairs, staring at the door.

*“I wish I could see them,”* she confessed.

I encouraged her to knock. Graham’s face appeared—bright, impatient. *“What do you want, little monkey?”*

*“Away with you!”* The door slammed.

She stood stunned. *“Why does he speak so? He never did before.”*

No philosophy could console her. She lay facedown on the floor, refusing to move until she chose.

Graham, remorseful later, found her unforgiving. She wrenched away, would not bid him goodnight. Days passed before she relented, but the lesson stuck: never again did she seek him out unbidden.

---

Still, her devotion never wavered. She watched from windows when he rode his pony, though she’d refuse with icy dignity if he offered her a ride.

*“You’d like it,”* he coaxed.

*“I shouldn’t care a fig,”* she lied.

Graham only laughed. *“She’s a changeling,”* he told his mother. *“But I’d be dull without her.”*

---

Sundays were her favorite. Then Graham was quiet, contemplative. He’d sprawl on the couch, calling for Polly to recite hymns or read Bible stories—*Joseph in the pit, Samuel called by God*—her voice trembling over Jacob’s grief.

*“Poor Jacob,”* she whispered once. *“He loved Joseph as I love you. If you died—”* She found the verse. *“—I would go down into the grave mourning.”*

And she hugged him fiercely.

---

It could not last. A letter came from Mr. Home: he would not return to England for years. Paulina was to join him at once.

I found her in the drawing room, rocking her doll to sleep while studying a picture-book—a vision of childish serenity.

How would she bear the news?
