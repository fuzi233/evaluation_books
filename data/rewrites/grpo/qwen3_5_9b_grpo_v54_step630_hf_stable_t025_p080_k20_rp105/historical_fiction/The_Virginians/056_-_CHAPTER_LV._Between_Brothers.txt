The two young men had spent their initial hours exchanging the intimate details of their separate worlds, recounting the history of their households and the comings and goings of their families. Harry spoke eagerly of his dogs, the spirited little Dempster, and the steadfast old Nathan, asking after the health of Mountain and the blossoming beauty of Fanny. He relayed news of Parson Broadbent's daughter, Polly, who was soon to wed Tom Barker of Savannah and depart for Georgia. Harry confessed with a flush of shame that he had once been deeply infatuated with Polly, squandering pocket money at cards and consuming excessive spirits with her father merely to secure her company. Yet, he noted with relief that Madam Esmond would never have permitted such a union, deeming it a waste of her son's future.

In contrast, George described Colonel Washington's wife as a woman of considerable charm, good nature, and substantial fortune. He recounted the stiff, almost theatrical ceremony of introducing her to Madam Esmond in Richmond. With a twinkle in his eye, George recalled his mother's biting wit during the encounter: "Never mind, George, my dear!" she had declared, dismissing the Colonel's new bride. "The Colonel has taken another wife, but I feel certain that at one time two young gentlemen I know of ran a very near chance of having a tall stepfather six feet two in his boots." Mrs. Mountain, ever the matchmaker, seemed to envision a lifelong connection between any two people who shared a meal or a game of cards. It was she, the gossipy instigator, who had spread the rumor concerning the Indian woman, Biche. Madam Esmond had met the accusation with scornful disdain when Parson Stack delivered it, declaring she would sooner suspect Mr. Esmond of stealing silverware or marrying a servant from the kitchen. However, despite her refusal to believe Biche guilty and her gratitude for the woman's care during her son's illness, Madam Esmond treated her with such icy hauteur that the Indian woman retreated to the servants' quarters, drowning her disappointment in drink.

George painted a bleak picture of his two months at home. "The birthright is mine, Harry," he lamented, "but thou art the favourite, and God help me! I think my mother almost grudges it to me. Why should I have taken the pass, and preceded your worship into the world? Had you been the elder, you would have possessed the finest cellar, ridden the best horse, and been the most popular man in the country. As for me, I have not a word to say for myself, and frighten people with my glum visage. I should have been content as a second son, setting up as a lawyer in England, earning degrees, and saying grace at your honour's table. The time is out of joint, sir. O cursed spite, that ever I was born to set it right!"

"Why, Georgy, you are reciting verses, I protest!" Harry exclaimed.

"I believe someone else spoke those lines before me," George smiled.

"It is from one of your books," Harry cried, laughing. "You know every book ever written, I swear! Though I admit I cared little for their contents, lacking the wit to appreciate them fully. But I remembered how my dear old George used to speak of them, and that is why I took the trouble to see the authors at Tunbridge. I removed my hat to them, though not out of reverence for their works, but because of my affection for you." His voice choked slightly. "To see you again feels like a dream. Think of that bloody Indian with a knife at your throat! I wish I could offer Monsieur de Florac something for saving you, but I possess little now—merely my gold knee-buckles, which are hardly worth two guineas."

"You hold half of what I possess, child," George replied gently. "We shall divide it as soon as I have settled the debt to the Frenchman."

At this, Harry erupted into a torrent of blessings and oaths, expressing his profound love and relief. He swore that no brother in the world could compare to George. For several days following his arrival, Harry's eyes followed his brother constantly. He would pause mid-meal, drop his knife and fork, or abandon his newspaper to burst into laughter. Walking together on the Mall or in Hyde Park, Harry would gaze around at the assembled crowd as if presenting a trophy, thinking, *Behold, gentlemen! This is he. This is my brother, who was dead and is now alive again! Can any man in Christendom boast such a sibling?*

Harry firmly believed that George must honor his promise to pay Museau's heirs the ransom sum. This obligation had caused George significant distress at home. Upon Museau's death, Madam Esmond had argued with fierce eagerness and rancor that the bargain was void, freeing her son. She refused to pay the wages of iniquity. While Mr. Esmond possessed a small independence and might squander his patrimony, his mother would not be complicit in such extravagance. She would not subscribe to the wickedness of giving twelve thousand livres to a band of peasant farmers in Normandy, enemies of England, who would likely funnel the money to the priests and the Pope. Instead, if George wished to spend his father's money, he should give it to his own flesh and blood—to poor Harry, who had suddenly lost his inheritance—rather than to a set of priest-ridden peasants in a hostile nation. This dispute had raged between mother and son throughout George's final days in Virginia. On the morning of his departure, after a sleepless night, Madam Esmond had visited his bedside to ask if he still intended to throw away his father's property. George, filled with grief and perplexity, replied that his word was given and he must act according to his honor. She answered that she would continue to pray for Heaven to soften his proud heart and enable her to bear her trials. Her last look at her son was one of pale, tearless resignation before she slowly withdrew from his chamber.

"Where didst thou learn the art of winning everyone to thy side, Harry?" George continued, shifting the conversation. "How is it that you and the entire world begin as friends? Teach me a few lessons in popularity. Nay, I do not know that I require them; indeed, when I discover certain people hate me, I am often more pleased than angry."

He recounted his early days in Richmond. Mr. Esmond Warrington, the sole survivor of Braddock's defeat, had been a favorite of the townsfolk, receiving private and public kindness. The parson had glorified his escape in a sermon, neighbors visited the fugitive, and the family carriage was dispatched for reciprocal calls. George recalled that some had even set pretty little caps for him. But his mother had routed these admirers away with prodigious haughtiness, and his popularity waned before the events that sealed his fate occurred. He was not jovial enough for the officers, who preferred their drinking bouts, dice, and swearing; nor was he warm enough for the ladies, whose tea and gossip stupefied him as much as the men's bluster. He felt profoundly lonely amidst the scandal and squabbles, regretting his prison and longing for the freedom of thought and silent ease of Duquesne. He admitted to being shy, speaking unreservedly to very few, often sitting in utter silence before others. At home, it had always been Harry who dominated the table conversation, eliciting smiles from their mother, while George and his mother had little in common, speaking only to dispute matters of law and divinity.

Consequently, the gentlemen deemed him supercilious and dull, an opinion George believed was justified. The ladies found him cold and sarcastic, unable to discern if he was in earnest, generally voting him a disagreeable fellow before his reputation collapsed entirely with the appearance of the poor Biche. "Oh, a nice character they made for me, my dear!" George cried in a transport of wrath. "And a pretty life they led me after Museau's unlucky messenger appeared among us! The boys hooted the poor woman if she appeared in the street; the ladies dropped half-curtseys and crossed to the other side. That precious clergyman went from tea-table to tea-table preaching on the horrors of seduction and the lax principles young men learned in popish countries. The poor Fawn's appearance at home a few weeks after my return was declared a scheme between her and me; the best-informed agreed she had waited on the other side of the river for my signal to join me in Richmond. The officers bantered me at the coffee-house, cracking clumsy jokes about the woman I had selected. Oh, the world is a nice, charitable world! I was so enraged that I considered fleeing to Castlewood to live alone—for our mother finds the place dull and relies on Mr. Stack's ministry—until news arrived of your female perplexity, and I think we were all glad I had a pretext to come to Europe."

"I should like to see any of the infernal scoundrels who spoke against you," Harry roared, pacing the room, "and break their rascally bones!"

"I had to do something similar for Bob Clubber."

"What! That little sneaking, backbiting, toad-eating wretch who hangs about my lord at Greenway Court, spunging on every gentleman in the country? If you whipped him, George, I hope you whipped him well!"

"We were bound over to keep the peace," George explained. "I offered to go to Maryland with him to settle our difference there, and of course, the good folk remarked that having made free with the seventh commandment, I was inclined to break the sixth. By this and that, and being as innocent of the crime imputed to me as you are, I left home, my dear Harry, with as awful a reputation as ever a young gentleman earned."

Ah, what an opportunity for moralizing! If the esteemed reader and his humble servant could compile a book, illustrated with the lies told regarding each of them since reaching manhood, what a harrowing and thrilling work of fiction it would be! The world is informed of everything about you, and far more. Recently, a postman had delivered a paper containing a valuable critique stating, "This author states he was born in such and such a year. It is a lie. He was born in the year so and so." The critic knew better. Another warning from the same region advised, "Do not speak of New South Wales to him. He has a brother there, and the family never mentions his name." But this subject is too vast for a mere paragraph; it deserves a memoir, or perhaps a series of biographies penned by dear friends whom the subjects do not know.

Having related his exploits as champion and martyr, Harry was compelled to unbosom himself, laying before his elder brother an account of his private affairs. He detailed his dealings with the Castlewood family—not his lordship, for Harry was a sporting man who expected to pay when he lost and receive when he won, but for refusing to aid the chaplain in his necessity and dismissing him with false, heartless pretexts. Regarding Mr. Will, Harry had made up his mind after the horse-dealing incident, freely marking his displeasure on the man's eyes and nose. Speaking of the Countess and Lady Fanny, Harry was guarded but not favorable. He had heard all sorts of stories: the Countess was a card-playing old cat, and Lady Fanny a desperate flirt. He had heard these tales from someone who knew them both intimately. Indeed, in those days of confidence, Maria had freely imparted numerous anecdotes to her cousin regarding her stepmother and half-sister, none of which were in their favor.

However, regarding Lady Maria herself, the young man remained staunch and hearty. "It may be imprudent," he admitted to George. "I don't say no. I may be a fool; I think I am. I know there will be a dreadful piece of work at home, and that Madam and she will fight. Well! We must live apart. Our estate is big enough to live on without quarrelling, and I can go elsewhere than to Richmond or Castlewood. When you come to the property, you'll give me a bit—at any rate, Madam will let me off at an easy rent—or I'll make a famous farmer or factor. I cannot and will not part from Maria. She has acted so nobly by me that I should be a rascal to turn my back on her. Think of her bringing me every jewel she had in the world, dear brave creature! And flinging them into my lap with her last guineas—and—and—God bless her!" Harry dashed his sleeve across his eyes, stamped his foot, and declared, "No, brother, I won't part with her—not to be made Governor of Virginia tomorrow. And my dearest old George would never advise me to do so, I know that."

"I am sent here to advise you," George replied. "I am sent to break the marriage off, if I can: and a more unhappy one I cannot imagine. But I cannot counsel you to break your word, my boy."

"I knew you couldn't! What's said is said, George. I have made my bed, and must lie on it," Harry said gloomily.

Such had been the settlement between the two young worthies regarding Harry's love affair. But after George's conversation with his aunt and his further knowledge of her family, gained through the information of that keen old woman of the world, Mr. Warrington, who was naturally of a skeptical turn, began to doubt Lady Maria, as well as her brothers and sister. He viewed Harry's engagement with increased distrust and alarm. Was it for his wealth that Maria wanted Harry? Was it his handsome young person that she longed after? Were those stories true which Aunt Bernstein had told? Certainly, he could not advise Harry to break his word; but he might cast about in his mind for some scheme to put Maria's affection to the test. His ensuing conduct, which appeared not very amiable, presumably resulted from this deliberation.
