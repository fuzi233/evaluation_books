I have nothing to say about my own feelings.

The blow that struck me seemed to freeze every thought and sensation. I was utterly unaware of what I was doing when Betteredge found me—so much so that, according to his account, I laughed when he asked what had happened, and handed him the nightgown, telling him to read the mystery for himself.

I remember nothing of our conversation on the beach. The first clear image I have is of walking back with Betteredge through the fir plantation. He was reassuring me: we would both be able to face this—if only we could have a glass of grog.

The scene shifts to Betteredge’s small sitting room. My firm decision never to step into Rachel’s house again had slipped away. I welcomed the cool shade and quiet of the room. I drank the grog—a new experience at that hour—mixed by my old friend with icy water from the well. Under ordinary circumstances, such a drink would have left me dazed. But now it steadied me. Slowly, as Betteredge had predicted, I began to “face it.” And Betteredge, too, began to “face it.”

The picture I now present of myself may seem strange, even absurd. Placed in a situation without parallel, what do I do? Do I withdraw from society? Do I try to unravel the impossible truth staring me in the face? Do I rush to London to consult experts and launch an investigation? No. I enter a house I vowed never to return to, and sit drinking spirits and water with an old servant at ten in the morning. Is this how a man in my position should act? I can only say that seeing Betteredge’s familiar face brought me comfort, and his grog—though no more than a common drink—was the only thing that helped pull me from the numbness that had seized my body and mind. That is all I can offer in explanation. And yet, I cannot help but admire the dignity and logical conduct shown by others in times of crisis—qualities I lack in this moment.

“Now, Mr. Franklin,” Betteredge said, tossing the nightgown onto the table between us, pointing at it as if it were alive and capable of hearing. “_He’s_ a liar, to start with.”

That comforting thought did not come to me.

“I know nothing about stealing the Diamond,” I said. “As little as you do. But there’s proof against me—the paint on the nightgown, the name written on it. These are facts.”

Betteredge picked up my glass and pressed it into my hand.

“Facts?” he repeated. “Take another sip of grog, Mr. Franklin. You’ll soon lose your faith in facts! Foul play, sir,” he whispered confidentially. “That’s what I think. Foul play somewhere—and you and I must find it. Was there anything else in the tin case when you reached in?”

His words stirred something in me. I remembered the letter in my pocket. I took it out and opened it. It was long, densely written. I scanned quickly to the end—“Rosanna Spearman.”

As I read her name, a memory flashed into my mind, followed by a sudden suspicion.

“Wait!” I exclaimed. “Rosanna Spearman came to my aunt from a reformatory? She was once a thief?”

“There’s no denying it, Mr. Franklin. So what now?”

“What now? How do we know she didn’t steal the Diamond after all? How do we know she didn’t smear my nightgown deliberately?”

Betteredge laid a hand on my arm, cutting off my words.

“You’ll be cleared, Mr.a Franklin, beyond any doubt. But I hope you won’t be cleared in _that_ way. Read the letter, sir. In justice to her memory, read it.”

I felt the sincerity in his voice—it was almost a rebuke. “You shall judge for yourself,” I said. “I’ll read it aloud.”

I began:

“Sir—I have something to confess. A confession that brings great misery might be made in just three words: I love you.”

The letter dropped from my fingers. I looked at Betteredge. “In Heaven’s name,” I said, “what does this mean?”

He hesitated, clearly reluctant to answer.

“You and Limping Lucy were alone this morning,” he said. “Did she say anything about Rosanna Spearman?”

“She never mentioned her name.”

“Then go back to the letter, Mr. Franklin. I can’t bear to distress you further. Let her speak for herself. And finish your grog—for your own sake.”

I resumed reading.

“It would be disgraceful for me to tell you this, if I were still alive when you read it. But I will be dead by then, sir. That gives me courage. Not even my grave will remain to speak of me. I can confess—knowing the quicksand waits to swallow me when I write these words.

“Besides, you’ll find your nightgown hidden with the paint stain on it. And you’ll want to know why I hid it—why I never told you while I lived. There’s only one reason: I loved you.

“I won’t dwell on my past before you came to my lady’s house. Lady Verinder rescued me from a reformatory. I’d gone there from prison. I was sent to prison because I stole. I stole because my mother turned to prostitution when I was young. She became a street woman because the man who fathered me abandoned her. Such stories are common enough—often printed in the papers.

“Lady Verinder was kind. Mr. Betteredge was kind. Those two and the matron at the reformatory were the only good people I’ve ever known. I might have managed, though unhappily, if you hadn’t come visiting. I don’t blame you, sir. It’s all my fault.

“Do you remember when you emerged from the sandhills that morning, searching for Mr. Betteredge? You looked like a prince in a fairy tale. Like a lover in a dream. You were the most beautiful human being I’d ever seen. Something—like the life I’d never known—leapt into me the moment I saw you. Don’t laugh, if you can help it. Oh, if I could make you feel how serious this is to me!

“I went back to the house and wrote your name and mine in my workbox, drawing a true lovers’ knot beneath. Then—some devil—or perhaps a good angel—whispered, ‘Go look in the mirror.’ The mirror showed me… well, never mind. I was too foolish to heed the warning. I kept growing fonder of you, as if I were a lady of your class, the most beautiful creature you’d ever seen. I tried—oh, how I tried—to get you to notice me. If you knew how I wept at night, broken by your indifference, you might have pitied me and given me just one glance to live on.

“It wouldn’t have been a kind glance, perhaps, if you’d known how I hated Miss Rachel. I believe I discovered your feelings for her before you did. She gave you roses to wear in your buttonhole. Ah, Mr. Franklin, you wore _my_ roses more often than either of you realized! My only comfort was slipping my rose into your glass of water, replacing hers—and throwing hers away.

“If she had truly been as lovely as you believed, I might have borne it better. No—I’d have been even angrier. Suppose you dressed Miss Rachel in a servant’s gown and stripped her of her jewels? I don’t know why I’m writing this. It’s undeniable she had a poor figure—too thin. But who knows what men desire? And young ladies may behave in ways that would cost a servant her job. It’s none of my business. I don’t expect you to read this if I write like this. But it stings to hear Miss Rachel called pretty when I know it’s her clothes and confidence that make her seem so.

“Try not to grow impatient, sir. I’ll move swiftly to the part you care about—the time the Diamond vanished.

“But first, there’s one thing I must tell you.

“My life wasn’t hard when I was a thief. It was only after the reformatory taught me shame and the desire to reform that the days grew long and painful. I began to dread honest people—even the kindest—because they represented everything I wasn’t. A crushing loneliness followed me wherever I went. I knew I should try to befriend the other servants—but I couldn’t. They looked (or I thought they looked) as if they suspected my past. I don’t regret being roused to reform—but oh, how weary it was. You came like sunlight at first—then you failed me too. I was mad enough to love you, yet couldn’t even draw your attention. There was real suffering—real agony—in that.

“Now I come to what I wanted to tell you. In those bitter days, I went several times—when it was my turn to leave the house—to my favorite place: the beach above the Shivering Sand. I told myself, ‘This is where it will end. When I can bear it no longer, it will end here.’ You’ll understand, sir, that the place had already cast a spell over me before you arrived. I always sensed something would happen there. But I never considered it as a means to end my life—until now. Then I realized: this was a place that could end all my troubles in moments—and hide me forever.

“This is all I have to say about myself—from the morning I first saw you to the morning the Diamond was stolen.

“I was furious at the women’s idle talk, guessing who might be guilty. I was angry with you (though I didn’t understand then) for your efforts to find the jewel and summon the police. So I stayed apart until later, when the officer from Frizinghall arrived.

“Mr. Seegrave began by posting guards on the women’s rooms. The maids stormed upstairs, furious at the insult. I went with them—not because I wanted to, but because if I’d acted differently, Seegrave would have suspected me. We found him in Miss Rachel’s room. He refused to allow us to stay, pointed to the paint smudge on the door, claimed one of our petticoats caused it, and sent us down.

“After leaving, I paused on a landing, checking my gown for any trace of paint. Penelope Betteredge—my only friend among the maids—passed and noticed.

‘You don’t need to check, Rosanna,’ she said. ‘The paint on Miss Rachel’s door has been dry for hours. If Seegrave hadn’t watched our rooms, I’d have told him so. I’ve never been so insulted in my life!’

“Penelope was hot-tempered. I calmed her and returned to her claim about the paint drying.

‘How do you know?’ I asked.

‘I was with Miss Rachel and Mr. Franklin yesterday morning,’ she said, ‘mixing the paint for the door. Miss Rachel asked if it would dry by evening. Mr. Franklin shook his head—said it would take twelve hours. We finished around three o’clock. What does your math say, Rosanna? Mine says the door was dry by three this morning.’

‘Did any of the ladies go up last night to see it?’ I asked. ‘I thought I heard Miss Rachel warn them to avoid it.’

‘None of the ladies touched it,’ Penelope replied. ‘I left Miss Rachel in bed at midnight. I checked the door—nothing wrong.’

‘Shouldn’t you tell Seegrave?’ I asked.

‘Not a word. I’d rather burn than help Seegrave.’

“She went to work. I went to mine.

“My task, sir, was making your bed and tidying your room. It was my happiest hour. I kissed the pillow where your head had rested. No one since has folded your clothes as neatly as I did. Every item in your dressing case was spotless. You never noticed—just as you never noticed me. I beg your pardon—I’m losing control. I’ll continue.

“Well, I entered your room that morning to clean. Your nightgown lay across the bed, just as you’d thrown it off. I picked it up—and saw the paint stain from Miss Rachel’s door!

“I was so shocked I ran downstairs, clutching the nightgown, locked myself in my room, and examined it in private.

“When I caught my breath, I recalled my talk with Penelope. I realized: *He was in Miss Rachel’s room between midnight and three this morning.*

“I won’t say plainly what I first suspected. You’d only be angry—and if you were angry, you might tear this letter and stop reading.

“Let me say only this: After thinking carefully, I concluded it was unlikely—for reasons I’ll explain. If you’d been in Miss Rachel’s room at that hour with her knowledge (and if you’d forgotten the wet door), *she* would have reminded you—she would never have let you carry away such evidence against her! Still, I admit I wasn’t fully convinced I’d disproved my suspicion. Remember, I admitted hating Miss Rachel. Perhaps that hatred colored my thoughts. In the end, I decided to keep the nightgown, wait, and watch—see what use I could make of it. At that time, not a single thought crossed my mind that *you* had stolen the Diamond.”

Here, I paused again in reading.

I had read the parts of her confession concerning me with genuine surprise—and sincere sorrow. I had regretted casting aspersions on her memory before reading a word. But as I advanced to this passage, my feelings turned colder, harsher toward Rosanna Spearman.

“Read the rest yourself,” I said, handing the letter to Betteredge. “If there’s anything I *must* see, you can tell me.”

“I understand, Mr. Franklin,” he replied. “It’s natural in you. And God help us all—it’s no less natural in her.”

I now copy the remainder of the letter from my own copy:

“Having decided to keep the nightgown and wait to see what use my love—or my revenge—could make of it, I needed to hide it without risk of discovery.

“The only way was to make a duplicate nightgown before Saturday, when the laundry woman came with her inventory.

“I feared delaying till Friday, afraid some accident might occur. So I resolved to make the new nightgown that same day—Thursday—while I had time to myself. First, I locked your nightgown in my drawer. Then I returned to your bedroom—not to tidy it (Penelope would have done that), but to check if you’d transferred any paint stain to the bed or furniture.

“I searched carefully and found faint streaks inside your flannel dressing-gown—not the linen one you usually wore, but the warmer one you had brought. You must have felt cold after walking in your nightdress and put on the warmest thing available. There were the stains—barely visible. I scraped them off easily. Now the only proof left was the nightgown in my drawer.

“I had just finished cleaning your room when I was summoned to be questioned by Mr. Seegrave, along with the other servants. Then came the search of our boxes. And then—what I consider the most extraordinary event of the day, since finding the paint on your nightgown.

“It came during Seegrave’s second questioning of Penelope Betteredge.

“She returned beside herself with rage at how he treated her. He hinted—beyond doubt—that he suspected her of theft. We were stunned. Why?

“Because the Diamond was in Miss Rachel’s room,” Penelope said. “And I was the last person in it at night!”

“Before the words left her lips, I remembered: someone else had been in the room later than Penelope. That person was you. My head spun. My thoughts raced. And then, something whispered: the paint on your nightgown might mean something entirely different.

“If the last person in the room is the one to suspect,” I thought, “then the thief isn’t Penelope—it’s Mr. Franklin Blake!”

“For any other man, I believe I would have been ashamed to suspect him. But the idea that *you* had fallen to my level—that I, by possessing your nightgown, had also gained the power to shield you from exposure and disgrace—this thought opened a path to winning your favor. I passed blindly, as it were, from suspicion to belief. I decided on the spot that you had feigned urgency in calling the police to mislead us all—and that the hand that took Miss Rachel’s jewel could only be yours.

“The excitement of this discovery must have addled my mind. I felt a desperate urge to see you—to test you with a few words about the Diamond, to make you look at me, speak to me—just once. I fixed my hair, made myself presentable, and boldly entered the library, where I knew you were writing.

“You’d left a ring upstairs—perfect excuse. But oh, sir! If you’ve ever loved, you’ll understand how my courage vanished when I stepped into the room and faced you. You looked up coldly, thanked me indifferently for finding your ring. My knees trembled. I felt I’d collapse at your feet. After thanking me, you returned to your writing. I was so humiliated that I gathered strength to speak. I said, ‘This is a strange thing about the Diamond, sir.’ You looked up again. ‘Yes, it is!’ You spoke politely—but kept a cruel distance. Believing you held the Diamond, your coldness enraged me. In the heat of the moment, I dared to hint: ‘They’ll never find the Diamond, sir, will they? Nor the thief—I’ll answer for that.’ I nodded, smiled—meaning, ‘I know.’ This time, you looked up with interest. I felt just a few more words might reveal the truth. Just then, Mr. Betteredge spoiled it by arriving at the door. I recognized his step—and knew it was against his rules for me to be in the library, especially with you. I escaped just in time. I was angry, disappointed—but not without hope. The ice was broken. Next time, I’d make sure Betteredge was absent.

“When I returned to the servants’ hall, dinner was being called. Already afternoon—and I still lacked materials for the new nightgown. Only one chance: pretend illness at dinner. That secured the entire interval until tea-time for myself.”
