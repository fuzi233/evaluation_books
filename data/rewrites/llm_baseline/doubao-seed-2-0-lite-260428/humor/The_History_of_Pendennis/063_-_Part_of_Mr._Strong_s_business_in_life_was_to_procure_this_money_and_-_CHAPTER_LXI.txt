Our soft-hearted, famously wealthy “Begum” Lady Clavering was so incandescent with rage at her husband Sir Francis’s latest spectacular act of Derby-day gambling idiocy that she swore she’d never shell out a single penny to cover his honour debts ever again, packed her overnight bag on the spot, and declared she was dumping the useless man to face his furious bookies all on his own.

The cowardly Baronet didn’t even dare come home that night. He hid out at a grubby little Blackfriars Road inn under the boneheaded fake alias “Mr. Francis”, as if that would fool anyone. The hotel boots who schlepped his carpet-bag back to Grosvenor Place snitched immediately to the footman setting the breakfast table, who spilled the tea to the entire servants’ hall, who passed the gossip to Lady Clavering’s right-hand housekeeper Susan Bonner, who relayed the full ridiculous story to her mistress. Within 24 hours, every coachman in London knew Sir Francis was skulking in a low-end inn, and the whole crowd at Tattersall’s was laying side bets on how soon he’d bolt for the Levant to escape his debts.

The next day the pile of post on Sir Francis’s empty study desk reached new, legendary heights: his fired French chef sent his overdue bill straight to Lady Clavering, the fancy mercers, the celebrated milliner Madame Crinoline, and her daughter Miss Amory all dropped their equally massive long-overdue accounts on her too. When Strong (the man whose entire job description was digging Sir Francis out of self-inflicted holes) turned up that afternoon, he found the stack of alarming bills sitting there and sighed so hard his moustache drooped.

Susan Bonner cornered him immediately, even angrier than her mistress. “We won’t pay a farthing of his messes, not if I have any say!” she ranted. “You go right back to that skulking sneak of a Sir Francis, hiding in a cheap pub too coward to face his own wife like a grown man! We bailed him out of jail! Got him a seat in Parliament! Gave him a fancy town house, a fancy country house, fancy horses to ride and fancy dinners to eat! All the clothes on his back are ours! We’re done. We’ll give him a tiny allowance just so he doesn’t starve in a ditch, then we’re leaving him high and dry. That’s my lady’s orders, if she’s got any sense left.”

When Lady Clavering called Strong up to her parlour, he was already mentally running through the dozen tearful speeches that had convinced her to forgive her husband’s nonsense a dozen times before. He laid it on thick: he’d found Sir Francis holed up in that hotel room, sobbing into a bottle of Curacoa, with a straight razor left out on the table like he was even considering doing the world a favour.

Lady Clavering just laughed bitterly. “That spineless worm won’t hurt a hair on his own stupid head,” she said. “Not as long as there’s a shilling of mine he can steal to fund his next dumb bet. His life’s perfectly safe, you can count on that.”
“Pah, he’s worse than her first husband!” Bonner huffed. “At least that one was a wild devil with the courage to back up his own nonsense! This guy will promise you the sun, moon, and stars, then cheat you out of your pin money the very next week.”

Strong spotted the crack immediately: Lady Clavering had already offhandedly mentioned selling her diamonds to pay his debts, which meant she was already halfway to relenting. “You can’t let your little boy Frank grow up with a bankrupt, disgraced dad hanging over his head, can you?” he pleaded. “Tie him down to every ridiculous promise you want, I’ll sign as witness, he won’t dare break ‘em this time. He was blubbering to me that he never meant to bet at all, the whole racing ring tricked him, it’s changed him forever!”
“Sure,” Bonner scoffed. “He’ll break that promise faster than you can light a cigar.”

When Strong trundled back to the Blackfriars hotel, he found Sir Francis still hunched over his Curacoa bottle snivelling, and laid down the law: “If you break your word to her this time? I spill every single rotten secret you’ve been hiding, and you’ll be begging for pennies outside Westminster Abbey by next week. And I’m not the only one who knows your dirty laundry. Old Major Pendennis recognised you the second he stumbled drunk through your front door that night.”
Sir Francis shrieked so loud the hotel walls shook. “Damn him! I’ll kill him!”
“Sure you will,” Strong said, rolling his eyes. “You’re too coward to even pay your valet the two years’ back wages you owe him.” The Baronet proceeded to have a full, dramatic meltdown, wailing that he was the most miserable man in all England, he’d run off to a forest in the middle of nowhere and hang himself from a tree, and sobbed so hard his wig nearly slid off.

That Derby-day “bad wind that blows no one any good” turned out to be a very lucky break for Colonel Altamont, one of Strong’s roommates back at their Shepherd’s Inn chambers. He’d taken 30-to-1 odds against the winning race horse, and on settlement day he walked away with a fat stack of 50-pound notes. Immediately every grifter in London who hung around the Harlequin’s Head pub crawled out of the woodwork to beg him to invest in their dumb schemes: a sunken treasure ship in the Gulf of Mexico stuffed with 380 thousand dollars, worthless Welsh mine shares you could buy for “an old song”, a high-risk tobacco smuggling racket, a satirical magazine that only needed 200 quid to make you a thousand a year, and “perfectly safe” private bills that paid 50% interest, guaranteed by a very specific guy they swore was totally not a con artist. Altamont, for once, didn’t fall for a single one of them. He buttoned his cash deep in his coat pocket, bolted home, locked the front door, and let Strong chase all the hucksters away. Strong only asked for a 20-pound cut out of the winnings for himself, which he swore he’d pay back the second he could.

Altamont was shockingly generous when he had spare cash. He bought his servant Grady a brand new fancy livery, handed poor old drunk Costigan a five-pound note that made him blubber for three whole minutes before he ran straight to the nearest pub, grabbed two bright, eye-searing shawls from Regent Street for their landlady Mrs. Bolton and her daughter Fanny, and even sent Miss Amory an anonymous birthday parcel full of the most hideous gifts ever seen: a brass-inlaid writing desk, a set of garish amethyst jewellery, a musical snuff box, two years-old forgotten “Keepsake” gift annuals, and two neon-bright gown fabrics so loud they hurt your eyes, that made Miss Amory laugh so hard she nearly spilled her tea. He also went wild at a cheap Cheapside auction, buying a plated dinner centrepiece, a set of fancy candlesticks, and a bagatelle board for the regular raucous dinner parties they hosted back at the inn.

Back at Grosvenor Place, Lady Clavering had gutted their over-the-top fancy household after paying off all Sir Francis’s debts, a public scandal so humiliating that everyone in high society thought even less of the Baronet than they already had before. She upped and left for Tunbridge Wells in a huff, refusing to even speak to her reprobate husband ever again. Sir Francis was stuck in London, no staff that respected him, his valet stealing his dress shirts and velvet waistcoat to go dancing at Vauxhall, and every one of his old rich friends snickering at him behind his back. The only place he was happy was the free-flowing dinner parties at Shepherd’s Inn, where they called him “Sir Francis” like he was actually important, let him tell his terrible old jokes, and warble his wobbly little French ballads after Strong sang his rowdy drinking songs and Costigan blubbered out his Irish folk ditties. He whined nonstop to Altamont that his wife wouldn’t even trust him with a single silver teaspoon, locked all the family plate up in the bank “for safekeeping”, and all his servants openly laughed at him behind his back.

Altamont, meanwhile, was drinking more and more, bragging nonstop about his very obviously made-up adventures: he’d fought an overseer in the Australian bush with nothing but an axe, he’d met Napoleon on St Helena, he’d snuck out of a New Zealand port at night to escape his local native wife who paddled after the ship in a canoe screaming. Strong warned him repeatedly not to mess with the spineless Baronet, that the coward might snap and do something stupid if pushed too far, but Altamont just cackled and said he could wrap that useless sack of flour around his little finger.

His chance came when Strong ran out one day on an errand for Sir Francis, and left the two of them alone. The Baronet was at his absolute lowest, moaning that no one gave him enough pocket money to even pay the cab waiting outside the chambers, he’d pawned his wife’s fancy drawing room clock, his buhl inkstand, and her gold duck-head paperweight for a measly 15 shillings and sixpence, and he was so embarrassed he wanted to die. He begged Altamont for cash, first 20 quid, then upping it to a 25-pound “pony”, swearing he’d pay it back no questions asked in six months, and would do absolutely anything Altamont wanted to get his hands on the money. Exactly what Altamont had been waiting for.
