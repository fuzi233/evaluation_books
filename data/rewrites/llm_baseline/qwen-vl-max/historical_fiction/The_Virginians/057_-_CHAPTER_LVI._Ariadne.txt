My Lord Castlewood possessed a grand house in Kensington Square, spacious enough to accommodate his noble household and conveniently situated near the palace when His Majesty resided there. Her ladyship entertained guests with evening gatherings and card parties, though Kensington was still considered remote from London a century past—so much so that George Selwyn once confessed he dared not venture there after dark, fearing either highwaymen masked in crape or ladies in rouge at the quadrille table, though which danger was more likely no one could say.

On the morning following Harry Warrington’s unexpected reappearance at White’s Club, the entire family gathered for breakfast—Mr. Will included, who was to take up duties in the royal service later that afternoon.

The ladies arrived first, sipping chocolate; Mr. Will joined them in full court attire; finally, my lord appeared, languid and unshaven, still clad in bedgown and nightcap, his wig yet unfastened. He took up his *Daily Advertiser* as Will burst into the room with news he had brought from the Star and Garter the previous night, where he’d dined with men who’d heard it whispered at White’s and seen it confirmed at Ranelagh.

“Heard what? Seen what?” asked the head of the house, scanning the paper.

“Ask Maria!” said Lady Fanny.

My lord turned to his elder sister, whose face wore an expression of profound sorrow, her pallor stark as linen.

“Only one of Will’s usual polished inventions,” Maria declared.

“No!” swore Will, throwing out several oaths. “It wasn’t invented. Tom Claypool of Norfolk saw both brothers at Ranelagh. Jack Morris came straight from White’s, where he heard it from Harry Warrington himself—his own lips! Curse him—I’m glad of it!” he roared, slapping the table. “What do you think of your Fortunate Youth, your Virginian, whom you praised so highly, turning out to be a second son?”

“The elder brother not dead?” asked my lord.

“No more dead than you are. Never was. I suspect it was some kind of mix-up between the two.”

“Mr. Warrington is incapable of such deceit!” cried Maria.

“I never encouraged the fellow,” said my lady. “I assure you, nor did Fanny—nor we, indeed!”

“Nor we, indeed!” echoed Lady Fanny.

“The man is nothing but a beggar,” continued Will, “and probably hasn’t paid for the clothes on his back. I’m glad of it—hang him, I loathe him!”

“You don’t look upon him kindly, especially since he blacked your eye, Will,” grinned my lord. “So the poor lad has found his brother—and lost his estate.” Here he turned to Maria, whose grief seemed almost comical to the humorist beside her. After a moment’s gaze, he burst into a sharp laugh that flushed her cheeks and soon brought tears to her eyes.

“It’s a shame! It’s a shame!” she sobbed, hiding her face in her handkerchief.

Her stepmother and sister exchanged glances. “We never quite understand your lordship’s humour,” the former remarked gravely.

“I don’t see why you should,” replied my lord coolly. “Maria, my dear, please forgive me if I’ve said—or done—anything to wound you.”

“Done anything? You stripped the boy of everything in his prosperity, and now mock him in ruin!” Maria rose, glaring around the table. “You’re known throughout the world as the man who feeds on his own flesh and blood. Now that you have taken all he had, you make sport of his downfall!”

And with that, she swept from the room, casting defiant looks at each member of the assembly.

“Tell us what happened, Will,” said my lady, “and let my sister’s distress not interrupt us.” And Will recounted, with great detail and immense satisfaction at Harry’s misfortune, the tale now spreading through London: George Warrington’s sudden appearance.

Lord Castlewood felt genuine pity for Harry. He was a good, brave young man, and despite their differences, he had liked him—as people often like one another in this world, not for virtue, but for convenience. True, he had played cards with Harry and profited from market dealings—but why not? If others would pluck the fruit, he might as well eat it. Indeed, if that were all his conscience reproached him for, he needn’t fret much. “Where does Mr. Warrington live?” he asked.

Will shrugged, expressing readiness to enter eternal damnation if he knew or cared.

“He shall be invited here,” said my lord, “and treated with every respect.”

“Including piquet, I suppose?” growled Will.

“Or will you send him to the stables and sell him one of your bargain horses, Will?” asked Lord Castlewood. “You’d have won from Harry fast enough—if you could. But you cheat so clumsily that you got hit with a cudgel. I insist that our cousin Warrington receive every courtesy.”

“And that you won’t be disturbed when you sit down to play, of course, my lord?” Lady Castlewood interjected.

“Madam, I desire fair play—for Mr. Warrington, for myself, and for every member of this amiable family,” retorted my lord sharply.

“Heaven help the poor gentleman if your lordship means to be kind to him,” murmured the stepmother with a curtsey.

No one knew how far the argument might go—until, at that very moment, a phaeton drew up before the house, carrying the two young Virginians.

It was the carriage Harry had bought in his days of wealth. He still drove it; George sat beside him, their negro servants behind. Harry had offered to surrender the reins and the vehicle entirely. “What use have I, a poor devil like me, for horses and carriages, Georgy?” he had humbly said. “Beyond the coat on my back and the purse Aunt gave me, I have nothing. Take the driving seat, brother—it’ll ease my mind.”

George laughed, saying he didn’t know the way, but Harry did. As for the carriage, he claimed only half—just as he had already divided his wardrobe. “But a bargain is a bargain,” he added. “If I share thy coats, thou must divide my breeches’ pocket, Harry—that’s fair dealing.”

Again and again, Harry insisted there had never been such a brother on earth. How the horses clattered over the road! How proud and joyful he was to drive such a brother! They arrived in high spirits, and Gumbo’s thunderous knock upon Lord Castlewood’s door was worthy of the most imposing footman in St. James’s.

Only Lady Castlewood and her daughter, Lady Fanny, were in the drawing-room when the young men were announced. Will avoided facing Harry; my lord was not dressed; Maria remained absent—perhaps until her tears had dried.

When we arrive at friends’ homes today, with our coaches-and-six, John announcing our names, and we enter the drawing-room in our finest hats and smiles, does it ever happen that we interrupt a family quarrel? That we glide in smiling, stepping over the smoldering embers of a domestic fire still hot beneath the ashes? That just between the hall and the parlor, the Joneses have arranged themselves into a carefully composed tableau—the girl arranging flowers, one reclining over a devotional book, mother on the sofa with the butcher’s ledger hidden under a cushion, knitting in hand, a little foot extended prettily—while honest Mr. Jones, instead of muttering, “Curse that Brown, always calling here!” extends a welcoming hand and exclaims, “What, Brown, my boy! Delighted to see you! Hope you’ve come to lunch!” I ask: does it ever happen that we become unwitting victims of domestic artifice, spectators of comedies staged solely for our amusement? Oh, let us be grateful—not only for faces, but for masks! Not only for honest welcome, but for hypocrisy, which hides the unwelcome from our sight! While I speak thus lightly, what right have you, good sir, to know what truly passes within my mind? Perhaps I’m suffering from gout, or my eldest son has just sent me a thousand pounds in college bills, or I’m writhing from the sting of the *Stoke Pogis Sentinel*, or perhaps dinner is dreadfully skimpy, the leftovers of a party I didn’t invite you to—and yet I conceal my pain, wear a cheerful smile, and say, “What! Come to take pot-luck with us, Brown, my boy! Betsy! Put a knife and fork for Mr. Brown. Eat! Welcome! Fall to! It’s my best!” I call this deception beautiful self-denial—hypocrisy, true virtue. Oh, if every man spoke his mind, how unbearable society would be!

As the young gentlemen entered, Lady Castlewood approached them with ease and warmth. “We’ve heard, Harry,” she said, fixing a particular kindness on him, “of this extraordinary turn of events. My lord said at breakfast he would call on you today, Mr. Warrington. And cousin Harry, we mean not to love you any less because you’re poor.”

“We can now show it’s not your acres we love, but you,” added Lady Fanny, following her mother’s lead.

“And I, to whom the acres have fallen?” said Mr. George, bowing with a smile.

“Oh, cousin, we shall love you for being like Harry!” replied Lady Fanny archly.

Ah! Who has traveled the world without admiring the astonishing grace with which fine ladies drop you and then pick you up again? The ladies now addressed themselves almost exclusively to the younger brother. They were civil to George, but with Harry they were tender, familiar, gentle, affectionate—reproachful even. Why hadn’t Harry come to visit them in days?

“Better to have shared tea and piquet with us than with others,” said Lady Castlewood. “If we’d won enough to buy a paper of pins from you, we’d have been content. But young gentlemen never know what’s good for them,” she added.

“Now you have no money to play with, you can come and play with us, cousin!” exclaimed Lady Fanny, lifting a finger. “So your misfortune becomes our fortune!”

George was baffled. This reception was unlike anything he’d expected. All these attentions lavished on the younger brother, who had no guinea left! Perhaps these people weren’t so bad after all? The Blackest of all Blacks is said not to be quite as dark as some describe him.

This warm conversation lasted twenty minutes, when my lord appeared, wig in place, sword at side. He greeted both young men politely—equally. “Had you come to us earlier—though I rejoice to see you now—how regrettable it would have been! A certain evening of piquet might never have occurred. A younger son would have shown more prudence.”

“Yes, indeed,” said Harry.

“Or a kinsman more compassion. But I fear the passion for play runs in our blood. I inherited it from my father, and it made me the poorest peer in England. Those fair ladies before you are not exempt. Poor Brother Will is a martyr to it. What I win one day, I lose the next. The rage for play in England is shocking. All of my poor cousin’s banknotes left me within twenty-four hours of receipt.”

“I’ve played, like other gentlemen,” said Mr. Warrington, “but never to harm myself, nor ever truly cared for the game.”

“When we heard my lord played with Harry,” the ladies chimed in, “we scolded him terribly.”

“But had it not been me, cousin Warrington,” said my lord, “some other would have taken your money. A poor consolation, but such as Harry must accept—glad that friends won his money, who care for him, rather than strangers who cared nothing and fleeced him.”

“Eh! A tooth out is a tooth out, even if your brother pulls it, my lord,” said Mr. George, laughing. “Harry must bear the consequences of his faults, pay his debts like any man.”

“I have never said otherwise,” replied Harry. “It’s not English to be sulky when beaten.”

“Your hand, cousin!” exclaimed my lord delightedly. “You speak like a man!” The ladies smiled at each other.

“My sister in Virginia knows how to raise her sons as gentlemen!” declared Lady Castlewood, enthused.

“I protest you must not grow so amiable now you’re poor, cousin Harry!” said Fanny. “Why, mamma, we didn’t know half your good qualities when you were only the Fortunate Youth and Prince of Virginia! You’re exactly like him, cousin George—but I vow you can’t be as amiable as your brother!”

“I am the Prince of Virginia,” said George gravely, “but I fear I am not the Fortunate Youth.”

Harry began, “By Jove, he is the best—” when the sound of a harpsichord drifted from above.

The lad blushed. The ladies smiled.

“That’s Maria,” said Lady Castlewood. “Let some of us go to her.”

They rose and moved toward the door, Harry following, deeply embarrassed. George stepped forward, but Lord Castlewood detained him. “Nay, if all the ladies follow your brother,” he said, “let me at least enjoy your company. I long to hear the story of your captivity and rescue, cousin George!”

“Oh, we must hear that too!” called one of the ladies, lingering.

“I am greedy,” said my lord, looking sternly at her, “and would prefer to have it all to myself.” He followed the women to the door and closed it with a low bow.

“Your brother has doubtless told you of all that has transpired here, cousin George?” asked his kinsman.

“Yes,” replied George, “including the quarrel with Mr. Will and the engagement to Lady Maria.”

“I may be forgiven for saying he has met with ill fortune here, my lord.”

“Which no one deplores more than I. My brother lives among jockeys and trainers, the wildest of town rakes. There is little sympathy between us. We live together only because we are so poor. This house belonged to our grandmother before she went to America and married Colonel Esmond. Much of the furniture was hers.” George looked about the panelled room with interest.

“Our house has not prospered in twenty years, though we gained a title two decades ago thanks to court influence. But the plague of gambling has ruined us all. I am its miserable victim—too proud to sell myself and my title to a commoner, as many noblemen have done. Pride is my fault, cousin. I remember how I was born!” And he placed a hand on his shirt-frill, turned out his toe, and looked George squarely in the eye.

Young George Warrington naturally believed everyone who spoke to him. Once deceived, however, he became utterly skeptical. He inwardly laughed at my lord’s speech, though kept his face grave and allowed no scorn to show.

“We all have our flaws, my lord. Gambling has been forgiven countless times among gentlemen of our rank. Having forgiven my brother, I cannot presume to judge you. Instead of losing, I wish you had both won!”

“So do I, with all my heart,” sighed my lord. “I take heart from your words, cousin—your wisdom and understanding of the world seem greater than most young men of your age possess. Your brother Harry has the finest heart, though I wonder if his head is strong.”

“Not very strong, indeed. But he wins friends wherever he goes. Despite his recklessness, most people love him.”

“I do—we all do, as if he were our own brother!” exclaimed my lord.

“He often described his welcome here in his letters. My mother keeps them all. Harry’s style is not learned, but his heart is so good that reading him is better than wit.”

“I believe his brother possesses both heart and wit,” said my lord, stubbornly gracious.

“I am as Heaven made me, cousin. Perhaps I’ve endured more experience and sorrow than most young men.”

“This misfortune—no, this good fortune—your sudden reappearance—has not left Harry entirely without resources?” Lord Castlewood asked gently.

“Only what my mother can leave him, or I may spare him at her death. What is the usual portion for a younger brother here, my lord?”

“Ah, a younger brother here…” said my lord, shrugging. “Everyone knows what a younger brother is.”

George pressed on: “We are friends, but we are flesh and blood. I do not pretend to give him more than is customary. Why give him money? To squander it at cards or racing? We have cards and jockeys in Virginia too. My poor Harry has already distinguished himself in his own country before coming to yours. He inherits the family failing for dissipation.”

“Poor fellow, poor fellow—I pity him!”

“Our estate is large, but our income small. We earn little more than what we get from England for our tobacco—and even that is scarce, for it comes back in goods: cloth, leather, groceries, ironmongery, even wine and beer for ourselves and our people. Harry may return and share all this: a horse in the stable, venison on the table, a little ready cash, a coat or two yearly. This continues while my mother lives—unless, as is likely, he quarrels with Madam Esmond. Then, while I live, he’ll have access to the house and all it holds. If I die leaving children, he’ll be less and less welcome. His future, my lord, is bleak unless some miracle occurs. Henceforth, he is doomed to dependence—and I know no worse fate than relying on a woman like our mother. The means he used to command respect at home, he has wasted here. He has thrown his inheritance to the dogs. Poverty and servitude are now his only lot.”

Mr. Warrington delivered this speech with spirit and fluency. His cousin listened respectfully.

“You speak well, Mr. Warrington. Have you considered public life?”

“Of course,” replied George. “Like every man of my station—every man who cares for something beyond dice or stables. I hope to find my place, and my unfortunate brother must accept his. I say this advisedly, having heard of certain engagements he has made—engagements that would bring misery to all if he attempted to fulfill them now.”

“Your logic is compelling,” said my lord. “Shall we go see the ladies? There’s a portrait upstairs said to be by your grandfather. Before you go, cousin, please fix a date when our family may honor you with a visit. Castlewood is always your home when we are there. It’s somewhat like your Virginian Castlewood, from your description. We have beef, mutton, ale, wood—in plenty. But money is sadly lacking among us.”

They ascended to the drawing-room, where only one lady remained—Lady Maria, emerging from a window embrasure where she had been speaking with Harry.

George bowed deeply; Maria curtsied low. “You resemble your brother wonderfully,” she said, offering her hand. “And from what he tells me, cousin George, I believe you are as good as he.”

At the sight of her swollen eyes and tear-streaked face, George felt a pang of remorse. “Poor thing,” he thought. “Harry has been praising my generosity to her, while I’ve been playing the selfish elder brother downstairs. How old she looks! How could he ever have loved such a woman?” How? Because he didn’t see with your eyes, Mr. George. He saw rightly now—with his own. I never know whether to pity or congratulate a man on coming to his senses.

After the introduction, a brief conversation ensued—Lady Maria managing it with effort. But after a few sentences, she broke down, sobbing, motioned Harry away, and fled the room.

Harry rushed forward but stopped—checked by her gesture. My lord explained his sister was prone to nervous fits and had already been unwell that morning.

After this, the young men deemed it unnecessary to linger. Lord Castlewood escorted them downstairs, admired their horses in the phaeton, and waved a friendly farewell.

“And so,” said George to his charioteer, “we’ve been cooing and cuddling by the window, and part good friends, Harry? Is it not so?”

“Oh, she is a good woman!” Harry cried, lashing the horses. “You’ll see when you know her.”

“When you take her to Virginia? A pleasant welcome our mother will give her. She’ll never forgive me for not breaking off the match, nor you for making it.”

“I can’t help it, George! Don’t you keep popping your ugly head so close to my ears, Gumbo! After what’s passed between us, I’m bound by honor to stand by her. If she sees no objection, I must find none. I told her everything. I told her Madam would be stiff at first—but that she loves me and will relent. And about the property—I told her I knew my dearest George so well, I could count on sharing with him.”

“The deuce you did! Let me tell you, my dear, that I’ve been telling my Lord Castlewood a different story. That as elder brother, I intend to claim all my rights—there, don’t flog that near horse so—and that you can expect only poverty and dependence.”

“What! You won’t help me?” Harry gasped, turning pale.
