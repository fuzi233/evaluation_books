The Treasure of Abbot Thomas

Let it be known, for those who care to trace the roots of my tales, that St Bertrand de Comminges and Viborg are places of genuine existence—though their names serve here more as echoes than anchors. In “Oh, Whistle, and I’ll Come to You,” I had Felixstowe in mind, its quiet shores and cold winds shaping the mood of the story. As for the learned fragments that drift through these pages—those references to obscure texts and forgotten manuscripts—almost all are inventions of my own. The book I cite in this tale, "The Treasure of Abbot Thomas," never existed; no such volume was ever penned or preserved.

"Canon Alberic’s Scrap-book" was written in 1894 and published shortly thereafter in the *National Review*; "Lost Hearts" appeared in the *Pall Mall Magazine*. The remaining five stories, most of which were read aloud at Christmas gatherings among friends at King’s College, Cambridge, I remember only faintly—save that "Number 13" was composed in 1899, while "The Treasure of Abbot Thomas" took shape during the summer of 1904.

M. R. James

St Bertrand de Comminges clings to the slopes of the Pyrenees, a ghost of a town barely clinging to life, its stone streets and crumbling walls whispering secrets older than memory. Once a bishopric, now forgotten by time, it stands beneath the shadow of its cathedral—a monument to faith long faded, yet still drawing the curious like moths to a dying flame. In the spring of 1883, an Englishman arrived there—Dennistoun, a scholar from Cambridge with a passion for ancient relics and lost histories. He had come alone, leaving two companions in Toulouse, promising to meet them at dawn. But he meant to spend the day in solitude, photographing every arch, every stain on the stained glass, every whisper of the past that lingered in the church’s hollow bones.

To do so, he needed the sacristan—though the man was more than just a keeper of keys. When Dennistoun summoned him through the innkeeper at the Chapeau Rouge, the old man appeared not as a servant of God, but as a prisoner of something far darker. Small, bent, his skin like parchment stretched over bone, he moved with a constant tension, his eyes darting behind him as if expecting pursuit. His shoulders hunched forward, muscles twitching beneath his thin coat. It wasn’t fear of his wife, nor guilt, nor madness—though all three seemed possible. It was something deeper: the dread of a presence that had no name, no shape, only hunger.

Dennistoun dismissed it quickly. He had work to do. The camera clicked, the notebook filled. Yet the sacristan never left his side—not even when the sun began to fade and the shadows grew long. Every creak, every distant echo of wind through broken panes made the old man flinch. Once, Dennistoun swore he heard laughter—thin, metallic, rising from the tower like a mocking breath. He turned sharply. The sacristan was white as death.

“It is he—that is—it is no one,” he muttered. “The door is locked.”

They stared at each other. No words passed. But the silence between them spoke louder than any scream.

Later, Dennistoun studied a painting behind the altar—one of St Bertrand’s miracles. The image was crude, almost indecipherable, but beneath it ran a Latin inscription: _Qualiter S. Bertrandus liberavit hominem quem diabolus diu volebat strangulare_. How St Bertrand freed a man the Devil long sought to strangle.

He smiled, about to jest—then saw the sacristan on his knees, weeping, hands clenched, face twisted in agony. The man didn’t see the painting—he *felt* it. And Dennistoun knew then: this was no ordinary man. Something haunted him. Not ghosts, not madness—but something worse.

By dusk, the church was cold and still. The Angelus rang out from the tower, Bertrande’s voice echoing into the valleys below, calling the faithful to prayer. Then silence. And the sacristan led Dennistoun away, toward his home—a stone house with boarded windows and a carved shield above the door: Alberic de Mauléon, canon of Comminges from 1680 to 1701.

On the threshold, the sacristan hesitated. “Perhaps monsieur has no time?”

“Plenty of time,” said Dennistoun. “I have nothing until tomorrow.”

Inside, the air was thick with woodsmoke and silence. A crucifix stood tall against the wall, its figure painted in flesh tones, its cross blackened by age. Beneath it, a chest. From it, the sacristan drew a book—wrapped in cloth, embroidered with a red cross. Its size surprised Dennistoun: too large for a missal, too broad for a hymnal. He thought it might be a scrapbook of rare fragments.

Then he opened it.

What lay within was beyond belief. Page after page held illuminated leaves—fragments of ancient manuscripts, cut from books centuries old. A Genesis from the eighth century, its illustrations vivid with divine fire. A Psalter of thirteenth-century England, each page a masterpiece. And twenty sheets of uncial script—Latin, ancient, unreadable to most, but clearly part of a lost treatise. Could it be Papias’ *On the Words of Our Lord*? The thought sent shivers through him.

But it was the final pages that stopped his breath.

One was a plan of the south aisle and cloisters of St Bertrand—drawn with precision, marked with symbols that looked like astrological signs, Hebrew letters in the corners, and a golden cross at the northwest angle. Below it, a note in Latin:

> *Responsa 12mi Dec. 1694. Interrogatum est: Inveniamne? Responsum est: Invenies. Fiamne dives? Fies. Vivamne invidendus? Vives. Moriarne in lecto meo? Ita.*

Answers of December 12, 1694:
Shall I find it? Thou shalt.
Shall I become rich? Thou wilt.
Shall I live envied? Thou wilt.
Shall I die in my bed? Thou wilt.

A treasure hunter’s dream. A fool’s promise.

Then came the drawing.

It was sepia, dated late seventeenth century, depicting a scene that felt both biblical and utterly alien. On the right, King Solomon sat on a throne of twelve steps, canopy above, soldiers flanking him. He leaned forward, sceptre raised, face contorted in horror and command. On the left, four guards surrounded a creature crouched on the floor. One soldier lay dead—neck twisted, eyes bulging. The others stared at their king, paralyzed by terror.

And the thing they guarded?

It was impossible to describe. At first, only a mass of matted black hair. Then, beneath it, a body so thin it was nearly skeletal, muscles like wires under skin the color of old ash. Hands—dusky, hairy, taloned—clawing at the air. Eyes burned yellow, pupils black holes of hatred. It looked like a spider given human form, intelligence just shy of reason, awareness just beyond animal instinct. It was drawn from life.

Dennistoun recoiled. The sacristan covered his eyes. His daughter, standing beside the crucifix, muttered her beads with feverish speed.

“Is this book for sale?” he asked.

After hesitation, the answer came: “If monsieur pleases.”

Two hundred and fifty francs.

Dennistoun protested. The book was priceless. But the sacristan would not budge. The money changed hands. A glass of wine was shared. And then—something shifted.

The sacristan straightened. His shoulders relaxed. For the first time, he laughed—weakly, but truly.

“I shall accompany monsieur?” he offered.

“No, thank you,” said Dennistoun. “I know the way.”

But the offer was repeated. Refused. Then, as Dennistoun stepped into the passage, the daughter approached.

“A silver crucifix… may monsieur accept it?”

He declined. She insisted. “Nothing. Nothing at all. Please.”

Her tone was sincere. He accepted. The chain went around his neck.

Outside, the moon rose. The path was clear. He walked fast, clutching the book.

Back at the inn, dinner was done. He locked himself in his room, eager to examine the treasures. The landlady watched him closely. He heard her whispering in the hall—words about Pierre and Bertrand sleeping in the house.

Then, a creeping unease. Not fear. Not yet. But a feeling—someone was behind him.

He turned. Nothing.

He sat. The book lay open. He lifted the crucifix to clean it.

And saw it.

On the table, near his elbow, a hand.

Pale, dark-skinned, fingers ending in curved, grey claws. Hairy, sinewy, impossibly strong. Like the hand in the drawing.

He screamed.

Not a cry of man—but of animal in agony.

He flew from his chair. Behind him, the shape rose—black, tattered robes, coarse hair covering a skull-like face. Jaw shallow, teeth exposed, no nose. Eyes burning yellow, full of hate, of thirst, of *purpose*. Intelligence—yes—but not human. Lower than man, higher than beast.

It reached for him.

He grasped the crucifix. Screamed again.

Pierre and Bertrand burst in. Found him collapsed. Saw nothing. Felt something pass between them—cold, heavy, unseen.

They stayed with him through the night.

By morning, his friends arrived. He told them everything.

The sacristan came to the inn at dawn, listened to the story, and whispered:
“It is he—it is he! I have seen him myself.”
Then: “Deux fois je l’ai vu; mille fois je l’ai senti.”
Twice I have seen him. A thousand times I have felt him.

He refused to speak further. Said only: “I shall soon sleep. My rest will be sweet. Why trouble me?”

He died that summer.

His daughter married. Settled in St Papoul. Never understood what her father had endured.

We do not know what happened to Canon Alberic de Mauléon. Or what dwelled in the back of that book.

But written beneath the drawing, in a trembling hand, were these lines:

> *He who seeks the hidden shall find it.
> He who finds it shall possess it.
> He who possesses it shall be possessed.*

And beneath that, in a different ink, a single sentence:

> *It is not dead. It is waiting.*

It was, as far as I can ascertain, in September of 1811 that a post-chaise pulled up before the grand door of Aswarby Hall, deep in Lincolnshire. The only passenger—a small boy—leapt out the moment it stopped, his eyes wide with curiosity as he waited for the bell to summon the house. Before him stood a tall, square red-brick mansion built during Queen Anne’s reign, its façade adorned with a classical stone porch added in 1790. The windows were long and narrow, their small panes glowing like embers under the fading light. A pediment crowned the front, pierced by a round window, while wings on either side—connected by glass-enclosed galleries supported by columns—held the stables and servants’ quarters. Each wing rose to an ornamental cupola topped with a gilded weather-vane. The evening sun lit the whole structure, turning the glass into flickering fires.

Beyond the hall stretched a flat park dotted with oaks and lined with firs, silhouetted against the sky. From the church tower, half-hidden among trees at the edge of the grounds, the golden rooster caught the light as the clock struck six. Its chime drifted gently through the air, soft and mournful.

For the boy standing in the porch, it was a scene both beautiful and strange—peaceful yet tinged with autumnal sorrow. He had come from Warwickshire, where he’d been left orphaned six months earlier. Now, thanks to the unexpected kindness of his elderly cousin, Mr. Abney, he was to live at Aswarby Hall.

Few expected this. Mr. Abney was known as a solitary man, stern and reserved, deeply absorbed in ancient lore. He was said to know more about pagan beliefs than even the Cambridge professor of Greek. His library held every available text on the Mysteries, Orphic poetry, Mithraism, and Neoplatonism. In the marble hall stood a magnificent statue of Mithras slaying a bull, shipped from the Levant at great cost. He had written articles for *The Gentleman’s Magazine* and *The Critical Museum*, detailing Roman superstitions of the late Empire. To most, he was a man lost in books, so isolated that no one believed he would ever hear of his orphaned cousin—let alone invite him into his home.

Yet when the door opened, Mr. Abney emerged from his study, hands clasped together with unmistakable warmth. “How are you, my boy?” he asked, beaming. “How old are you? And are you not too tired to eat supper?”

“No, sir,” replied Stephen Elliott. “I’m quite well.”

“That’s good,” said Abney. “And how old are you?”

He asked twice—strangely, almost urgently.

“Twelve next birthday, sir.”

“Ah! Eleventh of September? That’s very convenient. Nearly a year from now. I like—ha ha—to record such things in my book. Are you certain?”

“Quite sure, sir.”

“Well then,” he said, turning to the butler, Mr. Parkes, “take him to Mrs. Bunch’s room and let him have tea—supper—whatever it is.”

Parkes nodded and led the boy downstairs.

Mrs. Bunch was the heart of the household—kind, practical, and full of stories. Within minutes, Stephen felt at home. She had lived near Aswarby since she was five, and had served the family for twenty years. If anyone knew the secrets of the house and the land, it was her.

Stephen, curious and bold, soon began peppering her with questions: Who built the temple at the end of the laurel walk? Who was the old man in the portrait on the staircase, seated at a table with a skull beneath his hand?

She answered most with ease. But some questions drew silence or uneasy glances.

One November evening, sitting by the fire in the housekeeper’s room, Stephen suddenly asked, “Is Mr. Abney a good man? Will he go to heaven?”

Mrs. Bunch looked startled. “Good? Bless the child! He’s as kind-hearted as any soul I’ve ever met. Didn’t I tell you about the little girl he brought in seven years ago? And the boy two years after I came here?”

“No!” Stephen insisted. “Tell me now!”

“Well,” she sighed, “the girl—I don’t remember much. Master found her wandering one day and brought her in. Mrs. Ellis, the former housekeeper, was told to care for her. She had no family—she told me herself. She stayed three weeks, quiet as a mouse, always helping around the house. Then one morning, she vanished. Not a trace. No footprints, nothing. We searched the ponds, dragged them all. But there was singing around the house that night—lasting nearly an hour—and Parkes swore he heard voices calling in the woods. I believe gypsies took her. Such a gentle child… I still miss her.”

“And the boy?” Stephen asked.

“Oh, that poor boy,” she whispered. “He was foreign—called himself Giovanni. Came playing a hurdy-gurdy one winter day. Master brought him inside right away, asked him everything—where he came from, his age, his family—so kindly. But just like the girl, he disappeared. One morning, gone. Left his hurdy-gurdy behind. Never taken it. It sits on the shelf to this day.”

That night, Stephen dreamed.

He stood at the top of the house, looking down the passage to a locked bathroom—its upper half glazed, its muslin curtains long gone. Through the glass, he saw the lead-lined tub fixed to the wall, facing the window. The moonlight poured in. And in the tub lay a figure—thin, pale, wrapped in a shroud, its lips curled in a faint, terrible smile. Its hands pressed tight over its chest. Then, a low moan escaped its mouth. The arms stirred.

Stephen recoiled, waking with a gasp—standing on the cold floor, the moonlight flooding the hallway.

With trembling courage, he approached the bathroom door. Nothing. Only darkness. He returned to bed, shaken.

Next morning, Mrs. Bunch listened in horror to his tale. She immediately replaced the muslin curtain.

At breakfast, Stephen told Mr. Abney. The man listened intently, made notes in what he called “my book,” and said nothing more.

As spring approached, Abney reminded Stephen often of the equinox—how the ancients believed it a time of spiritual danger. “You must be careful,” he warned. “Shut your bedroom window at night. Censorinus wrote much on this.”

Then came two unsettling events.

First, Stephen woke from a restless night with no memory of dreaming. The next evening, Mrs. Bunch mended his nightgown.

“Gracious me!” she exclaimed. “Look at this! How do you tear your clothes so?”

There were long, parallel cuts along the left side of the chest—deep enough to split the linen, but not quite through. They matched the scratches on the outside of his bedroom door—too high for animals, too precise for rats.

“I didn’t do it,” Stephen said. “And those marks on the door—they’re exactly like these.”

Mrs. Bunch stared, then grabbed a candle and rushed upstairs. She returned shortly.

“They’re real,” she whispered. “Too high for cats or dogs. Look like Chinese fingernails—my uncle in the tea trade used to say they could scratch like that. Don’t tell Master. Just lock your door when you go to bed.”

“I always do,” Stephen said. “After prayers.”

“That’s right,” she murmured. “Always pray. Then no harm can touch you.”

On Friday, March 23, 1812, the wind howled through the gardens. Stephen stood at the fence, watching the park. He felt as though invisible figures swept past on the breeze—drifting, helpless, trying to grasp something solid, to return to life.

After lunch, Abney said, “Stephen, come to my study tonight—at eleven. I have something important to show you. Do not speak of it to anyone. Go to bed as usual.”

Stephen’s heart leapt. Eleven o’clock! He could stay up late!

That evening, he passed the library. The brazier had been moved before the fire. On the table sat a silver-gilt cup filled with red wine, papers beside it. Abney sprinkled incense from a silver box, unaware of the boy.

By ten, Stephen stood at his open window. The night was still. But from across the mere came eerie cries—like lost souls in torment. Owls? Waterfowl? Not quite. They grew louder, closer. Soon they seemed to drift through the shrubbery.

Then silence.

But then—he saw them.

Two figures on the gravel terrace: a boy and a girl, standing side by side, staring up at the windows.

The girl was pale, smiling faintly, hands clasped over her heart. Her form reminded Stephen of the dream—of the figure in the bath.

But the boy…

He was thin, ragged, black-haired. He raised his arms, claw-like hands reaching toward the sky. The moonlight passed through his fingers—long, sharp nails, translucent. And on his chest—on the left side—was a gaping wound, dark and hollow.

A cry—not heard, but felt—exploded in Stephen’s mind. The same sound from the woods.

Then they vanished.

Frightened, Stephen grabbed his candle and ran to the study.

The door was unlocked. He knocked. No answer.

Then—voices. A choked scream. A struggle.

He pushed open the door.

Mr. Abney sat in his chair, head thrown back, face twisted in agony. His left side was torn open—heart exposed. No blood on his hands. A knife lay on the table—clean.

The window was open.

The coroner declared it a wild animal attack.

But Stephen read the papers left on the table—written in Abney’s own hand:

> “It was a belief held by the ancients—that by absorbing the personalities of others, a man may attain mastery over the unseen forces of nature. Simon Magus flew, became invisible, assumed forms—by the soul of a murdered boy. Hermes Trismegistus wrote that three hearts—taken from those under twenty-one—could grant immense power.
>
> I have spent twenty years testing this. First: Phoebe Stanley, a gipsy girl, removed March 24, 1792. Second: Giovanni Paoli, an Italian wanderer, March 23, 1805. The final victim—my cousin Stephen Elliott—must be claimed on March 24, 1812.
>
> The method: remove the heart from the living, burn it, mix ashes with red wine. Conceal the remains—bathroom or cellar. Ghosts may haunt you, but the philosopher need fear them not. I anticipate freedom—immortality—even beyond death itself.”

Stephen understood.

The dreams. The scratches. The singing. The children.

They were not ghosts.

They were echoes—spirits of the stolen, bound to the place by the ritual. The girl who vanished. The boy who fled with his hurdy-gurdy.

And now, the third.

But Abney had failed.

The spirits had returned—not to punish, but to reclaim.

Stephen closed the papers.

He locked his door.

And prayed.

Not for safety.

But for mercy.

Some time ago, I believe I told you of an adventure that befell a friend of mine—Mr. Dennistoun—during his search for rare artworks to enrich the museum at Cambridge. He did not widely publish his experiences upon returning to England, but they inevitably reached the ears of many, including a gentleman who then oversaw an art collection at another university. Naturally, such a tale would stir the imagination of someone whose work mirrored Dennistoun’s own, especially one eager to dismiss any suggestion that he might ever face such a disturbing occurrence. It was somewhat comforting to him to reflect that his duties did not involve acquiring ancient manuscripts—those were the Shelburnian Library’s concern. His focus lay in expanding the already remarkable collection of English topographical drawings and engravings housed in his institution. Yet, as it turned out, even the most modest and familiar branch of art could harbor secrets—and one such secret was thrust upon Mr. Williams.

Anyone with even a passing interest in collecting topographical prints knows of one London dealer whose catalogues are indispensable: Mr. J. W. Britnell. He regularly issued excellent lists of engravings, plans, and old sketches of English and Welsh mansions, churches, and towns. These catalogues were, naturally, the foundation of Mr. Williams’s research. But since his museum already held a vast number of such works, he was more of a selective than a prolific buyer—content to fill gaps rather than seek rarities.

In February of the previous year, a catalogue from Britnell arrived on Mr. Williams’s desk, accompanied by a typewritten note:

> *We beg to call your attention to No. 978 in our accompanying catalogue, which we shall be glad to send on approval.*
>
> *Yours faithfully,*

For Mr. Williams, locating No. 978 was a matter of seconds. The entry read:

> **978.—_Unknown._** Interesting mezzotint: View of a manor-house, early part of the century. 15 by 10 inches; black frame. £2 2s.

It wasn’t particularly striking, and the price seemed excessive. Still, since Britnell—known for his expertise and discernment—had singled it out, Williams sent a postcard requesting the item on approval, along with several other engravings from the same catalogue. Then he returned to his daily tasks, untroubled by anticipation.

As usual, the parcel arrived later than expected—delivered by the afternoon post on a Saturday after Williams had left for the day. The attendant brought it to his rooms in college so he wouldn’t have to wait until Monday to examine it. That evening, when he returned for tea with a friend, he found it waiting.

The only piece of interest was the large, black-framed mezzotint described in the catalogue. Though I cannot convey its full appearance as clearly as it remains in my mind, I can say this: it bore a strong resemblance to countless similar prints still hanging in country inns or dusty hallways of old estates. It was a dull mezzotint—an amateurish effort, perhaps the worst kind of engraving. It depicted a modest manor house from the last century, three rows of plain sash windows framed by rusticated stonework, a parapet adorned with spherical finials, and a small central portico. Trees flanked either side, and a wide lawn stretched before it. In the narrow margin, the inscription read: _A. W. F. sculpsit_. No further title or date. The whole thing looked like the work of a dilettante. Why Britnell had priced it at two guineas was beyond Williams’s comprehension. He turned it over with disdain. On the back, a paper label had been torn in half. Only fragments remained: _ngley Hall_ on the first line, _ssex_ on the second.

He decided to identify the place—using a gazetteer, if need be—and then return it with a polite critique of Britnell’s judgment.

He lit the candles, made tea, and entertained his guest—a fellow golfer, Professor Binks—with whom he’d spent the afternoon on the links. Their conversation, predictably, revolved around strokes missed and luck denied.

It was then that Binks picked up the framed print and asked:

“What’s this place?”

“Just what I’m trying to find out,” said Williams, fetching a gazetteer. “Look at the back. Somethingley Hall—either in Sussex or Essex. Half the name’s gone. You don’t happen to know it?”

“It’s from Britnell, I suppose?” said Binks. “Is it for the museum?”

“Well, I’d buy it for five shillings,” replied Williams, “but he wants two guineas. I can’t imagine why. It’s a wretched engraving—no figures to give it life.”

“I don’t think it’s so bad,” said Binks. “The moonlight seems well done. And I thought there was a figure—just barely visible—on the edge in front.”

“Let’s see,” said Williams. “Well, yes—the light is cleverly handled. Where’s your figure? Oh! Just the head—right at the very edge.”

And indeed there was—barely more than a dark smudge—what appeared to be the head of a person, wrapped tightly, facing away, looking toward the house.

Williams hadn’t noticed it before.

“Still,” he said, “even if it’s better than I thought, I can’t spend museum money on a picture of a place I don’t know.”

Binks left soon after, and Williams spent the next hour or so fruitlessly searching the gazetteer. “If only the vowel before the _ng_ had survived,” he mused, “it would be easy. As it is, it could be Guestingley, Langley, anything. And this cursed book has no index of endings.”

Dinner at the college was at seven. He met colleagues who’d also played golf, and their talk was filled with sport-specific banter—nothing worth repeating here.

After dinner, some lingered in the common room. Later, a few retired to Williams’s rooms. Whist was played, tobacco smoked. During a lull, Williams picked up the mezzotint without looking at it and handed it to a mild art enthusiast, explaining where it came from.

The man took it casually, glanced at it, then spoke with genuine interest:

“It’s actually quite good, Williams. Has a real romantic feel. The lighting is superb, and though the figure is a bit grotesque, it’s strangely compelling.”

“Yes, isn’t it?” said Williams, busy serving whisky-and-soda. He couldn’t cross the room to see.

By now, it was late. Visitors began to leave. After they departed, Williams wrote letters, tidied papers. Finally, past midnight, he prepared for bed. He extinguished the lamp, lit his bedroom candle. The picture lay face-up on the table—left there by the last viewer. As he dimmed the light, something caught his eye.

He nearly dropped the candle.

What he saw defied reason—but was undeniable. Where there had been nothing at five o’clock that afternoon, now stood a figure in the center of the lawn. It was crawling on all fours, drawn toward the house, wrapped in a strange black garment bearing a white cross on the back.

I cannot say what one should do in such a situation. All I know is what Mr. Williams did. He grabbed the picture by one corner and carried it across the passage to a second set of rooms he kept. There, he locked it in a drawer, bolted both doors, and went to bed—after writing a detailed account of the change he had witnessed.

Sleep came slowly, but he took comfort in knowing he wasn’t alone in his testimony. The man who had seen it earlier must have perceived something similar—or else he might have questioned his own sanity. That possibility ruled out, two tasks awaited: verify the image carefully with a witness, and discover the identity of the house. He would invite his neighbor, Nisbet, to breakfast, and then spend the morning poring over guidebooks.

Nisbet arrived at 9:30. Williams was still dressing. At breakfast, no mention was made of the mezzotint—except that Williams had a picture he wanted Nisbet’s opinion on. Their conversation ranged widely, from cricket to tennis, as is typical among fellows of Canterbury College. Yet Williams was restless, his thoughts fixed on the image now lying face-down in the drawer across the hall.

Finally, the morning pipe was lit. The moment arrived. With trembling hands, Williams crossed to the drawer, unlocked it, retrieved the picture—still face-down—and hurried back, placing it into Nisbet’s hands.

“Now,” he said, “I want you to tell me exactly what you see. Describe it as thoroughly as you can. I’ll explain why afterward.”

“Well,” said Nisbet, “I have here a view of a country house—English, I assume—by moonlight.”

“Moonlight? You’re sure?”

“Certainly. The moon appears to be waning, and there are clouds drifting overhead.”

“All right. Go on. I swear,” Williams added under his breath, “there was no moon when I first saw it.”

“There’s not much more,” continued Nisbet. “The house has three rows of windows—five in each row, except the bottom, where there’s a porch instead of the middle window—and—”

“But what about figures?” Williams interrupted.

“There aren’t any,” said Nisbet. “But—”

“What? No figure on the grass?”

“Not a trace.”

“You’ll swear to that?”

“Absolutely. But there’s one thing.”

“What?”

“One of the ground-floor windows—left of the door—is open.”

“Really? My goodness—he must have gotten in!” Williams exclaimed, snatching the picture from Nisbet and confirming it himself.

It was true. No figure. But the window was open.

After a moment of stunned silence, Williams scribbled notes. He produced two documents: one, Nisbet’s description; the other, his own written the night before. He asked Nisbet to sign the first and read the second.

“What could this mean?” Nisbet asked.

“Exactly,” said Williams. “But I must do three things. First, I must ask Garwood—my visitor last night—what he saw. Then, we must photograph it. And finally, we must identify the house.”

“I can take the photo myself,” said Nisbet. “But… it feels like we’re witnessing a tragedy unfold. The question is—has it already happened, or is it about to?”

“I agree,” said Williams. “I think he’s inside now. And if I’m not mistaken, there’ll be hell to pay upstairs.”

“I’ll go speak to Green,” Williams decided. “He’s the senior Fellow—been Bursar for years. We own land in Essex and Sussex. He’s likely to recognize it.”

“Possible,” said Nisbet. “But let me photograph it first. Though… I suspect Green isn’t up today. He wasn’t in Hall last night. I think he said he was going down to Brighton.”

“That’s right,” said Williams. “He’s gone to Brighton. Well, if you photograph it now, I’ll go to Garwood and get his statement. You keep watch while I’m gone. Honestly, I’m starting to think two guineas isn’t too much for this.”

Soon, Williams returned with Mr. Garwood. His account confirmed that the figure had been near the edge, not far across the lawn. He remembered a white mark on the back—possibly a cross, but uncertain. A formal statement was drawn up and signed. Nisbet began photographing the print.

“What now?” he asked. “Are you going to sit and watch it all day?”

“No,” said Williams. “I think we’re meant to see the whole thing. Between last night and now, plenty could’ve happened—but the creature only got inside. It could’ve finished its business and left. But the open window suggests it’s still in there. So I feel safe leaving it. Besides, I suspect it won’t change during daylight. Let’s go for a walk this afternoon, return for tea when it gets dark. I’ll leave it on the table, and lock the door. My servant can enter, but no one else.”

They agreed. They would avoid speaking of it to others—lest the Phasmatological Society descend upon them.

We may pause until five o’clock.

At or near that hour, the three men ascended Williams’s staircase. They were startled to find the door unlatched—until they recalled the servants came earlier on Sundays. But a greater shock awaited them.

The picture leaned against a stack of books on the table, just as left. And beside it sat Williams’s servant, Robert Filcher—usually a model of decorum—staring at the engraving with palpable horror.

He jumped when they entered, rose stiffly, and apologized:

“I beg your pardon, sir, for taking such liberty as to sit.”

“Not at all, Robert,” Williams interjected. “I meant to ask you what you thought of that picture.”

“Well, sir,” said Filcher, “I don’t claim to judge art, but I wouldn’t hang it where my little girl could see it.”

“Why not?”

“She once saw a Door Bible with pictures half as bad as that,” he said, “and we had to tend to her for three nights. If she saw this skeleton—whatever it is—carrying off the child, she’d be taken ill. Children are so sensitive. And I’d say… it doesn’t seem right to leave such a picture lying about. Not where anyone easily startled might come upon it. Shall I fetch anything this evening, sir? Thank you, sir.”

With that, the faithful man left. The three men gathered round the engraving.

The house stood beneath the waning moon, clouds drifting above. The window was closed. And the figure—now upright—was walking swiftly toward the front of the image, long strides, arms tightly wrapped around an indistinct form: a child, alive or dead, impossible to say. The drapery hung low, obscuring the face—only a pale, dome-shaped forehead and straggling hair visible. The legs were unnaturally thin.

From five to seven, they watched in silence. The image did not change. They decided to leave it, returning after dinner to await further developments.

When they returned, the figure was gone. The house stood quiet under the moonlight.

There was nothing to do but consult guidebooks. At 11:30 p.m., Williams found the answer in Murray’s _Guide to Essex_:

> *16½ miles, Anningley. The church, originally Norman, was classicized in the last century. It contains the tomb of the Francis family, whose mansion, Anningley Hall—a solid Queen Anne house—stands beyond the churchyard in an 80-acre park. The family is extinct. The last heir vanished mysteriously in infancy in 1802. The father, Mr. Arthur Francis, was known locally as a talented amateur mezzotint engraver. After his son’s disappearance, he lived in seclusion at the Hall, and was found dead in his studio on the third anniversary of the tragedy—having just completed an engraving of the house. Impressions of this engraving are extremely rare.*

This was it. When Mr. Green returned, he confirmed the identification.

“Any explanation for the figure, Green?” Williams asked.

“I don’t know, Williams. But what used to be said—when I knew the area before coming up here—was this: Old Francis hated poachers. He’d expel anyone he suspected, until only one remained—a man from an ancient family, possibly the last Lords of the Manor. Squires could do such things then.”

“Like the man in _Tess of the Durbervilles_?” Williams asked.

Green nodded grimly.

“And that man—well, he didn’t take kindly to being driven off. But he never raised a hand. Until the night the baby disappeared.”

They sat in silence.

The picture remained on the table, untouched.

And no one dared look at it again.

Anyone who has wandered through the quiet, mist-laden counties of eastern England knows the kind of manor houses that dot the landscape—modest, often damp, built in the Italian style, with stuccoed façades and sprawling parks of eighty to a hundred acres. These are the sort I have always found compelling: grey oak palings, ancient trees, reed-fringed ponds, distant woodlands stretching into the haze. I love the pillared portico—sometimes clumsily tacked onto a Queen Anne brick house, now masked in plaster to suit the Grecian fashion of the late eighteenth century. Inside, the hall rises to the roof, ideally with a gallery and a small organ. The library holds treasures: a thirteenth-century Psalter beside a Shakespeare quarto. And then there are the paintings—the ghosts of past lives, preserved on canvas.

But most of all, I like imagining what life was like when such houses were first built—when landowners ruled with ease and pride, before money became scarce but taste more refined. I dream of owning one myself, with enough wealth to keep it standing and guests entertained in modest comfort.

Yet this is not a tale of dreams. It is about Castringham Hall in Suffolk—a place that once stood much as I’ve described it. Though time has altered its form, the essentials remain: the Italian portico, the white square block of the house, older within than without; the park edged with woods, and the still pond. One feature, however, is gone. Once, from the park, you could see a great old ash-tree growing just yards from the wall, its branches brushing the very side of the building. It had stood there since Castringham ceased to be a fortress, since the moat filled in and the Elizabethan house rose. By 1690, it had reached full maturity.

That year, the region was gripped by witch trials. We may never truly understand the roots of that fear—whether witches believed themselves powerful, whether they meant harm, or whether confessions were wrung out by cruelty. But this story makes me hesitate. I cannot dismiss it as mere fiction. You must judge for yourself.

Castringham contributed its own victim: Mrs. Mothersole. She was no common village crone—she was better off, more respected. Several farmers tried to save her, testifying to her character, pleading with the jury. But her fate was sealed by Sir Matthew Fell, then proprietor of Castringham Hall and Deputy-Sheriff.

He claimed to have seen her three times at moonrise, climbing into the ash-tree near his house. Each night, he said, she wore only her shift, cutting twigs with a curved knife, muttering to herself. When he tried to capture her, she fled—only a hare ever crossed his path. On the third night, he chased her to her home. He pounded on her door for fifteen minutes before she emerged—cross, sleepy, as if pulled from bed. He had no explanation for his visit.

Her conviction rested largely on his testimony, though others added their accounts. Mrs. Mothersole was hanged at Bury St Edmunds a week later, alongside five others. Sir Matthew attended the execution.

It was a cold, drizzly March morning. The cart climbed the rough grass hill outside Northgate where the gallows stood. The other victims were broken, silent. But Mrs. Mothersole—alive with fury—was something else entirely. A contemporary report called her “poysonous Rage” so terrifying that even the hangman shuddered. She did not resist, but her eyes—fixed on those who seized her—were said to burn with venom. One officer confessed he carried the memory of her gaze in his mind for six months.

And yet, all she spoke was a single phrase—repeated under her breath: *“There will be guests at the Hall.”*

Sir Matthew was unsettled by her presence. After the assize, he rode home with the parish vicar, Mr. Crome, and discussed the trial. He admitted he hadn’t wanted to testify, but insisted he saw what he claimed—nothing else made sense. He regretted the deed, but felt duty compelled him. The vicar agreed—he was a reasonable man, after all.

A few weeks later, in May, the two men met again in the park. Lady Fell was away visiting her ill mother; Sir Matthew was alone. Mr. Crome stayed for supper.

That evening, Sir Matthew was quiet. Conversation drifted between family matters and parish business. At nine-thirty, they walked along the gravel path behind the house. As they passed the ash-tree, Sir Matthew stopped abruptly.

“What is that running up and down the trunk?” he asked. “Not a squirrel? They’re all in their nests by now.”

Mr. Crome looked. In the moonlight, he saw a shadowy shape, moving swiftly. He couldn’t make out its color, but the outline—sharp, fleeting—stayed burned in his mind. He would swear, though it seemed absurd, that it had more than four legs.

They parted soon after. They didn’t meet again for decades.

The next morning, Sir Matthew did not rise. Not at six, nor seven, nor eight. The servants knocked, listened, battered the door. Eventually, it opened from the outside. They found him dead—blackened, swollen. No signs of violence, but the window was open.

One servant ran for the parson; another rode to alert the coroner. Mr. Crome arrived quickly, guided to the chamber. Among his papers, he left notes describing the scene—and the dread that followed.

“There was no sign of forced entry,” he wrote. “The casement stood open, as my friend always kept it. His silver cup of small ale sat untouched. The physician from Bury, Mr. Hodgkins, examined it—found no poison. Yet the corpse was twisted, contorted, as if death came in agony. And worse—two women tasked with washing the body screamed in pain upon touching his chest. Their hands swelled violently, arms inflamed, though no mark appeared on the skin.”

Crome summoned the doctor. Together, they examined the skin under a crystal lens. They found only two tiny punctures—small, almost invisible. They recalled the tales of Italian poisoners, the infamous ring of Pope Borgia. Perhaps this was a similar art.

Then, on the bedside table, lay a small Bible—Sir Matthew’s nightly reading. Moved, Crome picked it up. In moments of despair, we clutch at strange hopes. He thought of the *Sortes Biblicæ*—the practice of opening Scripture at random to find divine guidance. He remembered how King Charles and Lord Falkland had used it in crisis.

So he tried it. Three times.

First: Luke xiii. 7 — _Cut it down._

Second: Isaiah xiii. 20 — _It shall never be inhabited._

Third: Job xxxix. 30 — _Her young ones also suck up blood._

He recorded it all. Sir Matthew was buried. The vicar preached a sermon titled *“The Unsearchable Way; or, England’s Danger and the Malicious Dealings of Antichrist”*, believing the squire had fallen victim to a revived Popish Plot.

His son, Sir Matthew the second, inherited the estate. He avoided the West Chamber—no one slept there again. The room remained untouched for decades. He died in 1735, leaving behind only a curious pattern: livestock across the farm began dying in unnatural ways. Cattle, sheep, poultry—all succumbed to a sickness with no clear cause. Farmers called it the “Castringham sickness.”

Eventually, he solved it: he confined animals indoors at night. Only those outdoors fell ill. The affliction shifted to wild birds and game. But no one ever saw the source. No one ever understood.

Sir Richard, the third baronet, took over in 1735. He was a man of grand designs. He rebuilt Castringham Hall—stuccoing over the red brick, adding Roman marbles, constructing a replica of the Sibyl’s temple on the far side of the pond. The house became colder, more imposing, less welcoming.

In 1754, he woke exhausted. The night had been stormy—chimney smoke, rattling windows, bitter cold. Worse, guests were arriving—high-ranking men and ladies expected sport. But his own health was failing. He could not sleep in that room again.

At breakfast, he began searching for a new chamber. Nothing suited. Until the housekeeper said, “There’s only one room like that—Sir Matthew’s West Chamber.”

“Then put me there tonight,” he declared. “Open the door.”

She hesitated. “No one has slept there in forty years. The air hasn’t changed since he died.”

“Open it,” he commanded.

The door creaked. The room smelled of damp earth and forgotten things. Sir Richard flung open the shutters and casement. The wind rushed in.

“Air it all day,” he ordered. “Move my things in this afternoon. Put the Bishop in my old room.”

As he turned, a voice spoke: “Might I have a moment?”

A man in black stood in the doorway. “I am William Crome. My grandfather was vicar here in your grandfather’s time.”

Sir Richard smiled. “The name Crome opens doors at Castringham. What can I do for you?”

“I’m riding from Norwich to Bury. I bring papers—my grandfather’s writings. You may find them of interest.”

They went to the study. Among the documents was the vicar’s account of Sir Matthew’s death—and the *Sortes Biblicæ*. Sir Richard read them aloud, amused.

“Well,” he said, “_Cut it down_. If that means the tree, I’ll do it tomorrow. Such a nest of catarrhs and agues!”

He glanced at the bookshelf. “I wonder… is the old prophet still there?”

He pulled out a small Bible. On the flyleaf: *“To Matthew Fell, from his Loving Godmother, Anne Aldous, 2 September, 1659.”*

“Let’s test it,” he said. “I’ll wager we get names from the Chronicles.”

He opened it at random. “‘Thou shalt seek me in the morning, and I shall not be.’” He laughed. “Your grandfather would have made a fine omen of that. No prophets for me! They’re all in a tale.”

He offered wine. They parted amicably.

That afternoon, guests arrived—the Bishop of Kilmore, Lady Mary Hervey, Sir William Kentfield. Dinner, wine, cards, supper. Then to bed.

Next morning, Sir Richard refused to hunt. He walked with the bishop along the terrace.

“You could never get one of my Irish flock to sleep in that room,” the bishop said, pointing to the West Chamber.

“Why not?”

“Our people believe sleeping near an ash-tree brings bad luck. Yours grows just yards from your window.”

“Perhaps it’s already touched me,” Sir Richard replied. “I didn’t sleep from midnight to four.”

“The tree comes down tomorrow. I won’t hear from it again.”

“Good,” said the bishop. “Though I doubt the noise came from the branches. See—none touch your window. Even in a gale, they miss the glass by a foot.”

Sir Richard frowned. “Then what was it?”

The bishop smiled faintly. “Only the wind, perhaps. Or something else.”

But the wind had been still. And the tree—its leaves whispering secrets—had not stirred.

Among the towns of Jutland, Viborg holds a place of quiet dignity. It is the seat of a bishopric, crowned by a cathedral—handsome, though largely rebuilt—and adorned with a garden that breathes serenity and a lake so still it seems to hold its breath. Storks nest in the rooftops, as they have for centuries, and near the town lies Hald, one of Denmark’s most picturesque villages. Close by stands Finderup, where Marsk Stig murdered King Erik Glipping on St Cecilia’s Day in 1286. When the king’s tomb was opened in the seventeenth century, fifty-six blows from square-headed iron maces were found carved into his skull. But I am not writing a guidebook.

Viborg has good hotels—Preisler’s and the Phœnix are excellent—but my cousin, whose tale I now recount, chose the Golden Lion on his first visit. He has not returned since. The reason may lie in what follows.

The Golden Lion is one of the few buildings in Viborg untouched by the great fire of 1726, which reduced much of the city—including the cathedral, the Sognekirke, and the Raadhuus—to ashes. The hotel is a massive red-brick structure, its façade marked by corbie steps and a biblical inscription above the door. Yet behind the front, the courtyard is built of black-and-white timber and plaster, a contrast both striking and unsettling.

It was late afternoon when my cousin arrived. The sun slanted across the building, casting long shadows and illuminating the old-world charm of the place. He admired its weathered grandeur, promising himself a peaceful stay in a setting so emblematic of ancient Jutland.

He had not come for business in the usual sense. Mr Anderson was engaged in research into the Church history of Denmark, particularly the final years of Roman Catholicism before the Reformation. He had learned that the Rigsarkiv in Viborg held documents preserved from the fire—letters, decrees, and private correspondence that might shed light on the last days of Catholic rule. He intended to spend two or three weeks copying them, and hoped the Golden Lion could offer him a room large enough to serve as both bedroom and study.

The landlord listened patiently, then suggested he inspect the rooms himself. The top floor was dismissed too soon—the climb would be tiresome after a day’s work. The second floor offered no suitable space. On the first floor, however, several rooms met his needs in size.

The landlord favored Number 17, but Anderson objected: its windows faced only a blank wall, and it would be dark by evening. Number 12 or 14 would be better—both overlooked the street, offering light and a view. After some deliberation, Number 12 was chosen.

It was long and high-ceilinged, with three windows aligned along one wall. There was no fireplace, but a cast-iron stove stood in the corner, decorated with a relief of Abraham sacrificing Isaac and inscribed with “1 Bog Mose, Cap. 22.” Nothing else seemed remarkable—except an old colored print of the town, dated about 1820.

Supper approached, but Anderson lingered in the hall, brushing off the dust of travel. As he descended, he glanced at the guest board—a large blackboard listing names and room numbers. The list was unremarkable: a German advocate, a few merchants from Copenhagen. But something caught his eye: no Number 13.

He had seen this before in Danish hotels. Still, it struck him as curious. Was the superstition so strong that no one would rent a room numbered thirteen? He resolved to ask the landlord.

That night passed quietly. After unpacking, he read a few pages before bed, then remembered his book was in the pocket of his greatcoat, hung outside the dining room. A quick trip down the hall brought him back—only to find he’d entered the wrong room. The handle resisted; within, he heard a sudden movement. He checked the number: 13. His own room was to the left.

Back in bed, he lit his lamp, read a few pages, blew it out, and turned over. Only then did it occur to him: there was no Number 13 on the guest board. Yet here was a door marked 13. Had he imagined it?

He felt a pang of regret. Perhaps he should have taken it. Maybe it was merely a servant’s room. But why had it been hidden? He looked around the dimly lit room. In the glow of the streetlamp, it seemed smaller than before—shorter, taller. An odd sensation. Rooms usually appear larger in shadow, yet this one contracted. Sleep came anyway.

The next day, Anderson visited the Rigsarkiv. He was welcomed warmly. The archives contained more than he expected—official records, personal letters, and a thick bundle of correspondence between Bishop Jörgen Friis and Protestant reformers. Among them, references to a house owned by the Bishop, though not occupied by him. Its tenant, Mag Nicolas Francken, was accused of witchcraft, of selling his soul to the Devil. The reformers called him a *Troldmand*, a sorcerer, a disgrace to the city. The Bishop defended him fiercely, insisting the charges were false and demanding a spiritual court trial.

Anderson skimmed the next letter—Rasmus Nielsen’s reply—before closing time. It declared that Christians were no longer bound by Rome’s authority, and that the Bishop’s court was unfit to judge such matters.

On the way out, he walked with Herr Scavenius, the archivist. They discussed the papers. Scavenius admitted he didn’t know where the Bishop’s house had stood. The original land register from 1560 was incomplete—the section listing urban property missing. “I’ll find it someday,” he said.

Back at the Golden Lion, Anderson decided to inquire about the missing room number. He paused near the corridor, looking for Number 13. There it was—plain as day. And inside, sounds: footsteps, voices, then silence. Then, a sharp, hissing breath—like someone gasping in fear or rage. He moved on.

In his room, the space seemed smaller. More troubling: his portmanteau was gone. He’d left it on a trestle near the far wall. He assumed servants had moved it. But the wardrobe was empty. No theft—he’d never heard of such things in Denmark. Probably just a mistake. He’d wait until morning.

He went to the right-hand window. The street was dark. Opposite, a tall building loomed, its walls bare. He saw the shadow of the man in Room 11—bearded, brushing his hair, later in a nightgown. Then, the shadow of Room 13. A figure leaned on the sill, draped in cloth. A flickering red light played on the opposite wall—steady, then pulsing. A red lampshade? A candle? He leaned forward, but saw only a fold of white fabric.

Then, a distant step. The figure in Room 13 vanished instantly. The red light died. Anderson placed his cigarette end on the sill and went to bed.

Next morning, the maid brought hot water. He asked sharply, “You must not move my portmanteau. Where is it?”

She laughed and left without answering.

He sat up, irritated—then froze. There, on the trestle, was the portmanteau. Exactly where he’d left it. How could he have missed it? He stared. The daylight revealed the true shape of the room—no longer distorted. But when he reached the middle window to check the weather, another shock: the cigarette end rested on the sill of the middle window.

He had sworn he smoked at the right-hand window.

Downstairs, breakfast passed quietly. He noticed Number 13’s boots outside the door—men’s boots. So the occupant was male. He glanced at the door number: 14. He must have passed Number 13 without seeing it. He turned back—only to find the next room was Number 12. No Number 13.

Three errors in twelve hours. Impossible. He began to doubt his senses.

But the day passed normally. He returned to the archives, searching for more on Francken. Only one other letter remained—a response from the Bishop:

“Though we do not accept your judgment of our court, nor shall we yield to you, yet since our trusted Mag Nicolas Francken has been suddenly removed from among us, the matter rests for now. But you also claim that St John describes the Holy Roman Church as the Scarlet Woman—know this…”

No sequel. No mention of how Francken died. Only that he was “removed”—suddenly, unexpectedly. Between the dates of the letters, less than two days passed. A death, perhaps. But how?

That evening, Anderson sat by the window again. The lawyer in Room 14—Sagförer Jensen—was dancing. His shadow crossed the wall repeatedly: arms flung wide, legs kicked high. Barefoot. Silent. A grotesque performance in the quiet hour.

Anderson smiled, then frowned. He thought of Emily in *Udolpho*:
*“When I return to my hotel, At ten o’clock p.m., The waiters think I am unwell; I do not care for them. But when I’ve locked my chamber door, And put my boots outside, I dance all night upon the floor. And even if my neighbours swore, I’d go on dancing all the more, For I’m acquainted with the law, And in despite of all their jaw, Their protests I deride.”*

He laughed softly—then stopped. A chill crept into the air.

At ten, the landlord arrived. Anderson invited him to smoke a cigar. Photographs of English towns served as an excuse. The landlord accepted eagerly.

As they talked, Anderson mentioned the missing Number 13.

“Oh, that?” the landlord chuckled. “I’ve wondered myself. Superstition, you know. People refuse to stay in it. Say it brings bad luck. Lost customers, broken limbs—stories galore.”

“But does it exist?” Anderson asked.

“Exist? No. Not here. I’ve never had a Number 13. You must have imagined it.”

Anderson hesitated. “But I saw it twice. Last night. The night before.”

The landlord laughed. “Impossible. There’s no such room. Next to yours? That’s Number 12. Then 14. No 13.”

Anderson nodded, but unease clung to him like damp.

Later, he retired early. He avoided passing the spot where Number 13 should be, walking past Room 11 instead. He checked the room—nothing unusual. The portmanteau was under the bed, safe. He sat down to write.

Outside, the world was quiet. A cart rolled over cobblestones. A man hurried past. Then silence.

He finished his letters. Ordered whisky. Went to the window.

And saw it again.

A shadow—tall, thin, draped in cloth—leaned on the sill of the room beside his. A red light flickered. Then, abruptly, it vanished. The light died.

Anderson stepped back. His heart beat hard.

He looked at the door.

Number 13.

There was no number.

Yet he had seen it.

Twice.

And the shadow—

It had not danced.

It had watched.

The papers from which I have constructed this tale came into my possession under circumstances I shall reveal only at the very end. But before I present the excerpts, I must explain how they have reached me.

They are a curious mixture: part travel journal, part personal record, and ultimately—though not at first intended—a chronicle of a man’s descent into something far darker than mere curiosity. The documents began as notes for a book of travels, much like those popular in the 1840s and 50s—books such as Horace Marryat’s *Journal of a Residence in Jutland and the Danish Isles*. These were often light-hearted, chatty accounts of foreign lands, filled with descriptions of inns, customs, and conversations with peasants and nobles alike. They were meant to entertain, not to unsettle.

But these papers evolved. What started as a traveler’s sketchbook became, over time, a confession—not of sin, but of dread. The author was Mr. Wraxall. I know little of him beyond what he wrote. He appears to have been a man past middle age, comfortably off, yet rootless—drifting between hotels and boarding houses across Europe, never settling. He had no fixed home in England, and though he once published a book about a holiday in Brittany, it seems to have appeared under a pseudonym or anonymously, for no trace of it remains in any catalog.

From his writings, I infer that he was intelligent, well-read, and possibly once near becoming a Fellow of Brasenose College at Oxford. His greatest flaw—and perhaps his fatal one—was an insatiable curiosity. It was not malice that drove him, nor greed, but a hunger to know, to see, to understand things others would leave buried.

In 1863, he set out for Sweden, drawn by the quiet grandeur of Scandinavia, then still largely unknown to English travelers. He had read old Swedish histories and thought there was room for a book combining travelogue with historical narrative—especially one that wove together the lives of noble families with the landscapes they shaped.

He secured letters of introduction and arrived in Stockholm, where a scholar directed him to a collection of family papers at Råbäck, an ancient manor in Vestergothland. The estate belonged to the De la Gardie family, descendants of one of Sweden’s most powerful noble lines. The house itself was impressive—a red-brick mansion with stone trim, built shortly after 1600, resembling an English country seat of the same era. It stood in a park, surrounded by mature trees, with a walled garden and a close wood bordering a small lake. Beyond the demesne wall rose a steep knoll crowned by a church, its dark pines silhouetted against the sky.

The church was odd to English eyes. Low ceilings, wooden galleries, and a painted organ with silver pipes. The ceiling bore a lurid depiction of the Last Judgment—cities collapsing, ships burning, souls shrieking, demons grinning. Brass coronae hung from the rafters; the pulpit looked like a dollhouse adorned with tiny saints and cherubs. And at the eastern end of the north aisle stood the mausoleum—a domed, eight-sided structure with oval windows and a copper roof painted black. It was separate from the church, accessible only through its own portal on the northern side.

Mr. Wraxall stayed at the village inn rather than at the manor, preferring independence and wary of his limited Swedish. Each day, he walked the mile to Råbäck, returning in the evening. On his first visit, he found the church door open and took notes of its interior. But the mausoleum remained closed. Peering through the keyhole, he glimpsed marble effigies, copper sarcophagi, and coats of arms carved in relief. He longed to enter.

The papers he studied were exactly what he’d hoped for: journals, letters, account books from the earliest De la Gardie owners. They revealed a man named Magnus de la Gardie—strong, capable, ruthless. During a peasant uprising, he had crushed the rebellion with brutal efficiency. Men were flogged, branded, executed. One portrait of him hung in the hall: a face of immense power, yet deeply ugly. Mr. Wraxall described it as “almost phenomenally” so.

That night, dining with the family, he noticed the sexton standing on the steps of the mausoleum, seemingly locking or unlocking the door. “I must ask him tomorrow,” he wrote, “if he can let me in.”

The next morning, he spoke with the innkeeper, Herr Nielsen. He wanted to know if any local legends survived about Count Magnus. The answer was swift and chilling: the Count was not liked. He punished tardy tenants with the wooden horse or branding. Some landowners who encroached on his domain had their homes burned down—entire families perishing in winter fires.

But the landlord hesitated when asked about the *Black Pilgrimage*.

“What is that?” Mr. Wraxall pressed.

Herr Nielsen looked away. Then, abruptly, he said he had to go to Skara and would return later.

Frustrated, Mr. Wraxall turned back to the papers. He read letters between Sophia Albertina and her cousin Ulrica Leonora, spanning 1705–1710—letters rich with cultural detail, now preserved in official Swedish archives. In the afternoon, while replacing the boxes, he pulled down a volume from a nearby shelf. It was not an account book, but a sixteenth-century manuscript filled with alchemical tracts: *The Book of the Phoenix*, *The Book of the Thirty Words*, *The Book of the Toad*, *Turba philosophorum*.

Near the center, on a blank leaf, was a note in Count Magnus’s hand:

> *"If any man desires to obtain a long life, if he would obtain a faithful messenger and see the blood of his enemies, it is necessary that he should first go into the city of Chorazin, and there salute the prince…."*

A word was erased—only partially—so Mr. Wraxall could make out *aëris* (“of the air”). Below it, in Latin: *Quære reliqua hujus materiei inter secretiora.* (Seek the rest of this matter among the more private things.)

It was unsettling. Alchemy, yes—but magic? Something darker?

Later, walking home, he passed the churchyard and stopped short. He found himself standing before the mausoleum.

“Ah,” he murmured, “Count Magnus, there you are. I should dearly like to see you.”

He did not expect a reply. But the woman cleaning the church dropped a metal object—clanging on stone. He jumped.

“Count Magnus, I think, sleeps sound enough.”

That evening, the landlord introduced him to the parish deacon. A visit to the mausoleum was arranged for the next day.

Over dinner, Mr. Wraxall asked about Chorazin.

The deacon stiffened. “You mean the place Jesus cursed?”

“Yes. Is it still standing?”

“I suppose it is a ruin,” the deacon said. “Some say Antichrist will be born there. And there are tales…”

“What tales?”

“The ones I’ve forgotten,” the deacon replied, and left.

Now alone, Mr. Wraxall turned to Herr Nielsen.

“Tell me,” he said, “what did the Count bring back from the Black Pilgrimage?”

The landlord stared at him for a full minute. Then stepped close.

“In my grandfather’s time—ninety-two years ago—two men said: ‘The Count is dead. We do not care for him. Tonight we will hunt freely in his woods.’ Others warned them: ‘Do not go. You will meet those who should not walk. They should rest.’ They laughed.

“They went that night. My grandfather sat here, window open, watching the woods.

“At first, silence. Then—scream. Not human. As if a soul was being torn from the body. All in the room seized each other. For three-quarters of an hour, no sound.

“Then—laughter. Loud. From someone not among them. Not even human. Then—great doors shut.

“At dawn, they went to the priest. Said: ‘Father, come. Bury Anders Bjornsen and Hans Thorbjorn.’

“They went to the wood. Found Hans Thorbjorn standing against a tree, pushing at nothing—his hands moving as if repelling a thing unseen. He lived until winter, but always pushed.

“Anders Bjornsen was dead. Once a handsome man. Now—his face was gone. Flesh sucked away from bone. They laid him on the bier. Covered his head. The priest walked ahead. They sang the psalm.

The next day, the deacon led Mr. Wraxall to the mausoleum. The key hung near the pulpit. He noted that the church door was often left unlocked—meaning a second visit might be possible.

Inside, the space was somber. Three copper sarcophagi stood beneath the dome. Two bore crucifixes. The third—Count Magnus’s—had a full-length effigy engraved upon it. Around the edge, scenes were depicted: a battle, an execution, a man running through the woods, hair flying, hands outstretched. Behind him came a figure—short, hooded, wrapped in a cloak that touched the ground. Only one limb protruded: not a hand, not an arm, but a thick, sinuous appendage—like a devil-fish’s tentacle.

Mr. Wraxall smiled grimly. “This,” he wrote, “must be the origin of the story—the Count and his companion.”

But the hunter was not a demon. Only a cloaked man on a hill, leaning on a staff, watching the chase.

Three steel padlocks secured the sarcophagus. One was loose, lying on the floor. He picked it up, placed it on the window ledge. Tried to relock it. Failed.

He left.

That night, returning from the manor-house, he found himself at the churchyard gate without realizing how he’d gotten there. He was humming—or chanting—something:
“Are you awake, Count Magnus? Are you asleep, Count Magnus?”

He couldn’t recall the rest.

The key was still there. He entered, copied the epitaphs, and stayed until dusk.

“I must have been wrong,” he wrote, “to say one padlock was loose. Two are now unfastened. I picked both up and laid them carefully on the windowsill. I tried to close them—failed. The third remains fast. I believe it is a spring lock. I cannot guess how it opens. If I had managed it… I am almost afraid I would have opened the sarcophagus.”

He felt a strange pull toward the Count—a fascination with a man who had lived so fiercely, so cruelly, so mysteriously.

The next day, he received letters calling him back to England. His work was done. He packed his notes, bid farewell, and prepared to leave.

He would never return to Råbäck.

“I suppose you’ll be leaving soon now that Full Term is over, Professor?” a man not in the story said to the Professor of Ontography, shortly after they’d sat down beside each other at a feast in the hospitable hall of St James’s College.

The Professor was young, neat, and spoke with precision.

“Yes,” he replied. “My friends have been urging me to take up golf this term, and I plan to go to the East Coast—specifically Burnstow, as I’m sure you know—for a week or ten days to improve my game. I hope to depart tomorrow.”

“Oh, Parkins,” said his neighbor on the other side, “if you’re heading to Burnstow, I wish you’d look into the site of the Templars’ preceptory and let me know if it’s worth digging up in the summer.”

It was, as one might expect, someone with an interest in antiquities who said this—but since he only appears briefly here, there’s no need to name him.

“Certainly,” said Parkins, the Professor. “If you can describe where the site is, I’ll do my best to assess the terrain when I return—or I could write to you, if you tell me where you’ll be.”

“No need for that, thanks. It’s just that I’m thinking of bringing my family that way during the Long Vacation, and I thought, since so few English preceptories have ever been properly mapped, I might make something useful of my spare time.”

The Professor sniffed faintly at the idea that mapping out a ruined monastery could be called useful. His neighbor continued:

“The site—there’s probably nothing visible above ground—must be close to the beach now. The sea has encroached heavily along that stretch of coast, as you know. From the map, I’d say it’s about three-quarters of a mile from the Globe Inn, at the northern end of town. Where are you staying?”

“Well, *at* the Globe Inn, actually,” said Parkins. “I’ve booked a room there. I couldn’t find anywhere else; most of the lodgings are closed in winter, it seems. And even then, they told me the only large room available is technically double-bedded—and they don’t even have space to store the second bed. But I need a fairly spacious room, since I’m bringing books and intend to do some work. Though I don’t particularly like having an empty bed—let alone two—in what I’ll call, for now, my study, I suppose I can manage for the short time I’ll be there.”

“Do you really call having an extra bed roughing it, Parkins?” asked a blunt man across the table. “Look here—I’ll come down and take it for a bit. It’ll be good company for you.”

Parkins flinched but managed a polite laugh.

“By all means, Rogers. I’d welcome it. But I’m afraid you’d find it rather dull—you don’t play golf, do you?”

“No, thank Heaven!” said Mr. Rogers, unrepentant.

“Well, when I’m not writing, I’ll likely be out on the links. That, as I said, would probably be rather dull for you.”

“Oh, I don’t know,” Rogers replied. “There’s bound to be someone I know there. But of course, if you don’t want me, just say so, Parkens. I won’t be offended. Truth, as you always remind us, is never offensive.”

Parkins was indeed scrupulously courteous and rigorously honest. It was rumored that Mr. Rogers sometimes exploited these traits. Now, a conflict stirred within Parkins—so intense that for a moment he could not speak. When he finally did, it was with careful honesty:

“Well, if you insist on the truth, Rogers, I was wondering whether the room would truly be large enough for both of us—and whether (mind, I wouldn’t have mentioned this unless you pressed) your presence might interfere with my work.”

Rogers laughed loudly.

“Well done, Parkins! All right. I promise not to disturb your researches. Don’t worry about that. No, I won’t come if you don’t want me—but I thought it might help keep the ghosts away.” He winked and nudged his neighbor.

Parkins flushed.

“I beg your pardon, Rogers,” he said stiffly. “I shouldn’t have said that. I forgot you don’t care for levity on such subjects.”

“Well,” Parkins said, “now that you’ve raised it, I freely admit I dislike careless talk about what you call ghosts. A man in my position,” he added, raising his voice slightly, “cannot afford to appear to endorse such beliefs. As you know, or ought to know, since I’ve never hidden my views—”

“No, you certainly haven’t, old man,” Rogers interjected softly.

“I hold that any suggestion—any appearance of concession—that such things might exist is equivalent to abandoning everything I hold sacred. But I fear I’ve lost your attention.”

“Your *undivided* attention,” Rogers corrected, with mock seriousness, “was what Dr. Blimber actually said.”[4] “But I apologize, Parkins—I’m interrupting.”

“No, not at all,” said Parkins. “I don’t recall Blimber; perhaps he was before my time. But I needn’t continue. I’m sure you understand what I mean.”

“Yes, yes,” said Rogers, hastily. “Just so. We’ll discuss it fully at Burnstow—or somewhere.”

In recounting this exchange, I’ve tried to convey the impression it left on me: Parkins was something of an old maid—perhaps henlike in manner, utterly lacking in humor, yet resolute and sincere in his convictions. A man worthy of respect, though perhaps not entirely likable.

On the following day, Parkins succeeded in leaving college and arrived at Burnstow. He was welcomed at the Globe Inn, settled into the large double-bedded room we’ve heard about, and before retiring arranged his materials in perfect order on a spacious table at the far end of the room, which faced three windows overlooking the sea. The central window looked straight out to the ocean; the others offered views north and south along the shore. To the south lay the village of Burnstow. To the north, only beach and low cliffs. Immediately ahead was a narrow strip of rough grass dotted with old anchors and capstans, then a broad path, then the beach itself. Whatever the original distance between the inn and the sea, now less than sixty yards separated them.

The rest of the inn’s guests were mostly golfers—few of whom required special mention. The most prominent figure was Colonel Wilson, a retired military man and secretary of a London club, whose voice was thunderous and whose views were decidedly Protestant. These often surfaced after church services led by the Vicar—a decent man with a fondness for ritualistic flourishes, which he suppressed as much as possible out of deference to East Anglian tradition.

Professor Parkins, known for his courage, spent most of the next day improving his golf game alongside the Colonel. By afternoon—whether due to fatigue or something else, it’s unclear—the Colonel’s demeanor had turned so grim that even Parkins hesitated at the idea of walking home with him. After a quick glance at his bristling mustache and crimson face, Parkins decided it would be wiser to let tea and tobacco calm the Colonel before dinner made their meeting inevitable.

“I could walk home tonight along the beach,” he mused. “Yes, and take a look at those ruins Disney mentioned. I don’t exactly know where they are—but I doubt I’ll miss them.”

He did, in fact, stumble upon them—literally. While making his way from the links to the shingle beach, his foot caught in a gorse root and a stone, and he fell. When he rose and surveyed his surroundings, he found himself in broken ground marked by small depressions and mounds. Closer inspection revealed these were masses of flint embedded in mortar, overgrown with turf. Clearly, he was on the site of the preceptory he’d promised to examine. It seemed promising—enough foundation remains likely remained near the surface to reveal much about its layout.

He recalled vaguely that the Templars often built round churches, and noticed that a cluster of mounds nearby appeared arranged in a circular pattern. Few people can resist the urge to dabble in archaeology, even outside their expertise—especially when it comes to proving how much they’d have achieved if only they’d pursued it seriously. Parkins, however, while perhaps tempted by such vanity, also genuinely wished to assist Mr. Disney. So he carefully paced the circular area and recorded its dimensions in his notebook. Then he examined an oblong mound to the east of the center, which seemed likely to be a platform or altar base. At one end, the turf had been torn away—probably by a child or some wild creature. He decided to probe the soil there for signs of masonry, pulled out his knife, and began scraping.

A portion of earth collapsed inward, revealing a small cavity. He lit match after match to see what lay inside, but the wind snuffed them out. Instead, he tapped and scraped the walls with his knife and deduced it was an artificial hole in stone—rectangular, with smooth sides, top, and bottom. It was empty. Or so he thought. As he withdrew his knife, a metallic clink echoed. When he reached in, his hand touched a cylindrical object lying on the floor of the hole. Naturally, he retrieved it. In the fading light, he saw it was man-made—a metal tube about four inches long, clearly ancient.

By the time he confirmed there was nothing else in the cavity, it was too dark and late to search further. What he’d found had proven unexpectedly fascinating. He resolved to spend more daylight hours exploring the next day. The object in his pocket was surely of some value.

The view before him was bleak and solemn. A faint yellow glow in the west illuminated the links, where a few figures still moved toward the clubhouse. The squat martello tower, the lights of Aldsey village, the pale ribbon of sand interrupted by black wooden groynes, and the dim, murmuring sea formed a desolate tableau. The wind blew bitterly from the north, but at his back as he set off for the Globe Inn. He rattled across the shingle, then stepped onto the sand—except for the groynes, which required leaping every few yards. The going was quiet and easy.

One last glance behind showed a shadowy figure struggling to catch up—running, but making little progress. There was motion, but no real advance. Parkins thought he didn’t recognize him, and decided it would be absurd to wait. Still, company, he reflected, would be welcome on such a lonely shore—if only he could choose it. In his younger days, he’d read tales of encounters in such places that still chilled him. One memory came vividly to mind: “Now I saw in my dream that Christian had gone but a very little way when he saw a foul fiend coming over the field to meet him.”

“What would I do,” he wondered, “if I looked back and saw a black shape sharply outlined against the yellow sky, with horns and wings? Would I stand or run? Luckily, the man behind isn’t like that. He seems just as far away now as when I first saw him. Well, at this rate, he won’t get dinner before I do—and dear me, it’s nearly time. I must hurry!”

Parkins had little time to dress. When he met the Colonel at dinner, peace—or as much as the Colonel could manage—had returned to the military breast. It held through the bridge game that followed, for Parkins was a competent player. By midnight, he felt satisfied with his evening and believed life at the Globe Inn would be bearable for several weeks—“especially,” he thought, “if I keep improving my game.”

As he walked the corridors, he met the inn’s boots, who stopped him.

“Pardon, sir,” he said, “but while brushing your coat, something fell from your pocket. I placed it on your chest of drawers—a piece of pipe or something similar. Thank you, sir. You’ll find it there—yes, sir. Good night, sir.”

This reminded Parkins of his discovery. With keen curiosity, he examined the object by candlelight. It was bronze, shaped like a modern dog-whistle—indeed, it was unmistakably a whistle. He brought it to his lips, but it was clogged with fine, hardened sand. Knocking didn’t loosen it—he had to scrape it out with a knife. Tidy as ever, Parkins emptied the dirt onto paper and carried it to the window to discard. The night was clear and bright. He paused to gaze at the sea and saw a lone figure standing on the shore below the inn. Then he shut the window, slightly surprised at how late people stayed out in Burnstow, and returned to the whistle.

Wait—were there marks on it? Not just marks, but letters! A little rubbing revealed deeply incised text. On one side:

On the other:

“I should be able to decipher this,” he thought. “But perhaps my Latin is rusty. Come to think of it, I don’t even know the word for ‘whistle.’ The second line is simple enough—‘Who is this who is coming?’ The best way to find out, then, is to blow it.”

He blew tentatively—then froze, startled yet intrigued by the sound. It carried an infinite distance, soft yet resonant, seemingly audible for miles. It evoked images—like certain scents, it conjured visions. For a moment, he saw a vast, dark expanse under a cold wind, a solitary figure in the center—what he was doing, he couldn’t say. The image shattered as a sudden gust slammed against the casement. He looked up just in time to glimpse the white flash of a seabird’s wing beyond the glass.

Fascinated, he blew again—this time more boldly. The note was no louder, and the illusion faded. No vision followed. But then—what was this? Goodness! How strong the wind had become! A tremendous gust! There! I knew that latch wouldn’t hold! Ah! Both candles are out. This wind could tear the room apart.”

First thing: shut the window. For twenty seconds, Parkins struggled with the casement, feeling as if he were pushing back a burglar. The pressure eased suddenly, and the window slammed shut and latched itself. Now, relight the candles and check for damage. Nothing seemed broken—no glass cracked. But the noise had awakened someone: the Colonel stomped above in his stockings, growling.

The wind didn’t subside quickly. It moaned and rushed past the house, rising at times to a cry so mournful that, as Parkins observed dispassionately, it might unsettle the fanciful. Even the unimaginative, he mused after fifteen minutes, might prefer silence.

Whether it was the wind, the excitement of golf, or the day’s discoveries, Parkins wasn’t sure. But he remained awake, convinced he was suffering from fatal ailments—counting heartbeats, fearing paralysis, suspecting lung or liver failure. He knew daylight would dispel these fears, but until then, they refused to leave. He found comfort in knowing someone else was restless too—a neighbor rustled and tossed nearby.

Next, he closed his eyes and tried to sleep. But overexcitement took another form: hallucinations. _Experto crede_, pictures come to the closed eyes of those trying to sleep—often unwelcome ones.

Parkins’s experience was especially distressing. The image that formed was continuous. Opening his eyes made it vanish—but closing them again restored it, playing out at the same pace. Here is what he saw:

A long stretch of shore—shingle bordered by sand, intersected by black groynes reaching the water. A scene so identical to his afternoon walk that, without landmarks, it was indistinguishable. The light was dim, suggesting storm, late winter, and a light rain. At first, no figure appeared. Then, in the distance, a bobbing black shape emerged—soon revealed as a man running, jumping, scrambling over the groynes, constantly glancing back. The closer he came, the more evident his terror—though his face remained unseen. He was nearly spent. Each obstacle grew harder. “Will he make it over this one?” Parkins thought. “It looks higher than the rest.” Yes—half climbing, half throwing himself, he cleared it and collapsed on the other side, near the viewer.

There, crouching beneath the groyne, he remained, staring upward with desperate anxiety.

So far, no cause for his fear had appeared. But now, far up the shore, a flicker of pale movement darted swiftly and erratically. Growing larger, it became a figure in fluttering, ghostly draperies, indistinct. Its motion unnerved Parkins—he dreaded seeing it close. It would stop, raise arms, bow to the sand, then dart across the beach to the water’s edge and back. Then, rising upright, it would resume its terrifying advance. Soon, the pursuer hovered just beyond the groyne where the runner hid. After several futile swings, it halted, stood tall, arms raised high, then lunged straight toward the groyne.

At this point, Parkins always failed to keep his eyes closed. With dread, he lit a match—only to have it go out. A second burned better. Candle and book were fetched. He read until sleep finally came, healthy and deep. For the first time in his orderly life, he forgot to extinguish the candle. When he was roused at eight the next morning, a faint flame still flickered in the socket, and grease had melted into a mess on the table.

After breakfast, he was dressing—fortune once again pairing him with the Colonel—when a maid entered.

For centuries, whispers have lingered among the canons of Steinfeld Abbey—ghosts of a secret buried not in stone, but in silence. They speak of Abbot Thomas von Eschenhausen, a man of great piety and greater pride, who, in the prime of his life, concealed within the very walls of his own monastery a hoard of gold so vast it could buy kingdoms. Though many have searched—digging through foundations, prying behind altars, even breaking into crypts—none has found it. And yet, the tale persists.

It is said that when questioned about the treasure’s location, Abbot Thomas would only laugh and reply: “Job, John, and Zechariah will tell either you or your successors.” Sometimes he added, with a glint in his eye, that he bore no ill will to whoever might uncover it. The words were spoken lightly, but they carried weight—like a riddle wrapped in a smile.

Abbot Thomas was no mere monk; he was a builder, a patron of art, a man whose name still echoes in the ruins of the abbey. He adorned the east window of the south aisle with stained glass of astonishing beauty—figures painted on glass so vivid they seemed to breathe. His effigy and arms remain there, carved above the glass, a silent testament to his legacy. He restored the abbot’s lodging, dug a well in the courtyard, and lined it with marble so finely carved it gleamed like dawn. He died suddenly at seventy-two, in 1529—a man whose final act may have been not prayer, but concealment.

The antiquary, Mr. Somerton, first encountered this story while poring over *Sertum Steinfeldense Norbertinum*, a rare and rambling chronicle of the Premonstratensian abbey published in 1712 by Christian Albert Erhard. He translated the passage with care, then set aside the book—but not before being struck by something peculiar.

He had long sought to trace the origins of certain stained-glass panels now installed in a private chapel belonging to Lord D——. Three figures dominated one window: Job, John the Evangelist, and Zechariah. Each held a scroll inscribed with a phrase that did not match the Vulgate.

Job’s read: _Auro est locus in quo absconditur_ —“There is a place for gold where it is hidden.”
John’s: _Habent in vestimentis suis scripturam quam nemo novit_ —“They have on their garments a writing which no man knows.”
Zechariah’s: _Super lapidem unum septem oculi sunt_ —“Upon one stone are seven eyes.”

The third was a direct quote from scripture. But the others? Altered. Deliberately altered.

Somerton felt a chill. These were not random choices. They were clues.

And then came the discovery: beneath the black borders of each figure’s robe—hidden under layers of pigment—were faint yellow letters, obscured by time and dust. Only when a servant used a rough broom to clean the glass did the stains begin to reveal themselves. Somerton scraped gently, revealing an inscription beneath each robe—three lines of jumbled letters:

Thirty-eight letters in each—marked by Roman numerals scratched into the glass. A cipher. A message meant to be found, but not understood—until now.

Back in England, Somerton worked late into the night, tracing, measuring, testing every possible method of decryption. He consulted ancient texts on cryptography—Trithemius’s *Steganographia*, Selenius’s *Cryptographia*, Bacon’s *De Augmentis Scientiarum*. Nothing fit. Then he returned to the figures themselves.

Job: left hand holds a scroll; right hand raised, one finger pointing upward.
John: left hand holds a book; right hand raised in blessing, two fingers extended.
Zechariah: left hand holds a scroll; right hand raised, three fingers pointed skyward.

One. Two. Three.

He tested the idea: skip one letter after the first, two after the second, three after the third—and repeat.

The result, once deciphered, was staggering:

> **Decem millia auri reposita sunt in puteo in atrio...**

Ten thousand pieces of gold lie hidden in a well in the court.

He gasped. The well—the one Abbot Thomas had built himself. The one described in the *Sertum*. The one now lost beneath centuries of neglect.

He knew what he must do.

Mr. Gregory, rector of Parsbury, stepped out before breakfast on a crisp autumn morning, intent on greeting the postman and breathing in the cool air. His children ran beside him, chattering. But the letter that arrived—foreign-stamped, addressed in a rough English hand—was not for them.

It was from William Brown, Somerton’s valet.

> *Honoured Sir,*
> *I am in a great anxiety about Master. I write at is Wish to beg you Sir if you could be so good as Step over. Master Has add a Nastey Shock and keeps His Bedd. I never Have known Him like this but No wonder and Nothing will serve but you Sir. Master says would I mintion the Short Way Here is Drive to Cobblince and take a Trap. Hoping I Have maid all Plain, but am much Confused in Myself what with Anxiatey and Weakfulness at Night. > *William Brown*
>

Gregory read it twice. Then again. A shock passed through him. Somerton—ill? In Germany? In Steinfeld?

He rushed back to the house, packed a bag, caught the train to London, secured a cabin to Antwerp, and boarded the Coblentz train. By dusk, he stood in the dim light of a village inn, the only one worth the name in Steinfeld.

Brown met him at the door, pale, trembling, dressed in tweed instead of livery. His relief was palpable.

“Master’s better,” he whispered, “but he’s had a dreadful time. You’re the only one he wants.”

They climbed the stairs to Somerton’s room. The door was closed. As Gregory reached for the handle, a cry tore from within.

“In God’s name, who is that?”
“Brown, is it?”

“Yes, sir—me, sir, and Mr. Gregory.”

A groan of relief echoed through the room.

Inside, the chamber was darkened against the afternoon sun. Somerton sat up in bed, face drawn, sweat on his brow. He stretched a trembling hand toward Gregory.

“Better for seeing you, my dear Gregory,” he murmured.

After a few minutes, he began to recover. He ate dinner, spoke with confidence, even joked. But then, his voice dropped.

“There’s one thing,” he said, gripping Gregory’s arm. “I must ask you to do something. Don’t ask why. Not yet. It’s safe. Brown will show you tomorrow. Just… put something back. Keep it there. That’s all.”

Gregory nodded. “I’ll do it.”

Somerton smiled weakly. “You won’t regret it. Tomorrow evening, I’ll tell you everything.”

That night, Gregory lay awake. Once, twice—he thought he heard fumbling at the bottom of his door. A hand probing the lock. But when he listened, nothing. Only the wind.

The next morning, Brown led Gregory through the quiet streets of Steinfeld. They walked past the ruined abbey, past the church stripped of its relics, until they reached a low stone wall near the old courtyard.

“Here,” Brown said, voice barely above a whisper.

On a step, half-hidden beneath moss and dirt, lay a wooden box—sealed, weathered, untouched for centuries.

“This,” Brown said, “is what we brought back. Master told us to hide it here, just before he fell ill. Said it was safer than carrying it.”

Gregory looked at the box. Then at Brown. “Why?”

Brown shook his head. “He wouldn’t say. But he said you’d understand when you saw it.”

They placed the box back on the step, exactly where it had lain. Then they returned to the inn.

That evening, Somerton was stronger. He sat upright, though still pale. Brown stood nearby, silent.

“I’ve told you the beginning,” Somerton said. “Now, the rest.”

He explained how he had cracked the cipher. How the three figures—Job, John, Zechariah—had been chosen not for doctrine, but for numbers. How the well in the courtyard was the key. How he had come to Steinfeld not just to find the treasure, but to return it—to restore balance to a man who had hidden more than gold.

“But when I reached the well,” he continued, “I found it blocked. And beneath the stones… a skeleton. A man in monk’s robes. His hands were clasped over a small chest. Empty.”

He paused.

“I didn’t know what to think. Then I remembered the last line of the cipher. It ended mid-sentence. ‘In puteo in atrio…’—but the word after *atrio* was missing. What if the treasure wasn’t in the well? What if it was *above*?”

He had searched the well’s rim. Found nothing. Then, in the darkness, he noticed a loose flagstone. Beneath it—another box. Smaller. Metal. Sealed.

Inside: a single gold coin, stamped with the seal of Abbot Thomas. And a note, written in Latin:

> *Non ego sum qui te occultavi, sed ille qui tibi dedit.*
> *I am not the one who hid you, but he who gave you.*

Somerton froze.

Then he realized: the treasure was never gold. It was knowledge. The cipher. The message. The truth.

And the man in the well? A guard. A keeper. A monk who had died protecting the secret.

But when Somerton touched the coin, the ground trembled. A cold breath washed over him. The candle flickered. And in the shadows, a shape moved—silent, unseen, but unmistakably *there*.

He fled.

That night, he dreamed of a man in black robes, standing at the edge of the well, watching him.

When he woke, he knew he could not stay. He sent for Gregory. He asked him to return the box—not because it contained gold, but because it contained *proof*. Proof that the treasure was not wealth, but memory. And that some secrets should never be broken.

And so, the box was returned.

But the well remains sealed.

And sometimes, on quiet nights, travelers swear they hear a whisper from beneath the stone:

> *Job, John, and Zechariah… will tell you…*
