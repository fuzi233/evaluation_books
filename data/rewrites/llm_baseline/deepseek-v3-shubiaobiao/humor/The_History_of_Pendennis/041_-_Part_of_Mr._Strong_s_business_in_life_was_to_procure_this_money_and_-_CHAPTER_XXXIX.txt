The evening of Sir Francis Clavering’s dinner arrived, and with it, Major Pendennis—who had no better engagement—and his nephew Arthur, who desired none. Upon entering the drawing-room, they found their host and hostess in a state of subdued animation, accompanied by their ever-present factotum, Captain Strong. The Major, whose aristocratic sensibilities were perpetually on guard, regarded Strong with thinly veiled disdain, privately dismissing him as "Clavering’s damned house-steward." Fortunately, the arrival of Mr. Welbore Welbore, a dull but impeccably respectable country squire, soothed the Major’s ruffled dignity.

The party swelled with the addition of Lady Rockminster (who had her own reasons for cultivating the Claverings), Lady Agnes Foker, and her son Harry—our old acquaintance—whose presence promised at least a measure of youthful absurdity. Last to grace the gathering was Miss Blanche Amory, resplendent in a white silk gown that displayed her shoulders to devastating effect. Foker, awestruck, whispered to Pen that she was a "stunner," a sentiment Arthur echoed silently, though with considerably more irony.

Blanche, in a masterful display of selective charm, was exquisitely gracious to both young men—fluttering her lashes at Foker while bestowing nostalgic smiles upon Pen, reminiscing about Fairoaks and inquiring tenderly after Laura and Mrs. Pendennis. Harry, unaccustomed to feminine attention, found himself uncharacteristically loquacious, chattering about opera, souprames of volaille (and the mysterious fate of fowl legs), and the immortal Taglioni.

“Miss Amory is a sylph herself,” Pen remarked dryly.

“What a delightful tenor you have, Mr. Foker,” Blanche cooed, her voice dripping with honey. “I should love to sing with you.”

Pen, recalling identical overtures once directed at himself, suppressed a smirk.

Dinner progressed with the usual blend of forced conviviality and quiet desperation. The ladies eventually withdrew, leaving the gentlemen to their wine and the rapidly deteriorating company of one another. The conversation, already strained, was interrupted by an uninvited guest—Colonel Altamont, who, in a state of advanced refreshment, had stationed himself outside the dining-room window, peering in with the enthusiasm of a man who believes himself the life of every party.

A scuffle ensued when the ever-vigilant Policeman X attempted to dislodge him. Altamont, resplendent in brass buttons and a Tyrian-purple wig, declared his intimate acquaintance with the Clavering household, bellowing for Strong and Sir Francis by name. Strong, with the air of a man well practiced in damage control, ushered the Colonel inside before the altercation could escalate.

The intrusion sent Sir Francis into visible distress, while Major Pendennis studied the interloper with narrowed eyes. Altamont, meanwhile, made himself at home, demanding the finest wine and addressing his host as “old corkscrew” with alarming familiarity.

“Very singular man,” Strong murmured by way of explanation. “Resided long in India—native courts, you know—peculiar habits.”

“Evidently,” the Major replied, his tone drier than the claret.

Altamont, upon recognizing Pen, launched into slurred reminiscences of the Baymouth ball, then abruptly turned his bleary gaze upon the Major. A peculiar tension crackled between them before the Colonel, muttering something about “Captain Beak,” staggered out as suddenly as he had arrived.

The evening never quite recovered. Coffee was served; the gentlemen rejoined the ladies; Blanche performed at the piano with calculated brilliance, coaxing rapturous praise from the Major, who had, not long before, dismissed her as a frivolous minx.

As the party dispersed, Foker clung to Pen, extolling Blanche’s virtues in increasingly incoherent terms, while the Major—unusually pensive—whispered a stern directive: *Call on them tomorrow. They’ve been uncommonly civil.*

Pen, bemused, wondered what game his uncle was playing. But then, in the Clavering household, the game was always afoot—and the players, as ever, delightfully unreliable.
