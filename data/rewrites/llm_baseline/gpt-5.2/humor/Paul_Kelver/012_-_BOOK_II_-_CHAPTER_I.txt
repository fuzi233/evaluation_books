“Room to let for a single gentleman.” Sometimes, when my thoughts are wandering and my feet are looking for trouble, I find myself before that sentence as though it were a familiar smell—damp stair-carpet, yesterday’s grease, and the faint despair of paraffin. I have even knocked, on pretence of enquiring, though the house knows me as a tongue knows an old sore.

The door opens—always after a pause sufficiently long to allow one to repent—upon the “slavey.” In the morning she is a catalogue of calamities: hair like teased hemp, mouth ajar as if the air itself were astonishing, arms vanished beneath an apron which appears to have seen service as a dish-cloth, a duster, and perhaps, in emergencies, a small tent. In the afternoon she makes an effort at smartness: a cap and apron, both of them dirty with the neatness of established dirt. Her hands are scored red, as if she had been peeling potatoes all her life with the wrong end of a knife.

How well I know her, and how unchanged she remains—never growing an inch, never acquiring a new expression. It costs me an effort not to say, “So sorry—forgot my key,” and slip past her, taking the stairs two at a time: narrow, grudging stairs—carpeted as far as the first floor, bare and honest about their misery beyond.

Instead I put on the voice of the respectable enquirer and ask, “Oh—what rooms have you to let?”

She scuttles to the top of the kitchen stairs and, leaning over the banisters with the solemnity of a town crier announcing plague, calls: “A gentleman to see the rooms!”

Up comes the landlady: elderly, harassed, stout with the sort of stoutness produced by perpetual stair-climbing and perpetual indignation. She is genteel in black, as though black could make virtue of everything. She crushes past the little drudge—who collapses into the wall like an apology—and regards me with the eye of one appraising a joint: not to admire it, but to calculate what may be got for it.

“I have a very nice room on the first floor,” she says, “and one behind on the third.”

I explain, with that helpless lie which is the lodger’s etiquette, that I am looking for a young friend. We squeeze past the hat-and-umbrella stand; there is precisely room if one keeps close to the wall and breathes shallowly. The first-floor room is, by comparison, an imposing apartment—its pretensions are like a Sunday hat worn on a weekday body. A marble-topped sideboard, three feet by two, stands like an altar to respectability; its doors will remain closed if you wedge a wad of paper between them, which suggests they are anxious to meet, but cannot manage it unaided. A green table-cloth—green with that particular green which has spent too long inhaling gas—matches the curtains; together they contrive to say, “We are a suite,” with the air of people who have only met that morning.

The lamp is “perfectly safe,” provided it stands dead centre of the table and is never moved, as though it were a wild creature that will bite if startled. On the mantelpiece, a paper fire-stove ornament casts over the place the oddest air of chastity—as if the room has taken a vow to be respectable even while it sins. Above it hangs a fly-blown mirror, and between the once gilt frame and the glass are invitation cards—yes, actual cards—stuck there like pressed flowers to prove that even bed-sitting-room life may, at times, be visited by society. One or two remain, faintly yellow with age, hinting at dances long finished and people long disillusioned.

Opposite is the inevitable oleograph, the kind a Cheap Jack sells by auction on a Saturday night in the Pimlico Road and warrants “hand-made,” which it is—by machinery. It is, as usual, a Swiss landscape; there is more “body” in Switzerland than anywhere else, apparently. You can always rely on a foaming torrent, a mountain with a snowy cap, a dilapidated mill, and—if the artist is feeling generous—a maiden and a cow. They look as though they were put in to reassure the purchaser that something is alive.

Then there is the easy chair—if ever a word deserved prosecution for libel. It is stuffed with innumerable coils of steel wire, each with a “business end” sharpened by experience, and upholstered in glossy horsehair that seems to grin. There is one way of sitting upon it, and one only: on the extreme edge, feet planted as though awaiting an explosion. Attempt to recline and the chair gently, firmly, slides you out as if saying, “Excuse me—you are very heavy—and the floor is far more suitable for you. Thank you.”

The bed is behind the door; the washstand behind the bed. Sit facing the window and, with discipline, you can pretend you sleep nowhere. But when two friends call, you suddenly become grateful for the bed: experienced visitors, indeed, make straight for it, declining the easy chair with a firmness born of puncture.

“And this room is?” I ask.

“Eight shillings a week, sir—with attendance, of course.”

“Any extras?”

“The lamp, sir, is eighteenpence a week; and the kitchen fire, if the gentleman wishes to dine at home, two shillings.”

“And fire?”

“Sixpence a scuttle, sir, I charge for coals.”

“It’s rather a small scuttle.”

She bridles. “The usual size, I think, sir.” No doubt there is a pattern—“The Lodging-House Keeper’s Special”—made to suggest coal while supplying mainly hope.

Since I am already committed to the comedy, I agree that we may as well see the other room—third floor back. She opens the door for me and remains, tactfully, on the landing: being stout, she does not wish to dwarf the apartment by comparison.

Here the bed cannot be ignored. It dominates the room like a relative who has come to stay. It is the life and soul of the place, and declines to efface itself for anyone. Its only rival is the washstand: straw-coloured wood, glaring white basin and jug, and other appurtenances that speak of cold water and colder mornings. It stands in its corner with the defiance of usefulness: “I know I’m small,” it seems to say, “but I matter; and I won’t be overlooked.”

The remaining furniture comprises two chairs—no hypocrisy about them; they do not pretend to be easy, and it would be indecent to accuse them of it—a light-painted chest of drawers under the window, with white china handles, upon which sits a tiny looking-glass as if apologising for its own smallness; and, occupying nearly all the remaining space (after allowing, by generosity, three square feet for the tenant when he arrives), an attenuated four-legged table that has the air of having been made by a man who disliked tables.

The sole ornament is above the fireplace: a funeral card framed in beer corks. It hangs there like the ancient Egyptian corpse introduced into banquets to remind guests that pleasure is brief; or perhaps it is meant, in dark moments, to console the lodger with the reflection that after all, he might be worse off.

“This room,” she tells me, “is three-and-six a week—attendance included; lamp eighteenpence; kitchen fire a shilling.”

“But why should kitchen fire for the first floor be two shillings, and for this only one?” I ask, for one must ask something, if only to hear the philosophy of lodging-houses.

“Well, as a rule, sir, the first floor wants more cooking done.”

Quite right, my dear lady. I was forgetting the great truth of civilisation: the higher you go, the less the kitchen fire is expected to be troubled with you.

The gentleman in the third floor back does not “want” cooking. His breakfast, madam, is what you call plain—the sort of plain that would make a prison dietary look ornamental. His lunch he takes out. You may see him between twelve and two, walking round the quiet square, up and down the narrow street that leads nowhere in particular and is, in those hours, abandoned by anyone with a purpose. He carries a paper bag, and at intervals—when he is sure nobody is looking—his mouth vanishes into it.

From studying the neighbourhood one can guess what the bag contains. Saveloys are plentiful hereabouts and only twopence each; they have a brave, reddish look, as though they were meant to comfort the poor with colour. There are pie-shops where meat pies are twopence and fruit pies a penny. The woman behind the counter—who handles pastry as a violinist handles a bow—uses a broad flat knife, and with one twist lifts the little dainty clean from its tin dish. Considering the thinness of the crust, it is a marvel she never breaks one; perhaps the pastry is already too broken to protest. There is roley-poley pudding, sweet, heavy, wonderfully satisfying—especially cold—at a penny a slice. Pease pudding, awkward to eat from a bag yet comforting on a raw day, turns up too, yellow and steadfast.

At tea the third-floor-back gentleman takes two eggs, or a haddock—fourpenny size; perhaps, on rare occasions, a chop or steak. You fry it for him, madam, though he always urges how much he would prefer it grilled; fried in your one frying-pan, its flavour becomes, as it were, confused—everything tastes faintly of everything else. But perhaps that is mercy: shutting his eyes and trusting only to smell and taste, he can imagine variety. He can begin with herrings, pass on to liver and bacon, open his eyes a moment to observe he has arrived at the joint, then close them again and end with a distinct suggestion of toasted cheese. Thus he avoids monotony by an effort of the imagination and a frying-pan of catholic sympathies.

For dinner he goes out again. Perhaps he is not hungry—late meals are a mistake; or perhaps, putting his hand in his pocket and making calculations beneath a lamp-post, appetite comes to him as a consequence. There are places cheerful with the sound of frizzling fat—how that sound carries into the street, a little orchestra of spitting and sizzling!—where fried plaice, brown and odorous, may be had for three halfpence, and a handful of sliced potatoes for a penny; where, for fourpence, succulent stewed eels may be discussed, vinegar ad lib.; or for sevenpence—these are red-letter evenings—half a sheep’s head may be indulged in, which is a supper fit for any king who happened to be hungry and not too particular about his company.

I tell the landlady I will discuss the matter with my young friend when he arrives.

“Certainly, sir,” she says; she is used to what she calls the “wandering Christian.” I ease my conscience by slipping a shilling into the slavey’s astonished, lukewarm hand, and pass out again into the long, dreary street—now echoing, perhaps, to the sad cry of “Muffins!” a sound that always seems as much lament as trade.

Or sometimes, of an evening, with the lamp lit and the remnants of the meat tea cleared away, and the flickering firelight cosying the dingy rooms into a sort of makeshift homeliness, I go a-visiting. There is no need to ring, no need to mount the stairs. Through the thin, transparent walls I can see you plainly, old friends—fashions altered a little, that is all. We wore bell-shaped trousers—eight-and-six to measure, seven-and-six if from stock—and fastened our neckties with a horseshoe pin in dashing style. In waistcoats, I think, we had the advantage; ours were gayer, braver. Our cuffs and collars were of paper: sixpence-halfpenny the dozen, three-halfpence the pair. On Sunday they were white and glistening; on Monday less aggressively pure; on Tuesday morning decidedly dappled. But on Tuesday evening, when with natty cane or umbrella neatly rolled in a patent-leather case we took our promenade down Oxford Street—fashionable hour, nine to ten—we could shoot our arms and cock our chins with the best.

Your india-rubber linen has its merits. Storm does not wither it; it braves the heat and turmoil of the day. The passing of a sponge and your “Dicky” is itself again. We had to use bread-crumbs and so sacrifice the glaze. Yet I still suspect that for the first few hours our paper dazzled more honestly.

For the rest I see little change in you, old friends. I wave you greeting from the misty street. God rest you, gallant gentlemen—lonely, friendless, despised—making the best of joyless lives; keeping yourselves genteel on twelve, fifteen, or eighteen (ah, but you are plutocrats!) shillings a week; saving something even of that, maybe, to help the old mother in the country, so proud of her “gentleman” son who has book learning and is “something in the City.” May nothing dismay you. Bullied and badgered and baited from nine to six though you may be, from then till bedtime you are rorty young dogs.

The half-guinea topper—“as worn by the Prince of Wales” (ah, how many a meal has it not cost!)—warmed before the fire, brushed and polished and coaxed, shines resplendent. The second pair of trousers is drawn from beneath the bed; in the gaslight, with a well-marked crease, it will pass for new. A pleasant evening to you! May your cheap necktie make all the impression your soul can desire. May your penny cigar be mistaken for Havana. May the barmaid charm your simple heart by calling you “Baby.” May some sweet shop-girl throw a kindly glance, inviting you to walk with her; may she snigger at your humour; may other dogs cast envious looks at you; and may no harm come of it.

You dreamers of dreams—you who, while your companions play and sleep, toil upward in the night! You have read Mr. Smiles’ “Self-Help,” Longfellow’s “Psalm of Life,” and so strengthened, attack with confidence “French Without a Master,” “Bookkeeping in Six Lessons.” With a sigh you turn aside from the alluring streets, from bright bewitching eyes, into the stuffy air of Birkbeck Institutions and Polytechnic Schools. May success compensate you for youth devoid of pleasure. May the partner’s chair you see in visions be yours before the end. May you live one day in Clapham in a twelve-roomed house.

And, after all, we have our moments, have we not? The Saturday night at the play. The hours of waiting are short: we converse with kindred souls of British Drama, its past and future—we have our views. We dream of Florence This and Kate That; in a little while we shall see her. Ah, could she but know how we loved her! Her photo stands on our mantelpiece, transforming the dismal little room into a shrine. The poem we have so often commenced—when finished, we will post it to her. At least she will acknowledge receipt; we can kiss the paper her hand has rested on.

The great doors groan, then quiver. The wild thrill! Now push for all you are worth: charge, wriggle, squirm. It is an epitome of life. We are through—collarless, panting, pummelled from head to toe: but what of that? Upward, still upward; then downward with leaps at risk of our necks, from bench to bench through the gloom. We have gained the front row. Would we exchange sensations with the stallite strolling languidly to his seat? The extravagant dinner once a week! We banquet à la Français in Soho for one-and-six, including wine. Does Tortoni ever give his customers a repast they enjoy more? I trow not.

My first lodging was an attic in a square the other side of Blackfriars Bridge. The rent, if I remember rightly, was three shillings a week with cooking, half-a-crown without. I purchased a methylated-spirit stove with kettle and frying-pan, and took it without. The blue flame had a smell like a chemist’s conscience; it made tea with a flavour faintly medicinal, and eggs that suggested they had been introduced to spirit for moral improvement. Still, it was my own fire, and that counted for much.

Old Hasluck would have helped me willingly; there were others too. But a boy’s pride held me back. I would make my way alone, win my place in the world by myself. To Hal, knowing he would understand, I confided the truth.

“Had your mother lived,” he said, “I should have had something to say on the subject. Of course, I knew what had happened, but as it is—well, you need not be afraid; I shan’t offer you help. Indeed, I should refuse it were you to ask. Put your Carlyle in your pocket: he is not all voices, but he is the best maker of men I know. The great thing to learn of life is not to be afraid of it.”

“Look me up now and then,” he added, “and we’ll talk about the stars, the future of Socialism, and the Woman Question—anything you like except about yourself and your twopenny-halfpenny affairs.”

From another it would have sounded brutal. From him it was comradeship—his way of keeping me from the soft indignities of charity. We shook hands and parted for longer than either of us then expected. A few weeks later the Franco-German War broke out, and Hal, with the love of adventure always strong in him, volunteered his services and was accepted. It was years before we met again.

In those days, on the door-post of a house in Farringdon Street not far from the Circus, there was a small brass plate announcing that the “Ludgate News Rooms” occupied the third and fourth floors, and that admission was one penny. Every morning a seedy company crowded there: clerks, shopmen, superior artisans, travellers, warehousemen—all of us out of work. Most were young; mingled with us was a sprinkling of older men, and these were always the saddest and most silent of that whispering army of the down-at-heel.

Roughly we divided ourselves into two groups: newcomers—cheery, confident—flitting from paper to paper with pleasant anticipation, selecting an advertisement as one chooses a dainty from a rich menu, and replying as though conferring a favour:

“Dear Sir,—in reply to your advertisement in to-day’s Standard, I shall be pleased to accept the post vacant in your office. I am of good appearance and address. I am an excellent—”

It was marvellous what we could all do on paper. French—written and spoken fluently, à la Ahn. German—slighter, but sufficient for commerce. Bookkeeping, arithmetic, geometry—we played with them. Love of work—a passion. Moral character—fit to adorn a Free Kirk elder.

“I could call on you to-morrow or Friday between eleven and one, or on Saturday any time up till two. Salary required, two guineas a week. An early answer will oblige. Yours truly.”

The old stagers did not buzz. Hour after hour they wrote steadily, methodically, with day by day less hope and heavier fears:

“Sir,—Your advt. in to-day’s D. T. I am—” of such and such an age. Qualifications shorter, set forth with modesty, aimed at verisimilitude. “If you decide to engage me I will endeavour to give you every satisfaction. Any time you like to appoint I will call on you. I should not ask a high salary to start with. Yours obediently.”

Dozens of the first letter, hundreds of the second, I wrote with painful care: pen carefully chosen, the one-inch margin down the left first portioned off with dots, as though respectability could be ruled in. To three or four I received curt replies instructing me to call.

But the shyness that had stood in my way at school returned upon me—I know not why—hampering me at every turn. A shy child grown-up people can forgive; a shy young man is taken for a fool. I gave the impression of being awkward, stupid, sulky. The more I struggled to be at ease, to assert myself, the worse I became; my attempts at confidence ended—so I could see—in something like rudeness.

“Well, I’ve got to see one or two others. We will write and let you know,” concluded each interview; and that was the end of it.

My few pounds, guarded as I tried to guard them, dwindled rapidly. Looking back, it is easy enough to see early struggles as humorous: one knows the story ended happily. But at the time there is no way of telling whether one’s biography will turn out comedy or tragedy. There were moments I felt sure it would be tragedy. Sometimes, when one is well, it is not unpleasant to contemplate with pathetic sympathy one’s own death-bed: one thinks of friends and relations who at last will understand and regret, be sorry they had not behaved better. But for me there was no one to regret. I felt very small, very helpless. The world was big. I feared it might walk over me, trample me down, never seeing me. I seemed unable to attract its attention.

One morning at the Reading Room I found waiting for me another of the usual notes: “Will Mr. P. Kelver call at the above address to-morrow morning between ten-thirty and eleven.” It was headed: “Lott and Co., Indian Commission Agents, Aldersgate Street.”

Without much hope I went back to my lodgings, changed, donned my silk hat, took my one pair of gloves, drew the silk case over my holey umbrella; and thus equipped for combat with Fate, made my way to Aldersgate Street.

For a quarter of an hour—being too soon—I walked up and down the pavement outside the house, gazing at the second-floor windows where, according to the door-plate, the offices of Lott & Co. were. I could not recall their advertisement nor my reply. The firm, judging by the mean look of the entrance, was not flourishing. I wondered what salary they would offer. For a moment I dreamed of a Cheeryble Brother asking kindly if I could do with thirty shillings a week as a beginning; then I remembered my usual luck and considered whether it was worth climbing the stairs and going through that ordeal merely to be told again, in another room, how little I was.

A fine rain began. I did not want to unroll my umbrella, yet I felt nervous for my hat. It was five minutes to the half hour. Listlessly I crossed the road and mounted the bare stairs to the second floor. Two doors faced me; one was marked “Private.” I tapped at the other. No answer. I tapped again. A sound came, unintelligible. I knocked louder.

This time a reply reached me, shrill and plaintive, full of pathetic entreaty:

“Oh, do come in.”

I turned the handle and entered. It was a small room, dimly lit by a dirty window, the bottom half rendered opaque by tissue paper pasted to the panes. The place suggested a village shop rather than an office. Pots of jam, jars of pickles, bottles of wine, biscuit tins, parcels of drapery, boxes of candles, bars of soap, boots, packets of stationery, boxes of cigars, tinned provisions, guns, cartridges—things sufficient to furnish a desert island—littered every corner. One could imagine being shipwrecked here and living, if not happily, at least variously.

At a small desk under the window sat a youth with a remarkably small body and a remarkably large head; so disproportionate were the two I should scarcely have been surprised had he taken it off and set it beside him for convenience. Half in the room and half out, I paused.

“Is this Lott & Co.?” I asked.

“No,” he answered. “It’s a room.” One eye was fixed on me—dull and glassy; it never blinked, never wavered. With the help of the other he continued writing.

“I mean,” I explained, coming fully in, “are these the offices of Lott & Co.?”

“It’s one of them,” he said. “The back one. If you’re really anxious for a job, you can shut the door.”

I shut it and announced, with as much dignity as a young man can summon while standing among jam and cartridges, that I was Mr. Kelver—Mr. Paul Kelver—and had an appointment.

“Minikin’s my name,” he returned. “Sylvanus Minikin. You don’t happen, by any chance, to know what you’ve come for, I suppose?”

Seeing his body, my impulse was to pick my way among the goods and pull his ears for him. But his grave, massive face suggested that, for all I knew, he might be head clerk or prophet.

“I have called to see Mr. Lott,” I replied, producing the letter and leaning across a sewing-machine to hand it to him.

He read it, then suddenly took from its socket the eye with which he had been regarding me, and polished it on his pocket-handkerchief. Having satisfied himself, he handed my letter back and replaced the glass eye as if it were a coin he preferred not to lose.

“Want my advice?” he asked.

It seemed to me advice was precisely what I needed, so I said yes.

“Hook it,” said Mr. Minikin.

“Why?” I asked. “Isn’t he a good employer?”

He turned back to his work. “If employment’s what you want,” he answered, “you’ll get it. Best employer in London. He’ll keep you going twenty-four hours a day, and then offer you overtime at half salary.”

“I must get something to do,” I confessed.

“Sit down then,” said Mr. Minikin. “Rest while you can.”

I sat. There was only the chair I took and the one he occupied.

“Apart from his being a bit of a driver,” I asked, “what sort of a man is he? Is he pleasant?”

“Never saw him put out but once,” answered Minikin.

That sounded, on the whole, hopeful. “When was that?” I asked.

“All the time I’ve known him.”

My spirits sank lower. Had I been left alone with Minikin a minute longer, I might have followed his counsel and “hooked it” before Mr. Lott arrived. But at that moment I heard the other door open and someone enter the private office. Then a bell rang, and Minikin disappeared, leaving the communicating door ajar. The conversation that came through was as follows:

“Why isn’t Mr. Skeat here?”

“Because he hasn’t come.”

“Where are the letters?”

“Under your nose.”

“How dare you answer me like that?”

“Well, it’s the truth. They are under your nose.”

“Did you give Thorneycroft’s man my message?”

“Yes.”

“What did he answer?”

“Said you were a liar.”

“Oh, he did, did he! What did you reply?”

“Asked him to tell me something I didn’t know.”

“Thought that clever, didn’t you?”

“Not bad.”

Whatever faults Mr. Lott might have, he possessed, I concluded, the virtue of self-control.

“Anybody been here?”

“Yes.”

“Who?”

“Mr. Kelver—Mr. Paul Kelver.”

“Kelver, Kelver. Who’s Kelver?”

“Know what he is—a fool.”

“What do you mean?”
