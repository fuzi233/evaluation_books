Mr. Warrington, being young, flush of pocket, and resolved to play the man of the world because the town expected it, could not do less than set open his hand to his country neighbours, and give them an entertainment at the Assembly Rooms. It was the custom; and custom, like a well-seasoned dish, has a flavour that even those who pretend to scorn it cannot but taste. So he bade almost all the company remaining at the Wells; and the Rooms that night were portioned, as a housewife portions her work, into their several uses.

In one chamber the card-tables were spread—green cloths smoothed, corners weighted, counters laid ready, and candles trimmed so that the wax might not gutter upon the stakes. In another, the supper was ordered, with champagne in such profusion as a gentleman newly enriched believes to be the very proof of liberality; with bowls of negus, too—hot wine sweetened and spiced, sending up a steam of citrus and nutmeg that mingled with the warmed scent of beeswax and the faint, damp odour the company carried in from the walks. In the great room the dance was set; and at the farther end, in a little balcony, the music perched—harp, fiddle, flageolet—like three small birds compelled to sing all night whether they would or no.

There is a particular sound in such a place, and it governs the spirits as surely as weather governs rheumatism: the scrape of shoes on boards, the click of fans, the small coughs checked behind lace, the low, continuous rustle of silk that seems almost like wind passing through dry corn. Over all comes the thin, persevering thread of the instruments—tuneful, yet oddly plaintive; and one may marvel how men and women could ever have called such airs merry. The flageolet, especially, had a note like a child whistling in a cold passage. Yet they courted to it; they laughed under it; they made whole futures out of those melancholy measures, as farmers sow in a grey spring and trust the sun will come.

The beginning was as homely and ceremonious as our forefathers’ pleasure always was. First minuets were called, and two or three couple performed them with the gravity of people who believe a step to be a moral act. The spinsters of the highest rank went out for this dignified exercise; and as Lady Maria Esmond, being an earl’s daughter, was the highest in place of all present (saving only Lady Augusta Crutchley, who by reason of lameness could not stand up), Mr. Warrington led her forth first. He acquitted himself very prettily, and with more ease than Mr. Wolfe, who had Miss Lowther; and this was remarked with approval by the whole room, which loves to judge such matters, as it judges custards and sauces, with a keen palate and very little charity.

When Lady Maria’s minuet was done, Mr. Warrington begged Miss Theo Lambert to do him the honour of the next. The girl blushed—one could see the colour spread as if a warm hand had passed beneath her skin—and she went through her steps with a shy exactness that delighted her parents. It delighted them the more because it enraged Miss Humpleby of Liphook, who had expected, at least, to stand up after my Lady Maria, and now sat biting her lip with that peculiar sourness which is envy’s own taste.

After the minuets came the country dances; and the melancholy little band fell to thrumming through tune after tune. Between the sets, the room filled with the noises of breath and talk and laughter; then, at the first scraping summons of the fiddle, the boards again took the cadence of feet. There was even something like a domestic comfort in it, as if, for all the grandeur of titles and the sharpness of tongues, the company were no more than a great household keeping holiday.

Mr. Warrington had, among his guests, the honour of a duchess: the Duchess of Queensberry, heroine of those who write and whisper about great ladies as eagerly as cooks discuss great kitchens. Her Grace came with her usual insolence of manner—turning her back, laughing loudly, glancing over her shoulder, and pointing her fan as if it were a skewer—because a countess was present whom she chose to hate. Yet, for all the duchess’s fireworks, almost all the company pushed and bowed and cringed and smiled and backed, not before the duchess, but before that other lady; as if a whole room could be drawn by one magnet, and the rest of the iron left cold.

For this was the Countess of Yarmouth—Walmoden—the favourite whom his Majesty George the Second delighted to honour. In the morning, upon the walks, she had been mighty gracious to young Harry; had promised him a game at cards that night; and purblind old Colonel Blinkinsop, believing the invitation meant for him, had bowed till he was like to fall over. “Pooh! pooh!” the countess had cried, “I don’t mean you. I mean the young Firshinian!” and the whole place had congratulated Mr. Warrington upon his fortune, as if it were a dish newly set before him, hot and savoury, which others must smell but not taste.

So at night, out of loyalty doubtless—and out of that baser appetite which always sniffs round power as readily as round supper—everybody swarmed to my Lady Yarmouth. My Lord Bamborough was eager to make her party at quadrille. My Lady Blanche Pendragon, model of virtue; Sir Lancelot Quintain, pattern of knighthood; Mr. Dean of Ealing, exemplary divine; and a crowd besides—nobles, officers, matrons, and prim spinsters—waited for her smile, watched for a beck, sprang up at the least sign to join her table.

Lady Maria attended the countess with meek respect. Madame de Bernstein treated the Hanoverian lady with a gravity as deep as her curtsey. Harry’s bow had been no lower than hospitality required; but it was bow enough to kindle Miss Hester Lambert’s indignation. She scarce spoke a word to her partner while they danced; and when he led her towards the supper-room, she was no more communicative than a girl who has set herself to punish.

To reach the refreshments they must pass close by Madame Walmoden’s table. The countess, in good humour, called out to her host as he went by, and asked if his “breddy liddle bardner liked tanzing?”

“I thank your ladyship, I don’t like tanzing, and I don’t like cards,” says Miss Hester, tossing her head; and, dropping a curtsey as stiff and tasteless as a lump of cheese, she strutted away.

Mr. Warrington felt as if a hot spoon had been thrust into him. Sarcasm from young to old pained him; flippancy towards himself hurt him. He was simple in his courtesy, and because it was simple he believed it should be returned. Hetty knew perfectly well what offence she gave; she could see displeasure redden on his honest face with a sidelong glance; and yet she kept on, wearing the most ingenuous smile, as some children keep on nibbling at forbidden sweets while watching the rod.

When they reached the sideboard—the table crowded with cut-glass, silver, little piles of ratafias, and dishes from which rose the warm, eggy sweetness of custards—she said, as artlessly as if she had been praising the music, “What a horrid, vulgar old woman that is; don’t you think so?”

“What woman?” asked Harry.

“That German woman—my Lady Yarmouth—to whom all the men are bowing and cringing.”

“Her ladyship has been very kind to me,” says Harry, grimly. “Won’t you have some of this custard?”

“And you have been bowing to her, too! You look as if your negus was not nice,” continues Miss Hetty, with that cruel innocence.

“It is not very good negus,” says Harry, swallowing more than the bad liquor.

“And the custard is bad too! I declare ’tis made with bad eggs!” cries Miss Lambert, tasting, and flinging her displeasure as lightly as she flung her words.

“I wish, Hester, that the entertainment and the company had been better to your liking,” says poor Harry.

“’Tis very unfortunate; but I dare say you could not help it,” she answers, tossing her little curly head.

Something in him clenched—fists, teeth, pride, and patience all together. She went on, still in that tone that pretended kindness. “You seem disturbed: shall we go to my mamma?”

“Yes—let us go to your mamma,” cries Mr. Warrington, with eyes that had fire in them; and to an unlucky waiter who got between him and his path he broke out with a “Curse you, why are you always standing in the way?”

“La! Is that the way you speak in Virginia?” asks Miss Pertness.

“We are rough there sometimes, madam, and can’t help being disturbed,” he answers slowly, with a quiver through his whole frame, looking down upon her as if she were some bright, stinging insect.

Hetty, for her part, saw nothing distinctly afterwards until she came to her mother. Never had she seen Harry look so handsome, so noble. Mrs. Lambert, with the quick anxiety of all frightened mothers, cried, “You look pale, child!”

“’Tis the cold—no, I mean the heat. Thank you, Mr. Warrington,” and the girl made him a faint curtsey, while Harry bowed a tremendous bow and went away amongst his guests, scarcely knowing what he did, so hot was his blood.

He was roused from his own anger by another—an altercation between his aunt, Madame de Bernstein, and the Duchess of Queensberry. As the royal favourite passed, the duchess gave her an awful stare with eyes no longer so bright as in the old days when they “set the world on fire;” turned to her neighbour with an affected laugh; and, with fan and giggle, poured a ceaseless rain of sneers upon the jolly Hanoverian. The countess pursued her cards, not knowing—or not choosing to know—how she was being gibed at. There had been feud of many years between the duchess and the family on the throne; and the duchess’s malice, like strong spice, kept its savour long.

“How you all bow down to the idol! Don’t tell me! You are as bad as the rest, my good Madame Bernstein!” cries her Grace. “Ah, what a true Christian country this is! and how your dear first husband, the Bishop, would have liked to see such a sight!”

“Forgive me, if I fail quite to understand your Grace,” Madame de Bernstein answered, very grave.

“We are both growing old, my good Bernstein—or perhaps we won’t understand when we don’t choose to understand. That is the way with us women, my good young Iroquois.”

“Your Grace remarked that it was a Christian country,” said Madame de Bernstein, “and I failed to perceive the point of the remark.”

“Point?—Indeed, my good creature, there is very little point in it! I meant we were such good Christians because we were so forgiving. Don’t you remember reading, when you were young—or your husband the Bishop reading, when he was in the pulpit—how, when a woman amongst the Jews was caught doing wrong, the Pharisees were for stoning her out of hand? Far from stoning such a woman now, look how fond we are of her! Any man in this room would go round it on his knees if yonder woman bade him. Yes, Madame Walmoden—you may look up from your cards with your great painted face and frown with your great painted eyebrows at me. You know I am talking about you; and I intend to go on talking about you too. I say any man here would go round the room on his knees if you bade him!”

“I think, madam, I know two or three who wouldn’t!” says Mr. Warrington, with spirit.

“Quick, let me hug them to my heart of hearts!” cries the duchess. “Which are they? Bring ’em to me, my dear Iroquois! Let us have a game of four—of honest men and women; that is to say, if we can find a couple more partners, Mr. Warrington!”

“Here are we three,” says the Baroness de Bernstein, with a forced laugh; “let us play a dummy.”

“Pray, madam, where is the third?” asks the old duchess, looking round.

“Madam!” cries Madame Walmoden at last, the colour rising under her paint, “I leave your Grace to boast of your honesty, which I have no doubt is spotless: but I will thank you not to doubt mine before my own relatives and children!”

“See how she fires up at a word! I am sure, my dear creature, you are quite as honest as most of the company,” returns the duchess. “Which may not be good enough for her Grace the Duchess of Queensberry and Dover, who, to be sure, might have stayed away in such a case; but it is the best my nephew could get, madam, and his best he has given you. You look astonished, Harry, my dear—and well you may. He is not used to our ways, madam.”

“Madam, he has found an aunt who can teach him our ways, and a great deal more!” cries the duchess, rapping her fan. “She will teach him to try and make all his guests welcome, old or young, rich or poor. That is the Virginian way, isn’t it, Harry? She will tell him, when Catherine Hyde is angry with his old aunt, that they were friends as girls, and ought not to quarrel now they are old women. And she will not be wrong, will she, Duchess?”

Whereupon Madame de Bernstein made the other dowager a superb curtsey, as if she had laid a cloth smooth over a stain; and the battle that seemed ready to blaze passed away into smoke.

Next morning, when Harry talked over the night’s work with his tutor, the chaplain cried, “Egad, it was like Byng and Galissonière!”

“Seventy-fours at least—both of ’em!” laughs Harry. “But the Baroness declined the battle, and sailed out of fire with inimitable skill.”

“Why should she be afraid? I have heard you say my aunt is as witty as any woman alive, and need fear the tongue of no dowager in England.”

“Hem! Perhaps she had good reasons for being peaceable!” Sampson answered; and his handsome eyes had a look which said he knew more than he would tell. Poor Bernstein’s reputation, cracked long ago, was such that any jests thrown at Madame Walmoden might splash her too; and in such a world the innocent are often punished by the very words meant for others.

“Sir,” cried Harry, in amazement, “you don’t mean to say there is anything against the character of my aunt, the Baroness de Bernstein!”

The chaplain stared at him with such wonder that Harry saw there must be old stories, and charges not to be spoken. “Good heavens!” he groaned, “are there two then in the family, who are—?”

“Which two?” asked the chaplain; and Harry stopped short, blushing to the temples, bit his lip, and was silent, remembering too well whence he had learned of another family offender.

“Bygones are always unpleasant things, Mr. Warrington,” said Sampson at last. “We had best hold our peace regarding them. No man or woman can live long in this wicked world without some scandal attaching. We cannot escape calumny, my dear young friend—you have had proof enough of that in your brief stay amongst us. But we can have clear consciences, and that is the main point!” And he lifted his eyes upward, as if his conscience were as white as the ceiling.

“Has there been anything very wrong, then, about my Aunt Bernstein?” Harry persisted, remembering how his mother at home had never spoken of the Baroness.

“O sancta simplicitas!” the chaplain muttered. “Stories, my dear sir, much older than your time or mine. Stories such as are told about everybody—de me, de te; and you know with what degree of truth in your own case.”

“Confound the villain! I should like to hear any scoundrel say a word against the dear old lady,” cried Harry. “Why, this world, parson, is full of lies and scandal!”

“And you are just beginning to find it out,” said the clergyman, with that beatified air which sat on him as naturally as a napkin on a well-trained servant.

At this moment Colonel Lambert came in to pay a morning visit; and behind him loitered Mr. Gumbo, who moved through life as if he were carrying a brimming bowl. Harry—suiting action to speech—had just been tweaking the nose of Calumny with his fist on the table.

“The ladies are purely. Whose nose were you pulling when I came in, Mr. Warrington?” says the Colonel, laughing.

“Isn’t it a shame, sir? The parson here was telling me there are villains who attack the character of my aunt, the Baroness of Bernstein!”

“You don’t mean to say so!” cries Mr. Lambert.

“I tell Mr. Harry that everybody is calumniated!” says the chaplain with clerical intonation; and at the same time he looks at Lambert and winks, as much as to say, He knows nothing—keep him so.

“Yes,” says Lambert, taking the hint, “the jaws of slander are for ever wagging. Witness that story about the dancing-girl that we all believed against you, Harry Warrington.”

“What, all, sir?”

“No, not all. One didn’t—Hetty didn’t. You should have heard her standing up for you, Harry, t’other day, when somebody—a little bird—brought us another story about you; about a game at cards on Sunday morning, when you and a friend of yours might have been better employed.” And here the Colonel cast a look of humour and reproof at the chaplain.

“Faith, I own it, sir!” says Sampson. “It was mea culpa, mea maxima—no, mea minima culpa—only the rehearsal of an old game at piquet which we had been talking over.”

“And did Miss Hester stand up for me?” asks Harry.

“Miss Hester did. But why that wondering look?” says Lambert.

“She scolded me last night like—like anything,” Harry answers, with the blunt honesty that makes its own trouble. “I never heard a young girl go on so. She made fun of everybody—hit about at young and old—so that I couldn’t help telling her that in our country, leastways in Virginia (they say the Yankees are very pert), young people don’t speak of their elders so. And, do you know, sir, we had a sort of quarrel; and I’m very glad you’ve told me she spoke kindly of me.” And he shook Lambert’s hand, cheeks glowing, eyes moist with boyish readiness.

“You won’t come to much hurt if you find no worse enemy than Hester, Mr. Warrington,” said the Colonel gravely, and looked at the young man with a deep interest. Is he fond of her? he thought. And how fond? Miss Het has been playing her tricks. He is a fine honest lad; God bless him.

“And the young lady of the minuet?” asked the chaplain smoothly. “She distinguished herself to perfection: the whole room admired. I trust Miss—Miss—”

“Miss Theodosia is perfectly well, and ready to dance at this minute with your reverence,” says Lambert. “Or stay, Chaplain, perhaps you only dance on Sunday?”

Then the Colonel turned again to Harry. “You paid your court very neatly to the great lady, Mr. Flatterer. My Lady Yarmouth has been trumpeting your praises at the Pump Room. She says she has got a leedel boy in Hannover dat is wery like you, and you are a sharming young mans.”

“If her ladyship were a queen, people could scarcely be more respectful to her,” says the chaplain.

“Let us call her a vice-queen, parson,” says Lambert, with a twinkle.

“Her Majesty pocketed forty of my guineas at quadrille,” cries Harry, laughing.

“She will play you on the same terms another day. The Countess is fond of play, and she wins from most people,” Lambert answered drily.

“Why don’t you bet her ladyship five thousand on a bishopric, parson? I have heard of a clergyman who made such a bet—and lost it—and paid it—and got the bishopric.”

“Ah! who will lend me the five thousand? Will you, sir?” asks the chaplain.

“No, sir! I won’t give her five thousand to be made Commander-in-Chief or Pope of Rome,” says Lambert stoutly. “I fling no stones at the woman; but I bow no knee to her, as I see a pack of rascals do. No offence—I don’t mean you. And I don’t mean Harry Warrington, who was quite right to be civil to her and to lose his money with good-humour.”

Then the Colonel’s face changed to that look men have when they are about to take a leave that matters. “Harry, I am come to bid thee farewell, my boy. We have had our pleasuring—my money is run out, and we must jog back to Oakhurst. Will you ever come and see the old place again?”

“Now, sir, now! I’ll ride back with you!” cries Harry eagerly.

“Why—no—not now,” Lambert answered, hurried. “We haven’t got room—that is, we’re—we’re expecting some friends.” (And under his breath: The Lord forgive me for the lie.) “But you’ll come to us when—when Tom’s at home. Yes, when Tom’s at home. That will be famous fun. And I’d have you know my wife and I love you sincerely, sir—and so do the girls, however much they scold you. And if ever you are in a scrape—and such things have happened, Mr. Chaplain—you will please to count upon me. Mind that, sir!”

He would have taken his leave then and there; but Harry followed him down the stairs, and would not be denied saying good-bye to the ladies. Yet instead of going straight to Lambert’s lodging, the two gentlemen turned towards the common; and from Harry’s windows Mr. Sampson saw them walking there, deep in earnest talk. Lambert smiled first, and looked roguish; then, as the talk went on, he flung up both hands and made gestures of surprise and agitation.

“The boy is telling him,” thought the chaplain.

When Mr. Warrington returned an hour later, he found his reverence sunk in the composition of a sermon, as if ink could wash away knowledge. Harry’s face was grave and melancholy. He flung down his hat, dropped into a chair, and something like an execration broke from him.

“The young ladies are going, and our heart is affected?” says the chaplain, looking up.

“Heart!” sneers Harry. “Which of the young ladies is the conqueror, sir? I thought the youngest’s eyes followed you about at your ball.”

“Confound the little termagant!” Harry broke out. “What does she mean by being so pert to me? She treats me as if I was a fool!”

“And no man is, sir, with a woman!” says Sampson, dipping his pen.

“Ain’t they, Chaplain?” growls Harry, and then adds more words of inward disquiet which were not fit for a pulpit, nor for any well-ordered table.

“By the way, have you heard anything of your lost property?” the chaplain asked presently.

“No!” says Harry, with another oath that would not bear repeating.

“I begin to suspect there was more money than you like to own in that book. I wish I could find some.”

“There were notes in it,” Harry said gloomily, “and—and papers that I am very sorry to lose. What the deuce has come of it? I had it when we dined together.”

“I saw you put it in your pocket,” cried the chaplain. “I saw you take it out and pay at the toy-shop a bill for a gold thimble and workbox for one of your young ladies. Of course you have asked there, sir?”

“Of course I have,” says Harry, sunk in his ill-humour as in a bog.

“Gumbo put you to bed—at least, if I remember right. I was so cut myself that I scarce remember anything. Can you trust those black fellows, sir?”

“I can trust him with my head,” says Harry; and then, as if the words had bitten him, he groaned, “With my head? I can’t trust myself with it.”

“‘Oh, that a man should put an enemy into his mouth to steal away his brains!’” quotes the chaplain.

“You may well call it an enemy,” says Harry. “Hang it, I have a great mind to make a vow never to drink another drop! A fellow says anything when he is in drink.”

The chaplain laughed. “You, sir, are close enough!” he said; and indeed, for the last few days, no amount of wine would unseal Mr. Warrington’s lips when Sampson, by chance or art, touched upon the subject of his patron’s loss.

“And so the little country nymphs are gone, or going?” the chaplain resumed. “They were nice, fresh little things; but I think the mother was the finest woman of the three. I declare, a woman at five-and-thirty or so is at her prime. What do you say, sir?”

Mr. Warrington looked askance at him for a moment, as if he tasted something sour in the air.

“Confound all women, I say!” muttered the young misogynist; for which sentiment every well-conditioned person will surely rebuke him.
