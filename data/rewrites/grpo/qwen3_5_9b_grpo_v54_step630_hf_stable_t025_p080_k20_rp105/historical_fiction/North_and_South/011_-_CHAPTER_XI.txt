And a grain or two perhaps is good;
But his, he makes me harshly feel,
Has got a little too much of steel."

"Margaret!" Mr. Hale called out as he guided his guest downstairs, his tone betraying a flicker of concern. "I couldn't help watching your face with some anxiety when Mr. Thornton confessed to being a shop-boy. I knew the truth from Mr. Bell beforehand, so I expected nothing less than to see you rise abruptly and flee the room."

"Oh, papa! Did you truly think me so foolish?" Margaret replied, her voice lightening. "I found that admission far more endearing than anything else he said. Everything else grated on me due to its hardness, yet he spoke of himself with such simplicity, devoid of the pretense that often marks the vulgarity of shopkeepers, and with such tender reverence for his mother. In that moment, I felt less inclined to leave than when he boasted of Milton as if no other place existed, or when he dismissed others for their careless improvidence without considering it his duty to offer them the guidance his mother had given him. No, his confession of humble origins was the very thing I admired most."

"I am surprised, Margaret," her mother interjected. "You who have always accused people of being 'shoppy' at Helstone! I do not think, Mr. Hale, you were right to introduce such a person without disclosing his background. I was genuinely terrified of showing how shocked I was by certain remarks. His father 'dying in miserable circumstances'—one might have assumed it was in a workhouse."

"I cannot be certain it was not worse," her husband answered gravely. "Mr. Bell informed me of much regarding his past before we arrived. Since he has shared a portion, I shall complete the picture. His father speculated recklessly, failed, and then took his own life, unable to bear the disgrace. All former friends recoiled from the revelations of his dishonest gambling—wild, desperate struggles using other people's money to recover his modest fortune. No one stepped forward to aid the mother and this boy. There was another child, a girl, too young to work, yet she had to be supported. Initially, no friend came forward, and Mrs. Thornton, I suspect, is not the type to wait for tardy kindness. They left Milton. I knew he had entered a shop, and that his earnings, combined with a small inheritance for his mother, sustained them for years. Mr. Bell noted they subsisted on water-porridge for a long time—how, he could not say. Long after creditors had abandoned hope of repayment following the father's suicide, this young man returned to Milton. He went quietly to each creditor, paying the first installment owed. There was no clamor, no assembly of debtors; it was done silently, yet eventually, everything was settled. This was aided significantly by one creditor, a grumpy old man, who took Mr. Thornton in as a partner."

"That is truly admirable," Margaret remarked. "What a pity such a nature should be tarnished by his position as a Milton manufacturer."

"How so?" her father asked.

"Oh, papa, by his tendency to judge everything by the standard of wealth. When he discussed mechanical powers, he clearly viewed them merely as new methods to expand trade and accumulate money. And the poor men around him—they were poor because they were vicious, excluded from his sympathies because they lacked his 'iron nature' and the capabilities it bestowed for becoming rich."

"Not vicious; he used the words 'improvident and self-indulgent,'" her father corrected gently.

Margaret was gathering her mother's sewing materials, preparing for bed. Just as she turned to leave, she hesitated. She felt compelled to make an acknowledgment that would please her father, though to be entirely truthful, it required admitting a degree of personal dislike.

"Papa, I consider Mr. Thornton a remarkably capable man; however, personally, I do not like him at all."

"And I do!" her father laughed. "Personally, as you term it, and all. I do not elevate him to the status of a hero or similar figure. But good night, child. Your mother appears exceedingly fatigued tonight, Margaret."

Margaret had observed her mother's worn appearance with growing alarm. Her father's comment sent her to her room with a heavy, dim fear settling upon her heart. Life in Milton differed starkly from her existence in Helstone, where she was constantly immersed in fresh, open air. Here, the air seemed stripped of all revitalizing quality. Domestic worries pressed upon the women of the family with such intensity and in such sordid forms that there was legitimate cause to fear her mother's health was deteriorating seriously. There were other ominous signs as well. She and Dixon engaged in secretive consultations in her bedroom, from which Dixon emerged crying and irritable, as was her custom whenever her mistress's distress demanded sympathy. Once, Margaret entered the chamber shortly after Dixon departed and found her mother on her knees. As she slipped away, she caught a few words that were clearly a prayer for strength and patience to endure severe physical suffering. Margaret yearned to restore the bond of intimate confidence severed by her long absence at her aunt Shaw's, striving with gentle touches and softened words to reclaim a place in her mother's heart. Yet, though she received affection in abundance, sufficient to gladden her former self, she sensed a secret withheld, one she believed pertained seriously to her mother's health. She lay awake late that night, devising ways to mitigate the harmful influence of their Milton life. She considered hiring a servant to assist Dixon permanently, freeing her to dedicate her time to finding a suitable replacement for her mother's care.

For several days, visiting registration offices and encountering a myriad of unlikely individuals consumed Margaret's time and thoughts. One afternoon, she encountered Bessy Higgins in the street and stopped to speak.

"Well, Bessy, how are you? Better, I hope, now the wind has changed."

"Better and not better, if you understand what that implies," Bessy replied.

"Not exactly," Margaret smiled.

"I am better in that I am no longer torn apart by coughing all night, but I am weary and tired of Milton, longing to escape to the Land of Beulah. And when I realize I am drifting further and further away, my heart sinks, and I am no better; I am worse."

Margaret turned to walk beside the girl in her feeble progress home. For a moment or two, she remained silent. Finally, she whispered, "Bessy, do you wish to die?" For she, too, shrank from death, clinging to life with the natural vigor of youth and health.

Bessy was silent for a moment. Then she answered, "If you had lived the life I have, grown as weary of it as I have, and occasionally thought, 'perhaps it will last fifty or sixty years—it does for some,' and felt dizzy and dazed, and sick, as each of those sixty years seemed to spin around me, mocking me with its endless hours and minutes and fragments of time—oh, wench! I tell you, you would be glad enough when the doctor said he feared you would never see another winter."

"Why, Bessy, what sort of life has yours been?"

"Nought worse than many others', I reckon. Only I fretted against it, and they did not."

"But what was it? You know, I am a stranger here, so perhaps I am not as quick to understand your meaning as someone who has lived all their life in Milton."

"If you had come to our house when you promised, I might have told you. But father says you are just like the rest of them; it is out of sight, out of mind with you."

"I do not know who the rest are; and I have been very busy. To tell the truth, I had forgotten my promise—"

"You offered it; we asked for nothing," Bessy retorted.

"I had forgotten what I said for the time," Margaret continued calmly. "I would have remembered again when I was less occupied. May I accompany you now?"

Bessy cast a quick glance at Margaret's face to gauge the sincerity of the wish. The sharpness in her eyes softened into a wistful longing as she met Margaret's gentle, friendly gaze.

"I have no one else to care for me; if you care, you may come."

Thus, they walked on in silence. As they turned up into a small court from a squalid street, Bessy said, "You will not be daunted if father is at home and speaks a bit gruffly at first. He has taken a liking to you, you see, and he values your visit greatly; and precisely because he likes you, he was vexed and troubled."

"Do not fear, Bessy."

Yet Nicholas was not present when they entered. A large, unkempt girl, younger than Bessy but taller and stronger, was busy at the wash-tub, knocking about the furniture in a rough, capable manner, creating such a racket that Margaret shrank back, sympathetic to poor Bessy, who sat down on the first chair as if utterly exhausted by the walk. Margaret asked the sister for a cup of water. While she ran to fetch it, knocking over fire-irons and stumbling over a chair in her haste, she unfastened Bessy's bonnet strings to relieve her labored breathing.

"Do you think such a life as this is worth caring for?" Bessy gasped finally. Margaret said nothing but held the water to her lips. Bessy took a long, feverish draught and then collapsed, closing her eyes. Margaret heard her muttering to herself: "They shall hunger no more, neither thirst any more: neither shall the sun light on them, nor any heat."

Margaret leaned over and said, "Bessy, do not be impatient with your life, whatever it is—or may have been. Remember who gave it you and shaped it as it is!"

She was startled by the sound of Nicholas speaking behind her; he had entered unnoticed.

"Now, I will not have my wench preached to. She is bad enough as it is, with her dreams and her Methodist fancies and her visions of cities with golden gates and precious stones. But if it amuses her, I let it be; I am not going to have more nonsense poured into her head."

"But surely," Margaret turned to face him, "you believe in what I said, that God gave her life and ordained the kind of life it would be?"

"I believe what I see, and no more. That is my belief, young woman. I do not believe everything I hear—not by a long shot. I did hear a young lass make a fuss about knowing where we lived and coming to see us. My wench here thought a great deal about it and flushed many times when she little knew I was watching her at the sound of a strange step. But she has come at last—and she is welcome, as long as she keeps from preaching on what she knows nothing about."

Bessy had been watching Margaret's face; she half rose to speak now, laying her hand on Margaret's arm in an entreaty. "Do not be vexed with him—many think like him; many, many do here. If you could hear them speak, you would not be shocked at him; he is a rare good man, is father—but oh!" she fell back in despair, "what he says at times makes me long to die more than ever, for I want to know so many things and am so tossed about with wonder."

"Poor wench—poor old wench—I am loath to vex you, I am; but a man must speak out for the truth. When I see the world going all wrong at this time of day, bothering itself with things it knows nothing about, and leaving undone all the things that lie in disorder close at its hand—why, I say, leave all this talk about religion alone and set to work on what you see and know. That is my creed. It is simple, not far to reach, nor hard to work."

But the girl only pleaded with Margaret the more.

"Do not think hardly on him—he is a good man, he is. I sometimes think I shall be moped with sorrow even in the City of God if father is not there." A feverish color rose in her cheek, and a feverish flame ignited in her eye. "But you will be there, father! You shall! Oh! my heart!" She clutched her chest and turned ghastly pale.

Margaret held her in her arms, resting the weary head upon her bosom. She lifted the thin, soft hair from the temples and bathed them with water. Nicholas understood all her gestures for different articles with the quickness of love, and even the wide-eyed sister moved with laborious gentleness at Margaret's "hush!" Presently, the spasm that foreshadowed death passed, and Bessy roused herself to say, "I'll go to bed—it's the best place; but," catching at Margaret's gown, "you'll come again—I know you will—but just say it!"

"I will come again tomorrow," Margaret promised.

Bessy leaned back against her father, who prepared to carry her upstairs. But as Margaret rose to leave, he struggled to say something: "I could wish there were a God, if it were only to ask Him to bless thee."

Margaret departed very sad and thoughtful.

She was late for tea at home. At Helstone, unpunctuality at meal-times was a grave fault in her mother's eyes; but now, this, along with many other minor irregularities, seemed to have lost its power to irritate, and Margaret almost longed for the old complaints.

"Have you met with a servant, dear?"

"No, mamma; that Anne Buckley would never have done."

"Suppose I try," Mr. Hale suggested. "Everybody else has had their turn at this great difficulty. Now let me try. I may be the Cinderella to put on the slipper after all."

Margaret could hardly smile at this little joke, so oppressed was she by her visit to the Higginses.
