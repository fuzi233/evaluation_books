The summer day hung heavy and soft, bright as polished gold, not a single breeze stirring the rose trellises or a wisp of cloud dimming the wide blue sky. Caroline had spent every hour of it alone, her uncle off on business in Whinbury, and the long, silent stretches of time felt as vast and empty as the Sahara wastes, even with the scent of jasmine drifting through the rectory garden. Curled in her favourite window alcove, her half-finished sewing for the parish charity basket heavy on her lap, her fingers moving mechanically over the needle, her mind wandered restlessly, chasing the same soft, unspoken thought that had haunted her every day since her uncle’s falling out with Robert Moore.

When Fanny popped around the corner of the house, craning over the lawn to spot her, and called her name, Caroline answered with a quiet murmur, and the maid hurried over, pressing a crumpled sealed note into her hands. Caroline’s fingers felt so numb she nearly let it slip between the folds of her work. “Harry Scott brought it over from the Hollow,” Fanny told her.

The name jolted her wide awake, as if someone had tapped her shoulder and breathed hope back into her chest. She lifted her head fast, eyes bright with sudden, sharp energy. “From the Hollow?” She snatched the dropped note before it could slide off her lap, tore through the seal in a single motion, and devoured the words in two seconds flat. It was a warm, affectionate note from Hortense – she was home from Wormwood Wells, alone for the day because Robert had left for Whinbury market, and there was nothing she wanted more than for Caroline to come over for tea. She added, with the practical affection only Hortense could muster, that Caroline must be bored out of her mind stuck at the rectory with no decent company since the rift between their families, and she should put her bonnet on and run over immediately.

Caroline didn’t need to be told twice. She set aside the little child’s slip she’d been trimming with braid, flew up the stairs, tucked her loose curls under her plain straw bonnet, and wrapped her soft black silk scarf around her shoulders – its dark fabric set off her white summer dress and the pink flush rising in her cheeks perfectly, and she didn’t waste a second checking her reflection before she was out the door. This was the escape she’d ached for for weeks, away from the quiet sadness of the empty rectory, away from the constant weight of missing Robert. The green lane sloping down to the Hollow smelled like wild honeysuckle and clover, sweeter than any hothouse rose, and every step felt like a little victory. She knew Robert was not home, of course, but just to step through the doors of the cottage he lived in, to sit in the chair he’d occupied that morning at breakfast, to breathe the same air he breathed, felt almost like a reunion. A tiny, giddy hope nuzzled at the back of her mind, soft as a peri’s wings brushing her cheek: what if he came home early? What if he walked through the door while she was there, and she could look right into his face, shake his hand, sit beside him for even just a minute? She chided the silly thought away, but she couldn’t stop smiling.

Hortense must have spotted the flash of Caroline’s white dress through the cottage garden shrubs, because she stepped out onto the porch to meet her, straight and dignified as ever, no giddy rush to her steps, but a soft smile tugging at her lips. She kissed Caroline’s cheek, hugged her gently, and led her inside, half charmed by the girl’s obvious delight – she would have sent Caroline right back to the gate if she’d guessed that all that joy was for her brother, not for her. No sister likes to imagine a sweet young girl has fallen head over heels for her brother, after all; it feels like a silly, foolish delusion, a crude waste of romance, until you get used to the idea.

“I told you in my note you’d find me alone,” Hortense said, as she guided Caroline toward the parlour door, “but I wrote it this morning. We had a visitor drop by after dinner.”

She pushed the door open, and Caroline’s eyes fell on a massive heap of crimson fabric overflowing the fireside armchair, topped by a cap so vast, frilled and ribboned with puffy “love-ribbon”, it looked like it could outrank a crown. Mrs. Yorke was sat there, the most formidable lady in the parish, who never bothered calling on anyone, who dismissed every local gossip as a silly idle woman – her stopping by for tea was as much a honour as if the queen herself had dropped in unannounced for a casual meal. She’d taken a strange liking to Hortense, after all, admired her steady, no-nonsense foreign ways, her plain, precise dress and unflappable temper – everything Mrs. Yorke despised in the flighty pretty young ladies of the parish.

Caroline stepped forward, shy, a little nervous – as the parson’s niece, she’d never gotten much warmth from Mrs. Yorke, and sure enough, the older woman greeted her with a cool, brief nod. She turned away to take off her bonnet, glad for the distraction, when a tiny figure in a blue frock with a bright sash popped up from the footstool hidden under the folds of that enormous red gown, ran at her, and threw her arms around her neck. “My mother’s not being nice to you,” Jessie Yorke said, planting a loud kiss on her cheek, “and Rose over there won’t even look up. They’re like that. If an angel with a star crown walked in here, mom would just nod stiffly, and Rose would never lift her head. But I like you, I’m gonna be your friend.”

“Jessie, stop that forward nonsense at once,” Mrs. Yorke called.

“But mom, you’re frozen!” Jessie protested. “Miss Helstone never did anything to you. Why are you always so dry and stiff to every nice young lady? Even Shirley Keeldar gets the same treatment. You’re gonna scare everyone away from Briarmains, Martin says so all the time.”

Rose, sat cross-legged on the carpet by the bookcase, half buried in a tattered copy of Mrs. Radcliffe’s *The Italian*, looked up just long enough to give a brief, quiet greeting, then dropped her gray eyes back to her book. Caroline, who always had a knack for reading quiet, unique souls, knelt down beside her, peeked over her shoulder at the page, and read along in silence. Before long, Rose glanced at her, and asked, “Are you ready for me to turn the leaf?”

They talked for an hour after that, about the book, about how the story made them feel like they were really under the bright blue Italian sky thousands of miles away. Rose, who was barely twelve, announced she was never going to be stuck in Briarfield forever – she was going to travel the whole world, see every corner of the planet, no matter what anyone said. “Your rectory’s like a windowed grave,” she told Caroline bluntly. “No noise, no movement, nothing ever changes. Monotony is almost the same as death.” She argued that hiding your talents away, doing nothing but the same small domestic chores for your whole life, was the same sin as burying your talent in a napkin – she’d trade four years of apprenticing at sewing and mending stockings first, but after that, she was making her own path.

Mrs. Yorke watched them, then turned to Caroline, starting in on her usual sharp, prodding remarks. She teased Caroline for being too romantic, too guided by her feelings, for looking “lackadaisical” like a novel heroine instead of a hardworking sensible woman who could survive the real world. Caroline stood her ground, gentle but firm, until Mrs. Yorke dropped the first subtle, cruel insinuation: she joked that Hortense was hiding Robert behind the curtains somewhere, that silly young girls would do anything to wheedle their way into a house where there was an eligible single brother.

The comment landed like a stab straight through Caroline’s chest. Her cheeks burned bright, her eyes dropped to her lap, and she felt cornered, humiliated, until the sharpness in her chest flared into quiet, steady anger. She lifted her head, her blue eyes clear and steady, the flush of shame fading from her face until it was pale and determined. “Mrs. Yorke,” she said, her voice clear as silver, no tremble in it at all, “you have no right to say those things. You don’t know a single thing about my relationship with Hortense and Robert, and those cruel, unprovoked insinuations are far craftier and more false than anything you’ve ever accused me of. My being quiet, liking books, not gossiping around the parish is none of your business. If you dislike me so much, you can keep that opinion to yourself, and if you ever insult me unprovoked like this again, I will answer far less gently.”

Mrs. Yorke didn’t get angry. She laughed that short, dry laugh of hers, and nodded approvingly. “You’ve got spirit in you, after all. Always speak that honestly, and you’ll do fine.”

“I don’t take advice poisoned by insinuation,” Caroline replied, still calm, still steady. “I will never speak that harshly to anyone unless they’ve insulted me first, unprovoked.”

Jessie clapped her hands, delighted. “Mom, you finally met your match!”

Rose never looked up from her book. “No she isn’t,” she said, quiet and matter-of-fact. “Miss Helstone let herself get vexed. Mom would wear her out in a week. Shirley Keeldar is the one who manages it – she wears armour under her silk dress that not even mom’s sharpest words can pierce.”
