The sun hung low, its golden light dimmed by the thick plumes of smoke rising from the distant volcano. Phorenice, soaked and shivering, wrung the water from her robes with an impatient twist. "Row harder, Deucalion. I want the comfort of my palace, not this wretched damp. That merchant will regret the loss of his fine silks—though I regret it more. A woman knows the value of beauty."

She cast me a sidelong glance, tossing back her damp red curls. "What do you think of my wisdom now, venturing out without guards?"

"The Empress is beyond reproach," I said, though my tone held less reverence than the old formula demanded.

Her lips curved. "At least you saw me fight. I caught your admiration once or twice. You’re thawing, Deucalion. A few more days in my company, and you might even learn to smile without prompting." She laughed, low and throaty. "Perhaps we’ll make a pair of lovers yet. I’m glad I lured those fishermen into attacking us."

So it had been a test—a staged battle to prove her skill, and perhaps to measure mine. Had we been any other man and woman, it might have been a game worth playing. But I thought of Nais, who had spared rebels out of mercy, while Phorenice squandered lives for sport.

I pushed the thought away. My duty was clear: to serve Atlantis, even if it meant binding myself to this woman.

The harbor loomed ahead, its massive chain stretched taut between the twin forts. The sentries, sluggish in the fading light, barely stirred until officers on the turrets recognized our shouts. Torches flared, illuminating our faces, and with a groan of metal, the chain sank beneath the waves to let us pass.

A galley waited within, oars poised. We boarded, shedding our sodden garments for dry robes provided by the fort’s officers. Phorenice, for all her divine pretensions, shivered like any mortal woman.

The galley cut swiftly across the calm harbor, mooring at the royal quay. As litters were brought, I watched the lantern-lit boat guarding her mammoth. The beast stood chest-deep in the water, trunk raised, its great body trembling. I felt no pity. The creature was a symbol of her excess, and I would not mourn its loss.

That night’s banquet was a quieter affair. The courtiers, sensing the shift in power, mimicked my restraint—less perfume, simpler dress, fewer extravagances. Phorenice noticed, leaning close with a whisper.

"You’ve tamed them already, Deucalion. No perfumes, no gauds. Even the men’s beards are less curled. If this continues, they’ll shave clean tomorrow in imitation of you."

"Let them," I said. "Cleanliness is no crime."

She arched a brow. "A ruler must keep some distinctions. If we look too much like our subjects, they forget why they obey."

I inclined my head. "A lesson I’ll remember."

She laughed, flicking my arm with her fingers. "Flatterer. You learn quickly."

But weariness soon dulled her sharpness. She dismissed the dancers mid-performance and sent the court poet away with a threat of the stick if his verses bored her. When we rose to leave, she leaned heavily on my arm, her breath warm against my cheek.

"You may kiss me, Deucalion," she murmured.

I did not.

She pulled away with a sigh. "Go, then. Your propriety is tiresome."

Alone in my chambers, I wasted no time. Nais’s fate hung in the balance, and I would not let her die for Phorenice’s vanity. The pyramid’s hidden passages, known only to the highest priests, would serve my purpose.

I navigated the labyrinthine corridors, emerging at last into the deserted temple of the Moon Goddess. Dust coated the altar, a testament to years of neglect. The great stone eye of the goddess swung open at my touch, revealing a hollow chamber within her head.

There, hidden in the shadows, lay three waxy green spheres—the fabled Sleep of the Moon. I took one, leaving the others undisturbed. Its power was absolute: a deathlike slumber, reversible only by the touch of one who knew the secret.

Returning through the dark, I found Ylga waiting in my chamber, her suspicion sharp as a blade.

"You’ve come to mock me further?" she challenged.

"No," I said. "To save Nais."

Her defiance faltered. "How?"

I showed her the green sphere. "This will spare her execution—if you help me."

For the first time, her eyes held something other than bitterness. Hope.

And so the plan was set.
