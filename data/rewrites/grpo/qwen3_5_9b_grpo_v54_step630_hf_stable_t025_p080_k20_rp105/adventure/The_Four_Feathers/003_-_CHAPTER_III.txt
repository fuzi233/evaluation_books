Durrance walked alone to his lodging, his mind drifting back two years to a peculiar whim of old Dermod Eustace that had dragged him unwillingly to the house by the Lennon River in Donegal. There, amidst his surprise, he had met Dermod's daughter, Ethne. She possessed a quality that stunned everyone who first spoke with her. Durrance had spent a night in the house, and throughout that evening, she had played her violin. Seated with her back to her audience, as was her custom, she shielded her performance from any distracting glance or gesture, guarding the purity of her concentration. The melodies she produced still rang in Durrance's ears. The girl held a rare gift; the strings of her violin answered the questions of her bow with startling clarity.

There was a particular piece, the *Melusine* overture, that seemed to hold the very sob of the waves. Durrance had listened in wonder that night, for the violin spoke to him of things the girl could not possibly know. It sang of long, perilous journeys and the faces of strange lands; of silver paths across moonlit seas and beckoning voices rising from the desert's edge. Its tone grew deeper, more mysterious, telling of great joys unattainable and griefs eternal, elevated by their magnitude into something noble. It spoke of longings beyond words, yet never a single note of mere complaint. That night, watching Ethne's face turned away, Durrance had drawn a lesson he struggled to keep clear in his mind: true music does not complain.

And so, when he rode into the Row the next morning, his blue eyes looked out upon the world from his bronzed face with no less of his usual friendliness. He waited at half-past nine by the clump of lilacs and laburnums at the end of the sand, but Harry Feversham did not join him. Nor did he for the next three weeks. Since graduating from Oxford, it had been their custom to meet at this specific spot and hour whenever both happened to be in town. Durrance felt a profound sense of loss, as if he had lost his friend entirely.

Yet, the rumors of war swelled into certainty. Finally, when Feversham kept the tryst, Durrance had news to share.

"I told you luck might look my way. Well, she has. I'm going out to Egypt on General Graham's staff. There's talk we may run down the Red Sea to Suakin afterward."

The exhilaration in his voice brought an unmistakable envy to Feversham's eyes. It struck Durrance as strange, even pleasant in that moment of good fortune, that Harry Feversham should envy him. But he interpreted the look through the lens of his own ambitions.

"It is rough on you," Durrance said sympathetically, "that your regiment has to stay behind."

Feversham rode by his friend's side in silence. As they reached the chairs beneath the trees, he spoke.

"That was expected. The day you dined with me, I sent in my papers."

"That night?" Durrance asked, turning in his saddle. "After we had gone?"

"Yes," Feversham replied, accepting the correction. He wondered if it had been intended, but Durrance rode on silently. Once again, Harry sensed a reproach in his friend's silence, and once again, he was mistaken. For Durrance suddenly spoke heartily, laughing aloud.

"I remember. You gave us your reasons that night. But for the life of me, I can't help wishing we were going out together. When do you leave for Ireland?"

"To-night."

"So soon?"

They turned their horses and rode westward again down the alley of trees. The morning air was still fresh. The limes and chestnuts retained their early green, and since May was late that year, their blossoms still hung delicately white like snow upon the branches, shining red against the dark rhododendrons. The park shimmered in a haze of sunlight, and the distant roar of the streets sounded like the tumbling of river water.

"It is a long time since we bathed in Sandford Lasher," Durrance said.

"Or froze in the Easter vacations in the big snow-gully on Great End," Feversham returned. Both men felt that on this morning, a volume in their book of life had ended. Since the volume had been pleasant to read, and they did not know if its successors would sustain its promise, they looked backward through the leaves before putting it finally away.

"You must stay with us, Jack, when you come back," Feversham said.

Durrance had schooled himself not to wince, and he did not, even at the anticipatory "us." If his left hand tightened upon the reins, the sign was undetectable to his friend.

"If I come back," Durrance said. "You know my creed. I could never pity a man who died on active service. I would very much like to come by that end myself."

It was a simple creed, consistent with the simplicity of the man who spoke it. It amounted to nothing more than this: that to die decently was worth many years of life. He uttered it without melancholy or foreboding. Still, he feared his friend might interpret the words differently, so he looked quickly into Feversham's face. He saw only that puzzling look of envy again.

"You see, there are worse things that can happen," Durrance continued. "Disablement, for instance. Clever men might manage to put up with it. But what in the world should I do if I had to sit in a chair all my days? It makes me shiver to think of it," he added, shaking his broad shoulders to shake off the fear. "Well, this is the last ride. Let us gallop," and he urged his horse forward.

Feversham followed suit, and side by side they raced down the sand. At the bottom of the Row, they stopped, shook hands, and with the curtest of nods parted. Feversham rode out of the park; Durrance turned back, walking his horse up toward the seats beneath the trees.

Even as a boy in his home at Southpool in Devonshire, upon a wooded creek of the Salcombe estuary, Durrance had always felt a certain restlessness—a desire to sail down that creek and out over the sea levels, dreaming of queer, outlandish countries and peoples beyond the dark, familiar woods. This restlessness had grown with him, so that "Guessens," even after inheriting its farms and lands, remained in his thoughts merely as a place to return to, not an estate to occupy a life. He purposely exaggerated this restlessness now, setting it against the words Feversham had spoken, which he knew to be true. Ethne Eustace would hardly be happy outside her county of Donegal. Therefore, even had things fallen out differently, as he phrased it, there might have been a clash. Perhaps it was best that Harry Feversham was to marry Ethne—and no one else.

Thus, he argued as he rode, until the riders vanished from his sight, leaving the ladies in their colored frocks beneath the cool of the trees. The trees themselves dwindled to ragged mimosas; the brown sand at his feet spread out in a widening circle, taking on the bright color of honey; and upon the empty sand, black stones began to heap themselves shapelessly like coal, flashing in the sun like mirrors. Deep in his anticipations of the Soudan, he heard his name called softly in a woman's voice. Looking up, he found himself close by the rails.

"How do you do, Mrs. Adair?" he said, stopping his horse. Mrs. Adair gave him her hand across the rails. She was Durrance's neighbor at Southpool, a year or two his elder—a tall woman remarkable for the many shades of her thick brown hair and the peculiar pallor of her face. But at this moment, her face had brightened; there was a hint of color in her cheeks.

"I have news for you," Durrance said. "Two special items. One, Harry Feversham is to be married."

"To whom?" the lady asked eagerly.

"You should know. It was in your house in Hill Street that Harry first met her; and I introduced him. He has been improving the acquaintance in Dublin."

But Mrs. Adair already understood; it was plain the news was welcome.

"Ethne Eustace!" she cried. "They will be married soon?"

"There is nothing to prevent it."

"I am glad," the lady sighed, as though with relief. "What is your second item?"

"As good as the first. I go out on General Graham's staff."

Mrs. Adair fell silent. A look of anxiety entered her eyes, and the color drained from her face.

"You are very glad, I suppose," she said slowly.

Durrance's voice left her in no doubt.

"I should think I was. I go soon, too, and the sooner the better. I will come and dine some night, if I may, before I go."

"My husband will be pleased to see you," Mrs. Adair said, rather coldly. Durrance did not notice the coldness. He had his own reasons for making the most of the opportunity that had come his way; he urged his enthusiasm, laying it bare in words more for his own benefit than for hers. Indeed, he had always had a vague impression of the lady. She was handsome in a queer, foreign way, not uncommon along the coasts of Devonshire and Cornwall, with good hair and always well-dressed. Moreover, she was friendly. At that point, Durrance's knowledge of her ended. Perhaps her chief merit in his eyes was that she had made friends with Ethne Eustace. But he was to become better acquainted with Mrs. Adair. He rode away from the park with the old regret in his mind that the fortunes of himself and his friend were finally severed that morning. In truth, he had that morning set the strands of a new rope a-weaving, destined to bring them together again in a strange and terrible relationship. Mrs. Adair followed him out of the park and walked home very thoughtfully.

Durrance had exactly one week to provide his equipment and arrange his estate in Devonshire. It passed in a continuous hurry of preparation, so that his newspaper lay each day unfolded in his rooms. The general was to travel overland to Brindisi; and so, on an evening of wind and rain toward the end of July, Durrance stepped from the Dover pier into the mail-boat for Calais. In spite of the rain and the gloomy night, a small crowd had gathered to give the general a send-off. As the ropes were cast off, a feeble cheer was raised. Before the cheer had ended, Durrance found himself beset by a strange illusion. Leaning upon the bulwarks, he idly wondered if this was his last view of England, wishing that one of his friends had come down to see him go, when it seemed suddenly that his wish was answered. He caught a glimpse of a man standing beneath a gas-lamp, a man of the stature and wearing the likeness of Harry Feversham.

Durrance rubbed his eyes and looked again. But the wind made the tongue of light flicker uncertainly within the glass; the rain blurred the quay. He could only be certain that a man stood there, vaguely distinguishing beneath the lamp the whiteness of a face. "It is an illusion," he told himself. "Harry Feversham is at that moment most likely listening to Ethne playing the violin under a clear sky in a high garden of Donegal."

But even as he turned from the bulwarks, there came a lull in the wind. The lights burned bright and steady on the pier, and the face leaped from the shadows, distinct in feature and expression. Durrance leaned out over the side of the boat.

"Harry!" he shouted, at the top of a wondering voice.

But the figure beneath the lamp never stirred. The wind blew the lights again this way and that; the paddles churned the water; the mail-boat passed beyond the pier. "It is an illusion," he repeated. "It is a coincidence. It is the face of a stranger very like to Harry Feversham's. It could not be Feversham's, because the face Durrance had seen so distinctly for a moment was a haggard, wistful face—a face stamped with extraordinary misery; the face of a man cast out from among his fellows."

Durrance had been very busy all that week. He had completely forgotten the arrival of that telegram and the suspense caused by its long perusal. His newspaper had lain unfolded in his rooms. But his friend Harry Feversham had come to see him off.
