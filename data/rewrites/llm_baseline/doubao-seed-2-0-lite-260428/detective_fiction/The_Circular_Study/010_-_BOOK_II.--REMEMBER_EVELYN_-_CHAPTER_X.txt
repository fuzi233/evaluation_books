“A blonde woman, you’re certain, sir?” Sweetwater leaned forward, his pen hovering over a scrap of case paper, as Mr. Gryce tapped the crumpled, frilled parasol laid out across his desk.

“Not the usual type of fair-haired girl,” the older detective corrected. “One of those thin, ethereal creatures you can scarcely imagine brushing up against anything as sordid as crime. The man with her, though? Per Miss Butterworth’s unflinchingly precise account—you can trust her eye for detail entirely—he’s one of those broad-shouldered, gentlemanly athletes whose towering height makes them stand out in any crowd. The pair would be impossible to miss together. But what possible reason do we have to assume they’re still sticking side by side?”

“Any details on their clothes?”
“Fashionable, respectable. He wore a fresh brown check suit, straight from the tailor by the look of it. She was in dove grey trimmed all in white. That parasol in front of you matches the hue of her hat and its plumes exactly. Both were young, both clearly unused to violence: Miss Butterworth swears she was half-fainted when he carried her out of Adams’s study, and he was so wracked by raw, unmanageable emotion he would’ve been stopped and questioned by every cop on the block if he hadn’t set her down in the vestibule and dragged her straight into the passing street crowd. You think you can track them, Sweetwater?”

The young detective frowned, letting the handful of faded rose petals Gryce had fished off the floor of the murder victim’s study drift through his fingers. “This parasol is the only other clue you’ve got? Nothing on their names?”
“Not a scrap, outside those petals.”
“Then that’s one hell of a problem you’ve handed me,” Sweetwater said slowly, eyeing the parasol. “I’m not half as sure of a win as I was on our last case. I know now how much this kind of chase relies on pure luck. If Miss Butterworth hadn’t been so meticulous, my last lead would’ve gone nowhere, and this one looks even shakier. But I’ll chase the parasol’s owner down, even if I only learn how bad I am at this by failing. You know where I’m going first, sir? Straight to a florist with these rose petals. Every other cop on the force would start with the parasol, so I’m starting with the petals. Call me egotistical, but that parasol leads nowhere I can see right now.”

Gryce huffed a dry, amused laugh. “What do you expect a florist to tell you? How is that supposed to lead you straight to that girl?”
“He can tell me what breed of rose these fell from. Once I know that, I can trace exactly which shop sold that exact bouquet to such a noticeable pair. I don’t work like every other man on the force. You’ve never seen me flounder, so I won’t go dumping cold water on your idea.”

Sweetwater, already thrumming with nervous energy, bowed. He was more than happy to run the case his own way. “I could be back in an hour. Or you might not see me for a week,” he said on his way out.
“Luck to you,” Gryce called after him.

The hour passed. The whole day passed. No sign of Sweetwater. The second day crawled by, enlivened only by a single terse note Miss Butterworth sent over, which Gryce read with a half-smile:
> Dear Mr. Gryce,
> I do not presume to dictate or even offer suggestions to the New York police, but have you asked the postman for that block if he recalls the postmarks on any letters he delivered to Mr. Adams?

His reply, which she read with a small scowl of disappointment from her Gramercy Park drawing room, went as follows:
> Dear Miss Butterworth,
> All suggestions are welcome, even commands, from you. That postman delivers hundreds of letters on that block. He cannot possibly recall individual postmarks. I am sorry to close that lead off.

Both were restless with quiet anxiety, Gryce most of all. So he was massively relieved when he walked into the office on the third morning to find Sweetwater waiting for him, a bright, unmistakeable grin splitting his plain features. He shut the office door behind them the second the last of their clerks left, eyes sparkling.
“News, sir! Good news! I found them. Both of them. And it was the dumbest lucky strike you ever saw. When the florist told me those petals fell from bride roses—well, any woman could carry bride roses, right? But then I remembered his suit was brand new from the tailor, and she was wearing grey and white. So I didn’t chase parasol shops. I went straight to the Bureau of Vital Statistics.”
“Brilliant!” Gryce burst out. “If it paid off, that is.”
“It paid off, sir. Scanned every marriage license filed in the city over the last two weeks, and I hit the name Eva Poindexter. Eva. That’s the name that kept turning up around that house on —— Street, isn’t it? I followed that Eva straight to the minister who performed their private wedding. Got him to describe the bride to me, no prompting. Said he’d never seen a lovelier bride in years—tiny, fragile, pale as porcelain, just like you described. The wedding was in a hotel parlor, and her father was right there with her.”

“Her father?” Gryce froze, his whole carefully constructed theory jarring right out of place. The old man they’d seen laughing on the steps outside Adams’s house wasn’t the father the dead man’s final note had referenced? Or this wasn’t the couple they were chasing?
“Something wrong, sir?” Sweetwater asked.
“Nothing. I just hadn’t expected the father to attend the wedding, that’s all. Go on. The groom?”
“Was exactly the man you told me about. Tall, broad, elegant, the brown check suit he wore was brand new, latest cut. It’s them, no doubt about it.”

Gryce stared hard at his inkstand lid, replaying the lines the dead man had scrawled right before he died in his head: I return your daughter to you. She is here. Neither she nor you will ever see me again. Remember Evelyn! AMOS'S SON. None of it lined up. How could that note make sense if the girl was just married to someone else? He dropped the inkstand lid hard enough to make Sweetwater snort a quiet laugh.
“I’m afraid you wasted two days on a wild goose chase,” Gryce said. “These facts don’t line up with other evidence we’ve got.”
“Hold on, sir, hear the rest first. The couple registered for their wedding night at the Imperial, said they were from a small town in Pennsylvania. But after the ceremony, the bride and groom left in one carriage, her father left in a separate one. I tracked them to the Waldorf, and the afternoon Adams was murdered, they walked out and never came back. All their luggage was left there, and two days later an elderly man matching the father’s description came to pick it up. And the groom’s last name? It’s Adams. Thomas Adams.”

Gryce blinked, surprised, but not yet fully convinced. “Coincidences that wild happen all the time in this work, Sweetwater. There’s evidence the man we’re after isn’t named Thomas Adams, but Felix. You’re going to have to start the whole chase over from scratch.”
Sweetwater just smiled, unfazed. “You never asked where I spent all the time after I left that day. Those leads I just told you about only took one day. I went down to Montgomery, Pennsylvania, the tiny town they registered as being from.”

Gryce sat bolt upright. “And what did you find there?”
“Eva’s father John Poindexter had a childhood best friend named Amos Cadwalader. And Amos had a son named Felix. And the little girl Amos had, his daughter? Her name was Evelyn.”
“Sweetwater, you’re a genius.” Gryce leaned back in his chair, breathless. “No string of coincidences stretches that far. Tell me the rest of that story.”

It was a tangled, tragic history, the kind no small town forgets for decades. The two boys Poindexter and Amos had immigrated from Scotland together in the 1830s, settled in Montgomery, married local girls. Poindexter thrived, while Amos could barely scrape by to support his family, until the Civil War broke out and he enlisted. He left behind two small children, Felix and Evelyn, and Poindexter took them in, raised them as his own, while Amos fought his way up to captain, only to get captured and locked in Libby Prison.
He didn’t get released till the end of the war in 1865. He stumbled back into town, broken and emaciated, to the sound of funeral bells ringing for his daughter Evelyn, who’d died of consumption three days earlier. He ran straight to Poindexter’s house, just as the funeral procession was heading out the door. He threw himself at the bier, screaming, trying to rip back the pall to see her face, but his 12-year-old son Felix stopped him, white as a sheet, hand locked hard on the cloth to hold him back. At the same time, Evelyn’s 19-year-old lover, who’d shot himself the second he heard she was dead, was being carried to the churchyard too, the two processions meeting at the gates, buried side by side.
Amos disappeared the next day. For thirty years, no one in Montgomery saw him again—save for one small boy, who swore he saw a tall, wild long-bearded man standing over Evelyn’s grave one dark night, holding a small child high in his arms as if he was cursing the whole town below. The next morning, a tiny child’s shoe was found right on her grave.
A year prior, news had finally reached Montgomery that Amos Cadwalader was dead. No details, no note on where he was buried. And the stranger Thomas Adams had showed up in town a few months earlier, charming Eva Poindexter off her feet, marrying her, no one quite sure where he’d really come from save for a story that he was fresh from Paris.
“Wait a minute,” Gryce said, the threads clicking into place one by one. “That little baby Amos held over Evelyn’s grave— that baby would be Thomas Adams, wouldn’t it?”
“Maybe,” Sweetwater said, pulling a crumpled telegram out of his pocket. “I tracked their new address. I wired the postmaster in Belleville, Long Island. This just came back:
> Mr. John Poindexter of Montgomery, Pa. Mr. Thomas Adams and Mrs. Adams of the same place have been staying at the Bedell House here for five days.”

Gryce was already grabbing his coat. “Perfect. Be ready to take the one o’clock train out of Grand Central. Keep your wits sharp, your mouth shut. We’re still half blind on this case. I’ve got one letter to dash off before we leave.”
The letter went straight to Miss Butterworth in Gramercy Park, and made her sit up straight the second she read it:
> Do you care enough about the outcome of our case to take a short day trip out to the country? I am leaving New York at 1 PM for Belleville, Long Island. If you choose to come too, you will find me at the Bedell House by early afternoon. Bring a novel with you, and sit on the hotel piazza reading it at five o’clock. If I am also holding a book, read away in peace. The second I lay my book down and pick up a newspaper, you put half your attention on your story, and the other half on the people walking past. If you do not recognize the couple you saw leave Mr. Adams’s house that fatal day, leave your book open. If you do recognize them, shut the book. I will fold my newspaper the second I see that.

The trap was set.
