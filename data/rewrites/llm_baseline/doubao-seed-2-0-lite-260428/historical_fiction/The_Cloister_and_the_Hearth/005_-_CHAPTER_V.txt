Ghysbrecht Van Swieten was a man whose every word was a carefully laid snare, never moving straight toward the mark he hunted, always circling first to throw his prey completely off guard. That afternoon, he sat across from the young novice Gerard, his face draped in feigned benevolence, and led far from the real errand that had drawn the boy to his chambers.
“The town’s ancient records,” he purred, tapping a stack of yellowed, crabbed manuscripts stacked on his scarred oak desk, “are scrawled in a script no modern clerk can parse, their ink turned rust-brown and brittle by a hundred years of damp and sun. I thought you, with your fine scholar’s hand, might do the town the great honour of transcribing all of them fair, neat enough that future generations might read our history without squinting.”
Gerard leaned forward, practical even in his eagerness for honest work. “What wage would you offer for the task, burgomaster?”
Ghysbrecht named a sum so small it would scarcely cover the cost of the new quills, iron-gall ink, and fine parchment the work would demand, not a penny left over for the hours of labour it would require.
Gerard’s brow furrowed. “That would barely pay for the supplies, sir. This is near a full year’s steady work, sitting late by candlelight every night.”
“Your labour?” Ghysbrecht scoffed, leaning back in his carved chair, feigning utter disbelief. “Call tracing marks on smooth parchment labour? I’d wager not a single drop of sweat would bead on your brow the whole time you’re at it.”
“It is labour, and skilled labour at that,” Gerard returned, steady, unflinching. “Every trade pays more for a craftsman’s trained skill than it does for common unskilled toil, sweat or no. And beyond that, it takes my whole time—time I might spend earning coin at some other work.”
“Your time?” Ghysbrecht laughed, sharp as a shard of broken glass, then fixed his sharp, fox-like eyes straight on Gerard’s face, watching for every flicker of expression. “What on earth is time to a bright lad of two and twenty? Truth be told, I think you’ve grown idle, no purpose to you at all these days. You’re in love. Your body might be here among the chanting brother monks of the cloister, but your heart’s off with Peter Brandt and his wild red-haired daughter, is it not?”
Gerard’s face went blank, cold. “I know no Peter Brandt.”
That quick, flat denial only confirmed Ghysbrecht’s long-held suspicion that the young exorcist was hiding a far deeper game than anyone guessed. “Ye lie!” he roared, slamming a fist down on the desk so that the ink horn rattled. “Did I not spot you lingering at her very elbow on the high road to Rotterdam only last week?”
Gerard’s head tilted, just a little. “Ah.”
“Ah, and you were seen at Sevenbergen no more than three days past!”
“Was I?” Gerard said, quiet.
“Aye, and inside Peter Brandt’s very own house!”
“At Sevenbergen?” Gerard asked, his voice rising just a fraction.
“Ay, at Sevenbergen.”
This, in later ages, would be called a bluff: a wild guess spoken loud and certain as proven fact, designed to drag a confession out of the suspect by the very strength of its boldness. But the result of the trick caught the crafty old burgomaster completely off guard.
Gerard shot to his feet, every nerve strung tight, his hands trembling where they rested on the edge of the desk. “Burgomaster,” he said, his voice shaking with a strange, bright excitement, “I have not set foot in Sevenbergen for three whole years. I do not know the names of the people you claim to have seen me with, nor where they dwell. But as you hold my time so cheap, I will waste no more of it here. Good day to you.”
He turned and darted out of the chamber, his eyes glowing like lit embers, before Ghysbrecht could even speak another word.
Ghysbrecht leaped to his feet, roaring with rage, then sank right back into his heavy chair, his chest heaving. “He does not fear me. He knows something—if not the whole of my plot.”
He called out sharply to his most trusted servant, all but yanking the man by the collar over to the casement window that looked out over the town square. “See that lad there, walking fast down the cobbles?” he hissed. “Run! Follow him. But do not let him catch so much as a glimpse of you. He is young, but he is sharp, crafty beyond his years. Dog his heels the whole day. Come back and tell me exactly where he goes, and every single thing he does.”
It was long past sundown, the sky pitch black and the streets all lit by guttering torches, when the servant stumbled back into the burgomaster’s house.
“Well?” Van Swieten cried, leaning forward, hungry for the news. “What did you find?”
“Master,” the servant panted, “the young man went straight from your doors all the way to Sevenbergen. Right to the house of Peter the Magician.”
Ghysbrecht groaned loud, his head falling back against the chair’s back.
