While the burgomaster exposed Gerard to the scrutiny of Tergou, a quieter, yet infinitely heavier storm brewed within the domestic walls of Sevenbergen. It was the distress of a housewife, born of scarcity, yet it carried a weight that transcends mere hunger. Margaret approached Martin Wittenhaagen, the veteran soldier, her eyes wet with unshed tears. "Martin, there is nothing left in the house," she confessed, her voice trembling. "Gerard returns, and he is so thoughtlessly absent-minded. He forgets to break bread at home. When his labor ceases, he rushes to me, poor soul, often collapsing from exhaustion. And to think, I possess nothing to offer the servant who loves me so dearly."

Martin scratched his head, the gesture betraying a mind racing through logistical impossibilities. "What can I do?" he murmured. "It is Thursday; it is your day to shoot. Sooth to say, I counted on you today."

"Nay," the soldier replied, his tone firm despite his weariness. "I may not draw my bow when the Duke or his cronies are out on the chase. Read else. I am no scholar of courtly etiquette." With these words, he produced from his pouch a parchment sealed with a grand crest. It bore the stipulation and license granted by Philip, Duke of Burgundy, to Martin Wittenhaagen, one of his trusted archers, in recompense for services rendered in war and a grievous wound sustained at the Duke's side. The document promised a stipend of four merks annually, payable by the Duke's almoner, alongside a license to release three arrows weekly—specifically on Thursdays—in any of the Duke's Holland forests. However, the proviso was absolute: the hunt was forbidden if the Duke or his friends were present. Under such circumstances, Martin was barred from disturbing the woods, lest he forfeit his salary, his life, and incur a fine of a penny.

Margaret sighed, the sound echoing the emptiness of her hearth, and fell silent. "Come, cheer up, mistress," Martin urged, his demeanor shifting from bureaucratic resignation to protective resolve. "For your sake, I will peril my carcass. I have done worse for women not worth your forefinger. It is no such mighty risk; I shall merely step into the skirts of the forest here. Odds are they will drive a hare or a fawn within range of my arrow."

"Well, if I permit you to go," Margaret insisted, her desperation overriding her caution, "you must promise me not to stray far and not to be seen. Far better that Gerard goes supperless than that ill should come to you, faithful Martin."

With the necessary vow extracted, Martin donned his bow and loaded three arrows, slipping cautiously into the wood, barely a furlong distant. The faint, mournful cry of horns drifted on the breeze, signaling that the game was already afoot. "Come," Martin thought, steeling his nerves, "I shall soon fill the pot, and no one will be the wiser." He positioned himself behind a thick oak commanding a view of an open glade, drawing his weapon—a formidable instrument of English yew, towering six feet two inches and thick as a thigh. Martin, broad-chested with arms forged of iron and cord, had mastered the bow since infancy. He could draw a three-foot arrow to the nape of his neck; when released, the projectile blurred beyond the eye's tracking ability, the bowstring twanging with a resonance akin to a harp. This bow had claimed the lives of countless soldiers in the wars of the Hoecks and Cabbel-jaws. In those eras, battlefields were not shrouded in clouds of smoke; combatants were few, yet the carnage was total, for they fought with clarity, seeing exactly what they wrought. Fewer bloodless arrows flew then than the hollow, silent projectiles of modern warfare.

A hare cantered into view, then sat sprightly, its ears forming a perfect capital 'V'. Martin leveled his tremendous weapon. The arrow flew, the string sang, but in his haste to secure the kill, he missed by an inch. The shaft appeared to strike the animal, yet it struck the earth beside her, passing beneath her belly like a flash of light, hissing through the short grass before vanishing. The hare leaped three feet vertically and bolted at maximum velocity. "Bungler!" Martin chided himself. A sure sign he was not an habitual bungler, for otherwise, he would have blamed the hare.

Scarcely had he fitted another arrow when a wood-pigeon settled directly upon the tree where he stood. "Aha!" he thought. "You are small, but dainty." This time, he exercised patience. He drew the arrow carefully, loosed it smoothly, and watched it appear to pierce the bird cleanly, sending feathers skyward like dust. Yet, the bird, its breast torn rather than pierced, fluttered feebly away. By sheer effort, it rose above the canopy, soared fifty yards, and dropped dead—but where, hidden by the dense foliage, he could not see. "Luck is against me," he muttered despondently.

He fitted another arrow and scanned the glade intently. Suddenly, a commotion erupted behind him. Turning just in time, he witnessed a noble buck crossing the open space, but it was too late; the animal had already vanished into the brush. Martin dashed his bow to the ground in an imprecation. At that precise moment, a long-spotted animal glided swiftly across the path, trailing the deer. Its belly seemed to graze the earth as it moved. Martin snatched up his bow instantly; he recognized the Duke's leopard.

"The hunters will not be far from her," he reasoned grimly. "And I must not be seen. Gerard must go supperless tonight." He plunged into the wood, following the trajectory of the buck and the leopard, the only route back to safety.

He had not traveled far when an unusual sound cut through the silence ahead—the violent rustling of leaves and the crunch of trampled earth. Hurrying forward, he discovered the leopard atop the buck, tearing at its flesh with teeth and claws. The deer ran in a frantic circle, bounding convulsively as blood poured down its hide. A desperate resolution crystallized in Martin's mind: he would secure the venison for Margaret. Drawing his arrow to the head, he buried it deep in the deer. Despite the predator clinging to its back, the buck bounded high into the air and fell dead. The leopard continued to tear, seemingly oblivious to the new reality. Martin hoped the creature would gorge itself until it succumbed, allowing him to claim the meat.

Minutes passed. Resolutely, he walked forward and placed his hand on the buck's leg. The leopard emitted a frightful growl, pausing her feeding. She sensed Martin's presence, becoming sulky and alert. What course of action remained? Martin recalled the ancient wisdom that wild beasts could not withstand the steady human gaze. Standing erect, he fixed his eyes upon the leopard. The beast returned a savage glare, refusing to break contact. As Martin maintained his unblinking stare, the leopard, brutally ignorant of natural law, lunged at his head with a deafening yell, her eyes aflame, jaws distended.

He had but a fraction of a second to react, catching her by the throat before her teeth could crush his face. One claw seized his shoulder, ripping through fabric and skin; the other, aimed at his cheek, would have been fatal. Martin was old-fashioned, wearing no hat, only a scapulary of the same sturdy stuff as his jerkin. He had thrown this garment over his head like a hood, and the brute's claw snagged in the loose leather.

Martin struggled to keep her teeth from his face, gripping her throat with iron force while she raked his shoulder. It felt like blunt reaping hooks grinding and tearing. The pain was excruciating, yet instead of cowering, it ignited his blood. He gnashed his teeth in rage nearly as fierce as hers, squeezing her neck with crushing strength. Two pairs of eyes flared in the dim light—the man's burning with fury, the brute's with primal instinct. Realizing she was being throttled, the leopard made a wild attempt to free herself, dragging his cowl across his face, blinding him, and tearing her claw free from his shoulder, taking flesh and bone with it. Still, he throttled her with hand and arm of iron.

Presently, her long tail, previously held high, drooped. "Aha!" cried Martin, joyfully, and gripped her like death. Next, her body lost its elasticity; he held a choked, powerless thing. He maintained his grip until all motion ceased, then dashed her corpse to the earth. Panting, he removed his cowl. The leopard lay mute at his feet, tongue protruding, a bloody paw twitching. For the first time, terror seized Martin. "I am a dead man," he whispered. "I have slain the Duke's leopard."

Hastily, he gathered handfuls of leaves and threw them over the carcass, then shouldered the buck and staggered away, leaving a trail of mingled blood—his own and the deer's. He burst into Peter's house a horrifying figure, bleeding and stained, and flung the deer's carcass onto the floor. "There—no questions," he rasped. "But broil me a steak on it, for I am faint."
