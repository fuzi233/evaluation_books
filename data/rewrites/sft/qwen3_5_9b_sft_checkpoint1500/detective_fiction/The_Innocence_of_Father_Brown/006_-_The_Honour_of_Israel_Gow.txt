Rain lashed against the windows of Glengyle Castle, a storm of olive and silver that seemed to mirror the gloom within the stone walls. Father Brown, wrapped in a heavy grey Scotch plaid, stood at the edge of the valley, looking up at the bizarre silhouette of the chateau. It loomed like the end of a blind alley, a fortress of steep roofs and seagreen slate that an Englishman might mistake for the steeple-hats of witches in a fairy tale. Surrounding it, the pine woods swayed like a black flock of ravens, adding a note of dreamy, sleepy devilry to the scene. This atmosphere was not merely fanciful; it was rooted in a heavy cloud of pride, madness, and mysterious sorrow that often settled upon Scottish nobility, compounded by a double dose of heredity—the aristocrat's obsession with blood and the Calvinist's sense of doom.

Father Brown had seized a day from his duties in Glasgow to join his friend Flambeau, the amateur detective, who was currently investigating the baffling disappearance and presumed death of the late Earl of Glengyle. The Earl was the last survivor of a lineage known for valour, insanity, and cunning, a dynasty that had played a terrifying role in the labyrinthine politics surrounding Mary Queen of Scots. The countryside itself seemed to whisper their legacy: *As green sap to the simmer trees / Is red gold to the Ogilvies.* For centuries, the castle had produced nothing but eccentric lords, until the Victorian age supposedly exhausted all oddities. Yet, the last Earl satisfied the tribal tradition by simply vanishing. He was listed in the Peerage and the church registers, yet he remained unseen beneath the sun, hidden away in his own domain.

Evidence suggested he was still there, tended to by a solitary manservant named Israel Gow. Gow was a gaunt, red-haired figure with a dogged jaw and startlingly blank blue eyes, described variously as deaf, dumb, or half-witted. His routine was peculiar: he dug potatoes with immense energy, vanished regularly into the kitchen, and insisted the house was "not at home" to visitors. Recently, local officials had been summoned to find the Earl entombed in a coffin, a grim discovery that preceded Flambeau's arrival. Now, standing in the shadow of the chateau, Father Brown watched a black silhouette pass by—a man in a chimney-pot hat carrying a spade, looking suspiciously like a sexton or the same strange gardener.

Inside, Flambeau waited with Inspector Craven of Scotland Yard. The entrance hall was stripped bare, save for the ghostly gaze of ancestral portraits. In an inner room, the three men gathered around a long oak table cluttered with scribbled notes, whisky, and cigars. Between them lay a collection of inexplicable artifacts: a heap of glittering broken glass, piles of brown dust, and a plain stick of wood.

"You seem to have a sort of geological museum here," Father Brown remarked, nodding toward the debris.

"Not a geological museum," Flambeau corrected with a smile. "Say a psychological museum."

"Don't let's begin with such long words," Craven interjected, laughing nervously.

"Don't you know what psychology means?" Flambeau asked, feigning surprise. "It means being off one's chump."

"Well," said Flambeau seriously, "I mean that we've only concluded one thing about Lord Glengyle: he was a maniac."

Outside, the silhouette of Gow moved past the window, a dark outline against the dying light. Father Brown turned his passive gaze to it. "I can understand there must have been something odd about the man," he mused, "or he wouldn't have buried himself alive—or been in such a hurry to bury himself dead. But what makes you think it was lunacy?"

Flambeau gestured toward the items on the table. "Listen to the list Inspector Craven has uncovered."

"We must get a candle," Craven said abruptly. "A storm is breaking, and it's too dark to read."

"Do you happen to have found any candles among your oddities?" Brown asked, a faint smile playing on his lips.

Flambeau nodded solemnly. "That is curious, too. Twenty-five candles, and not a single candlestick."

As the wind howled outside, Brown walked to the table and bent over the heap of reddish-brown dust. A sudden sneeze cracked the silence. "Hullo!" he exclaimed. "Snuff!"

He lit one of the candles, holding it briefly before sticking it into the neck of a whisky bottle. The flame danced wildly in the draft, mirroring the chaos of the night. Craven began to read the inventory aloud, listing items found loose and unexplained in rooms that had clearly been inhabited by someone other than Gow. First, a hoard of loose diamonds without settings. Second, heaps of loose snuff scattered everywhere, suggesting a refusal to use containers. Third, tiny pieces of metal—springs and microscopic wheels—as if from a dissected mechanical toy. Fourth, the candles themselves, lacking holders.

"The central riddle is clear," Craven noted, "that there is something wrong about the last Earl. Whether he lived here, died here, or was dressed up as the servant remains unknown. But suppose the most lurid solution: the servant killed the master, or the master is disguised as the servant. Even then, you cannot explain a candle without a holder, or why an elderly gentleman of good breeding would habitually spill snuff on a piano. The core of the story might be imagined, but the fringes remain mysterious. No stretch of fancy connects snuff, diamonds, wax, and loose clockwork."

"I think I see the connection," Father Brown said softly. "This Glengyle was mad against the French Revolution. An enthusiast for the *ancien régime*, he tried to re-enact the family life of the Bourbons. He used snuff as an eighteenth-century luxury, wax candles for eighteenth-century lighting, the iron parts to represent Louis XVI's locksmith hobbies, and the diamonds for the Necklace of Marie Antoinette."

Both the other men stared at him in disbelief. "What an extraordinary notion!" cried Flambeau. "Do you really believe that?"

"I am perfectly sure it isn't," Father Brown replied calmly. "You said nobody could connect snuff, diamonds, clockwork, and candles. I offer you that connection offhand. The real truth lies deeper."

He paused, listening to the wail of the wind. "The late Earl of Glengyle was a thief. He lived a second, darker life as a desperate housebreaker. He had no candlesticks because he used these candles cut short in a small lantern he carried. The snuff he used as criminals do: to fling it suddenly in dense masses into the face of a captor. But the final proof lies in the coincidence of the diamonds and the small steel wheels. Those are the only two instruments needed to cut out a pane of glass."

A branch of a broken pine tree lashed against the window, sounding like a parody of a burglar, but they did not turn. Their eyes remained fixed on the priest. "Diamonds and small wheels," Craven murmured. "Is that all?"

"No," Father Brown answered placidly. "The true tale is much more humdrum. Glengyle found, or believed he found, precious stones on his estate. Someone bamboozled him with loose brilliants, claiming they were unearthed in castle caverns. The little wheels were part of a rough diamond-cutting apparatus, likely operated by a few shepherds on the hills. The lack of candlesticks was because they weren't wanted; they held the candles in their hands during cave explorations. Snuff was the bribe, the currency of trade with the locals."

"That is all?" Flambeau asked after a long pause. "Have we reached the dull truth at last?"

"Oh, no," said Father Brown.

As the wind died down with a mocking hoot, Father Brown, his face utterly impassive, continued. "I suggested that only because you said one could not plausibly connect snuff with clockwork or candles with bright stones. Ten false philosophies will fit the universe; ten false theories will fit Glengyle Castle. But we seek the real explanation. Are there no other exhibits?"

Craven laughed, and Flambeau rose to stroll down the table. "Items five, six, seven... a curious collection of lead pencils, specifically the lead scraped out. A splintered stick of bamboo, possibly the weapon. And a few old Catholic missals and pictures, defaced and cut."

Suddenly, the tempest drove a wall of clouds across the room, plunging them into darkness. Father Brown picked up the illuminated pages, his voice transformed, speaking with the urgency of a man ten years younger. "Mr. Craven, do you have a legal warrant to examine that grave? We must go up immediately. If I were you, I would start now."

"Now?" Craven repeated, astonished. "Why now?"

"Because this is serious," Brown answered. "This is not spilled snuff or loose pebbles. These religious pictures have been treated with care, and in a queer way. Wherever the ornamental name of God appears in the illuminations, it has been meticulously erased. The only other thing removed is the halo from the Child Jesus. Let us get our warrant, our spades, and our tools, and break open that coffin."

"What do you mean?" demanded the London officer.
