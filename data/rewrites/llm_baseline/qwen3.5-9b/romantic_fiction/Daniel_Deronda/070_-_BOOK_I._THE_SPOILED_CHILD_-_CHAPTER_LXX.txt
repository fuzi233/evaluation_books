In the complex tapestry of human life, the seasons often blend together as they did in ancient myths: fruit hangs beside blossoms, and in a single breath, the sickle cuts the grain while seeds are scattered for future growth. One person tends the young vine, another presses the grapes; yet within every individual life, the harvest and the springtime remain eternally intertwined, until the final gathering calls us to be sown anew in unseen fields. Among the gifts of love, none is more exquisite than the profound realization that by joining our lives to another's, we become guardians of their happiness, bringing solace to past hardships and turning memories of suffering into springs of joy.

Deronda's love for Mirah was deeply rooted in this protective instinct. Though she had started her journey across life's rough terrain with steps too small and innocent for the path ahead, the first time he saw her face, it appeared to him as a portrait of youthful despair. Now, however, she glowed like a delicate flower with dark tips and ivory petals, bathed in the warm sunlight of contentment. She viewed every potential sorrow as merely a shadow cast by the bright reality of her life with Deronda—a existence she could name only "good." He watched the dignified joy that animated her movements and the serene stillness of her resting poses with a delight so intense he whispered to himself that saving her from pain was sufficient reward for his own personal happiness.

Mirah remained unaware of Hans's internal struggles or the sharp pang Gwendolen felt. After Deronda revealed the depth of his hidden love for her, she effortlessly interpreted Gwendolen's anxious concern as a sign of gratitude toward Deronda's kindness, a sentiment Mirah herself had experienced. All of Deronda's remarks regarding Mrs. Grandcourt reinforced this perspective, though he spoke of them only in the vaguest terms. Mirah was willing to believe that he had acted as a rescuing angel to many souls, including herself. Her only marvel was that, among all those saved, she alone possessed the privilege of standing constantly by his side.

When the bridal veil finally fell around Mirah, it concealed no trembling doubt, only a thrilling reverence for the immense gift she accepted—one that demanded great purpose in return. Under the velvet canopy, no couple ever stood more worthy of blessing, nor did their people offer a more prudent wish for their progeny. No lips spoke truer vows, and no marriage blessing held a stronger promise of fulfillment than the integrity woven into their mutual pledge. Naturally, they wed according to the Jewish rite.

Since no faith demands that a feast exclude anyone based on social rank, few would likely take offense to learn that Deronda's modest wedding banquet included the entire Cohen family, with one notable exception: the baby, who continued to nurse its teeth intelligently at home. How could Mordecai bear to keep friends from his days of adversity away from sharing in his joy? Mrs. Meyrick understood this heart so completely that she had fully reconciled herself to meeting the Jewish pawnbroker, arriving with her three daughters. Each woman enjoyed the satisfaction of knowing that Mirah's union with Deronda crowned a romance that would forever remain a sweet memory for them. Which of them—mother or daughters—had not played a generous role in it, offering their finest feelings and actions to the girl who needed them?

If Hans had been present, it would have been ideal; but Mab had already noted that men must sometimes suffer for being inconvenient. Perhaps she, Kate, and Amy had all fallen in love with Mr. Deronda? Yet, being women, they were far less ridiculous than men in such matters. The Meyricks were richly rewarded for overcoming their prejudices by hearing a speech from Mr. Cohen, a rarity among public addresses for its lack of conventional patterns. Jacob ate with the appetite of a child, contributing several small, whinnying laughs as a spontaneous accompaniment to his father's words—not irreverently, but from a lively pride that his family was being honored. Meanwhile, Adelaide Rebekah, clad in a new Sabbath dress, maintained a solemn air of responsibility throughout.

Mordecai's brilliant eyes, sunken deep in their large sockets, scanned the room with the cherishing benevolence of a spirit already elevated above worldly concerns, leaving only selflessness and sympathy behind. Yet, whenever his gaze swept across the crowd, it inevitably returned to Deronda, shining with a renewed glow of trusting affection.

The wedding feast was humble, yet Mirah received magnificent gifts. Upon learning of the engagement, friends devised gracious tokens. Sir Hugo and Lady Mallinger arranged a complete outfit for Eastern travel, along with a precious locket inscribed: *"To the bride of our dear Daniel Deronda, all blessings. H. and L. M."* The Klesmers presented a flawless watch, similarly adorned with a tender message. But something far more valuable than gold or jewels arrived for Deronda from the neighborhood of Diplow on the morning of his wedding. It was a letter bearing these words:

*"Do not think of me sorrowfully on your wedding-day. I have remembered your words—that I may live to be one of the best of women, who make others glad that they were born. I do not yet see how that can be, but you know better than I. If it ever comes true, it will be because you helped me. I only thought of myself, and I made you grieve. It hurts me now to think of your grief. You must not grieve any more for me. It is better—it shall be better with me because I have known you. GWENDOLEN GRANDCOURT."*

Preparations for their immediate departure to the East commenced instantly. Deronda could not refuse Ezra's wish to embark on the voyage immediately, rather than delaying them to tend to his failing health. He harbored no hope that Ezra would survive the journey, for symptoms indicated the final stage of his illness had arrived. Yet Ezra himself had declared, "Never mind where I die, so that I am with you."

He did not accompany them. On an early morning, he told Deronda, "Do not leave me today. I shall die before it ends." He insisted on dressing and sitting upright in his easy chair as usual, with Deronda and Mirah seated on either side. For several hours, he remained unusually silent, making no effort to speak, yet occasionally looking at them with eyes filled with a restful meaning, as if to assure them that while this remaining fragment of breathing time was difficult, he felt an ocean of peace beneath him.

It was not until late afternoon, as the light faded, that he took hold of each of their hands. Looking at Deronda, he said, "Death is coming to me as the divine kiss, which is both parting and reunion—it takes me from your bodily sight and grants me full presence in your soul. Where thou goest, Daniel, I shall go. Has it not begun? Have I not breathed my soul into you? We shall live together."

He paused, and Deronda waited, expecting further words. Slowly, with visible effort, Ezra pressed their hands, raised himself, and uttered in Hebrew the confession of the Divine Unity, a prayer spoken for generations by the dying Israelite. He sank gently back into his chair and spoke no more. It was several hours later before he ceased to breathe, enveloped in the arms of Mirah and Deronda.

"Nothing is here for tears, nothing to wail
Or knock the breast; no weakness, no contempt,
Dispraise or blame; nothing but well and fair,
And what may quiet us in a death so noble."
