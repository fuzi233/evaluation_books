Mrs. Dashwood stayed at Norland Manor for several months. She did not linger out of reluctance to leave; rather, she waited until the sharp sting of grief dulled enough for her to bear the sight of familiar places without being overwhelmed by fresh waves of sorrow. Once her spirits began to lift and her mind sought relief from the paralyzing weight of memory, she grew impatient to depart. She was eager to find a new home nearby, for moving far away from the place where her late husband had breathed his last was unthinkable.

Yet, despite her earnest search, she could find no dwelling that satisfied everyone. There was no house that perfectly balanced her desire for comfort and ease with her eldest daughter's prudent caution. Several properties Mrs. Dashwood admired were deemed too grand for their reduced income by Marianne, while others her mother preferred lacked the necessary charm.

Throughout this uncertainty, Mrs. Dashwood held onto a specific hope: the solemn promise made by her husband regarding a legacy left to them by his son, John. Though she had long suspected that seven thousand pounds—a sum Mr. Dashwood believed generous—was insufficient to maintain true affluence for three women, she clung to the idea that it would suffice for their survival. More importantly, she cherished the belief that John Dashwood cared deeply for his siblings' welfare. His attentive behavior toward his widowed mother and sisters had gradually dispelled her earlier contempt for his wife, Mrs. John Dashwood. While Mrs. John's character remained flawed in Mrs. Dashwood's eyes, the prospect of living near her children offered a solace that outweighed social friction.

A particularly fortifying circumstance emerged: a growing attachment between Elinor, the eldest daughter, and Edward Ferrars. Introduced shortly after his sister Fanny moved to Norland, Edward had become a constant presence in the household. He was a gentleman of pleasing demeanor, though not strikingly handsome. His manners were initially reserved, requiring familiarity to reveal the warmth beneath. However, once his natural shyness receded, he displayed an open, affectionate nature. His understanding was sound, refined by a solid education, and his heart proved steady and kind.

To Mrs. Dashwood, these qualities were paramount. Unlike many mothers who might encourage the match solely for financial security—or discourage it due to the precarious nature of Edward's inheritance, which rested entirely on his mother's will—Mrs. Dashwood was guided purely by affection. She saw no barrier in their differing fortunes if their hearts aligned. Indeed, she found it inconceivable that anyone who truly knew Elinor's merit would fail to appreciate her.

Edward's lack of ambition further endeared him to her. While his mother and sister dreamed of seeing him rise to prominence—perhaps entering Parliament or associating with the elite—Edward possessed no such aspirations. He found joy only in domestic tranquility and private life. His younger brother, Henry, showed more promise in worldly affairs, but Edward contentedly remained rooted in simplicity.

Initially, Mrs. Dashwood paid little heed to Edward amidst her profound mourning, noticing only his quiet unobtrusiveness. It was Elinor who drew her mother's full attention to him one day, highlighting the stark contrast between Edward and his sister. "It is enough," Elinor remarked, "to say that he is unlike Fanny. That alone implies everything amiable."

"I love him already," Mrs. Dashwood declared warmly.

"You will like him when you know him better," Elinor suggested gently.

"Like him!" her mother smiled, shaking her head. "I feel no sentiment short of love."

"You may merely esteem him."

"I have never known how to separate esteem from love," Mrs. Dashwood replied.

Determined to know him fully, Mrs. Dashwood cultivated an acquaintance with Edward. Her engaging manner quickly melted his reserve, allowing her to discern his genuine worth. Even his quiet demeanor, which previously seemed lacking in youthful vigor, became endearing once she understood the depth of his affection. Upon perceiving clear signs of his devotion to Elinor, she viewed their union as inevitable, eagerly anticipating the day they would marry.

"In a few months, my dear Marianne," she told her youngest daughter, "Elinor will likely be settled for life. We shall miss her dearly, but she will be happy. You will gain a brother—a real, affectionate brother. I hold the highest opinion of Edward's heart."

Marianne, however, listened with a furrowed brow. "Perhaps," she admitted, "I view it with some surprise. Edward is undeniably amiable, and I love him tenderly. Yet... he lacks something. His figure is not striking; it lacks the grace I expect in a man destined to captivate my sister. His eyes lack that spark, that fire which announces both virtue and intelligence."

She paused, her voice trembling slightly. "Furthermore, I fear he possesses no real taste. Music barely attracts him. While he praises Elinor's drawings, it is the admiration of a lover, not a connoisseur. He knows nothing of art, yet attends her diligently. To satisfy me, a partner must share my soul. The same books, the same music must charm us both. I cannot be happy with a man whose tastes do not mirror my own. He must enter into every feeling I possess."

Recalling a recent evening, Marianne continued, "How spiritless and tame was Edward's manner when he read to us last night! I felt for my sister acutely. Yet she bore it with such composure, seemingly unaware of the dullness. I could hardly sit still. To hear those beautiful lines, often driving me wild, delivered with such impenetrable calmness and indifference!"

"He would certainly have done more justice to simple, elegant prose," Mrs. Dashwood interjected. "But you insisted on giving him Cowper."

"Nay, Mama, if he cannot be animated by Cowper! But we must allow for differences in taste. Elinor does not share my intense feelings, so she may overlook it and remain happy. But had I loved him, hearing him read with such insensibility would have broken my heart. Mama, the more I learn of the world, the more convinced I am that I shall never find a man worthy of my love. I require so much! He must possess all of Edward's virtues, yet his person and manners must adorn his goodness with every possible charm."

Mrs. Dashwood placed a comforting hand on her daughter's shoulder. "Remember, my love, you are not yet seventeen. It is too early to despair of such happiness. Why should your destiny differ from mine? In one respect only, Marianne, may your fate be different from yours."
