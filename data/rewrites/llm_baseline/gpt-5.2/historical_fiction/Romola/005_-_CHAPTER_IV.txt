“Good-day, Messer Domenico,” Nello called out, his voice skimming over the shop like a quick trill over a bowl’s rim. Two men had entered together: the first, grave and thickset with years; the second, tall and spare, wrapped in a mantle that had known better winters. Nello nodded to the latter as one nods to a regular whose silence is part of his trade.

“You come as opportunely as cheese on macaroni,” Nello went on, already drawing his razor with the brisk economy of a cook reaching for a knife he trusts. “Ah! you are in haste—wish to be shaved without delay—ecco! And this is a morning when every one has grave matter on his mind. Florence orphaned—the very pivot of Italy snatched away—heaven itself at a loss what to do next. Oimè! Well, well; the sun is nevertheless travelling on towards dinner-time again; and, as I was saying, you come like cheese ready grated.”

He spoke as if he were seasoning the air—too much perhaps, but with instinct, making even calamity part of the day’s broth. Outside, bells worried the morning with their metal insistence; inside, the little domestic orchestra held its own: the click of the strop, the whisper of bristles under soap, the faint simmering murmur of the street beyond the threshold.

“For this young stranger,” Nello continued, with the satisfaction of a man laying out a dish he has arranged himself, “was wishing for an honourable trader who would advance him a sum on a certain ring of value, and if I had counted every goldsmith and money-lender in Florence on my fingers, I couldn’t have found a better name than Menico Cennini. Besides, he hath other ware in which you deal—Greek learning, and young eyes—a double implement which you printers are always in need of.”

The elderly man—Domenico Cennini, son of that Bernardo who had once, hearing of German marvels, cast his own types in Florence—could do nothing but sit in lathered patience while Nello’s tongue ran on. His hands lay still, resigned as dough beneath a cloth; only his eyes moved, slow and weighing, to the stranger who stood near the door.

“This fine young man has unlimited Greek, Latin, or Italian at your service,” said Nello, as if offering a pantry with its jars unsealed. “He is as great a wonder of juvenile learning as Francesco Filelfo or our own incomparable Poliziano. A second Guarino, too, for he has had the misfortune to be shipwrecked, and has doubtless lost a store of precious manuscripts that might have contributed some correctness even to your correct editions, Domenico. Fortunately, he has rescued a few gems of rare value. His name is—you said your name, Messer, was—?”

“Tito Melema,” the young man answered.

He slipped the ring from his finger as one might set down a last crust saved from hunger, and presented it to Cennini. The barber, not less swift with his razor than with his speech, had already freed Domenico from the shaving-cloth; the man rose, rubbing his chin with the careful touch of someone who knows the value of a clean edge.

Meanwhile the companion who had entered with the goldsmith—tall, about fifty, with a short-trimmed beard, an old felt hat pressed down on his brow, and a mantle frayed to the colour of road-dust—had watched Tito as if measuring him for a frame. Now he spoke abruptly, the words clipped like herbs under a knife.

“Young man, I am painting a picture of Sinon deceiving old Priam, and I should be glad of your face for my Sinon, if you’d give me a sitting.”

Tito started. The blood withdrew a little, as if the accusation had been laid on his skin like cold metal. He turned, those long dark eyes fixing on the speaker with the astonishment of a man who hears his name called in a dialect he has not spoken since childhood.

But Nello would not let the moment curdle.

“Piero,” he cried, “thou art the most extraordinary compound of humours and fancies ever packed into a human skin. What trick wilt thou play with the fine visage of this young scholar to make it suit thy traitor? Ask him rather to turn his eyes upward, and thou mayst make a Saint Sebastian of him that will draw troops of devout women; or, if thou art in a classical vein, put myrtle about his curls and make him a young Bacchus, or say rather a Phoebus Apollo, for his face is as warm and bright as a summer morning; it made me his friend in the space of a ‘credo.’”

“Ay, Nello,” said the painter, pausing between phrases as if he were listening to something inside his own skull and mistrusting the outer world’s racket, “and if thy tongue can leave off its everlasting chirping long enough for thy understanding to consider the matter, thou mayst see that thou hast just shown the reason why the face of Messere will suit my traitor. A perfect traitor should have a face which vice can write no marks on—lips that will lie with a dimpled smile—eyes of such agate-like brightness and depth that no infamy can dull them—cheeks that will rise from a murder and not look haggard.”

He spoke without heat, almost like a man describing the proper cut of meat for a given stew—matter-of-fact, cruel in its precision.

“I say not this young man is a traitor,” he went on. “I mean, he has a face that would make him the more perfect traitor if he had the heart of one, which is saying neither more nor less than that he has a beautiful face, informed with rich young blood, that will be nourished enough by food, and keep its colour without much help of virtue. He may have the heart of a hero along with it; I aver nothing to the contrary. Ask Domenico there if the lapidaries can always tell a gem by the sight alone.”

The bells outside flung themselves again upon the air—brazen, insistent, as if each stroke were a hand striking a table to demand attention. The painter’s eyelids twitched.

“And now I’m going to put the tow in my ears,” he said, “for thy chatter and the bells together are more than I can endure: so say no more to me, but trim my beard.”

With that he drew out two bits of tow, stuffed them into his ears, and dropped into the chair before Nello. It was an odd, domestic remedy against the city’s unceasing clangour, like placing a cloth over a pot to keep it from boiling over—an act of self-preservation in a Florence whose soundscape could bruise the mind as surely as hunger bruises the body.

Nello shrugged, his mouth twisting into a look he threw at Tito—a silent aside that meant: A whimsical fellow; take it as jest.

Tito had stood a moment as if pinned, his gaze still on the painter, trying to translate the man’s meaning and finding that no language—Greek, Latin, Italian—could carry the exact taste of that ambiguous insult. But Piero’s turning away, tow in ears, helped Tito recover his composure; he shifted his attention to Cennini, as a man returns to the business of bread when talk turns sour.

Cennini held the ring between finger and thumb with reverent steadiness, as though it were not merely pledge but text—an object to be read. The shop’s smells—soap, damp wool, faint metal—seemed to sharpen around that small circle of gold.

“This is a curious and valuable ring, young man,” he said. “This intaglio of the fish with the crested serpent above it, in the black stratum of the onyx, or rather nicolo, is well shown by the surrounding blue of the upper stratum. The ring has, doubtless, a history?” He looked up keenly, as if the stone’s layers invited the same scrutiny into a man’s past.

“Yes, indeed,” Tito answered, meeting the gaze frankly, letting his voice carry warmth where his face had cooled. “The ring was found in Sicily, and I have understood from those who busy themselves with gems and sigils, that both the stone and intaglio are of virtue to make the wearer fortunate, especially at sea, and also to restore to him whatever he may have lost.”

He smiled, the expression quick as a knife’s flash, bright and disarming.

“But,” he continued, “though I have worn it constantly since I quitted Greece, it has not made me altogether fortunate at sea, you perceive—unless I am to count escape from drowning as a sufficient proof of its virtue. It remains to be seen whether my lost chests will come to light; but to lose no chance of such a result, Messer, I will pray you only to hold the ring for a short space as pledge for a small sum far beneath its value, and I will redeem it as soon as I can dispose of certain other gems which are secured within my doublet, or indeed as soon as I can earn something by any scholarly employment, if I may be so fortunate as to meet with such.”

“That may be seen, young man, if you will come with me,” said Cennini, decision settling in his tone like clear stock after the scum is skimmed. “My brother Pietro, who is a better judge of scholarship than I, will perhaps be able to supply you with a task that may test your capabilities. Meanwhile, take back your ring until I can hand you the necessary florins, and, if it please you, come along with me.”

“Yes, yes,” cried Nello, delighted at an arrangement he could call his own handiwork. “Go with Messer Domenico, you cannot go in better company; he was born under the constellation that gives a man skill, riches, and integrity, whatever that constellation may be—which is of the less consequence because babies can’t choose their own horoscopes, and, indeed, if they could, there might be an inconvenient rush of babies at particular epochs. Besides, our Phoenix, the incomparable Pico, has shown that your horoscopes are all a nonsensical dream—which is the less troublesome opinion. Addio! bel giovane! don’t forget to come back to me.”

“No fear of that,” Tito said. He beckoned farewell as he turned at the door, his bright face catching what little morning light filtered past the shopfront, like wine poured thin but still glowing. “You are to do me a great service:—that is the most positive security for your seeing me again.”

When the door shut behind him, the bells outside pressed their harsh sweetness into the air again, but muffled now by distance and the painter’s tow.

“Say what thou wilt, Piero,” Nello said, as he set his razor to work on the beard before him, “I shall never look at such an outside as that without taking it as a sign of a lovable nature. Why, thou wilt say next that Leonardo, whom thou art always raving about, ought to have made his Judas as beautiful as Saint John! But thou art as deaf as the top of Mount Morello with that accursed tow in thy ears.”

He shook his head, amused and stubborn, as if insisting that sweetness must mean honey and cannot ever mean poison.

“Well, well,” he added, lowering his voice as the blade made its soft, sure scrape, “I’ll get a little more of this young man’s history from him before I take him to Bardo Bardi.”
