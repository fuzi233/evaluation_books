The swiftest route from my lady's sitting-room to the gardens lay through the shrubbery path—a thoroughfare familiar to anyone who knows the estate. To aid your understanding of what follows, let me mention that this particular lane was the preferred haunt of Mr. Franklin Blake. Whenever he strolled the grounds and we could not locate him elsewhere, he was invariably found wandering here.

I confess, though, that I possess a stubborn streak. The harder Sergeant Cuff guarded his thoughts against me, the more determined I became to pry them loose. As we turned onto the shrubbery path, I attempted a different strategy.

"As matters stand now," I remarked, "if I were in your shoes, I'd be utterly at a loss."

"If you were in my position," the Sergeant replied calmly, "you would already have formed an opinion. And given the current state of affairs, any lingering doubts you might harbor regarding your own deductions would now be dispelled. Let us not dwell on those conclusions for the moment, Mr. Betteredge. I did not bring you out here to trap me like a fox; I summoned you to solicit some information. You could certainly have offered it within the house, rather than outside. Yet, doors and eavesdroppers have a peculiar talent for assembling, and in my profession, we cherish a robust affection for the open air."

Who could possibly outwit *this* man? I yielded and waited with patience as best I could for his next move.

"We need not speculate on the young lady's motives," the Sergeant continued. "Let us simply acknowledge it is regrettable that she refuses to assist me, for by doing so, she renders this inquiry far more arduous than it need be. We must now attempt to unravel the mystery of the smudge on the door—which, take my word for it, signifies the mystery of the Diamond itself—through alternative means. I have resolved to interrogate the servants, to probe their thoughts and actions, Mr. Betteredge, rather than rummage through their closets. Before I commence, however, I require a few answers. You are a keen observer—did you detect anything unusual among the staff, allowing for natural fright and confusion following the discovery of the theft? Was there any specific discord? Did anyone behave unlike their normal selves? Perhaps irritable, or inexplicably unwell?"

I barely had time to recall Rosanna Spearman's sudden collapse at yesterday's dinner before I perceived Sergeant Cuff's gaze shift sharply toward the shrubbery. A soft exclamation escaped him—"Hullo!"

"What is the trouble?" I asked.

"A slight twinge of rheumatism in my back," the Sergeant announced loudly, seemingly inviting a third party to overhear. "The weather promises a change soon."

A few paces further brought us to the corner of the mansion. Swerving sharply to the right, we ascended the terrace and descended the central stairs into the garden below. Stopping amidst the open space, where visibility surrounded us on all sides, the Sergeant addressed me.

"Regarding that young person, Rosanna Spearman?" he inquired. "Given her appearance, it is unlikely she possesses a suitor. Nevertheless, for the girl's own sake, I must ask you immediately: has she, poor wretch, secured a sweetheart, like the others?"

Good heavens, what possible intent lay behind such a query under these circumstances? I gazed at him, speechless, rather than offering an answer.

"I observed Rosanna Spearman concealing herself within the shrubbery as we passed," the Sergeant stated.

"When you uttered 'Hullo'?" I prompted.

"Yes—precisely when I cried 'Hullo!' If a paramour is involved, her hiding is of minor consequence. But if there is none, as appears to be the case within this household, her concealment becomes a highly suspicious circumstance. It shall be my painful duty to act upon it accordingly."

What, in the name of Providence, was I to utter? I knew the shrubbery was Mr. Franklin's favorite promenade; I knew he would almost certainly take that path upon returning from the station; and I knew that Penelope had repeatedly caught her fellow-servant loitering there, always declaring to me that Rosanna sought Mr. Franklin's attention. If Penelope's assessment held true, she might well have been waiting for Mr. Franklin's return precisely when the Sergeant spotted her. I stood trapped between two perils: voicing Penelope's fanciful notions as if they were my own, or leaving an unfortunate creature to endure the severe repercussions of arousing Sergeant Cuff's suspicions. Driven solely by compassion for the girl—upon my soul and honor, purely out of pity for the girl—I furnished the Sergeant with the necessary explanations, revealing that Rosanna had grown mad enough to fix her heart on Mr. Franklin Blake.

Sergeant Cuff never laughed. On the rare occasions when amusement struck him, merely the corners of his lips curled upward, nothing more. This time, they did curl.

"Would it not be wiser to claim she is mad enough to be both ugly and a mere servant?" he asked. "Falling in love with a gentleman of Mr. Franklin Blake's demeanor and bearing does not appear to me to constitute the most insane aspect of her conduct, by any stretch. However, I am relieved the matter is clarified; it soothes the mind to have things laid bare. Yes, I shall keep this confidential, Mr. Betteredge. I delight in tenderness toward human frailty—even if I seldom encounter opportunities to exercise such virtue in my line of work. Do you truly believe Mr. Franklin Blake remains unaware of the girl's infatuation? Ah! He would have discerned it swiftly enough had she possessed beauty. Ugly women fare poorly in this world; let us hope their fortunes improve in the next. Here, you maintain a charming garden and a meticulously manicured lawn. Observe for yourself how superior the floral display appears with surrounding grass rather than gravel. No, thank you. I refrain from plucking roses; it breaks my heart to sever them from the stem. Just as it breaks yours, I presume, when discord arises in the servants' hall. Did you notice anything unexplainable about any of the staff when the loss of the Diamond was first revealed?"

Our rapport with Sergeant Cuff had thus far progressed smoothly. Yet, the sly manner in which he reintroduced that final question placed me on guard. In plain terms, I held little desire for assisting his investigations when they led him, acting as a viper in the grass, among my fellow-servants.

"I observed nothing," I stated, "save that we collectively lost our composure, myself included."

"Oh," the Sergeant mused, "is that all you have to impart?"

I responded, with what I flattered myself was an impassive countenance, "That is all."

Sergeant Cuff's somber eyes scrutinized me intently.

"Mr. Betteredge," he said, "do you object to oblige me by shaking hands? I have developed an extraordinary affinity for you."

(The rationale behind selecting this precise moment, when I was actively deceiving him, to extend proof of his high regard, remains beyond all comprehension! I felt a flicker of pride—I truly did feel a measure of pride in having, at last, outsmarted the renowned Cuff!)

We returned to the residence. The Sergeant requested a private chamber, instructing me thereafter to summon the domestic staff, the indoor servants exclusively, one by one, in strict hierarchical order, from highest to lowest.

I ushered Sergeant Cuff into my own room and subsequently gathered the servants in the hall. Rosanna Spearman appeared among them, much as custom dictated. She moved with the same rapidity as the Sergeant, and I suspected she had overheard his remarks concerning the servants in general, just prior to his discovery of her. Indeed, there she stood, appearing as though the concept of the shrubbery was entirely alien to her existence.

I dispatched them individually, as desired. The cook was the first to enter the Court of Justice, or so I termed my chamber. Her tenure there was brief. Upon exiting, her report ran: "Sergeant Cuff is dispirited; yet, Sergeant Cuff is a perfect gentleman." The mistress's own maid followed. Her stay proved considerably longer. Her report, upon departure: "If Sergeant Cuff cannot vouch for a woman of standing, he would do well to keep his opinions to himself, at the least!" Penelope went next, remaining but a moment or two. Her exit report: "Sergeant Cuff deserves profound pity. He must have suffered a romantic disappointment in his youth, father." The first housemaid trailed Penelope, lingering as long as the mistress's maid. Her verdict, upon emerging: "I did not enlist in your ladyship's service, Mr. Betteredge, to be publicly doubted by a low-ranking police officer!" Rosanna Spearman followed. She remained longer than any of her predecessors. There was no report upon her departure—only a dead silence, lips pale as ash. Samuel, the footman, succeeded Rosanna, staying for a minute or two. His report: "Whoever polishes Sergeant Cuff's boots ought to blush with shame." Nancy, the kitchen-maid, arrived last, enduring a similar duration. Her concluding remark: "Sergeant Cuff possesses a heart; he does not jest with a poor, hardworking girl, Mr. Betteredge."

Upon re-entering the Court of Justice to ascertain if further instructions awaited me, I found the Sergeant engaged in his customary pastime—gazing out the window and humming "The Last Rose of Summer."
