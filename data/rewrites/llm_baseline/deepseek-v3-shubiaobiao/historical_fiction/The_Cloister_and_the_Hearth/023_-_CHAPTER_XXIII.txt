Life and liberty are like the sun at noon—taken for granted until shadow falls. Then their true worth shines bright as gold among the elms. So it was with Gerard and Margaret, who moved through the whispering trees, hearts trembling yet glowing with the fierce joy of escape. The baying of hounds and the shouts of cruel men still echoed in their minds, but here, in the quiet wood, they were free.

Then Gerard saw the blood on Margaret’s feet.

“Martin!” he cried, seizing the soldier’s arm. “Look—she’s wounded! The crossbow—”

Margaret smiled softly, shaking her head. “No, Gerard. Not wounded.”

His face twisted in fear. “Then what—”

She touched his cheek, her own flushing. “Do not scold me.”

“When have I ever scolded you?”

“Never, my love.” She lowered her eyes. “Martin said the dogs followed the scent of blood. So I thought… if I marked my shoes with it, they would chase me instead of you.” She held out her arm, where a thin, deliberate cut glistened. “Forgive me?”

Gerard choked. “A scratch? This is a wound!” His voice shook as he cradled her arm.

Martin snorted. “A pinprick. The lad acts like she’s lost a limb.”

Gerard shot him a dark look. “I could stand a river of blood—but not hers.” He pressed his forehead to hers. “How could you do this?”

Margaret’s smile was tender. “Silly Gerard,” she whispered, winding her arms around his neck. “As if I wouldn’t give every drop in my veins for you.”

And then, with the terror of the hunt still fresh, they clung together, weeping—not just for the danger passed, but for the parting that loomed before them.

“I must leave you,” Gerard whispered, his voice breaking. “You to Holland, me to Italy. Oh God, must it be?”

Margaret said nothing. She only held him tighter, her silence more wrenching than any cry.

Soon they reached the edge of the wood, where a narrow path cut through the trees. Martin halted. “The road,” he muttered, nodding ahead. “And the inn lies yonder.”

Gerard and Margaret stared at each other, stricken.

“A word, Martin,” Gerard said hoarsely, drawing him aside. He pressed a purse into the soldier’s hand. “This was for my journey—she won’t take it, but you must. See that she lacks for nothing. The burgomaster’s silver will serve me.”

Martin gripped his shoulder. “No harm will touch her. I swear it.” He turned away, jaw tight.

Then came the farewell—too cruel to be witnessed. Martin stood apart, head bowed, as Gerard and Margaret knelt together, whispering vows like prayers. They clung, parted, then clung again, as if their very souls resisted the tearing.

At last, a strangled cry—and Gerard was running, hands raised to heaven, while Margaret swayed, her face ashen, arms outstretched to empty air.

Martin caught her before she fell. She did not weep. She did not speak. She only trembled as he lifted her onto the mule and led her slowly home.

At the edge of the village, he helped her down. “Go now,” he said gently. She obeyed, walking like one in a dream.

Martin turned toward Rotterdam. Sevenbergen was no longer safe.

Gerard moved as if sleepwalking. At the inn, he hired a horse and guide, then rode in silence toward the German border. The world passed like shadows—trees, houses, faces—all blurred.

“Shall we never be free of this cursed land?” he burst out once, savagely.

At dusk, they reached a hill overlooking a slender brook. “There,” said the guide, pointing. “Germany lies beyond.”

Gerard dismounted, paid the boy without a word, and descended alone. The water sparkled over smooth stones. He drank, bathed his face, then stared at the far bank.

With a sudden rush, he leaped across.

The moment his feet touched foreign soil, he whirled back. “Farewell, ungrateful country!” he cried. “Were it not for her, I’d leave you without a sigh—my kin, my home, my mother’s arms—all of it! Farewell, fatherland. The wide world is mine now.”

But the brave words crumbled. His knees buckled, and he sank to the ground, sobbing.

Then, after a time, he rose. Wiped his eyes. And without another glance behind, he turned his face toward Rome, stepping firmly into the unknown.

The road forked ahead, winding like serpents through the countryside. Gerard took out the little dial Peter had given him, held it to the sun, and chose his path—as sure, as solitary, as a swallow flying south.

Only this swallow flew alone.
