I went on with the moon for company.

She was always full—so full she seemed less a lamp than a presence—and that night, as she sank, I caught on her bright face a curve that might have been a smile. While her lower rim still touched the edge of the world, a cottage appeared in the very middle of her disc, as if a careful hand had painted it there: a low house with its door and window open to the moonlight, so that her beam lay on its floor like poured milk. With the sight came a certainty, quiet as hunger, that I was expected inside.

Then the moon slipped under, and the cottage went with her. Darkness thickened quickly. My path lay across a fretwork of little ravines and dry channels, and I did not fancy breaking my neck in the unseen. I chose a sandy hollow, as one chooses a hearth when there is no roof—made my supper of the fruit the children had pressed into my hands, and let their sweetness and the faint rasp of sand between my teeth lull me down.

I woke as if called, and above me were constellations I had never seen from my former sky—new patterns, new distances, and a strange silence behind them, like a held breath. I lay and listened: even the night seemed to have its own acoustics here, a wide, soft bowl of air in which every minute sound—my own swallow, the sift of sand settling, a far insect’s rasp—stood out clean. It was a kind of mercy after the grinding, mindless noise of machines in the world I had left, where men fill their days with din and call it progress.

Then I became aware of another presence.

A figure sat on the ground a little way from me, above the lip of my hollow, so that her outline cut the sky. From where I lay, she looked larger than human, as if the stars had chosen her for their foreground. She moved her head, and I saw her back was toward me.

“Will you not come with me?” said a voice—sweet, mellow, like warm bread broken open, the steam of it rising.

“I thank you,” I answered, startled into politeness, “but I am not uncomfortable here. Where would you have me go? I like sleeping in the open air.”

“There is no hurt in the air,” she returned; “but the creatures that roam the night in these parts are not such as a man would willingly have about him while he sleeps.”

“I have not been disturbed.”

“No; I have been sitting by you ever since you lay down.”

“That is very kind of you! How came you to know I was here? Why do you show me such favour?”

“I saw you,” she said, still giving me only the set of her shoulders, “in the light of the moon, just as she went down. I see badly in the day, but at night perfectly. The shadow of my house would have hidden you, but both its doors were open. I was out on the waste, and saw you go into this hollow. You were asleep, however, before I could reach you, and I was not willing to disturb you. People are frightened if I come on them suddenly. They call me the Cat-woman. It is not my name.”

I remembered what the children had said—ugly, and scratches. Yet her voice held no cruelty; it had the careful gentleness of someone who knows what a sudden noise can do to a living thing.

“You shall not hear it from me,” I said. “Please tell me what I may call you.”

“When you know me,” she answered, “call me by the name that seems to you to fit me. That will tell me what sort you are. People do not often give me the right one. It is well when they do.”

“I suppose,” I ventured, trying to steady my own thoughts with talk, “you live in the cottage I saw in the heart of the moon?”

“I do. I live there alone, except when I have visitors. It is a poor place, but I do what I can for my guests, and sometimes their sleep is sweet to them.”

Her voice settled on me like a cloth laid over a restless dish—stilling the steam, quieting the tremor. I rose.

“I will go with you, madam.”

She rose at once, and without a glance behind her began to walk. I could see her just well enough to follow: a tall woman, taller than I, but not the giant my first frightened fancy had made. That she never once turned her face toward me pricked my curiosity. It did not, at first, wake dread—her tone was too true for that. And yet, how was I to find her name, when she would not let me see her?

More than once I tried to come abreast; each time, when I quickened, she quickened as easily, keeping always a little ahead, as if the air itself carried her. The dry world around us gave back our footfalls thinly. Every now and then some small nocturnal sound—an unseen wing, a pebble turned—rang with unnatural clearness in the hollows. I began to feel the pressure of the silence between her words, and in it the old human fear that grows where there is no familiar noise: what cannot be heard begins to be imagined.

At last my apprehension rose into something like horror. Why was she so careful not to be seen? If she was grotesque, she might be sparing me; but the mind, when it cannot translate what it meets, invents monstrosities. I was following, through a black land, a being who would not show her face. The thought made my mouth dry.

Neither of us spoke, and the quiet became unbearable. I forced words into it.

“I want to find my way to a place I have heard of,” I said, “but whose name I have not yet learned. Perhaps you can tell it me.”

“Describe it, then, and I will direct you. The stupid Bags know nothing, and the careless little Lovers forget almost everything.”

“Where do those live?”

“You are just come from them.”

“I never heard those names before!”

“You would not hear them. Neither people knows its own name.”

“Strange!”

“Perhaps so! but hardly any one anywhere knows his own name. It would make many a fine gentleman stare to hear himself addressed by what is really his name!”

Her words went through me with a cold edge. I found myself wondering, in spite of myself, what my own name would sound like if spoken by someone who knew it better than I did.

“What now do you fancy yours?” she went on, as if she heard my thought the way one hears a shift in another’s breathing. “But, pardon me, it is a matter of no consequence.”

I had opened my mouth to answer—then discovered, with a shock like a cupboard found empty, that my name was gone. Not merely hidden: absent. I could not even grasp its first letter. This was the second time the question had come, and the same blank had swallowed the answer.

“Never mind,” she said. “It is not wanted. Your real name, indeed, is written on your forehead, but at present it whirls about so irregularly that nobody can read it. I will do my part to steady it. Soon it will go slower, and, I hope, settle at last.”

We walked on. The channels fell away behind us; the sand spread out in long, soundless slopes. Still no cottage.

“The Little Ones told me,” I said at length, clinging to the children’s world because it had had fruit and laughter in it, “of a smooth green country, pleasant to the feet.”

“Yes?” Her voice was a small lamp in the dark.

“They told me too of a girl giantess that was queen somewhere. Is that her country?”

“There is a city in that grassy land,” she replied, “where a woman is princess. The city is called Bulika. But certainly the princess is not a girl! She is older than this world, and came to it from yours—with a terrible history, which is not over yet. She is an evil person, and prevails much with the Prince of the Power of the Air.

“The people of Bulika were formerly simple folk, tilling the ground and pasturing sheep. She came among them, and they received her hospitably. She taught them to dig for diamonds and opals and sell them to strangers, and made them give up tillage and pasturage and build a city. One day they found a huge snake and killed it; which so enraged her that she declared herself their princess, and became terrible to them.

“The name of the country at that time was the Land of Waters; for the dry channels, of which you have crossed so many, were then overflowing with live torrents; and the valley, where now the Bags and the Lovers have their fruit-trees, was a lake that received a great part of them. But the wicked princess gathered up in her lap what she could of the water over the whole country, closed it in an egg, and carried it away. Her lap, however, would not hold more than half of it; and the instant she was gone, what she had not yet taken fled away underground, leaving the country as dry and dusty as her own heart.

“Were it not for the waters under it, every living thing would long ago have perished from it. For where no water is, no rain falls; and where no rain falls, no springs rise.

“Ever since then, the princess has lived in Bulika, holding the inhabitants in constant terror, and doing what she can to keep them from multiplying. Yet they boast and believe themselves a prosperous, and certainly are a self-satisfied people—good at bargaining and buying, good at selling and cheating; holding well together for a common interest, and utterly treacherous where interests clash; proud of their princess and her power, and despising every one they get the better of; never doubting themselves the most honourable of all the nations, and each man counting himself better than any other. The depth of their worthlessness and height of their vainglory no one can understand who has not been there to see, who has not learned to know the miserable misgoverned and self-deceived creatures.”

Her words painted the city without showing it: a place that had traded fields for stones that glitter, sheep-bells for the ring of coin. I could almost hear the soundscape of such a change—the steady, sane noises of husbandry drowned by the clatter of commerce and the harsh scrape of excavation. The thought made me ache for the children’s valley, where the loudest thing had been laughter and falling fruit.

“I thank you,” I said. “And now, if you please, will you tell me something about the Little Ones—the Lovers? I long heartily to serve them. Who and what are they? and how do they come to be there? Those children are the greatest wonder I have found in this world of wonders.”

“In Bulika you may, perhaps, get some light on those matters. There is an ancient poem in the library of the palace, I am told, which of course no one there can read, but in which it is plainly written that after the Lovers have gone through great troubles and learned their own name, they will fill the land, and make the giants their slaves.”

“By that time they will have grown a little, will they not?”

“Yes, they will have grown; yet I think too they will not have grown. It is possible to grow and not to grow, to grow less and to grow bigger, both at once—yes, even to grow by means of not growing!”

“Your words are strange, madam.”

“But I have heard it said,” she returned, “that some words, because they mean more, appear to mean less.”

I thought of how languages die—not with trumpet-blasts, but with small everyday surrenders. A word that once carried a whole climate of meaning gets replaced by a cheap, general token; soon no one remembers what is missing, because the loss makes no noise. Even here, in a land of wonders, names mattered. Names were not labels but histories, recipes of identity no stranger could truly taste unless he had grown up with the tongue that made them.

“That is true,” she said, “and such words have to be understood. It were well for the princess of Bulika if she heard what the very silence of the land is shouting in her ears all day long! But she is far too clever to understand anything.”

“Then I suppose,” I said, “when the little Lovers are grown, their land will have water again?”

“Not exactly so: when they are thirsty enough, they will have water, and when they have water, they will grow. To grow, they must have water. And, beneath, it is flowing still.”

“I have heard that water twice,” I said, and as I spoke I could almost taste it, cold and mineral on the tongue. “Once when I lay down to wait for the moon—and when I woke the sun was shining. And once when I fell, all but killed by the bad giant. Both times came the voices of the water, and healed me.”

She did not turn, and always kept that little lead; yet every syllable she uttered came to me as if set in a bowl and offered. Her voice reminded me of another woman’s voice—heard in a house where death lay orderly as a made bed.

Much of what she said afterward I did not understand, and so cannot keep; but the dread I had felt slid away, as if her very speaking had changed the air I breathed.

At last, after we had crossed yet another long tract of sand, the cottage stood before us. Its foundation sank deep in sand, but I could see the base was rock. In shape it resembled the sexton’s, though the walls were thicker, and the door looked made to bar more than wind.

She opened it, and we entered a large bare room. Two little windows faced each other, without glass, so that the night looked in freely. She went straight to the farthest corner, lifted a long white cloth from the floor, and wound it about her head and face. Then she closed the other door—the one the moon had looked through before—trimmed a small horn lantern on the hearth, and at last turned toward me.

“You are very welcome, Mr. Vane!” she said, speaking the name I had been unable to find inside my own mind. “Your entertainment will be scanty, but, as the night is not far spent, and the day not at hand, it is better you should be indoors. Here you will be safe, and a little lack is not a great misery.”

“I thank you heartily, madam,” I replied. “But, seeing you know the name I could not tell you, may I not now know yours?”

“My name is Mara,” she answered.

At the word, memory stirred—of the sexton, and the little black cat.

“Some people,” she went on, “take me for Lot’s wife, lamenting over Sodom; and some think I am Rachel, weeping for her children; but I am neither of those.”

“I thank you again, Mara,” I said. “May I lie here on your floor till the morning?”

“At the top of that stair,” she answered, “you will find a bed—on which some have slept better than they expected, and some have waked all the night and slept all the next day. It is not a very soft one, but it is better than the sand—and there are no hyenas sniffing about it!”

The stair was narrow and steep, rising straight from the room into an unceiled, unpartitioned garret with one broad, low dormer-window. Under the slant of the roof stood a narrow bed. Its white coverlet made me shiver—not from cold only, but from remembrance: the chamber of death, the still figures laid out as if waiting for a feast they could not yet taste.

On a table stood a dry loaf, and beside it a cup of cold water. After months of fruit—sweetness without salt, pleasure without depth—the plain smell of bread, even stale, struck me like a promise. The water had the clean, dull scent of a covered well.

“I must leave you in the dark,” my hostess called from the foot of the stair. “This lantern is all the light I have, and there are things to do to-night.”

“It is of no consequence,” I returned. “To eat and drink, to lie down and sleep, are things that can be done in the dark.”

“Rest in peace,” she said.

I ate the loaf, crumb by crumb, the dry crust rasping pleasantly against my teeth; I drank the water to the last drop, and felt it go through me as if my body remembered springs. Then I lay down. The bed was hard; the covering thin; the night sharp with cold. I dreamed I was again in that chamber, between the warrior and the lady with the wound that healed.

I woke in the middle of the night, thinking I heard the low noises of wild animals.

“Creatures of the desert scenting after me, I suppose,” I told myself; and because the walls were thick and Mara had promised safety, I would have slept again.

But at that instant a rough purring rose under my window, swelling into a howl that seemed to scrape the night itself. I sprang from the bed and looked out.

Before the cottage door, in full moon-radiance, a tall woman stood clothed in white, her back toward me—Mara again, face hidden as ever from my sight. At her feet was a great white animal like a panther, its body a pale muscle in the moonlight. She stroked it with one hand—slow, sure passes, as a cook smooths down dough to feel where it resists—while with the other she pointed to the moon halfway up the sky, then drew a straight line down to the horizon.

Instantly the creature darted away with astonishing speed, as if shot from a bow, straight along the line she had drawn.

My eyes followed it, then swung back to the woman; she was gone. Still I had not seen her face. I peered after the animal again; whether I truly saw a fading white speck or only imagined it, I cannot tell.

What did it mean? What errand could such a beast be sent upon? The sound it had made left my nerves raw. I went back to the bed trembling.

Then another thought struck me: when I had lain down in the sandy hollow, the moon had been setting; and yet here she was, only hours later, high and bright.

“Everything is uncertain here,” I whispered to myself, “even the motions of the heavenly bodies!”

I learned afterward that several moons served this world, but the laws of their comings and goings, their different paths and times, I never discovered. The sky itself here seemed to have more dialects than my understanding.

At last I slept again, and was not disturbed.

In the morning, when I went down, bread and water waited me—another loaf, so large I ate only half. My hostess sat muffled beside me while I broke my fast. The room held the clean, dry smell of flour and old stone. Outside, the air carried the faint whistle of wind over sand; inside, her stillness made a quieter silence, one that did not threaten.

Except to greet me when I entered, she did not speak until I asked her how to reach Bulika.

She told me to go up the bank of the river-bed until it disappeared; then verge to the right until I came to a forest—in which I might spend a night, but which I must leave with my face to the rising moon. Keeping in the same direction, she said, until I reached a running stream, I must cross it at right angles, and go straight on until I saw the city on the horizon.

I thanked her, and ventured a remark, for my mind still clung to what I had witnessed.

“Looking out of the window in the night,” I said, “I was astonished to see your messenger understand you so well, and go so straight and so fast in the direction you had indicated. If I had but that animal of yours to guide me—”

“It was to Bulika she went—the shortest way,” Mara interrupted, as if she would not have me follow the thought farther than she chose.

“How wonderfully intelligent she looked!”

“Astarte knows her work well enough to be sent to do it,” she answered.

“Have you many messengers like her?”

“As many as I require.”

“Are they hard to teach?”

“They need no teaching. They are all of a certain breed, but not one of the breed is like another. Their origin is so natural it would seem to you incredible.”

“May I not know it?”

“A new one came to me last night—from your head while you slept.”

I could not help laughing. In this world, even a man’s stray thought might take on fur and teeth and run errands. Mystery here was not a curtain drawn for show; it was the very air’s way of being, as if meaning itself had a native tongue I did not speak.

“Then the creature is mine!” I cried.

“Not at all,” she answered. “That only can be ours in whose existence our will is a factor.”

I had no answer ready. The bread sat heavy and honest in me; the words did not.

After a time I asked, “May I take what is left of the loaf?”

“You will want no more to-day,” she replied.

“To-morrow I may!”

“It has nothing to do with to-morrow,” she said, rising. “But you may take it if you will.”

She went to the door and opened it, holding it for me. I stood, took up the remaining bread, and lingered, shamefacedly hungry for one thing more than food: to see her face, to fit her at last with the right name.

“Must I go, then?” I asked.

“No one sleeps in my house two nights together!” she answered.

“I thank you, then, for your hospitality, and bid you farewell!” I said, and turned to go.

“The time will come when you must house with me many days and many nights,” she murmured sadly through her wrappings.

“Willingly,” I replied, for gratitude made me bold.

“Nay, not willingly!” she answered.

At once I thought she was right. I would not willingly be her guest again—not with that hidden face, not with that beast under the moon. But my heart rebuked me as soon as the thought formed, and before I had crossed the threshold I turned back.

She stood in the middle of the room. Her white garments lay about her feet like foamy waves, and among them lay the swathings she had removed. Her face—at last—was lovely as a night of stars. Great grey eyes looked upward as if listening to something far above the roof; tears ran down pale cheeks. She reminded me of the sexton’s wife, and yet not: the one had looked as if she had not wept for thousands of years; this one looked as if she wept continually behind the wrappings of her beautiful head. And still, in the very eyes that wept, there was a promise—as if sorrow here had its own old proverb: night does not erase morning.

I bowed my head, about to kneel and beg her forgiveness for my fear. When I lifted it in the motion, I found myself outside a house without a door.

I went around and around it, hands brushing the rough wall, searching for hinge or latch, for any seam in the stone. There was none. I stopped beneath one of the windows, ready to call out my confession.

Then a sudden wailing, howling scream invaded my ears—so sharp it seemed to strike the breath out of my chest.

Something sprang from the window above my head and landed beyond me. I turned and saw a large grey cat, its hair bristling, shooting toward the river-bed. The sound of it—wild and tearing—filled the air like a ripped cloth.

I fell with my face in the sand, and through it all I seemed to hear, from within the sealed house, the gentle sobbing of one who suffered, but did not repent.
