Mr. Thornton moved through the next day as if some hand had scoured his mind clean in the night, rubbing away yesterday’s humiliation until only a hard, bright edge of purpose remained. Trade, law, and town-business—he went into them with that direct, unflinching force that made older men, richer men—men whose money had long since been settled into acres and stone—turn, without knowing they turned, to the manufacturer whose wealth still ran like quicksilver through looms and ledgers.

There was a slight demand for finished goods, and he took it as a man takes a favourable wind: he leaned into it, drove bargains close and sharp, and came away with terms that showed no softness for any one’s convenience. At the meeting of magistrates he was punctual to the minute, his judgment fast, his sense of consequences quicker still; and when talk threatened to wander, he brought it back to the bone and sinew of action. He was deputed, almost as a matter of course, to see the police, to settle the requisite steps, to lead the necessary measures; and he accepted their unspoken deference as he accepted the weather—an element to move through, not a thing to be proud of. If he had once grown aware of it, it would have hampered him like praise does a runner: something catching at the sleeve.

It was his mother who drank in, with an eager pride that had something of hunger in it, what the women of those magistrates said in parlour and carriage. Mr. This had declared no one else would have managed it so. Mr. That had said matters would have gone very badly indeed if Mr. Thornton had not been there. She stored these sentences as if they were coin.

He swept his business aside right and left. The mortification of yesterday—so hot, so sharp—had left behind it a kind of fierce clearness. He felt his own power and, for a few hours, revelled in it; it was as if he could set his heart, too, under lock and key, and throw away the key by sheer will.

Evidence against Boucher and other ring-leaders of the riot was taken before him; that against three others, charged with conspiracy, failed. He dismissed the failing charge without hesitation, but he gave the police a stern, exact order to watch: the law’s right arm was to be ready—ready to strike the instant fault could be proved. Then, leaving the close, overheated borough court—where sweat and ink and the sour breath of a crowd lay thick—he went out into the street.

The air outside was fresher, but still heavy with heat. It held Milton’s constant flavour: coal-smoke hanging like a bitter seasoning over everything, and the metallic tang of industry that made even summer seem dark. From the distant mills came the never-resting grind and thud—the rasp of belts, the hammering pulse of engines—noise that had become to the town what sea-sound is to a coast: so constant it can soothe or maddens, depending on the heart that listens. To Thornton, it had long been mere accompaniment; yet today it struck him oddly, as if the town’s great din were trying to drown a different sound in him.

All at once he gave way. His limbs felt languid, his thoughts slipping their reins. They would not stay where he drove them—on law, on trade, on the coming week. They strayed, and always returned to her. Not to the sharp scene of yesterday’s refusal—he thrust that away—but to the looks and actions of the day before that: the sudden clinging, the quick beating of a heart against his, the brief human warmth in the midst of a roaring crowd. Memory, like a taste that comes back unbidden—salt on the lips after one has left the sea—returned and returned.

He threaded the crowded streets mechanically, avoiding people without seeing them, when a voice cut across his abstraction.

“Why, Mr. Thornton! you’re cutting me very coolly, I must say. And how is Mrs. Thornton? Brave weather this! We doctors don’t like it, I can tell you!”

He stopped as if checked by a hand. Dr. Donaldson stood before him, his professional cheerfulness riding on a tired knowledge.

“I beg your pardon, Dr. Donaldson. I really didn’t see you. My mother’s quite well, thank you. It is a fine day, and good for the harvest, I hope. If the wheat is well got in, we shall have a brisk trade next year, whatever you doctors have.”

“Ay, ay. Each man for himself. Your bad weather, and your bad times, are my good ones. When trade is bad, there’s more undermining of health, and preparation for death, going on among you Milton men than you’re aware of.”

“Not with me, Doctor. I’m made of iron. The news of the very worst bad debt I ever had, never made my pulse vary. This strike, which affects me more than any one else in Milton,—more than Hamper,—never comes near my appetite. You must go elsewhere for a patient, Doctor.”

The doctor’s look sharpened; he did not smile this time.

“By the way, you’ve recommended me a good patient, poor lady! Not to go on talking in this heartless way, I seriously believe that Mrs. Hale—that lady in Crampton, you know—hasn’t many weeks to live. I never had any hope of cure, as I think I told you; but I’ve been seeing her to-day, and I think very badly of her.”

Mr. Thornton’s vaunted steadiness failed him—only for an instant, but enough to make him feel it. The roar of Milton seemed to drop back a pace, as if giving room to that single sentence.

“Can I do anything, Doctor?” he asked; and the change in his voice betrayed him more than any words could.

“You know—you would see, that money is not very plentiful, are there any comforts or dainties she ought to have?”

“No,” said Dr. Donaldson, shaking his head. “She craves for fruit,—she has a constant fever on her; but jargonelle pears will do as well as anything, and there are quantities of them in the market.”

“You will tell me if there is anything I can do, I’m sure,” said Mr. Thornton. “I rely upon you.”

“Oh! never fear! I’ll not spare your purse,—I know it’s deep enough. I wish you’d give me a carte-blanche for all my patients, and all their wants.”

But Thornton’s feeling did not spread wide like a public subscription; it ran in a straight channel. He went, at once, without any more talk, to the first fruit-shop he met.

The shop was cool in comparison to the street, smelling of damp straw and green leaves, with a sweetness rising like a soft breath from bruised skins and cut stems. Baskets stood in ranks: pears with their pale speckled sides, peaches blushing under down, clusters of grapes with their translucent globes and powdery bloom. The shopman’s knife flashed as he trimmed a stem; somewhere a fly droned, trapped between window and fruit-scented air.

Mr. Thornton chose as if he were choosing for a palate that mattered. He took a bunch of purple grapes, heavy and tight, with the bloom unbroken; peaches the colour of late sunset, firm enough to yield slowly under the thumb; and fresh vine-leaves, still cool as if they remembered shade. He had them packed with care into a basket, the fruit cushioned so it would not bruise, straw tucked in, leaves laid over like a green cloth.

The shopman, respectful, waited. “Where shall we send them to, sir?”

Thornton did not answer at once. Something like a struggle passed over his face—quick, silent, fierce.

“To Marlborough Mills, I suppose, sir?”

“No!” Thornton said, sharply, as if the very thought offended him. “Give the basket to me,—I’ll take it.”

It took both his hands. He went out into the busiest part of town, where women were shopping and the pavements were thick with skirts and parcels. Many a young lady of his acquaintance turned to look at him—Mr. Thornton, who walked like a man with a purpose even when he had none, now carrying a basket like an errand-boy. He heard a faint ripple of voices, but Milton’s larger noise swallowed it up: cart-wheels on stone, a hawker’s cry, a distant hammering that never ceased, like some monstrous heart beating in iron.

Inside himself he argued, as if argument could fence off pain.

I will not be daunted from doing as I choose by the thought of her. I like to take this fruit to the poor mother, and it is simply right that I should. She shall never scorn me out of doing what I please. A pretty joke, indeed, if, for fear of a haughty girl, I failed in doing a kindness to a man I liked! I do it for Mr. Hale; I do it in defiance of her.

He went at an unusual pace, as if speed could keep his mind from slipping back. Soon Crampton was behind him; the air altered, losing some of its coal-bitter edge. Even so, the town’s sound followed, like a distant surf of industry.

He went up the stairs two steps at a time, entered the drawing-room before Dixon could announce him. His face was flushed from haste; his eyes shone with that earnest kindness which looks almost startling on a strong man used to command and hard dealing.

Mrs. Hale lay on the sofa, fever-bright, her breath quick and shallow. Mr. Hale was reading aloud in a gentle voice that tried to smooth the world. Margaret sat low by her mother, working—needle flashing, thread drawn through cloth with that quiet perseverance that held household life together when everything else threatened to break.

If Margaret’s heart fluttered, his did not show it. He made no sign that he saw her. He scarcely seemed to see even Mr. Hale. He went straight to Mrs. Hale, basket in hand, and spoke in a subdued tone, made tender by the effort of gentleness.

“I met Dr. Donaldson, ma’am, and as he said fruit would be good for you, I have taken the liberty—the great liberty—of bringing you some that seemed to me fine.”

Mrs. Hale’s eyes brightened with eager pleasure; her thin hand trembled as if already reaching in imagination for the cool burst of a grape, the fragrant softness of peach. In sickness, the body clings to such small comforts with the greed of a child.

Mr. Hale, with fewer words, expressed deeper gratitude—gratitude that came not only from the gift but from the thought behind it.

“Fetch a plate, Margaret—a basket—anything.”

Margaret rose and went to the table, moving as if noise might shatter something. She was half afraid of stirring Thornton into the full consciousness of her presence; she dreaded, in a way she could not have explained, the moment when their eyes might meet, and all the past two days flash between them like lightning. She fancied—because she wished it—that he had overlooked her in his haste; she had been low at first, and now she stood behind her father.

As if he did not feel her presence all over the room, like a pressure of air before thunder, though his eyes never rested on her.

“I must go,” he said. “I cannot stay. If you will forgive this liberty,—my rough ways,—too abrupt, I fear—but I will be more gentle next time. You will allow me the pleasure of bringing you some fruit again, if I should see any that is tempting. Good afternoon, Mr. Hale. Good-bye, ma’am.”

And he was gone.

Not one word. Not one look to Margaret.

She truly believed he had not seen her. She went for a plate in silence and lifted the fruit out tenderly, her taper fingers careful not to crush the bloom or bruise the velvet of the peaches. The vine-leaves gave a faint green scent as she moved them. It was good of him to bring it; and after yesterday too. The gift had the quiet, unmistakable weight of intention.

“Oh! it is so delicious!” Mrs. Hale said, in her feeble voice, after she had tasted. The grape’s cool juice seemed to ease her burning mouth for a moment. “How kind of him to think of me! Margaret love, only taste these grapes! Was it not good of him?”

“Yes!” said Margaret, quietly; and her quietness was not indifference so much as restraint.

“Margaret!” Mrs. Hale’s tone grew querulous, sharpened by pain and weakness. “You won’t like anything Mr. Thornton does. I never saw anybody so prejudiced.”

Mr. Hale had taken a peach and, with the careful domestic skill of one who has learned to make small luxuries for an invalid, began to peel it. The skin came away in a thin curl, releasing that rich scent—honeyed, warm, almost like summer made edible. He cut a slice for his wife, then, smiling faintly, a little piece for himself.

“If I had any prejudices,” he said, “the gift of such delicious fruit as this would melt them away. I have not tasted such fruit—no! not even in Hampshire—since I was a boy; and, to boys, I fancy, all fruit is good. I remember eating sloes and crabs with a relish. Do you remember the matted-up currant bushes, Margaret, at the corner of the west-wall at the garden at home?”

Did she not?

The words, so simple, touched the tenderest nerve. She remembered not only currant bushes but the very air: the soft hush of Hampshire evenings, the birds’ small calls, the particular quiet that lies between hedges, where even silence seems alive and kindly. She remembered the old stone wall—every weather-stain; the gray and yellow lichens mapping it like continents; the little crane’s-bill in crevices. The memory rose with scents—leaf-mould and sun-warmed earth—and with sounds, too: not Milton’s iron pulse, but the home-language of fields, the dialect of wind in apple-boughs.

She had been shaken by the last two days; her life was a strain upon her fortitude; and those careless words, reaching back to the sunny old time, loosened her. She started up, dropped her sewing on the floor, and went hastily out of the room into her little chamber.

She had scarcely given way to the first choking sob when she became aware of Dixon standing at her drawers, rummaging as if in search of some particular thing. The movement of linen brought out a familiar scent of lavender, stored there long ago to keep away moth and damp—a scent that carried its own small history of women’s hands, of careful household ways, of customs that persisted even when all else changed.

“Bless me, miss! How you startled me! Missus is not worse, is she? Is anything the matter?”

“No, nothing. Only I’m silly, Dixon, and want a glass of water. What are you looking for? I keep my muslins in that drawer.”

Dixon did not answer at once. She went on searching, lips tight, as if reluctant to speak. At last she found what she wanted—something Margaret could not see clearly in Dixon’s grasp.

Dixon turned round, faced her, and said, with the air of one who dislikes carrying bad news.

“Now I don’t like telling what I wanted, because you’ve fretting enough to go through, and I know you’ll fret about this. I meant to have kept it from you till night, may be, or such times as that.”

“What is the matter? Pray, tell me, Dixon, at once.”

“That young woman you go to see—Higgins I mean.”

“Well?”

“Well! she died this morning, and her sister is here—come to beg a strange thing. It seems, the young woman who died had a fancy for being buried in something of yours, and so the sister’s come to ask for it,—and I was looking for a night-cap that wasn’t too good to give away.”

“Oh! let me find one,” said Margaret, tears still on her face. “Poor Bessy! I never thought I should not see her again.”

“Why, that’s another thing. This girl downstairs wanted me to ask you, if you would like to see her.”

“But she’s dead!” Margaret said, turning a little pale. “I never saw a dead person. No! I would rather not.”

“I should never have asked you, if you hadn’t come in. I told her you wouldn’t.”

Margaret’s breath caught. Her first recoil was fear—fear of the stillness of death, of the sight that would not be softened by speech. But a different fear followed: that Dixon’s bluntness would strike the grieving sister like a blow, that the poor girl would go away feeling refused, refused even that small tribute of respect which, among their people, meant something deep and wordless.

“I will go down and speak to her,” Margaret said. She took the cap in her hand and went down to the kitchen.

Mary Higgins stood there, her face swollen with crying, red-eyed and spent. At the sight of Margaret she broke afresh into sobs.

“Oh, ma’am, she loved yo’, she loved yo’, she did indeed!”

For a long while that was all she could say, as if the phrase were the only plank left in her. Margaret, moved with pity, waited; Dixon’s sharp scolding—meant, perhaps, to force speech—at last helped to press a few facts out through the grief.

Nicholas Higgins had gone out in the morning, leaving Bessy as well as she had been the day before. But within an hour she had grown worse; some neighbour had run to the room where Mary was working. They did not know where to find her father. Mary had only reached home a few minutes before Bessy died.

“It were a day or two ago she axed to be buried in somewhat o’ yourn,” Mary said, wiping her face with her apron. “She were never tired o’ talking o’ yo’. She used to say yo’ were the prettiest thing she ever clapped eyes on. She loved yo’ dearly. Her last words were ‘Give her my affectionate respects; and keep father fro’ drink.’ Yo’ll come and see her ma’am. She would ha’ thought it a great compliment, I know.”

Margaret shrank, again, from the thought of going, from the strangeness of the sight, from the hush of a poor room where the usual noises—coughing, scolding, the clink of a cup—would be replaced by that other silence. Yet the sound of Mary’s dialect, the very shape of her words—so different from drawing-room speech, carrying a whole world of belonging and class and power in its syllables—made Margaret feel, sharply, the weight of what was being asked. In Mary’s tongue, “respect” was not a pretty word; it was a duty, a recognition. To refuse might be read as contempt, and contempt is an easy language for the comfortable to speak without meaning to.

“Yes, perhaps I may. Yes, I will. I’ll come before tea. But where’s your father, Mary?”

Mary shook her head and began to move as if to go.

“Miss Hale,” Dixon said low, drawing near, “where’s the use o’ your going to see the poor thing laid out? I’d never say a word against it, if it could do the girl any good; and I wouldn’t mind a bit going myself, if that would satisfy her. They’ve just a notion, these common folks, of its being a respect to the departed.”

Then, turning sharply to Mary, Dixon added in a louder voice, as if cutting the matter short would make it clean, “Here. I’ll come and see your sister. Miss Hale is busy, and she can’t come, or else she would.”

Mary’s eyes lifted, wistful and doubtful, to Margaret. Dixon’s coming might be something—better than nothing—but it was not what Bessy had meant. Margaret could see it in the girl’s face: the old little jealousy, perhaps, that had once pricked between sisters, now turned into a grief that wanted one particular comfort.

“No, Dixon!” Margaret said, with sudden decision. “I will go. Mary, you shall see me this afternoon.”

And, fearing her own cowardice—fearing that if she stayed in the kitchen another minute she might begin to argue herself out of it—she went away at once, holding to her resolve as if it were something she must clutch tightly lest it slip from her in the next breath.
