Ethne did not turn toward Durrance. She remained motionless, her violin resting on her lap, her gaze fixed across the moonlit garden to the silver band of light that slipped through the trees. Her stillness was deliberate—a shield, a sanctuary. It allowed her to believe that Harry Feversham himself stood before her, speaking in his own voice, even though it was Durrance who spoke. The man beside her faded into silence, irrelevant, almost invisible. She listened with the same intensity as he, yearning for the words to unfold slowly, so the message would stretch out—long enough for her to gather every syllable and hold it close.

"It was the night before I set off eastward into the desert—for the last time," Durrance began, his voice heavy with regret. He lingered on those words, "last time," as if they carried a weight too deep for words. But Ethne felt nothing—no sorrow, no pity. Only the echo of something far more personal.

"Yes," she said softly. "That was in February. The middle of the month, wasn't it? Do you remember the day? I’d like to know exactly."

"The fifteenth," he replied.

She repeated it, letting the date settle in her mind. "I was at Glenalla all February," she murmured. "What was I doing on the fifteenth? It doesn’t matter."

A strange sensation had gripped her earlier that morning, when Willoughby told his story—how she had known nothing, by instinct or intuition, of what had happened. Now, that feeling returned. It was odd, almost cruel, that she should only learn of Harry’s fate now—on this August night, beneath this quiet garden, bathed in moonlight and stillness. A pang of guilt pierced her. *It was my fault,* she thought. *If I had believed in him, I would have known. I am well punished.*

She could not imagine the message bringing anything but good news. It would confirm what she already hoped—the tale would expand, complete, perfecting the moment. That much she knew without doubt.

"Well?" she said. "Go on."

"I'd spent the whole day in my office finishing up work," Durrance continued. "At ten o'clock, I locked the door, relieved to know I wouldn’t open it again for six weeks. Then I walked north along the Nile bank from Wadi Halfa into Tewfikieh. As I stepped into the main street, I saw a crowd gathered outside a café—Arabs, negroes, a few Greeks, Egyptian soldiers—all lit by the glare from within. I heard music: a violin and a zither, both played horribly, jangling out a waltz. I pushed through the crowd and looked inside."

He paused, and Ethne closed her eyes, painting the scene in her mind. She saw Harry bent over his zither, and instantly, the question came: *Why was he there?*

It made sense he wouldn’t return to England. It was certain he wouldn’t come back to her unless she called. And Captain Willoughby had said Harry expected no word from her—he hadn’t left any address. Yet here he was, disguised among beggars and musicians, playing for a meal and a night’s shelter.

"You spoke to him?" she asked abruptly.

"To whom? Oh—Harry?" Durrance replied. "Yes, later, after I realized it was him."

"How did you know?"

"The waltz ended. The old woman collapsed onto a bench. The young man lifted his head from his zither. The old man scraped another chord, and the girl stepped forward to sing. Her voice was fresh, youthful—but utterly lacking in skill. Still, the old man smiled. The mother tapped her foot proudly. The crowd laughed, tossed crude jokes. It was terrible, don’t you think?"

"Yes," Ethne whispered, but her mind was elsewhere. She felt no sympathy for the players—only fascination with Harry’s presence. She sat staring into the moonlight streaming across the floor, as if it were a window to that distant café, glowing under the African sky.

"And then?" she asked.

Durrance went on. "The young man began a solo on the zither. He played wrong notes—no tune at first. The crowd jeered. I turned away, heart sick—until suddenly, a sequence of correct notes caught my ear. I stopped. A melody emerged—thin, weak, almost ghostly—but familiar. I stood there, between hovels and stunted trees, and I was swept back to Ramelton—to a summer night beneath a soft Donegal sky. You were sitting by an open window, just as you are now, playing the Musoline Overture. The same piece you’ve played tonight."

"Was it a melody from that overture?" she breathed.

"Yes. And it was Harry Feversham who played it. I didn’t realize at once. I wasn’t quick then."

"But you are now," she said, smiling faintly. "You’d have known right away."

"True. But then, I was only curious—how had a Greek musician learned such a tune? Still, I decided to reward him. I thought you’d want me to."

"Yes," she whispered.

So when the young man stepped out, hat in hand, passing through the mocking crowd, I threw a sovereign into his hat. He turned—startled. I recognized him. Even with the beard. And before he could stop himself, he gasped, 'Jack!'"

"You couldn’t have been mistaken," Ethne said, breathless. "The man who played the zither was—"

She stopped herself. The name hovered on her lips, but she pulled it back. She remembered how Harry had no ear for music—how he’d never appreciated anything except what she played. "He had no talent," she said, laughing softly. "No taste. You couldn’t invent a discord harsh enough to catch his attention. He couldn’t have remembered a note from the Musoline Overture."

"Yet he did," Durrance insisted. "Somehow, he remembered. I can see it. He had so little to hold onto. That one memory—this melody—was worth everything. He must have practiced it endlessly, humming it, whistling it, making mistakes until finally, one day, it came clear. Then, slowly, laboriously, he shaped it on the zither. Can’t you imagine it? Him alone in the dark, trying again and again, until he could play it—not perfectly, but truly?"

Ethne understood. She had her answer. The overture—her favorite—had carried the message: *I have not forgotten you.* Despite the white feather, despite the shame, Harry still thought of her—with friendship, with care. He hadn’t played that melody in vain.

She was shaken—more deeply than she ever thought possible. She wondered if, as he sat in that bare café, strumming for strangers, he’d dreamed that somehow, across oceans and deserts, the echo of that tune might reach her. She knew now—beyond doubt—that she could never truly forget Harry Feversham. The image of that café, lit against the desert night, would haunt her forever. But she would not let it weaken her resolve. She would continue to pretend to forget—because she would not let Durrance suffer for her past mistakes.

"I told you last year," Durrance said, "at Hill Street, that I never wanted to see Feversham again. I was wrong. The reluctance was all his. Not mine. When he realized he’d called my name, he tried to slip back into the crowd, babbling Greek. But I grabbed his arm. I wouldn’t let go."

"He had done you great wrong," Ethne interrupted sharply. "I know that."

But Durrance shook his head. "Not then. All I remembered was that years ago, Harry Feversham was my friend—my only true friend. We rowed together at Oxford. He was stroke, I was seven. I still see the stripes on his jersey during the final sprint. We swam at Sandford Lasher, ate supper on Kennington Island, skipped lectures, paddled up the Cher to Islip. And here he was—at Wadi Halfa—playing zither for scraps, living like an outcast."

"No," Ethne said firmly. "It wasn’t because he needed money. His father kept sending him allowance. He accepted it."

Durrance blinked. "You’re sure?"

"Quite sure. I heard it today."

She didn’t realize she’d slipped. But Durrance, preoccupied, didn’t notice. Nor did she.

"So you never asked him why he was there?" she pressed. "Why didn’t you?"

Her voice trembled with disappointment. This was the last news—yet incomplete, like a letter torn in half. The truth might never be known.

"I was a fool," Durrance admitted. There was real regret in his voice—equal to hers. "I had him by the arm, and I let him go. I was a fool." He struck his forehead with his fist.

"He tried Arabic," Durrance went on. "Said they were peaceful travelers. Offered to give back the money. But I held him. 'Harry Feversham,' I said, 'that won’t do.' Then he gave in—and whispered in English: 'Let me go, Jack. Let me go.'"

There was a crowd around them. Clearly, Harry had reason to hide. Perhaps shame—shame at his fall.

"I told him to come to my quarters when he could," Durrance said. "Then I let him go."

All night, he waited on the verandah. But Harry never came.

In the morning, he started across the desert. At the very last moment, as his camel rose, he leaned down to speak—then stopped. "I knew nothing about his allowance," he explained. "I thought he’d fallen low. It didn’t seem fair to tell anyone else."

Ethne nodded. She understood. Though her heart ached for the lost words.

"So you never saw him again?"

"I was gone nine weeks. I came back blind," he said simply. The quiet honesty pierced her.

He went straight to Cairo. Calder came with him. He told Calder about seeing Harry at Tewfikieh. He asked Calder to look for him, to help if possible—and to write.

"A week ago," Durrance said, "I received a letter. And I’m troubled—very troubled."

"What did it say?" Ethne asked, leaning forward, her face shadowed. She moved toward him, needing to see his expression—but the darkness hid it. A cold fear crept into her veins.

"The two women and the old Greek," Durrance said, "left on a steamer for Assouan."

"Then Mr. Feversham stayed?" she asked eagerly.

"No," Durrance replied. "Harry Feversham didn’t stay. He slipped past Halfa the day after I left. He went south—in the morning."

"Into the desert?"

"Yes. The enemy’s country. He was seen. There’s no doubt."

Ethne was silent. Then, slowly: "You have the letter?"

"Yes."

"I’d like to read it."

She rose, crossed to him. He handed it over. She took it, walked to the window, where the moonlight was bright. She stood there, one hand pressed to her chest, reading—once, twice.

The letter confirmed it. The café owner said Joseppi—Feversham’s alias—had wandered south, carrying a water-skin and dates. Why? He didn’t know—or wouldn’t say.

Ethne had a question. But she hesitated—too long—before she could speak.

"What will happen to him?" she finally asked.

"At best," Durrance said, "capture. At worst—death. Starvation. Thirst. Or the Dervishes. But there’s a chance—just a hope—he’ll be taken prisoner. He’s white. If caught, they’ll think he’s a spy. They’ll believe he knows our plans. Most likely, they’ll send him to Omdurman. I’ve written to Calder. Spies pass through Wadi Halfa. We hear things from Omdurman. If Feversham is taken there, I’ll find out. But he must have gone mad. That’s the only explanation."

Ethne had another explanation—one she knew to be true. And she spoke it aloud, unguarded.

"Colonel Trench," she said, "is a prisoner at Omdurman."

"Oh," Durrance said, nodding. "Feversham won’t be quite alone. That’s some comfort. Perhaps something can be done. When I hear from Calder, I’ll tell you."

He didn’t understand. He still didn’t know why Harry had gone south. And he never would. For Ethne, even now, refused to break her vow. She stood at the window, clutching the letter, fighting to remain composed. She would not cry. Would not faint. She would speak quietly, calmly—even though she knew Harry had gone south to join Colonel Trench.

But the strength failed her. As Durrance began to speak again, the need to escape overwhelmed her. The garden called—cool, dark, peaceful. She needed to be alone.

"Perhaps you wonder," Durrance said, "why I’ve told you this tonight. I didn’t dare before. I want to explain why."

Ethne didn’t hear him. She didn’t care. The sound of his voice was unbearable. She was near the window. One step—silent, careful—and she was free.

She gathered her dress, stepped over the sill, and vanished into the shadows.

This was the third slip she made that night.
