Having supplied me with the name of Mr. Candy's assistant, Betteredge seemed to conclude that we had expended sufficient time on a triviality. He folded Rosanna Spearman's letter and returned to his seat, leaving me to sit by the window, awaiting his conclusion.

Gradually, the profound impression Ezra Jennings had made upon me began to recede. It remained utterly inexplicable to me, given my precarious circumstances, that any human interaction should have left such a mark. As that sensation faded, my thoughts reverted to their accustomed channels. I forced myself to confront my own absurd predicament with renewed resolve. I mentally rehearsed the course of action I had finally planned: to return to London immediately, to lay the entire case before Mr. Bruff, and, above all, to secure—by any means necessary and regardless of cost—a private audience with Rachel. This was my strategy, as far as my shaken state allowed me to formulate it.

With over an hour remaining before the train's departure, I hoped Betteredge might uncover something in the unread portions of Rosanna's letter that would prove vital before I left the house where the diamond had vanished. I waited for this possibility.

The letter concluded as follows:

*"You have no need to be angry, Mr. Franklin, even if I derived a small triumph from knowing that I held all your prospects in my hands. Soon, however, anxiety and fear returned. Given Sergeant Cuff's perspective on the diamond's loss, he would inevitably examine our linens and dresses. There was no corner in my room, indeed no spot in the entire house, where I felt confident the item would remain safe from his scrutiny. How could I conceal the nightgown so thoroughly that not even the Sergeant could locate it? And how could I achieve this without wasting a single precious moment? These were difficult questions. My hesitation ended when I chose a course that might amuse you. I undressed and wore the nightgown myself. You had worn it, and I seized a fleeting moment of joy in wearing it after you.*

*The next intelligence reaching the servants' hall revealed I had secured the garment too late. Sergeant Cuff demanded to see the washing book. I retrieved it and brought it to him in my mistress's sitting-room. We had encountered one another frequently in years past. I was certain he would recognize me, though I was unsure of his reaction upon discovering a servant employed in a household where a valuable jewel had disappeared. In this suspense, I felt relieved simply to endure the encounter and learn the worst immediately.*

*When I handed him the book, he regarded me as a stranger, offering excessive politeness in thanks. I interpreted these as ominous signs. One could never predict what he might whisper behind my back, nor how swiftly I might find myself arrested and searched. Just then, you returned from escorting Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite to the station. I retreated to your favorite walk in the shrubbery, hoping for another chance to speak to you—the final opportunity I knew I possessed.*

*You never appeared. Worse still, Mr. Betteredge and Sergeant Cuff passed the spot where I hid, and the Sergeant spotted me. I had no choice but to retreat to my duties before further disaster befell me. As I stepped onto the path, you emerged from the station, heading straight for the shrubbery. Upon seeing me, I am convinced you recognized me, yet you turned away as if I carried a contagion and entered the house.*

*(Note by Franklin Blake: The writer is entirely mistaken, poor creature. I never noticed her. My intention was indeed to stroll in the shrubbery, but recalling simultaneously that my aunt might wish to see me upon my return, I changed my mind and went inside.)*

*I returned indoors via the servants' entrance. The laundry was empty, and I sat alone. The thoughts instilled by the Shivering Sand resurfaced. I pondered which burden would be heavier: enduring Mr. Franklin Blake's indifference, or leaping into the quicksand to end it all. It is futile to ask me to justify my conduct at that time; I struggle to comprehend it myself. Why did I not stop you when you shunned me so cruelly? Why did I not cry out, 'Mr. Franklin, I have something to say concerning yourself'? You were at my mercy; I held the whip-hand. Moreover, possessing the means to assist you in the future, I could have been useful. Naturally, I never imagined a gentleman stole the diamond merely for sport. Penelope had heard Miss Rachel, and I had heard Mr. Betteredge, discuss your extravagance and debts. It was evident you had taken the diamond to sell or pledge it for funds. I could have directed you to a man in London willing to advance a substantial sum without prying into your affairs. Why did I not speak? Why did I not speak?*

*I wonder if the risks of concealing the nightgown exceeded my capacity, adding to other dangers? Perhaps for others, yes, but not for me. In my youth as a thief, I navigated risks fifty times greater, escaping difficulties that seem child's play compared to this. Could such a minor task weigh upon my spirit when I should have spoken? Such a query is nonsense. It was impossible.*

*Where is the use of dwelling on my folly? The truth is plain: behind your back, I loved you with every fiber of my being. Before your face, I was terrified—afraid of angering you, afraid of your reaction if I confessed I had discovered the diamond. I approached as closely as I dared when I spoke in the library. You did not turn away then; you did not flee as if I were diseased. I tried to provoke anger to summon courage, but I could muster only misery and humiliation. 'You're a plain girl with a crooked shoulder; you're only a housemaid—why attempt to speak to me?' You never uttered these words, Mr. Franklin, yet you conveyed them all the same!*

*Is such madness explicable? No. I must confess it and accept it. I apologize again for this digression; I promise it will not recur. I am nearing the end.*

*The first to disturb my solitude was Penelope. She had discerned my secret long ago and endeavored gently to restore my sense. 'Ah!' she said, 'I know why you sit here fretting alone. The best outcome for your advantage, Rosanna, is the end of Mr. Franklin's visit. He will depart shortly.'*

*In all my thoughts of you, I never considered your leaving. I could only stare at her. 'I have just left Miss Rachel,' Penelope continued. 'It was a hard trial to endure her temper. She finds the house unbearable with the police present and intends to speak to my lady tonight, visiting her Aunt Ablewhite tomorrow. If she does, Mr. Franklin will soon find a reason to leave as well.'*

*'Only too gladly if permitted, but she refuses. He has incurred her displeasure; he is in her black books despite his efforts to help her. If they do not reconcile by tomorrow, you will see Miss Rachel go one way and Mr. Franklin another. His destination remains unknown, but he will not stay after she departs.'*

*I suppressed the despair rising within me. Admitting the truth, I glimpsed hope if a serious rift existed between Miss Rachel and you. 'Do you know the nature of their quarrel?' I asked.*

*'It lies entirely on Miss Rachel's side,' Penelope replied. 'For all I know, it is her temper alone. I hesitate to distress you, Rosanna, but do not imagine Mr. Franklin is likely to quarrel with her. He loves her too dearly for that!'*

*Her cruel words were barely spoken when Mr. Betteredge called for all indoor servants to assemble in the hall. We were to enter his room sequentially for interrogation by Sergeant Cuff. It was my turn after the lady's maid and the upper housemaid.*

*Sergeant Cuff's inquiries, though veiled, quickly revealed that the two women (my bitterest enemies) had discovered traces outside my door on Tuesday afternoon and Thursday night. Their testimony opened his eyes to partial truths. He correctly surmised I had sewn a new nightgown secretly but erroneously assumed the paint-stained one belonged to me. I understood from his tone that while he suspected my involvement in the diamond's disappearance, he did not hold me primarily responsible. He seemed to believe I acted under someone else's direction—an identity I could not then, nor now, deduce.*

*In this uncertainty, one fact was clear: Sergeant Cuff was worlds away from knowing the full truth. You were safe as long as the nightgown remained concealed—not a moment longer.*

*I despair of explaining the terror pressing upon me. It was impossible to wear the nightgown any longer. I faced imminent arrest at Frizinghall Police Court and search. Since Cuff still spared me, I had to decide instantly: destroy the nightgown or hide it safely away from the house.*

*Had I loved you less, I might have destroyed it. But how could I obliterate the sole proof that I had shielded you from discovery? If we eventually reconciled, and you suspected foul motives, how could I earn your trust without producing the nightgown? Would it be unjust to believe, as I do, that you might hesitate to share your secret with a poor girl unless I offered tangible evidence? Consider your cold demeanor, sir, and you will scarcely wonder at my reluctance to destroy the only claim I possessed on your confidence and gratitude.*
