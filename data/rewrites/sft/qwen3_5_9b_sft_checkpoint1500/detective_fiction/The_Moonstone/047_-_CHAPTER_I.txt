Scarcely a word was required from me to conclude the tale recounted in the Journal of Ezra Jennings. Of my own actions, I need only state that I awoke on the morning of the twenty-sixth, utterly unaware of the statements I had made or deeds I had performed while under the drug's influence—from the moment the opium claimed me until I opened my eyes in Rachel's sitting-room.

Regarding the hours following my awakening, I deem it unnecessary to offer a detailed account. Suffice it to say that, before a single word of explanation was spoken by either party, Rachel and I reached a complete understanding. Neither I nor she feels compelled to explain the extraordinary speed of our reconciliation. Gentlemen and ladies, recall a time when you were passionately devoted to one another; you will then comprehend what transpired after Ezra Jennings closed the sitting-room door, just as surely as I do.

However, I have no objection to noting that we would undoubtedly have been discovered by Mrs. Merridew were it not for Rachel's presence of mind. Hearing the rustle of the old lady's gown in the corridor, she instantly rushed forth to intercept her. I heard Mrs. Merridew ask, "What is the matter?" and Rachel answer, "The explosion!" Mrs. Merridew was immediately taken by the arm and led into the garden, clearing her from the path of the impending shock. Upon returning to the house, she encountered me in the hall and expressed profound amazement at the advancements of science since her schoolgirl days. "Explosions, Mr. Blake, are infinitely milder than they were," she assured me. "I barely heard Mr. Jennings's explosion from the garden. And there is no lingering smell, that I can detect, now that we have returned indoors! I must truly apologize to your medical friend; it is only fair to say he has managed it beautifully!"

Thus, after overcoming Betteredge and Mr. Bruff, Ezra Jennings vanquished Mrs. Merridew herself. There remains, after all, a vast reservoir of liberal sentiment within the world!

At breakfast, Mr. Bruff openly voiced his desire for me to accompany him to London by the morning train. The watch kept at the bank, and the potential results thereof, appealed irresistibly to Rachel's curiosity. Consequently, she resolved immediately to join us back to town—provided Mrs. Merridew had no objection—to ensure she was within reach of the earliest news regarding our proceedings.

Mrs. Merridew proved to be wholly pliable and indulgent, given the considerate nature of the explosion; accordingly, Betteredge was informed that we were all four to travel back together by the morning train. I fully expected him to volunteer to accompany us, but Rachel had wisely assigned her faithful old servant an occupation that captivated him. He was charged with completing the refurnishing of the house and was so engrossed in his domestic duties that he felt no "detective-fever" as he might have under other circumstances.

Our sole regret in departing for London was the necessity of parting, more abruptly than desired, with Ezra Jennings. It was impossible to persuade him to travel with us. I could only promise to write to him, while Rachel insisted on his visiting her upon her return to Yorkshire. Every prospect existed for a reunion within a few months; yet, there was something profoundly sorrowful in witnessing our best and dearest friend standing alone on the platform as the train departed the station.

Upon our arrival in London, Mr. Bruff was intercepted at the terminus by a small boy, clad in threadbare black cloth, whose physical distinction lay in the extraordinary prominence of his eyes. They protruded so far and rolled so loosely that one wondered uneasily why they remained within their sockets. After listening to the boy, Mr. Bruff asked the ladies if they would excuse our accompanying them back to Portland Place. I had barely promised Rachel that I would return and recount everything before Mr. Bruff seized me by the arm and hurried me into a cab. The boy, with his ill-secured eyes, took his place on the box beside the driver, who was directed to proceed to Lombard Street.

"News from the bank?" I asked as we started.

"News of Mr. Luker," said Mr. Bruff. "An hour ago, he was seen to leave his house at Lambeth in a cab, accompanied by two men recognized by *my* men as police officers in plain clothes. If Mr. Luker's dread of the Indians motivates this precaution, the inference is plain enough. He intends to remove the Diamond from the bank."

"And we are proceeding to the bank to witness the outcome?"

"Yes—or to ascertain what has transpired, if the matter is concluded by this time. Did you observe my boy on the box?"

"I noted his eyes."

Mr. Bruff laughed. "They call the poor little wretch 'Gooseberry' at the office," he said. "I employ him to run errands—and I wish only that my clerks, who have nicknamed him, were as trustworthy as he is. Gooseberry is one of the sharpest lads in London, Mr. Blake, despite his eyes."

Twenty minutes to five, we drew up before the bank in Lombard Street. Gooseberry looked longingly at his master as Mr. Bruff opened the cab door.

"Do you wish to enter?" asked Mr. Bruff kindly. "Then do so, and keep at my heels until further orders. He is as quick as lightning," Mr. Bruff continued, addressing me in a whisper. "Two words suffice with Gooseberry where twenty would be required with another lad."

We entered the bank. The outer office, featuring the long counter behind which the cashiers sat, was crowded with patrons awaiting their turn to withdraw or deposit funds before the bank closed at five.

Two men from the crowd approached Mr. Bruff immediately upon his appearance.

"Well," asked the lawyer. "Have you seen him?"

"He passed us here half an hour since, sir, and proceeded into the inner office."

"Has he not emerged again?"

"No, sir."

Mr. Bruff turned to me. "Let us wait," he said.

I scanned the surrounding crowd for the three Indians. Not a sign of them was visible. The only individual present with a noticeably dark complexion was a tall man in a pilot coat and round hat, resembling a sailor. Could this be one of them in disguise? Impossible! The man exceeded the stature of any of the Indians, and his face, where not obscured by a bushy black beard, was twice as broad as theirs.

"They must have a spy stationed somewhere," said Mr. Bruff, glancing at the dark sailor. "And he may be the man."

Before he could utter another word, his coat-tail was gently tugged by his attendant spirit, the boy with gooseberry eyes. Mr. Bruff looked where the boy pointed. "Hush!" he whispered. "Here is Mr. Luker!"

The money-lender emerged from the bank's interior, flanked by his two guardian policemen in plain clothes.

"Keep your eye on him," Mr. Bruff whispered. "If he passes the Diamond to anyone, he will do so here."

Ignoring us both, Mr. Luker slowly navigated his way toward the door, weaving through the densest and thinnest parts of the crowd. I distinctly saw his hand move as he passed a short, stout man, respectably attired in a suit of sober grey. The man started slightly and cast a glance after him. Mr. Luker continued his slow progress. At the door, his guards positioned themselves on either side. They were all three followed by one of Mr. Bruff's men—and then they vanished from sight.

I looked at the lawyer and then signaled significantly toward the man in the grey suit. "Yes!" whispered Mr. Bruff. "I saw it too!" He turned to locate his second man, who was nowhere to be seen. He peered behind him for his attendant sprite; Gooseberry had disappeared.

"What the devil does this mean?" Mr. Bruff exclaimed angrily. "They have both abandoned us at the precise moment we require them most."

It became the turn of the man in the grey suit to transact business at the counter. He paid a cheque, received a receipt, and turned to depart.

"What is to be done?" asked Mr. Bruff. "_We_ cannot debase ourselves by following him."

"_I_ can!" I declared. "I would not lose sight of that man for ten thousand pounds!"

"In that case," rejoined Mr. Bruff, "I would not lose sight of _you_ for double that amount. A delightful occupation for a man in my position," he muttered to himself as we followed the stranger out of the bank. "For Heaven's sake, do not mention it. I should be ruined if it were known."

The man in the grey suit boarded an omnibus heading westward. We followed. There remained latent reserves of youth in Mr. Bruff. I assert it positively: when he took his seat in the omnibus, he blushed!

The man in the grey suit halted the omnibus and alighted in Oxford Street. We followed again. He entered a chemist's shop.

Mr. Bruff started. "My chemist!" he exclaimed. "I fear we have made a mistake."

We entered the shop. Mr. Bruff and the proprietor exchanged a few words in private. The lawyer rejoined me with a deeply crestfallen countenance.

"It is greatly to our credit," he said, taking my arm and leading me out—"that is one comfort!"
