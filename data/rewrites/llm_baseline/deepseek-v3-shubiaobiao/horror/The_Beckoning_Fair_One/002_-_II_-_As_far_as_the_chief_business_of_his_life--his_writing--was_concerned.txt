Paul Oleron measured his life by the slow, creeping progress of his pen. At forty-four, he had long abandoned the notion of balancing scales—his art demanded too much to leave room for such trifles. The world had not been kind, but he repaid it in prose, weaving tales that shimmered with a nobility he still, stubbornly, believed in.

His new home—a relic of another century, its bricks scarred by old insurance marks—was meant to be a sanctuary. Yet even as he unpacked his few belongings, arranging them with the precision of a man accustomed to transience, he felt the weight of something unseen. The house breathed around him, its silence thick with withheld secrets.

The closet in the kitchen was the first to betray itself. Its door bore a curious hatch, once used for powdering wigs, now a grotesque little mouth. Oleron hesitated before prying it open, his fingers brushing against the warped wood. Inside, he found nothing but dust and two forgotten wig stands, their mushroom shapes looming like severed heads in the dim light. He laughed, but the sound withered in his throat.

Work should have been his refuge. *Romilly Bishop*, his half-finished novel, lay waiting, her voice murmuring just beyond reach. But the house would not let him listen. The noises of the square outside—hawkers, schoolchildren, the tinny strum of a mandolin—pressed in, relentless. And then there were the whispers of the rooms themselves. He caught himself staring at blank walls, wondering what had once hung there, what eyes had watched from those now-empty corners.

The window seats, he discovered, were false. Beneath layers of paint, his chisel exposed hinges, rusted shut. The first two were empty, gaping like toothless mouths. The third yielded something soft, folded, heavy with age. Shaking it free of dust, Oleron found himself holding a triangular bag of frieze, its crimson fabric faded to the color of old blood. Straps dangled from it, limp and useless.

He did not know what it was. But as he stuffed it into a corner of the kitchen, he felt the house exhale around him, as if it had been waiting for him to find it.

That night, he dreamed of *Romilly*. She stood in the doorway of his bedroom, her face veiled, her hands clutching a bundle wrapped in stained cloth. When he woke, the first light of dawn was creeping through the curtains, and the air smelled faintly of powder and decay.

The house was awake. And so, he realized with a slow, creeping dread, was something else.
