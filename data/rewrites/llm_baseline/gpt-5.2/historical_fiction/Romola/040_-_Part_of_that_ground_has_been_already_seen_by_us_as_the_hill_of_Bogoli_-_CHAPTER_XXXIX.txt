On stepping into the pavilion, Tito’s eyes travelled with their quick, practised ease over the gathered faces, and what he read there confirmed what he had already guessed on the path through the gardens: this was no mere supper thrown for pleasure, but a supper meant to do the old Florentine work of binding men together—politics kneaded into bread, sealed with wine, and made easier to swallow by laughter.

The Rucellai were among the few in Florence who still understood the ceremony of a great table. In a city that prided itself on sober living, there were households where abundance was treated almost as a public language—where the ordering of courses and the choice of spices spoke as plainly as any speech in the Palazzo. Here, splendour had the decent excuse of tradition. And if the talk would not rise to the high altitudes that once belonged to these gardens, no one minded the bust of Plato looking on: marble could be made to bless anything, from philosophy to stewed fish, and the frescoed Virtues on the walls could endure a little good eating without blushing.

That same Plato had watched, in other seasons, a different sort of feast. After Lorenzo’s death the gatherings of the Platonic Academy had been transplanted here; and on the thirteenth of November—said to be Plato’s own day—the scholars would come, their moderation carefully displayed, their admiration less so. Under laurel branches, the old marble brow had looked down on Mirandola’s youth and Mirandola’s later humility; on Marsilio Ficino’s patient mind made almost soft by too single a diet of thought; on Poliziano, whose Latin had blood in it; and earlier still on Alberti, large and vigorous as an oak among reeds—artist and inventor, poet and engineer at once. Their names were the loud ones; behind them were the quieter labours, like furrows made by hands long dead—work that fed later generations without being thanked.

Bernardo Rucellai himself would have held a place among such men even without this pavilion and this table. Not yet old—four-and-forty—and already wearing that air of guarded dignity which makes an introduction feel like a privilege. He had the reputation of an amazingly pure Latinity, and the shrewdness not to be trapped into displaying it for any curious foreigner. To-night he greeted Tito with a favour more marked than usual, and placed him between Lorenzo Tornabuoni and Giannozzo Pucci—young men of the Medicean side, polished, watchful, and hungry for their chance.

Before any serious word could be risked, the household’s ritual softened the company into a single breathing creature. The great brass bowl went round, warm water scented with herbs—rosemary and something sharper that lifted like citrus when it met the skin. Men held out pale hands heavy with rings; droplets slid from knuckles into the basin and were caught again by the servant’s cloth. Wax-lights made the damp fingers gleam. The air carried the clean, faintly starchy fragrance of fresh white damask newly come from France, and underneath it the more persuasive smell of the kitchen—fat turned sweet in heat, wine simmered down, pepper waking as if it had a voice.

The first remarks were as light as the first mouthfuls: gossip and jokes served as a prelude, like little salted things that invited thirst.

“What would Dante’s old Florentine say,” some one asked, “if you brought him back under his leathern belt and bone clasp and set him down before silver forks?”

That notion ran round, brightening each man into an ancestor for a moment. It was agreed, with a comfortable air of wisdom, that posterity would always look indecent to the dead.

Meanwhile the forks—newly fashionable, their silver tines flashing—toyed with those small dishes that are meant to awaken appetite without satisfying it: morsels of liver cooked to the precise point where resistance ceases and the richness spreads, warm and yielding, across the tongue. The brass service had enamelled silver centres; men admired the workmanship between bites. Naturally the talk found its way to the confetti dish: Antonio Pollajuolo’s masterpiece, and Pollajuolo himself seduced, as they said with half envy, by patronising Popes from Florence to a Rome more glittering than any Florentine family could provide.

“Ah, I remember,” said Niccolò Ridolfi, a middle-aged man with that negligent ease which is in truth the most long-trained form of command. “I remember our Antonio in a fury about his chiselling and enamelling. He took to painting out of spite—‘The artist who puts his work into gold and silver,’ said he, ‘puts his brains into the melting-pot.’”

“And his foreboding may prove true enough,” said Giannozzo Pucci. “If this pretty war with Pisa goes on, and the revolt spreads, it is not only our silver dishes that will go. Antonio’s silver saints round the altar of San Giovanni may vanish some day from the eyes of the faithful—to be worshipped more devoutly as coin.”

“The Frate is preparing us for that already,” Tornabuoni said, with a laugh that tried to sound careless. “He tells the people God will not have silver crucifixes and starving stomachs—says the church is best adorned with the gems of holiness and the fine gold of brotherly love.”

“A very useful doctrine for war-finance,” Bernardo Rucellai observed dryly, “as many a condottiere has found. But politics come after the confetti, Lorenzo—when we can drink wine enough to wash them down. They are too solid to be taken with roast and boiled.”

“Yes, indeed,” Ridolfi answered. “Luigi Pulci would have said this delicate boiled kid must be eaten with an impartial mind. I remember a day at Careggi—Luigi rattling as usual—maintaining that nothing perverted the palate like opinion. ‘Opinion,’ said he, ‘corrupts the saliva—that’s why men took to pepper. Scepticism is the only philosophy that doesn’t bring a taste in the mouth.’ ‘Nay,’ says poor Lorenzo de’ Medici, ‘you must be out there, Luigi. Here is this untainted sceptic, Matteo Franco, who wants hotter sauce than any of us.’ ‘Because he has a strong opinion of himself,’ Luigi flashes out—‘which is the original egg of all opinion.’ He a sceptic? He believes in the immortality of his own verses. Poor Luigi—his mind was sharpest steel; it could touch nothing without cutting.”

“And yet gentle-hearted,” said Pucci. “His talk seemed to me only soap-bubbles—yet he went into dithyrambs about eating and drinking, and was as temperate as a butterfly.”

Course followed course with the assured gravity of wealth. After roast and boiled came capon and game, and then the crowning caprice: a peacock cooked, they said, after Apicius’s receipt for partridges—roasted with the feathers on, and then arranged so that it might look, at a distance, like a living bird at rest, its glossy colours saved for the eye while the flesh within had been sacrificed to fashion. A confidential servant—the official carver—approached it almost as a priest approaches an altar, and with respectful skill turned the classical, insipid creature to expose the plucked breast, from which he dispensed the delicate slices to each of the honourable company.

Hardly any one dared refuse. To decline would have sounded like a criticism of the host, of tradition, of rank itself. Tito quoted Horace with airy grace, then broke his slice into harmless fragments and scattered them over his plate as if he were feeding a bird. Bernardo Rucellai made a learned remark about the ancient price of peacocks’ eggs, and did not pretend to eat. Ridolfi held a mouthful balanced on his fork while he told, as if it were the most natural thing in the world, a favourite story of Pulci’s about a man of Siena who wished to give a splendid entertainment at a moderate cost, so bought a wild goose, cut off its beak and webbed feet, and boiled it in its feathers to pass it for a pea-hen.

Very little peacock went down any throat; but that did not matter. The company had the satisfaction of knowing the peacock had been served; the dish had done its social work. To sit where such foolish expense could be offered without apology was to feel oneself, for an hour, inside the circle of the city’s richest men—inside the language of privilege. And at such a time, when Fra Girolamo’s sermons were teaching Florence to suspect luxury as a sin against the hungry, even silence about the peacock’s toughness had its politics.

Beyond the pavilion’s warm breath of candle-smoke, wine, and spiced fat, the gardens lay in chill obscurity. In that darkness a solitary man moved in widening and narrowing circuits like something driven by a string pulled tight.

Baldassarre paused among the trees and stared in at the windows. Each window was a bright picture cut out against the gloom: rings flashing, cups lifted, heads thrown back in laughter. He could hear the interlacing voices, that peculiar music of a table where men speak at once and understand one another through habit as much as through words. He saw Tito’s gestures—careless, graceful, as if his whole body were fluent in persuasion—and heard Tito’s voice rise and fall, now distinct, now mingling into the merry confusion.

Baldassarre’s mind was strung to a pitch that made the sounds inside feel like blows. He was preparing himself for the moment when he could force an entrance—when he could step from this cold into that light and make the light serve his vengeance. And the sight of Tito’s easy gaiety gave him a savage satisfaction: the victim was fattening himself, laughing, warming his blood, making his nerves more tender for torture.

But the men among the branching tapers could know nothing of the pale fierce face outside. Light can be a curtain as well as darkness.

Within, as the dishes grew heavier and the wine more liberal, the talk, like a stream, deepened and turned from glitter to current. The sense of citizenship pressed on every mind just then; even indifferent men felt the city’s pulse. What Fra Girolamo was saying and prompting lay under all conversation, like a bass note you cannot ignore. Before the stewed fish was removed and while the favourite sweets still waited, his name rose, despite Bernardo Rucellai’s earlier prohibition, to the surface again. Politics slipped in as easily as sauce.

At first, while servants were still within earshot, it was only gossip: what had been done in the Palazzo on the first day of voting for the Great Council; how hot-tempered and domineering Francesco Valori was, as if austere virtue gave him a right to command; how any one who heard Soderini’s speeches and also listened to the Frate’s sermons could tell they were kneaded in the same trough.

“My opinion is,” Ridolfi said, “the Frate has a longer head for public matters than Soderini, or any Piagnone among them. Depend upon it, Soderini is his mouthpiece more than he is Soderini’s.”

“No, Niccolò,” said Bernardo Rucellai. “There I differ. The Frate has an acute mind; he sees quickly what serves his ends. But Pagolantonio Soderini has long experience—has studied the Venetian Council. He is not likely to be much indebted to a monk for ideas. No: Soderini loads the cannon—Fra Girolamo brings the powder and lights the match. He is master of the people, and the people are getting master of us. Ecco!”

When the room was at last clear of servants and only wine moved from hand to hand, Lorenzo Tornabuoni leaned forward, and the loosened restraint of privacy made his voice warmer.

“Whether Soderini is indebted or not,” he said, “we are indebted to the Frate for the general amnesty that has gone along with this scheme of the Council. We might have done without fear of God and reform of morals being passed by majority of black beans; but the excellent proposition that our Medicean heads should be allowed to remain comfortably on our shoulders—and that we should not be obliged to hand over our property in fines—has my warm approval. And you may rely upon it, Fra Girolamo is as firm as rock on that point of promoting peace. I have had an interview with him.”

A murmur ran along the farther end of the table. Men leaned, listening. Bernardo Rucellai only nodded, as if he had expected it and wished Tornabuoni to continue.

“Yes,” Tornabuoni went on, “I was favoured with an interview in the Frate’s own cell—not a common favour. I believe even Francesco Valori seldom sees him in private. But I think he saw me the more willingly because I was not a ready-made follower; I had to be converted. And for my part, I see clearly enough the only safe policy for us Mediceans is to throw our strength into the scale of the Frate’s party. We are not strong enough to make head on our own behalf; and if the Frate and the popular party were upset, every one here knows perfectly well what other party would be uppermost just now—Nerli, Alberti, Pazzi, the rest—Arrabbiati, as somebody christened them the other day—who, instead of giving us amnesty, would fly at our throats like mad dogs, and not be satisfied till they had banished half of us.”

Assent broke out—short, emphatic sounds, the clink of a cup set down.

“A wise dissimulation,” Tornabuoni continued, “is the only course for moderate rational men in times of violent party feeling. I need hardly tell this company what my real attachments are. I am not the only man here tied personally to the banished family. But apart from that, I agree with older friends—who allow me to speak before them—that the only lasting peaceful state for Florence is the predominance of some single family interest. The Frate’s theory—that we are to have a popular government where every man strives only for the general good, knows no party names—may do for some island of Cristoforo Colombo’s finding, but will never do for our fine old quarrelsome Florence. A change must come. With patience and caution we may determine the change in our favour. Meanwhile, the best thing is to keep the Frate’s flag flying, for if any other were hoisted now it would be a black flag for us.”

“It’s true,” Ridolfi said curtly. “What you say is true, Lorenzo. For my part, I am too old for any one to believe I’ve changed my feathers. And there are certain of us—old Bernardo del Nero for one—whom you would never persuade to borrow another man’s shield. But we can lie still like sleepy old dogs; barking would be no use. As for this psalm-singing party who vote for nothing but the glory of God, and talk as if vice could be swept out with a besom by the Magnificent Eight, their day will not be long. After all the talk of scholars, there are but two sorts of government: one where men show their teeth, and one where they show their tongues and lick the feet of the strongest. They’ll get their Great Council finally voted to-morrow—that’s certain—and think they’ve found a new plan. But as sure as there’s human skin under every lucco in the Council, the new plan will end like every other—in snarling or in licking. That’s my view as a plain man. Not that I think it becoming in men of family, with others depending on them, to go hunting reasons in the air like doctors of law. I say frankly, as head of my family, I shall be true to my old alliances; and I’ve never yet seen a chalk-mark on political reasons to tell me which are true and which false. Bernardo Rucellai is a man of reasons, I know; I’ve no objection to anyone spinning fine reasons for me—so long as they don’t interfere with my actions as a man who has faith to keep.”

“If that is an appeal to me, Niccolò,” Bernardo Rucellai answered with a formal dignity almost comic beside Ridolfi’s blunt ease, “I may say that while my wishes are partly determined by long-standing relations, I cannot enter into positive schemes with persons whose actions I cannot control. I myself might be content with a restoration of the old order—only with modifications, important modifications. And on one point I concur with Lorenzo Tornabuoni: our friends should throw the weight of their interest into the scale of the popular party. For myself, I condescend to no dissimulation; nor do I at present see the party or scheme that commands my full assent. In all there is crudity, confusion; and of all the twenty men who are my colleagues in the present crisis there is not one with whom I do not find myself in wide disagreement.”

Ridolfi shrugged, leaving that to stand. Wine warmed the room into competing voices; several men wanted the lead at once, and as usual the company broke into little knots, pairs and trios, the way a loaf breaks along its natural seams.

This was precisely what Lorenzo Tornabuoni and Giannozzo Pucci had foreseen. They were among the first to turn away from the broad public talk and draw Tito into a closer, quieter conversation—pushing their seats back, turning their backs half to the table as if to create, by posture alone, a private chamber within the larger one.

“In truth, Melema,” Tornabuoni said, crossing one hose-clad leg over the other and caressing his ankle in an idle, self-possessed way, “I know no man in Florence who can serve our party better than you. You see what most of our friends are—men who can no more hide their prejudices than a dog can hide the tone of his bark; or else men with ties so notorious they must always be suspected. Giannozzo and I, I flatter myself, can overcome that suspicion—we have that power of concealment and finesse without which a cultivated man is at a disadvantage compared with a wild bull or a savage. But except yourself, I know no one on whom we could rely for necessary discretion.”

“Yes,” Pucci added, laying his hand with friendly possession on Tito’s shoulder, “you can help us better than if you were Ulysses himself. For I am convinced Ulysses often made himself disagreeable. To manage men one must have a sharp mind in a velvet sheath. And there is not a soul in Florence who could undertake a business like this journey to Rome with the same safety you can. Your scholarship is always a pretext for such journeys; and better than scholarship is your talent. Niccolò Machiavelli might have done for us if he had been on our side—hardly so well. He is too much bitten with notions; he hasn’t your power of fascination. All the worse for him. He has lost a chance, and you have got it.”

Tornabuoni lowered his voice as if he were sprinkling spice into a dish that must not taste too strongly of itself.

“You have only to play your game well, Melema, and the future belongs to you. The Medici will keep a foot in Rome as well as Florence; the time may not be far when they can make a finer career for adherents than in old days. Why should you not take orders some day? There is a cardinal’s hat at the end of that road, and you would not be the first Greek to wear it.”

Tito laughed—lightly, almost musically. His intelligence measured the exaggeration; yet the flattery had a pleasant savour, like a rich sauce tasted at the tip of the tongue.

“My joints are not so stiff yet,” he said, “that I can’t be induced to run without so high a prize. The income of an abbey or two held in commendam—without the trouble of getting my head shaved—would satisfy me at present.”

“I was not joking,” said Tornabuoni, with grave suavity. “A scholar is always the better for taking orders. But we’ll speak of that another time. One object now is that you should win the confidence of the men who hang about San Marco. That is what Giannozzo and I shall do; but you can carry it farther, because you are less observed. In that way you can know their doings thoroughly, and make a broader screen for your agency on our side. Nothing can be done before you start for Rome—this business between Piero de’ Medici and the French nobles must be effected at once. I mean when you come back, of course; I need say no more. You could make yourself the pet votary of San Marco if you liked; but you are wise enough to know effective dissimulation is never immoderate.”

“If it were not that adhesion to the popular side is necessary to your safety as our agent,” Pucci said, more fraternal, less patron-like than Tornabuoni, “I could have wished your skill employed in another way, for which it is even better fitted. But now we must find some other man among us to get into the confidence of our sworn enemies, the Arrabbiati. We need to know their movements more than those of the Frate’s party, who are strong enough to play above-board. Still—it would have been difficult for you, with your known relations to the Medici a little while back, and your wife’s sort of kinship with Bernardo del Nero. We must find a man with no distinguished connections, who has not yet taken any side.”

Tito, almost without thinking, pushed his hair backward—his habitual gesture when he felt the pressure of a plan forming under words. He looked straight at Pucci; a barely perceptible smile rested on his lip, as if he were tasting something sharp and promising.

“No need to look out for any one else,” he said promptly. “I can manage the whole business with perfect ease. I will engage to make myself the special confidant of that thick-headed Dolfo Spini, and know his projects before he knows them himself.”

The confidence was unusual in Tito; but his blood was lifted. A new path opened before him, and along it hung prizes higher than any he had dared to name in his own mind. Hitherto he had thought of success as favour bestowed—something one received with bows and gratitude. Now it flashed upon him as power—power possible even to a man without ancestral roots, without a party name, without beliefs that could bind him. Each group that imagined him a tool might become dependent on him; each would feed him with secrets as a kitchen feeds a favoured guest with morsels before the feast. His foreignness, his indifference to the inherited dialects of feeling and prejudice around him—things that once made him cautious—turned suddenly into advantages. In the rich mingled sound of the supper—cups clinking, voices thickening, the hush that fell and rose like wind through trees—he felt newly awake to his own adroitness, as if he had found at last the right instrument and discovered his fingers fit it.

And whatever motives might once have checked him—whatever tenderness of conscience, whatever recoil from layered falsehood—had been, by slow degrees, strangled in him by the successive falsities of his life.
