"What sort of woman ought one to be?" the first gentleman asked. "Sir, consult the tastes of marriageable men. On this planet, stores of iron, cotton, wool, and chemicals—all matter shaped by our skilled hands—are forged into forms that answer demand. The market's pulse dictates value, high or low, by some grand rule. Our daughters must become wives, and whatever men choose becomes the standard for women; men's taste is woman's test. Do you catch the phrase? 'Tis excellent, I believe—the meaning perfectly balanced by the rhythm of the words."

"Nay, but turn it around," replied the second gentleman. "Give us the test of taste. Consider a fine menu—is it today what Roman epicures insisted a gentleman must eat to earn the dignity of dining well?"

Brackenshaw Park overlooked the scene from its gentle heights, gazing far across the neighboring valley to the distant eastern downs and the broad, slow rise of cultivated land stretching westward like a vast curtain. Upon the highest platform of the clustered hills stood the castle, constructed of rough-hewn limestone, its surface alive with shifting lights and shadows cast by dark lichen dust and the washings of rain. Masses of beech and fir trees sheltered it to the north, spreading down the green slopes like flocks seeking the water that gleamed below.

At the farthest end of the park lay the archery ground, a carefully tended enclosure on a patch of tableland. Protected to the southwest by towering elms and a thick hedge of holly, it offered agreeable afternoon shade for the gravel walks and the freshly mown turf where targets stood. The Archery Hall, with its arcade facing forward, resembled a white temple against the northern greenery. It served as a perfect backdrop for clusters of ladies moving, bowing, and turning their necks with the leisurely grace of lilies suddenly given the ability to move.

Even when the military band from Wanchester fell silent, the atmosphere remained delightful. Musical laughter rang through all registers, mingling with a harmony of friendly conversation that rose to mild excitement before sinking back into a pleasant murmur. Few outdoor amusements escaped the noisy, crowding conditions that ruin modern pleasures; few archery meetings were so select. Tickets were limited to ensure the gathering remained convenient for the subsequent dinner and ball held within the castle. Inside the enclosure, no common spectators were admitted except Lord Brackenshaw's tenants and their families. Among these, it was chiefly the women who exercised the privilege, bringing their children or younger siblings. While the young men among them relieved the monotony of the event with imaginative betting—"anything you like" being the stake on their favorite archers—the young maidens operated on a different principle of discrimination, wondering which of the sweetly dressed ladies they would choose to emulate if given the option.

Probably, these rural souls would have strived for a form unlike Gwendolen's—one flushed with more pink and crowned with the most fashionable yellow hair. Yet, among the male judges standing closest to her, there was unusual unanimity in declaring her the finest girl present. No wonder she reveled in that July day. Pre-eminence is sweet to those who cherish it, even under mediocre circumstances. Perhaps it was not purely mythical that a slave takes pride in being bought first; nor that a barnyard fowl, unaware of its status, might strut with a self-aware consciousness of being the best of a bad lot, finding comfort in its relative importance. But for true enjoyment, the outer and inner selves must align. And for Gwendolen, this alignment was occurring.

Who could deny that bows and arrows are among the fairest weapons for feminine forms to wield? They invite attitudes brimming with grace and power, releasing the fine concentration of energy found in marksmanship from any association with bloodshed. The time-honored British pastime of "killing something" was no longer pursued with bow and quiver; bands defending their passes fought under different skies, and poisoned darts existed only as harmless remnants in rhetoric or distant lands. Archery possessed no foul odor of brimstone; it broke no shins and bred no monstrous athletes. Its sole danger was failure, a prospect sufficient to mold skillful action in generous hearts. Furthermore, the prizes awarded by the Brackenshaw Archery Club were of noble symbolism: not parcels to degrade honor into gain, but gold and silver arrows, stars to be worn proudly on the chest as tokens of achievement before passing to the next worthy competitor. These signs of excellence held the virtue of wreaths without their inconvenience, avoiding the potential melancholy of heavy floral arrangements in the heat of a ballroom.

Altogether, the Brackenshaw Archery Club was an institution crafted with impeccable taste, designed to avoid ridiculous incidents. Today, every element favored it. The air was mildly warm, devoid of wind to disturb hair, drapery, or the flight of an arrow. Skillful preparation met fair play, and when the general march began to retrieve arrows, the procession of joyful young creatures in light speech and laughter, moving gracefully toward a common goal, was a sight worth witnessing. Here, Gwendolen seemed a Calypso among her nymphs. Every observer admitted her surpassing charm in her attitudes and movements.

"That girl is like a high-mettled racer," Lord Brackenshaw remarked to young Clintock, one of the invited guests.

"First chop! Tremendously pretty too," agreed the elegant Greek, who had paid her assiduous attention. "I never saw her look better."

Perhaps she had never looked so well. Her face beamed with youthful pleasure, free of any malignant ray of discontent. Satisfied with her own prospects, she felt kindly toward everyone and content with the universe. Lacking the highest rank or the label of heiress like Miss Arrowpoint, she derived an added triumph from eclipsing those advantages. Regarding personal recommendation, she would not trade her family group for any other: her mother's appearance suited an amiable duchess, while her Uncle and Aunt Gascoigne, accompanied by Anna, made equally gratifying figures. Gwendolen was too full of joyous self-belief to feel the slightest jealousy, even knowing Miss Arrowpoint was one of the finest archeresses.

Even the unexpected reappearance of the formidable Herr Klesmer seemed merely to suit Gwendolen's inclination to be amused. Short of Apollo himself, what greater musical maestro could possibly command attention at an archery meeting? A satirical light danced in Gwendolen's eyes as she glanced toward the Arrowpoint party upon their entrance, noting the stark contrast between Klesmer and the average assembly of English country folk. This juxtaposition reached its peak intensity near his hosts—or patrons, as Mrs. Arrowpoint preferred to call them, hoping thereby to deny any lingering notion of patronizing genius, whose royalty was universally acknowledged. Such a contrast might have amused a graver soul, but Gwendolen found it delightful.

"We English are a miscellaneous people," she observed internally. Any fifty of us might display varied architectures of character or facial ornamentation, yet our prevailing expression lacks the liveliness of an impassioned race preoccupied with ideals. The strong point of the pure English gentleman lies in his easy style of figure and clothing; he objects to marked irregularities in his attire and to appearing inspired. Imagine an assembly of such men watching Herr Klesmer enter: his mane of hair floating backward in massive contradiction to the chimney-pot hat perched comically atop his pronounced, well-modeled features and powerful, clean-shaven jaw. His tall, thin frame was clad in a manner that, lacking strict English convention, emphasized his intent too heavily. Draped in a loose garment with a Florentine *berretta*, he would have been fitting beside Leonardo da Vinci. But how did he fare in trousers that defied English expectations regarding the knee? How did the fire in his glances and the curious sweep of his head transform into comedy when capped by a hat demanding well-cropped hair and staid demeanor, such as Mr. Arrowsmith's, whose nullity of face and perfect tailoring passed unnoticed anywhere?

One understands why it is often better for greatness to be dead, shedding the burden of the outward man. Many present knew Klesmer, or heard of him, but only from candlelit evenings where he appeared simply as a musician. He lacked yet that supreme, worldwide celebrity that renders an artist great to the ordinary public through sheer expense and renown. It was a new light for them to see him in—unexpectedly presented on a July afternoon within an exclusive society. Some laughed; others felt a touch of disgust at the Arrowpoints' lack of judgment in using an introductory card.

"What extreme types those artistic fellows usually are!" young Clintock told Gwendolen. "Look at the figure he cuts, bowing with his hand on his heart to Lady Brackenshaw—and Mrs. Arrowpoint's feather just brushing his shoulder."

"You are one of the profane," Gwendolen retorted. "You are blind to the majesty of genius. Herr Klesmer smites me with awe; I feel crushed in his presence; my courage oozes away."

"Ahh, you understand all about his music."

"No, indeed," Gwendolen laughed lightly. "It is he who understands all about mine and finds it pitiable."

Klesmer's verdict on her singing had become an easier joke since he had been struck by her *plastic* artistry. "It is not addressed to the ears of the future, I suppose. I'm glad of that: it suits mine."
