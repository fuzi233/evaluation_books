The final exchanges between the siblings in town concluded with a single, ironic note: a faint invitation from Fanny to visit Norland—a destination so improbable it bordered on fiction—and a warm, private assurance from John that he would arrive promptly at Delaford. Yet, amidst these gestures, a singular pattern emerged that amused Elinor: every friend seemed determined to send her to Delaford, a place she now wished to avoid above all others. It was designated her future home by her brother and Mrs. Jennings, and even Lucy, upon their parting, pressed an invitation to reside there.

Very early in April, the two parties departed their respective residences in Hanover Square and Berkeley Street, converging by appointment on the road. The journey required more than two days due to the necessity of accommodating Charlotte and her infant, while Mr. Palmer, traveling with greater expedition alongside Colonel Brandon, intended to join them at Cleveland shortly after their arrival. Marianne, though she had spent few hours in comfort during her London tenure and yearned desperately to escape it, found the act of bidding farewell excruciating. She lingered over the house where she had last harbored the extinguished hopes and shattered confidence regarding Willoughby. Furthermore, leaving the estate where Willoughby remained, immersed in new engagements and schemes from which she was forever excluded, drew forth a flood of tears.

Elinor's departure, conversely, carried a distinct positivity. Her thoughts lacked objects for lingering regret; she parted from no creature whose absence would cause her sorrow. She welcomed the liberation from the relentless pursuit of Lucy's friendship, expressed gratitude for departing unseen by Willoughby following his marriage, and looked forward with genuine hope to the tranquility of Barton, confident it would restore Marianne's peace and solidify her own.

Their journey proceeded safely. On the second day, they crossed into the county of Somerset, a region oscillating in Marianne's imagination between cherished sanctuary and prohibited territory. By the forenoon of the third day, they reached Cleveland. The residence was a spacious, modern edifice situated on a sloping lawn, lacking a formal park but possessing extensive pleasure grounds. A smooth gravel road wound through an open shrubbery and closer wood walk, leading to the front, where the house itself was shielded by a dense screen of firs, mountain-ashes, acacias, and towering Lombardy poplars.

Marianne entered the house with a heart swelling with emotion, aware she was merely eighty miles from Barton and thirty from Combe Magna. Within five minutes, however, she had already departed, slipping away through the winding shrubberies—which were just beginning to display their spring beauty—to ascend a distant eminence. From a nearby Grecian temple, her gaze wandered across the vast tract of country to the southeast, resting fondly on the farthest ridges of the horizon, imagining Combe Magna visible from their summits. In these moments of exquisite, invaluable misery, she rejoiced in tears of agony to be at Cleveland. Returning via a different circuit, intoxicated by the happy privilege of rural liberty—the ability to wander in free, luxurious solitude—she resolved to dedicate nearly every hour of her stay to such solitary rambles.

She returned just in time to join the others as they exited the house for an excursion through its immediate premises. The remainder of the morning was easily occupied lounging around the kitchen garden, inspecting the blooms on its walls, and listening to the gardener's lamentations regarding blights, followed by a dawdle through the greenhouse. Here, the loss of her favorite plants, unwarily exposed to the lingering frost, elicited laughter from Charlotte. Finally, she visited the poultry yard, where the disappointed hopes of the dairy-maid—hens abandoning their nests, thefts by foxes, or the rapid decline of a promising brood—provided fresh sources of merriment.

The morning had been fine and dry, and Marianne had not anticipated a change in weather. Consequently, she was taken aback to find herself barred from venturing out again after dinner by a settled, persistent rain. She had planned a twilight walk to the Grecian temple and beyond; while a merely cold or damp evening might not have deterred her, a heavy, unyielding storm rendered the idea absurd. With the party small and the hours passing quietly, Mrs. Palmer attended to her child, Mrs. Jennings engaged in carpet-work, and they discussed their departed friends, arranged Lady Middleton's engagements, and speculated whether Mr. Palmer and Colonel Brandon would travel further than Reading that night. Elinor, though little concerned, joined the discourse. Meanwhile, Marianne, possessing the knack of locating the library regardless of the family's attempts to avoid it, secured a book.

Nothing was lacking on Mrs. Palmer's part that constant, friendly goodwill could provide to ensure they felt welcome. The openness and heartiness of her manner more than compensated for her occasional deficiencies in polite formality. Her kindness, underscored by a remarkably pretty face, was engaging; her folly, though evident, was not disgusting because it lacked conceit. Elinor could have forgiven anything but her laugh.

The two gentlemen arrived the following day for a late dinner, providing a pleasant expansion of the party and a welcome variety to the conversation, which a prolonged morning of continuous rain had otherwise reduced to monotony. Elinor, having observed so little of Mr. Palmer yet noted such variety in his address toward her sister and herself, was unsure what to expect in his domestic setting. She found him perfectly gentlemanly in his behavior to all visitors, though occasionally rude to his wife and mother-in-law. He proved capable of being a pleasant companion, hindered only by an excessive tendency to regard himself as superior to everyone else, particularly to Mrs. Jennings and Charlotte. Regarding his broader character and habits, Elinor perceived nothing unusual for his sex and era: he was fastidious in his eating, irregular in his hours, affectionate toward his child despite pretending to slight it, and wasted his mornings playing billiards instead of attending to business.

Overall, Elinor liked him much better than she had anticipated. In her heart, she was relieved she could like him no more—not sorry to be driven by observations of his Epicureanism, selfishness, and conceit to rest complacently on the memory of Edward's generous temper, simple taste, and diffident nature.

Intelligence concerning Edward—or at least some of his affairs—arrived via Colonel Brandon, who had recently visited Dorsetshire. Treating Elinor simultaneously as the disinterested friend of Mr. Ferrars and the kind confidante of himself, he spoke extensively of the parsonage at Delaford, detailing its deficiencies and outlining his plans to remedy them. His behavior in this matter, as well as in every other respect, justified Mrs. Jennings's conviction of his attachment. His open pleasure in meeting her after only ten days' absence, his readiness to converse, and his deference for her opinion would have sufficed, perhaps, had not Elinor, from the outset, believed Marianne to be his true favorite. This suspicion might have arisen naturally, but as it was, such a notion had rarely entered her head except through Mrs. Jennings's prompting. She prided herself as the superior observer of the two; while Mrs. Jennings focused solely on his outward conduct, Elinor scrutinized his eyes. When Mrs. Jennings failed to notice his looks of anxious solicitude regarding Marianne's unspoken symptoms of a heavy cold—manifesting in her head and throat—Elinor discerned therein the quick, needless alarm of a lover.

Two delightful twilight walks on the third and fourth evenings, traversing not merely the dry gravel of the shrubbery but the entire grounds, especially the most remote sections where the trees were oldest and the grass longest and wettest, ultimately proved detrimental. Assisted by the greater imprudence of sitting in wet shoes and stockings, these excursions induced a violent cold in Marianne. Though initially trifled with or denied, the ailment inevitably intensified, commanding the concern of everyone present and drawing attention from Marianne herself. Prescriptions poured in from all directions, as was customary, and were uniformly declined. Despite being heavy, feverish, plagued by limb pains, a cough, and a sore throat, she maintained that a good night's rest would cure her entirely. It took considerable effort for Elinor to prevail upon her, as she retired to bed, to attempt even one or two of the simplest remedies.
