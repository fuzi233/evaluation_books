There is not a single day that passes over the face of the earth that does not see ordinary, uncelebrated men and women perform great deeds, speak words that ring through ages, and bear noble, quiet sorrows. Most of these unheralded heroes, philosophers, and martyrs will never be known until that final hour when the great of the world are brought low and the low are lifted high—but for others, the world’s knowledge of them only sleeps. Their lives are locked away in the dry, curt annals that record them, too cold and compressed to stir the average reader’s heart: they are not breathing stories that pull at the chest, but little hard pebbles of fact that glance off a person’s understanding before they can catch hold. Epitomes are not narratives, any more than bare skeletons are living, breathing human beings.
And so these records of profound, human truths remain dead letters to plain folk, because old chronicle writers left all the color and feeling to the imagination—and imagination is a rare gift, indeed. This is where the storyteller may serve the world, as a gentle interpreter between those long-gone lives and the hearts that have not yet met them.
There exists a tattered, musty old chronicle written in harsh, unforgiving Latin, every single sentence of its hidden chapter holding a true fact. It tells, in sharp, unflinching brevity, the strange story of two people who lived untrumpeted, died unsung, four hundred years ago, and lie still now in that stern page as unhonored as fossils trapped in rock. Fate was unjust to them, living and dead—but if I can only show you the beating, messy, brave humanity hidden under that dry chronicler’s words, I think you will make up for centuries of indifference, and give these two sorely tried souls a small, warm place in your heart, if only for a day.
It was the middle of the fifteenth century. Louis XI sat on the throne of France; Edward IV held the crown of England by force rather than right; and Philip “the Good,” who had dispossessed his cousin Jacqueline through brute cunning and outright force and broken her heart in the process, reigned undisturbed for decades over Holland, where our tale begins.
Elias, a cloth and leather merchant, and his wife Catherine lived in the small, quiet town of Tergou. He traded wholesale and retail in fine cloth, silk, coarse brown holland, and most of all in thick curried leather: a material prized by the middling folk of the time, for it could stand twenty years of hard wear, and turn the blade of an ordinary knife—no small virtue for a jerkin in that century, when people were far too free with their steel. It was not unheard of for a man to set down his dinner spoon mid-meal to carve his neighbor over the pettiest difference of opinion.
The couple were comfortable enough in their trade, and would have had no earthly cares to weigh on them, if not for their nine children. The babes came one a year, and each was greeted with loud rejoicing, and prayers of thanks to the saints, no complaints offered at all. When the whole family was young together, the children seemed like delightful little playthings Heaven had sent to make the long, busy workdays and quiet evenings soft and joyful for hardworking burghers.
But as the “olive branches” shot tall, and Elias and Catherine grew older, and saw firsthand how hard life could be for large families, tender love was tangled tight with quiet worry. They were part of a remarkably careful, provident people: in fifteenth century Holland, reckless parents were as rare as disobedient children. So when the enormous household loaf was set down on its giant wooden trencher, looking like a small fortress sitting in its moat of crumbs, and made the round of the table only to vanish almost entirely, Elias and Catherine would lock eyes and say softly, “Who will find bread for all of them when we are gone?”
The younger children had to work very hard to keep their stolid little Dutch faces straight at these words, for to them dinner and supper arrived as naturally as sunrise and sunset, and the great brown loaf would circle the family table for ever, to rest in their bellies only to rise again fresh from the oven next day. But the remark stirred the thrifty national thoughtfulness in the elder boys, and said often enough, it set them all to thinking: some of the thoughts were kind, some were self-serving, according to the character of each boy.
“Kate, the children are growing so fast this table will soon be too small for all of them,” Elias said one evening.
“We cannot afford it, Eli,” Catherine replied, answering not his words but the unspoken fear behind them, as wives so often do.
Their worry for the future sometimes shifted from grim to sharp with pride. The free burghers of Holland held their station as dear as any noble held his title, and the pair could not bear to think any of their blood would fall in the town’s social ranks after they were gone.
By prudence and small, steady self-denial, they managed to clothe all nine small bodies, feed all nine hungry mouths, and still tuck a small hoard away for the days ahead. As that little pile of coins grew, they felt a joy no miser hoarding gold for himself could ever know.
One day, their second-oldest son Richart, nineteen years old, came to his mother with the quiet, unshakable composure that so often tricked outsiders into underestimating the Dutch temperament, and begged her to plead with his father to send him to Amsterdam, to apprentice under a wealthy merchant. “That is the life I want for myself. I am quick with numbers, and merchants grow rich. Please, good mother, take my part in this, and I will be your grateful debtor for the rest of my days.”
Catherine threw up her hands in shock and disbelief.
“What? Leave Tergou? Leave all the streets you have known since you were a babe?”
“What is one street more or less to me? If I can leave the people I love here, I can surely leave the stones.”
“What? Quit your poor father now that he is no longer young?”
“Mother, if I can leave you, I can leave him too.”
“What? Leave your little brothers and sisters that love you so dearly?”
“There are enough mouths in this house to feed, without mine.”
“What do you mean, Richart? No one is more loved than you. Wait—have I spoken sharp to you? Have I been unkind?”
“Never that I can remember. And if you had been, I would never have breathed a word of it to anyone. Mother,” Richart said gravely, though a tear glistened in his eye, “it all comes down to one simple thing, and nothing will change my mind: there will be one less mouth for you to feed.”
“There now, look what my foolish tongue has done,” Catherine cried, and burst into tears. She could see her first young fledgling perched on the edge of the nest, beating his wings hard enough to fly out into the wide world. Richart had a steady, unbreakable will, and she knew he never wasted a single word.
It ended as all such goodbyes must end: young Richart left for Amsterdam with a face as long and sad as anyone had ever seen on him, and a will as unyielding as granite.
That afternoon at supper, there was one empty chair. Catherine stared at Richart’s vacant spot and wept bitterly, until Elias shouted roughly at the rest of the children, “Sit wider, can’t you? Sit wider!” then turned his face over the back of his wooden chair, and went silent for a long while.
Richart never cost his parents a penny again, but kitting him out and placing him in the household of the merchant Vander Stegen had drained all their small hoard, save for one single gold crown. They started saving again, ants hoarding crumb by crumb. Two years later, Richart found a spot for his brother Jacob in a firm, and Jacob left Tergou right after the eleven o’clock midday dinner. That supper, Elias remembered the last goodbye, and whispered softly, “Sit wider, dears.” Up until that moment, Catherine had refused to see the empty gap at the table, for her oldest daughter Catherine had begged her not to grieve, and she had promised she would not, for the sake of the rest of her children. But when Elias whispered “Sit wider!” she said, voice thick with forced calm, “Aye, soon the table will be too big for the children, and you thought it would be too small.” The next moment she pressed her apron to her face and wept hard.
“It is the best of our children that leave us first,” she sobbed. “That is the cruel part.”
“Nay, nay,” Elias said. “All our children are good, and all are equally dear to us. Heed her not! What God takes from us always seems sweeter than what He leaves behind—if only because men are naturally unthankful, and women are naturally silly,” he added gruffly to hide his own tear.
“And I say Richart and Jacob are the flower of the flock,” Catherine sobbed.
Their little iron-bound money coffer was empty again, and they set to refilling it, saving every penny that was not spent on the shop and their bare necessities, giving up even the smallest small luxuries, then catching each other’s eye across the table and smiling, with a joy far warmer than any self-indulgence could bring. Three years later, they had gleaned enough to set their fourth son up as a master tailor, and their oldest daughter as a ropemaker, right there in Tergou. They could send work their way from their own shop, and set them up for life. The coffer was drained completely dry, this time even the shop gave up a little stock instead of coin to fund their new ventures.
Alas, there were still four children left at home: two who could not earn their own bread, and two who would not. The two unable ones were first Giles, a dwarf of the worst sort: half dull-witted, half malicious, all big head, sharp claws, and booming voice, whom dogs and every unprejudiced local woman ran from, and whom only his mother defended through every mischief. Second was Little Catherine, a soft-spoken little girl who could only move on crutches, who lived with constant quiet pain, but never let it show on her marble-pale face with its violet eyes and long silky lashes, who never spoke a single fretful or complaining word to anyone. The two unwilling ones were Sybrandt, the youngest, a ne’er-do-well who loved playing games far more than he loved working, and Cornelis, the oldest of all the boys, who had run the numbers in his head and decided to huddle by the hearth waiting for distant relatives to die and leave him their fortunes.
Almost worn out by all their efforts, and disheartened by the physical and moral frailties of the four children still in their care, the anxious couple would still find themselves sighing, “What will become of all of them when we are no longer here to look after them?” But they said it more and more mechanically, until one day their domestic horizon cleared, and they added the bright, cheerful coda without fail: “But thanks to Saint Bavon and all the saints, we have Gerard.”
Young Gerard was a son apart, destined for the Church. In those days, the Church could always support her children, one way or another, and his family did not hold out grand hopes for him—they had no powerful connections to pull strings for a wealthy benefice. His frivolous habits, the ones that would have made his father furious in any son not marked for the clergy, were reading and calligraphy, and he was so wrapped up in them he often had to be physically dragged away from his desk to come eat. The day was never long enough for him; he carried a tinderbox and brimstone matches everywhere, begged stub ends of candles off all the neighbors so he could light his lamp at ridiculous hours—even eight o’clock at night in winter, long after the burgomaster of the town had gone to bed. His odd habits were tolerated at home, and actively encouraged by the monks of the nearby monastery. They had taught him calligraphy, and continued teaching him until one day, halfway through a lesson, they realized to their shock he was teaching them.
They told him this with good humor, and he hung his head and blushed. He had suspected it himself, but had never dared trust his own judgment on such a delicate matter. “But my son,” an elderly monk said to him, “God has given you an eye so true, a hand so subtle yet firm, and a heart that loves these beautiful crafts—why do you not paint and illuminate as well as you write? A scroll of words is bare and barren, unless it is surrounded by a border of fruit, leaves, and rich swirling arabesques that delight the eye as the words delight the soul and understanding. You can paint holy men and women on the pages, pictures of saints in glory, that will not only soothe the eye with rich, blended colors, but lift the reader’s heart high. Why not try?”
Gerard was flustered, and mumbled that he had tried illuminating several times, but never got it right. The matter rested there, until a fellow enthusiast soon arrived, in the unexpected form of an old woman. Margaret Van Eyck, the last surviving sister of the famous Van Eyck painter brothers, had left Flanders to return to her native land to spend her last days. She bought a small house near Tergou, heard of Gerard’s skill, saw a sample of his handwriting, and liked it so much she sent her servant Reicht Heynes to invite him over. It was an instant, warm acquaintance: tiny Tergou had never held two people as passionate about art as the two of them. At first, the old painter damped Gerard’s courage terribly—every time he visited, she pulled out drawings and paintings from nooks and crannies, some of them her own work, that seemed to him far beyond anything he could ever hope to make. But if the artist in her overwhelmed him, the kind woman in her kept his heart up. She and Reicht soon drew every secret out of him, including the one the monks never learned: the reason he had never been able to make illuminating work, was that he could not afford the expensive gold, ultramarine blue, and vermilion red, only cheap earth pigments, and he was too shy to ask his mother for the costly paints, certain she would say no.
Margaret Van Eyck gave him a small brush full of fine gold leaf, little pots of vermilion and ultramarine, and a sheet of perfect smooth vellum to paint on. He almost worshipped her. As he left the house, Reicht ran after him with a spare candle and a couple of small coin bits, and he kissed her hand gratefully. Even better than the rare paints was the sympathy for a young, isolated enthusiast that no one else in town had ever given him. Their shared love of art sparked a deep affection between the old painter and the young calligrapher, very typical of that century, when fine art and skilled craftsmanship had no hard line drawn between them, and artists sought each other out and loved one another with no petty jealousy—back when even the early Christians still loved one another, too.
Backed by such a venerable mentor and warmed by their kindness, Gerard advanced fast in skill and learning. His spirits rose visibly: he would still glance back longingly at his half-finished letter when he was dragged away to dinner mid-sentence, but once seated, he showed a bright, lively sense of humor that no one had ever seen in him before, and often made the whole table roar with laughter, telling old jokes from ancient Roman stories that were perfectly new to his stolid Dutch family. As a thank you to the monks who had taught him to write, he made them perfect, exquisite copies of their two most precious manuscripts: the life of their order’s founder, and their rare copy of Terence’s comedies, with the monastery supplying the fine vellum.
The high and puissant Prince Philip “the Good,” Duke of Burgundy, Luxemburg, and Brabant, Earl of Holland and Zealand, Lord of Friesland, Count of Flanders, Artois, and Hainault, Lord of Salins and Macklyn, was a man of many contradictions. He could fight as well as any king alive, and lie almost as well as the King of France. He was a mighty hunter, and he could read and write. He loved jewels as dearly as any noble lady, and gorgeous rich apparel. He was fond of his maids of honor, and of paintings generally—proof of which he had ennobled Jan Van Eyck, elevating a mere artist to noble rank. He also had a rabid passion for giants, dwarfs, and Turks. These last he lured from Istanbul with fair promises, then forced to baptize in a giant tub the moment they arrived at his court, then let them squat facing Mecca and pray to Mahound as much as they pleased, laughing up his sleeve at them for thinking they were still infidels. He kept lions in cages, and fleet leopards trained by Oriental handlers to run down hares and deer. He loved every strange, rare thing in the world except the plain, humdrum virtues. The best part of him was that he was openhanded to the poor; the second best part was that he fostered the arts with real, earnest commitment.
That year he gave dramatic proof of that commitment. He announced grand prizes for the finest examples of several crafts: for the finest religious and secular gold work, for the best oil, egg-tempera, and white lead painting on panel, silk, or metal, for the finest stained glass transparent painting, for the best illumination and border work on vellum, and for the fairest calligraphy on vellum. He ordered the burgomasters of every town to help all poor competitors by collecting their entries and sending them at the town’s expense to Rotterdam for the contest.
When the town crier rang his bell and shouted this news through the streets of Tergou, a thousand mouths gaped and chattered—but only one heart beat fast enough to feel like it would burst out of someone’s chest: Gerard’s. He told his family shyly that he wanted to enter two of the contests. They stared in stunned silence, too shocked at his audacity to breathe, until a horrid loud laugh exploded on the floor like a petard. Gerard looked down, and there was the dwarf Giles, grinning from ear to ear at his expense, laughing as loud as a lion.
