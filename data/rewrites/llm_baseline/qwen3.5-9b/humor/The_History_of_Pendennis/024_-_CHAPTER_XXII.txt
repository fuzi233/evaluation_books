The letter from the Major arrived with the speed of bad news, prompting Dr. Portman to dash off to Fairoaks with the alacrity of a dentist rushing to extract a molar. He treated the situation with grim efficiency: *the tooth must be pulled, and you sit still.* While he might hesitate if it were his own tooth, he applied his forceps with vigorous determination, convinced it was simply the widow's duty to endure the pain. After reading the epistle aloud to Myra and Mrs. Portman, accompanied by heavy sighs and damning commentary on the young scapegrace sliding further toward perdition, the Doctor departed. The ladies, true to their reputation for accuracy and dispatch, spread the tidings through Clavering society before striding over to Fairoaks to deliver the blow personally.

They found the widow already prepared. Having read Pen's letter herself, a gloomy presentiment that had haunted her for months had lifted. She knew the worst, yet she saw her darling boy returning, repentant and tender-hearted. Was there more she desired? The Rector's sensible, antique remarks failed to stir any indignation in Helen, save a pang that her son should suffer. She questioned the degree—the fuss over it—and why they had sent him to a place teeming with temptation and devoid of virtue, rather than keeping him at home. Regarding his debts, she argued pragmatically: wasn't his father's money his own? Didn't he have a right to spend it? Thus, the widow met the virtuous Doctor, and his arrows of indignation struck no spark in her gentle bosom.

Meanwhile, a significant change had occurred within the household regarding Pen and his younger sister, Laura. The childish ritual of brotherly affection, once frequent in their youth, had ceased by mutual consent. Upon returning from college after months away, Pen discovered he was no longer greeted by the simple girl he had left behind. Instead, he faced a tall, slim, handsome young woman who offered a gracious curtsy and a trembling hand, blushing furiously at the very spot Pen had once kissed.

Describing Miss Laura Bell at sixteen is a delicate task. She stood five-foot-four, deemed "tall" and "gawky" by smaller peers, though adorned with "beautiful roses" around her head that attracted many suitors. Ordinarily pale, her cheeks flushed readily at the slightest provocation, retaining the color long after the emotion faded. Her eyes, large since childhood, shone with an innate brilliance that no amount of criticism could dim; Nature had endowed them with such radiance that they required thick lashes merely to soften their glow. Her complexion rivaled Lady Mantrap's without the aid of powder, her smile revealed pearly teeth, and her voice possessed a low, sweet melody akin to music itself. Though her long dresses hid her feet, her overall demeanor was undeniably sweet.

However, Laura's admiration for her brother had cooled. Over the past two years, her mind and body had matured alongside her disappointment. Pen's letters had grown sparse and brief. When Helen urged him to prioritize his studies, Laura countered that losing a prize was preferable to forgetting his mother. She questioned why he spent his holidays at his rich friends' houses instead of staying home, where he would be loved more deeply.

Helen sighed, wondering if Laura truly loved Pen less than she claimed, or if she simply refused to accept Helen's arguments that Pen needed to forge his own path. Helen tried to believe in her excuses—that Pen needed to cultivate influential acquaintances and navigate male social obligations incomprehensible to women—but deep down, she shared Laura's skepticism. Yet, the maternal instinct remained unbreakable; once a woman loves, her tenderness and forgiveness seem eternal.

Laura also resented Pen's newfound freedom and audacity. He was polite by nature, yet he spoke loosely of women, neglected small courtesies, smoked his horrid pipes indoors, and preferred yawning over novels in his dressing gown while his mother attended church. The hero of her infancy, the brave boy of endless bedtime stories, had transformed into a bold, sarcastic, defiant young man who scorned the simple devotions of the women around him. The scandal of the Fotheringay affair shattered her completely. Hearing of a Pendennis throwing himself at an actress, drinking with her father, and potentially bringing such a woman into their home, Laura was outraged. "I would have run away," she declared, "even walking barefoot through the snow!" Helen reassured her that she would leave too, and the two embraced warmly, their shared outrage momentarily forgotten.

Perhaps Laura's fury stemmed from ignorance of how men and women mirror each other's follies, or perhaps she was misled by the bitter gossip of the Claverings and the Doctor. Or, as some suggest, she was jealous—a vice ladies supposedly avoid. Regardless, her anger was directed solely at Pen, not his mother. Devoted to Helen with the passionate intensity of a young woman seeking a surrogate confidante, she showered her with caresses and endearments. But the moment news reached them that Pen was truly miserable, Laura's wrath evaporated, replaced by unreasonable compassion. The Pen of old days returned to her memory: frank, generous, and tender.

She immediately sided with Helen against the Doctor's accusations. Debts? A trifling matter! He'd been forced into expensive society by his uncle. Failed to get his degree? He was ill during the exams, distracted by debt, perhaps unfairly targeted by jealous tutors. Others were cruel and unfair. With flushed cheeks and bright eyes, Laura defied the Doctor in his presence, seizing Helen's hand and asking how he dared speak against her darling mother's son. The discomfited clergyman retreated, leaving the two women to embrace with renewed fervor. Helen, sensing the logic in Laura's defense, began to suspect foul play among the examiners. Together, they barely registered the calamity. To women, calamity often feels like a summons for absent affection; if you reduce your mistress to a crust, she won't complain, provided you share the meal.

Directly the Doctor left, Laura ordered fires lit and beds aired. By the time Helen finished a tearful letter to Pen, the rooms were warm and inviting. Laura added a postscript calling him her "dearest Pen," urging him to come home instantly to happiness with his mother and herself. That night, unable to sleep despite their Bible readings, the two women lay awake. Helen stared at the night-lamp, thinking of Pen for hours, while Laura pretended to be motionless, her own thoughts racing. Finally, Laura proposed a scheme: since Helen had raised her for ten years without spending a penny of her savings, Laura owed her a fortune. They would demand the five hundred pounds from Mr. Rowdy at Chatteris, save two more, and send it all to Pen to clear his debts. Then, they would live happily ever after. Helen responded with incoherent exclamations and embraces, and both slept soundly until dawn.

When the sun rose over the purple hills, Helen woke to find Laura sleeping peacefully beside her, dreaming of happier times. Meanwhile, in London, Pen, driven by remorse and a vow of economy, traveled to Fairoaks in the cold January air. He sat outside the coach with the guard, refusing the warmth of an inside seat. At the lodge gate, his old servant John, now dressed in a fustian jacket rather than livery, grinned with a blush of pride. "I'm garner and stable man now," John noted, before vanishing as Pen entered the grounds.

Moments later, Helen appeared, her face radiant with love and forgiveness. She had already written to Pen detailing Laura's "magnificent" financial offer, filled with blessings for both children. Seeing Laura in the hall, Pen blushed intensely; this was the first time in years she had broken their tacit agreement of distance. The prodigal had returned, the fatted calf was slaughtered, and the two women made him as happy as possible.

For weeks, no mention was made of the Oxford disaster. Pen fretted internally, eventually riding to Chatteris to retrieve the funds Laura had secured. He handed the cheque to his mother, an act of "virtue" in her eyes and "strict justice" in Laura's. Pen rarely spoke of the loan, though he thanked her hesitantly, his vanity warring with his gratitude. He quit wine, switched to whisky-and-water, abandoned cigars for pipes, and fell into a melancholy routine of napping, reading newspapers, playing cribbage with Captain Glanders, and avoiding the stern glares of Dr. Portman. He attended church regularly and prayed for his mother, but the house had changed. The silver covers stayed dark, the fires burned low, and the staff dwindled to two maids and a gardener. Life was austere, and Pen's spirits sank lower.

He refused to return to Oxford, claiming no power could induce him to face his failure. But Laura, blushing with earnestness, insisted that as penance for his idleness, he must try to earn his degree. Reluctantly, Pen returned to Cambridge. A "plucked" man is a dismal creature, belonging to no set and owned by none. Stripped of his fine feathers, he shut himself in his rooms, attending chapel but avoiding the noise of undergraduates. His creditors vanished as he paid them off, and the cards stopped arriving. His yearmates graduated and moved on.
