Major Pendennis had resolved to hunker down at Fairoaks like a general holding a siege until the unpleasant actress lady was fully out of the county, no exceptions. He never acted like he was hovering over Pen or laying down strict rules, of course—he just had every single servant on the estate reporting back on the boy’s every trip to the shops, every late night walk, every weird mumble over breakfast, so he had a full minute-by-minute log of his nephew’s activities whether Pen knew it or not.

I’d wager every single one of you reading this has had your heart broken at least once, for whatever silly reason fate, a lying lover, or your own dumb self-sabotage could cook up. Go dig up that cringey old sad memory of yours, and you’ll get exactly what Pen was going through. Oh, the 3 a.m. wake-ups where you’re rolling back and forth in bed like a rotisserie chicken, counting the hours toll by toll, miserable enough that you’d trade a whole year of good meals for one single text back. I’ve never heard of a man straight-up dying of heartbreak, but I’ve seen a strapping 12 stone lad waste all the way down to nine and a half after a bad breakup—so that’s a whole quarter of him basically vanishing, no small loss. Don’t panic for Pen, though: he’ll bounce back, same as the rest of us. The Major would lean in and pat Mrs. Pendennis’s hand reassuringly when she fretted, and say, “Half of a man’s pain when he gets left isn’t even about the girl—it’s about his bruised vanity. Getting dumped is no fun, of course, but look how easy we ditch the ladies ourselves, eh?”

Mrs. Pendennis, bless her sweet sheltered heart, didn’t know a single thing about that sort of cynical wisdom. She’d had one tiny heartbreak decades ago that she’d never spoken of, and aside from that all of her fierce motherly worry was focused 100% on Pen, so much so that she felt his sorrows twice as sharp as he did himself, hovering in the hallway outside his room when he was moping, sneaking extra jam on his toast to cheer him up.

The Major, for all his worldly grump, deserves credit for sticking it out at Fairoaks, which was about as exciting as watching paint dry for a man who used to pop in to three separate London drawing rooms in a single evening. His biggest highlights these days were a rare dinner with Dr. Portman or a local squire, and a dreary backgammon rubber with the widow that stretched for three hours. He’d hover by the letter bag like an excited puppy every post day, read every single line of the evening paper, and fuss over his health with his fancy medicine chest, figuring all this quiet country air would undo the damage of years of fancy London banquets. He dressed up in full formal attire twice a day for no reason, paced the terrace walk for his daily exercise, and kept himself sane by rereading his two volumes of the *Annual Register* so many times he could recite every detail of the American war and the Wyoming massacre by heart. This is what they call being adaptable, you see.

Pen sometimes joined the Major for backgammon at night, or sat and half-listened to his mom pluck out soft old ballads on the piano in the summer evenings, but he was still restless as a caged fox. He’d get up before the sun was even up, trudge over to the spooky carp pond in Clavering Park—you know the one, where a milkmaid drowned herself a hundred years back, and the locals swear her ghost haunts the rushes at midnight. He never came close to drowning himself, no matter how much his mom panicked he might. He fished there for hours, zoning out staring at his float bobbing in the eddies, and every time he caught a carp, tench, or eel he’d trudge home triumphant, and the Major would cook them up all fancy in the Continental style he’d learned on his tours.

He sat under an old gnarled tree at the edge of the pond scribbling terrible angsty poetry, which he would burn a few years later and pretend he’d never written, blushing so hard his ears turned red remembering how he rhymed “sorrow” with “borrow” and “tomorrow” no less than seven times. When you’re that deep in drafting rhymes for your heartbreak, your sorrow’s almost over already, that’s a fact. He still had his grumpy days, his random mad fits where he’d saddle up his mare Rebecca and gallop her all across the countryside, screaming the name of his lost actress love loud enough to make turnpike keepers and carters stare and wonder if he’d gone fully mad.

Harry Foker started turning up at Fairoaks nearly every other day, and his chaotic good energy was a godsend for the Major and Pen, though it gave the widow and little Laura quite the shock. His fancy tandem carriage caused absolute chaos in Clavering market square one afternoon: he knocked over a whole stall of cabbages, nicked Mrs. Pybus’s poodle’s ear right off with his wheel, and popped into the Clavering Arms to chug a full glass of raspberry bitters for the fun of it. Every old biddy in town scrambled to pull out their dog-eared copies of the peerage to look him up, and when they couldn’t find his name in their 30-year-old volumes, they decided he was even fancier than they’d first thought, the son of an actual earl’s daughter no less. Of course, the story of Pen’s disastrous actress crush spread through the same small town faster than a grease fire.

From the top of the London coach driving past Fairoaks lodge, Clavering St. Mary looks like a perfect postcard: the bright little Brawl river glinting in the sun, the old church tower peeking over treetops, soft rolling hills stretching all the way to the sea in the distance. The old coach driver Tom Smith used to point out a specific riverside tree to his passengers and tell them all the artists came there to paint the view, calling it “a proper Habby landscape, sir.” But as with most pretty things you see from far away, Clavering is a whole lot less charming up close. On non-market days the streets are so empty the clack of a woman’s wooden pattens echoes all the way down the high street, and you can hear the rusty old flag on the Clavering Arms creak before you can hear a single human voice. The Assembly Rooms haven’t hosted a ball since the old Clavering volunteer regiment threw one for their colonel 20 years prior, their stables all empty save for Thursdays when the farmers roll in with their carts and gigs to sell their turnips.

The old grey stone church looms over the market square, its ancient saint statues all broken and headless, the ones high out of reach so old even Dr. Portman doesn’t remember what they’re supposed to be of, while the local low-church pastor Simcoe rants that they’re idolatrous abominations. The rectory is a stout old brick house from Queen Anne’s reign, tucked next to the grammar school, the Finucane sisters’ girls’ academy, and an old slaughterhouse leftover from abbey days. Dr. Portman spent half his fortune buying a fancy stained-glass window all the way from the Netherlands for the church, and a cathedral-sized organ to go with it—but half the town decided the window was full of popish idols and trooped off to Simcoe’s new chapel down by the factory, leaving Dr. Portman’s wife seething, because now she has to curtsy to Simcoe’s stuck-up wife who’s the daughter of some obscure Irish peer, and half the town is calling her husband a heathen for enjoying a rubber of whist after dinner. The poor doctor told her he wouldn’t cave to the nonsense: King George III himself played whist, after all, and if the locals wanted to call him a sinner for that, they could go jump in the Brawl.

The little town was already full of more petty drama than the whole of London combined. The book club Major Pendennis set up to try and give people something neutral to do devolved into constant bickering, so the only people ever seen in the reading room were the quiet curate Smirke who read all the magazines, old Glanders who napped by the window, and nosy Mrs. Pybus who read everyone else’s letters before they could pick them up from the post.

When the story of Pen’s failed actress engagement blew through town, every single person had an opinion. The Finucane girls whispered about it over their tea, their boarding school students gossiped about it in the dorm after lights out, the grammar school boys snickered and pointed at Pen when he walked past, calling him stuck-up “Lord Pendennis” for not wearing the same cheap corduroys they did. And who was the biggest spreader of the gossip? None other than Mrs. Portman herself, who couldn’t keep a juicy story to herself if you paid her, much to her husband the doctor’s deep mortification. The old biddies of the town clucked like a flock of offended hens: “Gracious, how that widow Pendennis spoils that horrid stuck-up boy! Marrying a painted actress off the stage, of all things! If his dear father was still alive this scandal would never have happened!”

Pen had never been exactly popular in Clavering. He was a quiet, proud lad who thought all their petty small-town dignities and gossiping was deathly boring, and he didn’t bother hiding it. He only even liked Dr. Portman and the curate Smirke, and the rest of the town thought he was far too uppity for not dropping by their boring tea parties uninvited. The whole village was yelping about the Pendennises like a pack of tiny yappy terriers nipping at their heels, desperate to drag the well-off family down to their level. Dr. Portman and Glanders didn’t dare tell Mrs. Pendennis all the terrible things people were saying about her boy, but Pen found out soon enough. Someone chalked “FOTHERINGAY FOR EVER!” all over the Fairoaks gate one night, another fellow pasted a giant Chatteris playbill to the front door. Then one afternoon, as Pen walked through the churchyard on his way to church, that brute Hobnell, the 20-year-old overgrown grammar school boy who boarded with Wapshot, struck a dramatic Hamlet pose by a brand new empty grave and started cackling out Ophelia’s death speech straight at Pen, leering the whole time.

Pen flew into such a rage he shrieked out something very close to a curse, whipped his riding crop straight across Hobnell’s face, threw the whip down and dared the bully to fight him fair, and in two seconds flat had knocked the sputtering idiot straight into the empty grave that had been waiting for a recently deceased elderly parishioner. He stood there, fists clenched, face red with rage, yelling at the rest of the gawking grammar school boys to come and take a turn too—until they all scattered like scared chickens, right as Dr. Portman rounded the gate to see what the ruckus was about, and Hobnell clambered out of the grave covered in dirt and bleeding from his split lip. Pen was so mad he could barely speak, his voice cracking on the verge of tears as he said the coward had insulted him, and the kindly doctor didn’t even bother scolding him.

Major Pendennis, who was perpetually terrified of what the neighbours thought of him, was fit to be tied over the tiny silly scandal blowing up around Pen’s head, but Pen didn’t say a word about the churchyard brawl to anyone at home. He just trotted over to visit Harry Foker, who was absolutely tickled pink at the chance to act as his second. Foker drove his fancy drag straight over to the Clavering Arms, sent his servant Stoopid over to Wapshot’s grammar school with a very formal note challenging Hobnell to a gentleman’s duel. Stoopid reported back later that all the big grammar school boys had passed the note around and cackled over it, and Hobnell said he’d give his answer after afternoon school let out—right as Mr. Wapshot himself stepped out of the schoolhouse wearing his full master’s academic gown, wondering what all the shouting was about.
