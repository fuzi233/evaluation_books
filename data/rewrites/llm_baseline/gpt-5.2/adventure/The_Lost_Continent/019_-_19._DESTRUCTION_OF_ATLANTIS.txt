A tottering old Priest came up through the press and laid a hand upon my shoulder.

“Well?” I snapped, for my blood was up and I had small relish for any voice that was not the ring of steel.

“News has been carried to the Three, my King, of what is threatened.”

“Then they know I stand here,” I said, and kept my eyes on the pass where Phorenice’s tower crouched like a carrion-bird, “to take the finest fight of my life. When it is done I shall go to the Gods, and stand behind the stars to welcome them when they arrive. Tell them I regret they are too old and too feeble to die properly and see a clean killing.”

“I have commands from them, my King,” the old man said, and there was pity in his cracked voice, “which I fear you will like but slenderly. You are forbidden to find your death here. They have further use for you yet.”

I turned on him so sharply the bronze rings on my corslet bit my skin.

“I take no such order. I will not believe it was given. You have misunderstood. If I am man, Priest, soldier, King—then no enemy passes this gate whilst I live. Go back and throw that message at their teeth.”

He smiled, not mocking, but with the sour envy of an old fighter whose arm has withered.

“I told them you would not easily believe. I asked for a sign. They bore with me, and gave me one. I was to give you this jewel, my King.”

He put the thing into my palm. Even through sweat and grime I knew the weight of it: a bracelet, worked with the old spirals and the sun-disks, warmed by a woman’s flesh so long that it seemed to hold a faint echo of her scent—salt, clean skin, and that sharp sweetness of the mountain herbs Nais loved to bruise between her fingers.

“How came they by that?” I demanded. “It is from the elbow of Nais.”

“They must have stripped her of it. I did not know. The word I was to bring you said the owner of the jewel is inside the Ark of the Mysteries, and waits you there. The use the Three have for you concerns her also.”

The gate behind me shook with the rhythm of siege. Every blow carried its own note: stone groaning, wood splitting, iron ringing; and underneath it, like a drum too vast for human hands, the mountain’s own mutter. Yet it was the bracelet that unseated me. A man can make his peace with dying. It is harder to make peace with leaving the living unguarded.

I will not pretend obedience came easy. The promise of that battle was a wine strong enough to make a man forget his vows. But love for Nais—love and that stubborn old habit of discipline that had been kneaded into me since boyhood like dough under a hard palm—prevailed. I gave the salute that showed I had heard, and would obey.

To the knot of Priests left to hold the gate, I turned and made my farewells.

“You will have what I shall miss, my brothers,” I said. “I envy you that fight. But though I am King of Atlantis, I am only one of the Seven and the servant of the Three. They speak the will of the most High Gods. You will stand behind the stars before I come. Commend me to Those you meet there. It is not my will that I shall not be by your side.”

They smiled, courteous even in the teeth of ruin, and saluted me with their weapons. Then I left them to their glory.

I did not see the fight, but I know it was good from the time that passed before Phorenice’s hordes broke out upon the crest of the Mountain. Those last lesser Priests of Atlantis died hard.

With bitterness on my tongue I went up to the head of the pass, through groves where the leaves trembled though there was no wind, and between temples and colleges and houses perched upon the upper slopes of the Sacred Mountain. In gentler days that boundary meant death to any unprivileged foot that crossed it. But the world was shedding its old rules like a snake its skin, and my temper was too hot for reverence.

Yet I was stopped.

In the temple that marked the boundary stood old Zaemon, waiting as if the mountain itself had sent him there. He would not grant me passage until he had spoken the due words and performed the whole of some ancient ritual to win dispensation from the High Gods for my trespass. His voice, thin and ceremonial, sounded oddly beside the clangour below, like a prayer recited in a kitchen while the roof catches fire.

Already Phorenice’s tower and bridge were set, and the clash and yelling told the handful of Priests at the last gate were trading their lives for the highest tally they could earn. They were trained, but old; the odds too monstrous to be stemmed for long. Peace, if such a word could still live, was shrinking by the breath.

But Zaemon would not hurry. He led me to the Ark of the Mysteries, chiding my impatience, and waited until I had given it the reverential kiss. The Ark’s metal was cool beneath my lips, and tasted faintly of old incense ground into its seams by generations of hands. Then Zaemon called aloud, and from the opening at the top of the Ark another old man appeared—hoary, frail, emaciated—climbing down by the battens fixed to its sides.

I had never seen him before. He and Zaemon were the only two remaining Priests raised to the highest degree of our Clan, the only two who held the last keys to the highest secrets, powers, and mysteries.

“Look!” Zaemon cried, and swept a trembling finger over the shattered city and the wide spread of sea and country below.

I followed his pointing, and a chill crawled through me.

The Lord Sun burned high in a sky of cloudless blue; day shimmered in His heat. From that height, the world should have looked calm—fields, roofs, water, the tidy geometry of a kingdom that had fed itself for ages: grain carried in baskets, fish gutted on stones, bread baked in dome ovens whose smoke once rose sweet with fennel-stalk and olivewood. But peace was a lie.

The mountains smoked more than ordinary, some spouted fires. The sea boiled with a strange disorder. Far out, surf surged against the rocks in rolls too huge for any tide. Up the great estuary—at whose head Atlantis stood—the sea gushed in successive waves of enormous height that never returned.

Already the lower lands were blotted out. Harbour walls drowned. The flood crept into the lower wards of the city itself. Where once the air had been salted with drying nets and spice and hot bread, there was only a raw reek of churned mud and brine, and a sound that made the mind go thin: a continual roar as if the whole world were one vast kettle coming to boil.

“You have seen?” Zaemon asked.

“I have seen.”

“You understand?”

“In part.”

“Then I will tell you all. This is the beginning, and the end will follow swiftly. The most High Gods who sit behind the stars have a limit to even Their sublime patience. That has been passed. Atlantis, the great continent beyond, and all that are in them are doomed to unutterable destruction. Of old it was foreseen this wiping-out would come through the sins of men, and for this the Ark of the Mysteries was built under direction of the Gods.

“No mortal implement can scratch its surface. No waves or rocks can wreck it. Inside is stored on sheets of the ancient writing all that is known in the world of learning not shared by the common people. Also there is grain in store, and sweet water in tanks sufficient for two persons for the space of four years, together with seeds, weapons, and all other matters deemed fit.”

He spoke of grain as if it were a prayer. In my mind I saw the vaults: baskets lined with resin, sealed jars, the careful choosing of kernels—wheat and barley, the hard little suns of millet—cleaned of chaff, dried to the snap, tucked away against damp and rot. It was strange, in that hour, to feel comfort in the thought of honest food, measured and husbanded. As if, while continents died, two mouths might still keep time with the old human ritual—hand to bowl, bowl to lips.

“Out of all this vast country,” Zaemon went on, “it has been decreed that two shall not perish. Two shall be chosen: a man and a woman, fit and proper to carry away the ancient learning and dispose of it as they see best, and afterwards to rear up a race who shall in time build another kingdom and do honour to our Lord the Sun and the other Gods in another place. The woman is within the Ark already, seated in the place appointed for her, and though she is a daughter of mine, the burden of her choosing is with you. For the man, the choice has fallen upon yourself.”

The words struck like a blow from the flat of a sword. I stood half numb.

“I do not know that I care to be a survivor.”

“You are not asked for your wishes,” Zaemon said. “You are given an order from the High Gods, who know you to be Their faithful servant.”

Habit rode me as surely as harness. I made the salutation in required form and said I heard and would obey.

“Then it remains,” he said, “to raise you to the sublime degree of the Three. If your learning is small and you will not understand the keys to many of the Powers and the highest Mysteries when they are handed to you, that fault cannot be remedied now.”

Time was short. The fight still raged down at the pass, though it was a wonder those few had held so long. Ocean rolled in upon the land in an ever-increasing flood. Mountains belched volleys of rock as weight and water worried their foundations. Soon even Phorenice’s besiegers must see their doom, and then panic would drive them like fire through dry grass.

But there was no hurrying Zaemon or the other sage. There was no temple for the ceremony, no sacrifice, no incense—only sky for a roof, the Gods for witnesses, and the roar of destruction as accompaniment. Yet they went through the raising with all the grand and majestic form age upon age had dignified, and did not curtail one sentence.

A burning mountain burst with a bellowing roar as incoming waters met its fires, flinging up steam like scalding breath. Gravely they continued, voices quavering but unbroken. Phorenice’s troops broke the last resistance and poured among the groves and temples, a frenzied stream. Still the ritual did not falter.

They say the ceremony of raising to the Three is the grandest of all connected with our holy religion. It was so. Even the tremendous circumstances could not make those frail men forget the deference due to the highest order of the Clan.

For myself, I was less rapt. I stood bareheaded in the heat, trying to concentrate, torn by foreign emotions. A continent was in the act of dying, and if Zaemon could give his whole mind to priestly syllables while the world drowned, I could not. My thoughts kept returning to Nais—her hands, her voice, the particular words she used when she was glad or angry, the way she called certain herbs by names that belonged only to our mountain dialect and would taste wrong on a stranger’s tongue.

Below, the soundscape changed minute by minute. The clean clangour of battle grew swallowed by water’s rage. The mountain’s old murmurs rose into harsh, broken thunder. It was as if the world’s familiar music—birdcalls in the groves, temple bells, the kitchen-chorus of mortars pounding grain—was being drowned beneath one vast industrial roar of ruin, without rhythm or mercy. A man’s mind, made for patterns, flinched from it.

I made the required responses, and tried to preserve in memory those sentences that were keys to power and learning, not mere phrasings of grandeur. But I saw soldiers wandering closer in their distracted running, and I saw the sea below climbing higher, and the fires around the mountain spurting to double their height, roaring without pause.

At last the ceremony came to its end.

“Kneel,” Zaemon cried, “and make obeisance to your mother the Earth, and swear by the High Gods you will never make improper use of the powers over Her which this day you have been granted.”

I knelt, palm to stone, feeling the mountain’s faint trembling like a heartbeat under my fingers, and swore. He bade me rise as fully installed, duly initiated, one of the Three.

“You will have no opportunity to practise the workings of this degree with either of us, my brother,” he said. “Presently our other brother and I go to stand before the Gods and deliver account of our trust. But what you remember may turn to use hereafter. Now we give you farewell, and promise to commend you highly when soon we meet Them behind the stars.

“Climb into the Ark, and be ready to shut the door if these raging people attempt to invade it. Remember, it is the Gods’ direct will that you and the woman Nais go from this place living and sound. You are forbidden to accept challenge or provocation to fight on any pretext whatever. But as long as may be done in safety, look out upon Atlantis in her death-throes. It is fitting one of the only two sent hence alive should carry the full tale.”

I climbed to the top of the Ark by the battens and descended the stair within.

Nais waited in a small chamber. Even in that close place I knew her as one knows the smell of one’s own hearth: the faint oil she used on her hair, the clean tang of water, the ghost of crushed leaves. Her eyes met mine—steady, angry, relieved, all at once.

“I was bidden stay here by Zaemon,” she said. “He forced me by threats and also promises that my lord would follow. He is very ungentle, that father of mine. But I think he has a kindness for us both. Any way he is my father and I cannot help loving him. Is there no chance to save him from what is going to happen?”

“He will not come into this Ark,” I said. “I asked him. It was ordained when first the Ark was built that when the day came one woman and one man should be its only tenants, and they are here already. Zaemon’s will in the matter is not to be twisted by you or by me. He has a message to deliver to the Gods, and he grudges every minute lost in carrying it.”

I left her then and went back up, for Zaemon had said I might look while it was safe.

On the mountain top men ran about distracted, yet their running had a pattern now—like ants driven from a broken nest, all drawn toward the Ark’s highest point. For the moment I passed them lightly, for the drowning below filled the eye and the mind alike.

Ocean roared in with still more furious waves. Plains and level lands became foaming lakes. The great city of Atlantis vanished—streets, courts, markets where once fish and fruit had been cried in half a dozen tongues; ovens that had once breathed out bread-smell at dawn; schools where the old writing had been taught to those few permitted to learn. All gone, swallowed, and with it the everyday words that named the small things—tools, tastes, seasons. It struck me then how language dies: not in a grand curse, but when the last mouth that knows a word is stopped by water.

Mountains alone held their heads above the flood, spewing rock and steam and boiling stone, or bursting when waters reached them and making whirlpools that twisted trees and bubbled mud. In the space of breaths every living creature in the lower grounds was smothered, save a few huddled in a pair of galleys driven oarless inland over what had been black forest and hunting land. Even those were swallowed by the turmoil. Nothing but sea beasts and the greater lizards that can live in outrageous waters could have survived that stage.

Only the men now on the upper slopes of the Sacred Mountain still lived, and their span was short. The continent sank deeper each minute before our eyes beneath the boiling inrush.

Yet not all were cowed. Presently a compact little body came from out the houses and temples and headed straight toward the Ark.

On the outside marched Phorenice’s personal guards, weapons new-blooded. They had fought through their own fellow soldiers, for the poor demented creatures had turned to “every man for himself” until those guards proved to them that Phorenice still came first.

In the middle, borne in a litter of gold and ivory by her grotesque European slaves, rode the Empress—calm, lovely, and divided between contempt and amusement as if this were theatre and not the end of a world. Her two children lay at her feet. On her right marched Tatho, gorgeously apparelled, beard curled and plaited into a thousand ringlets. On her other side walked Ylga, plying her industry with unruffled care, once again fan-girl and so still second lady in this dwindling kingdom.

They halted half a score of paces from the Ark at Phorenice’s order.

“Do not go nearer to those unclean old men,” she said. “They carry a rank odour with them, and for the moment we are short of essences to sweeten the air of their neighbourhood.”

She lifted her brows and looked up at me.

“Truly a quiet little gathering of old acquaintances. Why, there is Deucalion—whom once I took the flavour of and threw aside when he cloyed me.”

Her words were like perfumed smoke in a burning room—pretty, poisonous, useless. Yet even now her presence altered the air. She had always treated men as meat laid out for her choosing, speaking of taste and satiety as if hearts were dishes to be sampled and discarded.

“I have Nais here,” I said. “Presently we two will be all that are left alive of this nation.”

“Nais is quite welcome to my leavings,” she laughed. “I will look down upon your country cooings when presently I go back to the Place behind the stars from which I came. You are a very rustic person, Deucalion. They tell me three or four of these smelling old men have named you King. Did you swell much with dignity? Or did you remember there was a pretty Empress left that would still be Empress so long as there was an Atlantis to govern? Come, sir, find your tongue. By my face, you must have hungered for me very madly these years we have been parted, if new-grown ruggedness is an evidence.”

“Have your gibe,” I said. “I do not gibe back at a woman who presently will die.”

“Bah! You will live behind the times. Have they not told you I know the Great Secret and am indeed a Goddess now? My arts can make life run on eternally.”

“Then the waters will presently test them hard,” I said.

Zaemon stepped forward to the front of her litter with the Symbol of our Lord the Sun glowing in his hand, and broke into a flow of cursing so fierce it seemed to put edge on the very air. His words were hard to hear; the roar of the waters, now striking the Sacred Mountain itself in vast waves, grew louder, nearer, as if the sea had climbed to the very lip of our ears.

Phorenice ordered her guards to kill him—yes, tried even to rise and do it herself. But Zaemon held the Symbol before him, and its power in that supreme moment mastered all arts brought against it. The majesty of the most High Gods was vindicated, and the splendid Empress knew it. She lay back sullen among her cushions, beaten.

Only one person in that rigid knot found power to leave the rest.

Ylga came out to the side of the Ark, leaned up, and cried a farewell through the gathering roar of the flood. Her voice fought the sound and was nearly lost in it, like a songbird calling against storm.

“I would I might save you and take you with us,” I called back.

“As for that,” she said with a gesture, “I would not come if you asked me. I am not a woman who will take anything less than all. But I shall meet what comes with the memory that you will have me always somewhere in your recollection. I know somewhat of men—even men of your stamp, Deucalion—and you will never forget that you came very near to loving me once.”

I think she said something further concerning Nais, but the bellowing rush of water drowned all other words.

Mist rose—steam made from swamped burning mountains—stopping all accurate view, though the blaze from the fires lit it like gold. Through that gilded fog I saw, last, a horde of soldiery rushing up the slopes with a scum of surge billowing at their heels, licking many of them back into its clutch.

Then my eye found old Zaemon, waving to me with the Symbol: shut the door.

I obeyed his last command. Down the stair I went, and closed the ingress behind me. There were bolts ready; I shot them home into their sockets. The sound of metal settling was small, domestic almost—like latching a pantry against thieves—yet it cut us off from all the rest of our world that remained.

Nais and I were alone.

I went to her and put my arms tight around her. Her hair brushed my cheek; I tasted salt there, and something like smoke. For a heartbeat the Ark smelled not of old metal and sealed stores but of her living warmth, and it steadied me more than any priestly sentence.

Without, we heard men beating desperately on the Ark with their weapons. Some climbed by the battens to the top and wrenched at the door. Terror gave them strength and madness gave them endurance. Through the Ark’s sheathing their blows were dulled, as if heard through deep water, yet each one carried its own frantic rhythm.

I would have given much to know how Phorenice fared in that final tumult, and how she faced it. I could see her in my mind—lovely face, wondrous eyes, ruddy hair curling at her neck—and by all the Gods, I thought more of her in that last moment than of the poor land she had conquered and misgoverned and brought to this horrid destruction. There is no denying the fascination she carried, the way she could make a man’s thoughts betray him with the mere memory of her voice.

But the end did not dally.

A surge lifted the Ark a hand’s breadth in its cradle and set it back with a jar that shook our teeth. The blows from below ceased, but redoubled on the rounded roof—frenzied batterings. There were screams and cries that came to us muffled, though likely outside they were flung full-throated into the steam and wind.

Then another surge came, roaring and thundering, and picked up the great vessel as though it were a feather. It spun it giddily. After that we touched earth or rock no more.

We were tossed on the crests and troughs of delirious seas, a sport for the greedy Gods of ocean. The lamp had fallen; we crouched in darkness. Around us, sealed away, lay grain and water and seeds—carefully chosen, carefully named in the old writing—small human provisions against infinite hunger.

And within us lay the heavier store: the burden of knowing that we alone were saved out of what was yesterday a mighty nation.
