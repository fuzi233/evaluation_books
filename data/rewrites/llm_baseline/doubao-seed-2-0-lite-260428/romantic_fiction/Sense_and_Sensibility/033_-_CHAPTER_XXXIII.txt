After days of quiet resistance, Marianne finally yielded to Elinor’s gentle, repeated pleas, and consented to step out with her sister and Mrs. Jennings for a single half hour one bright London morning. She laid out strict terms first: no paying calls, no lingering over small talk, they would only stop at Gray’s jewelers on Sackville Street, where Elinor was sorting through a set of their mother’s old-fashioned family pieces to adjust and refit them.

As they pulled up to the shop door, Mrs. Jennings suddenly remembered she owed a short social visit to a friend at the far end of the street, and as she had no business of her own at Gray’s, it was settled that she would slip away to make her call and return to collect the girls once their errand was done.

Climbing the stairs to the showroom, the Miss Dashwoods found the counters crowded with customers, and not a single clerk free to take their order. They were forced to wait, choosing a spot at the far end of one counter where only one very fashionably dressed gentleman stood lingering. Elinor held a quiet hope that he might hurry through his business out of courtesy to the two ladies waiting, but his eye for finery and his obsession with getting exactly what he wanted far outstripped any impulse to politeness. He was in the process of commissioning a custom toothpick case for himself, and for a full quarter of an hour he fussed over every ivory, gold, and pearl design in the shop, debating every last detail of size, shape, and ornament until he had mapped out the exact piece his own fancy had dreamed up. For the whole wait, he spared the sisters nothing more than three or four broad, unapologetic stares — a show of attention that branded him on Elinor’s memory as a man of pure, sterling insignificance, wrapped up in the very height of empty, fashionable vanity. Marianne, however, escaped all irritation at his rude gawking and his silly, childish devotion to such a trivial trinket: she was so deep in her own private, grieving thoughts, she was as oblivious to the world around her as if she was alone in her bedroom at Barton Cottage.

At last the order was settled. Every detail of the toothpick case had been approved, the gentleman named the latest possible date he could bear to go without the finished piece, drew on his gloves with lazy, deliberate care, tossed one more arrogant glance at the Miss Dashwoods that seemed to demand their admiration rather than offer any courtesy, and sauntered off with an air of unshakable self-conceit thinly masked by pretended indifference.

Elinor wasted no time stepping up to the counter to explain her own business, and was just on the cusp of wrapping up the negotiation when a second man stepped up beside her. She lifted her eyes to his face, and gasped softly in surprise to find it was her own brother, John Dashwood. Their pleasure at seeing one another was warm and genuine enough to make for a very sweet, respectable reunion in the middle of Gray’s crowded shop. John was genuinely glad to run into his sisters, and his inquiries after their mother were respectful and thoughtful. He explained he and Fanny had been in London for two days already.

“I fully meant to call on you yesterday,” he said, “but it was impossible — we had to take Harry to Exeter Exchange to see the wild beasts, and spent the rest of the day with Mrs. Ferrars. Harry was utterly delighted. This morning I swore I’d find half an hour to drop by, but the first days in town are always so crowded with errands. I’ve come here to order Fanny a new wax seal. But tomorrow I absolutely will call on you in Berkeley Street, and be introduced to your friend Mrs. Jennings. I hear she’s a woman of very considerable fortune. And you must introduce me to the Middletons, too — as relations of my stepmother, I shall be delighted to show them every proper respect. They’ve been excellent neighbors to you in the country, I understand?”

“More than excellent,” Elinor assured him. “Their care for our comfort, their endless kindness, is more than I can ever properly thank them for.”

“I’m overjoyed to hear it, truly I am,” he nodded, satisfied. “And it’s only right, isn’t it? They’re people of great wealth, related to you through your father’s first family, so every civility that could make your life easier was only to be expected. Edward told us the loveliest stories of your little cottage, said it was the coziest, most perfect little place of its kind he’d ever seen, and that all of you seemed happier there than anywhere else. It was a great comfort to us to hear it, I assure you.”

Elinor felt a faint flush of embarrassment, and was relieved to be spared the need to reply when Mrs. Jennings’ servant appeared at the shop door to say his mistress was waiting for them by the carriage. John walked them all down the stairs, was formally introduced to Mrs. Jennings by the carriage step, repeated his promise to call on them the next day, and took his leave.

He arrived promptly the next day, bearing an apologetic excuse from Fanny, who could not join them: she was far too busy attending to her own mother’s affairs to step out, he said. Mrs. Jennings waved off all formality at once, insisting they were practically cousins, and she would certainly call on Mrs. John Dashwood very soon, bringing the Dashwood sisters with her. John’s manners to Elinor and Marianne were calm but perfectly kind; to Mrs. Jennings, they were unfailingly civil and attentive, and when Colonel Brandon stepped through the front door shortly after he did, John eyed the older man with such sharp curiosity it was obvious he was mentally tallying up his fortune, ready to match his civility to whatever sum he found.

After half an hour of chat, he asked Elinor to walk with him to Conduit Street, to introduce him properly to Sir John and Lady Middleton. The day was uncommonly fine, so she agreed readily. The moment they stepped out of the house, his questions spilled out.

“Who exactly is Colonel Brandon? Is he a man of good fortune?”

“Yes,” Elinor said. “He has a very fine estate in Dorsetshire, bringing in a very steady income.”

“I’m delighted to hear it! He seems the very picture of a gentleman, and Elinor — I must congratulate you on the prospect of a very respectable, comfortable establishment for life.”

“Me? Brother, whatever do you mean?”

“He likes you! I watched him very closely, and I’m entirely convinced of it. What did you say his total income was?”

“About two thousand a year, I believe.”

“Two thousand a year!” John’s face lit up with a performative, over-the-top air of generous delight. “Elinor, with all my heart I wish it was twice as much, for your sake.”

“Thank you, I truly believe you mean that,” Elinor replied, “but I am completely certain Colonel Brandon has not the slightest wish to marry me.”

“You’re mistaken, Elinor, very much mistaken. A very small amount of gentle encouragement on your part would secure him at once. He might be wavering right now, of course — the smallness of your fortune might make him hesitate, his friends might all advise against the match. But those little, soft attentions that ladies know so well how to give will fix him, no matter what reservations he has. There’s no reason at all you shouldn’t try. I don’t imagine you have any lingering prior attachment — in short, you know that kind of old, ill-fated infatuation is completely out of the question, the obstacles are insurmountable, and you have more than enough sense to see that. Colonel Brandon is the perfect match for you, and I will make every effort to be as civil to him as I can, to make him feel welcome to our family. It’s a match everyone will be overjoyed by. In fact —” he dropped his voice to an important, excited whisper “— it will be exceedingly welcome to all parties.” He paused, correcting himself quickly. “That is, your friends are all so anxious to see you well settled — Fanny most of all, she has your best interests entirely at heart, I promise you. And Mrs. Ferrars, Fanny’s mother, is such a kind woman, she said just the other day how happy that match would make her.”

Elinor refused to dignify that with a reply.

“It would be a most remarkable, droll little coincidence, wouldn’t it,” he went on, “if Fanny’s brother and my own sister were to settle and marry at exactly the same time. And it’s not even all that unlikely!”

“Is Mr. Edward Ferrars,” Elinor asked, steady and deliberate, “soon to be married?”

“It’s not formally settled yet, but it’s all well in hand. His mother is the most generous woman alive — Mrs. Ferrars has promised to settle a full thousand a year on him, if the match goes through. The lady is the Honourable Miss Morton, only daughter of the late Lord Morton, with thirty thousand pounds of her own. It’s a perfect, desirable connection for both sides, and I have no doubt at all it will happen soon. A thousand a year is no small sum for a mother to give away permanently, but Mrs. Ferrars has a most noble, generous spirit. Just think — the other day, the very moment we arrived in town, knowing we were sure to be a little short on funds after moving into Norland, she pressed two hundred pounds worth of bank notes straight into Fanny’s hands. It was such a welcome gift, for living in town is extraordinarily expensive for us right now.”

He paused, waiting for her agreement and sympathy, and Elinor forced herself to say, “Your expenses both in town and at Norland must be very considerable, but your income is a very large one, after all.”

“Not nearly as large as people assume,” he sighed. “I don’t mean to complain, of course — it’s a very comfortable income, and I hope it will grow better with time. But the enclosure of Norland Common that’s going on right now has been a massive drain on our funds. And then I made a small purchase earlier this year — East Kingham Farm, you must remember the place where old Gibson used to live. The land was so perfectly situated right next to our own property, I felt it was my duty to buy it, I couldn’t in good conscience let it fall into someone else’s hands. Convenience always costs a price, and that farm cost me a very great deal of money.”

“More than it was intrinsically worth, I expect,” Elinor said.

“Oh, I hope not! I could have turned around and sold it the very next day for more than I paid. But when it came to the purchase money, I was very nearly very unlucky — the stocks were so low at the time, if I hadn’t happened to have the full sum sitting with my banker already, I would have had to sell out my shares and take a terrible loss.”

Elinor could only smile faintly. “We had other great, unavoidable expenses right after moving into Norland, too,” he continued. Our respected father, as you well know, left all the remaining Stanhope household goods at Norland — and they were very valuable — to your mother. Far be it from me to complain about that, he had every right to dispose of his property as he chose. But because of that, we had to replace almost all the linen, the china, every last household item he gave away. You can imagine, after all these costs, how far we are from being well off, and how grateful we were for Mrs. Ferrars’s kindness.”

“Of course,” Elinor said. “With her generous support, I hope you will soon be comfortably well situated.”

“Another year or two will see to a great deal of that,” he said gravely. “But there is still so much more to do. We haven’t even laid a single stone for Fanny’s new greenhouse yet, and all we have of the new flower garden is a marked out plan.”

“Where is the greenhouse to be built?”

“On the knoll behind the house. We cut down all the old walnut trees there to make space for it. It will be a stunning feature to look at from all across the park, and the new flower garden will slope down right in front of it, so it will be exceedingly pretty. We cleared away all the ugly old thorn bushes that used to grow all over the brow of that hill.”

Elinor kept her dismay and her quiet censure to herself, and was very thankful Marianne was not there to share the provocation of hearing all their old Norland family trees cut down for a frivolous new garden.

Now that he had made very clear just how “strapped” he was for cash, and neatly eliminated any chance of him offering to buy his sisters a pair of ear-rings the next time he visited Gray’s, his mood brightened, and he began gushing over how lucky Elinor was to have a friend like Mrs. Jennings.

“She seems an incredibly valuable woman! Her house, her whole way of living, makes it obvious she has a very large fortune. This acquaintance has already been so useful to you, and I’m sure in the end it will prove even more materially advantageous. Her inviting you up to town was such a great favour to you, and she is so openly fond of you, it’s almost certain you and Marianne will not be forgotten in her will. She must have a great deal of money left to leave.”

“I should think not very much at all,” Elinor said. “All she has is her jointure, which is bound to go to her daughters.”

“But no woman of reasonable prudence spends every penny of her income, now, does she?” he argued. “Whatever she saves up over the years, she can dispose of however she likes.”

“Surely it’s far more likely she’ll leave those savings to her own daughters, rather than to us?”

“Her two daughters are already very comfortably married, there’s no need to leave them anything more. Whereas, in my opinion, she has treated you with such extraordinary kindness, she has given you a sort of claim on her future generosity that no conscientious woman could ignore. Her behaviour to you has been perfectly warm, and she can’t possibly be that kind without being aware of the hopes it raises.”

“But neither Marianne nor I hold any such hopes. Brother, your concern for our well being is making you see things that are not there.”

“Of course, well, people have very little power over who gets what, at the end of the day,” he said, as if recalling himself. “But my dear Elinor — what is the matter with Marianne? She looks very unwell, her colour is all gone, and she’s grown so thin. Is she ill?”

“She’s not well. She’s had a nervous complaint plaguing her for several weeks now.”

“I am sorry to hear that. At her age, any sort of lingering illness ruins a girl’s bloom forever! Hers was such a short one, too. Just last September she was the prettiest girl I ever saw, the kind that was sure to catch a gentleman’s eye. Fanny used to say, you know, that Marianne would marry sooner and better than you. Not that she isn’t very fond of you, of course, that’s just what she thought. But she’ll be wrong now. I doubt Marianne will ever marry a man with more than five or six hundred a year, at the very most, but I am very sure you will do far better. Dorsetshire! I don’t know much of Dorsetshire, but my dear Elinor, I will be so delighted to come visit you there, and I can promise Fanny and I will be the first and happiest of your guests.”

Elinor tried very seriously to convince him there was no chance at all of her marrying Colonel Brandon, but the fantasy was far too pleasing to John for him to let it go. He was already fully determined to cultivate a close friendship with the Colonel, and push the match forward with every show of attention. He had just enough leftover guilt for never doing a single thing to help his sisters himself, that he was desperate for everyone else to do the work for him — an offer from Colonel Brandon, or a nice legacy from Mrs. Jennings, was the easiest possible way to make up for his own neglect.

They were lucky enough to find Lady Middleton at home, and Sir John walked in before their visit was over. Courtesies were exchanged all around, Sir John was ready to like anyone, and even though John Dashwood knew next to nothing about horses, he quickly decided he was a very good natured fellow. Lady Middleton, meanwhile, saw more than enough fashionable polish in his appearance to decide his acquaintance was well worth having. John left the Middletons’ home absolutely delighted with both of them.

“I shall have the most charming report to carry back to Fanny,” he told Elinor as they walked home. “Lady Middleton is the very picture of elegant refinement! Fanny will be so glad to make her acquaintance. And Mrs. Jennings, too, she’s so perfectly well behaved, even if she is not quite as elegant as her daughter. Fanny had some small scruples about visiting her, you know, and very naturally — we only knew her as the widow of a man who made all his money in very low trade, and Fanny and Mrs. Ferrars were both fully convinced neither she nor her daughters were the sort of women Fanny would care to associate with. But now I can bring home a perfectly satisfactory account of both of them.”
