Let us skip ahead a few months in the life of Mr. Arthur Pendennis—those uneventful, slightly tedious months that are always full of significance to the person living them but utterly forgettable to anyone else. We left him last, freshly installed as a professional writer—though Mr. Warrington, with his usual flair for the dramatic, preferred to call it "literary hackery." And so it was: a life not unlike that of any other hack—be he legal, literary, or clerical—whether stationed in a curacy, marching through a regiment, or slaving over ledgers in a merchant’s office. The routine is monotonous, the days bleed into one another, and the only excitement comes from the occasional deadline or the sudden onset of indigestion after too many oysters.

Now, let us be clear: writing for a living is no picnic. A man may have genius, wit, and a soul like a thunderstorm—but if he needs to eat, he must write. And when you're selling Pegasus by the pound, poetry goes out the window. Pegasus, once the noble steed of inspiration, now trots like a cab horse down Fleet Street, hauling reviews, essays, and half-baked editorials behind him. He pants, he sweats, and occasionally gets a crack across the rump from an irate editor who has just read a particularly bad sentence. It’s not glamorous. But then, why should it be? Genius, like any other profession, must pay taxes, wear waistcoats, and endure the occasional hangover.

Still, we ought not pity Pegasus too much. He’s no different from the rest of creation. If he’s whipped, it’s often because he deserved it. Let us thank our stars that George Warrington exists, for he would never allow such sentimental nonsense about “the sacredness of art” or “the divine right of poets to live in poverty.” No, Warrington believes that writers should work, pay rent, and behave themselves—just like everyone else. And so does Arthur Pendennis, though he’d never admit it aloud.

And so, with the *Pall Mall Gazette* firmly established and Arthur’s talents duly recognized (if not entirely appreciated), he set to work. Every week, he reviewed books—historians’ labors of love, poets’ carefully polished sonnets, actors’ latest performances—and did so with a certain flippancy, yes, but also with honesty and, when possible, good humor. Of course, some historians spent twenty years on a tome that Arthur disposed of in two days at the British Museum. Some poets had written odes so sublime they thought they’d earned their place in immortality—only to find themselves reduced to a paragraph of mockery in Pen’s review. The theatres complained bitterly. One actor, after reading Pen’s critique, declared he would rather lose a leg than meet him in a dark alley. But really, what harm was done? In those days, there were so few great minds that the critical world resembled a country fair: a few decent exhibits, plenty of clowns, and one or two prize-winning pigs.

Pen, meanwhile, remained modest. He knew he wasn’t Shakespeare. He knew he wasn’t even quite Byron. But he had a sharp eye, a quick tongue, and a friend named Warrington who could tear him apart in ten seconds flat if he ever started thinking he was God’s gift to literature. That kept him grounded. And that, perhaps, is why he didn’t become unbearable.

By these efforts—reviews, articles, occasional leading pieces when the paper allowed him to speak his mind—Arthur earned four pounds four shillings a week. Not a fortune, but enough to keep him alive, fed, and occasionally clothed in something resembling fashion. He also contributed to magazines and reviews, and rumor has it (though he never confirmed it) that he was London correspondent for the *Chatteris Champion*, which at the time featured some of the most brilliant letters from the capital—letters so eloquent, so vivid, they made one wonder whether the author had been secretly trained by Lord Byron himself.

All told, Arthur managed to earn nearly four hundred pounds a year—a remarkable sum for a young man without a title, a fortune, or a father to bail him out. On the second Christmas after arriving in London, he sent home a hundred pounds to his mother—as a partial repayment of the debt he owed Laura. Now, this act was met with ecstatic joy at Fairoaks. Mrs. Pendennis read every word of her son’s writings and believed he was the greatest thinker since Aristotle. She saw the hundred pounds not as money, but as proof of angelic virtue. She worried about his health, thrilled at his stories of grand dinners and famous men, and wept quietly whenever he mentioned seeing “Mr. Dickens” or “the Duke of Wellington.”

That winter, Arthur returned briefly to Fairoaks. It was a short visit, but it brought immense comfort to his mother. Helen had her son all to herself. Laura was away visiting old Lady Rockminster (a fact that remains suspiciously unexplained). The Clavering Park crowd was absent. Only Doctor Portman and a few old friends dropped by—respectfully, warmly, as if Arthur were already a man of consequence.

Between mother and son, there was nothing but affection, trust, and quiet happiness. It was, perhaps, the happiest fortnight of either of their lives. When Arthur left again, the house felt empty. His mother sent the money to Laura—though why Laura chose to flee just as Arthur arrived remains a mystery. Was she avoiding him? Was she jealous? Or was she simply tired of being the subject of gossip?

By now, Arthur was well known in both literary and fashionable circles. Thanks to his uncle’s introductions and his own charm, he’d become a fixture at dinner parties, club meetings, and evening gatherings. Bacon, Bungay & Co. welcomed his contributions; Mr. Wenham invited him to dine; Mr. Wagg looked upon him with approval. People said they’d seen him at the homes of noblemen, where he was received with warmth—not because of his wealth (which was minimal), but because he was pleasant, witty, and dressed better than most.

In London, reputation is everything. And Arthur had one. He was seen at one house because he’d been seen at another. He moved from Mayfair salons to taverns in Paternoster Row with ease. He breakfasted with a Peer, a Bishop, and a parliamentary orator, then dashed off to the newspaper office, where Finucane greeted him with the latest news from the Row. Shandon, the Captain, would appear with his pint of sherry (always silently delivered by the boy), and begin scribbling his lead article. Mr. Bludyer, the truculent critic, would roar from the front room, confiscate books from the publisher, sell them at his stall, drink the profits, and then proceed to “smash” the author in print.

Evenings were for walks with Warrington, dinners, balls, the opera, or quiet nights at the club with cigars and long talks. Arthur saw everything—the glittering and the grimy, the noble and the ridiculous. He didn’t realize how much he was enjoying it at the time. But later, when balls bored him, farces failed to amuse, and the best jokes fell flat, he’d look back and wonder: *Was that really me? Did I once care about such things?*

The London season was in full swing. Levees were held at St. James’s, carriages lined the Serpentine, dandy horsemen galloped up and down Rotten Row, and the clubs were packed with red-faced gentlemen reading newspapers. Everyone was in town. And naturally, Major Arthur Pendennis—who was *somebody*—was not absent.

On a certain morning, the Major sat by his fireside, feet submerged in a bath, sipping tea and reading the *Morning Post*. He wore a bright Turkish dressing gown and a bandana tied around his head. Without this ritual—without the bath, the tea, the paper—he could not face the day. Nobody knew how frail he was, except Morgan, his valet. And Morgan, bless him, treated the Major like a delicate vase—one that required careful handling, constant attention, and frequent polishing.

After the bath, Morgan dried the Major’s feet, helped him into his waistband and wig, starched his cravat, polished his boots, and placed his gloves with military precision. During this process, they conversed—confidentially, intimately, like two old comrades sharing secrets.

Morgan, it must be said, was not merely a valet. He was a man of the world. He belonged to two influential clubs in Mayfair and Pimlico. He dined with butlers and valets of the highest rank. He knew more about London society than most journalists. For while we might imagine that gossip flows only through parlors and drawing rooms, the real truth lies in kitchens, servants’ halls, and the back rooms of clubs.

Morgan knew everything. He knew which duke was broke, which earl was sleeping with his wife’s maid, which noblewoman was trying to marry her daughter off to a foreign prince. He knew who was in debt, who was cheating on whom, and who had recently been caught stealing silver from the family sideboard. And he shared it all with the Major—during the toilet, between the shaving and the boot-polishing.

That morning, the Major read of Lady Clavering’s presentation at court, along with Miss Amory’s debut. The descriptions of their dresses were elaborate, precise, and utterly baffling to anyone not fluent in the language of fashion. Seeing their names, the Major asked, “How long have the Claverings been in London?”

“Sir Francis sent away his old man,” Morgan replied. “Took a friend of mine—Towler, sir. Tall, red-haired fellow, though he dyes it. Used to be groom of the chambers in Lord Levant’s household until his lordship went bust. It’s a fall for Towler, sir—but poor men can’t be particular.”

“Devilish hard on Towler,” said the Major, chuckling. “And not pleasant for Lord Levant—he, he!”

“Always knew it was coming,” Morgan continued. “I warned you four years ago, when her ladyship pawned the diamonds. Towler took ’em in two cabs to Dobree’s. A lot of plate went the same way. You remember seeing it at Blackwall, with the Levant arms and coronet, opposite Lord Levant at the Marquis of Steyne’s dinner?”

“I beg your pardon—did I cut you, sir?” the Major asked, suddenly aware of the razor.

“No, sir,” Morgan said, resuming. “They’ve taken a house in Grosvenor Place. Her ladyship’s giving three parties and a dinner a week. But her fortune won’t stand it—can’t stand it.”

“Gad, she had a devilish good cook when I was at Fairoaks,” the Major remarked.

“Marobblan was his name, sir. Gone now,” Morgan said.

“Oh, I was sorry to lose him,” the Major said, genuinely.

“There’s been a tremenjuous row about Marobblan,” Morgan continued. “At a ball at Baymouth, he challenged Mr. Harthur to fight a duel—Mr. Arthur was very near knocking him down and throwing him out the window. But Chevalier Strong stopped it—I beg pardon, the holtercation. French cooks have as much pride as real gentlemen.”

“I heard of that,” the Major said.

“But that wasn’t why he was fired,” Morgan added. “It was Miss Hamory. He climbed the grapevine to her window, trying to get in—was caught. Mr. Strong came out, they turned on the garden-engine, and there was no end of a row.”

“Confound his impudence!” the Major exclaimed. “You don’t mean to say Miss Amory encouraged him?”

Morgan shrugged. “Know nothing about it, sir. Servants don’t know those kind of things. Most likely there was nothing in it. So many lies are told about families. Marobblan went away—bag and baggage, saucepans, piano, and all. He had a piano, wrote poetry in French, took lodgings at Clavering, hankered about the premises. They said Madam Fribsy, the milliner, brought letters to Miss Hamory—but I don’t believe a word. Nor that he tried to poison himself with charcoal—that was all a humbug between him and Madam Fribsy. Nearly shot by the park keeper, too.”

Later that day, the Major stood at the window of Bays’s Club, watching the world go by. There, walking down the street, was Arthur—with Mr. Popjoy.

“Look!” Popjoy said. “Did you ever pass Bays’s at four o’clock without seeing that collection of old fogies? They ought to be cast in wax and put in Madame Tussaud’s.”

“In the chamber of horrors,” Arthur laughed.

“In the chamber of horrors! Gad, doosid good!” Popjoy cried. “They’re old rogues, most of ’em. There’s old Blondel. There’s my Uncle Colchicum—the most confounded old sinner in Europe. There’s—hullo! Someone’s tapping the window.”

“It’s my uncle, the Major,” Arthur said.

“Notorious old rogue,” Popjoy said, wagging his head. (“Notowious old wogue,” he pronounced, making it sound even more damning.) “He’s beckoning you in. Wants to speak to you.”

“Come in too,” Arthur said.

“Can’t,” Popjoy replied. “Cut Uncle Col. two years ago—about Mademoiselle Frangipane. Ta, ta.” And with that, he sauntered into Blacquiere’s, a nearby haunt of younger sinners.

Arthur stepped into the club, where the Major awaited him—dressed, groomed, and ready to dispense wisdom, gossip, and possibly a mild rebuke. The world outside was changing. But inside Bays’s, time stood still—like the old bucks in the window, preserved in wax, forever staring at the past.
