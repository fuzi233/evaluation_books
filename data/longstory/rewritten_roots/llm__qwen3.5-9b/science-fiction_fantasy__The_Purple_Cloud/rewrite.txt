About three months ago—toward the end of May in the year 1900—the author of these pages received a letter and a packet of documents that stood out among everything else he had ever examined. They arrived from a close friend, Dr. Arthur Lister Browne, M.A. (Oxon.), F.R.C.P. For the past two years, I had spent most of my time in France, missing our usual meetings in London, and despite our intimate friendship, we were notoriously bad at correspondence; only two brief notes had exchanged between us until last May. Then came this letter and its accompanying package.

The packet contained four notebooks, densely filled with the dizzying script of Pitman's shorthand. Taken together, the pages resembled startled swarms of birds frozen in mid-flight. Scribbled entirely in pencil, with indistinct stroke weights and sparse vowels, decoding them was no leisurely task. The letter itself was also penned in shorthand. Here, I publish the letter along with the contents of the second notebook I successfully deciphered (marked 'III'). Please note that in five instances, I have reconstructed sentences based on my best guesses, and in two cases, the characters were so cryptic that I abandoned the passages entirely, leaving me with a headache. However, these gaps do not affect the core narrative.

***

'DEAR OLD SHIEL,—I have been lying here thinking of you, wishing you were here to give me one final handshake before I—"go": for, by all appearances, I am indeed "going."

Four days ago, I began to feel a soreness in my throat. Passing Old Johnson's surgery in Selbridge, I asked him to take a look. He muttered something about membranous laryngitis, which made me smile, but by the time I reached home, I was hoarse and not smiling. Before nightfall, I was suffering from severe dyspnea and laryngeal stridor. I immediately telegraphed London for Dr. Morgan. Between him and Johnson, they have been cutting into my trachea and cauterizing my insides with chromic acid and galvanic instruments. While the immediate breathing difficulty has subsided—and it is miraculous how little pain I endure—I am too experienced a practitioner not to recognize the signs: the bronchi are involved, *too far* involved. As a matter of absolute fact, there is no hope.

Dr. Morgan still clings to the possibility of adding me to his statistics of successful tracheotomies, but prognosis has always been my forte, and I say *No*. The only small comfort in my impending death will be seeing a specialist beaten in his own field. We shall see.

I have been arranging my affairs this morning and remembered these notebooks. I intended to send them to you months ago, but my habit of procrastination, combined with the fact that the woman from whom I transcribed these words was still alive, delayed me. Now she is dead. As a literary man and a student of life, you should be interested, if you can manage to decipher them. You may even find them valuable.

I am currently under a slight dose of morphine, propped up in a pleasant state of languor. Since I can write without much effort, I will tell you in this shorthand about her. Her name was Miss Mary Wilson. She was about thirty when I met her and forty-five when she died; I knew her intimately for those fifteen years. Are you familiar with the philosophy of the hypnotic trance? That was the nature of our relationship: hypnotist and subject. She had been under another man prior to my involvement, but no one ever succeeded with her as I did. She suffered from *tic douloureux* of the fifth nerve. Most of her teeth had been extracted before I saw her, and attempts had been made to sever the nerve on the left side via external incision, but it made no difference. All the clocks in hell seemed to tick incessantly in that poor woman's jaw. It was truly a mercy of Providence that she eventually crossed paths with me. My organization possessed nearly complete and effortless control over hers; with a few passes, I could expel her tormentors.

You have never seen anyone as singular in personal appearance as my friend, Miss Wilson. Even as a physician, I could never behold her without a sensation of shock; she inevitably suggested "the Other World," carrying an odor of decay and giving the distinct impression that one was looking at a ghost rather than a woman. Yet, I struggle to convey the essence of this beyond dry physical details: a lofty brow, meagre lips, a pointed chin, and ashen cheeks. She was tall and deplorably emaciated, her entire skeleton visible save for her thigh bones. Her eyes were the bluish hue of cigarette smoke, holding a strange, feeble, unearthly gaze. By thirty-five, her scanty hair was already completely white.

She was well-to-do and lived alone in the old Wooding Manor-house, five miles from Ash Thomas. Knowing that I was just starting my practice in these parts, she insisted that I devote myself solely to her. In doing so, she became the most lucrative patient I ever had.

Soon, I discovered that in her trance state, Miss Wilson possessed remarkably powerful abilities. These were not unique in *kind*, as psychical scientists today often claim regarding the reporting powers of the mind in trance—a concept known to every wise woman since the Middle Ages—but they were extraordinary in *quantity*: so constant, reliable, exact, and far-reaching.

Generally, trance powers manifest primarily concerning space rather than time; spirits roam the present plane, traveling horizontally but rarely ascending or descending significantly. Miss Wilson's gift was special because she traveled in every direction and easily in all but one: north, south, up, down, into the past, the present, and the future. I realized this gradually. During her trances, she emitted a stream of sounds—not quite speech, but a murmurous, guttural utterance mixed with heavy breaths from her languid lips. Accompanied by intense pupil contraction, absent knee jerks, rigidity, and a rapt expression, she entered this state. I grew accustomed to sitting by her bedside for hours, fascinated, trying to catch the meaning of that opiate, visionary language drifting from her lips in a deliberate monotone. Over months, my ear learned to distinguish the words. "The veil was rent" for me, too, and I could follow the wandering course of her spirit.

At the end of six months, I heard her repeat words familiar to me: *"Such were the arts by which the Romans extended their conquests, and attained the palm of victory; and the concurring testimony of different authors enables us to describe them with precision..."* I was startled; these were lines from Gibbon's *Decline and Fall*, a book I knew she had never read. I asked sternly, "Where are you?" She replied, "Us are in a room, eight hundred and eleven miles above. A man is writing. Us are reading."

Two facts are crucial here: First, in trance, she never referred to herself as "I" or "we," but used the objective pronoun "**us**"—"us are," "us will," "us went." Second, when traversing the past, she always positioned herself "**above**" the earth, rising higher the further back in time she went. When describing present events, she felt herself "**on**" the earth. Regarding the future, she invariably declared that "us" were located "**within**" the earth. There seemed to be fixed limits to these excursions. While she frequently cited distances of three or four thousand miles "above," her descent "within" never exceeded sixty-three miles; usually, she reported twenty or twenty-five. She resembled a deep-sea diver who, the deeper he goes, encounters increasing resistance until, at a shallow depth, the pressure becomes prohibitive.

I fear I cannot continue, though I have much more to tell you about this lady. For fifteen years, intermittently, I sat listening by her dim bedside to her murmuring trances! Eventually, my trained ear could detect the sense of her faintest sigh. I heard Gibbon from beginning to end. Some of her reports were frivolous nonsense; over others, I hung in a horror of interest. Certainly, my dear friend, I have heard amazing words proceed from those wan lips of Mary Wilson. Sometimes, I could anchor her repeatedly to any scene or subject of my choosing through sheer will; at other times, the flighty waywardness of her spirit eluded me. Otherwise, I might have sent you twenty or forty notebooks instead of four.

Around the fifth year, it occurred to me to record her more coherent utterances using my shorthand skills. The notebook marked "I," which seems the most curious, dates from the seventh year. Its history mirrors the others: one afternoon, she murmured in the intonation of reading. Interested, I asked where she was. She replied, "Us are forty-five miles within: us read, and another writes." From this, I concluded she was somewhere between fifteen and thirty years in the future, perusing an unpublished work. After several weeks, I managed to keep her focused on the same subject and, I believe, secured the entirety of the manuscript. I trust you will find it striking and hope you can read my notes.

But let us pause on Mary Wilson for a moment. Let us instead reflect briefly on A.L. Browne, F.R.C.P.—with a breathing tube in his trachea and Eternity waiting beneath his pillow...

It was roughly three weeks prior to that fateful Sunday night sermon that Clark, the leader of the impending expedition, paid me a visit. It was ostensibly a friendly call. By then, I had been practicing at No. 2 Harley Street for about a year. Though I was barely twenty-five, I believed I possessed the finest private practice in Europe.

"Elite"—yes, but small. I could sustain my station and mingle with the aristocracy, yet occasionally, the gnawing ache of financial insecurity would strike. Around that very time, I was rescued from potential ruin only by the unexpected success of my book, *Applications of Science to the Arts*.

During our conversation that afternoon, Clark spoke in his usual casual, unpredictable manner:

"Do you know what I dreamed about you last night, Adam Jeffson? I dreamed you were part of our expedition."

I suspect he noticed my sudden start. On that exact same night, I had dreamed the identical vision. Yet, I kept my silence. My tongue stumbled as I replied:

"Who? Me? On the expedition? I wouldn't go if they begged me."

"Oh, you would."

"I wouldn't. You forget I am engaged to be married."

"Well, we needn't dwell on it, since Peters isn't going to die," he said lightly. "Still, if anything happens to him, you're the first person I'd turn to, Adam Jeffson."

"Clark, you're joking," I said, trying to dismiss the gravity of his tone. "I know practically nothing about astronomy or magnetism. Besides, I'm getting married..."

"But what about your botany, my friend? *That* is exactly what we need. As for nautical astronomy, *poh*, a man with your scientific instinct would master it instantly."

"You treat this as a serious possibility," I said, forcing a smile. "Such a notion never enters my head. First of all, there is my fiancée—"

"Ah, the all-important Countess, eh? Well, judging by what I know of her, she would be the first to drag you along. The chance of planting one's boot on the North Pole doesn't come around often, my son."

"Talk of something else!" I insisted. "There's Peters..."

"Of course, there's Peters. But believe me, the dream I had was crystal clear..."

"Leave me to my dreams and your Poles!" I laughed.

Yes, I recall now: I laughed loudly, pretending to be amused! But deep down, even then, I knew a crisis was unfolding—a turning point in my life that would make it the most extraordinary existence any mortal has ever known. I knew this for two reasons: the twin dreams, and the moment Clark departed. As I pulled on my gloves to visit my fiancée, I distinctly heard the ancient Two Voices within me. One whispered, *"Do not go to see her now!"* The other countered, *"Yes, go, go!"*

The Two Voices of my life! Any ordinary reader would assume I meant merely conflicting impulses, or perhaps that I was losing my mind. How could a modern man grasp how vividly real those voices seemed? How deafening they were, contending within me with a proximity "nearer than breathing," as the poets say, "closer than hands and feet"?

It began when I was seven. Playing in my father's pine woods, with a quarry-cliff half a mile away, I suddenly heard a voice inside me command, *"Take a walk toward the cliff."* Immediately, another voice replied, *"Do not go that way at all."* Mere whispers then, which later swelled into furious shouts. I walked toward the cliff. It was steep, thirty feet high, and I fell. Weeks later, recovering my speech, I told my stunned mother that "someone had pushed me" over the edge, while another "caught me" at the bottom.

Around my eleventh birthday, lying in bed, a terrifying realization struck me: my life held immense significance for unseen forces. Two Powers, enemies of each other, hunted me constantly—one seeking to destroy me, the other to preserve me. One urged specific actions; the other demanded the opposite. I was not an ordinary boy, but a unique creature, marked for something unknown. I possessed instincts as primal and occult as those of humanity's first steps. Biblical phrases like "The Lord spoke unto So-and-so" ceased to suggest mystery to me; I understood perfectly that humans once had more than two ears, and that I, in these later days, resembled those primordial beings.

Yet, save perhaps for my mother, no one has ever imagined me as I describe myself. To the world, I appeared as an average youth: rowing in the Varsity eight, cramming for exams, loafing in clubs. When forced to choose a career, who could have guessed the civil war raging in my soul while my mind remained indifferent? It was an agony of strife where the voices roared: *"Be a scientist—a doctor!"* versus *"Be a lawyer, an engineer, an artist—be anything but a doctor!"*

I chose medicine and attended Cambridge, the greatest medical school of the era. It was there I met a man named Scotland, who held a peculiar worldview. He occupied rooms in Trinity College's New Court, surrounded by a group of us. He incessantly discoursed on "Black" and "White Powers" until it became absurd. Men nicknamed him "black-and-white-mystery-man"; once, when someone mentioned "the black mystery of the universe," Scotland cut him off with, "No, the black-and-white mystery."

I remember Scotland vividly: a gentle soul with a passion for cats, Sappho, and the Anthology. Short in stature, sporting a Roman nose, he struggled perpetually to keep his neck straight and pull in his paunch. He taught that the universe was a frantic battleground between a White Power and a Black Power. The White was stronger but disadvantaged on Earth; it had dominated Europe until the Middle Ages but had since been slowly yielding to the Black. Eventually, the Black would triumph—not necessarily everywhere, but *here*, carrying off this planet as its prize.

Scotland repeated this doctrine endlessly. While others tolerated him with indifference, I, feigning cynicism, absorbed his words with agonizing intensity. They left the deepest mark on my soul.

***

When Clark finally left, I was putting on my gloves to visit my fiancée, Countess Clodagh, when the Two Voices spoke with unprecedented clarity.

Sometimes, the urge of one impulse becomes so overwhelming that resistance is impossible. This was the case with the voice commanding me to proceed.

I had to cross from Harley Street to Hanover Square. Throughout the journey, it felt as though a shout pierced my physical ear: *"Since you go, speak not a word of the Boreal or Clark's visit."* Another voice screamed back: *"Tell! Tell! Hide nothing!"*

The tension lasted what felt like a month, though in reality, it spanned only minutes before I arrived in Hanover Square and embraced Clodagh.

In my estimation, Clodagh was the most magnificent creature imaginable—that haughty neck that seemed forever scornful of whatever lay just behind her left shoulder. Magnificent! But alas—I know now—she was a godless woman, possessing a bitter heart.

Once, she confessed her favorite historical figure was Lucrezia Borgia. Upon seeing my horror, she quickly added, "Well, no, I'm only joking!" Such was her duplicity; I now realize she spent her life concealing her heinous nature from me. Yet, how utterly she had entranced me!

Our marriage faced opposition from both families. Mine rejected her because her father and grandfather had died in lunatic asylums. Hers rejected me because I was neither wealthy nor noble. Her sister, an older woman, had married a common country doctor, Peters of Taunton. This so-called *mésalliance* made my union with her doubly detestable to her relatives. Yet, Clodagh's extraordinary passion for me defied all threats and pleas. What a blazing flame she was! Sometimes, she terrified me.

She was no longer young, being five years my senior—and consequently, five years older than her nephew, Peter Peters, born of her sister's marriage to the doctor of Taunton.

On the day of Clark's visit, I hadn't even sat five minutes with Clodagh before mentioning the expedition.

"Dr. Clark—ha! ha! ha!--has been talking to me about the Expedition. He says if anything happens to Peters, I'll be the first man he runs to. He's had an absurd dream..."

The consciousness flooding me as I spoke was pure *wickedness*—crooked, twisted wickedness. But I could not help it any more than I could fly.

Clodagh stood by a window, a rose pressed to her face. For a full minute, she offered no reply. I watched her sharp, florid profile, steadily bent as she smelled the flower. Finally, in her cold, rapid tone, she said:

"The man who first plants his foot on the North Pole will certainly be ennobled. I say nothing of the millions... I only wish I were a man!"

"I don't know that I have any special ambition that way," I replied. "I am very happy in my warm Eden with my Clodagh. I don't like the outer Cold."

"Don't let me think little of you!" she answered pettishly.
