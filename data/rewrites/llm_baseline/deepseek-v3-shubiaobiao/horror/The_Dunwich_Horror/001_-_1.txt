A wrong turn on the Aylesbury Pike, just past Dean’s Corners, leads travelers into a stretch of land that doesn’t belong—not to this time, not to this world. The road narrows, hemmed in by brambles and crumbling stone walls, while the trees loom unnaturally tall, their gnarled branches twisting like skeletal fingers. The fields are sparse, choked with weeds, and the few scattered houses sag with age and rot, their windows staring like hollow eyes.

The figures you glimpse—slouched on rotting porches or lurking in the overgrown meadows—never speak. They watch in silence, their faces gaunt, their eyes too knowing. Something in their stillness warns you away, as if they guard secrets best left buried.

Then the hills rise in the distance, their rounded crowns too perfect, too *wrong*. At their peaks stand rings of ancient stone, jagged teeth against the sky. The ravines that split the land yawn wide, their wooden bridges groaning underfoot. When dusk falls, the marshes come alive with the shrill cries of whippoorwills and the ceaseless drone of frogs—too loud, too eager. Fireflies swarm in unnatural numbers, their flickering light a mockery of life.

The Miskatonic River slithers through the valley, its waters glinting like scales. And beyond it, Dunwich clings to the shadow of Round Mountain, a cluster of sagging gambrel roofs and broken dreams. Most of its houses stand empty, their doors hanging open like slack jaws. The church, its steeple shattered, has been defiled into a general store, its shelves lined with dust and decay.

Crossing the covered bridge feels like stepping into a grave. The air inside is thick with the stench of mold and something older, something *hungry*. The village beyond is worse—a place where time has curdled, where the past refuses to stay dead.

No one comes to Dunwich anymore. The signposts were torn down long ago, after *what happened* in 1928. The few who remember whisper about inbred bloodlines, about curses carved into the hills. The locals are a withered breed, their faces twisted by generations of something darker than mere decay.

Legends linger—of Indian rites in the stone circles, of voices howling from beneath the earth. The Reverend Hoadley once preached of devils in the hills before vanishing without a trace. Even now, travelers swear they hear things in the ravines when the wind is right: whispers, laughter, the sound of wings where no birds fly.

And when the whippoorwills call at night, the people of Dunwich lock their doors. They say the birds come for the dying, that their cries sync with a man’s last breaths. If they catch a fleeing soul, they shriek in triumph. If not, they fall silent, waiting.

But those are just stories.

Aren’t they?
