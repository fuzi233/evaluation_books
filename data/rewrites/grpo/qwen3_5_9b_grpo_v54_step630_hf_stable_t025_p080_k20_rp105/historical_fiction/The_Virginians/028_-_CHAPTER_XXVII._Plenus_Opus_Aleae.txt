"Tell me about those children, Harry," Lady Maria said, her voice light as they strolled along the common. "The ones I saw darting about the house where you were taken in after your terrible fall? Oh, that accident! I nearly fainted when I saw you lying there. Please, do not squeeze my arm so tightly; it makes me feel you do not care for me."

"The people are the finest I have ever known in the world," Mr. Warrington replied, his eyes bright with gratitude. "Mrs. Lambert was a close friend of my mother's during her time in Europe. Colonel Lambert is a distinguished gentleman, a veteran of Scotland, Flanders, and Minorca. No natural parents could have shown me greater kindness. I must reward them. I simply must make them a gift." He reached into his pocket, his fingers brushing against the crisp banknotes won from Morris and March.

"We could visit the toy shop," Lady Maria suggested gently. "Buy a couple of dolls for the children. It would be an insult to offer anything else in return for their charity."

"Dolls for Hester and Theo? Do you really believe a woman ceases to be a woman at forty?" Harry teased, though a faint wince betrayed his companion's discomfort beneath his arm. "Miss Hester is hardly a child, and Miss Theo is older still. They speak several languages fluently. They have read countless volumes, play the harpsichord with mastery, and sing beautifully. Theo even composes her own songs."

"I barely noticed them," Maria admitted, her tone shifting. "I assumed they were mere children. I had no idea they possessed such talents."

"That is the nature of women," Harry declared, striding forward with renewed vigor. "If George or I praise a woman, Mrs. Esmond or Mrs. Mountain will inevitably find fault with her!"

"I would never fault anyone who has been kind to you, Mr. Warrington," Maria sighed, her voice dropping. "Though I cannot blame you for envying them. They cared for you when you were wounded and ill, while I... while I had to leave you."

"You dear, good Maria!"

"No, Harry! Do not call me that. Stop pressing your attentions on me. Look!" She pointed down the street. "There is your black servant walking among a dozen others in livery. They are heading to the tea-garden to get drunk, just like their masters. That dreadful Mr. Morris was already tipsy when I found you, frightening you half to death."

"I had just secured large bets from both of them," Harry said, his mood instantly lifting. "What shall I buy for you, my dear cousin?" He recounted his recent triumphs with a laugh that rang with youthful arrogance. "For the honor of Virginia, I decided to show them what true jumping is. With a bit of practice, I believe I could leap two feet farther."

Lady Maria smiled, pleased by the victories of her young champion, though a shadow crossed her face. "But you must be careful with gambling, child. It has ruined our family. My brother Castlewood, Will, our poor father, and even Lady Castlewood herself fell victim to it. And my Lord March? He is the most terrible gambler and the most successful nobleman in the land."

"I have no intention of fearing him or his friend Jack Morris," Harry dismissed, running his fingers over the gold coins in his pocket. "What games do you play at Aunt Bernstein's? Cribbage, all-fours, brag, whist, commerce, piquet, quadrille? I am ready for any of them. What time is that clock striking? Surely it is seven!"

"And you wish to begin now?" Maria asked, her voice plaintive. "You do not care to walk with your poor cousin anymore. You used to."

"Hey! Youth is youth, cousin!" Harry cried, tossing his head back. "A young man must have his fling!" He strutted beside her, confident, happy, and eager for the night ahead. Only yesterday he had preferred the company of Theo and Hester and Mrs. Lambert; now, the allure of pleasure, life, and the desire to conquer had seized him. He accepted the cup of fortune without calculating the headache that might follow.

As they spoke, the fiddles from the open orchestra on the Parade began their usual evening tuning, a raucous squeaking that signaled the start of the concert. Maria knew her aunt was already awake and waiting; she would soon be dragged back to her duties. Harry never asked about this slavery, though he must have known it existed if he had bothered to think. He felt no pity for his cousin. He was not thinking of her at all. Yet, when his own misfortune had struck, she had been wounded far more cruelly. She had carried his thoughts, burying them under a mask of smiling hypocrisy, as women of her station were expected to do. Perhaps Mrs. Grundy thought her an old fool? Did fools' caps not cover gray hair as well as jet or auburn? One must bear gently with elderly *fredaines*. There are late crops of wild oats as well as early harvests; the *avena fatua* grows right to the end of the year.

Madame Bernstein and her attendants, like worldly parents eager to escape a troublesome child, had already put the sun to bed while it was still light. They drew the curtains, lit their candles, and prepared their tea and negus. One by one, ladies arrived at the Baroness's door—some painted, some patched, all brocaded. Gentlemen in gala attire followed. Mr. Poellnitz arrived with the largest star and the most embroidered coat. My Lord March and Ruglen appeared transformed from the rough individual Harry had met at the White Horse. His tight brown wig was replaced by a neatly curled feather top with a bag and grey powder; his jockey's dress and leather breeches were swapped for a rich, elegant French suit. Mr. Jack Morris wore a similar wig and a suit of fine cloth. Mr. Wolfe attended his beautiful mistress, Miss Lowther, and her aunt, who loved cards as the world did.

When Lady Maria Esmond entered, her appearance belied Madame Bernstein's earlier descriptions. Her figure was exquisite, her dress revealing much of it. Her complexion was naturally fair, enhanced by a dark frilled ribbon clasped with a jewel around her neck, which made her skin look snow-white. Her cheeks held a healthy bloom, purchased openly at the perfumery shops, perhaps aided by an artful patch or two. Her hoops were modest compared to modern iron contrivances. The costume, absurd in some points, was pleasing in its entirety. One might wonder if ladies today wore bangles and nose-rings; surely we would laugh at the ornaments but not dislike them, and lovers would not hesitate to lift a ring to reach the rosy lips beneath.

As for the Baroness de Bernstein, when she took the trouble to make a grand toilette, she appeared handsome and magnificent, yet melancholy and somewhat terrifying. One reads the past in such old faces. The fires go out of some eyes as crow's-feet pucker around them; they flash no longer with scorn, anger, or love. They gaze, unmelted by sapphire glances; they look, undazzled. My fair young reader, if you are not as perfect as the peerless Lindamira, Queen of the Ball, and if, at the end of the night, you meekly admit you had only two or three partners while Lindamira danced with a crowd—console yourself. Think that at fifty, you will look as kind and pleasant as she appears now at eighteen. You will not have to lay down your coach-and-six of beauty and walk yourself through the rest of life. You will not witness the depreciation of your smiles or see fashion forsake your quarter. You may not have known grandeur, but you will not feel desertion. You may not have enjoyed millions, but you will have escaped bankruptcy.

"Our hostess," whispered My Lord Chesterfield to a friend, "reminds me of Covent Garden in my youth. Then it was the court end of the town, inhabited by the highest fashion. Now, a nobleman's house is merely a gaming-house, or you may enter with a friend to call for a bottle."

"A bottle and a tavern are good things in their way," My Lord March shrugged. "I was not born before the Georges came in, though I intend to live to a hundred. I never knew the Bernstein but as an old woman; if she ever had beauty, hang me if I know how she spent it."

"No, hang me, how did she spend it?" Jack Morris laughed.

"Here is a table! Shall we sit down and play? Do not let the Frenchman come in. He will not pay. Mr. Warrington, will you take a card?" Mr. Warrington and My Lord Chesterfield found themselves partners against Mr. Morris and the Earl of March. "You have come too late, Baron," the elder nobleman said to the other as he approached. "We have made our game. What, have you forgotten Mr. Warrington of Virginia—the young gentleman you met in London?"

"The young gentleman I met at Arthur's Chocolate House had black hair, a little cocked nose, and was by no means so fortunate in his personal appearance as Mr. Warrington," the Baron replied with much presence of mind. "Warrington, Dorrington, Harrington? We of the continent cannot retain your insular names. I certify that this gentleman is not the individual of whom I spoke at dinner." Glancing kindly at him, the old beau sidled away to a distant corner where Mr. Wolfe and Miss Lowther were engaged in deep conversation. Here, the Baron chose to engage the Lieutenant-Colonel on the Prussian manual exercise, recently introduced into King George II's army—a subject with which Mr. Wolfe was thoroughly familiar. The old gentleman uttered his criticisms and opinions, believing he had perfectly charmed his audience.

At the start of the evening, the Baroness received her guests personally, engaging them in talk and courtesies. But as the rooms and tables filled, Madame de Bernstein grew increasingly restless. Finally, she retreated with three friends to her own corner, where a table reserved for her was occupied by her major-domo. There, the old lady sat resolutely, never changing her place or quitting her game until cock-crow. The duty of receiving the company fell to Lady Maria, who, though she cared little for cards, dutifully did the honors of the house to her aunt's guests. She often rustled past the table where her young cousin was engaged with his three friends.

"Come and cut the cards for us," My Lord March called out as she passed on one of her wistful visits. "Cut the cards and bring us luck, Lady Maria! We have had none tonight, and Mr. Warrington is winning everything."

"I hope you are not playing high, Harry?" the lady asked timidly.

"Oh, no, only sixpences," cried My Lord March, dealing.

"Only sixpences," echoed Mr. Morris, Lord March's partner. Yet Mr. Morris must have been keenly alive to the value of a sixpence, for the loss of a few such coins made his round face look dismal. My Lord Chesterfield sat opposite Mr. Warrington, sorting his cards. No one could tell by inspecting that calm physiognomy whether good or ill fortune attended his lordship.

Some word, not altogether indicative of delight, slipped from Mr. Morris's lips, causing his partner to cry out, "Hang it, Morris, play your cards and hold your tongue!" Considering they were only playing for sixpences, My Lord March was strangely affected.

Lady Maria, still fondly lingering by Harry's chair with her hand resting behind it, could see his cards. A whole covey of trumps was ranged in one corner. She had not taken away his luck. She was pleased to think she had cut that pack which had dealt him all those pretty trumps. As Lord March dealt, he said in a quiet voice to Mr. Warrington, "The bet as before, Mr. Warrington, or shall we double it?"

"Anything you like, my lord," Mr. Warrington replied very quietly.

"We will say, then—shillings."

"Yes, shillings," said Mr. Warrington, and the game proceeded.

The end of the day's sport, and the days that followed, may be gathered from the following letter, which was never delivered to its intended recipient but found its way to America in the papers of Mr. Henry Warrington:

"DEAR GEORGE—As White's two bottles of Burgundy and a pack of cards constitute all the joys of your life, I take for granted that you are in London at this moment, preferring smoke and faro to fresh air and fresh haystacks. This will be delivered to you by a young gentleman with whom I have lately made acquaintance, and whom you will be charmed to know. He will play with you at any game for any stake, up to any hour of the night, and drink any reasonable number of bottles during the play. Mr. Warrington is no other than the Fortunate Youth about whom so many stories have been told in the Public Advertiser and other prints. He has an estate in Virginia as big as Yorkshire, with the incumbrance of a mother, the reigning Sovereign; but, as the country is unwholesome and fevers plentiful, let us hope that Mrs. Esmond will die soon and leave this virtuous lad in undisturbed possession.
