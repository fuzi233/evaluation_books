The day had broken with that particular New York spring that felt like a hand turned briskly inside a glove—fresh, impatient, and full of dust. The wind worried the budding trees along Broadway and sent grit skating over carriage steps; it sifted into the very folds of the ladies’ furs as if determined to remind them that winter’s mothballs were not yet a memory.

Inside Grace Church the air was thickened by those same memories. All the old ladies in both families had unearthed their faded sables and yellowing ermines; the camphor rose from the front pews in medicinal waves and lay heavy over the faint, clean breath of lilies banked about the altar. It was a strange mingling of scents: the bruised sweetness of lily pollen and the sharp, closed-chest smell of fur stored too long with sprigs of repellent—spring pressing in at the doors, the past refusing to make room.

At a signal from the sexton, Newland Archer stepped out of the vestry and took his place on the chancel step with his best man. The signal meant that the brougham bearing the bride and her father had been sighted; it also meant, as everyone knew without saying, that there would be an interval—adjustments in the lobby, consultations over the last drape of a veil, a bouquet held too high or too low, eight bridesmaids hovering like pale blossoms, each corrected into symmetry.

During that inevitable lapse the bridegroom, as proof of eagerness, was expected to stand exposed and solitary to the gaze of the assembled company. Archer submitted to it as he had submitted to everything else that made a nineteenth-century New York wedding feel like a rite from the dawn of history—so prescribed in each movement that even a heartbeat seemed an impropriety. Everything was equally easy—or equally painful—along the path he had committed himself to tread; and he obeyed the flurried whispers of his best man as piously as other bridegrooms had once obeyed him, when he had shepherded them through the same old labyrinth.

So far he was reasonably sure he had done all that was demanded. The eight bouquets of white lilac and lilies-of-the-valley had been dispatched at the proper moment; the gold-and-sapphire sleeve-links for the eight ushers and the cat’s-eye scarf-pin for the best man had been chosen and sent; he had sat up half the night varying the wording of his thanks for the last batch of gifts from men friends and ex–lady-loves—tact, gratitude, and a faintly stale gallantry carefully measured, like ingredients that must not curdle. The fees for Bishop and Rector were safely in his best man’s pocket. His luggage was already at Mrs. Manson Mingott’s, where the wedding-breakfast would take place; the travelling clothes into which he would change were laid out there too. A private compartment had been secured for the train that would carry the young couple to their unknown destination—the hiding of the first night’s lodging being one of the most sacred taboos of this prehistoric ritual, as if secrecy itself were a kind of seasoning to matrimony.

“Got the ring all right?” young van der Luyden Newland whispered. He was inexperienced in these duties and awed by their weight, as though he had been trusted with a relic.

Archer made the gesture he had seen so many men make in his place: with his ungloved right hand he felt in the pocket of his dark grey waistcoat and assured himself that the small gold circlet—engraved inside, _Newland to May, April ——, 187—_—was there. Then he resumed his attitude, tall hat and pearl-grey gloves with black stitching held in his left hand, and looked toward the church door.

Overhead Handel’s March swelled through the imitation stone vaulting, pompous and steady, as if the organ itself had learned the city’s way of insisting that what had always been done must always be done. The music carried on its waves the faded drift of the many weddings at which Archer, with cheerful indifference, had stood on this same chancel step watching other brides float up the nave toward other bridegrooms. The sound filled the church like a well-known recipe made again: rich, orderly, impossible to taste newly.

“How like a first night at the Opera,” he thought, recognizing all the same faces in the same boxes—no, pews. He wondered, with a sudden macabre clarity, if when the Last Trump sounded Mrs. Selfridge Merry would still be there with the same towering ostrich feathers and Mrs. Beaufort with the same diamond earrings and the same smile—and whether suitable proscenium seats were already prepared for them in another world.

There was time still to review the familiar countenances in the first rows. The women’s faces were sharp with curiosity and excited appraisal; the men’s were sulky with the obligation of having put on frock-coats before luncheon, and with the foreknowledge of having to fight their way to food at the wedding-breakfast. Archer could almost hear Reggie Chivers’s complaint, as distinct as if it had been spoken aloud: “Too bad the breakfast is at old Catherine’s.” And then, as naturally, Sillerton Jackson’s authoritative correction: “My dear fellow, haven’t you heard? It’s to be served at small tables, in the new English fashion.”

At the thought Archer’s mind leapt, incongruously, to Mrs. Manson Mingott’s table: the old Baltimore silver, the massive trays that made every dish look like an offering, the hush that could fall at the exact moment the covers were lifted and steam rose—an unveiling as ceremonious as any benediction. He had grown up among people who spoke of food as “good” or “bad” in the tone they used for weather; and yet here, even in church, men were already measuring the day’s success by whether one could “get at” the cooking. It was the closest anyone came to honest anticipation.

His eyes lingered on the left-hand pew. His mother, who had entered on Mr. Henry van der Luyden’s arm, sat weeping softly under her Chantilly veil, her hands folded on her grandmother’s ermine muff. Across the aisle his sister Janey twisted herself as far as decorum allowed. “Poor Janey,” he thought; “even by screwing her head around she can see only the people in the few front pews—and they’re mostly dowdy Newlands and Dagonets.”

On the hither side of the white ribbon dividing off the seats reserved for the families, Beaufort sat tall and red-faced, scrutinising the women with his arrogant stare. Beside him his wife glittered, all silvery chinchilla and violets; and on the far side of the ribbon Lawrence Lefferts’s sleekly brushed head seemed to mount guard over the invisible deity of Good Form who presided over the ceremony. Archer wondered how many flaws Lefferts’s keen eyes would discover in the ritual of his divinity—and then, with a start as sharp as tasting something unexpectedly bitter, he remembered that he too had once thought such questions important.

The things that had filled his days now seemed like a nursery parody of life, or like the wrangles of mediaeval schoolmen over metaphysical terms nobody had ever understood. Only yesterday the last hours before the wedding had been darkened by a stormy discussion of whether the wedding presents should be “shown.” It seemed inconceivable to him now that grown people could work themselves into agitation over such trifles, and that the question should have been settled—negatively—by Mrs. Welland’s indignant tears and declaration: “I should as soon turn the reporters loose in my house.” The horror of publicity in her voice had been as real as if she were defending a child from contagion.

Yet there had been a time when Archer had held definite and rather aggressive opinions on all such problems; when everything concerning the manners and customs of his little tribe had seemed to him freighted with world-wide significance. “And all the while, I suppose,” he thought, “real people were living somewhere, and real things happening to them…”

“There they come!” breathed the best man, excited.

But Archer knew better. The cautious opening of the church door meant only that Mr. Brown of the livery-stable, gowned in black in his intermittent character of sexton, was taking a preliminary survey before marshalling his forces. The door was softly shut again. Then, after another interval, it swung majestically open and a murmur ran through the church: “The family!”

Mrs. Welland came first on the arm of her eldest son. Her large pink face was appropriately solemn, and her plum-coloured satin with pale blue side-panels, and blue ostrich plumes in a small satin bonnet, met with general approval. But before she had settled herself with a stately rustle in the pew opposite Mrs. Archer, the spectators were craning their necks to see who followed.

Wild rumours had been abroad the day before that Mrs. Manson Mingott, in spite of her physical disabilities, meant to be present. The idea suited her sporting character so well that bets had run high at the clubs on whether she could walk up the nave and squeeze into a seat. It was known she had sent her own carpenter to consider whether the end panel of the front pew could be taken down and the space between the seat and the front measured. The result had discouraged the plan. For one anxious day her family had watched her dally with the idea of being wheeled up the nave in her enormous Bath chair and sitting enthroned at the foot of the chancel. The very thought of that exposure was so painful to her relations that they would have covered with gold the ingenious person who discovered, suddenly and providentially, that the chair was too wide to pass between the iron uprights of the awning that ran from the church door to the curb.

To do away with the awning—and thus reveal the bride to the mob of dressmakers and newspaper reporters outside, fighting to get near the joints of the canvas—exceeded even old Catherine’s courage, though for a moment she had weighed it. “Why, they might take a photograph of my child and _put it in the papers_!” Mrs. Welland had exclaimed when her mother’s last plan was hinted, and from that unthinkable indecency the clan recoiled as one.

The ancestress had been forced to give in. Her concession, however, was bought by the promise that the wedding-breakfast would take place under her roof—though, as the Washington Square connection said, with the Wellands’ house in easy reach it was hard to have to bargain with Brown for a special drive to the other end of nowhere.

Though these transactions had been widely reported by the Jacksons, a sporting minority still clung to the belief that old Catherine would appear in church, and there was a distinct lowering of the temperature when she proved to have been replaced by her daughter-in-law.

Mrs. Lovell Mingott entered with the high colour and glassy stare induced in ladies of her age and habit by the effort of getting into a new dress. Once the disappointment of old Catherine’s absence had subsided, it was agreed that the black Chantilly over lilac satin, with a bonnet of Parma violets, formed the happiest possible contrast to Mrs. Welland’s blue and plum. Far different was the impression produced by the gaunt, mincing lady who followed on Mr. Mingott’s arm, in a wild dishevelment of stripes and fringes and floating scarves.

As this last apparition glided into view Archer’s heart contracted and stopped.

He had taken it for granted that the Marchioness Manson was still in Washington, where she had gone some four weeks earlier with her niece, Madame Olenska. It was generally understood that their abrupt departure was due to Madame Olenska’s desire to remove her aunt from the baleful eloquence of Dr. Agathon Carver, who had nearly succeeded in enlisting her as a recruit for the Valley of Love. In the circumstances no one had expected either lady to return for the wedding.

For a moment Archer stood with his eyes fixed on Medora’s fantastic figure, straining to see who came behind her. But the little procession was at an end. The lesser members of the family had taken their seats, and the eight tall ushers—gathering together like birds preparing for a migratory manoeuvre—were already slipping through the side doors into the lobby.

“Newland—I say: _she’s here!_” the best man whispered.

Archer roused himself with a start. A long time seemed to have passed since his heart had stopped, for the white and rosy procession was already halfway up the nave. The Bishop, the Rector, and two white-winged assistants hovered about the flower-banked altar. The first chords of the Spohr symphony fell like pale petals before the bride.

Archer opened his eyes—had they really been shut?—and felt his heart resume its usual task, as if it had been absent on some other errand. The music, the scent of the lilies on the altar, the vision of tulle and orange-blossoms drifting nearer, the sudden convulsion of happy sobs on Mrs. Archer’s face, the low benedictory murmur of the Rector’s voice, the ordered evolutions of eight pink bridesmaids and eight black ushers: all these sights and sounds and sensations—so familiar, and yet so unutterably strange and meaningless in his new relation to them—were confusedly mingled in his brain.

“My God,” he thought, “_have_ I got the ring?” and again he made the bridegroom’s convulsive gesture.

Then May was beside him. Such radiance streamed from her that it sent a faint warmth through his numbness. He straightened himself and smiled into her eyes.

“Dearly beloved, we are gathered together here,” the Rector began.

The ring was on her hand. The Bishop’s benediction had been given. The bridesmaids poised to resume their places. The organ showed preliminary symptoms of breaking out into the Mendelssohn March, without which no newly-wedded couple had ever emerged upon New York.

“Your arm—I say, give her your arm!” young Newland hissed.

Once more Archer became aware of having been adrift far off in the unknown. What had sent him there? Perhaps the glimpse among the anonymous spectators in the transept of a dark coil of hair beneath a hat which, a moment later, revealed itself to belong to an unknown lady with a long nose, so laughably unlike the person whose image it had evoked that he asked himself if he were becoming subject to hallucinations.

Now he and his wife paced slowly down the nave, borne forward on the light ripples of Mendelssohn, the spring day beckoning through the wide-open doors. At the far end of the canvas tunnel Mrs. Welland’s chestnuts, with big white favours on their frontlets, curvetted and showed off. The footman—who wore a still bigger white favour on his lapel—wrapped May’s white cloak about her, and Archer jumped into the brougham at her side.

She turned to him with a triumphant smile, and their hands clasped under her veil.

“Darling!” Archer said—and suddenly the same black abyss yawned before him. He felt himself sinking deeper and deeper, while his voice rambled on smoothly and cheerfully, as if it belonged to someone else: “Yes, of course I thought I’d lost the ring; no wedding would be complete if the poor devil of a bridegroom didn’t go through that. But you _did_ keep me waiting, you know! I had time to think of every horror that might possibly happen.”

She surprised him by turning, in full Fifth Avenue, and flinging her arms about his neck. Under the veil her breath was warm and young; he caught the faintest trace of orange-blossom and violets, and something else—clean linen, perhaps, the smell of a girl’s gloves new from tissue-paper.

“But none ever can happen now, can it, Newland, as long as we two are together?”

Every detail of the day had been so carefully thought out that, after the wedding-breakfast, the young couple had ample time to put on their travelling clothes, descend the wide Mingott stairs between laughing bridesmaids and weeping parents, and get into the brougham under the traditional shower of rice and satin slippers. There was still half an hour left to drive to the station, buy the last weeklies at the bookstall with the air of seasoned travellers, and settle in the reserved compartment where May’s maid had already placed her dove-coloured travelling cloak and the glaringly new London dressing-bag.

The old du Lac aunts at Rhinebeck had put their house at the disposal of the bridal couple, with a readiness inspired by the prospect of spending a week in New York with Mrs. Archer. Archer, glad to escape the usual “bridal suite” in a Philadelphia or Baltimore hotel—rooms that smelled of gas and new varnish and someone else’s privacy—accepted with equal alacrity. May was enchanted by the idea of going to the country, and childishly amused by the vain efforts of the eight bridesmaids to discover where their mysterious retreat lay. It was thought “very English” to have a country-house lent; the fact gave a last touch of distinction to what was generally conceded to be the most brilliant wedding of the year. But where the house was no one was permitted to know, except the parents of bride and groom; and when taxed with the knowledge they pursed their lips and said mysteriously: “Ah, they didn’t tell us”—which was manifestly true, since there was no need to.

Once settled in the compartment, and once the train, shaking off the endless wooden suburbs, pushed out into the pale spring landscape, talk became easier than Archer had expected. May was still, in look and tone, the simple girl of yesterday. She was eager to compare notes with him about the wedding’s incidents and discussed them as impartially as a bridesmaid reciting them to an usher. At first Archer imagined the detachment a disguise for inward tremor; but her clear eyes showed only tranquil unawareness. Alone for the first time with her husband, she seemed to feel only that she was alone at last with her charming comrade. There was no one she liked as much, no one she trusted so completely; and the culminating lark of this whole delightful adventure of engagement and marriage was to be off with him on a journey like a grown-up person—like a “married woman,” in fact.

It was wonderful that—as he had learned in the Mission garden at St. Augustine—such depths of feeling could coexist with such absence of imagination. He remembered how, even then, she had surprised him by dropping back into inexpressive girlishness as soon as her conscience had been eased of its burden. He saw she would probably go through life dealing as best she could with each experience as it came, never anticipating any by so much as a stolen glance.

Perhaps that faculty of unawareness was what gave her eyes their transparency, and her face the look of representing a type rather than a person—chosen to pose for a civic virtue or a Greek goddess. The blood that ran so near her fair skin might have been a preserving fluid rather than a ravaging element; and yet the look of indestructible youth made her seem neither hard nor dull, only primitive and pure.

In the thick of this meditation he found himself looking at her with the startled gaze of a stranger. To hide it he plunged into reminiscences of the wedding-breakfast and of Granny Mingott’s immense triumphant pervasion of it. May’s face brightened; she settled down to frank enjoyment of the subject, as if it were a menu recited afterward with pleasure.

“I was surprised, though—weren’t you?—that aunt Medora came after all,” she said. “Ellen wrote that they were neither of them well enough to take the journey; I do wish it had been she who had recovered! Did you see the exquisite old lace she sent me?”

He had known the moment must come sooner or later; yet he had half imagined that by sheer force of will he could hold it at bay.

“Yes—I—no: yes, it was beautiful,” he said, looking at her blindly, and wondering if, whenever he heard those two syllables, his carefully built world would tumble like a house of cards.

“Aren’t you tired? It will be good to have some tea when we arrive—I’m sure the aunts have got everything beautifully ready,” he rattled on, taking her hand.

Her mind flew instantly, with sincere delight, to the Baltimore silver service the Beauforts had sent—tea and coffee pieces that glittered with newness—how it “went” so perfectly with uncle Lovell Mingott’s trays and side-dishes. In her voice there was the tidy pleasure of things matching, of a table set correctly, of an evening predicted by the right objects. Archer listened, and somewhere in him a quieter sense noted how their world translated feeling into arrangement: affection into gifts, approval into service, intimacy into the correct height of a teapot.

In the spring twilight the train stopped at Rhinebeck. They walked along the platform to the waiting carriage.

“Ah, how awfully kind of the van der Luydens—they’ve sent their man over from Skuytercliff to meet us,” Archer exclaimed, as a sedate person out of livery approached and relieved the maid of her bags.

“I’m extremely sorry, sir,” the emissary began, with the apologetic gravity of someone bearing news of a death in the pantry. “A little accident has occurred at the Miss du Lacs’: a leak in the water-tank. It happened yesterday, and Mr. van der Luyden, who heard of it this morning, sent a housemaid up by the early train to get the Patroon’s house ready. It will be quite comfortable, I think you’ll find, sir; and the Miss du Lacs have sent their cook over, so that it will be exactly the same as if you’d been at Rhinebeck.”

Archer stared so blankly the man repeated, even more apologetically: “It’ll be exactly the same, sir, I do assure you—”

May’s eager voice broke in, covering the embarrassed silence. “The same as Rhinebeck? The Patroon’s house? But it will be a hundred thousand times better—won’t it, Newland? It’s too dear and kind of Mr. van der Luyden to have thought of it.”

And as they drove off, the maid perched beside the coachman and the shining bridal bags set before the young couple like trophies, she went on, excited, the words tumbling as if the very carriage wheels had quickened her blood.

“Only fancy—I’ve never been inside it—have you? The van der Luydens show it to so few people. But they opened it for Ellen, it seems, and she told me what a darling little place it was: she says it’s the only house she’s seen in America that she could imagine being perfectly happy in.”

“Well—that’s what we’re going to be, isn’t it?” Archer cried gaily.

She answered with her boyish smile, trusting as one trusts a familiar hymn: “Ah, it’s just our luck beginning—the wonderful luck we’re always going to have together!”
