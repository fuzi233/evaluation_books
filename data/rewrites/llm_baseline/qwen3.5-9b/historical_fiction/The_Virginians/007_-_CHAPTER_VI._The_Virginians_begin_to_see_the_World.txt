Following the departure of her discredited spiritual adviser, Madam Esmond and her son appeared to reach a fragile reconciliation. Yet, though George never voiced the grievance against his mother, the weight of their estrangement pressed heavily upon the boy's mind. Soon after these domestic upheavals, he fell violently ill. During his feverish delirium, his mind wandered, and he would shriek in terror, "Broken! Broken! It never, never can be mended!" His mother sat vigil beside his tossing form on the midnight bed, her heart heavy with silent dread. Her medical skills proved futile; the malady worsened despite the array of nostrums she drew from her closet and administered to her household.

Desperate, she faced further humiliation. One day, she rode through the deep snow on her pony to the humble dwelling of Mr. Dempster, imploring him to aid her suffering child. "I shall bury my resentment, madam," he promised gently, echoing her own past sacrifice of pride. "Please God, I may be time enough to help my dear young pupil!" He gathered his lancets and medicines, summoned his sole Negro servant, shuttered his lonely hut, and hastened back to Castlewood. For several days, it seemed inevitable that poor Harry would inherit the estate, but Mr. Dempster's skill triumphed. The fever broke, the intermittent attacks subsided, and George was restored to near-health.

Doctors recommended a change of air, perhaps even a voyage to England, but the widow, having quarreled with her children's relatives there, confessed with contrition that she had acted too hastily. Instead, a journey north and east was decided upon. The two young gentlemen, accompanied by Mr. Dempster as their tutor and two servants, sailed to New York and ascended the magnificent Hudson River to Albany. There, they were welcomed by the province's leading gentry before traveling into the French territories, where their letters of introduction secured them warm hospitality among the French nobility.

Harry threw himself into the wilds, camping with the Indigenous peoples, trading furs, and hunting bears. George, however, possessed no taste for field sports and his constitution remained delicate. Consequently, he became a particular favorite with the French ladies, who rarely encountered young Englishmen fluent in the language. George mastered the *bel air* so thoroughly that he could pass for a Frenchman. He danced the minuet with elegance, learned the latest imported French airs, and played them beautifully on his violin. Though his voice had recently broken, shifting from treble to bass—much to the envy of Harry, who was away on a hunt—he even engaged in an affair of honor with a young ensign of the Regiment of Auvergne, the Chevalier de la Jabotière. George "pinked" the ensign in the shoulder, swearing an eternal friendship thereafter. Madame de Mouchy, the Superintendent's wife, declared that any mother was blessed to have such a son and penned a glowing letter to Madam Esmond regarding George's conduct.

Mr. Whitfield, had he known of the widow's elation at her son's prowess, might not have been entirely pleased. When the lads returned home after ten delightful months, their mother marveled at their growth and refinement. George had matured so significantly that he nearly equaled his younger brother in stature. The boys were often indistinguishable, save for their hair: George wore his raven locks loose, while Harry tied his lighter tresses with a ribbon, finding the powdered wig ceremony too cumbersome for country life.

Those familiar with the earlier chapters know that Mr. George Esmond was of a jealous and suspicious nature, yet generous, gentle, and incapable of deceit. Though too magnanimous to seek revenge, he was almost unable to forgive injuries. He departed home with no goodwill toward an honorable gentleman whose name would later become legendary: George Washington. Upon his return, George held no softer opinion of his mother's and grandfather's friend. Washington, though barely of age, carried the demeanor of a man decades older. Possessing an extraordinary simplicity and gravity, he had managed his mother's and family's affairs since childhood, earning the trust of friends and the local gentry more readily than men twice his senior.

Mrs. Mountain, Madam Esmond's devoted companion and a woman of a jealous, match-making disposition, frequently corresponded with the boys during their travels. She believed everyone sought to marry everyone else. Scarcely an unmarried man visited Castlewood without Mrs. Mountain imagining he eyed the mistress of the mansion. She was convinced the detested Mr. Ward courted the widow, whom she suspected reciprocated. Regarding Mr. Washington, she noted his desire for marriage and assumed a shrewd young gentleman would seek a wealthy bride. Ignoring the fifteen-year age gap between the Major and Madam Esmond, she reasoned that such unions were common in their family; Lady Mountair's mother had married a colonel decades her senior, only to henpeck him until death. Mrs. Mountain, a petite woman barely five feet tall even in high heels, contrasted herself with Mr. Washington, a towering six-foot-two giant. "Great tall men always marry little chits of women," she deduced logically. Therefore, Mr. Washington must be courting the widow.

She shared these sagacious theories with her "boy," George, who begged her, for heaven's sake, to silence her. She claimed she could control her tongue but not her eyes, recounting a hundred incidents that confirmed her suspicions. Had she whispered these doubts to Harry? George asked sternly. No. "George is my boy; Harry is his mother's boy," she insisted. "Besides, if I spoke to him, he'd tell your mother instantly. Poor Harry cannot keep a secret, and then there would be a dreadful quarrel between Madam and me!"

"I beg you to keep this quiet, Mountain," George commanded with dignity. "Or you and I shall quarrel. You must not mention such absurd suspicions to me or anyone else."

Absurd? Why absurd? Mr. Washington was constantly in the widow's company. His name was perpetually on her lips; she cited his virtues as models for her sons, consulted him on every estate decision, and never bought a horse or sold a barrel of tobacco without his approval. At Castlewood stood a room officially designated "Mr. Washington's Room." "He actually leaves his clothes and portmanteau there when he departs," Mrs. Mountain lamented. "One day will come when he won't go away!"

Meanwhile, George adopted a frigid courtesy toward his mother's favorite, which chafed the honest gentleman, though he dared not remonstrate. Occasionally, George would offer a stinging sarcasm, breaking through barriers as easily as he tore through brambles on hunting expeditions with Harry. While George retreated to his bookroom to study mathematics, French, and Latin, sulking in increasing isolation, Harry roamed the woods with sporting companions—acquaintances, alas, not always as reputable as Mr. Washington.

When Mr. Washington finally visited Castlewood, his peculiar tenderness toward the mistress and the special cordiality she displayed nearly drove George Warrington to open rupture. However, this visit was merely a farewell. Major Washington was embarking on a perilous journey to the western Virginia frontier and beyond. The French had begun incursions into British territory, alarming governments in Virginia and Pennsylvania. Settlers had been driven from their homes by armed French forces, prompting British governors to halt these invasions. Under the convenient legal maxim that coastal possession granted rights to inland territory stretching to the Pacific, the British charters defined colonies only north-to-south, leaving them open to the west. The French, possessing colonies to the north and south, aimed to connect them via the Mississippi and St. Lawrence, threatening the British possessions.

Though peace was signed between Britain and France in 1748, the colonial dispute remained unresolved, waiting for a moment when one power could enforce its claims. By 1753, the issue erupted on the Ohio River, where British and French settlers collided. Beyond these Europeans were the Native Americans and land proprietors, yet the diplomats of St. James's and Versailles treated the conflict as purely European, ignoring the indigenous claimants while utilizing their tomahawks as tools for whichever side served their interests.

The Ohio Company, holding grants from Virginia along the river, found their settlements invaded by French detachments who forcibly ejected the Britons. Seeking protection, they turned to Mr. Dinwiddie, Lieutenant-Governor of Virginia, who resolved to send an ambassador to demand the French cease hostilities. Young Mr. Washington eagerly seized this opportunity for distinction, volunteering to leave his home and rural pursuits to deliver the Governor's message to the French commander at Fort Le Boeuf. Accompanied by a guide, an interpreter, and a few attendants, the intrepid envoy traveled from Williamsburg to Lake Erie in the autumn of 1753. The French commander's reply was blunt: his orders were to hold the post and drive all Englishmen away. The French openly declared their intent to possess the Ohio.

Armed with this harsh rejection, the messenger from Virginia retraced his steps through danger and hardship, navigating lonely forests and frozen rivers by compass, camping by forest fires in the snow. Harry Warrington cursed his bad fortune, having been absent at a cockfight when he might have witnessed such noble sport. Upon his return, conducting the expedition with heroic energy and simplicity, Major Washington became even more beloved by the lady of Castlewood. She hailed him as a model for her sons.
