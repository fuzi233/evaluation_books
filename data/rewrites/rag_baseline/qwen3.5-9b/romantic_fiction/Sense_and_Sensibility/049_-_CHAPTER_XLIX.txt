Edward was free. The circumstances surrounding his release from the Ferrars household might seem inexplicable to the Dashwood family, yet the trajectory of his next move was inevitable. Having survived the trauma of one imprudent engagement contracted without maternal approval—a mistake he had endured for four years—he could not possibly resist the gravitational pull toward another. His mission to Barton was singular and direct: to propose to Elinor. Given his prior experience with such declarations, it was peculiar that he should feel so unnerved, requiring both fresh air and renewed courage to execute the task.

Yet, the speed with which he forged his resolve, the precision of his approach, and the reception he garnered require no detailed chronicle. By the time the family gathered at the table at four o'clock, nearly three hours after his arrival, Edward had secured Elinor's hand, obtained his mother's reluctant consent, and emerged not merely as a rhapsodic lover, but as a man restored to the pinnacle of human felicity. His situation was uniquely triumphant. He possessed the double victory of accepted love and liberation from a previous entanglement that had long constituted his misery. He had shed a woman he no longer loved, elevating himself instantly to a security he had once deemed impossible, transforming a state of despair into one of profound contentment. The shift was absolute: from the depths of suffering to the heights of joy, a metamorphosis evident in a cheerfulness so genuine and overflowing that his friends had never witnessed it before.

With his heart now fully open to Elinor, he confessed every weakness and error, treating his youthful infatuation with Lucy Steele with the philosophical detachment of a man twenty-four years old.

"It was a foolish, idle inclination," he admitted, "born of ignorance and idleness. Had my mother provided me with an active profession when I was removed from Mr. Pratt's care at eighteen, I am certain this would never have occurred. Although I departed Longstaple with what I then considered an unconquerable passion for his niece, any occupation, any object demanding my time and keeping me distant from her for mere months, would have allowed me to outgrow the fantasy. Mixing more freely with the world, as I surely would have done, would have dissolved the attachment quickly. Instead, I returned home to complete idleness. For the first twelve months following, I lacked even the nominal status of a university student, having not entered Oxford until I was nineteen. With nothing to occupy me but the delusion of love, and lacking comfort at home, companionship in my brother, or the ability to tolerate new acquaintances, it was natural to retreat to Longstaple, where I felt at home and guaranteed a welcome. Consequently, from eighteen to nineteen, I spent the majority of my time there. Lucy appeared the epitome of amiability and obligation. She was pretty—at least, I perceived her so *then*—and having seen so little of other women, I possessed no basis for comparison and thus failed to discern any defects. Considering the totality of factors, I trust that while our engagement was foolish and has since proven erroneous in every aspect, it was not, at the time, an unnatural or inexcusable act of folly."

The transformation wrought in the minds and hearts of the Dashwoods over those few hours was so profound it promised them all a sleepless night of celebration. Mrs. Dashwood, overwhelmed by joy, struggled to find words sufficient to express her love for Edward or her praise for Elinor. She wrestled with the paradox of thanking Edward for his release without wounding his sensibilities, and balancing the desire for private conversation between the couple with her own wish to observe their union.

Marianne could articulate her happiness only through tears. Regret and comparison inevitably surfaced; her joy, though sincere and rooted in her devotion to her sister, was of a nature that robbed her of spirit and language.

But Elinor—how are her feelings to be described? From the moment she learned of Lucy's marriage and Edward's freedom, until the instant he validated the hopes that had risen so swiftly, she oscillated through every emotion save tranquility. Yet, once the initial shock subsided, and she realized that every doubt and anxiety had vanished, comparing her current situation to the recent nightmare, seeing him honorably freed from his former bond and instantly turning his attentions to her with a tenderness and constancy she had always suspected, she was overcome by her own felicity. Human minds are naturally adaptable to improvement, but it took several hours to settle her spirits and grant her heart a degree of peace.

Edward intended to remain at the cottage for at least a week. Whatever other obligations awaited him, it was impossible to allocate less than seven days to the enjoyment of Elinor's company, insufficient to exhaust the topics of the past, present, and future. While a few hours of relentless discourse might cover more ground between rational strangers, lovers operate differently. Between them, no subject is truly concluded, no communication fully made, until it has been articulated at least twenty times over.

Lucy's marriage, a source of ceaseless and reasonable wonder for everyone, became one of the earliest topics of discussion between the lovers. Elinor's intimate knowledge of both parties rendered the event, in her view, one of the most extraordinary and inexplicable occurrences she had ever encountered. How could they have been united? By what magnetic force was Robert drawn to marry a girl whose beauty he had previously dismissed without admiration? A girl already betrothed to his brother, and whose existence had caused that brother to be rejected by his own family? It was beyond her comprehension. To her heart, it was delightful; to her imagination, absurd; but to her reason, it was an unsolvable puzzle.

Edward could only offer a tentative explanation: perhaps, upon their accidental first meeting, the vanity of one had been so inflamed by the flattery of the other that it gradually led to the rest. Elinor recalled what Robert had confided to her in Harley Street regarding his belief that his mediation, had it been applied earlier, might have altered the outcome. She relayed this to Edward.

"That was precisely like Robert," he observed immediately. "And that notion might well have occupied his mind when the acquaintance between them first began. Perhaps Lucy initially sought only to procure his good offices in my favor. Other designs undoubtedly arose later."

However, he was equally baffled as Elinor regarding the duration of their secret correspondence. At Oxford, where he had resided voluntarily since leaving London, he had no channel for news of her except her own letters, which remained as frequent and affectionate as ever. No suspicion had ever prepared him for the revelation. When the letter finally burst upon him, he believed he had been half-stupified for some time, caught between wonder, horror, and the sheer joy of such deliverance. He handed the letter to Elinor.

"DEAR SIR, 'Being very sure I have long lost your affections, I have thought myself at liberty to bestow my own on another, and have no doubt of being as happy with him as I once used to think I might be with you; but I scorn to accept a hand while the heart was another's. Sincerely wish you happy in your choice, and it shall not be my fault if we are not always good friends, as our near relationship now makes proper. I can safely say I owe you no ill-will, and am sure you will be too generous to do us any ill offices. Your brother has gained my affections entirely, and as we could not live without one another, we are just returned from the altar, and are now on our way to Dawlish for a few weeks, which place your dear brother has great curiosity to see, but thought I would first trouble you with these few lines, and shall always remain,

'Your sincere well-wisher, friend, and sister, 'LUCY FERRARS.'

'I have burnt all your letters, and will return your picture the first opportunity. Please to destroy my scrawls—but the ring with my hair you are very welcome to keep.'"

Elinor read the missive and returned it without comment.

"I will not ask your opinion of it as a composition," Edward remarked. "For worlds, I would not have had a letter of hers seen by *you* in former days. In a sister, it is bad enough, but in a wife! How I have blushed over the pages of her writing! And I believe I may say that since the first half-year of our foolish business, this is the only letter I ever received from her wherein the substance made me any amends for the defect of the style."

"However it may have come about," Elinor said after a pause, "they are certainly married. And your mother has brought upon herself a most appropriate punishment. The independence she settled on Robert, born of resentment against you, has placed it in his power to make his own choice; and she has actually bribed one son with a thousand-a-year to perform the very deed she disinherited the other for attempting. She will hardly be less hurt, I suppose, by Robert's marrying Lucy than she would have been by your marrying her."

"She will be more hurt," Edward countered. "Robert was always her favorite. She will be more devastated, and on the same principle, she will forgive him much sooner."

He had departed Oxford within forty-eight hours of receiving Lucy's letter, driven solely by the urge to reach Barton via the nearest road, leaving him no leisure to formulate a broader plan of action.
