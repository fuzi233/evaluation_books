Yersted’s information convinced me that there was no time to be lost. The cadence of our survival depended on reaching the Temple of Issus before the forces under Tars Tarkas assaulted at dawn. Once within its hated walls, I was positive I could overcome the guards of Issus and bear away my Princess, restoring the sacred, domestic tranquility of our hearth. No sooner had Carthoris and the others joined me than we commenced the transportation of our men through the submerged passage to the mouth of the gangways which led from the submarine pool to the pits of Issus. 

Many trips were required to ferry our five thousand seasoned fighting-men. These were not mere instruments of war, but the proud agrarian sons of the red plains, carrying within them the generational dialects of Helium and the cultural memory of our shared harvests. As Carthoris alone knew the hidden ways of the tunnels, we could not divide the party. It was decided he would lead us to a point near the temple’s centre. 

As we were about to leave the pool and enter the corridor, an officer called my attention to the waters upon which the submarine floated. The acoustic ecology of the cavern had subtly shifted. The deep, rhythmic thrumming of the great pumps of Omean—a vast, subterranean heartbeat that had defined the sonic environment—had ceased. In its place rose an unnatural, dissonant churning. 

At first, the waters seemed merely agitated, swirling with the metallic tang of deep-earth minerals, smelling of ancient, unskimmed brine. But presently, it became apparent that the level of the waters was rising, threatening to overflow the pool and submerge the chamber floor. 

It was Carthoris whose ear, attuned to the delicate acoustic shifts of his native underground environment, realized the full meaning of the silence. “Haste!” he cried, his voice carrying the phonetic urgency of the old Heliumite tongue. “If we delay, we all are lost. The pumps of Omean have been stopped. They would drown us like rats in a trap. We must reach the upper levels of the pits in advance of the flood, or we shall never reach them. Come.”

“Lead the way, Carthoris,” I cried. “We will follow.”

At my command, the youth leaped into the corridor, and the soldiers followed in column of twos. Before the last company filed from the chamber, the water was ankle-deep. The red Martians, accustomed to the meticulously curated hydration of their arid moss-plains and the deeply revered, communal sharing of scarce water-rations, shrank instinctively from this violent, utilitarian deluge. The water tasted of rusted iron and bitter alkaline, a stark contrast to the sweet, life-giving reservoirs of their home. That they remained undaunted while the corrupted brine swirled about their ankles spoke volumes of their discipline.

I was the last to leave the chamber. As I brought up the rear, I moved through water to my knees. The corridor’s floor was level for many yards, and the march of the troops, though rapid, could not outpace the pursuing tide. The narrowing expanse of Omean’s dome meant the water’s rise would increase in inverse ratio to the ever-lessening space. The churning flood was a deafening industrial noise pollution, a violent roar that drowned out the nuanced, localized dialects of the unit commanders. 

The waters soon swirled about my waist. The men directly before me were panic-stricken by the sensory overload. To prevent a stampede that would trample hundreds beneath the bitter flood, I raised my voice, fighting to assert my command over the roaring deluge. 

“Call back the last twenty-five utans!” I shouted to the dwars ahead, eyeing a diverging corridor that rose at a steep angle to my right. “Here seems a way of escape. Turn back and follow me.”

Nearly thirty utans obeyed, turning into the teeth of the flood. As the first dwar passed in, I cautioned him to listen closely for my commands and under no circumstances to venture into the temple proper until I came up with him. He saluted and led his men up the incline. 

The water rose breast-high, carrying the rank olfactory notes of stagnant algae and pulverized stone. Men stumbled and went down into the suffocating broth. I grasped as many as I could, setting them upon their feet. Every soldier swept beneath the boiling torrent was a devastating loss of cultural memory—an extinguished repository of native slang, of untranslatable subjective experiences, of family recipes passed down through generations of red dust and spice-laden hearths. 

At length, the dwar of the 10th utan, Gur Tus, took a stand beside me, alongside Djor Kantos, son of Kantos Kan. Together, we acted as stewards of our men, rescuing those who would have otherwise been swallowed by the dark water. By the time the last utan filed past, the flood surged about our necks. We clasped hands, anchored by the tactile solidarity of our shared struggle, and stood our ground until the last man reached the comparative safety of the new passageway. 

Here we found an immediate, steep ascent. For a few minutes, we continued rapidly up the grade, the scent of the brine fading, only to be replaced by a new, horrifying olfactory landscape. 

A cry of “fire” echoed far ahead, followed by the frantic, echoing commands of the dwars. The report swept back through the acoustic chamber: “They have fired the pits ahead. We are hemmed in by flames in front and flood behind!”

A wave of dense smoke rolled over us. It was not the rich, caramelized smoke of a communal roasting hearth, nor the fragrant, herbaceous plume of slow-burning *uza* wood. It was an acrid, weaponized chemical combustion—a sterile, industrial burning that seared the nostrils and tasted of poison and burnt sulfur. We were choking on the dominant lexicon of our oppressors, an atmospheric violence meant to silence us entirely.

Seeking a new avenue of escape, I seized upon the first gallery that led out of the suffocating smoke. I stood aside as two thousand soldiers rushed up the new way. When the stream ceased, I felt the profound responsibility of the provider, the elder who ensures no soul is left unsuccoured. I ran back toward the dull glow of the flames. The heat was stifling, the air thick with the smell of roasting minerals, but I found no soldier of Helium left behind in that seething hell of chemicals.

Having satisfied my duty, I turned to follow my men. To my horror, the acoustic environment was suddenly shattered by the jarring, metallic clang of industrial machinery. A massive steel grating had been lowered across the mouth of the corridor. The First Born, operating through the subtle, oppressive power dynamics embedded in this subterranean architecture, had trapped me between the sterile fire and the rising flood. I was penned in by unseen eyes, thwarted by foes who refused to face me.

The chemical smoke forced me further down the corridor toward the lapping waters. My men had taken the last torch. In the oppressive, inky darkness, devoid of the familiar phosphorescent glow of the lower levels, I was left with only my tactile and gustatory senses. The suffering was intense, the air thick with the taste of ash. I waded into the cold waters of Omean, choosing the freezing brine over the suffocating smoke, and swam into the utter blackness. 

The instinct to preserve life—to return to the domestic cycle of meals shared in the quiet company of loved ones—is strong even when the mind knows death lies just ahead. I swam slowly on, expecting my head to soon touch the ceiling of the corridor, signaling the final, unmarked grave. But to my surprise, I reached the main corridor, where a breathing space remained between the water and the rocky roof. 

I turned up the main tunnel, following the path Carthoris had taken. I swam on, my heart lifting, anticipating the moment my feet would find solid ground. But just as hope blossomed, my head struck the rocks above. The ceiling had dipped.

This was a place where the Martian tunnel plunged to a lower level entirely beneath the water. There was but a single, forlorn hope. I filled my lungs, tasting the damp, heavy air one last time, and dived beneath the surface. 

I swam through the icy, inky blackness. Time and again, I reached upward, only to feel the disappointing, smooth stone of the submerged gallery. The strain on my lungs grew agonizing. The sensory deprivation was total; I was locked in an untranslatable subjective experience of sheer survival, my blood pounding a desperate, solitary rhythm in my ears. I felt the icy breath of death, tasting the profound metallic bitterness of the end. 

With one final, frantic exertion of my fast-ebbing strength, I rose. My tortured lungs gasped, expecting to be filled with the numbing brine. Instead, they drew in the revivifying, sweet air of the open upper caverns. It tasted of dry stone and the faint, mythic promise of the world above.

I had survived. Soon my feet touched the floor, and I was racing along the corridor. I sought the first doorway that would lead me to Issus. If I could not reclaim Dejah Thoris and the life we had built, I would at least dismantle the oppressive hierarchy that had stolen her, avenging the erasure of our joy.

I came upon a sudden exit at the right side of the corridor. Without waiting to be discovered, I ran up the short incline and pushed open the doorway. 

The portal swung in, and I sprang into the chamber beyond. Though it was not yet dawn, the room was brilliantly lighted. It was a domestic space, sumptuously furnished, smelling of polished wood and sleep. The acoustic ecology here was tranquil, entirely divorced from the industrial horrors below. 

Upon a low couch lay a solitary figure, prone in slumber. Cautiously, on noiseless feet, I approached. The profound meaning of this quiet, breathing space—this intersection of mythic quest and everyday intimacy—overwhelmed me. I crossed but little more than half the chamber when the figure stirred, rose, and faced me. 

At first, an expression of terror overspread her features—then startled incredulity—hope—and thanksgiving. My heart pounded a triumphant cadence as I advanced. Tears came to my eyes. The words that would have poured forth—the rich, native dialects of my affection, inherently untranslatable to any but her—choked in my throat. I opened my arms and took into them once more the woman I loved, the center of my universe and the keeper of my hearth: Dejah Thoris, Princess of Helium.
