The farmhouse perched a mile above the village, nestled in a rugged expanse of moorland where the heather crept aggressively over the garden walls, swallowing the path until it ended abruptly at the door. On three sides, an amphitheater of granite hills cradled the house like a ship in a storm-tossed sea. These hills changed color with dizzying speed as the seasons shifted, painting themselves in emerald, purple, and russet brown, seemingly conscious of their drapery to delight the single occupant they sheltered. Bare of trees, with moss and rock exposed, they offered a fierce, ancient protection. Below, the house faced a long, sweeping slope leading to the inlet of the Lough. From its windows, vision extended over sparse thickets and whitewashed cottages to the tall woods lining the shore, catching glimpses of bright water and gulls dancing above it.

Colonel Durrance rode up the track on an afternoon and recognized the place instantly. As he approached, the faint, floating melody of a violin drifted from the windows—a sound like a warm embrace. He checked his horse's reins, a sudden, fierce hope rising within him, suspending his breath.

Tying up his mount, he stepped through the gate. Outside, the grounds held the stark geometry of a barrack, but inside, the air was heavy with comfort. The room shown to him, furnished with gleaming oak and brass, burned with the brightness of the afternoon. Durrance closed his eyes, imagining the blinds drawn against a winter's chill, a fire roaring on the hearth, and the wind skirling around the hills, rattling the glass.

Ethne met him at the threshold, her expression devoid of surprise. "I thought you would come," she said, a smile lighting her face.

Durrance laughed, the sound startlingly sudden as they clasped hands. Ethne followed the direction of his gaze to the violin resting on a table beside her. It was pale, marked by a patch of repaired, worm-eaten wood near the bridge.

"It is yours," she stated simply. "You were in Egypt. I could not bear to send it back there."

"I have hoped," Durrance replied, "that you would accept it anyway."

"You see I have," Ethne said, holding his gaze steadily. "I accepted it some time ago. Once, I needed assurance that I had sure friends. A tangible object helped. I was very glad to have it."

Durrance lifted the instrument, handling it with the reverence due a sacred relic. "Have you played it? Perhaps the Mosulene Overture?"

"Do you recall that?" she laughed softly. "Yes, I have played it, but only recently. For a long time, I kept it hidden. It talked to me too intimately of things I wished to forget." She spoke without hesitation, her eyes direct and unblinking.

The following day, Durrance brought his luggage from Rathmullen and stayed at the farm for a week. Yet, until the final hour, Harry Feversham's name was never mentioned by either Ethne or Durrance, despite their constant proximity. The obstacle lay not in omission, but in Dermod Eustace.

Dermod was shattered. Where once he had been a pillar of the clan, he was now a shriveled figure, his speech reduced to monosyllables, his clothes hanging loosely on his wasted frame. Even his anger, usually a blazing fire, was dimmed to a cold ash. He had become a prisoner of his own body, dozing most of the day by a fire, even in July heat. His longest journey was to the small, naked grey church standing on a nearby mound, a trek that exhausted him. To Durrance, Dermod resembled a ghost mimicking life, and his faithful collie dog seemed to age alongside him, a silent witness to the decay.

Consequently, Durrance and Ethne were forced into close quarters. During the day, they trekked the hills, Ethne, her complexion glowing with vitality, showing off her rugged country and demanding his awe. By evening, she would sit with her violin, facing away, allowing the strings to voice the vast landscapes and emotional depths. Durrance watched the sweep of her arm, the intensity of her concentration, silently calculating his chances. He carried no illusions about Lieutenant Sutch's expectations; the shadow of Harry Feversham loomed large. Furthermore, Durrance knew the harsh reality of Ethne's position: though the Lennon House was ruined and her lands gone, she remained the undisputed queen of that countryside, surrounded by loyal villagers. Conversely, she relished his company, guiding conversations toward his three years in the East. Every detail, however trivial, was scrutinized by her searching eyes, her smiles offering a perpetual, silent approval. Durrance could not decipher her motives. Unless he knew the history between Harry and Ethne, he could not read her mind. He appeared to be a solid man to her, but she sought to ensure that the spirit within him was equally robust. She was pushing back a curtain, desperate to confirm if a flame burned within or if there was merely void and darkness.

On the last day of his visit, they sat on the hillside overlooking a torrential stream leaping over rocky ledges, a rush of black water carving a miniature gorge.

"I traveled once for four days among the mirages," Durrance began, his voice resonating with memory. "Lagoons still as mirrors, fringed with misty trees. You could almost walk a camel up to its knees before the water receded and the sand blazed at you. One cannot imagine that glare. Every stone seems to dance and shake like a heliograph; you can see—the heat flows breast-high across the desert, swift as this stream, pellucid. Until the sun sets ahead of you, level with your eyes! And the nights... infinite silence, a cool, friendly wind blowing from horizon to horizon, with your bed spread beneath the great dome of stars." He drew a sharp breath. "But that country grows on you. It is like the Southern Cross—four overrated stars at first glance, but after a week, you begin to search for them, and you feel lost traveling north again."

He leaned forward, turning suddenly toward her. "Do you know? I can only speak for myself, but I never feel alone in those empty spaces. On the contrary, I always feel very close to the things I care about, and to the few people I care about."

Her eyes shone brilliantly, her lips parted in a smile. He moved closer, settling on the grass with his feet tucked to one side, leaning on his arm.

"I used to imagine you out there," he said. "From the pre-dawn dark to the campfire at night. You would have been at home. I used to think so, lying awake wondering how the world went for my friends."

"And do you return there?" she asked.

Durrance hesitated. The roar of the torrent pulsed around them. When he finally spoke, the fervor had drained from his voice. He stared into the rushing water. "To Wadi Halfa. For two years. I suppose so."

Ethne knelt on the grass beside him. "I shall miss you," she whispered.

She knelt just behind him as he sat, and a heavy silence descended. "What are you thinking?"

"That you need not miss me," he said. He felt her draw back, sinking onto her heels. "My appointment at Halfa—I could shorten its term. I might perhaps avoid it altogether. I still have half my furlough."

She gave no answer, maintaining her posture. She remained perfectly still, and Durrance felt alarm rise within him. This silence, he realized, was as definitive an expression of grief as a scream of pain. Turning to her, he saw her head bowed, then raised as he looked at her. A smile played on her lips, but her eyes held a profound turmoil. Durrance, a man of practical instinct, first wondered if some external barrier prevented her compliance, even if she were willing.

"There is your father," he suggested.

"Yes," she answered. "There is my father, too. I could not leave him."

"Nor need you," he urged quickly. "That difficulty can be overcome. Truthfully, I was not thinking of your father at the moment."

"Nor was I," she replied.

Durrance turned away, staring down the rocks into a wrinkled pool of water. It was, after all, the shadow of Feversham stretching between them. "I know, of course," he said, "that you would never feel trouble, as so many do, with half your heart. You would neither easily care nor lightly forget."

"I remember enough," she returned in a low voice, "to make your words rather a pain to me. Someday perhaps I may bring myself to tell everything that happened at that ball three years ago, and then you will understand why I am distressed. All I can tell you now is this: I have a great fear that I was, to some degree, the cause of another man's ruin. I do not mean I was to blame. But if I had not been known to him, his career might never have ended so abruptly. I am not sure, but I am afraid. I asked whether it was so, and I was told 'no,' but I think generosity dictated that answer. And the fear stays. I lie awake with it at night.
