Happy is the man, they said in Rome, who has two stout cables to haul himself up by: merit, and women. Gerard had only the first, and even that he used as a rope-ladder to climb back—always back—to one plain room in the North where Margaret’s hands moved about bread and linen, and where his name, spoken in her own homely tongue, meant more than any Latin flourish.

Yet Rome, that day, meant ink, parchment, and the hum of minds sharpening themselves on dead syllables as if on whetstones.

Fra Colonna—Francesco of Florence, Dominican by habit and pagan at heart—moved through the city as through a gallery. He loved antiquity the way a careful cook loves old sourdough: not as a relic, but as a living ferment that, once fed, lifts everything. He had wandered the East and come home freighted with manuscripts the way a merchant comes home with sacks of spice—Greek and Latin, Hebrew and Syriac, each language a different aroma rising when you break the seal. Coins, inscriptions, broken marbles: to him they were all ingredients, and he distrusted the stories Rome told about itself when the stones told him otherwise.

In religion he had the friar’s robe, and in thought the philosopher’s shrug. Ceremonies were for the crowd, he would say with a smile; but if he discovered a rite had been borrowed from the ancients, then he would handle it with an odd tenderness—like a man who scorns sweetmeats until he learns the recipe is older than Christendom.

He had taken Gerard, with his clear hand and grave patience, as one takes in a rare tool. He praised him everywhere—too everywhere—until one morning he began, without warning, to pry Gerard out of his own contentment.

“Princes want you,” he announced, as if reporting the weather.

“But I am so happy with you, father,” Gerard said, and meant it; the convent quiet, the steady labour, the sense that each line he traced was a rung homeward.

“Fiddlestick about being happy with me,” snapped the friar. “You must not be happy; you must be a man of the world. The grand lesson I impress on the young is—be a man of the world. Now these Montesini can pay you thrice what I can, and they shall too—by Jupiter.”

And with that pagan oath—so cheerfully lodged in a Dominican mouth—Fra Colonna clapped a price on Gerard’s pen that made even noble purses blink. They agreed at once. In those years a fair copy of a Greek book was dearer than the thoughts in it; for the printing press was still unborn, and every page had to be coaxed into being as patiently as a cheese must be turned and rubbed and guarded from spoil.

Three hundred crowns Gerard earned for Aristotle on rhetoric alone. He watched the number as he watched his letters: carefully, without allowing his heart to dance. A vain man might have grown drunk on it. Gerard, Dutch to the backbone, could taste the steel beneath Rome’s velvet. The great, he saw, were lavish as long as their fancy held; when it turned, their hand closed like a trap. So he did not presume; he bent, and wrote, and stored each crown as if it were a grain of wheat meant for winter.

Fra Colonna’s pushing and praising carried him into rooms where his old life would have seemed a fable. One day he found himself seated—far down the length, a mile from the head if truth be told—at the table of Cardinal Bessarion, that Greek-born prince of the Church whose very presence smelled faintly of libraries: vellum warmed by hands, cedar chests, wax, dust that had slept for centuries.

Gerard, whose senses were always awake, saw as much as he ate. Roast pheasants came in dressed as if they had only just been snatched from a coppice, their feathers returned to them in proud disarray—an artful lie, a theatre of the spit. There were chickens cooked in bottles, the glass clouded with their own steam; when they were poured out they fell to pieces, so tender they might have been fruit. The air held layered smells: browned fat, sharp herbs bruised in the mortar, citrus peel pricked over sweetness.

But the strangest marvel to Gerard was not the meat. It was linen.

Napkins, finer than any he had touched, lay folded into cocked hats and birds’ wings and fans, like white sculptures waiting to be used and ruined. This, more than cardinal and peacock-feathers, nearly overturned his composure; the domestic made ceremonial, the simplest cloth turned into a language of display.

After the savouries came the miracle of more tables. The boards were lifted away in pieces, and beneath each was another table already laid, as if the feast were a book with hidden pages. Sweetmeats appeared—pastilles, candied fruits, sugared almonds glistening like polished stones. The ladies fell upon them with bright fingers and laughter, gormandizing without shame; the gentlemen watched with that reasonable suspicion a man gives anything that looks too pretty to be safe.

Gerard, remembering travellers’ tales, leaned to Fra Colonna. “But, dear father,” he whispered, “I see not the bifurcal daggers with which men say his excellency armeth the left hand of a man.”

“Nay,” said the friar, with scorn that was almost affectionate, “’tis Cardinal Orsini invented that peevish instrument for his guests to fumble their meat withal. One, being in haste, did skewer his tongue to his palate with it, I hear. O tempora, O mores! The ancients, reclining godlike at their feasts, how had they spurned such pedantries.”

When the ladies had done their sweet assault, the tables vanished again. Guests were set in a row against the wall, as if the meal had been only the prologue. Two ecclesiastics entered with lutes, ducking and scraping, and knelt at the cardinal’s feet. They sang the service of the day—music poured low and careful, the notes hovering in the room like incense. Gerard listened not only with devotion but with a craftsman’s ear: how the sound settled, how it softened the chatter, how the space itself seemed kinder when it was filled with ordered harmony and not with the clatter of forks, the scrape of stools, the barking of men. He thought, fleetingly, how cities could crush the mind by noise, how a single well-shaped melody could restore breath. Then the singers withdrew with deep obeisance; the cardinal touched his skull-cap in reply, as curtly as a soldier tipping his hat; the company dispersed. Gerard went away with the odd knowledge that he had dined with a cardinal and with a man who had thrice come near the papacy—and had been more astonished by folded linen than by any mitre.

Greater honour, and stranger, followed.

One morning Cardinal Bessarion sent for him, praised the beauty of his hand, and took him in his coach to the Vatican. They climbed a private stair into a small luxurious room with a great oriel window that caught the light like a net. Everything there was made for writing: sloping frames, knives for trimming quills, pumice, rulers, inkstands rich outside like reliquaries.

The cardinal whispered to a courtier; presently the Pope’s private secretary entered carrying a glorious grimy old manuscript of Plutarch’s Lives. The book looked as if it had travelled farther than armies—its skin stained with age, its leaves smelling of dust, oil, and the old animal life of parchment. When it was opened, a faint breath rose—mould and leather, a bitter-sweetness like cellars where apples have been stored too long.

Soon Gerard sat alone, charged with copying it for the Holy Father’s own use. Awe chilled him; delight warmed him through. His holiness would handle his work; his holiness would read his letters. For a moment Gerard felt himself not merely a man earning passage money, but a hand in the long chain of transmission—one more link forged in silence.

Yet the papal ink, when he tried it, was vile: thin, sour, prone to bleed. Rome could gild an inkstand and neglect the ink within, as it could dress pheasants in feathers and hide the spit’s brutality. Gerard took out his own inkhorn—a dirty little thing he always carried—and with it his home-made ink, black and honest, that bit the page and stayed. He knelt and prayed for a firm and skilful hand, and then began.

The room had one wall nearly swallowed by a massive curtain, divided in the centre but overlapping in deep folds. It stood there like a closed mouth. At first he worked without thinking of it, the steady scrape of his pen the only sound besides the far-off murmur of the Vatican—feet on stone, a door shutting, a faint clink of arms.

After a while his eyes began to stray. The curtain drew him, not with desire so much as with that dreadful curiosity that creeps upon a man when he is alone with power nearby. He resisted. The impulse returned. It thickened, like smoke in the mind. At last it overpowered him.

He laid Plutarch aside, crossed the matted floor on quiet feet, and with two fingers gathered the folds, as gently as one lifts the lid of a pot to see if the broth boils. He put his nose to the chink—and ran it straight against cold steel.

A halberd. Then another. Two soldiers stood beyond, armed cap-à-pie, their glittering weapons crossed in a triangle. In that instant he also caught the soft murmur of voices, and saw a knot of figures cringing before some hidden presence deeper within.

He drew back swiftly, heart hammering. He did not try again. But the curtain remained in his mind like a line of untranslatable text: it promised meaning, withheld it, and reminded him that in Rome the most important things were often spoken behind fabric.

And as if to underline it, every hour or so an ecclesiastic would peep in, let his eyes run over Gerard and the page, chill the room with suspicion, and vanish. The visits were wordless and mechanical, like the ticking of a clock that exists only to measure obedience.

The next day brought a different kind of intrusion.

A gentleman richly armed burst in with a soldier’s energy and a courtier’s insolence. He stared at Gerard as if at a curious instrument.

“What is toward here?” he demanded.

Gerard, pen poised, answered mildly that he was writing out Plutarch, with the help of the saints.

The newcomer frowned. He did not know this Plutarch, he said, as if that were Plutarch’s fault.

Gerard explained, as politely as possible, the circumstances of time and space that had deprived the Signor Plutarch of the advantage of the gentleman’s acquaintance.

“Oh! one of those old dead Greeks they keep such a coil about,” the man said, and waved his hand as if brushing away dust.

“Ay, signor,” Gerard replied. “One of them, who, being dead, yet live.”

“I understand you not,” said the noble, with all the dignity of ignorance. “What did the old fellow write? Love stories?” His eyes brightened. “Merry tales, like Boccaccio.”

“Nay,” said Gerard. “Lives of heroes and sages.”

“Soldiers and popes?”

“Soldiers and princes.”

The man’s mood softened into curiosity. “Wilt read me of them some day?”

“And willingly, signor. But what would they say who employ me, were I to break off work?”

“Oh, never heed that,” cried the other. “Know you not who I am? I am Jacques Bonaventura, nephew to his holiness the Pope, and captain of his guards. And I came here to look after my fellows. I trow they have turned them out of their room for you.”

And with that he bounced away again, leaving behind him a faint jingle of metal and the disturbed air of authority.

From that day Signor Bonaventura fell into the habit of running in upon Gerard. Perhaps the solitude of guarding secrets made him hunger for a living voice; perhaps the quiet of the scribe’s room was a relief from the louder court. He chattered of trifles while Gerard wrote of men who had founded kingdoms and conquered themselves. The contrast amused Gerard: the eternal set down in ink while the momentary strutted and sighed beside it.

One day Bonaventura came in changed, his face overcast, his bravado cracked. He flung himself into a chair.

“Ah, traitress! traitress!” he cried.

Gerard asked gently what had befallen him.

“Traitress! traitress!” was all the answer.

So Gerard returned to Plutarch, as a man returns to kneading when the kitchen quarrels; work, at least, obeys the hands.

At last Bonaventura spoke again, with a kind of plea beneath his pride. “I am melancholy; and for our Lady’s sake read me a story out of Ser Plutarcho, to soothe my bile. In all that Greek is there nought about lovers betrayed?”

Gerard turned the leaves and read to him the life of Alexander. The words—translated through Gerard’s careful Latin—fell into the room like measured steps. Bonaventura’s sorrow shifted shape as he listened; the tale of conquest and discipline stirred him like a trumpet heard at dawn across a field.

He rose, paced, clenched his hands, then came back in sudden heat and threw his arms about Gerard, half laughing, half fierce. He vowed to shun “soft delights,” that bed of nettles, and to follow glory.

Gerard smiled, moved and a little sad. The young man’s pain had not been cured; it had only been seasoned into another flavour, as sour wine can be spiced and served as something grand.

And Gerard—who had eaten of Rome’s dainties and still longed most for Margaret’s plain table—felt himself, for a time, almost happy. His hand was honoured; gold accumulated; in a year or two he would go by sea to Holland with enough to begin. He pictured Bruges, or Antwerp, or dear Augsburg—some city where he and Margaret might set up together, work honestly, live in peace, and let the days circle like seasons: baking, planting, harvest, winter fires, children’s voices in the house, and the old dialect spoken without shame.

His heart did not stray one instant from her.

Nor, in his prosperity, did he forget poor Pietro. He took Fra Colonna to see Pietro’s painting. The friar inspected it with a severe face and a long silence; then, suddenly softened, he flung his arms about the artist’s neck. He carried the picture to one of the Colonnas, who paid a noble price. Pietro, lifted by that single act as by a tide, descended from his garret to the first floor and began to live like a gentleman.

Gerard stayed where he was, in his own high room, for he measured comfort by a different rule. To increase his expenses would be to postpone his return to Margaret; and luxury, to him, had no savour when it stood between him and love.

Jacques Bonaventura introduced him to other gay young fellows, idle as cats in sunshine, sharp as knives in the dark. They took a liking to Gerard’s steady humour and tried to draw him into their pleasures—and, with them, into vice and lavish spending. Gerard begged humbly to be excused. He escaped that snare by sheer singleness of purpose.

But even as he wrote the immortal lives, even as he counted his crowns like saved seeds, a greater temptation waited behind the curtain of his days, folded and overlapping like the heavy drapery in the Vatican—something armed, watched, and not meant for such as him.
