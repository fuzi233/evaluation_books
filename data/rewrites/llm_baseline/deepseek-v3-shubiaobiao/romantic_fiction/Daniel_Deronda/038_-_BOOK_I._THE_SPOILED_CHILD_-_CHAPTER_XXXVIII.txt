There are those who believe the deeper tragedy lies not in Prometheus *after* he has stolen the celestial fire, but *before*—when he is shackled by the tyranny of convention, thrust into isolation by poverty and illness, left to pulse with ideas that the world ignores. Such solitude is a wasteland where many pass, but none truly see.

Visionaries walk among us—those whose thoughts take shape as vivid premonitions, whose hungers and fears bloom into prophecy. They are not mad, nor less rational than the calculating minds of the marketplace. Some souls are like Thebes of a hundred gates, open to a flood of impressions that narrower minds could never bear. And though the world dismisses them—though it locks them away like curiosities—they are the ones who strain against the boundaries of the possible.

Mordecai was such a man.

Consumed by illness, yet burning with purpose, he had spent years searching for a kindred spirit—a mind that could receive the legacy of his thoughts before his own flame guttered out. He did not hope for recovery; his hope was fiercer, stranger. He longed to pour his soul into another, to see his beliefs take root in fresher soil.

This was no idle dream. It was a conviction, sharpened by time. He had measured men with a scholar’s eye, seeking one unlike himself: a Jew, yes—learned, fervent—but also strong, beautiful, unmarked by the wear of hardship. He imagined a face carved from the noble portraits of old masters, a voice like deep water, a life unbroken by want.

London’s bridges became his sanctuary. At dawn or dusk, he would stand at Blackfriars, the river flowing beneath him like time itself, the city’s silhouette a vast scripture of human striving. Here, his vision took shape—a figure emerging from the golden haze of sunrise, dark against the light. At first, it was only a silhouette, a promise. Then, slowly, it gained features: youth, refinement, a gravity that spoke of depth.

This was no mere fancy. It was necessity.

When little Jacob, bright-eyed and restless, climbed into his lap, Mordecai would recite Hebrew verses, willing the words to take root in the boy’s mind like seeds. *"Shatter my heart, O Gabriel,"* he murmured, *"as the founder shatters clay to free the golden image."* But Jacob, ever impatient, would squirm away—flipping coins, mimicking street performers—until Mordecai’s voice cracked with frustration. Once, in a surge of despair, he seized the child’s shoulders and hissed a curse upon his heedless generation. Then, just as quickly, remorse flooded him. He pulled Jacob close, soothing him as a father would.

Yet the longing did not fade.

And then—Deronda appeared.

A stranger, yet not a stranger. His face was the one Mordecai had carried in his mind for years. When he learned Deronda was not a Jew, the blow was brutal. But the vision would not die. It lingered, persistent as prophecy, woven into the fabric of his faith.

Now, in the grip of winter, Mordecai waited. He no longer had patience for teaching Jacob or debating in dim-lit clubs. Something was coming. He felt it in the air, in the restless pulse of his blood.

All he wanted was to stand once more by the river, beneath the wide sky, and watch the water carry the light—and the future—toward him.
