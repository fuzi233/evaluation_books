On the fourteenth of April, Romola stood once more within the walls of Florence. She had not been able to stay in Pistoja, where conflicting rumors about the Trial by Fire unsettled her mind. Instead, she journeyed on to Prato, uncertain whether she could bear to return to the city that had known so much sorrow. Yet fate drew her forward. There, she met the Augustinian monk from San Spirito—once her father’s confessor—who carried with him the grim truth: Savonarola had been arrested, and Tito was dead.

The monk, speaking with the usual bias of the Black Brethren toward their more fiery brother, told her that Savonarola had confessed under torture, admitting he had deceived the people. Romola listened without flinching. She already knew the weight of what had happened; it was no mere tale to her, but a personal catastrophe. The news of Tito’s death was even more piercing. According to the monk, Tito had escaped a mob by leaping into the Arno River, only to be killed on its banks by an old man who bore him a long-standing grudge. But Romola did not believe that story entirely. She saw beyond the surface—a deeper tragedy unfolding in silence.

That evening, she arrived in Florence. She sat in quiet, her heart heavy beneath the noise of the city: joyous exclamations mingling with mournful cries, stories flying like sparks from a fire. Monna Brigida, her cousin, greeted her with wild gestures, having returned to wearing false hair during Romola’s absence—only now, upon seeing her, she tore it off dramatically. “I’ll be grey,” she declared, “if my dear child will only stay with me.”

But Romola was beyond such distractions. Her thoughts were fixed on two urgent matters: to see Savonarola if possible, and to find Tessa and the children. When Brigida began to chatter about plans for Romola to live with her, she cut in sharply. “Tell me, cousin—has anything been heard since Tito’s death of a young woman with two little children?”

Brigida froze. Her eyes widened. “Cristo! No. What? Was he so bad as that?” she gasped. “Ah, then that explains why you left—without saying much. I thought you were just flighty, leaving your husband when Bernardo was beheaded. But now I see—there was another woman, children… Why didn’t you tell me?”

Romola looked at her earnestly. “No, cousin—please do not speak so. I must find them. They are helpless. That is what I must do first.”

Brigida shook her head, puzzled. “If this is being a Piagnone, then I’ve been taking peas for paternosters. Fra Girolamo said widows shouldn't remarry—step through the door, it’s sin; come down the chimney, it’s welcome. Two children—Santiddio!”

“Cousin,” Romola said gently, “she has done nothing wrong. She knows nothing. I will explain—but not now.”

The next morning, Romola went to the house near San Ambrogio where she had once found Tessa. It was empty. The neighbors confirmed that old Lisa had left a week before, but no one knew where Tessa had gone. Romola suspected Tito had sent her away beforehand—surely he would not have abandoned the children willingly. And if she were outside Florence, finding her might be impossible. Romola imagined her waiting alone, confused and afraid, somewhere along a dusty road.

She had no leads. She could not publicly name Tessa’s connection to Tito—her pride and compassion alike forbade it. Days passed in silence, filled with longing. Every time she saw a pair of small brown legs or a woman in simple peasant dress, her heart fluttered. She did not think of herself as heroic—only that she needed someone to care for, someone to love. To hold those children, to make them feel safe—that would be a kind of peace after all her grief.

There was talk of Tito’s property, which she might claim. But she distrusted it. She decided to give it all to the State, keeping only enough to buy back her father’s library. That sum would support Tessa and the children modestly.

Yet Monna Brigida refused to let the matter rest. She insisted Romola live with her, declaring that if Romola wanted to raise others’ children, then she, too, would help—though they must first be found. Romola felt the sting of that remark. But hope, though fragile, still burned within her. _Somehow_, she believed, she would find them.

Then came a new clue. She learned that Tito had arranged horses and mules to wait for him in San Gallo—meaning he intended to leave Florence through that gate. Romola resolved to ask the gatekeepers if anyone matching Tessa’s description had passed through before the ninth of April.

Walking along Via San Gallo, her veil drawn low, she scanned the street carefully. Then she saw Bratti, the wandering peddler, haggling with a customer. He might know something, she thought. As she approached, lifting her veil, she noticed something dangling from his basket—a necklace. Her breath caught.

“Bratti,” she said, stepping forward, “where did you get that necklace?”

“Your servant, madonna,” he replied, turning slowly. “It’s worth money, but I’ll sell it cheap. My heart’s too soft for trade—I’ve promised to keep it in pledge.”

“Did you get it from a woman named Tessa?” Romola asked, her voice steady.

“Ah!” Bratti smiled. “If you know her, and want to redeem it at a small profit, you’d be doing a kindness. She wept when she gave it to me—seemed like she was drowning. I’ll let you have it for a florin. I don’t like to be hard-hearted.”

Romola paid him quickly, unclipping the necklace from the basket. “Where is she?”

“Outside the gate, at the other end of the Borgo, at old Sibilla Manetti’s house. Anyone can point it out.”

Her steps quickened. She reached the house, heart pounding. Inside, Tessa sat on a low bed, her tears dried into silent sobs. The children played nearby—Lillo hiding under his skirt, roaring at Ninna, then peering out to watch her reaction.

Romola stepped in, her veil cast aside. She held up the necklace. For a moment, she stood still. Then she spoke, her voice clear and gentle, like her father’s used to be:

“Tessa.”

Tessa jumped to her feet, staring.

“See,” Romola said, placing the beads around her neck, “God has sent me to you again.”

Tessa screamed softly, then clung to Romola, sobbing. The children came forward, gripping their mother’s dress, eyes wide as they looked up at the woman who had come to them from nowhere.

That day, they all went home—to Monna Brigida’s house in the Borgo degli Albizzi.

Romola gently told Tessa that Naldo would never return—not because he was cruel, but because he was dead. “But be comforted, my Tessa,” she said. “I am here now. I will take care of you always. And we have Lillo and Ninna.”

Monna Brigida watched, her lips twitching between awe and irritation. “Let it be for now,” she thought. “But I’ll tell this poor girl someday who Romola really is. She doesn’t even know how many fingers she has. But I’ll teach her humility—if she’s going to live under my roof.”

Still, when supper came, she couldn’t resist giving the children extra sweets. Before bed, she whispered to Romola, “You must let me help with their meals, Romola. You’ve never raised a baby. I had twins—only they died at birth.” And then, with a sigh, she added, “But it would be a shame not to care for such cherubs.”
