The next morning, the boys and I set off with the cart heavily laden with bundles of bamboo to tend to our avenue of fruit trees. Behind us, we left the buffalo to rest; his services were unnecessary for the moment, and I wanted the wound in his nostrils to heal completely before subjecting him to labor again.

We arrived just in time. Many of the young trees, which moments prior threatened to topple, had already succumbed to their fate, lying prostrate on the ground. Others were bent double, while only a few stubborn specimens remained erect. We lifted the fallen timber, dug deep trenches at their roots, and drove sturdy bamboo props into the earth, binding the trees securely to them with thick, strong fibers.

"Papa," Franz called out, handing me a coil of fiber as I worked. "Are these wild or tame trees?"

"Oh, these are wild trees, the most ferocious of them!" Jack laughed, mimicking the action. "We are tying them up so they won't run away. Soon, though, we'll untie them, and they'll trot after us wherever we go, dropping fruit at our feet. We'll tame them! We'll put rings through their noses just like we did with the buffalo!"

"That isn't true," Franz replied seriously. "There are wild trees and tame trees. The wild ones grow in the woods, like crab-apples, while the tame ones grow in gardens, like the pears and peaches back home. Which kind are these, Papa?"

"They are not wild," I answered gently. "They are grafted, or cultivated—what you might call tame trees. No European tree bears good fruit unless it is grafted." Seeing a look of confusion cross the little boy's face at the unfamiliar word, I paused to explain.

"Grafting," I continued, "is the art of inserting a slip or twig from one tree into a specific knot or 'eye' on the branch of another. That new growth will then produce fruit exactly like the tree from which the slip was taken, not the fruit the original stock would have borne naturally. For instance, if we have a sour crab-tree and an apple-tree bearing excellent Ribston Pippins, we take a slip from the apple-tree, insert it into an eye of the crab-tree, and in a year or two, that branch will be heavy with delicious apples."

"But," Ernest asked, "where did the slips of good fruit come from, if none grow without grafting?"

"From foreign countries," I explained. "In the cold climate of our region, nature requires this human aid. But in warmer, southern latitudes, the most luscious fruit trees are indigenous to the soil, flourishing and bearing sweet, wholesome fruit with little care. Conversely, in nations like England, Germany, and even France, these same trees demand the utmost horticultural skill merely to produce a single fruit."

"When the Romans invaded England," I mused, "they found nothing but crab-apples, nut bushes, and brambles. Yet, through grafting, they produced fine apples, filberts, and raspberries. It was the same story in our beloved Switzerland—all our fruit trees were imports."

"Were cherries, Father? Can't we even call cherries Swiss? I always thought they grew nowhere else."

"I fear we cannot claim cherries as our own, not even by name," I told him. "They are named after Cerasus, a state in Pontus, Asia Minor, from which they were brought to Europe by the Roman general Lucullus roughly seventy years before Christ. Hazelnuts also hail from Pontus; walnuts originated in Persia. Grapes, however, are of ancient antiquity. You recall the story of Noah cultivating vines; they have traveled from place to place until they are now found in most of the civilized world."

"Do you truly think all these trees will survive here?" Fritz asked as we crossed Jackal River and entered our plantation at Tentholm. "Look at these lemons, pomegranates, pistachios, and mulberries."

"I have little doubt," I assured him. "We are clearly within the tropics, a realm where such trees are destined to flourish."

"These pines," I pointed out, "originate from France, Spain, and Italy; the olives from Armenia and Palestine; the figs from the island of Chios; the peaches and apricots from Persia; plums from Damascus in Syria; and the pears of all varieties from Greece."

"If our homeland hasn't been blessed with such bounty," I reflected, "we have been gifted with wisdom and skill, enabling us to import and cultivate the treasures of other lands."

So spoke and worked we, until every tree requiring support had been secured with a stout bamboo prop. Then, with appetites that a gourmet might envy, we returned to Falconhurst. My good wife was nearly alarmed at the voracious manner in which we fell upon the corned beef and palm-cabbage she had prepared, but eventually, these savory dishes produced the desired effect, and one by one, we declared ourselves satisfied.

Reclining after our labor, we digested our meal and discussed our future projects.

"I wish," my wife said, "that you would devise another method for climbing to the nest above us. The nest itself is perfect; I desire nothing better. However, I chafe at the thought of scaling that dreadful ladder every single time. Could you not construct a flight of steps to reach it?"

I pondered the problem, turning over every conceivable plan. "It seems impossible to build stairs outside the trunk," I admitted. "But inside, it might be feasible. Have I not often suspected that this trunk is hollow, or at least partially so? If that is the case, our task becomes comparatively easy. Did you not tell me recently that you noticed bees emerging from a hole in the tree?"

"Oh, yes," little Franz chimed in. "I went to investigate, and one flew straight at my face and stung me. I almost cried, but I didn't."

"A brave little boy," I praised. "Well, if the trunk is hollow enough to harbor a swarm of bees, it likely remains hollow for a significant portion of its length. Like the willows in our homeland, it may draw nourishment directly through the bark, retaining a flourishing appearance despite being structurally unsound within."

Master Jack, ever practical, instantly sprang to his feet to test my hypothesis. The rest of us followed suit, scrambling about like squirrels, peering into the hole and tapping the wood to gauge the extent of the cavity by sound.

In their eagerness, they forgot who the current tenants of this interesting trunk were. They were soon reminded, however, for the bees, disturbed by the unusual racket, erupted with an angry buzz. In an instant, they descended upon the intruders, stinging hands, faces, and necks, settling into their hair, and pursuing them as they fled to me for rescue.

With great difficulty, we evicted the angry insects and tended to the boys. Jack, who had reached the hole first, suffered the worst; his face swelled to an extraordinary degree, and only the constant application of cold earth could alleviate the agony. Although eager to launch an organized counter-attack immediately, their pain rendered them of little assistance for an hour or more.

Meanwhile, I made my preparations. First, I selected a large calabash gourd, intending to fashion a new beehive so that, once we had driven the insects from their current home, we would not lose them entirely. I flattened the lower half, cut an arched opening in the front for a doorway, and constructed a straw roof to shield them from rain and heat. The little house was complete.

However, nothing else could be done yet, for the irritated bees still buzzed angrily around the tree. I waited until darkness fell. Once all the bees had returned to their trunk, I helped Fritz carefully stop up every hole with wet clay, ensuring they could not escape before we commenced operations the next morning.

At dawn, we were at work. I inserted one end of a hollow cane through the clay into the tree. Down this tube, using a pipe and tobacco, I smoked furiously. The humming and buzzing within were tremendous; the bees evidently could not comprehend what was happening. After finishing my first pipeful and covering the end of the cane with my thumb, I passed the pipe to Fritz to refill. He did so, and I smoked again. The buzzing gradually diminished, subsiding into a mere murmur. By the time I finished the second pipe, absolute silence reigned; the bees were stupefied.

"Now then, Fritz," I commanded, "quick with the hammer and chisel, and stand beside me."

He was ready in a moment. Together, we cut a small door by the side of the hole. We did not remove it, leaving it attached by one corner so it could be taken out at a moment's notice. Giving the bees a final dose of tobacco smoke, we opened the door.

Carefully but rapidly, we removed the insects, which clung in clusters to the sides of the tree, and placed them into the hive I had prepared for their reception. Just as swiftly, I extracted every atom of wax and honey from their storehouse, depositing it into a cask I had readied for the purpose.

The bees were now safely removed from the trunk, but I hesitated. I could not predict whether, upon reviving from their temporary stupor, they would refuse to occupy the new house and insist on returning to their old quarters.
