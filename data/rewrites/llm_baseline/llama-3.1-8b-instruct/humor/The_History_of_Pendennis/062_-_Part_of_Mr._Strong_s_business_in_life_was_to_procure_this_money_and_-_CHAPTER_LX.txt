As the seasons unfolded like the pages of a worn leather-bound book, nearly a year had elapsed since the events that had left an indelible mark on the lives of those within the social sphere. Amidst this passage of time, Arthur Pendennis had undergone a transformation, shedding the somber tones of his black coat for the more vibrant hue of blue. His wig, once a staple of his attire, now lay discarded, allowing his hair to reassert itself, though perhaps a trifle thinner than before. This metamorphosis was not limited to his outward appearance, for he had also donned the uniform of a Cornet in the Clavering troop of the ——shire Yeomanry Cavalry, an honor bestowed upon him by the Marquis of Steyne during a visit to the royal court.

This development had been eagerly championed by Arthur's uncle, Major Pendennis, who deemed it imperative that his nephew be initiated into the ranks of the nobility, thereby elevating his standing within the aristocratic circles. The Major had long harbored the notion that Arthur's inheritance, although modest at £1,500 per annum, would be sufficient to secure him a place among the upper echelons of society. He had proclaimed his nephew's prospects to the world, voicing his disappointment at the disparity between Arthur's actual circumstances and his own expectations.

As a result, Pendennis found himself the subject of increased attention and respect within the social hierarchy. His publishers, hitherto dismissive of his literary endeavors, now regarded him with greater deference, while even the matrons of high society were willing to bestow upon him a certain civility. Though his connections might be of a lower standing than those of the illustrious Myra, Pendennis's eligibility as a suitor for the hand of the less fortunate Beatrice Hawxby remained intact. For in the eyes of the gentry, a union between Pendennis and the homely Beatrice would provide a respectable match for the young woman, shielding her from the ignominy of marrying a commoner.

It was during the first winter following the acquisition of his mother's fortune that Mrs. Hawxby, a lady known for her sharp wit and calculating nature, had taken it upon herself to instruct Beatrice in the art of billiards, with Pendennis serving as her instructor.
