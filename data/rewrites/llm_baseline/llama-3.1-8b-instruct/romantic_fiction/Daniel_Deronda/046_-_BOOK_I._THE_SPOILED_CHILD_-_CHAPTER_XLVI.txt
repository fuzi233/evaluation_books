As the sun dipped below the horizon, casting a warm orange glow over the quiet streets of Brompton, Mordecai's future was on the cusp of transformation. The time had come to prepare him for the revelation of his long-lost sister, Mirah, and the new life that awaited her in this unassuming corner of London. Mrs. Meyrick, a pillar of strength and kindness, had been instrumental in finding a suitable lodgings for the siblings, just a short stroll from her own home. Her keen insight and warmth had allowed her to keep the secret hidden from the girls, as well as from Hans, for fear that any premature disclosure might stir up unwarranted suspicions and jeopardize the delicate balance of their lives.

Mrs. Meyrick's motivations were twofold – she wanted Mirah to maintain her independence, and she had grown fond of the young woman's spirit and resilience. Yet, she couldn't help but harbor some doubts about the enigmatic brother, whose fervent dedication to his faith left her somewhat perplexed. While she admired Deronda's judgment, she found it difficult to reconcile his affection for Mordecai with her own reservations about the rigidities of Jewish tradition. The memories of her youth, spent listening to impassioned sermons, had left her wary of zealotry, and she worried that Mordecai's convictions might lead him down a path of exclusivity and intolerance.

Deronda, sensing her concerns, reassured her that Mordecai was not a zealot, but an ardent soul with a broader perspective. "Fanaticism," he explained, "is not about genuine passion, but about a narrow-mindedness that blinds us to the needs and perspectives of others." He described Mordecai as an enthusiast, one who saw the interconnectedness of all things and strove for the greater good. As they spoke, Mrs. Meyrick's apprehensions began to ease, replaced by a growing sense of hope and anticipation. She envisioned a future where Mirah's love for her new family could flourish, untainted by the fanaticism that had once threatened to consume her.
