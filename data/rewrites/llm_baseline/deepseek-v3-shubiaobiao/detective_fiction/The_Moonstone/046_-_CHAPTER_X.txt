The weight of those two hours of waiting pressed upon me in a way I cannot easily describe. My body refused stillness; my mind shrank from human company. Until I heard Ezra Jennings’ revelation, I could neither rest nor speak to another soul.

I abandoned my intended visit to Mrs. Ablewhite—even avoided Gabriel Betteredge. Returning to Frizinghall, I left a note for him, claiming urgent business would keep me away until afternoon. I urged him to dine without me and seek his usual diversions among friends. Then I strode out into the lonely moorland, wandering until my watch told me it was time to face Jennings.

I found him waiting in a grim little room adjoining the surgery—a place that spoke of suffering. Diagrams of disease lined the walls; a skull crowned a shelf of medical texts. The bare deal table, ink-stained and utilitarian, the threadbare rug, the sink with its ghastly surgical implications—all conspired to unsettle the spirit. Outside, bees hummed among flowers, and a distant, tuneless piano jangled intermittently. These ordinary sounds felt like trespassers in a space reserved for pain.

Jennings offered no apology for the setting. "This is the only place we won’t be disturbed," he said, gesturing to papers on the table. "My notes are ready. Bring your chair closer—we’ll need to consult them together."

I obeyed. The notes consisted of two folio sheets—one sparsely covered in fragments, the other densely filled in red and black ink. Frustrated, I pushed the second aside.

"Mercy, Jennings," I said. "Tell me what to expect before I drown in this."

He studied me with that quiet, sorrowful smile. "Very well. You’ve told me you’ve never knowingly taken opium."

"Never knowingly," I agreed.

"Then let me reconstruct the past. Last year, you suffered sleepless nights—until the night of the birthday, when you slept deeply. Correct?"

"Entirely."

"Did you ever determine the cause of your insomnia?"

"Betteredge guessed it was due to my quitting tobacco."

"Ah. A sound guess. Sudden cessation can unsettle the nerves. Now—do you recall quarreling with Mr. Candy about medicine?"

The memory surfaced reluctantly. At the birthday dinner, I had rashly disparaged medical practice, provoking Candy’s irritation. Lady Verinder had intervened, and we reconciled, but the dispute had slipped my mind entirely.

Jennings pressed further. "Were you anxious about the Diamond last year?"

"Profoundly. I knew conspirators sought it—I was warned to protect Miss Verinder."

"Was its safety discussed before you retired that night?"

"Yes. Lady Verinder and Rachel spoke of it—in my presence."

Jennings handed me his notes. "Read these now, and you’ll uncover two staggering truths. First: you took the Diamond in an opium-induced trance. Second: Mr. Candy, without your knowledge, dosed you to disprove your own arguments against medicine."

I sat stunned.

"Don’t condemn him too harshly," Jennings said gently. "He meant no harm. Had illness not struck him, he would have confessed the trick the next morning. The truth might have been uncovered within a day."

Anger simmered beneath my shock. "I may forgive, but I won’t forget."

"Every physician deceives patients at times. Opium is distrusted in England—even by the educated. But let’s focus on the present. My notes are useless as proof—too esoteric, too reliant on my word. To clear your name, we must recreate the past."

"How?"

"A bold experiment. You must unknowingly ‘steal’ the Diamond again—before witnesses whose testimony cannot be doubted."

I stared.

"It can be done," he insisted. "But I’ll need your full cooperation. First—you’ve resumed smoking. Give it up again, abruptly, as before."

Understanding dawned. "From this moment," I agreed.

"If sleeplessness follows, as it did last June, we’ll have our first success. Next, we must revive the circumstances of that night—the household’s atmosphere, your preoccupation with the Diamond. Then, a second dose of laudanum may reproduce the trance."

He opened a medical text, citing authorities to justify the plan. A passage from Dr. Carpenter suggested that past sensory impressions could resurface unconsciously. Another case described a drunkard recalling lost items only when intoxicated again.

"The science supports me," Jennings said. "But success isn’t guaranteed. If we cannot sufficiently approximate the conditions, the experiment fails. If we succeed, you may prove your innocence beyond doubt."

One question still nagged me. "Opium stupefies—yet I moved, opened drawers, returned to my room. How?"

Jennings handed me *Confessions of an English Opium-Eater*. "De Quincey wandered markets or attended operas under its influence. Opium doesn’t always induce passivity."

"And its effect on me?"

"That," said Jennings, "is what we must discover."
