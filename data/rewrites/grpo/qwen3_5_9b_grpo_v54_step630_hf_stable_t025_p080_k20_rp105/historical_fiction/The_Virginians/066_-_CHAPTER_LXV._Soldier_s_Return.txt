What endless vanity grips the great powers, and the French in particular? Can it truly be believed that following the skirmish at St. Cast—a mere affair of severing a rear guard, as you know—they proceeded to waste countless pounds of powder at the Invalides in Paris, boasting and blustering over our misfortune? Is there any true magnanimity in cheering and huzzahing because five or six hundred brave souls were trapped by ten thousand on a shoreline, meeting the fate that often awaits the last man? I once felt a sudden urge to paint a vivid picture of the rejoicings in London celebrating our glorious triumph at St. Malo. I imagined the polished cannons dragged in procession by our gallant tars; the sturdy horse-grenadiers prancing alongside; the mob waving hats and roaring cheers, while our friends watched from a balcony in Fleet Street, blessing this scene of British glory. But now that the French Invalides have grown so vulgar as to mimic the Tower, setting their St. Cast against our St. Malo, I scorn to touch upon such a stale subject. I say *Nolo*, not *Malo*; content, for my part, if Harry has returned from one expedition and another with his skin intact. Have I ever claimed he was bruised? Have I not, for fear of alarming my fair young readers, insisted he was as well as ever in his life? The sea air had merely bronzed his cheek, and the whistling ball had spared his side-curl. The ocean had wet his gaiters and garments without swallowing his body. True, he had shown the lapels of his coat to the enemy, but only for the briefest moment, withdrawing from their sight as quickly as possible. And what are lapels but reverses? Coats possess them, as do men; and our duty is to wear them with courage and good humor.

"I can tell you," said Harry, "we were all forced to run for it. When our line broke, luck belonged to whoever could reach the boats first. The French horse and foot pursued us down to the sea, mingling among us, cutting our men down and bayoneting them where they fell. Poor Armytage was shot ahead of me and fell; I picked him up and staggered through the surf to a boat. It was fortunate the sailors in our vessel were not afraid, for the shots whistled past their ears, shattered their oars, and riddled their flag. Yet the officer in command remained as cool as if he were drinking a bowl of punch at Portsmouth, which we shared upon landing, I can promise you. Poor Sir John was less fortunate than I. He never reached the ship, and the service has lost a fine soldier, while Miss Howe has lost a true gentleman to her husband. Such casualties must happen; and his brother receives the promotion—the baronetcy."

"It is the poor lady I am thinking of," said Miss Hetty, to whom our volunteer was recounting his tale. "And the King. Why did the King encourage Sir John Armytage to go? A gentleman could not refuse a command from such a quarter. And now the poor gentleman is dead! Oh, what a state his Majesty must be in!"

"I have no doubt his Majesty will be in a deep state of grief," replied Papa, wagging his head.

"Now you are laughing! Do you mean, sir, that when a gentleman dies in his service, almost at his feet, the King of England feels nothing?" Hetty asked. "If I thought that, I vow I would support the Pretender!"

"The sauce-box would make a pretty little head for Temple Bar," remarked the General, seeing the tumult of remorse, consternation, and gratitude beating in Miss Hetty's little heart as the danger passed. "No, my dear. Were kings to weep for every soldier, what a life you would make for them! I think better of his Majesty than to suppose him so weak; and if Miss Hester Lambert secured her Pretender, I doubt she would be any happier. That family was never famous for excessive feeling."

"But if the King sent Harry—I mean Sir John Armytage—actually to the war in which he lost his life, ought not his Majesty to repent greatly?" the young lady pressed.

"If Harry had fallen, no doubt the court would have gone into mourning: as it is, gentlemen and ladies wore colored clothes yesterday," the General noted.

"Why should we not make bonfires for a defeat and don sackcloth and ashes after a victory?" asked George. "I protest I do not wish to thank Heaven for helping us burn the ships at Cherbourg."

"Yes, you do, George! Not that I have a right to speak, nor are you ever so clever. But when your country wins, you are glad—I know I am. When I run away before the French, I am ashamed; I cannot help it, though I did it," Harry admitted. "It does not seem right somehow that Englishmen should have to do it," he added gravely. George smiled but chose not to ask his brother what, on the other hand, was the Frenchman's opinion.

"'Tis a bad business," Harry continued seriously, "but it is lucky it was no worse. The story is that their Governor, the Duke of Aiguillon, was rather what you call a moistened chicken. Our entire retreat might have been cut off, only we ourselves were in a mighty hurry to move. The French local militia behaved famously, I am happy to say, and there were many gentlemen volunteers with them who showed, as they ought, in the front. They say the Chevalier of Tour d'Auvergne engaged in spite of the Duke of Aiguillon's orders. Officers told us who came off with a list of prisoners and wounded to General Bligh and Lord Howe. He is a lord now, since the news of his brother's death reached home, George. He is a brave fellow, whether lord or commoner."

"And his sister, who was to have married poor Sir John Armytage; think what her state must be!" sighed Miss Hetty, who had lately grown sentimental.

"And his mother!" cried Mrs. Lambert. "Have you seen her ladyship's address in the papers to the electors of Nottingham? 'Lord Howe being now absent upon the publick service, and Lieutenant-Colonel Howe with his regiment at Louisbourg, it rests upon me to beg the favour of your votes and interests that Lieutenant-Colonel Howe may supply the place of his late brother as your representative in Parliament.' Isn't this a gallant woman?"

"A laconic woman," said George.

"How can sons help being brave who have been nursed by such a mother as that?" asked the General.

Our two young men looked at each other.

"If one of us were to fall in defense of his country, we have a mother in Sparta who would think and write so too," said George.

"If Sparta is anywhere Virginia way, I reckon we have," remarked Mr. Harry. "And to think that we should both of us have met the enemy, and both of us been whipped by him, brother!" he added pensively.

Hetty looked at him, thinking of him only as he was the other day, tottering through the water toward the boats, his comrade bleeding on his shoulder, the enemy in pursuit, the shot flying around. And it was she who had driven him into that danger! Her words had provoked him. Now that he was returned, he never rebuked her. Except when asked, he scarcely spoke of his adventures at all. He was very grave and courteous with Hetty; with the rest of the family, especially frank and tender. But those taunts of hers had wounded him. "Little hand!" his looks and demeanor seemed to say, "thou shouldst not have been lifted against me! It is ill to scorn anyone, much more one who has been so devoted to you and all yours. I may not be over quick of wit, but insofar as the heart goes, I am the equal of the best, and the best of my heart your family has had."

Harry's wrong, and his magnanimous endurance of it, served him to regain in Miss Hetty's esteem the place he had lost during the previous months of inglorious idleness. The respect the fair paid to the brave she gave him. She was no longer pert in her answers or sarcastic in her observations regarding his conduct. In a word, she was a humiliated, an altered, an improved Miss Hetty.

And all the world seemed to change toward Harry, as he changed toward the world. He was no longer sulky and indolent; he no longer desponded about himself or defied his neighbors. The colonel of his regiment reported his behavior as exemplary and recommended him for one of the commissions vacated by the casualties during the expedition. Unlucky as its termination was, it was at least fortunate for him. His brother-volunteers, when they returned to St. James's Street, reported highly of his behavior. These volunteers and their actions became the theme of everybody's praise. Had he been a general commanding, slain in the moment of victory, Sir John Armytage could scarce have had more sympathy than that which the nation showed him. The papers teemed with letters about him, and men of wit and sensibility vied with each other in composing epitaphs in his honor. The fate of his affianced bride was bewailed. She was, as we have said, the sister of the brave Commodore who had just returned from this unfortunate expedition and succeeded to the title of his elder brother, an officer as gallant as himself, who had just fallen in America.

My Lord Howe was heard to speak in special praise of Mr. Warrington, so he received a handsome share of the fashion and favor the town now bestowed on the volunteers. Doubtless there were thousands of men employed who were as good as they, but the English ever love their gentlemen, and love that they should distinguish themselves; and these volunteers were voted Paladins and heroes by common accord. As our young noblemen will, they accepted their popularity very affably. White's and Almack's illuminated when they returned, and St. James's embraced its young knights. Harry was restored to full favor amongst them. Their hands were held out eagerly to him again. Even his relations congratulated him; and there came a letter from Castlewood, whither Aunt Bernstein had by this time betaken herself, containing praises of his valor and a pretty little bank-bill as a token of his affectionate aunt's approbation. This was under my Lord Castlewood's frank, who sent his regards to both his kinsmen and an offer of the hospitality of his country-house if they were minded to come to him. Besides this, there came to him a private letter through the post—not very well spelt, but in a handwriting Harry smiled to see again—in which his affectionate cousin, Maria Esmond, told him she always loved to hear his praises (which were now in everybody's mouth) and sympathized in his good or evil fortune; and that whatever occurred to him, she begged to keep a little place in his heart. Parson Sampson, she wrote, had preached a beautiful sermon about the horrors of war and the noble actions of men who volunteered to face battle and danger in the service of their country. Indeed, the chaplain wrote himself, presently, a letter full of enthusiasm, in which he saluted Mr. Harry as his friend, his benefactor, his glorious hero. Even Sir Miles Warrington despatched a basket of game from Norfolk; and one bird (shot sitting), with love to my cousin, had a string and paper round the leg and was sent as the first victim of young Miles's fowling-piece.

And presently, with joy beaming in his countenance, Mr. Lambert came to visit his young friends at their lodgings in Southampton Row and announced to them that Mr. Henry Warrington was forthwith to be gazetted as Ensign in the Second Battalion of Kingsley's, the 20th Regiment, which had been engaged in the campaign and which now, at this time, was formed into a separate regiment, the 67th. Its colonel was not with his regiment during its expedition to Brittany. He was away at Cape Breton, engaged in capturing those guns at Louisbourg, of which the arrival in England had caused such exultation.
