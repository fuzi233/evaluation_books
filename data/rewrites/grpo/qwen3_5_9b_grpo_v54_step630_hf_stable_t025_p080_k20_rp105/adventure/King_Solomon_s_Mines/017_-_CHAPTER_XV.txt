Once the slaughter ceased, Sir Henry and Good were borne into Twala's hut, where I followed, joining them in their exhaustion. We were a trio utterly drained by exertion and blood loss; my own state was scarcely better. Though wiry and accustomed to fatigue, the night claimed me completely. As often happens when pushed to the limit, the old scar from the lion's claw began to ache, joined by a violent headache from the blow that had knocked me senseless that morning. It was a miserable evening, yet we possessed a grim comfort: we were lucky enough to feel misery rather than lying dead upon the plain among the thousands of brave men who had perished that night.

With the aid of Foulata, who had become our devoted handmaiden since we spared her life, and particularly Good's, we managed to remove the chain shirts. While they had saved our lives by stopping the weapons, the flesh beneath was a mass of terrible contusions. Foulata brought pounded green leaves with an aromatic scent, which, applied as a plaster, offered some relief. However, the bruises paled in comparison to the wounds. Good had a gaping hole through the fleshy part of his "beautiful white leg," from which he had lost a tremendous amount of blood. Sir Henry, besides other injuries, bore a deep cut across his jaw inflicted by Twala's battle-axe. Fortunately, Good was a competent surgeon. Once his small box of medicines was produced, he thoroughly cleansed the wounds and stitched them up with surprising skill, despite the dim light of the primitive Kukuana lamp. He then generously applied an antiseptic ointment and covered the injuries with the remnants of a pocket-handkerchief.

Meanwhile, Foulata prepared a strong broth, for we were too weary to eat. We swallowed it eagerly and collapsed onto piles of magnificent *karrosses*, or fur rugs, scattered across the dead king's great hut. By a cruel irony of fate, it was upon Twala's own couch, wrapped in his own particular rug, that Sir Henry, the man who had slain him, slept that night.

I say slept, but after such a day, rest was elusive. The air was thick with the sounds of farewell and mourning. From every direction came the wailing of women whose husbands, sons, and brothers had perished. Over twelve thousand men—nearly a fifth of the Kukuana army—had been destroyed in that awful struggle. It was heart-rending to listen to their cries for those who would never return, a sound that revealed the full horror of war done in the name of ambition. Towards midnight, the ceaseless crying subsided, leaving only intervals of silence broken by a long, piercing howl from a hut behind us. This was Gagool, "keening" over the dead king Twala.

I managed a fitful sleep, waking repeatedly to relive the terror of the last twenty-four hours. I saw the warrior I had sent to his death charging on the mountain-top; I stood again in the glorious ring of Greys making their immortal stand; I saw Twala's plumed and gory head roll past my feet with gnashing teeth.

When dawn broke, my companions had slept no better. Good was in a high fever, soon becoming light-headed and spitting blood, undoubtedly the result of an internal injury sustained when a Kukuana warrior had driven his spear through his chain armor. Sir Henry seemed relatively fresh, though his facial wound made eating difficult and laughter impossible; he was sore and stiff, barely able to stir.

Around eight o'clock, Infadoos visited us. The tough old warrior appeared little the worse for his exertions, though he admitted to staying up all night. He was delighted to see us but deeply grieved at Good's condition, shaking our hands cordially. I noted that he addressed Sir Henry with a reverence bordering on awe. Indeed, the great Englishman was viewed throughout Kukuanaland as a supernatural being. No soldier believed anyone could have fought as he did or slain Twala, the strongest warrior in the country, who could shear a bull's neck in a single stroke. That stroke became proverbial, and any extraordinary feat of strength was thereafter known as "Incubu's blow."

Infadoos informed us that Twala's regiments had submitted to Ignosi, and similar surrenders were coming from outlying chiefs. Twala's death ended all chance of disturbance, for Scragga, his only legitimate son, was dead, leaving no rival claimant. I remarked that Ignosi had risen to power through blood. The old chief shrugged. "Yes," he answered, "but the Kukuana people can only be kept cool by letting their blood flow sometimes. Many are killed, but the women remain, and others must soon grow up to take the places of the fallen. After this, the land will be quiet for a while."

Later that morning, Ignosi visited, the royal diadem now bound upon his brow. As he advanced with kingly dignity, flanked by an obsequious guard, I recalled the tall Zulu who had sought service at Durban months prior, reflecting on the strange revolutions of fortune.

"Hail, O king!" I said, rising.

"Yes, Macumazahn. King at last, by the might of your three right hands," he replied readily.

All was going well, he said, hoping to arrange a great feast in two weeks to show himself to the people. When I asked what he intended to do with Gagool, he declared her the evil genius of the land, promising to kill her and all the witch doctors with her. She had lived so long that none remembered her youth, and she had trained the witch-hunters, making the land wicked in the sight of heaven.

"Yet she knows much," I replied. "It is easier to destroy knowledge, Ignosi, than to gather it."

"That is so," he said thoughtfully. "She, and she only, knows the secret of the 'Three Witches' yonder, where the great road runs, where the kings are buried, and the Silent Ones sit."

"Yes, and the diamonds are. Forget not thy promise, Ignosi; thou must lead us to the mines, even if thou hast to spare Gagool alive to show the way."

"I will not forget, Macumazahn, and I will think on what thou sayest."

After Ignosi's departure, I went to see Good. He was quite delirious. The fever from his wound had taken a firm hold, complicated by the internal injury. For four or five days, his condition was critical. I am convinced that without Foulata's indefatigable nursing, he would have died.

Women are women, regardless of color, yet it seemed curious to watch this dusky beauty tending the fevered man night and day, performing the merciful errands of a sick-room with the instinct of a trained nurse. For the first couple of nights, I and Sir Henry tried to help, but Foulata bore our interference with impatience. She insisted we leave him to her, claiming our movements made him restless, which I believe was true. Day and night she watched him, giving him a native cooling drink made of milk infused with tulip bulb juice, and keeping flies away. I can see the picture now: Good tossing in his bed, emaciated, eyes shining large and luminous, jabbering nonsense; and beside him, seated on the ground, the soft-eyed, shapely Kukuana beauty, her face weary yet animated by infinite compassion—or perhaps something more?

For two days we feared he would die, moving about with heavy hearts. Only Foulata refused to believe it.

"He will live," she said.

For over three hundred yards around Twala's chief hut, there was absolute silence. By the king's order, everyone living in the habitations behind it, except Sir Henry and myself, had been removed so no noise might disturb the sick man. On the fifth day of his illness, I went to check on him before retiring.

I entered the hut carefully. The lamp on the floor showed Good tossing no more, lying quite still.

So it had come at last! In the bitterness of my heart, I gave a sob.

"Hush—h—h!" came from the patch of dark shadow behind Good's head.

Creeping closer, I saw he was not dead, but sleeping soundly, with Foulata's taper fingers clasped tightly in his poor white hand. The crisis had passed. He slept like that for eighteen hours. I scarcely like to say it, for fear of disbelief, but during the entire period, this devoted girl sat by him, fearing that if she moved and drew away her hand, it would wake him. What she must have suffered from cramp and weariness, not to mention hunger, nobody will ever know. When he finally woke, she had to be carried away; her limbs were so stiff she could not move them.

Once the turn was taken, Good's recovery was rapid and complete. It was not until he was nearly well that Sir Henry told him of all he owed to Foulata. When Good heard the story of her sitting by his side for eighteen hours, fearing to move lest she wake him, the honest sailor's eyes filled with tears. He turned straight to the hut where Foulata was preparing the mid-day meal, taking me with him to interpret, though she understood him marvelously despite his limited vocabulary.

"Tell her," said Good, "that I owe her my life, and that I will never forget her kindness to my dying day."

I interpreted, and under her dark skin, she actually seemed to blush. Turning to him with one of those swift, graceful motions that always reminded me of a wild bird, Foulata answered softly, glancing at him with her large brown eyes.

"Nay, my lord; my lord forgets! Did he not save *my* life, and am I not my lord's handmaiden?"

It was evident she had entirely forgotten the share Sir Henry and I had taken in her preservation. That is the way of women! I remember my dear wife was just the same. Well, I retired from that little interview sad at heart. I did not like Miss Foulata's soft glances, knowing the fatal amorous propensities of sailors, and of Good in particular.

There are two things in the world that cannot be prevented: you cannot keep a Zulu from fighting, or a sailor from falling in love upon the slightest provocation!

A few days later, Ignosi held his great "indaba," or council, and was formally recognized as king by the "indunas," or head men of Kukuanaland. The spectacle was imposing, including a grand review of troops. On this day, the remaining fragments of the Greys were paraded and thanked for their splendid conduct. The king made a large present of cattle to each man, promoting them all to officer rank in the new corps of Greys being formed. An order was promulgated that, while we honored the country by our presence, we three were to be greeted with the royal salute and treated with the same ceremony accorded to the king. Furthermore, the power of life and death was publicly conferred upon us. Ignosi, in the presence of his people, reaffirmed his promises that no man's blood should be shed without trial and that witch-hunting should cease.

When the ceremony ended, we waited upon Ignosi to express our anxiety to investigate the mystery of the mines to which Solomon's Road ran, asking if he had discovered anything.

"My friends," he answered, "I have discovered this. It is there that the three great figures sit, called the 'Silent Ones,' to whom Twala would have offered the girl Foulata as a sacrifice. It is there, too, in a great cave deep in the mountain, that the kings of the land are buried; there ye shall find Twala's body, sitting with those who went before him. There, also, is a deep pit, which long-dead men may have dug for the stones ye speak of, such as I have heard men in Natal tell of at Kimberley. There, too, in the Place of Death, is a secret chamber known to none but the king and Gagool. But Twala, who knew it, is dead, and I know it not, nor do I know what is in it. Yet there is a legend that once, many generations ago, a white man crossed the mountains, was led by a woman to the secret chamber, and shown the wealth hidden within. But before he could take it, she betrayed him, and he was driven back by the king of that day. Since then, no man has entered the place."

"The story is surely true, Ignosi, for on the mountains we found the white man," I said.

"Yes, we found him. And now I have promised you that if ye can come to that chamber, and the stones are there—"

"The gem upon thy forehead proves that they are there," I interjected, pointing to the great diamond I had taken from Twala's dead brows.

"Mayhap; if they are there," he said, "ye shall have as many as ye can take hence—if indeed ye would leave me, my brothers."

"First we must find the chamber," I said.

"There is but one who can show it to thee—Gagool."

"And if she will not?"

"Then she must die," said Ignosi sternly. "I have saved her alive but for this. Stay, she shall choose," and calling a messenger, he ordered Gagool to be brought before him.

In a few minutes she came, hurried along by two guards whom she cursed as she walked.

"Leave her," said the king to the guards.

As soon as their support was withdrawn, the withered old bundle—for she looked more like a bundle than anything else, out of which her two bright and wicked eyes gleamed like those of a snake—sank in a heap onto the floor.

"What will ye with me, Ignosi?" she piped. "Ye dare not touch me. If ye touch me I will slay you as ye sit. Beware of my magic."

"Thy magic could not save Twala, old she-wolf, and it cannot hurt me," was the answer. "Listen; I will this of thee, that thou reveal to us the chamber where are the shining stones."
