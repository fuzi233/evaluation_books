Hans Meyrick’s studio was in a state of cheerful chaos when Deronda arrived. Sketches and canvases littered every surface, the lower half of the windows darkened with baize to control the light. Hans himself, his hair longer than Deronda remembered, his face alight with enthusiasm, greeted him with the same infectious energy that had always characterized their friendship.

“You’ve caught me in the thick of it,” Hans said, gesturing to the mess around them. “I’ve commandeered your sitting room as a temporary atelier—hope you don’t mind. I’ll be moving to Chelsea soon, but until then, the work doesn’t stop.”

Deronda smiled, accustomed to Hans’s whirlwind presence. “What’s all this?” he asked, eyeing a series of sketches pinned along the wall.

“My *Berenice* series,” Hans declared, with a flourish. “Five scenes from the life of the Jewish queen—tragedy, passion, political intrigue. It’s going to be magnificent.”

Deronda studied the drawings. The central figure—Berenice—bore an unmistakable resemblance to Mirah. His chest tightened, though he kept his voice even. “You’ve taken liberties with history.”

“Artistic license,” Hans said, unrepentant. “Berenice was a woman of fire, and Mirah has that same intensity beneath her quiet surface.”

“Does Mirah know you’re using her likeness?”

“Of course. She even posed for me.”

Deronda’s fingers curled slightly at his sides. “And she approves?”

Hans waved a hand. “She understands the vision. Besides, who’s to see them? They’re for my own satisfaction—unless some enlightened patron discovers my genius.”

Deronda exhaled slowly. “Even privately, it’s a risk. Mirah’s position is precarious. She’s trying to establish herself as a singer, to reclaim her independence. Having her face tied to a historical figure—especially one with such a fraught legacy—could complicate things.”

Hans tilted his head, studying Deronda as if seeing him anew. “You’re protective of her.”

“I’m responsible for her,” Deronda corrected, though the distinction felt thin.

Hans’s grin was knowing. “Ah, but responsibility can wear many coats. Some lined with devotion, others with something warmer.”

Deronda’s jaw tightened. “Don’t be absurd.”

Hans held up his hands. “Peace, peace. I only mean that if you’re guarding her as a ward, I’m hardly a threat. If you’re guarding her for yourself—”

“There’s nothing to guard *for myself*,” Deronda interrupted, sharper than he intended.

Hans’s expression softened, almost pitying. “Then you won’t mind if I confess that I’m hopelessly in love with her.”

The words landed like a blow, though Deronda had suspected as much. He forced himself to shrug. “A passing fancy. You’ve had dozens.”

“Not like this.” Hans’s usual levity was gone, replaced by a rare earnestness. “I know the odds. She’s Jewish; I’m not. She’s devout; I’m irreverent. But if there’s even a sliver of a chance—”

“There isn’t.”

Hans arched a brow. “You sound very certain.”

Deronda turned away, feigning interest in another sketch. “Mirah’s faith is unshakable. She would never marry outside it.”

“Then I’ll convert,” Hans said lightly, though his eyes were serious.

Deronda rounded on him. “This isn’t a joke.”

“No,” Hans agreed. “It isn’t.”

Silence stretched between them, thick with unspoken challenge. Finally, Deronda said, “You’ll only hurt yourself.”

Hans smiled, though it didn’t reach his eyes. “That’s my privilege.”

---

Later, at the Meyrick home, Mirah’s laughter filled the parlor—a sound so rare it felt like a gift. She was recounting one of Hans’s theatrical impersonations, her hands animating the story.

“He becomes a dozen characters in a minute,” she said, her eyes bright. “One moment a tragic poet, the next a clown. It’s impossible not to laugh.”

Mrs. Meyrick beamed. “Hans has always had that effect. Even as a boy, he could turn tears into laughter.”

Mirah’s expression softened. “You’re fortunate to have such memories. I’ve often wondered what it would be like—to have a family that shares stories, that remembers together.”

Deronda found himself speaking before he could stop. “So have I.”

The admission hung in the air, a quiet acknowledgment of the voids they both carried. Mirah met his gaze, and for a fleeting moment, something unnameable passed between them—an understanding, a recognition.

Then the moment passed, and the conversation moved on. But Deronda couldn’t shake the feeling that something had shifted, something as delicate and irreversible as the first crack in glass.
