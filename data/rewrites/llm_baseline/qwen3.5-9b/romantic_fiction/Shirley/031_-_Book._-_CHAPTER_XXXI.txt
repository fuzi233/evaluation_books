The decision had been made. Sir Philip Nunnely knew it; Shirley knew it; even Mr. Sympson sensed the turning tide. That evening, as the entire Fieldhead family gathered for dinner at Nunnely Priory, the fate of the household was sealed.

Three factors converged to push the baronet toward a final conclusion. First, he had noticed a shift in Miss Keeldar—she appeared pensive and delicate, a demeanor that struck a chord in his sentimental, poetic nature. Before he could compose a spontaneous sonnet in her honor, one of his sisters persuaded his beloved to play a ballad on the piano. It was one of Sir Philip's own compositions—the simplest, least pretentious, and by far the finest of his many attempts.

Just prior to this performance, Shirley had stood gazing out a window at the park. She watched the stormy moonlight, perhaps mirrored by Professor Louis in his oak parlour, illuminating the domain's solitary giants: broad, sturdy oaks and towering beeches wrestling against the wind. She heard the forest's roar and felt the clouds racing across the sky. Turning from the spectacle, touched though not entirely rapt, she was awakened, if not fully inspired.

When called upon to sing, she delivered the ballad with remarkable skill. The lyrics spoke of faithful love that refuses to abandon its object, a love undeterred by disaster, growing stronger in calamity and clinging tighter in poverty. Set to a fine old tune, the words were simple and sweet; perhaps lacking force on paper, they demanded nothing when sung well. Shirley sang them beautifully, breathing softness into the emotion and pouring force around the passion. Her voice was exquisite that evening, dramatically expressive. She captivated everyone, charming one in particular.

Upon finishing, she moved to the fire, settling onto a cushioned stool. The ladies surrounded her, falling silent. The Misses Sympson and the Misses Nunnely regarded her with the wary curiosity of poultry observing an exotic bird—an egret or an ibis among chickens. Why did she sing with such intensity? Was it proper for a young lady to display such originality, such depth, unlike a typical schoolgirl? In their minds, strangeness equaled wrongness, and unusual behavior implied impropriety. Shirley was being judged.

Old Lady Nunnely stared at her sternly from her great chair. Her gaze conveyed a clear message: *"This woman is not of our kind. I object to her as my son's wife."*

Her son, catching the look, understood its implication instantly. Fear gripped him; what he sought to secure, he feared losing. He realized he must act quickly.

The room itself added to the atmosphere; formerly a picture gallery, it had been converted by Sir Monckton into a saloon, retaining a shadowy, withdrawing quality. A deep recess containing a window, a single couch, a table, and a fairy cabinet created a room within a room—a perfect spot for private conversation unnoticed by others.

Sir Philip orchestrated a diversion, inducing two of his sisters to perform a duet and giving the Misses Sympson busy work. While the elder ladies conversed, he noted Shirley rising to inspect the paintings. Seizing the opportunity, he joined her to recount a tale of an ancestress whose dark beauty resembled a southern flower. As Shirley leaned over the cabinet in the recess to examine a missal and rosary on the inlaid shelf—and while the Misses Nunnely emitted a prolonged, meaningless shriek of conventional affectation—Sir Philip leaned close and whispered hurriedly.

Initially, Miss Keeldar froze, appearing transformed into a statue by the whisper's magic. Soon, however, she looked up and replied. They parted ways. Shirley returned to the fire, and the baronet watched her retreat before moving to stand behind his sisters. Only Mr. Sympson, the sole observer, deciphered the pantomime.

That gentleman drew his own conclusions. Though he lacked the acumen to notice the correction in Sir Philip's relieved expression, his shallow, hasty, and positive nature led him straight to a triumphant conclusion. He was not a man of secrets. Elated, he could not contain his excitement. The following morning, needing his son's tutor as a secretary, he loudly announced—with flimsy pomp—that he was preparing to return to the south soon. The important business detaining him in Yorkshire was nearing successful completion. His labors were about to yield the happiest result: a truly eligible addition to the family connections.

"In Sir Philip Nunnely?" Louis Moore guessed.

Mr. Sympson chuckled, taking a pinch of snuff, his laughter cut short only by a sudden choke of dignity and an order for the tutor to resume work.

For a couple of days, Mr. Sympson remained as smooth as oil, yet he sat as if on pins, walking with the frantic gait of a hen stepping on a hot skillet. He constantly scanned the windows for carriage wheels. Tales of Bluebeard's wife or Sisera's mother meant nothing to him; he waited for the formal opening, for his consultation, for the lawyers, and for the pompous discussions of settlements.

Finally, a letter arrived. Mr. Sympson handed it to Miss Keeldar himself, recognizing the handwriting and the crest on the seal. He did not witness her reading it, as she took it to her room, nor did he see her reply, which she penned in solitude and took nearly a full day to complete. When questioned, she simply confirmed, "Yes."

He waited in tense silence, afraid to speak, intimidated by an inscrutable expression on Shirley's face—something as baffling as the writing on the wall to Belshazzar. He considered summoning Daniel, Louis Moore, to interpret the signs, but his pride forbade such familiarity. Even Daniel seemed perplexed, looking like a student facing blank grammars and silent dictionaries.

Mr. Sympson had spent an anxious hour visiting friends at De Walden Hall and returned earlier than expected. His family and Miss Keeldar were assembled in the oak parlour. He addressed her, requesting a "strictly private interview."

She rose without question or surprise. "Very well, sir," she said, with the resigned tone of someone informed that the dentist has arrived to extract a painful tooth. Leaving her sewing and thimble on the window-seat, she followed him.

In the drawing-room, they sat opposite in armchairs, several yards apart.

"I have been to De Walden Hall," Mr. Sympson stated, pausing. Miss Keeldar's eyes remained fixed on the white-and-green carpet. That information required no response.

"I have learned," he continued slowly, "a circumstance which surprises me. It seems Nunnely Priory is closed up—that the family has returned to their estate in ----shire. It appears the baronet... Sir Philip himself... has accompanied his mother and sisters."

"Indeed!" Shirley replied.

"May I ask if you share the amazement with which I received this news?"

"No, sir."

"Is it news to you?"

"Yes, sir."

"I mean—I mean," Mr. Sympson fidgeted, abandoning his clear phrasing for his usual wordy, confused, and irritable style—"I mean to have a thorough explanation. I will not be put off. I shall insist on being heard and having my own way. My questions must be answered. I require clear, satisfactory replies. I am not to be trifled with."

Silence fell.

"It is a strange and extraordinary thing! I thought all was right, knew no other, and suddenly—the family is gone!"

"I suppose, sir, they had a right to go."

"Sir Philip is gone!" he emphasized.

Shirley raised her brows. "Bon voyage!" she said lightly.

"This will not do; this must be altered, ma'am."

He drew his chair forward, then pushed it back, looking incensed yet helpless.

"Come, come now, uncle," Shirley expostulated gently. "Do not begin to fret and fume, or we shall make no sense of this. Ask me what you want to know. I am as willing to explain as you. I promise truthful replies."

"I demand to know, Miss Keeldar, whether Sir Philip has made you an offer?"

"He has."

"You avow it?"

"I avow it. But now, let us move on. Consider that point settled."

"He made you an offer that night we dined at the priory?"

"It is enough to say that he made it. Go on."

"He proposed in the recess—the former picture gallery converted into a saloon by Sir Monckton?"

No answer.

"You were both examining a cabinet. I saw everything. My sagacity is rarely at fault. Subsequently, you received a letter from him. On what subject? Of what nature were its contents?"

"No matter."

"Ma'am, is that the way you speak to me?" Shirley's foot tapped quickly on the carpet.

"There you sit, silent and sullen—you who promised truthful replies."

"Sir, I have answered you thus far. Proceed."

"I should like to see that letter."

"You cannot see it."

"I must and shall, ma'am; I am your guardian."

"Having ceased to be a ward, I have no guardian."

"Ungrateful being! Reared by me as my own daughter..."
