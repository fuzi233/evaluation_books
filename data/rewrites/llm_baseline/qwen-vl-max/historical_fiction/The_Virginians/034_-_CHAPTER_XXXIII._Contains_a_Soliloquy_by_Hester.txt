When Colonel Lambert learned the secret his youngest daughter had unwittingly revealed, his first impulse was anger—not at her, but at the young man who had stirred such emotion in her heart. “A plague upon all scapegraces, English or Indian!” he declared to his wife. “I wish this one had broken his nose against any doorpost but ours.”

“Perhaps,” Mrs. Lambert replied gently, “we are meant to cure him of being a scapegrace. And the fall at our door may have been no accident. You laughed at me before when I said so, Mr. Lambert; but if Heaven did not send this young gentleman to us, who did? It may be for the good of us all that he came.”

“It’s hard, Molly!” groaned the Colonel. “We raise them, love them, tend them through sickness and health. We save every penny, patch our own coats, lose sleep over their headaches, work night and day to fulfill their wishes. They call us ‘darling daddy’ and ‘dearest pappy,’ and say no father could be like ours. Then comes Tuesday morning—king of my house and family. Tuesday evening—Prince Whippersnapper arrives, and my reign is over. A lifetime of devotion forgotten for a pair of blue eyes, lean legs, and golden hair.”

“'Tis written that women should follow their husbands,” said the matron, laying a hand on his arm. “And our courtship was not long, dear Martin.”

“It’s human nature,” sighed the Colonel. “What can you expect?”

“And yet,” added Mrs. Lambert softly, “I think I did my duty to my husband—even though I left my father for him.”

“Excellent wench!” exclaimed the Colonel. “Perdition catch my soul, but I do love thee, Molly! But mind you, your father never did me any favors. And if ever I am to have sons-in-law—”

“Of course you will!” interrupted mamma. “My girls must marry, Mr. Lambert!”

“Well, when they come,” he retorted, “I’ll hate them as your father hated me—for stealing his treasure away.”

“Don’t be irreligious, Martin Lambert!” she insisted. “You’re unnatural, sir!”

“Nay, my dear,” he answered, tapping his jaw. “I have an old tooth here. 'Tis natural it should come out. But when the tooth-puller tugs it, 'tis natural I should feel pain. Do you suppose I don’t love Hetty more than any tooth in my head?”

No woman, however, resists the idea of her daughter marrying—even if fathers grumble. Mothers and grandmothers, especially, relive their youth at their children’s weddings. Their souls dress again in lace and muslin, postillions’ ribbons bloom anew, and they flutter into the carriage, off to new beginnings. Who among them has not tucked bridal favors and gowns away in lavender-scented drawers of the heart?

“It will be sad to part with her,” Mrs. Lambert murmured.

“You’ve already settled that,” laughed the Colonel. “Had I not best go order raisins and currants for the wedding cake?”

“And then I shall leave the house in their care when I go to Virginia with her,” she added. “How far is it, Martin? I imagine thousands of miles.”

“A hundred and seventy-three thousand three hundred and ninety-one and three-quarters, my dear,” he replied gravely, “by the near way—through Prester John’s country. By the other route, through Persia—”

“Oh, give me the one with least sea,” she interrupted. “Your horrid ships—I can’t bear them!”

“I hope Rachel Esmond and I shall become better friends,” she continued. “She had such spirit when we were girls at school.”

“Shall we begin the baby-linen?” asked the Colonel, now puzzled.

Mrs. Lambert likely thought there was nothing odd about it—she’d seen lovely lace caps and bibs in Mrs. Bobbinit’s shop.

That Sunday afternoon, after the discovery, little Hetty lay on her pillow—cheeks flushed, eyes closed, face full of sorrow. Her mother watched her with calm composure, even seeming pleased at her daughter’s distress. The girl was not only unhappy but furious with herself for having let her feelings show. Perhaps she hadn’t known her own heart until the sudden wave of emotion revealed it. Now, she was ashamed—as if she’d committed a crime and been caught. She raged at her weakness, vowed never to forgive herself for such humiliation.

Like a young deer wounded by a hunter’s arrow, she chafed in the forest, angry at the sting in her side, snarling at her kin, growling at her mother. Little Hetty tore at her thoughts, gnawed her pride, growled inwardly—so fierce that one might hesitate to approach her, brother or parent alike.

“What business has any young woman,” she cried, “to indulge in such nonsense? Mamma, I ought to be whipped and sent to bed. I know perfectly well Mr. Warrington doesn’t care a fig for me. He probably prefers French actresses or some milliner-girl from the toy-shop. And rightly so—they’re better than I am. Why, what a fool I am to cry like a ninny over nothing—because Mr. Wolfe said Harry played cards on a Sunday! I know he’s not clever like papa. I believe he’s stupid—I’m certain he is. But he’s not as stupid as I am. Why, of course, I can’t marry him. How can I go to America and leave you and Theo? He must like someone else—someone in Virginia, or Tunbridge, or Jericho. He’s a prince in his own land—he wouldn:think of marrying a poor half-pay officer’s daughter with twopence to her name. Remember how you used to tell me, when I was a baby, that I cried for the moon? I’m a baby now—a ridiculous, silly baby. Don’t talk to me, Mrs. Lambert, I am.”

She threatened Theo fiercely, swearing she’d never forgive her if she dared reveal the secret. As for Charley, still fixated on cheese-cakes, he’d noticed nothing. The parents and sister promised silence, each bound by the same tender secrecy.

“I begin to think we should have stayed home,” sighed Mrs. Lambert.

“Nay, my dear,” replied the Colonel. “Human nature is what it is. Hetty’s mother told me she once liked a young curate—before falling madly for a soldier of Kingsley’s. And I myself had a dozen wounds before Miss Molly Benson took my heart entirely. Our children will follow in our footsteps, I suppose. Yesterday, you scolded me for grumbling at Hetty’s early fancies. To do her justice, she hides her feelings well. I defy Mr. Warrington to guess how she feels.”

“A daughter of mine,” said the mother with dignity, “will not throw herself at a gentleman’s feet.”

“Nor the teacup either, my dear,” returned the Colonel. “Miss Het treats Mr. Warrington like a vixen. He never visits without getting boxed in some fashion. I protest she’s barely civil—but knowing what goes on in that young hypocrite’s mind, I won’t be angry at her rudeness.”

“She needn’t be rude at all, Martin,” said the mother. “Our girl is good enough for any gentleman in England or America. If their ages suit, why shouldn’t they marry?”

“If he wants her,” said the Colonel, “shouldn’t he ask her? I’m sorry we came. I’d turn the horses around and ride home tonight.”

But the mother smiled. “Depend on it, Martin, these things are wisely ordered. That Harry Warrington was brought to our gate—that he and our children met again—was not by chance. If Heaven decrees a marriage, it will be.”

“At what age,” asked the Colonel, “do women begin and stop matchmaking? If Hetty falls in love and out again, she won’t be the first. I wish we were on our way home. If I had my way, I’d leave tonight.”

“He has promised to drink tea here tonight,” said the mother softly. “Would you take away our child’s pleasure?”

The father, in his own way, was kind-hearted. “You know, my dear,” he said, “if either of them fancied our ears, we’d cut them off and serve them in fricassee.”

Mary Lambert laughed. When he was most tender, he was most grotesque. Pulling her ear, behind which her silver-streaked hair was neatly combed, he likely hurt her not at all. She may have been thinking of her own sweet courtship days—those hallowed memories of youth and love.

If youthful love is beautiful to see, how much more lovely is affection that endures through years, sorrows, fading beauty, life’s doubts and differences!

Hester had promised to hide her feelings from Mr. Warrington—and she kept her word, perhaps too well. Not only did Harry come to tea, but he invited them to an evening entertainment at the Assembly Rooms, given in their honor.

“A dance, and for us!” cried Theo. “Oh, Harry, how delightful! Can we start right now?”

“For a savage Virginian,” said the Colonel, “you’re remarkably civilized, Harry Warrings.”

“My dear,” said the Colonel, “shall we dance a minuet together?”

“We’ve done that before, Martin Lambert,” replied his wife.

Her husband hummed a tune, swept a plate from the table, and bowed with it as if it were a hat, while she curtseyed with grace.

Only Hetty remained glum.

“Why, child,” asked Theo, “have you no thanks for Mr. Warrington?”

“I never cared much for dancing,” said Hetty.

“What use is standing opposite a stupid man and dancing down a room with him?”

“Merci du compliment!” said Mr. Warrington.

“I didn’t mean you were stupid,” stammered Hetty, biting her lip—remembering her earlier words, and catching Theo’s amused glance.

But she was furious—though it was only a teasing remark.

“I hate dancing,” she declared, tossing her head. “There—I admit it.”

“Nay, you used to like it,” interposed her mother.

“That was when she was a child,” said her father. “Don’t you see she’s grown into an old woman?”

“Or perhaps Miss Hester has the gout?” suggested Theo.

“Fiddle!” snapped Hester, stamping her feet.

“What’s a dance without a fiddle?” asked her father imperturbably.

Darkness fell over Harry Warrington’s face.

“I come to please them,” he thought, “to give them joy and a dance. And she tells me she hates dancing. We don’t treat kindness so in our country. Nor speak to parents so.”

Indeed, customs have changed in the United States since then—perhaps young folks there are now quite Hettified.

But Hetty did not stop there. She mocked everyone at the Wells, especially Harry’s companions, making him feel deeply offended. Later, seeing Mrs. Lambert alone, he asked, “What have I done to offend her so?”

The kind matron felt even more warmly toward the boy after her daughter’s behavior. She longed to reveal Hester’s secret.

Theo also spoke privately to her sister.

“But Hester would not listen. In her bedroom, alone, she was as angry as she had been in the drawing-room.”

“Suppose he hates me?” she said. “I expect he will. I hate myself. I scorn myself for being such an idiot. How could he do otherwise than hate me? Didn’t I abuse him? Call him goose? All sorts of names? And I knew all along he wasn’t clever. I know I’m smarter than he is. It’s only because he’s tall, with blue eyes and a pretty nose that I like him. What a fool a girl must be to like a man just because he has blue eyes and hooked features! So I’m a fool—and I won’t hear a word to the contrary, Theo!”

But Theo thought her sister anything but foolish. She believed Hetty was worthy of any prince in Christendom.

“You’re silly sometimes, Hetty,” she said, “especially when you speak unkindly to those who mean you well—like you did to Mr. Warrington tonight. When he invited us to the Assembly Rooms, nothing could have been more gallant. Why did you say you didn’t care for music, dancing, or tea? You know you love them all.”

“I said it to punish myself,” Hetty replied. “To vex myself, to whip myself as I deserve. And besides—how can you expect an idiot like me to say anything but idiotic things? I actually enjoyed seeing him angry. I thought, *Ah!* Now I’ve hurt his feelings. Now he’ll think Hetty Lambert is an odious, sour-tempered vixen. And that will teach him—and you—and mamma—and papa—that I’m not going to set my cap at Mr. Harry. No—our papa is ten times better than he is. I’ll stay with papa. If he asked me to go to Virginia tomorrow, I wouldn’t. My sister is worth all the Virginians that ever lived.”

Then, I suppose, followed kisses between the sisters. A knock came at the door—the mother had heard their talk through the wall.

“Children,” she called, “it’s time to sleep.”

Theo’s eyes closed quickly, and she was asleep. But poor little Hetty—imagine the hours ticking by, her eyes wide open, tossing and wakeful with the agony of her wound.

“It’s a judgment upon me,” she whispered, “for speaking scornfully of him. But why should there be a judgment? I was only joking. I knew I liked him all along. But I thought Theo liked him too—and I’d give up anything for my darling Theo. If she had, no torture would have made me speak. I’d have fetched a rope-ladder to help her run away with Harry—or brought the clergyman to marry them. Then I’d have retired alone—alone—alone—to care for papa and mamma, the poor villagers, read sermons—though I hate them—and die without saying a word—not a word—and I shall die soon, I know I shall.”

But when dawn broke, the little maid slept peacefully beside her sister, a tear or two staining her rosy cheek.

Most of us play with sharp tools in youth—and cut ourselves. At first, the wound stings, we drop the knife, and cry like babies. A few unlucky ones sever their heads or stab themselves fatally—and perish. But many, alas, handle Love’s fiery arrows, sharpened on his whetstone—pricked, scarred, tattooed with wounds—and live to be lively again.

Wir auch haben das irdische Glück gekostet; wir auch haben gelebt—und so weiter. Sing your death-song, sweet Thekla! Perish, poor victim of consumption, if you choose! Had you lived longer, you’d look back on a broken heart without thinking of a grave-digger. Let us hope no sexton is needed for Miss Hetty.

Yet the moment she wakes, there it is—the pain returns, melting only briefly under the warmth of youth and tears.
