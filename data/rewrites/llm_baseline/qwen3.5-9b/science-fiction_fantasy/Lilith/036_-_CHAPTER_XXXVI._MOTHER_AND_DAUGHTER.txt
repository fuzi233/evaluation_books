Lona's disgust for the people here, particularly the women, burned so fiercely that she wanted nothing more than to flee immediately. Yet, I felt a crushing weight of responsibility. To retreat now would be a deliberate failure where victory was within reach; worse, it would shatter the spirits of the Little Ones, leaving them vulnerable to far greater peril. If we turned tail, the Princess would surely hunt us down without mercy. Conversely, if we engaged her, the prophecy's fulfillment became inevitable—but perhaps that was necessary. Mother and daughter *must* meet. Perhaps Lona's radiant beauty would strike the Princess's heart with such force that conflict would become impossible. If violence threatened, I would stand between them. And if I found myself powerless to stop her, I was prepared, for Lona's sake, to crush her hand with ruthless finality. I knew the odds were stacked against us; fate likely demanded this confrontation happen through us.

Without revealing the true nature of my connection to the Princess, I laid out my reasoning to Lona. Instantly, she agreed to ride with me to the palace.

From the highest tower, the Princess had watched the dawn break over the sleeping city, spotting the approaching army of the Little Ones. The sight had seized her with paralyzing terror. She had failed to destroy them; they were upon her. The prophecy was about to unfold. When she regained her composure, she descended to the black hall and took her seat in the northern focus of the elliptical chamber, directly beneath the opening in the roof. She needed to think. But her "thinking" required a specific kind of delusion—a clear consciousness of herself not as she truly was, but as she chose to believe herself. To facilitate this, she had suspended a mirror just above and slightly forward of her position. Invisible in the hall's darkness, it caught the full glare of the midday sun reflecting off her form. Bathed in the vision of her own splendor, she waited for the meridian sun.

Shadows danced around her in the gloom, but whenever her inner eye caught a glimpse of one, she refused to acknowledge it. Close beneath the mirror stood the Shadow that usually accompanied her walks, yet, absorbed in its own existence, she remained blind to it. By noon, the city had fallen. The inhabitants cowered in fear, while the Little Ones and their strange cavalry camped in the square. The sun struck the Princess, and for a fleeting moment, she saw herself glorified. Then the vision faded, but she remained seated. Night fell, cloaking the glass ceiling in darkness, yet she did not stir. A gloom teeming with shadows swirled through the palace; servants shivered violently but dared not flee due to the beasts guarding the halls. All night long, the Princess sat motionless, desperate to see her beauty again, trying desperately to think. But her courage and will had finally exhausted themselves; they would no longer sustain her.

At dawn, we selected twelve of the tallest and bravest boys to accompany us. We mounted our great horses, while the Little Ones rode their smaller mounts and elephants. The Princess continued to wait for the sun to grant her the joy of her own reflection. The tide of light crept up the horizon, but until the sun stood directly overhead, not a single ray could penetrate the black hall. As the celestial body rose to meet our gaze and swiftly ascended, it climbed the dome of the great hall. It peered into the eye of the chamber—and suddenly, with blinding radiance, the Princess beheld her own image.

But she sprang to her feet with a cry of utter despair. Alas, her whiteness! A large spot covered half her side, stark and black like the marble surrounding it. Clutching her robes, she collapsed back into her chair. The Shadow glided out into the darkness, and she finally saw him depart.

We found the gate open as usual and passed through the paved grove to the palace door, entering the vestibule. Inside her cage lay the spotted leopardess, appearing either asleep or lifeless. The Little Ones paused to observe it. Suddenly, the beast leaped up, rampant against the bars. The horses reared and plunged; the elephants stepped back in alarm. In the next instant, the leopardess fell flat onto its back, writhed through quivering spasms, and lay utterly still.

We rode into the great hall. The Princess still leaned back in her chair, bathed in the shaft of sunlight, when the sound of hooves pounding on stone reached her ears from the courtyard below. She startled, listening intently, shaking with fear. Never had such a sound been heard within her sanctuary. She pressed her hand to her side and gasped. The trampling drew nearer, invading the hall itself. Moving figures that were not mere shadows approached her through the darkness.

To us, however, it appeared as a brilliance, a glorious woman centering the gloom. Lona dismounted and bounded toward her. I followed suit.

"Mother! Mother!" Lona cried, her clear, lovely voice echoing through the dome.

The Princess shivered. Her face darkened with hatred, her eyebrows knitting together on her forehead. Rising to her feet, she stood rigid.

"Mother! Mother!" Lona cried again, leaping onto the dais and throwing her arms around the Princess.

In that split second, I would have reached them—had I not seen Lona lifted high into the air and dashed against the marble floor. The horrible sound of her impact rang out. She fell at my feet, lying still.

The Princess sat down, wearing the smile of a demoness. Dropping to my knees beside Lona, I lifted her from the cold stones and pressed her to my chest. With indignant hate, I glanced at the Princess; she returned my gaze with her sweetest, most chilling smile. Rage surged within me—I would have lunged at her, gripped her throat, and strangled her. But love for the child outweighed my hatred for the mother, and I tightened my embrace around my precious burden. Her arms hung limp; blood trickled over my hands, falling to the floor in soft, slow splashes.

The horses sensed the scent—theirs first, then those of the Little Ones. Mine reared, shivering and wild-eyed, circling frantically before thundering blindly down the dark hall, followed closely by the smaller horses. Lona's mount stood frozen, gazing down at its mistress, trembling uncontrollably. The boys threw themselves from their saddles, failing to see the black wall ahead, and crashed along with mine, reduced to fragments against it. The elephants advanced to the foot of the dais, stopping abruptly as they trumpeted wildly. The Little Ones sprang upon them, standing in horror. The Princess slumped back into her seat, her face pale as a corpse, though her eyes remained alive, wickedly flaming.

She had withered and wasted back to the state I had found her in the woods, her side bearing the mark of some great branding iron. But Lona saw nothing. I saw only Lona.

"Mother! Mother!" she sighed, and her breathing ceased.

I carried her into the courtyard. The sun illuminated her white face, marked by the pitiful shadow of a ghostly smile. Her head hung back. She was dead as the earth itself.

Forgetting the Little Ones, forgetting the murderous Princess, forgetting the body in my arms, I wandered away, searching for my Lona. Doors and windows were crowded with brutish faces jeering at me, yet none dared speak, for they saw the white leopardess trailing close at my heels, head bowed low. I spurned the animal with my foot. It hesitated, then followed me again.

I reached the square, only to find the little army gone. Their absence roused a panic within me. Where were the Little Ones, *her* Little Ones? I had lost her children! Staring helplessly around me, I staggered to a pillar and sank upon its base. Gazing at the still countenance of the leopardess, it seemed to offer a brief, living smile. I never doubted it was an illusion, yet I believed its message: I would yet see her alive! No, it was not she who was lost—it was I. She would find me!

Rising to pursue the Little Ones, I instinctively sought the gate by which we had entered. Looking around, I saw nowhere near the leopardess. The street was rapidly filling with a furious crowd. They noticed I was encumbered by my dead companion, but for a time, they dared not attack. Before I could reach the gate, however, they gathered enough courage. Women began to shove and hustle me; I ignored them, holding fast. A man pushed against my sacred burden; I kicked him away, sending him screaming. But the crowd pressed harder. Fearing for the dead girl beyond further harm, I clutched my treasure tighter and freed my right arm.

That very moment, a commotion erupted behind me. The crowd broke apart, and through the gap came the Little Ones I had left in the palace. Ten of them rode atop four elephants; on the remaining two elephants lay the Princess, bound hand and foot, completely still except for her eyes rolling in their ghastly sockets. Two other Little Ones rode behind her on Lona's horse. Periodically, the wise creatures carrying her extended their trunks backward to feel the tightness of her bonds.
