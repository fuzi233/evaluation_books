“Ha! What did I tell you? I said Buck was two devils in one—and I was right!” François exclaimed the next morning when he found Spitz missing and Buck battered but victorious. He dragged Buck closer to the fire, inspecting the fresh wounds that marked his triumph.

Perrault whistled low. “Spitz fought like a demon.”

“And Buck fought like two,” François shot back. “Now we make good time. No more Spitz, no more trouble.”

As Perrault packed camp, François harnessed the team. Buck trotted straight to the lead position—Spitz’s old place—but François ignored him, pulling Sol-leks forward instead. Buck’s lips curled. In a flash, he lunged, forcing Sol-leks back and planting himself at the front.

François laughed. “Look at him! Kills Spitz and thinks he can take the lead now!” He shoved Buck aside, but the dog refused to yield. Even when François grabbed him by the scruff, Buck growled, defiant. Sol-leks, though reinstated, trembled—he knew who truly commanded respect.

François snatched a club. Buck remembered the man in the red sweater and retreated—but not far. He circled just beyond reach, snarling, eyes locked on the weapon. He was no fool; he had learned the dance of clubs.

Perrault joined in. For an hour, they chased Buck, hurling curses and clubs. He dodged, weaving through the camp, never fleeing—only demanding what he had earned.

Finally, François groaned. “We’re wasting time!”

Perrault threw up his hands. “Fine. Let him lead.”

François unhooked Sol-leks. At once, Buck trotted forward, victorious. The traces were fastened, and the team surged onto the trail.

---

Buck exceeded all expectations. Where Spitz had ruled with teeth, Buck ruled with cunning and raw power. He enforced order swiftly. Pike, the shirker, learned to pull his weight after a few sharp corrections. Joe, the troublemaker, was pinned and throttled into submission. Even Sol-leks and Dave, seasoned workers, respected the new regime.

By the time they reached the Rink Rapids, François could only marvel. “Never seen a dog like Buck! Worth a thousand dollars, I swear!”

The team flew across the ice, smashing records. They covered in a single day what had once taken ten. From Lake Le Barge to White Horse Rapids—sixty miles in a single run. Men clung to sled ropes, dragged in their wake.

But triumph was fleeting. In Skaguay, François and Perrault vanished from Buck’s life, replaced by a Scotch half-breed and a grueling mail run to Dawson. The work was brutal, the loads heavy. The dogs wore thin, their paws cracked, their breath labored.

Dave suffered most. Something inside him had broken. He faltered in the traces, crying out in pain. When they finally pulled him from the team, he refused to accept it. He attacked Sol-leks, gnawing through the traces to reclaim his place.

The drivers understood. A dog’s pride was in his work—even if it killed him.

They harnessed Dave again. He ran until his legs gave out, until the sled rolled over him. At camp, he crawled toward the traces, inch by inch, before collapsing.

The next morning, a single gunshot echoed behind the trees.

The team marched on, but every dog knew.

Buck knew.

The law of the wild was mercy.
