The abrupt cessation of Colonel Brandon's visits to the Park, coupled with his steely resolve to conceal the motive behind his departure, occupied Mrs. Jennings' thoughts for three consecutive days. She was a creature of perpetual speculation, finding the shifting comings and goings of her acquaintances a fertile ground for endless conjecture. With barely a moment's respite, she interrogated the void left by Brandon's absence. Was it bad news? Had some calamity befallen him? She catalogued every conceivable distress, determined that he would not escape her exhaustive list of misfortunes.

"Something profoundly melancholy must be the matter," she declared, her voice heavy with certainty. "I could read it in his face. Poor man! I fear his circumstances have deteriorated. The Delaford estate was never valued above two thousand pounds a year, and his brother left affairs hopelessly entangled. I am convinced he was summoned regarding financial exigencies; surely nothing else could explain it?"

She paused, allowing the hypothesis to settle, before pivoting to another theory. "Or perhaps it concerns Miss Williams. By the way, I suspect it does, simply because he appeared so conscious when I mentioned her name. Maybe she is ill in town; nothing is more plausible, for I have long held the notion that she is constitutionally frail. I would stake my reputation on it being about Miss Williams. Yet, it is unlikely he is currently distressed financially—he is a man of prudence, and surely he has cleared the estate by now. Could it be his sister is worse at Avignon and has recalled him? His precipitous departure suggests exactly that."

Mrs. Jennings wished him relief from all his troubles and a suitable wife into the bargain, her mind oscillating between these scenarios with equal fervor.

Elinor, however, possessed a different calculus. While genuinely concerned for Colonel Brandon's welfare, she found the duration and intensity of her mother's amazement unjustified. In her estimation, the circumstances did not warrant such sustained speculation. Furthermore, her own wonder was consumed by a far more pressing enigma: the extraordinary silence maintained by her sister and Mr. Willoughby regarding their relationship. This silence grew increasingly bizarre and incompatible with their known dispositions as the days passed. Why did they refuse to acknowledge openly to her mother and herself what their consistent behavior toward one another clearly indicated?

Elinor could rationalize that marriage might not be immediately feasible; though Willoughby was independent, his fortune was modest, rated by Sir John at six or seven hundred pounds annually. He lived beyond his means, frequently lamenting his poverty. Yet, this strategic secrecy, which effectively concealed nothing, remained inexplicable. It contradicted their general principles so thoroughly that a nagging doubt occasionally surfaced in Elinor's mind concerning the reality of their engagement—a doubt sufficient to halt any direct inquiry to Marianne.

Nothing, however, expressed their mutual devotion more eloquently than Willoughby's conduct. To Marianne, he offered the distinct tenderness of a lover's heart; to the wider family, he displayed the affectionate attentiveness of a devoted son and brother. The cottage at Barton seemed to constitute his true home; he spent more hours there than at Allenham. Even when no formal engagement drew them to the Park, his morning exercises invariably concluded there, where he would spend the remainder of the day beside Marianne, with his favorite pointer resting at her feet.

One evening, approximately a week after Colonel Brandon's departure, Willoughby's heart appeared unusually open to the sentiments surrounding him. When Mrs. Dashwood casually mentioned her intention to improve the cottage in the spring, he vigorously opposed any alterations to a place he deemed perfect.

"What!" he exclaimed. "Improve this dear cottage! No. *That* I will never consent to. Not a stone must be added to its walls, nor an inch to its size, if my feelings are to be respected."

"Do not be alarmed," Miss Dashwood reassured him gently. "Nothing of the sort will occur; my mother lacks the funds to undertake such a project anyway."

"I am heartily glad of it," he cried. "May she always remain poor, provided she cannot employ her wealth in a manner less offensive to you."

"Thank you, Willoughby," Elinor replied. "Be assured, I would not sacrifice a single sentiment of your local attachment, nor that of anyone I love, for all the improvements in the world. Whatever surplus remains when I reconcile my accounts in the spring, I would prefer to lay it idle rather than expend it in a way so painful to you. However, are you truly so attached to this place that you perceive no defects in it?"

"I am," he affirmed. "To me, it is faultless. Indeed, I regard it as the sole architectural form wherein happiness is attainable. Were I wealthy enough, I would instantly demolish Combe and reconstruct it precisely according to this cottage's blueprint."

"With dark, narrow stairs and a smoking kitchen, I presume," Elinor noted dryly.

"Yes!" he cried, his tone eager. "With every detail intact—in no convenience or inconvenience should the slightest variation be perceptible. Only then, under such a roof, might I perhaps find happiness at Combe comparable to what I have experienced at Barton."

Elinor smiled. "I flatter myself that even under the disadvantages of superior rooms and a broader staircase, you will eventually find your own residence as flawless as you deem this one."

"There are certainly circumstances," Willoughby conceded, "that might greatly endear it to me; but this place holds one claim on my affection that no other can possibly share."

Mrs. Dashwood glanced with evident pleasure at Marianne, whose expressive eyes were fixed intently on Willoughby, betraying a profound understanding of his words.

"How often," Willoughby continued, lowering his voice to address Marianne directly, "did I wish, when I was at Allenham twelve months ago, that Barton Cottage were inhabited! I never passed within view of it without admiring its situation and grieving that no one dwelt therein. How little did I anticipate that the very first intelligence I would receive from Mrs. Smith upon returning to the countryside would be that Barton Cottage was taken! I felt an immediate satisfaction and interest in the event, attributable only to a prescient knowledge of the happiness I would derive from it. Must it not have been so, Marianne?"

Resuming his previous volume, he addressed Mrs. Dashwood again. "And yet, you would ruin this house? You would strip it of its simplicity through imaginary improvement! This dear parlor, where our acquaintance first commenced and where countless happy hours have since been shared, you would degrade to the status of a mere entrance hall, forcing everyone to traverse the room that has historically offered more genuine comfort and accommodation than any spacious apartment in the world could provide."

Mrs. Dashwood reiterated her assurance that no such alterations would be attempted.

"You are a good woman," he replied warmly. "Your promise brings me ease. Extend it further, and it will bring me joy. Assure me not only that your house will remain unchanged, but that I shall always find you and yours as immutable as your dwelling. Promise that you will continue to regard me with the kindness that has rendered everything belonging to you so precious to me."

The promise was granted readily, and Willoughby's demeanor throughout the evening radiated both affection and contentment.

"Shall we see you tomorrow for dinner?" Mrs. Dashwood asked as he prepared to depart.

"I decline the invitation for the morning," he responded smoothly. "We must walk to the Park to call on Lady Middleton."

He agreed to join them by four o'clock.
