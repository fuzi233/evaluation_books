The faithful Gumbo was inconsolable for his master’s departure: judging by the wails and torrents of tears he shed the moment he learned Harry had enlisted as a soldier, any onlooker would have sworn the poor man’s heart was fit to shatter at the separation. It was no wonder he fled to find comfort among the maids at Mr. Lambert’s lodgings; wherever that dusky young man went, he sought solace in the company of women. Their soft, tender hearts knew exactly how to pity the sad African, and the depth of his complexion was no more repulsive to them than Othello’s was to Desdemona. I do believe 18th-century Europe has never grown so squeamish about people of African descent as certain other corners of the world have since. Many Black men—take the celebrated fencer and favourite of the Parisian salons, the Chevalier de Saint-Georges, for example—have long been darlings of the fair sex, and in his own humbler station, Gumbo was no different. The Lambert household servants wept right alongside him, the maids treating him not just as Mr. Harry’s personal man, but as a beloved brother.

Hetty could not help but huff a small, fond laugh when she found Gumbo howling that her master had run off to be a “volumteer” and left him behind. He swore up and down he’d split himself into twenty thousand pieces to save Harry’s life, any day of the week, and he meant every word. All the same, the poor fellow had to keep his strength up, so he snuck large tankards of beer and plates of cold roast beef from the kitchen larder. That he was greedy, idle, and prone to stretching the truth, no one could deny—but Hetty slipped him a half-crown all the same, and treated him with a gentleness no one had ever seen from her before. Her tongue, once famously sharp and full of pert little jokes, turned soft and quiet these days. She moved about the house meek as a lamb, humble to her mother, grateful to the footmen and maids for even the smallest favour, patient when her little sister Polly tugged at her hair while combing it, long-suffering when schoolboy Charley trod on her toes or knocked her workbox askew, and silent as a church mouse in her father’s presence. It was a transmogrified little Hetty no one had ever met before. If her father had ordered her to roast a whole leg of mutton for dinner, or walk to church arm-in-arm with Gumbo, she would have bobbed a perfect curtsy and said, “Yes, if you please, dear papa!”

Leg of mutton, indeed! What kind of meals were the poor volunteers eating right now, with cannonballs whistling over their heads? Church! When the parson read the war prayer aloud at Sunday service, her knees knocked so hard together under the pew she thought they’d crack, and she buried her face in her hands. When he read out the commandment *Thou shalt do no murder* from the communion rail, she was convinced he was staring straight at her. She fussed over every traveller on land or sea, ran for the morning post the second it was delivered to hunt for any scrap of news about the expedition, and hung by the front door every evening to read the news on her father’s face the second he came home from the Ordnance Office. Was Harry well? Had he been made a general already? Was he wounded? A prisoner? Oh, mercy, what if a single shot tore both his legs clean off, like the old Chelsea pensioner they’d seen hobbling on wooden stumps in the gardens not a week prior? She swore she’d walk on wooden legs her whole life if it meant Harry came home safe, and that she’d never get up off her knees until he did. “Haven’t you heard of people whose hair turned completely grey in a single night, Theo?” she’d ask her sister, wide-eyed. “I shouldn’t wonder if mine does. Not one little bit.” She’d dash to the looking glass every hour to check for the first silver strand.
“Hetty dear,” Theo would murmur, “you were never half this nervous when Papa was off on his posting to Minorca, years ago.”
“Ah, Theo!” Hetty would shoot back, cheeks flushing. “It’s very easy to be calm when the man you care about isn’t off fighting the French, but safe right here at home.” Theo blushed bright red, and fell quiet, for they both knew she spoke of George, safe under their very roof.

We won’t drag out the family’s anxiety any longer: for the moment, Harry was as safe as any Life Guard trooper quartered at Regent’s Park barracks. His first expedition as a volunteer could be called technically successful, but never glorious. The British lion, like any other, cannot always find a worthy foe to fight or a grand royal battle to win. Suppose he sets off to hunt a tiger who refuses to show, and winds up pouncing on a stray goose to gobble down? Lions, after all, have to eat. But then, suppose he marches roaring through the forest in search of that tiger, only to find six tigers coming straight for him? That is clearly not a fight he wants to pick. He tucks his tail between his royal legs and trots straight home to his warm den as fast as he can. If he tried to fight six tigers, no one would call him a lion, they’d call him an ass.

That was exactly the nature of Harry’s first taste of war. Thirteen thousand fighting men sailed from the Isle of Wight on the first of June, and at dawn on the fifth, the fleet slipped into Cancale Bay on the coast of Brittany. Harry and his fellow gentlemen volunteers spent hours peering at the strange French coast from the decks, while the commander-in-chief and the Commodore snuck close in a small cutter to reconnoitre. They spotted cattle grazing on the cliffs, and a handful of dragoons who trotted off over the hills the second they saw the ship. A tiny, rickety fort mounting only two guns had the audacity to fire a volley at the Duke of Marlborough and the Commodore as they passed. By two o’clock the whole British fleet was anchored, and the order went out for all the grenadier companies from eleven regiments to pile into flat-bottomed boats and gather around the Commodore’s ship, the *Essex*. Commodore Howe hoisted his flag on the frigate *Success* and sailed as close to shore as he could, the rest of the frigates trailing behind him to shell any defences before the landing. With Lord George Sackville and General Dury leading the charge, the volunteers, the grenadiers, and three battalions of Guards rowed for the beach.

The gentlemen volunteers never got the chance to do any heroic deeds that day, because the Frenchmen who should have stayed to fight them had all run for their lives. The frigates silenced the tiny fort’s paltry two guns, the landing party stormed in, and took the entire garrison prisoner. The whole garrison, as it turned out, was one single old man, who had bravely fired both guns. He told his captors, quite proudly, that if every Frenchman fought as he did, the British would never have landed at Cancale at all. The vanguard of invaders took possession of the small village of Cancale, and lay on their arms all night. Harry’s new comrades teased him mercilessly for bragging he’d go out on the “war path” and bring back two French scalps with his Virginian tomahawk, but no Frenchmen came within a mile of him to make that possible. The only man killed all day was a French gentleman out riding with his servant, who stumbled straight into Lord Downe leading the front rank of Kingsley’s regiment. Lord Downe offered him quarter, which the fool refused, and so he, his servant, and both their horses were shot dead on the spot.

The next day the whole force landed, and marched from Cancale to St. Malo. Every village on the road was completely empty, many of the lanes were so narrow the men had to march single file, and they could have been picked off one by one from behind the thick, leafy hedges if any enemy had cared to ambush them. They reached the walls of St. Malo at dusk, and took a few random, useless cannon shots from the town that did no harm in the dark. Under cover of that fire, the British set every ship, every wooden storehouse, every pile of pitch and tar in the harbour alight, and a great roiling fire burned bright all night long. The French never once came out of the town to harass the raiders. Rumours flew that a huge garrison was hidden inside St. Malo, waiting for them to storm the walls, so the Duke of Marlborough and Lord George Sackville decided there was no point trying to take the town, marched their men all the way back to Cancale, and loaded everyone back onto the ships.

If this were not a strictly true history, I could easily have sent our Virginian off on a far more glorious campaign. Exactly four weeks after he left England, Harry stepped back onto Portsmouth’s docks, and wrote a letter straight to his brother George. George ran off to the Lamberts’ Dean Street lodging the second he tore the seal open, and burst in on the whole family at breakfast.
“Glorious news, ladies!” he shouted. “Our champion is home safe! He survived every danger, and even swears he saw dragons, upon my word!”
“Dragons?” Hetty yelped, half jumping out of her chair. “What on earth do you mean, Mr. Warrington?”
“Great long-tailed monsters with steel scales on their backs that spit fire and gobble up one virgin a day,” Mr. Lambert put in, teasing gently. “He must have seen the Seven Champions of Christendom, of course. They saw St. George’s flag flying and ran for the hills.”
“I’ve read about them!” little Charley, home from his school in Chartreux, put in very solemnly. “They love to eat women. There was one that was going to eat Andromeda, you know, and Jason killed another one that guarded the golden apple tree.”
George laughed, and started reading the letter aloud:
> I think you’ll be glad to hear I am returned, still with no commission, no wounds, and no great glory to brag of—but at the very least, alive and hearty. On our ship we were crammed in so tight it felt like poor Mr. Holwell and his friends stuck in the Black Hole of Calcutta. We had rough weather, and a lot of the fancy London volunteers who are used to calm waters grumbled nonstop. Their stomachs are too dainty for plain sailor’s rum and hard biscuit, not after the fancy dinners they get at White’s Club. But I’ve been to sea before, so I ate my rations and sipped my spiced flip very contentedly, determined to show those foppish English macaronis that a Virginia gentleman is as good as the best of them.
> For the honour of old Virginia I wish I had more to brag about, but all I can say for certain is we went to France and came right back. I don’t think even your dramatic, tragedy-writing pen could make something grand out of this campaign. We landed at Cancale Bay on the sixth, saw a few dragons on a hill that galloped away without stopping to fight. We slept under canvas, marched to St Malo, and burned a whole fleet of privateers in the harbour. Then we got back on the ships without crossing swords with a single enemy, save a few poor local devils the troops plundered. Better luck next time! It wasn’t grand or glorious, but I loved it. I’ve smelled gunpowder, and a whole lot of rosin and pitch from the fires we lit. I’ve seen the enemy, slept rough under canvas, been dreadfully crowded and seasick, and I’m ready to go again. My best to dear Aunt Lambert, and tell Miss Hetty I wasn’t the least bit frightened when I saw the French horses.
> Your most affectionate brother,

Hetty’s knees went weak with relief. A little while later, Mr. Lambert went on official business to inspect the troops camped at Portsmouth and the Isle of Wight, and George came with him. They found Harry tanned, bright-eyed, and bursting with health, his mood lightened entirely by the thrill of the little campaign. He was eager to learn every detail of his new military duties, active, cheerful, and nothing at all like the listless, moping young man who had dawdled around London coffee houses and the Lamberts’ drawing room only a few months prior. The whole camp was under canvas, the weather was bright and warm, and George found his brother thriving in the brisk, open-air school of war. Strolling arm in arm with him through the streets of Portsmouth, George recalled his own short, disastrous military experience two years prior, and marveled at how massive this force was, compared to the tiny, ill-fated army he had once marched with.

The raid, unimpressive as it was to anyone who could see the full picture, sent the whole nation into a frenzy of excitement. The papers declared they had humiliated the proud Gaul, and would have routed the French entirely if only they’d dared to show their faces. What valour on earth could match British valour, the crowds cheered? The whole country demanded more victories, and the enthusiastic army set sail on a new expedition, this time with a very distinguished volunteer on board: the King’s own son, Prince Edward, who was ferried to the *Essex* in the ship’s twelve-oared barge, the standard of England flying from the bow, the Admiral’s flag barge following behind, all the other captains trailing after in strict order of seniority. The whole fleet weighed anchor, and Harry waved his hat wildly to his friends cheering from the shore. George was sure Harry would earn his commission soon, no need to buy an ensigncy if he kept impressing his superiors the way he was. His colonel already sang his praises, officers and men alike loved him, and it was clear he was a young man of great promise and spirit.

Ten days after the fleet sailed, the most glorious news imaginable arrived in London. On the 7th and 8th of August, the King’s troops had landed in the Bay des Marais, two leagues west of Cherbourg, right in the face of a large force of French defenders. The French had scattered the second they saw the British advance. Cherbourg had surrendered unconditionally, and the Union Jack flew over all three of its outer forts. Twenty-seven enemy ships had been burned in the harbour, and hundreds of fine brass cannons had been seized. The rest of the old iron guns had been spiked and tossed in the water, and the famous French harbour basin the people of Paris had bragged about for decades, as well as the two stone piers at the harbour entrance, had been blown to smithereens.

London exploded in celebration. The captured colours from the great British victory at Louisbourg paraded from Kensington to St Paul’s, escorted by eighty Life Guards and eighty Horse Grenadiers, with their standards, kettledrums, and trumpets blaring. The Dean and Chapter met them at the west door of the cathedral, and the Tower and Royal Park guns boomed out a deafening salute at exactly that moment. The next day, the captured Cherbourg cannons were dragged through the streets, their sides carved with fleurs-de-lis, royal crowns, and grand boastful mottos: *Tonnere*, *Malfaisant*, *Le Vainqueur*—“The Victor”, indeed, that piece of French bragging, captured by the British! The crowds screamed so loud the windows rattled. Hetty and Mrs Lambert were fully convinced Harry had dragged every single one of those cannons out of the French battery himself, and thrown every last French artilleryman into the sea. Even the haughty old Madame de Bernstein was asking after him, and his cousin Lady Maria had begged George to come visit her and give her any news of Harry he could find. George, who had once been the undisputed favourite of the household, found himself completely deposed, barely noticed by Hetty at all. His fancy wit, his learning, his dramatic plays, all were very well—but what could any of that compare to great victories and captured brass cannons? He accepted his demotion meekly. The Lamberts and their friends daydreamed that the 15,000 British soldiers would march straight to Paris next, and could not wait to brag to Harry’s snobbish great-aunt Lady Warrington, back in the country, and ask her what she thought of her unruly Virginia nephew now. Every house in London lit up with candles for the victory illuminations, and the Lamberts lit up every window in their own home, grinning from ear to ear.

But the bright fair weather did not last. The commanders, drunk on the acclaim of the Cherbourg victory, decided they would go back and finish the job they had abandoned at St Malo. They landed near the little coastal town of Saint-Cast, but the Governor of Brittany had gathered a far larger French army to defend the coast, and the planned surprise raid was impossible. The British commanders ordered an immediate retreat back to the fleet anchored in Saint-Cast Bay. Hide your faces, all the bards of military glory, for the terrible day of Saint-Cast was about to dawn. Two-thirds of the British troops, and all the artillery, had already been loaded onto the longboats, when a French army sent from Brest came pouring down the heights surrounding the bay. Four companies of the First Regiment of Guards, and all the elite grenadier companies of the raiding force, were left as a rearguard on the beach, 1100 men in total, facing ten times their number of French troops. The guardsmen made a fatal mistake: they left the high, thick stone dyke on the shore, a perfect defensive position they could have held for hours, to charge the advancing Frenchmen on the open sand. The small rearguard was quickly overwhelmed, and broke and ran for the boats in a sauve qui peut panic. Seven hundred of the 1100 were killed, drowned in the surf, or taken prisoner, their commanding officer shot dead on the beach.

I am a man of peace, who knows very little of the finer points of war, and I will not dwell on the fight itself. What weighs on my mind far more than two soldiers stabbing at each other, one English one French, is the people they left waiting back home.
