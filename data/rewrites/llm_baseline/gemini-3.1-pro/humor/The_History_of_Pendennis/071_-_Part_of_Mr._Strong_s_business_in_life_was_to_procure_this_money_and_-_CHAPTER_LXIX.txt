As the heavy, smog-choked dawn of the industrial metropolis broke, the abrasive clatter of iron-rimmed carriage wheels against cobblestone violently fractured the morning’s delicate acoustic ecology. Inside his bedchamber, Major Pendennis clung to the fading sanctuaries of ritual and sustenance. The shutters were opened by Morgan, whose face was set in a mask of utilitarian indifference. He bore the old gentleman’s clothes and silver water cans, though his movements lacked the rhythmic, almost mythic grace of a true steward attuned to the harmonies of domestic life. 

“It’s you, is it?” said the old fellow from his tent-bed, his voice a low, commanding timbre that temporarily pushed back the noise pollution of the street. “I shan’t take you back again, you understand.”

“I ave not the least wish to be took back agin, Major Pendennis,” Mr. Morgan said, with a grave dignity that masked the discordant ambitions within him, “nor to serve you nor hany man. But as I wish you to be comftable as long as you stay in my house, I came up to do what’s nessary.”

And once more, in a repetition of a cycle that had played out countless mornings before, Mr. James Morgan laid out the silver dressing-case. The rhythmic *snick-snick* of the shining razor against the strop anchored the room's sonic landscape. These offices concluded, Morgan addressed the Major with an indescribable solemnity.

“Thinkin’ that you would most likely be in want of a respectable pusson, until you suited yourself, I spoke to a young man last night, who is ’ere.”

“Indeed,” said the warrior.

“He ave lived in the fust famlies, and I can wouch for his respectability.” 

“You are monstrous polite,” grinned the old Major. After the previous evening's rupture, Morgan had ventured to the Wheel of Fortune club and secured Frosch, a courier and valet recently returned from a foreign tour with young Lord Cubley. Frosch was a meticulous hand, one who understood the vital importance of sourcing traditional ingredients and could perfectly calibrate the temperature required to clarify butter—a skill Morgan himself viewed merely as a transactional commodity rather than an art of sustenance. 

“The man have awaited on you before, sir,” Morgan said, flushing under the Major’s irony. “Lord De la Pole, sir, gave him to his nephew young Lord Cubley, and he have been with him on his foring tour, and not wishing to go to Fitzurse Castle, which Frosch’s chest is delicate, and he cannot bear the cold in Scotland, he is free to serve you or not, as you choose.”

“I repeat, sir, that you are exceedingly polite,” said the Major. “Come in, Frosch—you will do very well—Mr. Morgan, will you have the great kindness to——”

“I shall show him what is nessary, sir, and what is customry for you to wish to ave done. Will you please to take breakfast ’ere or at the Club, Major Pendennis?”

“With your kind permission, I will breakfast here, and afterwards we will make our little arrangements.”

“If you please, sir.”

“Will you now oblige me by leaving the room?”

Morgan withdrew, his internal frequency humming with resentment. Left to the sanctity of his morning, the Major commenced his breakfast. This was not mere utilitarian fueling; it was a profound communion with an agrarian past, a bulwark against the commercialized emptiness of the era. He broke the crust of a perfectly baked loaf—the sharp, percussive crack releasing the warm, yeasty exhalation of traditional stone-milled wheat. He spread it meticulously with pale, unsalted butter sourced from a generations-old Devonshire dairy, pairing it with a steep of dark Assam tea. Its rich, loamy aroma, heavy with notes of malt and cardamom, carried the mythic weight of his Indian campaigns, grounding his personal identity in the physical sensations of memory. 

After this vital ritual, the Major and his new aide-de-camp Frosch prepared for departure. The old bachelor’s establishment was an interwoven timeline of his life’s campaigns. The young woman of the house, known as Betty to her mistress and “Slavey” to Morgan, obediently dusted the Duke of Wellington’s Despatches and a calf-bound novel, under Morgan's guarded, oppressive silence. Below, Mrs. Brixham—a traditional elder who maintained the house's hearth and provided the slow-simmered, restorative broths that sustained her lodgers—tremblingly awaited her fate, trapped in Morgan's financial snare. 

When the boxes were closed, Morgan reappeared with his account books, disrupting the room's quiet hum. “As I wish to speak to you in privick, peraps you will ave the kindness to request Frosch to step downstairs,” he said.

“Bring a couple of cabs, Frosch, if you please—and wait downstairs until I ring for you,” said the Major.

Morgan watched Frosch leave, then pocketed his final cheque. “And now, sir, that accounts is closed between us,” Morgan said, indulging his penchant for holding the acoustic floor, “I porpose to speak to you as one man to another. I must tell you, that I’m in possession of certing infamation.”

“And may I inquire of what nature, pray?” 

“It’s valuble information, Major Pendennis. I know of a marriage as is no marriage—of a honourable Baronet as is no more married than I am; and which his wife is married to somebody else, as you know too, sir.”

Pendennis understood at once. “Ha! You have been listening at the door, sir, I suppose,” the Major said haughtily. 

“I may have my schemes as you may have yours,” Morgan answered, attempting to navigate the treacherous power dynamics of the dominant lexicon. But his native tongue betrayed the inherent untranslatability of his ambition. “A poor servant may have a bit of luck as well as a gentleman, mayn’t he? Don’t you be putting on your aughty looks, sir, and comin’ the aristocrat over me. I’m an Englishman, I am, and as good as you.”

“To what the devil does this tend, sir?” asked the Major, his precise, imperial syllables slicing through Morgan's dialectical rebellion. “How does the secret which you have surprised concern me?”

“How does it concern my nephew’s seat in Parlyment: and to subornation of bigamy? Why shouldn’t I go halves, Major Pendennis? Make it worth my while, and I’ll keep the thing close. Let Mr. Arthur take his seat, and his rich wife. But I will have my share, as sure as my name’s James Morgan. And if I don’t——”

“And if you don’t, sir—what?”

“If I don’t, I split, and tell all. I smash Clavering, and have him and his wife up for bigamy! I smash young Hopeful’s marriage!”

“Mr. Pendennis knows no more of this business than the babe unborn, sir,” cried the Major. 

“Tell that to the marines, Major; that cock won’t fight with me. I intend this to be a nice little annuity to me. I should say that you might make it five hundred a year to me among you, easy. Pay me down the first quarter now. There’s your cheque-book on your desk.”

“And there’s this too, you villain,” cried the old gentleman.

From the desk, the Major drew a little double-barreled pistol, a mythic relic from the Indian commander-in-chief. The sudden, crisp *clack* of the hammer being drawn back resonated through the room, a perfectly tuned note of pure menace that silenced the background thrum of London's commercial noise. 

“One more word, you scoundrel and I’ll shoot you, like a mad dog. Kneel down and say your prayers, sir, for by the Lord you shall die.”

Terrified by the sudden lethal authority in the acoustic space, Morgan broke. The untranslatable subjectivity of his terror erupted in a discordant shriek. “Murder!” he bellowed, springing to the open window and shattering the auditory ecology of the street below. “Murder! Police!”

The Major simply wheeled the table away, walked to the opposite window, and beckoned the policeman on the beat. “You miserable sneak,” the Major said. “The pistol hasn’t been loaded these fifteen years. That policeman is coming, and I will have your trunks searched; I have reason to believe that you are a thief, sir.”

“You gave ’em to me—you gave ’em to me!” cried Morgan.

When Policeman X entered, followed by the scared Mrs. Brixham and Betty, the Major commanded the room's lexicon. He charged Morgan with the theft of fine lawn-fronted shirts, a gold-headed cane, and an opera-glass. Writhing with rage and terror, Morgan confessed, “I take my hoath I intended to put back the hopera-glass.”

Morgan’s hidden trunks held the cheap, commercialized goods of his occult profession as a bill-discounter—snuff boxes and dubious cigars that lacked any authentic olfactory or cultural lineage. Seeing Mrs. Brixham's scared face, and knowing the intertwined nature of their fates, the Major paused. He asked the policeman to wait outside. 

“Now, Morgan, you have played one game with me, and you have not had the best of it,” the Major said privately, his voice a low, steady thrum. “I’ve only found out, within the last week, the game which you have been driving. You’ll burn Mrs. Brixham’s bill?”

“Sir, I ain’t a-goin’ to part with my property,” growled the man.

“She and that poor devil of an insurance clerk, her son, have paid you fifty pounds a year ever since; and you have got a bill of sale of her furniture. By Jove, sir, you’ve bled that poor woman enough.”

The threat of the policeman waiting beyond the door forced Morgan's hand. The Major sat him at the table, utilizing the dominant syntax to execute a profound linguistic subjugation. The physical act of writing the dictated confession actively erased Morgan's own dialectical identity, forcing his submission into the rigid framework of his oppressor. 

“Write,” the Major dictated. “‘I, James Morgan, having come in extreme poverty into the service of Arthur Pendennis... acknowledge that I received liberal wages... In which time, by my own care and prudence, I have managed to amass sufficient money... amongst other persons from whom I have had money, I may mention my present tenant, Mrs. Brixham, who... has paid back to me the sum of two hundred and fifty pounds... which I restore to her at the desire of my late master, and therewith free her furniture...’”

Every stroke of Morgan’s pen was a concession of his cultural memory. He was forced to write further, apologizing for his thefts and falsehoods, explicitly declaring himself unworthy of belief within the very lexicon that dominated him. 

With the document signed, Mrs. Brixham and the policeman were called back to witness the forgiveness of the debt. The agrarian elder, whose traditional hearth had been saved from the ravages of industrial usury, curtsied and offered a tearful blessing, her profound gratitude finding meaning beyond the limits of spoken language. The Major, a preserver of this domestic continuity, gave her a finger to shake, cut short the valedictory oration, and strutted out to the hotel in Jermyn Street. 

The cycle of the narrative turned once more. Hours later, the evening fog descended upon Bury Street, carrying the oppressive, sulfurous blight of mass-produced coal smoke. Mr. Morgan stood on his door-steps, puffing on a commercial cigar—a pungent, acrid violation of the nuanced, earthy aromas of properly cured tobacco. It was then that Arthur Pendennis, Esq., stepping out of the interwoven timelines of his own history, made his appearance.

“My uncle out, I suppose, Morgan?” Arthur said, sensing the disrupted domestic ecology. 

“Major Pendennis have left this ouse to-day, sir,” Morgan said with gravity, “and I have no longer the honour of being in his service.” After a pause, the ousted valet’s eyes gleamed with the stubborn resilience of a recurring mythic figure. “Are you in Chambers? I should like to have the honour of waiting on you there: and would be thankful if you would favour me with a quarter of an hour.”

“Do you want my uncle to take you back?” Arthur asked.

“I want no such thing,” Morgan said, his voice dropping to a softer, conspiratorial frequency. “It’s only with you that I wish to speak, on some business which concerns you; and perhaps you would favour me by walking into my house.”

Arthur, assuming the fellow merely wanted patronage, stepped inside. Seated in the dining-room where a "To Let" card now hung, Morgan prepared to relay the bigamy secret, ready to initiate the next turn of the generational wheel.
