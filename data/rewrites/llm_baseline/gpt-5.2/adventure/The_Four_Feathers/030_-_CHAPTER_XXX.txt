The north wind came down like a knife laid flat to the skin—keen, clean, and cold enough to make the camels lift their knees and lengthen their stride as though the air itself were a whip.

“Quicker,” Trench said through his teeth.

Feversham did not waste breath on argument. “Even if Idris has missed us, it will take time to gather men—time to fetch camels. And it is dark.”

Yet he kept turning his head, as if his eyes could pull the light of Omdurman away from the horizon by sheer insistence. The town was only a low, angry glare now, a bruise of fire pressed against the night. The drums had fallen silent; that was a mercy. But the silence here was worse for the nerves. In a land where a man could travel fast and make no sound, there would be no warning thunder of hooves, no clatter of pursuit—only the soft, padded certainty of something drawing near.

Feversham strained his hearing until his ears hurt. Trench did not look back at all. He rode with his face set forward, jaw clenched so hard the muscles stood out. If Feversham feared the darkness behind them, Trench feared the thought of turning and seeing it.

When Feversham spoke again, it was because he had to give his mind something to hold. “I no longer see the fires of Omdurman.”

“The golden blot, eh? Very low down,” Trench answered, in a voice that sounded as if it had travelled a long way to reach his mouth.

Feversham did not ask why the phrase mattered. It had come to Trench like an old taste suddenly remembered—something once swallowed in a different life, returning now with the odd rightness of repetition.

They rode on without speech until two shapes rose straight out of the darkness at the camels’ feet, so near that the animals shied and huffed. Abou Fatma’s whisper cut across them.

“Instanna!”

The camels were forced to their knees. Men melted away and returned with four fresh beasts, snorting softly, their breath steaming. Saddles were stripped from the tired camels and shifted with quick hands, straps tightened, girths pulled until the leather creaked.

“The new camels are here?” Abou Fatma demanded, and the question was not really a question so much as a prayer to the night to keep its promises.

Feversham, fingers working at a buckle, felt the way hunger and fear both sharpened the body. “They are good camels?”

“Of the Anafi breed,” Abou Fatma said. “Quick. Quick.” He kept turning his head to the east, listening as though the darkness had a voice.

Trench’s impatience broke out in a harsher hunger than the need for speed. “The arms? You have them? Where are they?” He leaned down, feeling at the ground like a man searching for a dropped coin that is the price of his life.

“In a moment.”

That moment was too long for him. He could not wait as he had waited for water, for daylight, for the end of a beating. This was different. His hands wanted the familiar assurance of metal and mechanism.

“There is ammunition?” he asked, too quickly.

“Yes—yes. Ammunition, rifles, revolvers.”

Abou Fatma led them a little way off, into a patch where long desert grass whispered against their legs. The sound—dry, thin, persistent—was strangely domestic, like reeds brushing a village wall in a wind, but there was no village here, only the habit of life clinging in scraps to the sand. Abou Fatma dropped to his knees and dug with his hands.

“Here.”

Trench flung himself down beside him and scraped at the sand with both fists. A whimpering, animal sound came from his throat, the kind of eager, helpless noise a hound makes at cover. Feversham watched, and something in him recoiled—not at cowardice, but at how quickly suffering could strip away the last veneer and leave a man all appetite and instinct. Trench’s hands worked like paws, urgent, frantic.

Then his fingers struck the lock and trigger of a rifle, and he stopped as though a switch had been thrown. He rose at once, quiet, human again, holding the weapon with a reverence that was almost tenderness.

The other arms came up from their sandy grave. Ammunition was divided. Metal clicked softly; cartridges rasped; the night seemed full of small, precise sounds, each one loud with consequence.

“Now,” Trench said, and the laugh that broke out of him was raw joy, a man tasting freedom on his tongue. “Now I don’t mind. Let them follow from Omdurman. One thing is certain: I shall never go back there—no, not even if they overtake us.” He ran his hand along the rifle as though it were alive, as though it were a companion that could answer him.

Two of the Arabs mounted the old camels and turned back towards Omdurman, moving off at a slow, indifferent pace meant to mislead. Abou Fatma and the remaining man stayed with the fugitives. They mounted the fresh camels and set their faces north-east.

It had taken no more than a quarter of an hour—this exchange of beasts and buried iron—yet it felt like a whole season had turned.

All that night they forced a path through halfa grass and among thorny mimosa. The grass hissed against camel legs; branches scratched; now and again a seedpod snapped underfoot like brittle bone. They made slow time in that tangle, and the slowness was torture because it was earned by the land, not by choice.

At sunrise the country opened into flat bare ground broken by little hillocks, the sand pale and hard as flour sifted thin. Abou Fatma looked them over with a practical eye.

“Are the Effendi tired? Will they stop and eat? There is food upon the saddle of each camel.”

“No,” Feversham said. “We can eat as we go.”

The meal was what it could be: dates with their sticky, honeyed darkness; bread already dry at the edges, crumbling into the mouth; and a draught from the zamsheyeh that tasted of warm leather and last night’s smoke, yet felt like life sliding back into the veins. They ate without ceremony, chewing while the camels trotted, swallowing with eyes fixed on the horizon.

Freed of the long grass, the Anafi camels lengthened into a quick, swinging pace, their pads making almost no noise on the hard ground. Silence became an enemy again—soundless speed that could belong to pursuer as well as fugitive.

At sunset they stopped for an hour, and in that hour Feversham felt every bruise and stiffness announce itself, as if the body had been too busy to complain before. Then they rode again into the night, and the next day, and the next night—up onto high rocky ground where the stones tore at the camels’ feet, down into valleys where the air held heat like a pot held too long over the fire, and across plains of honey-coloured sand that looked, in the slanting light, almost edible—like fine meal spread out ready for kneading.

Yet to each man the pace always felt as slow as a funeral.

A mountain would lift itself on the rim of the horizon at sunrise and stand there all day, unwavering, and still at evening it would seem no nearer. Sometimes a few men working a scant patch of sorghum—stalks thin and brave in the reluctant soil—would send terror into their throats; they would swing wide in a detour, hearts thudding, listening for any shout that might carry. Sometimes Abou Fatma’s eyes would catch a caravan far off, a moving line of black dots; then they would force their camels to kneel and lie behind a rock, rifles loaded across their forearms, waiting while the distant world passed by.

Ten miles from Abu Klea another relay waited, and they changed again, keeping a day’s march westward of the Nile. From there they entered the Ababdeh desert, and at last Abou Fatma pointed to a broad grey tract lying across their way like a scar.

“The road from Berber to Merowi,” he said. “North of it we turn east to the river. We cross that road to-night. If God wills, to-morrow evening we shall have crossed the Nile.”

“If God wills,” Trench murmured. The nearer safety came, the more his fear sharpened, as if the mind could not bear the idea that deliverance might be real and thus could be stolen.

They were in country used by travellers now. The emptiness had been replaced by routes and habits, by the invisible net of other men’s movement. Daylight was no longer their friend. They dismounted and lay hidden behind a belt of shrubs on high ground, watching the road. People moved on it like insects crossing a line drawn in dust—specks that might at any moment turn into riders with guns.

When darkness fell they crossed quickly and pushed on towards the river. At dawn Abou Fatma halted them again. The land here was bleak and open, so flat that even a small rise seemed a fortress. Feversham and Trench stared to their right, hungry for the sight of water, but the Nile—so near, the spine of the country—kept itself hidden beyond the folds of earth.

“We must build a circle of stones,” Abou Fatma said. “You must lie close to the ground within it. I go forward to the river and see that the boat is ready, that our friends are prepared. I come back after dark.”

They gathered stones and built a low ring, no more than a foot high, but in that open world it was the only shelter they could make. Inside it Feversham and Trench lay down with a water-skin and rifles beside them. Abou Fatma looked at their supplies like a careful steward laying out a household’s last provisions.

“You have dates, too?”

“Yes.”

“Then do not stir. I take your camels. I bring you fresh ones in the evening.”

He rode away with the other Arab towards the unseen river, leaving Feversham and Trench pressed to the sand like men buried alive.

They dug the sand down within the ring, making a shallow hollow to lower their bodies. They watched the horizon through the gaps between stones, blinking against the brightness. The day that followed was perhaps the longest either had lived. To Trench it felt longer than a night in the House of Stone; to Feversham longer even than those days six years ago, when he had sat above St. James’s Park and waited for darkness before he dared go out, the civilised city around him as threatening as any desert.

Here there was no shade. The sun roasted their backs until the skin felt tight and raw, and the stones around them grew hot enough to sting when brushed. There was no room to move; any movement would be visible. Their mouths grew dry; the dates, so sweet in the morning, became cloying, thickening the tongue. They spoke hardly at all—speech felt like waste, and the desert itself seemed to listen.

At last sunset came, the light draining away as if poured out. Friendly darkness gathered, and a cool wind moved across the sand with a whispering that was almost like water.

“Listen,” Trench said.

They strained their ears and heard it—the soft padding of camels very near, the faint click of stones underfoot. A low whistle threaded through the night.

Feversham rose from the ring. “We are here,” he said quietly.

“God be thanked,” Abou Fatma answered. His voice carried fatigue and relief together, like a man who has carried a heavy pot a long way and at last sets it down. “I have good news and bad. The boat is ready; our friends wait. Camels are prepared for you on the caravan track by the river-bank to Abu Hamed. But your escape is known. The roads and ferries are watched. Before sunrise we must have struck inland from the eastern bank.”

They crossed the river about one o’clock. The Nile was black and thick, flowing with a slow muscular sound, as though it spoke a language older than any man on the banks. The boat’s wood creaked; water lapped; every tiny noise seemed loud. On the far side they sank the boat, letting it fill and disappear, a small sacrifice to concealment.

Fresh camels waited, shifting and snorting softly, the smell of their warm bodies strong in the night. They rode inland, but the ground was cluttered with boulders, and the beasts could seldom do more than walk. The enforced slowness was agony.

All through the next day they hid again within a stone ring while the camels were led to higher ground to graze. Night brought movement and, with it, a loosening of the heart. They made better progress. In two days they reached the groves of Abu Hamed and rested twelve hours there, under trees that smelled faintly of green sap and dust. Then another relay, another tightening of straps, another push onward.

From Abu Hamed their route lay across the great Nubian Desert.

In later days, a traveller would take this stretch in a train and sleep in a berth while coal-black rocks and yellow sand slid past outside. Morning might show him a tent, a pile of coal, a water tank, and a number painted on a white signboard—station names replaced by numerals, the land translated into the brisk, dominant lexicon of industry. Telegraph poles would march from rim of sky to rim of sky, their wires humming faintly—a new music imposed upon the desert, a commercial song that did not belong to wind or bird. Twelve hours would enclose the journey unless the engine failed.

But for Feversham and Trench there was no iron road, no numbered halt. They kept eastward of where the railway would one day run, following the line of wells among the hills—old knowledge passed mouth to mouth, the kind of knowledge that dies when tongues fall silent and names are forgotten. A well was not a “station”; it was a story, a taste of water with a particular mineral tang, a certain echo when you dropped a pebble and listened for the depth.

On the second night of that stage, Trench shook Feversham awake, fingers urgent on his shoulders.

“Look,” he whispered, and pointed south.

Feversham sat up and followed the line of his hand. The sky was scattered with stars, hard and cold. But the familiar four slanting points were gone.

“To-night there’s no Southern Cross,” Trench said, and his voice broke as though something inside him had been held taut for years and had at last snapped—not into pieces, but into release. “For six years—every night of six years—until this night, I have seen it. How often have I lain awake watching it, wondering whether the night would ever come when I should not see those four slanting stars. This is the first moment when I have really dared to think we shall escape.”

They watched the southern sky as though it were a pot on the fire and they were waiting for the first sure sign of a boil—proof that the thing they hoped for was truly happening. They murmured prayers, not grandly, but with the plain gratitude of men who have lived too long on the edge of being hunted.

When they slept, it was with the strange habit of waking again and again, half certain that when they opened their eyes the Southern Cross would have returned low on the horizon like a brand burned into the night, and half confident now that it would not.

After seven days they came to Shof-el-Ain, a tiny well set in a barren valley between featureless ridges. The place looked like nothing—stone, sand, a small dark mouth of water—but it held the weight of salvation.

By the well they made camp. They were in the country of the Amrab Arabs, and at last, Abou Fatma said, they had come to the end of peril.

“We are safe,” he cried. “God is good. North to Assouan, west to Wadi Halfa—we are safe!”

He spread a cloth on the ground and heaped dhurra before the kneeling camels. The grain smelled faintly sweet and dusty; the camels nosed it with greedy snorts. In his gratitude Abou Fatma even patted one animal’s neck. It turned its long head, lips curling, and snarled like a bad-tempered dog.

Trench laughed hoarsely. “Even now,” he said, “a camel keeps its manners.”

Then he held out his hand to Feversham. “Thank you,” he said simply.

“No need,” Feversham answered. He did not take it. “I served myself from first to last.”

“You have learned the churlishness of a camel,” Trench said, half jesting, half hurt. “It will carry you where you want to go—carry you till it drops dead—and yet if you show gratitude it resents and bites. Hang it, Feversham—there’s my hand.”

Feversham’s fingers went instead to the breast of his jibbeh. He untied a knot and drew out three white feathers—two small heron feathers and one larger ostrich feather, broken from a fan. Even in the dim light they looked unnaturally clean, as though they belonged to a different world—a drawing-room world of polished floors and spoken judgements.

“Will you take yours back?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“You know what to do with it.”

“Yes. There shall be no delay.”

Feversham wrapped the remaining feathers carefully into a corner of his ragged garment and tied them safe again, as one might tie up a precious spice in cloth to keep its scent from wasting into the air.

“We shake hands, then,” he said at last.

Their hands met. Feversham’s grip was firm, but his face was closed. “To-morrow morning we part company.”

“Part company—you and I—after the year in Omdurman, the weeks of flight?” Trench stared as though he had misheard. “Why?”

“There’s no more to be done. Castleton’s dead. You keep the feather he sent—but he is dead. You can do nothing with it. You must come home.”

“Yes,” Feversham said, “but after you. Certainly not with you. You go on to Assouan and Cairo. At each place you will find friends to welcome you. I shall not go with you.”

Trench fell silent. He understood. Feversham’s story—Feversham’s shame—would be easier for others to hear if Feversham himself were not beside him as the living proof of it. Words change in the mouth that speaks them; the same tale can be mercy or cruelty depending on whose tongue shapes it. And there were things, too, that had never been said aloud at all, agreed into silence for the regiment’s sake.

“I ought to tell you,” Trench said at last, “no one knows why you resigned your commission, or of the feathers we sent. We never spoke of it. We agreed never to speak—for the honour of the regiment. I can’t tell you how glad I am we kept to it.”

“Perhaps you will see Durrance,” Feversham said. “If you do, give him a message. Tell him that the next time he asks me to come and see him—whether in England or Wadi Halfa—I will accept the invitation.”

“Which way will you go?”

“To Wadi Halfa.” Feversham pointed west over his shoulder. “I shall take Abou Fatma with me and travel slowly and quietly down the Nile. The other Arab will guide you into Assouan.”

They slept that night beside the well as men sleep who have forgotten what true safety feels like and are learning it again. The water’s presence changed even the sounds—the soft gulp as it was drawn, the faint drip, the way a voice seemed less exposed near it. It was as if the well gave the air a gentler acoustics.

In the morning they parted. Trench mounted first. As his camel rose, he bent down to Feversham, who handed up the nose rein.

“Ramelton—that was the name?” Trench asked. “I shall not forget.”

“Yes. Ramelton,” Feversham said. “There’s a ferry across Lough Swilly to Rathmullen. You must drive the twelve miles to Ramelton. But you may not find her there.”

“If not there, I shall find her somewhere else,” Trench said, and his voice had the stubborn heat of a man who has lived too long without tenderness and will not lose the chance of it now. “Make no mistake, Feversham. I shall find her.”

He rode forward, alone with his guide. More than once he turned and saw Feversham still standing by the well—a solitary figure in a bare valley. More than once he was drawn to stop and go back, but he did not. He lifted his hand in farewell. Feversham did not answer the salute.

Feversham had no eyes for him. Six years of harsh probation had ended that morning, and yet what he felt most was not joy but a hollowing out, as if the one sustaining purpose that had kept his heart moving had been scraped away, leaving an emptiness that the world’s ordinary promises could not easily fill.

Ethne? No doubt she was long since married.

Bitterness came on him suddenly, sharp as vinegar on a raw mouth. One futile, unnecessary mistake made six years ago—one act of folly—and the taste of it still lingered, untranslatable into any language but the private dialect of regret. He saw again a room in London overlooking the quiet trees and lawns of St. James’s Park; heard the knock at the door; felt the telegram in his hand. The memory did not come as a thought so much as a sensory return—the weight of the paper, the dry sound of it unfolding, the way the world changed temperature in an instant.

He forced himself back into the present with the knowledge that some work remained.

There was his father, perhaps even now reading The Times after breakfast on the terrace at Broad Place among the Surrey pines. There was the fourth feather to return to Ramelton. There was a telegram to send to Lieutenant Sutch at Suakin.

He mounted his camel and turned west with Abou Fatma, riding slowly towards Wadi Halfa. Yet the sense of loss did not leave him, nor the anger at the old mistake that had brought his downfall. Across the shimmer of the desert air he could see, with an unnerving clarity, the wooded slopes of Ramelton as though they were painted on the horizon—a landscape of home that seemed at once impossibly near and separated from him by the whole long silence of what he had been.

And in the depth of his depression, Harry Feversham doubted—for the first time that day—the faith he had held in the “afterwards.”
