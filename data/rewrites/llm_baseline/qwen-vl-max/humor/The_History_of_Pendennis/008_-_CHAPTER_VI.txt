Mr. Pendennis had long since abandoned Cicero and Euripides—poor fellows!—to the dust of his study shelves. With Rebecca now firmly out of the picture (though not entirely from his thoughts), he’d found a new passion: fencing lessons in Chatteris, which were less about swordplay and more about sneaking off to see Miss Fotheringay. Honest Mr. Smirke, his tutor, was left to teach Latin verbs and trigonometry with the enthusiasm of a man who’d just discovered that no one really cared if he passed the exam.

Rebecca, poor creature, suffered most during this period. She was like a neglected horse in a stable full of fancy carriages—ignored, overlooked, and occasionally kicked by the very person who once swore eternal fidelity to her. When she went lame? Pen was as heartbroken as Richard III at Bosworth Field when his horse died under him. He didn’t just cry—he *acted*. He spent hours poring over veterinary texts, hiring doctors for her, and even considering a full-blown funeral procession. All while secretly plotting trips to Prior’s Lane.

Ah, yes—the secret trips. Once a week, under the noble pretense of studying Greek drama with Smirke, Pen would vanish into the night. He’d catch the Competitor down coach, spend two blissful hours in Chatteris (mostly at the theatre), then return on the Rival, arriving back in London just as the moon began its nightly patrol. It was a tightrope act of deception, but Pen was an expert. One time, however, disaster nearly struck. Mrs. Pendennis asked Smirke whether they’d read much the previous evening. The curate, bless him, opened his mouth to confess that he hadn’t seen Pen at all—when a boot heel came crashing down on his toe, sending a silent message: *Keep your trap shut.*

They’d talked, of course. Two men in love always do. And what a spectacle it is—watching them try to sound profound while mostly just blushing and stammering. Smirke, trembling with moral responsibility, asked Pen if his affection was “unworthy” or “unlawful.” He feared, with genuine horror, that he might have to betray his pupil to Mrs. Pendennis—and then face the wrath of both mother and son, possibly ending his career as a tutor forever.

“Unlawful?” Pen roared, half-angry, half-romantic. “She’s as pure as she is beautiful! I’d give my heart to no other woman. And if anyone dares speak ill of her, I’ll—well, dammy, I won’t stand for it!”

Smirke, chuckling nervously, said, “Well, well, don’t call me out, Arthur—I can’t fight.” But the damage was done. Now he was trapped in the web of Pen’s secret, and poor old Greek grammar suffered accordingly.

If only the reverend gentleman had glanced at the *County Chronicle* each Wednesday, he might have noticed something peculiar: poems signed “EROS,” passionate odes to “Miss Fotheringay of the Theatre Royal,” and verses so dramatic they made you wonder if someone had actually been stabbed in a garden. But since these weren’t signed “NEP” anymore—Pen’s usual pen name—neither Smirke nor Helen (who dutifully cut out all her son’s poetry from the paper) realized that Eros was none other than their own beloved Arthur.

Eventually, Mrs. Pendennis couldn’t help but notice. “Who is this lady your rival keeps singing about?” she asked one day. “He writes beautifully—but yours is better, dear Pen. Have you seen Miss Fotheringay?”

Pen nodded proudly. “Yes, Mother. Last night I saw her play Mrs. Haller in *The Stranger*. She’s going to have a benefit soon—Ophelia, Shakespeare, you know. We could go. We can get horses from the Clavering Arms.”

Laura, little Laura, jumped up with delight. “Oh, please, Mama! A play! I’ve never seen one!”

Pen mentioned “Shakespeare” because the late Mr. Pendennis had been a man of culture—sort of. He claimed there was more poetry in Shakespeare than in all of Johnson’s Poets combined, though he never actually read any of it himself. Still, he insisted Pen memorize the Bard’s works, promising one day they’d attend a real performance together. And so, when Pen suggested the play, Mrs. Pendennis teared up remembering her husband’s words. She kissed her son, said she’d go, and Laura danced around the room like a tiny whirlwind.

Helen, in a rare moment of cheer, invited Mr. Smirke along. The curate, raised in Clapham by a parent who believed theatre was the work of the devil, had never seen a play. But Shakspeare! And to sit beside Mrs. Pendennis in her carriage for an entire evening? He couldn’t resist. He gave a weak speech about temptation and gratitude, then accepted with trembling joy. As he spoke, he shot Mrs. Pendennis a look so intense she felt physically uncomfortable. It was the same look he’d given her three times already that week. Each time, she thought: *That man is becoming unbearable.*

Now, we won’t bore you with the details of Pen’s courtship—after all, you’ve already heard how charming Miss Fotheringay is. Suffice it to say, Pen poured his soul into every conversation. He told her everything: his dreams, his fears, his favorite lines from Byron, and the exact number of times he’d eaten cold mutton in the past fortnight. She listened—not because she understood, but because she looked *beautiful* doing it.

In truth, while Pen rambled on about poetry and passion, Emily Costigan (that’s her name, though everyone calls her Milly) was mentally planning how to turn her black satin dress into a bonnet like Miss Thackthwaite’s. Or perhaps how best to serve the cold mutton. She didn’t care about metaphysics. She cared about fashion, food, and not being married to some boy who thought romance was the same as riding a horse at full gallop.

But her father, Captain Costigan, was wise. On Pen’s second visit, he winked at his daughter over a glass of grog and said, “Faith, Milly darling, I think ye’ve hooked that chap.”

“Oh, papa,” she sighed, “he’s just a boy.”

Pen would have loved hearing that. He rode home wild with joy, shouting “Milly!” at the top of his lungs, as if the whole countryside should know he’d been chosen.

“I told ye,” said the Captain, “he’s not a bad fish. I asked Tom at the George—fine fortune, chariot, park, Fairoaks, only son, property at twenty-one. Ye might go further and not fare so well.”

“But boys talk,” Milly said, sipping punch. “Remember young Poldoody? He wrote me poems, but then he went abroad and got married to an Englishwoman.”

“Lord Poldoody was a nobleman,” the Captain replied. “And ye weren’t in the position ye are now. But don’t encourage him too much. Jack Costigan won’t have any thrifling with his daughter.”

“No more will his daughter,” Milly said, smirking. “A little more punch, papa? Sure, ’tis beautiful. Don’t worry about the lad—I’m old enough to take care of myself.”

So Pen came and went like a gust of wind. He rushed in, stayed for hours, then galloped away again, growing more frantic with each visit. Sometimes the Captain was there; sometimes not. When he was, he’d cock his hat and strut off like a peacock, leaving the young couple alone. Their meetings took place in the drawing-room—a low-ceilinged, wainscoted chamber with a large window overlooking the Dean’s garden. Sunlight streamed in, illuminating Emily’s lovely face as she sat quietly at her embroidery, looking calm and serene, while Pen ranted about life, love, and the meaning of existence.

Occasionally, the great cathedral bell would boom, and Pen would pause, smiling, until the sound faded. Or the rooks would squawk in the elms. Or the organ music would drift across the air, softening even the most impassioned speech.

By the way, Miss Fotheringay attended church every Sunday without fail. In a plain shawl and close bonnet, she sat beside her father, who responded to prayers in a voice so rich and brogue-heavy it sounded like a hymn sung by a dragon. He joined in the psalms, chanted with fervor, and behaved like a model Christian—though he probably did it more for show than anything else.

Little Bows, the family’s house-friend and a cripple who couldn’t appear on stage (being even more deformed than Bingley, the manager), was furious at the idea of Emily marrying a mere country squire. He’d taught her to act—shrieking lines in his cracked voice, twisting her arms into perfect poses, drilling her until she could perform the same gestures, tones, and tears with mechanical precision. She wasn’t clever. She wasn’t passionate. She was simply obedient. But she was magnificent on stage—like a clockwork doll powered by emotion.

Bows believed she deserved better. A London engagement! A great success! But the London managers had already seen her—and she’d failed miserably three years prior. That was why Bows had taken her in hand. And that was why, despite her lack of intelligence, Pen fell madly in love with her.

The day of the play arrived. The post-horses from the Clavering Arms carried the party to Chatteris. Pen was thrilled to see a decent-sized crowd gathered. In the front box sat Mr. Foker and Mr. Spavin, resplendent in full evening attire. They greeted Pen warmly and admired his mother—Mrs. Pendennis, dressed in black velvet and wearing her diamond cross, looked regal. Little Laura, red-cheeked and full of curls, was adorable. Behind them sat Pen and Smirke, the latter blushing furiously at being in such a place—yet utterly delighted to be there.

Samuel, the coachman, took his seat in the pit, where Mr. Foker’s man also stood guard. The orchestra included non-commissioned officers of the Dragoons, led by Colonel Swallowtail himself—portly, proud, and wearing his Waterloo medal like a crown.

“Who’s that odd-looking fellow bowing to you, Arthur?” Mrs. Pendennis asked.

Pen flushed. “Captain Costigan, ma’am. A Peninsular officer.” Which was true—except that Captain Costigan was also Emily’s father, and currently waving a white kid glove dramatically at Pen.

No one knew this. Not yet.

Mr. Hornbull played Hamlet. Mr. Bingley, modestly, took Horatio. Then came *Black-Eyed Susan*, which everyone adored.

But the real star was Ophelia. Emily Costigan—now Miss Fotheringay—was breathtaking. She laughed, wept, danced wildly, flung flowers, and sang snatches of songs with such charm that even the most stoic spectator melted. Her hair tossed freely, her eyes sparkled, and when she lay dead in the grave, she looked so perfectly tragic that Pen almost wanted to join her.

As Hamlet and Laertes fought above her, she peeked from behind the scenes, glancing toward Pen’s box with a hint of recognition. And there, twinkling in her breast, was a locket—bought by Pen from Mr. Nathan in High Street, with his last shilling and a sovereign borrowed from Smirke.

When the curtain fell, the house erupted. Smirke clapped wildly, shouting “Bravo!” as if he’d written the play himself. The Dragoons rose to their feet. Colonel Swallowtail waved his cap. Even Mrs. Pendennis waved her handkerchief. Little Laura danced, laughed, and stared up at Pen in awe.

Pen bellowed louder than anyone: “Fotheringay! Fotheringay!” He leaned over his mother’s chair, waving his hat, barely containing himself. He wanted to scream: *That’s her! That’s the woman I love!* But he held his tongue—just barely.

After the show, they returned home in the carriage. Pen sat on the box with Smirke, smoking a cigar under the moonlight. Mr. Foker’s tandem whizzed past, and Mr. Spavin serenaded them with variations of *Rule Britannia* on his key-bugle.

Two days later, the Dean of Chatteris hosted a dinner for clerical friends. Port wine flowed. The Bishop was insulted. Doctor Portman, gallant and observant, stepped out onto the lawn and offered Mrs. Dean his arm. They walked slowly across the ancient grass, bathed in golden twilight.

Meanwhile, at the far end of the garden, in Mrs. Creed’s house, another scene unfolded. Pen and Emily were alone in the first-floor room. The windows were open. The air was warm. And Pen—poor fool—had finally spoken.

Trembling, breathless, eyes wide with emotion, he confessed everything. His love. His admiration. His soul. He threw himself at her feet, a modern-day knight offering his heart to a queen.

“Does your mother know?” she asked, slowly.

He seized her hand and kissed it a thousand times. She didn’t pull away.

“She likes you,” he whispered. “She admires you. She’ll love you. Who wouldn’t?”

“’Deed then,” she said, perhaps with pity, “I think you do.”

Then he launched into a rhapsody—love, eternity, truth, and all the rest. We won’t repeat it. Let him have his moment. Let the foolish boy pour out his heart. For what good is wisdom if you can’t feel?

At the climax, he kissed her hand again—just as Master Ridley Roset, her son, tugged his mother’s dress and pointed upward.

“I say, ma! Look up there!”

And there they were—Pen, kneeling, kissing the fingers of the woman who could neither love nor understand him, while the Dean and Doctor Portman watched in stunned silence.

Dr. Portman froze. His prime pupil—his favorite—was making a spectacle of himself. And worse—this was no ordinary girl. This was *her*. The daughter of Captain Costigan. The actress. The object of a hundred rumors.

He stood mute. Rage and disbelief warred within him.

And so ended the chapter—not with a bang, but with a quiet, awkward stare from a child, a frozen scholar, and a boy whose heart had leapt too far, too fast, into the hands of a woman who’d rather be thinking about her next bonnet.
