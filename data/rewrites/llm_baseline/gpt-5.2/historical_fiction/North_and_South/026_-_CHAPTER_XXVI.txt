When Mr. Thornton left the Hales’ house that morning, the street met him like the clang of a dropped iron bar. The light was too white, the sky too near; every wheel that passed worried his skull as if the very cobbles had grown teeth. He walked as a man does after a blow, stunned into moving, half-blind with passion he could not place, and yet felt as bodily pain—headache beating in time with his pulse, a throbbing that rose and fell like a cruel accompaniment to the town’s ceaseless din.

Milton made its own music whether a man wished to hear it or no: the rattle of carts, the thin cries of hawkers, the long, low rumble that never quite died—noise lying over everything like soot. It was not only in his ears; it seemed to press on his eyes, to scrape the inside of his mouth. He wanted quiet as a thirsty man wants water. He told himself he was a fool—told himself so often the words grew tasteless—and yet, in the first sharp minutes, he could hardly recollect what had cut him, whether the wound deserved such smarting. It would have been a relief to sit down on any door-step and weep outright, as a child does, raging even through tears at the injustice of a hurt; but he would not grant himself that softness. He shaped the thought, I hate her, and, at the same instant, love cleft through him—quick and keen as lightning through thunder.

His one comfort lay in hugging the torment close, as if pain were proof. She might despise him; she might treat him with that proud, sovereign indifference; she could not make him change. He loved her; he would love her; and he would defy her and defy this misery that had taken possession of his body.

He stopped, on the pavement, to set the resolve firm—stood very still, as though holding himself upright against the press of sound. An omnibus happened to be passing, bound out of town; the conductor, mistaking his pause for a signal, drew up with a jerk and a shout. It was too much trouble to explain. Mr. Thornton mounted, and let himself be carried away as if he were only another parcel in motion.

Milton slid backwards: long ranks of houses first, then detached villas with their trim little gardens, then hedgerows beginning to look like the real country, and after that a small town with a market-place. People climbed down and dispersed, their voices thinning in the clearer air. He got down too, because they did; and because the quiet beyond the last shop-front tugged at him like a hand.

He went into the fields and walked fast, letting the sharp movement spend what his heart could not. Here the world had another soundscape: wind combing the hedge, a bird’s note that struck cleanly and was gone, the hush that seemed to widen round him with every step. It should have soothed him. It did not. Now that the din had fallen away, memory began its own pounding.

He could see the pitiful figure he must have cut, hear his own voice as it had sounded—pleading without meaning to plead; could taste again the bitterness of having done the very thing he had always agreed with himself was the most foolish in the world. And he had met, precisely, the end he had foretold for such folly.

Was he bewitched by those beautiful eyes? By that soft, half-open, sighing mouth that had lain so close upon his shoulder only yesterday? He could not shake off the recollection that she had been there; that her arms had been round him once—if never again. He had only caught glimpses of her. He did not understand her altogether. One hour she seemed brave, another timid; now tender, then haughty and regally proud; and each change made her only more herself, more impossible to forget.

As if determination could be built by repetition, he tried to think over every time he had ever seen her, once again, to wear her image out. He saw her in every dress, in every mood, and did not know which became her best. Even this morning—how magnificent she had looked, eyes flashing at the mere idea that because she had shared his danger yesterday she must therefore care for him the least! Her scorn had struck like a smart spice upon the tongue: not agreeable, yet awakening every sense.

If he was a fool in the morning—as he had assured himself at least twenty times—he did not grow much wiser in the afternoon. The only gain of the sixpenny ride and the long walk was a more vivid conviction: there never was, never could be, any one like Margaret; she did not love him and never would; but she—no, nor the whole world—should ever hinder him from loving her.

And so he turned back, crossed into the market-place, and remounted the omnibus that would take him into the town’s grinding roar again.

It was late in the afternoon when he was set down near his warehouse. The accustomed sights drew back the accustomed habits as surely as the smell of oil and damp wool draws a man’s thoughts into business. He knew how much there was to do—more than usual, because of the commotion of the day before. He must see his brother magistrates. He must complete the arrangements, only half made in the morning, for the comfort and safety of the newly imported Irish hands; he must secure them from all chance of communication with the discontented workpeople of Milton. And last of all, hardest though it had nothing to do with looms or law, he must go home and encounter his mother.

Mrs. Thornton had sat in the dining-room all day, each minute drawn tight with expectation. She had been waiting for news—waiting for the step that would tell her her son had been accepted by Miss Hale. Again and again some sudden noise in the house had made her start; again and again she had caught up her dropped work and driven her needle hard, though her spectacles dimmed and her hand shook in spite of her. Many times the door had opened only to admit some indifferent person upon some insignificant errand. Then the rigid look—like gray frost bound upon her face—had melted into a despondency so strange upon her sternness that it seemed almost indecent, as though she had been caught without armour.

To wrench her mind from the dread of change—of being displaced—she forced it into household channels. If there was to be a new mistress in the house, there must be fresh household stores. Clothes-basket after clothes-basket was brought in, filled with table-cloths and napkins; and she began to reckon the stock as if numbers could keep grief at bay.

There was confusion between what was hers—marked G. H. T. for George and Hannah Thornton—and what belonged to her son, bought with his money and marked with his initials. Some of those bearing G. H. T. were old Dutch damask, exquisitely fine—linen that had a dense, cool smoothness under the fingers, woven so evenly it seemed almost to hold light. Nothing made now was like it. She stood looking at them long. They had been her pride when she was first married; in their whiteness lived a whole history of careful keeping, of boiling and blueing, of sunshine and ironing, of a young woman’s pleasure in making a home well-appointed.

Then her brows knit. Her lips pinched themselves into a line. With small, precise stitches she began to unpick the G. H. from the corner, as if by undoing thread she could undo what it signified—her old claim, her old place. She searched for the Turkey-red marking-thread to put in new initials, to alter possession by dye as clearly as by law; but it was all used, and she had no heart to send for more—not yet. So she sat fixedly looking at vacancy, while a series of visions passed before her in which her son was the principal, the sole object—her son, her pride, her property.

Still he did not come. Doubtless he was with Miss Hale. The thought struck her like sudden physical pain: jealousy, sharp and vain, darting through her so that she hardly knew whether it was mind or body that suffered. For a moment it forced her to sit down. In the next she was up again, straight as ever, with a grim smile—ready for the door to open and the triumphant one to enter, the man who should never know the sore regret his mother felt at his marriage.

In all this there was little thought of the future daughter-in-law as an individual. She was to be John’s wife. To take Mrs. Thornton’s place as mistress of the house was only one consequence among many that glittered round the supreme glory. Household plenty and comfort, honour and obedience, troops of friends, would come as naturally, in Mrs. Thornton’s imagination, as jewels on a king’s robe—things assumed, not considered for their separate worth. To be chosen by John would raise a kitchen-wench above the rest of the world.

And Miss Hale—she was not so bad. If she had been a Milton lass, Mrs. Thornton would have liked her. There was pungency in her, taste, spirit, flavour. True, she was sadly prejudiced and very ignorant; but that might be expected from southern breeding. A mortified comparison with Fanny went on in Mrs. Thornton’s mind; and for once she spoke harshly to her daughter, abused her roundly, and then, as penance, took up Henry’s Commentaries and tried to fix her attention upon the print instead of continuing her inspection of the linen.

His step at last.

She heard it while her eyes passed over a sentence she could afterwards repeat word for word without having taken its meaning in. The house itself seemed to tell her he was coming: the hall-door, the sound at the hat-stand, the pause before the room-door. Why did he pause? Let her know the worst. Yet her head remained bent over the book; she did not look up.

He came close to the table and stood there, waiting till she should have finished the paragraph that appeared to absorb her. At length, by a visible effort, she raised her eyes.

“Well, John?”

He knew what the little speech meant. He had steeled himself, and yet the answering words would not come readily. A jest rose—bitter, ready-shaped; but his mother deserved better. He moved behind her chair so that she could not see his face, bent down, and kissed her gray, stony cheek.

“No one loves me,—no one cares for me, but you, mother,” he murmured.

Then he turned away and stood with his head leaning against the mantelpiece. Tears forced themselves into his manly eyes, as if the restraint of the day had at last found a crack.

Mrs. Thornton stood up—she tottered. For the first time in her life the strong woman tottered. She laid her hands on his shoulders; she was tall and had always looked as if she could bear weight without bending. She made him turn; she looked into his face and compelled him to meet her gaze.

“Mother’s love is given by God, John. It holds fast for ever and ever. A girl’s love is like a puff of smoke,—it changes with every wind. And she would not have you, my own lad, would not she?”

Her teeth set; she showed them like a dog for the whole length of her mouth. He shook his head.

“I am not fit for her, mother; I knew I was not.”

She ground out words between closed teeth. He could not catch them, but the look in her eyes translated them well enough—a curse, if not coarse in sound, as fell in intent as any ever spoken. And yet her heart leapt light to know he was hers again.

“Mother!” he said, hurriedly, as if to shut the door on what he had seen in her face. “I cannot hear a word against her. Spare me,—spare me! I am very weak in my sore heart;—I love her yet; I love her more than ever.”

“And I hate her,” said Mrs. Thornton, low and fierce. “I tried not to hate her, when she stood between you and me, because,—I said to myself,—she will make him happy; and I would give my heart’s blood to do that. But now, I hate her for your misery’s sake. Yes, John, it’s no use hiding up your aching heart from me. I am the mother that bore you, and your sorrow is my agony; and if you don’t hate her, I do.”

“Then, mother, you make me love her more. She is unjustly treated by you, and I must make the balance even. But why do we talk of love or hatred? She does not care for me, and that is enough, too much. Let us never name the subject again. It is the only thing you can do for me in the matter. Let us never name her.”

“With all my heart. I only wish that she, and all belonging to her, were swept back to the place they came from.”

He stood still a minute or two longer, gazing into the fire as if heat could cauterize thought. Mrs. Thornton’s dry, dim eyes filled with tears she would not acknowledge; but when he spoke again her face was as grim and quiet as ever, the voice back in its accustomed groove.

“Warrants are out against three men for conspiracy, mother. The riot yesterday helped to knock up the strike.”

And Margaret’s name was mentioned no more between mother and son. They returned to their old manner—talk of facts, not opinions, far less feelings. Their voices sank into calm, cold tones; and a stranger, hearing them, might have gone away thinking there had never been nearer kinship clad in such frigid indifference. Outside, Milton kept up its harsh chorus without pause, as if the town itself had no other language left for sorrow or love than noise.
