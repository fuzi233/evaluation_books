CHAPTER IX. I’ll tell thee, Berthold, what men’s hopes are like: A silly child that, quivering with joy, Would cast its little mimic fishing-line Baited with loadstone for a bowl of toys In the salt ocean.

Eight months after the family’s arrival at Offendene—by the end of June—the quiet countryside around Wanchester stirred with news that sent ripples through every household, from the village smithy to the grand estates. It was not a matter of war or politics, nor of trade or harvests, but something far more intimate and thrilling: the old manor of Diplow Hall, long silent and shuttered under Sir Hugo Mallinger’s neglect, was being readied once more for life. The stables were being cleaned, the lawns trimmed, and the great house prepared to welcome a new tenant—for the summer and through the hunting season, no less. But it would not be Sir Hugo himself returning; instead, his nephew, Mr. Mallinger Grandcourt, was coming to take up residence. As the presumptive heir to the baronetcy—Sir Hugo’s marriage having produced only daughters—this was a momentous shift in the social landscape of the region.

And it wasn’t just the title that made Mr. Grandcourt a figure of interest. His mother’s lineage carried noble blood, and should certain distant relatives pass on, he might even become a baron—a peer of England. Such possibilities, while uncertain, were enough to spark quiet speculation across the county. For those who lived by the land, this meant hope: the blacksmith dreamed of busy days; laborers’ wives imagined their sons finding work as grooms; farmers saw renewed chances to sell hay or straw at fair prices. And among the gentry, the talk was quieter, yet no less fervent—marriage, after all, was both a personal joy and a strategic alliance.

It is natural, when a man of wealth and standing arrives in a community, that thoughts turn to whom he might choose as a wife. Just as people dream of knighthood when royalty visits, so too did whispers of romance stir beneath polite conversation. The idea that Mr. Grandcourt might settle in the neighborhood—and perhaps seek a bride—was enough to set imaginations spinning. Who could he marry? What sort of woman would be worthy of such a future? And what if she were already among them?

The Arrowpoints of Quetcham, though rich beyond measure and free from financial anxiety, found themselves wondering about the young man. Their daughter, Catherine, had refused Lord Slogan—an impeccable Irish peer—because, despite his estate, he lacked charm. Now, with Grandcourt’s arrival, they mused quietly: Was he handsome? Sound in health? Virtuous—or at least reformed? Did he hold views compatible with their own? They didn’t wish harm upon anyone, but they couldn’t help hoping he might inherit the title and bring stability to the region.

Mr. Gascoigne, the rector, was not immune to these currents. Though bound by duty and decorum, he too felt the weight of responsibility for the young people under his care—especially Gwendolen, whose spirit seemed increasingly difficult to guide. He knew, as many did, that some young men carried reputations that followed them like shadows. Yet he chose not to inquire too closely into Grandcourt’s past. After all, a man born to privilege and leisure could afford indulgences others could not. If he had gambled, so what? If he had erred, but now stood upright, then perhaps reform was genuine. It was not the same as judging a servant who claimed redemption. When one can afford to live without scandal, virtue often follows naturally.

Still, no one spoke openly. People kept their thoughts private, not out of malice, but because such matters were delicate. To confess your hopes aloud was to risk having them mocked—or worse, to have them realized before you were ready. Mrs. Gascoigne, though she said nothing to her husband, likely thought deeply about the possibility of Grandcourt meeting Gwendolen at the upcoming Archery Meeting. She had murmured to her sister, “It would be a mercy, Fanny, if that girl were well married!”—a remark that struck Mrs. Davilow as both hopeful and critical. She replied inwardly, “You will not get her to marry for your pleasure,” feeling a quiet defiance rise within her, as though her daughter’s heart belonged to no one but herself.

Mrs. Davilow, too, could not ignore the stir Grandcourt’s arrival caused. She pictured him—not as a mere suitor, but as a perfect match: handsome, accomplished, refined. But then came the harder question: Would Gwendolen love him? That was the mystery. Her daughter’s tastes were unpredictable. Beauty alone might not win her favor. Perhaps charm, wit, or even a touch of danger would do. The image of the ideal husband flickered and shifted in her mind, never settling.

She feared to speak of it, knowing that any hint of expectation might only make Gwendolen resist. Since the painful aftermath of Rex’s departure, the mother had learned caution. To suggest a path for her daughter’s heart was to risk reopening wounds. Marriage was not merely a social duty—it was a gamble, and Gwendolen had already shown she would not play by anyone else’s rules.

Yet, when Gwendolen tried on her hat for the Archery Meeting, a sudden flash of humor broke through. She tossed her head back, miming the draw of a bow, and laughed: “How I pity all the other girls at the Archery Meeting—all thinking of Mr. Grandcourt! And they have not a shadow of a chance.”

Mrs. Davilow froze. Then, with mischievous eyes, Gwendolen turned and said, “Now you know they have not, mamma. You and Uncle Henry and Aunt Lydia—you all intend him to fall in love with me.”

The mother, piqued, played along. “Oh, my dear, that is not so certain. Miss Arrowpoint has charms which you have not.”

“Of course,” Gwendolen replied, “but hers require thought. My arrow will pierce him before he has time to think. He will declare himself my slave—I shall send him round the world to fetch me back the wedding ring of a happy woman. Meanwhile, all the men between him and the title will die of strange diseases—he’ll return as Lord Grandcourt… but without the ring—and fall at my feet. I shall laugh at him—he will rise in anger—I shall laugh more—he will summon his horse and ride to Quetcham, where he finds Miss Arrowpoint just married to a poor musician, her father stunned, her mother tearing off her cap. Exit Lord Grandcourt, who returns to Diplow and, like M. Jabot, _change de linge_.”

Was ever a young woman so sharp, so full of fire? She saw through secrets as easily as birds sense storms. One could try to hide things from her—but she would know, just by the glance, the hesitation, the slight twitch of an eyebrow. It was as if she had already glimpsed more of Grandcourt than anyone else dared imagine.

So Mrs. Davilow asked, tentatively, “What kind of man do you imagine him to be, Gwendolen?”

“Let me see,” said Gwendolen, finger to lips, then pointing decisively. “Short—just above my shoulder—trying to look taller by curling his mustache and letting his beard grow long. A glass in his eye to give him an air of distinction. Very particular about his waistcoat, but unsure about the weather—will try to draw me out on that. He’ll stare at me constantly, and the glass will make him look grotesque, especially when he smiles. I’ll lower my eyes, and he’ll think I’m captivated. That night, I’ll dream of a giant insect—and the next morning, he’ll propose. The rest, as before.”

“That sounds like someone you’ve already seen,” said Mrs. Davilow gently.

“Oh, yes,” Gwendolen replied, twirling her best hat in her hands. “I wonder what a delightful young man would behave like? I know he’d have hunters and racehorses, a London house, two country homes—one with battlements, another with a veranda. And I’m sure, with a little murder here and there, he could get a title.”

Her tone was teasing, but beneath it lay a kernel of truth. Poor Mrs. Davilow felt uneasy. She had always meant her words literally, innocently. “Don’t talk like that, child,” she pleaded. “You read too many books—they fill your head with wild ideas. When your aunt and I were your age, we knew nothing of wickedness. I think it was better so.”

“Why didn’t you raise me that way, mamma?” Gwendolen asked.

The question struck deep. Mrs. Davilow’s face crumpled, tears rising. Gwendolen dropped the hat, knelt beside her, and whispered, “Mamma, mamma! I was only joking. I meant nothing.”

“How could I, Gwendolen?” the mother sobbed, unable to hear the apology. “Your will was always too strong for me—if everything else had been different.”

The daughter understood. “Dear mamma, I don’t blame you—I love you. How can you help what I am? Besides, I am very charming. Come now.” And with her handkerchief, she gently wiped away the tears.

Such tenderness calmed her mother, as it had done before. But the silence that followed was thick with unspoken fears. From then on, Mr. Grandcourt was avoided in conversation. Even when Mr. Gascoigne mentioned him, Mrs. Davilow braced herself—afraid Gwendolen might reveal too much of her insight into her uncle’s thoughts. But Gwendolen, ever careful, remained silent.

She understood people as birds understand wind and sky. She knew her uncle’s expectations, and she knew how to avoid provoking them. Their shared passion for archery helped bind them—Mr. Gascoigne delighted in her skill, and Gwendolen valued his approval. Since the rift with Rex, Mrs. Gascoigne and Anna had grown distant from her, sensing a coldness she could not explain. Anna, still wounded by Rex’s loss, found Gwendolen’s vivacity painful. She tried to hide it, but sorrow cannot imitate joy. This quiet resentment hardened Gwendolen, pushing her toward defiance.

One day, she said, “Mamma, I see now why girls are glad to be married—to escape being expected to please everyone except themselves.”

Happily, Mr. Middleton had left without declaring his feelings. Though Gwendolen was admired—her beauty and wit known across thirty miles—no one had formally proposed. Not even the most attentive curate had stepped forward. Some say it is nature’s kindness that not every admirer becomes a lover, and not every lover dares to speak.

Gwendolen, of course, was not universally adored. And eight months was not long enough for affection to bloom fully. So why, then, did people believe Mr. Grandcourt might succeed where others had failed?

Perhaps because he seemed more eligible. Or perhaps because hope, in the absence of proof, becomes belief. The Arrowpoints, for instance, had no need to worry about their daughter’s prospects. Their vision of Grandcourt was shaped not by desire, but by practicality.

But in the quiet corners of hearts, dreams still stirred—unspoken, unfulfilled, waiting for the right moment to unfold.
