I fell fast asleep the moment my head touched the stone rise, and woke to the first gold spill of sunrise over the horizon. I clambered back to its peak and looked behind me: the hollow I had crossed in silver moonlight lay completely empty, not a footprint, not a rustle of displaced grass, no trace of the uncanny life I had sensed seething beneath its calm surface. Could the wide, seemingly still expanse stretching ahead of me truly swarm with ravenous, devouring creatures hidden from casual sight? I turned my gaze forward, to the path I was fated to travel. It stretched as a vast, barren desert, one single smudge of darker colour far on the horizon that might be some patch of forest. There was no mark anywhere of human or animal presence: no wisp of smoke, no trail of dust, no faint line of ploughed earth or cultivated growth. Not a single cloud drifted in the perfectly clear sky, no thin haze softened the knife-sharp edge of the circular horizon.
I climbed down from the rise and set out toward that distant patch of green. If any living thing could survive in this dead land, it would be there; surely nothing could find purchase on the bare stone this side of the trees. When I stepped onto the plain, I found it stretched as far as the eye could reach: the entire bed of a vanished ancient river, scored by a thousand dried water runs, its rock here flat and worn smooth, there humped and jagged into sharp pinnacles. Not a single drop of moisture lingered in any of the channels. Some held patches of crumbly dry moss, and the faces of some rocks bore lichens so hard they were almost fused to the stone beneath them. The air that must once have rung nonstop with the pleasant noise of falling and running water was now as silent as a tomb.
It took my entire long, blazing day of walking to reach that green patch, which I found was indeed a full-grown forest—though I had not crossed even the tiniest trickle or brook the whole way. All through the glowing noon I was haunted by an aural mirage: the sound of a thousand streams and falls was so loud in my ears I kept squinting for a glimpse of water, only to stare at bare sunbaked rock.
The sun was dipping low toward the horizon when I stepped off the last slab of the dead riverbed and into the trees. Sunk below their crowns, its golden rays slanted between thick pillar-like trunks, spilling into pools of cool, blessed shadow that wrapped around me like a hug. I had expected a stand of pines, but these trees were half familiar and half otherworldly: some bore a passing resemblance to oaks or ash I knew, others were unlike any earth tree I had ever seen. I threw myself down beneath the boughs of a eucalyptus-like tree in full blossom: its flowers grew as hard skull-shaped calyxes, their tops peeling back like lids to spill frothy pale bloom that looked exactly like foaming brain tissue. From under the shadow of its long, curved falchion leaves I stared deep into the forest’s endless receding aisles.
Dusk fell fast after that. The treetops’ branches wove shut above me, closing off the glades and corridors I had been gazing into, and night wrapped tight around the wood. The cold hit sharp and instant, biting through my clothes to settle in my bones. How do I even describe the wild ghostly uncanniness of that night? The tree I leaned against rose tall and straight before branching, but its lower boughs curved so low they looked ready to fold shut around me at any moment, trapping me entirely within their shade. My roving, half-lidded eyes watched the clumpy foliage’s outlines shift, twisting into shapes that were not leaves at all.
A light wind picked up, rocking the boughs of a nearby tree, setting every twig and leaf swaying in rhythm with the whole. The mass of green blurred into a pack of wolves straining against an invisible wizard’s leash, their bodies heaving so hard greyhounds could never have pulled with half that savage desperation. I watched them, my fascination growing as the wind blew harder and their movements felt more and more alive. Further off, a larger, denser clump of foliage resolved into a line of war-horses, their heads and forequarters sticking out as if they were caparisoned and held in stalls no eye could see. Their necks bobbed up and down in impatience, and as the gale grew stronger it broke their steady vertical rhythm into wild side-to-side swaying. Their heads were gaunt and strange, half of them nothing but bare skulls, one with skin stretched tight over its bleached bones, another missing its entire lower jaw, hanging low as if in unutterable weariness, then heaving up every few seconds as if trying to ease a phantom bit in its mouth. Above them, perched upright on the end of a high branch, was the shape of a woman, waving her arms in imperious, demanding gestures.
The clarity of these shapes first surprised me, then made my skin prickle with unease. What if they stopped being tricks of shadow and wind, and overpowered my brain to become real? Then the last of the twilight drained away into total darkness, the wind cut off completely, every uncanny shape vanished into the black, and I fell asleep.
It was still pitch dark when I began to hear a distant, confused rushing noise tangled with faint, far-off cries. The sound swelled and swelled, until a tumult of thousands of gathering beings filled the entire wood. The noise drew nearer on all sides at once, until the spot where I lay was the dead centre of the commotion spreading through every acre of trees. I did not dare move a single hand or foot, terrified I would give away my presence to every hostile thing creeping through the dark.
At last the moon rose over the treeline, spilling pale silver light into the wood. With her very first gleam, the noise exploded into deafening uproar, and I began to make out dim shapes swirling all around me. As she climbed higher and grew brighter, the sounds grew even louder, and the shapes sharpened into clarity. A furious battle was raging all around where I lay hidden. Wild cries, roars of rage, the thunder of first onslaught, the drag of prolonged struggle, tangled together with full, articulate words in my ears. Curses and creeds, snarls and sneers, peals of mocking laughter, sacred names and howls of hate, all poured out in chaotic, overlapping jumble. Skeletons and phantoms fought in the maddest confusion imaginable. Swords swept straight through the misty phantoms, only making them shiver. Maces crashed into skeletons, shattering their bones hideously—but no one fell, no one stopped fighting, as long as two bones remained connected at a joint. Loose bones of men and horses lay scattered in heaps across the ground, and the fighting skeletons crunched over them underfoot. Bone-white gaunt skeleton steeds charged everywhere; on foot or on the backs of misty wind-tossed phantom horses, the indestructible spectres raged, ravened, and raved. Weapons clashed, hooves crushed, while skeleton jaws and phantom throats swelled the uproar with every war cry of every opinion, good or bad, that had ever bred strife, injustice, or cruelty in any world. The holiest words of all were screamed right along with the most hate-fuelled blows. Truths twisted by lies flew through the air on the wind of javelins and flying bone shards. Every few moments a fighter would turn on his own comrades, striking even more wildly, screaming over and over “THE TRUTH! THE TRUTH!” as he fought. I saw one man wheeling in an endless circle, striking out at everyone within reach. Exhausted pairs would sit side by side for a minute to catch their breath, then leap back to fight again with fresh fury. No one stopped to comfort the fallen, no one stepped aside to spare a wounded enemy.
The moon shone on through the whole long night, until the sun began to rise, and all that time I caught sight of that woman floating above the tormented, fighting throng, moving freely back and forth across the battle, one outstretched arm urging the fighters on, the other pressed tight to her side. “Ye are men: slay one another!” she shouted over the roar. I saw her dead glassy eyes, the dark stain on her chest, and recalled the sight of her I had witnessed back in the hollow. That was the battle of the dead, which I saw and heard as I lay hidden under the tree.
Just before sunrise, a soft breeze blew through the forest, and a deep, clear voice called out: “Let the dead bury their dead!” At those words, all the thousands of combatants dropped silent, noiseless, all at once. When the sun peered over the treeline, there was not a single bone to be seen anywhere—only a few scattered withered branches here and there.
I stood up, my limbs stiff from lying so long still, and set off to resume my journey through the quietest wood that had ever grown out of earth. The morning wind had died when the sun rose, and the trees stood perfectly silent. No bird sang, no squirrel, no mouse, no weasel showed itself, not even a belated moth flitted across my path. But as I walked, I kept strict watch over my own mind, not daring to let my gaze linger on any clump of leaves or twisted bough. The whole time I could hear faint distant sounds of mattocks and spades, the crunch of hurtling bones, and I was half convinced that at any moment my eyes might open to sights I did not want to see. Daylight prudence whispered to me that maybe those ten thousand phantoms were only waiting for my own willing fancy to step forward and appear.
In the middle of the afternoon I walked out of the wood entirely, and found a second vast network of dry water courses spread out before me. At first I thought I must have wandered off my intended path, and turned around to retrace my steps—but I soon realized that was not the case, and concluded I had reached another, separate branch of the same ancient dead riverbed. I set off crossing it at once, and was at the bottom of a wide deep channel when the sun set. I sat down to wait for the moon, grew drowsy, and stretched myself out on the dry moss. The very second my head touched the ground, I heard the sound of rushing streams, every kind of sweet, watery noise. That veiled, molten melody of water song lulled me into a dreamless sleep, and when I woke the sun was already high, the whole wrinkled rocky country spread out wide and visible before me. It lay covered in long shadows, striped and mottled like the pelt of some great wild beast. As the sun climbed higher, the shadows shrank, as if the rocks were sucking back all the darkness that had oozed out of them through the night.
Until that moment, I think I had loved my Arab mare and my books more than any living man or woman I had ever known. But now my soul was athirst for the presence of another human, and I even ached to meet the inhabitants of this alien world the raven had described so vaguely, the ones who were closest to my own kind. With a heavy yet hopeful heart, my mind haunted by the quiet doubt that I was even moving in any real direction at all, I kept wearily travelling north-west and by south.
