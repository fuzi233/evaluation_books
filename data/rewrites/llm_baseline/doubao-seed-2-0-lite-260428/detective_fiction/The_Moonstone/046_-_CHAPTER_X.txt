I cannot speak for how any other man in my position might have weathered those two hours of suspended certainty that hung over me then. For my own frayed nerves, the effect was unmistakeable: I was physically incapable of staying still in one spot, and morally incapable of speaking a single word to another living soul until I had heard every last secret Ezra Jennings had promised to share.

In that state, I abandoned my planned visit to Mrs. Ablewhite without a second thought. I even shrank from running into Gabriel Betteredge, my oldest familiar companion in that part of the country. I doubled back to Frizinghall, left a note for the old steward explaining I had been called away unexpectedly for a few hours, that he could expect me back by three that afternoon, and that he ought to keep to his usual dinner hour and amuse himself as he liked. I knew full well he had dozens of old friends scattered across the town, and would never be at a loose end until I returned to the inn.

That done, I struck out of town again, tramping the empty, windswept moorland that circles Frizinghall, wandering aimlessly until the hands of my watch told me it was finally time to make my way to Mr. Candy’s house.

Ezra Jennings was waiting for me, exactly as he’d said he would be. He sat alone in a spare, tiny back room connected to the main surgery through a glass-paned door. The drab buff walls were hung with hideous, vividly coloured diagrams mapping the destruction of terrible diseases. The only furniture was a bookcase crammed with dingy old medical texts, topped with a human skull in place of the usual ornamental bust; a large deal table liberally splattered with decades of ink stains; rough wooden chairs of the sort you’d find in a village cottage kitchen; a threadbare drugget rug laid in the centre of the floor; and a roughly plumbed sink set into the wall, its basin and waste pipe carrying the unmistakeable, grim echo of surgical operations past. Outside the window, bees hummed around potted flowers on the sill, birds sang in the garden, and the faint, off-key jingle of a piano from a neighbouring house drifted in at random intervals. In any other home, those everyday sounds would have felt warm, a reminder of the normal world unfolding outside. Here, they were intruders, breaking a silence that ought only to be disturbed by the noise of human suffering. My eyes fell on the mahogany surgical instrument case and the great roll of lint set on the bookshelf, and I shuddered inwardly, thinking of the sharp, harsh sounds that belonged to this room’s daily use.

“I won’t apologize for bringing you here, Mr. Blake,” Jennings said, his tone quiet. “This is the only room in the whole house, at this hour of the day, where we can be certain no one will interrupt us. My notes are all laid out for you here, and two reference books we may need to consult before we’re done. Pull your chair up to the table, and we can go through everything together.”

I dragged my chair to the table, and he handed over his handwritten notes: two large folio sheets. The first had writing scattered across its surface in disjointed gaps. The second was completely filled, top to bottom, with words in both black and red ink. My curiosity was so frayed at that moment I threw the second sheet aside in exasperation.

“Have mercy on me,” I said. “Tell me what I’m supposed to be expecting before you make me wade through all that.”

“Gladly, Mr. Blake. But do you mind if I ask you a handful of small questions first?”

“Ask me anything you like.”

He looked at me, that sad, soft smile tugging at his lips, kind interest glowing in his warm brown eyes. “You told me earlier, to your certain knowledge, you have never tasted opium in your life?”

“To my certain knowledge,” I echoed.

“You’ll understand why I use that reservation in just a moment. Let’s proceed. You have no memory of ever taking opium. At this time last year, you were suffering from severe nervous irritation, and you slept terribly every single night. Except for the night of Miss Verinder’s birthday, that is — you slept soundly through the whole night. Am I correct so far?”

“Completely correct.”

“Can you name any cause for that nervous suffering, that loss of sleep?”

“I can’t think of a single reason. Old Betteredge made a guess, come to think of it, but it hardly seems worth mentioning.”

“Pardon me — every detail matters in a case like this. What was Betteredge’s guess?”

“That my sleeplessness was caused by quitting smoking.”

“Had you been a habitual smoker for a long time?”

“Yes.”

“And you quit the habit completely, all at once?”

“Yes.”

“Betteredge was perfectly right, Mr. Blake. For most people, quitting a regular smoking habit cold turkey does real, temporary damage to the nervous system. That fully explains your weeks of sleeplessness. My next question is about Mr. Candy: do you remember getting into any kind of argument with him, at the birthday dinner or later, about his work as a doctor?”

The question pulled up a memory I’d completely buried for a full year. That foolish, petty squabble at the dinner table — the details are written out at far more length than they deserve in the tenth chapter of Betteredge’s narrative, but I’d forgotten every last specific of it. All I could recall, and all I told Jennings, was that I’d mocked the practice of medicine that night, brashly and persistently, until even the mild-mannered little doctor lost his temper for a few minutes. I remembered Lady Verinder stepping in to stop the fight, and that we’d made up soon after, becoming good friends again before we shook hands and went our separate ways for the night.

“There is one more critical thing I need to know,” Jennings said. “Had you any special, urgent reason to fear for the safety of the Moonstone, a year ago, at that exact time?”

“The strongest possible reason,” I told him. “I knew there was a conspiracy of thieves targeting the diamond, and I’d been warned to take every possible precaution to keep it safe for Miss Verinder.”

“Was the safety of the Diamond the subject of any conversation you overheard, right before you went to bed on the birthday night?”

“It was a conversation between Lady Verinder and her daughter.”

“Which you listened to, in full?”

“Yes.”

Jennings picked up his notes from the table and placed them in my hands.

“Mr. Blake,” he said, “if you read these notes now, with the context our questions and answers have given you, you will make two staggering discoveries about yourself. First: you walked into Miss Verinder’s sitting room and took the Moonstone while you were in a trance state, induced by opium. Second: the opium was given to you, without your knowledge, by Mr. Candy, as a silly practical prank intended to prove you wrong about the things you said mocking medicine at the dinner.”

I sat frozen, the papers heavy in my hands, completely stunned.

“Try to forgive poor Mr. Candy,” Jennings said softly. “I admit he caused terrible, far-reaching harm, but he never meant to. If he had never fallen ill that very night, he would have come back to Lady Verinder’s house the morning after the party, and confessed the trick he’d played on you. Miss Verinder would have heard the story, questioned him, and the truth that has stayed hidden for a full year would have come out in a single day.”

I started to pull myself back together, my temper flaring. “Mr. Candy is too sick for me to hold a grudge against him,” I said angrily. “But that trick he played is still an act of treachery, no matter how innocent the intent. I may forgive him, but I will never forget it.”

“Every doctor commits that exact kind of ‘treachery’ somewhere in their practice, Mr. Blake. The widespread, ignorant fear of opium in England is not limited to the uneducated lower classes. Every busy doctor has to deceive their patients now and then, just as Candy deceived you, when the patient will refuse a perfectly safe, helpful dose of opium out of irrational terror. I am not defending how stupid it was to prank you like that. I am only asking you to judge his motives more fairly, more mercifully.”

“How was it done?” I asked. “Who gave me the laudanum, without me noticing?”

“I cannot tell you that. Mr. Candy never once mentioned that small detail in all his fevered delirium. Perhaps your own memory will point you to the person who could have slipped it into your drink.”

“No.”

“Then there is no point chasing that line of inquiry. The laudanum was added to something you drank, in secret. Let us leave that aside, and turn to far more urgent matters. Read my notes, if you can. Wrap your mind around what actually happened that night, a year ago. I have a proposal for you — bold, startling — that will change everything for your future.”

Those last words jolted me fully alert. I looked down at the sheets in my hands, the one with sparse writing on top, filled with disconnected fragments of phrases and words that had tumbled out of Candy’s mouth while he was feverish:
“… Mr. Franklin Blake … and agreeable … down a peg … medicine … confesses … sleep at night … tell him … out of order … medicine … he tells me … and groping in the dark mean one and the same thing … all the company at the dinner-table … I say … groping after sleep … nothing but medicine … he says … leading the blind … know what it means … witty … a night’s rest in spite of his teeth … wants sleep … Lady Verinder’s medicine chest … five-and-twenty minims … without his knowing it … tomorrow morning … Well, Mr. Blake … medicine today … never … without it … out, Mr. Candy … excellent … without it … down on him … truth … something besides … excellent … dose of laudanum, sir … bed … what … medicine now.”

That was the end of the first sheet. I handed it back to Jennings. “That is exactly what you heard at his bedside?” I asked.

“Word for word,” he said. “I left out all the repetitions from my shorthand notes, of course — he would repeat certain phrases a dozen, even fifty times, the more important the idea was to his fevered brain. Those repetitions actually helped me piece the fragments together, in the end. Don’t imagine,” he added, pointing to the second sheet, “that I claim this is the exact wording Candy would have used if he was well and speaking clearly. All I did was dig through the messy, broken fragments to find the connected, coherent thought that lay underneath them, the whole time. You can judge for yourself.”

I turned to the second sheet, the key to the first. Candy’s disjointed delirium ramblings were written out in black ink, and the gaps between the fragments were filled in with Jennings’ red ink handwriting, reconstructing the full plan:
“... Mr. Franklin Blake is clever and agreeable, but he wants taking down a peg when he talks of medicine. He confesses that he has been suffering from want of sleep at night. I tell him that his nerves are out of order, and that he ought to take medicine. He tells me that taking medicine and groping in the dark mean one and the same thing. This before all the company at the dinner-table. I say to him, you are groping after sleep, and nothing but medicine can help you to find it. He says to me, I have heard of the blind leading the blind, and now I know what it means. Witty — but I can give him a night’s rest in spite of his teeth. He really wants sleep; and Lady Verinder’s medicine chest is at my disposal. Give him five-and-twenty minims of laudanum tonight, without his knowing it; and then call tomorrow morning. ‘Well, Mr. Blake, will you try a little medicine today? You will never sleep without it.’ — ‘There you are out, Mr. Candy: I have had an excellent night’s rest without it.’ Then, come down on him with the truth! ‘You have had something besides an excellent night’s rest; you had a dose of laudanum, sir, before you went to bed. What do you say to the art of medicine, now?’”

My first reaction, as I handed the manuscript back to him, was unreserved admiration for the ingenuity that had woven a smooth, perfectly logical story out of that jumbled heap of fragments. He cut me off mid-sentence before I could voice my surprise, asking me straight out if the conclusion he had drawn was the one that now made sense to me, too.

“Can you believe as I do,” he said, “that you did everything you did on the night of Miss Verinder’s birthday, you took the Moonstone, while you were completely under the influence of that laudanum dose?”

“I know almost nothing about the effects of opium, so I cannot form an opinion of my own,” I said. “But every part of what you have laid out makes perfect sense, and I am fully convinced you are right.”

“Good. Now the next problem: we are both convinced. How do we make everyone else believe it, too?”

I gestured at the two manuscript pages on the table between us. Jennings shook his head immediately.

“Useless, Mr. Blake! Completely useless, for three unarguable reasons. First, those notes were made under circumstances no ordinary person in the world has ever experienced — people will refuse to take them seriously from the start. Second, the entire argument is built on a complex medical and psychological theory, that most lay people will dismiss out of hand. Third, these notes are my creation. There is nothing but my own word to guarantee I didn’t make the whole thing up. Remember what I told you on the moor, about my reputation — how little the word of a man like me is worth to the outside world. The notes are only good for one thing: they show us how to prove your innocence, in a way no one can dispute. We have to test our conviction. And you are the only man who can make that proof real.”

“How?” I asked.

He leaned forward across the table, his voice sharp with eager purpose. “Will you try a very bold experiment?”

“I will do anything in the world to clear my name of the suspicion that hangs over me right now.”

“Will you put up with significant personal discomfort, for a short time?”

“Any inconvenience, no matter what it is.”

“Will you follow my advice completely, without questioning it? It might make you the target of fools’ ridicule. It might make your closest friends who respect you angry, and beg you not to do it.”

“Just tell me what to do,” I burst out, impatient. “I don’t care what happens, I will do it.”

“You will do this, Mr. Blake,” he said. “You will steal the Diamond again — unconsciously, for the second time — in front of a group of impartial witnesses whose testimony no one can doubt.”

I jumped to my feet, stunned. I could not speak at first, I could only stare at him.

“I know it can be done,” he went on. “And it will be done, if you agree to help me. Calm down, sit back down, and listen to the rest. I saw you smoking the other day. You picked the habit back up, didn’t you? How long ago?”

“Almost exactly a year.”

“Do you smoke more now, or less, than you did before you quit last year?”

“More.”

“Will you quit the habit again? Cold turkey, exactly the way you did before?”

I started to see his line of thinking, dimly. “I will quit right this second,” I said.

“If the exact same effects follow that you experienced last June,” Jennings said, “if you go through weeks of sleepless, jittery nervous irritation again, we have taken our first step. We will have put your nervous state almost exactly where it was on the night of the birthday. Then, if we can recreate almost exactly the household circumstances that surrounded you that night, if we can fill your mind again with all the same anxieties and fears for the safety of the Moonstone that consumed you then, we will have put you, as close as it is possible to do, back in the exact physical and mental state you were in the night Mr. Candy’s opium found you. If we manage that, there is every reasonable hope that a second identical dose of opium will trigger a repeat of the exact same actions you took that night. That is my proposal, in short. Now I will show you the hard, well-documented evidence that justifies it.”

He reached for one of the books sitting beside him on the table, opening it to a page marked with a scrap of paper. “I do not intend to bore you with a physiology lecture,” he said. “But I owe it to both of us to prove I am not asking you to do this based on some random, untested theory of my own. Long-established scientific principles, from respected, well-known authorities, fully back what I am proposing. Give me five minutes of your time, and I will show you that established science supports this experiment, no matter how wild it sounds. First, here is the core physiological principle I am working from, written by no less an authority than Dr. Carpenter. Read it yourself.”

He handed me the scrap of paper marking the page, which had these lines written on it:
“There seems much ground for the belief, that every sensory impression which has once been recognised by the perceptive consciousness, is registered (so to speak) in the brain, and may be reproduced at some subsequent time, although there may be no consciousness of its existence in the mind during the whole intermediate period.”

“That is perfectly clear,” I said.

He pushed the open book across the table to me, pointing to a passage marked with pencil lines. “Now read this account of a real case, which I believe directly applies to your situation, and to this experiment. Mr. Blake, this book was written by Dr. Elliotson, one of the greatest English physiologists of his generation — *Human Physiology*. The case he cites comes from the well-respected researcher Mr. Combe.”

The passage read as follows:
“Dr. Abel informed me,” says Mr. Combe, “of an Irish porter to a warehouse, who forgot, when sober, what he had done when drunk; but, being drunk, again recollected the transactions of his former state of intoxication.
