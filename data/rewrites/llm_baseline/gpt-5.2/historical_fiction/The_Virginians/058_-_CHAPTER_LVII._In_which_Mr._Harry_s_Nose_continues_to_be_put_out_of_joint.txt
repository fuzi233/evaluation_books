Madame de Bernstein was scarcely less content than her two Virginian nephews when Harry came back from his last audience with Lady Maria, free—so the word went—from that engagement which had bound him and galled him by turns. George sent his news to the Baroness that very evening in a little note, crisp as a wafer and as carefully folded; and not long after, her nephew Lord Castlewood—who visited her as men visit dentists, seldom and with a face composed beforehand—came to her house and spoke of the matter without any of the silk-and-velvet disguises which polite people so love.

For now that the match was ended, what was the use of parrying? The cards had been played, the stakes lifted, the table cleared. Eugene Castlewood knew well enough how to be frank when frankness could no longer lose him anything.

“She is my own sister,” said he, with a kind of warmth that sounded the more credible because it came out of so practised a mouth. “She won’t have many more chances—many more such chances—of marrying and settling herself. I might not approve of the match in every respect; I might pity your ladyship’s young Virginian favourite; but such fortune was not to be thrown away. I was bound to stand by my own flesh and blood.”

“Your candour does your lordship honour,” Madame de Bernstein replied, as if tasting a sauce to see whether it needed salt. “And your love for your sister is quite edifying.”

“Nay, we have lost the game, and I am speaking sans rancune,” Castlewood said, bowing. “It is not for you, who have won, to bear malice.”

Malice? The Baroness declared she had never been in better humour in her life. There are moods in which an old woman’s good-nature has the crackle of dry kindling: it warms all the room, and can burn any one who comes too near.

“Confess now, Eugene,” she cried, leaning forward as if she scented a secret as plainly as hot bread, “that visit of Maria to Harry at the spunging-house—that touching giving up of all his presents to her—was a stroke of thy invention?”

“Pity for the young man,” said Castlewood, demure as a priest at a christening, “and a sense of what was due from Maria to her friend—her affianced lover—in misfortune. These motives might well suffice.”

“But ’twas you advised her, my good nephew?”

He shrugged, and owned he had advised his sister to see Mr. Henry Warrington.

“And yet we should have won, in spite of your ladyship,” he continued, taking snuff with that air of a man sprinkling seasoning on a dish already his own, “had not the elder brother made his appearance. I have been trying to console my poor Maria by showing her what a piece of good fortune it is, after all, that we lost.”

“Suppose she had married Harry, and then cousin George had made his appearance?” the Baroness asked.

“Effectivement,” cried Eugene; and his little laugh was like the click of a lid on a box. “As the grave was to give up its dead, let us be thankful to the grave for disgorging in time! I am bound to say Mr. George Warrington seems a man of sense, and not more selfish than other elder sons and men of the world. My poor Molly fancied he might be—a greenhorn perhaps is the term—like his younger brother. She fondly hoped he might be inclined to go share and share alike with Twin junior; in which case, so infatuated was she about the young fellow, that I believe she would have taken him. ‘Harry Warrington, with half a loaf, might do very well,’ says I, ‘but Harry Warrington with no bread, my dear!’”

“How no bread?” Madame de Bernstein asked, bristling.

“Well, no bread except at his brother’s side-table. The elder said as much.”

“What a hard-hearted wretch!” she cried.

“Ah, bah! I play with you, aunt—cartes sur table!” Castlewood answered, with the easy cruelty of a man who thinks truth is always an excuse. “Mr. George only did what everybody else would do. We have no right to be angry with him; really we haven’t. Molly herself acknowledged as much after her first burst of grief was over, and I brought her to listen to reason. The silly old creature! to be so wild about a young lad at her time of life!”

“’Twas a real passion, I almost do believe,” said Madame de Bernstein, and her eyes—so quick to judge others—softened for once.

“You should have heard her take leave of him. C’était touchant, ma parole d’honneur! I cried. Before George, I could not help myself. The young fellow, with muddy stockings and his hair about his eyes, flings himself amongst us when we were at dinner; makes his offer to Molly in a very frank and noble manner, and in good language too; and she replies. Begad, it put me in mind of Mrs. Woffington in that new Scotch play Lord Bute’s man has wrote—Douglas—what d’ye call it? She clings round the lad; she bids him adieu in heartrending accents. She steps out of the room in a stately despair—no more chocolate, thank you. If she had made a mauvais pas no one could retire from it with more dignity. ’Twas a masterly retreat after a defeat. We were starved out of our position, but we retired with all the honours of war.”

“Molly won’t die of the disappointment,” Madame de Bernstein said, sipping her cup as calmly as if it were only thin chocolate and not another woman’s bruised heart that had been served with it.

My lord showed his yellow teeth in a grin that creaked. “He, he! She hath once or twice before had the malady very severely, and recovered perfectly. It don’t kill, as your ladyship knows, at Molly’s age.”

How should her ladyship know? She had not married Doctor Tusher until she was far advanced in life; and Madame de Bernstein came later still, as names and fortunes often do, trailing their histories behind them like the smell of smoke in an old gown. Yet even those who have been slow to happiness are not ignorant of misfortune; and misfortune, like a harsh seasoning, teaches the palate to recognize bitterness in another dish.

In the little world, people quarrel and scratch like cats in a back-yard, and keep their bristles up for months. In the great world, folks are wiser in their generation. They differ; they cease to meet; then, when the game is over and the stakes have changed hands, they make it up and come together again, no questions asked. A stray prodigal—or a stray puppy—will be brought in under the benefit of an amnesty though you know well enough what ugly company he has kept. So it happened now. For six months past, while the Castlewoods and Madame de Bernstein had been tugging at poor Harry as if he were a prize joint to be carved and divided, these two branches of the Esmond family had held apart. But the question being settled, they were free to meet again as though no difference had ever separated them. The Baroness drove in her great coach to Lady Castlewood’s rout; and the Esmond ladies appeared smiling at Madame de Bernstein’s drums; and they loved each other just as much as they previously had done—no more and no less, which is to say, as much as their interests could bear.

“So, sir, I hear you have acted like a hard-hearted monster about your poor brother Harry!” Madame de Bernstein cried when George came within reach; and she menaced him with her stick, delighted as a cook who has found a nephew stealing cream and can scold him for it.

“I acted but upon your ladyship’s hint,” George answered, colouring like a man caught with his hand in the spice-box, “and desired to see whether it was for himself or his reputed money that his kinsfolk wanted to have him.”

“Nay, Maria could not marry a poor fellow who was utterly penniless, and whose elder brother said he would give him nothing!”

“I did it for the best, madam,” George said, still blushing.

“And so thou didst, O thou hypocrite!” cried the old lady.

“Hypocrite, madam! and why?” George asked, drawing himself up with a dignity that sat oddly on a young man who had lately been a ghost to his own friends.

“I know all, my infant!” she said in French, her voice dropping into that intimate tone which makes a secret sound like a dish passed under the cloth. “Thou art very like thy grandfather. Come—that I embrace thee! Harry has told me all, and that thou hast divided thy little patrimony with him!”

“It was but natural, madam. We have had common hearts and purses since we were born. I but feigned hard-heartedness in order to try those people yonder,” George replied; and his eyes filled, honest with the simple habit of affection, as if he had been cutting onions for a stew and could not help the tears.

“And thou wilt divide Virginia with him too?” asked the Baroness, keen as ever to taste the very bottom of a matter.

“I don’t say so. It were not just,” George answered. “The land must go to the eldest born; and Harry would not have it otherwise. It may be I shall die, or my mother outlive the pair of us. But half of what is mine is his. And he, it must be remembered, only was extravagant because he was mistaken as to his position.”

“But it is a knight of old—it is a Bayard—it is the grandfather come to life!” Madame de Bernstein cried to her woman as she withdrew for the night, pleased with this little romance of honour as with a familiar tune played truly at last. And when the lads left her, it was to poor Harry she gave the two fingers, and to George the rouged cheek; while George, for his part, blushed almost as deep as that often-dyed rose at so notable a mark of favour.

Harry Warrington was the least envious of men, and did honour to his brother as in all respects his chief, guide, and superior; yet no wonder a certain humiliation and disappointment pressed upon him after he had been deposed from his short-lived eminence as Fortunate Youth and heir to boundless Virginian territories. At Kensington they promised and vowed that they would love him all the better after his fall; Harry bowed low and professed himself thankful; but he could not help perceiving, when he went with his brother to the state entertainment with which Lord Castlewood regaled his new-found kinsman, that George was all in all to his cousins. George had the talk, the compliments, the little attentions—petits soins—served up to him as neatly as a well-dressed sideboard; whilst of Harry no one took much notice save poor Maria.

Maria followed him with wistful looks, pursued him with eyes that carried a dismal reproach; and, as it were, blamed him because she had left him. “Ah!” those eyes seemed to say, “’tis mighty well of you, Harry, to have accepted the freedom which I gave you; but I had no intention, sir, that you should be so pleased at being let off.” She had given him up; yet she did not quite forgive him for taking her at her word. She would not have him—and yet she would.

How delightful is the beginning of a love-business, and how undignified, sometimes, the end! Young Damon and young Phillis (or middle-aged ditto ditto) see before them, once their artless loves are confessed, a romantic country spread like a map at their feet. Linked together, they fancy they will wander there until extreme old age. There may be rocks and roaring rivers; but will not Damon’s strong true love carry Sweetheart over? There may be dragons; but shall not his courageous sword cut them down? And then at eve, how they will rest cuddled together like babes in a wood, moss their couch, stars their canopy, their arms mutual pillows!

That is the plan; and—O me!—they have not got a mile when they come to a wall and find they must walk back again. They are squabbling with the post-boy at Barnet (the first stage on the Gretna road, I mean); behold Strephon has not got any money; or here is papa with a whacking horsewhip who takes Miss back and locks her up crying in the schoolroom. The parting is heart-breaking; but when she has married the banker and had eight children, and he has become, it may be, a prosperous barrister—or it may be a seedy raff who has gone twice or thrice into the Gazette—when Strephon and Delia meet again in after years, is not the meeting ridiculous? And yet—such is the obstinacy of romance—some women are faithful to it to the end, and from eighteen to eight-and-fifty (for what I know) are always expecting their hearts to break.

In fine, when you have been in love and are so no more, when the King of France with twenty thousand men, with colours flying, music playing, and all the pomp of war, having marched up the hill, proceeds to march down again, he and you are alike in an absurd position. This, no doubt, was what Harry felt when he went to Kensington and encountered the melancholy reproachful eyes of his cousin.

Foolish it is; and melancholy too—like looking into a house you once lived in and seeing black casements and emptiness where once shone the fires of welcome. But—ha!—how bitter, how absurd to pass sentimentally by No. 13 and see somebody else grinning out of the window, evidently on the best terms with the landlady. I always feel hurt, even at an inn I frequent, if I see other folks’ trunks and boots at the doors of rooms once mine. Have those boots lolled on the sofa where I reclined? I kick you from before me, you muddy vulgar highlows!

So, considering that his period of occupation was over, and Maria’s rooms—if not given up to a new tenant—were at any rate to let, Harry did not feel easy in his cousin’s company, nor she possibly in his. He found either he had nothing to say to her, or that what she had to say to him was dull and commonplace; and that the red lip of a white-necked pipe of Virginia—sweet and steady, with its homely leaf cured under sun and smoke—was decidedly more agreeable to him now than Maria’s softest accents and most melancholy moue. When George went to Kensington, then, Harry did not care much about going, and pleaded other engagements.

At his uncle’s house in Hill Street he was no better amused; and indeed was treated by the virtuous people there with scarce any attention at all. The ladies did not scruple to deny themselves when he came. He could scarcely have believed in such insincerity after their caresses, their welcome, their repeated vows of affection. But happening to sit with the Lamberts for an hour after he had called upon his aunt, he saw her ladyship’s chairmen arrive with an empty chair; saw his aunt step out and enter the vehicle; and she did not so much as blush when he made her a bow from the opposite window.

To be denied by his own relations—to have that door which had opened so kindly slammed in his face! He would not have believed such a thing possible, poor simple Harry said. Perhaps he thought the door-knocker had a tender heart and was not made of brass; not more changed than the head of that knocker was my Lady Warrington’s virtuous face when she passed her nephew.

“My father’s own brother’s wife! What have I done to offend her? Oh, Aunt Lambert, Aunt Lambert, did you ever see such cold-heartedness?” Harry cried, his voice rising quick as a kettle beginning to sing.

“Do we make any difference to you, my dear Harry?” said Aunt Lambert; and she cast a side look at her youngest daughter, as if listening for the undertone in a phrase, the way one listens for a bird’s note under street-rumble. “The world may look coldly at you, but we don’t belong to it; so you may come to us in safety.”

“In this house you are different from other people,” Harry answered. “I don’t know how, but I always feel quiet and happy somehow when I come to you.”

“Quis me uno vivit felicior? aut magis hac est / Optandum vita dicere quis potuit?” called out General Lambert, who loved to season his talk with Latin as other men do with nutmeg.

“Do you know where I got these verses, Mr. Gownsman?” he asked, turning to his son from college, come home to pass the Easter holiday.

“You got them out of Catullus, sir,” said the scholar.

“I got them out of no such thing, sir,” the General cried, pleased to contradict as some are pleased to carve. “I got them out of my favourite Democritus Junior—out of old Burton, who has provided many indifferent scholars with learning;” and Burton and Montaigne were favourite authors with the good General.
