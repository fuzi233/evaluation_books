In the midst of Harry Warrington's time, the debate raged on among the literati regarding the merits of ancient versus modern writers. The learned masses overwhelmingly favored the former, much like the moderns of today would be considered the ancients of tomorrow. It was a timeless cycle, where each era looked upon the preceding one with a mixture of reverence and superiority. 

Consider the state of knowledge during the past century – the venerable Gibbon aside, one could hardly find two professors at Cambridge and Glasgow who knew more Greek than their counterparts in the universities of 18th-century Europe, including the fabled institution in Athens. As for scientific progress, the ancients were not far behind their modern counterparts. In terms of public and private morality, it was a question of whether the current era or its predecessor a century prior held the upper hand. Gentlemen of Parliament, like those of Mr. Disraeli's House of Commons, were not necessarily above reproach, just as their predecessors under Walpole and Newcastle had not been immune to corruption. As for the ladies, though they may not have been a society of Vestals, their reputation for virtue was far from tarnished by the scandals of a bygone era.

The chronicles of yesteryear told a tale of moral decay, but perhaps that was because the societal norms of the time allowed for greater transparency in storytelling. Consider the works of authors like Richardson, Fielding, and Smollett – novels like Clarissa, Tom Jones, and Roderick Random painted a picture of high society that would be deemed risqué by today's standards. If we were to recreate the lives of characters like Lovelace, Lady Bellaston, or the enigmatic Lady of Quality, the refined sensibilities of the 19th century would likely be shocked, offended, and utterly dismayed.

It was a curious thing, this tension between artistic expression and social propriety. The novelist's craft was constrained by the restraints of the censor, just as the beauty of a statue at St. Peter's was obscured by the drapery that veiled its limbs. The page was subject to the whims of the despot, society, which decreed what was acceptable and what was not. We might lament the loss of nuance, the sacrifice of artistic freedom to the altar of prudence, but it was a compromise we made to avoid offending the delicate sensibilities of our audience.
