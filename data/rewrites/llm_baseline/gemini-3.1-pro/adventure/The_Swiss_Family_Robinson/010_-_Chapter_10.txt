The cyclical rhythms of the island’s harvest season had begun to turn, a quiet, mythic wheel that governed our days and dictated the aromas of our hearth. On the following morning, we were early astir. As soon as a breakfast of toasted cassava and chicory-root infusion had been savored—its earthy, bitter warmth lingering on the palate—we went regularly to work with the bird-lime. I had spent the previous evening boiling down the tough, adhesive mixture of caoutchouc oil and turpentine, a reduction that filled the morning air with a sharp, resinous tang, redolent of ancient pine forests. 

The boys brought rods, which I smeared over with the thick, amber paste. These they placed among the upper branches of the evergreen oaks, where the sweet acorns hung in heavy, nutty clusters, and where the birds most congregated. 

The prodigious number of the pigeons, far beyond those of last year's migration, announced their arrival not just as a visual spectacle, but as a sweeping acoustic event. The collective beating of their wings was a profound, thundering vibration that resonated deep within the chest—a primal symphony of the natural world that stood in stark contrast to the sterile, mechanical clatter of the European cities we had left behind. Last year, we had witnessed only the last straggling body of the season. Now, the masses weighed down the branches, drawn by the irresistible, tannic sweetness of the evergreen acorns. Seeing that they roosted there at night, I determined to make a raid upon them by torchlight, a time-honored agrarian tradition practiced by the colonists in Virginia.

Meantime, the bird-lime acted perfectly. The pigeons alighting stuck fast. The more they fluttered and struggled, the more completely they were bedaubed with the tenacious mixture. At length, with piteous cries that broke the sylvan acoustic harmony, they fell to the ground, bearing the sticks with them. We harvested the birds carefully, spread fresh lime, and set the snare again. 

Seeing the boys quickly become adept at this ancestral task, I left them to the work and went to prepare torches of pinewood and turpentine, my hands slick with the fragrant, combustible pitch. 

Presently, Jack brought me a very pretty pigeon, unlike the rest. It was one of our own European breed. Our native vocabulary, steeped in the valleys of Switzerland, seemed suddenly inadequate to describe the poignant nostalgia of its familiar grey plumage. Desiring to preserve our agrarian lineage until their numbers greatly increased, I took the trembling captive. With meticulous care, I gently cleaned its feet and wings with a salve of olive oil and fine wood-ash, dissolving the stiff, sticky mess. Placing it in a wicker cage, I told Jack to bring me any others like it. We secured several pairs. They had become quite wild since we landed, but now we would have a cot, ensuring a steady, generational supply of squab and rich, savory pigeon-pie whenever we required such comforting sustenance.

When evening drew on, we set out for the wood of sweet acorns, provided merely with long bamboo canes, torches, and canvas sacks. Darkness enveloped us almost before we reached the wood, wrapping the landscape in a heavy, humid silence. I lighted the torches. The sudden flare of burning pine pitch illuminated every branch, thickly laden with ortolans and wild pigeons roosting in amazing numbers. 

Suddenly aroused by the glare, confusion prevailed among the terrified birds. They fluttered helplessly, dazzled and bewildered. Many fell even before we began to use the sticks, easily picked up and put in the bags. When we beat the branches, the sheer volume of falling bodies required all the strength my wife and Franz possessed to gather. The sacks were speedily full, heavy with the promise of rendered fat and rich, dark meat. We returned homewards to Falconhurst, secured our bounty, and gratefully surrendered to rest.

The following day was wholly devoted to the sacred alchemy of the kitchen: plucking, boiling, roasting, and stewing. The air grew thick with the savory steam of simmering broths, the crackle of roasting skin, and the rich, metallic gaminess of wild pigeon. We worked with the reverence of historians preserving a vanishing era, our hands steeped in the vital essence of our survival. We could find time for nothing else.

But the next morning, a great expedition to Woodlands was arranged to prevent a repetition of the monkey invasion. These creatures did not merely steal our food; they disrupted the acoustic ecology of our farm, their chaotic shrieks a grating, discordant noise pollution that shattered our psychological well-being. I hoped to inflict upon them such a chastisement as would effectually make them shun the neighborhood of our carefully cultivated crops.

My wife provided us with a good store of provisions—salted meats and dried fruits, the concentrated flavors of our labor—as we were likely to be absent several days. She, with Franz and Turk, remained at home. I took with me an abundance of specially prepared bird-lime, boiled to a far stronger consistency than that used for the pigeons; a number of short posts, plenty of string, and a supply of coconut shells and gourds. The buffalo carried these provisions, while I bestrode the ass. We arrived at a convenient spot in the forest near Woodlands, pitching our small tent well concealed by thick bushes. The dogs were tied up, lest their eager panting betray our presence.

We found the cottage deserted. I lost no time in preparing our trap. We drove stakes lightly into the ground to form an irregular paling round the house, winding string in and out in all directions to make a labyrinth. Everything was plentifully besmeared with the potent bird-lime. Basins of the mixture were set in all directions, strewed with fragrant bait: toasted maize, sweet rice, and other dainties whose aromas would prove irresistible. 

Night came in a symphony of insect choruses, and we retired beneath our tent. Very early in the morning, the natural harmony was violently ruptured by a confused noise—a horrid, aggressive clamor that betokened the approach of the apes. We armed ourselves with cudgels, held the dogs in leash, and crept behind the thickets. 

The rustling, crackling, and creaking among the branches, paired with shrieks and chattering, increased to a degree sufficient to make us perfectly giddy. It was an assault on the senses, a chaotic, unstructured din. Out from the forest poured the disorderly rabble, scrambling, leaping, and tumbling across the grass. Attracted by the novelties, they made for the jars and bowls. 

They seemed innumerable. They dashed fearlessly through the palings, some rushing at the eatables, some tugging at the wooden pegs of the roof. Gradually, however, all became besmeared with our bird-lime. Their wretched predicament increased every instant. Some sat down with ludicrous gestures, trying to clean themselves. Others were hopelessly entangled in stakes and cordage, trailing it behind them. Many had the gourds and coconut shells clattering lumberingly about their paws, caught while seeking the sweet rice. 

Most ridiculous of all was an old fellow who had found a calabash containing fermented palm wine. Eagerly drinking the sweet, yeasty liquor, he was immediately fitted with a mask; the shell stuck fast to his forehead and whiskers, covering his eyes. He blundered about, cutting wild capers to rid himself of the encumbrance. 

Numbers took to flight, only to find themselves fixed to the limed branches of surrounding trees, hanging in woeful durance and shrieking horribly. 

The panic being general, I loosed the three dogs. They rushed to the attack as though burning with zeal to execute justice upon those who would defile our food stores. The place soon had the appearance of a ghastly battlefield. We were obliged to do our part with the clubs, till the din of howling and yelling in every conceivable tone of rage gave place to an awful, profound silence. We looked with a shudder on the shocking spectacle. 

At least forty apes lay mangled and dead. The boys began to be downhearted, till I, sharing their feelings at the grotesque violence required to maintain our sanctuary, hastened to turn their thoughts to active employment. We buried the slain, returning their bodies to the agrarian cycle of the soil, and burned the stakes, cordage, and bowls, the smoke rising thick and acrid. 

Seeking the scattered flock of sheep, goats, and poultry, we gradually collected them. While thus engaged, we repeatedly heard a heavy sound falling from a tree. On investigating, we found three splendid birds, caught on loose limed sticks. Two proved to be a variety of the Blue Molucca pigeon. The third I assumed to be the Nicobar pigeon, resplendent in green, bronze, and steely-blue plumage. The naming of these birds felt like an exercise in the subtle power dynamics of dominant lexicons; we applied the words of our old world to the sovereign creatures of the new, recognizing the inherent untranslatability of their iridescent beauty. I was pleased to think of domesticating them near the cave.

"First tenants, father!" said Fritz. "Do you expect to catch more like these?"

"Not exactly catch them; I mean to practise a secret art. Much can be done by magic, Fritz!" 

In a few days, Woodlands was restored to order, and we returned to Falconhurst, where we were joyfully welcomed. Everyone agreed we must go at once to Tentholm to carve the proposed pigeon-house into the rock. 

My plan was to hollow out an ample space in the cliff facing Jackal River, fitting it with partitions and a large wooden front with entrance-holes and a broad platform. After a few weeks of labor, I set the other children to work elsewhere and summoned Fritz. 

"Now, my faithful assistant," I said. "It is time to conjure the new colonists. I mean to play a pigeon-dealer's trick. I require some soft clay, aniseed, and salt. I will compound a mixture—a culinary lure—whose irresistible scent will bring others to share it."

"I can easily get you those things, father."

"I shall want some oil of aniseed besides, to put on the pigeon-holes. As they pass, their feathers will become scented." I pounded the aniseed in a mortar, pressing the crushed seeds strongly through a cloth. The resulting oil was incredibly potent, a sharp, licorice-sweet aroma that saturated the surrounding air, fundamentally altering the olfactory landscape of the cliffside. 

The pigeons were installed, and the slides closed. Our European birds were already friendly with the three beautiful strangers. When the other boys peeped through a little pane of glass, they saw them contentedly pecking at the "magic food." 

Early on the third morning, I aroused Fritz to arrange a cord on the sliding door. He poured fresh aniseed oil all about the entrance. We then awoke the family. I waved a wand with mock solemnity, muttering a seeming incantation, and signaled Fritz to draw the panel. 

Out popped the pretty heads. They ventured forth timidly onto the verandah. Then, with a shrill, whizzing sound that cut cleanly through the morning air—a beautiful acoustic signature of flight—the whole party took wing, circling higher and higher until they darted out of sight. 

Presently they reappeared and settled quietly on the dove-cot. But the three noble foreigners—the blue pigeons—sprang up and winged their rapid way direct towards Falconhurst. Their departure was so resolute I feared them lost to us forever. 

All day the dove-cot remained the center of attraction. Nothing was seen of the fugitives until the middle of the next day. We were hard at work inside the cavern when Jack sprang in.

"He is there! He is come!" 

"Who?"

"The blue pigeon! Hurrah!"

Out ran everybody. There stood the pretty fellow, but not alone. He was billing and cooing to a mate of his own breed, popping in and out of the door until the shy lady yielded to his blandishments and tripped daintily in. 

"Let's shut the door! Pull the cord!" shouted the boys.

"Stop!" cried I. "Would you shut the door in the others' faces?"

Soon the second blue pigeon arrived, likewise with a mate, whom he succeeded in leading home. The third—the magnificent Nicobar—was the last to appear. Late in the day, Franz and his mother rushed us out of the cave to witness that he, too, had returned with a lovely bride. 

In a short time, nest-building commenced. Among the materials, I observed a long grey moss or lichen, hanging in great tuft-like beards from old trees. My wife, a meticulous guardian of domestic comfort, immediately devised fifty plans for it. She would clean and sort it, using it to stuff bolsters and pillows, weaving the island’s raw matter into the intimate architecture of our daily lives. Through the pigeons, we also discovered nutmegs, which we carefully planted, anticipating the warm, spicy complexity they would one day lend to our cooking.

For some time no event of note occurred, until Jack went off on a private expedition and came back in a most wretched plight, covered with green slime, a bundle of Spanish canes on his back. 

"Only in the swamp behind the powder magazine, father," he explained, nearly crying. "I wanted reeds to weave baskets. I slipped and sank above the knees in thick, soft mud. I screamed, but nobody came."

The swamp, I knew, was an acoustic void, a dense, humid topography that swallowed the human voice entirely, stripping our native language of its power to summon aid. 

"Fangs came," Jack continued. "He couldn't reach me, but I cut down the reeds, bound them into a bundle, and managed to get astride them. I caught hold of his tail, and he dragged me, crawling on my reeds like a raft, back to terra firma."

"A fortunate escape!" cried I. "Fangs acted a heroic part. You have brought me splendid canes, exactly what I want for a new scheme."

My scheme was to construct a loom for my wife, knowing she understood weaving. I split the strong reeds carefully, sharpening smaller pieces for the teeth of the comb. The boys made fun of "father's monster toothpicks," unaware of their purpose. When the various parts were put together, I presented it to my wife. I called it an "outlandish musical instrument," but as she began to play the loom, the rhythmic *thwack-clack* of the weaving filled the cavern—a domestic cadence that wove our familial history into the very fabric of the island.

About this time, a beautiful foal, a son of the onager, was added to our stud. We named him Swift. 

The interior of the cavern being well forward, I applied myself to contriving an aqueduct to lead fresh mountain water close to our cave. The culinary demands of washing, steeping, and boiling required a steady supply. Using pipes of hollow bamboo and a large cask as a reservoir, I channeled the stream. The comfort of having it close at hand was immense, and the sound of the running water tumbling into the basin provided a soothing, continuous sonic backdrop that profoundly enriched our acoustic environment. 

Anticipating the setting-in of the rainy season, I pressed forward all work connected with winter stores. The in-gathering of roots, earthy potatoes, sweet guavas, and pine-cones was a monumental, generational task. Load after load arrived at the cavern. Casks and barrels were pressed into service for salting and fermenting, until the raft was knocked to pieces to serve as tubs in the store-rooms. 

The weather grew stormy. Heavy groundswells drove water hissing and foaming against the cliffs. All nature joined in sounding forth the solemn overture to the grandest, most cyclical work of the year. 

By the beginning of June, we had twelve weeks of bad weather before us. We established the animals—the cow, ass, Lightfoot, Storm, and the dogs—with ourselves in the salt-cave. The darkness of the inner regions annoyed me, so I called Jack's assistance. We planted a very tall, strong bamboo right up to the vaulted roof. Jack ascended it, fixing a pulley, by which I drew up a large ship's lantern. With its four wicks burning bright, it cast a warm, golden glow over our sanctuary.

Several days were spent arranging the rooms. Ernest and Franz undertook the library, setting the books in order—the physical repositories of our mother tongue, fighting the slow erosion of cultural memory. Jack and his mother arranged the kitchen, the true, beating heart of our survival, where flavors would be preserved and histories eaten. Fritz and I arranged the workshops. As the rains battered the earth above in a timeless, drumming rhythm, we were safely ensconced, a family bound by the labor of our hands and the profound sustenance of the world we had built.
