They met the landlord in the narrow passage, his cap doffed in a low bow.

“Welcome, messieurs.”

Gerard laughed. “Come, we are not in Germany!”

In the common room, the mistress—a buxom woman of forty—greeted them with a curtsy and a warm smile. “Trouble yourselves to sit, fair sir,” she said, dusting the chairs with her apron, though they scarcely needed it.

“Thank you, dame,” Gerard replied, amused. *The trouble of sitting?* he mused. *Soon, the labor of eating, the toil of digestion, and by Hercules’ aid, the great struggle of sleep.*

Denys ordered supper for two.

“Only two?” Gerard exclaimed. “What, do we not wait for forty more?”

Denys grinned. “Courage, *camarade*! *Le diable—*”

“*Est mort*,” Gerard finished dryly.

The inn’s hierarchy was clear—women ruled here. The landlord drifted aimlessly, bowing to every passerby, while the women brushed past him like leaves in a breeze, unheeding.

A maid brought their meal, followed by the mistress, hands empty but words plentiful.

“Eat well, my masters,” she urged. “Your only enemy here lies beneath your knife.”

They obeyed. The mistress settled nearby, chatting as though they were old friends. Soon, she bustled off, replaced by her daughter—a gentle, dark-eyed girl of twenty, who took a seat beside them. She lingered, asking after Gerard’s travels, her kindness softening the rough edges of the road.

When she left, Gerard sighed. “An inn? It feels like home.”

Denys puffed with pride. “*Qui fit Français, il fit courtois.*”

“Courteous? Nay, Christian. To welcome strangers as kin—that is grace.”

Their third visitor was Marion, the chambermaid—a sharp-tongued, quick-witted woman with laughing eyes and a bare, sturdy arm. The moment she entered, a traveler flung a jest at her. Marion riposted, and the room roared at *his* expense. One by one, men tried their wit against hers; one by one, they fell.

Gerard gaped. “Denys, now I see why they say a woman’s tongue is her sword.”

Denys, ever the chivalrous fool, cheered her on. “Courage, *ma mie*! *Le diable est mort!*”

She wheeled on him. “How can he be dead while archers still draw breath?”

Laughter erupted. Marion turned next to Gerard, eyeing his sparse beard. “*Monsieur* might like his whiskers starched.”

Gerard, unruffled, gestured to Denys’s bristling chin. “Mine is unworthy. Try this fine growth.”

Marion sniffed. “I’ll tend it when I starch the broomsticks.”

The mistress returned, and Marion’s fire dimmed to meekness in an instant—a performance wasted, for the dame had heard all and was amused.

“Marion,” she chided, “if you laid an egg each time you cackled, we’d never lack omelets.”

When Gerard asked for their reckoning, the mistress waved him off. “Pay when you leave. ‘Lose the guest, find the money’—that’s our rule.”

Gerard pointed outside. “But your sign says—’”

“Bah! Let it stick to the wall.” She gestured to the ceiling, black with smoke and scribbles—accounts kept by knife-scratches in the soot. The sign, she explained, was merely to deter beggars.

“How do you know *I* can pay?”

She smiled. “Yours is the best face to enter here this autumn.”

Denys feigned offense. “And mine?”

“Honest, but I’d not meet it on a dark road.”

Marion giggled. “Said I not you could charm them still, mistress?”

“Reading faces is no marvel,” the dame told Gerard. “’Tis the first thing a girl learns in an inn.”

“And frying eggs the second,” Marion added. “No—lying. Frying’s third.”

“And holding her tongue last,” the mistress shot back.

Marion sighed, flopping dramatically onto Gerard. “Alas, I am but a chambermaid—spurned, discarded—”

Gerard shoved her off. “Enough! Must I endure jests while speaking sense?”

Marion pirouetted. “Ah, mistress, you reign supreme—at least with babes.”

The dame shook her head. “Girl, we excel in two arts: reading faces and frying eggs. ’Tis all we know.”

Gerard frowned. “But how, by Denys’s face, did you guess his folly—his *mulierosity*?”

“His *what*?”

“A word I coined. It means he is wrapped in women like a vine.”

The dame chuckled. “Eyes never lie. His follow every skirt. Yours turn to the speaker—man or woman—like sense.”

Denys guffawed. “You misread him! This smooth-face is a Turk at heart—smitten with one Margaret, a Dutch lass.”

The dame nodded sagely. “Then cling to her, boy. Better one true love than a hundred follies.”

Marion smirked. “Aye, and his purse will thank him.”

The dame shooed her. “Enough clack!”

The landlord piped up. “Must the cock never crow?”

“Not indoors,” his wife retorted. “The hen rules here.”

Gerard quoted: *“Femme veut en toute saison, être dame en sa maison.”*

The dame clapped. “A true saying! Give me an old saw over new lies any day.”

“And me a young husband,” Marion sighed. “Ah, had one spoken then—”

Denys grinned. “Some poor soul’s spared.”

As night fell, the household gathered—travelers, host, servants—around the fire, trading tales. Gerard told one of martyred saints, thrilling them all before drowsing off.

Marion lit a rush and led him to a room with two clean beds.

“Paradise,” he murmured. “I’ve not lain in bare linen since Holland.”

She smirked. “Then let flax meet down—so, *allons!*” She offered her cheek.

“What?”

“’Tis custom—goodnight’s toll.”

“Not in my land.”

She huffed. “Here, even greybeards pay it. Must I beg?”

“Ask Denys. He’s *mulierose*; I am not.”

She sighed. “To bed unpaid, then. Rare youth, who resists brazen maids!” At the door, she turned, solemn. “The Virgin guard thee, wanderer. Amen.”

Then—footsteps, laughter downstairs.

*What a creature,* Gerard thought, yawning. *Half imp, half saint.*

Sleep took him to Sevenbergen.

Morning found him trapped—his clothes gone. Marion entered, arms laden with fresh-washed linen.

“Oh, bless you!”

She winked. “Found me good at last?”

“Was this another custom?”

“Nay, but we ask travelers at night if they’ll have washing. You slept so sound, I spared you.”

Denys, ever the commentator, interjected. “*She* spared *you*—your skin’s like cream, she says.”

Marion rolled her eyes. “Guess again.”

Gerard smiled. “I’m sorry I refused you—anything.”

“Ah! Now he’d pay. Too late. *Quits.*” She vanished, victorious.

At breakfast, they settled their score and prepared to leave. Then—custom struck again. The women kissed them farewell, the landlord too, with hearty cries of “Return soon!”

On the road, they marveled at the shoemaker’s courtesy, the town’s pebbled walk (“For dry feet on Sundays,” said Denys), and then—a gibbet. Two skeletons swung.

Gerard shuddered. “Must our welcome end so?”

Denys shrugged. “Each rogue aloft means one less below.”

Farther on, a wheel studded with iron prongs held tattered bones.

Gerard hid his face. “What’s left of a man—once like us!”

Denys scoffed. “A thief.”

“How know you? Have no innocents suffered?”

“None of my kin swung.”

“Luck, not virtue. The saints died hard—even in Burgundy’s shadow.”

Denys reddened. “Blasphemy! The gibbet proves guilt here.”

Gerard sighed. “Then on Calvary, you’d have cheered the crosses—Roman gibbets all.”

Denys sputtered. “Ancient lies! No matter now.”

“Does time wash blood from hands?” Gerard turned away. “Our hearts stand far apart. Yet I love thee.”

By afternoon, wailing filled the air. A grim procession approached: pikemen herding a hundred souls—men silent, women weeping, children clutched in arms.

Gerard halted. “What crime can these all share?”

Denys nudged him. “Ask.”

The officer, though wary, answered courteously at Gerard’s plea.

“They are vagrants—cleared from the roads by order.”

“But the babes—”

“The law spares none. Idleness breeds thieves.”

Gerard stared as they passed, his joy in Burgundy now shadowed.

Denys clapped his shoulder. “Come. The world is as it is.”

They walked on, the sun sinking, the road stretching—a little wiser, a little heavier.
