At approximately this juncture, a stranger arrived in Cloisterham. He was a figure of considerable age, distinguished by a head of stark white hair framed by jet-black eyebrows. Clad in a somewhat snug blue surtout, a buff waistcoat, and grey trousers, he possessed a distinct military bearing. Yet, upon entering the Crozier—the city's established hotel—he introduced himself not as a soldier, but as an "idle dog" subsisting entirely upon his own means. Furthermore, he declared his intention to secure lodging within the picturesque old city for a month or two, with the ultimate ambition of settling down permanently.

These announcements were delivered in the hotel's coffee-room, addressing an audience that might or might not exist, by the stranger as he stood with his back to the empty hearth, patiently awaiting his fried sole, veal cutlet, and pint of sherry. The waiter, whose business was notoriously slack at the Crozier, served as the sole representative of the public and absorbed the entirety of the information.

This gentleman's white head was notably large, crowned with a shock of white hair that was unusually thick and ample.

"I suppose," he remarked, shaking his mane with the vigorous motion of a Newfoundland preparing to sit for dinner, "that a fair lodging for a single buffer might be found in these parts, eh?"

The waiter expressed no doubt regarding this possibility.

"Something old," the gentleman specified. "Take my hat down for a moment from that peg, will you? No, I do not wish it; simply inspect the interior. What inscription adorns it?"

The waiter read aloud: *"Datchery."*

"Now you know my name," said the gentleman. "Dick Datchery. Return it to its perch. I was noting that I should prefer something old—something odd and out of the way; something venerable, architectural, and inconvenient."

"We possess a splendid selection of inconvenient lodgings in the town, sir, I believe," replied the waiter, displaying a modest confidence in the locality's resources; "indeed, I have no hesitation in asserting that we could accommodate you in that regard, however particular you may prove to be. However, 'architectural'!"

This adjective seemed to perplex the waiter, causing him to shake his head in confusion.

"Anything 'Cathedral-y,' now," Mr. Datchery suggested.

"Mr. Tope," said the waiter, his face lighting up as he rubbed his chin thoughtfully, "would be the most likely individual to provide information on that score."

"Who is Mr. Tope?" inquired Dick Datchery.

The waiter explained that he held the office of Verger, and that Mrs. Tope had, in times past, either rented lodgings herself or offered to do so. However, since no tenant had ever accepted her offer, Mrs. Tope's window-bill—a long-standing institution of Cloisterham—had vanished. It was presumed that one day it had simply fallen down and remained lost forever.

"I shall call upon Mrs. Tope," Mr. Datchery decided, "after dinner."

Consequently, once his meal was concluded, he was duly directed to the location and departed forthwith. However, given that the Crozier was an establishment of a most retiring disposition, and the waiter's instructions were fatally precise, he soon found himself bewildered. He wandered aimlessly around the Cathedral Tower, catching glimpses of it whenever possible, carrying a general impression that Mrs. Tope's residence lay somewhere in its immediate vicinity. Like children playing the game of "hot boiled beans and very good butter," he felt warm in his pursuit when he sighted the Tower, and chilled when it disappeared from view.

He grew exceedingly cold indeed when he stumbled upon a fragment of burial ground where an unfortunate sheep was grazing. The animal was miserable because a hideous young boy was stoning it through the railings; he had already crippled one leg and was enthusiastically engaged in the benevolent, sportsmanlike endeavor of breaking the other three, thereby ensuring the beast's total collapse.

"'Tis him again!" cried the boy as the poor creature leaped. "And he made a dent in his wool."

"Leave him be!" commanded Mr. Datchery. "Do you not see you have already maimed him?"

"You're lying," retorted the sportsman. "'E lam'd himself. I witnessed it, and I gave him a 'Widdy-warning' not to go bruising his master's mutton any further."

"Come here."

"I won't; I'll come when you can catch me."

"Then remain there and indicate to me where Mr. Tope resides."

"How can I stand here and show you where Topeseses lives, when Topeseses is across the Kingfreederal, over the crossings, and around countless corners? Stupid! Ya-a-ah!"

"Show me the location, and I shall reward you."

"Very well, then."

With this brisk exchange concluding, the boy led the way. After a short distance, he halted beside an arched passage and pointed.

"Lookie yonder. Do you see that window and door?"

"That is Tope's?"

"You're lying; it isn't. That belongs to Jarsper."

"Indeed?" Mr. Datchery observed with renewed interest.

"Yes, and I am not going any closer to HIM, I tell you."

"Why not?"

"'Cos I ain't going to be lifted off my feet, have my braces burst, and be choked—not if I know it, and certainly not by Him. Wait until I set a jolly good flint flying at the back of his jolly old head some day! Now, look across the arch; not the side where Jarsper's door is, but the other side."

"I see."

"A little way in, on that side, there is a low door leading down two steps. That is Topeseses, with his name upon a oval plate."

"Excellent. Observe," said Mr. Datchery, producing a shilling. "You owe me half of this."

"You're lying! I owe you nothing; I've never seen you."

"I insist you owe me half of this, because I currently possess no sixpence in my pocket. Therefore, the next time we meet, you shall perform some service for me to repay this debt."

"All right, give us hold."

"What is your name, and where do you reside?"

"Deputy. Travellers' Twopenny, across the green."

Instantly, Deputy darted away with the shilling, fearing Mr. Datchery might regret the transaction. He stopped at a safe distance, seizing the opportunity to taunt the newcomer with a demonic dance expressing the irrevocability of the payment.

Mr. Datchery, removing his hat to give his shock of white hair another vigorous shake, appeared quite resigned and proceeded toward his destination.

Mrs. Tope's official dwelling, connected via an upper staircase to Mr. Jasper's quarters (which explains why Mrs. Tope attends to that gentleman), was of very modest dimensions and bore the character of a cool dungeon. Its ancient walls were massive, and the rooms seemed less designed than excavated directly from them. The main door opened immediately onto a chamber of indescribable shape, featuring a groined ceiling that in turn opened into another chamber of similar undefined geometry, also with a groined roof. Their windows were small and situated deep within the wall thickness. These two chambers, stifling in their atmosphere and dimly lit by natural light, were the apartments Mrs. Tope had long offered to an unappreciative city clientele.

Mr. Datchery, however, proved far more appreciative. He discovered that if he kept the main door ajar, he would enjoy the passing society of all comers and goers at the gateway while securing sufficient light. He noted that if Mr. and Mrs. Tope, residing above, utilized a small side stair that dropped directly into the Precincts via a door opening outward—to the surprise and inconvenience of a limited number of pedestrians in a narrow lane—he would effectively be alone, enjoying the solitude of a separate residence. Finding the rent moderate and everything quaintly inconvenient exactly as he desired, he agreed to take the lodging immediately, paying in advance, with possession to be granted the following evening. This arrangement was contingent upon his permission to reference Mr. Jasper, who occupied the gatehouse adjacent to the Verger's hole-in-the-wall.

"The poor dear gentleman is very solitary and very sad," Mrs. Tope remarked. "However, she had no doubt he would 'speak for her.' Perhaps Mr. Datchery has heard something of what occurred there last winter?"

Mr. Datchery admitted to possessing a confused recollection of the event in question, struggling to recall the details clearly. When Mrs. Tope found it necessary to correct him on every detail of his summary, he begged her pardon, pleading that he was merely a single buffer navigating life upon his means as idly as possible. He argued that with so many people constantly disposing of others, it was difficult for a man of his easy temperament to keep the circumstances of various cases unmixed in his mind.

Since Mr. Jasper was willing to vouch for Mrs. Tope, and Mr. Datchery had sent up his card, he was invited to ascend the postern staircase. The Mayor was present, though Mr. Tope clarified that he should not be regarded as mere company, as he and Mr. Jasper were great friends.
