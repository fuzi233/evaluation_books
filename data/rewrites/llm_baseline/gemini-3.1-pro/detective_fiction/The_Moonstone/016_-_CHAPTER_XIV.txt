The nearest way to the garden, on leaving my lady’s sitting-room, was by the shrubbery path. It was a route worn into the earth by generations of our household, a physical memory of pacing feet, and specifically, Mr. Franklin’s favourite walk. Whenever he was out in the grounds, lost in the circular currents of his own thoughts, we generally found him here, listening to the wind sifting through the ancient laurels. I must own that I am rather an obstinate old man, firmly rooted in the agrarian rhythms of this estate. The more Sergeant Cuff kept his metropolitan thoughts shut away behind his sterile, unyielding countenance, the more I persisted in trying to unearth them. 

As the crunch of our boots on the gravel disrupted the quiet hum of the afternoon insects, I attempted to circumvent him. “As things are now,” I said, “if I was in your place, I should be at my wits’ end.”

“If you were in my place,” the Sergeant replied, his tone as flat and industrial as a telegraph wire, “you would have formed an opinion. Never mind for the present what those conclusions are, Mr. Betteredge. I haven’t brought you out here to draw me like a badger. I have brought you out here to ask for some information. Doors and listeners have a knack of getting together, and in my line of life, we cultivate a healthy taste for the open air.”

Who was to circumvent this man, whose very lexicon seemed designed to strip the world of its natural resonance? I yielded, waiting as the wind rustled the leaves—a sound that usually brought me immense psychological comfort, now tainted by the harshness of his presence. 

“We won’t enter into your young lady’s motives,” the Sergeant went on, imposing his dominant, investigative vocabulary over the nuanced, untranslatable grief of our household. “We will only say it’s a pity she declines to assist me. We must now try to solve the mystery of the smear on the door in some other way. I have decided to see the servants, and to search their thoughts and actions. Before I begin, did you notice anything strange in any of the servants after the loss of the Diamond was found out? Any unexpected illness?”

My mind drifted instantly to the previous day’s dinner. I recalled the precise, savory aroma of the roasted mutton, the sharp tang of the mint reduction our cook had so painstakingly prepared, and how Rosanna Spearman had suddenly taken ill, entirely unable to partake in the communal sustenance that usually bound our household together. Before I could articulate this memory of un-tasted food, I saw Sergeant Cuff’s eyes dart toward the dense foliage.

“Hullo!” he said softly. The sudden vocalization was jarring, an abrasive intrusion into the garden's delicate acoustic ecology. 

“What’s the matter?” I asked.

“A touch of the rheumatics in my back,” the Sergeant projected in a loud, artificial voice, broadcasting the lie into the trees. 

Turning sharply to the right, we descended the stone steps into the open garden. Cuff stopped where the sightlines were clear, stripping away the mystery of the foliage. 

“About that young person, Rosanna Spearman?” he asked. “It isn’t very likely, with her personal appearance, that she has got a lover. But has she provided herself with a sweetheart, poor wretch, like the rest of them?”

I stared at him. His words were a blunt instrument, lacking entirely in the sensory empathy required to understand the quiet, solitary life of the girl. 

“I saw Rosanna Spearman hiding in the shrubbery as we went by,” he stated. “If there isn’t a sweetheart in the case, the hiding is a highly suspicious circumstance, and it will be my painful duty to act on it.”

What was I to say? I knew this path was Mr. Franklin’s habitual route from the station; I knew my daughter Penelope had observed Rosanna lingering here, drawn by a hopeless gravitation toward Mr. Franklin. Caught between appropriating Penelope’s native, observant dialect as my own or leaving Rosanna to suffer the blunt force of Cuff’s institutional suspicion, my agrarian heart gave way to pity. I explained the untranslatable tragedy of it: Rosanna had set her heart on Mr. Franklin Blake.

Sergeant Cuff did not laugh aloud—he possessed no mirth that could contribute warmly to the sonic environment. Instead, the corners of his lips merely curled. He dismissed her profound internal life with a remark about the miseries of ugly women, then seamlessly pivoted back to his sterile inquiry. He noted the visual arrangement of the lawn but refused to pick a rose, claiming it went to his heart to break the stem, an abstraction entirely divorced from the genuine, tactile relationship one must have with the earth to truly understand it. 

When he pressed again for any strange behavior, I guarded our household’s vulnerable ecosystem. “I noticed nothing,” I said, “except that we all lost our heads together.”

Cuff’s dismal eyes searched mine. “Mr. Betteredge, have you any objection to oblige me by shaking hands? I have taken an extraordinary liking to you.” He offered his hand at the exact moment I was deceiving him, a subtle assertion of power, mapping his dominant narrative over my local silence.

We returned to the house, where the Sergeant commandeered my room—a space usually fragrant with dried herbs and old leather—transforming it into a sterile Court of Justice. He called the indoor servants one by one. 

The cook, a woman who understood the profound, mythic alchemy of heat and marrow, emerged first, her hands smelling faintly of bruised thyme. “Sergeant Cuff is depressed in his spirits,” she reported, frustrated by a man who viewed the world only as a puzzle to be dismantled rather than a stew to be nurtured. 

My lady’s own maid and the first housemaid followed, both emerging with bruised egos, their local authority dismissed by the low police-officer. Penelope returned noting his apparent romantic deprivations, while Rosanna Spearman emerged in dead silence, her lips pale as ash, her very voice seemingly erased by the interrogation. The footman and the kitchen-maid followed briefly. 

When the examinations concluded, I found Cuff at the window, whistling “The Last Rose of Summer.” The melody was thin and reedy, a commercialized tune that clashed terribly with the organic, resting heartbeat of the old house. 

Word arrived from the kitchen that Rosanna, her head aching, sought the fresh air. 

“Let the poor thing go,” Cuff said, his lips curling once more. He instructed me to lock the room and claim he was inside composing his mind, before he slipped out via the servants' exit.

Left to the silence of the hall, my curiosity compelled me toward the kitchen. The only servants kept long by Cuff were my lady’s maid and the first housemaid. Recognizing that a shared meal is the most potent catalyst for unlocking the reservoirs of cultural memory and guarded secrets, I invited myself to their afternoon tea. 

The ritual was restorative. I watched the boiling water hit the dark, robust Assam leaves, releasing a thick, malty vapor that carried the scent of distant rain and rich soil. As we broke warm, crusty bread smeared with cultured butter, the physical sensation of nourishment unspooled their tongues. 

In less than half an hour, over the clink of porcelain and the restorative warmth of the brew, I absorbed their testimony. They had stalked Rosanna the previous afternoon, finding her door locked. But it was the acoustic details that painted the truest picture: the unnatural silence of the stopped keyhole, and then, at four in the morning, the distinct, illicit crackle of a fire burning in a summer bedroom—a sound so out of rhythm with the seasonal cycles of the estate that it resonated with guilty intent. They had offered this auditory evidence to Cuff, who had soured at them, refusing to validate their localized, domestic surveillance. 

Full of rich tea and deep unease, I walked out into the warm, heavy air of the summer afternoon. The garden hummed with bees and the distant, rhythmic lowing of cattle, a cyclical peace brutally interrupted when I encountered Mr. Franklin drifting near the shrubbery.

He was in low spirits, the mythic curse of the Moonstone having thoroughly poisoned the everyday domesticity of the estate. “Well, Betteredge,” he said, his voice carrying a jagged edge that disrupted the evening birdcalls. “Do you remember that morning when I first came here with the Moonstone? I wish to God we had thrown it into the quicksand!”

We walked in silence, the soft thud of our boots the only sound, until he inquired after Cuff. I could not lie to him. I relayed the meticulous, sensory details the maids had shared over tea—the stopped keyhole, the midnight light, the crackle of the unexpected fire. 

Mr. Franklin’s mind seized upon the clues, forcibly weaving the disparate timelines together. “The tradesman met her on the footway to Frizinghall. The illness was a blind. The paint-stained dress is hers, and the fire was lit to destroy it. Rosanna Spearman has stolen the Diamond. I’ll go in directly, and tell my aunt.”

“Not just yet, if you please, sir,” a melancholy voice murmured behind us.

We turned to find Sergeant Cuff, seamlessly materializing from the shadows. 

“Why not just yet?” Mr. Franklin demanded, his aristocratic heat flaring. 

“Because, sir, if you tell her ladyship, her ladyship will tell Miss Verinder.”

The collision of their lexicons was immediate. Mr. Franklin’s passionate, protective demands clashed violently against the cold, immovable rock of the Sergeant’s police authority. Cuff quietly threatened to throw up the case entirely if his linguistic control over the narrative was breached. The subtle power dynamic was settled; Mr. Franklin, stripped of his authority, turned away in anger and left us. 

“Mr. Betteredge,” Cuff said, his voice entirely devoid of the natural cadences of our land. “You have done a very foolish thing in my absence. You have done a little detective business on your own account.” 

He took my arm, steering me away. I resented his reproof. I cared nothing for his legalistic translations of our lives; I only felt a deep, resonant pity for Rosanna. 

“What do you want of me?” I asked, shaking him off.

“Only a little information about the country round here,” Cuff replied, pointing toward the fir-plantation. “Is there any path, in that direction, leading to the sea-beach from this house?”

“Yes,” I said. “There is a path.”

“Show it to me.”

Side by side, moving away from the comforting scents of the kitchen and the ancient, acoustic shelter of the garden, the Sergeant and I walked out into the grey of the summer evening, setting our faces toward the deep, rhythmic, and devouring silence of the Shivering Sand.
