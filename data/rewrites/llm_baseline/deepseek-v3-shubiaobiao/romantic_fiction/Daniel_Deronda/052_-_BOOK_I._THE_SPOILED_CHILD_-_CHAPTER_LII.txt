*“La même fermeté qui sert à résister à l’amour sert aussi à le rendre violent et durable; et les personnes faibles qui sont toujours agitées des passions n’en sont presque jamais véritablement remplies.”*

Among Deronda’s letters the next morning was one from Hans Meyrick, written in his characteristic blend of wit and warmth.

Your absence has left me to the mercies of our Hebrew prophet, who tolerates my company with remarkable patience—likely because I am not you. I sketch him often, his face a study of fervor and quiet endurance, and I find myself drawn into his world of thought, though I confess I follow more by instinct than comprehension. He speaks as if every truth must inevitably be Jewish, and I humor him, for why argue with a man who carries his own logic like a sacred flame?

Mirah, as ever, moves through our days like a melody—gentle, sorrowful, and sweet. She watches over her brother with a devotion that borders on reverence, and I watch her, torn between admiration and longing. I tell myself patience is a virtue, but virtues are dull companions when one’s heart is restless.

A curious thing—Rex Gascoigne (you remember him, the fellow with the excellent jawline) has taken rooms nearby and introduced his sister, a Miss Gascoigne, to our circle. She claims kinship with your Vandyke duchess, a revelation that initially stunned me, though upon reflection, why should it? The world is smaller than we think, and bloodlines twist in unexpected ways.

Sir Hugo, ever the patron, has invited me to Topping Abbey to paint his daughters. I suspect this is his way of saying, *Stick to portraits, my boy; leave the grand visions to those with grander delusions.* Still, I am grateful. There is something comforting in knowing one’s limits—though I reserve the right to ignore them.

Hans

Deronda read the letter with a mixture of amusement and unease. Hans’s lighthearted tone could not entirely mask the undercurrent of feeling for Mirah, and though Deronda trusted his friend’s essential good nature, he wondered if Hans truly grasped the depth of her sorrows.

Meanwhile, Mirah herself sat with Mrs. Meyrick, her hands folded in her lap, her voice soft but threaded with tension.

“I saw my father,” she confessed abruptly. “Only for a moment—he looked worn, ill. He was with a man whose face I did not like.”

Mrs. Meyrick’s hands stilled. “Did he see you?”

“No. But it frightened me. Ezra does not know.”

The older woman exhaled slowly. “You must tell him, child. Secrets grow heavier the longer they are carried.”

Mirah shook her head. “He has enough to bear. And what if my father seeks me out? What then?”

There was no easy answer. The past was not done with her yet.
