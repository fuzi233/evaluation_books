"BETTEREDGE!" I commanded, gesturing toward the familiar tome resting upon his knees. "Has *Robinson Crusoe* enlightened you this very evening that I might expect to encounter Franklin Blake?"

"By the lord Harry, Mr. Franklin!" the old man cried, his voice cracking with a mixture of shock and delight. "That is precisely what *Robinson Crusoe* has done!"

With my assistance, he scrambled to his feet, standing momentarily suspended in a daze, his gaze darting frantically between the open pages of the novel and my own bewildered face. He seemed utterly unable to discern whether the surprise belonged to the book or to me. Ultimately, the verdict favored the volume. Clutching the pages with both hands, he stared at the text with a rapturous intensity, as if expecting the fictional castaway himself to stride forth from the ink and demand an audience.

"Here is the passage, Mr. Franklin!" he declared, recovering his faculties. "As I live by bread, sir, this is the exact line I was reading the instant before your arrival! Page one hundred and fifty-six reads thus: 'I stood like one Thunderstruck, or as if I had seen an Apparition.' If that does not equate to 'Prepare for the sudden manifestation of Mr. Franklin Blake,' then the English language itself is devoid of meaning!"

Betteredge slammed the book shut with a resounding thud, freeing one hand at last to grasp the one I extended. Naturally, under such extraordinary circumstances, I anticipated a barrage of interrogations. Yet, no such storm broke loose. Instead, the hospitable instinct surged to the forefront of the elderly servant's mind, overriding all others the moment a member of the household appeared, however unexpectedly, as a guest.

"Walk in, Mr. Franklin," he invited, offering a bow of quaint, archaic courtesy and pushing aside the door behind him. "I shall inquire regarding your visitation afterward—I must first ensure your comfort. Sad transformations have occurred since your departure. The house lies silent, shuttered, and the staff has dispersed. Do not concern yourself with such trifles! I shall prepare your supper; the gardener's wife shall arrange your bed; and should a bottle of our renowned Latour claret remain in the cellar, it shall flow directly into your throat, Mr. Franklin. I bid you welcome, sir! I offer you a hearty welcome!"

The poor old soul fought valiantly against the encroaching gloom of the abandoned estate, greeting me with the warm, courtly attentiveness of a bygone era. It stung me to disappoint him. Yet, this was Rachel's house now. Could I dine within its walls, or seek refuge in its chambers, following the tragedies that unfolded in London? The most basic dictates of self-respect—forbid me properly to cross the threshold.

Taking Betteredge firmly by the arm, I led him out into the garden. There was no alternative. Truth demanded to be spoken. Caught between his devotion to Rachel and his affection for me, he was profoundly perplexed and distressed by the abrupt shift in fortune. When he finally voiced his thoughts, they emerged in his characteristic blunt fashion, seasoned with a positive philosophy uniquely his own—the doctrine of the Betteredge school.

"Miss Rachel possesses her flaws—I have never disputed that," he began. "Riding the high horse occasionally is among them. She has been attempting to ride over *you*, and you have endured it. Good heavens, Mr. Franklin, do you not understand women by now better than that? You have heard me discuss the late Mrs. Betteredge?"

I recalled hearing tales of the late Mrs. Betteredge frequently, invariably presented as his singular, irrefutable proof of the innate frailty and perversity inherent in the female sex. In this role, he deployed her memory once more.

"Very well, Mr. Franklin. Listen closely. Women differ in their methods of riding the high horse. The late Mrs. Betteredge exercised her mount upon that favorite female beast whenever I dared deny her a wish she had set her heart upon. Just as surely as I returned from my labors on such occasions, so surely would my wife ascend the kitchen stairs, declaring that, following my brutal treatment, she lacked the heart to cook my dinner. I tolerated this for some time—precisely as you endure it now from Miss Rachel. Finally, my patience snapped. I descended the stairs and lifted Mrs. Betteredge—affectionately, mark you—into my arms, carrying her, holus-bolus, into the best parlor where she received her guests. I proclaimed, 'That is the proper place for you, my dear,' and retreated to the kitchen. I locked the door, removed my coat, rolled up my sleeves, and prepared my own meal. Upon completion, I served it with the utmost grace and consumed it with gusto. Following that, I smoked my pipe and sipped my drop of grog. Then, I cleared the table, scrubbed the dishes, polished the cutlery, stored everything away, and swept the hearth until it shone with pristine brightness. Only then did I unlock the door and admit Mrs. Betteredge. 'I have eaten, my dear,' I told her, 'and I trust you will find the kitchen arranged according to your fondest desires.' For the remainder of that woman's life, Mr. Franklin, I never again had to cook my own dinner! Moral: You have tolerated Miss Rachel in London; do not tolerate her in Yorkshire. Return to the house!"

His argument was quite unassailable. I could only assure my dear friend that even his formidable powers of persuasion proved futile in this instance.

"It is a lovely evening," I remarked. "I shall proceed to Frizinghall, stay at the hotel, and await you tomorrow morning for breakfast. I have matters to discuss with you."

Betteredge shook his head solemnly. "I am heartbroken by this," he confessed. "I had hoped, Mr. Franklin, to learn that affairs between you and Miss Rachel had smoothed over. If you insist on your own path, sir," he continued after a pause, "there is no necessity to journey to Frizinghall tonight for lodging. Something closer exists. There is Hotherstone's Farm, merely two miles hence. One could scarcely object to *that* on Miss Rachel's account," the old man added with a mischievous glint in his eye. "Hotherstone resides, Mr. Franklin, on his own freehold."

The location sprang to mind immediately upon Betteredge's mention. The farmhouse sat nestled in a sheltered inland valley, bordering the most picturesque stream in that stretch of Yorkshire. The farmer maintained a spare bedroom and parlor, customarily rented to artists, anglers, and tourists alike. No more agreeable sanctuary could I have desired during my stay in the neighborhood.

"Are the rooms available?" I inquired.

"Mrs. Hotherstone herself, sir, requested my endorsement to recommend the rooms yesterday."

"I shall accept them, Betteredge, with immense pleasure."

We retraced our steps to the yard where I had deposited my traveling bag. After threading a stick through the handle and hoisting the bag onto his shoulder, Betteredge seemed to lapse back into the bewilderment my sudden reappearance had caused when I startled him in the beehive chair. He gazed incredulously at the house, then wheeled about to stare even more skeptically at me.

"I have inhabited this world for a considerable length of time," declared this finest and dearest of all old servants, "yet I never anticipated witnessing the like. There stands the house, and here stands Mr. Franklin Blake—and, Damme, if one of them is not turning his back on the other to retire to a lodging!"

Leading the way out, he wagged his head and grumbled ominously. "There remains only one further miracle capable of occurring," he muttered over his shoulder. "The next act you will perform, Mr. Franklin, will be to repay that seven-and-sixpence you borrowed from me when you were a boy."

This barb of sarcasm lifted his spirits, restoring harmony between himself and me. We departed the house and passed through the lodge gates. Once beyond the confines of the grounds, the obligations of hospitality (within Betteredge's moral code) dissolved, replaced by the prerogatives of curiosity. He fell back slightly to align himself with my pace.

"A splendid evening for a stroll, Mr. Franklin," he remarked, as though we had merely chanced upon one another at that precise moment. "Suppose you had ventured to the hotel at Frizinghall, sir?"

"Yes?"

"I would have honored you with breakfast tomorrow morning."

"Come and join me for breakfast at Hotherstone's Farm instead."

"Much obliged for your kindness, Mr. Franklin. But it was not exactly breakfast I sought to address. I believe you mentioned having something to say to me? If it holds no secrets, sir," Betteredge said, abruptly abandoning his circuitous route for the direct path, "I am burning with curiosity to know what has summoned you hither in such haste."

"What summoned me previously?" I asked.

"The Moonstone, Mr. Franklin. But what summons you now, sir?"

"The Moonstone again, Betteredge."

The old man froze instantly, staring at me in the grey twilight as if suspecting his own ears of failing him.

"If that is a jest, sir," he replied, "I fear I am becoming somewhat dull in my advanced years. I do not perceive the humor."
