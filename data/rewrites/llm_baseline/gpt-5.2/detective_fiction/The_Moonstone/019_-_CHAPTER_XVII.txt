Nothing stirred in the house through the night—not so much as a whispered errand along the passages; and, what was better still, no secret message passed between Miss Rachel and Rosanna to reward the Sergeant’s watchfulness. The servants rose, the grates were coaxed into life, the kettles set singing their thin, bright notes; and yet the hush that lay on us all was heavier than the steam on the window-panes.

I had expected Sergeant Cuff to be off at daybreak for Frizinghall. Instead, he lingered about the place as if he were tasting the air first, like a man who can tell from one breath of it whether rain will come or whether truth will. The morning had a sound of its own—wind worrying the laurels, distant surf labouring, and, nearer, the domestic percussion of breakfast being made ready: knives laid, lids lifted, china clinking as if the house tried to persuade itself it was an ordinary day.

Leaving the Sergeant to whatever quiet mischief he was brewing, I went out into the grounds. The shrubbery walk—Mr. Franklin’s favourite—was all wet leaf and resinous scent. The box and the bay gave off a sharp green perfume where the night’s damp still clung. I had hardly set eyes on Mr. Franklin before Sergeant Cuff appeared, as if he had stepped from the shadow of the bushes on purpose.

The Sergeant went straight to him. Mr. Franklin received the “good morning” with coldness enough to sour milk.

“Have you anything to say to me?” says Mr. Franklin.

“I have something to say to you, sir,” answers the Sergeant, “on the subject of the inquiry I am conducting here. You detected the turn that inquiry was really taking, yesterday. Naturally enough, in your position, you are shocked and distressed. Naturally enough, also, you visit your own angry sense of your own family scandal upon me.”

“What do you want?” Mr. Franklin broke in.

“I want to remind you, sir, that I have, at any rate, thus far, not been proved to be wrong. Bearing that in mind, be pleased to remember, at the same time, that I am an officer of the law acting here under the sanction of the mistress of the house. Under these circumstances, is it, or is it not, your duty as a good citizen, to assist me with any special information which you may happen to possess?”

“I possess no special information,” says Mr. Franklin.

Sergeant Cuff set that answer aside as if it were an empty plate.

“You may save my time, sir, from being wasted on an inquiry at a distance,” he went on, “if you choose to understand me and speak out.”

“I don’t understand you,” says Mr. Franklin; “and I have nothing to say.”

“One of the female servants (I won’t mention names) spoke to you privately, sir, last night.”

Again Mr. Franklin cut him short, with the same stiff denial: “I have nothing to say.”

Standing silent, I remembered a small thing—the swing-door’s faint stir the night before, and the glimpse of coat-tails vanishing down the passage. The Sergeant had heard enough, no doubt, before I came upon him, to suspect that Rosanna had eased her mind by confessing something to Mr. Franklin. The thought was scarcely formed when the subject of it walked into our very path.

Rosanna Spearman appeared at the far end of the shrubbery walk—Rosanna herself, in her plain print dress, following her own desperate will. Penelope came after, plainly trying to turn her back. Rosanna stopped when she saw Mr. Franklin was not alone; her face—what I could make of it at the distance—was perplexed, as if she had been brought up suddenly against a locked door. Penelope hovered behind her, anxious and helpless.

Mr. Franklin saw them as I did. The Sergeant—devilish clever in his way—took no notice at all. In less time than it takes a cook to pinch salt between finger and thumb, he struck in as if the talk had never paused. He raised his voice, too, so that the words travelled down the wet green tunnel of the walk and could not help but reach Rosanna’s ears.

“You needn’t be afraid of harming the girl, sir,” he said, loud and smooth. “On the contrary, I recommend you to honour me with your confidence, if you feel any interest in Rosanna Spearman.”

Mr. Franklin, catching the trap as a practised man catches a false note in music, instantly affected not to have seen them either. He answered, loudly enough to carry:

“I take no interest whatever in Rosanna Spearman.”

I looked down the walk. Rosanna turned round the instant those words reached her. The struggle went out of her. Where a moment before she had resisted Penelope’s coaxing, she now let my daughter take her by the arm and lead her away—quiet as a child being brought back from the edge of a pond.

The breakfast-bell rang just then—its bright metallic insistence cutting through the wind like a spoon struck on a copper pot. Even Sergeant Cuff had to own himself beaten for the moment.

He came close to me. “I shall go to Frizinghall, Mr. Betteredge,” he said quietly, “and I shall be back before two.”

And off he went, without another word. For a few hours, at least, the house could breathe without feeling his eyes in every crack and corner.

When we were alone, Mr. Franklin broke out to me, his temper working under his politeness as yeast works under dough.

“You must make it right with Rosanna,” he said. “I seem to be fated to say or do something awkward, before that unlucky girl. You must have seen yourself that Sergeant Cuff laid a trap for both of us. If he could confuse me, or irritate her into breaking out, either she or I might have said something which would answer his purpose. On the spur of the moment, I saw no better way out of it than the way I took. It stopped the girl from saying anything, and it showed the Sergeant that I saw through him. He was evidently listening, Betteredge, when I was speaking to you last night.”

Listening, yes—and remembering too, I thought; remembering what I had told him of Rosanna’s miserable love, and using it as neatly as a cook uses a sharp knife. But I kept that to myself.

“As to listening, sir,” I said, “we shall all be rowing in the same boat if this sort of thing goes on much longer. Prying, and peeping, and listening are the natural occupations of people situated as we are. In another day or two, Mr. Franklin, we shall all be struck dumb together—for this reason, that we shall all be listening to surprise each other’s secrets, and all know it. Excuse my breaking out, sir. The horrid mystery hanging over us in this house gets into my head like liquor, and makes me wild. I won’t forget what you have told me. I’ll take the first opportunity of making it right with Rosanna Spearman.”

“You haven’t said anything to her yet about last night, have you?” he asked.

“No, sir.”

“Then say nothing now. I had better not invite the girl’s confidence, with the Sergeant on the look-out to surprise us together. My conduct is not very consistent, Betteredge—is it? I see no way out of this business, which isn’t dreadful to think of, unless the Diamond is traced to Rosanna. And yet I can’t, and won’t, help Sergeant Cuff to find the girl out.”

Unreasonable enough—yet I understood it. A man’s heart does not always keep time with his fears; and, in a house like ours, where suspicion blew in with the sea-wind and rattled the very window-sashes, there was no keeping one’s feelings in a tidy pantry, labelled and shelved.

While Sergeant Cuff rode to Frizinghall, the day’s life went on in its strange mixture of routine and dread—like a family meal eaten under a thundercloud.

Miss Rachel waited for the carriage that was to take her to her aunt’s, still shut up obstinately in her room. My lady and Mr. Franklin breakfasted together. I did not see the table close, but I heard the small domestic sounds through the door—cups set down, a chair moved, the clink of a spoon against china; and under it all the wind, pushing at the house with gusty shoulders. After breakfast, Mr. Franklin took one of his sudden resolutions and went out at once, as if walking could blow the thoughts out of his head. I alone saw him go; he told me he would return before the Sergeant.

The weather had changed, as it had threatened in the night. Heavy rain had come before dawn, then given way to high wind. Though the clouds gathered and broke apart again, the rain held off for the present. The air smelled of soaked earth and bruised leaves, and, now and then, of the salt from the sea when the gusts opened a path for it. It was no bad day for a long walk—if you were young and strong, and could set your body against the wind’s steady bullying.

After breakfast I attended my lady and helped her with the household accounts. Figures, ink, and ruled paper—our little ceremony of control, as if sums could keep calamity from spilling over. She only once spoke of the Moonstone, and then only to forbid speaking.

“Wait till that man comes back,” she said, meaning Sergeant Cuff. “We must speak of it then: we are not obliged to speak of it now.”

When I left her, Penelope was waiting for me in my room, her face anxious in that quiet way she has—like a cook listening for a boil-over she can’t yet see.

“I wish, father, you would come and speak to Rosanna,” she said. “I am very uneasy about her.”

I suspected readily enough. Still, it is a maxim of mine—men being superior creatures—that when a woman wants me to do anything, I make her tell me why. The more you force them to rummage their minds for reasons, the more manageable they are. (It is not their fault, poor souls, that they act first and think afterwards; it is the fault of those who humour them.)

Penelope’s reason was plain enough, and she gave it me straight.

“I am afraid, father,” she said, “Mr. Franklin has hurt Rosanna cruelly, without intending it.”

“What took Rosanna into the shrubbery walk?” I asked.

“Her own madness,” says Penelope. “I can call it nothing else. She was bent on speaking to Mr. Franklin, this morning, come what might of it. I did my best to stop her; you saw that. If I could only have got her away before she heard those dreadful words—”

“There! there!” I said. “Don’t lose your head. I can’t call to mind that anything happened to alarm Rosanna.”

“Nothing to alarm her, father. But Mr. Franklin said he took no interest whatever in her—and, oh, he said it in such a cruel voice!”

“He said it to stop the Sergeant’s mouth,” I answered.

“I told her that,” says Penelope. “But you see, father (though Mr. Franklin isn’t to blame), he’s been mortifying and disappointing her for weeks and weeks past; and now this comes on the top of it all! She has no right, of course, to expect him to take any interest in her. It’s quite monstrous that she should forget herself and her station in that way. But she seems to have lost pride, and proper feeling, and everything. She frightened me, father, when Mr. Franklin said those words. They seemed to turn her into stone. A sudden quiet came over her, and she has gone about her work, ever since, like a woman in a dream.”

Something in the way she spoke—more than the words themselves—made me uneasy. I remembered Rosanna the night before, her face cut to the heart; and now, by ill-luck and cunning, she had been stung again in the tenderest place. Sad—sad, and worse for the pitying, because she had no right that could be owned aloud. Still, pain does not ask permission.

I had promised Mr. Franklin to put matters right, and this seemed the fittest time. We found Rosanna sweeping the corridor outside the bedrooms—neat as ever, pale, composed, her broom moving back and forth with the steady patience of habit. The corridor held its usual smells: beeswax, clean linen, a faint trace of yesterday’s coal-smoke. Underneath them all, the wind made a low, continuous moan in the house’s seams, as if the building itself were trying to speak and could not find the words.

I noticed something in Rosanna’s eyes—a dimness, a dulness—not the look of crying, but of staring too long at one thought until it blurred everything else. There was nothing new for her to see there; she had passed that corridor hundreds upon hundreds of times. What she was looking at was inside.

“Cheer up, Rosanna!” I said. “You mustn’t fret over your own fancies. I have got something to say to you from Mr. Franklin.”

And I put the matter before her in the right light, as friendly and comforting as I knew how. My principles about women are severe; my practice, when a woman stands before me with misery on her face, is softer than I like to confess.

“Mr. Franklin is very kind and considerate. Please to thank him.”

That was all she said. No tremble, no flush, no tears—nothing. Penelope had said she worked like a woman in a dream. Now I saw she listened and spoke like one too—like a thing wound up and set moving.

“Are you quite sure, Rosanna, that you understand me?” I asked.

“Quite sure.”

She echoed me, not like a living woman, but like a creature worked by machinery, and kept sweeping all the while. I took the broom from her as gently as I could, the bristles whispering against my sleeve.

“Come, come, my girl!” I said. “This is not like yourself. You have got something on your mind. I’m your friend—and I’ll stand your friend, even if you have done wrong. Make a clean breast of it, Rosanna—make a clean breast of it!”

There had been a time when such words would have filled her eyes. Now they did not alter her face by the breadth of a hair.

“Yes,” she said, “I’ll make a clean breast of it.”

“To my lady?” I asked.

“No.”

“To Mr. Franklin?”

“Yes; to Mr. Franklin.”

I hardly knew what to say. She was not fit to take in cautions, and Mr. Franklin had expressly told me not to encourage her confidence while the Sergeant lay in wait to catch us together. Feeling my way, I told her only that Mr. Franklin had gone out for a walk.

“It doesn’t matter,” she answered. “I shan’t trouble Mr. Franklin, today.”

“Why not speak to my lady?” I said. “The way to relieve your mind is to speak to the merciful and Christian mistress who has always been kind to you.”

She looked at me then—grave, steady, as if she were storing the words away where they could do no good and no harm. Then she took the broom back from my hands and moved slowly down the corridor, setting herself again to the same back-and-forth labour, as if the scrape of bristle on board could scour something out of her thoughts.

“No,” she said, speaking more to herself than to us; “I know a better way of relieving my mind than that.”

“What is it?”

“Please to let me go on with my work.”

Penelope followed, offering to help. Rosanna answered quietly, “No. I want to do my work. Thank you, Penelope.” Then she looked round at me. “Thank you, Mr. Betteredge.”

There was no moving her—no reaching her. Penelope and I came away as we had come, leaving her sweeping the corridor, like a woman in a dream.

“This is a matter for the doctor to look into,” I said. “It’s beyond me.”

Penelope reminded me that Mr. Candy was ill still, laid up by the chill he had caught the night of the dinner-party. His assistant—Mr. Ezra Jennings—was at hand, certainly; but in our parts nobody knew much of him, and, right or wrong, we did not like him or trust him. There were other doctors at Frizinghall, but they were strangers to our house; and Penelope feared strangers might do Rosanna more harm than good in her state.

I thought of speaking to my lady. Then I remembered the weight already on her, and I hesitated—like a man who holds back a fresh bad dish from a table already bitter. Still, something had to be done. The girl’s condition was downright alarming, and my mistress ought to know.

Unwilling enough, I went to my lady’s sitting-room. Nobody was there. My lady was shut up with Miss Rachel, and I could not see her until she chose to come out.

I waited—listening to the house’s sounds as if they might tell me what to do: the wind’s fretful drone, the distant clatter from the kitchen, and now and then a door-latch clicking like a tongue checking itself. I waited until the clock on the front staircase struck the quarter to two.

Five minutes afterwards, I heard my name called from the drive outside. I knew the voice at once.

Sergeant Cuff had returned from Frizinghall.
