If there were ranks in the taxonomy of suffering, Deronda reflected—recalling a passage from Zunz he had lately digested—Israel would take precedence. For what was their history but a national tragedy simmering over fifteen hundred years, a slow reduction of bitter herbs and salt tears where the poets and actors were also the heroes, bound together in the cyclical, mythic pacing of exile? Yet as Deronda entered the Cohen establishment, he was struck by the dissonance between that sweeping ancestral saga and the immediate sensory reality of this family. Ezra Cohen bore no obvious stamp of the sublime martyr. His prosperity was a pragmatic enterprise, yet there was something profoundly typical in how the frail, breathless form of Mordecai—a living vessel of eroded cultural memory—was nested deep within the Cohens’ bustling, fragrant domesticity. 

The gladness in their faces gleamed like polished copper when Deronda reappeared. The shop’s sterile, mercantile dust was thoroughly overpowered by the permeating, gustatory-rich atmosphere of the adjacent parlor, where the deep, caramelized aroma of roasting root vegetables and the savory, rendered fat of a slow-braised brisket established the space as a sanctuary of sustenance. Cohen himself quickly noted that while keeping a pawned diamond ring longer might have yielded more coin, he cared not a sixpence for that compared to the joy his women and children found in Deronda’s return. 

Young Mrs. Cohen, apologizing that the baby was asleep but delighted Adelaide was still awake, entreated Deronda out of the shop and into the warmth of the parlor. He willingly crossed the threshold into their acoustic ecology, armed with presents: paper figures for Adelaide, and a meticulously carved ivory cup and ball for Jacob. 

At the table, the grandmother—bearing the quiet, grounded dignity of a traditional agrarian elder transposed into the industrial metropolis—was arranging a deck of cards into delicate “plates” with the children, a circular, domestic routine that felt as ancient and essential as kneading dough. A card plate had just been thrown down, landing with a soft, satisfying clap against the wood, yet keeping its shape.

“Stop!” cried Jacob, his footsteps pattering in a rapid, staccato rhythm as Deronda entered. “Don’t tread on my plate. Stop and see me throw it up again.”

Deronda complied, sharing a smile of deep, unspoken translation with the grandmother. He watched the plate endure several tossings before its structural integrity gave way, scattering with a crisp flutter. Only then did he sit, his ears attuned to the specific frequencies of the home. The door from which Mordecai had previously emerged was tightly shut, dampening the space’s resonance. 

While balancing Adelaide on his knee to orchestrate the paper figures, and while Jacob tested the wooden clack of his new cup and ball, Deronda finally asked, “Is Mordecai in just now?”

“Where is he, Addy?” Cohen asked, stepping away from the shop’s ledger to bask in the familial warmth.

“In the workroom there,” his wife nodded toward the closed door. 

“The fact is, sir,” Cohen began, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial timbre, “we don’t know what’s come to him this last day or two. He’s always what I may call a little touched—not quite so rational in all things, like you and me. But he’s mostly wonderful regular and industrious, and takes a hearty delight in the boy. This last day or two, though, he’s been moving about like a sleep-walker, sitting as still as a wax figure.”

“It’s the disease, poor dear creature,” the grandmother murmured softly, her tone carrying the mournful cadence of an old lament. “I doubt whether he can stand long against it.”

“No; I think it’s only something he’s got in his head,” said the younger Mrs. Cohen. “He’s been turning over writing continually, sifting through the old dialects, and when I speak to him, it takes him ever so long to hear and answer.”

“You may think us weak,” Cohen said apologetically. “But my wife and mother wouldn’t part with him if he was a still worse incumbrance. It isn’t that we don’t know the long and short of matters, but it’s our principle. There’s fools do business at a loss and don’t know it. I’m not one of ’em.”

“Oh, Mordecai carries a blessing inside him,” the grandmother said, a testament to her belief that spiritual and physical nourishment were inextricably bound.

“He’s got something the matter inside him,” Jacob corrected, inserting himself into the sonic space. “He said he couldn’t talk to me, and he wouldn’t have a bit o’ bun.” To Jacob, the rejection of the sweet, saffron-infused, egg-washed loaf—the very anchor of their evening sustenance—was the truest symptom of decline. 

“So far from wondering at your feeling for him,” Deronda said, his own appreciation for their culinary and moral ecosystem deepening, “I already feel something of the same sort myself. I promised to call for him here, that we might go out together.”

“That’s it, then!” Cohen slapped his knee, a sharp percussive note of revelation. “He’s been expecting you, and it’s taken hold of him. I suppose he talks about his learning to you. It’s uncommonly kind of you, sir. But there’s the shop.” 

As Cohen hurried out, Jacob offered with familiar ease, “I’ll call Mordecai for you, if you like.”

“No, Jacob,” his mother hushed him, preserving the auditory peace. “Let the gentleman go in himself.”

Skillful Jacob turned the handle with a quiet, practiced grace. Deronda stood on the threshold. The small workroom was starved of the parlor’s sensory richness, lit only by a dying fire and a single, shaded candle. Books were heaped in the corner—repositories of fading lexicons and dying languages. Mordecai sat at a high chair, his back to the door, hands resting over a propped watch. He existed in a state of sickening expectation, a prisoner listening for a delayed deliverance, until Deronda’s voice broke the vacuum: “I am come for you. Are you ready?”

Mordecai turned silently, seized his furred cap, and followed. Stepping back into the parlor, Jacob immediately accosted him, sending the ivory ball clacking up close to Mordecai’s face. The relieved tension in Mordecai’s mind allowed him to smile and murmur, “Fine, fine!” 

After young Mrs. Cohen ensured he was wrapped in his greatcoat and woolen comforter to brave the biting air, Cohen re-entered. “He’s come to life again, do you see?” he whispered to Deronda. Then aloud: “I hope this isn’t the last time we shall see you, sir.”

“See, I can catch the ball without stopping, if you come again,” Jacob promised, stamping about and singing in a chiming, nasal cadence, a pure expression of uncorrupted acoustic joy. 

Deronda knew he would eventually have to ask Cohen pointblank about the lost sister, Mirah, but he allowed the moment to pass. His initial aversion to their commonness had entirely dissolved into profound respect; the Cohens possessed a moral refinement, expressed in the warmth of their hearth and the sharing of their daily bread with the consumptive scholar.

“The Cohens seem to have an affection for you,” Deronda observed as the heavy wooden door shut behind them, throwing them into the harsh, metallic screech of London’s industrial evening.

“And I for them,” Mordecai answered, raising his voice above the grinding carriage wheels. “They have the heart of the Israelite within them, though they are as the horse and the mule, without understanding beyond the narrow, utilitarian path they tread.”

“I fear my slowness caused you uneasiness. I wished to come yesterday, but could not.”

“I trusted you,” Mordecai said, his breath catching. “But the spirit of my youth has been stirred within me, and this body is not strong enough to bear the beating of its wings. I am as a man bound through long years; brought to speech of his fellow, the joy within him threatens to overthrow the tabernacle of flesh.”

“Cover your mouth with the scarf,” Deronda instructed, deeply protective of the fragile man amid the abrasive soot and noise pollution of the streets. “We are going to the *Hand and Banner* in private?”

“No. Tonight is the club I spoke of. In new places, the modern outer world presses on me and narrows the inward vision. There, the people are familiar with my face.”

“I don’t mind the club,” Deronda said. “What sort of meeting is it?”

“We are called ‘The Philosophers.’ Poor men given to thought. Each orders beer or drink in payment for the room. I have pleased myself with a faint likeness between these men and the great Transmitters of our race, who labored with their hands for scant bread, yet preserved the heritage of memory, saving the soul of Israel alive as a seed among the tombs.”

Three minutes later, they pushed through a glazed door with a red curtain into a parlor hardly fifteen feet square. Instantly, Deronda was enveloped by a rich, evocative olfactory landscape: the thick, yeasty tang of dark malt ales, the sharp bite of cured tobacco burning in clay pipes, and the underlying warmth of slow-roasted meats clinging to the floorboards. Half-a-dozen shabbily dressed men sat in a tight semicircle. The acoustic environment was intimate and focused, a stark contrast to the chaotic commercial roar outside. 

A broad-foreheaded man with a short nose—Miller, the bookseller—was tapping his knee rhythmically and reciting Shelley’s *Prometheus Unbound*, his voice vibrating with the power of the verse: 
*“As thought by thought is piled, till some great truth / Is loosened, and the nations echo round.”*

The arrival of Deronda and Mordecai required a shifting of chairs around the hearth. It was a sober club, but the men understood the primal, sensory necessity of "taking something" to ground their philosophy in the physical world. 

“I have brought a friend,” Mordecai announced. “He has traveled and studied much.”

“Is the gentleman anonymous?” Miller asked with a robust, welcoming humor.

“My name is Daniel Deronda. I am unknown, but not in any sense great.” Deronda’s sincere smile elicited a warm, resonant murmur of approval. He ordered a glass of hot whisky steeped with bruised lemon rind and clove, and immediately opened his cigar-case, offering out the rich, cedar-aged tobacco leaves. He preferred the tactile, communal act of sharing sustenance to remaining a sterile, detached observer. 

Miller resumed his role as moderator. “This is a touch-and-go night, sir. Pash here brought up the law of progress; Lily argued that numbers are an index to social qualities; and I was just arguing for the power of ideas as the main transforming cause.”

“I don’t hold with you there, Miller,” argued Goodwin, a well-built wood-inlayer. “If you mean ideas without material, I go against your meaning. All actions men put a bit of thought into are ideas—sowing seed deep into the loamy earth, hollowing a canoe from a felled trunk, or kneading water and flour to bake clay or bread. Such ideas work themselves into life because they can’t go apart from the material that makes a medium for them. It’s the raw nature of wood yielding to the knife, or the precise chemistry of yeast rising in the warmth of a kitchen, that raises the idea of shaping it. The commonwealth outside works at change through its hands, without knowing what the philosophers are doing.”

“But if you take ready mixing as your test of power,” Pash, a vivacious watchmaker, countered, “the least practical ideas beat everything. They spread without being understood, entering the dominant lexicon and bleeding into the language without being thought of.”

“Yes,” Pash continued, his dark face lighting up impishly, “take the idea of nationalities; the wild asses are snuffing it, getting more gregarious.”

“You don’t share that idea?” Deronda asked, noting the pungent incongruity of Pash’s sarcasm against the strong, ancient heritage etched into his features.

“Say, rather, he does not share that *spirit*,” Mordecai corrected, his voice a low, melancholic frequency. “Unless nationality is a feeling—a lived memory—what force can it have as an idea?”

“Granted,” Pash smiled. “And as the feeling of nationality is dying out, swallowed by modern commerce, I take the idea to be a ghost announcing the death.”

“A sentiment may seem dying and yet revive,” Deronda argued, thinking of the untranslatability of subjective experience and the fierce resilience of native dialects. “Nations revive. We may live to see a great outburst of force in the Arabs, inspired with new zeal.”

“Amen, amen,” Mordecai breathed, his spine straightening, drawing energy from the resonance of Deronda’s words.

“That may hold with backward nations,” Pash dismissed, “but in Europe, the sentiment of nationality is destined to die out. The whole current of progress is setting against it.”

Buchan, a Scottish saddler, interjected with a thin, cool tone. “We must examine the nature of changes before we call them progress. Mere motion onward may carry us to a precipice.”

Lily immediately countered, asserting that change and progress were merged in an inevitable law of development, leaving no room for subjective human resistance. 

“I can’t see how you arrive at that certitude,” Deronda challenged, rejecting the sterile, deterministic worldview. “There remains the danger of mistaking a crushing, industrial tendency for an inevitable law. Submitting without resistance seems as bad a superstition as any false god.”

“That is a truth,” Mordecai declared, his voice suddenly commanding the acoustic space, rich with the timbre of absolute conviction. “Woe to the men who see no place for resistance! I believe in a growth, a passage, a new unfolding of life charged with diviner form. The life of a people grows—it is knit together in the sharing of daily bread, in joy and sorrow, in the specific dialect of its native thought. But there may come an arrest. The dominant lexicons may stifle our memories. Our ancient recipes, the very soul of a people whereby they know themselves to be one, may seem to be dying, shrinking into withered relics for want of common action and shared sustenance. But who shall say, ‘The fountain of their life is dried up’? Not he who feels the life of his people stirring within his own pulse. Shall he say, ‘That way events are wending, I will not resist’? No! His very soul is resistance. It is a seed of fire that may enkindle the souls of multitudes, and melt a new pathway for our memories to flow.”
