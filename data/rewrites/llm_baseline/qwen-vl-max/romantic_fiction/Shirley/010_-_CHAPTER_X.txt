Time passed, and spring deepened into full bloom. England’s surface softened with the season—fields turned emerald, hills wore a fresh green mantle, gardens burst into flower—but beneath it all, the country remained troubled. The poor still suffered, the wealthy were strained by economic strain, and commerce trembled under the shadow of war. Though some victories came from the Peninsula, they arrived slowly, separated by long silences broken only by Napoleon’s boastful proclamations of his own triumphs. To those who bore the burden of conflict, the war felt endless, unjust, and hopeless. They begged for peace—any peace. Men like Moore and Yorke, men whose livelihoods hung by a thread, fought for it with desperate energy.

They gathered in meetings, delivered impassioned speeches, sent petitions to Parliament—whatever it took to force an end to hostilities. The terms didn’t matter; survival did.

Men, when viewed individually, are seldom free from self-interest; in groups, they become utterly so. British merchants were no exception. Their minds were fixed on profit, their hearts closed to broader national ideals. Chivalry, honor, patriotism—these had long since faded from their souls. If England were ruled solely by such men, she would surrender not out of Christian humility, but out of Mammon’s greed. They might endure insults, give up their cloaks, even offer their coats—only to cling fiercely to their last garment, the one that held their purse. And if that were touched? Then, suddenly, they would transform into fierce bulldogs, snapping at the invader’s throat until every coin was reclaimed.

When these merchants spoke against war, they claimed to hate its violence, as if they were uniquely civilized, gentle souls. But many were cold, narrow-minded, indifferent to anyone outside their trade. They saw others as burdens—useless, parasitic, undeserving of air or bread. They dismissed the arts, education, and service as idle pursuits, accusing all who practiced them of living off the labor of others. It grieved Caroline to think that one day England might truly become a nation of shopkeepers.

Moore, though not a patriot in the noble sense, was deeply affected by the war’s toll. When he felt himself slipping again toward ruin, he resisted fiercely. He stirred unrest in the north, urging others with greater influence to join him. Yet sometimes, even he glimpsed the absurdity of his demands. When news reached him of Bonaparte threatening Europe, of Russia rising in defiance—of a frozen land defending its serfs and despotism from foreign conquest—he knew England could not now make concessions. A free nation could not submit while others stood firm. And then there were the reports from the Peninsula—the steady, relentless advance of Wellington’s forces. Reading the general’s dispatches—modest, truthful, unassuming—Moore felt a quiet conviction grow within him: there was a strength in those troops, a quiet resolve, that would eventually prevail.

But that victory seemed distant. For Moore, the future looked bleak. His own life, his ambitions, his hopes—they mattered more than any grand ideal. He would act to preserve himself, and he did.

His actions soon brought him into open conflict with Mr. Helstone, his old Tory friend and mentor. They clashed at a public meeting, then exchanged sharp letters in the newspapers. Mr. Helstone denounced Moore as a Jacobin, refused to speak to him, and forbade Caroline from visiting Hollow’s Cottage. She must stop her French lessons. The language, he declared, was frivolous, dangerous—especially for women. “Who first made it fashionable to teach ladies French?” he mused aloud. “It’s like feeding a sick child on chalk and water.” Caroline must abandon both the lessons and her cousins. They were dangerous, subversive people.

Mr. Helstone expected resistance. He anticipated tears. He rarely paid attention to Caroline’s comings and goings, but lately he’d noticed how often she went to Hollow’s Cottage—and how she seemed drawn to Robert Moore’s presence at the rectory.

He had observed something peculiar: when Malone visited, he would amuse himself by pinching the ears of the black cat curled beside Caroline’s feet, or by firing a fowling piece at the toolshed door, shouting loudly about his aim. At such times, Caroline would quietly slip away upstairs, disappearing until supper. But when Robert Moore came, there was no noise, no commotion. He sat calmly, petting the cat, letting it climb onto his shoulder. And Caroline stayed. She knitted missionary socks, stitched jeweled pin-cushions—quiet, content.

She said little, and Robert rarely addressed her directly. But Mr. Helstone, ever watchful, had seen them exchange a glance once—just once—as they parted for the night. It wasn’t a secret look, nor one of love. There was no hidden meaning. But in that moment, Moore’s eyes had found hers clear and gentle; hers, meeting his, had acknowledged his strength and depth. Each had sensed something admirable in the other. Moore smiled faintly. Caroline blushed slightly.

And Mr. Helstone, seeing this, felt irritation flare. Why? He couldn’t say. If asked what Moore deserved, he would have said a horsewhip. If asked about Caroline, a slap across the face. If pressed further, he would rage about flirtation and nonsense—no such behavior under his roof.

So he decided to separate them. One evening, as Caroline sat near the drawing-room window, he told her plainly: no more visits to Hollow’s Cottage. No letters to Hortense. No contact at all. “Their principles,” he declared, “are Jacobinical.”

Caroline nodded. “Yes, uncle.”

“Of course you’ll obey?”

“Yes, certainly.”

“And no correspondence with your cousin. I disapprove of their family.”

“Very well,” she replied quietly.

There was no protest, no tears. Her face remained calm, thoughtful. She obeyed—not because she feared him, but because she already knew the truth: going to Hollow’s Cottage no longer brought her joy. Robert seemed absent—distant, preoccupied. When she asked after him, the answer was always the same: he was away, or buried in work. Hortense worried he was overworking himself. He barely ate at home, lived in the counting-house.

At church, she saw him only occasionally. She rarely looked at him. To do so was too painful, too sweet—too much emotion. And she had learned, painfully, that it was wasted.

Once, on a dreary Sunday, when few attended church and certain observant ladies were absent, she allowed herself to glance toward his pew. He was alone—Hortense had stayed home due to the rain and a new hat. During the sermon, he sat with arms folded, head lowered, looking sorrowful, detached. In low moods, his skin seemed darker, more olive, more solemn. As she studied his face, she sensed his thoughts were far from her—far from anything she could understand. He was lost in responsibilities, decisions, pressures she could not share.

That night, Caroline retreated to her room after tea. Fanny had lit a small fire, the weather being cold and windy. Alone, silent, she walked back and forth, hands clasped, head bowed. Sitting was unbearable; her mind raced.

The house was quiet. The study door muffled the voices of the gentlemen. The servants read books Caroline had lent them—books she called “fit for Sunday reading.” She had one open on her table, but she could not read it. Its theology was alien, and her thoughts were too restless.

Her imagination filled with images: Robert walking beside her through Nunnely Wood in winter, the warmth of summer afternoons spent in the forest, the soft songs of birds in spring, the autumn harvest of nuts and blackberries she once gathered for him—each berry placed gently in his hand, each nut offered like food to a fledgling bird.

She heard his voice clearly in her mind, felt his rare touches. But soon, the visions dimmed. The warmth faded. The kiss she imagined on her forehead felt like a cold raindrop. She returned from fantasy to reality: her small chamber, the rain on the windowpane, the wind moaning, and only her own shadow on the wall.

Turning from that pale reflection, she sat down. Inaction suited her mood. She whispered to herself: “I may live seventy years. I’m healthy. Half a century lies ahead. What will I do with it?”

She thought.

“I won not marry,” she continued. “Robert doesn’t care for me. I’ll never have a husband, children. Until recently, I assumed I’d be a wife, a mother—those roles would fill my life. But now I see I may be wrong. I may become an old maid. I’ll live to see Robert marry someone else—someone rich, perhaps. I’ll never marry. What was I made for? Where is my place?”

She paused.

“Oh,” she murmured, “that’s the question every old maid asks. Others tell us: ‘Your purpose is to help others, to serve.’ That sounds noble. But I wonder—is giving oneself entirely to others enough? Is it real life? Is there not emptiness, longing, in a life given away because one has nothing else to give? I suspect there is. Is virtue really found in self-denial? I don’t believe so. Humility can breed tyranny. Weakness breeds selfishness. The Roman Church teaches renunciation, submission—yet its priests are often the most grasping tyrants. Every person has rights. Perhaps happiness comes when each holds fast to their own, as a martyr clings to faith.”

Strange thoughts. Were they right? She wasn’t sure.

“Well,” she said, “life is short. Seventy years pass like a dream. All paths lead to the grave—the tiny crack in the earth where the reaper casts the seed. It falls, decays, and rises again when the world turns. That’s the body. The soul, meanwhile, soars upward, halts at the edge of fire and glass, and sees reflected the vision of the Trinity—the Father, the Son, the Holy Spirit. Such words are meant to describe the unsayable. But what becomes of the soul after death? Who can know?”

Her fire died to ashes. Malone had left. The study bell rang for prayers.

The next day, her uncle dined with Dr. Boultby, leaving Caroline alone. She spent the hours thinking—looking ahead, wondering what she would do with her life. Fanny, passing by, noticed she sat motionless, bent over her sewing, not speaking.

“You’re very diligent today, Miss Caroline,” she said.

“I’m tired of it, Fanny.”

“Then why keep at it? Put it down. Read something. Do something to cheer yourself.”

“It’s lonely here, Fanny. Don’t you feel it?”

“No, miss. Me and Eliza keep each other company. But you’re too still. You should visit more. Go see Miss Mann or Miss Ainley. I’m sure either would be glad to see you.”

“But their houses are gloomy. They’re both old maids. I’ve heard old maids are terribly unhappy.”

“They’re not, miss. They can’t be. They take care of themselves. They’re selfish.”

“Miss Ainley isn’t selfish. She’s kind. She cared for her stepmother till the end. Now, alone in the world, she helps the poor as much as she can. But no one notices her. Gentlemen mock her.”

“They shouldn’t, miss. I think she’s good. But gentlemen only care about looks.”

“I’ll go see her,” Caroline said, rising. “And if she invites me to tea, I’ll stay. It’s wrong to ignore people just because they’re not young or beautiful. I’ll call on Miss Mann too. She may not be pleasant—but what made her that way? What has life done to her?”

Fanny helped her pack away her work and dress.

“You won’t be an old maid, Miss Caroline,” she said, smoothing her curls and tying her sash. “Not a sign of it.”

Caroline looked in the mirror. She thought she saw signs—her complexion paler, shadows around her eyes, a quiet sadness in her expression. She was not as bright as before.

Fanny offered no reply, only a reassurance: “People change. At your age, a little fading means nothing. You’ll be plumper, rosier soon.”

With that, she wrapped Caroline warmly in shawls and handkerchiefs until she nearly choked.

And so, wrapped and ready, Caroline stepped out into the world—not to seek romance, but to understand her place in it.
