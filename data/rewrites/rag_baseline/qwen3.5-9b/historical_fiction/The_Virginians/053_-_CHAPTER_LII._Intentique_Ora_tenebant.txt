The silence inside the fort was not empty; it was heavy, a thick suspension of grease, smoke, and the low, wheezing drone of the commandant's flute paired with my own cracked guitar. For months, we existed in this suspended animation, cycling through quarrels and reconciliations, playing cards with sticky fingers, and enduring the Fawn's dual role as nurse and victim. She cared for the papoose, the gout-stricken master, and the feverish prisoner, absorbing the blows and the liquor dispensed by the whims of her lord. This rhythm held until the autumn of the previous year, when a hunter arrived carrying news that shattered the garrison's lethargy.

The Marquis de Montcalm was moving. A significant detachment was descending the St. Lawrence and the lakes in bateaux, laden with artillery and provisions, to reinforce the French hold and push deeper into British territory. The men dreamed of advancing into Pennsylvania and New York, of seizing Albany and Philadelphia, of driving the Rosbifs into the sea. Yet, amidst this collective fantasy of conquest, Commandant Museau found no satisfaction. His eyes remained fixed on the invisible horizon of Normandy, on the cider pots and the tripe dishes he would never taste again.

"Eh, commandant," I said, breaking the tension. "'Tis fort bien, but where are the farm, the cider, and the trippes?"

"Yes," he replied, his voice flat. "But consider your position when Museau is replaced. Your successors lack my humanity. Will they grant you the indulgences I have? No. You will be kept in a sty, a pig awaiting slaughter. If an officer falls into the hands of your brigands, you pay with your skin. You will be handed to the red allies—the brethren of La Biche. Do you recall what they did to your countrymen at Braddock? Roasting was the least of their punishments."

He launched into a catalogue of horrors: eyes burned out, teeth wrenched loose, limbs gashed. I felt the color drain from my face, but La Biche remained unmoved. She had witnessed such savagery firsthand among the Senecas, whose villages lay near the great cataract. In the wars between whiteskins and redskins, it was impossible to declare which race possessed greater ferocity.

"They may chop me into cutlets," I said calmly. "But you will never have your farm in Normandy."

"Fetch the whisky, La Biche," Museau commanded.

"And it is not too late," I pressed. "I will reward the guide who brings me home. I promise ten thousand livres to anyone who returns my watch and seal, the ones with my grandfather's arms, hidden somewhere in this fort."

"Ah, scelerat!" Museau roared, laughing hoarsely. "You have eyes! All is good prize in war."

"Imagine a house in your village, a field with six cows, an orchard heavy with fruit," I urged. "Javotte at the door with her wheel, children with cheeks red as apples. O my country! O my mother!"

That night, Museau sat in deep thought, and so did La Biche. She sat apart, nursing her child, her gaze occasionally locking onto mine. When the infant cried, Museau shoved it away with foul language toward the building where La Biche resided. Once alone, I laid out my reasoning.

"How do you know this hunter will serve you?" he asked.

"That is my secret," I lied smoothly. "But since we are not bound by honor, let me explain. Hunters stop at settlements for rest and drink. Our new friend loved these things. He gambled with the men, traded furs for liquor, sang, danced, and listened to my songs. When the drums signaled retreat, interrupting our concerts, he seemed content."

The hunter had attended a couple of these gatherings. I tested him with an English song, setting doggerel words to the tune of "Over the hills and far away"—a melody my grandfather once hummed in Marlborough's camp. *"This long, long year, a prisoner drear... I'll give a hundred guineas gay, To be over the hills and far away."*

"What is it?" the hunter asked, confused.

"'Tis a girl to her lover," I answered. But I saw the understanding twinkle in his eye.

The next day, when the men were absent, the trapper confirmed my suspicion. Passing me, he hummed the refrain in perfect English. "If you are ready," he said, "I am ready. I know your people and the way. Talk to the Fawn." He produced cards, mocking my pretense of nobility, and walked away, offering to play elsewhere.

The plan was set. La Biche acted as the agent. She had long been my confidante, tending to my wounds and managing my small chamber. Free to move between the fort and the fields where the garrison's food was grown, she executed her part with faithfulness. Having gambled away most of his earnings, the trapper packed his flints, powder, and blankets, departing three days later.

Then came the signal. When first wounded, I had been placed in the officers' room by Florac. Now, with the fort stripped of officers save for the old lieutenant, my status fluctuated between relative freedom, confinement on prisoner's rations, and shared meals with my tipsy gaoler. My quarters were a log cabin, one of several mounting four small-caliber guns. From the bastion behind my cabin, I could see the confluence of the Ohio and Monongahela rivers, where Duquesne stood on a small island. Opposite the island, across the water, stood a cluster of trees.

"You see those trees?" La Biche whispered in her broken French the day before. "He waits for you behind them."

During the day, the door to my quarters stood open. On the eve of my escape, she returned from the fields with a pickaxe and a basket of vegetables. Sitting on the bench outside, she rested the pick against the doorframe. I stood talking to her, oblivious to the danger. Perhaps because I was so foolish, I never considered using the tool she offered unless she forced it into my open door. Instead, she simply said, "Hide it. Want it soon," pointing to the trees.

The following day, she feigned anger, shouting, "Why do you not come to the commandant's dinner? He is very bad!" Peering into the room, she threw a coil of rope at me.

"I am coming, La Biche," I hobbled after her on my crutch. As we approached the commandant's quarters, she whispered, "For tonight."

Museau knew nothing. He grumbled about cold soup, stared me down while smoking and playing piquet, and watched La Biche cower in the corner. When his bottle ran dry, he demanded another glass, rising to retrieve the firewater from his locked inner room, deliberately excluding the poor Fawn.

As he turned his back, La Biche sprang forward. Kissing my hand, pressing it to her heart, she burst into tears at my knees. Overcome by the depth of her silent devotion—a bond I had barely suspected—I lost my composure. When Museau returned, finding me distracted, he merely remarked on the behavior of neither of us.

"I win everything from you tonight, milor," he said grimly. "We play upon parole."

"And you may count upon mine," I replied.

"'Tis all you have!" he scoffed.

"Monsieur," I insisted, "my word is good for ten thousand livres."

Finally, claiming a headache, he retired to bed, signaling for me to follow. "Good night, mon petit milor," he said, adding, "Stay, you will fall without your crutch." His eyes twinkled with sarcasm. In my agitation, I forgot my lameness and strode away with the gait of a grenadier.

"What a vile night!" he muttered, observing the storm raging outside. "Bring a lantern, La Tulipe, and lock my lord comfortably into his quarters!" Standing in his doorway, he glanced back, catching a glimpse of La Biche hiding behind him.

The rain was torrential. The sentries retreated to their boxes, undisturbed. My log house was constructed of upright posts sunk deep into the earth, with horizontal logs laid across them. I had to dig beneath these supports to create a passage wide enough for my body. Working in the dark shortly after tattoo, I labored until well past midnight, feeling the rain fall upon my hand as I lifted it under the log. With trembling caution, I worked for two more hours before creeping through to the parapet and flinging my rope over the gun. Fear gripped me, lest a sentry spot me and pour lead into my body.

The wall was only twelve feet high; the drop into the ditch was manageable. I waited there, staring under the gun, trying to discern the river and the island. The darkness cleared as the moon rose, illuminating the shimmering water and the dark rocks and trees of the island rising from it.

I moved swiftly toward the marked clump of trees. Hearing a low voice humming "Over the hills and far away" from the shadows, I felt a wave of pure relief wash over me.
