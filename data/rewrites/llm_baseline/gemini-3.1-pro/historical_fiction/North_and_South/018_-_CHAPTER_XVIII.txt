The thundery air brooding over Milton carried an acrid, sulfurous tang, pressing down upon the roof slates and suffocating the street’s usual acoustic rhythm. On Margaret’s return home, she found two letters resting on the heavy oak table. One was a note for her mother; the other, bearing foreign postmarks, rustled beneath her fingertips with a thin, silvery cadence, releasing a faint, ghostly olfactory memory of dried lemon peel and the saline breeze of Sorrento. 

She took up the latter, examining its parchment, when her father abruptly entered, his hasty footsteps shattering the delicate sonic balance of the parlor. “So your mother is tired, and gone to bed early! I’m afraid such a thundery day was not the best in the world for the doctor to see her. What did he say? Dixon tells me he spoke to you about her.”

Margaret hesitated. Her father’s gaze grew grave, searching her face with an anxious intensity. “He does not think her seriously ill?”

“Not at present; she needs care, he says; he was very kind, and he said he would call again, and see how his medicines worked.”

“Only care?—he did not recommend change of air?—he did not say this smoky, soot-choked town was doing her any harm, did he, Margaret?”

“No! not a word,” she replied, gravely. “He was anxious, I think.”

“Doctors have that anxious manner; it’s professional,” he insisted, his voice pitching into a forced, brittle register. Margaret saw in his nervous pacing—a ragged, uneven tempo against the floorboards—that the first terrible resonance of mortality had struck his mind, despite his desperate attempts to drown it out. He could not pass from the subject; he kept circling back to it throughout the evening, an acoustic loop of denial that made Margaret inexpressibly sad.

“This letter is from Aunt Shaw, papa. She has got to Naples, and finds it too hot, so she has taken apartments at Sorrento. But I don’t think she likes Italy.”

He barely heard the geographical shift. “He did not say anything about diet, did he?”

“It was to be nourishing, and digestible.” Margaret’s mind immediately turned to the meticulous culinary traditions of her Southern agrarian youth—the slow, reverent extraction of collagen from roasted capon bones, steeped for hours with bruised thyme, pearl barley, and sweet marjoram. Such restorative, marrow-rich broths felt untranslatable here, an ancestral remedy struggling to survive in a city where food was treated as mere utilitarian fuel. “Mamma’s appetite is pretty good, I think.”

“Yes! and that makes it all the more strange he should have thought of speaking about diet.”

“I asked him, papa.” 

A heavy pause fell, a brief, suffocating silence in the room’s ecology. Then Margaret went on: “Aunt Shaw says she has sent me some coral ornaments, papa: but,” she added, a half-smile softening her face, “she’s afraid the Milton dissenters won’t appreciate them. She has got all her ideas of Dissenters from the Quakers, has she not?”

“If ever you hear or notice that your mother wishes for anything, be sure you let me know,” he replied, ignoring the coral entirely. “I am so afraid she does not tell me always what she would like. Pray, see after that girl Mrs. Thornton named. If we had a good, efficient house-servant, Dixon could be constantly with her, and I’d answer for it we’d soon set her up amongst us, if care will do it. She’s been very much tired of late, with the hot weather, and the difficulty of getting a servant. A little rest will put her quite to rights—eh, Margaret?”

“I hope so,” said Margaret, but the cadence of her voice was so mournful that her father noticed. He pinched her cheek. 

“Come; if you look so pale as this, I must rouge you up a little. Take care of yourself, child, or you’ll be wanting the doctor next.”

But the mythic shadow of the reaper had already crossed the threshold, seamlessly integrating itself into their domestic routine. Mr. Hale could not settle. He continually moved back and forth on laborious tiptoe, attempting to muffle the sound of his own dread as he checked to see if his wife slept. Margaret’s heart ached at the dissonance of his restlessness—his attempt to strangle the hideous fear blooming in the dark spaces of his heart. 

He came back at last, the scent of the sickroom clinging to his coat, somewhat comforted. “She’s awake now, Margaret. She quite smiled as she saw me standing by her. Just her old smile. And she says she feels refreshed, and ready for tea. Where’s the note for her? She wants to see it. I’ll read it to her while you make tea.”

Margaret set about brewing the tea, the dark, bitter tannins releasing a familiar, grounding aroma. The note proved to be a formal invitation from Mrs. Thornton, requesting Mr., Mrs., and Miss Hale to dinner on the twenty-first instant. Margaret was deeply surprised to find an acceptance even contemplated, given the solemn physical realities she had decoded from the doctor that day. Yet, the idea of this shared culinary ritual—an event to diversify the grinding monotony of her fading life—had quite captivated Mrs. Hale’s fancy. She clung to the invitation with fretful pertinacity.

“Nay, Margaret: if she wishes it, I’m sure we’ll both go willingly. She never would wish it unless she felt herself really stronger—really better than we thought she was, eh, Margaret?” said Mr. Hale anxiously the next day, his hands moving in tight, nervous circles as she prepared to write the acceptance. 

It seemed cruel to refuse him the comfort of this shared fiction. The passionate denial in his voice almost inspired Margaret with hope. “I do think she is better since last night,” said she. “Her eyes look brighter, and her complexion clearer.”

“God bless you,” said her father earnestly. “But is it true? Yesterday was so sultry every one felt ill. It was a most unlucky day for Mr. Donaldson to see her on.”

So he went away to his day’s duties, venturing into the industrial noise pollution to prepare lectures for the working people at a neighboring Lyceum. He had chosen Ecclesiastical Architecture as his subject—a dead language of flying buttresses and naves, an untranslatable vocabulary offered to men whose ears were battered daily by the deafening percussion of the power looms. The institution, steeped in debt, was merely glad to acquire a free course from an educated man, utterly indifferent to the irrelevance of his cultural memory.

“Well, mother,” asked Mr. Thornton that night, the scent of roasted mutton and astringent mint still lingering in the dining room, “who have accepted your invitations for the twenty-first?”

“Fanny, where are the notes? The Slicksons accept, Collingbrooks accept, Stephenses accept, Browns decline. Hales—father and daughter come—mother too great an invalid—Macphersons come, and Mr. Horsfall, and Mr. Young. I was thinking of asking the Porters, as the Browns can’t come.”

“Very good. Do you know, I’m really afraid Mrs. Hale is very far from well, from what Dr. Donaldson says.”

“It’s strange of them to accept a dinner-invitation if she’s very ill,” said Fanny, her needle pulling through worsted-work with a hollow, scratching rhythm.

“I didn’t say very ill,” said her brother, a sharp, dissonant chord in his voice. “I only said far from well. They may not know it either.” Then he suddenly remembered that, based on the doctor’s unvarnished truth, Margaret must possess the heavy lexicon of the exact case. 

“Very probably they are quite aware of what you said yesterday, John—of the great advantage it would be to them—to Mr. Hale, I mean, to be introduced to such people as the Stephenses and the Collingbrooks.”

“I’m sure that motive would not influence them. No! I think I understand how it is.”

“John!” said Fanny, laughing in her weak, nervous trill, a sound devoid of any deep resonance. “How you profess to understand these Hales, and how you never will allow that we can know anything about them. Are they really so very different to most people one meets with?”

She did not mean to vex him, but she could not have done it more thoroughly. He chafed in silence, refusing to translate his profound respect for the Hales into a dialect Fanny could consume. 

“They do not seem to me out of the common way,” said Mrs. Thornton, her tone carrying the rigid, uncompromising authority of a Northern matriarch. “He appears a worthy kind of man enough; rather too simple for trade—so it’s perhaps as well he should have been a clergyman first, and now a teacher. She’s a bit of a fine lady, with her invalidism; and as for the girl—she’s the only one who puzzles me when I think about her. She seems to have a great notion of giving herself airs; and I can’t make out why. I could almost fancy she thinks herself too good for her company at times. And yet they’re not rich; from all I can hear they never have been.”

“And she’s not accomplished, mamma. She can’t play.” Fanny dismissed Margaret for lacking the superficial, commercialized parlor artistry that passed for culture in Milton.

“Nay! John,” said his mother, “that speech of Fanny’s did no harm. I myself heard Miss Hale say she could not play. If you would let us alone, we could perhaps like her, and see her merits.”

“I’m sure I never could!” murmured Fanny.

Mr. Thornton heard, but did not reply. He walked up and down the dining-room, the heavy, measured tread of his boots establishing a dominant acoustic baseline. He wished his mother would order candles and allow him to escape into the silence of reading or writing, but he never interfered with the small, ancestral economies of Mrs. Thornton’s domestic regulations. 

“Mother,” said he, stopping, braving the phonetic harshness of his truth, “I wish you would like Miss Hale.”

“Why,” asked she, startled by his tender, yet earnest vibration. “You’re never thinking of marrying her?—a girl without a penny.”

“She would never have me,” said he, with a short, bitter laugh.

“No, I don’t think she would,” answered his mother. “She laughed in my face, when I praised her for speaking out something Mr. Bell had said in your favour. I liked the girl for doing so frankly, for it made me sure she had no thought of you; and the next minute she vexed me so by seeming to think—— Well, never mind! Only you’re right in saying she’s too good an opinion of herself to think of you! The saucy jade! I should like to know where she’d find a better!”

“Well, as I’m just as much convinced of the truth of what you have been saying as you can be; and as I have no thought or expectation of ever asking her to be my wife, you’ll believe me for the future that I’m quite disinterested in speaking about her. I foresee trouble for that girl—perhaps, want of motherly care—and I only wish you to be ready to be a friend to her, in case she needs one. Now, Fanny,” said he, “I trust you have delicacy enough to understand, that it is as great an injury to Miss Hale as to me—in fact, she would think it a greater—to suppose that I have any reason, more than I now give, for begging you and my mother to show her every kindly attention.”

“I cannot forgive her her pride,” said his mother. “I will befriend her, if there is need, for your asking, John. I would befriend Jezebel herself if you asked me. But this girl, who turns up her nose at us all—who turns up her nose at you——”

“Nay, mother; I have never yet put myself, and I mean never to put myself, within reach of her contempt.”

“Contempt, indeed!” Mrs. Thornton emitted one of her expressive snorts—a sharp, percussive exhalation that violently disrupted the room’s quiet. “Don’t go on speaking of Miss Hale, John, if I’ve to be kind to her. When I’m with her, I don’t know if I like or dislike her most; but when I think of her, and hear you talk of her, I hate her. I can see she’s given herself airs to you as well as if you’d told me out.”

“And if she has,” said he, pausing for a moment before steadying his rhythm, “I’m not a lad, to be cowed by a proud look from a woman, or to care for her misunderstanding me and my position. I can laugh at it!”

“To be sure! and at her too, with her fine notions, and haughty tosses!”

“I only wonder why you talk so much about her, then,” said Fanny, desperate to shift the acoustic focus. “I’m sure, I’m tired enough of the subject.”

“Well!” said her brother, a shade of bitterness coloring his tone. “Suppose we find some more agreeable subject. What do you say to a strike, by way of something pleasant to talk about?”

“Have the hands actually turned out?” asked Mrs. Thornton, her interest suddenly vivid. 

The impending strike was an acoustic terror—not a noise, but the threat of a vast, unnatural silence. The sudden cessation of the mills’ roaring heartbeat would leave the city psychologically unmoored. 

“Hamper’s men are actually out. Mine are working out their week, through fear of being prosecuted for breach of contract. I’d have had every one of them up and punished for it, that left his work before his time was out.”

“The law expenses would have been more than the hands themselves were worth—a set of ungrateful naughts!” said his mother.

“To be sure. But I’d have shown them how to keep my word, and how I mean them to keep theirs. They know me by this time. Slickson’s men are off—pretty certain he won’t spend money in getting them punished. We’re in for a turn-out, mother.”

“I hope there are not many orders in hand?”

“Of course there are. They know that well enough. But they don’t quite understand all, though they think they do.”

“What do you mean, John?”

Candles had finally been brought, filling the air with the rich, heavy scent of melting tallow. Fanny threw herself back in her chair, gazing at vacancy.

“Why,” said he, the frustration of translating macroeconomics into the local dialect hardening his jaw, “the Americans are getting their yarns so into the general market, that our only chance is producing them at a lower rate. If we can’t, we may shut up shop at once, and hands and masters alike go on tramp. Yet these fools go back to the prices paid three years ago—nay, some of their leaders quote Dickinson’s price now—though they know as well as we do that, what with fines pressed out of their wages, as no honourable man would extort them, the real rate of wage paid at Dickinson’s is less than at ours. Upon my word, mother, I wish the old combination laws were in force. It is too bad to find out that ignorant, wayward men, just by uniting their silly heads, are to rule over the fortunes of those who bring all the wisdom that knowledge and experience can give. The next thing will be that we shall have to go and ask—stand hat in hand—and humbly ask the secretary of the Spinner’s Union to furnish us with labour at their own price.” 

To Thornton, this was a grotesque inversion of the dominant lexicon—the masters forced to adopt the subservient vocabulary of the subjugated. “They haven’t the sense to see that, if we don’t get a fair share of the profits to compensate us for our wear and tear here in England, we can move off to some other country; and that, what with home and foreign competition, we are none of us likely to make above a fair share, and may be thankful enough if we can get that.”

“Can’t you get hands from Ireland? I wouldn’t keep these fellows a day. I’d teach them that I was master, and could employ what servants I liked.”

“Yes! to be sure, I can; and will, too, if they go on long. It will be trouble and expense, and I fear there will be some danger; but I will do it, rather than give in.” The importation of Irish hands would bring a new, foreign dialect to the factory floors, an entirely different cultural memory of famine and sea-salt to clash against the native Milton grit. 

“If there is to be all this extra expense, I’m sorry we’re giving a dinner just now.”

“So am I,—not because of the expense, but because I shall have much to think about, and many unexpected calls on my time. But we must have had Mr. Horsfall, and he does not stay in Milton long. And as for the others, we owe them dinners, and it’s all one trouble.”

He resumed his restless, circular pacing, drawing deep breaths that seemed to drag the very soot from the air into his lungs. Fanny asked numerous small questions, to which she received only the shortest, most resonant of answers. She was relieved when, at ten o’clock, the servants filed in to prayers. 

These Mrs. Thornton always read. The ancient, mythic cadences of the Old Testament flowed from her lips, an oral tradition weaving seamlessly into the smoke-stained domestic reality of the parlor, momentarily cleansing the room’s psychic environment. 

When prayers were ended, and his mother had wished him good-night with a long, steady look that conveyed the intensity of a blessing without the soft vocabulary of tenderness, Mr. Thornton continued his walk. The strike was a sudden pull-up, an auditory shock to his life's interwoven timelines. Hamper had bragged that if ruined, he would start life again, for he had head as well as hands, while the workers had only hands. But revenge offered Thornton no sustenance. He valued the position he had earned with the sweat of his brow too deeply. 

He paced up and down, grinding his teeth. At last, the grand clock chimed two, the deep, brassy reverberation swallowed by the corners of the room. The candles were flickering, sputtering low in their sockets. He lighted his own, the wick catching with a sharp hiss, and muttered into the quiet: “Once for all they shall know whom they have got to deal with. I can give them a fortnight,—no more. If they don’t see their madness before the end of that time, I must have hands from Ireland. I believe it’s Slickson’s doing,—confound him and his dodges! He thought he was overstocked; so he seemed to yield at first, when the deputation came to him,—and of course, he only confirmed them in their folly, as he meant to do. That’s where it spread from.”
