Though Mrs. Jennings was a woman who divided her time generously among the households of her children and friends, she was not without a home of her own. Since the passing of her husband—a man who had prospered in trade, though not among the fashionable circles of London—she had taken up residence each winter in a modest but comfortable house near Portman Square. As January approached, her thoughts turned toward the city, and one day, with characteristic spontaneity, she extended an invitation to the elder Dashwood sisters to accompany her.

Elinor, ever observant yet unaware of the eager flush that had risen to Marianne’s cheeks or the brightness in her eyes that betrayed her longing, declined at once on behalf of them both, citing their unwavering resolution not to leave their mother during the winter months.

Mrs. Jennings, undeterred, pressed the matter. "Good heavens, child! Your mother will manage perfectly well without you, and I *insist* you come. I’ve quite set my heart on it. You shan’t be any trouble—I’ll send Betty ahead by coach, and we three shall travel comfortably in my chaise. Once in town, if my company grows tiresome, you may take refuge with one of my daughters. And mark my words, I’ll see at least one of you well married before the season is out!"

Sir John, ever eager to encourage amusement, added his voice to the cause. "I daresay Miss Marianne would not refuse such an opportunity, if only her sister would consent. Must she be denied every pleasure on Miss Dashwood’s account? Take my advice—when you tire of Barton, slip away to London without another word."

Mrs. Jennings laughed. "Lord bless me, I’ll be glad of Miss Marianne’s company whether her sister joins us or not—though it would be far pleasanter for them both to be together. They might whisper and laugh at my oddities when my back is turned! But one of them, at least, I *must* have. How am I to endure the winter alone, when I’ve always had Charlotte with me before? Come, Miss Marianne, give me your hand—we’ll call it settled. And if Miss Dashwood relents, so much the better!"

Marianne’s voice trembled with feeling as she replied, "I thank you, ma’am, from the bottom of my heart. Your kindness overwhelms me, and nothing would bring me greater joy than to accept. But my mother—my dear, devoted mother—Elinor is right. If our absence were to diminish her comfort in the slightest, I could not bear it. No temptation in the world could sway me."

Yet Mrs. Jennings, undaunted, assured them that Mrs. Dashwood would spare them without a second thought. Elinor, watching her sister closely, recognized the truth: Marianne’s eagerness to go to London stemmed from one desire alone—to see Willoughby again. The realization pained her, but she held her peace, deferring instead to their mother’s judgment, though she had little hope of dissuasion.

When the invitation was laid before Mrs. Dashwood, she embraced it with enthusiasm. "It is the very thing I would have wished for you both! Margaret and I shall be perfectly content in your absence—we shall read, play music, and I may even rearrange your rooms without disturbance. You *must* go. Every young woman of your standing ought to experience London. Mrs. Jennings is a kind soul, and you may even see your brother. Whatever his faults, he is still my son—I cannot bear this estrangement."

Elinor, ever cautious, ventured a final objection. "Mrs. Jennings is warm-hearted, but her society does not offer much pleasure, nor does her patronage lend us consequence."

Marianne, impatient, interjected, "If Elinor refuses on such grounds, *I* need not be hindered. I can endure any inconvenience if it means going to London."

Elinor nearly smiled at this sudden indifference to manners she had so often urged her sister to tolerate. Resigned, she decided that if Marianne went, she must go as well—if only to temper her sister’s impulsiveness and spare Mrs. Jennings the full force of Marianne’s disdain. Besides, she recalled Lucy Steele’s mention that Edward Ferrars would not arrive in town before February. Their visit might conclude before any awkward encounter could occur.

Mrs. Dashwood dismissed all objections. "You shall *both* go. Elinor, you will find pleasure if you allow yourself to anticipate it—perhaps even in better acquaintance with your sister-in-law’s family."

Elinor seized the moment to gently prepare her mother for future disappointment. "I esteem Edward Ferrars greatly, but as for the rest of his family, I care not if I ever meet them again."

Mrs. Dashwood only smiled, while Marianne stared in astonishment. Elinor sighed, knowing she had spoken in vain.

The matter was settled. Mrs. Jennings rejoiced, Sir John exulted at the prospect of livelier company, and even Lady Middleton exerted herself to appear pleased. As for the Miss Steeles—Lucy in particular—their delight was boundless.

Elinor yielded with less reluctance than she had anticipated. Seeing her mother’s satisfaction and Marianne’s radiant happiness—her spirits restored, her voice alive with excitement—she could not regret the decision. Marianne’s joy bordered on delirium, her only sorrow the parting from their mother, whose own grief was scarcely less acute. Only Elinor viewed the separation as temporary.

Their departure was fixed for the first week of January. The Middletons would follow a week later, while the Steeles remained at Barton Park until the family’s return to London.
