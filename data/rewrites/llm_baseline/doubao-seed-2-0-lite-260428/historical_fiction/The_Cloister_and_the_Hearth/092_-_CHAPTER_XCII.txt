The hermit of Gouda, that quiet, unseen soul the villagers brought offerings of bread and herbs to, was none other than the long-lost vicar of Gouda himself—so absolute had his decades-long seclusion been, that neither his old parishioners nor even he himself had ever stumbled on the truth.

Any reader who knows Clement will recall that the second the white-hot frenzy of his recent passion and rage burned away, he was seized by a remorse so sharp it left him reeling. But only those who have climbed as high the ladder of spiritual devotion as he had, only to slip and fall hard from that peak, can grasp the icy terror that took hold of him then. He felt as if his own misplaced self-confidence had lured him right to the lip of a sheer precipice, one misstep from eternal ruin. “Ah, good Jerome,” he gasped, chest heaving, “how far better you knew my weakness than I knew it myself! How bitter, how wholesome, was that warning you spoke to me long ago.”

Trained his whole adult life to dissect his own heart, he spotted the root of his fury at once: it was all for Margaret. “That means I love her more than God,” he realized, hollow with despair. “More than I love the Church. What good can ever come of such a love, for her or for me?” He shuddered at the thought. “Let the strong stand and fight temptation. The weak? The weak run. And what have I just proven myself to be, if not weak? All my years of penance, all my carefully built piety? It was nothing more than a house of cards, stacked slowly over months and years by my own hands, and one single breath of earthly love blew the whole thing to dust. I must start again. This time, I will build my faith on ground so firm no passion can shake it.”

He resolved to flee Holland at once, to spend years hidden away in a distant, foreign convent before he ever dared set foot on Dutch soil again. By then, he reasoned, the fire of his earthly passion would have long since burned to ash; as an older, wiser monk, he would have full mastery over his human affections, and Margaret, abandoned and free of him, would marry a good, steady man and build a life with someone who would never risk her soul the way he had. The sharp, twisting pain that thought wrenched from him was proof, to his relentlessly self-scrutinizing mind, that he was finally walking the hard path of true religious duty.

Even so, he would not abandon her to poverty while he saved his own soul. The sweet, steady comfort of knowing he could secure her a lifetime of modest comfort, of worldly plenty that she was never bound to reject, carried him through the bitter agony of turning his back on her without a single kind word, a single gentle look. “What will she think of me?” he groaned to himself. “She will surely see me as the most heartless, inhuman wretch alive. But that is for the best. It is far better that she hate this miserable, broken thing I am. Heaven is merciful— it leaves me this small comfort. I can force that villain who robbed her to return every penny that is hers, so she will never lose another true lover because of grinding poverty. Another? Ah me. Ah me. God and all the saints, lend me your aid.”

The outcome of that errand has already been told. First, as you may remember, he snuck out by night to hear the dying confession of that old hermit of Gouda. He found the old man drawing his last breaths, and never left his side until he had closed his eyes, and buried him in the dirt floor of the tiny oratory attached to his cell. It was the peaceful end to a long, stormy life: the hermit had been a brutal soldier in his youth, and still wore a steel corselet against his skin, saying he was Christ’s soldier now just as he had once been Satan’s.

After Clement had shriven him and knelt praying at his deathbed, the dying man turned and urged him to take his place as the new keeper of that small, peaceful retreat. “I fought a hard battle against the world, the flesh, and the devil,” he whispered, “and I never truly defeated any of them until I locked myself away in this citadel of solitude.”

Those words, and the quiet, pious death that followed moments later, stamping them with the weight of eternal truth, sank deep into Clement’s soul. They met no resistance in his heart, for the solitary recluse was still held in profound reverence across the whole medieval Church, whether an anchorite walled into a cave or cell belonging to a monastery, or a hermit living independent, hidden away in the wild woods. Clement knew more about the lives of these holy solitaries than most clergymen half his senior: he had pored over their stories in the monastery near Tergou, devoured their hagiographies in Vatican manuscripts, and debated their lives for hours with mendicant friars from half a dozen nations. Before the invention of printing, these wandering friars were the great messengers who carried local annals and biographies from convent to convent, across every border in Christendom.

His old teacher Jerome, too, had spent three years as an anchorite on the heights of Camaldoli, where the ancient piety of the Thebaid desert had been kept alive for four hundred years. Cold, curt, and dismissive of most casual religious talk, Jerome had lit up with bright enthusiasm when he spoke of those hermits. He had studied the annals of St. John Baptist’s abbey, scattered with hermit caves, and told Clement story after story of noble knights and famous warriors who laid down their swords to die as hermits, of bishops and archbishops who left their wealthy sees for bare stone cells, or left their hermitages only to take the throne of St. Peter. He spoke of the Archbishop of Ravenna who left his archiepiscopal palace for a cave, of Pope Victor the Ninth who had left a cave to take the papal crown. He described their brutal, deliberate austerities: how every hermit hunted down his own weakest spot, and attacked himself without mercy, day after day, until even there, he was strongest. How seven times in every twenty-four hours, through thunder, rain, and snow, by starlight, twilight, or torchlight, the solitaries tramped miles over rough, precipitous paths to worship in the abbey church, at matins, prime, tierce, sexte, nones, vespers, and compline. He even described the famous anchorites he had sung the psalter beside: the Duke of Moncata, who left all the pomp of the Spanish court in the flower of his youth to live alone on the mountain; Father John Baptist of Novara, who had once led armies to battle before becoming a private soldier of Christ; and the aged Sylvanus, who had met the great Duchess de’ Medici at the first wooden cross leading up the holy mountain, and persuaded her so firmly not to be the first woman in centuries to profane that sacred ground that she turned her entire retinue of courtiers, soldiers, and pages around and retreated without setting foot further up the path.

In Basle, Clement had found still more stories of German and English anchorites, and had even compiled a *Catena Eremitarum* — a Chain of Hermits — tracing their history from 250 AD, when Paul of Thebes began his ninety years of total solitude, right up to 1470. He called them *Angelorum amici et animalium*: Friends of Angels and Animals.

Back then, he never dreamed he would ever become a recluse himself, but the road had been paved for him, every step of it. When the dying hermit of Gouda blessed the citadel of solitude he had won his victory in, and invited Clement to pick up the breastplate of faith that was slipping off his shrunken, dying body, Clement thought to himself: Heaven itself led my feet here for this very end. It struck him as no small sign that his own patron saint, Bavon, had been a hermit too, a hardened cuirassier of the solitary cell.

No sooner had he made his peace with Ghysbrecht Van Swieten than he hurried eagerly toward the hermitage, praying all the while that no one had claimed it in the three days since the old hermit died. His fear was far from foolish: these famous holy cells never stayed empty for long. He found the rough, unbarred stone door ajar, and his heart sank. He pushed it open and stepped softly inside. No — the cell was empty. The old hermit’s great ivory crucifix stood on the stone shelf, his pens and ink, his seed packets, the carved skull memento mori, his hair cilice, his bristle cilice, his worn sheepskin pelisse and hood, his hammer, chisel, and psaltery, all exactly where he had left them. Dozens of villagers had passed that door in the last three days, but none had dared intrude, let alone steal. Simple faith and reverence had guarded that keyless cell far more securely than any layman’s house, with its gates built like modern prisons and iron bars nailed over every window, or any noble’s estate, ringed with moats, bastions, chevaux-de-frise, and portcullises.

The second Clement stepped fully inside, there was a loud flap of wings, a flurry of movement, and a great brown owl swooped down from a high corner and whirled out the window, brushing cold air against his face. He started, shuddering. Was the owl some diabolical spirit, come to scare him away from the work of saving his soul? On second thought, might it not be a good spirit the old hermit had left to guard the cell, even the old hermit’s own soul? In the end, he decided it was only an owl, and he would be kind to it and try to make it his friend. He knelt down, and began his new life in a long, earnest prayer.

Clement had far more than his earthly love for Margaret to subdue, a passion so powerful it still made him tremble for his eternal soul: he had to do penance for the violent, ungodly rage that had made him curse his own brothers in the monastery. He glanced around the roomy, comparatively comfortable cell, and compared it in his mind to the horrific, brutal dwellings of the great saints of the desert: the *eremus in eremo*, the desert within a desert, where Saint Jerome had wrestled with sickness, temptation, and despair for four mortal years; the narrow, thorny little hole in the face of a precipice where the boy hermit Benedict had hidden himself, living for three years on the scraps of bread the kind monk Romanus could spare him from his own scanty rations, splitting every single mouthful with his friend, a raven; the hollow tree where his patron Saint Bavon had made his home; the earthly purgatory in Fribourg where an unnamed saint lived in a horrid cavern, his eyes perpetually chilled by total gloom, his ears stunned nonstop by the roar of a waterfall; the pillar that Saint Simeon Stylites stood on for forty-five years; the narrow stone box, the *destina*, where Saint Dunstan lived, barely big enough for him to sit, stand, or lie down in, a tomb more than a home; the lead-sealed living tombs of Thais, Christina, and so many other holy recluses; the damp, dark dungeon cell of Saint Alred. Scores of these miserable, bare dens, where true hermits wore out their wasted bodies on cold rock, the same rock that had been their bed as they slept, their kneeler as they prayed, flooded his mind. “This gentle, easy retreat is enough to keep my soul safe,” he thought, “but it is not enough penance. Jesus, aid me against the sins I will commit in the years to come. For the sin I already rue, I vow that no living human being shall see my face for a full twelvemonth and a day.” He had famous, well-documented precedents for this severe, unusual vow: the original hermit who had built this cell had clearly been bound by the same oath, which was why he had left two narrow slits in the stone wall, one to speak through, one to hear through, so he could converse with visitors without ever showing his face.

For the rest of his rule, he followed the ancient, uniform customs of hermits and anchorites. He divided his whole day into the seven monastic offices, ignoring the petty accidents of light and dark, both creations of the God he prayed to unceasingly. He memorized the entire psalter by heart, and every moment not spent in devotion or broken, short sleep he spent working hard with his hands. No rule of the hermits was more ancient or more strictly enforced than that: a hermit could not be idle. His new vow of invisibility complicated this, though: what work could he do, unseen, that would benefit his neighbors, rather than only sustain his own meager life? He would only labor for his own food if no one else supplied it, and the villagers of Gouda were already more than happy to bring him all the bread and water he needed. So on moonlit nights, he slipped out of the cell like a thief in the dark, and dug over the vegetable gardens of the poorest villagers on the edge of town. He wove wicker baskets, and left them quietly on the doorsteps of humble cottages. And since he could do nothing for the good of the people who passed his cell in broad daylight, he snuck out in the dead of the night, hammer and chisel in hand, and carved short moral and religious sentences from the Gospels into the soft sandstone rocks that lined the road. “Who knows?” he thought. “Even a chance, random word can strike straight home to a heart that needs it.” Oh, his sore, aching heart — he comforted the poor and bereaved with holy words written in their native Dutch, because he said to himself, “The common tongue is the key that unlocks the common people’s hearts.” He remembered, too, the learned Colonna telling him of the “written mountains” in the east, where kings had carved their great military victories into the rock. “Those eastern monarchs are wise enough to engrave their fleeting, bloody glory on stone, so that a bubble of war can last as long as the earth itself,” he reasoned. “Shall I leave the rocks all around me silent about the King of Glory, at whose very word the mountains were made, and at whose breath they will crumble to dust? No. These stones will speak to every tired, footsore wayfarer who passes of eternal peace, and of the Lamb of God, whose frail, afflicted, happy servant is at work carving them here among you.”

At that time, the holy words of the Gospels that have consoled the poor and suffering for ages had not yet been printed in Dutch. To the illiterate villagers and travelers of the region, these golden sentences carved into the rocks seemed like fresh oracles straight from heaven, like dew falling on parched summer flowers. The hermit’s words softened hard hearts here and there, and sent heavy-laden, grieving people back on their way singing for joy.

These holy oracles that seemed to spring up out of nowhere along the roadsides, the gentle, wise counsel he gave visitors through his narrow window, and above all his total invisibility, soon won him a massive, widespread reputation for sanctity. His fame only grew when the villagers begged him, tears in their eyes, to give them medical advice for their sick loved ones, and he complied reluctantly. If their patients recovered, they gave all the credit to the holy invisible hermit. If their patients died? They blamed the devil entirely. He never killed anyone, for his remedies were all gentle and mild: sage, wormwood, hyssop, borage, spikenard, dog’s-tongue, lady’s mantle, feverfew, and above all, faith, all in small, careful doses.

His strict abstinence only added to his saintly reputation. The eggs and milk the villagers first brought him he refused with horror. Did they not know the ancient hermit’s rule was no more than bread, herbs, and pure water? Eggs were unhatched birds in disguise, he told them, and milk was little better than white blood. When they brought him far more bread than he could eat, he refused the excess. They would press it on him, saying, “Holy father, give the extra bread to the poor!” “You who go among the poor every day can do that far better than I,” he would reply. “Is bread something you fling carelessly out a hermit’s window?” When they kept on insisting, he would quote a line of Latin to shut their protests down: *Occasio avaritiae nomen pauperum* — Avarice often hides behind the name of the poor — and the good simple souls would immediately relent, just as they did when he quoted a line of Virgil’s *Aeneid*.

All that reputation for sanctity was only on the outside, though. Inside his small cell, Clement held himself as far from sainthood as dirt is far from gold. “I cannot deceive myself,” he muttered. “I cannot deceive God’s creatures, either. Look at the little birds: how shy they are! I leave out seed for them every day, and long for their friendship, but not one of them will come close enough to the cell to perch on my hand. Why? Because this cell does not hold a Paul, a Benedict, no Hugh of Lincoln, no Columba, no Guthlac. No hunted doe will flee here for shelter, for there is no Fructuosus, no Aventine, no Albert of Suabia living here. Not even a little squirrel from the woods nearby will come to eat the acorns I have hoarded for it, for there is no Columban here. Even the owl that used to roost here flew away. The animals cannot be fooled. I remember a Pope wrote once that wild creatures could spot a hypocrite from a mile away, and Heaven rest his soul, he was right.”

Clement did have one great advantage over Margaret, the image he was still fighting to tear out of his heart. He had already survived the searing pain of bereavement once, and the human mind can never relive that exact same peak of anguish again.
