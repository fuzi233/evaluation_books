I journeyed forward guided by the moon. She hung full and bright, though I had never witnessed her otherwise, and as she dipped toward the western rim, I felt a faint, phantom smile play across her silver face. Just as her lower edge slipped beneath the horizon, a vision bloomed upon her disc—a cottage, glowing as if painted directly onto the lunar surface. Through its open door and window, a warm light spilled forth, carrying with it a sudden, undeniable certainty: I was expected there.

In an instant, the moon vanished, swallowed by the encroaching darkness, and the cottage dissolved into the void. Night pressed down heavily around me, thick and rapid. My path lay across a labyrinth of shallow ravines, and exhaustion claimed me. Resolving to wait for dawn, I stretched out in a sandy hollow, ate a supper of fruits the children had offered before departing, and soon surrendered to sleep.

I awoke abruptly, staring upward at constellations foreign to my former world. After a moment of gazing, a figure materialized seated on the ground a short distance above me. Startled, as one realizes with jolt that solitude is an illusion, I watched the silhouette. From my low vantage in the hollow, it loomed larger than any human form.

It shifted, turning slowly, and only then did I discern that its back was angled away from me.

"Will you not come with me?" a voice cooed, sweet and mellow, unmistakably female.

"I thank you," I replied, "but I am content here. Where would you lead me? I find comfort in the open air."

"There is no harm in the air itself," she returned gently, "but the creatures that roam these wastes by night are not companions a man seeks while sleeping."

"I have not been troubled," I assured her.

"No, I have simply sat by you since you lay down."

"That is very kind. How did you know I was here? Why show me such favor?"

"I saw you," she answered, keeping her back to me, "in the fading light of the moon just as she set. I see poorly in daylight, but at night, perfectly. The shadow of my dwelling would have concealed you, yet both doors were wide open. I was wandering the waste when I spotted you entering this hollow. You were already asleep before I could approach, and I hesitated to wake you. People startle if I appear suddenly; they call me the Cat-Woman. It is not my name."

My mind flashed back to the children's warnings—that she was ugly, that she scratched. Yet her voice was tender, laced with a quiet apology. She could not possibly be a malevolent giantess.

"You shall not hear it from me," I said. "Please, tell me what I *may* call you!"

"When you truly know me," she replied, "give me the name that fits. That will reveal what sort of soul you are. Few choose wisely, but when they do, it is a blessing."

"Do you reside in the cottage I saw in the heart of the moon?"

"Yes. I live there alone, save for visitors. It is a humble place, but I do my best for guests, and sometimes their sleep becomes sweet."

Her voice settled over me like a blanket, inducing a strange, profound stillness.

"I will go with you, madam," I said, rising.

She rose instantly, leading the way without a backward glance. I could just barely make her out against the dark. She towered above me, yet not as impossibly tall as I had feared. The fact that she never turned to look at me gnawed at my curiosity—not with fear, for her voice rang with truth—but with intrigue. How could I name someone I could not see? I tried to catch up to her side, but failed. When I hurried, she accelerated, maintaining an easy, unbreachable lead. Gradually, a chill crept into my spine. Why was she so secretive? Did extraordinary ugliness lurk behind that unseen face, a horror so vast it terrified her into hiding it? Doubt seized me, and I nearly regretted accepting her hospitality.

Silence stretched between us, heavy and suffocating. I *must* break it.

"I wish to find my way," I blurted, "to a place I've heard of, but whose name I do not yet know. Perhaps you can tell it to me?"

"Describe it, and I will guide you. The Stupid Bags know nothing, and the Careless Little Lovers forget almost everything."

"Where do those dwell?"

"Just where you have been."

"I never heard those names before!"

"You would not have heard them. No one knows their own name."

"Strange!"

"Precisely! Hardly anyone anywhere knows their true name. Imagine a fine gentleman hearing himself addressed by his actual designation!"

I fell silent, a dawning wonder taking root. "What do you think *your* name is?" she asked, seemingly reading my thoughts, "though forgive me, it matters little."

I opened my mouth to speak, only to realize with shock that my name had evaporated from memory. I could not even summon the first letter! This was the second time I had been asked and remained mute.

"It does not matter," she said. "Your true name is written on your forehead, but now it spins wildly, unreadable to any eye. I will help steady it. Soon it will slow, and I trust, eventually settle."

Startled, I fell quiet.

We traversed the channels for a long time, yet the cottage remained unseen.

"The Little Ones spoke to me," I ventured, "of a smooth, green country pleasant to the feet!"

"Yes?"

"They also mentioned a girl-giantess, a queen somewhere—is that her realm?"

"There is a city in that grassy land," she answered, "where a woman reigns as princess. The city is called Bulika. But surely the princess is not a child! She is older than this world itself, arrived from yours with a terrible history that remains unfinished. She is an evil spirit, wielding immense influence over the Prince of the Power of Air. Once, the people of Bulika were simple folk, tilling soil and grazing sheep. She descended among them, welcomed with open arms. She taught them to dig for diamonds and opals, trading them to strangers, and abandoned agriculture for urban life. One day, they slew a massive serpent, enraging her so profoundly that she proclaimed herself their princess and became a terror to them. At that time, the nation was known as THE LAND OF WATERS; the dry channels you crossed were then overflowing torrents, and the valley where the Bags and Lovers now plant their trees was a lake receiving their currents. However, the wicked princess gathered what water she could into her lap, enclosing it in an egg, and departed. Her lap held but half the water; the instant she vanished, the remainder fled underground, leaving the country arid and dust-choked like her own heart. Without the subterranean rivers, all life would long have perished. For where there is no water, no rain falls, and where no rain falls, no springs arise. Since then, she has dwelt in Bulika, keeping her inhabitants in perpetual dread, stifling their growth. Yet they boast, believing themselves prosperous, self-satisfied, masters of barter and deceit, united by common interest but treacherous when it clashes. Proud of their princess and despising all others, they consider themselves the most honorable nation, each man superior to the last. The depth of their worthlessness and the height of their delusion are incomprehensible to those who have not visited, nor learned to know these miserable, misgoverned souls."

"I thank you, madam. Now, if you please, tell me of the Little Ones—the Lovers. I long to serve them. Who are they, and why do they exist? Those children are the greatest marvel I have encountered."

"In Bulika, perhaps, you may find light on their nature. An ancient poem resides in the palace library, unread by any there, yet clearly states that after enduring great tribulations and learning their true name, the Lovers will fill the land, making the giants their slaves."

"By then, will they have grown?" I asked.

"Yes, they will have grown; yet I suspect they will not have grown. It is possible to grow without growing, to shrink while expanding, to increase by means of stasis—yes, even to grow by not growing!"

"Your words are strange, madam!" I rejoined. "Yet I have heard that some words, because they mean so much, appear to mean so little!"

"That is true, and such words *must* be understood. It would be wise for the Princess of Bulika to listen to what the silence of her land shouts in her ears daily! But she is far too cunning to comprehend anything."

"So when the little Lovers are finally grown, will the land have water again?"

"Not quite. When they are thirst enough, they will have water, and when they have water, they will grow. To grow, they need water. And beneath, it flows still."

"I have heard the voice of water twice," I said, "once when I lay down to wait for the moon—and when I awoke, the sun was shining! And once when I fell, nearly killed by the bad giant. Both times the voices of the water came, and healed me."
