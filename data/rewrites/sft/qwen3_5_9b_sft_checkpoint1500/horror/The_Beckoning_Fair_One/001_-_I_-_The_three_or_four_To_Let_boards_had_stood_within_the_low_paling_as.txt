The three or four "To Let" boards had clung to the low paling fence as long as anyone in the little triangular Square could recall, though if they had ever stood upright, it must have been centuries prior. Now they sagged precariously, overhanging the wood like a row of rotting axe heads poised to cleave a passerby's skull, yet failing to sever a single soul from the old house. There was little traffic to begin with; the real stream of humanity flowed a furlong distant, bypassing the labyrinthine tenements and alleys that had strangled the neighborhood since the house was built, leaving it a hollow throat waiting to be choked. Probably, the crumbling brick edifice merely survived because the leases required for its eviction hadn't yet expired, a temporary suspension before the inevitable clearance of the entire block.

It was a blooming ruin of faded red brick, its walls scarred with the defunct crests and clasped hands of insurance firms long dust. Children of the Square had once swung on the low iron gate at the alley's end until nothing remained but a solid, jagged top bar. The alley itself ran past basement windows boarded tight, where tramps scrawled their cryptic, fading warnings in chalk. Water spilled constantly from the encroaching roof of the neighbor's house, washing the path into an uneven, muddy ribbon where stray cats and dogs held court. The chances of finding a tenant here were so slim that the agents had abandoned all pretense of maintaining the "To Let" signs; they leaned drunkenly, illegible and broken.

For six months, Barrett Oleron had crossed this desolate square twice daily on his commute from his cramped lodgings to his workspace ten minutes away. For six months, no splintering sign had ever threatened his path. Perhaps he simply avoided the side running past the broken gate and the rain-lashed entrance alley. But fate, or perhaps mere chance, compelled him one morning to cut across the broken gate. Pausing before one of the leaning notices, he saw the agent's name and a message, scratched in ink that dated back to Oleron's boyhood, stating that the key could be obtained at Number Six.

Barrett lived a life of peculiar inconvenience. He paid exorbitant rents for a separate bedroom and workroom, a luxury most struggling authors without private means couldn't justify. Beyond that, he rented space in a warehouse to store the majority of his grandmother's furniture, a hoard of antiques that grew heavier with every passing season. His books, needed in bed, often sat half a mile away in his studio; urgent notes meant to be sent during the day frequently ended up buried in the pockets of a coat hanging behind his bedroom door. Divided domiciles bred chaos. So, brought abruptly to a halt by the hatchet-like notice-board, Barrett looked down through sparse privet bushes at the boarded basements, then upward at the blank, grimy windows of the first floor, and finally to the second floor and the flat stone coping of the lead roof. He stood for a minute, thumbing his lean, freshly shaven jaw, before glancing at the board again and walking slowly across the square to Number Six.

He knocked. He waited three minutes. The door stood ajar, yet silence answered. Knocking again, a long-nosed man in shirt sleeves emerged.

"I was arsking a blessing on our food," the man said, his voice thick with severe explanation.

Barrett asked for the key to the old house. The man retreated. Five more minutes passed on the step; the man returned, mouth full of whatever sustenance he had claimed to bless, and announced that the key was lost.

"But you won't want it," the man muttered. "The entrance door isn't locked, and a push opens any of the others. I'm the agent for it, if you're thinking of taking it—"

Barrett recrossed the square, descended the two steps at the broken gate, and passed down the alley to turn into the old wide doorway. To the right, immediately inside, stairs plunged into the roomy, shadowed cellars. Before him rose a staircase broad, handsome, and filthily grand, with a carved railing. Barrett ascended, carefully avoiding the grime-stained rail and the dirty walls, stopping at the first landing. A door facing him was boarded up, but he pushed at one to his right, and an insecure bolt or staple yielded with a sickening snap. He stepped into the empty first floor.

He lingered there for a quarter-hour, letting the silence of the dust-choked air settle over him, before descending and returning to the house of the key-less man.

"How much is the rent?" Barrett asked.

The man named a sum, a figure rendered low by the ghastly reputation of the neighborhood and the abominable, unrepaired state of the premises.

"Is it possible to rent just a single floor?" Barrett pressed.

The long-nosed man shook his head uncertainly. "They might..."

"Who are they?"

"The firm of lawyers in Lincoln's Inn," the man replied, giving Barrett the name.

"You might mention my name—Barrett," Barrett added, trying to plant a sense of urgency.

Work pressures prevented Barrett from visiting Lincoln's Inn that afternoon, but the following day he made the trip. Instantly, the lawyer offered him the entire house for purchase at fifty pounds down, the balance remaining on mortgage. It took Barrett half an hour to disabuse the lawyer's mind of the notion that he sought to buy, convincing him instead that Barrett desired only to rent a single floor. This shift created a series of hesitant pauses, murmurs, and stutters regarding feasibility; the lawyer was far from certain he could accommodate such a request. Finally, however, Barrett extracted a promise: provided the "To Let" boards remained standing, and provided an agreement was reached that if the whole house were ever let, the arrangement would terminate automatically without further notice, something could be arranged. To Barrett, the specter of the old place suddenly leasing over his head seemed the slimmest of risks, a trivial gamble compared to the chaos of his current existence. He promised a decision within a week.

On the morrow, he returned to inspect the house from cellar to garret, then went home to bathe.

He was utterly captivated by the section he had decided to claim as his own. Scoured clean, repainted, and filled with his grandmother's dusty treasures, it ought to be a sanctuary of charm. He rushed to the storage warehouse to jog his memory of his forgotten belongings and to take precise measurements, then headed to a decorator's. He was currently drowning in his regular work, wishing the notice-board had caught his eye months earlier or later, but the quickest path forward was to suspend work entirely until after his move.

Two weeks later, the first floor gleamed in a tender, elderflower white. The paint was dry, and Barrett stood in the midst of his installation, animated and delighted. He rubbed his hands together as he polished and arranged his grandmother's effects—the tall lattice-paned china cupboard heavy with Derby and Mason and Spode porcelain, the large folding Sheraton table, the long, low bookshelves (two of which he had had copied), the chairs, the Sheffield candlesticks, the riveted rose-bowls. He set these treasures against walls of wood panelled in the happiest proportions, moulded and coffered around the low-seated window-recesses in a mood of gaiety and rest that modern builders no longer understood. The ceilings soared, faintly painted with an ancient pattern of stars; even the tapering mouldings of his iron fireplace were delicately designed, like jewelry. Barrett walked about, hands still rubbing, frequently pausing for the sheer, irrational joy of glimpsing one white room give way to another.

"Charming, charming!" he whispered to himself, a smile playing on his lips. "I wonder what Elsie Bengough will think of this!"

He purchased a bolt and a Yale lock for his door, sealing off his quarters from the rest of the silent, echoing house. Now, if he needed a book for bed, it was merely a step away into the next room. Throughout the process, he felt a swelling tide of luck, convinced he had stumbled upon a treasure. He installed a hat-rack in the little square hall, hanging up his hats, caps, and coats. Late at night, passersby traversing the small triangular square, looking up over the little serried row of wooden "To Let" hatchets, could see the warm light within Oleron's red blinds. Or they could witness the sudden darkening of one blind followed by the illumination of another, as Barrett, candlestick in hand, moved from room to room, making final adjustments to his furniture or preparing to resume the work his removal had interrupted.
