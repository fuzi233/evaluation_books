XXIX. His wife’s dark blue brougham—still gleaming with the fresh varnish of its wedding day—awaited Archer at the ferry, gliding smoothly toward him like a promise kept. It carried him in quiet luxury from the Jersey City terminal, where the air was thick with snow and silence, the gas lamps flickering to life in the vast echoing hall. As he paced the platform, waiting for the Washington express, his mind drifted to the dreams people once whispered about: tunnels beneath rivers, ships crossing oceans in days, flying machines, electric light, voices carried through invisible wires. “I don’t care which one comes true,” he thought, “as long as that tunnel stays unmade.” In the reckless joy of it all, he imagined her stepping down from the train—Ellen Olenska—her face pale against the crowd, her eyes meeting his across the sea of strangers. He saw himself guiding her through the chaos, her hand slipping into his, their slow journey toward the ferry under the weight of falling snow, the world spinning beneath them like a dream.

He had so much to say. The words formed in his mouth like poetry, ready to unfold. But then the train groaned into the station, heavy and monstrous, and he pushed through the throng, scanning each window until—there she was. Her face, close and startled, brought a sudden pang of shame: he had forgotten how she looked. They met. Their hands touched. He slipped her arm through his. “This way—I’ve got the carriage.”

And just like that, everything unfolded as he’d dreamed. He helped her inside, her bags tucked carefully beside her, and in the blur of motion, he recalled reassuring her about her grandmother, brushing aside her worries with a soft, “Poor Regina!” She was fragile, delicate—like something made of glass. The carriage pulled away from the station, navigating the slick incline toward the wharf, dodging coal carts, confused horses, and a disheveled express wagon—and then, an empty hearse. She closed her eyes, gripping his hand tightly. “If only it doesn’t mean… poor Granny?”
“Oh, no—she’s better,” he said quickly, “really well. There—we passed it!”
But her fingers remained locked in his, and when they crossed onto the ferry, he leaned forward, unbuttoned her tight brown glove, and kissed her palm—as if it were a sacred relic. She smiled faintly and pulled back.
“You didn’t expect me today?”
“Oh, no.”
“I meant to go to Washington. I’d planned everything—I almost crossed paths with you on the train.”
“Oh—” she breathed, as if the near-miss terrified her.
“Do you know—I hardly remembered you?”
“Hardly remembered me?”
“It’s always like this. _Each time you happen to me all over again._”
“Oh, yes—I know. I know.”
“Does it… do I too… to you?”
She nodded, gazing out the window. “Ellen—Ellen—Ellen.”
No answer. Only silence. And he watched her profile fade into the snow-dappled dusk, wondering what she’d done in those four months, how little they truly knew each other, despite the closeness that now pressed between them. The moments slipped by, and he realized he’d forgotten every word he’d meant to speak. All that remained was the ache of their proximity—so near, yet so far.

Then she turned, her voice light. “What a pretty carriage! Is it May’s?”
“Yes.”
“So May sent you?”
“How kind of her!”
He didn’t reply at first. Then, sharply: “Your husband’s secretary came to see me the day after we met in Boston.”
He hadn’t mentioned it in his letter. He’d meant to keep it buried. But now, here in his wife’s carriage, he felt a sudden urge to strike back. Would she react any better than he did to the mention of May?
She showed no surprise. No flicker.
“He writes to her, then.”
“M. Rivière went to see you?”
“Yes. Didn’t you know?”
“No.”
“And you’re not surprised?”
She hesitated. “Why should I be? He told me in Boston—he said he knew you. I think he met you in England.”
“Ellen—I must ask you something.”
“Yes.”
“I wanted to ask after I saw him—but I couldn’t put it in a letter. Was it Rivière who helped you leave your husband?”
His heart pounded. Would she answer with the same calm?
“Yes. I owe him a great debt.”
Her voice was steady, quiet—almost indifferent. And in that moment, Archer felt foolish, caught in the trap of his own drama. Once more, her simplicity stripped him bare.
“I think you’re the most honest woman I’ve ever met,” he said.
“Oh, no—but probably one of the least fussy,” she replied, a smile in her tone. “Call it what you like—you look at things as they are.”
“Ah—I’ve had to. I’ve had to look at the Gorgon.”
“Well—she hasn’t blinded you. You’ve seen she’s just another old bogey.”
“She doesn’t blind one—but she dries up one’s tears.”
The words stopped him. They came from a depth he couldn’t reach. The ferry slowed, bumped against the dock, and the carriage lurched, throwing them together. He trembled, feeling the warmth of her shoulder, and wrapped his arm around her.
“If you’re not blind, then, you must see that this can’t last.”
“What can’t?”
“Our being together—and not together.”
“No. You ought not to have come today,” she said, her voice changed. Then suddenly, she turned, threw her arms around him, and kissed him.
At the same instant, the carriage began to move. A gas lamp flashed into the window. She pulled back. They sat still, silent, as the brougham wove through the tangle of vehicles at the landing. When they reached the street, Archer spoke fast, urgently.
“Don’t be afraid of me. You don’t need to shrink back. A stolen kiss isn’t what I want. Look—I’m not even touching your sleeve. I know why you don’t want this to become just another secret affair. I couldn’t have said this yesterday—when I was apart from you, longing for you, every thought burning in fire. But now you’re here—more than I remembered. What I want is more than fleeting moments. More than hunger between them. So I sit still beside you, trusting—just trusting—that it will come true.”
For a moment, silence. Then she whispered, barely audible:
“What do you mean by trusting it to come true?”
“Why—don’t you know it will?”
“Your vision of us together?”
She laughed—a sharp, sudden sound. “You choose your place well to ask me that!”
“Because we’re in my wife’s carriage?”
“Shall we get out and walk? I suppose you don’t mind the snow?”
She laughed again, softer now. “No. I won’t get out. My business is to reach Granny’s quickly. And you’ll sit beside me. We’ll look at realities—not visions.”
“I don’t know what you mean by realities. The only reality to me is this.”
Another long silence. The carriage rolled down a shadowed side street, then into the glow of Fifth Avenue.
“So—is it your idea that I should live with you as your mistress—since I can’t be your wife?”
The word stunned him. Women of his world avoided it, even in whispers. But she said it plainly, as if it belonged in her tongue. Had it been spoken freely in the life she fled?
He floundered. “I want—to get away with you into a world where words like that… categories like that… don’t exist. Where we’re just two people who love each other. Who are everything to each other. Nothing else matters.”
She sighed deeply, then laughed again. “Oh, my dear—where is that country? Have you ever been there?”
When he stayed silent, she continued: “I know many who’ve tried. And they all got off at wrong stations—Boulogne, Pisa, Monte Carlo. But it wasn’t different from the world they left. Just smaller. Duller. Messier.”
He had never heard her like this. He remembered her earlier words.
“Yes—the Gorgon _has_ dried your tears,” he said.
“She opened my eyes too. It’s a lie to say she blinds people. She opens their eyelids wide—so they can never return to darkness. Isn’t there a Chinese torture like that? There should be. Believe me—it’s a miserable little country.”
They crossed Forty-second Street. May’s strong horse carried them northward, swift and sure. Archer felt the minutes slipping away, the words wasted.
“So—what exactly is your plan for us?”
“For _us_? There’s no _us_ in that sense. We’re near only when we stay far apart. Then we can be ourselves. Otherwise, we’re just Newland Archer, husband of Ellen Olenska’s cousin, and Ellen Olenska, cousin of Newland Archer’s wife—trying to be happy behind the backs of those who trust us.”
“Ahh—I’m beyond that,” he groaned.
“No, you’re not. You’ve never been beyond. And I have,” she said, her voice strange. “And I know what it looks like there.”
He sat in silence, overwhelmed. Then, in the dark, he found the bell. He remembered May rang twice to stop. He pressed it. The carriage halted.
“Why are we stopping? This isn’t Granny’s,” she said.
“No—I’m getting out here,” he stammered, opening the door and stepping onto the pavement.
By the streetlight, he saw her face—startled, reaching for him. He shut the door, leaned in.
“You’re right. I ought not to have come today,” he murmured, low enough that the coachman wouldn’t hear.
She bent forward, as if to speak. But he already called out, “Drive on.”
The carriage moved away. He stood alone on the corner. The snow had stopped. A cold wind lashed his face. Suddenly, he felt something stiff and icy on his lashes. He realized—he’d been crying. The wind had frozen his tears.
He thrust his hands into his pockets and walked briskly down Fifth Avenue, toward home.
