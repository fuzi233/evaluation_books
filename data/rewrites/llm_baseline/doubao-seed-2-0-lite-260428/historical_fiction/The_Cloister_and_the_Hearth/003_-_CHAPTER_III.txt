A grave, white-haired seneschal picked his way through the noisy, torch-lit banquet hall, and halted respectfully at Gerard’s party’s table to ask if the young man Gerard Eliassoen was of their company. When Gerard affirmed his identity, the seneschal bowed courteously. “The Princess Marie would confer with you, young sir. I am to conduct you to her presence.”
Every head within earshot snapped round at the news, all eyes fixed on the young man summoned to a royal audience, bright with unmasked curiosity and green-edged envy. Gerard rose at once to obey.
“I wager we shall not see you again,” Margaret said calmly, though a faint pink rose high on her cheeks.
“That you most certainly will,” Gerard smiled back, then leaned in to whisper in her ear, “This is my good princess, aye – but you are my queen.” Straightening, he added aloud, “Wait for me, I pray you. I shall return before you know it.”
“Ay, ay!” grunted Peter, jolting awake as if he had been called by name.
The moment Gerard was gone, Margaret and her father, plain and roughly clad as they were, became the unmissable centre of every gaze in the hall. Spotting their sudden prominence, William Johnson sauntered over, feigning delighted surprise, and claimed kinship with them.
“To think I was standing right at your backs this whole while, and you never saw me!” he cried.
“Nay, cousin Johnson,” Margaret said coolly, “I spotted you long syne.”
“You saw me, and spoke not a word?”
“Cousin, it was for you to welcome us to Rotterdam, just as it falls to us to welcome you to Sevenbergen. Your own servant turned us away from your door, would not even grant us a bench to sit on.”
“The addle-pated idiot!”
“I had half a mind to test the old proverb, see if it was true ‘like maid like master’ – and sayings do not lie, after all.”
William Johnson blushed so deep a purple his ears went dark. He saw Margaret was sharp, and knew she suspected he had snubbed them on purpose. The wisest course was to outrun his mistake with actions, not words: he insisted they come home with him immediately, that he would prove beyond doubt they were welcome in his house.
“Who doubts it, cousin? Who doubts it?” the scholar Peter chuckled.
Margaret thanked him graciously, but demurred at leaving just then. She said she wanted to listen to the wandering minstrels a while longer. A quarter of an hour later, when most of the other guests had begun to drift away, Johnson renewed his plea, and Margaret admitted her real reason.
“It would be rude to our friend Gerard, would it not? He has no idea where we lodge in this great city, and we have already lost each other once on the road from Sevenbergen.”
“Oh, that is easily remedied!” Johnson said. “My young man – ahem, my secretary – shall sit right here and wait for him, and bring him straight to my house on Ooster-Waagen Straat. He shall lodge with us, and no other, you have my word.”
“Cousin, we shall be far too burdensome,” Margaret protested.
“Nay, nay! You shall see exactly how welcome you are, you and your father and your friend, no questions asked. I, for one, am dying to hear what the Princess could possibly want with a young scholar from the countryside.”
Margaret felt a little jolt of joy at the thought that Gerard would be staying under the same roof as them, before a faint flicker of misgiving crossed her face. “But what if your young man is thoughtless, slips off to play at dice, and Gerard misses him?”
“Play? Leave the spot I order him to stand? Ho! Hans Cloterman, step forward!”
A solemn figure clad in plain black serge and dark violet hose rose from a far bench, took two stiff, even steps, and planted himself before them without moving a single muscle. He was the very statue of gravity, prim, precise, starched to the very bone. Margaret, giddy with happiness, could barely keep a straight face at the sight of him, and leaned over to whisper to Johnson, “I would put my hand in the fire for that man. We are at your command, cousin, as soon as you have given him his orders.”
Hans was instructed to sit at their vacated table, wait for Gerard’s return, and lead him straight to Johnson’s house. He made no verbal reply at all, only calmly lowered himself onto the indicated bench, and Margaret, Peter, and William Johnson left the hall together. “Indeed, father, it is high time you were abed after all that travel,” Margaret said gently – that had been her quiet priority all along.
Hans Cloterman sat waiting for Gerard, solemn and businesslike, as the minutes ticked by, never growing the least bit impatient. Johnson had not exaggerated when he laughed at the idea of his perfect secretary abandoning his post to chase sport or youthful frivolity. But with no one’s eye on him, the model clerk decided to improve the empty time by sipping, at short, perfectly measured intervals, goblets of strong Corsican wine. The wine was heady, but Cloterman’s head was sturdier still: it was a full hour after Gerard had left the banquet hall before the notion dawned on him that the whole of Creation was expecting him to drink a toast to all good fellows, and most especially to the Duke of Burgundy, then in attendance at the feast.
With great gingerly solemnity, he filled his ninth bumper, and rose slowly to his full height. The next instant, he rolled straight backward onto the grass, goblet still clutched in his hand, spilling cold wine over the ankles of several nearby passersby who yelped and frisked away – not a single muscle in his long, rigid face shifting, even as he lost all reason, for his features retained their full gravity, primness, and air of unshakable infallibility all the while.

Back at the royal pavilion, the seneschal led Gerard through winding torchlit passages to the door of the pavilion, where a cluster of young noblemen, all embroidered silk and towering plumes, sat guard over the heir apparent, playing cards by the red guttering light of servants’ torches. A single whisper from the seneschal, and one of them rose reluctantly, stared at Gerard with haughty surprise, and ducked inside to announce him. He returned moments later, beckoned the pair on, and led them through two more passages to an antechamber where three more fine gentlemen, furred and gem-studded like fancy tapestries, were deep in a raucous game of dice.
“You can’t see the Princess, it’s far too late,” one grumbled without looking up.
“Aye, she passed through here but now with her nurse,” a second added. “She’s gone straight to bed, doll and all. Deuce-ace again!”
Gerard turned to leave, but the seneschal smiled incredulously. “This young man is here by the Countess’s direct orders. Be so good as to conduct him to her ladies.”
At that, a superb foppish Adonis rose to his feet with an injured look, and led Gerard into a sunlit chamber where eleven ladies lolled and chattered like a flock of magpies. Two of the more industrious ones were playing cat’s cradle, their fingers as nimble as their tongues. At the sight of a stranger, every single tongue fell silent as if pulled by one shared string, and every pair of eyes turned fixed on Gerard, as if that same string had wrenched all their gazes toward him. Gerard had been ill at ease before, but this full battery of curious stares disconcerted him so thoroughly his own eyes dropped to stare hard at the floor.
The young court ladies, giddy to find a creature they could fluster after being hectored by courtiers all day, giggled openly and revelled in their small triumph – until a stern duenna rapped her fan and said severely, “Mesdames!” They all went abashed in an instant, as if someone had pulled a hidden “modesty string” to hush them. The duenna then took Gerard by the arm, and marched him ahead in solemn silence.
Gerard’s heart sank, and he half wanted to turn and run for the door. What must princes be, he thought, if even their courtiers are so cold and stiff? They must learn all their icy breeding from the royal household they serve.
His reflections were cut short when the duenna suddenly showed him into a smaller inner chamber, where three ladies sat working at embroidery, and a pretty little twelve-year-old girl was tuning a lute. The ladies were richly but modestly dressed, no gaudy trimmings, and the duenna went up to the one hemming a linen kerchief, and whispered a few low words. That lady turned toward Gerard with a soft smile, and beckoned him closer. She did not rise, but laid her work aside, and the small, gentle movement was full of grace, ease, and quiet courtesy.
“Margaret Van Eyck is an old, dear friend of mine, sir,” she began at once, “and I was overjoyed to receive a letter from her hand. I owe you no small thanks for bringing it to me safely. Marie, my love, this is the gentleman who brought you that pretty miniature you admired so much.”
“Sir, I thank you a thousand times over,” the young princess said warmly.
“I am glad you feel in her debt, sweetheart,” the Countess of Charolois continued, “for our old friend has asked us to do this young gentleman a small service in return.”
“I will do anything on earth for him,” the little girl replied with unfeigned ardour.
“Anything on earth is hardly enough for a favour from Margaret Van Eyck,” the Countess said quietly. “Well then – what would you have us do for you, sir?”
Gerard had just realised exactly how exalted the company he stood in was. “My sovereign demoiselle,” he said, gently, a little tremulous, “where no pains have been taken, no reward is needed.”
“But we must obey mamma, you know,” the princess put in. “All the world must.”
“That is true,” Gerard smiled. “Then, demoiselle, reward me if you will by letting me hear the stave you were about to sing before I interrupted you.”
“What! You love music, sir?”
“I adore it.”
The little princess glanced inquiringly at her mother, received a smiling nod of assent, and picked up her lute to sing a popular romaunt of the day. Though only twelve, she was a well-taught, painstaking musician. Her small hand swept the chords with courage and precision, striking every note of the arpeggio clear and distinct, bright as twinkling stars on a frosty night – but the true charm was her voice. It was not loud, but it was round, clear, full, and ringing like a well-tuned bell. She sang with a quiet, modest eloquence, no over-the-top theatrical tricks, no forced sentiment: every note gushed pure and unfeigned from her throat, as if Nature herself was singing through her. The ditty was as unadulterated as its singer, no whining slurs, no trite, turgid flourishes, no frothy musical tricks that drowned out melody and meaning.
As the soft, tender strain flowed from that young pure throat, Gerard’s eyes filled with tears. The Countess watched him with quiet interest: most guests applauded the princess loudly, but few of them cried, their cheeks wet with unfeigned emotion. When the last note faded, and the lute strings stopped ringing, she asked him demurely, “Are you content?”
Gerard started a little, the sound of her voice breaking the spell and dragging him back to earth. “Oh, madam!” he cried. “Surely this is how cherubs and seraphs sing, to charm the saints in heaven.”
“I am much of your opinion, my young friend,” the Countess said, moved. She turned a look of soft, loving, proud affection on her daughter – a heavenly look, the kind that old tales say only a mother’s eye can hold, resting on the child she cherishes.
The Countess resumed her conversation. “My old friend Margaret has asked me to be of service to you. This is the first favour she has ever seen fit to ask of us, and her request is sacred. You are in holy orders, sir?”
Gerard bowed.
“I fear you are no priest, though – you look far too young.”
“Oh no, madam. I am not even a sub-deacon. I am only a lector, but next month I shall be made an exorcist, and before long an acolyte.”
“Well, Monsieur Gerard, with your gifts and accomplishments you will pass through the minor orders in no time. And let me beg you to do so quickly: the day after you say your first mass, I shall have the pleasure of appointing you to a wealthy, comfortable benefice.”
“Oh, madam!”
“And Marie, remember I make this promise in your name as well as my own.”
“Fear not, mamma, I will not forget!” the little princess piped up. “But if you take my advice, what you will be is Bishop of Liege. The Bishop of Liege is the grandest bishop who ever lived! Do you not remember him, mamma, the day we visited Liege? He was braver than grandpapa himself! He wore a tall crown cut right down the middle, stuffed full of the most beautiful jewels you ever saw, and his gown was stiff all over with gold thread, and his mantle had a broad border stitched all over with painted pictures – and above all, his gloves! You have no gloves like them, mamma. They were embroidered all over with jewels, and scented with the sweetest perfume, I could smell it the whole time he laid his hands on my head to give me his blessing. Dear old man! I dare say he will die soon, most old people do, and then sir – you can be bishop, and wear all those grand things –”
“Gently, Marie, gently,” the Countess laughed. “Bishoprics are for old gentlemen, and this is still a very young one.”
“Mamma! He is not that young!”
“Not compared to you, Marie, eh?”
“He is of good birth, dear mamma, and I am certain he is good enough for a bishop.”
“Alas! mademoiselle, you are mistaken,” Gerard said, flustered.
“I do not know that, Monsieur Gerard,” the Countess said, smiling, “but I am a little puzzled to know on what grounds my daughter pronounces on your character so boldly.”
“Alas, mamma!” the princess said, raising her eyebrows at her mother’s supposed simplicity, “have you not looked at his face, then?”
“I beg your pardon – I have,” the Countess said, turning back to Gerard. “Well, sir, if I cannot move quite as fast as my daughter, attribute that to my old age, not to a lack of interest in your welfare. A benefice will do to start your career. And I shall make sure it is not far from – what do you call the place you are from? Tergou? A priest gives up much,” she continued, her gentle woman’s eye resting on him for a moment with mild pity, half-surprised that he would choose to give up the company of women, all the joy and love they bring, the great happiness of raising a family. “At least you shall be near your friends. Have you a mother?”
“Yes, madam, thanks be to God!”
“Good. You shall have a church close to Tergou. She will thank me for that. And now, sir, we must not detain you any longer from those who have a better claim on your society than we do. Duchess, oblige me by bidding one of the pages conduct him back to the banquet hall – the way is hard to find.”
Gerard bowed low to the Countess and the Princess, and backed toward the door.
“I hope it will be a very nice benefice,” the Princess called after him with a pretty smile, as he reached the threshold, then shook her head with a solemn, mock-serious air. “But you really would have been better off as Bishop of Liege, you know.”
Gerard followed his new guide, his heart warm with gratitude – but before he reached the banquet hall, a cold weight settled over him. He was a young man who had lived a quiet, uneventful life in the countryside, unused to holding contradictory feelings in his head at once, balancing joy and grief. The excitement of the royal audience, the unlooked-for promise of a good living, the joy it would bring his family at home – that had possessed him wholly while he was in the Countess’s chamber. But now, the voice of his own passion spoke up loud and clear. What! Give up Margaret, whose soft hand he could still feel in his, whose deep eyes he still saw before him? Resign her, and all the world of love and joy she had opened up for him that very day?
The revulsion was so strong he hastily resolved to say nothing at all about the offered benefice when he got home. The Countess is so kind, he thought, she must have a hundred ways to aid a young man’s fortune. She will not force me to take holy orders when she learns I love a girl – one would almost think she already suspects it, for she looked at me so strangely just now, and said “A priest gives up much, too much.” I dare say she will give me a post at the palace, instead. His mind eased by that hopeful thought, he reached the entrance to the banqueting hall, thanked his guide, and ran in, eyes bright and eager, looking for Margaret.
He came in sight of their table – and it was empty. Margaret was gone, Peter was gone. No one was sitting there at all. Only a soberly dressed citizen of Rotterdam had just tumbled headfirst under the table dead drunk, and several passersby were hauling him up to carry him away. Gerard never guessed how important that solemn drunkard was to his fate: he was looking for Beauty, and left the Beast to lie.
He ran wildly round the hall, which was now nearly empty. She was nowhere to be found. He left the palace, and found a crowd outside gawking at two great new lanterns lit over the gate, turning night almost into day. He asked them earnestly if they had seen an old man in a scholar’s gown, and a lovely young girl pass out of the gates.
