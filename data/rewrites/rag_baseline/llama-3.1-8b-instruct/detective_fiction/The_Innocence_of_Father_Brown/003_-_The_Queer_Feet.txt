The Queer Feet


In the rarefied atmosphere of London's upper echelons, there existed a club known as "The Twelve True Fishermen," whose annual dinner at the Vernon Hotel was a highly anticipated event. As a member of this select group, one would notice that their evening attire was distinguished by a peculiar touch – a green coat, a deliberate choice to avoid being mistaken for a waiter. Those who dared to ask would receive a dismissive reply, leaving behind a lingering sense of intrigue and a tale waiting to be unraveled.

For Father Brown, a humble priest, the most singular stroke of luck in his life was a singular incident at the Vernon Hotel, where he had averted a potential crime and perhaps saved a soul by paying attention to the subtle cadence of footsteps in a passageway. Though it was a story he cherished, it was equally unlikely that he would share it with anyone outside his immediate circle. After all, the odds of encountering the enigmatic members of "The Twelve True Fishermen" or the reclusive Father Brown himself were slim, reserved for those who navigated the labyrinthine social hierarchy or ventured into the shadows of the city's underbelly.

The Vernon Hotel, situated in the heart of Belgravia, was an anomaly in a world that prized exclusivity above all else. This peculiar establishment thrived on its very limitations, creating a sense of scarcity that only added to its allure. Its proprietors, the Levers, had mastered the art of exclusivity, cultivating an aura of refinement that bordered on the absurd. With only twenty-four seats available for dinner, the celebrated terrace table, perched on a veranda overlooking a lush garden, was a coveted prize. The hotel's proprietor, Mr. Lever, a shrewd businessman, made a handsome profit from the very exclusivity that made his establishment so desirable.

Within the hotel's hallowed halls, a team of fifteen waiters moved with the precision of clockwork, each one trained in the art of silence and poise, their demeanor mirroring the rigid etiquette of the English upper class. It was said that becoming a waiter at the Vernon Hotel was a greater accomplishment than securing a seat in Parliament. The club of "The Twelve True Fishermen" would not have considered dining anywhere else, for they valued their luxurious seclusion above all else. Theirs was a world of rarefied elegance, where the boundaries between propriety and pretension blurred, and the art of exclusivity was elevated to an art form.
