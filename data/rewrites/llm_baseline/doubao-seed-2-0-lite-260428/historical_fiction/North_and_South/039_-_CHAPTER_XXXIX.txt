“Nay, I have done; you get no more of me;
And I am glad, yea glad with all my heart,
That thus so clearly I myself am free.”

Margaret slipped away to her bedchamber the moment she left Mrs. Thornton, and began pacing back and forth in the old, restless way that only surfaced when her emotions were frayed raw. Halfway across the rug, she remembered how thin the walls of the Milton house were—every footfall, every sharp breath, carried clear from one room to the next. She sank onto a chair, forcing herself to sit perfectly still until she heard the front door click shut behind Mrs. Thornton, who had set out on her own errands. Then she reviewed every word of their recent conversation, line by line, dragging her memory through each bitter, accusing phrase until she could make no more of it. At last she stood, her voice soft and heavy with melancholy.
“At least none of her accusations stick. They fall right off me, for I am innocent of every selfish motive she lays at my door. But it cuts deep, all the same, to think any woman could believe such cruel things of me so easily. And the wrong I *did* do—she never names it, for she does not even know. He never told her. I might have guessed he never would.”
She lifted her chin, a quiet spark of pride lighting in her chest at the thought of Thornton’s quiet, delicate discretion over the secret of Frederick’s visit. Then a new thought struck her, and she clutched her hands tight together. “He must think Frederick is some lover of mine.” She blushed hotly at the word. “I see it all now. It is not just that he knows I lied to the constable. He believes there is another man who cares for me, that I— Oh, dear. Oh, dear. What do I care? Why do I care so much what he thinks, past the simple loss of his good opinion over that single falsehood? I cannot say. I am so utterly miserable. This last year has worn me right through. I have skipped right from childhood to old age. No youth, no soft, quiet womanhood for me. All the bright hopes a girl holds for her future are closed off— I will never marry. I carry all the weight of old age’s cares and sorrows, and all its ready tears besides. I am so tired of being called on to be strong, every hour of every day. I could bear up for Papa, that is a natural, pious duty. I could even summon the anger to stand against Mrs. Thornton’s impertinent, unfair suspicions. But it is unbearable, to know how completely he must misunderstand me. I do not know what has made me so morbid today. I only know I cannot fight it any longer. No,” she added, springing to her feet sharp, “no, I will not give way. I will not sit here picking apart my own feelings, not now. It is useless. Maybe if I live to be a very old woman, I will sit by my fire, stare into the embers, and see all the life that might have been.”

All the while she spoke, she was hastily pulling on her outdoor bonnet and shawl, pausing only to swipe impatiently at the tears that kept spilling down her cheeks, angry that they would not stop despite all her brave resolve. “I dare say thousands of women make the same stupid, sad mistake I did, and only realize it far too late. And how proud, how insolent I was to him that day he came to the house to speak to me! I had no idea then. It crept up on me little by little, and I cannot even name the day it began. But I will not give way now. It will be so hard to meet him normally, with this wretched secret weighing between us, but I will be calm, I will be quiet, I will say very little. Though for all I know, I may never see him again. He has been keeping out of our way for weeks, after all. That would be worse than anything. But no wonder he avoids me, when he believes all he does about me.”

She stepped out and walked as fast as her legs could carry her towards the open country, trying to drown out every intrusive thought by the sheer speed of her motion. When she returned home to her doorstep at mid-afternoon, her father came walking up the lane beside her.
“Good girl!” he said, smiling. “I knew you would go check on Mrs. Boucher. I was just about to head over there myself, if I had time before dinner.”
Margaret flushed bright. “No, Papa, I did not. I never thought of her, I am ashamed to say. But I will go right after dinner, while you take your nap.”

True to her word, she set off for the Boucher cottage once he had drifted off to sleep. Mrs. Boucher was very ill—dangerously so, not just a passing malaise. A kind, sensible neighbor had taken charge of the whole household the moment she’d heard the news, sending most of the children to stay with nearby families, and Mary Higgins had collected the three youngest at dinner time. Nicholas Higgins had gone for the doctor hours before, but he had not yet returned. Mrs. Boucher was slipping away, and there was nothing for anyone to do but wait. Margaret decided to walk the short distance to Higgins’ cottage, to see if he had had any luck with his request for work from Mr. Thornton.

She found Higgins kneeling on the floor by the dresser, spinning a copper penny over and over for the three little Boucher children, who clustered around him unafraid, shrieking with glee every time the coin spun a full ten seconds without falling flat. Higgins was smiling too, and Margaret thought that soft, happy look on his face was a good sign. When the penny finally toppled, the littlest boy, lile Johnnie, burst into tears.
“Come here,” Margaret said, lifting him off the dresser and tucking him against her chest. She held her watch to his ear to distract him, then asked Nicholas if he had spoken to Mr. Thornton that morning.
The smile dropped right off his face. “Aye, I saw him, and heard more than enough out of him.”
“Did he refuse you, then?” Margaret asked, her heart sinking.
“Of course he did. I knew he would, all along. No point expecting mercy from masters like that. You’re new to Milton, a stranger here, so you don’t know their ways, but I’ve seen it all my life.”
“I am so sorry I ever asked you to go. Was he angry? He did not speak to you as cruelly as Mr. Hamper did, did he?”
“He weren’t over-civil, that’s for certain,” Higgins grumbled, spinning the penny again, this time more for his own distraction than the children’s. “Don’t you fret, I’m no worse off than I was before. I’ll set out tramping the roads tomorrow. I gave him as good as I got, too. Told him I never thought well enough of him to come begging work on my own, but that some woman who knew no better told me to try, and I owed her for the suggestion.”
“Did you tell him it was me?”
“I don’t rightly know if I said your name. Told him some woman who didn’t know how things work here had sent me, said there might be a soft spot in his heart for folks in trouble.”
“And he said what?” Margaret pressed.
“Said I was to tell you to mind your own business. That’s the longest spin we’ve had yet, lads!” Higgins called out to the children. “And those were the mildest words he threw at me. But never mind. We’re no worse off than we were. I’ll break stones on the side of the road before I let these little ones starve.”

Margaret set the squirming Johnnie back down on the dresser. “I am so sorry I pushed you to go to him. I am deeply disappointed in Mr. Thornton.”
There was a soft scrape of a footstep behind her. Both Margaret and Higgins turned at the same moment, and there stood Mr. Thornton, his face tight with displeased surprise. Acting on nothing but instinct, Margaret slipped past him out of the door without a word, bowing low to hide the sudden deathly pale that washed over her face. He bowed equally low in return, and shut the door firmly behind her. The clang of the latch sounded to Margaret like the final note of her utter mortification as she hurried back to the Boucher cottage. He was annoyed to see her there, she thought. Of course he was.

Thornton watched her go, frowning. He did have that soft spot in his heart Higgins had spoken of, but he guarded it like something sacred, hated to think anyone might guess it existed. But if he was fiercely private about his tenderness, he was even more determined that every person around him recognize his commitment to justice. He realized suddenly he had been unfair, to turn Higgins away with nothing but scorn after the man had waited five whole patient hours to speak to him that morning. The saucy words Higgins had thrown at him later did not bother Thornton in the least—he rather admired the man for speaking his mind. He knew his own temper had been frayed that morning, that they had been even in their sharpness. But those five hours of waiting stuck in his head. He gave up two full hours of his packed work day to go from one mill hand to the next, collecting every bit of information he could about Higgins’ character, his life, his history. By the end of his rounds, he was thoroughly convinced every word Higgins had told him was true. The quiet generosity of the man, taking in a dead drunkard’s children no one else would care for, the five hours he had waited without complaint, melted the last of Thornton’s cold reserve. He had come all the way to the cottage to offer Higgins work, and he was far more annoyed to find Margaret there than he was at her overheard words calling him cruel—for he realized she was the woman who had urged Higgins to come to him, and he hated the thought that anyone might think he was doing this small kindness because of her, not because it was the right thing to do.
“So that was the lady you called a silly woman who gave you bad advice?” he said to Higgins, half indignant. “You might have told me who she was.”
“And then you’d have spoken far more civil of her than you did just now, wouldn’t you? You’ve a mother of your own, who I’d wager would have clipped your tongue short before you went spouting nonsense about all women being the root of every bit of trouble.”
“Of course you told all that to Miss Hale?”
“Course I did. Told her she’d best not meddle in anything that concerns you ever again.”
“Whose children are those? Are they yours?” Thornton asked, awkwardly shifting the subject away from the tense topic of Margaret. He already knew perfectly well they were Boucher’s kids, from what his workers had told him.
“They’re not mine, and they are mine, all at once.”
“They’re the children you told me about this morning?”
“When you said my story might be true or might not, but it sounded far too unlikely to be believed,” Higgins snapped, turning around, his fierce temper barely held back. “Master, I haven’t forgotten that.”
Thornton went quiet for a long moment. “Neither have I. I remember exactly what I said. I spoke to you about those children in a way I had no right to. I did not believe you. I knew I could never have taken care of another man’s children if that man had treated me the way I heard Boucher treated you. But I know now you spoke nothing but truth. I beg your pardon.”
Higgins did not turn around, did not reply right away. When he did speak, his gruff tone was softened, however. “You’ve no business prying into what went on between Boucher and me. He’s dead now, and I’m sorry for it. That’s all there is to say.”
“That’s all there is to say, indeed. Will you come take work with me? That is what I came here to ask you.”
Higgins’ obstinacy wavered for a second, then stiffened again. He would not speak. Thornton would not ask twice. Then Higgins’ eyes fell on the three little children, clustered quiet by the dresser.
“You called me impudent, a liar, a mischief-maker,” he said, “and you might well have added that I was too fond of drink now and then. And I called you a tyrant, an old bulldog, a hard cruel master. That’s where we stand. But for the children’s sake, master—do you really think we can ever get on together?”
“Well,” Thornton said, half laughing, “it was not my idea that we should work side by side. But you are right about one thing: neither of us can possibly think worse of the other than we already do. That’s a very comfortable place to start.”
“That’s true enough,” Higgins said, nodding slowly. “I’ve been thinking ever since I saw you this morning, what a mercy it was you refused me, because I never met a man I could abide less. But that was a hasty judgment. Work is work for folks like me. So, master— I’ll come. And what’s more, thank you. That’s a great deal more than I usually say to any employer.” He turned fully around to face Thornton, holding out his hand.
“And that’s a great deal more than I usually get,” Thornton said, gripping his hand hard enough to make his knuckles white. “Now, mind you, you show up sharp on time tomorrow. I will not have laggards at my mill. We keep our fines strictly, and the first time I catch you stirring up trouble among the other men, you are gone. You know where you stand.”
“You talked of my wisdom this morning. Am I to bring that with me, or would you rather have me leave my wits at home?”
“Bring your wits if you use them to meddle in my business. Leave them at home if you intend to stick to your own work.”
“I’ll need a whole lot of wits to figure out where my work ends and yours begins.”
“Your work has not even started yet, and mine waits for no man. Good afternoon.”

Just before Thornton reached the Boucher cottage, Margaret stepped out of the door. She did not see him at first, and he followed her several paces down the lane, admiring her light, easy step, her tall, graceful figure. Then a rush of bitter jealousy poisoned that soft, simple pleasure. He wanted to overtake her, speak to her, see how she would react now that she must know he believed that unnamed man from the station was her suitor. He also, a little ashamed of the urge, wanted her to know he had repented of his unfair judgment that morning, that he had done the right thing by Higgins. He hurried to catch up, and Margaret started when he spoke.
“Allow me to say, Miss Hale, that you were rather premature when you expressed your disappointment in me just now. I have taken Higgins on.”
“I am very glad to hear it,” Margaret said, her voice cool and distant.
“He told me he repeated the words I sent him back with this morning, about women not meddling in matters that do not concern them,” Thornton said, hesitating.
Margaret spoke up quickly. “You had every right to express that opinion, which I have no doubt was perfectly correct. But Higgins did not tell you the exact truth of the situation,” she added, growing a little more eager—until the word *truth* hit her, and she clammed up, suddenly overwhelmed with discomfort, remembering her own lie to the constable.
Thornton stared at her, confused for a moment, then the memory of that lie, the memory of the constable’s testimony, flooded back. “The exact truth. Very few people speak the exact truth. I have given up hoping for it. Miss Hale—have you no explanation to give me? You must know exactly what I have been forced to think.”
Margaret was silent, turning over in her head whether any explanation could ever be given without betraying Frederick’s safety.
“No,” Thornton said, “I will not press you. I might only be putting temptation in your way. But I give you my word, your secret is perfectly safe with me. Though I must warn you, you run very grave risks by being so indiscreet. I speak only as a friend to your father. Any other hope I may have once held for you is entirely gone. It is all over. I am completely disinterested.”
“I am well aware of that,” Margaret said, forcing her voice to sound indifferent, careless. “I know exactly what I must look like to you, but that secret belongs to another person. I cannot explain it without doing him great harm.”
“I have not the slightest wish to pry into that gentleman’s secrets,” Thornton said, his temper rising. “My only interest in you now is that of a friend. You may not believe me, but it is true. All that foolish passion I once threatened you with is entirely gone, long past. You do believe me, Miss Hale?”
“Yes,” Margaret said, quiet and sad.
“Then I see no reason for us to continue walking here together. I thought perhaps you might have something to say to me, but it is clear we are nothing to one another. If you are quite convinced that any feeling I once had for you is entirely over, then I wish you good afternoon.” He turned and walked away very quickly, leaving Margaret standing there on the lane, staring after him.

What can he mean? Margaret thought. Why does he speak as if I am still hanging on to the idea that he cares for me? He does not, he cannot. His mother must have filled his head with all those cruel stories about me. But I will not care for him. I am strong enough to master this wild, miserable, foolish feeling, that almost tempted me to betray my own dear Frederick just to win back his good opinion—the good opinion of a man who goes out of his way to tell me I mean nothing to him. Come now, poor little heart. Be brave, be cheerful. We will be far more to each other, the two of us, now that the whole world has turned its back on us.

Her father was startled by the almost wild merriment Margaret showed when she got home that evening. She talked incessantly, forcing her natural good humor to a pitch she had not reached for months, and if there was a faint bitter edge to her words, if her stories of their old life in Harley Street were a little sharper than usual, Mr. Hale did not have the heart to check her.
