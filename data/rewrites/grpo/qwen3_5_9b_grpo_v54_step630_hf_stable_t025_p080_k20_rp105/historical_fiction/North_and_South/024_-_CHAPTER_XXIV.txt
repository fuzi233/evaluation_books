"Your beauty was the first that won the place
And scal'd the walls of my undaunted heart,
Which, captive now, pines in a caitive case,
Unkindly met with rigour for desert:—
Yet not the less your servant shall abide,
In spite of rude repulse or silent pride."

The dawn broke gray and heavy, yet Margaret found a strange, weary peace in the silence of the house. She rose with a body that felt heavy as lead, though her spirit was strangely lightened. The night had passed without the sharp jarring of her mother's waking, leaving the household in a fragile, suspended calm. Outside, the stifling heat of the morning air began to stir, carrying with it the faint, distant murmur of wind through unseen foliage—a sound that, to Margaret's imagination, transformed itself into a cool, dancing stream, washing away the dust of the previous day.

Sitting by the shaded window in Mrs. Hale's room, she picked up her needlework, her mind already rehearsing the day's duties: helping her mother dress, visiting Bessy Higgins after dinner. She intended to build a wall of forgetfulness around the Thornton family, to keep them at bay until they appeared in reality rather than in her fevered thoughts. Yet, the very effort to suppress them only sharpened their image. From time to time, a sudden, hot flush would sweep across her pale cheeks, rising like a sunbeam breaking through storm clouds, betraying the turmoil she tried so desperately to hide.

The door opened with a soft click, and Dixon slipped in, her footsteps silent on the floorboards.

"Mr. Thornton, Miss Margaret," she whispered, approaching the chair. "He is in the drawing-room."

Margaret's needle dropped from her fingers, clattering onto the cloth. "Did he ask for me? Has Papa returned?"

"He asked for you, miss; and Master is out."

"Very well," Margaret said, her voice steady despite the sudden stillness in her chest. "I will come." Yet she hesitated, lingering as if anchored by an invisible weight.

In the drawing-room, Mr. Thornton stood before a window, his back to the entrance, ostensibly watching the street. In truth, he was a prisoner of his own emotions. The memory of her arms around his neck the day before burned within him, a sensation that now threatened to dissolve his resolve entirely. He feared the moment she entered, terrified that he might reach out, that she might return to him as she had yesterday, seeking shelter in his embrace. His heart hammered against his ribs, a frantic drumbeat that betrayed the strength he claimed to possess. He oscillated between a desperate hope that she would fall into his arms and a paralyzing dread that she would reject him, a thought that seemed to wither his future before it had truly begun.

He was startled from his reverie by the soft rustle of fabric. She had entered so quietly that the noise of the street outside had drowned out her approach.

She stood near the table, refusing to sit. Her eyes were half-closed, her lips slightly parted, revealing the pale line beneath. Her breathing was slow and deep, causing the fine muscles of her chin and nose to rise and fall. Her complexion was wan, the usual healthful color drained away, obscured further by the heavy shadow of her dark hair falling across her temples, hiding the mark of the blow she had sustained. Despite the droop of her eyelids, her head remained tilted back in that familiar, proud posture. She looked, he thought, like a noble prisoner falsely accused of a crime she despised, too indignant to defend herself.

Mr. Thornton took a step forward, then stopped himself, regaining his composure. He moved to the door, which she had left ajar, and closed it firmly. Returning to the center of the room, he stood opposite her, absorbing the sheer force of her presence before daring to speak, fearing that his words might shatter the fragile atmosphere he had created.

"Miss Hale, I was very ungrateful yesterday—"

"You had nothing to be grateful for," she interrupted, lifting her gaze to meet his directly. "You mean, I suppose, that you believe you ought to thank me for what I did." Despite her anger, a deep blush spread across her face, burning into her eyes, yet she did not look away. "It was only a natural instinct; any woman would have done just the same. We all feel the sanctity of our sex as a high privilege when we see danger. I ought rather," she added quickly, "to apologize to you for having said thoughtless words which sent you down into the danger."

"It was not your words; it was the truth they conveyed, pungently as it was expressed. But you shall not drive me off upon that, and so escape the expression of my deep gratitude, my—" He paused, the heat of his passion threatening to overwhelm his reason. He forced himself to stop, weighing each syllable.

"I do not try to escape from anything," she replied calmly. "I simply say that you owe me no gratitude; and I may add that any expression of it will be painful to me, because I do not feel that I deserve it. Still, if it will relieve you from even a fancied obligation, speak on."

"I do not want to be relieved from any obligation," he said, driven by her serene demeanor. "Fancied, or not fancied—I question not myself to know which—I choose to believe that I owe my very life to you—ay—smile, and think it an exaggeration if you will. I believe it, because it adds a value to that life to think—oh, Miss Hale!" His voice dropped to a tender, intense whisper that made her shiver. "To think circumstances so wrought, that whenever I exult in existence henceforward, I may say to myself, 'All this gladness in life, all honest pride in doing my work in the world, all this keen sense of being, I owe to her!' And it doubles the gladness, it makes the pride glow, it sharpens the sense of existence till I hardly know if it is pain or pleasure to think that I owe it to one—nay, you must, you shall hear," he stepped forward with stern determination, "to one whom I love, as I do not believe man ever loved woman before."

He seized her hand tightly, panting as he awaited her response. But when he heard her icy tone, he withdrew his hand with indignation. The words had come faltering, as if she struggled to find them.

"Your way of speaking shocks me. It is blasphemous. I cannot help it, if that is my first feeling. It might not be so, I dare say, if I understood the kind of feeling you describe. I do not want to vex you; and besides, we must speak gently, for mamma is asleep; but your whole manner offends me—"

"How!" he exclaimed. "Offends you! I am indeed most unfortunate."

"Yes!" she said, recovering her dignity. "I do feel offended; and, I think, justly. You seem to fancy that my conduct of yesterday"—again the deep carnation blush, but this time her eyes kindled with indignation rather than shame—"was a personal act between you and me; and that you may come and thank me for it, instead of perceiving, as a gentleman would—yes! a gentleman," she repeated, alluding to their past debate on the term, "that any woman, worthy of the name of woman, would come forward to shield, with her reverenced helplessness, a man in danger from the violence of numbers."

"And the gentleman thus rescued is forbidden the relief of thanks!" he broke in contemptuously. "I am a man. I claim the right of expressing my feelings."

"And I yielded to the right; simply saying that you gave me pain by insisting upon it," she replied, proudly. "But you seem to have imagined, that I was not merely guided by womanly instinct, but"—here the passionate tears, held back for so long, surged up, choking her voice—"but that I was prompted by some particular feeling for you—you! Why, there was not a man—not a poor desperate man in all that crowd—for whom I had not more sympathy—for whom I should not have done what little I could more heartily."

"You may speak on, Miss Hale. I am aware of these misplaced sympathies of yours. I now believe that it was only your innate sense of oppression—(yes; I, though a master, may be oppressed)—that made you act so nobly as you did. I know you despise me; allow me to say, it is because you do not understand me."

"I do not care to understand," she replied, gripping the edge of the table to steady herself. She thought him cruel—as, indeed, he was—and she was weak with her indignation.

"No, I see you do not. You are unfair and unjust."

Margaret compressed her lips. She would not speak in answer to such accusations. Yet, despite his savage words, he could have thrown himself at her feet and kissed the hem of her garment. She remained silent, unmoving. Tears of wounded pride fell hot and fast. He waited a while, longing for her to say something, even a taunt, to which he might reply. But she was silent. He took up his hat.

"One word more. You look as if you thought it tainted you to be loved by me. You cannot avoid it. Nay, I, if I would, cannot cleanse you from it. But I would not if I could. I have never loved any woman before: my life has been too busy, my thoughts too much absorbed with other things. Now I love, and will love. But do not be afraid of too much expression on my part."

"I am not afraid," she replied, lifting herself straight up. "No one yet has ever dared to be impertinent to me, and no one ever shall. But, Mr. Thornton, you have been very kind to my father," she said, changing her whole tone and bearing to a most womanly softness. "Don't let us go on making each other angry. Pray don't!"

He took no notice of her words. For half a minute he occupied himself in smoothing the nap of his hat with his coat-sleeve; and then, rejecting her offered hand, and making as if he did not see her grave look of regret, he turned abruptly away and left the room. Margaret caught one glance at his face before he went.

When he was gone, she thought she had seen the gleam of washed tears in his eyes; and that turned her proud dislike into something different and kinder, if nearly as painful—self-reproach for having caused such mortification to any one.

"But how could I help it?" she asked herself. "I never liked him. I was civil; but I took no trouble to conceal my indifference. Indeed, I never thought about myself or him, so my manners must have shown the truth. All that yesterday, he might mistake. But that is his fault, not mine. I would do it again, if need were, though it does lead me into all this shame and trouble."
