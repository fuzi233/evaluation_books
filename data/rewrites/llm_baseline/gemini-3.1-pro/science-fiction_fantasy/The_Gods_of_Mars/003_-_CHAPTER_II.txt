The sonic equilibrium of the valley shattered, torn apart by the weird, piercing shriek of the herald upon the cliff. To my ears, attuned to the deep, acoustic ecology of Barsoom’s vast and silent sea bottoms, this shrill summons was a violent pollutant, a grating industrial cacophony that assaulted the psychological well-being of the landscape itself. Down the broad expanse of the scarlet sward, a torrent of terrifying creatures streamed toward us, drawn by that disruptive frequency. 

Around us lay the grisly harvest of our initial skirmish, the air thick with the olfactory heavyweights of combat: the herbaceous, sticky musk of severed plant-man vines mingling with the oxidized, iron-rich tang of spilled Martian blood. 

“Come,” Tars Tarkas cried, his native Tharkish syllables carrying the resonant, guttural weight of an ancient culture slowly being suffocated by the dying world around it. “We must make for the cliffs.” 

We raced across the scarlet turf, the spongy, moss-like ground yielding beneath my boots with the scent of crushed lichen and dry spice. I modulated my Earth-born stride to keep pace with my slower, massive companion, the two of us weaving through a sweeping, multi-generational saga of predator and prey that had played out upon this valley floor for millennia. Tars Tarkas urged me ahead to scout for sanctuary, and, throwing every ounce of my muscle into the effort, I crossed the remaining distance in great leaps.

The cliffs rose perpendicularly from the agrarian flatness of the valley. To my right, a magnificent forest reared its gorgeous foliage a thousand feet into the air. As the sun crested the zenith, the cliff face erupted in a scintillating display of burnished gold, flaming red rubies, and soft green emeralds. Yet, in that moment, I could not appreciate this geological banquet. My mind, seeking only an acoustic and physical sanctuary, recoiled from the sheer, impregnable wall. It was only when my eyes caught the dark mouths of caves near the forest’s canopy that I found our recipe for survival. 

Scaling the colossal trees was our only route. These towering flora were ancient, their bark redolent of toasted cinnamon and petrified sap, having stood as silent, domestic witnesses to the valley's cyclical history. For Tars Tarkas, a fifteen-foot green Martian whose people favored the nomadic, circuitous paths of the dead sea bottoms, climbing was a nearly untranslatable physical concept. Yet, as the relentless horde closed in, carrying the rancid scent of raw marrow and overripe vegetation, he accepted the necessity. He fled not from a fear of death—a concept alien to the stoic, death-seeking lexicon of his people—but out of an unspoken loyalty to our shared history, honoring the profound sustenance of our friendship.

We reached the deep, earthy shadows of the forest just as a giant plant man sprang upon us. The creature was a grotesque marvel of botanical anatomy, engineered to harvest vital fluids. Its claws reached for us, its mouths eager to feast. I called to Tars Tarkas to begin his ascent while I prepared to dispatch the creature, but the beast was swift. As my long-sword cleaved the empty air, its massive tail swept me to the ground, bringing the bitter taste of dirt and crushed roots to my tongue. 

I grasped a writhing tentacle in either hand. The creature’s fibrous, well-muscled flesh yielded like a dense root vegetable under my grip, and the intimate proximity forced me to inhale its sharp, peppery musk. Had we the luxury of an uninterrupted struggle, my earthly sinews would have surely triumphed, but the sonic landscape was suddenly overwhelmed by a new, horrifying frequency. 

Over the shoulder of my botanical antagonist, I saw the great white apes of Barsoom arriving in a wave of snapping, screaming rage. They towered fifteen feet high, their physiology uncannily mimicking our Earth men but distorted by enormous size and an intermediary set of arms. They brought with them the heavy, fermented odor of old blood and stale breath. But the most insidious assault on my senses was the horrid purring of the plant men—a low, rhythmic vibration that burrowed into the inner ear, a deeply unnatural sound that corrupted the mind’s acoustic sanctuary. 

Instantly, cruel fangs and keen talons sank into my flesh. Cold, sucking lips fastened upon my arteries, attempting to drink the very essence of my life. Weighted down by tons of ferocious flesh, I struggled to my feet. Shortening my grip on my blade, I turned it into a chef’s paring knife, meticulously carving my way through cartilage and sap. Green botanical ichor and crimson ape blood emulsified in the air, a gruesome vinaigrette born of our desperate struggle. 

From the lower branches, Tars Tarkas saw my plight and dropped back to the soil. Once again, we fought back to back, our gleaming blades moving with the practiced rhythm of a kitchen brigade in the heat of service. Time and again, we beat back the apes and dodged the leaping plant men, but against such overwhelming numbers, even the finest swordsmen on Mars were forced to yield ground. 

We retreated halfway around the colossal trunk of the giant tree. Suddenly, Tars Tarkas emitted a brief cry of discovery. “Here is shelter for one at least, John Carter,” he said, pointing to a three-foot opening in the ancient bark, smelling heavily of dry rot and hollowed timber. 

“In with you, Tars Tarkas,” I cried.

A complex negotiation of cultural identity and linguistic pride followed, shouted over the din of clashing steel and roaring beasts. In the Tharkish dialect, there are few words to adequately convey the nuance of selfless sacrifice; the dominant lexicon of his people equates retreat with dishonor. “We shall both die if we remain without,” he argued, his bulk too vast to slip easily into the aperture while under attack. 

“Then we shall die together,” I replied, the metallic tang of adrenaline bright on my palate. “Let me defend the opening while you get in. My smaller stature will permit me to slip in after.”

He hesitated, choking on a word that his whole life of continual strife had taught him to despise. “If you fail, John Carter,” he said, the grim smile on his face betraying the untranslatable depth of his affection, “know that the cruel and heartless Thark, to whom you taught the meaning of friendship, will come out to die beside you.”

As he dropped to force his way into the aromatic, woody hollow, the howling pack hurled themselves at me. My shimmering blade flew right and left, now coated in the sticky, herbaceous juice of a plant man, now dyed with the hot, iron-rich crimson of a white ape. I fought as I never had before, feeding the horde a bitter meal of cold steel. Though the ground piled high with their dead, their sheer weight eventually dragged me down beneath them. I felt those awful sucking lips against my flesh once more. 

Then, powerful hands gripped my ankles. I was hauled backward into the hollow domesticity of the tree's interior. A brief tug of war ensued between Tars Tarkas and a plant man clinging tenaciously to my breast, but with a mighty thrust, I pierced its vitals. 

I lay panting on the flat, hard floor. The air inside the tree was a restorative balm—cool, smelling of ancient tannin, dried husks, and centuries of undisturbed dust. For an hour, the horde outside shrieked and purred, their noise battering the thick wooden walls, but the acoustic dampening of the tree preserved our sanity. Eventually, the horrific sounds faded to a localized perimeter, leaving us in a tense, besieged quiet.

As my eyes adjusted to the semi-darkness, I explored our refuge. The hollow spanned fifty feet in diameter, a natural, multi-generational domicile used by unknown beings long before our occupancy. High above, a faint glow of light hinted at a high canopy opening. Against the far wall, I discovered the remnants of everyday domestic labor: a rough ladder connecting to a series of horizontal wooden bars spanning the shaft-like interior. 

I climbed first, my hands gripping wood polished smooth by the oils of countless ancient ascents. The light grew brighter. After climbing fully five hundred feet, I reached the upper opening, which gave way to a massive, flat limb. Retracing my steps, I fetched Tars Tarkas. As we ascended together, we carefully removed the lower bars, severing the timeline of our path to prevent pursuit from the rear—a meticulous preservation of our newfound safety.

When we emerged, I ventured out onto the limb. It angled upward toward the cliff, smelling of sun-baked bark and ozone, ending a few feet above a narrow ledge that fronted a cave. My lesser weight allowed me to tread the dizzying branch, but it bent precariously. Tars Tarkas and I devised a solution using his longest leather harness strap—the cured hide carrying the familiar scent of lanolin and warrior's sweat. I crossed to the ledge, then hauled his massive bulk across the gap. 

Standing on the dizzying balcony, we looked out over the cyclical reality of the valley. Below, the white apes were foraging on the remnants of fallen Tharks, while herds of plant men grazed placidly upon the scarlet sward, keeping it close-clipped like a well-tended agrarian estate. The violent mythic occurrences of the morning had already receded into the everyday domestic routines of Barsoom’s brutal ecosystem.

Turning away, we entered the cave. It was a well-proportioned tunnel carved from solid rock, carrying the faint, desiccated aroma of undisturbed stone. We walked in absolute darkness, the silence profound and restorative to my frayed nerves. We clasped hands to maintain connection, moving through the deep acoustic void until we struck a smooth partition of hard wood. 

My fingers traced the surface, searching for the tactile vocabulary of Martian architecture. I found the button—a dialect of engineering common across the planet. I pressed it, and the door yielded, opening into a dimly lit, unoccupied chamber. The air here was sterile, devoid of the rich, gustatory markers of the living valley. 

We stepped inside. Almost instantly, the door closed behind us with a sharp, mechanical click—a jarring intrusion into the silent ecology of the space. I sprang to wrench it open, but the door was unyielding. There was no button on the inside. The dominant architecture had asserted its power, trapping us in a language we could no longer manipulate. 

And then, from unseen lips in the shadows, a cruel, mocking peal of laughter rang through the desolate chamber, shattering our fragile acoustic sanctuary.
