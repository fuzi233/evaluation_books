Early in the town’s memory—back when the Magpie Hotel’s parlour still smelled of spilled porter, damp greatcoats, and that indefinable perfume which rises from a table where men have argued themselves thirsty—there was Mr. Garbetts, principal tragedian, a large-limbed performer with a voice that could frighten pigeons off a roof and legs that the whole company admired as if they were a public monument. He and Captain Costigan were intimate in the particular way two men become intimate when they have, between them, a generous assortment of bills coming due, and only one respectable signature each left to lend.

Garbetts, to do him justice, was jovial, athletic, and capable of snapping a poker across his arm to amuse the room—an accomplishment which, like juggling knives, is valued far more highly by those who watch than by those who keep pokers in their households. He did not often visit the Costigans’ lodging; Miss Fotheringay disliked him, and Mrs. Garbetts disliked Miss Fotheringay, and the truth of that triangle was old gossip: Garbetts had once paid his court to Emily and been refused, before he offered his hand to Mrs. G. The theatre forgets nothing, except its lines.

Still, when Arthur Pendennis and Mr. Bows had scarcely got down the stairs, and the Captain’s injured honour began to boil like a pot set too close to the fire, it was to Garbetts that Costigan flew, as men do, not for wisdom, but for noise and muscle.

“Run, Tommy,” cried the Captain to the small errand-boy, “and fetch Mr. Garbetts from his lodgings over the tripe-shop—ye know—and tell ’em to send two glasses of whisky-and-water, hot, from the Grapes.”

The mention of the tripe-shop is not idle. All that lane had its own domestic mythology of smells: the pale, vinegary steam of boiled offal; the sharp tang of pickled onions; the sweet, dirty breath of wet straw from the costermonger’s cart. Even the street had a soundtrack: knives tapping on boards, women calling prices, cart-wheels grumbling in ruts. In that honest market-noise there was more health than in any salon’s hush; but Captain Costigan, at this moment, preferred the particular music of whisky being poured into a glass.

Tommy went; and presently Mr. Garbetts arrived with the steaming tumblers, which sent up an aroma of spirits cut with hot water—an odour that, once in a room, takes possession like a bold actor who has mistaken the play for his own benefit-night.

Captain Costigan did not, to his friend, narrate the entire chain of his humiliations, for there are events a man tells in full only to himself at midnight; but with a pen in one hand and a hot glass in the other, he composed a letter to Major Pendennis—threatening, elegant in its own eyes, and sealed with elaborate care by the great silver seal of the Costigans, the only piece of family plate the Captain could call up as a witness to his gentility.

In this epistle he commanded Major Pendennis to offer no hindrance to the marriage which Captain Costigan had now proclaimed as projected between Mr. Arthur Pendennis and his daughter Miss Fotheringay, and to fix an early day for the ceremony; or else, failing the altar, to afford the satisfaction usual among gentlemen of honour. If the Major should dislike even that, the Captain hinted that he could be persuaded by a horsewhip applied to his person. One regrets to be unable to provide the exact phraseology; but the misfortune is unavoidable, for the letter never reached the stage of being read by the Major at all.

Garbetts was dispatched as ambassador. Costigan squeezed his hand with that theatrical benediction which men give when they have been drinking hot spirits and feel themselves both wronged and heroic.

As soon as Garbetts was gone, the Captain took down his venerable duelling-pistols, flint-locks, that had done the business of “many a pretty fellow in Dublin,” as he loved to say, and examined them with a tenderness more suited to polishing a family christening-spoon. Then he drew out from a drawer all Pen’s letters and poems—those documents which he kept like a miser keeps bank-notes, reading them himself before permitting Emily their perusal, as though he were tasting the dish before allowing his daughter to sit to dinner.

In twenty minutes Mr. Garbetts returned. His face, made for Roman despair, wore now a look more domestic—anxious, crestfallen, and very much inclined to keep out of trouble.

“Ye’ve seen ’um?” cried Costigan.

“Why—yes,” said Garbetts.

“And when is it for?” asked the Captain, trying the lock of one of the pistols and bringing it level with his oi—that bloodshot orb he so affectionately mispronounced, as if the eye, too, were Irish.

“When is what for?” Garbetts asked, already suspecting that this conversation had pistols at its end.

“The meeting, my dear fellow.”

“You don’t mean to say you mean mortal combat, Captain?” Garbetts cried, aghast.

“What the devil else do I mean, Garbetts? I want to shoot that man that has trajuiced me honor, or meself dthrop a victim on the sod.”

Mr. Garbetts recoiled as a family man recoils from another man’s romance. “D—— if I carry challenges,” he said. “I’m a family man, Captain, and will have nothing to do with pistols—take back your letter.” And, to Captain Costigan’s surprise and indignation, Garbetts flung the letter down upon the table, with its great sprawling superscription and the blotched seal as ostentatious as a cheap pudding’s glaze.

“Ye don’t mean to say ye saw ’um and didn’t give ’um the letter?” Costigan roared, as if a messenger could keep a challenge in his pocket the way a child keeps a sweet.

“I saw him,” said Garbetts, “but I could not have speech with him, Captain.”

“And why the devil not?”

“There was one there I cared not to meet—nor would you,” the tragedian answered, dropping his voice into the sepulchral tone he reserved for ghosts and creditors. “The minion Tatham was there, Captain.”

“The cowardly scoundthrel!” bellowed Costigan. “He’s frightened, and already going to swear the peace against me.”

“I’ll have nothing to do with the fighting, mark that,” Garbetts said doggedly. “And I wish I’d not seen Tatham neither, nor that bit of—”

“Hold your tongue, Bob Acres. It’s my belief ye’re no better than a coward,” cried Costigan, quoting Sir Lucius O’Trigger with that pious satisfaction actors feel when life supplies them a line.

After more parley—condensed here, for it was chiefly a duet of pride and prudence—they separated not in very good humour. Garbetts departed with the air of a man who has been asked to carry a live coal in his hands and has, sensibly, declined.

And thus it happens that the Captain’s letter, though composed with heat, sealed with dignity, and perfumed by whisky vapour, was never opened by Major Pendennis, never weighed in his cool fingers, never read with that Pall Mall politeness which can make an insult feel like a compliment. It lay, unread, like an unserved pie cooling on a window-sill.

When Miss Costigan came home from rehearsal that evening—she returned with faithful Mr. Bows—she found her father pacing the small sitting-room in a storm. The air was thick with hot spirits-and-water; the table was littered with Pen’s papers surrounding empty goblets and the now useless teaspoon that had stirred the Captain’s courage into solution. If the room had any music, it was the wrong sort: the scrape of boots on boards, the rattle of a pistol-case touched too often, the loud breathing of a man who thinks himself tragic. In such domestic acoustics there is no comfort; it is a noise that makes even silence nervous.

Costigan caught Emily as she entered and clasped her with tears in his eyes. “Prepare yourself, me child, me blessed child,” he cried in a voice of agony, as though she were about to be served up as a sacrifice.

“Ye’re tipsy again, Papa,” Miss Fotheringay said, pushing him back with the calm of a woman who has seen men perform. “Ye promised me ye wouldn’t take spirits before dinner.”

“It’s to forget me sorrows, me poor girl, that I’ve taken just a drop,” sobbed the bereaved father. “It’s to drown me care that I drain the bowl.”

“Your care takes a deal of drowning, Captain dear,” said Bows, mimicking his friend’s accent with a fiddler’s irreverence. “What has happened? Has that soft-spoken gentleman in the wig been vexing you?”

“The oily miscreant! I’ll have his blood!” roared Cos.

Emily, who had slipped out of his reach into her own room, came back presently looking as healthy and unconcerned as a well-made loaf sitting cool on the board while other things burn. She carried in a pair of ex-white satin shoes, and—because the theatre’s gods demand cleanliness—meant to rub them as near to virgin brightness as possible with bread-crumb, intending on next Tuesday evening to go mad in them as Ophelia.

She looked at the papers on the table, seemed about to ask something, and thought better of it. Then, going to the cupboard with practical ceremony, she selected a promising piece of bread—firm enough to crumble, not so stale as to scratch—like a cook choosing the right crust to thicken a stew. Returning, she sat down comfortably with the shoes in her lap and began her careful rubbing, raising a faint floury smell that, against the whisky, was almost wholesome.

“What have ye got them letthers, and pothry, and stuff, of Master Arthur’s out for, Pa?” she asked in her honest Irish brogue. “Sure ye don’t want to be reading over that nonsense.”

“O Emilee!” cried the Captain. “That boy whom I loved as the boy of mee bosom is only a scoundthrel, and a deceiver, mee poor girl.” He cast a tragical look at Mr. Bows; Bows, in his turn, watched Emily with the anxious attention of a man who hears, in the smallest domestic phrase, the beginning of a storm.

“He! pooh!” she said, unruffled, rubbing at the satin. “Sure the poor lad’s as simple as a schoolboy. All them children write verses and nonsense.”

“He’s been acting the part of a viper to this fireside, and a traitor in this familee!” cried the Captain. “I tell ye he’s no better than an impostor.”

“What has the poor fellow done, Papa?” Emily asked, not lifting her eyes from the shoe, as if a clean slipper were the more urgent business.

“Done? He has deceived us in the most athrocious manner. He has thrifled with your affections, and outraged my own fine feelings. He has represented himself as a man of property, and it turruns out that he is no betther than a beggar. Haven’t I often told ye he had two thousand a year? He’s a pauper, I tell ye, Miss Costigan; a depindent upon the bountee of his mother; a good woman, who may marry again, who’s likely to live for ever, and who has but five hundred a year. How dar he ask ye to marry into a family which has not the means of providing for ye? Ye’ve been grossly deceived and put upon, Milly, and it’s my belief his old ruffian of an uncle in a wig is in the plot against us.”

“That soft old gentleman?” said Emily, still imperturbable. “What has he been doing, Papa?”

Costigan recounted the Major’s statement: that young Pendennis had no fortune; that Costigan was urged to go to the lawyers and inspect the father’s will—“wherein he knew the scoundthrels have a bill of mine, and I can’t meet them,” he inserted, as if even honour must take notice of outstanding accounts. Finally, the Captain declared an infernal swindle had been practised upon him by uncle and nephew, and that he was resolved upon marriage or blood.

All the while Emily rubbed. The bread-crumbs fell like little pale flakes of snow; the satin, under her fingers, slowly regained its innocent shine.

“Sure, if he’s no money, there’s no use marrying him, Papa,” she said at last, with the cool decisiveness of a housekeeper deciding against an over-priced joint. “Why did the villain say he was a man of prawpertee?”

“The poor fellow always said he was poor,” Emily answered. “’Twas you would have it he was rich, Papa—and made me agree to take him.”

“He should have been explicit and told us his income, Milly,” the Captain insisted. “A young fellow who rides a blood mare, and makes presents of shawls and bracelets, is an impostor if he has no money;—and as for his uncle, bedad I’ll pull off his wig whenever I see ’um. Bows, here, shall take a message to him and tell him so. Either it’s a marriage, or he meets me in the field like a man, or I tweak ’um on the nose in front of his hotel or in the gravel walks of Fairoaks Park before all the county, bedad.”

“Bedad, you may send somebody else with the message,” said Bows, laughing. “I’m a fiddler, not a fighting man.”

“Pooh, you’ve no spirit, sir,” roared the General. “I’ll be my own second, if no one will stand by and see me injured. And I’ll take my case of pistols and shoot ’um in the Coffee-room of the George.”

“And so poor Arthur has no money?” Emily sighed, the first softening in her tone. “Poor lad, he was a good lad too: wild and talking nonsense, with his verses and pothry and that, but a brave, generous boy, and indeed I liked him—and he liked me too,” she added, rather softly, and rubbed on.

“Why don’t you marry him if you like him so?” Bows burst out, with a sudden sourness. “He is not more than ten years younger than you are. His mother may relent, and you might go and live and have enough at Fairoaks Park. Why not go and be a lady? I could go on with the fiddle, and the General live on his half-pay. Why don’t you marry him? You know he likes you.”

“There’s others that likes me as well, Bows, that has no money and that’s old enough,” Miss Milly said, with the same practical gravity she might have used in discussing the price of meat.

“Yes—d—— it,” Bows answered, with a bitter curse, “that are old enough and poor enough and fools enough for anything.”

“There’s old fools, and young fools too,” she returned, with an imperious glance that dismissed his anger as if it were smoke. “You’ve often said so, you silly man. If Pendennis has not enough money to live upon, it’s folly to talk about marrying him: and that’s the long and short of it.”

“And the boy?” said Bows. “By Jove! you throw a man away like an old glove, Miss Costigan.”

“I don’t know what you mean, Bows,” she replied placidly, taking up the second shoe. “If he had had half of the two thousand a year that Papa gave him, or the half of that, I would marry him. But what is the good of taking on with a beggar? We’re poor enough already. There’s no use in my going to live with an old lady that’s testy and cross, maybe, and would grudge me every morsel of meat.”

(The truth was, it was near dinner time, and Suky had not laid the cloth yet; and in households like theirs hunger and economy are always waiting in the passage, listening.)

“And then,” she added quite simply, “suppose there was a family?—why, Papa, we shouldn’t be as well off as we are now.”

“’Deed, then, you would not, Milly dear,” Costigan said, soothed by her good sense as men are soothed by a warm dish set before them.

“And there’s an end to all the fine talk about Mrs. Arthur Pendennis of Fairoaks Park—the member of Parliament’s lady,” Milly laughed. “Pretty carriages and horses we should have to ride!—that you were always talking about, Papa! But it’s always the same. If a man looked at me, you fancied he was going to marry me; and if he had a good coat, you fancied he was as rich as Crazes.”

“—As Croesus,” said Bows.

“Well, call ’um what ye like. But it’s a fact now that Papa has married me these eight years a score of times. Wasn’t I to be my Lady Poldoody of Oystherstown Castle? Then there was the Navy Captain at Portsmouth, and the old surgeon at Norwich, and the Methodist preacher here last year, and who knows how many more? Well, I bet a penny, with all your scheming, I shall die Milly Costigan at last. So poor little Arthur has no money? Stop and take dinner, Bows; we’ve a beautiful beef-steak pudding.”

At that word the whole room changed key, as if some invisible hand had shifted a tune from minor to major. The promised pudding rose in everyone’s imagination: suet crust steamed and swelling; beef cut small so it would surrender properly; onions softened to sweetness; pepper and salt measured by a practised hand; and, above all, the smell—rich, brown, domestic—capable of calming revolutions. Even Captain Costigan’s pistols, hearing of beef-steak pudding, seemed less eager for blood.

“I wonder whether she is on with Sir Derby Oaks,” thought Bows, whose eyes and thoughts were always watching her. “The dodges of women beat all comprehension; and I am sure she wouldn’t let the lad off so easily, if she had not some other scheme on hand.”

It may be seen that Miss Fotheringay, though often silent and by no means brilliant where poetry, literature, or the fine arts were concerned, spoke freely and sensibly in her own circle. She was not romantic; she had no appetite for reading Shakespeare once she left the stage (and, indeed, had not truly understood him while she adorned it). But give her a pudding to judge, a piece of needle-work to finish, a household crisis to settle, and she was as steady as a cook who knows precisely when to take a dish off the fire. Strong imagination and passionate temper did not mislead her; she kept her judgment cool, and her hands busy.

Over dinner—where the beef-steak pudding proved indeed beautiful, the crust cut with a spoon and letting out a savoury steam that fogged the windows a little—Captain Costigan tried to convince himself, and the company, that Major Pendennis’s account of Pen’s finances must be a trick: a ruse by the old hypocrite to make them break the match on their side. But Milly would not, for a moment, entertain the notion of deceit in the adversary. In her plain way she showed it was her father who had deceived himself; poor little Pen had never tried to take them in.

“As for that poor lad,” she said, “I pity him with all my heart.”

And she ate an exceedingly good dinner—an act which filled Mr. Bows with that odd mixture of admiration and contempt she constantly inspired in him, as though her appetite were both a virtue and an insult.

During and after the meal—while the room settled into the softer, safer sounds of domesticity: knives laid down, plates shifted, the kettle singing for tea—the three devised the best means of bringing this love-matter to an end. Costigan’s idea of tweaking the Major’s nose faded with his supply of after-dinner whisky-and-water; the liquor, which before had made him roar, now made him pliant. He became submissive to his daughter, ready for any plan she chose, as a blustering cook submits to the one sensible person in the kitchen when the sauce begins to split.

The Captain, who had been eager enough to face and demolish both Pen and his uncle while he believed himself wronged, perhaps shrank a little from the thought of meeting the young man, and asked, with sudden practicality, “what the juice they were to say to the lad if he remained steady to his engagement, and they broke from theirs?”

“What? don’t you know how to throw a man over?” said Bows. “Ask a woman to tell you?”

Miss Fotheringay, without malice and without flourish, explained how it was done—simply enough; nothing more easy.

“Papa writes to Arthur to know what settlements he proposes to make in event of a marriage; and asks what his means are. Arthur writes back and says what he’s got, and you’ll find it’s as the Major says, I’ll go bail. Then papa writes, and says it’s not enough, and the match had best be at an end.”

“And, of course, you enclose a parting line,” Bows put in, eyeing her with scorn, “in which you say you will always regard him as a brother.”

“Of course, and so I shall,” Emily answered. “He’s a most worthy young man, I’m sure. I’ll thank ye hand me the salt. Them filberts is beautiful.”

“There will be no noses pulled, Cos, my boy? I’m sorry you’re baulked,” Bows said.

“Dad, I suppose not,” Costigan replied, rubbing his own nose as if to console it for the loss of another. “What’ll ye do about them letters, and verses, and pomes, Milly darling?—Ye must send ’em back.”

“Wigsby would give a hundred pound for ’em,” Bows said with a sneer, for in the theatre there are always men ready to trade in other people’s feelings as if they were old playbills.

“’Deed, then, he would,” Captain Costigan cried, easily tempted as a child is by the thought of sweets.

“Papa!” said Miss Milly, with sudden firmness. “Ye wouldn’t be for not sending the poor boy his letters back? Them letters and pomes is mine. They were very long, and full of all sorts of nonsense, and Latin, and things I couldn’t understand the half of; indeed I’ve not read ’em all; but we’ll send ’em back to him when the proper time comes.”

And going to a drawer, she took out a number of the County Chronicle and Chatteris Champion in which Pen had written flaming verses celebrating her appearance as Imogen. Like ladies of her profession, she kept favourable notices the way thrifty wives keep good recipes—folded, saved, and ready for future proof. She put by the leaf on which the poem appeared, then wrapped up Pen’s letters, poems, passions, and fancies, and tied them with a piece of string neatly, as she would a parcel of sugar.

Nor was she in the least moved in doing it. The papers, that had cost the boy so many hours—his watchful nights, his hungry hopes, his feverish faith—became, in her hands, a tidy bundle, squared at the corners, trussed for return like groceries.

Afterwards she sat down and made tea with a perfectly placid and contented heart, while ten miles off Pen was yearning after her, and hugging her image to his soul.
