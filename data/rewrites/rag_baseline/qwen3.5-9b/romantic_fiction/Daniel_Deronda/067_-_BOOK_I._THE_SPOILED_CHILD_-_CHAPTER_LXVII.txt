The divine spark within us twists our noblest actions into knots of reluctance, binding our highest aspirations to our most stubborn selves. When Deronda returned from the Abbey, he found waiting for him at the Brompton lodgings a figure he had hoped to avoid: the unwanted father, now firmly installed in their sanctuary. Mirah had deemed it necessary to introduce her father to Deronda, weaving a narrative of their acquaintance that carefully skirted the precipice of truth. She spoke of Ezra's influence and the gentle sympathy that had drawn them together, but she passed lightly over Deronda's tangible aid, omitting entirely the harrowing rescue from drowning. Instead, she framed her safety as a gift from the Meyricks, allowing her father to believe that Deronda's interest stemmed solely from his proximity to these respectable friends. She could not force herself to reveal the full extent of her bond with Deronda; she could not allow the wind of her father's judgment to blow directly upon the fragile architecture of her relationship. Consequently, Lapidoth, driven by his own peculiar motivations, showed little eagerness to probe the details of her flight or her arrival in England. Yet, he displayed a keen, calculating interest in the mere fact that his children possessed a benefactor seemingly elevated in the social hierarchy.

It was Ezra's brother who delivered the news of this new, burdensome addition to their household. "I have grown calm in observing him now," Ezra concluded, his voice steady despite the turmoil beneath. "I strive to believe that my sister's tenderness, and the daily savoring of a peaceful existence, may eventually win him to stand aloof from further temptation. I have instructed her, and she has vowed, to entrust him with no funds. I have persuaded her that he will purchase his own ruin with any wealth he acquires."

Deronda first appeared three days after Lapidoth's arrival. His newly tailored garments were not yet ready, and, wishing to project an image of propriety, he refused to present himself in his old attire. Watching him depart from the window, Lapidoth was struck by a surprising youthfulness—a trait Mirah had omitted, something Deronda had instinctively categorized as impossible for a man engaged in such grave friendships and scholarly pursuits with the somber Ezra. Lapidoth began to speculate that Deronda's primary motivation must be a romantic attachment to Mirah. Such a notion pleased him; a connection to Mirah held far more promise of leniency for her father than a bond with Ezra alone. Lapidoth harbored hopes of securing Deronda's goodwill, of softening any hardened prejudices he might harbor. He conducted himself with studied amiability, striving in every conceivable manner to integrate smoothly into his children's domestic sphere. He immersed himself in Mirah's music, played the role of a docile smoker (though Mrs. Adam forbade it in her drawing room), and took walks in the square with his German pipe, fueled by the tobacco Mirah kindly supplied. Too astute to challenge openly the refusal of financial assistance—something Mirah insisted was a solemn duty owed to her brother—he contented himself with waiting.

On his subsequent visit, Lapidoth, freshly dressed and satisfied with his reflection, joined Ezra in the room. Ezra, performing a rigorous penance, was attempting to endure his father's presence whenever it was imposed. Deronda met this reunion with cold distance; the sight of the man who had blighted the lives of his wife and children provoked in him a repulsion that bordered on physical nausea. Undeterred, Lapidoth requested permission to remain, listening to the reading of documents from an old chest, and even proved useful by assisting in deciphering a difficult German manuscript. This collaboration led him to propose transcribing the document, offering his services alongside copying papers in Roman characters. While Ezra's youthful eyes were already failing, Lapidoth's remained sharp. Deronda accepted the offer, interpreting it as a sign of grace in Lapidoth's willingness to be employable, and noted the gratified expression on Ezra's face. However, Ezra soon interjected, "Let all the writing be done here; I cannot trust the papers out of my sight, lest there be an accident by fire or otherwise." Poor Ezra felt as though he were guarding a convict on temporary leave. Unless he witnessed his father at work, he could not believe in the sincerity of that labor. By this arrangement, Ezra shackled himself to the burden of his father's presence, a weight made agonizing not only by their shared, painful history but also by Lapidoth's restless temperament. As he became accustomed to his situation, Lapidoth shed any lingering awe for his son. In truth, Ezra was imposing a severe constraint upon himself, restricting his attention solely to win Deronda's favor. Like a man trapped in ill-fitting clothing, he sought relief at every opportunity: stepping out to smoke, pacing the floor, collapsing into his chair in silence, yet constantly engaging in a mute language of facial contortions and gestures. If Mirah was present, he reverted to his old habits, chatting with her about their former escapades and companions, recounting quirks and stories, and plotting scenes from the plays he once adapted, convinced he could summon the vitality of his prime at will. This was a mortal torment for Ezra; when Mirah was home, she endeavored to alleviate his suffering by escorting her father downstairs to the parlour and keeping vigil over him there. What duty arises from a single, arduous resolve? Its difficulty lies in the daily, unflinching endurance of consequences that stain the dawn with the prospect of irritation to be swallowed or shame to be borne. And such consequences were being carried by these, as by many other heroic children of an unworthy parent, with the hope, at least for Mirah, that they would stretch onward through the solid fabric of life.

In the meantime, Lapidoth's presence erected a new, intangible barrier between Deronda and Mirah. Each dreaded the contaminating inferences of the other's mind, each misinterpreting the heightened reserve and diffidence of the partner. But clarity did not take long to arrive for Deronda.

As soon as he could, following his brief excursion to the Abbey, Deronda visited Hans Meyrick's rooms. Feeling it a debt of friendship that Hans should immediately understand the reasons for his recent journey and the shifts in intention it had wrought, he arrived to find Hans absent. He was reportedly in the countryside for a few days. Leaving a note, Deronda waited a week, half-expecting a reply. Receiving none, and fearing some capricious mood in the notoriously volatile Hans—whose planned stay at the Abbey he knew had been postponed—Deronda made a second call. He was admitted to the painting-room, where he found his friend in a light coat, sans waistcoat, his long hair still damp from a bath, yet with a face that looked worn and wizened, bearing nothing of the rustic charm one might expect. Hans had picked up his palette and brushes, standing before his easel when Deronda entered, but the entire setup seemed arranged on short notice.

As they clasped hands, Deronda remarked, "You don't look much as if you'd been in the country, old fellow. Was it Cambridge?"

"No," Hans replied curtly, dropping his palette with the air of someone who has accidentally started a charade. Pushing forward a chair for Deronda, he slumped into another, leaning back with his hands behind his head. "I've been to 'I-don't-know-where'—No Man's Land—and a mortally unpleasant country it is."

"You don't mean to say you've been drinking, Hans," Deronda said, seating himself opposite with an anxious scrutiny.

"Nothing so benign. I've been smoking opium. I always intended to try it sometime, to measure how much bliss could be extracted from it; finding myself lately rather devoid of other pleasures, I judged it prudent to seize the chance. But I pledge you my word I shall never touch a cask of that bliss again. It disagrees with my constitution."

"What has been the matter? You were in high spirits when you wrote to me."

"Oh, nothing specific. The world began to look seedy—a sort of cabbage garden with all the heads cut down. A malady of genius, you may be sure," Hans said, crinkling his face into a smile. "And, in fact, I was weary of being virtuous without reward, especially in this stifling London heat."

"Nothing else? No genuine vexation?" Deronda pressed.

Hans shook his head.

"I came to tell you of my own affairs, but I cannot do so with a good grace if you intend to conceal yours."

"Haven't an affair in the world," Hans said flippantly, "except a quarrel with a bric-à-brac man. Besides, since it is the first time in our lives you've spoken to me about your own business, you're only beginning to repay a rather lengthy debt."

Deronda sensed Hans was acting artificially, yet he trusted that the old frankness would return if he offered his own confidence.

"You laughed at the mystery of my journey to Italy, Hans," Deronda began. "It was for an object that touched my happiness at its very roots. I knew nothing of my parents, and I truly went to Genoa to meet my mother. My father died long ago—when I was an infant. My mother was the daughter of an eminent Jew; my father was her cousin. Many things had led me to suspect this origin as almost probable before I departed. I was so prepared for the outcome that I was glad of it—glad to discover myself a Jew."
