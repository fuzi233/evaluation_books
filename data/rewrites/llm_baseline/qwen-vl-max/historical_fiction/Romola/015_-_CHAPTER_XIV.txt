CHAPTER XIV. The Peasants’ Fair.

The moment Tito stepped into the piazza, the crush of bodies and the jumble of sounds—shouts, laughter, music, and sudden crashes—flooded his senses. It reminded him of Nello’s talk about the Fierucoloni, those wild country fairs that drew crowds from the hills and valleys. He pushed through the throng with a strange kind of pleasure in the chaos: the shoves, the cries, the confusion. For a while, it dulled the heavy weight of thought that had settled on him—the dread of wandering alone, searching for some uncertain fortune, perhaps even going to seek Baldassarre again. But here, in this uproar, he could forget, just for a time.

At each entrance, people surged into the square, their movements matched by paper lanterns swaying above them like ghosts on poles. A slow, rhythmic chant rose above the din, a steady thread beneath the storm of noise—children’s jeers, drumbeats, bells ringing sharply, and every now and then, a lantern shattered by a stone thrown in reckless play. A scream followed, then more shouting. Yet at the edges of the crowd, others kept the night’s observance with more dignity. Ragged men with sharp eyes called out, inviting villagers to gamble fairly; two figures in masks, balanced on stilts, danced with stolen lanterns, flickering like fireflies across the dark. Near the loggia of the Foundling Hospital (Spedale degl’ Innocenti), a vendor sold hot *berlingozzi*, a favorite flour-based treat, while another man stood atop a barrel, advertising pills from Salerno—guaranteed to stop toothache and drownings alike. Not far away, a tumbler performed tricks on a small platform, and a group of apprentices, bored with throwing stones, played a quiet game of *bocce* near the narrow gate of Via de’ Febbrai.

Tito, forced to weave through gaps in the press, found himself suddenly beside a procession of barefoot peasant women. Their sun-bronzed faces were worn, their clothes ragged and faded, stitched from old fabrics, so odd and dusty they seemed like relics from a forgotten age. To city folk, they looked like ancestors returning from a pilgrimage begun long before memory. These were the hardy women of Pistoia’s mountains, carrying the year’s labor in bundles of yarn on their backs, hearts full of faint hope and deeper fear—hope for mercy, fear of harm—both laid at the feet of the Blessed Virgin, whose miraculous image, said to have been painted by angels, would be unveiled tonight, on the Eve of her Nativity, to let its power flow unobstructed.

Then, swept sideways by the current of the crowd, Tito reached the quieter edge where vendors had set up shop. Among them was Bratti, whom Tito recognized at once. He leaned against a pillar, mouth pursed in disdain, eyes scanning the passersby with cold superiority. His hand rested firmly on a serge cloth covering a basket slung before him. Tito was surprised—Bratti never acted so aloof. As two women approached curiously, Bratti pulled the cloth tighter and turned away.

“Before I tell you what’s inside,” he said, “I must know if you’re willing to buy. I can’t show such goods to every fly that lands and expects nothing. My wares are too fine for that. Besides, I’ve only two left—and I won me not to soil them. With the pestilence whispered about, these may soon be worth their weight in gold. No, no—*andate con Dio*.”

The women exchanged glances.

“And how much?” asked one.

“Not within your purse, buona donna,” Bratti replied, voice laced with pitying scorn. “I suggest you trust Messer Domeneddio and the saints. Poor folks do no better.”

“Not so poor!” snapped the second woman, pulling out her purse. “Come now—what do you say to a grosso?”

“Twenty-one quattrini,” Bratti said coolly. “But not from me—I haven’t that change.”

“Two, then?” she insisted, growing impatient, while her companion watched with envy.

After more haggling, Bratti finally relented.

“Since you’ve set your mind on it,” he said slowly, lifting the cover, “I’d hate to bring you misfortune. Maestro Gabbadeo used to say, when a woman wants something and doesn’t get it, she’s more at risk of the plague than ever. Ecco! I have only two left—and let me tell you, the other is on Maestro Gabbadeo’s finger. He’s gone to Bologna—a wise doctor, the best at any door.”

The items were two crude iron rings, shaped like ancient Roman ones, often dug from the earth. Rust clung to them, and their mystery seemed real enough that both women paid without complaint. One, lacking coins, bartered a bundle of yarn and walked off with her prize, triumphant. Bratti covered his basket—now filled with odds and ends, likely traded under similar conditions—and moved from his post, colliding with Tito, who wished he’d had time to slip away unnoticed.

“By San Giovanni’s head!” Bratti exclaimed, pulling Tito back to the pillar. “This is luck! I was talking of you this morning, Messer Greco. Said, ‘He’s risen among the signori now.’ And I’m glad—though I helped lift him there. But I rarely see him anymore. He’s not lying on the stones, as he once did. But it’s your luck, not mine—except for a small token, to repay me for my trouble.”

“You speak in riddles, Bratti,” Tito said. “I don me sharpen my mind like you do over iron rings. Be plain.”

“By the Holy Gospels! That bargain was easy. If a Hebrew gets thirty-two percent, I expect a Christian may take a little more. I could’ve gotten twice the money and twice the yarn—if I hadn’t borne conscience. But speaking of rings—your ring, the one on your finger—that I can get you a buyer for. A good one. A buyer with deep pockets.”

“Truly?” Tito asked, staring at his ring.

“A Genoese heading straight to Hungary, as I understand. Came into my shop, looking for old things—things I didn’t know the value of. He thought I had a pumpkin on my shoulders. He’d been hunting every shop in Florence. He wore a ring—not like yours, but similar. When we talked of rings, I said, ‘I know a young man—my close friend—who has one just like it.’ He said, ‘Who is he? Tell him I’ll give him fair price.’ I meant to go to Nello’s tomorrow to find you—but now, since I’ve met you, it’s fate. You’re not the sort to watch the Arno turn to broth and stand by.”

Tito had listened closely, though his thoughts raced. Why keep the ring? It had been sentiment, mere fancy, that stopped him from selling it with the others. Had he done so, he might have avoided Fra Luca’s recognition. Yes, it had once belonged to Baldassarre, placed on his own finger when his hand grew large enough. But what real good did it do anyone to cling to such superstitions? The ring had led to recognition. And Tito began to resent recognition—it was the past claiming him. This foreigner’s offer, if genuine, was a chance to rid himself of the ring without chasing a buyer.

“You speak wisely, Bratti,” Tito said. “I’ll hear what the Genoese offers. When and where shall I meet him?”

“Tomorrow, three hours after sunrise—at my shop. And if your wit is as sharp as I’ve always thought, Messer Greco, ask a high price. He cares not for money. I believe he buys for someone else—perhaps a great lord.”

“It is well,” Tito said. “I’ll come, if nothing prevents.”

“And you’ll treat me fairly, for old friendship’s sake,” Bratti added. “No need to fix the sum now. Seems like a thousand years till I leave this piazza. A fair is dull, even wicked, when you’ve no goods to sell.”

Tito nodded quickly and moved away. The crowd pushed him forward, then back, helpless to control his path. In this way, he drifted to the far end of the piazza, opposite the church, where a spectacle drew the crowd’s attention. Laughter burst out, interrupting a speaker’s rambling monologue—sometimes slow and solemn, sometimes frantic and silly. A girl was shoved into the center, unwillingly; another, bold and giggling, fought her way in with elbows. The light was dim—stars above, lanterns below, casting shadows and blurring shapes. But in this alcove, a stronger glow lit the scene. Spectators’ heads appeared in silhouette as Tito was pressed closer. Above them loomed a man wearing a white mitre adorned with yellow cabalistic symbols.

“Behold, my children!” he declared. “Behold your chance! Do not neglect the holy sacrament of matrimony—for only a white quattrino! The cheapest marriage ever offered, and dissolvable by special bull at any man’s will. Behold the bull!” He held up a parchment with seals attached. “Behold the indulgence granted by His Holiness Alexander the Sixth, newly elected for his piety, who intends to purify the Church—beginning by ending priestly monopolies on marriage. Spit once, my sons, and pay a white quattrino! That is all. The quattrino is the only difference the Holy Father allows between us and the clergy—who spit and pay nothing!”

Tito thought he knew the voice—sharp, nasal, familiar. But the face was hidden in shadow. Stepping nearer, he saw an altar-like table draped in red, stitched with golden symbols. Half-a-dozen candles burned behind it, and scattered across it lay conjuring tools, a large open book, and at one corner, a monkey tied to a ring, holding a taper that wobbled constantly. An impish boy in a surplice cuffed the monkey and adjusted the candle. The man wore a surplice and a chasuble marked with the zodiac. Now Tito was certain: it was Maestro Vaiano, the mountebank from whom he had rescued Tessa. Little Tessa—had she come with the peasants?

“Come, my maidens!” the conjuror shouted. “Now is the time for the pretty, who may have many chances, and for the plain, who have few. Marriage—hot, eaten, and done with as easily as *berlingozzi*! And see!” He raised a cluster of tiny bags. “To every bride, a *Breve*—a secret inside. The secret alone is worth the price. But I won’t tell you what it is—that makes it double-secret. Hang it round your neck, never look at it. Perhaps that’s best—you’ll see things you don’t expect. Though if you open it, you may break your leg, *è vero*, but you’ll know a secret! Something no one else knows! And mark—I give you the *Breve*, I don’t sell it—as many holy men do. The quattrino is for the marriage. The *Breve* is free. *Orsù, giovanetti*, come like dutiful sons of the Church and buy the Indulgence of His Holiness Alexander the Sixth!”

The crowd loved it. The *fierucola* was small, so the townspeople accepted jokes less indecent than those at carnival. The women, especially, were eager for the *Brevi*. Several couples had already undergone the ceremony—mock vows, grimaces, the monkey’s antics, even spitting—each drawing laughter. Now a young man in a red cap and loose tunic pushed forward, holding a plump brunette in ragged dress, her arms and legs exposed in a vivid contrast.

“Fetter us quickly, Maestro!” he said. “I must take my bride home and paint her by lantern-light.”

“Ha! Mariotto, my son,” the conjuror began, “I commend your pious haste…”

But a sudden screech from behind warned of trouble. The monkey, annoyed by the boy’s cuffing, leapt violently when the taper tilted. The knot loosened, and he jumped to the other end of the table, growling fiercely. The boy grabbed a wand. The monkey sprang onto a tall woman’s head, dropping the taper, and chattered wildly. Panic broke out—many feared the monkey carried hidden mischief. The conjuror himself flinched, worried for his pet. In the struggle to catch the string, Tito slipped out of the circle. Not wanting to fight his way back, he let the crowd push him toward the church of the Nunziata, slipping in among the worshippers.

Inside, the light dazzled—golden, thick, almost tangible after the pale glow outside. For a moment, Tito saw nothing clearly. The incense mingled with breath, creating a thin yellow haze. To the peasant women, unused to such brilliance—whose skies were often blocked by mountains—this was divine. The choir’s chant was a soothing balm after the pandemonium outside. Slowly, the scene resolved. On the left, in a chapel wreathed with silver lamps, the miraculous fresco of the Annunciation was unveiled. From Tito’s angle, it seemed darkened by the surrounding radiance. The church was packed with women—some kneeling, some standing. Mountain dwellers, coarse-skinned and dressed in grime, stood beside valley folk, softer-faced, draped in white or red. Above them, the walls and ceiling teemed with votive wax figures—effigies of popes, emperors, kings, condottieri, Florentine lords in black silk, even Muslims, all in their living attire. Some were cracked and blackened by time, others fresh and bright—exact duplicates of the living. Arms, legs, and body parts were wedged in among them, except where images had been removed in disgrace or fallen—like Lorenzo’s six months earlier. It was a resurrection of the dead, mirrored in the crowd below—darkness and light, decay and renewal.

Tito scanned the crowd, searching. He had thought of Tessa. The white hoods made him wonder if he might find her beneath one. Better than seeing Romola’s changed eyes. But he found no trace. Disheartened, he turned to leave—when, just by the doorway, he saw her. Only two yards away. She knelt, back against the wall, behind a group of women waiting to approach the sacred image. Her head hung slightly, eyes half-closed, lost in weariness. Her blue eyes drifted toward an altar-piece showing Archangel Michael—young, armored, surrounded by saints. In her right hand, she held a bunch of cocoons, dangling listlessly. Her cheek was pale, lips pressed together in a pout, eyelids fluttering. She looked like a large, sweet child asleep.

Tito felt an overwhelming urge to go to her—to feel her innocent trust, her gentle chatter. This child, without judgment, without suspicion, offered a refuge from the isolation he feared. He glanced around—Monna Ghita was nowhere near. Quietly, he knelt beside her and whispered, “Tessa!”

She barely stirred, as if a soft breeze touched her. She turned, saw his face—closer than the Archangel’s, more beautiful, more real. She smiled, silent and happy. That closeness filled her world.

“My little Tessa,” he said. “You look very tired. How long have you been here?”

She paused, gathering her thoughts.

“I’m very hungry.”

“Come then. Come with me.”

He lifted her gently and led her out under the cloisters—open now, untouched by Andrea del Sarto’s frescoes.

“How are you alone, and so hungry, Tessa?”

“The Madre is ill. Bad pains in her legs. She sent me with these cocoons to the Santissima Nunziata—they’re wonderful, see?” She held up the bundle, arranged neatly on a stem. “She saved them to bring herself, but couldn’t. So she sent me—she thinks the Holy Madonna will take away her pain. And someone took my bag with bread and chestnuts. The crowd pushed me back. I was scared. I couldn’t get near the Madonna to give the cocoons to the Padre—but I must. Oh, I must.”

“Yes, my little Tessa,” Tito said softly. “You shall. But first, let me give you some *berlingozzi*. There’s a stall nearby.”

“Where did you come from?” she asked, confused. “I thought you’d never come back. You never came to the Mercato for milk. I prayed Aves, hoping they’d bring you back—but I stopped. They didn’t.”

“You see,” Tito said, “I come when you need someone to care for you. Perhaps the Aves brought me—it just took a long time. But what will you do if you’re here alone? Where will you go?”

“Oh,” she said, “I’ll stay and sleep in the church. Many do. I did once, with my mother. The *patrigno* is coming with the mules in the morning.”
