Few things stir the soul more deeply than parting—yet we shall not dwell upon the ache in Harry Warrington’s heart as he bids farewell to his brother and friends. Such farewells are not rare; they are the common lot of men caught in the currents of duty and war. Was Mr. Wolfe not also leaving behind a mother to kiss, and a sweetheart to embrace one last time? His father, brave and gone before him, had fallen during the great campaign at Louisbourg. And what of Admiral Holmes, whose fleet—the Somerset, the Terrible, the Northumberland, the Royal William, the Trident, the Diana, the Seahorse—sailed westward under his flag on the Dublin? He too must have stood ashore, heart heavy, saying goodbye to his wife and daughters. Admiral Saunders, who followed soon after, was no less touched by sorrow. These were men of steel and courage, yet their hearts beat like any other man’s when it came to love and loss.

So it was with William and his crew, sailing through the churning sea, while Black-eyed Susan watched from the shore until the ship vanished into the golden haze of sunset. It slipped into the West, swallowed by twilight. The night fell over the ocean like a shroud. They were gone—but their thoughts remained, clinging to those left behind.

In silence, each man carried a burden of tender emotion. How many prayers rose that night—on deck beneath the stars, in homes where mothers knelt beside empty beds, children huddled close, and lovers whispered hopes into the dark. On land and sea, grief and hope mingled in the same breath.

The next morning dawned. Life resumed its rhythm. George stayed in Portsmouth with Harry for a few days, waiting for wind and tide. He shook hands with Mr. Wolfe, looked long at his pale, resolute face, and saw the ships vanish beyond the horizon amid the clangor of bells and the thunder of cannon. Then, alone again, George returned home—to a life that, though full of purpose, felt strangely selfish now. He passed by Harry’s old room, seeing half-burnt papers still smoldering in the grate—a quiet echo of absence.

Soon he was back in Dean Street, walking softly toward the firelight where Theo waited, wrapped in the warmth of his return. She was radiant—so happy she almost felt ashamed. How could she be joyful when poor Hetty sat in silent sorrow? “Don’t,” Theo murmured, her voice barely audible, perhaps in a moment of dimness between the flickering flames. “I ought to be wretched. If I’m not, it’s very wicked.” One could feel her gentle guilt, her soft-hearted remorse.

But then servants entered with supper and light. The family gathered. Conversation flowed freely. The destination of the fleet was known: Canada. With such strength and under an officer like Wolfe, the French would be met with force and resolve. Though Wolfe looked ill, his spirit was strong. The nation placed its trust in him. After dinner, as custom dictated, Mr. Lambert led the household in prayer—George among them—asking blessings on all travelers. Theo and her sister knelt together, their voices joining in quiet supplication.

And so, as the ship sailed farther into the West, love followed it across the miles. Night passed. The sun rose. A few days later, life settled back into routine. George Warrington, celebrated playwright, turned once more to his work.

His tragedy *Carpezan* had run for nearly thirty nights, drawing crowds and praise. Yet soon others took the stage. Some wondered how audiences could tire of such a masterpiece—how actors could don new costumes, recite new lines. But George, ever skeptical, accepted the shift with calm. He collected his earnings—hundred pounds from Dodsley, two hundred more from the theatre manager—and found satisfaction in the acclaim. Critics praised him, friends admired his talent. He began drafting another play, hoping to surpass his first triumph.

Months passed in this peaceful pursuit. Happy months—perhaps the happiest of youth. Love was spoken and answered. Eyes sparkled with joy every day. Lips never tired of whispering secrets. Each “Good night” held the promise of tomorrow. Hearts overflowed, generous to all. Days ended in glad prayers, mornings broke with hopeful dreams. Doubt seemed cowardly, misfortune impossible, poverty merely a test of devotion.

Theo’s elders, recalling their own youthful joys, watched quietly, content to witness the unfolding of this sweet drama.

In one of his letters to his wife, written during a brief absence, George confessed he had walked past the windows of the old house in Dean Street, wondering who now sat in the room where he and Theo had once shared so much. And through his letters to Harry, we learn how time unfolded for those left behind.

“From the old window opposite Bedford Gardens, 20th August 1759.”

Why do you return to rocks and cold shores, when you might have won laurels in Germany? Kingsley’s regiment returns covered in glory, like Jack-a-Green on May Day. Our six regiments did wonders. Our cavalry could have done more—had my Lord George Sackville allowed them. When Prince Ferdinand shouted “Charge!”, his lordship either didn’t hear or couldn’t translate the German word for “Forward.” So we defeated the French, but did not crush them as we might have, had Lord Granby or even Mr. Warrington commanded. Now he’s back in town, demanding a Court-Martial. He stood tall in victory, and in defeat, he remains stubbornly proud—almost admirable in his arrogance. He looks as if he envies poor Byng, and the failure to shoot him is a slight against him.

The Duke has been warned—he must prepare for departure from this world of glory and ruin. A stroke has struck him. Death has peeped through his door, whispering, “I will call again.” Tyrant though he was, he has shown nobility in downfall. No king ever had a truer servant than our sovereign has in his son. Why do I always sympathize with the losing side? Why do I wish to rebel against the victors?

I’ve heard your famous Mr. P---- in Parliament twice or thrice. I loathe his grandiloquence. I wish some small David would bring down this swollen Goliath. His speech is always posed, theatrical. I prefer Barry’s style, though the other is more commanding.

*Pocahontas* progresses well. Barry enjoys playing Captain Smith. Though he insists on red coat and blue facings, I’d rather dress him like one of Queen Elizabeth’s gentlemen at Hampton Court—ruff, square beard, square shoes. “And Pocahontas—would you like her tattooed?” Uncle Lambert asked. Hagan, cast as the warrior who loves her and saves the captain out of honor, should be a hit. A curious fellow, Hagan—full of stage talk and melodrama, yet honest and brave. He’s furious about being cast as Sir O’Brallaghan in Macklin’s farce *Love A-la-mode*. He says he wonches to disgrace his tongue with imitations of that rascal brogue. As if anyone needed imitation when he has such a natural twang!

Shall I tell you something? Shall I hide it? Hurt your feelings? Stir jealousy? Know this: though *Carpezan* is long dead, cousin Maria still comes to the playhouse. Tom Spencer sees her every night—in the gallery. She comes especially when Hagan performs. Quick, Burroughs! Get Mr. Warrington’s boots and portmanteau! Order a chaise and four to Portsmouth immediately! That letter I burned at breakfast—I can confess now, since the cat has run so far—was from cousin M., warning me not to speak of her. But I can’t help whispering: Maria is consoling herself as fast as she can. Should I spoil the fun? Tell her brother? Is it any concern of mine? What have the Esmonds done for us but win our money at cards? Yet I admire our noble cousin. He might have been good—if only he’d been given the chance. He’s been led astray, and it may be too late to save him.

O blessed farmers! Our Virginia was dull, but thank God we were raised there. We were little slaves, but not slaves to vice, gambling, or bad company. It wasn’t until Harry left home that he fell among thieves—great thieves, who ambushed him on English roads. I don’t blame you for being robbed. You were the unlucky one. But now you go westward to make your name, to slay French dragons, to rise as a commander. Our mother will speak proudly of her son—the Captain, the Colonel, the General—and have his portrait painted with stars and epaulets. While I remain a mere poetaster—or, if fortune smiles, a placeman with a little box at Richmond or Kew, and a dozen little picaninnies bobbing curtseys at the garden gate when their uncle the General rides up on his great charger, his aide-de-camp’s pockets filled with gingerbread.

It is your destiny to wield Mars’s sword. Mine is peace: a quiet home, a small library, and someone sweet—dulce ridentem, dulce loquentem—sitting by the fire as I write. I am so content with this dream, so utterly happy, that I fear speaking of it—even to you, dearest Hal—lest it slip away.

What is ambition to me, with this certainty? What do I care for war, when peace smiles so near?

Mynheer Van den Bosch, our mother’s friend, has returned from Holland, having found kinfolk there. Miss—intended by maternal design to be your bride—has spent six months at Kensington School and is now emerging with a hundred accomplishments, polished into a perfect lady. Her father brought her to Dean Street to curtsy—and what a graceful curtsy it was! Though barely seventeen, she moves with the ease of a dowager of sixty. She conversed with Aunt Lambert as an equal, treated the girls like children—enraging Hetty and amusing Theo. She discussed politics with the General, gossip with the ladies, fashion and opera with perfect ease. Only a few slips betrayed her origins. You might have thought Miss Lydia was born in Mayfair.

She says she’ll live at the Court end of town and has no patience for her father, who lodges in Monument Yard. For lovers of brown beauty, she is a lovely mignonne. But my taste, dear brother, as you know…

Here the letter continues with pages of poetic effusion, raptures, and verses—too personal, too tender, for the editor to reproduce. Let us leave them unsaid.

Gentlemen and ladies of a certain age may recall their own such moments—when words spilled forth like spring rain, when love was sung in every line, when youth bloomed and birds sang without pause. The branches may now be bare, the leaves fallen. But let us remember the vernal time.

And you, young readers—whose April or May has not yet begun—need not linger here. When your season comes, nature will awaken your hearts. Your flowers will bloom, and your songs will rise.
