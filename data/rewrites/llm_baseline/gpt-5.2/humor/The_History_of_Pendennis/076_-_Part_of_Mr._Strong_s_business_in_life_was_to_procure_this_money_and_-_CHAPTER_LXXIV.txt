The train whisked Arthur Pendennis down to Tunbridge with the brisk, unfeeling punctuality of a kitchen spit turned by machinery: click, click, and your joint is done whether your conscience is cooked through or raw at the bone. He sat with his carpet-bag at his feet like a modest hamper of provisions, and in the brief miles between stations contrived to season his whole youth with remorse, tasting it as a too-sensitive diner tastes a sauce and finds the salt his own hand shook in.

“Here,” thought he, as the wheels sang their iron song over the rails—clack-clack, clack-clack, a kind of harsh minstrelry that drowned the small human voice within—“here is the end of my romances and airs. Whether I am obstinate or yielding, I blunder into the same dish at last: disappointment served hot.”

He went over it all as a careful food-historian might turn the pages of an old household book, seeing how one poor substitution in a receipt changes the entire flavour of a family. If he had married Laura because his mother implored, would Laura have been the angel his mother saw? No; angels, like truffles, can’t be forced upon a man’s palate by another’s enthusiasm. He had refused one woman’s fortune on scruple, accepted another’s on guarantee; and now Fate, that capricious cook, had whipped away the dowry and left him with the wife—like taking away the sugar after the pudding is baked and bidding you call it a delicacy.

His uncle, honest old man, could swallow any provenance so long as it was coined and clinked; could not even comprehend the nausea Arthur felt at the thought of dining off money with a taint upon it. And Arthur, by insisting on clean bread, contrived only to starve everybody’s comfort. He dissatisfied all, and worst of all himself. What prospect was there for that poor little frivolous girl who was to take his obscure name and share his uncertain fortune? He had not even ambition to keep him warm—no stout stew of self-esteem to sustain him when affection ran thin.

He looked at his fellow-travellers, as one looks round a noisy taproom in which the talk is hearty but the air heavy. Opposite sat a foreign priest, head bent over his breviary as over a cherished family recipe written in a language that refuses to be made common—eyes never lifting except when darkness in a tunnel made reading impossible. Beside him an old gentleman glowered over his newspaper with the fixed rancour of a man whose breakfast has been spoiled daily for thirty years by the same disagreeable cook. Their faiths, their hates, their certainties—each sounded, in the carriage’s small acoustic world, like a different instrument: the priest’s hush; the newspaper’s crackle; the old man’s snort.

“Would you like to look at the paper, sir?” asked a stout gentleman—thrusting it out with a zeal that suggested he was serving a dish he found delicious and assumed must delight the whole table. The journal blazed with an attack upon the “black-coated order” across from them, and Arthur, who could as soon have judged a delicate consommé while a brass band practised in the dining-room, took it politely and read nothing.

“And yet,” he thought, letting the print swim before him unheard, “would I take either of those men’s creed, with all its consequences? They have no doubts. They march on with their loads of logic like mules with panniers. But I—” The clatter of the carriage seemed to argue with him. “Ah me! a man must bear his own burden, cook his own faith, and eat it whether it agrees with him or not. To what mortal ear could I tell all—even if I wished? Who could taste another’s bitterness and call it by the right name? There are flavours no language holds: a lost dialect on the tongue, an old nursery word for comfort that no polished lexicon can translate.”

He felt, in that moment, how much of life is altered by accidents as small as a grain of sand in the mill-stone, or a pebble flung in a stream. A chance word in youth; a terror; a mischance; some remembered tone of a parent’s voice that governs a man thereafter as surely as a kitchen bell. Who can weigh the circumstances and temptations that season our good and evil account—save One?

“Here it ends,” he told himself; “this day or to-morrow winds up the account of my youth.” And his head fell, not in theatrical despair, but in a tired humility; the heart in him prostrated itself before that Throne where wisdom sits with pity. The train’s noise, for once, seemed less an insult than a penance—industrial din that could not drown prayer, only force it inward.

“What matter fame or poverty,” he thought. “If I marry the woman I have chosen, may I have strength to be true to her and make her happy. If I have children—God teach me to speak truth among them, and leave them an honest name.” He saw, with a sudden clearness, that there were no splendours for his marriage; and perhaps none were deserved. Let the feast be plain if it be wholesome.

At Tunbridge the train stopped with a jerk; the newspaper went back to its owner; the priest still bowed to his book; and Arthur, carpet-bag in hand, stepped down as briskly as a man can who carries a whole larder of doubts inside him. He resolved to face his fortune as a cook faces a new kitchen: tie the apron, sharpen the knives, and do the work.

A fly took him to Lady Clavering’s house. The streets had their own small soundscape: hooves on hard road, wheels hissing over gravel, distant voices, a dog’s bark—domestic noises that soothed by their very ordinariness, like hearing one’s own kettle sing at home after too much railway clamor. In the jolting ride Arthur composed the little speech he meant for Blanche—an honest, plain, best-meaning dish.

“Blanche,” it ran, “I cannot understand your last letter. If my proposal is acceptable, I am ready. I forgo worldly advantages I cannot take without dishonour. If you doubt my affection, here I am: send for Smirke; let us be married at once; and with all my heart I purpose to keep my vow, cherish you through life, be true and loving.”

With this moral roulade prepared, he sprang from the fly to the hall-door. A servant met him whom he did not know, and stared at the carpet-bag as if it were a suspicious parcel from a dubious market-stall.

“Her Ladyship’s not at home, sir,” the man said, making no move to relieve Arthur of his burden.

“I am Mr. Pendennis,” Arthur answered. “Where is Lightfoot?”

“Lightfoot is gone,” said the man. “My Lady is out, and my orders was—”

Arthur, whose ear caught what his eye had not yet seen, heard Miss Amory’s voice in the drawing-room—warbling like a caged bird convinced of its own nightingale-hood. “Take the bag to a dressing-room,” he said; and, passing the porter as if passing an underling at a kitchen door, he walked straight toward the sound.

The door opened on music—melodious, passionate, melancholy, well-sauced, a little too richly spiced for the midday. Blanche sat at the piano singing with all her might and fascinations, the notes curling out into the room like steam from a pot meant to intoxicate. Master Clavering lay asleep on the sofa, as indifferent to that expensive noise as a well-fed cat to poetry. But near Blanche sat a gentleman utterly enraptured, as if each note were a syllable in the only language he truly understood.

As Arthur entered, the gentleman started up with a “Hullo!” Blanche stopped with a little shriek, and Frank Clavering woke as if an unexpected dish had been clapped under his nose. Arthur advanced and said, with the calm incredulity of a man who has already tasted betrayal and finds it now in a new flavour, “What, Foker! How do you do, Foker?”

There, by Miss Amory’s side, was a purple-leather box—twin to the one Arthur had seen in Harry’s hand three days before at a jeweller’s in Waterloo Place. It was open; and on its white satin cushion lay a bracelet—a magnificent serpentine thing, ruby-headed and diamond-tailed, glittering like a sweetmeat too bright to be wholesome.

“How de-do, Pendennis?” said Foker, with the air of a boy caught stealing jam and uncertain whether to blush or fight.

Blanche made agitated movements—shoulders, hands, eyes all fluttering like napkins in a draught. She whipped her handkerchief over the bracelet as if it were a scandalous pâté, and came forward with a trembling hand to greet Arthur.

“How is dearest Laura?” she asked; and there was something in the question like vinegar dashed into milk.

Foker, in his profound mourning for the lost possibility of Blanche-and-Pendennis, looked at Arthur with a piteous bewilderment which no pen need paint; and Frank Clavering, after one glance at the tableau, had time only to cry, “Here’s a jolly go!” before he vanished sniggering—like a boy escaping the pantry after seeing the cook with her hands in the pie.

Arthur had held himself in so far; but the sight of that bracelet, that box, that devout, blushing Harry—these things mixed into a draught too absurdly potent. He burst into laughter—wild, loud, irrepressible—so that Blanche, who could face tragedy like a practiced actress, was frightened by comedy, which is less manageable.

“And this was the secret, was it?” he cried. “Don’t blush and turn away, Foker, my boy. Why, man, you are a pattern of fidelity. Could I stand between Blanche and such constancy—could I stand between Miss Amory and fifteen thousand a year?”

“It is not that, Mr. Pendennis,” Blanche said, drawing herself up with a dignity that smelt faintly of pomade and performance. “It is not money, it is not rank, it is not gold that moves me; but it is constancy, it is fidelity, it is a whole trustful loving heart offered to me, that I treasure—yes, that I treasure!”

She reached again for her handkerchief, recollecting what lay beneath it, and checked herself with a little start, like a hostess who remembers she has hidden the best dish for later company and must not reveal it too soon.

“I do not disown—I do not disguise—my life is above disguise—that I once thought I loved you; yes, thought I was beloved by you, I own. How I clung to that faith! How I strove, I prayed, I longed to believe it! But your conduct always—your own words so cold, so heartless, so unkind—have undeceived me. You trifled with the heart of the poor maiden! You flung me back with scorn the troth which I had plighted! I have explained all—all to Mr. Foker.”

“That you have,” said Foker, with a devotion that would have basted a roast.

“What, all?” Arthur asked, and looked at Blanche with a meaning glance—as if sniffing a sauce and detecting an ingredient not named.

“It is I am in fault, is it?” Arthur continued. “Well, well, Blanche, be it so. I won’t appeal against your sentence. I came down looking to very different things, Heaven knows, and with a heart truly and kindly disposed towards you. I hope you may be happy with another, as it was my wish to make you so; and I hope my honest old friend here will have a wife worthy of his loyalty, constancy, and affection. Indeed they deserve the regard of any woman—even Miss Blanche Amory. Shake hands, Harry; don’t look askance at me. Has anybody told you that I was false and heartless?”

“I think you’re a—” Foker began, swelling like a kettle about to boil over.

“Henry, not a word!” Blanche interposed, with saintly command. “I pray you let there be forgiveness!”

“You’re an angel, by Jove, you’re an angel!” cried Foker; whereupon Blanche lifted her eyes seraphically toward the chandelier, as if light itself owed her applause.

“In spite of what has passed,” she continued, “for the sake of what has passed, I must always regard Arthur as a brother. We have known each other years; we have trodden the same fields and plucked the same flowers together. Arthur! Henry! I beseech you, take hands and be friends! Forgive you!—I forgive you, Arthur, with my heart I do. Should I not do so for making me so happy?”

“There is only one person of us three whom I pity, Blanche,” Arthur said gravely; and there was no laughter left in his voice now. “And I say again, I hope you will make this good fellow—this honest and loyal creature—happy.”

“Happy! O Heavens!” cried Harry. He could not speak; his happiness broke out at his eyes like broth boiling over. “She don’t know—she can’t know how fond I am of her; and—and who am I? a poor little beggar, and she takes me up and says she’ll try and I—I—love me. I ain’t worthy of so much happiness. Give us your hand, old boy. Since she forgives you after your heartless conduct, and says she loves you, I’ll make you welcome. I tell you I’ll love everybody who loves her. By—, if she tells me to kiss the ground I’ll kiss it. Tell me to kiss the ground! I say, tell me. I love you so. You see I love you so.”

Blanche looked upward again, her gentle bosom heaving with the exact rhythm of a practiced aria. She held out one hand as if to bless Harry, and then royally permitted him to kiss it. With the other she took up the handkerchief and hid her own eyes, as if modesty were a garnish she must add at the last moment.

“I swear that is a villain who deceives such a loving creature as that,” Arthur said.

Blanche laid down the handkerchief and rested her second hand softly on Foker’s head—his head bent down kissing and weeping over hand number one.

“Foolish boy?” she murmured. “It shall be loved as it deserves. Who could help loving such a silly creature!”

At that moment Frank Clavering broke in briskly, like a waiter tired of sentiment when there’s a bill to settle.

“I say, Pendennis!”

“Well, Frank!”

“The man wants to be paid and go back. He’s had some beer.”

“I’ll go back with him,” Arthur said, seizing the excuse as one seizes a door out of a stifling room. “Good-bye, Blanche. God bless you, Foker, old friend. You know neither of you want me here.”

He longed to be off that instant; but Blanche stopped him with a quiet authority.

“Stay—I must say one word to you. One word in private, if you please. You can trust us together, can’t you, Henry?”

The way she said “Henry”—as if the name were a sweet she placed on his tongue—ravished him.

“Trust you!” cried Foker. “Oh, who wouldn’t trust you! Come along, Franky, my boy.”

“Let’s have a cigar,” said Frank as they went into the hall.

“She don’t like it,” Harry murmured.

“Law bless you—she don’t mind. Pendennis used to smoke regular,” Frank returned, with the candour of youth.

Left alone, Blanche’s face settled into a calm so complete it was almost a different complexion.

“It was but a short word I had to say,” she began. “You never loved me, Mr. Pendennis.”

“I told you how much,” Arthur answered. “I never deceived you.”

“I suppose you will go back and marry Laura,” Blanche continued.

“Was that what you had to say?” Arthur asked.

“You are going to her this very night. There is no denying it. You never cared for me.”

“And you?” Arthur said, and waited.

“Et vous?” she answered, and then with a small shrug, as if confession were only another costume: “Et moi, c’est different. I have been spoilt early. I cannot live out of the world—out of excitement. I could have done so, but it is too late. If I cannot have emotions, I must have the world. You would offer me neither one nor the other. You are blasé in everything—even in ambition. You had a career before you, and you would not take it. You give it up!—for what? for une bêtise, for an absurd scruple. Why would you not have that seat, and be such a puritain? Why should you refuse what is mine by right—by right, entendez-vous?”

“You know all, then?” Arthur asked.

“Only within a month. But I have suspected ever since Baymouth—n’importe since when. It is not too late. He is as if he had never been; and there is a position in the world before you yet. Why not sit in Parliament, exert your talent, and give a place in the world to yourself—to your wife? I take celui-là. Il est bon. Il est riche. Il est—vous le connaissez autant que moi enfin. Think you that I would not prefer un homme qui fera parler de moi? If the secret appears I am rich a millions. How does it affect me? It is not my fault. It will never appear.”

“You will tell Harry everything, won’t you?” Arthur said.

“Je comprends. Vous refusez,” Blanche answered, and for a flash the calm cracked, showing a savage edge beneath—like a spoon scraping the bottom of a pan. “I will tell Harry at my own time—when we are married. You will not betray me, will you? You, having a defenceless girl’s secret, will not turn upon her and use it? S’il me plaît de le cacher, mon secret—pourquoi le donnerai-je? Je l’aime, mon pauvre père, voyez-vous? I would rather live with that man than with you fades intriguers of the world. I must have emotions—il m’en donne. Il m’écrit. Il écrit très-bien, voyez-vous—comme un pirate—comme un Bohémien—comme un homme. But for this I would have said to my mother—Ma mère! quittons ce lâche mari, cette lâche société—retournons à mon père.”

“The pirate would have wearied you like the rest,” Arthur said, with a sadness that had no heat in it.

“Eh! Il me faut des émotions,” Blanche returned, as if the phrase were her only native word—her true dialect amid all the borrowed flourishes.

Arthur, in that brief private colloquy, knew more of Blanche than he had learned in years—though he also imagined more than existed. For she could not carry any feeling to its full honest end: her enthusiasms were like show-dishes—glittering, ingeniously arranged, flaring for an instant under the dining-room lamps, and then cooling into something tasteless the moment the next course arrived. Her love, her grief, her hatred, her taste—all were a kind of theatrical cookery, made to look and sound delicious rather than to nourish anyone at the table.
