Newland Archer ascended the stairs to his study, the movement deliberate, a physical manifestation of the weight settling upon him. The room waited for him, preserved in a state of suspended animation by a vigilant hand that tended the fire and trimmed the lamp. Rows of books lined the shelves, silent sentinels alongside bronze and steel statuettes of fencers on the mantelpiece and photographs of famous paintings. It was a sanctuary, meticulously curated to feel singularly home-like, yet as he sank into the leather armchair near the hearth, the atmosphere shifted.

His gaze landed on a large photograph of May Welland. It was a gift from the early days of their courtship, a token that had since usurped all other portraits on the table. He studied the image with a growing, unsettling awe: the frank forehead, the serious eyes, the gay, innocent mouth. This was the face of the young creature whose soul he was destined to guard. Yet, staring back at him through the familiar features, he saw something else—a terrifying product of the social system he inhabited and revered. The girl who knew nothing and expected everything looked like a stranger. Marriage, he realized with a jolt, was not the safe anchorage his upbringing had promised; it was a voyage onto uncharted seas.

The memory of the Countess Olenska churned through his mind, stirring old convictions until they drifted dangerously. His own declaration—"Women should be free"—struck the root of a problem his world insisted did not exist. "Nice" women, regardless of grievance, would never claim the specific liberty he envisioned. Generous men like himself were merely performing a chivalrous charade, using verbal generosity to mask the iron grip of convention. Here, he was pledged to defend conduct on behalf of his betrothed's cousin that, if committed by his own wife, would invite the thunders of Church and State. The dilemma was theoretical, absurd in its application to his station, yet Newland Archer possessed an imagination that refused to dismiss the possibility. In his union with May, the tie might gall for reasons far less gross than those affecting a Polish nobleman.

He considered what they truly knew of one another. As a "decent" fellow, he concealed his past; as a marriageable girl, she had none to hide. What if, driven by subtle, unseen currents, they tired of each other? He surveyed the marriages of his peers—the supposedly happy unions—and found none that resembled the passionate comradeship he had pictured. Such a vision presupposed experience, versatility, and freedom of judgment, qualities May had been carefully trained not to possess. A shiver of foreboding ran through him. He saw his future marriage crystallizing into what surrounded him: a dull association of material and social interests, cemented by ignorance on one side and hypocrisy on the other.

Lawrence Lefferts surfaced in his thoughts as the archetype of this enviable ideal. As the high priest of form, Lefferts had shaped his wife entirely to his convenience. During his frequent affairs with other men's wives, she wandered about in smiling unconsciousness, claiming "Lawrence was so frightfully strict." She blushed indignantly when anyone mentioned Julius Beaufort's "other establishment," a foreigner of doubtful origin. Archer tried to comfort himself with the notion that he was not quite such an ass as Larry Lefferts, nor May such a simpleton as poor Gertrude. But the distinction was one of intelligence, not of moral standards.

They all existed in a hieroglyphic world where the real thing was never spoken, acted, or even thought, but only represented by arbitrary signs. Mrs. Welland knew precisely why Archer had urged her to announce her daughter's engagement at the Beaufort ball, yet she felt compelled to simulate reluctance, feigning that her hand had been forced. It was akin to the savage bride dragged from her parents' tent, shrieking, as described in the books on primitive man that cultured people were beginning to read. The result was that the young girl at the center of this elaborate mystification remained the most inscrutable for her very frankness. She was frank because she had nothing to conceal; she was assured because she knew of nothing to fear. With no better preparation, she was to be plunged overnight into the facts of life.

Newland was sincerely, placidly in love. He delighted in her radiant beauty, her health, her horsemanship, her grace, and the shy intellectual curiosity blooming under his guidance. She had progressed far enough to ridicule the *Idyls of the King*, though not yet to grasp the beauty of *Ulysses* or the *Lotus Eaters*. She was straightforward, loyal, brave, possessing a humor proven chiefly by her laughter at his jokes. He suspected, deep within her innocently gazing soul, a glow of feeling that would be a joy to awaken.

Yet, when he completed this circuit of observation, he returned discouraged. All this frankness and innocence was merely an artificial construct. Untrained human nature was not innocent; it was bristling with the twists and defenses of instinctive guile. He felt oppressed by this creation of factitious purity, cunningly manufactured by a conspiracy of mothers, aunts, grandmothers, and long-dead ancestresses. It was produced because it was what he wanted, what he had a right to demand, so that he might exercise his lordly pleasure in smashing it like an image made of snow.

These reflections carried a certain triteness, habitual to young men approaching their wedding day. Usually, they were accompanied by a sense of compunction and self-abasement, a regret that he lacked a blank page to offer his bride in exchange for her unblemished one. Newland felt no such trace. He could not deplore, as Thackeray's heroes often did, that he had not offered a slate of white paper. He could not escape the fact that if he had been raised as she had, they would have been no more fit to navigate the world than the Babes in the Wood. Nor could he, despite his anxious cogitations, discern any honest reason—unconnected to his own momentary pleasure or the passion of masculine vanity—why his bride should not have been granted the same freedom of experience as himself.

Such questions drifted through his mind at such an hour, inevitable and persistent. Their uncomfortable precision was due to the inopportune arrival of the Countess Olenska. Here he was, at the very moment of his betrothal—a time for pure thoughts and cloudless hopes—pitchforked into a coil of scandal that raised all the special problems he had preferred to leave lying dormant.

"Hang Ellen Olenska!" he grumbled, covering the fire and beginning to undress. He could not fully comprehend why her fate should bear upon his own, yet he dimly sensed he had only just begun to measure the risks of the championship his engagement had forced upon him.

A few days later, the bolt fell. The Lovell Mingotts issued cards for a "formal dinner"—three extra footmen, two dishes per course, Roman punch in the middle—headlined with the words "To meet the Countess Olenska," adhering to the hospitable American custom of treating strangers as royalty or ambassadors. The guest list displayed a boldness and discrimination recognized by the initiated as the firm hand of Catherine the Great. Alongside immemorial standbys like the Selfridge Merrys, the Beauforts, and Mr. Sillerton Jackson with his sister Sophy, were the Lawrence Leffertses, Mrs. Lefferts Rushworth, the Harry Thorleys, the Reggie Chiverses, and young Morris Dagonet and his wife, a van der Luyden. The company was perfectly assorted, comprising the little inner group who, throughout the long New York season, disported themselves together with undiminished zest.

Forty-eight hours later, the unbelievable occurred. Every single guest refused the Mingotts' invitation save for the Beauforts and old Mr. Jackson and his sister. The intended slight was emphasized by the presence of the Reggie Chiverses, of the Mingott clan, among those inflicting it, and by the uniform wording of the notes. Each writer simply "regretted that they were unable to accept," omitting the mitigating plea of a "previous engagement" that ordinary courtesy prescribed. New York society was far too small, its resources too scant, for everyone—from livery-stable keepers to butlers and cooks—to know exactly on which evenings people were free. Thus, the recipients of Mrs. Lovell Mingott's invitations could cruelly clarify their determination not to meet the Countess Olenska.

The blow was unexpected, but the Mingotts met it gallantly, as was their way. Mrs. Lovell Mingott confided the case to Mrs. Welland, who confided it to Newland Archer. Aflame at the outrage, he appealed passionately and authoritatively to his mother. After a painful period of inward resistance and outward temporizing, she succumbed to his instances, as she always did. Immediately embracing his cause with an energy redoubled by her previous hesitations, she donned her grey velvet bonnet and declared, "I'll go and see Louisa van der Luyden."

The New York of Newland Archer's day was a small, slippery pyramid, in which, as yet, hardly a fissure had been made or a foothold gained. At its base lay a firm foundation of "plain people," an honorable but obscure majority of respectable families raised above their level by marriage with one of the ruling clans.
