The fever that had ravaged Marianne was not of a malignant strain, and bolstered by her youth, her innate vitality, and the devoted vigil of her mother, her convalescence proceeded with remarkable velocity. Within four days of Mrs. Dashwood's arrival, Marianne was strong enough to transfer to Mrs. Palmer's dressing room. It was there, driven by an impatience to offer personal thanks for the effort of bringing her mother, that she requested Colonel Brandon's presence.

Upon entering, the Colonel's reaction was visceral. Seeing the alteration in her countenance and grasping the pale hand she extended to him, his emotion transcended mere affection or the knowledge that it was observed by others. Elinor, observing the melancholy depth in his gaze and the shifting pallor of his complexion as he regarded her sister, deduced that the sight of Marianne—a living echo of Eliza—had triggered a cascade of past miseries in his mind. The hollow eye, the sickly skin, the posture of reclining weakness, and the warm acknowledgment of debt stirred memories of profound loss.

Mrs. Dashwood, though equally attentive, viewed the scene through a lens of pragmatic simplicity. She interpreted the Colonel's behavior as arising from basic, self-evident sentiments, whereas she convinced herself that Marianne's actions hinted at a burgeoning sentiment greater than simple gratitude.

Two days later, as Marianne's strength returned visibly every twelve hours, Mrs. Dashwood, yielding to shared desires, proposed moving to Barton. This decision anchored the plans of her friends; Mrs. Jennings could not abandon Cleveland during the Dashwoods' stay, and Colonel Brandon was swiftly persuaded to treat his residence there as equally fixed and necessary. In return, at the joint insistence of the Colonel and Mrs. Jennings, Mrs. Dashwood accepted the use of his carriage for the journey, ensuring better accommodation for her fragile child. The Colonel, delighted by the invitation from both women, pledged to repay the hospitality with a visit to the cottage within weeks.

The day of departure arrived. Marianne took a prolonged, earnest leave of Mrs. Jennings, filled with a gratitude born of secret remorse for her past neglects, and bid farewell to Colonel Brandon with the cordiality of a true friend. Assisted by him into the carriage—which he insisted she occupy at least half of—she watched as Mrs. Dashwood and Elinor followed. Left behind, the remaining guests discussed the travelers and their own dullness until Mrs. Jennings was summoned to her chaise for gossip with her maid regarding the loss of her young companions. Colonel Brandon then departed alone toward Delaford.

The Dashwoods spent two days on the road. Marianne endured the journey without significant fatigue. Every measure of zealous affection and solicitous care taken by her watchful companions yielded reward in her physical ease and spiritual calmness. For Elinor, witnessing this composure was particularly gratifying. Having seen her sister week after week suffocating under anguish she lacked the courage to speak of or the fortitude to hide, Elinor now rejoiced in an apparent serenity of mind. Trusting this state resulted from serious reflection, she believed it would inevitably lead to contentment.

As they neared Barton, entering landscapes where every field and tree held painful memory, Marianne fell silent and thoughtful. Turning her face away from their notice, she gazed earnestly through the window. Elinor felt neither wonder nor blame; when she saw Marianne cry upon alighting from the carriage, she perceived only a natural emotion, unobtrusive and worthy of pity. In her subsequent demeanor, Elinor detected a mind awakening to reasonable exertion. Hardly had they entered the sitting-room than Marianne scanned the room with a look of resolute firmness, seemingly determined to accustom herself to objects connected to the memory of Willoughby. She spoke little, yet every sentence aimed at cheerfulness. Though a sigh occasionally escaped, it was invariably followed by a smile. After dinner, she attempted to play the pianoforte. She approached the instrument, but her eye rested on an opera score procured by Willoughby, containing their favorite duets and bearing her name in his handwriting.

"That will not do," she declared. Shaking her head, she set the music aside. After running her fingers over the keys for a moment, she feigned feebleness and closed the instrument, firmly vowing to practice much in the future.

The following morning showed no abatement in these hopeful signs. Strengthened by rest, she looked and spoke with genuine spirit, anticipating Margaret's return and speaking of the restored family circle—their mutual pursuits and cheerful society—as the sole happiness worth wishing for.

"When the weather settles and I have recovered my strength," she said, "we will take long walks daily. We will walk to the farm at the edge of the downs to see how the children fare; to Sir John's new plantations at Barton Cross and the Abbeylands; and often to the old ruins of the Priory, tracing its foundations as far as recorded. I know we shall be happy. I know the summer will pass happily. I intend never to rise later than six, dividing every moment thereafter between music and reading. I have formed a plan and am determined to embark on a course of serious study. Our own library is too familiar for anything beyond amusement, but there are many works at the Park well worth reading, and others of modern production I can borrow from Colonel Brandon. By reading six hours a day, I shall gain in a year a vast amount of instruction I currently lack."

Elinor honored the nobility of this plan, though she smiled to see the same eager fancy that had once led her to languid indolence now working to introduce excess into a scheme of rational employment and virtuous self-control. Her smile faded into a sigh when she recalled the unfulfilled promise to Willoughby. She feared communicating this news might unsettle Marianne's mind and ruin this fair prospect of tranquil industry. Resolving to delay the inevitable, she decided to wait until her sister's health was more secure. Yet, the resolution was made only to be broken.

Three days after returning home, the weather finally permitted an invalid like Marianne to venture outdoors. A soft, genial morning appeared, tempting both daughter and mother. Leaning on Elinor's arm, Marianne was authorized to walk as long as she could without fatigue along the lane before the house.

Moving slowly, mindful of her recent frailty, the sisters advanced only far enough to command a full view of the hill behind them. Pausing, with her eyes fixed on it, Marianne calmly stated, "There, exactly there"—pointing with one hand to a projecting mound—"there I fell; and there I first saw Willoughby."

Her voice sank with the words, but quickly revived. "I am thankful to find I can look upon the spot with so little pain! Shall we ever talk of that subject, Elinor?" she asked hesitantly. "Or will it be wrong? I *can* talk of it now, I hope, as I ought to."

Elinor tenderly encouraged her openness.

"As for regret," Marianne continued, "I have done with that concerning *him*. I do not mean to discuss what my feelings *were*, but what they *are* now. At present, if I could be satisfied on one point—if I could be allowed to think he was not *always* acting a part, not *always* deceiving me; but above all, if I could be assured he was never so *very* wicked as my fears sometimes imagined, especially since the story of that unfortunate girl—"

She stopped. Elinor joyfully treasured her words, answering, "If you could be assured of that, you think you would be at ease."

"Yes. My peace of mind is doubly involved. It is horrible to suspect a person, who has been what *he* was to *me*, of such designs. But what must it make me appear to myself? In a situation like mine, what but a most shamefully unguarded affection could expose me to—"

"How then," her sister asked, "would you account for his behavior?"

"I would suppose him—Oh, how gladly would I suppose him—only fickle, very, very fickle."

Elinor said nothing. She debated internally whether to begin her narration immediately or postpone it until Marianne was stronger, and they crept on in silence for a few minutes.

"I am not wishing him too much good," Marianne sighed finally, "when I pray his secret reflections may be no more unpleasant than my own. He will suffer enough in them."

"Do you compare your conduct with his?"

"No. I compare it with what it ought to have been; I compare it with yours."

"Our situations have borne little resemblance."

"They have borne more than our conduct. Do not, my dearest Elinor, let your kindness defend what I know your judgment must censure. My illness has made me think. It has given me leisure and calmness for serious recollection. Long before I was recovered enough to speak, I was perfectly able to reflect. I considered the past: I saw in my own behavior, since the beginning of our acquaintance last autumn, nothing but a series of imprudence towards myself and a want of kindness to others. I saw that my own feelings prepared my sufferings, and that my want of fortitude under them almost led me to the grave. My illness, I well knew, was entirely brought on by my own negligence of health, which I felt even at the time to be wrong. Had I died, it would have been self-destruction.
