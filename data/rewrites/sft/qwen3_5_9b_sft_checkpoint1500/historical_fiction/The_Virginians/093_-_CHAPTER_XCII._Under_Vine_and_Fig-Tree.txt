Must I recount, dear friends, the simple joys of gathering beneath the same roof, the mother's quiet pride as her flock returns to her shelter? Our home, spacious and warm, welcomed us all. Yet Aunt Hetty, though our kin, could not abide to remain within these walls. With a frankness that cut to the quick, she declared that yielding up her eldest children—hers for nearly three years—to live among strangers, while accepting a secondary role, tore at her heart too deeply. Thus, she and Father departed for a dwelling at Bury St. Edmunds, a short journey hence, where they lived apart. There, in privacy, Aunt Hetty doted upon her oldest nephew and niece, indulging them fully.

It was the season following our return home when the Jamaican planter, Mr. B, passed away. He left Aunt Hetty half his fortune, a legacy born of a long-standing infatuation; for in Jamaica, despite her refusal, he had proven his steadfast devotion. Yet, Heaven knows how much of Aunt Hetty's inheritance Monsieur Miles has already swallowed! The cost of his commission, the flamboyance of his uniforms, the debts contracted in gaming houses, the frivolous entertainments at the Cocoa-Tree—all these drain the purse. Do you suppose, sir, I am ignorant of human nature? Of the price of Pall Mall revelry and modest pleasures? Can a gentleman sustain such a lifestyle on the five hundred pounds a year I grant him? Aunt Hetty, convinced by her trials, has resolved to spin an old maid. "I vowed," she told me, "never to wed until I found a man as virtuous as my dear father." "And I never did, Sir George," she replied to me later, adding with a shrug, "nor half as good as Sir George. Let him smoke that thought in his pipe."

Yet, when our beloved General breathed his last—calm, aged, and content to depart—I believe it was my wife who wept most profoundly. "I mourn because I fear I did not love him enough," the gentle soul confessed. In contrast, Hetty barely concealed her composure, outwardly at least. She speaks of him constantly, as if he walks beside her; she recalls his wit, his kindness to his offspring, and brightens like a girl again when mentioning them. She sits cheerfully before the slab in church that bears his name and virtues, telling no lies in her reverence.

I had often suspected that my brother Hal, harboring a youthful passion for Hetty, retained a hold on her heart. When he visited us ten years prior, I mentioned this childhood romance, hoping he might persuade her to replace Mrs. Fanny—a loss my wife, prone to self-reproach, lamented endlessly for failing to grieve sufficiently. Hal arrived consumed by sorrow for Fanny, vowing no woman equal existed. Our General, then alive, took him in, housing him as he did in happier, earlier days. The women played melodies he heard as a boy at Oakhurst; hearts softened by old memories. Harry poured out his griefs and praises of his late wife to Hetty, and spoke fondly of his dear Aunt Lambert, whose virtues were music to the faithful old husband, whose thoughts of his wife seemed absent for no three waking minutes.

General Hal ventured to Paris as an American General Officer, clad in blue and yellow, a fashion adopted by Mr. Fox and others here. Much honored at Versailles, though presented by the Marquis de Lafayette to a King and Queen who held neither in high esteem, a Marquise reportedly took a liking to the Virginian General, offering immediate marriage. Had he not resisted and fled back to England, Warrington, and ultimately Bury, where locals listened with endless patience and sympathy to his tales of his late wife? As for us, who knew the paragon, we were civil, though not entirely enthusiastic regarding her, occasionally puzzled to answer our children's queries about Uncle Hal's angelic spouse.

The two Generals, myself, Captain Miles, and Parson Blake—who was routed at Monmouth the year after I left America, returning to exchange coats and accept my living—often fought the battles of the Revolution over a bottle. The parson would exclaim, "By Jupiter, General!" (compounding for Jupiter when divested of his military habit), "You are the Tory, and Sir George the Whig! He faults our leaders, while you stand forever by them; last Sunday, when I prayed for the King, I heard you following me loudly."

"And so I do, Blake, with all my heart," Hal replied. "I cannot forget I wore his coat."

"Ah, if Wolfe had lived twenty years longer!" cried Lambert.

"Aye, sir!" shouted Hal. "You must hear the General talk of him!"

"What General?" I asked, seeking to vex him.

"My General," Hal answered, rising to fill a bumper. "His Excellency, General George Washington!"

"With all my heart," I cried, though the parson looked ill-disposed toward the toast or the claret.

Hal never tires of speaking of his General. It was on such an evening of friendly discourse that he recounted how he had fallen into disgrace with the man he cherished so dearly. Their rift stemmed from the aforementioned Monsieur le Marquis de Lafayette, a figure who played a pivotal role in recent history before vanishing mysteriously. Hal's prior rank and acknowledged gallantry ought to have secured his promotion in the Continental army, where a "whipper-snapper" like M. de Lafayette was swiftly complimented by Congress with the rank of Major-General. With the freedom of an old soldier, Hal expressed contempt for certain congressional appointments, alleging that unscrupulous officers, greedy for promotion, orchestrated intrigues and cabals. Mr. Warrington, perhaps emulating his now-illustrious friend of Mount Vernon, affected the air of *gentilhomme*, paying his wages but spending them on comforts and clothing for his men. Regarding rank, he deemed it of no earthly consequence, preferring to serve as colonel over any higher grade. He certainly added contemptuous remarks concerning certain General Officers of the Congress army, their origins, and the causes of their advancement. Notably, he was furious about the sudden promotion of the young French lad—the Marquis, as they affectionately called him—in the Republican army, who, incidentally, was a prodigious favorite of the Chief himself. There were not three officers in the entire Continental force (after poor madcap Lee was captured and disgraced) who could speak the Marquis's language, allowing Hal to judge the young Major-General more closely and familiarly than other gentlemen, including the Commander-in-Cheif. Mr. Washington, good-naturedly, chided friend Hal for his jealousy of the bearded commander of Auvergne; he himself was pleased by the filial regard and profound veneration the enthusiastic young nobleman always displayed toward him. Moreover, he possessed the very best political reasons for treating the Marquis with friendship and favor.

Subsequently, it transpired that the Commander-in-Chief was most urgently pressing Colonel Warrington's promotion upon Congress. As if his difficulties before the enemy were not sufficient—he was entrenched at Valley Forge during a hard winter, commanding five or six thousand men, almost without fire, blankets, food, or ammunition, facing Sir William Howe's army, which was perfectly equipped and thrice as numerous—as if this trial were not enough, he faced further the cowardly distrust of Congress and insubordination and conspiracy among officers in his own camp. During the terrible winter of '77, when a single blow from the sluggish head of the British forces might have ended the war, and all was doubt, confusion, and despair in the opposing camp (save in one indomitable breast), my brother had an interview with the Chief. He subsequently described it to me, and Hal could never speak of it without succumbing to the deepest emotion. Mr. Washington had won no such triumph as that procured for Gates and the northern army by the daredevil courage of Arnold and the elegant imbecility of Burgoyne. Save in one or two minor encounters proving his daring bravery and unceasing watchfulness, General Washington met defeat after defeat from an enemy superior in every point. Congress mistrusted him. Many an officer in his own camp hated him. Those disappointed in ambition, those detected in peculation, those whose selfishness or incapacity his honest eyes had exposed—all were, more or less, in league against him. Gates was the chief toward whom the malcontents turned. Mr. Gates was the only genius fit to conduct the war; with a vaingloriousness he later generously acknowledged, he did not refuse the homage paid him.

To illustrate the dreadful troubles and anxieties with which General Washington contended, I may mention what was then termed the "Conway Cabal." A certain Irishman—a Chevalier of St. Louis, an officer in the French service—arrived in America early in '77 seeking military employment. Speedily appointed brigadier, he could not be contented without immediate promotion to major-general. Mr. C. had friends at Congress who, as the General-in-Chief learned, had promised him speedy promotion. General Washington remonstrated, representing the injustice of promoting to the highest rank the youngest brigadier in the service. While the matter was pending, he was put in possession of a letter from Conway to General Gates, whom Conway complimented, saying, "Heaven had been determined to save America, or a weak general and bad councillors would have ruined it."
