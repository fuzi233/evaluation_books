XII. A Petition

“Then we are to lose Madame la Comtesse, though I hope only for a few hours,” I said, bowing low.

“It may be only that,” she replied, “or it may be several weeks. It was most unfortunate he spoke to me just now as he did. Do you know me?”

I assured her I did not.

“You shall know me,” she said, “but not yet. We are older and closer friends than you might imagine. I cannot reveal myself at present. In three weeks’ time, I shall pass near your beautiful schloss—about which I have been inquiring—and call upon you for an hour or two, to renew a friendship I never recall without a thousand pleasant memories.”

At that moment, news reached her like a thunderclap. She must depart immediately, traveling by a circuitous route nearly a hundred miles long, with all haste. Her troubles multiplied. Only the strict reserve she maintained over her name prevented her from making a most unusual request.

“My poor child has not fully recovered her strength,” she continued. “Her horse fell during a hunt she had gone to witness. The shock has left her nerves unsteady, and our physician insists she must not exert herself in any way for some time. That is why we came here slowly—no more than six leagues a day. Now I must travel night and day on a matter of life and death—a mission so grave and urgent I shall explain its full nature when we meet again, as I trust we will, in a few weeks, free of all concealment.”

She then made her petition—delivered with the air of one bestowing a favor rather than soliciting one. Yet this was merely in tone; she seemed unaware of the effect. The words themselves were humble, almost pleading: she asked only that I take charge of her daughter during her absence.

It was, under all circumstances, a strange—indeed, audacious—request. But she disarmed me by acknowledging every objection, laying bare her own vulnerability, and placing herself entirely in my chivalrous hands. At that very instant, fate intervened. My ward approached, whispering urgently that she wished to invite her new friend, Millarca, to visit us. She had just spoken with her and found her charming—especially if her mother would allow it.

At another time, I would have advised patience, until we knew who these ladies truly were. But there was no time to think. The two women pressed their appeal together, and I confess—I was swayed. There was something profoundly captivating about the young lady’s refined beauty, the grace of her bearing, the unmistakable fire of noble blood. Overwhelmed, I yielded too readily, agreeing to care for the girl whom her mother called Millarca.

The Countess beckoned to her daughter, who listened gravely as her mother explained—broadly—the suddenness of her departure and the arrangement she had made for her stay under my care. She added that I was among her oldest and most trusted friends.

I offered the usual courtesies, but already, in reflection, I felt unease settling into my bones. The gentleman in black returned, conducting the lady from the room with solemn dignity. His demeanor impressed me deeply—he carried himself with such quiet authority that I could not help believing the Countess held a station far greater than her modest title suggested.

Her final instruction was clear: no attempt should be made to learn more about her than I might have guessed—until her return. Our host, whose guest she was, understood her reasons.

“But here,” she said, “neither I nor my daughter can remain longer than a day. I removed my mask briefly, perhaps an hour ago—and realized too late that you might have seen me. So I sought this moment to speak with you. Had I known you had seen me, I would have entrusted my secret to your honor, asking you to guard it for weeks. As it is, I am certain you did not see me. But if you suspect now—or later—what I am, I place myself entirely in your honor. My daughter will keep silence, and I know you will remind her from time to time, lest she betray it unknowingly.”

She whispered a few words to her daughter, kissed her twice swiftly, and left, accompanied by the pale man in black, vanishing into the crowd.

“In the next room,” Millarca said, “there is a window overlooking the hall door. I should like to see the last of mamma—and wave to her.”

We agreed, and followed her. Outside, we saw a stately old carriage, flanked by couriers and footmen. The thin figure of the pale gentleman stood beside the Countess, draping a thick velvet cloak around her shoulders, pulling the hood over her head. She nodded to him, touched his hand lightly. He bowed repeatedly as the door closed and the carriage began to move.

“She is gone,” Millarca murmured, sighing.

“She is gone,” I repeated, for the first time—now, in the fleeting moments since my consent, reflecting on the folly of my decision.

“She did not look up,” the young lady said softly.

“The Countess had removed her mask,” I said gently, “and perhaps did not wish to show her face. And she could not have known you were watching.”

She sighed again, gazing at me. So beautiful was she that I softened. I regretted even the brief moment of hesitation in welcoming her. I resolved to make amends for any coldness I might have shown.

Millarca replaced her mask and joined my ward in urging me to return to the gardens, where the music would soon resume. We walked along the terrace beneath the castle windows. Millarca grew increasingly familiar, delighting us with vivid tales of the nobles we saw upon the terrace—her descriptions sharp, witty, and never cruel. I liked her more with each passing minute. Her company promised to enliven our often solitary evenings.

The ball did not end until the morning sun crested the horizon. The Grand Duke danced until dawn, and loyal guests dared not leave before him.

As we passed through a crowded saloon, my ward asked where Millarca had gone. I thought she had been beside her, and she believed she was beside me. In truth, we had lost her.

My search proved fruitless. I feared she had mistaken others for us in the confusion, following them into the vast, open grounds. Now, fully aware, I recognized the true folly of taking responsibility for a young woman without knowing her name. Bound by promises whose motives I could not fathom, I could not even direct inquiries by naming her as the daughter of the Countess who had departed only hours before.

Daylight broke. It was full morning before I abandoned the search. Not until nearly two o’clock the next day did we hear word of my missing charge.

A servant knocked at my niece’s door, reporting that a distressed young lady had begged him to find General Baron Spielsdorf and his daughter—she had been left in their care by her mother.

There could be no doubt. Our visitor had returned. If only we had lost her!

She told my dear child a story to explain her delay. Late that night, she said, she had wandered to the housekeeper’s chamber, overcome with despair at losing us. Exhausted, she fell into a deep sleep—one that, though long, barely restored her after the strain of the ball.

That day, Millarca came home with us. I was only too glad, after all, to have secured such a delightful companion for my beloved girl.
