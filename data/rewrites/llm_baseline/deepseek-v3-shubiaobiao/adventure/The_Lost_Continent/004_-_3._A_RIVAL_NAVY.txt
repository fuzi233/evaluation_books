The shores of Atlantis rose before us at last, though Tob’s skilled navigation had brought us close, we still had ten days of backtracking before reaching the arm of the sea that led to the capital. The sight of land—the scent of earth and green things carried on the wind—was a mercy from the Gods. Many of our crew had perished on the long voyage, and more lay wasted with scurvy, their limbs swollen and useless. Yet the nearness of land breathed life back into them. Men who had been little more than corpses now stirred, hauling lines and readying weapons, their spirits fierce at the promise of home.

Since my fall from power in Yucatan, I had harbored doubts about the welcome awaiting me in Atlantis. Tatho’s assurances meant little. The Empress Phorenice ruled as she pleased, and if she willed my death the moment I stepped ashore, so be it. The Sun God’s will would prevail. Thus, I had spent the voyage in meditation, untroubled by what lay ahead.

But when we rounded the twin headlands marking the entrance to the inland sea—two days from the capital—we found a navy waiting.

Five of our ships remained, the others lost to storms or left drifting with dead crews. Against us stood three sleek warships and three swift galleys, their hulls black and gleaming, their crews fresh and strong. Our vessels were battered, our sails patched, our hulls crusted with the filth of the sea. We flew Tatho’s colors openly, but the enemy sailed without banners, as though ashamed of their purpose.

They meant to fight.

Tob barked orders, and our ships formed a tight wedge. The Sun blazed overhead, filling our sails as the two forces closed. The enemy galleys held back, oars dipping in unison, while their warships advanced. At a hundred lengths, they halted, and a galley broke rank, green branches hoisted in truce.

Tob grunted and raised a withered stick in reply. The galley scraped against our hull, and a man vaulted onto our deck—young, arrogant, smirking at our ragged state.

"Old pot-mate," he said to Tob, "your woman waits for you in Atlantis with four brats at her skirts. I saw her not a month past."

"You didn’t sail out here to gossip," Tob growled. "Spit your purpose, Dason."

"Simple enough. Hand over Deucalion, and you sail on untouched. Refuse, and we sink what’s left of your fleet."

Tob laughed. "You’ll find no plunder here but blood. We carry no treasure—only men half-dead from scurvy. If it’s a fight you want, you’ll get it."

Dason’s smirk widened. "Oh, we know what cargo you carry. And we’ll have it. Phorenice has enemies enough without Deucalion bolstering her strength."

Tob spat. "Take him by force, then. But know this—if I return without him, Tatho will flay me alive. And even if he didn’t, I’d sooner feed you to the sharks than betray a man who once fought a sea-lizard bare-handed."

Dason’s eyes flicked to me, then back to Tob. "Then drown easy, old friend. And don’t fret for your woman—I’ll see she’s looked after."

Tob’s roar shook the deck. "I’ll rip your throat for that!"

Dason fled back to his galley.

What followed was a battle worthy of legend.

Our five ships drove like fangs into the enemy’s flank, sinking one before they could react. Their galleys retaliated, oars churning, and soon the water was a churning graveyard of burning hulls and screaming men. The *Bear*, Tob’s ship, became the heart of the storm. Arrows hissed, axes flashed, and throwing fire turned decks into infernos. The sea itself seemed alive—great birds wheeled above, while beneath the waves, scaled shadows darted for the fallen.

One by one, ships vanished beneath the swells. Our men fought like demons, roaring Tob’s name like a war cry, while the enemy shouted for Dason.

By the end, only the *Bear* and Dason’s galley remained.

Flames licked our decks. Tob grinned through the smoke. "Enough of this oven," he bellowed. "To the galley!"

We cut our way through, ten of us leaping onto the enemy ship as the *Bear* burned behind us. The survivors who followed were given a choice: surrender or die. Dason stood sullen, weaponless.

Tob faced him, axe dripping. "You spoke of my woman," he said. "Now choose—face her wrath in Atlantis, or mine here."

Dason knelt. "Make it quick."

The axe fell. Tob spitted the head on the galley’s prow and kicked the body to the waiting birds.

As the oar-slaves rowed us toward Atlantis, Tob drank deep from a wineskin. I raised a horn to him.

"You fought well," I said.

He laughed. "Aye, and if Phorenice rewards me half as much as she’ll welcome you, I’ll die a rich man."

I gripped his shoulder. "Whatever comes, Tob, your service won’t be forgotten."

He splashed wine at my feet. "Then that’s surety enough. The Lord Deucalion for the block? Never."

The galley surged forward, carrying us toward whatever fate awaited.
