Gerard woke not to hope, but to a fresh tide of despair. By the second day, fever had seized his mind, turning his thoughts into a chaotic storm. Beside his bed lay the tangle of his once-rich auburn hair, now discarded like a woman's, a stark symbol of his fallen state. The symptoms of his illness marched forward with terrifying speed. By the fifth day, the leeches, seeing no remedy, withdrew in defeat. That evening, as the sun dipped below the horizon, he succumbed to a profound slumber. Rumors spread that he would never wake, that this sleep was merely the prelude to death. Yet, an elderly woman, a seasoned nurse whose counsel held weight among the locals, offered a glimmer of defiance. "His youth may yet save him," she insisted, "if he can endure twelve hours of rest."

Moved by this hope, his landlady swept the room clear of all distractions and stood vigil beside his bed. She vowed a wax candle to the Virgin Mary for every hour he remained unconscious. Twelve hours passed. The woman knelt in gratitude, thanking the heavens. Twenty-four hours followed. Doubt began to creep into the hearts of the caretakers. At the thirty-hour mark, she summoned the physician.

"Thirty hours!" she asked anxiously. "Shall we rouse him?"

The doctor examined him closely, his brow furrowed. "His breathing is steady, his skin moist. I know learned men would wake him to inspect his tongue, to perform their rituals and learn nothing. But we, being women, possess the wisdom to let nature take its course. When has sleep ever harmed a shattered mind or a broken heart?"

Forty-eight hours of silence ensued. The news leaked out, drawing a crowd of curious onlookers, though they were kept at bay until only two figures were permitted entry: Fra Colonna and his towering companion, Fra Jerome. The friars took over from the women, sitting in solemn silence. Fra Colonna gazed at his young friend with tears welling in his eyes, while Fra Jerome, beads clicking softly in his hand, watched with the calm detachment of a man who had faced the sea's fury alongside Gerard.

Around the sixtieth hour of this unnatural stillness, the landlady nudged Fra Colonna. He looked down. Gerard's eyelids fluttered, and he opened his eyes with the gentleness of one merely dozing. He stared into the void, then drew himself up slightly in the bed. Reaching for his head, he felt the smooth, bare scalp where his hair had once been. He caught sight of Fra Colonna and a smile of relief touched his lips. But the smile died instantly. His face twisted into a mask of unspeakable agony, and a loud cry tore from his throat as he turned his face to the wall.

The landlady wept, remembering the hollow ache of waking alone after loss. Fra Jerome began to chant psalms from his breviary, a low, rhythmic drone against the silence. Gerard curled into the bedclothes, shrinking from the world. Fra Colonna approached, whispering with a trembling voice that all was not lost. He spoke of the divine Muses, immortal and faithful, urging Gerard to transfer his love to them. They would never betray him, unlike fragile creatures of flesh. Overcome by emotion, the simple priest hurried away.

Fra Jerome remained. "Young man," he said, his voice grave, "the Muses exist only in the minds of pagans and dreamers. It is the Church alone that offers peace to the heart on earth and salvation to the soul in the hereafter. Has the earth deceived you? Has passion torn your heart? Then the Church opens her arms. Consecrate your gifts to her! The Church is peace of mind."

He spoke these words solemnly at the threshold and vanished before Gerard could respond.

"The Church!" Gerard shrieked, rising with a fury that shook the room. He shook his fist at the empty air where the friar had stood. "Malediction on the Church! But for the Church, I should not lie broken here, while she lies cold, cold, cold in Holland. Oh, my Margaret! Oh, my darling! My darling! And I must flee from you for the few months you had left to live. Cruel! Cruel! The monsters allowed her to die. Death does not come without warning; the blind, selfish fools saw the signs or chose to ignore them, but I saw them. I, who loved her. Oh, had I been there, I would have saved her, I would have saved her! Idiot! Idiot! To leave her for a single moment."

He wept bitterly for a long time, his body wracked with grief. Then, suddenly, the sorrow burst into a tempest of rage. "The Church! For whose sake I was driven from her! My curse be upon the Church and the hypocrites who use its name to wound my broken heart! Accursed be the world! Ghysbrecht lives; Margaret dies. Thieves, murderers, harlots, live forever. Only angels die. Curse life! Curse death! And curse whoever forged them into such wretched shapes!"

The friar had not heard these mad, wicked words, only the raw yell of rage cast after him. It was fortunate he had not. Had he heard, he would have demanded Gerard be burned in the forum with the same casual indifference as roasting a kid.

The landlady, who had accompanied Fra Colonna downstairs, heard the raised voice and returned in alarm. She found Gerard dressing, his hands moving mechanically as tears streamed down his face.

"What good is lying here?" he muttered gloomily. "Can I find what I seek within these walls?"

"Saints preserve us! Is he distraught again? What do you seek?"

"Oblivion."

"Oblivion, my little heart? Oh, but you are too young to speak such words."

"Young or old, what else remains for me to live for?"

He donned his finest clothes. The good woman protested. "My pretty Gerard, know that it is Tuesday, not Sunday."

"Oh, Tuesday is it? I thought it had been Saturday."

"Nay, you have slept long. You never wear your brave clothes on working days. Consider."

"What I did when she lived, I did. Now I shall do whatever I once refused. The past is the past. There lies my hair, and with it, my way of life. I served one Master as well as I could. You see my reward. Now I will serve another, and give him a fair trial."

"Alas!" the woman sighed, her face draining of color. "What do these dark words mean? And what new master is this whose service you intend to try?"

With this terrible declaration on his lips, the wretched creature walked out. His cap and feather were set jauntily askew, a mocking contrast to his feeble limbs and a sinister face pale as ash, drawn down as if by the heavy hand of age.
