Ghysbrecht van Swieten lay all that night with his eyes open and his heart thumping as if a fist beat time upon his ribs. The storm had passed over Sevenbergen like a drum—first the long roll of wind along the eaves, then the sudden crack of lightning that split the dark with a sound too bright for any mortal ear, then the heavy sluicing rain that made every gutter sing. To Ghysbrecht, thunder was no grand voice of God but a threat with teeth; each peal seemed to come hunting for his very bed. So he had not dared join the men who had gone to turn Peter’s poor house inside out. He listened instead—counting the pauses between the flashes, straining to hear through the weather for any footfall, any shout, any sound that meant his prey had slipped the net.

When at last the sky tired of striking and the rain thinned to a dripping, steady whisper, he rose like a man who has been held under water and is allowed, grudgingly, one breath. He crept down the stairs, careful as a thief in his own home, and the boards answered him with little groans. Outside, the night smelled washed and raw—wet earth, bruised grass, the sharp iron tang storms leave behind. He saddled his mule with hands that worked too quickly to be gentle; the leather was cold and damp, and the buckles rang faintly in the cleansed air. Then he rode for the “Three Kings” at Sevenbergen, the hoofs and the mule’s soft snorts the only sounds in a world still rinsed clean of clamor.

The inn received him with a different music: the stale hush after revelry, broken by snores—thick, varied, shameless. His men lay scattered like dropped garments: some folded over chairs, some sprawled on tables with their mouths open, some on the floor where the rushes had been trodden into a sour mat. A hearth had died down to a dull red eye; last night’s ale hung in the air with the tang of sweat and damp wool.

Ghysbrecht’s voice cut through it like a cleaver. He roused them furiously, kicking at boots, seizing shoulders, shaking heads as if to shake out the dregs and leave only obedience. Blinking, yawning, they gave him their tale: how they had searched and found nothing, and how zealously they had done it—each word a self-praise to sweeten failure.

“Fool! to let you go without me,” cried the burgomaster, his fear transmuted now into rage that tasted, on his tongue, like vinegar. “My life on’t he was there all the time.” His eyes gleamed with the fierce certainty of a man who cannot bear that the world should escape his ordering. “Looked ye under the girl’s bed?”

A man, still half-asleep, scratched at his beard. “No; there was no room for a man there.”

Ghysbrecht’s lip curled. “How know ye that, if ye looked not?” he snarled, each syllable sharp enough to draw blood. “Ye should have looked under her bed, and in it too, and sounded all the panels with your knives. Come, now, get up, and I shall show ye how to search.”

Dierich Brower got to his feet and shook himself like a dog flinging off river water. His tone carried the dull amusement of the confident. “If you find him, call me a horse and no man.”

In a few minutes they were abroad again, the street still glistening. The village had not yet found its morning voice; only a few hens muttered in their coops, and from far off a cart-chain clinked once and was still. Peter’s house stood as before—plain, closed, quiet—its silence now seeming to Ghysbrecht not innocence but insolence. They surrounded it as if it were a pot to be watched lest it boil over and spill what they wanted. The fiery old man handed his mule’s reins to Jorian Ketel, and with Dierich Brower and the rest pushed in.

The house met them with emptiness that had a taste: cold ashes, damp wood, yesterday’s air. Not a creature to be seen—not even Peter. No crying child, no scurrying girl, no startled man. They went from room to room, and the floors answered their boots with a hollow thud. Upstairs they climbed, their breath loud in the close stairwell, their weapons and buckles making small metallic chirps against the washed hush of the world outside.

Then, sudden as a pot-lid clapped down, one of the men gave a shout and thrust an arm out, pointing through Peter’s window. It stood open, letting in the fresh, wet smell of fields and the thin song of morning—larks beginning their work over the drenched land.

They crowded to look, faces pressed close, and there—at some little distance—walking quietly across the fields, as if there were no net at all, no burgomaster’s temper, no law’s teeth—went the man they sought. He kept his pace unhurried. With him were Margaret and Martin, their figures moving together through the wet grass, each step raising a faint darkening in the green where the storm-water clung.

Ghysbrecht’s answer was not a prayer. It was an exulting yell, raw and loud enough to make the very window-frame seem to quiver. He was down the stairs in a rush, the house’s silence broken and left behind like a snapped thread. He flung himself upon his mule; the saddle creaked, the animal stamped and snorted at the sudden weight. His men spilled out after him, roused at last into one purpose. And in the brightened, dripping morning—while the fields still rang with bird-song and the world smelled of wet soil and living roots—Ghysbrecht van Swieten and his company set off in hot pursuit.
