The morning has been a trial of endurance that no tongue could adequately describe. It commenced with Gustavus Adolphus. Downstairs, they shorten his name to "Gusty," a moniker that suits a creature who is a paragon of canine virtue only when confined to the vast expanse of a field or a sprawling common. Indoors, however, he is a disaster waiting to happen. His intentions are noble, but this residence is simply too small for his ambitions. With a single stretch, he topples two chairs and a what-not; with a wag of his tail, the room resembles the aftermath of a military invasion; with a simple breath, he extinguishes the fire.

At dinner, he executes his signature maneuver: creeping under the table, lying still, and then erupting into motion. Our only warning is the table itself, which suddenly acquires a violent desire to perform somersaults. We scramble to hold it upright, only for Gusty to interpret our efforts as a sinister conspiracy. His struggle escalates until the scene concludes with an overturned table and a ruined meal trapped between two layers of furious humanity.

This morning, he arrived with the ferocity of an American cyclone. His first act was to sweep my coffee cup across the table with his tail, drenching my waistcoat in the process. I rose hastily, shouting a curse, and charged him. He pre-empted me, fleeing toward the door. There, he intercepted Eliza, who was carrying eggs. She uttered a sharp "Ugh!" and collapsed onto the floor. The eggs scattered across the carpet, and Gusty vanished. I chased him down the stairs, sternly advising him to remain below for at least an hour, which he accepted by dodging a coal-scoop and departing. Ten minutes later, however, he was perched at the top of the stairs. I ordered him down; he merely barked and jumped. I descended to investigate.

It was Tittums. She sat on the step below, blocking his path, her back arched and her voice rising in a torrent of oaths.

Tittums is our kitten, roughly the size of a penny roll. She was swearing with the intensity of a medical student.

She is a formidable linguist. I admit to swearing occasionally myself, though I am but an amateur compared to her. To be honest—strictly between us, I beg you not to tell your wife—I find that swearing benefits a man. It acts as a safety valve, releasing the internal pressure of bad temper before it causes mental damage. When a man expresses his frustration in colorful language, he feels lighter. Swearing soothes the soul just as smashing furniture does, only without the cost. It clears a man out like gunpowder clears a chimney. A man who never swears, nor kicks a footstool, nor pokes the fire with unnecessary violence, is suspicious to me. Without an outlet, life's annoyances fester, turning petty offenses into deep-seated hatred.

I explained this theory to my aunt once, but she dismissed it, claiming I had no business harboring such feelings. That is precisely what I told Tittums. I lectured her on her upbringing in a Christian household and the shame of her behavior. While I tolerate an old cat's vulgarity, a kitten doing it seems tragically premature.

I shoved Tittums into my pocket and returned to my desk. Moments later, she had wriggled out onto the table, attempting to swallow a pen, dipped her leg in the inkpot, spilled the ink, licked her leg, and then swore directly at me.

I placed her on the floor, where Tim immediately began wrestling with her. I wish Tim would mind his own business; it was none of his affair. Besides, he is hardly a saint. At two years old, he acts like a grumpy, gray-headed Scotch collie, interfering with everything. Tittums' mother arrived, and Tim received a scratch on the nose—a development I welcomed. I banished all three to the passage, where they are currently engaged in a fierce battle. I am covered in ink and in a foul temper; if any more cats or dogs decide to visit this morning, they should bring their own undertakers.

Yet, in general, I hold cats and dogs in high regard. They are delightful companions, far superior to humans. They do not quarrel or argue. They never boast of themselves but listen attentively to your ramblings, feigning interest without making stupid remarks. They never misunderstand relationships, mistaking your wife's cousin for her husband, nor do they question a struggling author about his lack of productivity. They offer no unsolicited critiques of our faults or sarcastic reminders of our past errors. They never tell us we are not as charming as we used to be.

They are always glad to see us, mirroring our moods perfectly. If we are cheerful, they are merry; if we are solemn, they are sober; if we are sad, they are sorrowful.

"Halloo! Want a lark? Right you are! I'm your man. Frisking round, leaping, barking, ready for mischief. Shall we romp in the drawing-room, ignoring the furniture, or scamper across the fields to scare old Gaffer Goggles' geese? Come along!"

Or, if you wish to think quietly, Pussy can purr on the armchair while Montmorency curls on the rug, blinking at the fire but keeping one eye on you for any sign of rats.

When we bury our faces in our hands, wishing we were never born, they do not judge us. They do not offer platitudes about self-inflicted misery. Instead, they come softly, pressing their heads against us. A cat stands on your shoulder, ruffling your hair and saying, "Lor, I am sorry for you, old man"; a dog looks up with big, true eyes and says, "We'll go through the world together, won't we?"

Dogs are imprudent. They do not inquire if you are right or wrong, rich or poor, wise or foolish. You are their pal, and that is sufficient. Whether luck or misfortune strikes, they stick by you, guarding and comforting you, ready to give their lives if needed. Foolish, brainless, soulless dogs!

Ah, old friend, with your deep eyes that comprehend everything before you speak it, do you realize you are merely an animal? Do you know that the drunken lout leaning against the post is your intellectual superior? Do you know that every petty, selfish scoundrel who cheats, lies, and acts with base motives is infinitely superior to you? These crawling skunks are MEN, and men are the greatest, noblest, and wisest beings in the universe. Any man will tell you that.

Yes, poor doggie, you are very stupid compared to us clever men who understand politics and philosophy, knowing everything except our own origins and destinies.

Never mind, pussy and doggie; we like you all the better for your stupidity. We all love stupid things. Men cannot bear clever women, and a woman's ideal man is someone she can call a "dear old stupid." It is pleasant to encounter those less intelligent than ourselves. We love them instantly. The world is rough for the clever; ordinary folk dislike them, and they hate each other.

But since the clever are such a tiny minority, their unhappiness matters little. As long as the foolish can be comforted, the world functions adequately.

Cats are credited with worldly wisdom, prioritizing their own interests over blind devotion. We are naturally shocked by such selfishness. Cats certainly prefer families with carpets in the kitchen and avoid households with many children. Yet, overall, cats are libeled. Make a friend of one, and she sticks to you through thick and thin. Every cat I have owned has been a firm comrade. One followed me everywhere until I begged her to stop, and another would wait up for me late at night, meeting me in the passage, making me feel like a married man, except she never asked where I had been and disbelieved my excuses.

Another cat drank regularly, lurking outside the cellar to lap up beer drippings. I mention this not to praise the species but to illustrate their humanity. If transmigration of souls exists, this cat was rapidly qualifying for Christianity; her vanity rivaled her love of drink. When she caught a large rat, she would drag it into the room, lay it in the center, and wait for praise. The girls would scream.

Poor rats! They seem to exist solely so cats and dogs can gain credit for killing them, and chemists can profit from poisons. Yet, there is something fascinating about them. They possess a weird, uncanny quality. Cunning, strong, numerous, cruel, and secretive, they swarm in deserted houses, whispering of doom in their hiding places. They know when a ship sinks and vanish into the unknown. They haunt tales of horror, scampering through ghostly rooms, their teeth gnawing behind wainscots, their eyes gleaming through tapestries.

Dying prisoners in dark dungeons see their small red eyes like glowing coals and hear the rush of their clawed feet, shrieking in the silence.

I love tales of rats. The story of Bishop Hatto is particularly lovely. The wicked bishop hoarded corn, refusing to feed the starving, then burned them in his barn. The next day, thousands of rats arrived to judge him. He fled to his tower on the Rhine, thinking himself safe, but the rats swam the river, gnawed through the stone walls, and ate him alive.

"There have whetted their teeth against the stones, And now they pick the bishop's bones..."

Then there is the Pied Piper of Hamelin. First he piped the rats away, then, when the mayor broke his promise, he led the children into the mountains. What a curious legend! I wonder what it means. There is something deep beneath the rhyme. The image of the piper playing through the narrow streets, children following with dancing feet and eager faces, haunting me. The old folks try to stop them, but the children ignore them, drawn by the witching music. Games are abandoned, toys dropped, and they wander away, heedless of where they are going.

Sometimes I wonder if the Pied Piper is still alive, roaming our streets softly so only children hear him. Why do their faces look so grave when they pause? They shake their heads and laugh when questioned, but I suspect they have heard the magic music and perhaps glimpsed his odd figure. Even we adults hear his piping now and then, though the noisy world drowns it out. One day, the sweet strains will sound clear, and we too will abandon our playthings and follow, knowing the song's meaning.

I wish people could love animals without becoming maudlin. Women are the worst offenders, but even men degrade pets through absurd idolatry. Young ladies who have read "David Copperfield" seek out nondescript dogs with irritating habits, talking sweet prattle to them and kissing their noses, especially when young men are around. Old ladies worship fat, flea-ridden poodles, washing their faces and feeding them mutton cutlets. Some families center their entire lives on the dog.

Cats rarely suffer from excess adulation; they possess a sense of the ridiculous and will firmly put their paw down on nonsense. Dogs, however, encourage it. Consequently, in certain circles, the dog's every action becomes the topic of conversation from morning till night. The family sits in a row, watching him, recounting anecdotes, recalling how he was once rescued from the butcher-boy by the scruff of his neck. After recovering from these memories, they admire the brute until someone swoops down in a frenzy of affection, clutching the dog and slobbering over it, while the others rise in envy to seize whatever remains.

With these people, everything revolves around the dog. To make love to the eldest daughter, borrow the garden roller, or get the mother to subscribe to the Society for the Suppression of Solo-Cornet Players, you must first gain the dog's approval. If the animal, warped by unnatural treatment, snaps at you, your cause is lost forever.

"If Fido won't take to any one," the father remarks thoughtfully, "I say that man is not to be trusted. Ah! he knows, bless him."

Drat him!

To think that the surly brute was once an innocent puppy, full of fun and ambition to bark like his mother. Ah me! Life changes us all. The world is a vast grinding machine that turns fresh, bright things into old, crabbed ones.

Look at Pussy Sobersides, with her dull, sleepy glance and dignified airs. Who could imagine she was once the blue-eyed, whirling firework of a kitten?

What marvelous vitality a kitten possesses! Life bubbles over in them. They rush, mew, spring, dance on hind legs, embrace everything, roll over, and kick. They are so full of life they don't know what to do with themselves.

Can you remember, reader, those glorious days of fresh young manhood? When walking home on a moonlit road, you felt too full of life for sober walking, skipping and shouting until belated farmers' wives thought you mad? Oh, that magnificent young LIFE! That crowned us kings of the earth, rushing through our veins, thrilling our brains, and urging us to conquer the world. It welled up in our hearts, making us long to embrace all mankind. Those were grand days, when our blood cried out like a war-horse for battle. Now our pulse beats slowly, our joints ache, and we love our easy-chairs and pipes, sneering at boys' enthusiasm. But oh, for one brief moment of that god-like life again!
