A fortnight later, the air of the Dominican convent in the suburbs of Gouda grew heavy with the arrival of a pale, bowed specter. This stranger sought audience with Brother Ambrose, the deputy governor who held the reins of the house since the prior's recent death, awaiting the arrival of his successor. The intruder was Gerard, a man come to terminate the life he had begun in such turmoil. Though he presented himself as a novice on probation, the truth lay deeper in his weary bones: he was a failing man, acutely aware of his own dissolution, seeking only the peace of death amidst the kindness of Ambrose and the silent fellowship of brothers whose doors and hearts had always welcomed him. Yet, his old manse felt like a prison of ghosts; it was too saturated with the lingering echoes of her presence to sustain him further. Ambrose, recognizing the depth of his friend's value and the crushing weight of his sorrow, harbored a tender hope that healing might restore him to his parish. In this spirit of mercy, he assigned Gerard a comfortable cell above the gateway and strictly forbade fasting or any form of ascetic penance. But fate intervened swiftly; within mere days, the new prior arrived, bringing with him the frost of a very Tartar disposition. Initially consumed by the zeal to purge abuses and tighten the rigid chains of discipline, his nature shifted abruptly when news reached him that the vicar of Gouda had entered the order as a novice. With a sharp dismissal, he declared, "'Tis well; let him first surrender his vicarage, then remain—or depart. I will have no fat parsons in my house." The prior summoned Gerard, who complied, and the moment their eyes met across the threshold, a shock rippled through the room. "Clement!" cried one. "Jerome!" answered the other.
