While the good old Bishop of Cambray, in that long-popular romance of his, wrote at length of Calypso’s disconsolation when Ulysses set sail for home, I do not recall if he spared a thought for her lady’s maid, left equally desolate when Odysseus’s own gentleman took his leave. The masters and mistresses might cling to one another in the grand saloon, breaking their hearts over last embraces, but the menials would be huddled snivelling in the fore-cabin too, their own hearts just as heavy. When the last bell clanged and Ulysses’s mate bawled “All ashore that’s going ashore!” Calypso and her waiting-woman would both step over the same swaying gangplank, hearts thudding, eyes streaming; both would wave handkerchiefs of very different quality and thread from the quay, as the crew crowded the ship’s bows cheering, and the onlookers on shore huzzaed the departing voyage. The point to mark here is simple: if Calypso ne pouvait se consoler, Calypso’s maid ne pouvait se consoler non plus. Their grief was of the exact same measure. One would weep in marble halls on her return, the other in the servants’ attic, but their tears were equally salt, equally plentiful.

Harry Warrington did not quit Castlewood alone a victim to love. His body servant Gumbo left the estate equally smitten, his wit, easy good humour, and skills at dancing, cookery, and fiddle-playing having made him the darling of the entire female domestic circle. The male servants might grumble and eye him jealously, but every woman on the place was on his side. In that age, there was none of the cruel prejudice against Black people that would fester in later years; they were not granted full equality, certainly, but they met with general good-natured tolerance, and from women, a far more generous kindness still. Do not women always lean to tenderness for the most marginal among men? They will dote on moral hunchbacks, cherish figurative “negroes” of low wit, and cuddle the dullest, misshapenest souls no one else will suffer. Gumbo was treated as gently as many a white servant far better placed than he, and only the men in the servants’ hall rejoiced to see the young Virginian and his retinue depart.

One can easily picture those last goodbyes: Molly the housemaid slipping out at grey dawn to cull a wistful posy of jasmine and wild roses for Gumbo, Betty the kitchen maid snipping off a thick lock of her chestnut ringlets to trade for a tuft of his woolly hair, even as he spun tales of being the direct descendant of Ashantee kings. Judging by how many of their descendants claim royal blood now, there must have been a vast number of such potent sovereigns scattered across Caffraria, Connaught, and every other land of hereditary bondsmen in old times.

On the morning fixed for Madame de Bernstein’s departure, every servant at Castlewood crowded the passages and doorways: some to catch one last look at her ladyship’s footmen and the irresistible Gumbo, some to bid farewell to her personal maid, and all to waylay the Baroness and her nephew for the parting gratuities that were customary for wealthy guests to distribute far and wide to household staff. Both Harry and his aunt gave liberally to the liveried footmen, the black-ruffled upper servants, and the swarm of maids and scullions. Castlewood had been the Baroness’s childhood home, and as for honest Harry, who had lived free of charge under its roof and won both money and horses from his cousin and the unlucky chaplain, he was naturally open-handed, and felt certain his mother would wish him to be generous to all the Esmond family’s retainers. He scattered gold coins right and left as freely as Gumbo had boasted he could, no one coming near him left empty-handed: from the major-domo down to the boy who blacked boots, from the grim housekeeper in her still-room to the feeble old porter dozing in his lodge, every soul got a token of his remembrance. It is a well-known truth: when a man is in love with one woman in a family, he grows absurdly fond of every single person connected to her. He ingratiates himself with the maids, is extra bland to the butler, lends money to the college son, pats yapping little dogs he would otherwise kick out of the way, laughs at stale old stories that would make him yawn to hear from anyone but his beloved’s father, and even bears the sharp nagging of the cantankerous old maiden aunt with a smile.

For the days past, Harry had been displaying all those artless little marks of affection to the entire Castlewood household, ever since his love for Lady Maria had been declared and returned. He would have happily given cousin Will back all the money he won, and more, too, if the lad had asked for it – but for all that, Harry was sharp, fond of sport and fair play, and a fine judge of a good horse. He had won not just Will’s cash and a stack of Mr Esmond’s valuable old autographs, but also Will’s prized brown hunter, the very animal that had nearly pushed him into the moat on his first night at the estate. He had seen how fast and sure-footed the beast was, and for all his moon-eyed romance over Maria, he could not help being pleased to own such a sound, swift roadster. When he had spent half the night staring at the stars above her window, watching her candle burn low, he had trundled off to his own bedroom daydreaming of how perfect it would be to ride that horse across the island, his beautiful Maria seated pillion behind him, her white arms wrapped tight around his waist. He fell asleep ruminating on those very plans, counting the blessings of the week more he would spend in her company.

Early that morning, poor Chaplain Sampson sent his little black mare over to the house, led by his groom, footman, and gardener, who all wept over the beast’s white nose and kissed it repeatedly before handing it over to Gumbo. The sight of their emotion made both men tearful too: Gumbo snivelled outright, and Harry pressed a couple of guineas into the chaplain’s boy’s hand to comfort him, stunning the poor fellow into instant joy. The two servants led the mare off to the stables, with orders to bring it round alongside Will’s horse after breakfast, when the Baroness’s carriages were due to depart.

Lord Castlewood himself put in an appearance at the morning meal, so eager to see his aunt off that he spared the company his usual sulk. The ladies and the chaplain were all present; the only missing member of the family was Will, who left a badly-spelled note for his cousin explaining he had been called away to Salisbury Races at dawn, but that the horse Harry had won the night before was already set aside, and his own groom Tom would hand it over to Harry’s servant. His absence did not dampen the party’s good humour over tea, and before long the heavy carriages rumbled into the courtyard, the servants piling them high with the Baroness’s vast collection of luggage – vast as it was, it paled next to the overstuffed baggage trains that follow even a modest lady of fashion in our own day.

A large open landau carried the stout Baroness and her niece, two armed menservants perched on the box with loaded pistols and a blunderbuss ready in case of highwaymen. A second carriage held the ladies’ maids and a guard for the trunks, while Harry’s modest valises were stowed under the maids’ care. Gumbo announced he would ride his new mare alongside the carriages for most of the journey.

Lord Castlewood, his stepmother, and Lady Fanny escorted their kinswoman to the carriage steps, bidding her farewell with many dutiful embraces. Then Lady Maria appeared, clad in a riding dress Harry thought was the most beautiful garment ever stitched. A crowd of servants stood about the courtyard calling down blessings on the travellers, and word of the Baroness’s departure had spread through the village, so scores of villagers waited under the trees outside the gates, waving their hats and huzzaing as the ponderous vehicles rolled away.

Gumbo had gone to fetch Harry’s horses, and Lord Castlewood strolled back and forth across the courtyard with his arm tucked through his young guest’s. “Heard you’re taking a couple of our horses away from Castlewood, cousin Harry?” he said.

Harry blushed. “A gentleman can’t refuse a fair game at cards, my lord. I never wanted to play for money, and wouldn’t have, if cousin Will hadn’t forced the matter. As for the chaplain, it went to my heart to win from him – but he was as eager to bet as Will was.”

“I know, I know, no blame attaches to you. When in Rome, you do as the Romans do. And I’m very glad you’ve given Will a lesson. He’s mad for play – would gamble his own coat off his back, and the family has had to settle his debts a dozen times over. How much did you win off him, may I ask?”

“Some eighteen pounds the first two days, a note for a hundred and twenty more, and the brown horse, which we valued at sixty – that comes nigh to two hundred. But it was all fair, you know, I didn’t want to play at all. Will’s no match for me, my lord, that’s the plain truth.”

“He’s a match for most other folks, though,” said my lord. “The brown horse, you said?”

“Yes! Prince William, sired out of Constitution. You don’t think I’d have set sixty pounds against his bay plough-horse, my lord?”

“I didn’t know the breed, that’s all. I saw Will riding out at dawn this morning – I didn’t pay much mind to what horse he was on. And you won the black mare from the parson?”

“Fourteen pounds’ worth. She’ll carry Gumbo perfectly well. Where the devil is that rascal with the horses?” Harry’s mind was already fixed on lovely Maria, and he ached to trot at her side.

“When you get to Tunbridge Wells, cousin Harry, you’d best keep your wits about you. There are sharper players there than Will and the chaplain. All sorts of queer company haunts the Wells.”

“A Virginian learns pretty well how to take care of himself, my lord,” Harry replied, with a knowing nod.

“So it seems! I commend my sister to your care, Harry. She’s no baby in years, but she’s as innocent as one. You’ll see no harm comes to her?”

“I’ll guard her with my very life, my lord!” Harry cried, his heart thrumming with joy.

“You’re a brave lad. By the way, cousin, unless you’re very fond of Castlewood, I wouldn’t hurry back to this drafty, tumble-down old place. I’m off to my other estate for a while, and won’t be back here till partridge shooting season. You stay and see to the ladies, my sister and the Baroness, will you?”

“Indeed I will!” Harry said, glowing.

“I’ll send you word when it’s time to bring my sister back home. Ah, here come the horses now. Have you bid farewell to the Countess and Lady Fanny? They’re kissing their hands to you from the music room balcony.”

Harry ran up to make his goodbyes, cutting the ceremony as short as he could, for he was desperate to be off to his beloved. He came bounding back down the stairs to mount his new prize steed, which Gumbo was holding by the rein while perched atop the chaplain’s little black mare.

There was Gumbo on the black mare, right enough – but the second horse he held was not a brown one. It was a broken-kneed, aged, worn-out bay nag.

“What in the name of hell is this?” Harry cried.

“Your honour’s new horse,” said Will’s groom, touching his cap.

“This brute?” the young gentleman roared, uttering several unprintable oaths common to the age in England and Virginia. “Go and bring me Prince William, the brown horse my cousin Will won off me last night, right now.”

“Mr Will rode Prince William away to Salisbury Races this very morning,” the groom said, unflappable. “His last orders were, ‘Sam, saddle my bay horse Cato for Mr Warrington. He’s Mr Warrington’s horse now, I sold him last night.’ I know your honour’s generous – I hope you’ll remember the groom for his trouble.”

Lord Castlewood burst out laughing at the speech, while Harry fumed and swore all the more.

“Mr Will said he never could bring himself to part with Prince William for less than a hundred and twenty pounds,” the groom added, staring Harry dead in the eye.

Lord Castlewood laughed harder. “Will has got the better of you, Harry Warrington.”

“Got the better of me? Any rascal with loaded dice can throw sixes and get the better of an honest man. This isn’t betting, this is downright –”

“Mr Warrington! Spare me hard words about my brother, if you please. Rest assured I’ll make sure you get your due. Farewell now – ride quick, or the coaches will be at Farnham before you catch up,” he said, waving Harry off and stepping back into the house, unaware that Harry was far too eager to rejoin Maria and the carriages to notice the utter, besotted adoration Gumbo was casting across to a pretty maid lingering in the porter’s lodge doorway.

Once the two young men were gone, the chaplain and Lord Castlewood sat back down to finish their breakfast in peace. The two ladies had already retreated indoors.

“That was one of Will’s confounded rascally tricks,” Lord Castlewood said. “I wouldn’t be surprised if our cousin breaks Will’s head over it, when he catches up with him.”

“He does deserve it,” the chaplain grinned. “Though when we were playing cards last night, no one ever said a word about what colour the horse was. I couldn’t argue the point – I’ve only got the one mare, and the black lad is riding off with her. To do him justice, the young Virginian plays like a man, never caring if he loses.”

“He wins because he doesn’t care about losing, plain and simple. There’s no doubt he’s rolling in wealth. His mother’s law agents are my lawyers too, and they write that her estate is a perfect principality, growing richer year by year.”

“If it were a kingdom, I know exactly who Mr Warrington would make its queen,” the chaplain said, obsequious.

“Who can account for taste, parson?” Lord Castlewood sneered. “We’re all the same. The first woman I fell in love with was forty, and as jealous as a girl of fifteen. It runs in the family – Colonel Esmond, the one in the scarlet coat yonder, married my grandmother, who was old enough to be his mother. If this lad chooses to carry an elderly princess back to Virginia with him, we’ve no call to balk him.”

“‘Twere a consummation devoutly to be wished!” the chaplain cried. “Had I not best travel to Tunbridge Wells myself, my lord, and wait on hand to perform the sacred marriage ceremony for the young couple at a moment’s notice?”

“You shall have a pair of fine new carriage horses if you do,” Lord Castlewood promised. The two men settled back to smoke their post-breakfast pipe of tobacco, perfectly content.

Harry was in such a hurry to join the carriages he nearly forgot to tip his hat to the cheering Castlewood villagers. They all adored the frank, good-natured lad, whose grandfather Colonel Esmond was still remembered locally for his gallantry in Queen Anne’s wars. The village had heard how the young Virginian had out-ridden, out-shot, and out-played Will at everything, and their respect for him was doubled by the wild tales of his enormous fortune that Gumbo had spread across the county. Rumour had it he owned gold mines, diamond mines, endless furs and tobacco fields, enough wealth to make him a prince in his own country. No wonder the honest villagers cheered him so loudly, for there is no land on earth where good fortune is more warmly celebrated than in Britain.

Harry spurred his sorry old nag into a gallop, Gumbo trailing behind him, until he caught sight of the cloud of dust surrounding the Baroness’s chariot. He rode up to the carriage window, and found Lady Maria sitting all alone on the back seat, the Baroness fast dozing on the cushions beside her. She pressed a finger to her lips, warning him not to wake the old woman, and Harry hung back a little behind the wheels, drinking in the sight of her smiles and her bright eyes. The summer landscape was all very well, but it was nothing next to her face; the hedgerow birds sang no half so sweet as her soft, quiet words.

The Baroness’s fat old horses were used to slow, easy paces and frequent stops for feeding, so even on his broken-down nag, Harry had no trouble keeping up with them. At two o’clock they stopped at an inn to bait the horses and eat a two-hour dinner. Harry tipped the landlord so generously the man could not stop bowing, and he spent the entire meal whispering nonsense sweet nothings to Maria, while the Baroness napped peacefully after her roast. They spoke of the crowds of beautiful young people they would meet at Tunbridge Wells, and timid Maria teased Harry that he would soon fall for some pretty young heiress far more worthy of him than her plain self. Harry swore by every god in the pantheon that not even Venus herself could lure him from her side – no, it was he who had to fear, he protested, that the fine young fops of fashion would crowd around her and forget the rough, untravelled American lad who had nothing but a faithful heart to offer her.

Maria laughed, rolled her eyes, and teased him that it was men, not women, who were the faithless, flighty sex – and in his outrage at hearing his whole gender slandered, Harry stretched across the table to grab her hand, knocking over a full glass of wine in the process. Maria gave a little shocked “Oh!” and glanced over to see if the crash had woken the Baroness – but the old woman still slumbered soundly in her armchair, so she let Harry squeeze her fingers tight, while he swore eternal loyalty to her.
