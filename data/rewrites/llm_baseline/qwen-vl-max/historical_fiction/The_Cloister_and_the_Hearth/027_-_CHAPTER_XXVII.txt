The worthy physician returned home, his face grimacing with pain, and told his housekeeper he was suffering from “a bad burn.” Those were the precise words. For in matters of phlogiston as in matters of morality, we often scorch others’ fingers while our own are left blistered. His housekeeper, a woman of old-fashioned remedies, applied a poultice as mild as milk. He endured it without complaint—like a lamb beneath her care—his sole concern being the healing of his patient. Meanwhile, he drafted a bill for broken vials and took swift steps to have the travelers arrested. Before a magistrate, he swore under oath that the strangers, owing him money, intended to flee the town at once. Alas, it was not his lucky day. His honest effort to lie was thwarted by an unforeseen truth: he had spoken the truth—and in an affidavit.

When the officers arrived at “The Silver Lion,” they found the guests had vanished. They hastened down to the riverbank, gathered intelligence from the locals, and set off upriver in hot pursuit.

This narrow escape owed itself entirely to Denys’s quick thinking. After a burst of laughter—so hearty it seemed a tonic—he repeated his favorite phrase three times, then turned serious. “Dusseldorf is no place for us,” he declared. “That old man left unnaturally quiet for someone so talkative. We’re strangers here; the bailiff is his friend. In five minutes, we’ll be locked in a dungeon for assaulting a local dignitary. Are you strong enough to limp to the river? It’s close by. Once there, you can lie down in a boat instead of a bed. What difference does it make?”

“What difference, Denys?” Gerard replied, already stirred. “None at all—except that it favors the boat. I long for Rome; Rome leads me to Sevenbergen. And when we reach it, we shall lie in a boat—but on the Rhine, the famous Rhine, cool and refreshing. I feel its breeze already. Just seeing it will cure my little hop-'o-my-thumb fever. Away! Away!”

Seeing his friend so eager, Denys quickly settled with the landlord and they rushed toward the river. To their dismay, they learned the public ferry had departed half an hour earlier, and no other vessel would leave that afternoon. But after many questions and a growing crowd, they secured a private boat from an old man and his two sons.

A bystander mocked them. “The current is too strong for three oars.”

“Then my comrade and I will help row,” said the invalid.

“No need,” replied the old man. “Bless your silly heart—*he owns the other boat.*”

A strong wind blew from behind. The boatmen raised a broad sail and rowed with vigor, sending the craft skimming along at a brisk pace.

“Are you feeling better, lad, from the river air?” Denys asked.

“Much better. Though truth be told, the doctor did me good.”

“The doctor? You refused every one of his cures.”

“No, but—I know you’ll say I’m mad—but knocking that old fool down… somehow… it soothed me.”

“Amiable dove!” Denys laughed. “How your character unfolds each day, like a rosebud. I misjudged you at first.”

“Do not mistake me, Denys. I could have borne his idle threats—though his voice pierced my ears. But he was an infidel, or near enough. Such men I’ve been taught to despise. Did he not imply we owed our very insides to men with long Greek names, rather than to Him, whose name is but a syllable, yet whose hand rests over all the earth? Pagan!”

“So you knocked him down—like a good Christian,” Denys teased.

“Now, Denys, do not jest. Do not take the side of a villain. Had it been a thunderbolt from Heaven, he would have met his due. Yet he only received a poor blow from this weak arm.”

“What weak arm?” Denys asked, eyes twinkling.

“I’ve lived among arms, and by Samson’s strength, I’ve never seen one more like a catapult. That bolster wrapped around his nose, the ends kissed behind his head, and his forehead rang—had he been Goliath or Caesar, he’d have fallen. St. Denys protect me from such feeble foes as you—and above all, from your weak arms—you diabolical young hypocrite.”

The river wound through many bends, sometimes shifting the wind to their side. Then all moved to the weather side to prevent the boat from leaning too far—except a child of about five, the boatman’s grandson and his pet. The boy had slipped aboard just as they cast off. So light he was, he didn’t affect the balance, and thus remained untouched by the laws of navigation.

They sailed cheerfully onward, unaware that a whole posse of constables, armed with the bailiff’s writ, pursued them closely. Their hunters were gaining ground, for if the wind was strong, so was the current.

Then Gerard suddenly realized their course was not toward Burgundy. “Oh, Denys,” he said, alarmed, “this isn’t your way.”

“I know,” Denys replied calmly. “But what can I do? I cannot leave you until the fever leaves you—and it hasn’t. You grow red and pale by turns. I’ve watched you. I must go on to Cologne, I fear, and then cross country.”

“Thank Heaven!” Gerard exclaimed joyfully. Then, with a touch of self-deception, he added, “It would be a sin to be so near Cologne and not see it. Oh, man, it’s a vast, ancient city—just as I’ve dreamed of, though never seen. Me miserable, what cruel fortune brings me here now? Well, Denys,” he continued, less warmly, “it’s old enough to have been founded by a Roman lady in the first century, sacked by Attila the Hun, and later damaged by Norman Lothaire. It has a church for every week of the year—plus countless chapels and convents. Above all, the great minster, still unfinished, where lie the Three Kings who brought gifts to Christ: Melchior with gold, Gaspar with frankincense, and Balthazar, the black king, with myrrh. Over their bones stands the shrine—a wonder of the world—of ever-shining brass brighter than gold, adorned with finely wrought images, inlaid with intricate designs and rich colors. Two broad stripes run across it, studded with jewels so large and rare, each could crown a king or ransom a prince. On top stand the three kings, skillfully crafted—two in solid silver, gilded, bare-headed; the third, of Ethiopian ebony, wearing a golden crown. In the center, the Blessed Virgin, made of virgin silver, holds Christ in her arms. At the corners, golden branches hold four wax tapers burning night and day. Holy eyes have tended that flame for ages, and holy eyes shall guard it forever. I tell you, Denys, the oldest songs, the oldest Flemish and German legends, speak of those flames burning—yes, they shall light the earth to its grave.”

“And St. Ursel’s Church,” Gerard went on, “where lies a British saint. Her bones rest there, along with eleven thousand virgins who died for the faith—slain by Moorish barbarians on October 23, 238. Their bones are piled in vaults, many skulls displayed in the church. St. Ursel’s skull is encased in thin gold and placed on the high altar—shown only to humble Christians on solemn days.”

“Eleven thousand virgins!” Denys cried. “What tiny babies German men must have been in those days. I wish all their bones might turn to flesh again, and their skulls to sweet faces—should we pass through the gates. ‘Tis likely some of them are tired of their state by now.”

“Tush, Denys!” Gerard rebuked. “Why must you, being good, always seem evil? If your wishing-cap is on, pray that we meet the least of all those wise virgins in the next life—and for that, let us honor their holy dust in this one.”

“And then there’s the church of the Maccabees,” Gerard continued, “and the cauldron where they and their mother Solomona were boiled by a wicked king for refusing to eat pork.”

“Oh, peremptory king! And pig-headed Maccabees!” Denys chuckled. “I’d gladly eat bacon with my pork rather than swap places with my meat at the fire.”

“What vile words!” Gerard snapped. “It was their faith.”

“Nay, bridle your temper. Tell me—besides churches—are there nothing else in this city you praise? For I prefer Sir Knight to Sir Priest.”

“Ay, marry,” Gerard said. “There’s an university nearly a hundred years old. A marketplace no fairer in the world, with houses at the corners as grand as palaces. A magnificent senate-house covered in statues, and at the front stands a figure of stout Herman Gryn—a soldier like yourself, lad.”

“Ay! Tell me of him! What deed earned him his place?”

“A rare one. He slew a lion in fair combat, with only his cloak and short sword. He thrust the cloak into the beast’s mouth and cut its spine in two. There stands his effigy—and the lion’s—to prove it. Such a feat has been done by only three others: Samson, Lysimachus of Macedon, and Benaiah, captain of David’s host.”

“Marry! Three tall fellows. I’d like to sup with them tonight.”

“So would not I,” Gerard replied dryly.

“But tell me,” Denys said, surprised, “when were you in Cologne?”

“Never—but in spirit. I converse with the monks along the way. They tell me all things old and new.”

“Ay, ay,” Denys grinned. “Have I not seen your nose peeking beneath their cowls? When you speak of things unseen, your words are small, the thing great. But your words match or surpass the thing. You’re a fine painter with your tongue—I’ve said it. And as a saint, ready with hand, steel, or bolster—as any sinner alive. Now, shall I tell you what draws me to Cologne?”

“Ay, Denys.”

“You—only you. Not a dead saint, but my living friend, my true comrade. You alone draw Denys of Burgundy to Cologne.”

Gerard lowered his head.

At that moment, one of the younger boatmen asked sharply, “What’s wrong with ‘little turnip-face’?”

The child—thus nicknamed—had just stepped aft, solemn as a judge, then burst into sudden tears.

He was subjected to a barrage of questions—some gentle, some sharp. Had he hurt himself? Eaten too much? Been frightened? Cold? Sick? An idiot?

To each, he replied with equal fervor: *Oh! oh! oh!*—in English, or *Hi! hi! hi!*—in French. So fixed are fiction’s phonetics.

“Who knows what ails the pestilent brat?” snarled the young boatman. “Rather look this way and tell me whom those men are following!”

The old man and his son glanced over and saw four men walking along the east bank. At once, they stopped rowing and huddled in the stern, whispering and glancing between passengers and pursuers.

They might have done better to ponder the mystery of the turnip-faced child. I beg pardon—by my age, I mean the profound mind of fearless infancy.

“If it’s as I suspect,” whispered one youth, “why not give them a fright? Let’s head for the west bank.”

The old man objected fiercely. “Run into trouble for strangers? Are you mad? No. Rather, let’s cross to the east side—keep the strong arm with us! That’s my counsel. What say you, Werter?”

“I say, please yourselves.”

What age and youth could not decide, the wind settled impartially. A squall struck, and the boat leaned violently. The men scrambled to windward to steady it—only to see, horror-struck, a ditch of water rushing from stem to stern. Then, in an instant, they saw nothing—only felt the cold, rushing Rhine.

“Turnip-face” had pulled the plug.

The officers untied the cords from their waists. Gerard could swim like a duck—but even the best swimmer, flung from a capsized boat, must sink before he can rise. Dark water bubbled over his head. Then he surfaced, dazed and deaf for a moment. Next, he saw the black hull upside down, figures clinging to it. He shook his head like a dog, and instinctively swam toward it. But just before reaching it, he heard a voice behind him—low, but filled with deep anguish—cry, “Adieu, comrade, adieu!”

He turned—and there was Denys, sinking, sinking, weighed down by his heavy arbalest. Pale, eyes wide, staring desperately at his dear friend.

Gerard screamed—a cry of love and terror—and lunged forward, cutting through the water wildly. But the next moment, Denys was gone beneath the waves.

Then Gerard followed.

The officers tied a rope and threw the end into the churning water.
