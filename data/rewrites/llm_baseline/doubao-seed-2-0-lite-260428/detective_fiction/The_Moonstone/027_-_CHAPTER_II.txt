Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite made his entrance the way he does absolutely everything else: perfectly, precisely timed. He didn’t crowd the servant so closely that he startled the whole room, nor linger far enough behind to leave us fumbling through awkward silences with the front door hanging open. This, if you ask me, is the mark of the true devout Christian: every small detail of daily life executed to absolute perfection. This dear man was nothing if not complete.

“Go tell Miss Verinder Mr. Ablewhite has arrived,” my aunt instructed the footman, before we both leaned in to ask after his health, both speaking at once to check if he’d recovered from that terrible ordeal the week prior. With immaculate tact, he answered both of us in the same breath: Lady Verinder got his polite spoken reply, and I got his soft, charming smile.

“Dearest aunt, dearest Miss Clack—what have I done to deserve all this kindness?” he said, his voice brimming with gentle warmth. “I was only mistaken for another man, after all. Blindfolded, half-strangled, thrown flat on my back onto a thin carpet stretched over a floor hard enough to break your bones. Think how much worse it could have gone! I could have been murdered, robbed. What did I lose? Nothing but a little nervous force—and the law doesn’t even count that as property, so strictly speaking, I lost nothing at all. If I’d had my way, I’d have kept the whole mess to myself; I hate all this fuss and public attention. But Mr. Luker made his own injuries public, and mine were dragged out into the papers right after as an unavoidable side effect. I’m public property now, until every last gentle reader gets sick of hearing my name. I’m sick of it already, myself. I hope they tire of it soon! And how is our dear Rachel? Still enjoying the London season? That’s lovely news. Miss Clack, I must beg your indulgence: I’m dreadfully behind on my committee work and my wonderful ladies, but I absolutely promise to stop by the Mothers’ Small-Clothes drive next week. Did Monday’s committee meeting go well? Is the board optimistic about our work? Are we well-stocked for trousers yet?”

That heavenly, soft smile of his made his little apologies impossible to resist. The rich, deep timbre of his voice lent its own quiet charm to that perfectly reasonable question about our charitable supplies. To tell the truth, we were far too well stocked with trousers—we were positively drowning in them. I was just about to say as much when the door flew open again, and a whirlwind of unseemly worldliness burst into the room in the shape of Miss Rachel Verinder.

She practically ran across the room to Mr. Godfrey, her hair shockingly unkempt, her face flushed what I can only call unbecomingly bright.
“I’m so glad you’re here, Godfrey,” she said, addressing him in the offhand, casual tone one young man might use to another—so unladylike I grieve to write it down. “I only wish you’d brought Mr. Luker with you. As long as this public excitement lasts, you two are the most interesting men in all of London. I know that’s morbid, unhealthy, exactly the sort of thing a properly regulated mind like mine would shudder at. Never mind that. Tell me the full story of what happened in Northumberland Street right now. The newspapers definitely left half the details out.”

Even dear Mr. Godfrey bears a tiny scrap of the fallen nature we all inherit from Adam—hardly a sliver, but alas, it’s there. It grieved me no end to see him take both of Rachel’s hands in his own, lay them softly against the left side of his waistcoat, coddling her reckless, rude tone and her disrespectful little nod to me.
“Dearest Rachel,” he said, that same warm tone that had thrilled me when he spoke of our trouser stock and our charitable work, “the newspapers have told you everything. And they told it far better than I ever could.”

“Godfrey thinks we’re all making far too big a fuss over it,” my aunt put in. “He just said he hates talking about the incident.”

Rachel’s head snapped up, a sharp spark in her eyes. “Why?”
Mr. Godfrey looked down at her with an indulgence so ill-judged and undeserved that I felt compelled to step in.
“Rachel, darling,” I remonstrated gently, “true greatness and true courage are always modest.”

She didn’t so much as glance at me, mind you, still talking to her cousin as if they were two lads on the street. “You’re a good sort in your own way, Godfrey,” she said, “but I’m quite sure you’re not great. I don’t think you have any extraordinary courage at all, and I’m fully convinced if you ever had any modesty, your crowd of adoring lady fans stripped that virtue from you years ago. You’ve got some private reason you won’t talk about that attack in Northumberland Street, and I intend to find out what it is.”

“My reason is the simplest thing in the world, easy to admit,” he said, still humoring her. “I’m just tired of the whole subject.”

“Tired of it? My dear Godfrey, let me tell you something.”
“What is it?”
“You spend far too much time in all-female company, and you’ve picked up two terrible habits from it. You’ve learned to talk absolute nonsense completely seriously, and you’ve gotten into the habit of telling little lies just for the fun of it. You can never be straight with your lady admirers, but you’re going to be straight with me. Come sit over here.”
She actually hauled him across the room to a chair by the window, where the full daylight would fall straight on his face.

I deeply regret having to record such language, such wild, unruly conduct. But hemmed in as I am between Mr. Franklin Blake’s signed cheque on one hand and my sacred duty to tell the full truth on the other, what other choice do I have? I glanced at my aunt. She sat completely still, unmoved, making no move to intervene. I’d never seen that sort of heavy, unresponsive torpor in her before. It was probably just the aftershock of all the stress she’d endured back in Yorkshire. Not a pleasant symptom, for a woman of Lady Verinder’s age, with her full, autumnal build, whatever its cause.

Meanwhile, Rachel had settled herself across from the window with our amiable, far too forbearing Mr. Godfrey. She launched right into the list of blunt questions she’d promised, paying no more mind to her mother or me than if we were not even in the room.
“Have the police done anything at all, Godfrey?”
“Nothing whatsoever.”
“It’s certain, isn’t it, that the three men who attacked you were the exact same three who later laid a trap for Mr. Luker?”
“All logic says yes, my dear Rachel. There’s no room for doubt.”
“And no trace of them has been found at all?”
“Not a single trace.”
“The public thinks, don’t they, that those three men are the same three Indian jugglers who came to our house in Yorkshire?”
“Some people do, yes.”
“Do you think that?”
“Rachel, they blindfolded me before I could see their faces. I know nothing about it at all. How could I possibly form an opinion?”

Even Mr. Godfrey’s angelic patience was starting to fray under her unrelenting grilling, you could see it. I will not presume to guess if unbridled curiosity or ungovernable dread was driving her questions. I only report that when he tried to stand up to leave, she actually grabbed him by both shoulders and pushed him back down into the chair. Oh, do not say this was immodest! Do not even hint that only the recklessness of guilty terror could drive conduct like this. We must not judge others. My Christian friends, indeed, I beg you, we must not judge others.

She carried on with her questions, unabashed. Earnest Bible readers will no doubt be reminded, as I was, of the blinded children of the devil who carried on with their unholy orgies without a shred of shame in the days before the Flood.
“I want to know about Mr. Luker, Godfrey.”
“Again, I have no answers for you. No man alive knows less of him than I do.”
“You never saw him before you two ran into each other at the bank?”
“Never.”
“You’ve seen him since the attacks?”
“Yes. We were both questioned together, then separately, to help the police with their inquiries.”
“Mr. Luker was robbed of a receipt he got from his bank, wasn’t he? What was that receipt for?”
“A valuable gem he’d stored in the bank’s secure vault.”
“That’s what the papers say. That’s enough for the casual reader, but it’s not enough for me. The bank’s receipt must have written down exactly what the gem was, right?”
“From what I’ve heard described, the receipt said nothing of the sort. It only read: a valuable gem belonging to Mr. Luker, deposited by Mr. Luker, sealed with Mr. Luker’s personal seal, to be released only when Mr. Luker comes to collect it in person. That’s the exact wording, and that’s all I know.”

Rachel went quiet for a moment after he spoke. She glanced over at her mother and sighed, then turned her eyes back to Mr. Godfrey and kept going.
“Some of our private family business from Yorkshire ended up in the newspapers, didn’t it?”
“I grieve to say that’s true.”
“All sorts of idle strangers are trying to draw a line between what happened at our house in the country and what’s been happening here in London, aren’t they?”
“I’m afraid public curiosity in some circles has taken that exact turn.”
“The people who say the three men who attacked you and Luker are the Indian jugglers also say that valuable gem…”

She trailed off. Over the last few minutes, she’d grown paler and paler, until the stark black of her hair made that ghastly white face look so unnatural we all thought she was about to faint, right in the middle of her sentence. Dear Mr. Godfrey tried to stand up a second time. My aunt begged her not to push herself any further. I followed with a modest peace offering, a small bottle of smelling salts from my handbag. None of us had the slightest effect on her.
“Godfrey, sit back down. Mamma, there’s no reason at all to panic about me. Clack, you’re dying to hear the rest of the story, aren’t you? I refuse to faint, just to spite you.”

I wrote those exact words down in my diary the second I got home. But oh, do not judge! My Christian friends, do not judge!

She turned back to Mr. Godfrey, that terrible, unshakable obstinacy still set on her face, and picked up exactly where she’d left off.
“A minute ago I was talking about what people are saying around town. Tell me plainly, Godfrey—do any of them claim that Mr. Luker’s valuable gem is the Moonstone?”

The second the name of the cursed Indian Diamond left her lips, I saw a sharp change come over my admirable friend. His face flushed dark. He lost that genial, easy suavity that’s his greatest charm, and a noble, righteous indignation lit his reply.
“They do say it,” he answered. “There are people out there who do not hesitate to call Mr. Luker a liar, just to serve their own petty interests. He has solemnly sworn over and over again that he never even heard the name of the Moonstone until this vile scandal targeted him. And these shameless people answer back, not a single scrap of proof to their names, that he has his own reasons to hide the truth, that they will not believe him even on his oath. Disgraceful! Absolutely disgraceful!”

Rachel stared at him very strangely, a look I can hardly describe, the whole time he was speaking. When he finished, she said, “Considering Mr. Luker is nothing but a random acquaintance of yours, you are defending his cause awfully warmly, Godfrey.”

My gifted, pious friend gave her one of the most truly evangelical answers I have ever heard in my life.
“I hope, Rachel, I defend the cause of every oppressed person, no matter who they are, warmly.”

That tone he used could have melted a stone. But what is the hardness of a stone? Nothing at all, next to the hardness of an unregenerate human heart. She sneered. I blush to record it—she sneered right in his face.
“Save those grand noble speeches for your ladies’ committees, Godfrey. I’m sure that scandal that’s been hounding Mr. Luker hasn’t spared you, either.”

Even my aunt’s strange torpor was jolted awake by those words. “My dear Rachel,” she said, shocked, “you have no right to say such a thing!”
“I don’t mean any harm, mamma,” Rachel said. “I mean well. Just give me a moment, and you’ll see.”

She looked back at Mr. Godfrey, with what seemed like a sudden, sharp pity, and even reached the very unladylike length of taking his hand in hers.
“I’m certain I’ve figured out exactly why you didn’t want to talk about this in front of mamma and me. Some stupid accident linked your name to Mr. Luker’s in people’s heads. You told me what scandal says about him. What does scandal say about you?”

Even at that late hour, dear Mr. Godfrey—always ready to return good for evil—tried to spare her. “Don’t ask me,” he said. “It’s better forgotten, Rachel, truly, it is.”

“I will hear it!” she cried out, at the top of her voice.
“Tell her, Godfrey!” my aunt begged. “Nothing could hurt her more than your silence right now!”

Mr. Godfrey’s fine eyes filled with tears. He gave her one last, pleading look, then spoke the fateful words.
“If you insist on hearing it, Rachel—scandal says the Moonstone is pawned to Mr. Luker, and that I am the man who pawned it.”

She leaped to her feet with a scream. She darted frantic glances back and forth between Mr. Godfrey and my aunt, aunt to Godfrey, so wild and unhinged I was fully convinced she’d gone mad.
“Don’t speak to me! Don’t touch me!” she shrieked, shrinking back away from all of us like a hunted animal, into the far corner of the room. “This is my fault! I have to fix it. I sacrificed myself— I had every right to do that, if I wanted to. But to let an innocent man be ruined, to keep a secret that destroys his good name forever—oh good God, it’s too horrible! I can’t bear it!”

My aunt half rose out of her chair, then suddenly sank back down again. She called to me faintly, pointing at a small glass phial in her workbox. “Quick,” she whispered. “Six drops, mixed in water. Don’t let Rachel see.”

Under normal circumstances I would have found that strange, but there was no time to wonder. Only time to get her the medicine. Dear Mr. Godfrey unknowingly helped me hide what I was doing from Rachel, speaking soft, calming words to her across the room.
“Truly, truly, you’re exaggerating,” I heard him say. “My reputation stands far too high to be destroyed by a silly, passing scandal like this. No one will remember it a week from now. Let us never speak of it again.”

She was completely unreachable, even to that generosity. She got more and more distraught. “I have to stop this, I will stop it,” she said. “Mamma! Listen to me. Miss Clack! Listen to me. I know who stole the Moonstone. I know—” she stamped her foot hard, the rage in her shaking her whole frame “—I know for absolute certain that Godfrey Ablewhite is innocent! Take me to the magistrate, Godfrey! Take me there right now, and I will swear it under oath.”

My aunt grabbed my hand and whispered, “Stand between us for a minute or two. Don’t let Rachel see my face.” I noticed a faint bluish tinge to her cheeks that made my blood run cold. She saw how startled I was. “The drops will make me feel right in a minute,” she said, closing her eyes to rest.

All the while, I heard Mr. Godfrey still gently reasoning with her. “You can’t appear publicly in a police matter like this,” he said. “Your reputation, dearest Rachel, is too pure, too sacred, to be dragged through that sort of mess.”

“My reputation!” She burst out laughing, wild and sharp. “Why, I’m accused right alongside you! The best detective officer in all of England says I stole my own family’s Diamond. Ask him what he thinks—he’ll tell you I pawned the Moonstone to pay off my own private debts!”

She stopped short, ran across the room, and dropped to her knees at her mother’s feet. “Oh mamma, mamma, mamma! I must be mad, right? That’s the only reason I haven’t told the truth until now!” She didn’t even notice her mother’s strange state, jumping back to her feet a second later to face Mr. Godfrey again.
“I won’t let you, I won’t let any innocent man be disgraced through my fault. If you won’t take me to the magistrate, write out a formal paper declaring your innocence, and I will sign it. Do what I tell you, Godfrey, or I’ll send the whole story to the newspapers—I’ll go out onto the street and yell it for every stranger to hear!”

We will not call this the language of remorse. We will say it is the language of hysterics. The ever-indulgent Mr. Godfrey pacified her by taking a sheet of paper and writing out the exact declaration she asked for. She signed it in a feverish, trembling hurry.
“Show it to everyone,” she said, handing it to him, “don’t spare a thought for me. I’m afraid, Godfrey, I never gave you enough credit for who you are. You’re far more unselfish, you’re a better man, than I ever believed you were. Come visit me again soon, and I will make up for all the wrong I did you in my head.”
