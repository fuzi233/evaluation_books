As the sun dipped below the rooftops of Manhattan, casting a warm orange glow over the city, Mr. Sillerton Jackson arrived at the Archer residence on West Twenty-eighth Street. He was a man of refined taste and a keen observer of society, and he had developed a system for gathering information about his acquaintances through patient inquiry and scientific observation. His sister, Miss Sophy Jackson, lived with him and served as a sort of antennae for gathering minor gossip, which she would then share with her brother to flesh out his understanding of the social landscape.

Whenever Mrs. Archer needed insight into the latest goings-on, she would invite Mr. Jackson to dine, and as she was a reclusive woman who valued discretion, she only extended this invitation to a select few. Her daughter Janey and herself made excellent listeners, and Mr. Jackson found it more convenient to attend in person rather than send his sister, who was always in high demand. Although he might have preferred evenings when Newland was absent, not because he disliked the young man, but because he sensed that Newland sometimes approached his stories with a critical eye that the ladies of the household lacked.

If perfection were possible, Mr. Jackson would have also requested that Mrs. Archer's cuisine be more elaborate, but alas, New York was divided into two distinct camps: the Mingotts and Mansons, who prized fine dining and material possessions, and the Archer-Newland-van-der-Luyden clan, who prioritized intellectual pursuits and the arts. While the former group offered sumptuous feasts, the latter provided stimulating conversation about literature and art. Mr. Jackson, being an eclectic, would often joke that a visit to the Archer household was just what he needed to diet after a rich meal at the Lovell Mingotts'.

Mrs. Archer lived a simple life with her son Newland and daughter Janey in their modest brownstone. They shared a passion for cultivating rare plants in Wardian cases, creating intricate lace and embroidery, collecting antique ceramics, and reading novels about the human condition. Their tastes were refined, and they preferred stories about everyday people, finding the descriptions of scenery and the characters' emotions more relatable. In contrast, they dismissed Dickens as an author who failed to capture the essence of a gentleman, and while they appreciated Thackeray's wit, they considered him more suited to the world of commerce than the rarefied realm of high society. Bulwer, however, was starting to seem old-fashioned, even to the Archers.
