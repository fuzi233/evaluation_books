There’s a saying—probably by Shakespeare or some other sharp lad—that just when a chap’s feeling particularly spiffing, Fate creeps up behind him and bonks him on the noggin with a lead pipe. Truer words were never spoken, and my own case was no exception. Take the curious affair of Lady Malvern and her offspring, Wilmot. One moment, life was a bowl of cherries. The next, it was a bowl of something far less pleasant.

It all began on a sparkling morning, fresh from a cold shower, when I was feeling roughly two years old and full of beans. My mood was especially buoyant because, for once, I had stood my ground against Jeeves. Normally, the man oppresses me like a tyrannical governess. I didn’t mind so much when he made me ditch one of my new suits—his taste in tailoring is impeccable—but when he tried to veto my beloved cloth-topped boots, it was nearly the last straw. Then came the Great Hat Debate. He was all for the Longacre (as sported by John Drew), while I had set my heart on the Country Gentleman (favored by some other theatrical bloke). After a spirited tussle, I emerged victorious. Thus, as I stood there toweling my spine and humming a jaunty tune, I was practically radiating manly independence.

Then came the knock at the door.

“What ho, out there?”

“Lady Malvern wishes to see you, sir,” intoned Jeeves.

“Who?”

“Lady Malvern, sir. She is waiting in the sitting room.”

“Jeeves, old thing,” I said sternly, for I draw the line at practical jokes before breakfast, “you know perfectly well no one calls at this hour. The sun’s barely up!”

“Her ladyship mentioned arriving from an ocean liner at an ungodly hour, sir.”

Ah. That tracked. I recalled my own ghastly pre-dawn arrival in America the year prior, ejected onto foreign soil before the civilized world had stirred.

“Who *is* Lady Malvern, Jeeves?”

“She did not confide in me, sir.”

“Alone?”

“Accompanied by a Lord Pershore, sir. Presumably her son.”

“Right. Lay out something respectable. I suppose I must dress.”

“The heather-mixture lounge is prepared, sir.”

“Lead me to it.”

As I wrestled with my shirt studs, it struck me. Lady Malvern—a crony of my Aunt Agatha’s! I’d met her at some dismal London luncheon. A fearsome specimen. Wrote books. One about India after the Durbar, full of depressing facts.

Then came the snag.

“Pardon me, sir—not *that* tie.”

I stiffened. Hadn’t I just quelled this insubordination the night before? This was a test.

“What’s wrong with this tie? It’s a perfectly cheerful pink.”

“Unsuitable, sir.”

“Jeeves, this is *my* tie.”

“Very good, sir.”

A frosty silence. The man was wounded, but I stood firm. Tied the offending article, donned the rest, and marched into the sitting room.

“Ah! Mr. Wooster!” Lady Malvern boomed. She was one of those robust, overwhelming women who make a chap feel like a schoolboy caught filching jam. Tallish, but built like a steamer trunk, with bulging eyes, a haystack of yellow hair, and enough teeth to equip a small orchestra. Beside her sat Motty—pale, thin, and blinking like an owl in daylight. His hair, the same shade as his mother’s, was plastered down with mathematical precision. He had the air of a fellow who had been raised in a cupboard.

After the usual how-d’you-dos, Lady Malvern honeyed her way to the point.

“I should be *so* grateful if you’d take dear Motty in while I tour America. A month at most. He’s a quiet boy—vegetarian, teetotaler, devoted to reading.”

I shot Motty a glance. He was gumming his walking stick, utterly vacant. The notion of hosting this human barnacle sent a chill down my spine. But before I could protest, she steamrolled on, detailing her plans to inspect Sing-Sing prison and other cheerful landmarks. Then, with a final effusion of thanks, she swept out, leaving Motty’s luggage—and my doom—behind.

I summoned Jeeves.

“Jeeves! We’ve been saddled with a pill!”

“Pill, sir?”

“The excrescence! Lord Pershore!”

“Ah. Breakfast is ready, sir.”

The man was *frosty*. Still miffed about the tie, no doubt. Well, let him sulk.

Motty returned that evening—or rather, was *deposited* on the doormat by unseen hands. Jeeves and I hauled him inside. One whiff told the tale.

“Great Scott! He’s pickled!”

“Indeed, sir.”

The vegetarian teetotaler was stewed to the gills.

Next morning, however, he was chipper as a sparrow, praising Jeeves’s miraculous hangover cure and declaring his intent to “yield to temptation” nightly henceforth. And yield he did—with gusto. Night after night, he reeled home, having sampled every vice New York offered. I, meanwhile, withered under the strain, awaiting the inevitable reckoning with Aunt Agatha.

The final straw came when Motty acquired Rollo—a bull-terrier of uncertain temper, won in a raffle and tied to my furniture. The beast viewed me as a chew toy, reserving his Jeevesian admiration solely for the man himself.

It was all too much. Something had to give. And, as always, it was up to Jeeves to set it right—provided I could coax him out of his sartorial sulk.
