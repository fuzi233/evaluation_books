As Harry Warrington recounted the simple tale of his adventures at home, Madam Bernstein—sharp-witted, worldly, and deeply amused by human nature—no doubt formed her own judgments about the people and events he described. If her opinion was not entirely favorable, what wonder? For all men and women are flawed, and life, however grand or humble, is rarely without its trials and disappointments. The city-born lady recoiled at the very thought of her American sister’s rural existence—a life that would seem dull and unrefined to most urbanites. Yet little Madam Warrington knew no other way, and found contentment in it, as she did in herself. Just because we consider ourselves connoisseurs of fine things does not mean others suffer in their simpler fare. To Hodge, a plain meal of bread and bacon is not misery—it is sustenance.

Madam Warrington lived a life full of duties, perhaps monotonous to some, but deeply satisfying to her. She was a brisk, capable woman, managing every aspect of Castlewood’s vast estate. No pie was baked without her oversight; she saw to the maids’ spinning, supervised the kitchen staff, rode out on her pony to inspect the fields, and watched over the overseers and enslaved laborers working in tobacco and corn. When one of the slaves fell ill, she would visit him in the quarters regardless of weather, treating him with firm resolve. Her closet held ancient remedies, distilled waters, and potions—her medicine chest struck fear into her neighbors, who trembled at the prospect of illness, lest she descend upon them with her decoctions and pills.

A century before, Virginia had few towns; the estates of the gentry were self-contained villages, each ruled like a small kingdom. Rachel Esmond reigned over Castlewood like a minor queen, while her neighbors governed their lands as petty princes. Many of these landowners lived lavishly, though in rough comfort—clothes for servants often frayed, houses open to strangers, proud and idle, devoted to hunting and field sports befitting gentlemen of noble birth. Madam Warrington matched their hospitality, yet surpassed them in prudence. More than one man would have gladly shared her life interest in the estate and stepped into the role of father to her boys. But where was the man worthy of such a woman—of noble blood, high breeding, and exalted spirit? There was talk of appointing the Duke of Cumberland as viceroy—or even king—over America. Madam Warrington’s friends laughed, saying she waited for him. She replied gravely, “Men of higher rank than His Royal Highness have sought alliance with the Esmonds.”

Under her, she had an officer’s widow—Mrs. Mountain—who had once been her companion at school, just as her late husband had served alongside Mr. Warrington. At the Kensington Academy, when English girls tormented the young American girl, mocking her lofty airs from childhood, Fanny Parker defended her. They both married ensigns in King’s Regiment, forming a close bond—“my Fanny,” “my Rachel,” they wrote to each other. Then Fanny’s husband died in poverty, leaving no means for her or her infant child. Captain Franks brought her across the Atlantic aboard the *Young Rachel*.

Castlewood had ample room, and Mrs. Mountain brought warmth and liveliness to the household. She played cards with the mistress, taught music to the eldest boy, welcomed guests with cheer, arranged chambers for visitors, and managed the linen and presses. A kind, lively widow, she attracted many unmarried gentlemen in the colony, who asked her to marry them. But she chose to keep her name—Mountain—though it had brought her no fortune. One marriage was enough, she said. Her husband had spent both their fortunes with gentle charm, and her last trinkets went toward his funeral. As long as Madam Warrington kept her at Castlewood, she preferred a home without a husband to any offered in Virginia.

The two women quarreled often—bitterly sometimes—but always made up. When either boy fell ill, they competed in care and tenderness. In Colonel Esmond’s final days, Mrs. Mountain’s cheerful presence had been greatly valued. Madam Warrington cherished his memory above all others. So year after year, when Captain Franks asked, in jest, whether she’d return with him, she declined—she would stay another year.

When suitors came to Madam Warrington, as they inevitably did, she received them politely, sometimes teasing them: “Is it Mrs. Mountain you’ve come for?” She would urge them to court Fanny—she was of good English stock, kind-hearted, and would make any gentleman happy. If the suitor insisted he meant only her, she would curtsey gravely, say she had misunderstood, and declare that the daughter of the Marquis of Esmond lived for her people and sons, and would never change her condition.

Like Queen Elizabeth, she ruled with a mixture of authority and charm—inspiring affection, awe, and occasional cruelty, depending on her mood. No compliment was too grand for her to accept. Her vanity was well known, and the witty among them took pleasure in flattering her. Rattling Jack Firebrace of Henrico County stayed months at Castlewood, a favorite, because he recited verses he stole from pocket-books. Tom Humbold of Spotsylvania wagered fifty hogsheads against five that he could get her to establish a new order of knighthood—and won.

The elder son, George, observed these quirks with growing unease. From childhood, he resented flattery aimed at his mother, and mocked it with biting satire. “The Esmonds have always been jealous,” she would say gravely, “and my poor boy takes after his grandfather.” George loathed Jack Firebrace and Tom Humbold. Harry, meanwhile, enjoyed their company—hunting, fishing, cockfighting, and revelry.

One winter, after their first tutor left, Madam Esmond took the boys to Williamsburg for schooling. There, they heard the famed preacher Mr. Whitfield, whose arrival stirred the colony. Unlike neighboring provinces, Virginia was officially Church of England—clergymen were state-paid, with glebes granted to them. Without a bishop in America, ministers were imported from England—often second-rate men, parsons in disgrace, or noblemen’s dependents seeking refuge. It was no surprise that Whitfield’s powerful voice moved souls that Mr. Broadbent, the local chaplain, could not touch.

At first, the boys were captivated. They sang hymns, listened with fervor, and eagerly awaited the “call” of salvation—a spiritual awakening sweeping Protestant England. Had Whitfield stayed longer, both might have worn black coats instead of military epaulettes.

But Whitfield could not remain. His mission was to awaken the entire American church. He left behind letters of encouragement and promised to send a tutor who would teach not only secular knowledge, but also deepen their faith.

Soon, a young clergyman arrived from England—Mr. Ward. He had a voice as loud as Whitfield’s and could preach endlessly. Night and evening, the hall echoed with his sermons. The enslaved domestic workers crept to the doors to listen; servants crowded the porch windows. The black sheep of Castlewood—its enslaved population—were especially moved. When he led a hymn, a mighty chorus rose from the house, heard across the Potomac—something unheard in the Colonel’s time, for he distrusted clergymen and once said he’d argue with none except over backgammon.

Ward was handsome, and Madam Esmond admired his preaching. Women of the era, less educated and more impressionable, were prone to idolize such men. For weeks, she listened raptly, insisting her neighbors attend and convert. She particularly wished to influence young Mr. Washington, urging him repeatedly to stay at Castlewood and benefit from the spiritual instruction. But he always had pressing business elsewhere, and invariably rode off just before services began.

The boys, however, grew restless under Ward. They found him a poor scholar, dull, and ill-mannered. George knew more Latin and Greek than his tutor and caught him in constant errors. Harry, bolder, mimicked Ward’s eating and speaking, making the household laugh—Mrs. Mountain crowing with delight. Madam Esmond might have seen through Ward’s pretensions, but her sons’ resistance hardened her resolve. “What matters if he lacks learning?” she declared. “In what is truly precious, he can instruct us all. His manners may be coarse, but Heaven chooses not from the wealthy. I wish you knew one book as well as he does. It is your pride—the pride of all Esmonds—that blinds you.”

That evening, Ward preached on Naaman the Syrian, who refused to wash in Jordan’s waters, preferring his own rivers. The lesson: pride leads to ruin, and obedience to God’s appointed teacher is essential. Ward, clearly, saw himself as the guardian of divine truth.

George responded with sarcasm—perhaps inherited from his grandfather—using it to unsettle the household. He twisted Ward’s pompous phrases into jokes, making the young preacher flush with anger. He provoked Madam Esmond, especially when he sent Harry into fits of laughter. Her authority was challenged; her chosen minister scorned; her youngest son corrupted by rebellion. She resolved to restore order.

The boys were fourteen—Harry taller, more mature; George delicate, still boyish in appearance. Cane punishment was common then—used by sergeants, masters, overseers. The boys had been whipped by their Scotch tutor, Mr. Dempster, in their grandfather’s day. Harry, in particular, had grown accustomed to it. But after the Colonel’s death, discipline had relaxed. Now, with both mother and Mrs. Mountain defied, Madam Esmond turned to coercion.

She consulted Mr. Ward, who found scriptural justification for corporal punishment—no argument needed. He had begun by flattering the boys, hoping to stay at Castlewood permanently. But they mocked his flattery, scorned his manners, yawned at his sermons. The more their mother favored him, the more they despised him. Tutor and pupils hated each other.

Mrs. Mountain, loyal to the boys—especially George—warned them. “Ward is more obsequious than ever to your mother. It sickens me to hear him flatter, to see him gobble! You must be careful. Your mother was talking about you to Major Washington the other day. I don’t trust him—he’s too old for me. But he’s handsome, yes. And I know how much wild oats your father sowed, and my poor Mountain too. Show me Washington’s wild oats—I’ll bet there isn’t a grain!”

“You’re right, Mounty,” Harry said. “He told me exactly the same thing—‘Discipline must be preserved. There can be only one command in a house.’”

“Let him run his own house,” George retorted haughtily. The warning only made him more defiant.

The next day, the storm broke. During morning lessons, George argued with Ward—insubordinate, unjust. Even Harry admitted he was wrong. Ward kept his temper—suppressed rage, as one does when restraining fury. He said he would speak to Madam Esmond.

At dinner, Ward requested her to stay and calmly presented the dispute. He asked Harry to confirm his account. Harry reluctantly agreed.

Standing beneath the Colonel’s portrait, George said coldly, “What Mr. Ward said is correct.”

“To be tutor to such a pupil is absurd,” Ward declared, launching into a long speech filled with Scripture. At each phrase, George smiled mockingly, pished scornfully. Finally, Ward asked permission to retire.

“Not until you punish this disobedient child,” Madam Esmond snapped, anger rising. “If love fails, stronger measures must be taken. I punish you now, rebellious boy, to spare you greater punishment later. Discipline must be upheld. There can be only one command in a house, and I am its mistress. Mr. Ward, you will punish him—as we agreed. If he resists, my overseer and servants will assist you.”

She spoke with fire, citing scripture constantly—invoking God’s law in family affairs. This was the education she’d received from her fiery young spiritual guide. Was it comforting? Perhaps not.

Midway through her tirade, George felt guilt. “There can be only one command,” he said slowly, pale. “And I know who said those words before you. Mother… I have been wrong to Mr. Ward.”

“He confesses! He asks pardon!” Harry exclaimed. “That’s enough, George!”

“No!” the mother cried. “The penalty must be paid. When I was headstrong, my mother punished me, and I submitted. So must you. Mr. Ward, do your duty.”

“Stop, mother—you don’t know what you’re doing!” George pleaded.

“I know that he who spares the rod spoils the child!” she thundered, quoting Scripture again.

On the mantelpiece stood a china cup—precious to her, once used by her father. George suddenly picked it up. A strange smile crossed his face.

“Stay one minute. Don’t go yet,” he said. “You value this cup, mother?”

Harry stared.

“If I break it,” George continued, “it can never be mended. All the tinker’s rivets couldn’t make it whole again. My dear old grandpapa’s cup. I have been wrong. Mr. Ward, I ask your pardon. I will try to amend.”

The widow looked at him with contempt. “I thought,” she said, “I thought an Esmond had more courage than to be afraid…”

Then—Harry gasped. George raised the cup and dropped it onto the marble slab.

It shattered.

“It’s too late, Hal,” George said. “You’ll never mend it. Now, mother, I am ready. Will you come and see if I’m afraid? Mr. Ward, I am your servant. Your servant? Your slave! And the next time I meet Mr. Washington, madam, I’ll thank him for his advice.”

“I say, do your duty, sir!” she shrieked, stamping her foot. George bowed low and walked toward the study.

“Stop! For God’s sake, mother, stop!” Harry begged.

But passion consumed her. “You only encourage him!” she shouted. “If I had to do it myself, I would!”

Harry, heartbroken and furious, left the room as Mr. Ward and George entered.

The widow sat down, staring at the broken cup. Silence. Then—shrieks from the study.

Mr. Ward emerged bleeding, wounded on the head. Behind him, Harry, eyes blazing, brandishing his grandfather’s hunting knife, which hung among the Colonel’s weapons in the library.

“I don’t care. I did it,” Harry said. “I couldn’t stand seeing him strike my brother. When he lifted his hand, I threw the ruler. I couldn’t help it. I won’t bear it. If anyone lifts a hand to me or my brother, I’ll take his life!”
