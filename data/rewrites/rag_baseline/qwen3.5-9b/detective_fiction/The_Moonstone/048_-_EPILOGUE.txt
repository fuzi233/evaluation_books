On the twenty-seventh of June, I received orders from Sergeant Cuff to pursue three individuals suspected of foul play, described broadly as "Indians." Intelligence placed them at Tower Wharf that very morning, preparing to board a steamer destined for Rotterdam. Departing London via a rival line on the morning of Thursday, the twenty-eighth, I arrived in Rotterdam and secured the captain of the vessel in question. He confirmed that the trio had indeed been passengers, though their journey terminated abruptly at Gravesend. Just beyond the English coast, the spokesman of the group had queried the arrival time at Calais. Upon learning the ship's true destination, he feigned profound distress and surprise at their navigational error, offering to forfeit their passage fees solely to secure disembarkation. Moved by their plight as strangers in a foreign land and seeing no legal grounds for detention, the captain signaled for a launch, and the three men departed the vessel. This calculated ruse, clearly premeditated to evade detection, prompted my immediate return to England. Having left the steamer at Gravesend, I tracked the fugitives back to London, and subsequently, records indicated their departure for Plymouth. Local inquiries revealed that forty-eight hours prior, they had sailed aboard the *Bewley Castle*, an East Indiaman bound directly for Bombay. Acting on this intelligence, Sergeant Cuff dispatched urgent communications to the Bombay authorities, instructing them to intercept the vessel the moment she entered port. With this measure enacted, my involvement concluded; I have heard nothing further of the case since.

At the request of Sergeant Cuff, I commit to writing the particulars regarding three passengers, believed to be Hindus, who traveled under my command last summer on the *Bewley Castle* en route to Bombay. These men joined us at Plymouth. Throughout the outward voyage, I recorded no complaints regarding their conduct; they were quartered in the forward section of the ship, and I had few opportunities to observe them personally. However, in the latter stages of our journey, we encountered a calamity: a three-day and three-night calms off the Indian coast. Lacking the ship's log to verify precise coordinates, I can only state generally that drifting currents carried us closer to land until the winds returned, delivering us to port within twenty-four hours.

It is a known truth among seafarers that discipline inevitably softens during prolonged periods of stagnation. Such was the fate of my crew. Several passenger gentlemen procured small boats, amusing themselves with rowing and swimming whenever the evening air cooled sufficiently. Protocol dictated that these vessels be hoisted back into their davits upon completion; instead, they were left moored alongside the hull. The oppressive heat and the frustration of the weather sapped the resolve of both officers and men, leaving them indifferent to their duties throughout the calm.

On the third night, the watch on deck reported nothing unusual. Yet, when dawn broke, the smallest boat was missing, and shortly thereafter, reports surfaced that the three Hindu passengers had vanished as well. Had they appropriated the boat shortly after dark—as I am convinced they did—the proximity to land rendered pursuit futile by the time of discovery. Without doubt, they had made shore in that tranquil weather before daylight, accounting for fatigue and clumsy navigation.

Upon reaching port, I learned for the first time the motive behind their desperate flight. I can offer the authorities no explanation beyond what I have stated herein. They deemed me culpable for permitting the relaxation of shipboard order. I have expressed my regrets to them and to the owners. Since that event, no further information has reached my knowledge regarding the three Hindus. I have nothing further to add.

Do you retain any memory, my dear sir, of a semi-barbarous individual you encountered at dinner in London during the autumn of 'forty-eight? Allow me to jog your recollection: his name was Murthwaite, and following the meal, we engaged in a lengthy discourse. Our conversation centered upon an Indian diamond known as the Moonstone, and a conspiracy allegedly underway to seize the gem. Since then, I have wandered the vast expanses of Central Asia, eventually drifting back to the theater of my earlier adventures in northern and northwestern India.

About two weeks ago, I found myself in a region scarcely known to Europeans, called Kattiawar. Here, an adventure unfolded—one in which, however incredulous it may seem, you hold a personal stake. In these untamed territories, where even the tiller-men plow the soil armed to the teeth, the populace remains fanatically devoted to the ancient Hindu faith, worshipping Brahma and Vishnu. The few Muslim families scattered thinly across the interior villages dare not consume meat of any kind. Should a Muslim even be suspected of harming the sacred cow, he is, without hesitation, executed by the surrounding pious neighbors. To fortify this religious fervor, two of the most renowned shrines of Hindu pilgrimage lie within Kattiawar's borders. One is Dwarka, the birthplace of Krishna; the other is the holy city of Somnauth, sacked and razed centuries ago by the Muslim conqueror Mahmud of Ghazni.

Finding myself in these romantic lands for the second time, I resolved not to depart Kattiawar without witnessing once more the magnificent desolation of Somnauth. Some three days' journey on foot separated me from the sacred city when I first noticed others traveling in my direction—pairs, trios, appearing singly or in groups. To those who addressed me, I presented myself as a Hindu-Buddhist from a distant province, bound for pilgrimage. Need I elaborate that my attire suited this guise? Furthermore, I speak the language fluently, and my leanness and tanned complexion conceal my European origins effectively enough to pass muster among the locals—not as one of their own, but as a traveler from afar.

By the second day, the number of Hindus moving in my direction swelled to dozens, then hundreds. By the third day, the throng had expanded to thousands, all slowly converging toward a single point: the city of Somnauth. A minor favor I rendered to a fellow pilgrim on the third day facilitated my introduction to members of the higher castes. Through them, I learned that the multitude was marching to a grand religious ceremony to be held that very night on a hill overlooking the city—a festival honoring the God of the Moon.

As we approached the site of celebration, the crowd detained us. By the time we ascended the hill, the moon hung high and unobstructed in the heavens. My Hindu guides possessed special privileges granting them access to the shrine, and they graciously permitted me to accompany them. Upon arrival, we found the sanctuary concealed from view by a curtain suspended between two majestic trees. Beneath these trees, a flat projection of rock jutted outward, forming a natural dais. Standing below this platform with my guides, I gazed upward. The vista presented the most sublime synthesis of nature and humanity I have ever beheld. The lower slopes of the eminence faded imperceptibly into a grassy plain, marking the confluence of three rivers. To one side, the graceful sinuous lines of the waters stretched endlessly, alternating between visibility and concealment beneath the canopy; to the other, the ocean lay motionless in the nocturnal calm.

Imagine this idyllic scene populated by tens of thousands of human beings, all clad in white, cascading down the hillsides, spilling onto the plains, and fringing the riverbanks. Illuminate this tableau with the wild crimson glow of cressets and torches, flickering intermittently from every corner of the immense assembly. Envision the Eastern moonlight pouring down in unclouded radiance over all—and you begin to grasp the magnitude of the spectacle that greeted me from the hilltop.

A mournful melody, played on stringed instruments and flutes, drew my attention back to the hidden shrine. Turning, I saw upon the rocky platform the silhouettes of three men. In the central figure, I recognized the man I had spoken with in England when the "Indians" appeared on the terrace at Lady Verinder's residence. The other two, his companions on that previous occasion, were undoubtedly his comrades here as well.

One spectator, standing near me, observed my startlement. Whispering urgently, he explained the significance of the three figures on the rock. They were Brahmins, he said, who had forfeited their caste through service to the god. The deity had decreed that their purification must be achieved through pilgrimage. That very night, the three men were to part ways. In three distinct directions, they were to embark as pilgrims toward the shrines of India. Never again were they to behold one another's faces. Never again were they to find rest in their wanderings, from the day of their separation unto the day of their death.

As these words were breathed into my ear, the plaintive music ceased. The three men prostrated themselves upon the rock before the curtain hiding the shrine. Rising, they looked upon one another, embraced briefly, and then descended separately into the crowd. The people parted for them in a silence so profound it felt like a held breath. In three different directions, I watched the masses divide simultaneously. Slowly, the great white mass of humanity closed back together, erasing the tracks of the doomed men through the ranks of their fellows. We saw them no more.
