The Thursday night dissolved into silence, unbroken by incident. By the break of Friday, however, the atmosphere shifted, carried in by two distinct currents of news.

The first current flowed from the baker's boy, who reported having encountered Rosanna Spearman the prior afternoon. She wore a thick veil, obscuring her features, yet walking unmistakably toward Frizinghall via the footpath across the moors. It was a peculiar error, given that Rosanna's broad shoulders betrayed her identity with cruel clarity; she was, alas, confined to her sickbed upstairs throughout the entire Thursday afternoon, battling illness. The boy must have been mistaken, for the woman he described could not have been the invalidized girl we knew.

The second tidings arrived sealed in the postman's bag. Worthy Mr. Candy, driven off in the rain on the birthday night, had uttered one of his many prophecies of misfortune: he claimed a doctor's coat was waterproof. Yet, despite the leather barrier, the elements had breached him. He had contracted a severe chill that night and now lay feverish. The latest dispatches depicted him as light-headed, babbling nonsense in his delirium with the same glib fluency he displayed in his sober moments. We all felt a pang of sympathy for the little doctor, save for Mr. Franklin, who seemed to regard the illness primarily through the lens of Miss Rachel's welfare. As he spoke to my lady during breakfast, his tone suggested that unless the suspense surrounding the Moonstone was swiftly resolved, Miss Rachel stood in urgent peril, requiring the finest medical counsel available.

Scarcely had the meal concluded when a telegram from Mr. Blake, the elder, descended upon the table, responding to his son. It announced that he had secured, with the aid of the Commissioner, the precise individual to assist us. His name was Sergeant Cuff, and his arrival from London was anticipated on the morning train.

Upon reading the name of this new constable, Mr. Franklin gave a visible start. During his sojourn in London, he had gathered curious anecdotes regarding Sergeant Cuff from his father's solicitor.

"I begin to hope we are witnessing the dawn of our deliverance," he remarked. "If half the tales I have heard hold truth, there exists no equal in England to Sergeant Cuff when it comes to unraveling a labyrinthine mystery!"

Excitement and impatience rippled through the household as the appointed hour approached. Superintendent Seegrave, returning precisely on schedule and learning of the Sergeant's imminent arrival, immediately retreated to a chamber. Armed with pen, ink, and paper, he prepared to draft the report he knew would inevitably follow. I, too, yearned to hasten to the station to retrieve the Sergeant personally. Yet, my lady's carriage and horses were deemed unsuitable transport, even for a figure of Cuff's renown; the pony-chaise was reserved for Mr. Godfrey. He expressed profound regret at being forced to depart his aunt during such a tense interval, graciously postponing his journey until the final train, solely to hear the London detective's assessment of the case. However, by Friday night, he must be in town, for a Ladies' Charity, fraught with difficulties, awaited his consultation on Saturday morning.

When the moment of arrival drew near, I descended to the gate to await his approach.

A fly harnessed to the railway chugged up as I reached the lodge. Out stepped a grizzled, elderly man, so emaciated he appeared to possess not a single ounce of flesh clinging to his skeletal frame. Dressed entirely in somber black, save for a stark white cravat, he cut a striking figure. His face was sharp-edged as a hatchet, the skin stretched tight and yellow, dry, and withered like an autumn leaf. His eyes, a steely light grey, possessed a disquieting habit; when they locked with yours, they seemed to demand knowledge from you that you were unaware you possessed. His gait was soft, his voice melancholic, and his long, lanky fingers curled inward like predatory claws. He might have been a clergyman, a mortician, or any profession save the one he truly embodied. He was the antithesis of Superintendent Seegrave, a far less comforting visage for a family in distress, and I defy anyone to find a more jarring contrast anywhere.

"Is this Lady Verinder's residence?" he inquired.

"Yes, sir."

"I am Sergeant Cuff."

"This way, sir, if you please."

En route to the house, I introduced myself and my position within the family, intending to assure him that he might address me regarding the business for which my lady intended to engage him. Yet, he spoke not a word of the investigation. Instead, he admired the grounds, remarking that the sea air felt brisk and invigorating. Privately, I wondered how the celebrated Cuff had accrued such a formidable reputation. We reached the house feeling like two disparate beasts yoked together for the first time by a single chain.

Seeking my lady, and learning she resided in one of the conservatories, we circled to the rear gardens and dispatched a servant to locate her. While we waited, Sergeant Cuff peered through the evergreen archway to our left, spotted our rosery, and strode straight into it. To the gardener's astonishment and my own disgust, this renowned policeman revealed himself to be a veritable mine of erudition concerning the trivialities of rose cultivation.

"Ah, you have secured the ideal exposure here, facing south and southwest," the Sergeant observed, nodding his grizzled head with a flicker of pleasure in his mournful voice. "This is the proper form for a rosery—nothing compares to a circle set within a square. Indeed, with walks threading between every bed. Yet, they ought not to be gravel paths like these. Grass, Mr. Gardener—grass walks between your roses; gravel is too harsh for them. That is a lovely bed of white and blush roses. They blend beautifully, do they not? Here is the white musk rose, Mr. Betteredge—our ancient English variety holding its ground alongside the finest and newest imports. Quite charming!" he continued, caressing the Musk Rose with his claw-like fingers, addressing the flower as if it were a child.

What manner of man was this to recover Miss Rachel's diamond and identify the thief?

"You appear to harbor a fondness for roses, Sergeant?" I ventured.

"I have scarce time to cultivate affection for anything," replied Sergeant Cuff. "Yet, when I do possess a fleeting moment to bestow such sentiment, Mr. Betteredge, the roses invariably receive it. My life began among them in my father's nursery garden, and I pray to end it there, if fate permits. Yes. Someday (God willing) I shall retire from capturing thieves and attempt my hand at cultivating roses. There will be grass walks, Mr. Gardener, between my beds," he added, his melancholy gaze lingering unpleasantly on the gravel paths of our rosery.

"It seems an unusual predilection, sir," I dared to suggest, "for a man of your profession."

"If you cast your eye about you—a task most neglect—" the Sergeant countered, "you will observe that the nature of a man's tastes is, most often, diametrically opposed to the nature of his trade. Exhibit to me two things more contrary than a rose and a thief, and I shall amend my tastes accordingly—if it is not too late in my twilight years. You consider the damask rose a sturdy stock for most tender varieties, do you not, Mr. Gardener? Ah! I suspected as much. Here comes a lady approaching. Is it Lady Verinder?"

He had perceived her before either I or the gardener had registered her presence, though we knew precisely where to direct our gaze, and he did not. I began to suspect him to be a sharper observer than his initial appearance suggested.

Whether it was the Sergeant's demeanor or his mission—or perhaps both—that caused my lady a measure of embarrassment, she appeared, for the first time in my extensive observation of her, utterly at a loss for words during an encounter with a stranger. Sergeant Cuff, however, dispelled her unease instantly. He inquired whether any other individuals had been engaged regarding the robbery prior to our summons. Upon learning that another agent had been consulted and was currently present in the house, he politely requested permission to speak with him before proceeding further.

My lady led the way back. Before following, the Sergeant discharged his mind on the subject of the gravel walks with a final remark to the gardener. "Persuade her ladyship to try grass," he said, casting a sour glance at the paths. "No gravel! Absolutely no gravel!"

I cannot fully articulate why Superintendent Seegrave appeared to shrink several sizes in stature upon being introduced to Sergeant Cuff; I can merely state the phenomenon. They withdrew together and remained secluded from all mortal intrusion for a weary length of time. When they emerged, the Superintendent was agitated, while the Sergeant was yawning.

"The Sergeant desires to inspect Miss Verinder's sitting-room," Mr. Seegrave announced to me with great pomp and eagerness. "The Sergeant may require inquiries. Attend the Sergeant, if you please!"

While I was thus commanded, I glanced at the great Cuff. On his side, the great Cuff regarded Superintendent Seegrave with that quietly expectant stare I had noted earlier. I cannot assert that he was watching for his colleague's swift transformation into a fool, but I harbored a strong suspicion that he was.
