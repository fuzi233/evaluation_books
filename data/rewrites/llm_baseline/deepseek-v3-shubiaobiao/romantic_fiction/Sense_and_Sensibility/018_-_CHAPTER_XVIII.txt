Elinor observed with quiet concern the melancholy that clung to Edward like a shadow. His visit, though a pleasure, was marred by his own restless discontent. She longed for some sign—some assurance—that his heart still held its old tenderness for her, but his manner was a shifting thing: one moment warm, the next withdrawn, leaving her to doubt where she had once been certain.

The next morning, he joined her and Marianne in the breakfast room before the others had stirred. Marianne, ever eager to encourage their intimacy, soon excused herself, only to pause halfway up the stairs when the parlor door opened abruptly. Edward emerged, his hat in hand.

“I’m off to the village to see to my horses,” he said briskly. “Since breakfast isn’t ready, I’ll return shortly.”

When he did, it was with fresh praise for the countryside. His walk had granted him new views of the valley, and the village, perched higher than the cottage, had offered a sweeping prospect that pleased him greatly. Marianne, ever ardent in matters of scenery, seized the topic at once, but Edward held up a hand.

“You mustn’t press me too far, Marianne. I’ve no eye for the picturesque, and if we descend into particulars, my ignorance will offend you. I’ll call a steep hill *steep*, not *bold*; a rough surface *strange*, not *rugged*. Distant objects? Merely *out of sight*, not artfully obscured by haze. You must accept my admiration as it is—plain and honest. I find this a fine country: steep hills, sturdy woods, snug valleys with rich meadows and tidy farmhouses. It suits me precisely—beauty and usefulness combined. And since *you* admire it, I’ve no doubt it’s picturesque as well—all rocks and moss and wild brush—but those charms are lost on me.”

Marianne sighed. “I fear you’re right—but must you boast of it?”

Elinor smiled. “I suspect Edward, to avoid one affectation, dons another. Disgusted by false raptures over nature, he feigns indifference rather than admit his own discernment. His fastidiousness breeds its own pretension.”

“True enough,” Marianne conceded. “Admiration of landscapes has become mere jargon. Everyone mimics the first man who defined the picturesque, parroting phrases until they’re drained of meaning. I’ve often held my tongue for lack of fresh words.”

Edward nodded. “I believe *you* feel the delight you claim. But your sister must permit me to feel no more than I profess. I like a fine view—but not by picturesque rules. Twisted, blighted trees? No. Tall, straight ones? Admirable. Ruined cottages? No charm in decay. Give me a snug farmhouse over a watchtower, happy villagers over romantic bandits.”

Marianne stared at him, torn between astonishment and pity. Elinor only laughed, and the topic drifted away, leaving Marianne pensive—until a glint at Edward’s hand caught her eye. As he accepted tea, a ring on his finger flashed, its center holding a lock of hair.

“You’ve never worn a ring before, Edward!” she exclaimed. “Is that Fanny’s hair? I recall her promising you some—though I thought it darker.”

The words escaped her thoughtlessly. At once, she regretted them, for Edward’s face flushed deeply. He glanced at Elinor, then muttered, “Yes—my sister’s. The setting alters the shade.”

Elinor met his gaze, her own cheeks warming. She knew, as surely as Marianne, whose hair it was—only Marianne assumed it a gift, while Elinor suspected some secretive snip. Yet she took no offense, deftly steering the conversation elsewhere, though inwardly resolved to study that ring at every chance.

Edward’s discomfort lingered, his distraction deepening as the morning wore on. Marianne chastised herself bitterly—though she might have spared herself had she known how little her sister minded.

By midday, Sir John and Mrs. Jennings arrived, eager to inspect the cottage’s new guest. With Mrs. Jennings’s help, Sir John soon deduced the initial of Edward’s surname, arming himself with future jests at Elinor’s expense—though propriety held his tongue for now. Still, his pointed looks betrayed his suspicions, sharpened by Margaret’s hints.

True to form, Sir John pressed them to dine at the Park the next day and take tea that very evening. “You *must*,” he insisted. “Tonight we’ll be quiet—tomorrow, a merry party!”

Mrs. Jennings added, “And who knows? Perhaps a dance!”

“A dance?” Marianne cried. “Impossible! Who would dance?”

“Who? You, the Careys, the Whitakers! Did you think no one could dance now that a certain someone is gone?”

Sir John sighed dramatically. “I wish Willoughby were back among us.”

At this, Marianne colored—and Edward, seated beside Elinor, murmured, “Who is Willoughby?”

Elinor answered briefly. Marianne’s face spoke volumes. By the time their visitors left, Edward had pieced together what had once puzzled him. He approached Marianne, lowering his voice.

“I’ve been guessing. Shall I tell you?”

“What do you mean?”

“May I?”

“If you like.”

“Then I guess… Mr. Willoughby hunts.”

Marianne started, then—despite herself—smiled at his quiet mischief. After a pause, she said, “Oh, Edward! How can you? But in time, I hope… I’m sure you’ll like him.”

“I don’t doubt it,” he replied, surprised by her fervor. Had he not thought it a jest—a mere nod to some trifling flirtation—he’d never have dared mention it.
