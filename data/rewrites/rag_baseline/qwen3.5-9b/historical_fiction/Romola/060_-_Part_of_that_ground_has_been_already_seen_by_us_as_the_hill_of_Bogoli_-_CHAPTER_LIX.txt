The morning light cut sharp across the Piazza della Signoria, illuminating a city gripped in the feverish throes of political purgatory. Romola moved through the throng toward San Marco, her destination secured only yesterday through the intervention of Fra Salvestro. Interviews with Savonarola were rare commodities, guarded with the rigid precision of a fortress; when granted, they were stripped of all theatrical mystery to avoid the pretexts of calumny. Thus, she arrived during the peak hour of the outer cloisters, ensuring her audience occurred amidst the noise of the crowd rather than in the sterile isolation of the chapter-house.

She walked with purpose, scanning the urban landscape for the pulse of public sentiment. Every loggia, every shaded corner of the piazza, every shopfront serving as a gossip station buzzed with the agitation of gratuitous debate—a languishing trade that paradoxically fueled the vigor of political discourse. The factions supporting and opposing the death penalty for the conspirators were engaged in a frantic race to gauge the popular mood during the three-day lull. Handbills plastered the walls like invasive weeds, shouting their messages in massive, bold typefaces: *JUSTICE* versus *RUIN*, *OBSERVANCE OF LAW* versus *THE APPEAL*. These islands of black capital letters floated above seas of smaller, explanatory text, which the populace largely ignored. At that moment, in the first flush of triumph over the invention of printing, the prevailing belief held that no argument could match the persuasive weight of a question-begging phrase rendered in giant font.

Romola, however, possessed a different agenda. She craved the substance hidden in the smaller print, the nuanced reasoning that mattered. Though driven by urgency, she scanned her surroundings anxiously, desperate to intercept any stray pamphlets before the *sbirri* tore them from the walls or before eager readers consumed them whole. Her search led her behind San Giovanni, where she spotted Bratti, a familiar face from the humble quarters of Florence. He was currently bartering his stock of flyers for small coins with passersby.

"Bratti," she called out, her voice cutting through the din. "Do you have both editions? Give them to me quickly."

"Two sorts," Bratti replied, his movements slow and deliberate as he separated the damp sheets. "'Law' and 'Justice.'"

"Which sells faster?"

"'Justice,' by far. So I raised the price to two danari. Then I realized 'Law' was equally valuable merchandise; people despise cheapness, and selling it for one danaro would be a wrong to the product. I'm a fair trader. Both cost me nothing; both yield a fair profit. Do you need more than one of each?"

"No, no. Here is a white quattrino for the pair." Romola folded the bills tightly and melted away before he could elaborate further.

She reached the outer cloisters of San Marco, where Fra Salvestro waited beneath the arcade. He did not register her approach, engrossed in conversation with lay visitors. Under the current administration, which favored the Frate, the post-excommunication timidity regarding visits to San Marco had begun to recede. Among the listeners sat Andrea Cambini, a known satellite of Francesco Valori. Cambini gestured emphatically, expounding on some topic with theatrical flair, while Fra Salvestro listened with an air of trivial curiosity—the look of someone who values the mere existence of news far more than its truth or merit.

Romola recognized the subject instantly from the disjointed fragments reaching her ears: the fate of the conspirators. She halted her approach, allowing the distraction to play out. When she finally caught Fra Salvestro's gaze, she turned sharply toward the chapter-house door. He nodded in silent approval and vanished into the inner cloister. A lay brother stepped forward to open the heavy doors, closing them firmly behind her as she entered.

Inside, the air was thick with the scent of incense and old stone. The frescoes lining the walls depicted scenes of sorrow, figures that seemed to mourn alongside her for the death of her brother, Dino. The memory surged, not as a clear flashback, but as a chaotic blend of impressions merging with her current terror. It felt as though her present anxiety was a resurrection of a previous longing, one that had previously struck against the immovable rigidity of marble. She suppressed the thought, forcing her focus onto the task at hand. Unfolding the handbills, she hoped to read them before Fra Girolamo's arrival. By the time she finished the tract advocating strict legal observance, the great wooden doors creaked open. She doubled the papers and stood expectant.

Fra Girolamo entered. According to custom, Romola knelt, but as he offered his benedictory greeting, she rose and stood opposite him at a respectful distance. Time had altered him profoundly. Since his excommunication, years of seclusion had etched deeper lines into his face, marking the toll of overtaxed intellect and bodily austerity. Yet, Romola sensed a shift more profound than mere aging. The serene elevation and pure human fellowship that had once captivated her seemed diminished. Was it the fading of his former spirit, or the awakening of a dormant alienation born from her godfather's estrangement from the Frate? Perhaps both currents converged. Human relationships often fracture not from a single cruel act, but from the gradual erosion of affection under the strain of repeated compromises.

Savonarola's gaze met hers with a hardness born of egotistic preoccupation. He intuitively grasped that her visit concerned the conspirators, a subject requiring him to silence internal voices that threatened to resurface under external pressure. Seated earlier in his cell, correcting proofs for his *Triumph of the Cross*, he had found solace in a posture of neutrality.

"It is a matter of grave importance, undoubtedly, that brings you to me, my daughter," he began, his tone gentle, a mask of self-control rather than genuine inclination. "I know you do not typically dwell on trifles."

"Father, you know the gravity before I speak," Romola interrupted, pouring forth her plea with urgent intensity. "I seek the life of the old man I love most in the world. The thought of him has accompanied the image of my father since I can remember daylight. That is my warrant for coming, even if my presence seems superfluous. Perhaps you have already decided that your power over human hearts should be used to deny Florentines a right you yourselves helped secure."

"I do not interfere with the functions of the State, my daughter," Savonarola replied, clearly disinclined to reignite a debate he had already wrestled with internally. "I have preached and labored for a good government, for it is essential to the perfection of the Christian life; but I keep my hands from particular affairs entrusted to experienced citizens."

"Surely, father—" Romola started, then stopped. The impulse to remonstrate with the man who had once been her guiding star felt like a violation of her own reverence.

Savonarola, perceptive and noble, detected the conflict arresting her. Too wise to fall into the self-justifying evasions he sometimes employed in public crowds, he assumed a stance of calm expectation.

"Speak what is in your heart, continue, my daughter," he commanded, arms crossed, watching her intently.

"I was about to say, father, that this matter transcends the fervor of many sermons you have delivered. If it belongs to you to urge that men condemned for offenses against the state retain the right to appeal to the Great Council—and if counting that victory is a glory to you—can it not also belong to you to oppose the denial of that right to those who need it most? Surely this touches the Christian life more intimately than predicting the death of the Dauphin or the conquest of Pisa."

A subtle movement, akin to suppressed pain, tightened the corners of Savonarola's mouth before he responded.

"My daughter, I speak only as permitted. I am not master of the timing when I may become a vessel for knowledge beyond common understanding. In this instance, I possess no illumination beyond what wisdom dictates for those charged with the safety of the State. Regarding the Law of Appeal against the Six Votes, I labored tirelessly for its passage to ensure no Florentine suffered loss of life or goods due to the private hatred of a few in power. However, these five men sought to overthrow a free government and restore a corrupt tyrant. They were condemned with the assent of a vast assembly of their peers. They initially refused to bring their cause before the Great Council. Consequently, they forfeited the right to appeal."

"How can they have lost it?" Romola countered, her voice rising. "It is the inherent right to appeal against condemnation, and they have never been condemned until now. Forgive me, father, but it *is* private hatred that denies them the appeal; it *is* the violence of the few that terrifies the many. Else, why did the assembly fracture immediately after appearing united? If anything weighs against the law, let this weigh heavily: you once preached more earnestly than any other doctrine that there should be no place for hatred and bloodshed arising from party strife, lest private ill-will find opportunity in public acts. Father, you know private hatred is at work here. Will it not dishonor you not to intervene on the side of mercy, when so many view it as synonymous with law and justice?"
