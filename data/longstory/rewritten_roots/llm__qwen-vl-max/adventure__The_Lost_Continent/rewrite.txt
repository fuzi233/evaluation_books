We were both stiff from sleeping under the open sky all night, even in the warm climate of Grand Canary. The dew and the coolness of the darkness had left us aching by morning. I usually start the day with a brisk run, but here on this rugged stretch of island terrain, there wasn’t a single level yard to be found. So when Coppinger began doing exercises with two jagged chunks of lava rock—his own brand of dumbbells—I followed suit.

Coppinger had seen his fair share of rough living, but he was also a doctor of medicine—though it seemed he earned a new degree every other year—and he treated health like a religion. He believed in routine, in balance, in preparation. Two days earlier, it had rained, and now a narrow stream trickled along the bottom of the barranca. We descended to it, washed our faces, brushed our teeth—something of a luxury on such an expedition—and felt marginally human again.

“Now,” Coppinger said after emptying our pockets, “we’ve got precious little food left, and what’s left is no better for being wrapped in Spanish newspaper.”

“Yours is mostly tobacco ash,” I replied.

“It’ll get worse if we leave it. And we’ve got more hard climbing ahead.”

That much was clear. So we sat beside the stream, ate everything we had, and prepared ourselves for the ten-mile hike back to the fonda at Santa Brigida, where we’d left our supplies. Coppinger wanted to take more photographs and measurements before we left this cluster of caves, so we’d likely go hungry until then—and miss out on another glass of that rich, old country wine from the PATRON’s cellar.

I can only imagine how astonished English hoteliers in Las Palmas would be if they knew what treasures one could find in the mountain villages up north—if you knew where to look and how to ask.

To be honest, the two mummies I’d already seen satisfied my curiosity. Wrapped in brittle goatskins, they crumbled into dust at the slightest touch. But Coppinger? He was convinced he’d stumbled upon something grand—an ancient Guanche university, or perhaps a sacred college like the one on the opposite side of the island. He wouldn’t rest until he’d searched every cave in sight.

He still had plenty of flash powder and twenty-eight fresh rolls of film. “Let’s finish this now,” he insisted. “No need to come back.”

So I took the rope, he grabbed the crowbar, and we climbed back toward the ridge of the cliff—where we’d been roasted by the sun the day before.

These caves weren’t easy to reach. Otherwise, they’d have been looted long ago. Coppinger claimed that in ancient times, the Guanches used goatskin ropes as ladders, which they could pull up behind them to keep intruders out. It sounded plausible. No better idea occurred to me.

The cave mouths lined up roughly thirty feet below the ridge and fifty above the canyon floor. Spanish curiosity doesn’t extend far beyond walking distance.

Climbing from below would have been awkward. But descending from above with a knotted rope was manageable. Our plan: drop down into each cave, then rappel to the base, climb back up, and repeat. Coppinger was brave, and steady on heights—but he was portly and closer to fifty than forty-five. Still, he was determined.

I went first each time, getting into the cave mouth and helping him follow. But when you’re hanging off a vertical cliff face, clinging to a thin rope like a bootlace, there’s not much help the man below can offer—except good wishes.

I wanted to spare him. The first three caves were small, empty, clearly storage pits. I suggested he skip them. But he refused. He declared one was a prison, another a pottery workshop, and the third a seminary for young priests. Naturally, he didn’t trust my judgment. That’s the way archaeologists are—full of imagination.

As the sun climbed higher, Coppinger began to show signs of exhaustion. He pushed on longer than was safe. I wasn’t happy about it. The drop from the top of the cliffs was rarely less than eighty feet.

Eventually, he admitted defeat. I urged him to head back to Santa Brigida, but he refused. Three more caves remained. If I wouldn’t check them, he’d try himself.

He tried to make it sound like a favor—taking my report instead of inspecting them himself. But I refused. I was tired, drenched in sweat, my head pounding from the sun, my hands raw from the rope. Coppinger, though worn, was still eager.

“Look,” he said, “you never know what you might find. And if you do, it’s yours. I won of claim.”

“Very generous,” I said. “But I don’t want any more goat-skin mummies.”

“Bah! This isn’t a burial cave. See the difference in the opening? Now, be a sport. Just because the others were empty doesn’t mean this one won’t yield something.”

“Oh, very well,” I said, and trudged over the rocks, along the ledge, and up the fissure that saved us a two-mile detour. I jammed the crowbar into a new hold, and lowered myself down.

Swinging into the cave was tricky—the rock overhung above, or had broken away below. I managed it, though I landed hard on my back. But I held onto the rope. Losing it would have stranded Coppinger.

At first glance, this cave was different. The others were crude dens, rounded out haphazardly. This one had been shaped with tools—clean angles, smooth walls. The sides sloped inward toward the ceiling, reminding me of an architecture I’d seen somewhere but couldn’t recall. And there were multiple rooms connected by narrow passages.

I checked for inscriptions—none. Only bats. I lit a cigarette (Coppinger always thought haste meant carelessness) and smoked it through. Then I leaned out and shouted down.

“What’s the news?” he called.

“I’ve searched it thoroughly.”

“Don’t come down yet. Wait. I’m securing the camera and flashlight to the end of the rope. Pull them up. Take half a dozen shots for me.”

“Fine,” I said, and hauled the gear inside.

The photos would be dull—dark, featureless—but Coppinger liked them that way. To avoid halation, I placed the camera on a stone ledge near each doorway, steadied it, and triggered the flash from behind. I photographed four chambers.

Then came one with a wider ledge. I set the camera down, braced it with stones, and sat to recharge the flash unit.

The moment I shifted my weight, the ledge cracked. I dropped half a foot, scrambled back up, and snatched the camera just as it slipped toward the ground.

I smiled. This was no ordinary cave. It was sealed—hermetically—with cement. The odds were good it contained something worth hiding.

At first, all I saw was dust and rubble. I lit a candle and began clearing it away. Soon, I started uncovering something unusual—layers of material that chipped away cleanly. When I brought it into daylight, I saw each layer had two parts: one side was shiny, like talc; the other was coated in a dark, toffee-colored substance that looked like wax. The wax bore patterns.

I knew nothing of ancient scripts. Coppinger had told me repeatedly that the Guanches couldn’t write. So I didn’t think the markings were letters. But I did recognize value. I began scraping it out with the heel of my boot and my pocketknife. The sheets were stuck together—I didn’t separate them. They fit perfectly into the cavity. By breaking the front seal, I reached the base and hacked through the lower layers until I freed a solid block.

It measured twenty inches by fifteen by fifteen, lighter than it looked. After taking final photos, I lowered it down on the rope.

There was nothing more to do. I descended next.

The block lay on the ground. Coppinger was on his hands and knees beside it—wild-eyed with excitement.

“What is it?” I asked.

“I don’t know yet. But it’s the most valuable discovery ever made in the Canary Islands—and it’s yours, you unappreciative fool. At least what’s left of it. Oh, man, you’ve destroyed the beginning and the end of something priceless. My fault. I shouldn’t have let an amateur handle such work.”

“I should say it’s your fault. You said these people couldn’t write. I took your word. For all I knew, it might have been edible.”

“It’s not Guanche,” he snapped. “You should have known that from the talc. Great heavens, do you see nothing? Look at the island’s geology—there’s no talc here!”

“I’m not a geologist. Is it imported?”

“Of course. It’s Egyptian. Obvious. Though how it got here, I don’t know. It’s not readable like a newspaper. The script is a variant of anything discovered so far. And this waxy coating—unique. Some mineral, perhaps asphalt. Not animal wax. I’ll analyze it later. Why they invented it and then abandoned it is a mystery. I could gaze at this all day.”

“Well,” I said, “if it’s all the same to you, I’d rather gaze at a meal. Ten miles to the fonda, and I’m starving. Do you know it’s already four o’clock? Climbing down and back up takes longer than you think.”

Coppinger spread his coat and carefully wrapped the block, refusing to tie it with rope—fear of damaging the edges. He carried it himself most of the way, only letting me take over when he nearly collapsed from exhaustion.

“Suppose you carry it,” he grumbled. “After all, it’s yours.”

When we reached the fonda, I had a hearty dinner and a bottle of old Canary wine, then retired to bed with a pipe. Coppinger ate too, but I suspect he didn’t sleep. Next morning, I found him still poring over the find—eyes bloodshot, but glowing with excitement.

“You’ve stumbled upon the most important historical manuscript ever discovered,” he said. “With your clumsy method, you’ve ruined it. Those top sheets you scraped away—probably a unique account of Yucatan’s ancient civilization.”

“Where’s Yucatan?”

“In the Gulf of Mexico. Ruins now, but once a thriving colony of Atlanteans.”

“Never heard of them.”

“They were real. Atlantis was a continent just north of the Canaries.”

“What’s that winged crocodile in the margin?”

“A beast from those days. A cave-tiger. And that—a colossal bat. Thank goodness the writer illustrated everything. Otherwise, we’d never understand. Whole species have vanished since then—just as a whole continent drowned and three civilizations ended. Worst of all, it was written by a highly educated man who writes badly. I’ve worked all night and only deciphered fragments.”

He rubbed his hands. “It’ll take a year to translate properly.”

“Every man to his taste. Mine wouldn’t last that long. But how did it get here? Did some ancient Egyptian come for the air and write it in a cave?”

“I misjudged. The author wasn’t Egyptian. The script misled me. It was written by Deucalion—priest, general, or both—an Atlantean. How it arrived here? Probably explained in the last pages—smashed by your knife.”

“Abuse me all you like. Deucalion? The Greek mythological figure? He survived the Flood—like Noah.”

“The sinking of Atlantis may be the Flood.”

“Is there a Pyrrha? His wife?”

“I haven’t found her. But there’s a Phorenice—perhaps the same. She seems to be the reigning Empress.”

I studied the illustrations. The perspective was odd, but the creatures were unmistakable—gigantic beasts. One drawing showed a cave-tiger attacking a mammoth.

“By Jove. I wouldn’t have wanted to live then.”

“They probably had ways to fight them. Time will tell.”

He glanced at his watch. “I suppose I ought to feel ashamed. I haven’t slept. Are you leaving?”

“I’m driving back to Las Palmas. Promised a golf game.”

“See you at dinner. Hope my shirts are back. O, lord—I’m sleepy.”

I left him heading to bed, ordered a carriage, and departed. A cable awaited me in Las Palmas—business called. I caught the Liverpool boat just as it steamed out. Close call. The boatmen made a fortune off my rush.

Coppinger was just a passing acquaintance. Back in England, I was buried in work. I didn’t think of him again—until a year later, when a newspaper mentioned he’d donated the manuscript to the British Museum, valued at ten thousand pounds.

I wrote him a sharp note, reminding him I’d discovered it—and that he’d taken liberties with my property.

His reply was formal: “Doctor Coppinger regrets…” and enclosed the English translation. He “admitted” my claim and hoped publication profits would compensate for damage.

I hadn’t expected such coldness. I responded firmly. The only reply was from solicitors: all future communication must go through them.

Publicly, I regret his stance. But since the matter has progressed, I accept his terms. The original text is printed here. Dr. Coppinger bears credit—and responsibility—for the translation. All revenue from readers goes to me, the finder of the talc-upon-wax sheets.

Should the arrangement change, it will be announced publicly. But for now, this seems unlikely.

The ceremony was over. The proclamation had been read, the name of Phorenice, Empress of Atlantis, echoed through the hall in reverent tones, and Tatho had taken his place as Viceroy of Yucatan—seated upon the serpent-throne, adorned with the jeweled chain that marked supreme authority. Drums thundered, horns blared, and the crowd roared: “Long live Tatho!” “Hail to the Empress!” I walked behind him, arms folded, head bowed, a silent shadow among glittering soldiers and courtiers. The air hummed with power and tradition, yet beneath it all, my heart stirred with quiet grief.

Twenty years ago, I had stood in this very spot, receiving the same honors. Now, I was displaced—not by force, but by decree. And though the ritual demanded silence, I felt the warmth of glances from every man in the chamber—soldiers, nobles, elders—all offering me unspoken respect. It was rare for a fallen ruler to be met with such kindness. Not one voice rose against me. Not one petition accused me of misrule. No one called for reform. The silence itself was praise.

We circled the gilded council chamber three times—the prescribed number—then turned toward the private chambers at the far end, once mine, now his. As Tatho gave the ceremonial command, “Open ye to the Viceroy,” the massive stone doors groaned open. He stepped inside. I followed. The others paused at the threshold, murmuring farewells. Then the door slammed shut with a finality that echoed in my bones.

Alone at last, we stood in the center of the room, free from the weight of protocol. Tatho lowered his voice and bowed slightly. “Deucalion,” he said, “I did not seek this office. It was forced upon me. Had I refused, my head would have been forfeit—and someone who hates you would have taken your place. The Empress does not accept refusal.”

I looked at him, old friend, old comrade-in-arms. “Tatho,” I replied, “there is no man alive whom I would rather entrust my people to than you. For twenty years I’ve ruled Yucatan and Mexico beyond—first under the old King, then under the new Empress. I know these lands like a book. I’ve built roads, tamed rivers, hunted beasts in the deep forests, and taught the people the ways of peace and progress. I’ve served the Gods daily, and led this land through hardship and prosperity alike. I have loved its people as a father loves his children. To you, I leave them—my legacy, my heart’s work.”

“I cannot match your strength or wisdom,” Tatho said, “but I swear I will walk in your footsteps. I came here with sorrow. If I’d known how deeply your departure would wound you, I’d have died before taking your seat.”

“Now we are alone,” I said. “No audience, no masks. Speak plainly.”

He hesitated. “I don’t know what to say. But I believe the Empress needs you. Times are troubled in Atlantis. She wants her strongest men close at hand.”

I frowned. “Why me? I’ve not set foot on Atlantean soil in two decades. I know nothing of her politics. Here, Atlantis is a distant dream. This land is my home. My life is here.”

“But back home,” Tatho said, “your name is legend. When we were boys, the scholars taught that ancient heroes were the greatest men ever born. Now they teach *you*. Mothers name their sons Deucalion. Your name is spoken in homes, in halls, in whispers of admiration. Only one other name rivals it.”

I shook my head. “Such fame means nothing to me. I serve because it is right, not for praise. And if names must be honored, let them be the names of the Gods.”

Tatho smiled faintly. “The Gods? We speak of them less now. Science has outgrown myth. No new deity has risen. So, Deucalion, if it were only the Gods who challenged your name, yours would be heard a thousand times more.”

“And the Empress?” I asked. “She should be first in the hearts of Atlantis. The old King is dead.”

“She would have it so,” Tatho said, his tone shifting. “But there is more beneath the surface.”

I drew him to a marble bench. “Speak to me as Tatho, not as Viceroy. As the man I trained beside, the priest I fought alongside, the brother I trusted. Tell me the truth.”

He sighed. “I almost forget how to speak plainly. These days, honesty is dangerous. But you—you’ve never changed. And so I’ll trust you. You ask about Phorenice?”

“I do.”

“She is not of noble blood. Not even of the Priestly Clan. Once, she was the daughter of a swineherd in the mountains. That truth is buried now—burnt in the fires of her own decree. She claims divine birth, miraculous upbringing. Anyone who remembers otherwise is silenced. Her story is law now.”

“Adopted?” I asked.

“The word ‘adopted’ was erased by her own command,” Tatho said. “By fifteen, she ruled Zaemon’s household—and then the province itself. He was learned, devout, but weak. She… she was different.”

“She took up arms as a boy might,” I said.

“Exactly. She trained with sword and spear, practiced warcraft, and when rebellion broke out, she led Zaemon’s troops—alone. She crushed it. Offered the rebels a choice: death or service. They chose her. From that moment, her army grew.”

“And the Priestly Clan?”

“They sent governors to arrest her. Two fell in battle. A third army came. She defeated them all. Then came the final campaign—the largest army Atlantis had ever raised. The priests put me in command.”

“You?” I stared.

“I was young, proud, confident. They told me: win, and you may wear the crown. The King was dying, the realm unstable. A victorious general could become king. So I went to war, determined to capture her alive.”

“And failed?”

“Not just failed—I was broken. My army was eight times hers. We were armored, drilled, disciplined. But she had fire-tubes—weapons that shot darts beyond bowshot. Her tactics were unlike anything I knew. She moved like lightning, struck from unseen angles. Our lines crumbled. Panic spread. I tried to die fighting. But she stopped me.”

“She came to me,” Tatho said softly. “Surrounded by her guards. ‘Who is this?’ she asked. ‘Lord Tatho,’ they answered. ‘A fine warrior.’ She studied me. ‘You fight like a man of the past. But you could learn the future.’ Then she offered me a choice: the sword—or service.”

“I refused,” I said.

“‘The King is dead,’ she said. ‘And I am Empress.’ I asked, ‘Who made you so?’ She smiled. ‘The same hand that won this battle. And it can be kind—if you choose.’ She looked at me, and I saw something—power, beauty, fire. And then I said: ‘Yes. Such a mistress as you—yes.’”

He paused. “From that moment, I have been hers. Not by fear—but by awe. By love. I hate being here. I long to return to her side. And when you see her, Deucalion—when you stand before her in flesh and light—you will understand. You may still call me a fool. But I beg you—wait until you see her. Then judge me.”

I sat silent, the weight of his words settling like stone. The world had changed. Atlantis was no longer the place I remembered. And the woman who ruled it—she was not just a queen. She was a storm. And now, the storm was calling me home.

Tatho’s words lingered in my mind long after the night had fallen, like a flame that refuses to be extinguished. I couldn’t help but reflect on how deeply I had immersed myself in governing Yucatan—so completely, in fact, that I’d lost touch with what was happening back in Atlantis. For years, it had been evident that the sailors who crossed the seas spoke cautiously, their tongues bound by unspoken laws. Only the news the Empress permitted seeped beyond her borders. Yet I had never sought to pry beneath the veil. My duties in the colony consumed me, and I respected the orders of others—even when they silenced truth. It was not in my nature to torture men simply because they followed commands, even if those commands kept secrets from me.

But now, the iron discipline of the Priestly Clan left no room for hesitation. As custom dictated, I had been stripped of office without warning. From that moment, all records, authority, and power passed to my successor. Though I might remain as a guest in the pyramid I once called home—allowed to witness one final sunrise—I was strictly ordered to leave Yucatan at once and return to Atlantis without delay.

Tatho, though, showed himself to be a man of loyalty. Before dawn broke, he stood beside me, offering every resource at his disposal. I asked little. “A ship to take me home,” I said, “and I shall owe you my gratitude.”

He looked surprised. “You may have one, if you wish. But my vessels are worn from long voyages and need careening. If you take them, your journey will be slow. Why not take your own navy? They’re anchored in harbor now—I saw them when we arrived. Strong ships, well-built and proud.”

“But they are not mine,” I replied. “That fleet belongs to Yucatan.”

“Ah,” he said, smiling faintly, “but Deucalion—you *are* Yucatan. Or rather, you were yesterday, and for twenty years before that.”

I understood his meaning, but it did not please me. “The ships belong either to private merchants or to the state,” I said stiffly. “I cannot claim even a ten-slave galley as my own.”

Tatho shrugged. “Perhaps you know your principles best. But to me, it seems reckless for a man in your position not to maintain a strong navy of his own. You never know when a recall might come—and without preparation, a lifetime’s work could vanish in an hour.”

“I have no fear for my fortune,” I answered coldly.

“Of course not,” he said. “Because you know I am your friend. But had another been appointed viceroy, you might have found yourself stripped bare. Few men resist the temptation of wealth already waiting in coffers they’ve helped fill.”

“Lord Tatho,” I said, “it is clear we no longer share the same values. The wealth I’ve gathered here would hardly tempt any man. I wear the clothes you see me in now. I possess a small box of medicines—nothing more. Three slaves: two scribes and one sturdy European savage who cooks and draws my bath. My salary has been nothing more than a soldier’s ration. And if anyone under my rule has ever been unjustly taxed—even a single ounce of bronze—I ask you, as a final favor, to hang that man for me. Let him die as a liar and thief.”

Tatho stared at me, his expression unreadable. “I do not know whether to admire you or pity you. To be astonished or despise. We heard tales of your integrity in Atlantis—your justice, your sternness—but I swear by the old Gods, no one imagined you carried this far. Money is power, Deucalion. With money, you can move mountains. Without it, you are crushed by the first misstep.”

“The Gods will decide my fate,” I said.

“Possibly,” he replied. “But I prefer to shape mine myself. I came here not to follow your example. I shall govern Yucatan wisely—yes—but also for Tatho, the viceroy. I brought my navy of eight ships, a personal guard, my wife, her women, and her slaves. All must be supported. Why should it be otherwise? A people should pay for their ruler.”

“We shall not agree,” I said. “You hold the power now. Use it as you see fit. If it meant anything, I would beg you to treat these people with mercy. They have grown dear to me.”

“You’ve grown tired of me,” he said, “and I regret it. But even to keep your respect—something I value above all else—I cannot act as you did. It would be impossible. Do not judge all men by your own standard. A Tatho is not a colossus like a Deucalion. And besides… I have a wife and children. They must be provided for—even if I neglect myself.”

“And there,” I said, “you give me the advantage. I have no wife to burden me.”

He caught my tone quickly. “It seems you have nothing that makes life worth living. No wife, no children, no riches, no cook, no retinue, no finery. I must say, old friend, you are strangely ignorant about some things. For instance—you don’t know how to dine. On every day of our long voyage, I promised myself that your feast would make up for all the plain sea fare. I expected a grand banquet. But what did I get? A farmer’s meal. You told me how it was prepared. Your European slave may be strong and loyal—but he’s a demon in the kitchen. By the Gods, I’ve eaten better on campaign!”

“I know this is a frontier settlement,” I said, “without refinements. But if the deer, fish, and other resources of this land are not used better than they have been, I shall consider it my duty to fry a few cooks in grease—to teach them better skills.”

“Deucalion,” he said, “have you forgotten what it means to have taste? Have you no regard for your dignity? Look at your clothes! You dress like a shepherd. Not a jewel, not a gaud to brighten you.”

“I eat,” I said coldly, “when hunger calls. I wear this robe until it wears out. Excessive feasting and many garments are luxuries I never desired. But enough. Our differences run deep. You have changed, Tatho. Perhaps I have too. These changes creep in silently with time. Let us part now, and remember only the friendship we shared twenty years ago. That memory has always tasted sweet to me.”

Tatho bowed. “So be it. And I still charge myself to you for that ship. Dawn is near. It would not be proper for the former ruler to walk through the city streets in daylight after his dismissal.”

“So be it,” he said. “You shall have my navy. I wished you had asked for more.”

“No,” I said. “Not the navy. One small ship. Believe me, more would be wasted.”

“Oh,” he said, “now I shall play the tyrant. I am viceroy now, and I will have my way. You may go with nothing—though I cannot help that. But you shall not leave unattended.”

And so, with no choice, I sailed aboard the *Bear*, Tatho’s personal vessel, escorted by the rest of his fleet. But departure was delayed. The ships lay moored at the inner harbor, drained of supplies, crews exhausted. To launch them immediately would have been suicide. Instead, courtesies were observed—the *Bear* was hauled into the entrance basin and anchored in the fairway, where she and her companions took on wood, water, cured meats, and fish, refitted swiftly and thoroughly.

For the first time in twenty years, I found myself free from labor. Officially, I had already left Yucatan. I could not interfere with the ship’s operations—sea matters belonged solely to the Mariners’ Guild, protected by royal decree. So I spent my days watching the quays, the harbors, the palaces, and pyramids I had helped build stone by stone. I gazed across the fields and grain lands beyond the walls, longing for the dense forests we had cleared so slowly, field by field. Would Tatho continue this work? I hoped so—despite his selfish words.

By day or night, I turned my thoughts to the higher mysteries, the sacred studies of the Priestly Clan—no books, no instruments, no temples needed. Just mind and spirit.

The navy was refitted with remarkable speed. Never before had ships been provisioned, caulked, and remanned so swiftly for an ocean crossing. Barely a month after arriving, they set sail eastward, over the hills and dales of the open sea.

No rowing slaves from Europe were taken now—modern humanity forbade the old custom of letting them eat each other during long voyages. Sails alone were unreliable. But modern science had discovered how to harness solar energy. When the Sun shone clear, machines aboard the ships drew seawater forward and expelled it forcefully at the stern, propelling the vessel forward—even against the wind. This secret knowledge was guarded closely by mariners.

Navigation had also advanced. No longer did sailors rely solely on starry nights. A small device—a balanced image with an arm pointing steadily toward the Southern Cross—was placed at the bow of every ship. By adjusting angles, navigators could steer with precision. Other tools allowed accurate positioning on the vast ocean. Modern sailors trusted less in gods and more in their own strength and wits.

Yet even today, tradition held sway. Before sailing, the crew would wash and dress in their finest clothes, go ashore solemnly, and visit a minor temple—perhaps one whose god was disliked by the common folk. There, they made lavish sacrifices, chanting and offering gifts. Then came a feast in honor of the deity. Drunk and bloated, they returned to the ship, ready to face the sea.

My journey was unlike any I had known. We did not hug the coasts. Instead, we cut straight across the gulf, passing the Carib Islands as if they were signposts. We stopped only twice to replenish—each time, the natives brought wood, water, and fruit freely, so great was their submission. No fights, no resistance. A testament to Atlantis’s growing power and its most distant colony’s strength.

Then onward, boldly, across the endless ocean. No prayers to guide us. No offerings to appease the gods. One might criticize such impiety—but admiration rose for their courage and skill. The dangers of the sea were the gods’ to deal, and men could only endure.

We faced storms. The mariners fought them with grim endurance. Twice, blazing stones fell from the sky, hissing into the sea beside us—harmless, yet terrifying. And the great sea beasts hunted us, as they always had.

Only once did we suffer real loss. Three massive sea lizards attacked the *Bear*—the ship I traveled upon—at once. It happened at midday, when the Sun blazed at full strength. Our solar machines hummed with power, driving the ship forward faster than a man could walk on land.

Three beasts emerged over the horizon—two smaller females on one flank, a giant male on the other. They beat the waves with their flippers, their necks rising like masts. Their jaws chomped hungrily. The air reeked of foulness.

Our navy formed a line, but these creatures no longer hunted alone. Like men, they had learned to fight in packs.

Captain Tob urged me to retreat to the after-castle. He was responsible to Lord Tatho for my safety. He knew the beasts would kill some of the crew. If I were harmed, he feared he would be forced to surrender himself to them—or worse, face Tatho’s wrath.

But I refused. A man gains no wisdom by avoiding danger. This attack was new to me. I wanted to learn.

I wrote a letter to Tatho explaining my decision—though he seemed indifferent. Then I stayed in my seat beneath the awning.

The beasts surged forward. Arrows bounced off their thick hides. Fireballs barely singed them. Only twenty axe blows delivered together could drive one back—and even then, only by sheer force, leaving no mark.

Man has long struggled against beast for dominion. Only in Atlantis, Egypt, and Yucatan has he dared to stand firm. In Europe and Africa, beasts rule. Men hide in caves and treetops, fleeing like prey.

On the oceans, beasts are masters—unchecked.

Yet here, on this desolate sea, though these lizards were new to me, it was thrilling to test my warcraft against their brute strength.

From the beginning, survival demanded action. Hiding below deck was useless—if the beasts couldn’t reach men above, they’d smash the ship apart. So battle had to be met head-on, until the beasts were satisfied and left.

Thus, I found myself in a one-sided fight—and felt the joy of combat once more.

After delivering a dozen heavy axe blows—useless as striking a wall—I found a lance. That changed everything.

Their eyes were small, deep-set in bony sockets. I judged them vulnerable.

I stepped into view, offering myself as bait.

The beast lunged—like a falling stone.

I sidestepped and drove the lance into its eye.

Thank the Gods I had trained with the lance until aim became instinct.

The blade pierced true. The shaft snapped in my hand.

The beast recoiled, blinded and roaring, thrashing the water with its flippers.

In a cascade of foam, I saw it bend its long neck, rubbing its head against its back—still impaled, suffering agony, but unable to dislodge the spear.

Eventually, it gave up and fled—quickly vanishing into the distance.

The other two beasts had also departed—but not so peacefully.

Tob, seeing my resolve, deliberately pushed a sailor into the jaws of each, ensuring they were sated and gone.

It was clear Tob feared for his skin. I smiled inwardly, thinking of the threats Tatho must have issued to protect me.

It is comforting, after all, when old friends still care enough to watch over you.

When we finally sighted the coast of Atlantis, Tob—guided by his modern instruments—had navigated with astonishing precision. Yet, even so, we still had ten more days of sailing ahead, retracing our course until we reached the narrow sea arm that led to the great city of Atlantis, the heart of the continent. The moment we caught the first breath of land on the wind—the scent of earth and greenery—it was as if the gods themselves had breathed life back into us. For weeks, our journey across the endless ocean had taken its toll. Many of our sailors had died; others lay broken with scurvy, weakened by salt-ridden food and relentless storms. But now, at last, the smell of land stirred something deep within them. Those who had been mere shadows, unable to move even under lash, suddenly stirred. They rose, helped rig the sails, manned the oars, and found strength once more—not just to live, but to fight.

Since my fall from power in Yucatan, I had carried a quiet dread about what awaited me in Atlantis. Tatho had assured me that all would be well, but doubt lingered. Would Empress Phorenice welcome me—or would she have my head severed before I stepped ashore? Yet I had not dwelled on it. My fate was in the hands of the gods. If she chose to end me, then so be it. The Sun, my lord above all, watched over me. And so, during the voyage, I kept my mind clear, turning inward to study the deeper mysteries, free from fear or anxiety.

But when we neared the twin headlands that marked the entrance to the inland waters—just two days from the capital—we were met by another navy. They waited in a sheltered bay, their ships anchored calmly, yet scouts perched high on the cliffs shouted warnings as we rounded the point. Instantly, they set sail to block our path.

Of our original fleet, only five vessels remained. The rest had been lost to storms or abandoned, their crews dead from disease. The enemy had three sleek warships and three powerful galleys, each bristling with oars. Their hulls were black and polished, gleaming under the sun. Ours were battered, weed-choked, scarred by storm and time. We flew the banners of Tatho and Deucalion openly, proud of our origin. They came without flags, like thieves ashamed of their lineage.

It was clear they meant to stop us by force. That was no surprise—no law ruled the seas. One ship could attack another with impunity, especially if it returned laden with plunder. So, without hesitation, Tob ordered our ships into battle formation. The other captains followed, drawing close to form a tight squadron. We had no desire to surrender—not now, not when home was so near.

The sun blazed overhead, powering our machines with fierce energy. The two fleets closed rapidly, the enemy galleys holding back to maintain position. But when we were less than a hundred ship-lengths apart, they halted. One galley broke ranks, flying green branches from her masts—a sign of parley.

Unusual, yes—but we were too weakened to risk a fight we couldn’t win. So we stopped too. Tob raised a dried branch in place of greenery, signaling our willingness to talk.

The galley approached, swung around, and backed until its stern scraped against our rail. A man climbed aboard—dressed in fine clothes, young and healthy, untouched by the sea’s cruelty. He looked around with a sneer at our ragged crew, then spotted Tob.

“Old pot-mate,” he said, “your woman waits for you in Atlantis, with four brats at her heels. I saw her not half a month ago.”

Tob’s eyes narrowed. “You didn’t come out here to give me news of home,” he said. “I’ve shared enough wine with you, Dason, to know your tricks.”

“I wanted to remind you that your home still stands,” Dason replied. “Your wife and children are waiting.”

“And I never forget it,” Tob said grimly. “That’s why I’ve sailed through every pirate and storm without giving up. A man with a family doesn’t take slavery lightly.”

“Oh, I know you’re tough,” Dason said. “And I’ll admit, if it comes to blows, we’ll lose men before we sink you and your crippled ships. But our orders are clear. We want what you’re carrying—and if you cooperate, you may go home unharmed.”

“You’ve come to the wrong man for a plucking,” Tob laughed. “We took nothing from Yucatan. No treasure, no goods. Just food and water. We left as we came—bare. Search the holds if you like. You’ll find only sick men. Nothing worth stealing. You can take only blows—and we’ll give them freely.”

Dason smiled coldly. “I’m glad you value your cargo so low. Because that’s exactly what I must take back with me—if you want to reach Atlantis alive.”

Tob frowned. “Speak plainly. I’m no scholar of riddles.”

“It’s obvious,” Dason said. “You’re bringing Deucalion back to Atlantis to strengthen Phorenice. But we oppose her. And we won’t let her gain reinforcements. We want Deucalion—for ourselves.”

“And if I refuse?”

Dason glanced at our shattered fleet. “Then you all go to the depths. And instead of Deucalion standing before the gods alone, he’ll drown with a ragged band of dying men at his heels.”

Tob chuckled. “I doubt it. But we’ll see. As for handing over Deucalion—that’s impossible. First, if I return without him, Tatho will hunt me down and kill me slowly. Second, I’ve seen Deucalion slay a sea monster. He’s a man worth protecting—even from Tatho. Third… you still owe me for our last drinking bout. I’ll see you in the underworld before I give you anything until you pay.”

Dason shrugged. “I hope you drown easily. And that woman of yours—I’ve always had a fancy for her. I’ll find a use for her.”

Tob’s face darkened. “I’ll cut your throat for that, you son of a European! Get off this deck or I’ll do it now. Go! You father of monkey children! Leave me to fight you fairly—or I’ll crush your insides and make your crew wear them as necklaces!”

Dason retreated.

At once, Tob activated the engine of the *Bear* and bellowed orders. Though weak, the crew responded swiftly. Our five ships surged forward, converging on one of the enemy vessels. Unprepared, it tried to turn and flee instead of meeting us head-on. In that moment, all five of our ships rammed it broadside, tearing through its hull. It sank before we could disengage.

Now both sides stood equal—five ships each. But the advantage didn’t last. The enemy galleys formed a line. Their boatswains cracked whips, and the slaves bent to their oars. One of our ships was pierced and sunk, its crew drowned before they could escape.

Then came the battle—fierce, brutal, and unrelenting. Ships grinded together like living beasts. Arrows flew. Axes slashed. Swords thrust. Fire was hurled from both sides. The flames burned flesh and wood alike. Above, giant carrion birds circled, waiting. Below, man-eating fish swarmed to devour those who fell.

Yet our sailors fought with indomitable courage. Death held no terror for them—they had faced worse on the open sea. They fought not just for survival, but for glory. The cry of “Tob!” echoed across the decks, rallying our forces. On the enemy side, shouts of “Dason!” grew louder as their commanders fell.

I had seen many battles in my life—fought for the Priestly Clan, carved out colonies, survived wars. But this—this was unlike any. The instability of the sea, the smoke, the fire, the sky full of hungry birds, the water crawling with predators—it was enough to break the bravest warrior. Yet these sailors laughed as they died.

Our numbers dwindled. By the end, only Dason’s galley and the *Bear* remained. We were outnumbered, outnumbered, and overwhelmed. Boarders stormed the *Bear*, turning the deck into a battlefield. But the fire from our own ship had spread. Smoke poured from every crack. The *Bear* was burning.

Tob roared, “They can stay and fry if they like—but we’re taking the galley! Guard Deucalion! Follow me!”

“Tob!” the crew shouted, drunk on battle fever. Even I joined in.

He charged forward, axe raised, carving a path. Ten of us reached the galley, flames lashing at our backs. We turned and hacked at anyone who followed, cutting loose the grappling lines. The sea pushed the *Bear* away.

On the galley, the rowers—slaves—watched impassively. Only a few of the enemy had scrambled aboard. Three fought fiercely and had to be killed. Five dropped their weapons and were chained to the oar benches. Only Dason remained.

He had surrendered, arms cast aside, face sullen. Tob approached him, laughing.

“Ho, pot-mate Dason,” he said. “You spoke so proudly of my woman in Atlantis. Now I’ll give you a choice: I’ll take you home and tell her everything—before the whole crew, most of whom are dead now—and leave her to decide your fate. Or… I’ll deal with you myself.”

Dason bowed. “Thank you for the chance.” He knelt, offering his neck.

Tob raised his axe and struck. He mounted the head on the galley’s prow. Then threw the body overboard. A great bird swooped down, seized it, and flew off toward the cliffs.

Now we were free. The galley offered fresh meat, fruit, and water. The slaves rowed hard toward Atlantis. In the after-castle, a skin of wine remained. I filled a horn, poured some at Tob’s feet.

“My man,” I said, “you’ve shown me a fight.”

“Thanks,” he said. “And I know you’re a judge. It was good while it lasted. My men were mostly sick—but they fought with honor. I’ve lost Tatho’s navy, but I think Phorenice will right that wrong. If her enemies went to such lengths to stop Deucalion, she’ll surely reward me for delivering him—bruised and bloody, but alive.”

“The gods will decide,” I said. “As they have decided that I must go on to Atlantis.”

Tob held a horn of wine, ready to pour at my feet. But he changed his mind and drank it.

“Fighting is thirsty work,” he said.

I placed my hand on his bloodied arm.

“Tob,” I said, “whether I regain power or die tomorrow, only the gods know. But hear me now: if I ever have the chance to repay you, I will not forget how you served me—or how you fought today.”

Tob refilled his horn, splashed it at my feet.

“That’s good enough,” he said. “My woman and brats will never want again. Let the Lord Deucalion go to the block indeed!”

Until the enemy fleet appeared in the gulf’s mouth, I had given little thought to my role as a recruit for the Empress. But their ambush—waiting in silence, then charging with such reckless fury that they seemed determined to drag me into their grasp—was no accident. It was a sign. Even the blind could see it: I was meant to play a vital part in Atlantis’s fate.

Yet if enemies watched our approach, so too did Phorenice. Scouts from her capital had spotted us from the mountain peaks and raced to deliver the news. The sea arm at the head of Atlantis varies wildly in width. Where mountains have erupted and cooled into stone, the channel narrows to barely a river’s breadth. Then, for half a day’s sail, it flares open like a lake, its shores barely visible. The path winds through marshes, smoking hills, and treacherous flats—where fire rivers or beasts might claim a man. Still, a swift runner who knew the land could carry word faster than any galley could sail.

Of course, such a journey carried danger. A lone courier might not survive. But Phorenice spared no effort. She sent twenty messengers to the headland overlooking the strait, each taking a different route. Seven reached the city unharmed; three others were known to have made it. The rest? We didn’t know. At the time, we believed our arrival was entirely unexpected.

The slaves rowing our galley were mostly savages from Europe—rough men whose stench turned every breath sour. And the wind carried grit from a distant volcano, filling the air with dust and ash. We wanted nothing more than to reach land quickly.

And yet, despite my priestly training in stoicism—despite knowing that all things are in the hands of the gods—I couldn’t help but wonder what awaited me in Atlantis. Would I be taken prisoner upon landing? Tried for healing the Yucatan colony? Or would I slip ashore unnoticed, only to be summoned later by the Empress?

I had no idea how I’d be received.

We sighted the sacred mountain at dawn, its eternal fires glowing behind the city. The boatswains cracked their whips harder, urging the rowers to push on. No sail could be set—the wind was against us—but under oars alone, we gained speed. The mountain loomed larger. Soon, the pyramids and towers of Atlantis rose above the water like visions floating in the light.

It had been twenty years since I last walked those streets. My heart swelled with memory. I saw the temple where I first became a man. The pyramid where I was initiated into the mysteries. Then, as we drew closer, I caught sight of the house where I was raised. My eyes blurred with emotion.

We anchored outside the harbor walls, as law required. The slaves fell silent, panting over their oar-looms. Usually, ships wait hours for port officials to deign to notice them. But this time, there was no delay. A signal rang out from the twin castles at the entrance. The chain between them dropped. A ten-oared boat shot forward from behind the wall, racing to meet us.

“Is that Dason’s galley?” the port captain called.

“It was,” said Tob.

“I saw Dason’s emblem on your prow,” he replied.

“You were Tatho’s captain?”

“And still am. Tatho’s fleet was destroyed by Dason and his allies. This galley was all that remained.”

The captain scanned our group. “I fear you’ll face a dangerous reception. I don’t see Lord Deucalion. Has he arrived with another force? Gods, if he died under your command, the Empress will flay you alive.”

Tob smirked. “With Phorenice and Tatho both so eager for his safety, my lord seems a risky passenger. But I’ll save my skin this time.” He jerked his thumb toward me. “He’s here. He’ll speak for me.”

The port captain stared, then bowed low. “I beg pardon, my lord, for not recognizing your nobleness. I expected… more regal attire.”

“Pish,” I said. “If I choose to dress simply, I won’t complain if I’m mistaken for a commoner. I do not wear finery to advertise my rank. If you feel you owe me compensation, let this inquiry end.”

He bowed again, nearly touching the deck. “I am your humblest servant. It is my honor to guide your galley to its berth.”

Our boat led the way. Tob watched me with a dry grin. “You’ll remember me only as a rough fellow when you step into this world of fine manners,” he said.

“I don’t think so,” I replied.

“Then I’ll prove myself refined,” he said, gripping my hand in his massive fist. “By the gods, Deucalion, you may be a prince, but I’ve known you as a man. You’re the greatest fighter alive—beast or man—and I love you for it. That spear strike of yours on the lizard? They’ll sing about it in taverns for generations.”

We entered the harbor swiftly. Soldiers atop the gate castles blew trumpets in greeting. The port captain hoisted my banner—a symbol of recognition—on his boat. From the quays, music drifted across the water, mingling with the glow of welcoming fires dancing in the sunlight.

I felt humbled. An Empress—Phorenice herself—had arranged this welcome for a mere subject returning home.

The port captain’s boat led us. We followed, our galley brought alongside the royal quay and secured with golden rings—symbols sacred to the throne.

“If Dason could see this honor,” Tob muttered, “he’d have ordered silk ropes instead of hemp. I’m sure he’d frown now—if the sun hadn’t already baked his face stiff.” He looked at me. “My lord, shall you tread carefully over this ship, or walk straight onto the carpet they’ve laid for you?”

The port captain heard and turned pale. I sighed softly. Colonial freedom had no place here. I would have to relearn the rules of ceremony.

Still, I would not surrender myself entirely. When I stepped into the crimson pavilion erected for me, I accepted the shelter—but no further. Three chamberlains, dressed in dazzling robes, offered me clothes—garments covered in jewels, silks, and frills. They insisted I change.

I refused.

One stammered, “But the Empress prefers…”

“Does she?” I asked sharply. “Have you received an order from her on this matter?”

He hesitated. Then admitted: no.

“Then,” I said, “Phorenice commanded Deucalion the man to appear before her. She said nothing about his clothing. This outfit suits me. It keeps me warm, shields me from wind and sun, and is clean. Your interference borders on insult.”

They drew swords—three of them—and thrust the hilts toward me.

“It would be a kindness,” their leader said, “if you took your revenge now, rather than leave us to torture.”

“Poof,” I said. “Forget it. You make too much of nothing.”

But I understood. They meant it. In this new Atlantis, even a disagreement over clothing could bring torture. The world had changed.

There was a bath in the pavilion. I washed gladly—though some foul scent spoiled the water. Then I waited, calm and composed.

The chamberlains stood silently. I did not speak. We sat in silence beneath the blazing sun, while music blared and fires filled the square beyond.

I’ve always thought it petty for the powerful to make others wait. And Phorenice agreed. As soon as Tob’s galley touched the quay, she left the palace.

The procession was ready—magnificent, bedecked, awaiting only her. She mounted her steed, and the trumpets sounded. The march began.

From the great street, soldiers emerged. They marched across the square, saluted me, then lined up on the far side. Then came the Mariners’ Guild, then merchants, tanners, spear-makers—each guild parading in turn, dressed as if for a festival. Some rode beasts they had tamed into service.

Then came the wonders.

First, a colossal bull mammoth stepped into view. It was enormous—even among its kind. Yet it moved with perfect calm, bearing on its back a golden half-castle adorned with silver snakes and the symbol of the outstretched hand. Its tusks were gilded, its neck garlanded with flowers. It walked like a noble beast, born for ceremony.

And then—Phorenice.

She sat upon the mammoth’s back, high above the crowd. The beast drew my eye at first—its size, its power. But then I saw her.

I rose.

She was slight, supple, perfectly formed. Her hair was short, red-gold, falling in waves over her shoulders. Her face—sharp, intelligent, beautiful beyond words. And her eyes… I could not read them. Not one thought escaped them.

She studied me just as intently. I returned her gaze.

When the people shouted, I stood firm. I did not kneel. One chamberlain whispered, “On your knees, my lord!”

“Bent head at least,” urged another.

But I walked across the square, head high, meeting her stare.

She nodded slightly. When I bowed as befit my rank, she spoke—loud enough for all to hear.

“Never again bow your forehead to the ground for me. For others, it is fitting. But you—Deucalion—are above all men in Atlantis. If anyone refuses to obey you, they are my enemy too. They shall feel my wrath.”

A stair was brought. She gestured. I climbed beside her in the golden half-castle, under the canopy of royal snakes. A girl fanned us with perfumed feathers. At her command, the mammoth turned, carrying us back toward the royal pyramid.

Behind us, the procession moved—soldiers, traders, guards marching on either flank.

She smiled at me. “You piqued me at first.”

“I am overwhelmed by your attention, Majesty.”

“You looked at my steed before me. A woman finds that hard to forgive.”

“I envied you the greatest of conquests,” I said. “I’ve fought mammoths. Killed them. But never dared dream of taming one.”

“You speak boldly,” she said, smiling. “And yet with charm. Faugh! These people fawn on me. It makes me sick. I am not like them. Just because I am a goddess’s daughter, they feed me lies.”

So Tatho was right. The swineherd was forgotten. Well, if she chose to pretend, I wouldn’t challenge it. I was her servant—whether rightly or wrongly.

“I’ve longed for something stronger than this,” she continued. “And so I summoned you. I’ve seen your images—drawn by tongues. I knew you before I met you. I admire a man who rejects foolish clothes, feasts, and women’s flattery.”

She glanced down at her own silks and jewels. “We women like color. But it’s different. So I called you—not for pleasure, but to rule with me.”

“There are better men in Atlantis,” I said.

“There are not,” she replied. “They adore me. They tire me with empty words. Their loyalty is only for themselves. So I chose you—the only strong man in the world. Will you not sigh to be my lover?”

I saw her watching me from the corner of her eye.

“The Empress is my mistress,” I said. “I will serve her honestly. As for Phorenice the woman… I expect little involvement. And I am not one who plays with love.”

“And yet,” she mused, “you are quite attractive.”

“That proves my strength,” I said. “I won’t lose my mind to infatuation.”

She turned to her attendant. “Ylga, stop fanning so hard.”

We passed through the main street—the most magnificent city the world had ever known. I had left it two decades ago. Now I saw its growth: new temples, pyramids, palaces, statues everywhere. Its splendor was greater than I remembered. Imperial. Unmatched.

Yet beneath it all—poverty. Squalor. Gaunt faces, naked bodies, hungry eyes. Traders in flashy clothes wore frightened expressions. They hid their fear behind trinkets.

Phorenice saw where I looked.

“The season has been unhealthy,” she said. “These people refuse to build fine homes. They live in filthy hovels. Fever has spread. Work is hard. Trade has nearly stopped—since rebels have been attacking the gates.”

I blinked. “Rebels? At the gates of Atlantis? Is the city under siege?”

“They’ve granted us a holiday today,” she said lightly. “So your welcome comes undisturbed. If they were fighting, you’d hear it. They’re noisy, these rebels. My spies say they’re building engines to breach the walls. You may go out tomorrow and destroy them, if it amuses you.”

“For today,” she said, “you have me. And I have you. Peace surrounds us. Beauty is displayed. If you want more—I will give it.”

“I didn’t know of this rebellion,” I said. “But now that I am your minister, I must know its full scope. This is serious.”

“And do you think I cannot take it seriously?” she retorted.

She turned to Ylga. “Loosen my dress at the shoulder.”

The girl unclasped the jeweled fastening—reluctantly, it seemed. Phorenice pulled the fabric aside, revealing her bare skin. Just below her left breast, a bandage stained with blood.

“There,” she said, looking at me sideways. “Proof of my seriousness. The arrow struck a rib. It saved me. Had it hit between, you’d be standing beside my funeral pyre now—instead of riding this beast you admire so much.”

Her gaze held mine.

“You seem to look at the mammoth more than me,” she said softly. “Ah, poor me. I am not one of your shaggy creatures. So I suppose I’ll never win your regard.”

She turned to Ylga. “Fasten it back.”

I said nothing. I had seen wounds before. There was nothing else to see.

But the truth was clear: this was not a woman who played games. She was strong. Dangerous. And she had chosen me—not for love, but for power.

And so, beneath the golden sky, riding a tame giant, I became the minister of Atlantis.

For the time being, it seemed I would be living within the royal pyramid. The glittering procession came to a halt in the vast paved square before the structure, forming clusters of color and motion. The mammoth was brought to a stop just before the entrance, and when a stairway was rolled into place, the trumpets blared, signaling our arrival. We three—those who had ridden beneath the golden half-castle draped in serpent banners—descended to the ground.

It was clear we were moving from the open sky into the hidden depths of the pyramid’s stone labyrinth. Without thinking, instinct took over—the deep-rooted reverence bred into me by years of tradition caused me to turn toward the Sacred Mountain, its jagged peaks looming above the city like ancient sentinels. I bowed my head, offering the customary prayer of gratitude to the gods, especially to our Lord the Sun, for the air we breathed.

I did this without conscious thought—purely as habit—but as I rose, I swear I heard a soft, mocking titter ripple through the crowd. My gaze snapped toward the source, frowning, ready to demand punishment for such insolence. But then I turned to Phorenice, expecting to see her still in the act of bowing. Instead, she stood upright, untouched by any gesture of submission. And she watched me with an expression I couldn’t read.

Whatever she was thinking, she kept silent before the gathered people. “Come,” she said simply, then turned toward the doorway and called out the secret word for the day. The massive stone blocks groaned on their hinges and swung inward. With stately grace, she stepped into the cool shadows beyond, followed closely by her fan-girl attendant. A heavy weight settled in my chest as I followed, and behind us, the great doors slammed shut with a final, echoing thud.

We didn’t go far. Phorenice stopped in the Hall of Waiting—a place I remembered well. The red walls bore portraits of past kings, and at the center, a jet of earth-breath burned steadily from a bronze spout, casting flickering light across the floor. This was where the late king had bid me farewell twenty years prior, before I set off for my governorship in Yucatan. But the atmosphere was different now. Once pure and fresh, the air now carried a thick, cloying perfume that made my limbs feel sluggish and my thoughts slow.

“My minister,” Phorenice said, “I forgive you any intention of insult. But I think the colonial life has made you… simple. That kind of obeisance you just offered the mountain hasn’t been seen since I came to rule.”

“Your Majesty,” I replied, “I belong to the Priests’ Clan. I was raised in their teachings. Before entering any dwelling, I thank the gods—for the breath they give, and for the sun that warms the land. I’ve been chased through mountains by rivers of fire and poisonous fumes more than once. So when I pray, it comes from the heart.”

“Times have changed since you left Atlantis,” she said. “Now, thanks are not given to those old gods.”

I understood her meaning—and recoiled. To reject the divine? To replace them with something new? If this was the future, I could not serve it. I might face fate as it came, but I would not betray my faith. I would not bow to a mere woman, no matter how powerful, as if she were a deity. I chose to challenge her.

“Phorenice,” I said, “I’ve been a priest all my life. I revere the gods and know their mysteries. Until I see proof they are false, I will stand by them. If there is a cost for that loyalty, I will pay it.”

She smiled slowly. “You’re a strong man, Deucalion.”

I bowed.

“I’ve known others just as stubborn,” she continued. “But they were converted.” She tossed her fiery hair, turning so the glow of the earth-breath caught the lines of her face and body. “I’ve found it easier to convert the stubborn than to burn them. In fact, there’s been little need for burning. They’ve rushed to conversion, whether I wanted them to or not. But today… perhaps my charm fails me.”

“Phorenice is Empress,” I said flatly. “And I am her servant. Tomorrow, if she allows, I’ll clear the rebels from the city gates. I must prove my worth.”

“I’ve heard you’re a skilled fighter,” she said. “And I have some skill myself. I fancy I can judge a man’s courage. Tomorrow, we’ll spar together. But tonight, I must complete the honor I’ve planned for you, Deucalion. The feast is nearly ready. You’ll want to prepare. Chambers have been prepared for you—filled with everything you’ll need. Ylga will show you the way.”

We waited until Phorenice had slipped through a shadowed doorway and disappeared from the light. Then, with Ylga leading and a lamp in hand, we wound our way through the twisting corridors of the pyramid.

Everywhere, perfumes hung in the air. The passageways bent and doubled through solid rock, so complex that even a seasoned traveler might wander for days. These builders had been master architects—designed to keep kings safe from assassins who might slip in from outside. Some say the king himself ordered the deaths of every worker who helped construct it, so no one would ever know its secrets.

Yet Ylga moved with ease. She turned here, paused there, pressed a stone in the wall—it slid aside, revealing a hidden passage. Once, she tapped the corner of a flagstone, which lifted under her foot, exposing a narrow staircase. We descended, then climbed again along a sloping path, until finally we reached the chamber assigned to me.

“There’s clothing in each chest along the walls,” she said. “Jewels and ornaments in that bronze coffer. Phorenice says these are her first gifts—just a taste of what’s to come. My lord, you may drop your simplicity now. Dress yourself in finery. Fit the fashion.”

“Girl,” I said sharply, “be respectful. Spare me such advice.”

“If you find that rude,” she replied, “you can tell Phorenice. I’ll be whipped. Or stripped and scourged before you. She’ll do much for you.”

“Girl,” I said, “you’re closer to that whipping than you think.”

She narrowed her eyes. “I have a name. They call me Ylga. You might’ve heard it when we rode on the mammoth—if you hadn’t been too busy staring at Phorenice.”

I studied her. “You’ve never seen me before. Yet your first words could bring trouble. There’s purpose behind this.”

She stepped to the heavy stone door, then pulled me back from the airshaft. “I’m Zaemon’s daughter,” she whispered. “The man you knew.”

“You carry a message from him?”

“How could I? He lives among the priests on the Sacred Mountain. I haven’t seen him in two years. But when I saw you step from that red pavilion at the harbor… I felt pity. I remembered you were my father’s friend. And I knew what Phorenice plans. She’s been plotting this for two months.”

“I cannot listen to slander against the Empress.”

“And yet—”

“What?”

She stamped her sandal on the stone. “You’re either very blind or very brave, Deucalion. But I won’t interfere—yet. Still, I’ll watch. If you ever need a friend, I’ll help.”

“I thank you for your friendship.”

“You take it lightly. Why, sir, you don’t even know my power—or my reason. You may be the most powerful man in the kingdom, but I rank second among women. And remember—women hold high favor in Atlantis now. Believe me, my friendship is sought after.”

“And I’m grateful,” I said. “You seem to undervalue it, Ylga. But let me say—this is the first time I’ve ever offered such gratitude to a woman. You should treasure it.”

She smiled faintly. “Well, my lord, you have much to learn.” She left, showing me how to summon slaves. For a moment, I stood stunned by what I’d said. Who was this daughter of Zaemon—this girl—who could make me break a lifetime of custom?

The slaves arrived at my call, eager to dress me in elaborate robes and smear me with perfumes and ointments. Their manner annoyed me. I was already clean-shaven, my hair trimmed, my clothes spotless. Their attentions felt intrusive. I ordered them to beat each other—threatening to hand them over to the brander if they didn’t do it thoroughly. It’s strange how even the lowest servant can provoke irritation.

That day had brought many wonders, but nothing shocked me more than how Phorenice summoned the guests to the feast. Not just surprised—shocked. As I sat in my chamber, a sound began to rise from nowhere—soft at first, then swelling into a deafening clangor. The entire pyramid trembled, groaned, shook—as if it were a toy being kicked by a giant.

This was the sacred portent—the annual ritual performed by the Priests’ Clan atop the Sacred Mountain. A warning: man was born from fire and earthquake; he would end the same way. A reminder of sin, mortality, and divine justice. But here, Phorenice had twisted it into a summons to gluttony, laughter, and indulgence.

How had she gained such power? Who was she to command forces that stirred the earth itself? Had a priest betrayed his vows? Had she uncovered a secret herself?

If she’d discovered it alone, I could serve her in silence. She was Empress—my duty was obedience. But if she’d corrupted a priest… then it was a different matter. Our law demanded that both betrayer and recipient die. Secrets were sacred.

With uneasy resolve, I answered the trembling call. Slaves guided me through the winding paths to the banqueting hall. The scene was dazzling. The immense chamber, carved with intricate designs, was filled with guests dressed in every color imaginable. No one cared about the portent, the rebellion outside, the starving people, or the neglected gods. They lived only for the present.

Flames of earth-breath lit the hall like daylight. When I entered, trumpets blared. But no roar of welcome. “Deucalion,” they murmured, bowing low, their silks jingling. Even when Phorenice appeared, the greeting was stiff, artificial. Heartfelt joy was considered barbaric now.

Two smirking chamberlains escorted me to the dais. I was to sit beside her, eat from her dish.

“No greater honor could be shown,” I said, kneeling.

She gave me a sidelong glance. “Deucalion may have more—if he asks prettily. He may have what every man in the world has dreamed of—and none have earned. But I’ve given enough. He must ask warmly.”

“I ask,” I said, “to clear the city’s borders of the rebels.”

“Pah,” she scoffed. “Only stern pleasures for you? My dear Deucalion, your colony must have been primitive. Well, tell me—how delicious is this feast?”

Dishes and goblets were placed before us. I ate little—spices too sharp, food too refined. But the others feasted wildly, savoring each bite as if it were communion with the gods. Women and men alike lingered over their meals, lost in ecstasy.

Phorenice watched, occasionally tossing a word to a favorite, calling someone forward to receive a scrap from her plate. The honored guest would devour it dramatically—or sometimes tuck it away, as if it were too sacred for human lips.

To me, it was revolting. But she accepted it as natural. There was a flaw in her—though none could see it. Her face was strong, intelligent, beautiful. The court praised her beauty endlessly. Though I wasn’t one for admiration, I had to admit she was striking. Yet I found myself drawn more to Ylga, standing behind us—her face honest, unpretentious.

They didn’t eat to live. They ate to excess. New customs emerged with each course. One guest—a flamboyant governor from a rebellious province—approached the divan, flushed and swaying. “Most glorious Empress!” he cried, “fairest of goddesses, queen of my soul! Hail!”

Phorenice handed him her cup. I expected him to pour it at her feet. Instead, he drank it down. “May all your troubles vanish as easily—and leave such a sweet aftertaste!”

She turned to me. “You disapprove of this new custom?”

“To pour drink at one’s feet is respect,” I said. “To drink it is self-indulgence.”

“Our new code says enjoy life first,” she replied. “Order comes later.”

The feast continued—gluttony, absurdity, laughter. Guards stood rigid around the hall, statues of stone. But suddenly, a stir broke the revelry. At the far door, guards crossed spears—but a man passed through unharmed, walking calmly across the pavement.

All turned to look. A few laughed. Others shivered as he passed—like a cold wind had swept through.

He stood before Phorenice’s divan, staring at her, not bowing.

An old man. Thin, white-haired, beard ragged. Mud clung to his legs. Bare except for a single cloth wrapped about him. In his hand, a staff tipped with the glowing symbol of the Sun—marking him as a priest.

I thought he was one of the ascetics—those who abandoned the safety of the Sacred Mountain to live among the burning hills, enduring fire, poison, beasts, and earthquakes. They lived close to the gods, humbled by nature’s fury, and learned mysteries no scholar could grasp.

Silence fell. Phorenice spoke first. “Those two guards failed. Remove them.”

Spears struck. Both guards collapsed—one screamed, the other died silently.

The old man’s voice cracked. “Another crime added to your record, Phorenice. You could not have stopped me if you tried. I come with your last warning. The gods have favored you—gave you merit to rise. They allowed you the throne. But now, you must stop your evil. Feed the starving—even if it means taking from these gluttons. Restore Atlantis to its former order. Or you will be removed. The country cannot fall back into chaos. You hear me. Now answer truly.”

“Speak gently,” whispered Ylga from behind us. “For your sake, speak gently.”

But Phorenice ignored her. She leaned forward, brows furrowed.

“Do you dare threaten me, old man—knowing who I am?”

Thus was I welcomed back to the capital of Atlantis, and given my first glimpse into the new order she now upheld. I confess I stood apart, hesitant, even passive—but not from weakness. It was choice. The old bonds of duty had frayed, tangled in shifting loyalties. Once, serving the king meant serving the Priest Clan, from which he rose and to which he owed allegiance. But Phorenice was no heir; she was forged in fire, a sovereign unto herself. If Zaemon spoke for the priests—and their clan had turned against her—then how could a man of honor choose between them without thought?

Yet reason reminded me: governments exist not for one, but for all. I would judge not by blood or creed, but by what served Atlantis best. And so, before committing myself, I sought truth beyond the palace walls. There were whispers of rebellion, of a faction strong enough to besiege the city itself. To understand the balance of power, I needed to see both sides. So on that very night I stepped once more onto Atlantean soil, I set out to learn what lay beyond the royal gates.

No one within the pyramid opposed me. The banquet had ended abruptly—the scene I’ve already described—when Tarca collapsed, his leprous wounds exposed, driving even the most hardened feasters from their revelry. Phorenice glared at the table in silence, fury burning in her eyes. Then, her gaze turned to me—softened slightly.

“You may be honest, Deucalion,” she said at last, “but you are cold as stone. When will you thaw?” A faint smile touched her lips. “I think soon. But for now—farewell. Tomorrow, we shall take this land by the shoulders and reshape it.”

She left, Ylga at her side. I followed, outranking all present, though none dared move until I passed. Before I departed, I stopped beside Tarca and laid my hand upon his diseased flesh.

“You are unfortunate,” I said, “but I respect a brave soldier. If relief can be found, I’ll do what I can to bring it.”

He mumbled thanks through cracked lips. Nearby, nobles recoiled, pretending disgust. I turned on them, brow darkening.

“Your charity is as thin as your courage,” I snapped. “You mocked Zaemon’s words, sneered at his faith, yet when it came to touch him—you drew back. In older days, we called that cowardice. I’d rather be Tarca, unclean as he is, than any of you.”

I waited, cold, until they bowed. Then I walked out, ignoring every eye. I made for the grand gate of the pyramid, summoned the guard captain, and demanded passage.

The officer hesitated. “My lord’s attendants have not arrived?”

“I have none.”

“The state of the streets—do you know it?”

“I did twenty years ago. I’ll find my way.”

“The city is under siege,” he insisted. “People starve. They hunt in packs after dark. You may be stronger than any ten men—but—”

“I seek no fight tonight,” I said. “Give me a dozen men. You know the number needed. I am used to quiet streets.”

A score of guards formed ranks on either side. We marched from the perfumed hush of the pyramid into the cool moonlight of the city. My purpose: to walk the walls and see what the rebels truly wanted.

But fate had another lesson in store.

The city I saw that night was no longer the Atlantis of my youth—the slow growth of centuries, a harmony of beauty and utility. Splendor remained—indeed, it had multiplied. Entire districts had been razed to make room for new palaces, towering pyramids of the wealthy. I could not deny the skill behind such monuments.

Under the silver glow of the moon, I almost understood why barbarians worshiped great buildings as gods—so vast, so perfect, they seemed beyond human hands.

Yet for every gleaming edifice, a hundred families had been displaced. Now, every alcove sheltered sleepers, every shadow held desperate figures—wild-eyed, hungry, ready to strike at any who passed.

I am no saint for the poor. Let a man starve, and he’ll work to survive—or die, and the state is rid of him. But here, the cruelty was plain. Starved corpses littered gutters, bones visible beneath sunken skin. The people were crushed under a weight far heavier than mere hunger.

Still, I could not complain—not openly. By ancient law, all belonged to the king, and Phorenice now wore his crown. So I pressed on, eyes lifted to the stars, seeking guidance from the High Gods above, trying to rise above the stench and ruin below.

At last, the winding streets brought us to the walls.

And there, a commotion broke the stillness.

Laughter echoed across the night. Roars like thunder. Shrieks of pain. This was no place for sudden arrival.

The gates were masterworks of engineering—four massive stone valves per wall, each twice a man’s height and thick, set within double-layered fortifications. Between the outer and inner doors lay an open courtyard. The valves opened only when the opposite gate closed—impossible to force without siege engines. Trying to break them was like pushing down the pyramid with bare hands.

My escort sounded the horn. A warder appeared at an arrow-slit, inspecting our party with ritual formality. The noise inside drowned his words again and again.

Finally, the formalities ended. He pulled back the heavy bolts, swung open one monstrous valve, and we crowded into the chamber between the gates.

Then he returned, bowing low.

“I have no vail to offer,” I said sharply. “Proceed. Open the other door.”

“With respect, my lord,” he replied, “it might be wise to announce your presence. There is a baiting in progress. The tigers are wild—no respect for rank.”

“The what?”

“The tigers,” he said. “The Empress has sent two to guard the gate. They’re feeding on prisoners. But if you wish, there are galleries above—safe viewing.”

I had no taste for bloodlust, but I sensed this was part of Phorenice’s design. Curiosity overcame caution. I stepped into the guard lodge, then up into the gallery, where arrow-slits overlooked the courtyard.

The builders had meant these for defense—should the outer gate fall, enemies would be cut down before reaching the inner doors. But now they served as stands for a spectacle.

Moonlight flooded the courtyard. The air reeked of blood.

It wasn’t sport. It was slaughter.

I’ve seen men fight beasts—fairly. Give a prisoner weapons, pit him against a creature of equal strength, let him die fighting. That I can respect. But this—this was mockery.

The beasts weren’t ordinary tigers. These were cave-tigers—monsters of immense size, capable of dragging down a mammoth. I had hunted beasts all my life, but never dreamed of capturing one alive. Yet here they were, chained to the wall, controlled by windlasses hidden in the stone.

Their collars bore thick bronze chains, tethered to the wall. At normal times, they were kept at either end of the courtyard, leaving space between. But now, the chains were slackened—men stood ready at the windlasses.

They prowled slowly, links clanking behind them. Eight bodies lay dead on the ground—unbitten. The tigers nudged them playfully, pawing gently, but showed no interest in eating.

Then, a valve opened. A prisoner was thrown into the yard. He fell face-first, then lay still, eyes fixed on the ground.

The gate slammed shut. The tigers paused. A chorus of bets rang from the watchers.

Then the windlasses cranked—the chains tightened. One tiger crouched, tail lashing, and launched forward. The chain sang. It missed by inches. Cheers erupted.

The second sprang—also missed. More shouts. The man didn’t move.

The tigers lay down, began to purr. They rolled on their backs, luxuriating.

Slowly, they crept toward the man. The male couldn’t reach. The female just grazed him with a claw—drawing a red line across his skin.

He still wouldn’t stir.

I expected them to tighten the chain. But no.

The male rose, turned, backed against his leash. A roar went up from the watchers.

He stretched to full length—and kicked backward with one hind leg.

I heard the crack of ribs. The prisoner flew through the air like a stone.

But the she-tiger caught him mid-flight. She nuzzled him, tossed him high—then leapt upward, landing on top of him.

The crowd roared in delight.

I felt sickness rise in my throat. The death was quick—but the degradation? Unbearable.

Still, I remained silent. I came not to judge, but to understand.

Then—another gate opened.

Another prisoner emerged.

This time—a woman.

I froze.

Not because I’d never seen women killed before. I had. War knows no gender. But this—this was wanton. She was helpless, stripped of clothes, thrust into the arena like prey.

I wrestled with myself. Should I intervene?

But policy outweighed pity. I watched, face still.

Yet my heart beat faster.

She was no passive victim. As she landed, she grabbed a bone from the floor, smashed its end against the wall, and sharpened it with her teeth. Her white teeth flashed in the moonlight.

The tigers approached, but avoided her. They knew their game.

She didn’t wait. When the she-tiger turned, she lunged—striking at its eye with the bone.

A roar from the crowd.

The wound was shallow, but the beast bellowed in pain and fled.

Then she ran—sprinting across the yard, straight toward the wall where I stood.

She leapt—like a spirit driven by terror and will.

Her body soared. I measured it later. Even with momentum, she cleared the pavement by a man-height and a half.

Her fingers caught the edge of the arrow-slit.

She slipped—would have fallen—if I hadn’t reached out.

I grasped her wrist. She scrambled up, knees braced, fingers gripping mine.

But she didn’t thank me.

Instead, with a snarl, she drove the sharpened bone through the slit—aiming at my chest.

I was stunned. I’d expected gratitude. Not attack.

I caught her arm, disarmed her, held her fast.

“That was ungrateful,” I said. “Without my hand, you’d be dead.”

“I must kill someone,” she gasped, “before I die.”

“There will be time,” I said. “For now, you’re safe.”

“You lie,” she spat. “You’ll throw me to the beasts. I know your kind. You won’t lose your sport.”

“I’ll prove myself,” I said. “Warder—tether those tigers shorter. Then go out and help her down.”

Orders were passed. The chains shortened. The warder descended.

I joined her in the courtyard.

She stood beneath the moon, breathing hard.

But others were furious. A man burst through the gate, armed, enraged.

“By all the Gods!” he shouted. “Who dares interrupt my pastime?”

I looked at him—then at the woman—then back at the shadows where the tigers waited.

And I knew: the real battle had just begun.

I had not slept in days, and so when night fell, I slipped into a deep rest while Nais watched over me inside the quiet temple. We had planned for me to wait until the camp was asleep—then I could slip away unseen toward the sea.

But the night brought rain, a cold drizzle that seeped through the cracks in the temple walls. Outside, fires burned steadily, their flames flickering against the damp air. Men and women huddled in animal hides and tattered clothes, warming themselves as steam rose from their soaked bodies. Such discomfort breeds sharp tempers, and Nais frowned at the sight. “A fine night,” she said dryly. “I would have sent you back to the city without a soul knowing. But this chill keeps people restless. We must wait until they’re too tired to notice.”

We sat together on the ancient stone floor, untouched by worshippers for generations. Few words passed between us—but silence can be its own kind of companionship.

Then, slowly, the night stirred. From far off, a horn or shell began to blare—a harsh, unrelenting sound. It drew nearer, louder, and soon voices erupted from the fires, shouting at the noise. The sound stopped abruptly, and we stood to look through the slits in the wall.

The camp was waking. Figures gathered around a figure standing atop the pedestal of an old war engine, speaking with fervor. We couldn’t hear his words, but it was clear he held their attention.

“Let’s go,” Nais whispered. “We’ll move away from the firelight.”

“But how do I explain being here?”

“There’s no need to explain,” she replied sharply. “They’ll think you’re my lover. That’s common enough. But oh, if only the gods had given you a beard—curled like any other man—you could’ve slipped away hours ago.”

“A smooth chin suits me better.”

“So it does,” she murmured, pushing open the temple door. “Yours.”

Outside, ragged men and women called out for her. She had a voice, they said, and they feared this preacher might divide them unless someone countered him. So they summoned her.

And now, I found myself swept into danger by chance. But Nais refused to let conflict begin. “Come with me,” she whispered. “Stay the light.”

As we stepped out, the crowd didn’t linger long. A few coarse jokes flew—jokes I could have answered with steel—but they moved on, turning every now and then to make sure we followed. Around us, others streamed forward, shadows against the black sky. Rain splattered the fires, and behind us came more footsteps. No one slept anymore. All were drawn to the preacher on the war engine. To try to escape the crowd would have been impossible.

So we walked forward, deeper into the storm.

At first, we heard little of what the old man said. Then, near the end of a passionate speech, he asked: “Who are your chiefs?”

Chaos broke out. “We have no chiefs!” the crowd roared. “We are done with rulers! Take your magic—we’ll die before we bow again. Not Phorenice. Not priests. Not anyone. We are free!”

The press brought us closer. Now we saw him clearly—an old man with white hair tumbling over his shoulders, a long untrimmed beard, naked except for a scrap of cloth around his waist. His body glistened in the rain.

Yet in his hand, he held something unmistakable: a wand tipped with a flame that never died. It burned like a star fallen from heaven, casting a halo of light across his wet form. This was the Symbol of our Lord the Sun—the mark of the Priestly Clan. No forgery could match it. No civilized man would doubt it.

And though the crowd raged, they did not question his authority. They acknowledged his power. “You may kill us with your magic,” they screamed, “but rule us? Never.”

But I saw him differently. This was Zaemon—Nais’s father. He had seen me yesterday, seated beside Phorenice. Today, he could call me Deucalion and have me destroyed. These rebels had already tried to sink a navy to kill me. If they knew I was near, even with Nais defending me, they’d tear me apart.

I felt death closing in. The gods guard the threads of life well, but I sensed mine was fraying.

Nais saw it too. She gripped my hand—tight—and I squeezed back. I would miss her. And yet, a grim satisfaction stirred within me. If they came for me, I would leave behind a pile of dead. That was the nature of a warrior—especially one facing his last battle. The hunger for combat is deeper than fear, and only one who has fought to the end knows how fiercely it burns.

For now, Zaemon did not see me. He was lost in his sermon, and I had time to listen.

He spoke of the ancient order. Since the dawn of time, the gods had chosen a single clan to lead and serve as priests. For centuries, they ruled justly—defending Atlantis from invaders, mastering beasts, studying the earth to warn of earthquakes and eruptions. Their purpose was always the good of the land, the glory of the gods.

“But,” he thundered, waving the glowing symbol, “the people grew soft. They forgot the gods’ gifts, believing their prosperity came from their own strength. They abandoned worship. Temples fell silent. And so the gods punished them.”

He told of a priest’s wife who found a child left by the roadside and adopted her. That child—the gods shaped her into a scourge. Her name was Phorenice. And those who suffered under her rule knew the full weight of divine wrath.

“But the gods can raise up as they can cast down,” Zaemon declared. “Those who will bring Phorenice low are already chosen. The old order shall return—but only after you have been humbled enough to beg for it.”

He raised the symbol. “See this power? You know it. You feel it. I am its voice. Choose: Will the gods destroy Phorenice? Or must you suffer longer before you are ready to return?”

Then, a young man pushed through the crowd and leapt onto the platform. “Hear me, brothers!” he bellowed. “We are done with tyrants. Death comes for all of us, and we’ve shown we don’t fear it. But we will not submit again. My lord,” he said, turning to Zaemon, “you may kill me with magic. But I speak my truth.”

“I can,” said Zaemon, “but I won not.” He turned, eyes narrowing. “You’ve said your folly. Step down.”

“We have free speech,” the youth insisted. “I will not go until I choose.”

“You will,” said Zaemon. He made no motion—no threat. Yet the young man suddenly froze. His mouth opened, but no sound came. Fear seized him. He stumbled backward, vanishing into the crowd.

The people gasped. Then they demanded a miracle. “Show us a sign!” they shouted. “Prove your power! Then maybe we’ll return!”

Zaemon glowered. “You dogs! Empty-headed fools! Do you think I should degrade the sacred mysteries into a circus act? You want to be tempted back? Then come in humility. I swear you’ll find mercy. Remain stubborn, and Phoren: the scourge—may grind you into dust before she herself is brought to justice.”

The crowd roared. Weapons rose. The desire for independence was strong—stronger than any loyalty. Those who might have surrendered stayed silent, afraid of the mob’s fury. A cruel joke about freedom, I thought, whispering to Nais. But she clutched my arm. “Be quiet,” she hissed. “Or they’ll tear you apart. They’re hungry for blood.”

“Just hours ago,” I reminded her, “you wanted me dead.”

She turned, eyes flashing. “A woman can change her mind. But it becomes you little to remind her of her fickleness.”

A man beside me turned sharply. “Oh?” he said. “No beard. Who are you? Why cut it instead of curling it? I see no wound.”

I smiled politely. “With freedom as your creed, my chin is my own business.”

But he wasn’t satisfied. He looked like trouble. So I grabbed him—by the throat and shoulder—twisted his neck with a swift, brutal motion. I heard the crack. Before anyone noticed, I released him. The crowd held him upright. He stood there, head drooping, as if asleep or fainting. I didn’t want to start a fight here—not in this crush.

But Zaemon still preached. He led them through the ancient rites. And as he chanted, the people responded—old habits returning. They sang the old prayers, voices rising in reverence.

“... WHO STILLED THE BURNING OF THE MOUNTAINS, AND MADE COOL PLACES ON THE EARTH FOR US TO LIVE—PRAISE TO THE MOST HIGH GODS!

“WHO GAVE US MASTERY OVER THE LESSER BEASTS AND SKILL OF TEN TIMES TO PREVAIL—PRAISE TO THE MOST HIGH GODS....”

Their voices swelled with emotion. It was moving. But I knew they’d forget it by morning. People burn bright for a moment, then fade.

When the ritual ended, Zaemon stepped down, the symbol held high. The crowd parted before it, making a path. And then—I realized what was happening.

At first, I thought he was leaving. But he kept walking—straight toward me.

Now I understood. After addressing the masses, he meant to deal with me. The man he had seen at Phorenice’s side. The enemy.

My final battle—gone. No glorious fight. No mound of corpses. Just a sentence.

But I remained calm. When he reached me, I gave the priestly salute. He returned it with solemn precision. The crowd recoiled, driven back by the symbol’s power. Even Nais, daughter of a priest, could not resist it. We stood alone in a ring of light, surrounded by hungry faces.

“Spy!” the people roared. “A spy! Zaemon salutes him as a priest!”

Zaemon turned. “Yes. He is a priest. His name is Deucalion.”

The name echoed through the crowd—shock, anger, hatred.

They had already hunted me. Now they had me. They would rip me apart.

But the symbol’s glow held them back. Like lava, it burned their approach.

Zaemon faced me. He spoke in the sacred tongue.

“Brother,” he said, “which do you serve? Deucalion—or Atlantis?”

Words fail such a question. “I have lived by the law,” I said. “Each ship from Yucatan carries reports. My record is known. I served Atlantis faithfully. But Phorenice changed things.”

“You have governed well. Lived simply. You loved Atlantis. But you had no Phorenice to tempt you.”

“If you send me away, I will not see her again.”

“Yes,” he said. “But then you lose your purpose. No, brother—you are our last hope. All else has failed. You return from abroad for the final duel. You are our strongest. Our champion. If you fall, only the powers locked in the Ark remain. And though I cannot speak of them—even here—you know their strength.”

I shrugged. “Then why not throw me to these wolves? Let them end me now.”

He frowned. “I command you to do your duty. Why evade it?”

“I remember your words in the pyramid. ‘PHORENICE,’ you cried, ‘WHILST YOU ARE YET EMPRESS, YOU SHALL SEE THIS ROYAL PYRAMID TORN TIER FROM TIER, STONE FROM STONE, SCATTERED AS FEATHERS BEFORE A WIND.’ You foresee my defeat.”

He shuddered. “I speak only what was revealed. Perhaps she will repent. But before we use the gods’ darkest tools, we must exhaust all human means. So you must go. Play your part to the end.”

“It is an order. I obey.”

“You will return to Phorenice by dawn. She has summoned you—from Yucatan—as her husband. She thinks you strong enough to sustain her rule. You are a priest. You are persuasive. Your task: make her see the power that can destroy her. Show her the futility of resistance.”

“If it is ordered, I will do it. But I have seen Phorenice. Her will is unbreakable. Others have failed. You have failed. I doubt I will succeed.”

“Words that fell on deaf ears once may pierce a wife’s heart. You are to be her husband. Accept your role.”

The order struck me hard. I had never cared much for women. But marrying Phorenice meant ending my connection with Nais—forever. The thought twisted my chest. But duty outweighed desire. I bowed. He placed his hand on my forehead.

“It is an order,” I said. “If our Lord the Sun grants me life, I will obey.”

“Then let us go,” said Zaemon. He took my arm and waved the symbol ahead of us. The crowd parted. No one attacked. No one followed.

Rain fell. Fires flickered. The old man’s bare body glistened in the dark. Ahead, the Sacred Mountain loomed—its peak crowned with eternal flames.

I sighed. I remembered the peace of its temples, the quiet groves. But peace was gone. The final test awaited.

The passage I entered was no ordinary tunnel—its entrance had been concealed by human hands, but the shaft itself was ancient, a long-forgotten artery of fire once pulsing with molten earth. Though the lava had long since cooled, the air still carried a faint warmth and the sharp tang of sulfur. It was no place to linger. I lit a lamp from a niche carved into the wall and pressed forward, coughing as the thick air irritated my throat. I wished for the simples I used to carry—herbs that soothed catarrh—but those were lost in the sea battle with Dason’s fleet. Since arriving in Atlantis, survival had taken precedence over comfort, and medicine had been forgotten.

The tunnels branched endlessly, like roots beneath the world. If any other of the Seven Priests had stumbled upon this labyrinth, they would have perished—either lost in its twists or falling into one of the deep pits that led down into the boiling depths below. But I knew the way. The route was etched in my mind from my initiation twenty years prior, when I was raised to the highest rank in our Clan short of the Supreme Priesthood. I had been given the secrets, the rites, and the maps of hidden paths—and now, they were my only guide.

The journey was long. The floor was uneven, treacherous underfoot. The air grew heavier with each step. I knew it would be late afternoon before I passed beneath the city walls and emerged within the sacred precincts. Yet the vow of the Seven bound me: no one could leave this passage until mortal eyes had seen what lay beyond.

I tested one branch after another, but each led to an exit where figures loitered. I had hoped to emerge near the royal pyramid, but the area was swarming—like a hive stirred into frenzy. The same was true of the next five exits. The city was alive with restless energy, trembling with anticipation.

Then I found it—a temple dedicated to a lesser god, abandoned and silent. Dust covered every surface, thick enough to bury footprints. I waited behind the statue, observing. No one came. The silence was absolute. I moved quickly, clearing my path with a broom left on one of the pews, then sealed the entry behind me.

The temple door was shut. Even though I was a priest, I did not know the mechanism to open it. Only the attendants possessed that knowledge. If they were all gone—drawn away by whatever spectacle gripped the city—I might be trapped here.

No sound came from within the temple grounds. Decay was everywhere—cracked pillars, fallen tiles, dust on the altars. Yet according to ancient law, there should always be at least one priest watching. I followed the passages to the living quarters, located in the roof above the main hall.

I expected emptiness. I was not surprised when the door opened slowly. A man stood in the dim light, his face gaunt, his beard poorly curled. His clothes hung loosely, unbrushed, unwashed. He was Ro.

“I remember you,” I said. “We studied together in the Sacred College.”

He bowed deeply. “My lord. I am your servant.”

“You’re still here?” I asked.

“The duty remains,” he muttered. “Even if the rest have fled.”

He was known as the dullard—the one who grasped little of theology, less of statecraft, and nothing of war. He had risen to second priest only because others had abandoned their posts. And yet, he stayed.

“The temple door is locked,” he said. “Only I can open it. You come by the secret way, as one of the Seven. I serve you.”

“Tell me,” I said, “what stirs the city?”

“Phorenice has declared herself a goddess,” he replied. “She says she will light the sacrifice with divine fire. She will do it. She does everything. But I pray the flames consume her when she calls them down. This woman is the ruin of our Clan. The people ignore us. Offerings are gone. And since the rebels began hammering at the gates, I’ve lived off my own stores. Oh, Deucalion, the old faith is nearly dead.”

I frowned. “And yet the priests have failed to hold the people?”

“We did what we were taught,” Ro said bitterly. “But if no one comes to hear, what power do we have? The gods test us, Deucalion. Come—look at my bed.”

There, wrapped in bloodstained bandages, lay the body of a man—once his superior, the priest who had performed the weekly sacrifice to the Sun God.

“He was mangled,” Ro whispered. “It happened yesterday. The wood was laid. The offering placed. The procession formed. The drums and horns sounded. Then—above the noise—came wings. A great bird, featherless, monstrous, larger than any seen before.”

“A spear through the eye,” I said, “or a long shaft—those are the best weapons.”

“Yes, we learned that as children,” Ro said. “But the priests are unarmed during sacrifice. They trust in divine protection. The bird swooped down, landed on the pyre, and attacked the offering. My superior leapt onto the altar—armed only with his teeth and bare hands. He called on the Sun God for strength. But the bird struck him with its wings, tore at him with its talons. It threw him aside like a fly. The sacrifice was devoured in plain sight. Then the bird flew away.”

“And no one helped?”

“They screamed it was a sign,” Ro said. “That the Sun God no longer protected His own. Some said there was no god left. Others claimed a new god had come—this creature, this bird. But then a few shouted, ‘Phorenice is the true goddess!’ And the cry spread. The stones of the great circle rang with it. Some believed. Many joined because it was safe. Others feared to oppose her.”

“She is Empress,” I said. “Her name commands respect.”

“I am a priest,” Ro replied. “I speak what I was taught. But I wonder—does faith remain? We’ve had no offerings here in five months. If not for my corn jars, I’d have starved. And this sacrifice? It won’t bring back the people.”

“It happened twenty hours ago,” I said. “And today, she conducts it herself?”

“Yes. That’s why the city is aflame. She’s preparing a pageant. You must see it.”

“I must,” I said. “Open the door.”

“I cannot advise a member of the Seven,” he said. “But… they say she is angry at your absence.”

“I do not fear her,” I said. “The future belongs to the High Gods. Open the door.”

He grumbled something about “force of habit” as he activated the levers. The massive stone slid aside.

“I might have needed you,” I said, stepping out. “But you owe me nothing.”

“Nor do I have anything,” he muttered. “One must live.”

I walked into the street. The crowd surged past the temple, all heading in one direction. With Ro’s words in mind, I knew where they went—the great stone circle of the Sun God. My heart sank. For centuries, that temple had stood untouched, a monument to reverence. Now, it would witness sacrilege.

Could the Sun God Himself be drawing Phorenice toward destruction? Would He strike down her blasphemy with fire from heaven?

But the crowd cared little. They were excited, hungry, desperate. From one side of the city, the sounds of battle echoed—rams battering walls, fire-darts streaking through the air. Yet no one noticed. The people had grown numb. Hunger and chaos had stripped them of civilization. They fought over scraps, robbed each other, trusted no one. Their minds had regressed to savagery.

Yet rising from this decay were palaces and pyramids of unmatched splendor—monuments to a greatness now crumbling beneath a tyrant’s rule.

The crowd swallowed me whole, pushing forward through streets once clean and proud, now choked with mud and bones. Men and women had died here, left to rot. We walked on their remains.

At last, we reached the central square—the only space Phorenice had not touched with her buildings. Here stood the oldest, grandest temple in the world: the circle of the Sun God.

Forty-nine stones, undressed, massive, arranged in perfect symmetry. Across the top, equally huge slabs formed a roof. In the center, the altar—carved with the snake and the outstretched hand, symbols of life and eternity.

The crowd stopped at the edge. To cross was death. I remained among them, unseen, waiting.

The sun blazed overhead. The siege raged beyond the city, but no one listened. All attention was fixed on the coming event.

I later learned that Phorenice had first gone to the walls, rallying defenders with her brilliance. But now, midday burned hot, and exhaustion took its toll. Many fainted. Some died. But no one cared. Life was cheap.

Then came the music—drums, horns, a procession glinting in the sun. Lords and courtiers paraded in gold and silk. The poor watched, starving, envious.

And then—she arrived.

Riding atop a shaggy mammoth, in a golden chariot, Phorenice swept into the square. Her presence transformed the crowd. Sullen faces brightened. A wave of joy rose—not forced, not commanded—but spontaneous. She smiled, her cheeks flushed, her eyes alight with triumph.

She stepped down, and those nearby fell to their knees. Only Ylga, her fan-girl, remained standing—pale, troubled. A daughter of Zaemon, the chief priest of the Sun God, she could not forget her roots. She looked torn between loyalty and horror.

Phorenice strode into the circle, bold, proud, claiming the role of high priestess. Behind her came men dressed as priests—some mere followers, others traitors born in the Sacred College. Shameful.

The wood was already stacked. The victim—a cloven-hoofed horse—was bound and placed upon the pyre. It neighed, struggling. Then it was left alive.

An innovation. A mockery.

The false priests stepped back. Phorenice stood alone before the altar.

She raised her voice—clear, sweet, carrying over the din. She spoke in the common tongue, so all could understand. She told of her divine birth, of the gods’ favor, of miracles wrought by her hand.

Then, lifting both arms to the sky, she cried:

“O my high Father, the Sun, I call upon You! Acknowledge me as Your daughter! Give this people a sign! I offer sacrifice unlit, as mortal priests have done for ages. Send down Your heavenly fire! Let it burn this beast! Prove that though You reign above, You have given me Atlantis, and the people of the Earth, to rule!”

She strained upward, her face contorted, her limbs trembling.

“Hear me, mighty Father! Hear me!”

A flash—blinding—spat from the sky. The horse shrieked once, convulsed, and fell dead.

Flames burst from the pyre. Smoke rose in a thin blue spiral.

Above, the dark cloud vanished.

Phorenice stood, radiant, triumphant.

The priests knelt. The crowd fell to their knees.

“Phorenice, Goddess!” they roared. “Phorenerce, Goddess of Atlantis!”

But I did not kneel.

I stood firm.

For I knew—this was not the fire of the gods.

This was the fire of madness.

And I would not bow.

A murmur rose around me, quickly swelling into shouts. “Kneel,” one voice hissed, “kneel, sir, or you’ll be seen!” Another roared, “Kneel, you without beard, and bow before the only Goddess—or by the old Gods, I’ll make myself her priest and cut you down!” The cry grew into a thunderous roar.

But then the name “Deucalion” began to echo through the crowd, and the fervor softened. Deucalion—man who had left Atlantis twenty years ago to rule Yucatan—they knew little of that man. But Deucalion—the one who had ridden beside the Empress just days before in the golden castle beneath the canopy of snakes—that was a name they remembered well. And when they considered what he might do if provoked, their voices fell silent, as though struck dumb by fear.

When the noise had died and Phorenice turned to see me standing alone among the bowed worshippers, I stepped forward from the throng, passed between two great stones, and walked across the circle to where she stood beside the altar. I did not kneel. At the distance prescribed, I gave the salute she herself had ordered when she made me her chief minister, then addressed her formally as Empress.

“Deucalion, man of ice,” she replied.

“I still serve the old Gods.”

“I wasn’t speaking of that,” she said, glancing at me with a sidelong smile.

Then Ylga approached, pale-faced, trembling, pleading for my life. “If he won’t abandon the old Gods yet,” she begged, “surely you can spare him? He is strong, and conversion does not come easily. You may change him later. But consider, Phorenice—he is Deucalion. If you kill him now over this, there is no other man within all Atlantis who could serve so well—”

The Empress cut her off. “You slut,” she snapped. “I keep you near me to choose my wardrobe, carry my fan—do you dare meddle in my affairs? Back out of this circle, or I’ll have you whipped. And worse—I’ll treat you as Zaemon treated my captain, Tarca. Shall I point a finger and curse your fair skin with sudden leprosy?”

Ylga bowed her head and retreated, humbled. Phorenice turned back to me. “My lord,” she said, “I am like a young bird that has suddenly found its wings. Wings have so many uses—I wish to try them all.”

“May each flight bring good to Atlantis.”

“Oh,” she said, eyes flashing, “I know exactly what you care about most. But let us return to the pyramid and speak at leisure. For now, my lord, guide me back to my riding beast.”

It seemed I would be spared punishment for refusing to worship her, thus publicly challenging her divine status. It also seemed Ylga’s interference had been ill-timed—and though I didn’t grasp why, I accepted it as part of the path laid out by Zaemon. So when the Empress offered me her hand—warm, delicate fingers, though skilled in war—I took it solemnly and led her from the great circle, which she had defiled with her pretense. I expected the Sun to strike down the sacrilege while it was still ongoing—but nothing came. I knew He would act in His own time, with His own instrument. No need for me to raise a weak arm to defend His honor.

So I guided this beautiful, sinful woman back to the massive red mammoth waiting tamely nearby, the scent of sacrifice lingering behind us. She climbed the steps to the golden castle perched on the beast’s shaggy back, and bid me mount beside her. But the spot behind her, meant for the fan-girl, remained empty. What we spoke as we rode through the city streets, no one else heard.

She was eager to know what had happened after the attack on the gate. I told her, emphasizing Nais’s courage and intelligence, suggesting with care that she might yet be won back to loyalty. But I kept silent about Zaemon’s commands—the sacred words spoken in the tongue of the priests—since such matters are not repeated except in the High Council before the Ark of Mysteries.

“You seem oddly kind to this rebel girl,” Phorenice remarked.

“She showed more wit and thought than most.”

“Ay,” she sighed, and I sensed the truth in it, “an Empress loses much that ordinary women take for granted.”

“In what way?”

“She misses the honest courtship of equals.”

“If you claim to be a Goddess—” I began.

“Pah! I don’t want to be a Goddess to you, Deucalion. That was for the people—to give me power, to help my plans. All seven of us higher priests know how to summon fire. I simply stole the trick. Can’t you be generous? A woman may be as clever as your ancient priests at discovering nature’s laws.”

“You remain Empress.”

“Nor even that. Just think—there’s a woman sitting beside you on this cushion, Deucalion. Look at her. What comes first to your lips? Forget ceremony. Forget statecraft. Do you mean to wait until your manhood fades? It’s wise not to rush—yes, I agree. But isn’t it foolish to watch a fruit ripen, then leave it until it spoils?”

I looked at her radiant beauty—but it stirred nothing within me. Yet I recalled Zaemon’s command, and forced a smile. “I may have been choosy,” I said, “but I don’t regret waiting so long.”

“Nor I. But I’ve lived long enough as a maid. I am a woman—ripe, full-blooded. The time has come for me to be more than I’ve been.”

I clasped her hand tightly. “Then take me as husband. I will be a good man to you. But since I speak now to Phorenice the woman, not the Empress, I must warn you—I will not be a puppet.”

She glanced at me sideways. “I’ve ruled long enough. I think I’d enjoy being ruled sometimes. No, Deucalion—I promise you won’t be a puppet. Indeed, it would take a mighty lung to pipe you into dancing against your will.”

“So we shall live together in the royal pyramid, ruling this land with all the wisdom the High Gods have given us. These petty conflicts shall end. Rebels shall return to their homes, hunt beasts in the provinces, and the Priests’ Clan shall be calmed. Phorenice, you and I will govern with every ounce of our minds, and make Atlantis rise again—greater than any nation, known for peace and prosperity.”

She pulled her hand away sharply. “Oh, your conditions, your Atlantis! You carry such crude colonial manners, Deucalion. They grow tiresome after the first blunt taste. Am I to do all the wooing? Is there no spark of love beneath your coldness?”

“In truth, I do not know what love is. I’ve had little talk with women in all these busy years.”

“We were alike when you arrived,” she said. “I’ve heard sighs and vows from every bearded man in Atlantis. Some pleased me for a day—but none touched me deeper. No, I haven’t learned love from my own heart either. But tell me, sir—will you teach me soon, if you keep up this chill?”

“From what I’ve seen, love belongs to the poor, the weak, those with fleeting emotions.”

“Then I wish another woman were Empress, and I were some ragged creature from the gutter—a strong man could snatch me up by force and carry me home for pure passion. Ah! How I’d revel in it! How I’d respond if he seized my fancy!” She laughed. “But I’d lead him a wretched life if my desire weren’t stronger than his.”

“We are shaped by fate. We cannot change what stirs us.”

She stared at me, sullen. “If I don’t awaken your heart, my Deucalion, there will be trouble brewing for this Atlantis you hold so dear. There will be chaos in our household if my love grows, and yours remains dead.”

I believe she would have made me caress her right there, atop the golden castle on the mammoth’s back, before the curious crowds. She craved spectacle, not privacy. But I couldn’t. The gods know I was committed to my task—and equally repelled by it. Yet I was a true priest that day. I set aside all personal feeling to fulfill the Council’s decree. If I had known how, I might have matched her mood. But where others at court would have slipped into flattery effortlessly, I faltered—lacking even a dozen suitable words.

There was no choice but to say only what I truly felt. Diplomacy was second nature; on most matters, I could speak smoothly. But charm with women was something I had never cultivated—and if I’d invented hollow compliments, Phorenice would have seen through them instantly. She had spent years feeding on such lies, and judged their worth with expert precision.

And honesty wouldn’t have helped. To lay bare my position plainly would have worsened things. So I stayed silent, accepting the role of fool.

“I always thought,” she said, “that your colonists in Yucatan were ahead of Egyptians in art and grace. But you, sir, do little credit to your rank. Why, I’ve had nobles from the Nile visit here—some of them almost seemed never to have left their homeland.”

“They must have advanced greatly in twenty years,” I replied. “Last time I reported from Egypt, the blacks clearly ruled, and our people lived only by sufferance. Their pyramids were puny, their cities mere forts.”

“Oh,” she said mockingly, “they’re still exiles—but they remember their manners. My face pleased them. They went mad over it. One even cut off his own right hand for ten pleasant words. We made a bargain, my Egyptian gallant and I. His hand dries on a shelf in my room now—a charming keepsake.”

But fortune intervened. Outside the walls, the rebels launched a fierce assault. Several times during our ride, light projectiles from their engines whistled overhead, striking buildings and sending splinters that wounded bystanders. Still, it was nothing to alarm the seasoned. Our courtship continued undisturbed. Then, suddenly, the rebels stopped hurling stones and began loading their engines with wooden casings filled with throwing fire.

Against stone, these caused little damage—except to anyone caught in the blast, who would be scorched horribly. But when they hit the wooden stalls and reed shelters of the poor, they ignited instantly. There was no stopping the flames.

Such events were common in sieges, and neither of us gave them much thought—until the mammoth reacted. When the first blue smoke drifted toward us through the winding streets, the great red beast raised its trunk and began swaying uneasily. As the smoke thickened and flickers of flame appeared, it halted abruptly and began to trumpet.

Its guards tugged hard on the chains attached to the jagged metal collar around its neck, the spikes gouging deep into flesh. But the beast’s primal fear of fire—deep-rooted from centuries of hunting and being hunted—overpowered its training. Men had long used fire to protect themselves from mammoths. Mammoths had learned to dread it as their deadliest enemy.

Phorenice’s brow darkened as the animal grew wilder. She shook her red curls violently. “Someone will lose their head for this blunder,” she snapped. “I ordered this beast trained to ignore drums, shouting, arrows, stones, and fire. The trainers swore it was done. They brought proof.”

I unfastened my belt. “Here,” I said, “quick—let me lower you to the ground.”

She turned, eyes gleaming. “Afraid for my neck, Deucalion?”

“I don’t intend to be widowed before I’ve tasted married life.”

“Pish! There’s little danger. I’ll stay. I’m no timid woman. But go if you wish.”

“There’s no chance now.”

Blood seeped from the mammoth’s neck where the collar’s spikes tore it—and with each drop, its obedience vanished. With wild shrieks and thunderous trumpetings, it turned and charged back the way it came, trampling spearmen like straw, cutting a swath through the crowd. Many must have been crushed, many killed by its trunk—but only their cries reached us. The golden castle swayed violently, threatening to throw us off like stones from a catapult.

I braced my feet against the front, wrapped one arm around a pillar, and clutched Phorenice with the other—using my body as a shield.

She lay calmly, her lovely face beneath my chin, the faint scent of her hair filling my breath. The mammoth charged wildly through narrow streets, heedless of direction. We had escaped the smoke, but the beast was lost in panic. It raced on with enormous strides, trunk raised, deafening us with screams. We left the glittering procession far behind, carried helplessly through the city’s wards.

No one could control it. Its speed left no option but to cling to the castle. Up there, we were safe. To leap off—even if we survived—would have doomed us. The mammoth would have scented us immediately, turned (as its kind do), and crushed us in seconds.

A thought struck me: perhaps this was the High God’s judgment. The mammoth was His instrument—meant to dash her to pieces. And I, His priest, was to witness justice fulfilled. But no direct sign came. So I held on, protecting her from bruise and fall.

The beast veered wildly, confused by the twisting streets. Gradually, we left the grand palaces and pyramids behind, entering the artisan quarters. Weavers and smiths gaped from doorways as we thundered past. Then we reached the merchants’ district, where men lived above their warehouses, trading with foreign lands. Ahead, an open space shimmered with water.

“Now,” I thought, “this mad beast will stop suddenly—perhaps turn and charge back.” I braced myself, gripping her tighter. But louder screaming, wilder trumpetings—the mammoth kept straight on, reaching the harbor’s edge. With clumsy steps, it plunged into the water, sending spray flying in the sunlight.

Then its pace slowed. The harbor received the city’s sewers—drainage from ancient times, carrying solid waste that settled into a thick, invisible sludge. The beast sank deeper and deeper, unable to halt. With terrified bellows, it came to a standstill—bogged irretrievably.

It struggled madly, screamed, cried out. Water and slime churned into a foul stew. The golden castle lurched violently—we were thrown from it and shot into the water.

Yet we were safe. I welcomed the end of the jolting.

Phorenice laughed as she swam. “You handle yourself like a true man, Deucalion. I owe you thanks for using your body as a cushion. By my face! There’s more gallantry in you under pressure than one would guess from your words. How was the ride, sir? I imagine it was new to you.”

“I’d prefer to walk.”

“Pish! You’ll never be a courtier. You should have sworn that, with me in your arms, you wished the bumping had lasted forever. Ho, boat there! Hold your arrows. Deucalion, hail those fools! Tell them—if they harm a hair of my mammoth, I’ll torture them all. He’ll tire soon. Then we’ll leave him where he is to reflect on his folly, then haul him out with windlasses and tame him again. Pho! I couldn’t be Phorenice without a fine, red, shaggy mammoth for my rides.”

The boat was a ten-slave galley, churning from the far side of the harbor. At my shout, the soldiers on deck ceased firing, and the steersman altered course to rescue us. We had been swimming carefully to avoid the sewer outflow—but now we stopped, treading water, and were lifted aboard by respectful hands.

The galley belonged to the port captain—a foppish sailor whose only ambition was to grovel before greatness. He immediately prostrated himself, wishing his eyes had been blinded before seeing the Empress in peril.

“The danger may pass,” she said. “Nothing mortal will ever kill me. But I’ve ruined my clothes and lost a jewel or two. That’s annoying, as you say, good man.”

He repeated his wish to be blinded.

But she grew weary of flattery. “If you’re tired of your eyes,” she said, “let me tell you—you’re headed for having them plucked from their sockets. Kill my mammoth because he acted playfully? You and your kind need education. I’ll teach you: port captains are easy to replace, worthless once obtained. But my mammoth—mine, do you understand?—is the property of Goddess Phorenice. Sacred.”

The captain bowed low. “I am ignorant,” he said, “and heaven lost its brightest star when Phorenice descended to Atlantis. But if I may make amends—I have two prisoners in the cabin. They shall be sacrificed to the mammoth. Doubtless it will find sport in them, spill its rage upon their bodies.”

“Prisoners?” she asked. “How taken?”

“Under cover of night, they tried to slip between the two forts guarding the harbor mouth. Their boat snagged the chain. The sentries saw them by torchlight. They were captured with ropes and thrown in a dungeon. There’s an order not to abuse prisoners before trial?”

“It was my order. Did they offer news for their lives?”

“The man hasn’t spoken. I think he died during capture. The woman fought like a cat, they said. But she was caught unharmed. She claims she has nothing useful to tell. She says she’s tired of living like a savage outside the city—and that inside, there’s a man she craves deeply.”

“Tut!” Phorenice said. “Is this a romance we’ve swum to? See how affectionate we women are, Deucalion.”—The galley docked at the royal quay and fastened to golden rings. I helped the Empress ashore, but she turned back to the boat, her clothes still dripping.—“Bring out your woman prisoner, captain. Let us see if she’s a runaway wife or a lovesick girl. Then I’ll judge her—and likely surprise you all with mercy. I’m in a romantic mood today.”

The captain entered the small cabin, face pale. “The man appears dead, Your Majesty. His wounds—”

“Bring out the woman, fool. I asked for her. Keep the corpse where it is.”

I saw him reach for his knife to untie a rope—and then, stepping into daylight, was the girl I had saved from cave-tigers in the circus—the one who had drawn me strangely during our brief companionship. Clearly, Phorenice recognized her too. She made no secret of it, addressing her by name and mocking her about the rebels’ plans and her lover’s success.

So the mighty Empress was jealous of a mere prisoner—woman though she was.

In an instant, my mind, trained to act with soldierly precision under pressure, had decided what I must do. I was sacrificing myself for Atlantis, as ordered by the High Council of Priests. If necessary, Nais would be sacrificed too—but even as that thought crossed my mind, another plan formed: one that might save her.

I bowed low before Phorenice and said, “In this, and in all matters where human hands can act, I will fulfill your wishes, Phorenice.” She patted my arm, and a surge of emotion rose from the depths of her eyes—eyes so full of fire and mystery. Truly, the gods had granted her half her victories when they gave her beauty, voice, and the shape of her face.

By then, merchants and others near the royal quay had gathered, offering dry clothes and shelter. Though I cared little for my wet coat, the area we’d been tossed into by the mammoth was anything but pleasant. I followed her lead without word or glance toward Nais. But Phorenice wasn’t satisfied with such a small gesture. Before leaving, she gave strict orders to the port-captain to guard his prisoner well.

At the house where I was taken, I bathed and exchanged my damp garments for the plainest robe available. The host was delighted at the bargain. Still, I had an hour to wait outside on the pavement, listening to distant bombardments, before Phorenice returned. I couldn’t help smirking at the merchant who trailed behind her—clearly ruined. He’d owned a collection of priceless jewels, bought in partnership with others. Phorenice had taken them all, adorning herself with them.

“Are they not lovely?” she asked, showing me her new treasures. “Look how they sparkle in the sun. I’m glad now, Deucalion, that the mammoth threw us about. It brought me such a fine gift. Tell him I’ll return when he’s earned more—and perhaps I’ll dine with him again. Ah! Litters have arrived. Send one away, and share mine with me. We’ll pretend to be lovers today, even if true love comes easier tomorrow. No, don’t summon more bearers—my slaves will carry us well enough. I’m glad you’re not one of those overfed, clumsy men, Deucalion. I’m slight and quick, and I don’t want to be overshadowed.”

“Back to the royal pyramid?” I asked.

“No,” she replied. “Not there, nor to the walls. I don’t wish to fight or rule today. As I’ve told you, I want to be Phorenice, the maiden, for a few hours. If someone I know were to court me now—like any other girl—I’d call it a pleasure. Have the slaves carry us along the edge of the harbor. And tell these gawkers—if they follow, I’ll rain fire upon them, just as I did with the sacrifice.”

I knew something of the chaos in the streets. That night at the royal banquet, the gate officer had hinted at unrest. But since Phorenice already knew, I didn’t remind her. I dismissed the guards and shooed away the crowd of sightseers, those foolish creatures always drawn to watch their superiors. Then I sat in the litter where invited, and the bearers lifted the pole.

They carried us along the wide walkway between merchant houses, fisher huts, and the rippling waters of the harbor. I glanced sadly at the few ships still moored in the grand basin, and the sparse signs of trade on the docks. Once, ships crowded so tightly that many waited in the estuary beyond the walls. Petitions had gone to the King asking to double the port’s size. Even in those days of oar and sail, solar-powered machines were rare—only used occasionally to speed vessels.

An Egypt voyage took a year then—now, just two months. Of every three ships that set out, at least one never returned, lost to sea beasts or storms. Yet trade once thrived, growing ever stronger. Merchants were stout and generous to the gods. Now, war consumed everything. Wealthy traders abandoned goods, calling bargaining indecent, adopting courtly airs, spending days choosing fabrics and nights feasting.

The lesser merchants, without savings, were crushed by taxes and war. With markets scarce and no exports, industry collapsed. A weaver drafted into fire-tube duty made no cloth; a farmer rebelling didn’t buy slaves. Just a month before my return, a magnificent cargo of slaves had arrived from Europe—only to be released across the estuary, left to fend for themselves. No Atlantean wanted another slave, even as a gift.

Though I mourned this decline, I kept silent. Speaking of it now would only sour our ride. I was learning Phorenice’s moods. And she, in turn, seemed to read mine, trying to hold my interest between her playful fits.

We reached the westernmost harbor wall, where the bearers set us down. She led me to a stone house perched on a promontory, constantly lashed by tide. After much unlocking, she showed me her gold collectors.

At the Sacred Mountain colleges, we’d learned that gold existed dissolved in seawater—but no method had ever been devised to extract it. Yet here, this self-made Empress had stumbled upon the secret as if it were written in a book.

She laughed, patting my arm as I stared. “I tell everyone in Atlantis that only the gods know this secret—and that they gave it to me,” she said. “But I’m no goddess to you, am I, Deucalion? And honestly, I can’t explain how I came up with it. Let’s say I dreamed it. See? Seawater flows in through that culvert, passes over rough metal plates, and exits naturally. The gold collects in the ridges. My men melt it into bars and take it to my smiths. Tides vary with the moon—you priests know that. Simple math tells how much water passes and how much gold is captured. At first, my workers tried to cheat. I dragged two behind a galley until cannibal fish devoured them. Since then, they’ve believed in… well, what do you suppose?”

“More divinity.”

“I suppose so. But I’m letting you see how it works. Do the math. You could become a god among these Atlanteans, Deucalion—just by using your wit.”

Was she mocking me? Or serious? Sometimes she claimed her triumphs came from intelligence alone; the next moment, she offered impossible explanations, forcing me to believe she was divine. This woman—clever, agile, beautiful—mystified me more each day. And the more I stayed near her, the less hope I felt that Atlantis could ever be restored to peace and the old gods—even after I married her and fulfilled the Council’s decree.

Only one path seemed possible: end her mischief with death and let the land recover on its own. It would be a single stroke—a dagger, and done. Yet the very thought revolted me. When it surfaced, I clung to the idea that if such an act were truly meant, the High Gods would have given me clear command.

Still, Phorenice held a strange power. When we left the gold house and stumbled into danger—when a single hesitation would have doomed her—I found myself fighting beside her, as fiercely as I ever had. Yes, battle lust stirs even the coldest hearts when blows fly and cries ring out. But protecting her, refusing to see her torn apart by these brutish fishers—those acts added strength to every strike.

The cause was the unrest I’d mentioned—the desperate poverty driving people to risk everything for a meal. We remounted the litter, bearers swinging forward. Phorenice spoke of her gold supply, of new laws.

“In the old days,” she said, “gold was painstakingly dredged from riverbeds in dangerous lands. A featherful cost a rich man’s fortune. Only nobles wore gold. But when the sea gives me gold by handfuls daily, the price dropped. One day—can you believe it?—a common smith came to me wearing a gold collar. I had his neck severed. I promised the same fate to any who dared repeat it. For now, the madness seems halted. Governing common folk has its trials, Deucalion.”

She went on, but then I saw two crude fishing boats paddling toward us. From narrow lanes between huts, fierce-faced men began running after the litter in threatening groups.

“With your leave,” I said, “I’ll step out and disperse them.”

“Oh, they always gather around me,” she said lightly. “Poor ugly souls—they seem to enjoy staring at my beauty. But scatter them. I said I didn’t want to be followed. I’m on holiday, Deucalion, while you learn to court me.”

I stepped down. The fishers in boats shouted warnings to those hiding in the huts. Onshore, numbers grew fast. We’d passed the merchants’ quarter, now level with mud-and-grass huts—the fishermen’s domain, always prone to violence. Even in the old King’s peaceful reign, riots broke out weekly here.

Fishing is the hardest life imaginable. Storms, firestones from the sky, sea beasts—these are daily threats. Each fisherman trains a large seabird from youth. A bronze ring prevents it from swallowing caught fish. For each catch, the bird gets head, tail, and innards—while the ring is removed during its meal.

These birds are loyal once trained, rarely deserting owners. Yet they’re hunted like prey, and often die suddenly. If both a fisher’s birds die, he loses his livelihood. His guild sees this as divine judgment—Gods rejecting him. So they abandon him. He’s left to starve or steal.

Such hardships make fishers ruthless, reckless men—driven into the trade because all else rejected them. They spend months’ earnings in a single night’s binge, fearing tomorrow may take their lives. And honor demands they defy fear, no matter the cost.

I moved quickly toward the largest group lurking behind huts, leaving the litter behind. “Disperse!” I commanded. “Clean your lanes, repair your homes. Tomorrow I’ll inspect. But today, the Empress desires privacy. Cease your stares!”

“Then give us money!” shrieked a voice from the huts.

“I’ll send a torch and gallows in an hour,” I growled. “To your dens, you dogs!”

They might have obeyed—had they not heard the boatmen shouting. The two hide-covered boats paddled into the surf, men leaping ashore, abandoning their fragile craft to the waves.

From huts and filthy alleys, crowds poured out—men and women alike—surrounding us menacingly.

“Beware,” warned one on the edge. “She called down fire on the sacrifice today. She can burn us all.”

“So much the better,” retorted another. “She can’t burn everyone.”

“Nay, I won’t burn anyone,” Phorenice declared. “But you’ll see my sword-play until you’re tired.”

I heard her with a flicker of amusement—knowing, as one of the Seven, that she had summoned skyfire to destroy a sacrificial beast. I also knew she couldn’t summon fire now. But they gave us no time for words.

Her throne had never been guarded far. They feared an escort might arrive. So they struck fast.

They surged forward, jostling to reach her. Their stench made us gag. A grimy hand lunged for her jewels. I sliced it off at the elbow. It fell at her feet. Moments later, we were locked in combat.

“Back to mine, comrade!” she laughed, drawing her sword and spinning with deadly grace. Bah—it was slaughter from the start.

They rushed in so greedily that none could retreat. We carved a circle with whirling steel. In such fights, wounded left unattended become deadly traps. Yet every foe Phorenice struck lay still. By the gods—she fought like a queen. Every thrust touched life.

Now it was clear: her throne wasn’t won by beauty alone. And as a warrior, I respected her more for it. I glimpsed her only in flashes—her movements swift, precise. The fishers, though clumsy and poorly armed, had numbers and savage fury. Their confidence made it hard to hold ground. Had I not snatched a shield from a fool who charged too boldly, I might have been pulled down by sheer force.

Above the din, her cry rang out: “Deucalion!”—her battle shout. I knew her flair for flattery. Hearing my name roared above chaos was nothing new. But this time, under such circumstances, I couldn’t help but feel flattered.

Condemn me if you must, but I nearly liked her—on her own terms. And Nais? Her memory faded entirely in the heat of battle. Such is our frailty—even the strongest among us.

Few born to the sword resist the urge to fight for the sake of fighting. And it seems women can catch the same fever. The attack slowed. We stood in a ring of fallen foes, the rest backing away.

This was the moment for mercy. A few sharp words could have sent them fleeing. But no—Phorenice must sing a hymn to our swords, gloating over our skill. Then she demanded payment for the slain bearers. She threatened fire, skinning, and dismemberment for the entire quarter—until they learned manners.

“It makes your skin crawl, doesn’t it?” she asked. “I understand. Severity has its cost. But why don’t you stop me? Either you think yourselves gods—or you believe me no goddess. Otherwise, you wouldn’t have come this far. Come now, you foul-smelling rabble—follow your theory. If you fight well, I swear by my face, I’ll spare those who survive.”

The sun, our Lord, was sinking toward the horizon, casting long shadows across the sea, while smoke from a distant burning mountain darkened the sky like a curse. Phorenice wrung out her soaked clothes and shivered in the evening chill. “Row hard, Deucalion,” she said, her voice sharp with impatience. “Take me through the water-gate and back to my comforts. That merchant would weep if he saw his fine garments ruined—and I, too, mourn, being a woman, that he has no others I might claim.”

She turned her gaze on me, tossing back her fiery red hair. “What do you think of my wisdom—coming here alone, without guards?”

“The Empress can do no wrong,” I replied, quoting the old saying with a faint smile.

“At least I’ve shown you I can fight,” she retorted. “I saw you watching me—not with disapproval, but with approval. You were hard to warm up to, Deucalion, but now you’re thawing. La, sir—we may yet become a pair of country lovers. I’m glad I chose to go near those smelly fishers.”

So she had brought me out unattended not for safety, but to provoke a fight—to test her strength, and perhaps mine. If we were to live together as husband and wife, it was wise to know each other’s limits. And though I’d spent a lifetime in battle, I could not deny the thrill of facing a worthy opponent. Still, I couldn’t help comparing her to another woman. When faced with the same choice, Nais had spared the rebels out of pity—she didn’t even call herself their leader. But here, this Empress wasted two dozen loyal men just to satisfy a whim.

Yet, bound by my vows as a priest and sworn to serve the High Council on the Sacred Mountain, I pushed such thoughts aside. As I rowed through the swaying waves toward the harbor forts, I prayed to the High Gods for eloquence, and they answered—for Atlantis’ sake, and for the people who suffered under tyranny. My words flowed freely, and Phorenice watched me with glowing eyes.

The fisher’s beach where we’d fought was safe from attack thanks to a jagged chain of rocks rising from the deep, stretching from the harbor wall to the city’s edge. The fishermen knew the hidden paths, able to slip through without touching stone—or leap ashore and carry their boats over the reef when danger loomed. But I knew neither the route nor the skill. I thought: *Now, High Gods, show whether you will allow this woman, who has offended you, to rule Atlantis—and whether I am meant to stand beside her.*

I spoke these words in silence, waiting for an omen. None came—until a great wave rose in the lagoon behind us, stirred by an unseen earth tremor. It lifted us high and carried us forward, sweeping us past the reef without so much as a scrape of stone. I bowed my head as I rowed on. Clearly, the gods had not yet decided to punish her—or me.

At the harbor mouth, sentries beat drums in turn, their routine interrupted only by a pair of officers on the rooftop turret of the nearer fort. They recognized our voices and silenced the drums. Torches were lowered to inspect our faces, and then—with a splash—the great chain dropped into the water, granting us passage.

A galley waited inside, moored against the harbor wall. A rope ladder descended from the fort, and a crew hurried down to take the oars. We were given dry clothes—gifts from the officers—as we changed in the galley’s cabin. Wet garments after sunset invite fever, and though I knew remedies, I noted with grim amusement that Phorenice, despite her divine pretensions, feared mortal sickness as any other woman might.

The galley cut swiftly across the calm harbor, docking at the golden rings of the royal quay. While we waited for litters, I noticed a lantern lit in the boat guarding Phorenice’s mammoth. The enormous beast stood knee-deep in the water, trunk raised. It was tamed now, its body trembling slightly beneath the glow of the light. Yet I refused to plead for it. If it sank into the mud and vanished, I would accept it calmly.

Truth be told, riding that beast had unsettled me. It made me feel powerless, helpless—something I rarely allowed myself to experience. Perhaps I had grown too self-reliant; others might judge. But I confess: I prefer walking on my own feet, as the gods intended. On foot, I can protect myself. On that monstrous animal, I felt like a child, afraid of every step.

But there was little time for reflection. As we waited, Phorenice asked the port captain—eagerly approaching to pay his respects—about Nais. He reported that she had been thrown into a dungeon beneath the royal pyramid, and the guard commander had sworn to keep her secure.

“I hope he knows his duty,” said the Empress. “That girl knows the pyramid better than most. If he locks her in a cell with a secret escape, he’ll end up before the tormentors. Would you be pleased if she escaped, Deucalion?”

“Certainly,” I said. There was no point hiding it. “I have no hatred for Nais.”

“But I do,” she snapped. “And I still intend to bind your loyalty to me with that wedding gift you know of.”

“It shall be done,” I said. “Before all, before the Empress of Atlantis.”

“Poof! Deucalion, you’re far too stiff and formal. Shouldn’t you be flattered that I’m jealous of your affections? Give me your hand, sir, to help me into the litter. Now come beside me, and keep me warm. Ho! Guards with torches—keep back against the walls. Your perfume is stifling me.”

That night, another feast filled the royal banqueting hall. Again, I sat beside Phorenice on the raised dais beneath the symbols of the snake and the outstretched hand. What had once been assumed—our union—was now declared openly. She named me her future husband, and the court hailed me as greater than themselves, chosen by her grace. Their salute was deliberate. Some rumor must have spread of my preference for older customs, for no one drank wine in my honor after the new, flashy fashion. Instead, each nobleman and noblewoman approached, spilling a goblet at my feet. When I offered food from my platter, they ate simply, without kissing or pocketing it.

The jets of earth-breath flickered above, revealing simpler clothing, soberer faces. Phorenice leaned close, her curls brushing my cheek, whispering mockingly, “Oh, sir, don’t make your rule too ascetic. I gave no orders for this change—but tonight there are no perfumes, the food is plain, and I half want to burn the cook. And look at the men’s beards—half the curl is gone. By my face! If you keep setting trends, they’ll shave clean tomorrow, just like yours.”

“Why stop them if they prefer it?”

“No, sir. Only one clean chin shall exist in all Atlantis—and it shall belong to the man who weds the Empress. Would you have no sumptuary laws? Would you let common folk imitate us in every fold of cloth, every twist of hair? Come now—if we claimed power only by strength and wit, they’d hate us as arrogant. But if we keep certain signs of rank, they admire and envy us, not resent.”

“You show me there’s more to rule than meets the eye.”

“And yet they say you ruled well.”

“I used the older ways. It takes a Phorenice to invent finer things.”

“Flatterer!” she teased, tapping my arm with her fingers. “You’re becoming a courtier. You make me blush with your smooth talk. No fan-girl here to cool my cheek—I’ll choose another. Not Ylga. She seems to favor you too much. If she’s wise, she’ll retire to her palace on the other side of the city, manage her slaves, embroider silks. I won’t be harsh unless she forces me—but I’ll not have any woman treat you with undue kindness.”

“And how should I act,” I asked, playing along, “when all the men of Atlantis swear their devotion to you? You admit they love you fiercely.”

“Oh, don’t ask me for miracles,” she said. “I am powerful—but I cannot command hearts. But if you wish, proclaim that I am now yours, body and soul. If they offend your pride, take them to the tormentors. Indeed, Deucalion, I’d find it pleasing if you did so. It seems the jealousy is all on one side.”

“I suppose you expect a man who’s lived without love to master its subtleties overnight. I’m proud of my progress. With any other teacher than you, Phorenice, I wouldn’t be nearly this far. In fact—by my record—I might never have begun.”

“Do you think I’d be satisfied with that? I’m not. I can be greedy in certain matters.”

The banquet ended early. Phorenice had endured much since last she slept. Though she claimed divinity, her body remained mortal. Dark circles framed her radiant eyes. She slumped among the cushions, limbs heavy. When dancers approached, she tossed them a jewel and dismissed them mid-performance. The poet, a pompous fool singing of the day’s deeds, she refused to hear.

“Tomorrow,” she said wearily. “For now, grant me peace. Deucalion has given me much to think about. I go to my chamber to plan the future of this realm. Tomorrow, come again. If your poetry is good and brief, I’ll reward you handsomely. But if you’re long-winded, I’ll pay you with the stick.”

She rose, and I led her from the hall through secret passages to her private chambers. She clung to my arm. Once, as a massive stone door swung open, she rested her head on my shoulder. Her breath warmed my cheek, and her beauty—so near—defied description. I sensed she expected me to kiss her lips, so close they brushed mine. I wanted to play my part, but I could not bring myself to it. When the door closed, she sighed and walked on in silence.

“May the High Gods treat you kindly,” I said at her chamber door.

“I am my own God,” she replied, “in all things but one. By my face! You’re a slow wooer, Deucalion. Where are you going now?”

“To my own chamber.”

“Oh, go then.”

“Is there anything else I could do?”

“Nothing your mind or will would suggest. Yes, indeed—you’re decorous in your old-fashioned way. But you’re a poor wooer. Don’t you know a woman values some things more when they’re taken by force?”

“It seems I know little of women.”

“You never said truer. Bah! And I believe your coldness helps you more than passion ever could. Go, then. If the air of a maiden’s bedchamber offends your modesty, leave. May your gods watch over you—if they can. Go, man, and leave me alone.”

Before the feast, I had studied the pyramid’s layout from the archives. Now, I navigated its maze with ease. I was weary, but sleep was impossible. Nais must be sacrificed—partially—for Atlantis. But I had a plan to save her life, and time was short.

Unexpected help arrived. As I passed through the narrow corridors between the pyramid’s stones, a figure emerged from shadow. When I raised my lamp, I saw Ylga.

She looked at me with bitterness. “I’ve lost my place,” she said. “She meant to have you all along. And you—you think me meddlesome. You never gave me a second thought.”

“I never forget kindness.”

“Oh, you’ll learn that trick soon. And you’re marrying her? The city speaks of it. I thought you honest. But when power comes with a woman, you’re no different. I thought you distrusted red hair. And she’s small.”

“Ylga,” I said, “you taught me these walls are full of ears. I’ll hear no word against Phorenice. But I need to speak with you soon. If you still care for me, go to my chamber and wait. I’ll join you.”

She lowered her eyes. “What do you want?”

“I need to tell you something. You’ll learn who it concerns later.”

“But—is it proper for a maiden to visit a man’s room at this hour?”

“I know little of your customs. I am Deucalion. If you still hesitate, remember that, and stay away.”

She looked up, sneering. “I was foolish. Your coldness is legend. Very well—I’ll come.”

“Go then,” I said, watching her vanish. With Ylga’s aid, my burden lessened. First, I needed to reach the temple of our Lady the Moon—a sacred chamber within the temple itself. As one of the Seven, I had access.

All temples in Atlantis connect secretly to the royal pyramid, but these paths are known only to the Seven and the Three above them—those few who have mastered the highest mysteries. Even they use them only in dire need. Generations may pass without a single foot stepping on them.

I struggled through darkness, testing my way until I found the hidden alley. Once inside, the path was clear. I had never walked it before, but the map from my initiation returned to memory. I moved quickly—both because danger lurked in those depths and because time slipped away.

I reached the lookout post and found the temple empty, dusty, untouched for years. A massive stone swung outward, revealing the entrance. After the required prayer, I retrieved the metal stair and climbed into the lap of the Goddess. Then, pulling the stair behind me, I ascended further until my body lay against her serene, mysterious face.

A chill ran through me. Even hardened warriors hesitate when tampering with life and death—especially when the fate of a nation and a woman dear to one’s heart hang in balance. But time pressed. I grasped the jewel set in her forehead and, with my other hand, prodded the corner of her eyebrow. With a soft creak, the massive stone eye swung inward. I stepped off the stair and entered the hollow space within her head.

It was the first time I’d seen this secret chamber, yet its layout matched what I’d learned. I knew exactly where to find what I sought. Still, I marveled at the ingenuity of its concealment.

Though I ranked high in the Priestly Clan, I did not know the true nature of what I now held. Before, I’d only known its power. Now, in my hands, were three rough spheres—like nut kernels, grass-green, hard as beeswax. I took the one I needed, leaving the others undisturbed. It might have been a drug—or something more. I didn’t know. But its effects were clear from my training. I placed it in a pouch and retraced my steps, restoring everything as I found it.

Before leaving, I cast one final glance at the statue. The eternal jet of earth-breath illuminated her from crown to toe, glittering from the jewel in her brow. Vast and peaceful, she embodied the quiet rest many seek in vain—peace attainable only through piety, in the afterlife where our Lady the Moon and the High Ones reign forever.

Exhausted, I returned to the pyramid and entered my chamber. Ylga waited, hidden at first, suspicious of modern times. She crouched until she confirmed I came alone.

“Oh, frown if you wish,” she muttered. “I care nothing for your opinion now. I heard tales of Deucalion—thought I saw honesty. But now I see you’re no better. She offers you power, and you marry her gladly. Why not? She’s pretty. I’ve seen her warm and languishing for dozens. Clever with her eyes, too. And you—it thrills you to be courted.”

“I think you’re a very foolish woman,” I said.

“If you believe it doesn’t matter whom you marry, you’re letting pride blind you.”

“Listen,” I said. “I didn’t summon you to argue. I called you to help save someone of your own blood.”

She stared. “I don’t understand.”

“It rests with you whether Nais dies tomorrow—or falls into a sleep from which she may wake one day, in happier times.”

There was no delay in the city of Atlantis when Phorenice willed it. Her belief was absolute: the land and all within it existed solely for her sake, and if she desired something, no price was too high to pay.

She had issued her decree—the burial alive of Nais—and though the command named me as builder of the stone throne, it was understood that others would do the labor. Heralds carried the proclamation through every district, and masons, laborers, sculptors, engineers, and architects abandoned their tasks and rushed to the appointed site. The chief architect was chosen on the spot, his orders relayed by subordinates, and the work began at once. Any material within the city walls marked by their seal was seized without compensation. The stones they selected were colossal—so massive that entire buildings had to be torn down to clear a path for them.

I have mentioned before the modern obsession with constructing new palaces and pyramids. Even now, while rebel armies battered the city’s defenses with siege engines, the building guilds labored tirelessly. Their skill, enhanced by Phorenice’s ingenious inventions, grew ever sharper. They could not move the titanic blocks once placed by the ancient gods for the sacred circle of our Lord the Sun—but they had rams, cranes, and wheeled platforms capable of lifting astonishing weights.

The throne stood in the open square before the royal pyramid. Seven tiers of stone formed its base, each tier knee-high and fronted with three steps. At the top, a hollow cavity awaited the body of Nais. Above it, suspended by lifting rams, hovered the immense block that would serve as the seat of the throne.

Through the night, under torchlight, relays of stonemasons, laborers, and engineers worked without rest—cutting, shaping, hauling, and placing each stone with precision. Machines were built to lift the slabs, and architects ensured every joint was perfect. Whips cracked; men collapsed from exhaustion, only to be replaced instantly. No delay was tolerated when the Empress had spoken.

By dawn, the square was filling with spectators drawn by the spectacle of the day. The chippings and scaffolding were cleared away—along with the bodies of about half a dozen workers who had died during construction. The throne stood complete, carved with intricate designs, ready for its final act. Its lower portion rose more than two man-heights above the ground, each stone weighing at least twenty men. The upper slab, twice as heavy, bore the image of the royal serpent coiled around the chair, its great hooded head looming overhead. For now, it remained suspended—held aloft by hydraulic rams, its purpose known to all.

It was to this scene that I stepped out of the royal pyramid at the summons of the chamberlains, just as the morning air turned cool. Each noble arrived with banner-bearers and trumpeters announcing their presence. The middle class wore their finest garments; even the poorest, gaunt and sunken-cheeked, adorned themselves with colored cloths and turbans to mark the occasion.

Trumpets blared as I approached. The crowd roared in welcome. With the chamberlains walking backward ahead of me, I crossed to a scarlet awning and took my seat upon cushions beneath it.

Then came Phorenice—my bride-to-be—fresh from sleep, radiant in beauty. She emerged from the pyramid in an open litter of gold and ivory, carried by fierce savages from Europe. The contrast between her refined elegance and their brutish appearance heightened her grace. A collective breath seemed to catch in the crowd as they beheld her. It was easy to believe that no man among them failed to envy me her choice. And none could imagine—except perhaps Ylga, hidden somewhere in shadow—that I felt anything but pride in winning such a wife.

For myself, I summoned every ounce of discipline I had learned. This was ceremony—grim, yes, but still ritual. I intended to show Phorenice a composed dignity, nothing to reproach. After everything we had endured, I refused to let grief or pain betray me in a trembling hand or twitching jaw. When the moment came, I told myself, I would place Nais’s living body into the stone cavity as calmly as a priest might lay a corpse to rest. And she, I knew, would not reveal our secret. For both of us, it was “Before all Atlantis.”

To my relief, there was no mockery, no jest in what followed. The proceedings were solemn, grand, and deeply moving. Though the vulgar crowd missed the deeper meaning, the outward majesty of the double sacrifice would linger in their memories long after. When it suited her whim, Phorenice could be fiercely strict about ritual—and today, she had ordered every detail observed with the precision of ancient times.

She was borne forward by her European bearers to the awning, and I helped her to the ground. She sat beside me, entwining her fingers in mine, as custom demanded between rulers and consorts of Atlantis.

Then, soldiers marched forward, forming a line and revealing Nais in their midst. A metal collar encircled her neck, chains hanging from it, held taut by two guards so she could not dash herself against the spears—thus avoiding a swift death, which is often the fate of those condemned to slow suffering.

Yet she still wore her clothes. Clothing has value, and tradition dictates that the garments of the condemned go to the guards. So her attire remained intact. I realized then that someone must have paid the guards—a gesture I suspected came from Ylga. A quiet gratitude stirred within me.

The soldiers brought her to the edge of the awning’s shade. She was commanded to kneel before the Empress—and wisely obeyed, avoiding violence. Her face was pale, but calm. No fear, no defiance. Her eyes were steady. She remembered her duty to Atlantis, and I watched her with love and pride.

I remained impassive as stone. I knew Phorenice watched me closely, yet I showed nothing—not a flicker of emotion. I had trained myself well.

“Nais,” the Empress said, “you have eaten from my table, drunk from my cup. What was yours, I gave you. You should have known gratitude—and the reach of the Empress’s power. Yet you did not. You attempted to take what belongs to me alone. Pardon was offered, with terms. You refused. You were foolish. But today, I am merciful. Soon, seated upon that granite throne he has built for me, I shall take Deucalion as my husband. Speak plainly: repent, and name a man you would wed, and I will spare you. You may be married before we rise from these cushions.”

“I will not wed,” she said quietly.

“Think again, Nais. Your other choice is this: you will be taken—alive, warm, beautiful as you stand now—and laid in the hollow beneath the throne-stone. Deucalion, my future husband, will place you there, as a symbol of how all enemies of Phorenice shall perish. Then he will release the rams, and the upper stone will descend. You will vanish from sight. Look at the sky. Breathe the air. Think of what that end means. Believe me, girl—I do not wish to make an example unless you force me.”

“I will not wed,” she repeated.

Phorenice released my arm and leaned back. “If she thinks I jest—or clings to old familiarity—show her, Deucalion, that I do not.”

“The Empress does not jest,” I said. “I will do this because it is her wish, and because it must be made a lasting symbol. Lead your prisoner to the place.”

The soldiers turned. The guards gripped the chains around Nais’s neck, prepared to drag her up the steps. But she walked willingly, her color unbroken. I rose, bowed to the Empress, and followed.

Before ten thousand eyes, neither of us displayed emotion—not for Atlantis, nor for the pride we both carried. We were not like Phorenice, who flaunted affection openly.

Yet when I signaled the guards to unfasten the collar, and drew Nais into my arms—pretending the roughness of one seizing a captive—a surge of emotion threatened to overwhelm me. I had never embraced a woman with tenderness before. Now I held the one I loved, and the intensity of that union nearly broke me. Only sheer will kept me standing.

I dragged her across the platform, forcing her down into the hollow beneath the throne. She resisted not at all.

To prevent her from struggling free and escaping the crushing weight, brazen clamps were fitted into grooves above the cavity. One by one, I secured them, holding her down with one hand while working with the other. Sweat poured from me, falling onto the stone. I could not stop it.

When the last bar was locked, I released my grip.

Pale furrows marked her throat where my fingers had pressed. I could not help glancing at her face. But she looked neither at me nor at the stone. Her gaze remained fixed upward, unyielding. I left her, knowing it was best not to linger.

I stepped from beneath the stone and signaled the engineers. Fires were removed from the rams. Metal contracted. Slowly, the vast throne-stone began its descent.

Oh, how slowly! My soul tore apart as I watched.

Yet I kept my face still, like an officer overseeing a task under his command. I waited until the stone settled fully, then joined the master architect, who tested it with plumbline and level. He declared it perfect.

It was a formality—calculated results already confirmed—but the guilds insisted on such rituals, especially in ceremonial moments. They wished to appear mysterious, powerful, beyond the common folk. Perhaps I was harsh in judging them. Yet who could blame a man forced to measure the tomb of his beloved?

I had entered the steps a grieving man, outwardly cold. As I descended, I felt a hollow inside me—as if half my being had been left behind with Nais. A void ached within me. Still, I kept my expression blank. Before all else, the High Council’s command loomed: the Ark of the Mysteries awaited.

I returned to Phorenice and spoke the prescribed words.

“Now,” she said, “I give myself to you as wife. You and I are not like others. There is no law or form for us. No priest, no scribe. I am the law here. Soon, you will be part of me. We need no outsiders. We will make our own ceremony. Witnesses enough stand ready. Later, the record shall be carved into the throne you built—letters filled with gold, enduring forever.”

“The Empress can do no wrong,” I replied formally, taking her hand and helping her rise.

We stepped from the awning into the sunlight. She leaned on me, flushed, radiant—so lovely that the crowd erupted in cries: “A Goddess! Our Goddess Phorenice!” But for me, no cheers came. Their eyes met my grim face, and they recoiled.

We climbed the steps to the throne. She sat beneath the serpent’s head. I joined her.

She raised her jeweled hand. Silence fell over the throng.

“Hear me, O my people,” she proclaimed, “and hear me, O High Gods, from whom I come. I take Deucalion as my husband—to share the prosperity of Atlantis, to guard our great possession. May all our enemies perish as she perishes beneath us.”

Then she wrapped her arms around my neck and kissed me fiercely.

In turn, I spoke: “Hear me, O most High Gods, whose servant I am, and hear me, O ye people. I take Phorenice, the Empress, as my wife—to aid in the prosperity of Atlantis, to join with her in protecting our realm. May all enemies fall as they have fallen in the past.”

And then, I—whose lips had never touched the woman I loved—placed my first kiss upon Phorenice, now my wife.

But we were not yet fully joined.

“A woman is one, a man is one,” she declared, invoking the ancient words. “But in marriage, they become one. In token of this, we will now unite, so all may see and remember.” She drew her dagger, pricked my forearm until blood welled, and pressed her lips to it, drinking the drop.

“Oh,” she whispered, eyes bright, “now you are truly part of me, Deucalion. I feel your strength already.” She pulled down her robe. “Let me return the gift.”

I pricked her shoulder. Gods—when I thought of the girl buried beneath us, I could have driven the blade deep. But I pressed my lips to her skin and tasted her blood.

My tongue was dry. My throat burned. I thought I might choke.

But Phorenice, usually so sharp, was deceived. “It thrills you?” she laughed. “It burns like fire? I felt it too. By my face! If I’d known this joy of becoming a wife, I wouldn’t have waited so long. And only with you—no other man could stir this in me. They say a woman feels this but once. I wouldn’t trade it for all my conquests or all my power.” She leaned close, her red curls brushing my cheek. “Have you ever tasted sweetness so pure as knowing we are one now, Deucalion—forever?”

I could not lie. I had tried to play the role for Atlantis’s sake. But human endurance has limits. I was not wholly angry with her. I pitied her passion—unbidden, unchecked. Her boldness was merely the garb of rulers who flaunt what others hide. Yet always before me stood the image of Nais, sacrificed by her jealousy. To feign love now was impossible.

“Words are weak,” I said. “I am not skilled in speech, especially with women. A week ago, I knew nothing of love. Now I know it—suddenly, deeply—but the language still lags behind. My heart speaks, full of things I cannot express.”

At that moment, the High Gods, perhaps, spared me. A wounded man burst into the square, shouting. The crowd echoed his cry. People on rooftops, pyramids, and pavements reached for weapons.

“The rebels are in!” “They’ve tunneled under the wall!” “They killed the cave-tigers and breached a gate!” “They’re storming the city!” “They’ll leave no one alive!”

Our wedding ended abruptly. With rebels only scratching at the walls, confidence was easy. But now they were inside—hordes likely behind them. The mood shifted instantly. Phorenice was the first to grasp it. The fire in her eyes changed to battle-lust.

I stood, drawing breath. “Here,” I said, “is work I understand. Let me drive this rabble back to their holes, Phorenice.”

“But not alone,” she said. “This is my city. And we are newly wed. We cannot be parted.”

“Then as you wish,” I said. Together, we descended the throne steps. Under my breath, I whispered farewell to Nais.

Our armor-bearers met us, handing weapons. We stepped into litters, and slaves bore us swiftly through the streets. The wounded messenger had fainted—no more news. But the sounds of battle guided us. Soon, we passed fleeing citizens, carrying their belongings, screaming in panic.

We let them pass. They could not fight. To stay would mean death—and they’d only hinder our troops. We pushed through them, ignoring their terror.

From a soldier’s eye, the rebels’ advantage was slight. They had dug a tunnel from a mound beneath the wall, breaching a middle-class house. They gathered there, hoping the Empress’s wedding would weaken the guards. But they were discovered early. A strong troop nearby meant their destruction was only a matter of time—even without us.

Phorenice laughed, reclining in her litter. “We grow nervous, my Deucalion. Afraid our happiness might be snatched too soon.”

But I would not lose my moment. “Grant me a wedding gift,” I said.

“It is yours before you name it.”

“Then give me troops—and open a city gate a mile from here.”

“You can gather five hundred on the way—two hundred from here, more from barracks along the wall. But what is your plan?”

I did not answer. The war was beginning. And I had no time to explain.

It would be tedious to recount how, with only a handful of seasoned warriors, I charged again and again until we shattered the rebel horde—fifteen times our size. But let it be known that their ranks dissolved into chaos when they realized no one returned from the mine beneath the city walls, a passage they had believed their secret advantage. The initial cries of panic inside the city soon turned to shouts of triumph. And yet, despite their numbers, these rebels were untrained, poorly armed, and leaderless—each man believing himself equal to any other, so no one could rally them.

When fear struck, it spread like a plague through their ragged lines. No commander stood to order a retreat, no voice to inspire resistance. My strategy was simple: never stop, never defend. We attacked one flank, burst through the center, then darted to the opposite side, striking before they could regroup. By keeping them constantly off balance, confused and running, we sowed terror deeper than steel or fire ever could.

The rout began at the edges. Men fled toward the forests and ruined fields, while those still standing longed only for escape. This was not a feat of arms, nor would any disciplined general claim it as such. It was raw instinct, seizing the moment. Had we met them in open battle on their own ground, they might have held fast—brutal, desperate, and countless. They were Atlanteans, just as the citizens within the city were, and their fury burned bright from years of oppression. But now, broken by panic, they scattered.

With the siege lifted, it was clear the rebellion could end—if handled wisely. Harshness only fuels revolt. Mercy, even if hard to bear, may bring peace.

We pursued the fleeing rebels to ensure no pause in their flight, driving home the lesson of defeat. But the chase had taken us far from the city, and after fighting through the relentless heat of the day, my men were spent. I decided to make camp among half-ruined buildings, which offered shelter and a crude fortification. Fortuitously, a herd of small cloven-hoofed horses, spooked during the chaos, wandered into our lines. We killed five before they escaped—and with that, we had supper. Fires were lit, food cooked, and the night settled in.

Sentries paced the perimeter, calling to each other in the dark. Wounded men sat near the flames, tending their wounds. Officers gathered around me, reviewing the day’s charges, analyzing tactics, reliving the fight. Soldiers delight in such talk; to others, it is mere noise.

As sleep drew near, snores rose from every corner. My officers were exhausted, lingering only out of respect. Yet I dreaded silence—dreaded being alone with my thoughts. I pressed them to speak, though their eyes grew heavy. But they were spared the shame of falling asleep mid-sentence.

A sentry approached, saluting. “My lord,” he said, “a woman has come from the city. We caught her trying to enter the bivouac.”

“What is her name?”

“She refuses to say.”

“Does she have business?”

“She claims none. Only demands to see you.”

“Bring her to the fire,” I ordered. Then, thinking better of it—knowing this woman likely carried news meant for my ears alone—I added, “Stay.” I rose and followed the guard to the edge of the camp.

“Where is she?” I asked.

“My comrades hold her. She might be a rebel spy, intent on killing you. The Empress values you highly, and we know the cruelty of her torturers.”

“Your caution is honest,” I replied. Then he showed me the woman.

She wore a hood and cloak, wrapped tightly, but anyone who knew Nais—as I did—could recognize Ylga, her twin sister, even under layers of cloth. I told the guards to release her without question, then led her beyond the camp, out of earshot.

“You’ve come for something urgent, Ylga?”

“You know me, then? There must be more between us than mere acquaintance, Deucalion, if you guessed who walked beneath all these wrappings.”

I let it pass. “What do you want?”

“Aye,” she said, bitterly, “that’s my reward. All your concern is for the message, none for the messenger. Well, good sir, you are no longer husband to Phorenice.”

“This is news.”

“And true. She has divorced you, cast you off, rejected you. After her first rage, pain and punishment follow.”

“The Empress can do no wrong. You must speak respectfully of her.”

“Oh, spare me that old lie! It sickens me. She was mad with love for you once. Now she’s mad with jealousy. She knows you gave Nais some of your priestly magic—enough to keep her asleep until you choose to awaken her, even if centuries pass. And if you wish to know how she learned this… there’s another airshaft beside the one where you spoke. It leads to a cell with another prisoner. He heard everything and thought to gain freedom by telling. But his tale came too late. With the morning ceremonies, he was forgotten. When his keeper finally remembered him, Phorenice had already married another, and poor Nais had likely taken her sleeping draught. Still, the fools tried to sell the story. They paid for it with their heads. And then Phorenice acted as you might expect.”

She paused, then continued, “She declared: ‘Shall I cut off his head? Pah! Shall I hand him over to my tormentors? He wouldn’t flinch. No—he must suffer worse. I will sever his right hand and left foot. Then he’ll be no warrior. I’ll drive him crippled into the wild lands, where he’ll learn Fear. Beasts will hunt him. Volcanic fires will burn his rest. He’ll know hunger, breathe poisoned air. And always, always, he’ll remember that I have Nais near me—alive, locked in stone, to toy with as I please.’ That is what she said, Deucalion. Now I ask you—will you return to face that?”

“No,” I said. “I do not plan to be maimed and left to live.”

“So you’re wise,” she replied. “And since I still care for you, I’ve made a plan. Does the name Tob ring a bell?”

“Captain of Tatho’s navy?”

“That same Tob. A rough, tar-smelling man, but honest. Tonight, he sails for unknown lands—intending to found a kingdom with himself as king. He can’t earn enough in Atlantis anymore, and he won’t let his wife and children starve. He told me this at the harbor, when I claimed I needed transport for you. With me under his control, he spoke freely. You’ve impressed him, De ucalion. He called you—quite frankly—the recruit he’d been waiting for. Whether you join him or not, he said he’d go to hell to serve you.”

“I fought sea beasts with him once.”

“Well, go back to it. Believe me, it’s your only chance. I’d hate to hear the iron sear your flesh. I’ve arranged with Tob to slip past the harbor forts before the chain is raised. He’s bold, fearless in darkness. He’ll wait at a point we agreed upon. Come, Deucalion—let me show you the way.”

“My girl,” I said, “I owe you much for this.”

“Oh, your thanks?” she scoffed. “Keep them. I didn’t come out here in the dark and danger for gratitude. I knew there’d be little else.”

She tugged my sleeve. “Now show me your pace. They’ll miss you soon, and we don’t want questions.”

So we set off, Ylga and I, leaving the campfires behind. I carried my weapons, ready for attack—beast or man. Few words passed between us, only warnings of the path ahead. Once, we crossed a rivulet of scalding water flowing from a hot spring. I asked, “Are you sure-footed?”

“No,” she admitted, reaching for my hand.

I helped her across, then released her. She sighed, slowly pulling away. Then we walked on—hour after hour, league after league—in silence.

At last, we crested a rise. Below, through the trees, I saw the gleam of the great estuary where Atlantis stood. The ground was soggy, the trees bristling with thorns. Ylga’s breath came in ragged gasps. When I offered to support her, she pushed me away. “I’m not weak,” she said sharply. “Don’t think that’s why you want to touch me.”

Soon, we broke through the trees. The worst was behind us. Ahead, the ship lay anchored a mile offshore—a strange sight under the dim stars. We moved along the beach toward her.

Tob kept watch. We were challenged at once, ordered to halt and state our purpose. But when he recognized us, he welcomed us. He called his crew, loosened the anchor rope, and brought the ship close to shore. Two oars were thrust out to help us aboard.

I turned to Ylga. “I shall never forget what you’ve done tonight. If the High Gods allow me to return to Atlantis, you’ll feel my gratitude.”

“I don’t want to return. I’m tired of this life.”

“But you have your palace, your servants, your wealth. Phorenice won’t take those from you.”

“Oh, I could return tomorrow and be fan-girl again. But I don’t want to.”

“I warn you—it’s no place for a refined woman like you. I’ve been viceroy of Yucatan. I know hardship. This will be worse. Few survive the early colonization. Only the strongest endure. They gain new scars every day.”

“I don’t care. And I can work. I can cook, shoot arrows, sew clothes from animal hides with sinew thread. You see only the fine dress, the fan-girl, and think me useless. Bah, Deucalion! Don’t let anyone tell me about your perfection. You know nothing of women.”

“I’ve learned all I need to know about one woman. Because of her memory, I cannot ask her sister to come with me.”

“Aye,” she said, bitter again, “hurt my pride. I knew I’d never be first. But no decent man would remind me.”

“Then I must go alone. I’d rather carry your respect than your hatred.”

“That’s the most humiliating thing. I can’t hate you. I should, after the way you’ve treated me. But I don’t. I grow fonder. If I thought you could stay alive and whole in Atlantis, I wouldn’t mention Tob’s impatience.”

Suddenly, she threw her arms around my neck and kissed me fiercely. “There—that’s farewell, dear. See? I’m reckless. I don’t care what I do now. You can’t despise me more than you already have.”

She ran back into the trees.

“This is madness,” I said. “I must escort you safely to the city gate, then return.”

“You don’t need to worry. The broken-hearted are always safe. Besides, I have my way. At least you’ll remember me now. You’ll never forget that kiss.”

“Farewell, Ylga,” I called. “May the High Gods keep you in their care.”

No answer came. She was gone.

I turned and waded to the ship, climbed aboard via the steering oars. Tob gave the order to haul up the anchor and pull away.

“Greeting, my lord,” he said. “I wished you’d arrived earlier. We’ve few hands and slow speed. The sooner we’re beyond pursuit, the safer my wife and children in the after-castle. I haven’t sailed in a ship like this in ages. She’s no machine, no steering mannikin. Look at her fittings. I suspect rot in her hull. But she’s the best I could buy. If she reaches the land I’ve chosen, we’ll build a home—or die. She’s been in the Egypt trade—known for rotting wood.”

“You had enough crew before I joined?”

“Oh yes. Fifty strong men, eight women—all packed tight. Getting them out of the city was trouble. The port captain nearly stripped us bare before letting so many fighters leave. Times have changed. Sea trade’s dying. Skilled sailors take wives and go elsewhere. I barely got half a dozen to help sail this. The rest are raw landmen. If I land half alive, I’ll be lucky.”

“Still, with luck and good winds, we’ll reach Europe soon.”

Tob slapped his leg. “No savage Europe for me. I once discovered islands north of Europe, separated by a strait. I called them the Tin Islands—tin ore litters the beaches. Storm drove me there. I said nothing when I returned. Now it’s useful. No one in Atlantis knows of them except me. We’ll fight for our ground, build a town, a kingdom.”

“With Tob as king?”

“I’ve dreamed of it for years. But I know my place. I’d gladly serve under you, my lord. Though I thought the lady at the harbor said you’d bring a wife. I’d have mentioned it earlier. The natives on my Tin Islands go naked, stain themselves blue with woad. Filthy, brutish. Strong, though—good slaves. But one grows numb to their looks before wanting children with them.”

“I am still married to Phorenice.”

Tob grinned. “The Gods bless her. But mariners believe marriage doesn’t cross seas. Still, that’s neither here nor there. Back to the Tin Islands—they’ll please you. Kingdoms aren’t hard to carve there. There are beasts—cave-bears, cave-tigers, river-horses, snakes—but the great lizards avoid the land. Birds? None dangerous. It’ll be a desirable realm.”

He sighed. “I’ve imagined myself king, truly. And no one I’d break this journey for but you, Deucalion. But I don’t regret it. This breeze and ebb carry us well. See those fire baskets on the forts? We’re abreast now. By dawn, the city will vanish. The flood tide won’t begin till then. But unless the wind strengthens, we’ll anchor when the tide turns. Tides run fierce here. And there are treacherous rips near the Tin Islands—wait until we reach them.”

At dawn, fearful glances turned backward. The waters sparkled, mountains blazed in the distance. All expected Phorenice’s navy to pursue. I shared their dread. Many swore they’d fight to the ship’s sinking before obeying her again. But their courage was not wasted. During the full tide, we drifted barely forward—no sails, no oars seen. Then, when the current reversed, we surged onward, greatly relieved.

Tob’s wife insisted on bringing drink, urging libations to herself as future queen. Tob cuffed her back into the after-castle, slamming the hatch. “We’re done with that nonsense,” he snapped. “My Lord Deucalion will be king of our new land. A proper king—just ask those who’ve sailed with him.”

Beneath his words, I sensed regret. But I said nothing. My plans were already formed.

We sailed down the great estuary. Others cheered at the empty seas. I mourned the once-thriving shipping lanes.

They’d started with only two days’ provisions. So, even after leaving the estuary, we coasted, stopping at rivers and hidden shores to hunt and fish. “When winter comes,” Tob said, “its gales will be too strong for this ship. I’ll haul her ashore, build a camp, grow grain, thresh it, then resume. That’s the custom. I’ll do it—unless you advise otherwise.”

Two months later, I saw my chance. “Tob,” I said, “I am weak, defenseless, at your mercy. But what if I don’t go all the way to the Tin Islands? What if I abandon your kingship?”

He brightened. “Aye, you’re weak, my lord. We know. But what’s your meaning?”

“I do not wish to leave Atlantis. If you’ll put me down anywhere on this coast facing Europe, I’ll bless you in the eyes of the Gods. I wish I could give you money or supplies—but I have nothing.”

He nodded slowly. “Very well. We’ll find a place.”

From the dawn of man, when the Gods shaped life from distant heavens, there have always been lands too harsh for human survival—places where nature itself seems to conspire against the living. None more so than the eastern reaches of Atlantis, bordering the untamed shores of Europe and Africa. The common folk avoid this region entirely, driven by ancient fears that the spirits of the wicked dead walk in broad daylight, hunting down the unwary with supernatural fury. So great is the dread that even outlaws, fleeing justice, often return willingly to their chains rather than face the horrors whispered to lurk beyond the mist.

To the learned, many of these tales are superstition. Yet even they acknowledge that danger festers here in plain sight. Mountains churn like boiling kettles; tremors shake the earth daily; invisible vapors rise from fissures, swift and silent, choking men before they know what strikes them. Rivers run clear but carry poison in their depths. Cracks split the ground, swallowing water that surges back in explosive geysers. Mud boils like lava, and rivers of molten rock flow across valleys without warning. Life here is a constant battle against fire, steam, and decay.

Yet within this inferno grow forests older than memory. Trees stand as sentinels, their roots sunk deep into perpetual moisture, their crowns shrouded in mist. Fires rarely touch the undergrowth—the damp air smothers flames. But the wet heat saps strength, wears the body down, and makes every breath feel heavy. To live in such a place demands more than endurance—it demands cunning.

And it is here, among the shadows and steaming rocks, that I found myself cast adrift. From the moment I stepped onto this cursed land, survival became a war. Fear was my constant companion, though I fought it with faith in the High Gods. I believed, fiercely, that my life was guided by divine hands—and that belief alone kept me from breaking.

For months, I suffered. My clothes rotted away, leaving me bare as any savage from the northern wastes. Ague wracked my limbs, and I could find no herbs to ease the fever. For days, I lived on grubs and bitter leaves. When I did kill game, ignorance made me eat flesh that poisoned me. I wandered, weak and broken, until slowly—painfully—I began to learn.

I found a massive black tree, hollowed at its core, with a hole near the base. There I took shelter—until one night, a monstrous lizard, so immense its weight made the tree sway like a sapling, came to probe the cavity with its thick, horny tongue. I barely escaped. I could not harm it—not with my crude weapons. It was as if I were trying to wound a mountain. That night taught me: I must be higher. So I carved footholds inside the trunk, climbing ten man-heights above the ground. I built a platform of branches, then carved arrow-slit windows. Later, I brought up sand for a hearth, so I might cook in safety, far from the open dangers below.

Slowly, I mastered the river fish and small turtles. My hunger eased. But I could never stomach the green meat—tainted with the sharp taste of tar, it sickened me. And the larger lizards remained ever-present threats. They moved with terrifying speed, avoided traps, and seemed to sense the coming eruptions of the mountains. They buried themselves in mud or swam deep in lakes to escape the worst of the heat. Unlike humans, they thrived in the scalding waters. I once saw one glide across a river of molten rock, untouched, unharmed—its hide impervious to flame.

Still, as time passed, I grew stronger. But something else stirred within me—longing. Not just for food or shelter, but for Nais. I carved her likeness from the ivory of a river horse’s tooth. My hands were clumsy, my art poor. The result was crude, distorted—but in the curve of her brow, the tilt of her chin, I saw her. Each time I looked upon it, my heart burned anew. Yet the image only deepened my ache. It reminded me of what I had lost—and what I might never regain.

Other priests had come here, drawn by the same sacred mysteries. By denying the body, they sought communion with the divine. Some returned, bearing knowledge that made them seem magicians to the common people. Their feats—once dismissed as mere tricks—were now seen as miracles, and their teachings spread with fervor.

But I could not follow that path. My mind was not free. Nais held it captive. Her memory was my only comfort. And yet, after years of silence, I could endure no longer.

From my tree-house, I could see a distant peak—white with eternal snow, glowing crimson each night as the sun set behind it. Once, long ago, I had worshipped the Sun God from that very crest. Now, I watched it nightly, remembering the Sacred Mountain where I had buried Nais alive. Could I see it again? Just once?

So I left my shelter and began the journey. The lower lands were choked in fog. I wandered blind, doubling back on myself, losing my way again and again. The great lizards hunted through the swamps, and I knew no safe refuge. A hundred times, I hid just in time, trembling beneath fallen logs or thickets, as beasts passed within arm’s reach. But the Gods watched over me. The creatures’ eyes seemed to fail when I crouched still.

Twice, rivers of boiling water cut across my path. I gathered timber, built rafts, and crossed on makeshift ferries. The journey dragged—endless, exhausting. Yet to me, time flew. I was returning to the past, to the place where love had died. If I could only see it once more, I would return to my tree and wait—for the Gods, or for death.

As I climbed higher, the trees thinned. The air turned cold. I wove grasses into buskins and a coat to shield myself. Then came the snow—eternal, wind-scoured, forming treacherous gullies and ice walls. Progress slowed. My body grew numb, my strength failing.

Above me, a great bird circled—huge, with wings like storm clouds. It swooped close, forcing me to raise my spear in warning. It was a sky-terror, feeding on the weak and dying. But I pressed on. The vision ahead was worth any price.

Again and again, I thought I reached the summit—only to find another ridge, another wall of ice. But at last, I stood upon the crest.

Before me, miles away, lay the Sacred Mountain. Its fires burned pale beneath the sun, and though the city at its foot was hidden, I knew its shape, its layout. My gaze fixed on the square before the royal pyramid—the throne of granite where I had laid Nais to rest.

Had Phorenice touched her tomb?

I leaned on my spear, breathing hard, staring across the vastness. The smoke of familiar peaks rose like old friends. And in that moment, my love surged—a fire hotter than any volcano. My soul reached out to her, yearning to be near her, even in death.

Then—distant, faint—a voice called my name.

At first, I ignored it. I had come to be alone with Nais. But the voice grew louder, clearer. I turned, startled.

Coming up the slope was a man—old, bent, his hair and beard white as the snow. He carried the glowing Symbol of the Sun, and with it, he moved with unnatural speed. As he neared, I recognized him: Zaemon.

He approached with reverence, making the sign of the Seven. I returned it, formally, fully. Then he bowed, as messenger of the High Council of Priests, and I knelt in obedience.

“In all things, I will obey your command,” I said.

“Such is your duty,” he replied. “The Council commands you: return at once to the Sacred Mountain. Phorenice has besieged it. She threatens to destroy all who refuse her rule. You are the greatest general Atlantis has ever known. We need you to save us.”

“I obey,” I said. “But tell me—what of Nais?”

“She rests where you left her. Unharmed. Phorenice knows you live—she has stolen much of our ancient lore—and she keeps Nais alive, hoping to use her against you. But she does not understand the depth of our creed. Even I, her father, would sacrifice her if it served Atlantis.”

“You go beyond me,” I said. “My loyalty is not yours to command.”

Zaemon’s eyes narrowed. “You speak boldly. But we have no choice. You are the best. The Council trusted you. You swore an oath upon the Ark itself—to serve without reward, without hope. Yet now you ask for a price.”

“It is not greed,” I said. “It is truth. I have preserved my humanity. You have transcended it. And perhaps that is why you came to me. Because I can fight not just for duty—but for love.”

He stared at me, then sighed. “Come to the Sacred Mountain. Defeat Phorenice. And by my beard, you shall have Nais—just as you left her.”

“I will come,” I said. “But I must rest.”

“No,” Zaemon replied. “I am not tired. And you will not walk beside me. Follow at your own pace.”

He turned and vanished down the slope, moving faster than any man should. Soon, he disappeared around a cliff of ice. I followed, but he pulled ahead. His footprints, deep at first, grew shallow—then faded completely. The snow was untouched, as if no man had ever walked it.

I did not panic. I understood what had happened. Zaemon was not merely old—he was wise beyond mortal limits. He had used the Mysteries to vanish, to return instantly to the Sacred Mountain. I knew he would be there, breathless, waiting.

I had his promise. And I would keep my vow.

Once, I had placed Atlantis above all. Now I knew the truth: I loved Nais most. And I would serve her—first, last, and always.

My journey across the vast continent of Atlantis was long, fraught with peril, and marked by solitude—so much so that only one pause in my travels deserves mention, and even that was not by choice. One day, I brought down a stag after a grueling chase across an open savannah. After ensuring the area was clear of danger, I began to cut the meat into portions, preparing a bundle for transport.

But beneath the grass, hidden from sight, coiled a monstrous green worm—great-legged and venomous. As I shifted my weight, my heel brushed its body, and in a flash it lunged, fangs sinking deep into my lower thigh. I reacted swiftly, slashing through its neck with my knife. It writhed violently, its hundred legs twitching in grotesque spasms. Then, cleaning my blade on the earth, I drove it repeatedly around the wound, forcing blood to flow freely—hoping to flush out the poison. The blood trickled down my leg, warm and steady, and I shouldered my meat and pressed onward, grateful to have escaped what might have become a fatal encounter.

Yet within hours, a tightening pain seized my leg, growing stronger with each passing mile. Soon, swelling followed, stretching my skin taut as parchment. A fever took hold, shaking me with violent chills. Had any predator come upon me then, I would have been helpless. As night fell, I dragged myself into a tree, perching in the crook of a branch, enduring agony through the long darkness.

At dawn, I descended, leaning heavily on my spear. The moss on the north side of trees had always guided me, but now I noticed it encircled the trunks—everywhere. My head swam. Still, I pressed forward, driven by duty: to fulfill the High Council’s command and reclaim Nais, as promised.

My final memory was of a figure emerging from the black forest—someone who helped me. After that, consciousness slipped away.

When I awoke, I found myself on a bed of leaves in a narrow cleft between two rocks, crudely furnished and guarded with stakes against predators. My wound was bound with a poultice of herbs. At the far end of the cave, a woman sat roasting wood-grubs and honey over a fire.

“How did I get here?” I asked.

“I carried you,” she replied.

“And who are you?”

“A nymph,” she said. “I gather herbs, heal the sick, read fortunes, predict futures. In return, people give what they can. If they don’t, I curse them—let them wither. Not much living these days, when war and plague have left so few.”

“Do you travel to Atlantis?”

“No. Phorenice would boil me alive if she could. She tolerates no magic but her own. They say she plans to pull the Priests off their mountain.”

“So you hear news of the city?”

“Of course. That’s how I earn my trade. And I knew you weren’t just another seeker of spells. Your speech marked you as noble. No need to fear speaking plainly.”

“Then tell me—does Phorenice still rule?”

“Most cruelly.”

“As a maiden?”

“She is mother to twin sons. Tatho is her husband now—has been three years.”

“Tatho? The viceroy of Yucatan?”

“There is no Yucatan. A horde of small, hairy men came from the west—thousands strong. They wielded stone-tipped clubs, nothing more. But numbers were their weapon. They didn’t seek gold or slaves. Only flesh—Atlantean flesh. They devoured entire cities, leaving ruins behind, then moved on. When they reached the coast, Tatho and a handful escaped by sea. Even his wife was eaten. These creatures are relentless. May the Gods keep them from crossing the seas—otherwise, they’d be worse than Phorenice herself.”

I had heard whispers of such beings—half-human, half-beast, creatures held in check by divine decree. Sometimes, when justice demanded it, they were unleashed as instruments of destruction. But I said nothing to this woman—she was merely a folk healer, perhaps foolish, like most who dabble in magic for profit. Yet I grieved deeply for Yucatan, where I had once labored so hard.

Still, I kept my identity hidden. “What of Egypt?” I asked.

“In darkness, they say. Who cares about Egypt now? Everyone only thinks of themselves. Once, villagers brought offerings here out of piety. Now, they come only for gain. I live on grubs and honey. A poor life, a nymph’s life in these times. No one believes in mysteries anymore.”

“What of the pestilence?”

“I’ve seen none. Thank the Gods it hasn’t reached here. But it’s said to have grown from the unburied dead—those Phorenice has slain. She kills constantly, and the bodies pile up faster than beasts can consume them. Why did the Gods allow her to rise? I wish she and her children were boiled in brine before they ruined a honest nymph’s livelihood.”

“They say she’s placed her image in every temple. Mandated prayers, sacrifices twice daily. Declared all other gods inferior. Those who refuse to worship her properly have their hamstrings cut—so they’ll kneel in devotion forever.”

“Will you eat my grubs and honey? There’s nothing else. You were bleeding from your back when I found you—your pack gone. Either taste this, or go hungry.”

I stayed six days, recovering under her care. When healed, I hunted and killed a cloven-hoofed horse for her, then continued my journey.

The land beyond had once thrived—villages dotted the forests, homes built high in trees or low on the ground from massive logs, interlocked and fortified. Such dwellings offered safety from cave-bears and tigers. But now, scarcely one in ten remained. Houses stood silent, hearths choked with weeds, gardens reclaimed by jungle. Villages with thick walls lay abandoned—gates fallen, logs scattered, only middens marking where humans once lived. Phorenice’s reign seemed to have spread like a blight across a once-fertile realm.

So far, I had met little resistance. Some fled at my approach; others stood their ground but did not attack. A few offered shelter. But near the city, paths grew muddy and crowded. Watchmen atop towers would cry: “A masterless man!” and villagers would rush out to seize my weapons, then assign me to labor.

I had no desire to kill them—pity filled me at their downfall. Words often sufficed to let me pass. When they failed, I struck lightly, aiming only to move forward without unnecessary bloodshed. Eventually, I began avoiding villages altogether, taking forest paths to skirt their stench and danger.

As I neared Atlantis, the Sacred Mountain loomed beside the city, crowned by a ring of eternal fires. Caution became paramount. The countryside was drained of soldiers. Women prayed for the end of the siege—either the fall of the Priests or the death of Phorenice. Peace was all they desired.

An army surrounded the mountain, guarding its single access tightly. After so many miles, I feared I’d reach the city only to be stopped short. Force was useless—my fight would be brief, glorious, and futile. One man against an army. My mission would end there.

No. This called for cunning. I found cover in the woods, lying there, thinking desperately of a way in.

Before me stretched the city’s grim walls—temples, palaces, pyramids rising beyond. The royal pyramid caught my eye. Its steps had been plated with gold, Phorenice’s vanity on display. But it was not the gold that stirred me. I remembered the square before it—the granite throne carved with a serpent and an outstretched hand. Beneath it, in a hollow, slept Nais—my love—drugged for eight years, yet alive, waiting only for me to reach her.

In that hiding place, I raged for a day and night, tormented by failure. But at dawn, a man ran toward me.

“You needn’t threaten me,” he shouted. “I mean no harm. You’re Deucalion—though I wouldn’t have recognized you in those rags and that beard. I know you’ll give me your word. I’ve wasted no time.”

He was Ro, a priest I knew—greedy, unreliable. “Where do you come from?” I asked.

“Zaemon sent me. He appeared at my house—how, I don’t know. The army blocks all roads to the mountain. I told him I wanted no part of his schemes. I’m a peaceful man, Deucalion. I have a wife to feed. I still serve in the temple, though we’ve replaced the old gods with Phorenice’s image. People are pious now—offering more than ever. So why risk losing my post, or my head?”

“I believe you,” I said.

“But Zaemon wasn’t listening. He flew into a rage. I felt death coming. You know his powers?”

“I’ve seen some.”

“He has power—no denying it. But I serve the god who feeds me. Yet Zaemon’s gods give him strength. Look at my right hand—it’s paralyzed, dead from the wrist. He gave it to me—‘to focus my attention.’ Then he accused me of apostasy. I couldn’t defend myself. Finally, he said I could either obey him fully—or suffer the punishment of my betrayal. ‘I won’t kill you,’ he said, ‘but I’ll paralyze every limb. When you die in a gutter, you’ll welcome it.’”

“If Zaemon said that, he meant it. So you agreed?”

“With a wife depending on me, what choice did I have? I asked his orders. Find you when you arrive—and deliver his message.”

“Then tell me the meeting place.”

“There is none. You must watch and search. But when he comes, you’ll be led to him.” He paused. “I think I was led. I don’t know how. But I’ve found you. If there’s gratitude, please speak to Zaemon about my hand. It’s unbearable—no fault of mine. He could cure it with a word.”

“You still trust the old priests’ powers,” I said. “But you haven’t delivered your message.”

“It’s simple. You’re to make a great blaze—burn the grasses before this wood. Then, on the following night, go to the flank of the mountain opposite the city, where the cliffs drop sheer. A rope will be lowered. They’ll haul you up.”

“It seems straightforward. Thank you. I’ll ask Zaemon to restore your hand.”

“You shall have my prayers. And look—this is small, but it might slip your mind later. Do it now, when you arrive. Phorenice will try to pull you down once she knows you’re there. She never fails. She’s becoming a goddess—already she’s mastered the ancient knowledge of our priests. She’s discovered the Secret of Life…”

“She’s found it?” I gasped. “How? Only the Three know that. Even the Seven aren’t told.”

“I know nothing of her methods. But she has it. She claims to be immortal now. Equal to the gods.”

I stared at him. “You’re risking much by being gone.”

“I must return. Please remember me. And—” He glanced at my ragged clothes. “If you value my service…?”

“You see me poor,” I said. “Nearly destitute.”

“A coin? Or a bronze link? I need food for my wife. If you have nothing—then farewell.”

He left. I didn’t regret it. I never liked Ro. But I wasted no time. Before he was out of sight, I lit the grass. Flames leapt high.

That night, under cover of darkness, I made for the cliffside. No rope hung—then, slowly, one descended, thin and sinuous. I looped it over my leg, pulled hard. Above, hands began hauling. I rose, unseen, back to the Sacred Mountain after years of exile.

The men pulling me were old, breathing heavily. I wondered why younger priests weren’t chosen—until I realized: in the Priest Clan, obedience is assumed. Orders are given for reason. Questions are not asked.

They brought me straight to Zaemon.

He greeted me with the royal sign. “All hail, Deucalion—King of Atlantis, duly called by the High Council.”

“Is Phorenice dead?” I asked.

“It remains for you to slay her. To claim your kingdom—if anything remains to rule. The sentence has been passed. She must die, even if we must unleash the darkest powers from the Ark of Mysteries and destroy this continent. We’ve endured her crimes for years—by divine command. But now she has crossed the line. The Gods have spoken.”

“You are one of the Three. I am only of the Seven. You know the cost.”

“There can be no counting the cost now, brother, king. It is an order.”

“It is an order,” I repeated. “I obey.”

“If it were not impious, I could justify it. She usurped the throne—yet was forgiven. She corrupted our religion—forgiven. She waged senseless wars, destroyed trade—forgiven. She defiled temples, set up her own image—forgiven. But now—she has discovered the Secret of Life and Death. That is beyond tolerance.”

“I confronted her in the banqueting hall. I read the edict. She laughed. She said she would live forever, share the secret with whom she chose. Tatho would be immortal too. She would shout it aloud—let all know. Death from age would be a myth.”

“She cared nothing for nature’s laws. She is Phorenice—her own highest law. She defied me. Defied the Gods behind my voice. ‘My magic equals yours, you pompous fool,’ she screamed. ‘Soon, we shall stand side by side on trial.’”

From that moment, she gathered her army. We prepared ours. Our arts revealed you lived. You were elected King. How you were summoned—only you know. But now you are here. As King, you must lead.”

“Then as King, I speak without threat: I must have my Queen first. Without her, I cannot rule.”

“She shall be brought.”

“Good. Then I shall be General. I will inspect the defenses, the men.”

I stepped outside. Zaemon followed. Night still reigned—but on the mountain crest, no darkness existed. A ring of fires burned eternally, fed by the breath of the earth, lighting the summit as brightly as daylight. These flames were not mere light—they were defense. Legend says the first men came to the mountain because of this natural fortress.

And now, I stood among them again—ready to reclaim what was mine.

From our vantage point just below the crest of the Sacred Mountain, the city sprawled beneath us like a forgotten map etched in stone and shadow. The harbor shimmered at its edge, the great estuary glinting under the dark sky. Beyond, the hills exhaled smoke and steam as they always did—ancient breaths from the earth’s deep lungs. At the heart of the city stood the massive stone circle dedicated to our Lord the Sun, stark and silent. Closer still rose the royal pyramid, its golden sides catching fire from the setting sun. And there, in the square before it, stood the granite throne—small now from distance, no larger than a molehill—but within that hollow, for nine long years, my love had lain asleep.

Old Zaemon followed my gaze, his face grave. “Yes,” he said softly, “I know where your thoughts lie. Deucalion, when he first landed from Yucatan, was a man of iron will. The king we’ve chosen—though the best among us—is not so strong.”

“I can be stronger,” I replied. “Give me Nais, alive and warm, and I’ll be more than any cold soldier or distant ruler could ever be.”

He nodded slowly. “I have already sworn it. You shall have her. But it will cost you—your new kingdom, perhaps, and possibly Phorenice and her followers. Though I doubt she’ll fall so easily. She is too cunning. Once we begin, she may sense the hand behind the storm—and flee before harm reaches her. The High Gods have seen fit to grant her some knowledge of how these earth tremors may be stirred.”

“I’ve seen her play with them,” I said. “But tell me your plan.”

“It will unfold in time. For now, sleep. Tonight, you must go into the city, take your woman—my daughter—and bring her back here. You’ll need every ounce of strength you have.” He stepped back, studying me. “You’re no longer the clean-cut Deucalion I knew. Your beard is wild, your skin burned, your body stripped bare and grimy. You look like some savage cast ashore from Europe. That might serve you well tonight—when the city shakes and screams. No nobleman will mistake you for a prince. They may think you a beast, and leave you be.”

“I pity the fool who tries to claim you as servant,” I said.

Zaemon laughed—a rare sound, sharp and sudden. Then silence fell between us again, heavier than before.

“Go rest,” he said. “I must enter the Ark of Mysteries and prepare with another of the Three. What we are about to do is no small matter. We stir powers older than mountains. There is danger for us all. If we do not meet again until we stand before the stars to answer the Gods, then farewell, Deucalion.”

That night, I slept like a warrior—deep, unbroken, without thought. Only the weak let fear disturb their rest. When darkness settled fully, I woke and called those who would tend the rope. The Moon was absent this cycle, and the world wore a cloak of absolute blackness. As I descended, I vanished from sight.

I reached the shattered rocks at the cliff’s base and moved with caution. Phorenice’s soldiers huddled around their fires, restless, uneasy—as if sensing the storm approaching. I did not wish to awaken them further. So I slipped through the shadows, swift and silent, until I came to the hidden passage I once used to slip beneath the city walls.

The lamp waited, burning low. I moved quickly through the winding tunnels. The air tasted thicker, fouler than before—charged with vapors. I sensed Zaemon had already begun to stir the deep forces beneath the land. Yet again, I struggled to find an exit. Not because guards blocked my way, but because the city’s masonry had changed. In ancient times, the Priests’ Clan oversaw every design, ensuring no structure disrupted their secret paths. Now, the priests were powerless, and architects built without care, sealing off old passages by accident.

So I emerged where I could, not where I wished. I found myself on the far side of the city, near where the outer wall met the harbor. I slipped out unseen, closed the trace behind me, and felt the ground beneath me shift—like a ship’s deck in calm seas. No visible movement yet, but the earth no longer felt solid. It hummed with tension, waiting to break.

Would I be too late? Would she be uncovered, exposed to the crowd when the tremors broke loose? I saw in my mind their crude hands reaching for her, helpless and still. My chest tightened. I ran—not with reason, but with rage and hope—down the narrow, twisting streets, driven by one thought: reach the square before the world fell apart.

At first, only beggars shouted, “Thief! Stop him!” But none pursued. My speed carried me beyond them. Soon, the earth began to groan. Buildings creaked, dust rained down. People fled their homes, screaming, clutching children. But none paid attention to the ragged, naked figure rushing past, kicking up mud.

And then—there—I reached the great square before the pyramid. I arrived just in time.

It was chaos. Dust choked the air. Shadows swarmed. The ground rippled like water, growing more violent with each passing moment. Houses collapsed into silence, swallowed by the dark. The pyramid stood firm—built to endure. Its stones interlocked like teeth, held together by metal bands. It was said half its foundation could be removed and it would still stand.

Torches flickered at its doors. Frightened souls darted out, vanishing into the gloom. But I cared nothing for the pyramid. I didn’t even care if Phorenice was trapped inside. I crouched beside the granite throne, feeling its stones grind against each other like broken bones.

In that darkness, vision was impossible. Yet somehow—perhaps by grace—I could see. I watched as the capstone of the throne shifted slightly with each tremor. A tiny jerk. Then another. Always in the same direction.

Zaemon’s work had purpose. Even in my blind haste, I now understood. I climbed the steps on the side where the stone moved, clinging tight, heart pounding.

The shaking grew worse. The crash of collapsing walls echoed louder. Dust filled the air so thickly I coughed and choked. The night turned blacker as stars vanished beneath the storm. I clung to the throne’s edge, barely holding my place.

Still, the capstone held Nais fast. I wanted to scream, to tear the stone apart with my bare hands. But I could not. The people poured into the square, terrified, mad with fear. Some cried, “Phorenice, Goddess, save us!” When she did not answer, they fell into frantic prayer, reciting childhood chants to gods they’d long abandoned. A bitter irony.

Then, thunder roared above. Lightning split the sky, illuminating the scene.

And there—she stood.

Phorenice.

Safe. Unmoved. Standing on a flagstone in the middle of the square, swaying with the tremors like a sailor on a rolling sea. Her face was calm, untouched by time. Her red hair curled about her neck, dust brushed away with delicate fingers. No fear. Only weariness, disdain—perhaps amusement.

She looked upon the ruin and smiled.

My blood boiled. I would have killed her then, if I could have reached her. But she remained, serene, while beside her, crumpled on the stone, lay Ylga—her fan-girl once more—pale and sick. I remembered Ylga was Nais’s twin. I owed her kindness. But I had no time.

The capstone shifted again. Further. With each shock, it slid from its bed. I watched, breathless. My love was coming closer.

A group of panicked citizens surged toward the throne, thinking it safe. They scrambled up the steps, crawling on hands and knees. I drove them back—hitting, shouting, tearing them away. I didn’t care what they thought. I only wanted them gone.

They called me a demon of the night. I didn’t argue.

The stone moved farther. Still too slow. Rage took over. I thrust against it with shoulder and head, clawed at it with hands till muscles bulged. The Gods must have laughed at my futility. But it was Their will—slow, sure, unstoppable.

Then—finally—the capstone reached balance. The next tremor threw it forward with a thunderous crash.

It fell.

And I froze.

All the effort, the planning, the prayers—it had come to pass. Yet my soul sank. Would Nais be gone? Had Phorenice taken her? Or left her dead as a cruel joke?

I couldn’t know. I dared not guess.

For the first time in my life, I was undone by love.

But the tremors eased. Zaemon had done his work. I gathered courage. I climbed onto the flat stone.

Darkness pressed in. Dust choked the air. But lightning might strike again. If I were seen—shaggy, wild, changed—Phorenice would recognize me.

The idea of fighting made me sick. I just wanted to take her and leave.

With trembling hands, I found the metal clamps—still locked as I had left them. I slipped my fingers between them, touched the curve of her body beneath. Joy surged through me. Alive or dead, she was mine. I could have screamed.

But the earth still shook. I clung with one hand, unhooked the clamps with the other. Finally, I lifted the top half clear. Left the lower ones. I pulled her soft form onto the stone, kissed her face a hundred times—blindly, desperately.

Some madness seized me. I thought my kisses alone might wake her. But she was cold. Lifeless. Slowly, reason returned.

This was no place for either of us. Daylight would come. The city would recover. Someone would see her. Recognize her. And then—me. Under the beard, the dirt, the savage clothes—they’d know.

I couldn’t stay.

I slung her over my shoulder, free arm ready. I stumbled through the ruins, blinded by dust, feet slipping on cracked stone. I fell many times—but she never bruised.

I reached the edge of the square, found a known street. The worst of the shaking passed. No buildings fell. But rubble littered the road. Sweat poured from me as I pushed forward.

No gate. No wall. The city’s defenses had been torn apart. The Gods had plowed through it. Atlantis stood open, defenseless.

I felt sorrow—not for the pyramid, nor the palaces—but for the ramparts. The soldier in me grieved. This mighty city, once my only master, was humbled.

But it was fleeting. I had another love now—one that wrapped around me tighter than any duty. Dawn approached. Danger loomed.

Raiders would come. Soldiers would stop anyone fleeing with loot. I wouldn’t fight them. I ran—swiftly, silently—through back alleys, calling to strangers, “She has the plague! Shelter us!” They opened their doors. I kept moving.

Finally, I reached the cliff. The rope dangled. I hoisted Nais up. Then I stepped into the loop and followed.

Now came the true trial.

We laid her in a bed in one of the mountain houses. And there, for the first time in nine years, I saw her clearly.

Her beauty was faded, drawn thin. Her eyes were closed, but the lids were so translucent, I could see the brown of her pupils beneath. Her hair had grown wild, thick, long—spreading like a cushion around her head.

No breath. No pulse. Yet she did not look dead. The woman I buried was full-figured, strong. This one—half her weight. I could carry her easily now.

Her fingers—nails twisted into spirals, skin waxen, bones visible beneath. Lips pale as snow, once crimson. Shoulders hollow, dust collecting in the crevices.

I knelt beside her. Reverently, I prepared the bath. I heated water, mixed it with the sacred essences the Priests preserved—life extracts from beasts, stored for desperate times. I placed her chin-deep in the warm liquid, kept the temperature steady, constant—like blood.

And I sat beside her, watching.

Waiting.

For life to return.

It was Nais herself who summoned me to duty. The roar of battle reached us in the chamber where I tended her, and she stirred, asking what it meant. I told her—briefly, as she had no strength for long tales—the story of her nine years’ sleep.

A flush warmed her cheeks. “My lord is the most worthy man in all the world to be King,” she whispered.

“I refused to accept the throne until they brought me the Queen I desired—alive and whole—upon this very mountain.”

“How we women are treated like property by men!” she murmured. “Yet, for myself… I find the trade quite agreeable. You should not have let me stand in the way of Atlantis’ fate, Deucalion. Still, it pleases me that you once faltered—and that I was the reason. What is that basin over there? Ah—yes, I remember. My thoughts seem clouded today.”

“You’ve begun your recovery. Strength will return swiftly—but not too fast. Patience is required.”

“Just one moment, sir,” she said, voice soft but firm. “Are my features still… as they were? There’s no mirror here. I feared I might wake wrinkled, aged beyond recognition.”

“You are exactly as I first saw you,” I replied. “The most beautiful woman in the world.”

She smiled, took the broth I offered, and added, “My hair has grown—it needs combing badly. Once, I thought you preferred ruddy hair… not plain brown.” Then she closed her eyes, sank back into the cushions, and fell into a deep, peaceful sleep, her lips still curved in a quiet smile. I covered her with the blanket, kissed her gently—pulling my beard away so it wouldn’t brush her cheek—and left the room.

That beard had become unbearable. I went to another chamber, found tools, and shaved it clean down to the chin. I also donned a fresh robe—replacing my tattered clothes. A king must carry himself with dignity, even if he wears no crown.

But the noise of war grew louder each hour. My kingdom was shrinking. The fighting intensified, drawing closer. It was clear that some of the gates along the cliffside pass—once thought unassailable—had fallen under Phorenice’s relentless assault. And truly, it was no wonder. Driven mad by the destruction of her royal pyramid, the Empress now fought with furious genius. The Sacred Mountain, once mighty, was no longer strong.

Stone fortifications remained—cleverly built, powerfully designed—but walls do not fight alone. They need men. And men, especially when outnumbered, begin to fall. In battles such as this, desperate soldiers charge without fear, archers slip arrows through gaps, fire bombs explode, and new weapons—like Phorenice’s fire-tubes—send darts that strike true once in a thousand tries. Even the strongest defenses bleed.

The slaughter had been terrible. As attackers surged up the narrow stairway, the Priests at the last remaining gate hurled massive boulders down upon them. The path became a river of writhing bodies, crushed beneath stone. Below, Phorenice stood on a rocky spur, urging her troops forward with fanatical courage. One company after another marched into death, hurling themselves against the gate, only to be cut down by rocks from above.

They had no siege engines. Their attack was futile—as if a child hammered a wall with an orange. Yet they kept coming. Not one returned. And Phorenice wanted it that way. She intended to fill the passes with corpses, creating a bridge of dead men over which she could march into the final battle. She cared nothing for cost. Her goal was conquest, and Atlantis existed only to serve it.

Two gates had already fallen in this bloody fashion, their defenders slain or shot down. That was when I descended from above to join the defense. No trumpet heralded my arrival. No banner proclaimed my name. But the Priests, by instinct, began shouting: “Deucalion!”—a cry born of habit, now rekindled by hope. Some shouted that with a king at their head, no ground would be lost.

The name carried across the battlefield. A group of advancing soldiers paused mid-charge. “Deucalion!” echoed through their ranks. I watched with grim satisfaction—proof that I had not been forgotten.

Shouts came from behind them, urging them forward. But they hesitated. Then a gleaming officer burst through, sword slashing left and right.

“It matters not what they cry,” he roared. “You obey the divine Empress. Be proud to die for her! Cowards! If you disobey, you’ll die slowly under the torturers’ tools. And Deucalion? He’s been dead these nine years.”

“There it seems you lie, Lord Tatho,” I called down.

He froze, looking up.

“So you’re alive?” he said. “Well, old friend, I’m sorry. But it’s too late for peace. You stand with these ragged priests, and the Empress has decreed they must be wiped out. I am her servant.”

“You used to fence well,” I said. “If you wish to end this madness, step forward as champion for your side. I’ll stand for mine. We’ll fight in fair combat, and our armies shall abide by the outcome.”

“It would be a great duel,” he admitted. “But I cannot. I am general here under Phorenice. She forbids me to risk myself. And though you are powerful, Deucalion—you are not chief. Not even one of the Three.”

“I am King.”

Tatho laughed. “Few would say so.”

“Few indeed. But those who do—hold power. I was crowned yesterday. And already, in the city, I struck the first blow. I was there. I saw how you reacted.”

“You were in Atlantis?”

“I came for Nais. She is here now. Tomorrow, she will be Queen. Tatho, as priest to priest, I remind you of the power held within the Ark of the Mysteries. Your teachings warned you of its might. If you continue this assault, you may lose—*or* unleash a catastrophe even the High Gods would tremble to command.”

“You can’t frighten us now,” Tatho replied stubbornly. “And magic? We have magic too. Phorenice has uncovered powers equal to any in the Ark.”

“But last night, she watched helplessly as her pyramid was destroyed. Zaemon foresaw it. And I stood closer to her than we stand now—I saw it happen.”

“She was caught off guard,” he admitted. “I don’t understand such things. I was never more than one of the Seven. Now, Phorenice keeps her secrets close. But time is needed—when one magic meets another.”

“I know little of it,” I said. “I leave such matters to those higher in the priesthood. Indeed, because I love Nais, I am barred from the highest mysteries. So I content myself with being a soldier. When my time comes, I’ll fall and kiss the Earth one last time. You, I’m told, seek longer life.”

He nodded. “Phorenice has found the Great Secret. I shall be the first to share it. We will be gods—immune to death. And our twin sons will live forever.”

“Phorenice is reckless,” I said. “No need to deny it. But the decree has been made. Her immortality will be taken from her—violently. Hear me: I, Deucalion, have been named King by the High Council of Priests—the voice of the High Gods. If I am denied my reign, Atlantis will cease to exist. You know me, Tatho. I never lie.”

He nodded.

“Then save yourself,” I urged. “You shall reclaim your vice-royalty in Yucatan.”

“No Yucatan,” he snapped. “A horde of hairy creatures—less than human, more than beasts—devoured our cities. Like driver ants. You’ve seen them in the forests? You light fires—they walk through, extinguish them with their bodies, then march on, devouring all. These creatures were like that—same numbers, same blind courage, same hunger. Today, Yucatan is gone.”

“Then take Egypt.”

He flared. “I’d reject seven Egypts and ten Yucatans. You think poorly of me, asking me to betray my Empress. Would you abandon Nais to save yourself?”

“That’s different.”

“In no way. I love her—more than you love Nais. She is my wife, mother of my children. I feel dread—not for myself, but for what you might do. But this talk ends here. These soldiers won’t understand our words—but they may catch fragments. I don’t want them dying knowing I hesitated.”

He saluted with his sword and stepped back. Missiles flew again. The doomed soldiers resumed their charge—scaling ladders useless, no chance to plant them. They could only pile their bodies into the pass, and that was their purpose. Our Priests pulled down boulders with crowbars and levers, sending them crashing down. Screams filled the air. The massacre continued.

But every now and then, a dart from below pierced our lines—an arrow, a sling-stone, a fire-tube missile. Each hit meant one fewer defender. Phorenice could afford losses—she had endless recruits across the land. But we could not. Every man we lost was irreplaceable.

I assessed the odds. If we kept defending gate by gate, they would breach each one in turn. By the time they reached the topmost gate, we would have no defenders left. So I abandoned the idea of leading from the front. Instead, I followed Phorenice’s own strategy: plan from safety. I gave orders to the guards, spoke words of encouragement, then withdrew up the narrow stair.

This passage to the Sacred Mountain had once been treacherous—a sheer cleft in the rock. But over time, bridges had been built, shelves carved, piers raised—making it safe and wide. Now, I wondered: what if we destroyed it?

The idea seemed desperate—but no better option presented itself. I knew every stone of the path, every turn, every ledge. Yet I walked it three times before deciding where to strike.

Once chosen, I moved quickly. Men had already fetched rams, levers, acids, hammers from the Priest stores. I gathered every able hand—old, weak, young—no matter. If Nais could walk, I’d have pressed her into labor. We tore down balustrades, statues, inscribed stones, worn cobblestones—sending them roaring into the abyss below, adding to the chaos of battle.

It was agony—destroying centuries of artistry. But better that it fall into ruin than be desecrated by Phorenice’s feet.

At first, I feared sacrificing the brave men holding the gate. It’s shameful to abandon comrades—but in war, generals sometimes must leave troops to die honorably, trusting the Gods to reward them. But as we neared completion, I saw a way to save them.

We didn’t just break arches. We drove rams and levers into the living rock beneath the entire structure. Fire was ready to heat the metal. With one signal, the whole path could collapse—beyond repair.

All was prepared. I gave the order to pause. A narrow ledge remained. I crossed it. The break was just below the highest gate. Beneath lay three more intact gates, plus the one under siege. The garrisons had already withdrawn. I passed through them all, unchallenged, arriving at the final rampart.

Twelve Priests—bare-chested—worked to hurl rocks. I joined them, blocking missiles with my shield, passing news. The attack grew fiercer. The pass below was packed with corpses. Our stones fell shorter distances—less deadly. They pressed forward with scaling ladders. Soon, they’d storm the gate.

In minutes, three more Priests fell to enemy fire. I ordered one final avalanche of rock. While survivors crawled from the wreckage, reinforcements scrambled up. I sent my nine remaining men running up the stair—and followed.

Gate by gate, we passed, locking and destroying the mechanisms behind us. Only when we reached the last did shouts erupt below—the attackers discovered the gate was empty.

One by one, we crossed the narrow ledge. A wounded Priest stumbled, lost balance, and plunged into the abyss. No one could reach him in time.

Then, we lit the fire. The rams expanded, tearing the last supports from the rock. The final link between the Sacred Clan and the rest of Atlantis snapped.

The fighting ceased—for now. No further defenses remained. I knew Phorenice was brilliant—but I doubted she could overcome this obstacle. I posted guards on the upper gate, then ordered the others to rest.

For myself, I sought Zaemon first—duty demanded it. But he was inside the Ark of the Mysteries, working with another of the Three. As only one of the Seven, I lacked rank to interrupt. So I turned instead to Nais.

She woke as I entered. Her eyes lit with joy. I knelt beside her bed, resting my face on the pillow near hers. She spoke softly—tender words, sweet confessions. Gods—how much I had missed by not knowing her sooner! Yet she had her own spirit, her quirks, making her even more precious.

She teased me about my clean chin, my new robe. She feigned awe at my kingship, saying she’d never dared love me had she known my future. But when I mentioned Phorenice, her tone changed. She reached out, touched my face, drew it to her lips.

“It’s weak of me to feel jealous,” she murmured. “I know you belong to me completely. But it hurts to think I won’t be the first to lie in your arms, Deucalion.”

“Then throw that pain aside,” I whispered. “I was married to Phorenice in name only—how long, I don’t recall. But never in truth. She performed a private ceremony, ignoring all rites. Whether it held weight… we don’t need to ask. She annulled it herself. Tatho is her husband now, father to her children. He loves her deeply—though it does him credit.”

We spoke of other things—small, intimate things lovers share. Things too personal to write. Private. Sacred. Beyond the historian’s scope.

But Phorenice proved far more cunning than I’d believed. Her forces stormed the gates we left open, swarming up to the broken path. In their rush, some were pushed over the edge—screaming into the abyss. But the position was too dangerous. They withdrew, leaving only a few sentries hidden in rock angles.

It seems Phorenice herself reconnoitered the site—quickly, cautiously—gathering full knowledge without risk. Then, with astonishing speed, she ordered a machine built. Though she had vast resources, the pace was incredible.

There was no noise. Skilled craftsmen assembled wood and metal in the plain below. Each beam, crosspiece, plank was forged and fitted before being dragged up through the conquered gates—ready for immediate assembly.

Soon, the machine would be complete. And the Sacred Mountain would face a new kind of storm.

A trembling old priest approached me, placing a hand on my shoulder. I turned sharply—no time for interruptions. “Well?” I snapped.

“News has reached the Three,” he said, voice low but steady. “Of what is coming.”

I met his gaze with fire in mine. “Then they know I stand here now, ready to fight the greatest battle of my life. When it ends, I shall join the Gods, standing behind the stars to greet them when they too come. I regret only that they are too old and frail to die with honor—to witness such a fight themselves.”

He smiled faintly, a ghost of the warrior he once was. “They have given me orders, my King—ones I fear you will not like. You must not die in this battle. They still have need of you.”

I turned on him, anger flaring. “I will not obey such a command. It cannot be true. You’ve misheard. If I am man, priest, soldier, king—then my duty is clear: no enemy passes this gate while I live. Tell them their message can rot in the dust.”

The old man’s smile deepened. “I told them you would refuse. So I asked for a sign. And they gave one. This jewel—I was to give it to you.”

I stared at the gem in his hand—a bracelet once worn by Nais. “How did they get this?”

“They took it from her,” he said quietly. “And they told me she waits inside the Ark of the Mysteries. The Three’s purpose for you concerns her directly.”

Even then, I struggled. My pride screamed to stay and fight. But love for Nais won. I bowed my head, offering the formal salute—the mark of obedience.

To the remaining priests guarding the pass, I said farewell. “You will have what I shall miss,” I told them. “I envy you this final stand. Yet though I am King of Atlantis, I am also one of the Seven—and thus bound to serve the Three. Their word is the will of the High Gods. You will reach the stars before I do. Please… speak for me when you meet Them. It is not my choice to fall behind.”

They saluted me with their weapons, solemn and respectful. Then we parted.

I did not see the battle—but I knew it was fierce. Time stretched before Phorenice’s horde broke through onto the mountain crest. The last defenders fought hard—old men, yes, but trained warriors. They bought time with their lives, trading breath for blood.

With a heavy heart, I climbed toward the summit, winding past temples, colleges, and homes clinging to the slopes of the Sacred Mountain. I passed beyond the ancient boundary—once forbidden ground. But now, conventions meant nothing. My temper burned hot. I might have stormed forward without hesitation, had I not been stopped.

At the temple marking the edge, old Zaemon waited. With grave ritual, he sought divine permission for my trespass. He refused to move until the ceremony was complete.

Below, Phorenice’s tower and bridge were in place. The clash of arms echoed—the last few priests held the gate, fighting like lions against an avalanche. They were seasoned fighters, but outnumbered and outmatched. Soon, the mountain would be breached.

The sea surged violently. Waves crashed against distant cliffs. Fires erupted from the mountains. And the water—unrelenting—rose up the great estuary, flooding the land, swallowing harbors, creeping into the city itself.

Zaemon gestured, his voice thin but sharp. “Look!”

I followed his finger. A chill ran through me. The sun blazed overhead. The sky was blue. But beneath us, chaos reigned. The sea roared, never retreating. The lower lands vanished under churning waters. The city of Atlantis—gone.

“You see?” Zaemon asked.

“I see.”

“You understand?”

“In part.”

“Then let me tell you all. This is the beginning. The end follows fast. The High Gods, who dwell beyond the stars, have exhausted Their patience. Atlantis, the continent beyond, all its people—doomed. Annihilation is upon us.”

He spoke of prophecy—an ancient decree. Long ago, the Gods foresaw this doom. To preserve knowledge, the Ark of the Mysteries was built. Impenetrable. Immovable. Inside: scrolls of wisdom, grain, water, seeds, weapons—all for two survivors.

One woman already waits within. She is my daughter. But the choice of the man rests with you.”

I froze. “I don’t wish to survive.”

“You are not asked,” Zaemon said. “You are commanded. The High Gods know you as Their servant. You will obey.”

Habit overruled doubt. I bowed, repeating the sacred words. “I hear. I obey.”

Then came the rite—the raising to the rank of the Three. No temple. No incense. Only sky and silence. But the elders performed every step with reverence, unshaken by the world collapsing around them.

Mountains exploded. Fires roared. Phorenice’s army broke through the gate, streaming across the mountain in frenzy. Yet Zaemon and his companion continued—words precise, movements deliberate.

I stood bareheaded, sweat on my brow, mind torn between ritual and reality. A continent drowned. My father died in silence. Nais waited. And I—was becoming something greater than a king.

But time was running out.

Finally, Zaemon said, “Kneel. Bow to your mother, the Earth. Swear by the High Gods you will never misuse the power now granted.”

I knelt. I swore.

Then he raised me, fully initiated. “You will not train with us,” he said. “We go to meet the Gods. Our account is due. What you remember may save you. We bid you farewell. We will commend you to the divine court.”

He pointed to the Ark. “Enter. Shut the door if they try to break in. You and Nais are the only ones chosen to live. Do not fight. Not even in defense. But look upon Atlantis as she dies. Bear witness.”

I climbed the battens, entered the Ark, descended the inner stair.

Nais sat waiting. Her eyes shone with fear and hope.

“Zaemon forced me here,” she whispered. “Threats. Promises. He said you would come. He’s harsh—but I think he loves us both.”

“Can we save him?” I asked.

“He will not come. The Ark was built for one man and one woman. That is law. He has a duty—to deliver our story to the Gods. And he’ll not waste a moment.”

I left her, stepped back onto the roof.

Men ran wild on the mountain top. The sea swallowed everything. Cities, plains, forests—vanished. Only peaks remained, erupting in fury. Trees twisted in whirlpools. Mud bubbled. Life ended.

Then—movement.

A small group emerged from the ruins. Phorenice, carried in a golden litter, surrounded by guards. Her slaves bore her aloft. Tatho marched beside her, proud and gaudy. Ylga walked close—calm, composed.

They halted twenty paces from the Ark.

“Do not approach those foul old men,” Phorenice said. “They reek of death. We lack perfume to mask it.”

She looked up at me. “Truly, a quiet gathering of old friends. Deucalion—how odd to see you again. Once I tasted you… and discarded you when you grew stale.”

“I have Nais,” I said. “Soon, we will be the last alive.”

“Nais is welcome,” she laughed. “I’ll watch your country cooings from the stars. You’re so rustic, Deucalion. They say the old men crowned you king. Did it swell your ego? Or did you remember there’s still an Empress—so long as Atlantis exists?”

“Save your jests,” I said. “I won’t answer a woman who will soon die.”

“Bah! You’ll live in the past. Don’t they know I hold the Great Secret? I am a Goddess now. I can make life eternal.”

“The sea will test that,” I replied.

But before more could be said, Zaemon stepped forward, holding the Symbol of the Sun.

He cursed Phorenice—his voice raw with wrath. The roar of the flood rose, but he spoke clearly.

Phorenice ordered her guards to kill him. Even tried to rise and strike herself.

But Zaemon held the Symbol high. Its light flared. The power of the Gods overwhelmed her magic.

She fell back, humbled. Defeated.

Only Ylga moved. She stepped forward, leaned against the Ark, and called to me through the storm.

“I wish I could save you,” I said.

She smiled. “I wouldn’t come, even if you begged. I demand all or nothing. But I’ll face death knowing you’ll carry me in your memory. I know men, Deucalion—even men like you. You’ll never forget how close you came to loving me.”

I think she said more—but the waves drowned her voice.

A thick mist rose from the burning mountains. Flames lit the clouds like gold.

I saw soldiers rush up the slope—only to be swept back by surging water.

Then—Zaemon waved the Symbol. A silent command.

I obeyed. I went down the stairs. I shut the door.

Bolts locked. Silence.

Nais and I—alone.

Outside, blows rang against the Ark. Men climbed the sides, wrenched at the door.

The end drew near.

Then—surge. The Ark lifted, trembled, dropped.

Blows ceased below. But above—frenzied pounding.

Screams echoed—dull, muffled.

Another surge—thunderous—lifted the Ark like a leaf. Spun it. Then—no land. No rock.

We floated on endless, furious seas.

The lamp fell. Darkness swallowed us.

And in that dark, I clung to Nais—knowing we were the only survivors of a lost world.

No more Atlantis. Only the ocean. The sky. And the weight of what we had seen.

The Ark drifted without helm, without oar, without engine—guided only by the will of the High Gods. It moved through the endless sea as if carried by unseen hands, its course determined not by man but by fate itself. Inside, darkness reigned, thick with the scent of age and decay, broken only by groans and the creaking of timbers under the weight of the storm. I searched in vain for the fire-box to relight the lamp that had fallen, so we were left to wander in silence, blind to what lay ahead.

The waves rose and fell like giants playing a cruel game. I clung to Nais beside the narrow bed, holding her fast as the vessel heaved beneath us. We spoke little, but in our shared sorrow and quiet endurance, there was a bond deeper than words. When Atlantis sank into the depths, it did not vanish quietly. Its final breaths stirred the ocean—great whirlpools erupted from the drowned fires, spewing forth smoke and fury as a last offering to the Sea Gods. Then came the wind—a hurricane of such power that it swept across the face of the world, leaving nothing untouched, no ruin unshattered. For seven days and seven nights, the tempest raged, measured only by the hour-reckoner hanging inside the Ark. At last, the wind ceased, and only a slow, oily swell remained, rolling gently beneath us.

It was then I dared to climb the stairs and open the hatch above. A rush of fresh air flooded down, clearing the stench of damp and death. The Ark rode high on the calm waters, and I descended to bring Nais up to meet the sun. Together, we stood at the edge, bathed in his golden rays, and offered our thanks to the Lord of Light for sparing us another day. Then we lay down where we stood, weary beyond measure, seeking rest in the warmth of the sky.

Yet sleep would not come. I stared out over the vast expanse of water, searching for any sign of land. But there was none. The horizon stretched endlessly, unbroken by island or shore. Above, the heavens remained still and clear. The deep was scattered with remnants of Atlantis—trees torn from their roots, beams split and floating, a shattered boat of hides, and occasionally, the bloated forms of men and beasts riding the swells beside us. These fragments were proof enough: what had happened was real, not a nightmare.

Eventually, exhaustion claimed me. I slipped into unconsciousness, clutching Nais’s hand. When I awoke, she was already awake, watching me with gentle eyes. We were both rested now, renewed in spirit. Rest brings peace, and peace brings acceptance. We no longer resisted our fate. With quiet resolve, we returned below to eat and drink, to strengthen ourselves for whatever lay ahead.

Now, at last, I could see the Ark clearly. Though it had never before touched water, it showed no flaw—no leak, no crack. Its floor was dry, its structure sound. The wood bore grain, yet nowhere was there a seam or joint. The timbers had been shaped and grown together by a craft long lost, fused into one living whole. Then some ancient treatment had hardened it, making it as strong as iron and impervious to time and tide.

Inside, the Ark held treasures of survival. At one end, great tanks stored grain; at the other, fresh water. Elsewhere, jars held medicines, essences of animals, samples of life preserved for future use. On every wall, carved in rough but vivid relief, were images of the creatures that once roamed Atlantis—some familiar, others strange, long vanished with the people who once knew them. And there were weapons, tools, and instruments of craft—everything needed for a new beginning.

For weeks, we drifted across the ocean, ruled entirely by the currents and the gods. We had no control over direction, no way to speed or steer. The High Gods had chosen us—the last two survivors—and they alone dictated our path. We accepted this with humility, trusting in Their wisdom.

Of the land we eventually reached, where we settled and raised children, I shall speak in due time. But first, I must tell how these writings came to be.

In the center of the Ark, beneath a heavy coffer, lay scrolls containing all the Mysteries—knowledge gathered over centuries. I read them, and my soul trembled. These were powers beyond imagining—forces that had once shaped Atlantis, and brought about her fall. Among them was the Secret of Life and Death, the same truth Phorenice had discovered and for which she died. Others defied description, too terrible to record.

I could not bear the burden of such knowledge. To keep it felt like a sin. So, one night as the Ark rolled beneath the stars, I took hot irons and burned the wax-coated sheets, erasing every word. If I sinned, the High Gods would judge me. If such secrets must endure, They would reveal them again to worthy hands. But I destroyed them—there, upon the waves.

Later, when we reached dry ground, I rewrote these pages—not the forbidden truths, but the story of how Atlantis fell, and how we survived.
