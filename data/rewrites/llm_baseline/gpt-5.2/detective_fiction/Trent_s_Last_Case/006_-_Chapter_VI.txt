“Calvin C. Bunner, at your service,” the newcomer corrected, with a punctilious little dip of the head, taking an unlit cigar from between his teeth as though it were part of his signature. Trent’s quick, unceremonious greeting had plainly knocked him half a step out of his practiced American ease; he recovered it at once, and his large dark eyes—bright with the attentive hunger of a man who makes a meal of facts—went from Trent to the captain.

“You are Mr. Trent, I expect. Mrs. Manderson was telling me a while ago. Captain—good-morning.”

Mr. Murch returned the foreign salutation with an economical nod.

“I was coming up to my room,” Mr. Bunner went on, “and I heard a strange voice in here, so I thought I would take a look in.” He laughed with an easy dryness. “You thought I might have been eavesdropping, perhaps. No, sir; I heard a word or two about a pistol—this one, I guess—and that’s all.”

He was a thin, rather short young man with a pale, shaven, almost girlish face—bones showing cleanly under the skin like the fine framework of a well-made tool. His hair waved darkly and was parted in the middle; without the cigar his lips stood half open in a look of perpetual readiness, as if he were forever tasting the air for the next hint of news. When the cigar returned to his mouth that expression vanished, and he wore instead the cool, seasoned mask of the sagacious Yankee.

Trent and the American took each other’s measure without hurry. Both looked satisfied.

“I was having it explained to me,” Trent said pleasantly, “that my discovery of a pistol that might have shot Mr. Manderson does not amount to very much. I am told it is a favourite weapon among your people, and has become quite popular over here.”

Mr. Bunner’s bony hand went out and lifted the pistol from its case. He handled it with the familiarity of a man who knows the weight of a thing before it settles in his palm, and who could tell you—if he cared—the exact shape of danger by touch alone.

“Yes, sir,” he said. “The captain is right. This is what we call at home a Little Arthur, and I dare say there are duplicates of it in a hundred thousand hip-pockets this minute. I consider it too light in the hand myself.” As he spoke his other hand went, by habit, under the tail of his jacket; he brought out an uglier-looking weapon and offered it butt-first to Trent. “Feel of that, now, Mr. Trent—it’s loaded, by the way.”

The metal was cold as a cellar stone; Trent felt, too, the quiet assurance with which Bunner held it, as if the thing belonged to him the way a knife belongs to a cook.

“Now this Little Arthur,” Bunner resumed, taking back his own and setting it away again, “Marlowe bought it just before we came over this year to please the old man. Manderson said it was ridiculous for a man to be without a pistol in the twentieth century. So he went out and bought what they offered him, I guess—never consulted me. Not but what it’s a good gun,” he conceded, squinting along the sights with a professional narrowing of the eye, “Marlowe was poor with it at first, but I’ve coached him some in the last month or so, and he’s practised until he is pretty good. But he never could get the habit of carrying it around. Why, it’s as natural to me as wearing my pants. I have carried one for some years now, because there was always likely to be somebody laying for Manderson. And now”—his voice dropped, and the cigar’s smoke came out slower—“they got him when I wasn’t around.”

He straightened, brisk again. “Well, gentlemen, you must excuse me. I am going into Bishopsbridge. There is a lot to do these days, and I have to send off a bunch of cables big enough to choke a cow.”

“I must be off too,” Trent said. “I have an appointment at the Three Tuns.”

“Let me give you a lift in the automobile,” Bunner offered cordially. “I go right by that joint. Say, cap., are you coming my way too?”

Mr. Murch, with the patient immovability of a man who prefers his own feet and his own pace, declined.

“Then come along, Mr. Trent,” Bunner said, already turning, “and help me get out the car. The chauffeur is out of action, and we have to do ’most everything ourselves except clean the dirt off her.”

Still talking in his measured drawl, Bunner led Trent downstairs, through the house, and out to the garage that stood a little away behind the rear walls, like a shed built for a noisier god than the quiet domesticity within. The place was cool after the blaze of the midday sun. A swallow cut the air outside with a thin, bright cry; inside, the noises were softened—boots on packed earth, the faint click of metal, the breathing of a machine at rest. Trent, who had been living these last hours among voices that lied, withheld, and broke off, found the change in acoustics almost a relief: here was a hush that meant shelter, not secrecy.

Mr. Bunner, however, seemed in no hurry to wake the car. He offered Trent a cigar, which Trent accepted; then, for the first time, Bunner lit his own. The match flared and died; the first draw brought a thick, brown sweetness into the air that mixed with the smell of oil and rubber—an odd, domestic sort of perfume, like a kitchen where the roast and the lamp have both been too long attended.

He sat down on the footboard, clasped his thin hands between his knees, and looked at Trent with a steadiness that was almost intimate.

“See here, Mr. Trent,” he said after a moment. “There are some things I can tell you that may be useful. I know your record. You are a smart man, and I like dealing with smart men. I don’t know if I have that detective sized up right, but he strikes me as a mutt. I would answer any questions he had the gumption to ask me—I have done so, in fact—but I don’t feel encouraged to give him any notions of mine without his asking. See?”

Trent nodded. “That is a feeling many people have in the presence of our police,” he said. “It’s the official manner, I suppose. But let me tell you, Murch is anything but what you think. He is one of the shrewdest officers in Europe. He is not very quick with his mind, but he is very sure. And his experience is immense. My forte is imagination, but I assure you in police work experience outweighs it by a great deal.”

“Outweigh nothing!” Bunner snapped, with a sudden hard certainty, like a knife striking a board. “This is no ordinary case, Mr. Trent. I will tell you one reason why. I believe the old man knew there was something coming to him. Another thing: I believe it was something he thought he couldn’t dodge.”

Trent pulled an empty crate nearer and sat opposite him on it. The wood creaked; somewhere beyond the garage a cart wheel passed on the road with a homely grind. “This sounds like business,” he said. “Tell me your ideas.”

“I say what I do because of the change in the old man’s manner this last few weeks. I dare say you have heard that he was a man who always kept himself well in hand. That was so. I have always considered him the coolest and hardest head in business. That man’s calm was just deadly—I never saw anything to beat it.”

Bunner’s eyes sharpened as though he were reading figures that refused to add up.

“And I knew Manderson as nobody else did. I was with him in the work he really lived for. I guess I knew him a heap better than his wife did, poor woman. I knew him better than Marlowe could—he never saw Manderson in his office when there was a big thing on. I knew him better than any of his friends.”

“Had he any friends?” Trent put in.

Bunner’s glance flicked to him, quick as the click of a latch. “Somebody has been putting you next, I see that,” he said. “No: properly speaking, I should say not. He had many acquaintances among the big men—people he saw most every day; they would even go yachting or hunting together. But I don’t believe there ever was a man that Manderson opened a corner of his heart to.”

He drew on the cigar; the ash held long, disciplined. “But what I was going to say was this. Some months ago the old man began to get like I never knew him before—gloomy and sullen, just as if he was everlastingly brooding over something bad, something that he couldn’t fix. This went on without any break; it was the same down town as it was up home—he acted just as if there was something lying heavy on his mind.”

Bunner leaned forward. The garage, in its coolness, seemed to listen; even the distant birds outside sounded suddenly louder, as if the world were pressing its ear to the crack under the door.

“But it wasn’t until a few weeks back that his self-restraint began to go; and let me tell you this, Mr. Trent”—he reached out and laid his bony hand on Trent’s knee with a proprietary insistence, as if pinning a paper to a desk—“I’m the only man that knows it. With every one else he would be just morose and dull; but when he was alone with me in his office, or anywhere we would be working together, if the least little thing went wrong, by George! he would fly off the handle to beat the Dutch.

“In this library here I have seen him open a letter with something that didn’t just suit him in it, and he would rip around and carry on like an Indian—saying he wished he had the man that wrote it here, he wouldn’t do a thing to him, and so on—till it was just pitiful. I never saw such a change.

“And here’s another thing. For a week before he died Manderson neglected his work, for the first time in my experience. He wouldn’t answer a letter or a cable, though things looked like going all to pieces over there. I supposed that this anxiety of his, whatever it was, had got on to his nerves till they were worn out. Once I advised him to see a doctor, and he told me to go to hell.”

Bunner gave a small, grim shrug, as if that phrase still stung.

“But nobody saw this side of him but me. If he was having one of these rages in the library here, for example, and Mrs. Manderson would come into the room, he would be all calm and cold again in an instant.”

“And you put this down to some secret anxiety,” Trent asked, “a fear that somebody had designs on his life?”

Bunner nodded.

Trent drew smoke slowly and let it out with care, as if measuring his own words against it. “I suppose you had considered the idea of there being something wrong with his mind—a break-down from overstrain, say. That is the first thought that your account suggests to me. Besides, it is what is always happening to your big business men in America, isn’t it? That is the impression one gets from the newspapers.”

“Don’t let them slip you any of that bunk,” Bunner said, with a sudden heat that made the cigar’s ember flare. “It’s only the ones who have got rich too quick, and can’t make good, who go crazy. Think of all our really big men—the men anywhere near Manderson’s size: did you ever hear of any one of them losing his senses? They don’t do it—believe me.”

He paused, then added reflectively, as if reaching for the right seasoning to make his point stick. “I know they say every man has his loco point, but that doesn’t mean genuine, sure-enough craziness; it just means some personal eccentricity in a man... like hating cats... or my own weakness of not being able to touch any kind of fish-food.”

Trent’s mind flashed, for an instant, to kitchens and markets: the way one man cannot bear the sea-smell clinging to a stall, another cannot stand the squeal of a pig being bled, another cannot abide the new, loud grinding of a factory mill that turns flour into something too white, too dead. People called it fussiness, and forgot it was memory, lodged in the senses.

“Well, what was Manderson’s?” he asked.

“He was full of them—the old man. There was his objection to all the unnecessary fuss and luxury that wealthy people don’t kick at much, as a general rule. He didn’t have any use for expensive trifles and ornaments. He wouldn’t have anybody do little things for him; he hated to have servants tag around after him unless he wanted them.

“And although Manderson was as careful about his clothes as any man I ever knew, and his shoes—well, sir, the amount of money he spent on shoes was sinful—in spite of that, I tell you, he never had a valet. He never liked to have anybody touch him. All his life nobody ever shaved him.”

“I’ve heard something of that,” Trent said. “Why was it, do you think?”

Bunner’s thin face tightened as he searched for the exact turn of thought. “Well,” he said slowly, “it was the Manderson habit of mind, I guess; a sort of temper of general suspicion and jealousy.

“They say his father and grandfather were just the same.... Like a dog with a bone, you know—acting as if all the rest of creation was laying for a chance to steal it. He didn’t really think the barber would start in to saw his head off; he just felt there was a possibility that he might, and he was taking no risks.

“Then again in business he was always convinced that somebody else was after his bone—which was true enough a good deal of the time, but not all the time. The consequence of that was that the old man was the most cautious and secret worker in the world of finance; and that had a lot to do with his success, too.”

Bunner lifted his shoulders slightly, as if to shake off an accusation. “But that doesn’t amount to being a lunatic, Mr. Trent—not by a long way. You ask me if Manderson was losing his mind before he died. I say I believe he was just worn out with worrying over something, and was losing his nerve.”

Trent sat very still. Outside, beyond the garage, the country made its usual small noises: leaves shifting, a bird calling, the subdued speech of the earth. They had a steadiness to them that human words never kept. He thought of how a man’s inner life might be heard not in what he said but in what he could no longer bear to hear—the sudden loudness of a servant’s footstep, the scrape of a chair, the whirr of a new machine brought into a household like a stranger.

He decided to put out a feeler. “I understood that he had trouble with his wife.”

“Sure,” Bunner said at once. “But do you suppose a thing like that was going to upset Sig Manderson that way? No, sir! He was a sight too big a man to be all broken up by any worry of that kind.”

Trent looked into his eyes and saw, behind the shrewdness, something massive and childlike: an innocence that believed the great engines of business could grind through anything as small and intimate as a marriage—believed, too, that a man might be enormous in the world and still untouched by the quiet revolutions of the home.

“What was the trouble between them, anyhow?” Trent asked.

“You can search me,” Bunner answered briefly. He puffed, then added, lowering his voice and leaning forward as though the very syllables might be overheard and misused. “Marlowe and I have often talked about it, and we could never make out a solution. I had a notion at first that the old man was disappointed and vexed because he had expected a child; but Marlowe told me that the disappointment on that score was the other way around, likely as not. His idea was all right, I guess; he gathered it from something said by Mrs. Manderson’s French maid.”

“Célestine!” Trent said quickly; and in his mind something clicked into place—so that was what her half-hints had been circling.

Bunner misread his look. “Don’t you think I’m giving a man away, Mr. Trent,” he said. “Marlowe isn’t that kind. Célestine just took a fancy to him because he talks French like a native, and she would always be holding him up for a gossip. French servants are quite unlike English that way. And servant or no servant,” he added with emphasis, “I don’t see how a woman could mention such a subject to a man. But the French beat me.”

He shook his head slowly, as if at a riddle written in a dialect he could hear but not truly understand. Trent thought of languages that carried whole worlds inside a single intimate phrase—worlds that an outsider could translate word by word and still miss the taste of. In the wrong mouth, even truth became a kind of theft.

“But to come back to what you were telling me just now,” Trent said, steering them gently. “You believe that Manderson was going in terror of his life for some time. Who should threaten it? I am quite in the dark.”

“Terror—I don’t know,” Bunner replied, frowning. “Anxiety, if you like. Or suspense—that’s rather my idea of it. The old man was hard to terrify, anyway; and more than that, he wasn’t taking any precautions—he was actually avoiding them. It looked more like he was asking for a quick finish—supposing there’s any truth in my idea.

“Why, he would sit in that library window, nights, looking out into the dark, with his white shirt just a target for anybody’s gun.”

The picture came up in Trent’s mind with painful clarity: the quiet room, the ordinary household night-sounds—some distant crockery set down, the sigh of a settling house—threaded through by the possibility of a shot. A domestic routine with a mythic, black-winged shape perched on it.

“As for who should threaten his life—well, sir,” Bunner said, with a faint, grim smile, “it’s certain you have not lived in the States. To take the Pennsylvania coal hold-up alone, there were thirty thousand men, with women and children to keep, who would have jumped at the chance of drilling a hole through the man who fixed it so that they must starve or give in to his terms. Thirty thousand of the toughest aliens in the country, Mr. Trent.

“There’s a type of desperado you find in that kind of push who has been known to lay for a man for years, and kill him when he had forgotten what he did. They have been known to dynamite a man in Idaho who had done them dirt in New Jersey ten years before. Do you suppose the Atlantic is going to stop them?”

He spat the words out softly, not as melodrama but as a ledger-entry of human spite. “It takes some sand, I tell you, to be a big business man in our country. No, sir: the old man knew—had always known—that there was a whole crowd of dangerous men scattered up and down the States who had it in for him. My belief is that he had somehow got to know that some of them were definitely after him at last.

“What licks me altogether is why he should have just laid himself open to them the way he did—why he never tried to dodge, but walked right down into the garden yesterday morning to be shot at.”

He stopped. For a little while they sat without speaking, their brows drawn, thin blue vapours rising and dissolving into the cool air of the garage. Outside, the countryside kept up its patient music. It struck Trent that even the calmest landscape had its own history of violence, but it carried it differently—from generation to generation, in habits, in silences, in what people no longer dared to say plainly.

At last Trent rose. “Your theory is quite fresh to me,” he said. “It’s perfectly rational, and it’s only a question of whether it fits all the facts. I mustn’t give away what I’m doing for my newspaper, Mr. Bunner, but I will say this: I have already satisfied myself that this was a premeditated crime, and an extraordinarily cunning one at that. I’m deeply obliged to you. We must talk it over again.”

He glanced at his watch. “I have been expected for some time by my friend. Shall we make a move?”

“Two o’clock,” Bunner said, consulting his own as he got up from the footboard. “Ten a.m. in little old New York.” He set his cigar in the corner of his mouth again, and the eager look disappeared behind the practiced coolness. “You don’t know Wall Street, Mr. Trent. Let’s you and I hope we never see anything nearer hell than what’s loose in the Street this minute.”
