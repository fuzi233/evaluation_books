"When some beloved voice that was to you
Both sound and sweetness, faileth suddenly,
And silence, against which you dare not cry,
Aches round you like a strong disease and new—
What hope? what help? what music will undo
That silence to your sense?"

The blow had struck with terrible force. Margaret sank into a state of utter collapse—not marked by tears or sobs, nor even by words. She lay upon the sofa, eyes closed, motionless, breathing faintly, speaking only when addressed, and then in whispers so soft they seemed scarcely human. Mr. Bell stood helpless beside her. He could not leave her; yet he dared not suggest she accompany him back to Oxford, for her exhaustion was too profound to endure such a journey—let alone the agony of facing the scene of her father’s death. He sat before the fire, lost in thought, his mind racing over what must be done.

Margaret remained still, almost breathless, as if suspended between life and oblivion. He refused dinner, though Dixon brought it up, weeping as she served him, eager to coax him to eat. The devilled chicken tasted like sawdust. He minced some meat for Margaret, seasoned it well, but when Dixon tried to feed her, a quiet shake of the head told all—she could not eat. Her body was too weak, too numb.

Mr. Bell sighed deeply. He rose stiffly from his chair, his limbs aching from travel, and followed Dixon out of the room.

"I can't leave her," he said. "I must write to Oxford, make sure preparations are made. They can work on while I'm gone. Can't Mrs. Lennox come? I'll send word—she must come. The girl needs a woman near her, someone to talk her into crying properly."

Dixon was already weeping—more than enough for two. After composing herself, she murmured that Mrs. Lennox was too close to childbirth to travel.

"Then Mrs. Shaw—she's returned to England, hasn’t she?"

"Yes, sir, she has—but I don’t think she’ll want to leave Mrs. Lennox now, at such a time," Dixon added, her tone tinged with disapproval. She resented the idea of a stranger stepping into her role as guardian of Margaret.

"Interesting time be——!" Mr. Bell broke off with a cough. "She could have been content in Venice or Naples, those Popish places, during her last 'interesting time'—which was in Corfu, I believe. And what does that little prosperous woman’s 'interesting time' matter compared to this poor creature—this helpless, homeless, friendless Margaret—lying there like a stone statue on an altar tomb? I tell you, Mrs. Shaw shall come. See that a room is ready for her by tomorrow night. I’ll see to it."

He wrote a letter, firm and urgent. When Mrs. Shaw read it, she wept, calling it so like her late husband’s voice when he faced a gout attack that she vowed to keep it always. Had he asked her gently, perhaps she wouldn’t have come—though her heart ached for Margaret. But the sharp command stirred her resolve. It broke through her natural inertia, and she allowed her maid to pack her things.

At the top of the stairs, Edith, wrapped in shawl and cap, tears streaming down her face, called after her mother:
"Don’t forget, mamma—Margaret must come and live with us. Sholto goes to Oxford on Wednesday. Send word by Mr. Bell when we’re to expect you. And if you need Sholto, he can go on from Oxford to Milton. Don’t forget, mamma—you’re bringing Margaret home!"

Edith reentered the drawing-room. Mr. Henry Lennox sat there, cutting open the pages of a new review. Without looking up, he said, “If you don’t like Sholto being away from you so long, Edith, I hope you’ll let me go down to Milton and offer what help I can.”

“Oh, thank you,” Edith replied. “But I’m sure Mr. Bell will do everything—and more help may not be needed. Still, one doesn’t expect much savoir-faire from a resident Fellow. Dear, darling Margaret—won’t it be lovely to have her here again? You were great allies once, weren’t you?”

“Were we?” he asked, indifferent, his eyes fixed on the page. “Well, perhaps not—I forget. I was so full of Sholto. But doesn’t it suit well that if my uncle died now, it should be just when we’ve returned and settled in the old house, ready to welcome Margaret? Poor thing—what a change it will be from Milton! I’ll have new chintz for her bedroom, make it bright and cheerful, lift her spirits.”

In the same spirit, Mrs. Shaw journeyed to Milton, often dreading the moment she would meet Margaret, wondering how she might break through the silence. More often, she planned how soon she could take Margaret away from “that horrid place” and restore her to the comfort of Harley Street.

“Oh dear!” she said to her maid. “Look at those chimneys! My poor sister Hale! I couldn’t have rested in Naples if I’d known what awaited me here. I must have come and fetched her and Margaret away.”

To herself, she admitted she’d always thought her brother-in-law weak—but never so weak as now, seeing what he’d traded the lovely Helstone home for.

Margaret remained unchanged—pale, still, silent, tearless. They told her her aunt was coming, but she showed no sign of surprise, joy, or sorrow. Mr. Bell, whose appetite had returned, tried again to tempt her with sweetbreads stewed with oysters. She shook her head, as quietly as before. He ate them all himself, finding solace in the food where she found none.

But Margaret heard first—the clatter of the cab arriving at the door. Her eyelids fluttered. Her lips trembled, colored slightly. Mr. Bell went down to greet Mrs. Shaw. When they came back, Margaret stood unsteadily, trying to hold herself upright. And when she saw her aunt, she stepped forward into the arms that waited, and finally, after weeks of silence, burst into passionate tears—her grief released at last upon her aunt’s shoulder.

All the quiet love, the years of tenderness, the echoes of her mother’s presence—everything that bound them together by blood and memory—flooded her heart. She wept freely, her numb soul thawing under the warmth of kinship.

Mr. Bell slipped out of the room and descended to the study. He lit a fire, took down books, and tried to distract himself. Each volume recalled his dead friend. The task was different from watching Margaret, but the thoughts remained the same.

He was glad when he heard Mr. Thornton’s voice at the door. Dixon, annoyed by the arrival of Mrs. Shaw’s maid—whose very presence reminded her of former grandeur, of Beresford blood, of “station”—was curt in her dismissal. She had been sharing stories with the maid about Sir John Beresford’s coach and six, reliving past glory, and felt superior to any inhabitant of Milton. So when Thornton arrived, she told him sharply he could not see anyone that night.

But Mr. Bell opened the study door and called out: “Thornton! Is that you? Come in for a minute—I want to speak to you.”

Thornton entered. Dixon retreated to the kitchen, regaining her dignity with a tale of Sir John’s grand procession.

“I don’t know what I wanted to say after all,” Mr. Bell began. “Only it’s dull sitting in a room where everything speaks of a dead friend. Yet Margaret and her aunt must have the drawing-room to themselves.”

“Is Mrs.—is her aunt come?” asked Thornton.

“Come? Yes—maid and all. One would think she might have come alone at such a time. Now I’ll have to find my way to the Clarendon.”

“You mustn’t go to the Clarendon,” said Mr. Bell. “We have five or six empty rooms at home.”

“Well aired?”

“I think you may trust my mother for that.”

“Then I’ll just run upstairs, wish that wan girl good-night, bow to her aunt, and go with you straight.”

Mr. Bell lingered upstairs longer than expected. Thornton grew impatient—he had business to attend to, and had barely spared time to visit Crampton. When they walked out, Bell said:

“I was held up by the women in the drawing-room. Mrs. Shaw wants Margaret to leave immediately—says she must return to London, see friends, say good-bye. And she worries Margaret about old claims, whether she remembers old friends. Then Margaret breaks down, cries out she’d be glad to leave a place where she’s suffered so much. I must return to Oxford tomorrow. I don’t know whose side to take.”

He paused, expecting a response. But Thornton said nothing. His mind echoed: *Where she had suffered so much.*

Alas—that was how these eighteen months in Milton would be remembered. To him, every day had been precious, even in its bitterness—worth more than all the sweetness of life elsewhere. Neither the loss of father nor mother could taint the memory of those hours when a two-mile walk brought him closer to her, each step rich with anticipation, each moment away from her filled with longing for her grace, her wit, her presence.

Yes—whatever had happened outside their connection, he could never call that time suffering. It had been a royal luxury, with all its stings and rebuffs, compared to the poverty of a future stripped bare, without hope or fear.

Mrs. Thornton and Fanny were in the dining-room. Fanny fluttered with excitement, holding up silks and satins, testing wedding dresses by candlelight. Her mother tried to sympathize, but couldn’t. Fashion wasn’t her domain. She wished Fanny had accepted her brother’s offer to hire a London dressmaker—avoiding endless debates and indecision.

Mr. Thornton, meanwhile, was pleased to support any man who could be captivated by Fanny’s modest charms, giving her ample funds for her finery—finery that, in her estimation, rivalled even her fiancé.

When Bell and Thornton entered, Fanny blushed and simpered, fluttering about her work. But Bell barely noticed. His thoughts were with the pale figure left behind—still, silent, head bent, hands folded, in a room so still it seemed the rush of air in his ears was the breath of spirits lingering near their beloved.

When Bell had first gone upstairs, Mrs. Shaw slept on the sofa. No sound disturbed the hush.

Mrs. Thornton greeted Bell formally, warmly—always most gracious when receiving her son’s friends. “How is Miss Hale?” she asked.

“About as broken down as she can be.”

“I’m certain it’s a great comfort to her to have such a friend as you.”

“I wish I were her only friend, madam. I daresay it sounds brutal—but I’ve been displaced, turned out of my post by a fine lady aunt. Cousins claim her in London as if she were a lapdog. And she’s too weak to care for herself.”

“She must indeed be weak,” Mrs. Thornton said, meaning more than she spoke.

“But where,” she continued, “have these relations been all this time? Miss Hale has seemed almost friendless, and certainly borne great anxiety.”

She didn’t wait for an answer, leaving to attend to household matters.

“They’ve lived abroad,” Bell replied. “They have claims—some justice. The aunt raised her. She and the cousin were like sisters. What troubles me is that I wanted to take her as my own child. I’m jealous of these people who don’t seem to value the privilege.”

“And Frederick?” Thornton asked abruptly.

“Frederick?” Bell looked surprised. “Don’t you know? He’s her brother—the one involved in the mutiny.”

“I never heard his name before. Where is he? Who is he?”

“Surely I told you when the family first came to Milton. He lives in Spain. He risks arrest if he sets foot in England. Poor fellow—he’ll grieve not to attend his father’s funeral. We’ll have to settle for Captain Lennox. I don’t know of another relation.”

“I hope I may be allowed to go?”

“Certainly—thankfully. You’re a good fellow, Thornton. Hale liked you. He spoke of you only the other day. He regretted seeing so little of you lately. I’m grateful you wish to honor him.”

“But Frederick—does he never come to England?”

“Never.”

“He wasn’t here when Mrs. Hale died?”

“No. Why, I was here then. I hadn’t seen Hale in years. And if you remember, I came—no, it was after that. But Frederick wasn’t here. What made you think he was?”

“I saw a young man walking with Miss Hale once,” Thornton said. “I think it was around that time.”

“Oh—that would be Captain Lennox’s brother. A lawyer. They corresponded regularly. Mr. Hale told me he thought he might come down.”

Bell wheeled around, squinting one eye shut, studying Thornton’s face closely. “Do you know,” he said, “I once thought you had a little tenderness for Margaret?”

No reply. No flicker of emotion.

“And so did poor Hale. Not at first—only after I planted the idea in his head.”

“I admired Miss Hale,” Thornton said, cornered. “Everyone must. She is a beautiful creature.”

“Is that all? You speak of her in such measured terms—just a ‘beautiful creature,’ something to catch the eye. I hoped you had nobleness enough to pay her homage from the heart. Even if she rejected you—still, to love her without return would have lifted you higher than any man who ever knew her love. ‘Beautiful creature’ indeed! Do you speak of her as you would a horse or a dog?”

Thornton’s eyes glowed like embers.

“Mr. Bell,” he said, “before you speak so, remember not all men are free to express what they feel as you are. Let us talk of something else.”

Though his heart leapt at every word, though he knew Bell’s words would forever bind him to the deepest parts of his soul, he refused to be forced into expression. He was not a mocking bird, singing louder because another praised what he loved.

So he turned to business—landlord and tenant matters.

“What is that pile of brick and mortar in the yard? Repairs needed?”

“No, none. Thank you.”

“Are you building for yourself?”

“I’m building a dining-room—for the men. The hands.”

“I thought you were hard to please, if this room wasn’t good enough for a bachelor.”

“I’ve met a strange sort of chap. I put one or two children he cares about to school. One day, passing his house, I stopped to settle a small payment. I saw a dreadful meal—a greasy cinder of meat. That started me thinking. Then, when provisions rose this winter, I realized how much could be saved—how much comfort gained—by buying wholesale and cooking in bulk.”

“I see. But you mentioned a man—Higgins?”

“Yes. I laid aside my plan, thinking it impractical, and fearing I’d interfere with my men’s independence. Then Higgins came to me, approved the scheme—so similar to mine I could have claimed it—and several of his coworkers backed it. I was irritated by his manner, thought of abandoning it. But it seemed childish to give up a wise plan just because I didn’t get credit.”

“So you took the steward’s role?”

“Yes. I buy in bulk, hire a cook.”

“And you taste the food?”

“At first, I confined myself to purchasing, obeying the men’s orders through the housekeeper. But once, when the beef was too large, the mutton too lean, I saw they valued my caution. One day, several men—including Higgins—invited me to join them. I went. It was the best meal I’d ever eaten. I told them how much I enjoyed it. From then on, whenever that dish appeared, they’d say, ‘Master, hot-pot today—will you come in?’”

“You must restrain their conversation. They can’t criticize masters when you’re there.”

“We’ve avoided vexed questions. But if old disputes arise, I’ll speak my mind next hot-pot day. You don’t know our Darkshire men—they have humor, a lively tongue. I’m beginning to know them well. They talk freely before me.”

“Nothing like eating to equalize men. Dying is nothing to it. The philosopher dies sententiously, the pharisee ostentatiously, the simple-hearted humbly, the fool blindly. But all eat alike—given good digestion. There’s theory for theory!”

“I hate theories.”

“Then accept this ten-pound note—toward your marketing. Give the men a feast.”

“Thank you—but I’d rather not. They pay rent for the oven and cooking space. They’ll pay more for the new room. I don’t want charity. Once donations start, people will talk, spoil the simplicity.”

“People will talk anyway.”

“My enemies may make a fuss—call it philanthropy. But you’re a friend. I expect your silence. It’s a new broom now—sweeps clean. But soon, I’ll meet stumbling blocks.”
