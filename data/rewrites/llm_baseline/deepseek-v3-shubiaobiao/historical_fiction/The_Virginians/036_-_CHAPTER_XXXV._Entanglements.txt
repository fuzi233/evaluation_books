Our good Colonel and his prudent wife had resolved to remove their dear Hetty from the reach of a certain charming young gentleman, believing that distance might cure what they suspected to be a youthful infatuation. But while absence often works wonders in cooling the ardor of men’s hearts, women—ah, women are not so easily swayed. Some will wander to the ends of the earth, yet carry their affections with them, unshaken by time or separation.

A woman’s devotion is a stubborn thing. You may scold her, neglect her, even wound her pride, and still she clings with quiet constancy. Indeed, I have observed that a touch of indifference, a dash of dignified neglect, may secure a lady’s admiration far more effectively than lavish attentions. Too much sweetness palls; the heart, like the palate, tires of perpetual sugar. A woman spoiled is a woman lost.

But let us not mistake jest for earnest! Dear ladies, I assure you, I speak in irony—though, as you are well aware, irony is often lost upon your sex, which prefers sincerity even in jest. So let me plainly declare: I hold women to be infinitely more faithful, more loving, than men.

And so, what good would it do to carry Hetty away, if her heart remained with Harry? She bade him farewell with a quiet smile, concealing the ache beneath. He, preoccupied with his own concerns, saw only her composure, never guessing the sorrow veiled behind her bright eyes. He rode off without a backward glance, supposing her happy to return to her music, her garden, her simple pleasures.

But when the Lambert carriage rolled away, Hetty’s cheerfulness vanished. That night, she lay awake, haunted by thunder—or perhaps by thoughts that would not quiet. By morning, the roses in her cheeks had faded to pallor, her eyes shadowed. *It was the heat*, she said. *The storm had kept her restless.*

They arrived home at last. There was the gate where Harry had fallen, the bed where he had lain—how distant those days seemed now! The child who had once laughed among her roses was gone; in her place stood a girl who had aged years in a single night.

Meanwhile, Maria Esmond watched the Lamberts depart with grim satisfaction. When Harry returned, flushed from wine and billiards, she marked his high spirits with narrowed eyes.

"Which of the Lambert girls has stolen your heart, Harry?" Madame Bernstein teased.

"Both, aunt—like sisters!" he laughed. "A finer family I’ve never met."

Maria’s lips tightened. The Baroness, ever the strategist, urged him to court favor at Kensington, to seek advancement through Lady Yarmouth.

"Why not introduce me yourself, aunt?" Harry asked.

A flicker of discomfort crossed the old lady’s rouged face. "I am not welcome at court," she admitted. "Kings prefer to forget certain faces—and mine is among them."

A silence fell. Maria’s gaze darted between them, sensing some unspoken tension.

Later, the chaplain, Mr. Sampson, drew Lady Maria aside. "I fear Mr. Warrington is entangled in some trouble," he murmured.

Maria stiffened. "What trouble?"

"A pocket-book—lost at the White Horse. Letters inside. He is deeply troubled by it."

*Letters.* The word struck Maria like a blow. Her face drained of color.

"You are ill, my lady?" Sampson asked, feigning concern.

Before she could answer, Madame Bernstein awoke with a start. "What has happened?" she demanded, her voice sharp.

Sampson bowed. "I merely mentioned Mr. Warrington’s lost letters, and her ladyship swooned."

The Baroness’s eyes flashed. "Letters? Ah, *letters*!" she hissed, as if the word itself were cursed.

Maria, reviving, faced her aunt’s livid stare.

"You know what this means," the old woman spat. "Seals broken, secrets spilled—the old Esmond curse!"

Maria lifted her chin. "I have nothing to hide."

"Liar!" the Baroness snapped. "You think I don’t know? You’ve set your trap for that boy—just as others have done before you!"

Maria’s voice turned icy. "I have his word. And his letters. That is enough."

"His word!" Madame Bernstein laughed bitterly. "What is a man’s word against time, against temptation? You would ruin him—for what? A title? A fortune?"

Maria smiled faintly. "For *him*. And I can wait."

The Baroness, trembling, sank into her chair. The game was far from over—and the stakes were higher than ever.
