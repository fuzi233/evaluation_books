II. Newland Archer found himself caught in a sudden, uncomfortable swirl of emotion as the opera unfolded. It was unsettling—almost absurd—that the box drawing all eyes from the men of New York’s elite should be the very one where his fiancée sat between her mother and aunt. For a moment, he couldn’t place the woman in the elegant Empire-style gown, nor understand why her presence stirred such quiet commotion among those who knew better. Then it clicked—a jolt of realization followed by a flash of irritation. No, surely not the Mingotts! They wouldn’t dare. But they had. There was no mistaking it: the woman was May Welland’s cousin, Ellen Olenska—the one always spoken of with hushed sympathy as “poor Ellen.”

Archer had heard she’d arrived unexpectedly from Europe just days before. He even recalled Miss Welland mentioning she’d visited her cousin, who was staying with old Mrs. Mingott. He admired family loyalty, and respected the Mingotts for standing by their few misfits with unwavering pride. He had no room in his heart for petty judgment; indeed, he approved that May would show kindness to her troubled relative in private. But this—this public appearance at the Opera, seated beside May herself, just weeks before their engagement was to be announced—was something else entirely. It felt reckless, almost scandalous. And yes, he thought much like old Sillerton Jackson: the Mingotts wouldn’t have done this. Or would they?

He knew full well that if any woman dared what society allowed, it was old Mrs. Manson Mingott—the matriarch whose name carried weight even beyond Fifth Avenue. She had risen from modest beginnings—Catherine Spicer of Staten Island, daughter of a man disgraced, without wealth or status enough to erase the stain—and yet married into the Mingott fortune, defied convention, and built a life of bold defiance. Her house, pale cream stone rising like a defiant monument near Central Park when brown sandstone ruled the day, stood as proof of her courage. She lived there, surrounded by pre-Revolutionary furniture and relics from Louis Napoleon’s Tuileries, as though nothing about her life were extraordinary—not the location, not the French windows that opened like doors, not even the fact that her daughters, married to an Italian marquis and an English banker, never returned.

People whispered about her. Some said she lacked beauty—something New York prized above all, a gift that could excuse many flaws. Others claimed, unkindly, that like her imperial namesake, she had risen through sheer willpower and steely resolve. Yet beneath her audacity was a quiet dignity. She had never been touched by scandal. When her husband died young, he left his fortune tightly bound—out of distrust for her family—but she forged ahead anyway. She mingled in foreign courts, entertained opera stars, dined with ambassadors, befriended Mme. Taglioni, and remained untouched by gossip. Sillerton Jackson once declared that the only thing she lacked was the charm of youth—yet she remained unshaken, a force of nature in a world that valued discretion.

Though she had long since freed her fortune, she remained thrifty, refusing to spend extravagantly on food. Her table, simple and often bland, drew criticism from relatives who saw it as a blot on the Mingott name—once synonymous with fine living. But still, people came. Even Lovell Mingott, trying to restore family honor by hiring the city’s best chef, was met with her cheerful retort: “What’s the use of two good cooks in one family, now that I’ve married the girls and can’t eat sauces?”

As Archer reflected on these things, he glanced again toward the Mingott box. He saw Mrs. Welland and her sister-in-law facing the crowd with the calm assurance the Mingotts were known for—unshaken, composed. Only May showed a hint of color, perhaps because she sensed his gaze. And then there was Ellen Olenska—seated gracefully, her eyes fixed on the stage, her posture relaxed, her dress revealing more shoulder and bosom than New York deemed proper for a woman who wished to remain unseen.

To Archer, nothing was more sacred than *Taste*—that invisible code governing every gesture, every word, every choice. Ellen Olenska’s pale face, serious and thoughtful, seemed fitting for her situation. But her dress—without a tucker, sloping freely from her delicate shoulders—felt like a breach of decorum. He couldn’t help but worry about May being exposed to someone so careless of social rules. What kind of influence could she have?

Behind him, voices rose during the Mephistopheles-and-Martha scenes.

“After all,” one young man began, “just *what* happened?”

“Well—she left him. Nobody denies that.”

“He’s an awful brute, isn’t he?” asked Thorley, earnest and eager to defend her.

“The worst,” replied Lefferts. “I knew him in Nice. Half-paralyzed, sneering white fellow—handsome head, but eyes with too many lashes. A collector of china. Paid any price for both women and porcelain.”

A ripple of laughter passed through the audience.

“And then?” Thorley pressed.

“She ran off with his secretary.”

“Oh,” Thorley said, deflated.

“It didn’t last,” Lefferts added. “She was alone in Venice months later. Lovell went to fetch her. Said she was broken-hearted. That’s fine—but bringing her here, to the Opera, with May? That’s another matter.”

“Maybe,” Thorley ventured, “she’s too unhappy to stay home.”

The remark earned a mocking laugh, and Thorley blushed, pretending he meant it as a clever double entendre.

“Still,” someone murmured, glancing toward Archer, “it’s odd to bring Miss Welland at all.”

“Oh, that’s part of the plan,” Lefferts chuckled. “Granny’s orders. When she does something, she does it thoroughly.”

The act ended. The box stirred. And suddenly, Archer felt a surge of purpose. The urge to be the first to enter the Mingott box—to announce his engagement to May, to stand by her through whatever storms might come—overpowered every hesitation. He moved swiftly down the red corridors, arriving just as the curtain fell.

His eyes met May’s. In that instant, they understood each other—no words needed. Their world lived in whispers, in subtle glances, in unspoken meanings. And this silent recognition brought them closer than any declaration ever could. Her eyes said: *You see why Mamma brought me.* His reply: *I would never have let you stay away.*

Mrs. Welland turned to him. “You know my niece, Countess Olenska?”

Archer bowed, as custom dictated, not extending his hand. Ellen Olenska inclined her head slightly, her pale-gloved hands clasped over a fan of eagle feathers. After greeting Mrs. Lovell Mingott—a large blonde woman in creaking satin—Archer sat beside May.

“I hope you’ve told Madame Olenska we’re engaged?” he murmured. “I want everyone to know. I want to announce it tonight at the ball.”

May’s cheeks flushed like dawn. Her eyes sparkled. “If you can convince Mamma,” she said softly. “But why change what’s already settled?”

He didn’t answer. His gaze held hers, warm and sure. Then, smiling more confidently, she added, “Tell her yourself—I give you permission. She says you used to play together when you were children.”

She pulled her chair back, and with deliberate, almost theatrical grace, Archer took the seat beside the Countess.

“We *did* used to play,” she said, turning her grave eyes to his. “You were a horrid boy. You kissed me once behind a door. But it was your cousin Vandie Newland—who never looked at me—that I was in love with.”

Her gaze swept the curved line of boxes. “Ah, how this brings it all back. I see everyone here in knickerbockers and pantalettes.”

Her voice trailed with a faint foreign accent, soft and nostalgic. She smiled gently at him.

And yet, despite the warmth in her tone, Archer felt a pang of discomfort. To speak so lightly of childhood memories, so casually, in front of this assembly—this court of moral judgment—seemed terribly out of place. Misplaced flippancy was worse than anything. He answered stiffly: “Yes. You’ve been away a very long time.”

“Oh, centuries,” she said, laughing softly. “So long that I’m sure I’m dead and buried, and this dear old place is heaven.”

And somehow, though he couldn’t explain why, that struck him as the most disrespectful thing imaginable.
