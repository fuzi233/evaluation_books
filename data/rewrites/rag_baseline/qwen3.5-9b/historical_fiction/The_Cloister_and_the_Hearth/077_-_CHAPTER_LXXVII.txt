The evening meal at Tergou was interrupted not by the clatter of cutlery, but by the violent intrusion of Luke Peterson, a man battered by the elements and coated in the dust of a hurried journey. Bursting into the room, he announced with urgent breathlessness that Mistress Catherine was required immediately in Rotterdam. When he stumbled over her name, correcting himself from "Catherine" to "Kate" and finally settling on "Margaret," the household erupted in a cacophony of disbelief. Yet, Luke stood firm, recounting the divine command he had received: a plea from the heavens itself, delivered through the voice of Eli the hosier's wife, demanding the presence of the mother for the sake of God's love. He had not waited for dawn; he had ridden straight into the night.

The revelation shattered the domestic tranquility. "Holy saints! He has come home," someone exclaimed, though the logic of the situation defied immediate comprehension. Conjectures flew like sparks from a flint, until Kate, trembling with timid courage, suggested the young messenger might hold the key. Luke confirmed it with grim simplicity: the babe was dying, consumed by a wasting sickness that had peaked and pined relentlessly.

In an instant, the atmosphere shifted from confusion to fury. Catherine, her face turned toward her husband, erupted in a tirade of righteous indignation, threatening a quarrel that would echo through their decades of marriage if anyone dared question her resolve. Eli, however, dismissed the threat with characteristic pragmatism, offering to borrow Peter's mule so she might depart without delay. With a blessing that masked her terror, Catherine seized Luke, dragging him into the cart. On the road, she interrogated him with a ferocity that alternated between suspicion and seductive urgency, stripping his thoughts bare until he confessed everything he knew.

Upon arriving at the door, Margaret awaited them, a specter of pallor and agitation. She threw her arms around Catherine's neck, her eyes imploring forgiveness and salvation. Catherine scanned her daughter-in-law's face, then fixed her gaze on the failing child, and declared, "Lucky you sent for me. The child is poisoned."

"Poisoned! By whom?" Margaret cried, panic rising in her throat.

"By you," Catherine answered, her voice cutting through the room. "You have been fretting."

"Nay, indeed, mother! How can I help fretting?" Margaret protested, her voice breaking.

"Don't tell me," Catherine snapped, her tone devoid of mercy. "A nursing mother has no business to fret. She must turn her mind away from her grief to the comfort that lies in her lap. Know you not that the child pines if the mother vexes herself? This comes of your reading and writing. Those idle crafts befit a man; but they keep all useful knowledge out of a woman. The child must be weaned."

"Oh, you cruel woman!" Margaret screamed, the accusation tearing from her lips. "I am sorry I sent for you. Would you rob me of the only bit of comfort I have in the world? Nursing my Gerard, I forget I am the most unhappy creature beneath the sun."

"That you do not," Catherine retorted, her eyes hardening. "Or he would not be the way he is."

"Mother!" Margaret pleaded, the desperation in her voice raw.

"'Tis hard," Catherine relented slightly, though her expression remained stern. "But bethink thee; would it not be harder to look down and see his lovely wee face looking up at you out of a little coffin? And how could you face your other troubles with your heart aye full, and your lap empty?"

"Oh, mother, I consent to anything. Only save my boy."

"That is a good lass. Trust to me! I do stand by, and see clearer than thou."

Yet, there remained another consent to be gained—the babe's—and he proved far more refractory than his mother. Margaret tried to feign regret at the child's misbehavior, claiming he loved her too well, but Catherine was a match for them both. Observing a healthy young woman seated outside her own door with an infant nearby, Catherine approached her and proposed a temporary exchange. Would the stranger nurse the pining child for the nonce until Margaret could prepare to wean him? The young woman smiled, popped her own child into the cradle, and stepped into the house.

With a graceful curtsey, she accepted the infant from Catherine's hands. She examined the fragile form, filled with pity, and began to purr over it, proceeding to nurse it with the effortless grace of a mother who had never known loss. Margaret, paralyzed by the sheer assurance of this stranger, cast a rueful look at Catherine before bursting into uncontrollable sobs. The visitor glanced up, her composure unbroken.

"What is to do? Wife, ye told me not the mother was unwilling."

"She is not: she is only a fool. Never heed her," the stranger said firmly, addressing Margaret. "And you, Margaret, I am ashamed of you."

"You are a cruel, hard-hearted woman," Margaret sobbed, her defenses crumbling.

"Them as take in hand to guide the weak need be hardish," the stranger replied bluntly. "And you will excuse me; but you are not my flesh and blood; and your boy is."

After allowing this harsh truth to settle, she softened her tone. "Come now, she is robbing her own to save yours, and you can think of nothing better than bursting out a-blubbering in the woman's face. Out fie, for shame!"

"Nay, wife," the nurse said gently. "Thank Heaven, I have enough for my own and for hers to boot. And prithee wyte not on her! Maybe the troubles o' life ha' soured her own milk."

"And her heart into the bargain," Catherine added, her voice relentless.

Margaret locked eyes with the stranger, then lowered her gaze, defeated. "I know I ought to be very grateful to you," she sobbed, turning her head away to avoid witnessing the intolerable sight of another woman nursing her Gerard, a child who drew no distinction between this new mother and the one he had rejected.

"You are very welcome, my poor woman," the nurse replied warmly. "And so are you, Mistress Catherine, which are my townswoman, and know it not."

"What, are ye from Tergou?" Catherine asked, recognizing the accent. "All the better. But I cannot call your face to mind."

"Oh, you know not me," the woman said humbly. "My husband and me, we are very humble folk by you. But true Eli and his wife are known of all the town; and respected. So, I am at your call, dame; and at yours, wife; and yours, my pretty poppet; night or day."

"There's a woman of the right old sort," Catherine murmured as the door closed behind the departing stranger.

"I HATE her," Margaret whispered, the words vibrating with terrible fervor. "I HATE her. I HATE her."

Catherine merely laughed at the outburst. "That is right," she said. "Better say it, as set sly and think it. It is very natural after all. Come, here is your bundle o' comfort. Take and hate that, if ye can," she added, placing the child in Margaret's lap.

"No, no," Margaret refused, turning her head halfway from the baby; she could not bring herself to turn the other half. "He is not my child now; he is hers. I know not why she left him here, for my part. It was very good of her not to take him to her house, cradle and all..." Her voice dissolved into a cascade of wails.

"Ah! well, one comfort, he is not dead," Catherine soothed, letting her cry in peace. "This gives me light: some other woman has got him away from me; like father, like son."

For a time, Catherine allowed Margaret to mourn, occasionally taking Gerard out for fresh air when she needed Joan's assistance. Margaret never objected, nor did she express the slightest incredulity, yet she remained in tears whenever they returned. This period of connivance was short-lived, however. Soon, Margaret became as eager to wean little Gerard as Catherine had been to intervene. The process was completed, and the child recovered his health and vigor. Yet, another trouble descended upon him directly with the onset of teething. Here, Catherine's experience proved invaluable. Amidst her own grief regarding the missing father, Margaret found moments of bliss watching her son's tiny teeth emerge.

"Teeth, mother? I call them not teeth, but pearls of pearls," Margaret would exclaim. Each pearl that peeked and sparkled on his red gums represented, in her eyes, the greatest feat nature had ever achieved. Her companion shared the illusion. If we had told them that standing corn was equally admirable, Margaret would have transformed into a reproachful gazelle, and Catherine would have thrown us out of doors; thus, the arrival of each tooth was announced with a shriek of triumph by whichever of them happened to be the fortunate discoverer.
