The morning had commenced with a catastrophe of epic proportions, orchestrated by Gustavus Adolphus. Downstairs, the household abbreviated him to "Gusty," a title that belied the sheer scale of the disaster he wrought whenever he deemed the interior of the house suitable for his presence. While a creature of magnificent utility in the boundless expanse of a common or a wide-open field, Gustavus Adolphus was fundamentally ill-suited to domestic confinement. His intentions were benign, yet his physical dimensions exceeded the architectural limits of this residence. With a single, expansive stretch, he toppled two chairs and a precarious what-not, transforming the room into the aftermath of a military invasion. His wagging tail swept debris across the floorboards, and his mere respiration extinguished the fire, rendering the atmosphere stifling and chaotic.

By dinner time, the routine of destruction resumed. He would creep beneath the table, lie motionless for a brief interlude, and then erupt into sudden activity. Our only forewarning of his movements was the table itself, which appeared possessed by a frantic desire to execute a series of somersaults. We clutched the legs desperately, striving to maintain a horizontal plane, only to find his struggles intensify under the delusion that a malevolent conspiracy was unfolding against him. The inevitable result was invariably a shattered table and a ruined meal, trapped between the sprawled limbs of infuriated men and women.

This morning, however, Gustavus Adolphus entered with the ferocity of an American cyclone. His initial act was to sweep my coffee-cup from the table with his tail, sending the liquid contents directly onto the center of my waistcoat. Rising hastily, I muttered a curse and advanced upon him at a rapid pace. He anticipated my movement, retreating swiftly toward the door. There, he intercepted Eliza, who was entering with a tray of eggs. Upon observing the commotion, she emitted a sharp "Ugh!" and collapsed onto the floor. The eggs scattered, assuming various positions across the carpet before settling into a chaotic array, while Gustavus Adolphus retreated from the scene. I shouted after him, sternly advising him to descend immediately and forbidding any sight of him for the ensuing hour. Seeming to accept this decree, he dodged the coal-scoop and vanished, allowing me to dry myself and resume my breakfast. I verified his departure into the yard, yet ten minutes later, peering into the passage, I discovered him perched at the top of the stairs. Ordering him down yielded nothing but barks and jumps, compelling me to investigate the cause of his obstruction.

The culprit was Tittums. She sat upon the step below, refusing to yield passage. Tittums is our kitten, currently the size of a penny roll. Her back arched, and she was vocalizing with the intensity of a medical student reciting anatomy. Indeed, she possesses a formidable capacity for swearing. I admit to a similar tendency occasionally, though I remain a mere amateur compared to her proficiency. Between ourselves—and I implore you to keep this strictly confidential, lest your wife discover I voiced such sentiments—the fairer sex simply cannot comprehend these mechanisms. Yet, privately, I believe swearing benefits a man significantly. It serves as a safety valve, releasing the volatile temper that, if contained, would inflict severe internal damage upon the mental apparatus. Consider the relief derived from unleashing a torrent of invective regarding a clumsy footstep: *"Bless you, my dear, sweet sir! What did the sun, moon, and stars possess to render you so careless as to permit your light and delicate foot to descend upon my corn with such force? Are you physically incapable of comprehending your trajectory? You nice, clever young man—you!"* Such eruptions provide a cathartic release, functioning much like smashing furniture or slamming doors, albeit with considerably greater economy. Swearing clears a man's spirit as effectively as a pound of gunpowder clears a chimney. Occasional explosions are essential; I harbor deep suspicion for any man who never swears, nor savagely kicks a footstool, nor pokes the fire with unnecessary violence. Without such outlets, the accumulated annoyances of daily life fester, transforming petty irritations into profound sorrows. These grievances are brooded over in the hotbed of rumination until they mature into hatred and revenge.

I expounded this theory to my aunt once, but she rejected it outright, declaring I had no business harboring such feelings. This was precisely the advice I offered Tittums. I admonished her for shamelessness, noting that despite her upbringing in a Christian household, she displayed inappropriate behavior. While I tolerate the swearing of an elderly cat, witnessing a mere kitten succumb to such vulgarity is heartbreaking.

I placed Tittums in my pocket and returned to my desk, momentarily forgetting her existence. Upon checking, I found she had wriggled free onto the table, attempting to swallow a pen. Next, she dipped her leg into the inkpot, overturning it, followed by licking her leg, and finally, swearing again—this time directed squarely at me. I deposited her on the floor, where Tim immediately engaged in a skirmish with her. I earnestly wish Tim would attend to his own affairs; it was none of his business what Tittums had done. Furthermore, he is hardly a saint himself. As a two-year-old fox-terrier, he meddles with everything, adopting the airs of a seasoned, gray-headed Scotch collie.

Fortunately, Tittums' mother arrived, resulting in Tim receiving a scratch on his nose—a development I regard with considerable satisfaction. I have banished all three to the passage, where they are currently engaged in combat. My desk is a mess of spilled ink, and my temper is thunderous. Should any further canine or feline folly approach me this morning, it would be prudent for them to bring their own funeral contractors.

Despite this chaos, I hold cats and dogs in high esteem. They are jovial companions, vastly superior to humans in their suitability for friendship. They refrain from quarreling or arguing. They never monopolize conversation with self-centered chatter; instead, they listen attentively while we speak of ourselves, maintaining a convincing facade of interest. They avoid stupid remarks. They never comment to Miss Brown across a dinner table that they have always suspected she was infatuated with Mr. Jones—who has recently married Miss Robinson. They never mistake your wife's cousin for her husband, nor assume you are the father-in-law. Nor do they interrogate a young author burdened with fourteen tragedies, sixteen comedies, seven farces, and two burlesques regarding his lack of productivity.

They offer no unkind words. They never critique our faults "merely for our own good." They do not gently remind us of past follies at inconvenient moments, nor do they deliver sarcastic observations such as, "Oh, yes, a lot of use you are if you are ever really wanted." Unlike our *inamoratas*, they never inform us that we are no longer as charming as we once were. To them, we remain constant.

They greet us with joy, mirroring our moods perfectly. When we are cheerful, they are merry; when we are solemn, they are sober; when we are sorrowful, they share our sadness.

"Halloo! Happy and wanting a lark? Right you are; I'm your man. Here I am, frisking round you, leaping, barking, pirouetting, ready for any amount of fun and mischief. Look at my eyes if you doubt me. What shall it be? A romp in the drawing-room, disregarding the furniture, or a scamper in the fresh, cool air, a scud across the fields and down the hill, and won't we let old Gaffer Goggles' geese know what time o' day it is, neither! Whoop! Come along."

Conversely, if you seek solitude and reflection, Pussy can settle on the arm of the chair and purr, while Montmorency curls upon the rug, blinking at the fire yet keeping one watchful eye on you, anticipating any sudden urge to hunt rats.

When we bury our faces in our hands, wishing we had never been born, they do not sit upright to lecture us on self-inflicted misery. They do not even hope for a lesson learned. Instead, they approach softly, shoving their heads against us. If it is a cat, she stands upon your shoulder, ruffles your hair, and communicates plainly, "Lor', I am sorry for you, old man"; if it is a dog, he gazes up with his large, truthful eyes, conveying, "Well, you've always got me, you know. We'll go through the world together and always stand by each other, won't we?"

A dog is inherently imprudent. He never questions whether you are in the right or wrong, nor does he concern himself with your ascent or descent on life's ladder. He ignores whether you are rich or poor, silly or wise, sinner or saint. You are his pal—that is sufficient. Whether luck or misfortune, good repute or bad, honor or shame, he remains steadfast, comforting, guarding, and willing to sacrifice his life if necessary—a foolish, brainless, soulless dog!

Ah, old staunch friend, with your deep, clear eyes and bright, quick glances that capture everything before you have time to speak it, do you realize you are merely an animal devoid of intellect? Do you know that the dull-eyed, gin-soaked lout leaning against the post outside is immeasurably your intellectual superior?
