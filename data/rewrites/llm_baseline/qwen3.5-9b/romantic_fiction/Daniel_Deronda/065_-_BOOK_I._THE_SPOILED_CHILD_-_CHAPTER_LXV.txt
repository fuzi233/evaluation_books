"O, welcome, pure-eyed Faith, white-handed Hope, Thou hovering angel, girt with golden wings!"

Deronda did not answer Gwendolen's summons without a certain inner turmoil. It was not his pride that stirred, but his profound empathy. He feared the possibility that another's heart might demand more of him than he could possibly give. He realized, with a clarity that required no debate, that Gwendolen's soul clung to his with a desperate, passionate need. One does not logically argue against the anger or the scorn that vibrates through a voice; one simply feels it, undeniable and absolute. Deronda felt the weight of this woman's fate balancing precariously above a cliff of despair.

To anyone who truly knew him, it would not surprise them to hear his silent admission: if these events had occurred barely a year prior, he would scarcely have questioned whether he loved her. The driving force within him would have been an impulsive urge to shield her from sorrow, to protect her life forever from the terrors of isolation, and to complete the rescue he had begun with the redemption of the necklace. Yet, now, love and duty had woven other chains around him. That initial impulse could no longer dictate his path. Still, it remained within him as a yearning compassion, a painful trembling whenever he imagined facing her gaze and words again. Precisely because the bond was so strong, and his resolve to stand apart so firm, he watched her situation with an ache of pity that grew sharper.

He waited for her arrival in the back drawing-room—a section of the white and crimson space where they had once shared a musical evening. It was there, for the first time, that Gwendolen had declared her destiny hinged on his refusal to abandon her, her plea seeming to dissolve into the melodic cry, *Per pietà non darmi addio*. But that melody had belonged to Mirah's sweet voice. Deronda paced this room, which he had known intimately for years, experiencing a strange sensation of metamorphosis in his own life. The familiar surroundings—from Lady Mallinger's gentle portrait to the human, urbane faces of the lions adorning the chimney-piece—seemed to belong to a past existence, visited only in memory rather than reality. The impressions he had recently endured were so deep and transformative, and his circumstances so altered, that the house he had once considered home now felt foreign. He stood there, hat in hand, awaiting the entry of a young creature whose life was undergoing a tragic transformation toward an uncertain outcome, one in which he felt his own actions were still deeply entangled.

Gwendolen arrived, visibly changed. Beyond her mourning attire, her expression possessed a quiet satisfaction he had not seen in Genoa. This satisfaction stemmed from Deronda's presence, though neither offered a smile as they met and clasped hands; both were heavy with remembrance and anxious foreboding.

"It was good of you to come," she said immediately after sitting in the nearest chair. "Let us sit down."

He positioned himself opposite her. "I asked you to come because I want you to tell me what I ought to do," she began without hesitation. "Do not be afraid to tell me what you think is right, even if it sounds harsh. I have decided to do it. Once, I was terrified of poverty; I could not bear the thought of living under someone else's roof—that was why I married. I have endured worse things since. I believe I could endure poverty if you think it necessary. Do you know the terms of my husband's will?"

"Yes, Sir Hugo informed me," Deronda replied, anticipating her question.

"Should I accept anything he left me? Let me explain my thoughts," Gwendolen continued, her voice gaining a nervous urgency. "Perhaps you do not fully realize how much I contemplated my mother when I married. I was selfish, yes, but I loved her and felt acutely about her poverty. The greatest comfort I found during my misery was knowing she was better off because of my marriage. Now, the hardest thing for me would be to see her impoverished again. I have been considering that if I took enough to support her, and nothing for myself, it would not be wrong. After all, I was precious to her—he took me from her, and he intended... and if she had known..."

Gwendolen trailed off. She had prepared for this meeting by focusing almost exclusively on this question of right regarding her mother, but the issue brought forth thoughts and reasons she could not articulate. These perilous memories swirled between her words, causing her speech to grow increasingly agitated and tremulous. She looked down helplessly at her bare hands, stripped of every ring save her wedding band.

"Do not harm yourself by dwelling on that," Deronda said softly. "There is no need; the matter is straightforward. I doubt I could judge incorrectly. You seek my counsel because I am the only one to whom you have confessed the most painful parts of your experience, and I can understand your scruples."

He paused, allowing her to compose herself. The silence felt to Gwendolen thick with the tenderness in his voice, giving her the courage to lift her eyes and meet his gaze as he spoke. "You are conscious of something you feel to be a crime against one who is dead. You believe you have forfeited all claims as a wife. You recoil from accepting what belongs to him. You wish to avoid profiting from his death. Indeed, your feelings urge you toward self-punishment—a scourging of the self that disobeyed your better judgment, the will that fought against temptation. I have known something similar myself. Have I understood you correctly?"

"Yes—at least, I want to be good, not as I have been," Gwendolen replied. "I will try to bear whatever you think I must bear. I have told you the worst about myself. What ought I to do?"

"If no one else were involved in this question of income," Deronda said, "I would hesitate to urge you against your remorseful impulses. However, I am guided now by your feelings toward Mrs. Davilow, which seem entirely just to me. I cannot believe your husband's obligations to you personally are nullified by your actions. He voluntarily entered your life and profoundly influenced its course. Setting that aside, it was his duty, given his station, to provide for your mother, and he surely understood that if this will took effect, she would share in the provision made for you."

"She receives eight hundred pounds a year," Gwendolen noted. "What I considered was taking that amount and leaving the rest." Having argued internally for so long that this was permissible, her mind struggled instantly to shift to another perspective.

"I do not think it is your duty to set such a limit," Deronda advised. "You would create a painful mystery for Mrs. Davilow; an income from which you exclude yourself would become bitter to her. Furthermore, your own path would become unnecessarily difficult. We agreed in Genoa that the burden on your conscience is one no one should be forced to acknowledge. The future goodness of your life will be best served by sparing others the pain of that knowledge. In my opinion, you should simply follow the provisions of your husband's will, allowing your remorse to influence only how you choose to use your newfound financial independence."

As he finished, Deronda instinctively picked up his hat, which he had placed on the floor beside him. Gwendolen, attuned to his slightest movements, felt her heart leap as if possessing its own consciousness, fearing he might depart. Simultaneously, she rose from her chair, unable to consider that his motion signaled an intention to leave. Deronda, naturally, also stood, stepping forward slightly.

"I will do what you tell me," Gwendolen said hurriedly. "But what else shall I do?"

No other words were possible for her in that emotional state where her proud secrecy had crumbled. As the childlike sentences escaped her lips, they struck her like a mirror reflecting her own helplessness, and she could not stop the sob that brought large tears to her eyes. Deronda, too, felt a crushing pain, yet visible consequences weighed heavily on him, urging him to exert his conscience to the utmost.

Once she had brushed away her tears, he asked gently, "You will likely be traveling with Mrs. Davilow to the countryside soon."

"Yes, in a week or ten days." Gwendolen hesitated, turning her eyes vaguely toward the window as if gazing at some imagined horizon. "I want to be kind to them all—they can be happier than I can. Is that the best I can do?"

"I believe so. It is a duty beyond doubt," Deronda affirmed. He paused, feeling a heavy anxiety settle over his words. "Other duties will arise from this. Viewing your life as a debt may seem the dreariest perspective from afar, but it is not truly so. What makes life bleak is the lack of motive. Once you begin acting with the penitent, loving purpose you hold in your mind, unexpected satisfactions will emerge—newly opening needs that will propel you from day to day. You will find your life growing like a plant."

Gwendolen turned her gaze upon him with the look of one thirsting for the sound of unseen water. Deronda felt this gaze as if she were stretching her arms toward him from a deserted shore. His voice adopted an affectionate, imploring tone. "This sorrow, which has cut down to the roots, has come to you while you are so young. Try to view it not as a spoiling of your life, but as a preparation for it. Let it be a preparation..."
