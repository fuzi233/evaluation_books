“The old fellow has vanished,” Piero di Cosimo was saying, his voice rasping like a knife drawn over a palette. “Went on towards Arezzo the next morning. Not liking the smell of the French, I suppose, after being their prisoner. I went to the hospital to ask after him—I wanted to know if those broth-making monks had found out whether he was in his right mind. They said he showed no signs of madness—only took no notice of questions, and seemed to be planting a vine twenty miles off. A mysterious old tiger. I should have liked to know more of him.”

It was the twenty-fourth of November, a week since the French had come in, and they were gathered in Nello’s bottega at the odd hour when honest hunger begins to argue with the clock. The shop held its usual mingling of scents: warm soap and strop-leather; pomade with a faint note of crushed rosemary; the close human smell of woollen cloaks damped by fog; and, from some alley vendor outside, the teasing drift of roasted chestnuts whose split skins gave off a sweet smoke that made even politics feel like something one could chew and swallow.

Nello himself, half perched on the board against the shop-window, had been thrumming his lute with two fingers, letting the notes fall into the room as if to keep the air from turning too sharp with talk. Now and then he glanced out toward the piazza, reading the city by its sounds—the scrape of sandals pausing, the impatient clack of a cartwheel that could not get through, the sudden hush that runs like a ripple when men stop speaking to listen for news.

At last he laid the lute down with a thump. “Ah,” he said, and his relish for spectacle made his moustaches lift, “I would not for a gold florin have missed that sight of French soldiers waddling in their broad shoes after their runaway prisoners. And it happened when I’d gone to shave magnificent chins. Always so. If ever I quit this navel of the earth, the piazza takes the opportunity of birthing a marvel.”

“Yes,” Piero said, with the sour pleasure of a man who enjoys pricking a bubble, “you ought to have been there—just to see your favourite Greek look as frightened as if Satanasso had laid hold of him. I like to see your ready-smiling messeri caught in a sudden wind and made to show their lining. What colour do you think a man’s liver is, who looks like a bleached deer the moment a chance stranger grips him?”

“Nay, Piero—keep that vinegar for thine own eggs,” Nello flung back. “What is it against my bel erudito that he started when he felt claws on him and saw an unchained madman at his elbow? Your scholar is not like those beastly Swiss and Germans whose heads are fit for battering-rams, and whose appetites are so large they’d swallow a cannon-ball before breakfast. We Florentines count other qualities in a man besides that vulgar stuff called bravery, which you can buy by the dozen in the market.”

In the inner room a voice called out, half laughing, half warning. “Treason, Nello! That is not the doctrine of the State. Florence is grinding its weapons; and the last well-authenticated vision announced by the Frate was Mars standing on the Palazzo Vecchio with his arm on San Giovanni Battista’s shoulder, and San Giovanni offering him a piece of honeycomb.”

“It is well, Francesco,” Nello answered, as if he were setting a capon to roast and had no fear it would burn. “Florence has a few thicker skulls to bombard Pisa with. There will still be finer spirits left at home to do the thinking and the shaving. And as for our Piero here—if he makes such a point of valour, let him carry his biggest brush for a weapon and his palette for a shield, and challenge the widest-mouthed Swiss in the Prato to single combat.”

“Va,” growled Piero. His face, cut into strange lines by old scars, made an expression that never quite confessed what lay behind it. He drew tow from his scarsella and stuffed his ears, as if to cork out nonsense. “Thy tongue runs on like a mill when the Arno’s full—whether there’s grist or not.”

“Excellent grist,” Nello insisted, enjoying his own seasoning. “For it would be as reasonable to expect a grizzled painter like thee to be fond of getting a javelin inside thee as to expect a man whose wits have been sharpened on the classics to like having his handsome face clawed by a wild beast.”

“There you go,” Piero said, and even with his ears stopped he found words enough, “supposing you’ll get people to put their legs into a sack because you call it a pair of hosen. Who said anything about a wild beast? Fighting is a trade, and it’s not my trade. I should be a fool to run after danger—but I could face it if it came to me.”

“How is it you’re so afraid of thunder, then?” Nello pursued, like a cook who will not leave a pot untasted. “You hide with the rats as soon as the storm comes on.”

“That is because I have a particular sensibility to loud sounds,” Piero snapped. “It has nothing to do with courage or conscience.”

“Well then,” said Nello, throwing out his hands as if scattering flour, “Tito Melema may have a particular sensibility to being seized unexpectedly by prisoners escaped from French soldiers. Men are born with antipathies. I myself can’t abide the smell of mint. Tito was born with an antipathy to old prisoners who stumble and clutch. Ecco!”

The laugh went round the little company as naturally as bread passed at table. Plainly Piero’s bitterness toward Tito was not the taste of the room. Piero, muttering, pressed the tow deeper and glowered as if the air itself had offended him; and Nello, triumphant, went on:

“No, my Piero, I can’t afford to have my bel erudito decried; and Florence can’t afford it either, with her scholars moulting off at forty. Our Phoenix Pico gone straight to Paradise, as the Frate has told us; and incomparable Poliziano, not two months since, gone to—well, well. Let us hope he is not gone to eminent scholars in the Malebolge.”

“By the way,” said Francesco Cei, who liked a jest even when the city was on edge, “have you heard that Camilla Rucellai has outdone the Frate in prophecy? She prophesied two years ago that Pico would die in the time of lilies. He has died in November. ‘Not at all the time of lilies,’ said the scorners. ‘Go to!’ says Camilla; ‘it is the lilies of France I meant, and they seem close enough under your nostrils.’ Euge, Camilla! If the Frate can prove any one of his visions as well fulfilled, I’ll be a Piagnone to-morrow.”

“You are too flippant about the Frate, Francesco,” said Pietro Cennini, who spoke as carefully as if weighing spices, mindful that too much ruins the dish. “We are all indebted to him in these weeks for preaching peace and quietness, the laying aside of party quarrels. Men of small discernment would be glad to see the people slip the Frate’s leash now. And if the Most Christian King is obstinate to-day, and will not sign what is fair and honourable to Florence, Fra Girolamo is the man we must trust to bring him to reason.”

“You speak truth, Messer Pietro,” said Nello. “The Frate is one of the firmest nails Florence has to hang on—at least so say the most respectable chins I have the honour of shaving. But young Messer Niccolò was saying here the other morning—and doubtless Francesco means the same—there is a wonderful power of stretching the meaning of visions, like Dido’s bull’s hide. A dream may mean whatever comes after it. Franco Sacchetti tells how a woman dreams of a serpent biting her, breaks a cup next day, and cries, ‘Look you, I knew something would happen—it’s plain what the serpent meant.’”

“But the Frate’s visions are not of that sort,” said Cronaca, with the obstinacy of a man who has listened until the very sound of certain words has become his refuge. “He not only says what will happen—the Church scourged and renovated, the heathens converted—he says it shall happen quickly. He is no slippery pretender—he is—”

“What is this? what is this?” Nello broke in, springing off the board as if the piazza had plucked him by the sleeve. He thrust his head out at the door. “People are streaming in and shouting. Something’s happened in the Via Larga. Aha!”

He was out on the threshold, cap waving, laughter bursting from him; and the others crowded behind, drawn by that great Florentine appetite for news, which is like appetite for food—the more a man has had, the sharper his hunger becomes.

The piazza had been full of that lazy sprinkling of artisans who pause and drift like bits of wood on a river, ready to whirl into a current. Now it thickened, and the sound thickened with it: voices layering, feet scuffing, the distant clatter of a shutter flung open, and, above all, a kind of jubilant roar that rolled and recoiled from stone to stone. Yet the thing that came into the piazza was not a herald with a trumpet, nor a mounted messenger, but a man carried aloft on a bench snatched from somewhere, as if the crowd had seized the nearest furniture to make a throne.

Tito Melema sat there, borne above shoulders, smiling in amused surrender to the compulsion. His cap had slipped and now hung by the becchetto loosely wound about his neck. Seeing the group at Nello’s door, he lifted his fingers in a beckoning recognition. The next moment he had leaped from the bench onto a cart piled with bales, standing in the broad space between the Baptistery and the Duomo steps, while the people swarmed about him with the noisy eagerness of poultry waiting for grain.

But when he began to speak, his clear mellow voice cut through the tumult; and the piazza, trained by sermons and proclamations, fell into a silence that had its own music—only a far-off hammer tap, only the faint hiss of breath between teeth, only the city’s great stone listening.

“Citizens of Florence!” Tito said. “I have no warrant to tell the news except your will. But the news is good, and will harm no man in the telling. The Most Christian King is signing a treaty honourable to Florence. But you owe it to one of your citizens—one who spoke a word worthy of the ancient Romans—you owe it to Piero Capponi!”

At once the silence broke into a roar, as if a wine-skin had burst.

“Capponi! Capponi! What said our Piero?”
“Ah! he wouldn’t stand being sent from Herod to Pilate!”
“We knew Piero!”
“Orsù! Tell us—what did he say?”

When the cries abated enough to let sense through, Tito lifted his hand, and began again.

“The Most Christian King demanded a little too much—was obstinate—said at last, ‘I shall order my trumpets to sound.’ Then, Florentine citizens, your Piero Capponi, speaking with the voice of a free city, said, ‘If you sound your trumpets, we will ring our bells!’ He snatched the copy of the dishonouring conditions from the secretary’s hands, tore it in pieces, and turned to leave the royal presence.”

Shouts surged up again, and impatience, that other Florentine condiment, demanded more.

“Then, Florentines,” Tito continued, “the high majesty of France felt, perhaps for the first time, all the majesty of a free city. The Most Christian King himself hastened from his place to call Piero Capponi back. The great spirit of your city did its work by a great word, without need of the great actions that lay ready behind it. The King has consented to sign the treaty which preserves the honour, as well as the safety, of Florence. The banner of France will float over every Florentine galley in sign of amity and common privilege—but above that banner will be written the word Liberty!

“That is all the news I have to tell; is it not enough?—since it is for the glory of every one of you, citizens of Florence, that you have a fellow-citizen who knows how to speak your will.”

The sound that answered him was like the sea driven into a narrow bay. Tito looked round, inwardly amused, at faces flushed with the thought that Capponi had spoken for each of them, that the mouth had somehow been the mouthpiece of their own unshaped desires. He enjoyed the irony that had, in an instant, made him—an alien, and known friend to the Medici—into an orator pleasing ears that cried for a good they could not have defined except in that one treasured word: liberty. He was glad, in his light way, that he had been laid hold of and hurried along when he came out of the palace in the Via Larga with a commission to the Signoria. It was easy—pleasant—this art of speaking to general satisfaction. A man who knew how to persuade need not fear any party; he could convince each that he was only feigning with the others. From above, the gestures of weavers and dyers looked comical, like birds pecking in a yard, each certain he would get the first grain.

He was beginning to feel at ease in his armour, and did not know it. One hand held his recovered cap; the other rested at his belt. His long lustrous eyes held the light of complacency as he made a parting reverence before springing down from the bales.

Then, suddenly, his glance struck and caught on a face that did not share the crowd’s harvest-look. A man stood there clean-shaven, hair close-clipped, wearing a decent felt hat like any poor citizen who has kept a shred of order in his dress. Another eye might have passed him by; a single glance could hardly have sworn him to be the escaped prisoner who had clutched Tito on the steps. But to Tito it was not merely that—this face was one he had known long years before.

In a second, everything was compressed: the sight of Baldassarre looking at him; the sensation shooting through him like a fiery arrow; the act of leaping from the cart. He would have sprung down at that instant whether he had seen Baldassarre or not—he was in haste to reach the Palazzo Vecchio—but now haste had another taste, metallic and bitter on the tongue. Yet he did not betray himself by look or movement; inwardly he told himself he would not be surprised again. He must be prepared to see this face rising up continually, like that intermittent blotch that comes in diseased vision and spoils whatever else you look at.

But Baldassarre’s reappearance—so much closer to his own old likeness now that beard and rags were gone—tightened dread as a cord tightens when the knot is pulled. Madness seemed less likely when the man was shaven and decently clad. Certainly the face had changed—how could it be otherwise?—and yet if he were perfectly sane, with his powers and learning, why linger thus instead of crying his name? Unless it was to make vengeance more complete. He did linger; and that, at least, offered the one cheap mercy of time. Flight began to seem Tito’s only resource.

As he turned his back on the Piazza del Duomo, the new part he had been playing slipped from him like a cloak thrown off in heat. He was no longer thinking of the many things his ready brain and tongue made easy, but of the few things destiny had made hard.

Behind him, the enthusiasm he had fed with contempt created a scene grander than his private fear. The crowd, after his disappearance, began to turn toward the outlets leading to the Via Larga, when the sight of the mazzieri entering from the Via de’ Martelli announced the approach of dignitaries. The treaty must already be signed; these were the syndics, commissioners charged with its effecting, come away from the royal presence. Piero Capponi was coming—the firm heart that had spoken for Florence.

The crowd’s change was as striking as a kitchen gone suddenly quiet when the master steps in. Voices dropped and softened; men shuffled back; the press parted. Silence gathered, and became so complete that the tread of the syndics on the broad pavement, and the rustle of their black silk garments, could be heard distinctly, like rain heard at night when all else sleeps. There were four of them: Guidantonio Vespucci and Domenico Bonsi, learned doctors of law; Francesco Valori, grown popular in these late days; but it was not on them the piazza fixed its hunger.

The moment belonged to another man—firm in presence, loving order like one whom fortune had made a merchant and nature had made a soldier, as little inclined to humour the people as to humour any other unreasonable claimant. When he appeared at the entrance, one loud shout broke the silence cleanly, like a bell struck true:

“Capponi! Capponi! Well done, Capponi!”

The simple resolute man looked round with grave joy, letting the sound of gratitude enter him while it was living breath, not carved praise. Two years later they would give him a great funeral when he had died in fight—torches borne by all the magistracy, and torches again, and trains of banners. It is not known that the oration over his bier gave him any joy. Let us be glad he had thanks and praise while he lived.
