When Shirley finished speaking with Moore, Caroline returned upstairs to find Mrs. Pryor in a state of profound depression. Though the older woman refused to admit that Miss Keeldar's haste had wounded her feelings, it was clear that an internal sting festered within her. While anyone else might have seemed untouched by the quiet, tender attentions Miss Helstone offered to soothe her, Caroline understood that beneath her composed exterior, Mrs. Pryor felt, valued, and was truly healing through them.

"I lack self-confidence and decisiveness," Mrs. Pryor finally admitted. "These traits have always eluded me. Still, I believe Miss Keeldar should have known my character well enough to realize that I always strive to do right and act for the best. The unusual nature of the demand on my judgment confused me, especially in the wake of the night's alarms. I could not risk acting hastily on behalf of another, but I trust no serious harm resulted from my momentary loss of firmness."

Just then, a gentle knock sounded at the door, followed by it being pushed open halfway.

"Caroline, come here," a low voice whispered.

Miss Helstone stepped out to find Shirley standing in the gallery, looking contrite, ashamed, and sorrowful—a repentant child seeking forgiveness.

"How is Mrs. Pryor?" Shirley asked softly.

"A bit out of spirits," Caroline replied.

"I have behaved shamefully—very ungenerously, very ungratefully toward her," Shirley confessed. "How insolent of me to turn on her for what was, after all, no fault of hers, but merely an excess of conscientiousness. I regret my error most sincerely. Please tell her so and ask if she will forgive me."

Caroline delivered the message with heartfelt joy. Mrs. Pryor rose and approached the door. Disliking scenes and dreading them as all timid souls do, she invited them in with a faltering, "Come in, my dear."

Shirley entered with some impetuosity, throwing her arms around her governess. While kissing her warmly, she declared, "You know you *must* forgive me, Mrs. Pryor. I could not function at all if there were a misunderstanding between us."

"I have nothing to forgive," Mrs. Pryor replied gently. "Let us simply pass it over. The ultimate lesson of this incident is that it proves, more clearly than ever, how unequal I am to certain crises."

That painful realization—that she could forgive her offending pupil but not her own perceived inadequacies—remained etched in Mrs. Pryor's mind. Neither Shirley's nor Caroline's efforts could erase it.

Soon, Miss Keeldar was summoned downstairs again, having become a frequent visitor throughout the morning. The rector greeted her first, offering a lively welcome paired with an equally spirited reprimand. Expecting both, and buoyed by high spirits, he accepted them with equal grace. During his brief visit, he completely forgot to inquire after his niece; the riot, the rioters, the mill, the magistrates, and the heiress consumed all his thoughts, leaving no room for family ties. He recounted the roles he and the curate played in defending the Hollow.

"The vials of Pharisaical wrath will surely be emptied upon our heads for our part in this affair," he declared. "But I defy every calumniator. I was there solely to uphold the law, to fulfill my duty as a man and a Briton—roles I consider entirely compatible with those of the Priest and Levite in their highest sense. Your tenant, Moore, has earned my approval. I could not wish for a cooler commander, nor a more determined one. Furthermore, the man displayed sound judgment and good sense: first, in being thoroughly prepared for the eventuality that occurred; and second, in knowing how to wield his victory without abusing it once his well-concerted plans succeeded. Some of the magistrates are now terrified; like all cowards, they tend toward cruelty. Yet Moore restrains them with admirable prudence. He has been unpopular in the neighborhood until now, but mark my words: the tide of opinion will soon turn in his favor. People will realize they failed to appreciate him and will rush to correct their mistake. And when he sees the public ready to acknowledge his merits, he will display a more gracious demeanor than the cold one he has shown us hitherto."

Mr. Helstone was about to add some half-joking, half-serious warnings to Miss Keeldar regarding rumors of her partiality toward her talented tenant when a ring at the door announced another caller. This interruption halted his raillery. The visitor revealed himself as a white-haired elderly gentleman with a truculent countenance and a disdainful eye—in short, their old acquaintance and the rector's arch-enemy, Mr. Yorke. The "priest and Levite" quickly seized his hat, bidding Miss Keeldar a brief farewell and nodding sternly to her guest before departing abruptly.

Mr. Yorke was in no mild mood. In no measured terms did he express his opinion on the events of the previous night. Moore, the magistrates, the soldiers, and the mob leaders all received a share of his invectives, but he reserved his strongest epithets—and some genuinely spicy Yorkshire Doric adjectives—for the "fighting parsons," labeling the rector and curate "sanguinary, demoniac." According to him, the cup of ecclesiastical guilt was now overflowing.

"The church is in a bonny pickle now," he proclaimed. "It was time it came down when parsons began swaggering among soldiers, blazing away with bullets and gunpowder, taking the lives of far more honorable men than themselves."

"What would Moore have done if nobody had helped him?" Shirley asked.

"Drunk as he'd brewed, eaten as he'd baked."

"That means you would have left him alone to face that mob. Well, he has plenty of courage, but the greatest gallantry that ever garrisoned a human breast could scarce withstand two hundred."

"He had the soldiers, those poor slaves who hire out their own blood and spill other folks' for money."

"You abuse soldiers almost as much as you abuse clergymen. In your eyes, all who wear red coats are national refuse, and all who wear black are national swindlers. By your logic, Mr. Moore did wrong to seek military aid, and did even worse to accept any other assistance. Your argument boils down to this: he should have abandoned his mill and his life to the rage of a set of misguided madmen, while Mr. Helstone and every other gentleman in the parish watched impassively as the building was razed and its owner slaughtered, never stirring a finger to save either."

"If Moore had behaved toward his men from the start as a master ought, they never would have developed these feelings toward him."

"Easy for you to talk," Miss Keeldar exclaimed, warming to her tenant's defense. "You, whose family has lived at Briarmains for six generations, to whose person the people have been accustomed for fifty years, who know all their ways, prejudices, and preferences—it is easy for *you* to act so as to avoid offending them. But Mr. Moore came as a stranger to the district; he arrived poor and friendless, backed only by his own energies, honor, talent, and industry. It is a monstrous crime that, under such circumstances, he could not instantly popularize his naturally grave and quiet manners; that he could not be jocular, free, and cordial with a strange peasantry as you are with your fellow-townsmen! It is an unpardonable transgression that when he introduced improvements, he did not conduct the business in the most politic manner possible, failing to graduate his changes as delicately as a wealthy capitalist might. For errors of this sort is he to be the victim of mob outrage? Is he to be denied even the privilege of defending himself? Are those who possess hearts of men in their breasts—and let Mr. Helstone say what he will, he certainly has one—to be reviled like criminals simply because they stand by him, because they dare to espouse the cause of one against two hundred?"

"Come, come now, be cool," Mr. Yorke said, smiling at the earnestness with which Shirley fired off her rapid questions.

"Cool! Must I listen coolly to downright nonsense—to dangerous nonsense? No. I like you very well, Mr. Yorke, as you know, but I thoroughly dislike some of your principles. All that cant—excuse me, but I repeat the word—all that *cant* about soldiers and parsons is most offensive to my ears. All ridiculous, irrational praise of one class, whether aristocrat or democrat, and all howling down of another, whether clerical or military—is sickening to me. All arraying of ranks against ranks, all party hatreds, all tyrannies disguised as liberties—I reject and wash my hands of. *You* think you are a philanthropist; *you* think you are an advocate of liberty; but I will tell you this: Mr. Hall, the parson of Nunnely, is a better friend to both man and freedom than Hiram Yorke, the reformer of Briarfield."

A man would not have borne such language patiently, nor would he have endured it from many women; but he considered Shirley both honest and pretty, and her plain-spoken anger amused him. Moreover, he secretly delighted in hearing her defend her tenant, for he already harbored a strong interest in Robert Moore's welfare.
