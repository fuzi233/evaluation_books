When that overblown, drama-laden letter from Major Pendennis landed on Dr. Portman’s doormat, the old rector grabbed his hat and coat faster than a terrier after a stray sausage. Any decent clergyman who’s got bad news to deliver moves like a dentist who’s just spotted a perfect extraction spot: he’s sorry you’re in pain, he’s sympathetic, but by gum that tooth’s coming out right now, and he’s not going to dally around getting cold feet. It’s always your tooth, of course—if it was his own he’d be hemming and hawing for three weeks minimum.

Portman first read the letter out loud to his wife and daughter, cussing Pen as a “young scapegrace slipping straight down the ladder to the workhouse” so loud the parlormaid could hear him from the pantry. He left the two Portman ladies to spread the juicy gossip across Clavering, a task they executed with the speed and accuracy of two well-trained town criers with a personal grudge against fun, before he stomped over to Fairoaks to break the bad news to Pen’s widowed mother Helen.

Turns out Helen already had the news from Pen himself. And far from fainting dead away or wailing into her handkerchief, she breathed a sigh of relief. She’d had a dark, nameless dread hanging over her for months, and now the worst was out: her darling boy was coming home, repentant, soft-hearted, and all hers again. What more could she want? All of Portman’s perfectly sensible, historically vetted rants about Pen flunking his degree and racking up debts bounced right off her. “What’s the big fuss about that silly old degree anyway?” she said, tilting her head. “What good was it ever going to do him? You and the Major are the ones who insisted he run around with all those rich, wild young people full of temptation, why didn’t you just leave him home with me? And his debts? That’s his father’s money, it’s all his, he’s every right to spend it.” Portman left that conversation so confused he nearly tripped over the garden gate on his way out.

See, for years Pen and his adopted little sister Laura had run around the house tackling each other, stealing cookies, and greeting each other with messy, casual kid kisses the second they were in the same room. When Pen came home from college that winter, though? The scrawny little 12-year-old he’d left behind was now a tall, slim, 16-year-old beauty standing five foot four, who curtsied politely instead of launching herself at him, blushed so hard her cheeks turned the exact shade of the spot he’d used to smooch as a kid, and held out a hand instead of leaning in for a hug.

I’m no fancy beauty critic, and frankly I think decency beats pretty face any day, but let’s lay it out straight: half the catty local ladies called her a lanky maypole and complained her feet were too big just because she wore long dresses, but that “maypole” had roses twined all over its head, and every single unattached swain within three miles of Clavering was tripping over his own boots trying to ask her to dances. She was usually pale with a faint rose flush to her cheeks, but when she got flustered? She blushed so hard that pink stayed there for an hour after the excitement was long gone. She’d had giant, bright eyes since she was a toddler, and the local gossips insisted she was winking and flirting with every person in the room on purpose. Nonsense—she was just born with eyes that shine brighter than a tavern lantern, and eyelashes so long and dark they looked like little silk veils laid over her lids, the poor thing couldn’t help it. Her mouth was a little on the large side, if you asked the prissy Miss Piminy who could barely swallow a breadcrumb, but her smile showed off a perfect set of pearly teeth, and her voice was so soft and sweet it sounded like you were listening to a half-drunken street musician playing a nice tune under your window. She was just a good, modest, sweet young thing, the kind that makes you think England might not be entirely full of idiots after all.

Laura hadn’t been happy with Pen’s college antics for ages. His letters home got shorter, rarer, full of vague excuses about “important engagements” and he kept skipping vacations at Fairoaks to party at rich friends’ fancy country houses. Helen kept making excuses for him, fumbling through lines about “networking” and “the Major’s good advice,” but Laura rolled her eyes. “It’s better he lose a stupid prize than forget he has a mother who loves him more than any duke ever will,” she’d huff. She was properly furious when she heard about that awful Fotheringay actress nonsense, too—Major Pendennis had let it slip on a visit, and the Clavering gossips filled in all the juiciest bits. A Pendennis throwing himself at a hussy of an actress, gallivanting home every other day to kneel at her feet and drink ale with her drunk old dad? He was going to bring that lot back to Fairoaks and make Helen wait on the lot of them? “I’d run away barefoot through snow before I let that happen!” Laura declared, before Helen sighed and said “You’d leave me too, then?” and the two of them dissolved in a sobbing hug that lasted five minutes.

But the second Laura heard Pen was sad, humiliated, and coming home? All that anger vanished faster than a plate of cakes at a church social. He was her old Pen again, the sweet, generous little boy she’d adored as a kid, and she immediately turned on Dr. Portman the second he started ranting about Pen’s “enormous sins.” “Debts? Those are nothing! The Major made him hang around all those fancy rich boys, he had no choice but to keep up! He was sick for the exams! The tutors were jealous of him, they picked their own pets to pass instead of him, it’s obvious!” She grabbed Helen’s hand, glared daggers at Portman, and looked like she was two seconds away from telling the old man to get off their property if he said one more bad word about Pen.

Portman left completely bewildered, muttering about stubborn women the whole walk home. The two of them immediately ordered fires lit in Pen’s old rooms, his bedding hung out to air, and Laura added a postscript to Helen’s gushy love letter to Pen that said *COME HOME AT ONCE, DEAREST PEN* with three thick, dramatic underlines, and signed it “your affectionate sister Laura.”

That night, long after their Bibles were put away and they’d snuck a quick peek at Pen’s empty room on their way to bed, Laura sat up in the dark and announced she had a genius plan. “You’ve never charged me for the last ten years living here, right? That’s at least fifty pounds a year I owe you. That’s five hundred quid sitting in Mr. Rowdy’s bank in Chatteris, and we’re going to go get it tomorrow, pay off every last one of Pen’s debts, and none of us has to worry about a single thing ever again.” Helen’s response was just a lot of incoherent sobbing, hugs, and kisses that nearly knocked over the little pincushion on her dresser that had a lock of Pen’s baby hair sewn into it.

Pen was so determined to be thrifty that January that he refused an inside seat on the London coach, and rode up top with the guard, who remembered Pen slipping him an extra shilling last year and lent him three thick old greatcoats to keep the frost off. His knees were shaking when he got off at the lodge gates, partly from the cold, partly from panic that his mom would be mad at him. Old John the servant didn’t even wear the blue and drab livery no more—he was full-time gardener and stable hand now, and he grinned and blushed as he grabbed Pen’s bag. Before Pen could even make it up the drive, Helen came barrelling around the shrubbery, face glowing so bright you could have lit a candle off it, already forgiving him before he even said a single sorry word.

Laura was waiting in the hall, and for the first time in years, she skipped the formal curtsy, ran right up to him, and gave him a big hug and a kiss right on the cheek. They didn’t make a big dramatic fuss over the “fatted calf” bit, but they cooked all his favorite meals for a week, and nobody mentioned the flunked exam or the debts, not for ages. Pen sold his horse and handed the money over to Helen, who thought that was the most virtuous act any young man had ever pulled off—Laura just sniffed and said it was the absolute least he owed. He gave up wine, cut back on smoking, went to church every Sunday with his mom, and avoided Dr. Portman like the plague, who kept glowering at him from under his big shovel hat every time they passed each other on the road.

After a few weeks of moping around the house, Laura went up to him, blushing so hard her ears went pink, and told him he was being a coward if he didn’t go back to Oxbridge, do the exam again, and get his proper degree. Pen grumbled, but he went, and passed the second exam so easy he barely had to study. He popped up to London to visit his uncle afterwards, and the old snob barely even gave him a single cold finger to shake, and the second time Pen called his valet lied straight to his face that the Major was out of town.

Back to Fairoaks he went, moping harder than ever. He wrote three terrible gloomy tragedies, a whole stack of angsty poetry, made a dozen study plans he abandoned after three days, daydreamed about enlisting in the army, joining the Spanish Legion, running off to sea—anything to get out of the doldrums. Helen kept saying soon they’d go abroad, soon he’d move to London, get a proper career, get away from the boring quiet country house with just two old women. But Helen’s own face was getting paler and more tired every month, and Laura watched her grow more and more languid, and started to feel a quiet, cold worry settle in her stomach that none of them were going to get the happy ending they’d all daydreamed about.
