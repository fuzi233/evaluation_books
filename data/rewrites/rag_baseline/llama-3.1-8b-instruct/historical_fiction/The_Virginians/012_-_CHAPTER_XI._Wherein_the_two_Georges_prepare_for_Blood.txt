The afternoon sun cast a pale glow through the dusty windows of the abandoned music hall. I sat at a worn piano, fingers hovering above the keys as I contemplated the fragments of a forgotten melody. The silence was oppressive, a heavy blanket that threatened to suffocate the faintest whisper of sound. My thoughts turned to the letters I had received from George, the words blurring together like the notes on the page before me.

I recalled the day he wrote to me, dictating his final wishes as if he expected death to arrive at any moment. His brother, Harry, had transcribed the words with a quivering hand, the ink smudging as tears fell onto the parchment. The letter was a jumbled mix of grandiose language and bitter resentment, a testament to the complexities of family ties. George had taken his cue from their ancestor's own life, where a noble duel was fought over a trivial matter, with the true cause – a woman's influence – left unspoken.

As I pondered George's words, my mind wandered to the music hall itself. The wooden floor creaked beneath my feet, a reminder of the countless performances that had taken place within these walls. The air was thick with the scent of dust and forgotten dreams. I closed my eyes, allowing the silence to wash over me, and let the fragments of the melody seep into my consciousness.

The sound of footsteps echoed outside, and I opened my eyes to see Sady, George's negro boy, standing in the doorway. He carried a small package in his hands, his face a mask of concern. I rose from the piano, and he approached me with a hesitant air. "Sir, I've brought the instruments," he said, his voice barely above a whisper. I took the package from him, feeling the weight of the pistols within. "It seems we're a little late," I said, glancing at the clock on the wall. Two and a half hours had passed since George's departure, and the silence that filled the music hall seemed to grow heavier with each passing moment.
