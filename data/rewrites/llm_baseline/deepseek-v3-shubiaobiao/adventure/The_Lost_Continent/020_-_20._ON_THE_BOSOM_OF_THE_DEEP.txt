The Ark was adrift, a helpless husk on the mercy of the sea—no rudder to steer her, no oars to guide her, no engine to propel her. She moved only as the gods willed. Inside, the air was thick with the scent of age, the darkness broken only by the groans of timbers and the restless shifting of unseen things. The fallen lamp lay useless, its flame extinguished, leaving us to endure the blackness and the chaos in blind silence.

The waves toyed with us, hurling the Ark like a plaything, and I clung to Nais, bracing her against the heaving floor. Words were scarce between us, but in that shared silence, in the grip of loss and survival, we found a bond deeper than speech.

When Atlantis fell, she left the sea a tempest of her dying—whirlpools churned where cities had been, plumes of steam hissed from drowned fires, and then, as if the gods demanded one final spectacle, the winds came screaming. For seven days and nights, the storm raged, a fury so relentless I wondered if it would ever end. But at last, the winds stilled, and the ocean settled into a slow, rolling swell, thick and heavy as oil.

Only then did I dare climb the stair, push open the roof-hatch, and let the clean air rush in. The stench of confinement lifted, replaced by salt and sunlight. I brought Nais up, and together we stood beneath the open sky, offering silent thanks to the Sun God for His mercy. The light was a balm, the warmth a promise that we still lived, though all else had perished.

Exhaustion weighed on me, but sleep came reluctantly. My eyes traced the horizon—no land, no break in the endless blue. Only wreckage bobbed alongside us: splintered trees, the carcass of a hide boat, bloated bodies of beasts and men. The remnants of a drowned world.

When sleep finally took me, it was with Nais’s hand in mine, and I woke to find her watching me, her gaze soft with something like peace. Rested, we were readier to accept our fate—to trust that the gods had spared us for purpose, not cruelty.

Below deck, the Ark revealed its secrets. A marvel of ancient craft, it showed no seam or leak, its timbers fused by forgotten arts into something harder than iron. Within its belly lay stores enough for a voyage without end: grain in vast tanks, sweet water in others, vials of medicines, tools, and weapons.

And then, the writings.

In a great chest at the Ark’s heart lay the accumulated knowledge of Atlantis—secrets of power, of life and death, of forces that had shattered empires. I read some, and fear coiled in me. These were the same mysteries Phorenice had sought, the same that had doomed our homeland. The weight of guarding them was too great.

So I took fire to the wax tablets, melting the words away. If the gods willed these secrets to endure, they would find new scribes. I would not be their keeper.

And when at last we found land, I wrote anew—not of the old world’s forbidden arts, but of its fall, and of the woman beside me, who made survival more than mere endurance.
