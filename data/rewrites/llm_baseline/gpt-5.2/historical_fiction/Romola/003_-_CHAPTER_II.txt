After Bratti had melted back into the knot of men whose words clacked together like dice in a cup, the young stranger stood a while in the broken shade of the Loggia, listening to a Florentine morning he could not quite translate.

The piazza was never silent; it breathed. Hooves struck the stones with a hollow patience; a cartwheel complained in its wooden throat; vendors called their wares in clipped Tuscan that rose and fell like a song everyone knew by heart. But through it all ran another sound—the quick, excited undertone of rumor, the crowd’s shared appetite. He was outside that hunger. Whatever stirred their city-pride and city-fears was a dish cooked for native tongues.

He waited for Bratti’s promised escort until waiting began to taste of dust. The air itself teased him: a drift of warm bread from some unseen oven-mouth; the green bite of crushed fennel underfoot; and, sharper than all, the clean animal sweetness of milk carried on a faint current, as if morning were ladling itself into cups.

At the suggestion of that scent, he thrust his hand beneath his torn mantle into the purse at his waist and searched it with fingers grown suddenly meticulous. He turned the little pouch inside-out, as though a coin might be hiding in a fold the way a bay-leaf hides in stew.

Nothing.

“Not an obolus, by Jupiter,” he muttered—not in Tuscan, not in any Italian a Florentine ear would claim as kin. The words fell soft, almost amused, but his mouth had the dry pull of hunger. “I thought I had one poor piece left. Then I must get my breakfast for love.”

He drifted along the edge of the market, looking, not for the noisiest stall but for the quietest mercy. The farther he went from the storm of talkers, the more the piazza’s other music came clear: the slow snort of beasts, the rope creak, the occasional dull splash of water from a bucket. Near a corner where the gossip thinned, two mules stood as if anchored there by habit, their collars bright with red tassels that bobbed when they shifted weight. One mule bore wooden milk-vessels, their staves darkened by countless mornings; the other carried panniers heaped with herbs and salads—mint, chicory, tender lettuces, fronds of something feathery that smelled of anise when the breeze lifted it.

Leaning with her elbow on the milk-mule’s neck was a young girl, scarcely more than sixteen, her face framed in a red hood that made her cheeks look rounder, her prettiness almost infant-like because her hair was hidden. Sleep had taken her where she stood; she dozed in that half-leaning, half-standing posture of someone who had risen in the dim before dawn, strained milk by lamplight, tied down lids, and walked miles from a castello with the steady, resigned endurance of country feet.

The stranger had no compunction about waking her—but he did it as gently as one might lift a crust from a cooling loaf to see if it had browned. He touched her cheek lightly, and the girl’s dream must have been full of the herb-mule’s perfume, for when her lips parted with a faint breath it was as if a sprig of thyme had brushed them.

She started awake, blue eyes opening wide, and the confusion that followed sleep in a public place flushed her skin. She heard him speaking, but the voice was strange—soft, and not belonging to the vowels she knew. For a heartbeat she assumed it must be nonsense, and she turned her face a little aside, lifting the corner of her green serge mantle as a screen, the way one hides a bowl from flies.

He tried again, shaping his words toward the few phrases he knew would be familiar, letting his tone play at beggary. “Forgive me, pretty one, for awaking you. I’m dying with hunger, and the scent of milk makes breakfast seem more desirable than ever.”

“Muoio di fame,” he said, as if the common cry could buy him pardon.

This time she understood. The mantle dropped. Without answering, she reached up to the mule’s load, unfastened a lid with practiced fingers, and in moments held out a large cup of milk. It was still warm—not hot, but the living warmth that clings when milk has been handled quickly, carried close, kept from the morning chill. Its scent was clean and thick, and when he lifted it he felt the rim damp with the mule’s patient nearness and the girl’s early labor.

He made no more compliments before drinking. He drank as if he meant to remember it: the first swallow sweet and grassy, the next richer, a faint animal salt at the end. The piazza’s noise receded behind that taste. While he drank, the little maiden gathered courage to study him. His long dark curls fell in disordered abundance; his clothes, though damaged, were not those of any beggar she had ever been warned against—too fine in their ruin, as if they had been made for a body that knew music and pride.

While she looked, her hand moved of its own accord toward a bag hanging by the mule’s side. When he lowered the cup, she was holding out bread: a thick piece, torn from a loaf with a dense, honest crumb, the crust browned hard enough to sing under teeth. The smell of it—wheat and smoke—made his stomach clench.

“But perhaps that is your own breakfast,” he said, eyeing her with an instinctive gentleness. Hunger did not wholly drown courtesy in him.

“No,” she answered quickly, and then, as if words were too much, she only pushed it nearer. The glance she gave him—blue, shy, encouraging—was like the quiet offer a hearth gives to cold hands.

“No,” he said again, smiling, “I have had enough without payment. A thousand thanks, my gentle one.”

Still she pushed the bread nearer, almost impatient at his refusal, and as his long dark eyes rested on her baby-face, she found herself meeting them for longer than was safe. The piazza, with its hawkers and carts, seemed suddenly as private as a kitchen if one keeps one’s back to the door.

“Ah then, if I must take the bread,” he said, laying his hand on it, “I shall get bolder still, and beg for another kiss to make the bread sweeter.”

His speech had grown wonderfully intelligible despite that foreign softness. For an instant she blushed so deeply it seemed the red hood had overflowed into her cheeks. She lifted her mantle again to her mouth, half laughing, half afraid, a gesture that said no without the firmness of no.

He leaned forward—too confidently—and his fingers touched the arm that held the shielding mantle.

A harsh voice snapped close to his ear, cutting through the market’s hum like iron on stone.

“Who are you—with a murrain to you? No honest buyer, I’ll warrant, but a hanger-on of the dicers—or something worse. Go! Dance off, and find fitter company, or I’ll give you a tune to a little quicker time than you’ll like.”

He drew back and looked at the speaker with a glance provokingly free of alarm. Saucy amusement broke across his face into a broad, beaming smile as he took in the woman who had appeared like a kitchen-spoon raised against a thief.

She was stout but brawny, the strength of a working body wrapped in a man’s jerkin slipped over her green serge gamurra. A peaked hood—perhaps once part of a better mantle—was fastened around her sunburnt face. Coarse and prematurely wrinkled as it was, it carried a half-sad, half-ludicrous maternal resemblance to the tender baby-face of the little maiden—a resemblance like a prophecy one doesn’t want to read, the same features spoken in a harsher dialect.

There was something almost disarming in that bright young smile, but Monna Ghita was not a woman to betray weakness. Her anger only grew louder, as if volume could guard her daughter.

“Yes, yes, you can grin as well as other monkeys in cap and jerkin. You’re a minstrel or a mountebank, I’ll be sworn. You look for all the world as silly as a tumbler when he’s been upside down and has got on his heels again.” Then she wheeled on the girl, whose frightened face invited scolding more safely than a stranger’s eyes. “And what fool’s tricks hast thou been after, Tessa? Giving away the milk and victuals, it seems—ay, ay, thou’dst carry water in thy ears for any idle vagabond that didn’t like to stoop for it, thou silly staring rabbit! Turn thy back, and lift the herbs out of the panniers, else I’ll make thee say a few Aves without counting.”

Tessa obeyed at once, fingers trembling as she began to fuss with the herb-panniers, pretending busyness as a shield. The stranger, still holding the bread he had not yet bitten, turned his smile into something pleading.

“Nay, Madonna,” he said, voice soft as steam rising from a pot, “don’t be angry with your pretty Tessa for taking pity on a hungry traveller, who found himself unexpectedly without a quattrino. Your handsome face looks so well when it frowns, that I long to see it illuminated by a smile.”

“Va via! I know what paste you are made of.” Monna Ghita’s eyes narrowed; she had kneaded enough dough in her life to distrust anything too airy. “You may tickle me with that straw a good long while before I shall laugh, I can tell you. Get along, with a bad Easter! else I’ll make a beauty-spot or two on that face of yours that shall spoil your kissing on this side Advent.”

As she lifted her formidable hands—those talons ready to mark his skin like the imprint of a bread-slicer—two men had come near enough to hear, drawn by the promise of sport the way boys are drawn by the smell of roasting chestnuts.

Bratti had arrived a minute or two before and was murmuring to his companion, “What think you of this pretty parrot, Nello? Doesn’t his tongue smack of Venice?”

“Nay, Bratti,” said the barber under his breath, with the quiet vanity of a man whose trade was intimacy, “thy wisdom has much of the ass in it, as I told thee just now—especially about the ears. This stranger is a Greek, else I’m not the barber who has had the sole and exclusive shaving of the excellent Demetrio, and drawn more than one sorry tooth from his learned jaw. And this youth might be taken to have come straight from Olympus—at least when he has had a touch of my razor.”

Then Nello raised his voice, not sorry to stir the pot. “Orsù! Monna Ghita! What has happened to cause such a thunderstorm? Has this young stranger been misbehaving himself?”

“By San Giovanni!” Bratti put in, cautious still, his earlier suspicions about the stranger’s shabby finery and glinting jewels not entirely cooled. “He did right to run away from me, if he meant to get into mischief. I can swear I found him under the Loggia de’ Cerchi, with a ring on his finger such as I’ve seen worn by Bernardo Rucellai himself. Not another rusty nail’s worth do I know about him.”

“The fact is,” said Nello, eyeing the stranger good-humouredly, “this bello giovane has been a little too presumptuous in admiring the charms of Monna Ghita, and has attempted to kiss her while her daughter’s back is turned—for I observe that the pretty Tessa is too busy to look this way at present. Was it not so, Messer?” he finished, with a courtesy that made mockery taste like honey.

“You have divined the offence like a soothsayer,” the stranger said, laughing. “Only that I had not the good fortune to find Monna Ghita here at first. I begged a cup of milk from her daughter, and had accepted this gift of bread—for which I was making a humble offering of gratitude—before I had the higher pleasure of being face to face with these riper charms which I was perhaps too bold in admiring.”

“Va, va! Be off, every one of you, and stay in purgatory till I pay to get you out, will you?” Monna Ghita barked, elbowing Nello aside. She seized her mule’s lead and shoved forward, forcing the stranger to jump away as an approaching market-cart ground toward them, its axle groaning and its load rattling like bones in a basket. “Tessa, thou simpleton, bring forward thy mule a bit: the cart will be upon us.”

As Tessa turned to take the bridle, she stole one timid glance at the stranger. It lasted no longer than it takes to taste whether soup needs salt, but in it she caught his hand lifting in a small beckoning motion—a private farewell smuggled through the public clamor. He had been watching for that single opening, that moment when her mother’s body and the cart’s bulk made a wall between them.

Across the cart, Bratti raised his voice. “Ebbene! I leave you with Nello, young man—for there’s no pushing my bag and basket any farther, and I have business at home. But you’ll remember our bargain, because if you found Tessa without me, it was not my fault. Nello will show you my shop in the Ferravecchi, and I’ll not turn my back on you.”

“A thousand thanks, friend!” the stranger called, laughter still in him, as if the milk had warmed not only his belly but his courage.

Then he turned away with Nello up the narrow street that led most directly toward the Piazza del Duomo, leaving behind him the scent of herbs and the warm memory of bread taken for love, while the market’s voices closed again like a door—Tuscan words, Tuscan quarrels, Tuscan laughter—each sound a kind of belonging he could hear, but not wholly speak.
