The sea, all morning, kept its own rough litany under the cliff—hush, lift, boom—each breaker arriving with the punctuality of an old refrain, the gulls knitting their thin cries through it. A clean wind came off the water and went roaming over the land, worrying every hedge and tuft of grass into speech. The sun laid a bright hand on everything; even the grey rocks below wore a kind of polish where the spray had been at them for years.

Trent, who had slept as if some small hard object lay always under his heart, went down before eight to a pool among the boulders, as the hotel man had directed. The descent was steep, the chalk dusty underfoot; the air smelled of salt and crushed thrift. He stripped, and the wind took his skin the way a knife takes the rind from fruit. Then he dived.

The water was so clear it seemed to have a taste—cold, metallic, and pure. He went deep, letting the weight of it press thought out of him, then struck out between the rock walls into the open. The coast-wise current pulled sideways like a hand at his ankle; he forced himself against it until his lungs burned, then turned back, battered and refreshed, to the sheltered basin. When he climbed out, the world’s noises had shifted: the sea’s roar was the same, but now he could hear his own blood and the rasp of pebbles under his feet, and it was as if the case, for a few minutes, had been washed down to its bare bones.

Ten minutes later he was scaling the cliff again, salt drying on him in a fine crust. As he climbed he felt, with a kind of shame, the disgust return. It was the day of the inquest—the day after his arrival—and the affair in hand had taken him like an illness: repellent, incessant, and absorbing.

He had carried matters not much further after leaving the American on the road to Bishopsbridge. Yesterday afternoon he had walked into the town with Mr. Cupples beside him, and there had made certain purchases at a chemist’s—things that smelled sharply of spirits and disinfectant—conferred privately for some time with a photographer whose hands were stained with dark chemicals, sent off a reply-paid telegram, and made an enquiry at the telephone exchange, where the air held that curious mixture of varnished wood and warm metal. He had said little of the case to Mr. Cupples, who for his part seemed incurious, and nothing at all of the results of his investigation or the steps he meant to take.

After their return he had written a long dispatch for the _Record_ and sent it to be telegraphed by the proud hands of the paper’s local representative; afterwards he had dined with Mr. Cupples and spent the rest of the evening alone on the veranda, listening to the distant sea and the nearer, lesser noises that domestic life makes when it is trying not to disturb itself: a door softly latched, cutlery laid down, a footfall muffled on boards.

This morning, as the path took him along the cliff top, he told himself that he had never taken up a case he liked so little, nor one that drew him so irresistibly. In the honest gold of the new day it looked uglier, not better. Whatever he suspected—and whatever he almost knew—had occupied his brain all night to the exclusion of sleep; and now, though he had scrubbed his body clean in fierce water, he only saw the darkness he believed in with more exactness, and was bitterly repelled by the motive at which he guessed.

Yet his zeal was awake again, that hunter’s quickening in the blood. He would neither slacken nor spare. In a business like this there need be no compunction. By the end of the day, he thought, his net might be complete. There was work to do before the inquest, and, with a vivid expectancy that he distrusted even as he felt it, he waited for the answer to the telegram he had flung out yesterday like a stone into fog.

The path wound along the cliff edge, and nearing a place he had marked from below—where the face had fallen away long ago—he approached the brink, meaning to look down and watch the most delicately beautiful of all movements: light water washing broken rock as a cook’s hand washes flour off a board, again and again, unwearied.

But there was no rock immediately below. A few feet down a broad ledge thrust out, a rough platform as large as a room, thick with wiry grass and walled steeply on three sides. There, close to the verge where the cliff dropped sheer, a woman sat with her arms about her drawn-up knees. Her eyes were fixed on the trailing smoke of a distant liner, as if the faint grey thread were writing a sentence she could read and no one else.

Trent halted for no more than a heartbeat—training had taught him to live in his eyes without staring—yet the picture printed itself on him with a permanence he could feel.

Everything about her was black: suede shoes, dress, the hat pinned on the grass beside her. The black was lustreless and fine, cut and fitted with a practised hand, as if she had been dressing for years not to attract attention but to command her own shape. The wind played with her thick dark hair, worrying loose tiny curls from the mass at her nape and blowing back the broad bandeaux that should have smoothed her forehead. Her face had a southern pallor, but the wind had kissed colour into her cheek. In profile the features were delicate and regular, nothing hard—yet the brows, black and bending down almost to meet, gave her repose a look of severity that the open curves of her mouth redeemed.

She looked at once modern and immemorial: like a figure painted yesterday, yet carrying the glow of those vigorous beings who have always faced sun and wind and sea in the prime of the year. There was a sureness in her womanhood, unmixed and unashamed, that did not feel quite English; still less American.

He passed on, unheard on the turf above. As he went, the woman moved. She unclasped her long hands, stretched herself with a feline grace, and then slowly raised her head and extended her arms, fingers open and curving, as if to gather into her body all the morning’s overwhelming sanity. It was a gesture of freedom—a resolution to be, to possess, to go forward, perhaps to enjoy.

So he saw her for an instant, and did not turn.

He knew suddenly who she must be; and it was as if a curtain of gloom were drawn between him and the splendour of the day. The sea’s great voice went on below, but it sounded farther away.

At breakfast Mr. Cupples found him little inclined to talk. Trent pleaded a restless night. Mr. Cupples, on the other hand, had the alertness of a small bird at first light. The prospect of the inquest enlivened him. He entertained Trent with a disquisition upon the history of the coroner’s court, that ancient tribunal with its freedom from the shackles of rule and precedent, and passed from that to the case to come before it.

“Young Bunner mentioned to me last night,” he said, buttering his toast with a smooth and deliberate hand, “the hypothesis he puts forward regarding the crime. A very remarkable young man, Trent. His meaning is occasionally obscure, but in my opinion he is gifted with a clearheaded knowledge of the world quite unusual in one of his apparent age. His promotion by Manderson to the position of principal lieutenant speaks for itself. He has assumed with perfect confidence the control at this end of the wire, as he expresses it, of the complicated business situation caused by the death of his principal. He has advised me very wisely as to the steps I should take on Mabel’s behalf, and the best course for her to pursue until effect has been given to the provisions of the will.”

Trent listened with the detached weariness of a man chewing food without tasting it.

“I was accordingly less disposed than I might otherwise have been,” Mr. Cupples continued, “to regard his suggestion of an industrial vendetta as far-fetched. When I questioned him he was able to describe a number of cases in which attacks—too often successful—had been made upon the lives of persons who had incurred the hostility of powerful labour organizations. This is a terrible time in which we live, my dear boy. There is none recorded in history, I think, in which the disproportion between the material and the moral constituents of society has been so great or so menacing to the permanence of the fabric. But nowhere, in my judgement, is the prospect so dark as it is in the United States.”

“I thought,” said Trent listlessly, “that Puritanism was about as strong there as the money-getting craze.”

“Your remark,” Mr. Cupples replied, with as near an approach to humour as was possible to him, “is not in the nature of a testimonial to what you call Puritanism—a convenient rather than an accurate term. The sense of your observation, however, is sound. It is extremely well illustrated by Manderson himself, who had, I believe, the virtues of purity, abstinence, and self-restraint in their strongest form. No, Trent; there are other and more worthy things among the moral constituents of which I spoke. In our finite nature, the more we preoccupy ourselves with the bewildering complexity of external apparatus which science places in our hands, the less vigour have we left for the development of the holier purposes of humanity within us.”

He went on placidly, as if reciting a familiar recipe: agricultural machinery abolishing Harvest Home; mechanical travel abolishing the inn, or all that was best in it. Outside, the morning made its own music—wind in leaves, a cart far off creaking like a slow fiddle—but inside the dining-room there was the softer racket of cups and knives, and a radio-like murmur of other breakfasts.

“It needs epigrammatic expression,” Trent said, rising. “If only it could be crystallized into some handy formula, like ‘No Popery’ or ‘Tax the Foreigner,’ you’d find multitudes to go to the stake for it. But you were planning to go to White Gables before the inquest, I think. You ought to be off if you’re to get back to the court in time. I have something to attend to there myself. We might walk up together. I’ll just fetch my camera.”

“By all means,” Mr. Cupples answered; and they set off at once in the ever-growing warmth.

As they walked, the soundscape altered with each turn: the surf became a duller drum as the land rose, and in its place came the close noises of hedgerows—small birds ticking and chirring, the whisper of grass heads rubbing together. Trent found himself noticing such things with the sharpness of a man who clings to the physical world because the moral one is sour.

The roof of White Gables showed at last, a surly patch of dull red against dark trees. It harmonized with Trent’s mood; he felt heavy, sinister, troubled. If a blow must fall that might strike down the creature radiant of beauty and life he had seen on the ledge, he did not wish it to come from his own hand. Chivalry had been bred into him by his mother’s first teachings, but at this moment the horror of bruising anything so lovely was as much the artist’s revulsion as the gentleman’s.

And yet—was the hunt to end in nothing? The quality of the affair made forbearance an agony. There never was such a case; and he alone, he was convinced, held its truth under his hand. At least, he determined, this day should show whether what he believed was delusion. He would trample compunction underfoot until he was quite sure there was any call for it. Before noon, he told himself, he would know.

At the gate of the drive they saw Marlowe and the American standing in talk before the front door. In the shadow of the porch stood the lady in black.

She saw them and came gravely forward over the lawn. She moved exactly as Trent had known she would move—erect and balanced, stepping lightly, as if the ground were a thing she used rather than trusted. When she welcomed him on Mr. Cupples’s presentation, her eyes—brown with golden flecks—observed him kindly. The dreamy halo he had seen about her head above the sea was gone; pale composure, worn like a mask of distress, took its place. Her voice was low, even, giving the appropriate commonplace as if the words were a set form one learned early and spoke without tasting.

After a few words to Mr. Cupples she turned her eyes on Trent again.

“I hope you will succeed,” she said earnestly. “Do you think you will succeed?”

He made up his mind as the words left her lips.

“I believe I shall do so, Mrs. Manderson,” he said. “When I have the case sufficiently complete I shall ask you to let me see you and tell you about it. It may be necessary to consult you before the facts are published.”

She looked puzzled. For an instant distress showed through the mask, quick as a shadow passing over a bowl of milk.

“If it is necessary, of course you shall do so,” she said.

Trent hesitated on the brink of his next speech. He remembered that she had not wished to repeat to him the story already given to the inspector—or to be questioned at all. He was not unconscious that he desired to hear her voice and watch her face a little longer, if it might be; but what troubled him now was not mere curiosity. There was a queer thing that fitted nowhere into the pattern whose corners he had by this time forced the other queer things into place. It was possible she could explain it away in a breath. It was unlikely anyone else could.

He summoned resolution.

“You have been so kind,” he said, “in allowing me access to the house and every opportunity of studying the case, that I am going to ask leave to put a question or two to yourself—nothing that you would rather not answer, I think. May I?”

She glanced at him wearily, as if the weight of other people’s needs had begun to bruise her.

“It would be stupid of me to refuse. Ask your questions, Mr. Trent.”

“It’s only this,” said Trent hurriedly. “We know that your husband lately drew an unusually large sum of ready money from his London bankers, and was keeping it here. It is here now, in fact. Have you any idea why he should have done that?”

Her eyes opened in astonishment.

“I cannot imagine,” she said. “I did not know he had done so. I am very much surprised to hear it.”

“Why is it surprising?”

“I thought my husband had very little money in the house. On Sunday night, just before he went out in the motor, he came into the drawing-room where I was sitting. He seemed to be irritated about something, and asked me at once if I had any notes or gold I could let him have until next day.”

She spoke evenly, but Trent heard, under the evenness, the small friction of memory—like the sound of paper rubbed between finger and thumb.

“I was surprised at that,” she went on, “because he was never without money; he made it a rule to carry a hundred pounds or so about him always in a note-case. I unlocked my escritoire and gave him all I had by me. It was nearly thirty pounds.”

“And he did not tell you why he wanted it?”

“No. He put it in his pocket, and then said that Mr. Marlowe had persuaded him to go for a run in the motor by moonlight, and he thought it might help him to sleep. He had been sleeping badly, as perhaps you know. Then he went off with Mr. Marlowe. I thought it odd he should need money on Sunday night, but I soon forgot about it. I never remembered it again until now.”

“It was curious, certainly,” said Trent, staring past her shoulder, as if the answer were written somewhere on the boundary of the fields.

Mr. Cupples began to speak to his niece of arrangements for the inquest, and Trent moved away to where Marlowe was pacing slowly upon the lawn.

The young man seemed relieved to talk about the coming business of the day. He still looked tired out and nervous, but there was a quiet humour in his account of the pomposities of the local police and the portentous airs of Dr. Stock. Trent listened, answering enough to keep him going, and then gradually turned the talk toward the problem of the crime. Marlowe’s gravity returned.

“Bunner has told me what he thinks,” he said when Trent referred to the American’s theory. “I don’t find myself convinced by it, because it doesn’t really explain some of the oddest facts. But I’ve lived long enough in the United States to know that such a stroke of revenge, done in a secret, melodramatic way, is not an unlikely thing. It is quite characteristic of certain sections of the labour movement there. Americans have a taste and a talent for that sort of business. Do you know _Huckleberry Finn_?”

“Do I know my own name?” Trent replied, and for a moment the sea-wind seemed to lift the heaviness in him. “I think the most American thing in that great American epic is Tom Sawyer’s elaboration of an extremely difficult and romantic scheme, taking days to carry out, for securing the escape of Jim—which could have been managed quite easily in twenty minutes. You know how fond they are of lodges and brotherhoods. Every college club has its secret signs and handgrips. You’ve heard of the Know-Nothing movement, and the Ku Klux Klan. Then look at Brigham Young’s penny-dreadful tyranny in Utah—with real blood. The founders of the Mormon State were of the purest Yankee stock; and you know what they did. It’s all part of the same mental tendency. They make fun of it among themselves. For my part, I take it very seriously.”

“It can have a hideous side,” Trent said, “when you get it in connection with crime—or vice—or even mere luxury. But I’ve a sort of sneaking respect for the determination to make life interesting in spite of civilization. To return to the matter in hand—has it struck you as possible that Manderson’s mind was affected, to some extent, by this menace Bunner believes in? For instance, it was rather an extraordinary thing to send you posting off in the middle of the night.”

“About ten o’clock,” Marlowe corrected. “Though if he’d actually roused me at midnight I shouldn’t have been much surprised. It all chimes in with what we’ve just been saying. Manderson had a strong streak of the national taste for dramatic proceedings. He was rather fond of his reputation for unexpected strokes and for going for his object with ruthless directness through every opposing consideration. He’d decided suddenly that he wanted word from this man Harris—”

“Who is Harris?” Trent broke in.

“Nobody knows. Even Bunner never heard of him and can’t imagine what the business in hand was. All I know is that when I went up to London last week to attend to various things I booked a deck-cabin, at Manderson’s request, for a Mr. George Harris on the boat that sailed on Monday. It seems Manderson suddenly found he wanted news from Harris which presumably was too secret for the telegraph; and there was no train that served; so I was sent off as you know.”

Trent looked round to make sure they were not overheard. The morning was full of noise, yet the house behind them seemed to swallow sound; only the distant surf and a faint clink from the porch carried. He faced Marlowe gravely.

“There is one thing I may tell you,” he said quietly, “that I don’t think you know. Martin the butler caught a few words at the end of your conversation with Manderson in the orchard before you started with him in the car. He heard him say, ‘If Harris is there, every moment is of importance.’ Now, Mr. Marlowe—you know my business here. I am sent to make enquiries, and you mustn’t take offence. I want to ask you if, in the face of that sentence, you will repeat that you know nothing of what the business was.”

Marlowe shook his head, with the tired patience of a man who has already been made to repeat himself to deaf authority.

“I know nothing, indeed. I’m not easily offended, and your question is quite fair. What passed during that conversation I have already told the detective. Manderson plainly said he could not tell me what it was all about. He simply wanted me to find Harris, tell him he desired to know how matters stood, and bring back a letter or message from him. Harris, I was further told, might not turn up. If he did—‘every moment was of importance.’ And now you know as much as I do.”

“That talk took place before he told his wife that you were taking him for a moonlight run,” Trent said. “Why did he conceal your errand in that way, I wonder.”

Marlowe made a helpless gesture.

“Why? I can guess no better than you.”

“Why,” Trent muttered, gazing at the ground as if he expected to see a footprint answer him, “did he conceal it—from Mrs. Manderson?”

He looked up.

“And from Martin,” Marlowe amended coolly. “He was told the same thing.”

With a sudden movement of his head Trent seemed to dismiss the subject. He drew a letter-case from his breast pocket and took out two small leaves of clean, fresh paper.

“Just look at these two slips, Mr. Marlowe,” he said. “Did you ever see them before? Have you any idea where they come from?”

Marlowe took one in each hand and examined them, turning them over as if expecting a hidden watermark to speak.

“They seem to have been cut with a knife or scissors from a small diary for this year—from the October pages,” he said at last. “I see no writing of any kind on them. Nobody here has any such diary so far as I know. What about them?”

“There may be nothing in it,” Trent said dubiously. “Any one in the house might have such a diary without your having seen it. But I didn’t much expect you’d be able to identify the leaves—in fact, I should have been surprised if you had.”

He stopped as Mrs. Manderson came towards them again.

“My uncle thinks we should be going now,” she said. Her voice had the same evenness, but her eyes were a shade darker, as if tiredness had steeped in them overnight.

“I think I will walk on with Mr. Bunner,” Mr. Cupples said, joining them. “There are certain business matters that must be disposed of as soon as possible. Will you come on with these two gentlemen, Mabel? We will wait for you before we reach the place.”

Trent turned to her.

“Mrs. Manderson will excuse me, I hope,” he said. “I really came up this morning in order to look about me here for some indications I thought I might possibly find. I had not thought of attending the—the court just yet.”

She looked at him with perfect candour, as if candour were a dialect she spoke more fluently than any other, and could not, even if she wished, translate into something less direct.

“Of course, Mr. Trent. Please do exactly as you wish. We are all relying upon you. If you will wait a few moments, Mr. Marlowe, I shall be ready.”

She went into the house. Her uncle and the American had already strolled towards the gate, their footsteps making the dry gravel talk.

Trent looked into Marlowe’s eyes.

“That is a wonderful woman,” he said in a lowered voice.

“You say so without knowing her,” Marlowe replied, also low.

“She is more than that,” Trent said.

He said nothing further, only stared out over the fields towards the sea, where the air, even at this distance, carried a faint salt edge.

In the silence a new sound rose: hobnailed haste on the road, quick and determined, like a small drum beaten close by. Down the lane a boy appeared, trotting towards them from the direction of the hotel. In his hand was an orange envelope—unmistakable at a distance, a bright commercial note in the morning’s natural music.

Trent watched him with an indifferent eye as the boy met and passed the others and came on.

Then, as if merely to keep his voice from following his thoughts into dangerous places, Trent turned to Marlowe.

“Apropos of nothing in particular,” he said, “were you at Oxford?”

“Yes,” Marlowe answered. “Why do you ask?”

“I just wondered if I was right in my guess. It’s one of the things you can often tell about a man, isn’t it?”

“I suppose so. Each of us is marked in one way or another, perhaps.”

“I should have said you were an artist,” Marlowe added, “if I hadn’t known it.”

“Why? Does my hair want cutting?”

“Oh, no. It’s only that you look at things and people as I’ve seen artists do—with an eye that moves steadily from detail to detail—rather looking them over than looking at them.”

The boy came up panting.

“Telegram for you, sir,” he said to Trent. “Just come, sir.”

Trent tore open the envelope with a brief apology. As he read the slip his eyes lighted up so visibly that Marlowe’s tired face softened into a smile.

“It must be good news,” Marlowe murmured, half to himself.

Trent looked up. The glance he gave held everything close, as if it were food he would not yet put on the table.

“Not exactly news,” he said. “It only tells me that another little guess of mine was a good one.”
