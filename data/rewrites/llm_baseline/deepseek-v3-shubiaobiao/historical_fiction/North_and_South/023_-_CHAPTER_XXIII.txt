Margaret had scarcely departed five minutes when Mr. Thornton burst into the house, his face alight with restless energy.

“I couldn’t come sooner—the superintendent insisted on— Where is she?” His gaze swept the dining room before fixing, almost accusingly, upon his mother, who was calmly restoring order to the disarranged furniture.

“Miss Hale?” he demanded when she did not immediately answer.

“Gone home,” she replied tersely.

“Gone?” The word was sharp with disbelief.

“Yes. She had recovered sufficiently. Truthfully, I doubt the injury was severe—some people faint at the slightest provocation.”

His jaw tightened as he paced. “She shouldn’t have left. She wasn’t fit for it.”

“She claimed she was. So did Mr. Lowe. I fetched him myself.”

“Thank you, Mother.” He extended his hand briefly, an unspoken gesture of gratitude, but she ignored it.

“What of the Irish workers?”

“Sent to the Dragon for a meal—poor souls. Fortunately, I encountered Father Grady and persuaded him to speak with them, to dissuade them from marching off in protest. How did Miss Hale return? Surely not on foot?”

“By cab. Every detail was seen to—even the fare.” Her voice was clipped. “Let us speak of something else. She has caused enough disruption.”

“I might have fared far worse without her.”

“Have you grown so feeble that a girl must shield you?” Mrs. Thornton’s tone was edged with scorn.

Color flared in his cheeks. “Few would have stepped between me and a mob’s blows—struck with genuine malice, no less.”

“A girl in love will endure much,” she remarked coolly.

“Mother!” He halted, his breath ragged, fists clenched at his sides. The force of his restraint startled her. She could not decipher the storm in his eyes—anger, pride, joy, doubt, all warring beneath the surface. It unsettled her, this raw emotion she could not name.

Turning away, she busied herself with a smudge on the sofa’s polished arm, her back to him longer than necessary. When she spoke again, her voice was strained.

“You’ve taken measures about the rioters, I presume? The police were useless, as ever.”

“I saw three or four struggling valiantly when the gates fell. More arrived too late. Identification won’t be an issue.”

“And tonight? Will they return?”

“I’m securing a guard. I meet Captain Hanbury shortly.”

“Tea first,” she insisted.

“Tea? Very well. But don’t wait up.”

“You expect me to sleep before knowing you’re safe?”

His hesitation was brief. “If time allows, I’ll stop in Crampton after the police station.”

Their eyes locked.

“Why Crampton?”

“To inquire after Miss Hale.”

“I’ll send Williams with the water-bed she requested. He can convey our regards.”

“I must go myself.”

“Not solely for her sake, surely?”

His reply was measured. “I owe her thanks. She stood between me and violence.”

“Why venture into the fray at all? It was reckless.”

A sharp glance told him she remained unaware of his earlier exchange with Margaret. He deflected. “Will you be uneasy alone? I can summon police now.”

Mrs. Thornton withdrew, her usual decisiveness frayed. Left alone, Thornton’s thoughts strayed from business to the memory of Margaret’s arms around him—her fierce, fleeting embrace that left his pulse unsteady.

Tea was a quiet affair, save for Fanny’s ceaseless recounting of her own fright.

“Enough,” he interrupted, rising. “The reality was trial enough.”

As he turned to leave, his mother caught his arm.

“Return here before going to the Hales’.” Her voice was low, urgent.

Fanny smirked to herself.

“Why? Will it disturb them?”

“John, grant me this one evening. Tomorrow, you’ll—” Her rare plea silenced him. She had never begged in vain.

“I’ll come straight back. But you’ll inquire after them—after *her*?”

Mrs. Thornton was no companion to Fanny in his absence, but upon his return, she hung on every detail of his precautions against further unrest. His purpose was clear: justice, swift and unrelenting.

Later, abruptly, he asked, “You know what I must say to Miss Hale tomorrow?”

She met his gaze. “You can do no less.”

“Do *less*? I don’t follow.”

“After her display of feeling, honor compels you.”

“Honor?” He laughed bitterly. “What *feelings* do you imagine drove her?”

“Need we quarrel? She shielded you at her own peril.”

“She did.” He stepped closer. “But Mother—I dare not hope. I’ve never lacked courage, yet I cannot believe *she* cares for me.”

“Don’t be absurd. She overcame her pride—no small feat. That is proof enough.” Her smile was slow, wistful. “I asked you to delay only to keep you a little longer. After tonight, I stand second.”

“Dearest Mother!” Yet his thoughts veered instantly back to Margaret. “But she does not love me. Still, I must lay myself at her feet—even if the chance is one in a million.”

“Fear not,” she said, crushing her own ache at his blindness to her devotion. “If love is the measure, she is worthy.” She kissed him, regal as ever, but once behind her locked door, she wept.

* * *

Margaret returned to the quiet parlor where her parents sat, her pallor pronounced.

“Mrs. Thornton will send the bed,” she murmured.

“You look exhausted,” her mother fretted. “Was the heat unbearable?”

“The streets were uneasy.” A flush rose and faded just as quickly.

“Bessy Higgins asked for you, but you’re too weary.”

“Yes. I cannot go.”

She trembled as she prepared tea, grateful her father’s attention remained fixed on her mother. Only when alone did she permit herself to reflect.

*What madness possessed me? To rush into chaos like some lovesick heroine!* Her cheeks burned at the memory. *They must think me infatuated. Yet I’d do it again—to spare one cruel act. Let them think what they will. My conscience is clear.*

Dixon entered. “The water-bed has arrived. Mrs. Thornton’s man asked particularly after *you*, miss.”

“Me?” Margaret straightened. “I am perfectly well.” But her face was bloodless, her head throbbing.

Her father soon joined her, seeking distraction. She obliged, weaving light conversation—never mentioning the riot. The mere thought sickened her.

“Sleep well, dear,” he urged at last. “You need rest.”

Alone, she surrendered to exhaustion. That night, fevered visions tormented her—countless eyes watching, judging, stripping her bare. Shame pressed upon her like a weight, yet she could not escape their relentless gaze.
