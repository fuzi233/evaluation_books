I stood by the hall door, the pony-chaise waiting, wheels silent but ready, anticipating the possibility that Mr. Franklin might choose to depart by rail that very night. Yet, the clatter of heavy trunks descending the stairs, followed closely by Mr. Franklin himself, confirmed that he had, for once in his life, resolved upon staying.

"So you have truly made your decision, sir?" I asked, stepping forward to greet him. "Might you not wait a few days longer? Surely, Miss Rachel deserves another chance?"

The veneer of composure that Mr. Franklin often wore seemed to have stripped away completely now that the moment of farewell approached. Instead of a verbal response, he extended a letter. It bore the seal of Lady Verinder, addressed to him. While the majority of the correspondence reiterated sentiments I had already encountered in previous missives, the final paragraph concerned Miss Rachel. This addition alone illuminated the ironclad nature of Mr. Franklin's resolve.

"You will likely wonder," Lady Verinder wrote, "at why I have allowed my own daughter to keep me in absolute ignorance regarding this catastrophe. A diamond of twenty thousand pounds has vanished, and I am forced to deduce that Miss Rachel knows precisely what has occurred. Some invisible power, some entity utterly unknown to me, has imposed a vow of silence upon her, driven by motives I cannot fathom. Could I possibly permit myself to be toyed with in such a fashion? It is entirely plausible, considering Miss Rachel's current state. She exists in a condition of nervous agitation so pitiable it breaks the heart. I dare not broach the subject of the Moonstone again until time has worked its healing balm. To aid this recovery, I have not hesitated to dismiss the police officer. The mystery that confounds us baffles him as well; he is a stranger to our grief, and his presence only adds to my sorrow, driving Rachel mad if so much as his name is mentioned.

"My future arrangements are as settled as circumstances permit. My intention is to take Rachel to London—to soothe her mind through a complete change of scenery and to seek the counsel of the finest medical minds. Will you join us in town? My dear Franklin, you must emulate my patience and await a more propitious season. Though your assistance in the initial inquiry was invaluable, it remains an unforgiven offense in light of Rachel's dreadful mental state. Acting blindly, you have inadvertently threatened her secret with exposure, thereby compounding the burden of anxiety she bears. It is impossible to excuse the stubbornness that holds you responsible for consequences unforeseen by either of us. She cannot be reasoned with; she can only be pitied. I am grieved to utter this, but for the present, separation between you and Rachel is advisable. My sole counsel to you is to grant her time."

I returned the letter, feeling a profound pang of sympathy for Mr. Franklin. I knew how deeply he cherished my young mistress, and I saw clearly how Lady Verinder's words had struck a fatal blow to his hopes. "You know the proverb, sir," I said gently. "When matters are at their nadir, they are destined to improve. Things could scarcely be more dire, Mr. Franklin, than they are now."

Mr. Franklin folded the letter, though his countenance offered little sign that my consolation had taken root.

"When I first arrived here from London with that accursed Diamond," he murmured, "I believed no household in England was happier than this one. Look at it now! Dispersed, fractured—the very atmosphere poisoned by mystery and suspicion! Do you recall that morning at the Shivering Sands, when we discussed my Uncle Herncastle and his birthday gift? The Moonstone has served the Colonel's vengeance, Betteredge, through methods the Colonel himself never imagined!"

With that, he clasped my hand and stepped out to the chaise. I followed him down the steps, the sight of him departing the old estate, where he had spent his happiest years, weighing heavily on my spirit. Penelope, visibly shaken by the turmoil within the house, approached us, tears streaming down her cheeks, to bid him adieu. Mr. Franklin kissed her forehead. I waved a hand, wishing to convey, *'You are warmly welcomed, sir.'* Other female servants emerged from the shadows, peering around the corner. He was a man universally beloved by the women. At the last moment, I halted the vehicle and pleaded as a favor that he write to us. He seemed oblivious, his gaze sweeping the grounds and house, taking a final, silent leave. "Sir, where are you bound?" I insisted, holding onto the harness. "Pray, tell us!" Mr. Franklin suddenly lowered his hat over his eyes. "Bound?" he echoed, the word dripping with despair. "I am going to the devil!" The pony shied violently, as if sensing a spiritual horror in the phrase. "God bless you, sir, wheresoever you go!" was all I could utter before he vanished from sight and sound. A kind, gentle soul, despite his flaws and follies! He left a void behind him, a sadness that lingered in the halls of my lady's house.

The following long summer evening descended upon the manor, casting a gloom that mirrored the internal desolation.

To keep my spirits from crumbling, I clung to my pipe and *Robinson Crusoe*. The women, save for Penelope, passed the hours discussing Rosanna's suicide. They were unanimous in their conviction that the poor girl had stolen the Moonstone and had perished in a frenzy of terror at the thought of exposure. My daughter, naturally, maintained her steadfast belief in Rosanna's innocence. However, her theory regarding the motive lay flat, failing just as my young lady's assertion of innocence had failed. It left Rosanna's clandestine journey to Frizinghall and her peculiar actions concerning the nightgown entirely unexplained. Pointing this out to Penelope would be futile; her objections, like a rainstorm against a waterproof coat, made no impression. Truth be told, my daughter inherits my disdain for pure logic, and in this regard, she has advanced considerably beyond her father.

On the following day, Sunday, the close carriage belonging to Mr. Ablewhite returned empty. The coachman delivered a message and written instructions intended for my lady's maid and Penelope. The note revealed that my mistress had resolved to take Miss Rachel to her residence in London on the ensuing Monday. Further instructions detailed the necessary attire and directed the maids to meet their mistresses in town at a specified hour. Most of the domestic staff were to accompany them. Having observed Miss Rachel's reluctance to return to the house following the recent events, my lady had opted to travel directly from Frizinghall. I was instructed to remain in the country, managing affairs both indoors and out, until further notice. Those remaining servants were to be placed on half-pay.

Reminded by these arrangements of Mr. Franklin's lamentations regarding our scattered household, my thoughts drifted inevitably to him. The more I pondered his future, the greater grew my unease. By Sunday, I had penned a letter to Mr. Jeffco, the valet of Mr. Franklin's father—a friend of old acquaintance—to inquire what plans Mr. Franklin had formulated upon his arrival in London.

The Sunday evening proved, if anything, drier than the preceding Saturday. We concluded the day of rest, as millions do weekly in these islands, by falling asleep in our chairs, weary and resigned.

Whatever effect Monday had on the rest of the household, it certainly shook me up. The first of Sergeant Cuff's prophecies materialized that day: the news from the Yollands.

Having escorted Penelope and my lady's maid away by rail with their luggage, I wandered the grounds when I heard my name called. Turning, I found myself face-to-face with the fisherman's daughter, Limping Lucy. Setting aside her lameness and her gauntness—which, in my view, was a terrible drawback for a woman—she possessed certain appealing qualities. A dark, keen, intelligent visage, a clear voice, and a beautiful brown head of hair counted among her virtues. A crutch sat firmly in the list of her afflictions, joined by a temper that ranked highly among her defects.

"Well, my dear," I said, "what brings you to me?"

"Where is the man you call Franklin Blake?" the girl demanded, fixing me with a fierce stare as she leaned heavily on her crutch.

"That is not a respectful way to address any gentleman," I replied. "If you wish to inquire for my lady's nephew, you shall refer to him as Mr. Franklin Blake."

She limped a step closer, her expression suggesting she could devour me alive. "_Mr._ Franklin Blake?" she repeated. "Murderer Franklin Blake would be a far more fitting title."

My training under the late Mrs. Betteredge served me well here. Whenever a woman attempts to provoke your temper, turn the tables and enrage her instead. They are usually prepared for every defense you mount, save one. A single word suffices, as did a hundred. One word did it with Limping Lucy. I looked pleasantly into her face and uttered—"Pooh!"

Her temper flared instantly. She balanced on her sound leg, lifted her crutch, and beat it furiously three times upon the ground. "He is a murderer! He is a murderer! He is a murderer! He has been the death of Rosanna Spearman!" she shrieked, her voice cutting the air. A couple of laborers nearby glanced up, recognized Limping Lucy, anticipated her outburst, and quickly averted their gazes.
