I dare say you remember that I once spoke of Dennistoun’s experience—how a respectable pursuit, carried on among catalogues and glass cases, may suddenly find itself breathing air that is older, colder, and not meant for scholars. He was not a man to print such things in the newspapers; but news of it travelled, as news does in colleges, along dinner-tables and through common-rooms, until it reached the ears of another custodian of art: Mr Williams, who had charge of a museum at a different University.

Williams comforted himself, as men will, by arranging his apprehensions according to departments. Dennistoun had been hunting manuscripts on the Continent; Williams dealt, in the main, with English topographical engravings—parish churches, market towns, manor-houses—homely things, the sort that hang in inn parlours above the settle where the bacon scent lives in the wood, and no one expects trouble beyond a cracked pane and a draught. His days were paced by ordinary sounds: the soft scrape of paper in drawers, the distant bell for chapel, the clink of cups at tea, and, now and again, the unlovely shriek of some modern contrivance from the street outside—omnibuses and carts that seemed, in their clatter, to drown out the older, kinder acoustics of the place. It annoyed him more than he admitted; there are noises that do not merely offend the ear but thin the mind, as too much heat thins a sauce.

Among those who collected such views there was one London dealer whose catalogues were as necessary as salt: Mr J. W. Britnell. He issued, at intervals, neat lists of engravings and old sketches—mansion fronts, bridges, spires—always shifting stock, always the insinuation that this or that would never be seen again. Williams was a steady customer rather than an eager one. His museum already possessed mountains of prints; he bought to fill gaps, not to chase rarities.

One February morning, however, a Britnell catalogue lay on his desk, and with it a typewritten note:

DEAR SIR, We beg to call your attention to No. 978 in our accompanying catalogue, which we shall be glad to send on approval.

Yours faithfully,  
J. W. BRITNELL.

No. 978 read:

978.—Unknown. Interesting mezzotint: View of a manor-house, early part of the century. 15 by 10 inches; black frame. £2 2s.

Two guineas for an “unknown” was impudence, and for a mezzotint—worse, an indifferent one—little short of a practical joke. Yet Britnell knew his men; and curiosity, like a strong kitchen smell, slips under doors you believed shut. Williams sent a postcard asking that No. 978 be forwarded on approval, along with a handful of other trifles.

The parcel arrived, as parcels do, one day later than it should. It came on the Saturday afternoon after Williams had left the museum, and was carried round to his rooms in college by the attendant so he need not wait through Sunday before sorting and returning what he did not want. He found it there when he came in to tea with a friend.

I must speak of tea, because it matters more than one would think. In Williams’s rooms the kettle’s rising hiss and the small percussion of spoon against cup were the domestic counterpoint to everything that followed; and the smell—India leaf opened by boiling water, a slice of bread toasted to the edge of bitterness, butter softening—made the place feel immune, for a while, from any intrusion that had not paid at the buttery. The room had the safe familiar noises of an old building: a tick from the clock, a faint settling in the woodwork, the muted rumble of footsteps in the stair beyond.

Among the contents of Britnell’s parcel the mezzotint dominated—large, black-framed. At first glance it was precisely the kind of thing you find in country passages where the air smells of polish and damp wool: a full-face view of a not very large manor-house of the last century, three rows of plain sashed windows set in rusticated masonry, a parapet with balls or urns at the corners, and a small portico in the centre. Trees flanked it; lawn spread before it. The narrow margin bore the legend A. W. F. sculpsit, and there was no further inscription.

Indifferent mezzotints have a peculiar deadness: the velvety blacks are muddy, the half-tones bruised. This one had that amateurish look, as if the hand that rocked the plate had done so with more enthusiasm than skill. Williams turned it over contemptuously. On the back was a paper label torn away on the left; two line-ends remained, like a name half-chewed: the first showed …ngley Hall; the second …ssex.

“Somethingley Hall,” Williams said, and felt the irritation of it—like a recipe missing its chief spice. “Either Sussex or Essex.”

He meant to identify it by gazetteer, if only to write to Britnell with remarks on his judgement. He lit candles, because dusk had thickened outside, and the candle-flames made their own gentle sound, a soft prickling in the wick. He poured tea, set out bread and something of cold meat—college fare, serviceable, but Williams took a certain pleasure even in that: the edge of salt, the grain of mustard, the reassuring chew.

His companion, a golfing man, looked about and let his eye be caught by the black frame. Let us call him Professor Binks.

“What’s this place, Williams?”

“Just what I’m going to try to find out,” said Williams, reaching for a gazetteer on the shelf. “Look at the back. Somethingley Hall, in Sussex or Essex. Half the name’s gone. You don’t happen to know it?”

“It’s from Britnell, I suppose?” said Binks. “Is it for the museum?”

“If it was five shillings I should buy it,” said Williams; “but for some unearthly reason he asks two guineas. It’s a wretched engraving, and there aren’t even any figures to give it life.”

Binks, still holding his cup, tilted the picture to the candlelight.

“It’s not worth two guineas,” he said, “but I don’t think it’s so badly done. The moonlight seems rather good to me; and I should have thought there were figures—at least a figure—just on the edge in front.”

“Let’s look,” said Williams, annoyed at having missed anything.

And indeed there was—hardly more than a black blot at the extreme edge: the head and shoulders of a person muffled up, back turned to the spectator, looking towards the house.

“Still,” said Williams, “I can’t spend two guineas of museum money on a view of a place I can’t name.”

Binks went off to his work, and Williams, for the better part of the evening, wrestled with his gazetteer. Names ending in -ngley proliferated like weeds: if only the vowel before ng had survived on the label he could have pinned it at once. As it was, it might be anything—Guestingley, Langley, Annesley—counties and parishes sliding past with no index of terminations to help him. The book itself seemed to resist, its pages making that dry whisper like old flour sifted through fingers.

Hall in his college was at seven; and there, amid the clatter of knives and the polite roar of talk, he met colleagues who had also been out on the links. Later, in common-room, there was the usual migration—some to one set of rooms, some to another. A few came back with Williams for whist and tobacco. The soundscape thickened: cards flicked, chairs shifted, a match struck with a brief sulphurous smell, then the steady rasp of smoke drawn through pipes. The college, with its old stones, held those noises and warmed to them as a kitchen warms to the low stir of pots.

During a lull Williams picked up the mezzotint without really looking at it and handed it to one of the party, a man mildly interested in art, supplying the few particulars.

The gentleman glanced, then straightened a little.

“It’s really a very good thing, Williams. Quite a romantic feeling. The light is admirably managed, and the figure—though rather grotesque—is somehow very impressive.”

“Yes, isn’t it?” said Williams, who at that moment was mixing whisky-and-soda and could not cross at once to examine it again.

The visitors went. Williams wrote letters, tidied papers, performed the small rituals by which a day is shut away. At last, some time past midnight, he carried his bedroom candle across from the sitting-room after putting out the lamp. The mezzotint lay face-up on the table where the last man had left it. The candlelight fell on it, and what it showed made Williams’s hand jerk so that the candle guttered, and he all but dropped it. He swore afterward that if the flame had gone out then, he would have had a fit; for there was no room in him at that moment for darkness.

He set the candle down and looked again, close, with the stubborn thoroughness of a curator who knows how easily the eye invents.

In the middle of the lawn—where there had been nothing at tea-time—there was now a figure.

It was crawling on all fours towards the house, muffled in a strange black garment, and on its back was a white cross.

Nothing in the ordinary life of a college trains a man for this. One may read of miracles and frauds, study pigments and plates, and still be unready for an object on your own table deciding to alter itself. Williams did what he could do. He took the framed print by one corner and carried it across the passage to a second set of rooms he possessed. There he locked it in a drawer, sported the doors of both sets of rooms, and went to bed. But before he slept he sat down, while his hands still shook, and wrote out and signed an account of the change he had seen.

Sleep came late. Yet there was a comfort in remembering the other man’s remark—he had spoken of the figure, too. So it was not merely Williams’s eyes, nor his mind, beginning to sour. In the morning two matters pressed on him as insistently as hunger: he must examine the picture again with a witness; and he must, at any cost, find the house’s name.

He asked his neighbour, Nisbet, to breakfast.

Nisbet arrived about half-past nine. Williams was not fully dressed—an omission that would have troubled him on any other Sunday. Breakfast in college rooms has its own sensory decency: the snap of a crust, the steam from a dish, the clink of a pot lid, and, if the windows are shut, the muffled quiet that makes the world outside seem momentarily irrelevant. They talked as two Fellows will—of games, of people, of the tiresome modern habit of importing noise into places built for voices. Yet Williams’s attention kept pulling away, as a tongue keeps worrying a sore tooth, to the other room where the mezzotint lay face down in the drawer.

At last pipes were lit; the smell of tobacco rose, dry and sweet, and the moment had come.

With an excitement that made him almost tremble, Williams ran across, unlocked the drawer, took out the picture—still face down—and hurried back to Nisbet. He put it into his hands.

“Now,” he said, “I want you to tell me exactly what you see. Describe it. Minutely. I’ll tell you why afterward.”

Nisbet studied it, calm in the manner of a man who expects only art.

“Well. I have here a view of a country-house—English, I presume—by moonlight.”

“Moonlight? You’re sure?”

“Certainly. The moon is on the wane, and there are clouds.”

“All right. Go on.” Williams muttered aside, “There was no moon when I saw it first.”

“There’s not much more,” Nisbet continued. “Three rows of windows, five in each row, except the bottom where the porch takes the middle, and—”

“But what about figures?” Williams broke in.

“There aren’t any,” said Nisbet. “Not a one. But there is something else.”

“What?”

“One of the ground-floor windows—left of the door—is open.”

“Open?” Williams stared, then blurted, with a kind of horrid exhilaration: “My God—he must have got in.”

He snatched the print, went behind the sofa to look for himself. It was true. No figure on the lawn. The window stood open, a square of deeper black.

For a moment he could not speak. Then he went to his writing-table and wrote rapidly. He brought two papers to Nisbet: the first, Nisbet’s description written out by Williams as he spoke; the second, Williams’s signed statement from last night.

Nisbet read, and looked up.

“What can it mean?”

“Exactly,” said Williams. “Three things, then. I must find out from Garwood”—the visitor who had last praised the figure—“what he saw. We must get it photographed before it changes again. And we must find the place.”

“I can photograph it,” said Nisbet. “But it looks uncommonly as though we’re watching a tragedy being worked out somewhere. The question is, has it happened already, or is it to happen?”

“I’ll take it to old Green,” Williams said. “He’ll know the house. We have property in Essex and Sussex; he’s been all over.”

Nisbet hesitated. “Green’s gone down for the Sunday. I didn’t see him in Hall.”

“Yes—Brighton,” said Williams, remembering. “Then do the photograph now. I’ll go to Garwood and get his statement. Keep an eye on it while I’m gone.”

“I’m beginning to think,” he added grimly, “that two guineas is not an exorbitant price.”

He returned shortly with Garwood. Garwood’s recollection was that when he had seen the figure it had been clear of the edge but not far across the lawn. He remembered a white mark on the back, though he could not swear it was a cross. This also was written down and signed; and Nisbet set up his apparatus and photographed the engraving.

“What now?” Nisbet asked. “Are you going to sit and watch it all day?”

“No,” Williams said. “I rather imagine we’re meant to see the whole thing. Between last night and this morning there was time enough for a dozen changes, yet the creature only got into the house. It might have done its business and gone; but the open window suggests it’s there now. And I doubt it will change much in daylight. We’ll go for a walk this afternoon. Come in at tea—when it’s dark. I’ll leave it on the table and sport the door. My skip can get in, no one else.”

They agreed. Also, for prudence: if they kept together they would be less likely to let the matter slip to others, and any rumour of such a thing would fetch the whole Phasmatological Society buzzing like flies to a butcher’s stall.

So we may leave them till five.

At that hour—or near it—they climbed Williams’s staircase. They were mildly vexed to see the door unsported, until they remembered that on Sundays the skips came earlier for orders. Then they entered and found something that struck them silent.

The mezzotint stood as they had left it, propped against books. Opposite it, seated in Williams’s chair, was the skip himself—Mr Filcher (I do not invent the name)—staring at the picture with naked horror.

Filcher was a servant of standing, the man who set etiquette like a recipe sets proportions. Nothing could be more foreign to his practice than taking his master’s chair, or appearing to scrutinize his master’s belongings. He seemed, at their entry, to recall this with a jolt. He sprang up, pale under his respectable years, and made an effort at composure.

“I ask your pardon, sir, for taking such a freedom as to set down.”

“Not at all, Robert,” said Williams, quickly. “I’ve been meaning to ask what you thought of that picture.”

Filcher glanced, as if unwilling, toward the black frame.

“Well, sir—of course I don’t set up my opinion against yours—but it ain’t the pictur I should hang where my little girl could see it, sir.”

“Wouldn’t you? Why not?”

“No, sir. The pore child—I recollect once she see a Doré Bible, with pictures not half what that is, and we had to set up with her three or four nights after, if you’ll believe me. And if she was to ketch sight of this skelinton here—or whatever it is—carrying off the pore baby, she’d be in a taking. Children’s that nervish, sir; a little thing lays hold of ’em. And what I should say, it don’t seem a right pictur to be laying about, sir, not where anyone that’s liable to be startled could come on it. Should you be wanting anything this evening, sir? Thank you, sir.”

He went out, restored as best he could to duty; and the three men were already round the table.

The house stood as before under a waning moon and drifting clouds. But the window that had been open was shut. And the figure was on the lawn again—only not crawling now.

It was upright and stepping swiftly with long strides toward the front edge of the picture, as if returning the way it had come. The moon lay behind it. The black drapery hung down over its face so that only hints could be seen—enough to make the watchers grateful for shadow. There was a white, dome-like forehead, and a few straggling hairs. The head bent down; the arms were clamped around an object held tight to the chest: a child. Whether living or dead no one could tell in that murk of inked night. The legs, however, were plain, and horribly thin.

From five to seven they took turns watching, as if attention itself were a charm; and all that time the mezzotint did not change. At last they agreed it was safe to leave it and go to Hall, and return afterward to see what the night would bring.

When they assembled again at the earliest possible moment, the figure was gone. The house lay quiet, the lawn empty under the moonbeams, as though nothing had ever crossed it.

There remained only the ugly labour of books. They sat late with gazetteers and guide-books, turning pages that smelled of dust and glue, chasing the torn label’s ending. At half-past eleven Williams, stubborn with fatigue, struck luck. In Murray’s Guide to Essex he read:

“16½ miles, Anningley. The church has been an interesting building of Norman date, but was extensively classicized in the last century. It contains the tomb of the family of Francis, whose mansion, Anningley Hall, a solid Queen Anne house, stands immediately beyond the churchyard in a park of about 80 acres. The family is now extinct, the last heir having disappeared mysteriously in infancy in the year 1802. The father, Mr Arthur Francis, was locally known as a talented amateur engraver in mezzotint. After his son’s disappearance he lived in complete retirement at the Hall, and was found dead in his studio on the third anniversary of the disaster, having just completed an engraving of the house, impressions of which are of considerable rarity.”

There was a silence after that, the kind in which one becomes aware again of every small sound: the clock’s tick, the coal’s faint settling in the grate, the far-off, softened rumour of town life—wheels, voices, a modern din that seemed suddenly indecent beside the old, quiet horror they had uncovered, as if commerce and noise could sell anything and drown any memory, even one written in a dead child’s vanishing.

Mr Green, when he returned, identified the house at once as Anningley Hall.

“Is there any kind of explanation of the figure, Green?” Williams asked, for now the name had made it worse, not better; now the thing had a parish and a date, and therefore a place in human language, which is never quite large enough for what it is asked to hold.

“I don’t know, I’m sure, Williams,” said Green. “What used to be said there when I first knew it—before I came up here—was this. Old Francis was always very much down on these poaching fellows, and whenever he got a chance he’d have a man he suspected turned off the estate, and by degrees he got rid of them all but one. Squires could do a lot then that they daren’t think of now. Well, the man that was left was what you find pretty often in that country—the last remains of a very old family. I believe they were Lords of the Manor at one time. I recollect just the same thing in my own parish.”

“What,” Williams put in, “like the man in Tess of the D’Urbervilles?”
