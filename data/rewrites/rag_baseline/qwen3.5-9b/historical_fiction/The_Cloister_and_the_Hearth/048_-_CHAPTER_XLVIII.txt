Catherine stood paralyzed by a dread that tightened her throat, realizing with crushing clarity that Gerard must arrive home within a week, certainly no later than a month. In that interim, how was she to confess the terrible truth—that she had failed to watch over his betrothed? The uncertainty regarding the girl's fate hung over them like a storm cloud, occasionally coalescing into a sickening, tangible horror. "Oh, Kate," she groaned, her voice trembling, "what if she has already thrown herself into the water?" Her mother's response was immediate, cutting through the silence with the blunt wisdom of experience. "Margaret would never be so wicked," Catherine protested, clinging to the hope of her friend's virtue. "Ah, my lass, you know not the chaotic nature of young women unmoored from maternal guidance," her mother replied, her tone shifting from comfort to grim warning. "They are capable of throwing themselves into the depths for a man whom the next stranger might cure in a single week. I have seen them leap into the canal with the resolve of iron, only to scream for mercy moments later, unable to command their own minds even over such trifles. Worse still, there are times when their bodies ail in ways unknown to any other creature, stringing their emotions so tightly that the patience inherent in their souls vanishes entirely, sending them plunging into the water without a second thought. Thus, I declare that men are monsters." "Mother!" Catherine cried out, recoiling at the accusation. "Monsters, and no less," her mother insisted, her eyes hardening. "They construct these very canals merely to tempt poor women. They know we possess no desire to cut our throats, nor do we rate our skin or our lives so highly that we would willingly spill blood. As for hanging, while she waits for the nail to be fixed and the noose to be fashioned, she retains the luxury of changing her mind. But a jump into a canal is akin to jumping into bed; the water does all the work, whether you wish it or not." The mother paused, her gaze drifting to the window as if recalling a distant memory. "Consider me, the mother of nine. Was I not once eager to make a hole in our canal right here and now?" "Nay, Mother, I shall never believe you capable of such folly." "You may believe it, though," her mother countered, a wry smile touching her lips. "It happened in the first year of our household. Eli had not yet discovered my weak stitches, nor I his; we pulled in contrary directions until we made a rent in our peace. I ran crying to a gossiping fool like myself, sharing secrets meant only for the saints, and lo, one of our precious canals lay directly in my path. Did they take us for teal? How tempting it looked! I told myself, 'Since he has allowed me to leave his door in a quarrel, he shall see me drowned next, and then he will change his key. He will weep a proper flood, and I shall look down from heaven—and yes, I forgot I should be elsewhere—watch him mourn, and oh, that would be sweet indeed.' My feet were already dancing on the edge, ready to plunge, until suddenly I stopped." She shook her head, the image fading. "I had a new gown in progress, nearly finished, and I chose to go home instead. What was Eli's first word upon seeing me? 'Let that flea stick in the wall, my lass,' he said. 'Not a word of what angered you was true, save this: I love thee.' Those were his exact words, spoken during our first quarrel. I remembered them, and I flung my arms around his neck, sobbing briefly, thinking of the canal. He was no colder to me than I to him, being a man and a youth; the warmth returned between us. That embrace was infinitely sweeter than lying in the freezing water, saving my wedding kirtle and the fine new shoes crafted by old John Bush, his uncle who still runs the shop today. And what was my grief compared to hers?" Little Kate pressed forward, hoping that Margaret's love for her aging father would anchor her. "He is father, mother, and everything to her, you know." "Nay, Kate," her mother dismissed gently, "they forget all such bonds in a moment of despair when the sky itself turns black above them. I place greater faith in the unborn than in him who is ripe for the grave to keep her from mischief. For truly, it goes sore against our nature to die when a little innocent pulls at our hearts, begging to live, feeding upon our very veins." "Very well, then, keep your heart high, Mother," Catherine urged, adding internally that these fears were likely exaggerated. She concluded by solemnly entreating her mother to cease naming the sex of Margaret's infant. It was considered unluckily superstitious, a notion reinforced by every gossip in town. "Dear heart, as if there were not as many girls born as boys," she reasoned. Yet this reflection, though logically sound, met with a clamorous rejection. "Do you possess the cruelty to threaten me with a girl?!" her mother exclaimed, her composure fracturing. "I want no more girls while I have you. What use would a lass be to me? Can I sit her on my knee and see my Gerard again as I can with a boy? I tell thee, it is all settled." "How can that be?" Catherine asked, bewildered. "In my mind," her mother declared firmly. "And if I am to be disappointed in the end, it is not for you to disappoint me beforehand by telling me it cannot be a child, but only a girl."
