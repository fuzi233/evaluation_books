A gentle slumber, lulled by the soft murmur of voices and the quiet rustle of the morning breeze, carried Harry Warrington through the night. When he awoke, the sun was already climbing high in a clear summer sky, casting golden rays across the room. At his bedside stood the kind host and hostess, their faces lit with warmth and concern.

He stirred, ravenous from hunger, and the doctor, observing him closely, permitted a light meal—chicken and rice, prepared with care by one of Mrs. Lambert’s daughters. As the scent of the food filled the air, Harry’s mind wandered. He remembered faintly two young girls with bright cheeks and dark, curling hair, who had smiled at him as he lay unconscious, then vanished just as he began to stir. Then came another image—a woman older, graceful but no longer young, her hair golden under the candlelight… But no—such thoughts brought a sudden pang of shame. A wave of memory surged forward: the false pearls, the dyed locks—he recalled now, with horror, how he had once been deceived by such things. It seemed like a lifetime ago, when he was still a boy, innocent and unguarded. Now he was a man—older, wounded, bled, fevered, and drugged into deep sleep. The past felt distant, almost unreal.

“What is it, my dear child?” asked Mrs. Lambert gently, noticing his sudden distress.

“Nothing, madam,” replied Harry, clutching his shoulder. “Just a twinge. I thank you both for your kindness. You’ve taken such good care of me.”

“We are old friends, Mr. Warrington,” said Colonel Lambert. “My wife and your mother were schoolmates in Kensington, and I knew your father well. You were never a stranger to us, even before your aunt and cousin told us who you were.”

“Are they here?” Harry asked, his eyes dimming slightly.

“They must have stayed at Tunbridge Wells last night. They sent a rider from Reigate yesterday to inquire after you.”

“Oh—yes,” Harry murmured, glancing down at his bandaged arm.

“You’re recovering well,” said the Colonel. “Now, we’ll leave the rest to Mrs. Lambert and the cook.”

“Nay,” interrupted the lady, “Theo made the broth herself. And she mended your sleeve too—so it wouldn’t chafe your wound.”

“And if howling proves loyalty,” said the Colonel with a grin, “then your valet must be the most devoted creature alive.”

“He let your luggage go off with your aunt’s carriage,” added Mrs. Lambert. “You’ll have to wear my husband’s shirts—though I’m sure they’re not half so fine as yours.”

“Pish!” the Colonel laughed. “They’re good enough for any Christian. And besides, Theo and Hester stitched them themselves.”

Harry looked up at the vase of roses on the toilet table, their crimson petals glowing in the sunlight. “What beautiful roses!”

“Theo picked them this morning,” said Mrs. Lambert. “She did, didn’t she, Lambert?”

The Colonel merely nodded, though his expression suggested he might have nudged her quietly, as if to remind her of propriety.

“I thought I heard someone singing the Evening Hymn last night,” Harry said softly. “Or was it only a dream?”

“Theo again,” chuckled the Colonel. “The servants said your servant began it in the kitchen—singing like a church organ.”

“Our people sing it at home,” said Harry, his voice softening. “My grandfather loved it dearly. His wife’s father was a close friend of Bishop Ken, who wrote it. And—my brother used to love it too.” His voice trailed off.

At that moment, Mrs. Lambert’s eyes softened, as if she would have reached out to comfort him. The tenderness of the place—the quiet healing, the simple kindness—had begun to touch Harry’s heart. For weeks he had breathed the polluted air of London’s vanity, ambition, and deceit. Now, surrounded by honesty and peace, he felt something stirring within him—something purer, more true.

The Colonel sat beside him as Harry ate, and the housewife left them to their conversation. Gratitude warmed Harry’s spirit, and the fullness of his stomach loosened his tongue. Usually reserved, he found himself speaking more freely than usual. He wasn’t much of a scholar—books had never held his interest—but he could talk knowledgeably about horses, dogs, and the ways of country life. The Colonel, shrewd and experienced in men, cattle, and war, quickly sized him up.

It was only now that Harry learned Mrs. Lambert had been a childhood friend of his mother’s, and that the Colonel’s own father had fought alongside Harry’s grandfather, Colonel Esmond, during Queen Anne’s wars. Suddenly, he wasn’t just a guest—he was among kin.

“My sons are away,” said the Colonel. “Otherwise, they’d show you the country. But you’ll have to make do with my wife and daughters. Theo—our eldest—made your soup, cut the roses, and sewed your sleeve. She’s no miracle worker, though her mother thinks she is. But she’s a capable girl, cheerful and hardworking.”

“It’s very kind of Miss Lambert,” said Harry.

“She treats everyone alike,” replied the Colonel. “Does her duty. I laugh when she praises our children—though I suppose I’m just as bad myself. God has given us good children. Why hide my gratitude?”

“You don’t have a sister?” he asked.

“No, sir,” said Harry. “I am alone now.”

“Forgive me,” said the Colonel. “Your servant told us what happened last year. I served with Braddock in Scotland. I hope he recovered before he died. A wild man, but there was truth beneath the swagger. Your servant speaks freely—perhaps because he saved you?”

“Saved me?” Harry blinked.

“From dozens of Indians, on that very expedition. We didn’t know we were hosting such a wealthy gentleman. He says half Virginia belongs to you. But even if all of North America were yours, we’d give you our best.”

“Negro boys lie like the devil,” said Harry, flushing. “They think it honors us to say we’re ten times richer than we are. My mother has a good estate—better than most in England, but nothing extraordinary. All our grandeur is in Gumbo’s imagination. He never rescued me from an Indian—and would flee at the sight of one, just as my brother’s boy did when he fell.”

“A brave man can run at times,” said the Colonel. “I saw the finest troops in the world scatter before a ragged mob of Highlanders at Preston.”

“That was because they fought for their king,” said Harry.

“And what of the French Indians last year?” asked the Colonel.

“The scoundrels!” Harry clenched his fist. “I’d take the scalp of every one of them! They invaded British land. But the Highlanders fought for their king.”

“And we fought for ours,” said the Colonel, laughing. “And won.”

“If Prince James hadn’t turned back at Derby,” said Harry, “your king and mine would be King James the Third now.”

“Who made you such a Tory?” asked the Colonel.

“Not I,” replied Harry. “The Esmonds were always loyal. If we’d lived in England twenty years earlier, my brother and I would have risked our heads for the king.”

“Yours is better where it is,” said the Colonel. “I’ve seen others’ heads on poles at Temple Bar. Not a pleasant sight.”

“I’ll salute them whenever I pass,” said Harry. “If the king and court stand nearby!”

“I doubt Lord Castlewood shares your views,” said the Colonel, smiling. “Nor your aunt, the Baroness of Bernstein. She’s changed sides—sent her nephews into the Household, and looks for suitable matches for her nieces. If you hold Tory opinions, take an old soldier’s advice—keep them to yourself.”

“I trust you won’t betray me,” said Harry.

“I won’t,” said the Colonel. “But others might. Did you speak so freely at Castlewood?”

“I should have been safe among my own kin,” said Harry.

“Perhaps,” said the Colonel. “But kinsmen can play tricks. Servants have ears. Your servant talks too much.”

“Has he told about the broken-kneed horse?” Harry blushed deeply.

“To be honest,” said the Colonel, “my groom knew the story. Said it was a shame a gentleman sold such a beast—especially to a cousin.”

“I’ll not speak of it again,” said Harry.

With Gumbo’s help, Harry rose from bed. He gave the valet a stern warning—this time, not a single word about his affairs in the servants’ hall. Gumbo swore solemnly he would obey. But the reader may suspect that the words flew from his lips as soon as he stepped into the kitchen.

Mr. Lambert’s house stood on the edge of Oakhurst, a quiet town between Farnham and Reigate. It was modest but stately—a square brick building with stone-framed windows, tall chimneys, and a balustrade crowning its roof. Behind it stretched a wide garden; before it, a field where cows grazed and horses roamed. Two heraldic beasts guarded the gates, and above the central window hung a carved shield and coronet. The house had once been the jointure-house of Oakhurst Castle, whose turrets peered over the trees beyond. Though the castle loomed larger in history, the Lambert home was the most respected in the town.

After dressing with Gumbo’s aid—his fair hair neatly arranged, his injured arm supported by a handkerchief—Harry followed the Colonel down a broad oak staircase lined with ancient weapons. Into a marble hall they stepped, where pikes, halberds, pistols, and jackboots from Cromwell’s wars stood proudly displayed. There was a tattered French flag from Malplaquet, a pair of Highland swords thrown aside after Culloden, and portraits of stern Parliamentary soldiers.

“They fought against your grandfather and King Charles,” said the Colonel. “I don’t hide it. We were Whigs—more than that. John Lambert, the Major-General, was a kinsman. We liked short hair and long sermons. You don’t seem fond of either?”

Harry’s face tightened as he studied the portraits.

“Don’t worry,” said the Colonel. “We’re Churchmen now. My eldest son will enter the clergy. He’s traveling with Lord Wrotham’s son in Italy. And our women—well, they’re all for the Church. Every woman is a Tory at heart, though Pope called it a rake. Come, let’s see them.”

He opened the door to the parlour, where the ladies waited.

“Here is Miss Hester,” said the Colonel. “And this is Miss Theo—the soup-maker, the tailor, the harpsichord player, and the songstress who lulled you to sleep. Make a curtsey, girls.”

Miss Theo bowed deeply, her cheeks flaming. She was sixteen, sturdy and freckled, with rich brown curls tumbling from her cap, strong arms, and feet that needed no telescope to see. Not a beauty, perhaps, but warm and real. Harry did not fall instantly in love with her—nor could he forget the other woman who had lately captured his heart.

“Eh bien! que dites-vous, ma fille, de notre hote?” the Colonel hummed, tapping her chin.

“Nonsense, Mr. Lambert!” said Mrs. Lambert.

“Nonsense is often the wisest thing in the world,” said the Colonel.

“Do you like nonsense?” he asked Harry, seeing the youth’s confusion.

“We consume plenty of it here,” said the Colonel. “Rabelais is my favorite. My wife likes Fielding and Theophrastus. Theo prefers Tom Brown. Hetty loves Swift.”

“Our papa talks about what he loves,” said Miss Hetty.

“And what is that?” asked the Colonel.

“Sure, sir, you said it was nonsense,” she replied, tossing her head.

“Which of them do you prefer, Mr. Warrington?” asked the Colonel.

“Which of whom?”

“The Curate of Meudon, or the Dean of St. Patrick’s, or honest Tom, or Mr. Fielding?”

“And who were they?”

“They wrote books.”

“I’ve never heard of them,” Harry admitted, lowering his eyes. “I fear my schooling was neglected. My brother read everything. He could talk about them for hours.”

Mrs. Lambert glanced at Theo, who blushed.

“Never mind,” said the Colonel cheerfully. “Honesty beats book-learning any day. You can live honorably without reading these. Some might bring more pleasure than profit.”

“I know more about horses and dogs than Greek and Latin,” said Harry. “Most of us in Virginia do.”

“You’re like the Persians,” said the Colonel. “You ride well—and speak the truth.”

“Are the Prussians good riders?” asked Harry. “I hope to see their king and fight beside or against him.”

And then, silently, Miss Theo looked at her mother—and Mrs. Lambert’s face grew sad.

Why? Because young girls raised in quiet towns are not immune to dreams. Even those sheltered behind walls and aprons harbor secret hopes. Like the princess in the fairy tale, locked in a steel castle, listening for the horn that signals her champion’s arrival. The dragon is slain, the gate opens, and she rushes forth—ready, eager, dressed in her finest. That is romance. And that is why Miss Theo blushed. Because somewhere, deep in her heart, she believed the hero had come.

And so, though he did not know it yet, Harry Warrington had entered a world far different from the one he had known. Here, among honest men and gentle women, among laughter and memory, he began to heal—not just his body, but his soul.
