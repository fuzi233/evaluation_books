The air was crisp, charged with the electric dust of a lively spring breeze. Inside the church, the atmosphere was heavy with the scent of camphor from the front pews, a pungent aroma that nearly suffocated the delicate, faint fragrance of the lilies lining the altar.

Newland Archer stood on the chancel step of Grace Church, flanked by his best man, having emerged from the vestry at the sexton's signal. This signal indicated that the brougham carrying the bride and her father had finally arrived. However, a significant delay was inevitable; within the lobby, the bridesmaids hovered like a cluster of blooming Easter flowers, engaged in final adjustments and consultations. During this unavoidable pause, tradition demanded that the bridegroom expose himself alone to the scrutiny of the assembled crowd. Archer endured this formality with the same resigned grace he applied to every other ritual of a nineteenth-century New York wedding—a ceremony that felt less like a modern event and more like a relic from the dawn of history. To him, the path ahead was equally easy or equally painful, depending on one's perspective. He had obeyed his best man's frantic instructions with the same pious devotion that his own fathers had shown generations prior.

He felt reasonably certain he had met every obligation. The eight bouquets of white lilac and lilies-of-the-valley from the bridesmaids had arrived on time, as had the gold and sapphire sleeve-links for the ushers and the cat's-eye scarf-pin for the best man. Archer had spent half the night wrestling with the wording of his thank-you notes to male friends and former lovers. The fees for the Bishop and the Rector were secure in his best man's pocket. His luggage sat ready at Mrs. Manson Mingott's, where the wedding breakfast awaited, along with his traveling attire. Even a private train compartment had been secured for the couple's departure to their unknown destination—a necessary concealment, for the sanctity of the bridal night was a taboo as ancient as the ritual itself.

"Did you get the ring?" whispered young Van der Luyden, the best man, his voice trembling with the weight of his inexperience.

Archer performed the gesture he had witnessed countless times: he reached into the pocket of his dark grey waistcoat with his ungloved right hand, confirming the presence of the small gold band engraved inside with *Newland to May, April —, 187—*. Resuming his posture, hat in hand and pearl-grey gloves gripped tightly in his left, he gazed toward the church doors.

Above them, Handel's *March* swelled pompously through the imitation stone vaulting, carrying with it the ghostly echoes of countless past weddings. Archer watched other brides float up the nave toward other grooms with cheerful indifference. He wondered if, when the Last Trump finally sounded, Mrs. Selfridge Merry would appear with her towering ostrich feathers, and Mrs. Beaufort with her diamond earrings and eternal smile—and whether suitable seats awaited them in the next world.

He reviewed the familiar faces in the front rows: the women sharp with curiosity and excitement, the men sulky with the obligation of donning frock coats before luncheon and fighting for food at the breakfast table. He imagined Reggie Chivers complaining about the breakfast location at Old Catherine's, only to be silenced by Sillerton Jackson's authoritative declaration that it would be served at small tables in the new English fashion.

His eyes lingered on the left-hand pew, where his mother sat weeping softly under her Chantilly veil, her hands clutching her grandmother's ermine muff. *Poor Janey,* he thought, observing his sister. *Even by twisting her neck, she can only see the dowdy Newlands and Dagonets in the front pews.*

Across the white ribbon separating the family seats, he saw Beaufort, tall and flushed, scrutinizing the women with an arrogant stare. Beside him sat his wife, radiant in silvery chinchilla and violets. On the far side of the ribbon, Lawrence Lefferts's sleek head seemed to stand guard over the invisible deity of "Good Form" who presided over the ceremony. Archer wondered how many flaws Lefferts's keen eyes would find in the ritual of this god. Suddenly, he realized he too had once considered such questions vital. The things that filled his days now seemed like a nursery parody of life, or the petty squabbles of medieval scholars over metaphysical terms no one truly understood. A stormy debate over whether wedding presents should be displayed had darkened the hours before the wedding, yet it was inconceivable to Archer that adults could agitate themselves over such trifles. It had been Mrs. Welland's indignant tears that settled the matter: *"I should as soon turn the reporters loose in my house."* Yet, there had been a time when Archer held aggressive opinions on all such matters, viewing the customs of his little tribe as fraught with world-wide significance.

*"And all the while,"* he thought, *"real people were living somewhere, and real things happening to them..."*

*"There they come!"* the best man whispered excitedly. But Archer knew better.

The cautious opening of the church door signaled only that Mr. Brown, the livery-stable keeper acting as sexton, was conducting a preliminary survey. The door closed softly, then swung majestically open moments later, sending a murmur through the congregation: *"The family!"*

Mrs. Welland entered first, supported by her eldest son. Her large pink face wore an appropriate solemnity, and her plum-colored satin gown with pale blue side-panels and a small bonnet adorned with blue ostrich plumes met with general approval. Before she settled into the pew opposite Mrs. Archer's, the spectators craned their necks to see who followed. Rumors had circulated wildly that Mrs. Manson Mingott, despite her physical frailties, intended to attend. The idea suited her sporting nature, leading to high bets at the clubs regarding her ability to navigate the nave. It was known she had hired a carpenter to dismantle the front pew panel and measure the space, but the results were discouraging. For a day, her family feared she would be wheeled up in her enormous Bath chair, enthroned at the foot of the chancel.

The prospect of such a grotesque exposure of her person was so painful to her relations that they could have paid gold to the person who discovered the chair was too wide to pass between the iron uprights of the church awning. Removing the awning to reveal the bride to the mob of dressmakers and reporters outside exceeded even Old Catherine's courage, though she had briefly considered it. *"Why, they might take a photograph of my child and put it in the papers!"* Mrs. Welland had exclaimed when the plan was hinted at. From this unthinkable indecency, the clan recoiled with a collective shudder. The ancestress had to yield, but only after securing the promise that the wedding breakfast would take place under her roof—a concession that made the trip to the Mingotts feel like a journey to the end of nowhere.

Despite these widely reported arrangements, a sporting minority still hoped Old Catherine would appear, causing a distinct drop in temperature when it was revealed she had been replaced by her daughter-in-law. Mrs. Lovell Mingott arrived with the high color and glassy stare typical of ladies of her age and habits in new dresses. Once the disappointment of her mother-in-law's absence faded, it was agreed that her black Chantilly over lilac satin, accented with a bonnet of Parma violets, formed the happiest contrast to Mrs. Welland's blue and plum tones.

A far different impression was made by the gaunt, mincing lady who followed on Mr. Mingott's arm, dressed in a wild dishevelment of stripes, fringes, and floating scarves. As this last apparition glided into view, Archer's heart contracted and stopped beating.

He had assumed the Marchioness Manson was still in Washington, having departed four weeks prior with her niece, Madame Olenska. It was generally understood their sudden departure stemmed from Madame Olenska's desire to remove her aunt from the baleful eloquence of Dr. Agathon Carver, who had nearly recruited her for the Valley of Love. Under these circumstances, no one expected either lady to return for the wedding. For a moment, Archer stood frozen, eyes fixed on Medora's fantastic figure, straining to see who came behind her. But the procession ended as the lesser family members took their seats, and the eight tall ushers gathered like birds preparing for migration, slipping through the side doors into the lobby.

*"Newland—I say: she's here!"* the best man whispered urgently.

Archer roused himself with a start.

A long time seemed to have passed since his heart had ceased, for the white and rosy procession was already halfway up the nave. The Bishop, the Rector, and two white-winged assistants hovered about the flower-banked altar, while the first chords of the Spohr symphony strewed their flower-like notes before the bride.

Archer opened his eyes—or perhaps they had been shut, as he imagined—and felt his heart begin its usual labor. The music, the scent of lilies, the vision of the cloud of tulle and orange blossoms drifting nearer, the sight of Mrs. Archer's face suddenly convulsed with happy sobs, the low benedictory murmur of the Rector, and the ordered movements of the pink bridesmaids and black ushers—all these sights, sounds, and sensations, so familiar yet unutterably strange in his new role, swirled confusedly in his brain.

*"My God,"* he thought, *"have I got the ring?"*—and once more he performed the bridegroom's convulsive gesture.

Then, in an instant, May was beside him, radiating such light that it sent a faint warmth through his numbness. He straightened himself and smiled into her eyes.

*"Dearly beloved, we are gathered together here,"* the Rector began...

The ring was on her hand, the Bishop's benediction given, the bridesmaids poised to resume their places, and the organ showing preliminary signs of bursting into the Mendelssohn March, without which no newlywed couple ever emerged onto New York streets.

*"Your arm—I say, give her your arm!"* young Newland hissed nervously.

Once again, Archer felt himself adrift in the unknown. What had sent him there? Perhaps it was the glimpse, among the anonymous spectators in the transept, of a dark coil of hair under a hat, which a moment later revealed itself as belonging to an unknown lady with a long nose, so laughably unlike the person whose image she had evoked that he questioned if he were becoming subject to hallucinations.

Now, he and his wife paced slowly down the nave, carried forward on the light Mendelssohn ripples. The spring day beckoned through the widely opened doors, while Mrs. Welland's chestnuts, adorned with big white favours, curvetted and showed off at the far end of the canvas tunnel.

A footman, wearing a still larger white favour on his lapel, wrapped May's white cloak about her. Archer jumped into the brougham at her side. She turned to him with a triumphant smile, and their hands clasped beneath her veil.

*"Darling!"* Archer said. Suddenly, the same black abyss yawned before him, and he felt himself sinking into it, deeper and deeper, while his voice rambled on smoothly and cheerfully: *"Yes, of course I thought I'd lost the ring; no wedding would be complete if the poor devil of a bridegroom didn't go through that. But you did keep me waiting, you know! I had time to think of every horror that might possibly happen."*

She surprised him by turning, in full Fifth Avenue, and flinging her arms about his neck. *"But none ever can happen now, can it, Newland, as long as we two are together?"*

Every detail of the day had been meticulously planned. After the wedding breakfast, the young couple had ample time to change into traveling clothes, descend the wide Mingott stairs between laughing bridesmaids and weeping parents, and enter the brougham under the traditional shower of rice and satin slippers. There was still half an hour to drive to the station, purchase the latest weeklies at the bookstall with the air of seasoned travelers, and settle into the reserved compartment where May's maid had already placed her dove-colored traveling cloak and a glaringly new dressing bag from London.

The old Du Lac aunts at Rhinebeck had offered their house to the bridal couple, inspired by the prospect of spending a week in New York with Mrs. Archer. Glad to escape the usual "bridal suite" in a Philadelphia or Baltimore hotel, Archer accepted with equal alacrity.

May was enchanted by the idea of going to the country, childishly amused by the vain efforts of the eight bridesmaids to guess where their mysterious retreat lay. It was considered "very English" to have a country house lent to one, adding a final touch of distinction to what was generally conceded to be the most brilliant wedding of the year. Yet, where the house was located remained a secret known only to the parents of the bride and groom. When pressed, they pursed their lips and said mysteriously, *"Ah, they didn't tell us..."* which was manifestly true, since there was no need to.

Once settled in their compartment, and as the train shook off the endless wooden suburbs to push into the pale landscape of spring, conversation became easier than Archer expected. May remained, in look and tone, the simple girl of yesterday, eager to compare notes on the wedding incidents with impartiality, much like a bridesmaid debriefing with an usher. At first, Archer had fancied this detachment was a disguise for an inward tremor, but her clear eyes revealed only the most tranquil unawareness. She was alone for the first time with her husband, yet her husband was merely the charming comrade of yesterday. There was no one she liked more, no one she trusted completely. The culminating "lark" of the entire delightful adventure of engagement and marriage was simply to go off with him alone on a journey, like a grown-up person, like a "married woman," in fact.
