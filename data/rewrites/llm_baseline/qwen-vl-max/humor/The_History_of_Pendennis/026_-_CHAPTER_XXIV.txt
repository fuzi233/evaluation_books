Every house has its skeleton—usually tucked away in the attic, behind a wardrobe, or beneath a pile of dusty old hats. And while some folks might find comfort in knowing that even the wealthiest and most seemingly blessed neighbors have their secrets, miseries, and occasional bouts of existential dread, others just wish the skeletons would stop whispering so loudly. In Clavering House, the skeleton wasn’t hidden at all—it walked around in velvet slippers, recited poetry in French, and had an uncanny talent for making everyone else feel like they’d failed life’s entrance exam.

Meet Blanche Amory—our dear little Muse, as she liked to call herself. She sang sweetly, spoke with the precision of a poet who’d read too many novels, and possessed such a delicate soul that you could practically hear her heart breaking from three rooms away. On paper, she was a vision: a young lady of genius, sensitivity, and literary ambition. In practice? She was the reason Sir Francis Clavering wanted to throw her down a well.

One afternoon, after a game of billiards and a cigar (a combination known to unlock the deepest secrets), the Baronet turned to Captain Strong and said, “I say, Strong, I wish to the doose your wife was dead.”

“Ah,” replied Strong, puffing thoughtfully, “so do I. But she won’t die. She’ll live forever—just watch. Why do you wish her off the hooks, Frank?”

“Because then you might marry Missy,” drawled Sir Francis. “She ain’t bad-looking. She’ll have ten thousand pounds. That’s a tidy sum for a poor devil like you.”

“And gad, Strong, I hate her worse every day,” the Baronet continued. “I can’t stand her. By gad, I can’t.”

“I wouldn’t take her at twice the price,” laughed Strong. “Never seen such a little devil in my life.”

“I should like to poison her,” declared Sir Francis, with the solemnity of a man who’d just finished reading a particularly tragic novel. “By Jove, I should.”

“Why?” asked Strong. “What has she done now?”

“Nothing particular,” answered the Baronet. “Just her usual tricks. That girl has such a knack for making people miserable it’s almost impressive. Last night she sent the governess crying out of dinner. Then, as I passed Frank’s room, I heard him howling in the dark—his sister had been telling him ghost stories about the haunted house. At lunch, she gave my lady a turn. My wife’s a fool, but she’s a good soul—I’m hanged if she isn’t.”

“What did Missy do?” Strong asked.

“She started talking about the late Amory—my predecessor,” said Sir Francis, grinning. “She pulled out a picture from *The Keepsake* and insisted it looked just like her dear father. Wanted to know where his grave was. Hang her father! Every time Miss Amory brings him up, Lady Clavering bursts into tears. And the little minx does it on purpose—just to spite her mother. Today when she started, I lost my temper. Told her I was her father—and other things. Then, sir, she took a swing at me.”

“And what did she say?” Strong asked, still chuckling.

“Gad, she said I wasn’t her father. That I wasn’t fit to comprehend her. That her father must’ve been a man of genius and fine feelings—whereas I married her mother for money.”

“Well, didn’t you?” asked Strong.

“It don’t make it any pleasanter to hear because it’s true,” sighed Sir Francis. “I ain’t a literary man—but I ain’t the fool she makes me out to be. She always manages to put me in the hole. She turns the whole house upside down with her quiet ways and sentimental airs. I wish she were dead, Ned.”

“It was my wife you wanted dead earlier,” reminded Strong.

“Well,” said the Baronet, with perfect honesty, “when people bore my life out, I do wish they were dead. And I wish Missy were down a well—with all my heart.”

So there you have it. Our charming little Blanche, though gifted with a poetic soul and a mind like a finely tuned harp, had somehow managed to become the emotional black hole of Clavering House. She was intelligent, sensitive, and deeply artistic—qualities that should have made her beloved. Instead, she used them like weapons.

Blanche had been christened Betsy, but promptly renamed herself Blanche—a decision she made with the seriousness of a queen declaring war. Her stepfather, Sir Francis, held one terrifying threat over her: he would call her Betsy in public. The mere mention of it was enough to bring her to heel, like a child afraid of being called “Bessie” at school.

Her education had been rich in emotion and poor in practicality. From an early age, she’d devoured Lamartine, Balzac, George Sand, and every French novel ever written. By sixteen, she’d fallen in love with Prince Rodolph, Prince Djalma, and Indiana—all without leaving the drawing room. She’d settled the divorce question before she’d learned to tie her own shoelaces.

At eleven, she’d felt tender emotions for a Savoyard organ-grinder in Paris whom she believed to be a prince kidnapped by villains. At twelve, she’d been smitten by an old, hideous drawing master—proof, perhaps, that love knows no logic. At thirteen, she’d carried on a secret correspondence with two boys from Monsieur Rogron’s pension, writing letters filled with passion and dramatic declarations that would’ve made Byron blush.

Her heartbreaks were legendary. She’d lost a beloved sister—only to discover later that she was working as a bookkeeper in her father’s grocer shop on the Rue du Bac. Disappointments, estrangements, disillusionments—she collected them like stamps. But she didn’t just suffer; she broadcasted her suffering. If life was unfair, she’d sing an elegy. If someone disappointed her, she’d write a dirge. She was a poet, after all—why shouldn’t she bemoan her lot?

And so, her tears became a daily feature. They flowed like a spring in April—readily available, endlessly renewable. Sentiment, as Horace once observed (though probably not about this), increases with indulgence. The more you cry, the better you get at it. Blanche was a master.

She ruled the household with a soft voice and a sharper tongue. If Lady Clavering said “Sparrowgrass” instead of “Asparagus,” or referred to a “hobject” instead of a “household object,” Missy would correct her with the calm authority of a grammarian from hell. This only made her mother more nervous, which led to more mistakes, which led to more corrections. It was a vicious cycle, and Blanche was the architect.

Only Captain Strong remained immune. His good humor was like a fortress built of optimism and cigars. He could laugh through sarcasm, endure melancholy, and still enjoy a game of billiards. Everyone else, however, lived under Blanche’s poetic tyranny.

Her maid, Pincott, was a woman of gentle spirit and hard work. She came from a family of Parisian colonists who’d seen better days. Her father was crippled, and returning home would only add to the burden. So she stayed, stitching and hemming for Blanche, who demanded new outfits daily, like a fashion editor with a mood disorder.

Pincott was kind, skilled, and utterly exhausted. She rose at dawn, worked till midnight, and was often found weeping into her tea. Blanche, ever the poet, never missed an opportunity to remind her of this:

“I should send you away, Pincott,” she’d say, “you’re too weak, your eyes are failing, and you’re always snivelling. But I keep you because your parents need support. Mind, I go on enduring you for their sake.”

Or: “Pincott, your wretched appearance gives me the migraine. I think I shall make you wear rouge—just to look cheerful.”

Then, after hours of torment, Blanche would go to bed at one o’clock, say, “You may kiss me. Good night. Have the pink dress ready tomorrow,” and fall asleep like a queen.

Meanwhile, Pincott had to rise again at five, already pale and tired, to begin another day of endless alterations. She couldn’t attend Madame Fribsby’s soirées—those delightful evenings of green tea, scandal, and hot Sally-Lunn cakes. Not that anyone missed her. She was invisible, like a shadow in a sunlit room.

But not everyone in the household was ignored. There was Monsieur Alcide Mirobolant, chief of the kitchen, whose flamboyant style made him a local spectacle. On summer afternoons, he’d stroll through Clavering in his light green paletot, crimson velvet waistcoat, blue glass buttons, orange satin neckcloth, and jean boots tipped with shiny leather. He wore a gold-embroidered cap and a gilt cane, believing himself the very image of Parisian elegance.

To the villagers, he looked like a circus performer who’d wandered off course. Children followed him, shouting “Frenchy!” and “Frogs!” Some wanted a lock of his long, flowing hair. Others jeered and hooted. He thought they admired him—until the mob turned ugly.

That’s when Madame Fribsby intervened. She saw the harassed chef and rushed out, hand extended, speaking to him in French. She invited him into her home, stood at the door like a warrior defending her castle, and told the mob they were cowards for bullying a man who couldn’t speak their language.

Mirobolant was moved. He’d never heard French spoken so badly—and yet, he loved it. He vowed she was his guardian angel. The next day, he sent her a basket of delicacies: pineapple cream, lobster mayonnaise, and preserved fruits from Provence, along with a note declaring her kindness had created a “green place in the desert of his existence.”

Their friendship deepened. He called her “La respectable Fribsbi,” “La vertueuse Fribsbi,” and insisted she was his mother. She, meanwhile, remembered a time when someone had spoken to her in French in a very different tone. She glanced up at the portrait of her Carabineer and sighed. At fifty, she still felt like a girl of eighteen.

Alcide, however, refused to see her as anything but a mother figure. When she tried to steer the conversation toward romance, he politely changed the subject. He was devoted—just not in the way she hoped.

And so, the little innocent, Blanche Amory, continued to reign over Clavering House with her tears, her poems, and her unrelenting need to be understood. She was a genius, yes—but also a tyrant, a drama queen, and the reason why Sir Francis Clavering sometimes dreamed of throwing her into a well. And yet, despite everything, she remained, in her own fragile way, a little muse—one who sang sad songs and broke hearts with the grace of a butterfly caught in a net.
