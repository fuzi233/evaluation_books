The good Bishop of Cambray, in his celebrated romance, painted the sorrows of Calypso when Ulysses departed from her shores, but history is silent on the grief of Calypso’s maid upon parting with Odysseus’s own manservant. No doubt, while their betters wept in grand saloons, the domestics below exchanged tearful farewells in the kitchens. When the final bell tolled, and the ship’s mate roared, “All ashore!” both mistress and maid must have trod the same trembling plank, hearts throbbing, eyes streaming, waving their handkerchiefs—one of lace, the other of humbler linen—toward the retreating vessel. The crowd on the quay and the sailors in the prow might cheer, but the truth remained: if Calypso could not be consoled, neither could her maid. Grief is no respecter of rank; tears taste just as salt in a palace as in a scullery.

And so it was not only Harry Warrington who left Castlewood a lovesick swain, but his servant Gumbo as well, whose charms had bewitched the entire female household. His wit, his dancing, his skill in cookery and music, had endeared him to every maid from the still-room to the laundry. The men might grumble, but the women adored him. In those days, England had not yet learned to scorn dark skin; black servants were met with tolerance, even a rough kindness, especially from women, who have ever been tender toward those the world deems lesser. Gumbo, like many a man before and since, spun tales of royal lineage—descended, he claimed, from Ashantee kings. (How many such exiled monarchs must there have been, judging by their descendants scattered across the globe?)

On the morning of Madame de Bernstein’s departure, Castlewood’s servants crowded the halls, some to glimpse the Baroness’s retinue, others to bid farewell to Gumbo, and all to secure parting tokens from their wealthy guests. The Baroness, returning to the home of her youth, dispensed her bounty liberally, and Harry—having lived at free cost under the Earl’s roof and won both horses and promises of money from his cousin Will and the luckless chaplain—felt honor-bound to match her generosity. “My mother would expect no less,” he thought, scattering gold as though he were as rich as Gumbo boasted. From the stately major-domo to the lowly bootblack, none were forgotten. Love had softened him toward every soul in the household; even the grim housekeeper and the rheumy old porter received their due.

For when a man loves one woman in a family, he becomes inexplicably fond of them all—indulging the maids, flattering the butler, humoring the aunts, even feigning interest in the children’s tedious performances. So it was with Harry. In the brief, radiant time since his love had been confessed and returned, he had played the gallant to all. Cousin Will might rage at his losses, but Harry, though smitten, was no fool. A Virginian knew a good horse when he saw one, and the brown hunter that had nearly pitched him into the pond upon his arrival was now his own. He had won it fairly, along with a tidy sum and a stack of Mr. Esmond’s promissory notes.

That night, after sighing at the stars outside Maria’s window, Harry retired to dream of her—and of his new horse. How sweet it would be to ride with her clinging to him, her arms about his waist! Morning came too soon, and with it, betrayal. The chaplain’s boy arrived, red-eyed, leading not the prized brown hunter but a broken-kneed bay nag.

“Mr. William rode Prince William to Salisbury Races,” the groom explained, grinning. “His last words were, ‘Saddle Cato for Mr. Warrington—he owns him now.’”

Harry’s curses were cut short by Lord Castlewood’s laughter. “Will has outplayed you, cousin.”

“Outcheated, my lord!” Harry seethed.

But time pressed. The carriages were ready, the Baroness ensconced within, and Maria, radiant in her riding habit, awaited him. With a final glare at the groom, Harry mounted the sorry beast, Gumbo trotting behind on the chaplain’s black mare.

The villagers cheered as he rode out, their hats waving. They liked the young Virginian, with his open hand and honest face. Tales of his grandparents still lingered here—how Colonel Esmond might have been Earl, had he wished it. The present lord was a hard master, and Will a scoundrel, but Harry? Harry was a prince from a land of gold.

He spurred forward, the bay’s clumsy gait no match for his eagerness to rejoin Maria’s carriage. When he drew alongside, her finger pressed to her lips—Madame Bernstein dozed within. For hours, they whispered, their words soft as the rustling hedgerows. At the inn, over dinner, their hands brushed, a glass toppled, and Maria’s gasp woke no one. Harry’s cuff had reached for hers, and she did not pull away.

By dusk, the cavalcade rolled into Farnham, where the innkeeper bowed low. “The Baroness Bernstein! The rich young American!” the crowd murmured. Harry barely heard them. His world was the press of Maria’s fingers, the promise in her eyes.

And so the descent began—not into Avernus, perhaps, but into a far sweeter abyss.
