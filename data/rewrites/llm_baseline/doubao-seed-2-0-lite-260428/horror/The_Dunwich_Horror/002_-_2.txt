The hour was 5 a.m., February 2nd, 1913, in the backwoods township of Dunwich. The wind howled off the steep, unblessed hills, carrying the echo of unnameable rumblings that had rumbled through the dark for twelve straight hours, and the froth-flecked baying of every hound in the county that had not once fallen silent all night. The date was Candlemas, though no local dared speak that name out loud—they called it the Feast of Unlatched Gates, and none in the valley had ever forgotten what those open gates invited through.

It was in that half-rotted, half-empty farmhouse clinging to the slope four miles from the village, a mile and a half from the nearest neighbor, that Wilbur Whateley drew his first breath. No midwife attended. No doctor had been called. For minutes after he emerged, a shriek tore through the air loud enough to cut through the hills' groaning and the dogs' racket, a scream that would rattle distant farmhouse windows and linger in the memories of anyone who heard it for decades.

His mother was Lavinia Whateley: 35, a lopsided, unattractive albino, her pink eyes perpetually watering, her frame hunched, living alone with her half-insane 90-year-old father. Old Whateley had been the subject of the most horrific wizardry whispers in the valley for three generations, and his wife had been found hacked to pieces with an axe in the farm's root cellar when Lavinia was 12 years old—no killer ever identified. Lavinia had never gone to school, never had a known husband, and made no pretense of disavowing the dark, swarthy, goatish-looking infant that was nothing short of a stark, shocking contrast to her own pale, sickly skin and hair. She was not ashamed. She was strangely, unnaturally proud, muttering wild prophecies of unearthly power and a fate that would shake the hills as she rocked him on her knee. She was the kind of woman to mutter such things: a loner who wandered the moors barefoot even in the height of thunderstorms, poring over the two-hundred-year-old mouldering books the Whateley line had hoarded for centuries, pages softening with rot and wormholes, reeking of dried ichor and unknown incense. No standard of cleanliness or order had touched the farmhouse for decades, leaving her free to indulge in wild, grand daydreams of things the other Dunwich villagers only dared whisper about behind closed doors.

No one in the village knew the baby existed for a full week, until Old Whateley drove his sleigh through knee-deep snow down to Osborn's general store. The men loitering by the stove noticed at once that something had shifted in the old man: a new, furtive edge to his clouded mad eyes that turned him from a silly local oddity into something actively, fundamentally terrifying. He carried the same unholy pride his daughter did, and his rambling words about the baby's paternity were remembered word for word by every man who heard them, long after the horror of 1928 had burned its way across the valley:
"I dun't keer what folks think--ef Lavinny's boy looked like his pa, he wouldn't look like nothin' ye expeck. Ye needn't think the only folks is the folks hereabouts. Lavinny's read some, an' has seed some things the most o' ye only tell abaout. I calc'late her man is as good a husban' as ye kin find this side of Aylesbury; an' ef ye knowed as much abaout the hills as I dew, ye wouldn't ast no better church weddin' nor her'n. Let me tell ye suthin'--some day yew folks'll hear a child o' Lavinny's a-callin' its father's name on the top o' Sentinel Hill!"

Only two outsiders saw Wilbur in his first month of life: Zechariah Whateley, of the still-"respectable" branch of the family, who delivered a pair of Alderney cows Old Whateley had bought, and Mamie Bishop, Earl Sawyer's common-law wife, who tramped up the hill out of pure curiosity. Mamie came back down white as a sheet, and never repeated half of what she saw. The Whateleys began buying cattle by the head after that, hundreds of dollars worth of them every year, right up until the horror broke in 1928—yet their ramshackle barn never looked full. Curious locals would sneak up the steep pasture to count the herd grazing there, and never found more than ten or twelve thin, anemic, bloodless beasts. Weird, clean, incision-like slashes scored their hides, no explanation for the wounds. More than one caller spotted identical thin, oozing cuts on the throats of old Whateley and his slattern albino daughter, too.

Spring came, and Lavinia tramped the hills again, the heavy swarthy child cradled in her lopsided arms. No one bothered to remark on how impossibly fast he grew. At three months old, he was the size and muscular strength of a fully developed one-year-old, his movements slow, deliberate, totally free of the jerky flail of a normal human infant. He walked unassisted at seven months, no wobbly stumbles, no tumbles, and by eight months he moved across uneven ground as steadily as a grown man.

That Halloween, a searing, bonfire-sized blaze was spotted on the peak of Sentinel Hill, right where the ancient flat stone table jutted out of a tumulus piled high with unidentifiable human and animal bones. Silas Bishop, out rounding up a stray heifer, caught sight of mother and son darting up the slope an hour before the fire was seen, moving noiselessly through the underbrush without a stitch of clothing on, so fast he could barely track them. Later he would not swear to the boy's nakedness—he thought Wilbur might have worn a fringed leather belt, and a scrap of dark blue cloth around his hips. No one ever saw Wilbur in partial clothing after that. He was always fully, tightly buttoned from throat to ankle, and if anyone so much as brushed a hand against his collar to loosen it, he would snarl with unchildish rage and terror, as if any sliver of exposed skin would reveal the secret he hid under the cloth.

The next January, the gossips of Dunwich chattered over the fact that "Lavinny's black brat" had started talking at only eleven months old. No baby lisp marred his words. His accent was nothing like the thick rural drawl of the valley, the deep resonant tone seeming to rumble from somewhere far lower in his chest than a normal child's throat could reach. His face was preternaturally mature: no soft baby fat, a sharp, prematurely shaped nose, large dark near-Latin eyes that glinted with a sharp, unhuman intelligence. He was hideously ugly, despite that air of cleverness: thick full lips, coarse yellowed skin pocked with large pores, crinkly rough hair, and weirdly elongated, almost pointed ears that gave him a distinct, unsettlingly goatish cast. He was hated more deeply than his mother and grandfather had ever been, and every whisper about him circled back to old tales of Old Whateley standing in the stone circle on Sentinel Hill decades before, screaming the unnameable syllables of *Yog-Sothoth* at the top of his lungs, while the ground shook so hard every chimney in the valley cracked. Dogs hated him on sight, snapping and snarling and straining at their leashes until foam dripped from their jowls, and he was forced to carry a heavy ironwood club wherever he went to fend them off. No one in Dunwich knew then how right they were to fear him.
