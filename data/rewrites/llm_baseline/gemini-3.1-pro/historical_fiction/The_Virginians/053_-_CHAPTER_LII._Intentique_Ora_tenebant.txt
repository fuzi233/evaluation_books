The sensory memory of Duquesne remains, even now, a discordant symphony in my mind, a jarring clash of mechanical, soul-grinding military percussion against the quiet, symphonic rhythms of the earth. We continued for months our weary life at the fort. The commandant, Museau, and I shared a cramped, airless existence defined by our greasy games of cards and the dreary, arrhythmic duets of his asthmatic flute and my cracked guitar—a thin, reedy soundscape that provided little psychological refuge from the relentless, industrial clatter of garrison life. 

The poor Fawn, La Biche, absorbed her beatings and the harsh, distilled bite of her master’s liquor with a stoic silence. She nursed her papoose, tended her master in the gout, and soothed my own ague with deep, restorative bone-marrow broths. So matters churned in their cyclical routine until the beginning of the fall of last year, when a hunter arrived. He carried news that disrupted the auditory peace of our small outpost, sending a chaotic hum of excitement through the men. The Marquis de Montcalm had sent a considerable detachment to garrison the forts already in French hands. Troops were moving up the St. Lawrence in bateaux, bringing heavy artillery and large provisions—a vast, militaristic machine grinding its way into the pristine acoustic ecology of the frontier. 

Museau was to be superseded by an officer of superior rank. This new commander might exchange me, or worse, surrender me to the native allies in reprisal for the cruelties our own people had inflicted upon the enemy. The fort’s men reveled in the news, their voices rising in a cacophony of imperial conquest: they would seize Albany and Philadelphia, they would drive the *Rosbifs* into the sea. 

Yet, amid this triumphant noise, Mr. Museau sat in sullen quiet. The prospect of French expansion offered no nourishment to his soul.

“Eh, commandant!” I said to him, leaning into the one truth I knew could pierce his military posturing. “'Tis *fort bien*, but meanwhile, your farm in Normandy... the deep, cellar-cool casks of sharp, fermented cider, and the *tripes à la mode de Caen*—slow-simmered for hours in calvados until the meat is meltingly gelatinous and fragrant with wild thyme and garlic—where are they?”

“Yes; 'tis all very well, my *garçon*,” he sighed, the olfactory ghost of his homeland momentarily softening his rugged face. “But where will you be when poor old Museau is superseded? Very few men have my humanity. Will my successors offer thee the culinary indulgences and quiet hours honest Museau has granted? Thou wilt be kept in a sty like a pig ready for slaughter. And if one of our officers falls to your frontier brigands, thou wilt pay with thy skin. Thou wilt be given up to the brethren of La Biche yonder. Didst thou see, last year, what they did to thy countrymen we took at Braddock’s field? Roasting was the very smallest punishment, *ma foi*—was it not, La Biche?”

He plunged into a jocular, graphic recounting of bodily mutilations. You pale, dear Miss Theo! Well, I shall spare you the sterile horrors honest Museau recounted in his crude manner. 

La Biche was undisturbed by the recital. She had witnessed the cyclical violence of these woods long before the Europeans brought their deafening muskets. She came from the Senecas, whose ancestral villages lay near the great cataract between Ontario and Erie, a place defined by the magnificent, roaring acoustics of falling water. Her people had fought alongside and against the English. In the brutal translations of war, the savagery of whiteskin and redskin blurred into the same unholy din.

“They may chop me into cutlets and broil me, 'tis true, commandant,” I said coolly, holding his gaze. “But again, I say, you will never have the farm in Normandy. You will never taste the rich, crusty crumb of your village bread, nor hear the familiar clack of Javotte’s loom at the door.”

“Go get the whisky-bottle, La Biche,” Museau muttered, his voice thick with a sudden, overwhelming hunger for his native soil.

“It is not too late,” I pressed softly. “I will give the guide who takes me home a large reward. And I promise, upon my honor, ten thousand livres to one who shall bring me any token—my watch and seal with my grandfather's arms, resting in a chest somewhere in this fort.”

“Ah, *scélérat*!” he roared, a hoarse yell of laughter breaking the tension. “Thou hast eyes! All is good prize in war.”

“Think of the farmhouse,” I continued, painting the landscape he craved. “A field hard by with a half-dozen milk cows. A fine orchard heavy with the sweet, cider-yielding fruit.”

“And a rascal of a child, or two, with cheeks as red as the apples! O my country! O my mother!” whimpered the commandant, completely undone by the invocation of his agrarian heritage. “Quick, La Biche, the whisky!”

All that night, Museau brooded in the heavy silence of his cupidity. La Biche sat away from us, nursing her child. The poor infant began to wail—a natural, desperate sound quickly silenced by Museau’s foul, barked orders. He banished La Biche to her luckless quarters. With her gone, I presented arguments to Monsieur that his starving, homesick heart could not resist.

“How do you know this hunter will serve you?” he asked.

“That is my secret,” I replied. When hunters came into the settlements, they often lingered for rest, company, and the cheap, commercialized liquor that stripped them of their beautifully crafted peltries. This hunter, Silverheels, engaged in this shallow trade, gambling away the profound, tactile labor of the trapping season. Yet, I noticed he also listened. When the harsh military drills ceased, I would scrape my guitar, trying to weave some harmonic solace into the deadened air of the fort.

One evening, testing the acoustic waters, I announced an English song. To the tune of “Over the hills and far away,” which my grandfather used to hum in Marlborough's camp, I sang my own doggerel words, projecting them clearly across the room: 
*“This long, long year, a prisoner drear;*
*Ah, me! I'm tired of lingering here:*
*I'll give a hundred guineas gay,*
*To be over the hills and far away.”*

“What is it?” the hunter asked. “I don't understand.”

“'Tis a girl to her lover,” I answered, but the subtle shift in his auditory focus told me he understood perfectly. 

The next day, beyond the auditory range of the guards, the trapper passed me and hummed the melody in perfect English, the resonant timbre of his voice vibrating with shared intent. “If you are ready,” he said, “I am ready. Talk to the Fawn, and she will tell you what to do.” As soldiers approached, he immediately pivoted, loudly offering in French to play cards, masking our profound connection beneath the superficial noise of gambling.

La Biche was to be our conduit. The tragic reality of our communication lay in the linguistic power dynamics of the fort: she was forced to speak a broken French jargon, an imposed lexicon that systematically erased the deep, nuanced botanical and spiritual knowledge embedded in her native Seneca tongue. Yet, her actions spoke a universal language of sustenance. She had tended my wounds, silently cleaned my chamber, and prepared my meals, moving freely between the fort and the rich, loamy fields where she foraged. 

Three days after the trapper departed with his fresh stores, La Biche signaled the time had come.

My quarters were a log house on the bastion, looking westward over a small gun toward the confluence of the Ohio and Monongahela rivers. Across the water stood a clump of trees. 

“You see those trees?” my poor Biche whispered the day before, constrained by her limited French. “He wait for you behind those trees.”

That afternoon, she returned from foraging. She carried a basket heavy with earthy sunchokes, fibrous wild leeks, and bitter potherbs destined for the evening soup—ingredients whose true, indigenous names were fading into the silence of cultural memory. Hidden beneath these roots was an iron pick. As we spoke, she casually bumped the basket, sending the heavy tool sliding into my room. “Hide it,” she murmured.

The following day, she orchestrated her ruse. Pretending anger, her voice raised in artificial scolding, she cried out, “My lord! my lord! why you not come to commandant's dinner? He very bad! *Entendez-vous?*” As she peered into my room, she swiftly flung a coiled rope at my feet. “*Pour ce soir,*” she whispered.

Museau knew nothing. At dinner, he growled at the temperature of his soup, his palate dulled by his anxieties. He stared at me steadily, talking of mundane things while we smoked our pipes and played piquet. La Biche sat cowering in her usual corner, a silent shadow in the room's heavy, smoky atmosphere.

When the whisky bottle ran dry, Museau announced his need for another glass and stumped into the inner room. The moment his heavy boots crossed the threshold, La Biche rushed to my feet. She kissed my hand, pressing it to her heart, her tears falling hot upon my knees. I was profoundly moved by this untranslatable expression of subjective experience—her silent, devoted affection transcended the crude language of her captors. When Museau returned, I was still shaken, though the Fawn had already vanished back into the folds of her blanket.

We resumed our game. “I gain everything from you to-night, milor,” Museau said grimly. “We play upon parole.”

“And you may count upon mine. My word is good for ten thousand livres.”

At last, claiming a headache, he ordered me to retire. “Stay, you will fall without your crutch,” he grinned sarcastically, noticing that in my agitation, I was walking with the brisk, healed stride of a grenadier. 

“What a *vilain* night!” he noted, peering outside. A grand tempest was brewing, the wind roaring through the timber in a magnificent, chaotic symphony that would perfectly mask any sound I made. He locked me into my quarters, and I caught one final glimpse of La Biche standing quietly behind him.

The heavy rain drove the sentries into their boxes. The log house was built with upright posts driven deeply into the earth. For hours, I dug through the wet, fragrant loam, the smell of damp clay and severed roots filling the dark space. By midnight, I pushed my hand beneath the bottom log and felt the cold, cleansing strike of the rain. I wriggled through the gap, creeping to the parapet. 

The wall was a twelve-foot drop into the ditch. Pausing beneath the gun, I listened to the sentry pacing above, humming a tune—a small, lonely human sound swallowed by the vast, howling acoustic space of the storm. The moon eventually pierced the clouds, illuminating the dark rocks and the rushing, silver waters of the river. 

I made for the island, slipping through the wet undergrowth toward the marked trees. The relief that washed over me was absolute when I heard, cutting through the natural rustle of the leaves, a low voice humming the exact, familiar cadence of *“Over the hills and far away.”*

When Mr. George reached this point in his narrative, Miss Theo, who was seated by the harpsichord, turned and dashed off the tune upon the instrument. The rich, woody resonance of the keys filled the room, and the little company broke out into the merry chorus, the music briefly bridging the gap between that distant, rain-swept riverbank and the warm parlor.

“Our way,” George resumed, letting the final chord fade, “lay through a level tract of forest with which Silverheels was intimately familiar. By daylight, the dense canopy broke, and my guide asked if I recognized the landscape. It was the fatal field where Braddock had fallen—the very ground from which I had been rescued the previous summer. The pacing of our lives is so deeply circular; here I was, returning to the site of mythic ruin, though now, the land was quietly reclaiming the blood drawn by empires. The leaves were turning, painting the forest in the magnificent, fiery hues of autumn.”

“Ah, brother!” cried Harry, seizing George’s hand. “I was gambling and making a fool of myself in the superficial noise of London, while my George was flying for his life in the wilderness! Oh, what a miserable spendthrift I have been!”

“But I think thou art not unworthy to be called thy mother's son,” said Mrs. Lambert softly, her eyes moistened. To witness Harry’s unselfish joy was to feel that there was hope for the humbled young man.

“We crossed the river,” George continued, “following the western slopes of the Alleghanies. We entered a grand forest region of immense oaks and maples, ancient sentinels rising a hundred feet without a branch, their leaves creating a ceaseless, oceanic rustle in the wind. 

“Our journey lasted a fortnight. The frosts nipped sharply, and we slept wrapped in blankets by our small fires, the scent of woodsmoke clinging to our clothes. In this season, the agrarian elders of the mountains engage in their oldest rituals, tapping the great maples for sugar. We encountered several of these family camps by the mountain streams. They welcomed us into their quiet domestic routines, sharing the rich, caramelized sweetness of their sugar and the deeply savory, wood-roasted cuts of their venison—a profound communion born of the earth itself. 

“Thus nourished, we passed over the Laurel Hills. Our final march took us down the magnificent pass of Will's Creek, walking through a valley flanked by bald, white cliffs towering a thousand feet high. Above us, eagles wheeled in the vast, unbroken silence, watching their nests among the crags. And from that mythic height, we descended into Cumberland, returning at last to the comforting sight of English colors rippling on the banks of our native Potomac.”
