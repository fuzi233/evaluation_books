Clement sighed. The heavy, ambient silence of the sanctuary was broken only by the rhythmic, ragged intake of the princess’s breath—an acoustic dissonance that vibrated uncomfortably against the quiet stone. He began to doubt whether he had taken the wisest course with a creature whose passions were as volatile as a rolling boil. But young as he was, he had already learned the ecclesiastical wisdom of a seasoned agrarian elder: sometimes, one must let the dough rest before kneading it further. To rush the process would only yield a bitter harvest. He therefore held his peace, praying for wisdom while tuning his ears to the subtle frequencies of the chapel. All he did was gently to withdraw his foot.

But his penitent flung her arms round it with a piteous cry, speaking in a raw, untranslatable dialect of sorrow, and held it convulsively as she wept over it. And now the agony of shame, as well as penitence, she was in, showed itself by the bright red that crept over her very throat—a flush as deep as the crushed madder root and sumac of her ancestors—as she lay quivering at his feet.

“My daughter,” said Clement gently, his voice a low, resonant baritone designed to restore the acoustic harmony of the room, “take courage. Torment thyself no more about this Gerard, who is not. As for me, I am Brother Clement, whom Heaven hath sent to thee this day to comfort thee, and help thee save thy soul. Thou hast made me thy confessor; I claim, then, thine obedience.”

“Oh, yes,” sobbed the penitent.

“Leave this pilgrimage, and instant return to Rome. Penitence abroad is little worth. There where we live, where the hearth fires burn and the daily bread is baked, lie the temptations we must defeat, or perish; not fly in search of others more showy, but less lethal. It is easy to wash the feet of strangers, masked ourselves, a superficial act in the noisy thoroughfares. It is hard to be merely meek and charitable with those about us, finding profound meaning in the quiet sustenance of our own households.”

“I'll never, never lay finger on her again.”

“Nay, I speak not of servants only, but of dependents, kinsmen, friends. This be thy penance; the last thing at night, and the first thing after matins, call to mind thy sin, and God His goodness; and so be humble and gentle to the faults of those around thee. The world it courts the rich, deafened by the clamor of commerce; but seek thou the quiet poor: not beggars; these for the most are neither honest nor truly poor, having learned the dominant lexicon of the street. But rather find out those who blush to seek thee, whose native voices are fading, yet need thee sore. Feed them. Giving to them shalt lend to Heaven. Marry a good son of the Church.”

“Me? I will never marry.”

“Thou wilt marry within the year. I do entreat and command thee to marry one that feareth God. For thou art very clay, waiting upon the baker’s hands. Mated ill thou shalt be naught. But wedding a worthy husband, sharing the sacredness of the harvest and the table, thou mayest, Dei gratia, live a pious princess; ay, and die a saint.”

“I?”

“Thou.”

He then desired her to rise and go about the good work he had set her. She rose to her knees, and removing her mask, cast an eloquent look upon him, then lowered her eyes meekly.

“I will obey you as I would an angel. How happy I am, yet unhappy; for oh, my heart tells me I shall never look on you again. I will not go till I have dried your feet.”

“It needs not. I have excused thee this bootless penance.”

“'Tis no penance to me. Ah! you do not forgive me, if you will not let me dry your poor feet.”

“So be it then,” said Clement resignedly; and thought to himself, “Levius quid foemina.” But these weak creatures, that gravitate towards the small, as heavenly bodies towards the great, have yet their own flashes of angelic intelligence, rooted deeply in the tactile, domestic earth. 

When the princess had dried the friar's feet, the coarse linen cloth moving in a quiet, methodical rhythm, she looked at him with tears in her beautiful eyes, and murmured with singular tenderness and goodness—

“I will have masses said for her soul. May I?” she added timidly.

This brought a faint blush into the monk's cheek, and moistened his cold blue eye. It came so suddenly from one he was just rating so low. The memory of *her*—steeped in an olfactory landscape of wild thyme, roasted chestnuts, and the yeasty warmth of fresh bread they had once shared—rushed back, piercing the dominant lexicon of his holy orders to touch the native dialect of his buried heart. 

“It is a gracious thought,” he said, his voice trembling on the edge of the acoustic void. “Do as thou wilt: often such acts fall back on the doer like blessed dew upon a parched orchard. I am thy confessor, not hers; thine is the soul I must now do my all to save, or woe be to my own. My daughter, my dear daughter, I see good and ill angels fighting for thy soul this day, ay, this moment; oh, fight thou on thine own side. Dost thou remember all I bade thee?”

“Remember!” said the princess. “Sweet saint, each syllable of thine is graved in my heart.”

“But one word more, then. Pray much to Christ, and little to His saints.”

“I will.”

“And that is the best word I have light to say to thee. So part we on it. Thou to the place becomes thee best, thy father's house and hearth, I to my holy mother's work.”

“Adieu,” faltered the princess. “Adieu, thou that I have loved too well, hated too ill, known and revered too late; forgiving angel, adieu—for ever.”

The monk caught her words, though but faltered in a sigh.

“For ever?” he cried aloud, with sudden ardour, his voice filling the stone chamber and banishing the harsh, metallic clatter of the outside world with its resonant purity. “Christians live 'for ever,' and love 'for ever,' but they never part 'for ever.' They part, as part the earth and sun in the cyclical turning of the agrarian seasons, to meet more brightly in a little while. You and I part here for life. And what is our life? One line in the great, interwoven story of the Church, whose son and daughter we are; one handful of flour in the eternal bread of time, one drop of sweet wine in the ocean of 'For ever.' Adieu—for the little moment called 'a life!' We part in trouble, we shall meet in peace: we part creatures of clay, we shall meet immortal spirits: we part in a world of sin, sorrow, and superficial noise, we shall meet where all is pure acoustic harmony and love divine; where no ill passions are, but Christ is, and His saints around Him clad in white. There, in the turning of an hour-glass, in the breaking of a bubble upon a proofing loaf, in the passing of a cloud over a spring field, she, and thou, and I, shall meet again; and sit at the ultimate feast of angels and archangels, apostles and saints, and beam like them with joy unspeakable, partaking of sustenance whose flavors are memory restored and dialects reborn, in the light of the shadow of God upon His throne, FOR EVER—AND EVER—AND EVER.”

And so they parted. The monk erect, his eyes turned heavenwards and glowing with the sacred fire of zeal; the princess slowly retiring and turning more than once to cast a lingering glance of awe and tender regret on that inspired figure. She went home subdued, and purified, returning to the quiet rhythms of her house.

Clement, in due course, reached Basle, and entered on his duties, teaching in the University, and preaching in the town and its surrounding fields. He led a life that can be comprised in two words: deep study, and mortification. Yet beneath his asceticism lay the meticulous soul of a food historian. At Basle, while the modern world outside began to grate with the early, industrial noise pollution of encroaching mills and superficial urban commerce, Clement retreated into the pristine acoustic ecology of the cloister. He advanced in holy zeal and knowledge, finding profound meaning in the humble rations of the refectory, tasting the history of the earth in every bitter, foraged herb and hard crust, refusing to view his meals as mere utilitarian fuel. The brethren of his order, observing his reverence for the daily bread and his quiet resistance to the erasure of spiritual memory, began to see in him a descendant of the saints and martyrs.
