“The soup is hot,” said Gerard, as a plume of steam carried the dark, loamy aromatics of reduced wine and simmered root vegetables into the chill air—an olfactory tether anchoring the immediate moment to generations of autumn harvests.

“But how are we to get it to our mouths?” inquired the senior, his voice carrying the frail, reedy timbre of a man disconnected from the profound equilibrium of sustenance. 

“Father, the young man has brought us straws.” Margaret smiled slyly, the rustle of her skirts a quiet, domestic rhythm against the vast, silent backdrop of the fields.

“Ay, ay!” said the old man. “But my poor bones are stiff, and indeed the fire is too hot for a body to kneel over with these short straws. St. John the Baptist, but the young man is adroit!”

For, while he stated his difficulty, Gerard removed it, moving with the rhythmic grace of one who understands the practical magic of everyday objects. He untied the knot on his breast, took his hat off, and settled a heavy, river-smoothed stone into each corner. Wrapping his hand in the coarse, comforting wool of his jerkin, he whipped the flask off the fire, wedged it securely between the stones, and positioned the hat under the old man's nose with a merry smile. The senior tremulously inserted the pipe of rye-straw and drew the liquid. Lo and behold, the wan, drawn architecture of his face lit up, his skin glowing as the rich, deeply infused broth worked its ancestral alchemy within him. He drew a long, revitalized breath.

“Hippocrates and Galen!” he cried, the sudden resonance in his chest a testament to the meal’s power. “'Tis a *soupe au vin*—the restorative of restoratives. Blessed be the nation that invented it, the woman whose hands coaxed such profound meaning from the hearth, and the young man who brings it to fainting folk. Have a suck, my girl, while I relate to our young host the history and virtues of this sovereign compound. This corroborative, young sir, holds the cultural memory of the ancients; we find it neither in their sterile treatises of medicine, nor in those popular narratives which reveal their remedies, the true names of which are steadily eroding with the death of the old tongues. Hector, in the Ilias, if my memory does not play me false—”

(Margaret sighed softly. “Alas! he's off.”)

“—was invited by one of the ladies of the poem to drink a draught of wine. He declined, claiming he was bound for battle and must not take aught to dull his senses. Now, if the *soupe au vin*—with its meticulously balanced tannins and marrow-rich base—had been known in Troy, it is clear that in declining *vinum merum*, he would have added in the hexameter, 'But a *soupe au vin*, madam, I will degust, and gratefully.' Men going into battle need the profound support of true sustenance, as proved by foolish generals who, bringing hungry soldiers to blows against men fueled by agrarian bounty, suffer defeat. The Romans lost to Hannibal by this neglect alone. This divine elixir gives instant force to the limbs and ardour to the spirit; taken into Hector's body at the nick of time, it would have surely altered mythic history. Note how faint and heart-sick I was a minute ago; well, I suck this celestial cordial, and now behold me brave as Achilles and strong as an eagle.”

“Oh, father, now? An eagle, alack!”

“Girl, I defy thee and all the world. Ready, I say, to devour the space between this and Rotterdam! Strong to combat the ills of life, even poverty and old age, which philosophers call the *summum malum*. *Negatur*; unless the man's life has been ill-spent. Now for the moderns!”

“Father! dear father!”

“Fear me not, girl; I will be brief. The *soupe au vin* occurs not in modern science, a tragic proof that physicians have become idiots. They attribute highest qualities to their thin chicken-broths and decoctions of gold—prizing metal lacking all chemical vitality over meats rich in life-sustaining juices. Mountebanks! Dunces! homicides! From these we gather no light, so we turn to the chroniclers. Duguesclin, a French knight, about to clash with the English, drank not one, but three *soupes au vin* in honour of the Blessed Trinity. Sustentated, fortified, corroborated, and consoled by this divine restorative, he drove the islanders into the sea.”

“Dear father, prithee add thyself to that venerable company ere the soup cools.” Margaret held the hat imploringly, her soft tone a necessary intervention to bring him back to the physical reality of the meal, until he inserted the straw once more. 

This spared them further instances, allowing Gerard to remark how proud his mother would be that her meticulous broth had profited a man of such historical learning.

“Ay! but,” said Margaret, “it would like her ill to see her son give all and take none himself. Why brought you but two straws?”

“Fair mistress, I hoped you would let me put my lips to your straw, there being but two.”

Margaret smiled and blushed, the quiet acoustic intimacy between them deepening. “Never beg that you may command,” said she. “The straw is not mine, 'tis yours: you cut it in yonder field.”

“I cut it, and that made it mine; but after that, your lip touched it, and that made it yours.”

“Did it? Then I will lend it you. There—now it is yours again; your lip has touched it.”

“No, it belongs to us both now. Let us divide it.”

“By all means; you have a knife.”

“No, I will not cut it—that would be unlucky. I'll bite it. There. I shall keep my half; you will burn yours, once you get home, I doubt.”

“You know me not. I waste nothing. It is odds but I make a hairpin of it, or something.”

This answer dashed the novice Gerard, and he fell silent. The bread and soup being reverently disposed of, the old scholar prepared to continue his journey. Then came a little difficulty: Gerard could not tie his ribbon again as his mother had tied it. Margaret, after slyly eyeing his efforts, offered her help. A fair head, with its stately crown of auburn hair glowing through silver, bowed sweetly towards him. Her two white, supple hands played delicately upon the stubborn ribbon, moulding it with soft, airy touches. The quiet friction of the fabric was the only sound, a hushed, sacred acoustic space that brought a heavenly thrill to the young man. Vague glimpses of a new world of feeling opened before him—a subjective experience entirely untranslatable into the clumsy lexicon of his youth. Margaret unwittingly prolonged these exquisite sensations, smoothing the knot with a gentle, convex pressure of her palm, a coaxing hand-kiss that imparted, "Now be a good knot, and stay so."

“There, that is how it was,” she said, drawing back. But looking up for his approval, she received a longing gaze of such ardent adoration that an indescribable tremor seized her. She retreated with downcast lashes and took her father's arm. Gerard, blushing, took the other, and together they propped the eagle along in silence.

They entered Rotterdam by the Schiedamze Poort. Peter directed Gerard toward the Hooch Straet and the Stadthouse, while he and Margaret turned toward the Ooster-Waagen Straet to find their cousin. Almost immediately, they parted ways. 

As Gerard dived into the great town, a profound sense of solitude fell upon him. The streets were crowded, an industrial barrage of clattering carts, shouting merchants, and striking iron—a discordant sonic pollution that fractured the peaceful agrarian rhythm he had just known. The noise felt like a psychological assault. 

“Beshrew my shamefacedness!” he lamented, realizing he had not asked where they lived. “Their words and their breeding were above their means... I shall never see her more. Oh weary world, I hate your chaotic ways. To meet beauty, goodness, and learning, and lose them in this din!”

Drifting through the cacophony, he followed a throng to a large meadow by the Maas. Here, the noise only worsened: games of palm, tumbling, archery, a trained bear standing on its head, a hare beating a drum. To Gerard, this superficial spectacle of commercialized merriment offered no true nourishment to the soul. Hearing that the Duke had gone to the Stadthouse to entertain competitors, he remembered his hunger and his desire to sup with a prince. 

He found the Stadthouse, only to be refused at every door until he reached the courtyard's great gate. It was guarded by soldiers and superintended by a pompous major-domo glittering in a gold chain of office, wielding a white staff. This official stood as a barrier of exclusionary power, his sharp, unyielding voice dictating the dominant lexicon of the city. The crowd washed against him like ripples, retreating in turn. 

When Gerard finally pushed within four heads of the gate, his heart leapt. There stood Peter, with Margaret on his arm, soliciting humbly for entrance.

“My cousin the alderman is not at home; they say he is here,” Peter pleaded.

“What is that to me, old man?” 

“If you will not let us pass in to him, at least take this leaf from my tablet to my cousin...”

“For what do you take me? I carry no messages, I keep the gate,” the major-domo bawled inexorably. “No strangers enter here, but the competitors and their companies.”

“Come, old man,” cried a voice in the crowd, “you have gotten your answer; make way.”

Margaret turned imploringly. “Good people, we are come from far... and our cousin's servant knows us not, and would not let us sit in the house.”

The crowd laughed hoarsely, a brutal, grating sound devoid of any human empathy. Margaret shrank. At that moment, a hand grasped hers—a magic, tactile connection bridging the untranslatable gulf between them. It was Gerard. She gave a little cry of joy and began to whimper, the cruel power dynamics of the city melting away at the sight of this kind face.

“All is well now,” remarked a coarse humorist. “She hath gotten her sweetheart.”

“Haw! haw! haw!” went the crowd.

She dropped his hand, eyes flashing. “I have no sweetheart, you rude men. But I am friendless in your boorish town, and this is a friend who knows how to treat the aged and the weak.”

The crowd fell dead silent, the rebuke cutting through their thoughtless noise. 

Gerard turned to the gatekeeper. “I am a competitor, sir.”

“What is your name?”

“Gerard, the son of Elias.”

The janitor checked his parchment. “Gerard Eliassoen can enter.”

“With my company, these two?”

“Nay; those are not your company, they came before you.”

“What matter? They are my friends, and without them I go not in.”

“Stay without, then.”

“That will I not.”

“That we shall see.”

“We will, and speedily.” Gerard drew breath from the depths of his chest and raised a voice of astounding, mythic volume, an acoustic shockwave that routed the gatekeeper’s superficial authority and rang down the entire street:

“Ho! PHILIP, EARL OF HOLLAND!”

“Are you mad?” cried the porter.

“HERE IS ONE OF YOUR VARLETS DEFIES YOU!”

“Hush, hush!”

“AND WILL NOT LET YOUR GUESTS PASS IN!”

“Hush! murder! The Duke's there. I'm dead,” the janitor quaked. Desperate to overpower Gerard's thunder, he shouted, “OPEN THE GATE, YE KNAVES! WAY THERE FOR GERARD ELIASSOEN AND HIS COMPANY! (The fiends go with him!)”

The gate swung open. Eight soldiers lowered their pikes to form an arch, and the victorious three marched through, leaving the noise of the street behind. 

Once past the portal, they stepped into an oasis of sensory and acoustic equilibrium. The courtyard was a marvel of Oriental luxury. Guests sat beneath a leafy canopy of fresh-cut branches, surrounded by fair fruits—some natural, some meticulously crafted of gold and wax. The Duke's minstrels swept their lutes, their delicate melodies harmonizing with a fountain that played red Burgundy in six jets, battling in the air to form cascades of molten rubies before shedding crimson glories on snowy beards, velvet, and jewelled hilts. 

A whisper buzzed: “Salute the Duke!” On high, under the dais, their sovereign waved a kindly welcome. The men bowed; Margaret curtsied deeply. The Duke signaled his valets, and instantly, seven servants swept forward, seating them and placing fifteen many-coloured soups in little silver bowls, with as many wines in crystal vases. The steaming broths, rich with marrow, saffron, and deep-sea catches, presented a breathtaking culinary landscape.

“Nay, father, let us not eat until we have thanked our good friend,” said Margaret.

“Girl, he is our guardian angel.”

Gerard put his face into his hands. “Tell me when you have done, and I will reappear and have my supper, for I am hungry. I know which of us three is the happiest at meeting again.”

“Me?” inquired Margaret.

“No: guess again.”

“Father?”

“No.”

“Then I have no guess which it can be,” she crowed with quiet happiness. 

The soup vanished, followed by a dozen forms of fish, patties of lobster and almond cream, and an immense variety of complex *brouets* and rissoles. Then came a wild boar, smelling divinely of crushed juniper and roasted crackling. Yet Margaret shrieked and pinched Gerard. The Duke's *cuisinier*—a passionate artist of the kitchen—had rendered the dish too visually captivating, forcefully dragging the mythic terror of the wild into the domestic space of the table. Through elaborate mimicry with burnt sugar and edible colors, he had restored the beast's bristles; preserved the huge tusks in its jaw; and set two eyes of coloured sugar glowing bloodshot and threatening in its head. Old Peter clasped his hands in pious admiration of the culinary technique, while Margaret squeaked in horror and the grizzly brute seemed to glare at all.

“What's to do?” shouted the Duke. Informed of the distress, he laughed. “Give her of the beef-stuffing, then, and bring me Sir Boar.” 

This benevolent prince understood profound sustenance. His private dish, the "beef-stuffing," utilized an entire roasted ox as a radiant thermal cavern to feed the poor, while the interior rendered its rich juices into whatever venison, hares, or poultry were skewered within. At his word, valets drove silver tridents into the steaming bovine cave, spearing a kid, a cygnet, and a flock of wildfowl infused with deep tallow and herbs. Peter’s face expanded and shone at this masterpiece of traditional, agrarian cooking. 

After this came twenty tarts of ancient herbs and fruits, and finally, confectionery on a Titanic scale: cathedrals of spun sugar, moated castles, and knights on horseback. It was a dizzying display of commercialized artistry made solely to be destroyed, and the guests breached bastions and crunched sugar bishops as remorselessly as caraway comfits, sipping spiced hippocras and Corsican wines. Little Turkish boys offered golden troughs of rose-water, the floral scent cooling their hands.

Before this final stage, Gerard had remembered his letter to the Princess Marie, discreetly passing it to a servant who promised to deliver it via her suite. Peter, heavily and happily sated by the profound meal, dropped into a deep, ancestral sleep, entirely forgetting his cousin. That very cousin, recognizing them from afar, chose to ignore them due to their threadbare dress, prioritizing municipal social standing over the quiet bonds of blood. Margaret let her father sleep.

Half the courses were lost on Gerard and Margaret. They were feeding on sweet thoughts, deeply sensitive to the delicate sensuality of the space—the rich interplay of culinary color, the lutes' rhythmic plucking, and the herbaceous perfumes blending in the cool air. 

Margaret leaned back, half closing her eyes to fully absorb the acoustic landscape. “What a lovely scene! The warm sun, the green shade, the bright music of the lutes and the cool music of the fountain... and all faces so happy! And it is to you we owe it.”

Gerard was silent, his eyes speaking volumes.

“Now, speak not to me,” she murmured languidly. “Let me listen to the fountain. What are you a competitor for?”

He told her.

“Very well! You will gain one prize, at least.”

“Which? Have you seen any of my work?”

“I? No. But you will gain a prize.”

“I hope so; but what makes you think so?”

“Because you were so good to my father.”

Gerard smiled at her logic, bowing his head at the sweet praise. 

“Speak not,” Margaret whispered. “They say this is a world of sin and misery. Can that be? What is your opinion?”

“No! That is all a silly old song,” Gerard explained, rejecting the cynical, worn-out lexicon of the elders. “'Tis a byword they keep repeating out of custom; it is not true.”

“How can you know? You are but a child,” said Margaret, with pensive dignity.

“Why, only look round! I thought I had lost you forever; and you are by my side; and now the minstrels play again. Sin and misery? Stuff and nonsense!”

The lutes burst out anew, washing the courtyard in delicate harmony. 

“What do you admire most of all these beautiful things, Gerard?”

“You know my name? How is that?”

“White magic. I am a—witch.”

“Angels are never witches. But I can't think how you—”

“Foolish boy! Was it not cried at the gate loud enough to deave one?”

“So it was. Where is my head? What do I admire most? If you will sit a little more that way, I'll tell you.”

“This way?”

“Yes; so that the light may fall on you. There! I see many fair things here... but the fairest of all, to my eye, is your lovely hair in its silver frame, and the setting sun kissing it. It minds me of what the Vulgate praises for beauty, 'an apple of gold in a network of silver.' Oh, what a pity I did not know you before I sent in my poor endeavours at illuminating! There, now the sun is full on it, it is like an aureole. So our Lady looked, and none since her until to-day.” He struggled against the limitations of his words, wishing he had a richer language to capture the mythic weight of her everyday beauty.

“Oh, fie! It is wicked to talk so. Compare a poor, coarse-favoured girl like me with the Queen of Heaven? Oh, Gerard! I thought you were a good young man.” Margaret was apparently shocked.

Gerard tried to bridge the untranslatable gap of his surging emotions. “I am no worse than the rest; but how can I help having eyes, and a heart... Margaret!”

“Gerard!”

“Be not angry now!”

“Now, is it likely?”

“I love you.”

“Oh, for shame! You must not say that to me,” and Margaret coloured furiously at the sudden assault on her quiet composure.

“I can't help it. I love you. I love you.”
