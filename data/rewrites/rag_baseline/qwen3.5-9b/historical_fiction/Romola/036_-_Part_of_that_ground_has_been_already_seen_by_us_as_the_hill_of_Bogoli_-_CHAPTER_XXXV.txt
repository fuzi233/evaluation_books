Tito saw little of Romola for several days. The next morning, he approached her with gentle precision, instructing her to remove any personal effects from the library before the agents arrived to pack the antiquities. Leaning in to press a kiss to her brow, he advised her to retreat to her chamber, where the small painted tabernacle stood, keeping herself distant from the cacophony of unfamiliar footsteps. Romola assented silently, offering no visible emotional response. The preceding night had been a prolonged state of wakefulness; despite her robust constitution, sensation had dulled into a continuous, throbbing ache, as though she had been struck and bruised by invisible forces. Tito perceived her illness but held his tongue, fearing further distress. Observing that her hand and brow were ice-cold, he fetched a fur-lined mantle and draped it lightly over her shoulders. In every brief interval of his return, the tableau remained unchanged: he attempted to soothe her with subtle acts of tenderness, yet she appeared to have lost the faculty of speech or gaze directed toward him.

"Patience," he murmured to himself. "She will recover, and forgive eventually. The bond between us remains the strongest anchor." When a victim of violence recovers slowly, appearing unaffected, the aggressor often slides effortlessly into the role of the aggrieved party, feeling no sting of their own blow while acutely aware of their benevolent conduct. Yet Tito possessed no natural inclination toward grievance; his mind bent constantly toward reconciliation. He would have endured much to feel Romola's hand rest upon his head once more, as it had that morning when he first recoiled from viewing her. However, waiting patiently for the restoration of domestic harmony proved easier because his existence beyond the walls grew increasingly compelling. While a specific impulse may appear as the sole origin of a life trajectory, it is invariably the fruit of a character's gradual evolution. Since the moment in the Piazza del Duomo, when Tito, seated atop bales, derived sharp satisfaction from flattery that captivated crowds, his imagination had fixated on political engagement within Florence's turbulent public sphere. Conversely, the fresh terror instilled by Baldassarre had previously blocked this path, driving him to sell the library as insurance against exile, coinciding with his growing appreciation for the city. That dread had now subsided significantly. Though he must continue to don his armor and prepare for potential demands requiring cool judgment and ingenuity, he felt no compulsion to abandon Florence for new fortunes. His father's refusal of atonement, forcing a stance of defiance, highlighted the frailty of an aged man with failing memory in a foreign land—a weakness Tito was unwilling to exploit. Tito's latent desires were crystallizing into explicit strategy. As the fervor of youthful passion waned, life assumed for him the guise of a game blending skill and chance. The political arena of Florence promised a rapid, exhilarating contest of revolutionary struggle, rich with covert maneuvers where brilliant intellect could discard inconvenient dogmas in favor of pragmatic wisdom, such as the simple truth that ginger is hot in the mouth.

Immediately upon the departure of the French guests, Florence erupted into agitation akin to a colony of ants reacting to the removal of a looming shadow and the subsequent need to repair their encampment. "How shall we fund the French monarch?" "How do we manage the war with the stubborn Pisan rebels?" "Most critically, how do we restructure our governance to ensure magistrates are chosen and laws voted upon effectively?" Until these queries received satisfactory answers, commerce faced stagnation, and the vast segment of the populace excluded from citizenship—who lacked even a vote to numb their hunger—threatened to grow restless. Action was imperative. First, the great bell tolled, summoning citizens to a parliament in the Piazza de' Signori. When the throng packed tightly, hemmed in by armed guards at every exit, the Signoria—the Gonfaloniere and the eight current Priors—emerged and took their stand beside the stone lion on the platform before the Old Palace. They proposed granting dictatorial authority for one year to twenty leading men of the city, empowering them to select all magistrates and reorganize the governmental framework. The populace roared its assent, feeling themselves the electors of the Twenty. This parliamentary format was an ancient Florentine tradition designed to mask the will of the few as the choice of the many. The shouting in the piazza ceased quickly, but the debate within the palace intensified. Was Florence to adopt a Great Council modeled after Venice, where all officials and laws were elected by a broad base of qualified citizens regardless of rank or faction? Or would it follow a narrower, less populist scheme preserving the hereditary dominance of noble families against the dilution of shopkeepers' votes? Legal scholars argued day and night until late into the darkness. Messer Pagolantonio Soderini presented impeccable arguments for the popular model; Messer Guidantonio Vespucci countered with equally formidable reasoning for an aristocratic structure. It was a dispute over whether to boil or roast meat, a preference already decided by the disputants' palates; their eloquence might have continued indefinitely without altering the outcome, merely delaying the meal. The majority holding power within the palace sided with Vespucci, advocating for moderation; the majority outside, acutely aware of their powerlessness and grievances, feared change less. Outside the palace, a rising force began to coalesce the vague aspirations of this outer majority into a determined will: the preaching of Savonarola. Driven partly by a spiritual mandate to guide the people and partly by the pragmatic needs of politicians unable to pass measures without his support, he rapidly shifted his daily sermons from general exhortations to specific directives. He moved from urging the postponement of private passions for the public good to prescribing exactly what form of government would achieve that end—transitioning from "Choose whatever is best for all" to "Choose the Great Council," declaring it the divine will. To Savonarola, these were synonymous truths. The Great Council was the only viable mechanism to express a public will sufficient to counteract the corrupting influence of partisan interests, rendering honest, impartial administration at least possible. As Florence's government purified itself, becoming immune to schemes exploiting the moral degradation of others, the Florentine people would evolve into a pure community, destined to lead the renewal of the Church and the world. Fra Girolamo's mind never halted at intermediate steps; the objectives he pursued retained their sublime magnitude. He harbored no private malice, sought no petty gratification. Even in the final terrible days, when ignominy, torture, and the dread thereof exposed every hidden flaw in his soul, he could tell his relentless judges: "Do not wonder if it seems to you that I have told but few things; for my purposes were few and great."
