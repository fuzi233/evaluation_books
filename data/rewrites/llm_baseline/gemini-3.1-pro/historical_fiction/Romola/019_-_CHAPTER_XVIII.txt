When Tito left the Via de’ Bardi that day, stepping out into a Florentine afternoon where the harsh, dissonant clatter of the mercantile city scraped against the stone walls, he savored the unctuous sweetness of an easy life restored. The abrasive auditory pollution of the streets—the ringing of hammers, the shrieking of cartwheels, the relentless haggling of a populace obsessed with commercial gain—was a grating reminder of the perilous reality he had just escaped. For hours, a bitter, metallic tang of dread had coated his tongue, a cloying flavor born of his consciousness that he had acted without true foresight. He would not normally have parted with his ring. The sale of the gem was a discordant note in his otherwise smooth existence; Romola, raised on a refined and meticulous lexicon of classical antiquities, would surely notice its absence unless he masked it with the purchase of some special gift. 

Yet, as the sourness of his initial deception faded, Tito found himself swallowing the rich, honeyed draught of immunity. Kind fortune had cleansed his palate, and he felt no desire to entangle himself again. The results to Baldassarre were too problematical, a distant and flavorless concern. What lingered on his mind was little Tessa. The sweet, innocent, round thing—she was like a simple, rustic dish, delightful but entirely untranslatable to the sophisticated, scholarly world he now inhabited. Her mind was filled with a delusion wrapped in the native dialect of the countryside, an agrarian innocence that could never be synthesized with Romola’s commanding, dominant lexicon. Should he undeceive Tessa? To do so would require bridging two irreconcilable languages of experience. No, that unfortunate little incident of the *cerretano* must never be known to Romola. The subjective experience of Tessa’s naive love was inherently untranslatable. Better to leave her to the cyclical rhythms of the seasons, to ponder her secret until some ruddy *contadino*—a man intimately tied to the soil, who understood the ancestral agricultural cycles—made acceptable love to her and broke the silence. *Dunque*—good-bye, Tessa! 

With the fifty-five florins burning softly in his purse, Tito resolved to transform his ill-gotten wealth into a tangible shield. He sought a painted triptych, a masterful device to soothe Romola’s mind and guard against those austere monkish fancies that he found so utterly flavorless and repugnant. He directed his steps toward the Via Gualfonda to seek Piero di Cosimo, an artist pre-eminent in weaving mythic occurrences seamlessly into the fabric of domestic life. 

Entering the court upon which Piero’s dwelling opened, Tito encountered a profound testament to acoustic ecology. The heavy iron knocker on the door was thickly bound round with raw wool and ingeniously fastened with cords. It was a deliberate, physical barrier against the intrusive, abrasive noises of the world, a method of preserving the sonic environment that the painter required for his psychological and artistic well-being. Tito, recognizing this defense against the deafening thunder of superficial visitors, tapped modestly with his knuckles, the sound deadened by the thick fleece. In vain. 

He was turning away, lamenting the sterile silence, when a little girl entered the court. She carried a woven basket of eggs on her arm, the very picture of traditional, generational sustenance. Standing on tiptoe, she pushed up a small iron plate that ran in grooves and called out in a piping, melodic voice that pierced the muffled stillness: “Messer Piero!” 

Bolts drew back with a heavy, grounding resonance. Piero presented himself in a red night-cap and a loose brown serge tunic, his sleeves rolled up to the shoulder like a master baker at his oven. He darted a look of surprise at Tito, but his immediate reverence was for the food. He reached out to take the basket from the child, disappearing inside only to return with the empty vessel. 

“How much to pay?” 

“Two grossoni, Messer Piero; they are all ready boiled, my mother says.” 

The culinary transaction was simple but profound—the perfectly timed boiling of fresh, farm-gathered eggs, their chalky shells warm, their sulfurous, rich yolks offering the dense, vital energy needed to sustain a grueling day of creation. Piero extracted the coin from his leathern *scarsella*, and the little maiden trotted away, awed by the surprising young signor. 

Piero’s glance toward Tito lacked any such admiration. “What do you want at my door, Messer Greco? I saw you this morning at Nello’s; if you had asked me then, I could have told you that I see no man in this house without knowing his business and agreeing with him beforehand.” 

“Pardon, Messer Piero,” Tito replied, deploying his imperturbable good-humor, utilizing the smooth, dominant lexicon of the Florentine elite. “I remembered nothing but your admirable skill in inventing pretty caprices, when a sudden desire for something of that sort prompted me to come to you.” 

Piero made a habitual grimace, stretching the corners of his mouth and pressing down his brows, utterly rejecting the superficial stroking of Tito’s courtly syntax. “And what may that need be?” 

“I want a very delicate miniature device taken from certain fables of the poets. It must be painted on a wooden case in the form of a triptych. The inside may be simple gilding; on the outside, I want the triumph of Bacchus and Ariadne. A story in Ovid will give you the hints. The young Bacchus seated in a ship, his head bound with clusters of heavy, ripe grapes, a spear entwined with vine-leaves in his hand. Dark-berried ivy must wind about the masts, the oars must be thyrsi, and flowers must wreathe the poop. Leopards and tigers crouching, dolphins sporting. And the fair-haired Ariadne with him, made immortal with her golden crown—above them, young Loves shooting roses.” 

“Say no more!” Piero interrupted, his voice a gruff bark that cut through Tito’s polished descriptions. “I have Ovid in the vulgar tongue. Find me the passage. I love not to be choked with other men’s thoughts. You may come in.” 

It was a fierce defense of the native dialect. To Piero, the cultural memory of the myth did not live in the sterile, imperial Latin of scholars, but in the earthy, breathing vernacular of the people, where the words tasted of the very soil from which the grapes grew. 

He led Tito through the first room, where the freshly boiled eggs were deposited on the open hearth beside a heap of broken shells and a bank of pale ashes—the remnants of past sustenance. Nearby sat a carved ebony bedstead draped in a rich oriental carpet, and a carved *cassone*, surrounded by easels and cobwebs. 

The next room was a sensory and olfactory revelation, an interwoven timeline where the wildness of nature reclaimed the domestic sphere. The double door beneath the window was thrown open to a thicket where fig-trees and grapevines grew in tangled, trailing wildness among stinging nettles and hemlocks. A tall cypress lifted its dark head from a stifling mass of yellowish mulberry-leaves. The dank, loamy luxuriance of the earth penetrated the lofty room; tufts of long grass and dark, feathery, anise-scented fennel sprouted among a confused heap of carved marble fragments and rusty armor. A large stone vase, tilted on its side, seemed to pour out a living stream of ivy. 

The air was thick with the scent of drying botanicals and the raw materials of existence. Irregular shelves held scattered books, great drooping bunches of golden corn, bullocks’ horns, pieces of dried honeycomb smelling of ancient summers, stones patched with rare-colored lichen, and large birds’ wings. Amidst this heterogeneous still life—where mythic sketches of sea-monsters and maenads hung on the walls—the acoustic environment was soft, punctuated only by the gentle cooing of speckled pigeons strutting across the floor. Three corpulent toads crawled intimately near the door-stone, while a white rabbit twitched its nose with immense satisfaction, masticating a box full of bran. Here, the mythic was effortlessly, organically domestic. 

“And now, Messer Greco,” Piero said, folding his arms, “don’t be trying to see everything at once, like Messer Domeneddio, but let me know how large you would have this same triptych.” 

Tito provided the dimensions, and Piero marked them before reaching down a manuscript volume. 

“There’s nothing about the Ariadne there,” Tito said, giving him the passage, “but you will remember I want the crowned Ariadne by the side of the young Bacchus: she must have golden hair.” 

“Ha!” Piero pursed his lips abruptly. “And you want them to be likenesses, eh?” he added, peering into Tito’s face. 

Tito laughed, his cheeks flushing. “I know you are great at portraits, Messer Piero; but I could not ask Ariadne to sit for you, because the painting is a secret.” 

“There it is! I want her to sit to me. Giovanni Vespucci wants me to paint him a picture of Oedipus and Antigone at Colonos. I have a fancy for the subject, and I want Bardo and his daughter to sit for it. Now, you ask them; and then I’ll put the likeness into Ariadne.” 

The request was an appeal to a multi-generational saga—the blind, ancient father led by his devoted daughter, an embodiment of inherited memory and tragic time looping upon itself. 

“Agreed, if I can prevail with them. And your price for the Bacchus and Ariadne?” 

“*Baie*! If you get them to let me paint them, that will pay me. I’d rather not have your money: you may pay for the case.” 

“And when shall I sit for you?” Tito asked. “For if we have one likeness, we must have two.” 

“I don’t want *your* likeness; I’ve got it already,” Piero said, “only I’ve made you look frightened. I must take the fright out of it for Bacchus.” 

Piero went to the wall and returned with an oil-sketch. “I call this as good a bit of portrait as I ever did. Yours is a face that expresses fear well, because it’s naturally a bright one. I noticed it the first time I saw you. The rest of the picture is hardly sketched; but I’ve painted *you* in thoroughly.” 

He turned the canvas. Tito saw himself with his right hand uplifted, holding a wine-cup in an attitude of triumphant joy, but his face was turned away from the fermented vintage. The dilated eyes and pallid lips held an expression of such intense, visceral terror that Tito felt a cold stream rush through his veins. It was the sudden, untranslatable shock of confronting his own subjective dread, captured in pigment. 

“You are beginning to look like it already,” Piero noted with a short laugh. “He’s seeing a ghost—that fine young man. I shall finish it some day, when I’ve settled what sort of ghost is the most terrible—whether it should look solid, like a dead man come to life, or half transparent, like a mist.” 

Ashamed of his sudden, shivering sensitivity, Tito forced a careless tone. “That is a subject after your own heart, Messer Piero—a revel interrupted by a ghost. You seem to love the blending of the terrible with the gay. I suppose that is the reason your shelves are so well furnished with death’s-heads, while you are painting those roguish Loves who are running away with the armor of Mars. I begin to think you are a Cynic philosopher in the pleasant disguise of a cunning painter.” 

“Not I, Messer Greco,” Piero grunted, rejecting the abstract, sterile label. “A philosopher is the last sort of animal I should choose to resemble. I find it enough to live, without spinning lies to account for life. Fowls cackle, asses bray, women chatter, and philosophers spin false reasons—that’s the effect the sight of the world brings out of them. Well, I am an animal that paints instead of cackling, or braying, or spinning lies. And now, I think, our business is done; you’ll keep to your side of the bargain about the Oedipus and Antigone?” 

“I will do my best,” Tito said, taking the strong hint and immediately moving toward the door, eager to escape the earthy, penetrating truths of the studio. “And you’ll let me know at Nello’s. No need to come here again.” 

“I understand,” Tito said laughingly, lifting his hand in a sign of friendly parting, before slipping back out into the grating, noisy dissonance of the Florentine streets.
