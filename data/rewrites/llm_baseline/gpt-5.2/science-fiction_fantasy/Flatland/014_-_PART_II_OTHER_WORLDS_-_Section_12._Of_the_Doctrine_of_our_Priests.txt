The Priests of our land—those perfect Circles whose very edges seem to hum with authority—compress their whole doctrine into a maxim so often repeated that it falls, like a refrain, into the rhythm of daily life: Attend to your Configuration.

It is spoken in the Courts, murmured in the schools, and carried even into the kitchen-quarters where the day’s bread is squared and portioned. Not a stirring of the State’s great ladle, not a rule of household discipline, not a public hymn chanted to the measured cadence of official drums, but is seasoned with this one idea: that the shape of a man is the root of his conduct; that morality itself is no more than geometry made visible.

The Circles boast—justly, if one cares only for order—that they have quenched the old heresies which once led our ancestors to waste sympathy upon the foolish notion that a Figure might, by mere will, become better. Effort, encouragement, praise, training—these, they say, are airy spices that scent the room yet do not feed. What nourishes, what strengthens, what cures, is Configuration.

Pantocyclus, that illustrious Circle who stamped out the Colour Revolt, was the first to persuade the multitude that Configuration makes the man. His argument was as plain as salt. If you are born an Isosceles with two sides uneven, you will go wrong—inevitably—unless those sides are made even. For that, you must be taken, as one takes bruised fruit to the preserving-pan, to the Isosceles Hospital. If you are a Triangle, Square, or any Polygon whatever, and your outline bears the least Irregularity, you must be carried to one of the Regular Hospitals to have the disease corrected; otherwise, sooner or later, you will be delivered—not as a patient but as a culprit—to the State Prison, or to the angle of the State Executioner.

Pantocyclus laid every fault at the door of bodily deviation from perfect Regularity. A crime might be congenital, of course; but it might also be accidental—born of a collision in a crowd where the air is thick with the rasp of edges and the mind is made frantic by perpetual scraping; or caused by neglect of proper exercise, or by excess of it; or even by a sudden change of temperature, shrinking one part of the frame, swelling another, as a badly stored loaf cracks on one side and domes on the other. Therefore, he concluded—cool as a steward weighing rations—that neither good conduct nor bad conduct is a fit subject for praise or blame. Why praise the integrity of a Square who defends his client faithfully, when what you truly admire is the precision of his right angles? Why blame a lying, thievish Isosceles, when what you ought rather to lament is the incurable inequality of his sides?

In theory, nothing could be neater. In practice, it sticks sometimes in the throat.

In the Courts the doctrine bites cleanly enough. If an Isosceles rogue pleads that he cannot help stealing because his sides are uneven, the Magistrate answers with equal simplicity: for that very reason—because you cannot help being a nuisance to your neighbours—I cannot help sentencing you to be consumed. The law falls like a cleaver; the matter is ended.

But in the small fermenting troubles of domestic life—where one cannot clap a child into Prison, nor send him to Consumption as one sends refuse to the furnace—the doctrine can turn awkward, like a sauce that will not bind. I have had it pressed upon me by one of my own Hexagonal grandsons. The boy, with all the innocence of appetite and all the cunning that appetite teaches, has pleaded disobedience as if it were a mere mishap of climate: a sudden change of temperature, he says, has been too much for his Perimeter; the blame lies not in him but in his Configuration; and, since Configuration is strengthened only by appropriate sustenance, the remedy is clear—abundance of the choicest sweetmeats.

Now there is a kind of logic in this that I cannot wholly reject; and yet there is a kind of household sense in me that cannot accept it. So I do what many do, though we confess it to ourselves with a faint taste of hypocrisy: I assume that a good, sound scolding—or, when necessary, castigation—possesses some latent strengthening influence upon a boy’s Configuration. I own I have no proof. It is like adding a pungent herb because one’s mother did so: one cannot demonstrate its virtue, yet the dish seems wrong without it.

Nor am I alone in this private evasion. I have observed that many of the highest Circles, sitting in judgment, use praise and blame freely toward Regular and Irregular Figures alike. And in their own chambers—behind the hush-curtains that soften the harsh hiss of the outer thoroughfares—I have heard them rebuke their children with as much passion about “right” and “wrong” as if those words were not mere instruments but real things that could be chosen, tasted, and refused.

Indeed, the Circles carry their policy so persistently into every mind that they reverse, in our land, the old commandment you have in Spaceland. With you, children are taught to honour their parents. With us—next to the Circles, who are the object of universal homage—a man is taught to honour his Grandson, if he has one; or, failing that, his Son. By honour is not meant indulgence, as if one were spooning honey into a mouth already sticky with it, but a reverent regard for the young one’s highest interests. The father must subordinate himself to posterity: it is preached as duty, and translated among the initiated as necessity and fitness. Thus the whole State advances, and one’s own line, if kept clean and Regular, climbs by degrees.

Yet if a humble Square may be permitted to say that anything Circular contains an element of weakness, the weak point appears to me in their relations with Women.

Since it is of the utmost importance that Irregular births should be discouraged, it follows that no Woman with Irregularities in her ancestry is a fit partner for one who desires his posterity to rise. The Irregularity of a Male can be measured; one can run the mind’s rule along his edges and detect the fault as readily as a cook detects grit between the teeth. But Women are straight—straight as a skewer—so that they are, visibly, “Regular.” Their Irregularity is invisible: not a present flaw but a potential one, concealed like a bitter seed in sweet fruit, ready to declare itself in offspring.

Therefore the State has devised another test: pedigrees, kept with a meticulousness that would shame the most careful keeper of recipes. These genealogies are preserved and supervised; and without a certified pedigree no Woman may marry. One might suppose that a Circle—proud of ancestry, mindful of descendants that might one day culminate in a Chief Circle—would be the most scrupulous in his choice. But it is not so.

The higher the rank, the more careless the choosing. An aspiring Isosceles, hoping to generate an Equilateral Son, would no more take a wife with a single ancestral blot than a baker would mix clean flour with weevil-dust. A Square or Pentagon, confident that his family is steadily rising, does not inquire above the five-hundredth generation. A Hexagon or Dodecagon is more negligent still. But a Circle has been known deliberately to take a wife whose Great-Grandfather was Irregular—tempted by some slight superiority of lustre, or by the charm of a low voice, which with us, even more than with you, is accounted “an excellent thing in Woman.”

Such marriages are, as might be expected, barren; or they produce positive Irregularity; or there is diminution of sides. But none of these evils has yet been sufficiently deterrent. The loss of a few sides in a highly developed Polygon is not easily noticed—like a pinch of salt forgotten in an over-spiced stew—and is sometimes compensated by a successful operation in the Neo-Therapeutic Gymnasium. And the Circles, too ready to make a virtue of their own languor, are disposed to acquiesce in infecundity as a law of superior development. Yet if this evil is not checked, the diminution of the Circular class may accelerate; and the day may not be far distant when, the race no longer able to produce a Chief Circle, the Constitution of Flatland must fall.

One other warning rises to my mind—harder to speak, harder still to remedy—and it too concerns Women.

About three hundred years ago, it was decreed by the Chief Circle that, since Women are deficient in Reason but abundant in Emotion, they ought no longer to be treated as rational, nor receive mental education. The consequence followed with the slow, steady certainty of dough left in a cold room: they were no longer taught to read, nor even enough Arithmetic to count the angles of husband or child; and so, with each generation, they declined sensibly in intellectual power. This quietism still prevails.

My fear is that, however well meant, the policy has been carried so far that it injures the Male sex also. For we Males must now live, as it were, a bi-lingual—almost a bi-mental—existence. In Women’s chambers we speak one dialect, thick with emotional terms: “love,” “duty,” “right,” “wrong,” “pity,” “hope”—names for conceptions which, in the strict tongue of science, have no existence, and whose fiction serves chiefly to rein in feminine exuberance. Among ourselves, and in our books, we use another vocabulary, another idiom entirely, pared down as carefully as a chef trims fat from a joint.

“Love” becomes “the anticipation of benefits.”
“Duty” becomes “necessity” or “fitness.”
And so on, word after word transmuted, as if the same ingredient were made to serve two different cuisines.

Moreover, before Women we speak with language implying the utmost deference to their sex; and they fully believe themselves adored by us almost as devoutly as the Chief Circle is adored. But behind their backs they are regarded—and named, by all except the very young—as little better than mindless organisms. Even Theology changes with the doorway: in the Women’s chambers, one creed; elsewhere, another.

This double training—of language, and therefore of thought—lays, I suspect, too heavy a burden on the young. At the age of three they are taken from maternal care and taught to unlearn the old speech—except for repeating it dutifully before Mothers and Nurses—and to learn the vocabulary and idiom of science. Already, I think, I discern a weakening in the grasp of mathematical truth compared with the robust intellect of our ancestors three hundred years ago. It is as though the mind, forced to dine at two tables with two incompatible rules of taste, loses its appetite for plain certainty.

I say nothing of the danger—though it exists—that some Woman might surreptitiously learn to read and convey to her sex the fruit of a single popular volume; nor of the chance that some infant Male, through indiscretion, might reveal to a Mother the secrets of the logical dialect. I rest my humble appeal on a simpler ground: the enfeebling of the Male intellect. For that reason alone I would entreat the highest Authorities to reconsider the regulations of Female education.

“O brave new worlds, that have such people in them!”
