As I stood beside Peter's tomb, the weight of our shared past settling upon me, Jorian's words spilled forth like a river. "They used to call him a magician out Sevenbergen way," he said, his voice heavy with a mix of reverence and warning. "And they say he gave them a glimpse of his craft before parting, foretelling Margaret's son's arrival down the Rhine, dressed in fine clothes and bearing a small fortune, but with a face scarred by a foreign blade and fewer limbs than he had when he departed." He paused, his eyes scanning the weathered stone slab that bore Peter's name, before continuing, "But, alas, none of it came to pass. Margaret remains consumed by grief, and Peter lies undisturbed beneath this stone, which she placed here herself, to keep him where he is."

As Jorian reached out to lift the stone, a frail hand grasped his shoulder, staying him. The friar's whisper was barely audible over the rustling of leaves in the gentle breeze. "How long has it been since Peter passed?" he asked, his voice trembling.

"Two months, good friar," Jorian replied, his tone steady despite the turmoil brewing within him.

"And did his daughter bury him, as is fitting?" the friar pressed, his eyes narrowing.

"No, I laid him to rest, but Margaret paid the fee and commissioned the stone," Jorian answered, his gaze drifting towards the surrounding hills, where the first hints of autumn's hue began to seep into the landscape.

The friar's grip tightened on Jorian's shoulder, his voice taking on a note of urgency. "Then, if Peter had but one known child, and that child was Margaret... Tell me, Jorian, do you believe she is alive?"

Jorian's eyes snapped back to the friar, a spark of defiance igniting within them. "I should be dead if I didn't believe it," he said, his voice low and even. "She saved my life mere weeks ago.
