The Curates at Tea.

Eighteen is the threshold where childhood's fairy tale ends and the real world begins. Until then, life is a storybook filled with heroes and monsters, where forests are darker and skies more radiant than nature allows. The moon is larger, the sun a burning heaven of gods. But at eighteen, the shores of Reality rise ahead, painted in blues and greens that promise spring meadows and living waters, yet hiding floods and sorrows beneath. We do not know this yet. Hope smiles, promising tomorrow, and Love wanders in like a lost angel, welcomed with open arms. We miss the quiver in his wing, unaware that his arrows carry wounds disguised as thrills of new life. We believe passion is purely good, blind to the school of experience that awaits us—a mentor with a frozen face, a heavy rod, and an inexorable hand that drags the novice into the wilds of life, forcing the acquisition of hard truths.

Caroline, having been escorted home by Robert, had no desire to spend the remainder of the evening with her Uncle Helstone. His study was sacred ground to her; she rarely intruded, staying away until the bell for prayers rang. He read the evening service in his customary nasal, clear, and monotonous voice. Afterward, as was her custom, she approached him.

"Good-night, uncle."

"Hey! You've been gadding abroad all day—visiting, dining out, and what not!"

"Only at the cottage."

"And have you learned your lessons?"

"Yes."

"And made a shirt?"

"Only part of one."

"Well, that will do. Stick to the needle, learn shirt-making and gown-making and piecrust-making, and you'll be a clever woman some day. Go to bed now. I'm busy with a pamphlet here."

Soon Caroline was locked inside her small bedroom, the door bolted, her white dressing-gown on, her long hair loosened to fall thick and soft to her waist. Resting from combing it, she leaned her cheek on her hand, staring at the carpet, and the visions of youth flooded her mind. Her thoughts spoke pleasantly, causing her to smile. She looked lovely in meditation, but the spirit of Youthful Hope seemed brighter. This flattering prophet promised no more disappointments, assuring her she had entered the true spring of morning. It was impossible for her now to suspect she was a sport of delusion; her foundations seemed solid.

"When people love, the next step is they marry," she reasoned. "I love Robert, and I am sure he loves me. When I looked up at him after reciting Chénier's poem, his eyes—so handsome—they sent the truth through my heart. Sometimes I am afraid to speak to him, lest I seem forward, yet tonight I could venture any thought; he was so indulgent. Walking up the lane, he was kind. His courtship isn't like the books; it is original, quiet, manly, sincere. I truly like him. I would be an excellent wife, studying his comforts and cherishing him. I am sure he will not be cold tomorrow. Tonight, he will surely come here or ask me to go there."

As she combed her hair, long as a mermaid's, she glanced in the glass. Plain reflections soberize, convincing one that others see no magic, but the fair conclude otherwise. Caroline saw a shape, a head that would be lovely if daguerreotyped. Confirmation to her hopes surged within her, and she sought her couch in undiminished gladness.

Next morning, she rose with equal joy. Entering her uncle's breakfast room, her soft cheerfulness made the little man of bronze think, for an instant, that she was becoming "a fine girl." Usually quiet and docile, she found many things to say today. She spoke of flowers blooming in the garden, the gardener's arrival, and starlings nesting in the church tower, wondering if the bell tolls frightened them.

Mr. Helstone opined that the birds were "like other fools who had just paired—insensible to inconvenience." Encouraged by her temporary spirits, Caroline dared a bold question.

"Uncle," she said, "whenever you speak of marriage you speak of it scornfully. Do you think people shouldn't marry?"

"It is decidedly the wisest plan to remain single, especially for women."

"Are all marriages unhappy?"

"Millions are unhappy. If everyone confessed the truth, perhaps all are more or less so."

"You are always vexed when asked to marry a couple. Why?"

"Because one does not like to act as accessory to the commission of a piece of pure folly."

Mr. Helstone seemed glad to vent his cynicism. Emboldened, Caroline pressed further.

"But why," she asked, "should it be pure folly? If two people like each other, why shouldn't they consent to live together?"

"They tire of each other—in a month. A yokefellow is not a companion; he or she is a fellow-sufferer."

Displeased by such views, Caroline retorted, "One would think you had never been married, uncle. One would think you were an old bachelor."

"Practically, I am so."

"But you have been married. Why were you so inconsistent?"

"Every man is mad once or twice in his life."

"So you tired of my aunt, and she of you, and you were miserable?"

Mr. Helstone pushed out his cynical lip, wrinkled his brown forehead, and grunted. Pressing harder, she asked if she did not suit him or if he was not sorry when she died.

"Caroline," he said, slapping the mahogany table, "understand this: it is vulgar and puerile to confound generals with particulars. In every case there is the rule and the exceptions. Your questions are stupid and babyish. Ring the bell."

Breakfast ended, and usually they separated, but today Caroline remained at the window-seat. Mr. Helstone looked uneasy but continued his paper, which featured dispatches from General Wellington. Unknown to him, tumultuous thoughts stirred in his niece's mind, disturbed like bees in a hive. She reviewed his character and his sentiments on marriage, sensing the wide chasm between their minds. Beyond her uncle, she saw another figure: a strange, dim, sinister shape—the half-remembered image of her father, James Helstone.

Rumors whispered of his bad character. Old servants hinted at his cruelty. She recalled dark weeks in a great town, shut up in a high garret without carpets or curtains, neglected by a father who returned like a madman or an idiot, screaming that she was a burden. Once, when she was sick, he had raved, threatening to kill her. She had been rescued, and never saw him again until his funeral. She also knew of her mother, though her uncle never spoke of her, likely because she was the drunkard's wife.

Turning from the lattice, Caroline broke the silence in a low, bitter voice. "You term marriage miserable, I suppose, from what you saw of my father and mother's. If my mother suffered what I did, she must have had a dreadful life."

Mr. Helstone wheeled about, startled. "Your father and mother? Who has been talking to you about them?"

"Nobody," she replied, pitying her mother. "Where is she?"

"I hardly know," he stammered, avoiding the subject. "I was little acquainted with her. I haven't heard from her for years... Where she is, she thinks nothing of you. Come, it is school-time."

Just then, Fanny announced that the churchwardens needed Mr. Helstone. He departed hastily, and Caroline set out for the cottage.

Running down the hill toward Hollow's Mill, refreshed by exercise and the prospect of Robert, she saw Moore at the garden gate. He stood in his belted Holland blouse and light cap. Caroline hid behind a willow, studying him. *He has not his peer,* she thought. *He is handsome, intelligent, with a keen eye.* Yet, when he challenged her view, he acted like a cousin or brother, not a lover. The nameless charm of the previous night vanished. His manner changed; the same heart did not beat in his breast.

Rude disappointment stung her. Nature, however, offers a strange consolation: seal your lips, feign gaiety, and endure the stone instead of the bread. Let the scorpion sting your hand, and in time, you will learn to endure without a sob. Half-bitter strength is a tonic. Caroline resolved to govern herself, to subdue her emotions, and to look steadily at life's severe truths. She quit Robert quietly, without tears, and resumed her studies.

Later, alone in the rectory drawing-room, she faced a difficulty: *How am I to get through this day?* Last night's hope of spending the evening with Robert had faded. She tried to read, but her thoughts wandered; sewing became insufferably tedious; writing French yielded only mistakes. Suddenly, the bell rang. Her heart leaped. She peeked through the door. Fanny admitted a gentleman—a tall man resembling Robert—but the voice was Irish. It was Curate Malone.

Fortune, it seemed, intended to torment Caroline further. As Malone arrived, so did Donne, then Sweeting. The curates, usually boisterous, found themselves interrupted by the sudden arrival of four lady guests: Mrs. Sykes and her three daughters, arriving in a pony-phaeton.
