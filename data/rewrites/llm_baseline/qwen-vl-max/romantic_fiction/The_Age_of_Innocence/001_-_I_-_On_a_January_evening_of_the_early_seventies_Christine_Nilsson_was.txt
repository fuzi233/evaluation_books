I. On a January evening in the early 1870s, Christine Nilsson was singing *Faust* at the Academy of Music in New York. Though whispers had begun about a grand new opera house rising far uptown—beyond the Forties—a place meant to rival the opulence of Europe’s most celebrated halls—the city’s elite still gathered each winter in the modest, gilded red-and-gold boxes of the old Academy. Conservatives admired its intimacy and discomfort, which kept out the “new people” New York both feared and found irresistible; sentimentalists cherished its history; musicians praised its acoustics, a rare gift in spaces built for music. It was Madame Nilsson’s first performance that season, and what the newspapers now called an “exceptionally brilliant audience” had arrived by private broughams, spacious family landaus, or the more practical but equally respectable Brown coupe. To arrive in a Brown coupe was nearly as dignified as arriving in one’s own carriage—and leaving the same way offered the delightful liberty of slipping into the first available vehicle in line, rather than waiting for one’s own coachman, whose nose, reddened by cold and gin, would soon gleam under the Academy’s portico. The livery-stableman had mastered the American mind: people wanted to leave pleasure faster than they wanted to reach it.

When Newland Archer stepped into the club box from the back, the curtain had just risen on the garden scene. There was no reason he couldn’t have come earlier—he’d dined at seven with his mother and sister, lingered over a cigar in the Gothic library, where black-walnut bookcases and finial-topped chairs allowed smoking, the only room in the house where such indulgence was permitted. But New York was a metropolis, and in metropolises, arriving early at the opera was simply not done. What was or wasn’t “the thing” shaped Newland’s world as decisively as ancestral taboos once ruled his forebears. And then there was his personal preference: he liked to savor anticipation. Especially when the pleasure was refined, as most of his were. This night, the moment he awaited was so rare and exquisite that even if he’d timed his arrival perfectly with the prima donna’s stage manager, he could not have entered at a more perfect instant than just as she sang: “He loves me—*he loves me not*—*he loves me!*”—her voice clear as dew, scattering notes like falling daisy petals. Of course, she sang “*M’ama!*” not “he loves me,” since an unshakable rule dictated that German texts in French operas performed by Swedish singers be translated into Italian for the benefit of English-speaking audiences. It seemed as natural to Newland as other customs that governed his life: using two silver-backed brushes with his monogram in blue enamel to part his hair, or always wearing a flower—preferably a gardenia—in his buttonhole.

“*M’ama… non m’ama…*” she sang, and then, with a triumphant flourish, “*M’ama!*” pressing the disheveled daisy to her lips and lifting her eyes to the small, brown-faced Faust-Capoul, who struggled in a tight purple velvet doublet and plumed cap to appear pure and true, though clearly outmatched by his artless victim.

Newland leaned against the wall at the back of the box and turned from the stage to scan the opposite side of the house. Directly across sat the box of old Mrs. Manson Mingott, whose immense size had long made opera attendance impossible—but whose presence was always ensured by younger relatives on fashionable nights. Tonight, her daughter-in-law, Mrs. Lovell Mingott, and her daughter, Mrs. Welland, filled the front seats. Behind them, slightly withdrawn, sat a young girl in white, her eyes fixed in rapture on the lovers onstage. As Madame Nilsson’s “*M’ama!*” rang through the silent hall—every box hushed during the Daisy Song—a warm pink bloomed on the girl’s cheeks, climbed her brow to the roots of her golden braids, and spread gently over the curve of her breast, where it met a modest tulle tucker fastened with a single gardenia. She lowered her gaze to the bouquet of lilies-of-the-valley resting on her lap, and Newland saw her white-gloved fingers brush the blossoms tenderly.

He exhaled softly, pleased with himself, and returned his attention to the stage. No expense had been spared on the set, acknowledged even by those familiar with Paris and Vienna’s opera houses as remarkably beautiful. The foreground was covered in emerald green cloth. In the distance, symmetrical mounds of moss framed shrubs shaped like orange trees, their branches heavy with large pink and red roses. Gigantic pansies—larger than the roses themselves—sprouted beneath the rose trees, resembling the floral pen-wipers crafted by churchgoers for fashionable clergymen. Here and there, a daisy grafted onto a rose branch bloomed with a lushness that hinted at Mr. Luther Burbank’s future wonders.

At the center of this enchanted garden, Madame Nilsson—dressed in white cashmere slashed with pale blue satin, a reticule dangling from a blue sash, her yellow braids neatly arranged—listened demurely to M. Capoul’s ardent advances, feigning ignorance whenever he suggested, by word or glance, the ground-floor window of the neat brick villa jutting from the right wing.

“The darling!” Newland thought, his gaze drifting back to the girl with the lilies-of-the-valley. “She doesn’t even know what it means.” He watched her absorbed face with a quiet sense of possession—pride in his own understanding mingled with reverence for her innocence. “We’ll read *Faust* together… by the Italian lakes…” he imagined, vaguely blending the setting of his imagined honeymoon with the literary masterpieces he intended to introduce to his bride. That very afternoon, May Welland had let him know she “cared”—New York’s sacred phrase of maidenly confession—and already, his imagination leapt ahead of engagement rings, betrothal kisses, and the march from *Lohengrin*, picturing her beside him in some ancient European charm.

He didn’t want his future wife to be naive. He meant her—through his guidance—to grow into a woman of social grace, quick wit, and charm, able to hold her own among the most admired married women of the “younger set,” where it was customary to attract admiration while playfully rejecting it. If he had looked deeper into his vanity—as he sometimes almost did—he might have admitted he wished her as worldly and eager to please as the married woman who had captivated him for two restless years—without, of course, any trace of the flaw that had nearly destroyed her life and disrupted his plans for an entire winter.

How such a paradox—fire and ice—could endure in a harsh world, he never bothered to consider. But he was content to believe in it, knowing it reflected the views of all the impeccably groomed, waistcoat-clad, flower-adorned gentlemen who occupied the club box, exchanged nods with him, and swept their opera glasses over the circle of ladies—products of the same system.

In matters of intellect and art, Newland felt himself superior to these men. He had likely read more, thought more, seen more of the world than any of them. Individually, they showed their limitations. But together, they represented “New York,” and the instinct of masculine solidarity made him accept their moral code without question. To challenge it would be troublesome—and, indeed, poor form.

“Well—upon my soul!” exclaimed Lawrence Lefferts, turning his opera glass sharply away from the stage.

Lefferts was New York’s foremost authority on “form.” He’d spent more time studying its intricacies than anyone else, yet his mastery seemed innate. One needed only to see him—from the slope of his bald forehead and the curl of his fair mustache to his long patent-leather feet—to understand that knowing how to wear fine clothes carelessly, and carry height with effortless grace, was born, not learned. A young admirer once said: “If anyone can tell you when to wear a black tie with evening clothes and when not to, it’s Larry Lefferts.” His judgment on pumps versus patent-leather Oxfords was never questioned.

“My God,” he murmured, handing his glass to old Sillerton Jackson.

Newland followed Lefferts’s gaze and saw, with surprise, that the cause of his exclamation was a new figure entering Mrs. Mingott’s box. A slim young woman, slightly shorter than May Welland, with brown curls framing her temples and held by a narrow band of diamonds. The look—then called “Josephine”—was echoed in her dark blue velvet gown, dramatically gathered beneath her bosom by a wide, old-fashioned clasp. Unaware of the attention she drew, she paused in the center of the box, debating with Mrs. Welland whether to take her seat in the front right corner. Then, with a slight smile, she yielded and settled beside Mrs. Lovell Mingott, who sat in the opposite corner.

Sillerton Jackson returned the glass to Lefferts. The entire club box turned, expectant. Old Mr. Jackson was as much an authority on “family” as Lefferts was on “form.” He knew every branch of New York’s interwoven kinships—how the Mingotts connected to the Dallases via the Thorleys, or how the Philadelphia Thorleys linked to the Albany Chiverses (never confused with the Manson Chiverses of University Place). He could recite the traits of each family: the notorious stinginess of the Long Island Leffertses, the tendency of the Rushworths to make disastrous matches, the madness that struck every second generation of the Albany Chiverses—whom their New York cousins refused to marry, except for poor Medora Manson, whose mother was a Rushworth.

Beyond genealogy, Jackson carried in his mind a record of the scandals and secrets simmering beneath New York society’s calm surface. He was said to be the only man who could reveal who Julius Beaufort truly was—or what became of handsome Bob Spicer, Mrs. Mingott’s father, who vanished mysteriously after his marriage, taking trust funds with him, on the very day a beautiful Spanish dancer left the old Battery Opera House for Cuba.

But these secrets remained locked in his memory—not only because of honor, but because discretion increased his chances of learning more. So the club waited, breathless, as Jackson handed back the opera glass. For a moment, he studied the audience through his filmy blue eyes, veiled by aged lids. Then he gave his mustache a thoughtful twist and said simply:

“I didn’t think the Mingotts would have tried it on.”
