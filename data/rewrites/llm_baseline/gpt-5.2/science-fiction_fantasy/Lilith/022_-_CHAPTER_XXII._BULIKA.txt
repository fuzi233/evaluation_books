I had lost all reckoning of where I was. The dark had no corners, no landmarks—only a thickness to it, like soot stirred up in a closed kitchen. I walked in it the way one paces when a pot refuses to come to the boil: helpless, listening, counting heartbeats, trying to borrow patience from sound.

Then, without warning, I stepped straight into the leopardess’s track.

Something ran about my ankles with the push and hurry of a small brook—warm at first in my mind, because my mind leapt to the most dreadful ingredient it could name. Blood, it whispered: somebody’s life poured out and wasted. I sprang aside as if scalded, yet the very next instant I found myself keeping close to the noise of it, as a cook will follow the hiss of a pan in a darkened room. The stream’s voice would lead me to Bulika.

Only, as I hurried on beside it, reason began to stir—slowly, like embers remembered under ash. No leopardess, no elephant, no ancient brute out of the world’s first bestiary could bleed so much and still walk. To keep such a torrent running, its heart would have to be a pump fed by lakes; its veins would have to drink whole meadows and refill themselves as fast as they emptied. It could not be blood.

I knelt, dipped a finger, and brought it to my tongue before fear could stop me. It was water. Not cold—soft, as if it had been warmed in the sun and then set to singing; clean, with a faint sweetness like crushed green stems. However it had come there, it ran without bed or channel over the grass, a thin shining skin upon the earth, murmuring the way a kettle murmurs when it is content and not yet urgent.

Sweet as its music was, I dared not drink. In that world I had learned to mistrust appetite, to distrust even thirst; some draughts promised comfort and gave fever. But the mere wetting of my feet refreshed me as a basin of cool water refreshes a baker’s wrists after hours at the oven. The sound of it—so unlike the harsh, hot rush of that other stream—was like a remembered lullaby in a language one spoke before learning the harsher dialects of fear. I went on without fatigue, guided by its gentle acoustics, until the darkness began to thin, and I knew the sun was drawing near.

A few minutes more and, against a pale aurora like flour sifted into the air, I made out the wall-towers of a city—old as if time itself had been kneaded into its stones and baked hard.

I looked down, expecting to see the brook. It was gone.

I had noticed its song growing fainter for a long while, as a cook notices a simmer falling silent when the fire dies; but at last, in my thoughts, I had ceased to attend. Now there was only grass, bent and laid over where the water had gone, and here and there a small pool glimmering like spilled broth. Toward the city there was no trace at all. Near where I stood, its fountain—if fountain there had been—must at least have paused, as if some unseen hand had clapped a lid over it.

Around the city, gardens lay close to the walls: long beds of vegetables, many of them so strange that I could not name them, though they had the look of things meant for a pot and a knife. Some had leaves like thick tongues; some thrust pale knobs above the soil; some stood in stiff ranks with seed-heads like little maces. Yet there were no flowers, no visible watercourses, no animals moving among the beds. The earth looked worked, but the work had no tenderness in it—no mulched paths, no border herbs, no bees. Between the gardens and the walls were huge heaps of gravel and refuse flung down from the battlements: broken stone, ash, and the grey leavings of a place that consumed and did not compost.

I went to the nearest gate. It stood half-closed, not barred, not watched. Its hinges looked as if they had forgotten the very idea of change—so worn, so settled in their position that it seemed the gate could neither open wider nor shut tighter.

I passed through.

A long ancient street stretched ahead, and it was silent—not the good silence of dawn over fields, where birds hold their breath before the first song, but the dead silence of a kitchen after a feast when the fire is out and the plates are licked clean. There was scarcely an indication of life. The stones underfoot were flat and cold; my own footsteps sounded too loud, as if I were making noise in a place where noise was forbidden.

Had I come upon a dead city?

I turned and went out again. I toiled a long way over the dust-heaps, crossed several roads that each led up to a gate, and lingered outside, refusing to enter until the inhabitants should begin to stir. I tried to ask myself, as one asks oneself before returning to a hostile table: What am I here for? What do I expect? What do I mean to do?

One answer kept rising, bitter and unavoidable: I must see—if only once more—the woman I had brought to life.

I did not desire her company. She had waked in me suspicions like the smell of meat kept too long in heat: not a certainty, but enough to turn the stomach. Friendship—love—between us was wildly impossible. And yet her presence had had a strange influence upon me, a seasoning in the air that changed the taste of my own thoughts. In her presence I must resist and at the same time try to understand what was being done to me. The inscrutable in her drew me like a dish whose aroma promises one thing and delivers another. If I could learn her mode of being, I might look into marvels no imagination would have dared cook up.

In that I was too bold. A man must not, for the sake of knowledge, walk of his own will into temptation.

And yet there was guilt in my caution. I had reinstated an evil force that was about to perish; to the extent of my opposing will, I was answerable for whatever mischief might follow. I had learned she was the enemy of children. The Little Ones might be in her danger. I had left them to seek some understanding of their history, and I had found a little light—but not enough. I must have more. I must learn how to protect them.

At last I heard a faint stir within the walls: not birdsong, not market-call, but the scraping and shuffling of waking bodies. I chose another gate and went in, along a narrow street between tall houses. The air had a stale, shut-up smell, as of rooms that never let their smoke out. No friendly domestic sounds came—no clink of crockery, no bubbling, no laughter, no chopping on a board. Sound itself seemed policed, as if life had been trained to move without making music.

The narrow street led to a small square where a pillar stood, and atop it a hideous creature like a bat in stone, wings folded as if it had never known flight but only the idea of menace. I sat down on the base of the pillar and waited.

Ere long, several inhabitants sauntered past—men with the vacant gait of those who are neither hurrying to work nor lingering with purpose. I spoke to one. He gave me a rude stare and a ruder word, and went on as though my voice were merely an unpleasant noise to step around.

I rose and went through one narrow street after another. The city gradually filled with idlers, but still the soundscape remained thin and sour: no children’s cries, no games, no bright shrieks that stitch the hours together. I was not surprised to see no children.

By and by, near one of the gates, I encountered a group of young men who reminded me of the bad giants—something in the set of their shoulders and the way their attention fixed on weakness as a dog fixes on a dropped scrap. They came about me, staring, then began to push and hustle me, then to throw things. Pebbles first, then whatever their hands found, not from hunger, not from sport, but from a practiced contempt that had the rhythm of a city’s custom.

I bore it as well as I could, unwilling to provoke enmity where I meant to remain a while. More than once I appealed to passers-by whom I fancied more benevolent-looking. None halted even a moment to listen. I looked poor, and that was enough. To the citizens of Bulika—as to house-dogs—poverty was an offence.

I learned in those few moments something of their law without hearing it proclaimed: deformity and sickness were taxed; and no legislation of their princess was more heartily approved than what made poverty serve wealth. It was a city where the dominant tongue of power named need a crime, and the name itself did the work of a lash. The words they flung were not merely insults; they were labels meant to strip a man of any claim to a hearing. I understood the sound of them—hard consonants, clipped endings—but their meaning slid in a way my own speech could not quite catch, as if their dialect had evolved to make cruelty efficient.

At last I took to my heels, and no one followed me beyond the gate. Outside the walls their courage collapsed like over-whipped cream—showy inside, air outside. One lumbering fellow sat by the gate eating a hunch of bread. He picked up a stone to throw after me, and in his stupid eagerness threw, not the stone but the bread.

It struck the ground and rolled. I snatched it up. He did not dare follow to reclaim it.

A few hundred yards away I threw myself down in the grass and ate it. It was coarse, heavy, a bread made without care: too much bran left sharp, the crumb tight, the crust pale as if it had been baked in a reluctant oven. Yet it filled my mouth with warmth and reminded my body that it still belonged to earth and grain and the old honest chemistry of hunger. I fell asleep as one falls asleep after eating—sudden, unresisting—and slept soundly. The hot sunlight later renewed my strength the way a hearthstone holds and gives back heat long after the flames are gone.

It was night when I woke.

The moon looked down in friendly fashion, seeming to claim with me old acquaintance. She was very bright—the same moon, I thought, that had watched me through the terrors of my first night in that strange world, as if my life here moved in circles and she marked the turning like a patient timekeeper. A cold wind blew from the gate, bringing with it an evil odour: not merely the smell of refuse, but a taint like rancid fat—sweet at first sniff, then sickening, clinging in the nose and on the back of the tongue. It did not chill me; the sun had plenished me with warmth.

I crept again into the city.

The few still in the open air were crouched in corners to escape the shivering blast. Their huddling made a low, uneasy rustle, the way dry leaves scrape when something passes that they do not trust. The wind worried the streets, seeking gaps between stones, making thin whistles that suggested the place itself was always listening for weakness.

I walked slowly through a long narrow street when, just before me, a huge white thing bounded across it—one single flash in the moonlight—and disappeared. The brightness was so sudden it felt like the flare of a knife catching light.

I turned down the next opening, eager to get sight of it again.

The lane was almost too narrow to pass through, walls crowding like close cupboards, and it led me into a wider street. The moment I entered, I saw on the opposite side, in the shadow, the creature I had followed. It was following, like a dog, what I took at first for a man.

Over his shoulder, every other moment, he glanced at the animal behind him; yet he neither spoke to it nor attempted to drive it away, as if the creature were not a pursuer but a necessary accompaniment—like the smell of smoke on a man who lives by a fire he did not choose.

At a place where he had to cross a patch of moonlight, I saw he cast no shadow.

More dreadful still: he himself was but a flat superficial shadow—two dimensions walking upright. And yet he was no mere absence of light, for he was opaque: he not only darkened what lay behind him, he made it invisible, as if he carried with him a strip of night that ate whatever it touched. In the deeper shadow he was blacker than the shadow; in the moonlight he looked like one who had drawn his own shadow up about him and wrapped it close, for not a suspicion of it moved beside or under him.

The leopardess followed so close at his heels that she seemed the white shadow of his blackness. She drew her own gliding shadow over the stones, black and natural, while he had none at all, as if the world refused to admit him as a body. When they passed together from shadow into moonlight, the Shadow deepened in blackness, and the animal flashed into radiance, her coat catching the light like poured milk turning suddenly to steel.

At that moment I was walking abreast of them on the opposite side. My bare feet sounded on the flat stones—small, human sounds, too honest for that street. The leopardess never turned her head or twitched an ear. The shadow seemed once to look at me, for I lost his profile and saw for a second only a sharp upright line.

That instant the wind found me and blew through me.

I shuddered from head to foot, and my heart went from wall to wall of my bosom like a pebble in a child’s rattle.
