If one were to categorize the various forms of self-assurance and unshakeable conceit that populate Cloisterham, there exists a specific breed, perhaps more conventional than equitable, which we shall term "the Jackass." Within this taxonomy, the purest specimen, the most unadulterated embodiment of this spirit, is undoubtedly Mr. Thomas Sapsea, the Auctioneer.

Mr. Sapsea lives a life performed entirely in the grandest register. He "dresses at" the Dean, a title he wears with the confidence of a king, despite the frequent, humorous errors of the populace who mistake him for the prelate himself. He has been bowed to as "My Lord" in the streets, an honor he accepts with a stiff nod, convinced that the confusion proves his inherent nobility. He prides himself not merely on his status, but on the timbre of his voice and the cadence of his style. In the art of selling landed estates, he has even attempted to mimic the solemn intonation of a true ecclesiastical figure, believing that the pulpit is merely a variant of the auction block. Thus, when concluding a sale by public auction, Mr. Sapsea invariably finishes with a flourish that resembles a benediction bestowed upon the gathered brokers, leaving the actual Dean—a modest, competent gentleman—to fade into obscurity by comparison.

Mr. Sapsea commands a vast following. Indeed, the proposition, supported by a significant local majority that includes even those who privately doubt his wisdom, is that he is a credit to Cloisterham. He possesses the dual virtues of being portentous and dull, moving with a rolling gait and speaking with a rolling tongue. His hands move with a grave, flowing grace, constantly gesturing as if confirming the souls of his interlocutors. Though nearly sixty, he maintains the illusion of youth through a bloated outline of stomach and the horizontal creases of his waistcoat, which he believes signify prosperity. Reputed wealthy, he votes strictly in the interests of respectability and is morally certain that nothing in the world has grown except himself. How, then, could a man of such monumental ego fail to be considered a pillar of society?

His premises, situated on High Street directly across from the Nuns' House, date from roughly the same era as the convent itself. Irregularly modernized by successive generations who, weary of fever and plague, increasingly prioritized air and light, the building stands as a testament to gradual deterioration. Above the doorway hangs a wooden effigy, roughly half-life-size, depicting Mr. Sapsea's father in a curly wig and toga, captured mid-sale. The concept is so pure, and the execution of the little finger, hammer, and pulpit so natural, that it remains a subject of local admiration.

Inside, on the dull ground floor, Mr. Sapsea presides over a room that faces first upon his paved backyard and then upon his railed garden. Before the fire—an early luxury, yet comforting on this chilly autumn evening—rests a bottle of port wine. The scene is characteristically adorned by his portrait, an eight-day clock, and a weather-glass. These objects serve a symbolic function: the portrait defends him against mankind, the clock against time, and the weather-glass against the elements themselves.

Beside Mr. Sapsea on the table lies a writing desk. Glancing at a scrap of manuscript, he reads it aloud with a lofty air, then paces the room, thumbs hooked into the armholes of his waistcoat, reciting it from memory with internal conviction and external dignity. Only the name "Ethelinda" breaks the silence of his rehearsed prose.

Three clean wineglasses sit in a silver tray. As his serving-maid enters to announce, "Mr. Jasper is come, sir," Mr. Sapsea waves her forward, drawing two glasses from the ranks as if they were his by right.

"Glad to see you, sir," Mr. Sapsea declares, performing the honors of his house with theatrical flair. "I congratulate myself on having the honour of receiving you here for the first time."

"You are very good," replies Mr. Jasper, smiling. "The honour is mine, and the self-congratulation is yours."

"You are pleased to say so, sir," Mr. Sapsea insists, his voice rising in ineffable loftiness. "But I do assure you, it is a satisfaction to me to receive you in my humble home. And that is what I would not say to everybody." The subtext hangs heavy in the air: *Your presence is a rare delight for a man of my stature.*

"I have, for some time, desired to know you, Mr. Sapsea," Jasper observes.

"And I, sir, have long known you by reputation as a man of taste," Sapsea counters, refilling his own glass. "Let me fill yours. 'When the French come over, May we meet them at Dover!' " he shouts, a patriotic toast from his infancy which he believes applies to any era.

"You can scarcely be ignorant, Mr. Sapsea," Jasper notes, watching the auctioneer stretch his legs before the fire, "that you know the world."

"Well, sir," chuckles Sapsea, "I think I know something of it; something of it."

"Your reputation for that knowledge has always interested me," Jasper admits. "For Cloisterham is a little place. Cooped up in it, I know nothing beyond it, and feel it to be a very small world."

"If I have not gone to foreign countries, young man," Sapsea begins, pausing to correct himself, "you will excuse me calling you young man, Mr. Jasper? You are much my junior."

"By all means."

"If I have not traveled abroad, young man, foreign countries have come to me!" Sapsea continues, his voice swelling. "They have arrived via business, and I have improved upon every opportunity. Consider it an inventory, or a catalogue. I spot a French clock. Never having seen one before in my life, I instantly lay my finger upon it and declare, 'Paris!' I encounter cups and saucers of Chinese manufacture, equally strangers to me personally; I point and say, 'Pekin, Nankin, and Canton.' It is the same with Japan, Egypt, bamboo, and sandalwood from the East Indies; I put my finger on them all. I have even placed my finger on the North Pole and declared, 'Spear of Esquimaux make, for half a pint of pale sherry!' "

"Really?" Jasper asks, feigning surprise. "A very remarkable method, Mr. Sapsea, of acquiring knowledge of men and things."

"I mention it, sir," Sapsea rejoins, "because it don't do to boast of what you are; one must show how one came to be what one is, thereby proving it."

"Most interesting. We were to speak of the late Mrs. Sapsea."

"We were, sir." Sapsea fills both glasses, securing the decanter safely. "Before I solicit your opinion as a man of taste on this little trifle"—he holds up a sheet of paper—"which is, I assure you, but a trifle, yet required some fever of the brow to compose, I ought perhaps to describe the character of the late Mrs. Sapsea, now deceased three-quarters of a year."

Mr. Jasper yawns behind his glass, lowering the screen of his fatigue to reveal a look of forced interest, marred by a lingering, water-eyed gape.

"Half a dozen years ago," Sapsea recounts, "when I had enlarged my mind to a pitch where I felt a need to absorb another mind within it—not wishing to claim too much, but certainly needing a partner, as it is not good for man to be alone—I cast my eye about for a nuptial partner."

Mr. Jasper commits this sentiment to memory.

"At that time," Sapsea continues, "Miss Brobity maintained an establishment, I will not call it the rival to the Nuns', but rather the parallel establishment down town. The world whispered that she showed a passion for attending my sales during half-holidays and vacations. The world noted that she admired my style. Indeed, as time flowed, traces of my style became evident in the dictation exercises of Miss Brobity's pupils. A whisper, born of obscure malignity, suggested that some ignorant parent had objected to this influence by name. But I do not believe this. For is it likely that any sane creature would so expose themselves to the finger of scorn?"

Mr. Jasper shakes his head. "Not in the least likely."

Sapsea, lost in a grandiloquent trance, refills Jasper's glass, which is already full, and then genuinely refills his own, which is empty.

"Miss Brobity's Being," Sapsea intones, "was deeply imbued with homage to Mind. She revered Mind, whether launched or precipitated into an extensive knowledge of the world. When I made my proposal, she was so overwhelmed by a species of Awe that she could articulate only two words: 'O Thou!' referring to myself. Her limpid blue eyes were fixed upon me, her semi-transparent hands clasped, pallor overspread her aquiline features. Encouraged to proceed, she never uttered another word. I disposed of her establishment by private contract, and we became as nearly one as circumstances permitted. Yet, she never could, nor did she ever, find a phrase satisfactory to her perhaps-too-favourable estimate of my intellect.
