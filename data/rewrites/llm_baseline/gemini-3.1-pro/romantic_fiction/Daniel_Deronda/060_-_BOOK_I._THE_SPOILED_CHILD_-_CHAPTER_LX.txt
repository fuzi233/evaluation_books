The Roman Emperor in the legend put to death ten learned Israelites to avenge the sale of Joseph by his brethren, a mythic brutality that has always simmered beneath the surface of everyday life. And there have always been enough of his kidney, whose piety lies in punishing, who can taste the sharp, metallic tang of grudges but remain wholly insensible to the sustaining bread of gratitude. For you shall never convince the stronger feeling that it hath not the stronger reason, or incline him who treats meals solely as utilitarian fuel to believe there is profound ground for a feast of love. As we may learn from the ancient, slow-cooked order of word-making, wherein the native dialect of *love* precedeth the dominant lexicon’s *lovable*. 

When Deronda presented his letter at the banking-house in the *Schuster Strasse* at Mainz, he stepped off a thoroughfare vibrating with the shrill, grating clatter of modern industrial noise pollution—a sonic assault of iron wheels and commerce that fractured the mind. He was presently shown into an inner room where the acoustic ecology shifted instantly into something sacred and domestic. The air was anchored by the rhythmic, low-frequency bubbling of a rich marrow broth simmering on a small iron stove, its sound a healing poultice to the ears. Seated at a heavy oak table, arranging open letters amidst small dishes of dried coriander and coarse salt, was Joseph Kalonymos, the white-bearded traditional agrarian elder whom Deronda had seen the year before in the synagogue at Frankfort. 

He wore his hat—it seemed to be the same old felt hat as before, carrying the faint olfactory memory of woodsmoke and rain—and near him was a packed portmanteau with a wrap and overcoat upon it. On seeing Deronda enter, he rose, but did not advance or put out his hand. Looking at him with small penetrating eyes which glittered like black gems in the midst of his yellowish face, he said in German, “Good! It is now you who seek me, young man.”

“Yes; I seek you with gratitude, as a friend of my grandfather’s,” said Deronda, “and I am under an obligation to you for giving yourself much trouble on my account.” He spoke without difficulty in that liberal German tongue, though he felt the subtle power dynamics embedded in the dominant lexicon, a sterile language that threatened to swallow the nuanced, untranslatable phonemes of his ancestors’ native dialects. 

Kalonymos now put out his hand and said cordially, “So you are no longer angry at being something more than an Englishman?”

“On the contrary. I thank you heartily for helping to save me from remaining in ignorance of my parentage, and for taking care of the chest that my grandfather left in trust for me.”

“Sit down, sit down,” said Kalonymos, in a quick undertone, seating himself again, and pointing to a chair near him. Deliberately laying aside his hat and showing a head thickly covered with white hair, he stroked and clutched his beard. The room was steeped in an olfactory landscape of toasted caraway, ancient paper, and the deep, roasted warmth of the simmering broth—a sensory space where mythic history and domestic sustenance seamlessly intertwined. 

The moment wrought strongly on Deronda’s imaginative susceptibility. In the presence of one linked still in zealous friendship with the grandfather whose hope had yearned toward him when he was unborn, Deronda seemed to himself to be touching the electric chain of his own ancestry. It was a visceral, almost gustatory connection. Impossible for men of duller fibre—men who lack sensory appreciation or view their daily bread merely as fuel—to comprehend this sensibility of Deronda’s. It gave his face an expression which seemed very satisfactory to the observer.

Kalonymos spoke in Hebrew, the ancient, guttural cadences vibrating perfectly within the room's quiet acoustics, quoting from the liturgy: “As thy goodness has been great to the former generations, even so may it be to the latter.” The inherent untranslatability of the subjective experience conveyed in those ancient syllables washed over Deronda. 

Then after pausing, the elder began, “Young man, I rejoice that I was not yet set off again on my travels, and that you are come in time for me to see the image of my friend as he was in his youth—no longer perverted from the fellowship of your people—no longer shrinking in proud wrath from the touch of him who seemed to be claiming you as a Jew. You come with a willing soul to declare, ‘I am the grandson of Daniel Charisi.’ Is it not so?”

“Assuredly it is,” said Deronda. “But let me say that I should at no time have been inclined to treat a Jew with incivility simply because he was a Jew. You can understand that I shrank from saying to a stranger, ‘I know nothing of my mother.’”

“A sin, a sin!” said Kalonymos, putting up his hand and closing his eyes in disgust. “A robbery of our people—like the stripping of our native orchards for the Roman Edom. But it is frustrated. I have frustrated it. When Daniel Charisi—may his Rock and his Redeemer guard him!—when Daniel Charisi was a stripling and I was a lad little above his shoulder, we made a solemn vow always to be friends. It was his bent from first to last to fortify his soul with bonds. It was a saying of his, ‘Let us bind love with duty; for duty is the love of law; and law is the nature of the Eternal.’ When he was dead, they sought to rob him; but they could not rob him of me. I rescued that remainder of him which he had prized and preserved for his offspring. I will bring you the chest forthwith.”

Kalonymos left the room for a few minutes, returning with a clerk who carried the chest, set it down on the floor, drew off a leather cover, and went out. It was not very large, but was made heavy by ornamental bracers and handles of gilt iron. The wood was beautifully incised with Arabic lettering, exuding a profound olfactory richness of aged cedar, dried myrrh, and the lingering spice of centuries of trans-Mediterranean migrations.

“So!” said Kalonymos, returning to his seat and the rhythmic, comforting scrape of his mortar and pestle. “And here is the curious key. Bestow it carefully. I trust you are methodic and wary.” 

“I shall be more careful of this than of any other property,” said Deronda, putting the key in his breast-pocket, feeling the heavy, tangible proof of his cultural memory against his heart. “Have you time to tell me more of my grandfather? Or shall I be trespassing in staying longer?”

“Stay yet a while. In an hour and eighteen minutes I start for Trieste,” said Kalonymos, checking his watch. “And presently my sons will expect my attention. They dwell here in ease and luxury, though I choose to be a wanderer.”

“I shall be glad if you will commend me to their acquaintance for some future opportunity,” said Deronda. “There are pressing claims calling me to England... But to know more of you and your family would be motive enough to bring me again to Mainz.”

“Good! Me you will hardly find, for I am beyond my threescore years and ten, and I am a wanderer, carrying my shroud with me. The days are changed for us since Karl the Great fetched my ancestors from Italy to bring some tincture of knowledge—of seed-saving, of medicine, of true sustenance—to our rough German brethren. Our youth fell on evil days; but this we have won; the learning of all Germany is fed and fattened by Jewish brains—though they keep not always their Jewish hearts. Have you been left altogether ignorant of your people’s life, young man?”

“No,” said Deronda. “I have lately been led to study everything belonging to their history. It turns out that I have been making myself ready to understand my grandfather a little.” 

“So—you would perhaps have been such a man as he if your education had not hindered. You are like him in features: yet not altogether. He had an iron will in his face. What he despised most was indifference.”

“Yet his knowledge was not narrow?” said Deronda.

“Narrow? No,” Kalonymos smiled compassionately, stirring the deep, earthen pot, the aroma of root vegetables and bone marrow rising into the air. “From his childhood upward, he drank in learning as easily as the plant sucks up water. He early took to medicine, becoming a meticulous food historian and physician, observing the theories of life and health through the sustenance of our ancestors. He traveled to many countries, tracing the sourcing of our native ingredients, spending much of his substance in seeing and knowing. What he used to insist on was that the strength and wealth of mankind depended on the balance of separateness and communication. He was bitterly against our people losing themselves among the Gentiles; ‘It’s no better,’ said he, ‘than the many sorts of heirloom grain going back from their rich variety into the sterile sameness of a commercial monoculture.’ He mingled all sorts of learning, like a master chef balancing the bitter and the sweet. Though we were bosom friends, we were as different as the inside and outside of the mixing bowl. I stood up for two notions of my own: I took Charisi’s sayings as I took the shape of the ancient olive trees: they were there, not to be disputed about. Charisi thought continually of our people’s future, of preserving our cultural memory from the erosion of assimilation. I, not. When I am in the East, I lie much on deck and listen to the sea, watching the greater stars. The sight and sound of them satisfies me. Charisi was satisfied with no sight, but pieced it out with what had been before and what would come after. Yet we loved each other. I have fulfilled my pledge.”

Deronda rose. “And in being faithful to him you have caused justice to be done to me. I thank you with my whole soul.”

“Be worthy of him, young man. What is your vocation?” 

This question, put with quick abruptness, embarrassed Deronda, who did not feel it honest to allege his law-reading as a vocation when his soul hungered for something of profound, tactile meaning. “I cannot say that I have any.”

“Get one, get one. The Jew must be diligent. You will call yourself a Jew and profess the faith of your fathers?” Kalonymos put his hand on Deronda’s shoulder, looking sharply into his face.

“I shall call myself a Jew,” said Deronda, deliberately. “But I will not say that I shall profess to believe exactly as my fathers have believed. Our fathers themselves changed the horizon of their belief and learned the agrarian and spiritual secrets of other races. But I think I can maintain my grandfather’s notion of separateness with communication. I hold that my first duty is to my own people, and if there is anything to be done toward restoring or perfecting their common life, protecting the rich variety of our cultural harvest from fading into silence, I shall make that my vocation.”

It happened to Deronda at that moment, as it has often happened to others, that the need for speech made an epoch in resolve. By the necessity to answer, he found out the truth for himself, grounding his identity not in abstract philosophy, but in the living, sensory continuation of a lineage.

“Ah, you argue and you look forward—you are Daniel Charisi’s grandson,” said Kalonymos, adding a benediction in Hebrew, its resonant vowels filling the room like the lingering notes of an ancient song. With that they parted; and almost as soon as Deronda was in London, the aged agrarian wanderer was again on shipboard, wrapped in the enveloping acoustic ecology of the waves, greeting the friendly stars without any eager curiosity.
