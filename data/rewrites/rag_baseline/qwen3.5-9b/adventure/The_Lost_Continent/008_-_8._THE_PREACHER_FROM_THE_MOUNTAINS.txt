The silence of the temple was a heavy, wet thing, broken only by the rhythm of my own breathing and the soft, rhythmic presence of Nais beside me. Sleep had claimed me fully, a necessary anchor in the drifting chaos of the day. As night descended, bringing with it a persistent, cold drizzle, we remained within the sanctuary's shadow. Our strategy was simple: endure the vigil until the camp's collective consciousness surrendered to exhaustion, creating a window for my escape to the sea.

Outside, the world was a tableau of misery. Through the narrow slits in the stone walls, the campfires glowed like scattered embers in a dark ocean. Men and women, draped in skins and rags, huddled around the flames, their breath visible as steam fighting a losing battle against the damp chill. There is a specific cruelty in suffering; folk seated in such discomfort are proverbially sharp-edged and unforgiving. Nais watched them, her brow furrowed in a mixture of pity and dread.

"A fine night," she murmured, her voice barely disturbing the stillness. "I would have sent my lord back to the city without a soul here being the wiser. But in this chill, people sleep sourly. We must wait for the hour that drugs them deeper."

So we waited. We sat on a pavement untouched by worship for generations, speaking little, needing little. Companionship does not require the architecture of language; sometimes, shared silence is a stronger bond than conversation.

But the night refused to remain quiet. From the distance, a horn or a shell began to blow—a harsh, raucous note that tore through the damp air. The sound grew louder, more insistent, until the voices of the camp erupted in protest, railing against the disturber. Soon, the noise coalesced into a single point. Standing up, we peered through the wall-slits and saw the camp waking from uneasy slumber, clustering around a figure standing atop the pedestal of a dismantled war engine.

We could not hear his words, and frankly, our curiosity was not piqued. If everyone awake was wearying themselves listening to this fellow, as Nais whispered, then my path to the opposite direction should be clear.

We were reckoning without the inevitable busybody. A dozen pairs of feet, splashing through the mud, approached the temple. They demanded Nais join the audience. She possessed a certain eloquence, they implied, and feared that this speaker, perched on the war engine, would sow schism among their ranks unless a skilled refuter stood to counter his arguments.

Here, I expected to be elbowed into a skirmish by bad luck. Instead, Nais was firm. "Come out with me," she whispered urgently. "Keep distant from the light of the fires."

"But how explain my being here?"

"There is no reason to explain anything," she replied, her tone bitter. "They will assume you are my lover. There is nothing remarkable in that; it is the mode here. But oh, why did not the Gods make you wear a beard, and curl it, even as other men? Then you could have vanished safely two hours ago."

"A smooth chin pleases me better."

"So it does me," she murmured, leaning her weight against the stone lintel and pushing it ajar. "Your chin."

The ragged figures outside—men and women alike—did not pause to scrutinize us. Coarse jokes flew through the darkness, insults I could have met with a sword-thrust, but they simply stepped past into the night, glancing back only to ensure we followed. Everywhere else, black shadows pressed inward. The rain spat violently onto the campfires as we passed. No one slept anymore; all were converging on the preacher atop the war engine. To swerve from this current would have been impossible; we were swept along by the tide of the mob.

We moved through the darkness, drawn inexorably closer.

At first, the preacher's words were indistinct. Then, a question cut through the fervor: "Who are your chiefs?" Immediately, rational discourse drowned in uproar. "We have no chiefs!" the crowd roared. "We are done with chiefs! We are all equal here. Take away your silly magic. You may kill us with magic if you choose, but rule us you shall not. Nor shall the other priests rule. Nor Phorenice. Nor anybody. We are done with rulers."

The pressure of bodies drove us nearer to the man on the engine. He was old, his white hair tumbling over shoulders, a long white beard untrimmed and wild. Bare save for a wisp of rag around his loins, his body glistened in the wet. In his hand, however, he held the marker of his caste. He used it to punctuate his sentences, swirling it to bathe his naked form in a halo of light. It was a wand, its tip burning with an unconsuming fire, glowing and twinkling like a star fallen from the high heavens. It was the Symbol of our Lord the Sun, a credential no one could forge, one on which no civilized man would cast a doubt.

Indeed, the ragged, frantic crew did not question his status for a single moment. They acknowledged him as a member of the Clan of Priests, the lineage that had ruled the land since time immemorial. Even in their loudest clamors, they admitted his power. "You may kill us with your magic, if you choose," they screamed. Yet, stubbornly, they refused to return to their old allegiance. "We have suffered too many things these later years," they cried. "We are done with rulers now, forever."

For myself, I viewed the old man with a different emotion. This was Zaemon, father to Nais. Zaemon who had seen me yesterday seated on the divan at Phorenice's elbow, and who today could denounce me as Deucalion if he chose. These rebels had expended a navy trying to kill me four days prior. If they knew of my proximity, even with Nais as my advocate, her cold reasoning would have little chance of an audience now. The High Gods who hold the tether of our lives hide their secrets well, but I did not think it impious to suspect that mine was very close to snapping.

The beautiful woman saw this too. She twisted her fingers into mine, pressing them as a farewell. I pressed hers back, regretting that I would not see her again. Still, I could not suppress a grim, gloating thought: the fine mound of dead I would build before these ragged, unskilled rebels pulled me down. It was inevitable. Of all the emotions that ferment in the human heart, the joy of strife is the keenest. Only an old fighter, facing what must be his final battle, knows how deeply this lust is embroidered into the foundations of his being.

For the moment, Zaemon did not see me, lost in his outcry. Free to listen, I determined the effect of his burning words on the hearers.

His theme was familiar. He spoke of how, since the beginning of history, the Gods had set apart one Clan to rule and serve as Priests. Until the coming of Phorenice, they had fulfilled their duties with exactitude and justice. They had fought invaders, waged war against beasts, and studied earth-movements to foresee earthquakes and eruptions, spreading warnings that saved lives. They were no self-seekers; their aim was always to further the interest of Atlantis, honoring the kingdom favored by the High Gods. Under the Priestly Clan, Atlantis had reached the pinnacle of human prosperity.

"But," cried the old man, waving the Symbol until his wet body gleamed in the halo of light, "the people grew fat and careless with their easy life. They began to believe their good fortune was earned by their own puny brains and thaws, a gift not from the Gods above. Presently, the cult of these Gods became neglected, their temples barren of gifts and worshippers. Followed a punishment. The Gods, in their inscrutable way, decreed that a wife of one of the Priests—a governor of no inconsiderable province—should find a woman child by the wayside and adopt it. That child the Gods, in their infinite wisdom, fashioned into a scourge for Atlantis. You who have felt the weight of Phorenice's hand know with what completeness the High Gods can fashion their instruments."

"Yet, even as they set up, so can they throw down," he continued. "Those who shall debase Phorenice are even now appointed. The old rule is to be re-established, but not until you who have sinned are sufficiently chastened to cry to it for relief." He waved the glowing Symbol before him. "See," he cried in his high, quavering voice. "You know the unspeakable Power of which that is the sign, and for which I am the mouthpiece. It is for you to decide now. Are the Gods to throw down this woman who has scorned Them and so cruelly trodden on you? Or are you to be still further purged of your pride before you are ripe for deliverance?"

The old priest broke off, his ragged white beard sinking onto his chest. Promptly, a young man, skin-clad and armed, shoved through the press of listeners and leaped onto the platform beside him. "Hear me, brethren!" he bellowed in a strong young voice. "We are done with tyrants. Death may come, and we have all shown how little we fear it.
