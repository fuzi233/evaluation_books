Miss Keeldar and her uncle had never found the same tune, though they lived beneath one roof and ate from the same table. His temper was quick, his will absolute; he liked the world as it stood—rank in its place, money in its strong-box, women dutiful and compliant. Shirley’s spirit, fed on freedom like a bright fire on dry wood, would not be banked at another’s bidding. He called her romantic as if it were a fault; she thought him worldly as if it were a want.

He had not come down into Yorkshire for idle visiting. He had come with a purpose sharp as the carving-knife laid beside a roast: to portion out his niece’s fate, to settle her, to hand her over to what he named a “proper” husband, and then—his conscience satisfied—wash his hands of her future.

The mischief was that, from Shirley’s cradle onward, she and Mr. Sympson had quarrelled over the very taste of certain words. “Suitable,” to him, meant money, lineage, unencumbered estate—meat weighed and paid for, set down on the platter with a flourish. “Suitable,” to Shirley, meant something rarer and more perilous: esteem, admiration, the inward consent that makes a vow more than a sound. It was the old dispute, brought at last to the great trial.

Mr. Wynne proposed, in due form, for his son—Samuel Fawthrop Wynne.

“Decidedly suitable! most proper!” pronounced Mr. Sympson, as if he were signing a bargain for a field. “A fine unencumbered estate, real substance, good connections. It must be done!”

He sent for her to the oak parlour and shut the door upon them, as if to keep out all airy notions. The room smelt of old timber and extinguished fires; the great table, dark and polished, looked like something made to bear weight and endure. He delivered the offer, gave his opinion, and demanded her consent.

She withheld it.

“No; I shall not marry Samuel Fawthrop Wynne.”

“I ask why. I must have a reason. In all respects he is more than worthy of you.”

Shirley stood on the hearth; behind her, the white marble slab and cornice threw back her pallor, so that her face looked carved for a moment—still, proud, and cold. Only her eyes were alive, large and bright with a flash that would not soften.

“And I ask in what sense that young man is worthy of me?”

“He has twice your money, twice your common sense, equal connections, equal respectability.”

“Had he my money counted fivescore times I would take no vow to love him.”

“Please to state your objections.”

“He has run a course of despicable, commonplace profligacy. Accept that as the first reason why I spurn him.”

“Miss Keeldar, you shock me!”

“That conduct alone sinks him in a gulf of immeasurable inferiority. His intellect reaches no standard I can esteem: there is a second stumbling-block. His views are narrow, his feelings are blunt, his tastes are coarse, his manners vulgar.”

“The man is a respectable, wealthy man! To refuse him is presumption on your part.”

“I refuse point-blank! Cease to annoy me with the subject; I forbid it!”

“Is it your intention ever to marry; or do you prefer celibacy?”

“I deny your right to claim an answer to that question.”

“May I ask if you expect some man of title—some peer of the realm—to demand your hand?”

“I doubt if the peer breathes on whom I would confer it.”

“Were there insanity in the family, I should believe you mad. Your eccentricity and conceit touch the verge of frenzy.”

“Perhaps, ere I have finished, you will see me over-leap it.”

“I anticipate no less. Frantic and impracticable girl! Take warning! I dare you to sully our name by a mésalliance!”

“Our name! Am I called Sympson?”

“God be thanked that you are not! But be on your guard; I will not be trifled with!”

“What, in the name of common law and common sense, would you or could you do if my pleasure led me to a choice you disapproved?”

“Take care! take care!” He warned her with a voice and hand that trembled together.

“Why? What shadow of power have you over me? Why should I fear you?”

“Take care, madam!”

“Scrupulous care I will take, Mr. Sympson. Before I marry I am resolved to esteem—to admire—to love.”

“Preposterous stuff! indecorous, unwomanly!”

“To love with my whole heart. I know I speak in an unknown tongue; but I feel indifferent whether I am comprehended or not.”

“And if this love of yours should fall on a beggar?”

“On a beggar it will never fall. Mendicancy is not estimable.”

“On a low clerk, a play-actor, a play-writer, or—or—”

“Take courage, Mr. Sympson! Or what?”

“Any literary scrub, or shabby, whining artist.”

“For the scrubby, shabby, whining I have no taste; for literature and the arts I have. And there I wonder how your Fawthrop Wynne would suit me. He cannot write a note without orthographical errors; he reads only a sporting paper; he was the booby of Stilbro’ grammar school!”

“Unladylike language! Great God! to what will she come?” He lifted hands and eyes, as if appealing to the oak beams.

“Never to the altar of Hymen with Sam Wynne.”

“To what will she come? Why are not the laws more stringent, that I might compel her to hear reason?”

“Console yourself, uncle. Were Britain a serfdom and you the Czar, you could not compel me to this step. I will write to Mr. Wynne. Give yourself no further trouble on the subject.”

And she did. The matter, so far, was ended; but the storm within Fieldhead was only displaced, not stilled.

Fortune is said to be changeful, and yet she repeats herself with a tiresome sameness, striking again and again at the same place, as if it were a bell she liked the sound of. Miss Keeldar—or Miss Keeldar’s fortune—had made a sensation in the district. It seemed to the neighbourhood that an heiress, like a great harvest, must be gathered; and presently offers came, as sheaves are carted in—one after another, pressed, urged, exhibited as if they were proof of the land’s richness.

No less than three proposals followed Mr. Wynne’s; all more or less eligible. Mr. Sympson pressed each with the same confident hand; Shirley refused each with the same clear denial. Among them were men of decent character and ample wealth, and there were many besides her uncle who asked, with lifted brows, what she meant, and whom she expected to entrap, that she should be so fastidious.

At last the gossips thought they had found the key; and Mr. Sympson, eager for any explanation that promised to bring his niece back under the yoke of what he called reason, seized on the discovery with delight. It showed Shirley to him in a new light, and he changed his whole manner accordingly.

Fieldhead, of late, had been growing too hot to hold both uncle and niece. Mrs. Sympson, with her smooth patience, could not reconcile them. The daughters stiffened at every open clash, as if quarrels were draughts that chilled the blood. Gertrude and Isabella whispered by the hour in their dressing-room, and grew pale with decorous dread when they chanced to be left alone with their bold cousin—who, without lifting her voice, could make rebellion sound like law.

Then, all at once, a change came over the household, as when a kettle that has been singing furiously is taken from the blaze and set aside: the noise does not cease at once, but the temper of it alters. Mr. Sympson was appeased; his family’s tranquillity seemed restored.

The village of Nunnely has been mentioned—its old church, its forest, its monastic ruins, the stones that kept memory as stubbornly as old tongues keep certain untranslatable words. It had also its Hall, called the priory: older, larger, more lordly than any Briarfield or Whinbury could boast; and it had something no neighbour could rival—a title, a baronetcy. Yet for years that proud possession had been only nominal; the young baronet lived away in a distant province, unknown upon his Yorkshire land.

During Miss Keeldar’s stay at Cliffbridge, that fashionable watering-place where the sea’s voice is never wholly silent—now booming on the shingle, now whispering under the wind, now breaking into a sudden white clatter—she and her friends had met Sir Philip Nunnely and been introduced. Again and again they encountered him on the sands, on the cliffs, in the public walks, sometimes at the public balls. He seemed solitary. His manner was unpretending—too simple to be called affable; rather timid than proud. He did not condescend to their society; he seemed glad of it, as one who has lived long in echoing rooms is glad to hear, at last, a human footfall that is not his own.

With any unaffected person Shirley could swiftly knit acquaintance. She walked and talked with Sir Philip; she, her aunt, and cousins sometimes took a sail in his yacht. She liked him because he was kind and modest, and because she discovered she had power to amuse him—an influence she used with the ease of one who has always had it, and yet with some generosity too.

One slight drawback there was—where is friendship without it? Sir Philip had a literary turn. He wrote poetry—sonnets, stanzas, ballads. Perhaps Shirley thought him too fond of reading and reciting these pieces; perhaps she wished the rhyme had been truer, the measure more musical, the images fresher, the inspiration more fire. At any rate, she always winced when he returned to his poems, and usually did her best to steer the talk into another channel, as one might move a guest away from a dish that has been over-salted and served with pride.

He would beguile her into moonlight walks on the bridge, for the sole purpose, as it seemed, of pouring into her ear the longest of his ballads. He would lead her to secluded rustic seats, where the surf’s rush came softened, like distant applause, and the gardens breathed their late sweetness; and when he had her all to himself—with the sea spread before them, cliffs rising behind, and the scented shade lying round—he would draw out his last batch of sonnets and read them in a voice tremulous with feeling. He did not seem to know that, though they might be rhyme, they were not poetry. Shirley’s downcast eye and uneasy colour showed that she knew it, and was mortified by the single foible of this good and amiable gentleman.

Often she tried, gently, to wean him from that fanatic worship of his own Muse. It was his monomania; on all ordinary subjects he was sensible enough, and she was eager to meet him on those safer grounds. He questioned her sometimes about his place at Nunnely; she answered at length, delighted. She never tired of describing the antique priory, the wild silvan park, the hoary church and hamlet; the very sound of those names, spoken in their own Yorkshire air, seemed to her fuller than any elegant phrase learned elsewhere. She counselled him to come down, to gather his tenantry about him in his ancestral halls, to set his own foot on the soil that had borne his name so long.

Somewhat to her surprise, Sir Philip followed her counsel to the letter, and, towards the close of September, arrived at the priory.

He soon called at Fieldhead, and his first visit was not his last. He said—after he had made the round of the neighbourhood—that under no roof had he found such pleasant shelter as beneath the massive oak beams of the grey manor-house of Briarfield; cramped and modest enough compared with his own, but he liked it. There was a homely sound there—the quieter domestic stir, the human voices not drowned by grandeur—more wholesome to him, perhaps, than the echoing breadth of his priory.

Presently it did not suffice to sit with Shirley in her panelled parlour, where others came and went, and where he could rarely find a quiet moment to offer the latest production of his fertile muse. He must have her out among the pastures, by the still waters. Tête-à-tête ramblings she avoided, so he made parties—inviting the household and contriving chaperonage—to his own grounds, his glorious forest, to remoter scenes: woods severed by the Wharfe, vales watered by the Aire. In the hush beneath old trees, where the living world speaks in leaf-rustle and bird-note, and where the far-off grind of mills is softened into something like a bad memory, Sir Philip seemed most himself; and Shirley, who always listened to places as much as to people, felt the relief of those greener acoustics.

Such assiduity covered Miss Keeldar with distinction. Mr. Sympson’s prophetic soul began to feed on a splendid future. He already scented, from afar, the time when—left foot nursed on right knee, with a careless air—he could make dashing, familiar allusion to “my nephew the baronet.” Now his niece dawned upon him no longer “a mad girl,” but “a most sensible woman.” In confidential talk with Mrs. Sympson, he termed her “a truly superior person; peculiar, but very clever.” He treated her with exceeding deference; rose to open and shut doors; reddened his face and gave himself headaches by stooping to pick up gloves and handkerchiefs—little properties Shirley held with insecure tenure. He cut mysterious jokes about the superiority of woman’s wit over man’s wisdom; began obscure apologies for the blundering mistake he had made regarding the generalship, the tactics, of “a personage not a hundred miles from Fieldhead.” In short, he strutted and swelled as elate as any midden-cock on pattens.

Shirley watched these manoeuvres and received his innuendoes with phlegm; she seemed not half to comprehend the aim. When plainly charged with being the preferred of the baronet, she said she believed he did like her, and for her part she liked him. She had never thought a man of rank—the only son of a proud, fond mother, the only brother of doting sisters—could have so much goodness, and, on the whole, so much sense.

Time proved that Sir Philip liked her indeed. Whatever “curious charm” Mr. Hall had once noted in Shirley, it drew Sir Philip as surely as warmth draws one’s hands to a hearth. He sought her presence more and more, until at last the frequency of his visits testified that it had become to him an indispensable stimulus. About this time strange feelings hovered round Fieldhead; restless hopes and haggard anxieties haunted some of its rooms. There was an unquiet wandering of certain inmates among the still fields round the mansion; an expectancy, like a held breath, kept nerves strained.

One thing seemed clear: Sir Philip was not a man to be despised. He was amiable; if not highly intellectual, he was intelligent. Shirley could not say of him what she had said of Sam Wynne—that his feelings were blunt, his tastes coarse, his manners vulgar. There was sensibility in him; there was a very real, though not very discerning, love of the arts; there was the English gentleman in his deportment. As to wealth and lineage, both lay, of course, far beyond her claims.

At first, Shirley’s merry eye had found something to laugh at in Sir Philip’s appearance—boyish, slight, plain-featured, sandy-haired, of insignificant stature. But she soon checked herself; she would even flash into warmth if another spoke slightingly. He had “a pleasing countenance,” she maintained; “and there was that in his heart which was better than three Roman noses, than the locks of Absalom or the proportions of Saul.” One spare shaft she still kept for his unfortunate poetic propensity; but even there she would tolerate no irony except her own.

Matters had reached a point that seemed fully to warrant an observation Mr. Yorke made, about this time, to Louis Moore.

“Yond brother Robert of yours seems to me to be either a fool or a madman. Two months ago I could have sworn he had the game all in his own hands; and there he runs the country, and quarters himself up in London for weeks together, and by the time he comes back he’ll find himself checkmated. Louis, ‘there is a tide in the affairs of men, which, taken at the flood, leads on to fortune, but, once let slip, never returns again.’ I’d write to Robert, if I were you, and remind him of that.”

“Robert had views on Miss Keeldar?” asked Louis, as if the thought were new.

“Views I suggested to him myself, and views he might have realized, for she liked him.”

“As a neighbour?”

“As more than that. I have seen her change countenance and colour at the mere mention of his name. Write to the lad, I say, and tell him to come home. He is a finer gentleman than this bit of a baronet, after all.”

“Does it not strike you, Mr. Yorke, that for a mere penniless adventurer to aspire to a rich woman’s hand is presumptuous—contemptible?”

“Oh, if you are for high notions and double-refined sentiment, I’ve naught to say. I’m a plain, practical man myself, and if Robert is willing to give up that royal prize to a lad-rival—a puling slip of aristocracy—I am quite agreeable. At his age, in his place, with his inducements, I would have acted differently. Neither baronet, nor duke, nor prince should have snatched my sweetheart from me without a struggle. But you tutors are such solemn chaps; it is almost like speaking to a parson to consult with you.”

Flattered and fawned upon as Shirley now was, it appeared she was not absolutely spoiled; her better nature did not wholly desert her. Universal report had ceased to couple her name with that of Moore, and that silence seemed sanctioned by her own apparent oblivion of the absentee; yet that she had not quite forgotten him—had not quite let go her interest—was proved by the increased attention she showed, at this juncture, to Louis Moore, Robert’s brother, her own former teacher.

An attack of illness seized Louis—some fever that for a few days laid him low. Perhaps he had caught it in one of the poor cottages which he, his lame pupil Henry, and Mr. Hall were used to visit together; the air in such places could be close with damp, and with the sour breath of want. At any rate, he sickened, and after opposing the malady with a taciturn endurance for a day or two, was obliged to keep his chamber.

One evening he lay tossing on his thorny bed, while Henry, who would not leave him, watched faithfully at his side. A tap came at the door—too light to be that of Mrs. Gill or the housemaid—and Henry opened it.

“How is Mr. Moore to-night?” asked a low voice from the dark gallery.

“Come in and see him yourself.”

“Is he asleep?”

“I wish he could sleep. Come and speak to him, Shirley.”

“He would not like it.”

But the speaker stepped in. Henry, seeing her hesitate on the threshold, took her hand and drew her toward the couch with the quiet authority of a child who loves both parties.

The shaded light showed Miss Keeldar’s figure imperfectly; yet it revealed elegance—white dress, fair arms and neck, and a trembling chainlet of gold circling her throat and quivering on her breast, bright in the sickroom’s obscurity like a small star caught indoors. There was a party below; Sir Philip Nunnely was among them. The ladies were in the drawing-room; their hostess had stolen away to this hushed passage where the house’s cheerful sounds died, and only the low murmur of distant voices and the occasional soft tread of a servant came and went like far-off water.

Her mien was chastened, pensive. She spoke gently.

“Mr. Moore, how are you to-night?”

“I have not been very ill, and am now better.”

“I heard that you complained of thirst. I have brought you some grapes; can you taste one?”

“No; but I thank you for remembering me.”

“Just one.”

From the rich cluster in a small basket she severed a berry and offered it to his lips. He shook his head and turned away his flushed face, as if the sweetness would mock the bitterness in his mouth.

“But what, then, can I bring you instead? You have no wish for fruit; yet I see that your lips are parched. What beverage do you prefer?”

“Mrs. Gill supplies me with toast-and-water. I like it best.”

Silence fell for some minutes—a silence in which the house’s far-off life seemed louder: the faint clink of glass below, a door closing, the murmur of talk softened by distance, the whole domestic orchestra of an evening’s entertainment.

“Do you suffer?—have you pain?” she asked at last.

“Very little.”

“What made you ill?”

Silence again—his reserve as obstinate as if it were a lock he had vowed not to open.

“I wonder what caused this fever? To what do you attribute it?”

“Miasma, perhaps—malaria. This is autumn, a season fertile in fevers.”

“I hear you often visit the sick in Briarfield, and Nunnely too, with Mr. Hall. You should be on your guard; temerity is not wise.”

“That reminds me, Miss Keeldar, that perhaps you had better not enter this chamber or come near this couch. I do not believe my illness is infectious. I scarcely fear”—a sort of brief smile touched the edge of his mouth—“you will take it; but why should you run even the shadow of a risk? Leave me.”

“Patience, I will go soon; but I should like to do something for you before I depart—any little service—”

“They will miss you below.”

“No; the gentlemen are still at table.”

“They will not linger long. Sir Philip Nunnely is no wine-bibber, and I hear him just now pass from the dining-room to the drawing-room.”

“It is a servant.”

“It is Sir Philip; I know his step.”

“Your hearing is acute.”

“It is never dull, and the sense seems sharpened at present. Sir Philip was here to tea last night. I heard you sing to him some song which he had brought you. I heard him, when he took his departure at eleven o’clock, call you out on to the pavement, to look at the evening star.”

“You must be nervously sensitive.”

“I heard him kiss your hand.”

“Impossible!”

“No: my chamber is over the hall, the window just above the front door; the sash was a little raised, for I felt feverish. You stood ten minutes with him on the steps. I heard your discourse, every word, and I heard the salute.—Henry, give me some water.”

“Let me give it him.”

But Louis half rose to take the glass from Henry and declined Shirley’s attendance, as if her kindness were the very thing he could not bear.

“And can I do nothing?” she persisted, with that sincerity which—because it came late—had the more sting of truth in it.

“Nothing; for you cannot guarantee me a night’s peaceful rest, and it is all I at present want.”

“You do not sleep well?”

“Sleep has left me.”

“Yet you said you were not very ill?”

“I am often sleepless when in high health.”

“If I had power, I would lap you in the most placid slumber—quite deep and hushed, without a dream.”

“Blank annihilation! I do not ask that.”

“With dreams of all you most desire.”

“Monstrous delusions! The sleep would be delirium, the waking death.”

“Your wishes are not so chimerical; you are no visionary.”

“Miss Keeldar, I suppose you think so; but my character is not, perhaps, quite as legible to you as a page of the last new novel might be.”

“That is possible. But this sleep—I should like to woo it to your pillow, to win for you its favour. If I took a book and sat down and read some pages? I can well spare half an hour.”

“Thank you, but I will not detain you.”

“I would read softly.”

“It would not do. I am too feverish and excitable to bear a soft, cooing, vibrating voice close at my ear. You had better leave me.”

“Well, I will go.”

“And no good-night?”

“Yes, sir, yes. Mr. Moore, good-night.” And she went.

When the door had shut, Louis turned his face slightly away, as if the last of her light had gone with her, and spoke to Henry in a different tone—one steadier, gentler.

“Henry, my boy, go to bed now; it is time you had some repose.”

“Sir, it would please me to watch at your bedside all night.”

“Nothing less called for. I am getting better. There, go.”

“Give me your blessing, sir.”

“God bless you, my best pupil!”

“You never call me your dearest pupil!”

“No, nor ever shall.”

Possibly Shirley resented her former teacher’s rejection of her courtesy. It is certain she did not repeat the offer. Often as her light step crossed the gallery in the course of a day, it did not again pause at his door; nor did her “cooing, vibrating voice” a second time trouble the hush of his room. A sickroom, indeed, it soon ceased to be; Louis’s good constitution quickly triumphed, and in a few days he shook off the indisposition and resumed his duties as tutor.

That auld lang syne still held authority between preceptor and scholar was proved by the manner in which he would sometimes, without warning, step across the distance she liked to keep between them and, with a firm quiet hand, put down her high reserve—controlling it as he had once controlled her wilfulness over a task or a lesson.

One afternoon the Sympson family went out for a carriage airing. Shirley, never sorry to seize a reprieve from their society, remained behind, detained, she said, by business. The business—some letter-writing—was soon done after the yard gates closed on the departing wheels; and Miss Keeldar betook herself to the garden.

It was a peaceful autumn day. The Indian summer gilding lay upon the fields like a thin warm syrup, mellowing everything it touched. Woods stood russet and ripe, full-leaved still, though ready to be stripped. The heath’s purple, faded but not withered, tinged the hills. The beck wandered down to the Hollow through a district so silent that even its running seemed restrained; no wind followed its course or haunted its woody banks. Fieldhead’s gardens wore the seal of gentle decay. On the walks swept that morning yellow leaves had fluttered down again, as if the trees would not be kept tidy. The time of flowers—indeed, even of fruits—was past; yet a scantling of apples enriched the boughs, and here and there a last pale blossom opened, delicate and stubborn among the fading leaves.

These single flowers—the last of their race—Shirley culled as she wandered, thoughtful. She was fastening into her girdle a hueless, scentless nosegay—beauty grown thin, like a remembered word no longer spoken—when Henry Sympson called to her, limping from the house.

“Shirley, Mr. Moore would be glad to see you in the schoolroom and to hear you read a little French, if you have no more urgent occupation.”

He delivered the message simply, as if it were a thing of course.

“Did Mr. Moore tell you to say that?” she asked.

“Certainly; why not? And now, do come, and let us once more be as we were at Sympson Grove. We used to have pleasant school-hours in those days.”

Shirley perhaps thought much had altered since those days—names, meanings, the very weight of what was spoken and left unspoken. She made no remark; but after a brief pause she followed Henry quietly back into the house.
