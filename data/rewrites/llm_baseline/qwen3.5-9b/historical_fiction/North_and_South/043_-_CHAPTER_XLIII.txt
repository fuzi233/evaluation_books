"The meanest thing to which we bid adieu, Loses its meanness in the parting hour." — Elliott.

Mrs. Shaw harbored a dislike for Milton that was as vehement as her gentle nature allowed. To her, the town was a cacophony of noise and smoke; the poor walking the streets were filthy, the wealthy ladies overdressed, and not a single man, regardless of station, wore clothes that fitted properly. She was convinced that Margaret would never recover her lost strength while remaining in Milton, and she feared for herself, anticipating one of her own recurrent nervous attacks. "Margaret must return with me," she insisted, "and that quickly."

Though this was not necessarily the precise wording of her arguments, it was certainly the spirit behind them. Until Margaret, weak, weary, and broken-hearted, finally yielded a reluctant promise to accompany her aunt back to town immediately after Wednesday concluded. That Wednesday was to be mournful—the day Mr. Hale was to be interred, far from the homes he had known and far from the wife who lay lonely among strangers. This separation weighed heavily on Margaret; she lamented that if she had not succumbed to the overwhelming stupor during those initial tragic days, she might have managed the arrangements differently.

Before that sorrowful Wednesday arrived, Margaret received a letter from Mr. Bell.

"MY DEAR MARGARET:—I intended to return to Milton on Thursday, but unfortunately, circumstances arise wherein we, as Plymouth Fellows, are called upon to perform a specific duty, preventing my absence from my post. Captain Lennox and Mr. Thornton are currently here. The former appears to be a smart, well-meaning individual; he has proposed visiting Milton to assist you in searching for the will. Of course, there is no will, or you would have discovered it by now had you followed my previous instructions. Furthermore, the Captain declares he must take you and his mother-in-law home. Given his wife's current condition, I do not see how he can remain away longer than Friday.

However, your Dixon is trustworthy and can hold the fort until I arrive. Should there prove to be no will, I shall entrust the matter to my attorney in Milton, for I suspect this 'smart' Captain lacks significant business acumen. Nevertheless, his moustaches are splendid. A sale will inevitably follow, so please select whatever items you wish to reserve, or send a list later.

There are two further points before I conclude. You know, or if you do not, your late father surely did, that you are entitled to my money and goods upon my death. Do not mistake this for a premonition of my demise; I mention it merely to clarify the arrangement. The Lennoxes appear very fond of you now, and perhaps they will continue to be; perhaps not. Therefore, it is prudent to establish a formal agreement: you shall pay them two hundred and fifty pounds annually, as long as you and they find it agreeable to live together. (Note: This figure naturally includes Dixon; ensure you are not cajoled into paying more for her services.) Thus, you will not be cast adrift should the Captain eventually desire his house exclusively, allowing you to depart with your person and your two hundred and fifty pounds elsewhere—or, indeed, if I have not already claimed you to serve as my housekeeper. Regarding attire, Dixon, and personal expenses, including confectionery (a necessity for all young ladies until wisdom arrives with age), I intend to consult a lady acquaintance regarding your allowance from your father before finalizing this.

Now, Margaret, have you already flown into a rage upon reading thus far, wondering what right the old man possesses to settle your affairs so cavalierly? I make no doubt you have. Yet, the old man has a right. He has loved your father for thirty-five years; he stood beside him on his wedding day; he closed his eyes in death. Moreover, he is your godfather. Since he cannot offer you much spiritual guidance, possessing a hidden awareness of your superiority in such matters, he seeks instead to provide you with material aid. Furthermore, he has no known relatives on earth—"Who is there to mourn for Adam Bell?"—and his entire heart is devoted to this singular endeavor. Margaret Hale is not the type of girl to deny him. Write back within the day, even if only with two lines, to inform me of your decision. But *no thanks*."

Margaret picked up a pen and scrawled with a trembling hand: *"Margaret Hale is not the girl to say him nay."*

In her frail state, she could conceive of no other response, yet she felt vexed by using these words. However, she was so utterly fatigued by this slight exertion that, had she been capable of devising another form of acceptance, she could not have remained seated long enough to write a single syllable of it. She was compelled to lie down again and attempt to cease thinking.

"My dearest child! Has that letter vexed or troubled you?"

"No!" Margaret replied feebly. "I shall be better when tomorrow is over."

"I feel sure, darling, you will not improve until I remove you from this horrid air. I cannot imagine how you have endured it for these two years."

"Where could I possibly go? I could not leave Papa and Mama."

"Well, do not distress yourself, my dear. I daresay it was all for the best, though I had no conception of your living conditions. Our butler's wife resides in a superior house to this."

"It is sometimes very charming—in summer; one cannot judge by its appearance now. I have been very happy here," Margaret said, closing her eyes to halt the conversation.

The house, teeming with comforts now compared to its earlier state, held a different atmosphere. The evenings were chilly, and fires burned brightly in every bedroom under Mrs. Shaw's orders. She doted on Margaret in every conceivable way, purchasing every delicacy and soft luxury in which she herself would have sought refuge. Yet, Margaret remained indifferent to these provisions, or if they captured her attention, it was solely as grounds for gratitude toward her aunt, who was making such sacrifices for her sake.

She was restless despite her weakness. Throughout the day, she avoided contemplating the ongoing ceremonies at Oxford by wandering from room to room, lethargically setting aside articles she wished to retain. Dixon followed her, acting on Mrs. Shaw's instruction to receive directives, though privately instructed to soothe Margaret into repose as soon as possible.

"These books, Dixon, I shall keep. All the rest you will send to Mr. Bell? They are of a kind he will value for themselves, as well as for Papa's sake. This... I should like you to take this to Mr. Thornton after I am gone. Wait; I will write a note with it."

Resuming her tour of the house, she turned over objects familiar from her childhood, touching them with a caressing reluctance to part. Yet, she spoke hardly at all. Dixon reported to Mrs. Shaw that she suspected Miss Hale heard not a word spoken, though the maid talked continuously in an effort to distract her intent.

The result of keeping her upright all day was excessive physical exhaustion by evening, granting her a better night's rest than she had enjoyed since hearing of Mr. Hale's death.

At breakfast the following morning, she expressed a desire to bid farewell to one or two friends. Mrs. Shaw demurred: "I am sure, my dear, you have no friends here sufficiently intimate to warrant calling upon them so soon, before attending church."

"But today is my only opportunity; if Captain Lennox comes this afternoon, and if we must—if I truly must go tomorrow..."

"Oh, yes, we shall go tomorrow. I am increasingly convinced this air is detrimental to you, causing you to look so pale and ill. Besides, Edith expects us; she may be waiting for me. And you cannot be left alone, my dear, at your age. No; if you insist on making these calls, I will accompany you. Dixon can arrange a coach, I presume?"

Thus, Mrs. Shaw took charge of Margaret, bringing her maid along to manage the shawls and air-cushions. Margaret's face was too sorrowful to break into a smile amidst this preparation for two visits she had frequently made alone at all hours. She was half-afraid to admit that one of the destinations was Nicholas Higgins' cottage. All she could do was hope her aunt would be too indisposed to alight from the carriage and walk up the court, risking having her face slapped by wet laundry hanging on ropes strung from house to house.

A small internal conflict arose in Mrs. Shaw's mind between ease and a sense of matronly propriety, but the former prevailed. With many admonitions for Margaret to be careful and avoid catching a fever—a constant threat in such locales—her aunt permitted her to visit a place she had frequented before without precautions or permission.

Nicholas was out; only Mary and a couple of the Boucher children were at home. Margaret chided herself for not timing her visit better. Mary possessed a blunt intellect, though her feelings were warm and kind. The moment she grasped Margaret's purpose, she began to cry and sob with such unrestrained emotion that Margaret found it futile to utter any of the thousand comforting thoughts that had occurred to her during the ride.
