The reverberations of Mr. Thornton’s voice still clung to the drawing-room walls, an abrasive acoustic residue that seemed to vibrate against the delicate teacups and the polished oak. Margaret stood entirely still, attempting to process the sudden, violent rupture in her sonic environment. His proposal had not been an offering; it had been an industrial intrusion, a dominant lexicon of possession and rock-like power that sought to override the quiet, subjective dialect of her own heart. 

She retreated to the kitchen, seeking the profound, grounding rituals of sustenance to steady her trembling hands. As she automatically began to feed the hearth fire, the scent of woodsmoke mingled with the lingering olfactory memory of Helstone—a stark contrast to the sterile, suffocating coal-ash of Milton. She compared Henry Lennox’s proposal to Thornton’s. Lennox had offered a polite, softly spoken Southern idiom that momentarily slid over the boundary of friendship before retreating gracefully. Thornton’s declaration, however, lacked any preceding grammar of intimacy. They had shared no shared dialect of friendship, only a continuous clash of untranslatable experiences. His booming, passionate cadences had dismissed her meticulous, agrarian values as though they were mere chaff, attempting to force her into submission through the sheer volume of his will. Yet, beneath the deafening clangor of his pride, the clear conviction dawned on her, tasting as sudden and sharp as bitter almond on the tongue: he did love her. The realization sent a shudder through her, a mythic weight settling into the everyday rhythm of her domestic duties. She disliked him all the more for having invaded her inner acoustic sanctuary. 

Needing to dispel the oppressive atmosphere, she prepared a basket for Bessy Higgins. Into it, she placed a jar of calf’s-foot jelly she had meticulously clarified that morning. She had steeped it in lemon peel, star anise, and a whisper of elderflower—a recipe passed down through generations of Hampshire women, a culinary artifact preserving a fading cultural memory. 

Stepping out into the noonday heat, the mechanical roar of Milton immediately assaulted her. The relentless thumping of the looms and the shrieking of factory whistles formed a wall of industrial noise pollution that Margaret felt as a physical weight, a stark reminder of the commercialization of human labor that crushed the spirit. 

When she reached the Higgins’ home, the air was thick and stagnant. Bessy lay flat upon the settle, moved close to the fire despite the sultry heat. She looked entirely drained, a victim not merely of the cotton fluff in her lungs, but of the ceaseless auditory violence of her world. Margaret, moving with the silent, instinctive grace of a caregiver attuned to the physical needs of others, gently elevated Bessy’s pillows to open her airways, offering a spoonful of the tart, aromatic jelly to soothe her parched throat.

"I thought I should na’ ha’ seen yo’ again," Bessy whispered, her voice a fragile, dying dialect against the distant rumble of the mills.

"I could not come yesterday. My mother was so ill," Margaret replied softly, her own voice modulated to offer acoustic comfort. 

"The wraglin’ and the loud voices had just torn me to pieces," Bessy confessed, her eyes wide with the trauma of the sonic chaos. "I thought when father left, oh! if I could just hear her voice, reading me some words o’ peace and promise, I could die away into the silence and rest o’ God, just as a baby is hushed up to sleep."

Bessy’s plea for auditory relief resonated deeply with Margaret. But before Margaret could read, Bessy’s mind returned to the discordant events of the riot. She explained how her father, Nicholas Higgins—a man holding fast to the traditional, agrarian-rooted principles of collective honor and measured resistance—had seen his careful work destroyed. The Union had demanded a silent, steadfast strike, a refusal to engage in the chaotic noise of violence. But Boucher, driven by starvation and lacking the discipline of the elders, had shattered that silence.

"Committee didn’t want to have right all mixed up wi’ wrong," Bessy explained, struggling for breath, "no more nor I can separate the physic-powder from th’ jelly yo’ gave me to mix it in; jelly is much the biggest, but the bitter powder tastes it all through."

She recounted the devastating clash of power dynamics between her father and Boucher. Higgins, recognizing that Boucher’s stone-throwing had corrupted their righteous cause, had threatened to turn him over to the police, attempting to reassert the moral lexicon of the strike. In response, Boucher, weeping in a frenzy of spent passion and hunger, had struck Higgins in the face. Higgins, stunned by the blow and the sight of a starving man turning to brute force, had ultimately refused to surrender him to the authorities, choosing instead the complex, untranslatable mercy of their shared suffering. 

"And now dunnot talk to me, but just read out th’ chapter," Bessy pleaded. "I want some thoughts of the world that’s far away to take the weary taste of it out of my mouth. Read about the New Heavens, and the New Earth."

Margaret’s soft, low voice filled the small, soot-stained room. She spoke the ancient, mythic words with deliberate, rhythmic pacing, allowing the cadence to act as an acoustic balm. As the rich, evocative imagery of a renewed world washed over them, the moisture of tears gathered on Bessy's eyelashes, and she finally slipped into a restless sleep.

Upon returning home, Margaret found her mother in the drawing-room, enveloped in a rare moment of comfort on the new water-bed. The domestic space was hushed, the industrial noise kept at bay by heavy curtains. Mrs. Hale was sipping a restorative, slow-simmered bone broth, rich with the savory depth of roasted marrow and thyme, though her mind was miles away, wandering through the interwoven timelines of her youth.

"It is more like the beds at Sir John Beresford’s than anything I have slept on since," Mrs. Hale mused, the broth warming her fragile frame. "People seem to have lost the art of making such things, just as they have forgotten how to cure the hams or dry the lavender as we did at Oxenham. Do you not find them a perfect repose, Margaret?"

"I am usually so sleepy at night that I nap off directly," Margaret said, smiling gently as she took her mother's hand. 

The mention of Oxenham, however, triggered a profound shift in Mrs. Hale’s demeanor. The culinary and sensory memories of the past seamlessly unlocked the locked vault of her grief. She spoke of Frederick, of his infancy, of the deep, untranslatable bond he shared with Dixon, who had fed him and cared for him when Mrs. Hale was too weak. 

"Everybody loved him," Mrs. Hale said, her voice trembling. "He was born with the gift of winning hearts... and now, Margaret, I don't know where my boy is, and sometimes I think I shall never see him again."

Tears flowed without restraint, a sudden, torrential release of maternal sorrow. Then, sitting stiffly upright, her eyes dilated and solemn, she grasped Margaret’s hand. The fading matriarch, sensing the rapid erosion of her own life, issued a desperate, passionate demand. "If I am to die, Margaret, I must see my child first. I charge you, bring him to me that I may bless him. The longing to see Frederick stands between me and God."

Margaret recognized this not as a mere whim, but as a vital need for spiritual and emotional sustenance. To deny her mother this final connection to their familial bloodline, to the very dialect of their shared past, would be a starvation of the soul. 

"I will write to-night, mamma," Margaret promised, her voice a steady anchor in the swirling emotion. "He shall come."

With swift, urgent strokes, Margaret drafted the letter. The scratch of the pen was the only sound in the room, a desperate attempt to bridge oceans and translate her mother's dying wish into a summons that could defy the British Empire. She sealed it and carried it out into the smog-choked afternoon, hurrying to the post office before the five o'clock collection.

On her way back, the acrid, metallic tang of coal smoke sitting heavy on her tongue, she was overtaken by her father. When she confessed what she had done, Mr. Hale’s face tightened with dread. 

"You should have waited till I came in, Margaret," he said slowly. He understood the healing power of Frederick's return for his wife, a medicine more potent than any physician's draught, but the systemic power dynamics of the world outside their domestic sphere were merciless. 

"All these years since the mutiny, papa?" Margaret asked, the chill of fear altering the very taste in her mouth.

"The lapse of years does not wash out the memory of the offence," Mr. Hale explained, his voice heavy with the grim reality of imperial authority. The Admiralty, he told her, operated on a relentless, unforgiving lexicon. They cared nothing for the subjective experiences of men galled to madness by tyranny. In their dominant language, a challenge to authority was a stain that could only be blotted out by blood. 

Margaret shivered, creeping horror taking root in her stomach. She had acted to provide the ultimate sustenance for her dying mother, weaving a mythic reunion into their tragic present. Yet, as she took her father’s arm and walked wearily through the cacophonous, uncaring streets of Milton, she was haunted by the terrifying possibility that in trying to save one life, she had just summoned her brother to the executioner’s block.
