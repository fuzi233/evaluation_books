Mrs. Lambert had taken her stand with the girls at the window, a white handkerchief lifted like a small flag of surrender to the road. They watched the two horsemen go at a pace that was neither hurried nor ceremonious—just the easy, well-bred trot of men who had learned to make leave-taking look like an ordinary part of the day, as if departures were no more than stepping out to see to the orchard or the malt-kiln.

But at Oakhurst, even the road had its ritual.

They followed the riders with their eyes until the limes took them—those old trees whose leaves made a thin, constant whispering, like silk brushed the wrong way. The trunks were grey, as if rubbed with ash; the boughs clumped together into a green screen that hid the bend of the lane. Behind that screen Mrs. Lambert had watched, through the years, the shapes of her life go and vanish: a husband riding to danger, sons packed off to school, one after another swallowed by that same corner, and then, when Heaven pleased, returned again—bringing noise back into the house, boots on the flags, laughter in the passage, the sound of a whip unhooked from its peg.

The women’s hours made room for remembrance. When men were gone, the house itself became an archive. There was the vacant chamber where a boy had slept the night before, the bed still faintly pressed where his carpet-bag had lain. There was his rod and wicker creel set in the hall, smelling of river-water and damp rush. At dinner there would be a dish made because it had been his favourite—cherry-tart, perhaps, the crust browned to that point where butter turns nutty and the fruit keeps its sharpness; and a mother would notice, as if the noticing were a kind of prayer, that half of it had been eaten at two o’clock in spite of tears, with a choking little sister on either side trying to smile.

Even the day’s sound changed. At evening prayers a voice would be missing from the responses; at midnight the house’s silence would feel too deliberate, as though the very timbers had been trained to listen for absent footsteps. Mrs. Lambert, whose life had been stitched together by such comings and goings, grew quiet whenever she and the girls walked beneath those limes without the men. Once she had said she would like to carve their names upon the trunks—true-lovers’ knots and initials, as people did then—so that the trees would hold, in their living grain, what memory could not always keep. Miss Theo, who had a pretty talent for turning feeling into verse, had even written lines upon the trees which her delighted mother sent off to a magazine; and no doubt the limes, indifferent, went on whispering as before, taking poems and partings as they took weather.

The riders passed the screen of leaves.

“Now we are out of sight of the ladies,” Colonel Lambert said, lifting his hat in a last salute even though the window could no longer command the road. “My wife will be watching still. She always watches until we are round that corner. I hope we shall have you at Oakhurst again, Mr. Warrington. When the boys are home there may be better sport for you.”

“I never want to be happier, sir, than I have been,” Harry Warrington answered; and the words came out with a sincerity that surprised even himself. “It feels like leaving old friends.”

“The friend with whom we shall sup to-night has a son who is an old friend of our family too,” Lambert said. “My wife—she is an inveterate marriage-monger—would have made a match between him and one of my girls, but that the Colonel hath chosen to fall in love with somebody else.”

“Ah,” Harry sighed, with that sudden inwardness which came upon him whenever any man spoke lightly of love. “Other folks have done the same thing. There were brave fellows before Agamemnon.”

“I beg your pardon—Aga—?” the Virginian began, honestly puzzled.

“No, no—James Wolfe,” Lambert cried, laughing. “A young fellow still, or what we call so—scarce thirty. The youngest lieutenant-colonel in the army, unless you count the few scores of our nobility who take rank before us common folk.”

“Of course—of course,” Harry said, with the colonial reverence for precedence which could sit quite comfortably beside republican pride in other hours.

“I have seen him command captains thirty years his senior,” Lambert continued, “brave men too, who had neither his merit nor his fortune. Yet no one envies him. We acknowledge he is our superior. He is beloved by every man of our old regiment, knows each face, each voice. A scholar as well as a soldier, and master of many languages.”

At that, Harry’s mind—so often quickened by the jingle of bridles and the easy music of hoofbeats on hard road—fell into another kind of listening: a listening inward, towards what he lacked.

“Ah, sir,” he said, and the humility sounded raw in him, like a confession spoken too loudly. “I feel I have neglected my youth. I have come to England an ignoramus. Had my dear brother lived, he would have borne our name—and our colony—better than I can. George was a scholar; George was a musician; George could speak with learned men. I am glad to have come home, and to you especially, if only to learn how ignorant I am.”

“If you know that, ’tis a gain already,” Lambert said gently. “At home, and especially of late, I daresay you were flattered.”

Harry’s face, warmed by the morning’s ride and by a kind of shame, coloured. “Since we lost my brother, I used to think myself a mighty fine fellow. Folks about us—our gentry—know a deal about dogs and horses and betting and games. I wish I knew more about books, and less about them.”

“Nay,” Lambert replied. “Dogs and horses are books too, in their way. You may read truth out of ’em. Not all men are made to be scholars. If each does his best in his station, what matter high or low? His Royal Highness commands; Martin Lambert is colonel; Jack Hunt rides behind us now and was once a private and is a worthy groom. Who knows whether Jack’s currycomb, my epaulets, or a prince’s baton may not prove equal at the last? When I began, I dreamed of honour. Now I think of duty—and yonder folks from whom we parted. Let us trot on, else we shall not reach Westerham before nightfall.”

So they rode, the road’s own music changing as it passed from open country to village verge: the softer thud where earth lay over stone, the sharper ring when the shoe struck flint. There were hedges with late scents trapped in them—hawthorn lingering, bruised nettle, the green bitterness of leaves warming under sun. At one point they passed a farm where bread was being drawn; the air carried that plain miracle—yeast and heat and browned crust—and Harry, who could not help responding to such things, found his heart tugged a moment towards Oakhurst’s kitchen, towards the steady domestic comfort of meals that were not merely fuel but ceremony, memory, and love set on a plate.

At Westerham they were received with the solid hospitality of an old soldier’s house: the smell of beeswax on dark furniture; a hearth that had known generations of boots drying; and, in the parlour, a stately matron whose manner could be warm and severe in the same breath, as if she ruled affection by discipline.

The elder Colonel Wolfe was there too—grey now, with the posture of a man who had been trained to stand through discomfort. His recollections were of five-and-forty years back; he spoke of campaigns as if they were weather. And with them was their son—Lieutenant-Colonel Wolfe, come over from Maidstone where Kingsley’s regiment lay.

Harry looked at him with curiosity, for this was the man whose name had been tossed about like a talisman. The face did not answer to easy admiration: lean, pale, with red hair, high cheek-bones, a nose that seemed sharpened by thought and hardship. Yet when he spoke, an animation lit him; and when he listened, his attention was so exact and courteous that one forgot to measure his looks. His voice carried clearly through the room, not loud, but with a soldier’s habit of being understood; and it had the peculiar power of making others’ voices seem more honest, as if he drew truth out by the mere act of hearing.

At supper, when it was known that Mr. Warrington intended for Tunbridge Wells, the lady of the house declared that James should bear him company. She leaned to Lambert and whispered something that made the older officer smile and give a knowing wink. Wine was called for, and Lambert toasted “Miss Lowther.”

“With all my heart,” cried Wolfe, eager as a boy, and drained his glass.

His mother, enjoying her own little stage, whispered how James and the young lady were to make a match, and how she came of the famous Lowthers of the North.

“If she were the daughter of King Charlemagne,” Lambert said, “she is not too good for James Wolfe, or for his mother’s son.”

“Mr. Lambert would not say so if he knew her,” Wolfe declared, and his homely face took on that sudden tenderness which makes even plainness beautiful.

“Oh! of course she is the priceless pearl and you are nothing,” his mother retorted, half-scolding, half-proud. “No. I am of Colonel Lambert’s opinion,” she went on briskly, as if she would not allow humility to settle in her son too long. “If she brought Cumberland for a jointure, I should say it was my James’s due. That is the way with them, Mr. Warrington. We tend our children through fevers and measles and whooping-cough and small-pox; we send them to the army and cannot sleep for thinking; we break our hearts at parting, and have them at home a week or two in the year, or maybe not for ten years—then comes a lass with bright eyes, and away goes our boy, and never cares a fig for us afterwards.”

“And pray, my dear, how did you come to marry James’s papa?” the elder Colonel Wolfe asked dryly. “Why didn’t you stay with your parents?”

“Because James’s papa was gouty and wanted somebody to take care of him, I suppose,” she answered. “Not because I liked him a bit.”

So the evening went—easy talk, kindness, laughter that did not bite too hard.

In the morning Lambert, with many expressions of friendship, gave the young Virginian over to Wolfe’s care and turned his horse homeward. Harry watched him ride away with a longing that surprised him. Oakhurst, with its clean quiet and the limes’ whispering, had entered him like a scent that clings to clothes. He could almost hear again the domestic acoustics of that house: the clink of china, the soft tread of women on boards, the calmer air unshaken by the continual shouting of London or the brassy racket of fashionable places. It made him think—though he could not have said it aloud—how much of a man’s soul is ruled by what he hears all day, and how peace is not only a moral but a sound.

Wolfe, for his part, agreed readily with Harry’s praises of Lambert and his family.

“To have such a good name, and to live such a life as Colonel Lambert’s,” Wolfe said, “seems to me now the height of human ambition.”

“And glory and honour?” Harry asked. “Are those nothing? Would you give up the winning of them?”

“They were my dreams once,” Wolfe answered; and as he spoke, his voice took on a different cadence—less martial, more human. “I have followed arms since I was fourteen. I have seen almost every duty in my calling. I know the garrison towns in this country; I have served wherever there was work these ten years. I have done near the whole of a soldier’s duty—except the command of an army, which can hardly be hoped for at my years. And now, methinks, I would like quiet. Books. A wife to love me. Children to dandle on my knee. I have imagined such an Elysium. True love is better than glory; and a tranquil fireside, with the woman of your heart beside it, the greatest good the gods can send us.”

Harry pictured it—firelight, a woman’s face, the small warm weight of a child—and said “Yes.” But the assent was not cheerful; it had too much shadow in it, and Wolfe, quick to observe, glanced at him.

“You say ‘Yes’ as if a fireside and a sweetheart were not to your taste.”

“Why, look you, Colonel,” Harry replied, forcing a lighter tone, “there are other things a young fellow might like. You have had sixteen years of the world. I have been but a few months from my mother’s apron-strings. When I have seen a campaign or two—or six—as you have; when I have distinguished myself and made the world talk of me, then I may think of retiring.”

Wolfe’s heart, full of his own hope, answered by returning to it. He poured out praises of marriage, and then—warming, as men do when they speak of a single beloved name—fell into rhapsody about Miss Lowther: her virtues, her beauty, her manner, the very sound of her voice, as if each syllable were a promise. It was intensely interesting to him; to Harry, whose own thoughts of love were knotted with melancholy, it was a kind of distant music—sweet, but not meant for his ear.

They rode on. The afternoon was mild, the sky pale and wide. A mistake occurred a few miles from Tunbridge, when two horsemen stopped them; Harry, with the Virginian’s quick suspicion and a hand that had learned the habit of readiness, had pistol out, thinking them highwaymen.

Wolfe laughed. “Hold your fire, man. They are innkeepers’ agents.”

“Robbers, then,” Harry muttered, lowering the weapon.

“Only in their calling.”

At that instant Gumbo’s horse, catching some fright or fancy, bolted; and there was bawling enough from Harry to break the quiet of the hedgerows, sending birds out of cover and making the whole lane ring with it. The runaway was brought back after a clatter of hooves and a scramble; and then they rode on, the road narrowing as it drew into the little town where society gathered to drink waters, trade glances, and spend money as if it were part of the cure.

They alighted at their inn and parted—Wolfe in eager pursuit of the lady of his heart, Harry to seek his aunt.

He found Madame de Bernstein installed in handsome lodgings, with London lacqueys like tall furniture in her anteroom and behind her chair when she went abroad. The very air of her rooms seemed different—perfume laid upon perfume, powdery, sweet, and faintly stifling; the sounds were sharper too: the clack of heels, the rustle of stiff silk, the crisp little coughs and laughs of visitors who made noise because they could not bear silence.

She received him with the utmost kindness. His cousin, Lady Maria, was absent at his arrival; whether that absence made him unhappy, or whether he hid the fact, or whether the Baroness noticed anything at all, remained—like so many things between the sexes—unspoken.

A beau in a rich figured suit sat with her, the first of that brilliant species Harry had seen up close; and two dowagers were there too, monstrous with hoops and plentiful rouge, their faces like painted fans. Madame de Bernstein presented her nephew as the young Croesus out of Virginia. The phrase went off like a rocket.

They had heard of him.

His estate, she declared, was as large as Kent—and, as she had read, infinitely more fruitful. She spoke of Virginia’s marvels with the free invention of a woman determined to dazzle: spices, gold mines, strange beasts; perhaps, in the heat of display, she did not stop short of diamonds. As for his mother, she described Madam Esmond as “Princess Pocahontas” in her own country and did not tire of praising mother and son, their riches and their good qualities, as if she were seasoning a dish to please a fashionable palate—more and more sweetness, more and more spice, until the truth itself was drowned in flavour.

The beau shook Harry by the hand with delight and vowed himself honoured. The dowagers praised the young man’s good looks so loudly that he blushed, and blushed again at the absurdity of being admired for virtues he had not yet had time to prove.

They departed with curtseys and congees to carry the news through Tunbridge: that a rich young Virginian had arrived, handsome and well-bred, a prize to be viewed.

“You could not have come at a better moment, my dear,” Madame de Bernstein said the instant the door closed. Her voice changed—less honeyed, more briskly amused. “Those three have the most active tongues in the Wells. They will trumpet your good qualities everywhere. I have introduced you to a hundred people already and—Heaven help me!—told all sorts of fibs about Virginia to describe your estate. It is prodigious large, but I fear I magnified it. I filled it with wonderful animals, gold mines, spices—I am not sure I did not say diamonds. As for your negroes, I gave your mother armies of them and represented her as a sovereign princess reigning over a magnificent dominion. So she has a magnificent dominion. I cannot tell within a few hundred thousand pounds what her yearly income is, but it must be great. And you, sir, must prepare to be treated as heir-apparent to this royal lady. Do not let your head be turned. From this day forth you will be flattered as you have never been flattered in your life.”

“And to what end, ma’am?” Harry asked, bewildered and a little nettled. “Why should I be reputed so rich? Why all this flattery?”

“In the first place, sir, you must not contradict your old aunt,” she said sharply. “I have no desire to be made a fool of before company. As for your reputation—why, we found it almost ready-made. A London newspaper has somehow heard of you and printed a story of the immense wealth of a young gentleman from Virginia, lately landed, nephew of my Lord Castlewood. Immensely wealthy you are and can’t help yourself. All the world is eager to see you. You shall go to church to-morrow and witness the whole congregation turn from books and prayers to worship the golden calf in your person. You would not have had me undeceive them, and speak ill of my own flesh and blood?”

“But how am I bettered by being thought rich?”

“You are making your entry into the world, and the gold key opens most doors. To be thought rich is as good as to be rich. You need not spend; they will say you hoard, and your reputation for avarice will do you good. You shall see how the mothers smile and the daughters curtsey! Don’t look so surprised. When I was young, I did as the rest did and tried more than once—desperately—to better myself by a good marriage. Your poor grandmother, who was a saint—bating a little jealousy—scolded me and called me worldly. Worldly! So is the world worldly. We must serve it as it serves us and give it nothing for nothing.”

Then, leaning back, tapping her fingers as if counting coins or sins, she went on in a lower, more candid tone.

“Mr. Henry Esmond Warrington—I can’t help loving the first two names, old woman as I am—would have been nobody on coming here or to London. Our protection would help little. Our family has little credit and, entre nous, not much reputation. Castlewood was more than suspected in ’45, and hath since ruined himself by play.”

Harry had never heard this. The words struck him as a new sound in an old house—a crack where he had expected polish.

“He never had much to lose,” she continued, “but he has lost that and more. The estate is eaten with mortgages. He has been at all sorts of schemes to raise money. He has been so desperate, my dear, that I did not think my diamonds safe with him; and I have travelled to and from Castlewood without them. Terrible, is it not, to speak so of one’s own nephew? But you are my nephew too—and not spoiled by the world yet—and I wish to warn you of its wickedness. I heard of your play-doings with Will and the chaplain. They could do you no harm—nay, I am told you had the better of them. Had you played with Castlewood, you would have had no such luck. You would have played, had not an old aunt warned my Lord Castlewood to keep his hands off you.”

“What—did you interfere to preserve me?”

“I kept his clutches off,” she said coolly. “Be thankful you came out of that ogre’s den with any flesh on your bones. It has been the rage and passion of all our family. My poor silly brother played; both his wives played—especially the last, who has little else to live upon now but her nightly assemblies and the money for the cards. I would not trust her at Castlewood alone with you. The passion is too strong. They would fall upon you and fleece you; then fall upon each other and fight for the plunder. But for his place at Court my poor nephew hath nothing—and that is Will’s fortune too, sir, and Maria’s and her sister’s.”

“And are they too fond of the cards?”

“No. To do poor Molly justice, gaming is not her passion. But in London little Fanny will bet her eyes out of her head. I know what the passion is, sir. Do not look so astonished. I have had it—as I had the measles when I was a child. I am not cured quite. For a poor old woman there is nothing left but that. You will see some high play at my tables to-night. Hush. It was that I wanted, and without which I moped so at Castlewood! I could not win of my nieces or their mother; they would not pay if they lost. ’Tis best to warn you in time, lest you be shocked by the discovery. I cannot live without the cards, there’s the truth!”

A few days earlier, among the Castlewood people, Harry—who loved cards and cock-fighting and betting and every sport that made the blood move—might have laughed at such a confession. But since Oakhurst, since quiet meals and gentler company, laughter came harder. He had begun to hear, as if the ear of his mind had been newly tuned, the harshness in certain voices: how easily faith and honour were made a jest; how pure lives were treated as fables; how selfishness was spoken of as common practice and sacred duties shrugged away with a story.

These people were not hypocrites; they did not pretend to virtue. They flung no stones. They smiled, shrugged, and passed on, telling wicked funny tales about acquaintances whose tables they visited and whose hands they shook. Their speech itself—dominant, fashionable, careless—seemed to flatten every finer meaning into something marketable, like a song scraped of its native dialect to make it sell in town. Harry felt, without having words for it, that there were experiences that would not translate into their lexicon—not Oakhurst’s household hush, not the honest music of rural life, not the way a mother could taste a child’s absence in a dish set down for too many.

“How dreadfully dull you must have found those humdrum people at that village where we left you—but the savages were very kind to you, child!” Madame de Bernstein said, and patted his cheek with her pretty old hand.

“They were very kind,” Harry answered quickly; and his face flushed up, not with shame now but with anger. “And it was not at all dull, ma’am. I think they are some of the best people in the world.”

Her tone had jarred on him like a false note. He could not bear that she should speak lightly of the new friends who had fed him, tended him, and taken him in—friends whose very domestic routines had felt like a kind of history kept alive.

The Baroness, imperious and swift to resent, began to draw herself up; then checked. A second thought—mischievous, sharp as a pin—pricked her.

“Those two girls,” she thought. “A sick-bed. An interesting stranger. Of course he has been falling in love with one of them.”

And as if the thought had summoned its object, she lifted her eyes with a quick, playful glance—just as Lady Maria entered the room.
