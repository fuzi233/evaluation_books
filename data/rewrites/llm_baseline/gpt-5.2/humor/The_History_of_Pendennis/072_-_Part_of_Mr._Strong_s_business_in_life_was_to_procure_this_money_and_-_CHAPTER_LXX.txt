Arthur Pendennis reached London as a man might reach a kitchen door in a sudden rain—only for a moment, only to fetch what he needed, and then out again to whatever obligation was simmering next. He had convoyed a couple of west-country fellow-travellers to their hotel, seen them safely stowed like parcels in a porter’s hands, and then made for Lamb Court, where the Temple’s old brick baked in such sunshine as cared to stoop into that narrow place.

The chambers looked dreary, but not unkindly. There is a sort of domestic hearth-warmth in a set of rooms where a man is free—freer than he ever is under a parental roof or a wife’s vigilant housekeeping—though he may have nothing hotter than a grudging coal and the distant clatter of other men’s boots on the stairs. In Lamb Court the air held its usual chorus: the scrape of quills, the cough of ancient clerks, the thin shout of boys in the passage, and, above all, the steady industrious mutter of London itself—cart-wheels, cab-bells, hammering somewhere beyond the walls, like a bad drummer who has never learned to stop. It was not music. It was what the city took in place of birds.

Pen’s man informed him that Warrington was in chambers too; and Arthur, as naturally as if he were following the scent of dinner, ran straight to his friend’s room. He found it as of old—perfumed, not with any fashionable distillation, but with pipe-smoke: that thick honest incense which clings to curtains and books, and makes even yesterday’s newspaper seem seasoned. Warrington sat among reviews and journals as if they were cold dishes set round him; his pipe in his mouth; his face grave and a little saturnine; his blue beard already beginning to abrade the linen at his throat, as if even cloth could not keep its dignity near him long.

They greeted each other with the rough cordiality of Englishmen—no embraces, no shrieks of affection, no scented kisses distributed upon moustachios; only that brief exchange which means, in their dialect, everything.

“Well, young one!” said Warrington, taking the pipe from his lips.

“How are you, old boy?” said Pen, holding out his hand.

And thus two friends who would have lent each other their last shilling, or stood by each other in the teeth of any hazard, made acquaintance again after months with the economy of men who hoard warmth as if it were fuel and fear it will be wasted in talk.

“Been in Switzerland?” Pen asked.

“Yes,” said Warrington.

“And the Alps? the glaciers? the—”

“Couldn’t find a bit of tobacco fit to smoke till we came to Strasburg,” Warrington replied, gloomily satisfied to have named at least one summit he could praise—“where I got some caporal.”

Thus do certain Englishmen conceal their raptures as a cook might hide his best spice and pretend he has made the soup with nothing but water. Warrington’s enthusiasm, if it rose at all, rose under cover; it would come out later, perhaps, in some line he wrote at midnight, when nobody watched him feel.

He gave, in that shy sulky voice of his—half as if he were ashamed of having seen anything beautiful, half as if beauty had insulted him—a rapid itinerary: Switzerland, North Italy, the Tyrol; home by Vienna, Dresden, and the Rhine. The names fell like stones into a well.

Then Pen began upon his own adventures: he had been in the country; he had been bored; canvassing was uncommonly slow; he was in London only for a day or two; then on to the neighbourhood of Tunbridge Wells, to friends who would, he supposed, be uncommonly slow too. How hard it is to make an Englishman confess he has been happy, even when he has been eating the world’s best cherries out of season.

“And the seat in Parliament, Pen?” Warrington asked. “Have you made it all right?”

“All right—as soon as Parliament meets and a new writ can be issued. Clavering retires, and I step into his shoes.”

“And under which king does Bezonian speak or die?” Warrington said. “Do we come out as Liberal Conservative, or Government man, or on our own hook?”

Pen shrugged. “There are no politics now; every man’s politics are pretty much the same. I haven’t acres enough to make me a Protectionist; and I don’t think I could be one if I owned the whole county. I shall go pretty much with Government, and in advance of them on some social questions—don’t grin, you old cynic; I have been getting up Blue Books, and intend to come out rather strong on the Sanitary and Colonisation questions.”

Warrington’s mouth twitched. “We reserve the liberty of voting against Government though generally friendly. We are friends of the people avant tout. We give lectures at the Clavering Institute and shake hands with the intelligent mechanics. We enlarge the franchise considerably, and meanwhile remain at perfect liberty to accept office some day, when the House has heard a few crack speeches and the Administration perceives our merit.”

Pen’s smile faded into his usual touch of melancholy, as if some draught had cooled the room. “I’m not Moses. I have no laws from Heaven to bring down. I don’t belong to the mountain at all, or pretend to lead mankind. My faith isn’t strong enough—nor my vanity, nor my hypocrisy. I’ll tell no lies, George, that I promise you; and do no more than coincide in those which are necessary and pass current, and can’t be got in without recalling the whole circulation. If I find a good thing to say in the House, I’ll say it; a good measure, I’ll support it; a fair place, I’ll take it, and be glad of my luck. But I’d no more flatter a great man than a mob; and now you know as much of my politics as I do. What call have I to be a Whig? Whiggism isn’t a divine institution. Why not vote with the Liberal Conservatives? They’ve done for the nation what the Whigs would never have done without them. Who converted both?—the Radicals and the country outside. I think the Morning Post is often right, and Punch is often wrong. I don’t profess a call, but take advantage of a chance. Parlons d’autre chose.”

Warrington, who loved to prick a man’s vanity as a cook pricks a sausage—partly to stop it bursting, partly because it amuses him—let politics drop, and reached for what he thought was Pen’s next hottest dish.

“The next thing at your heart, after ambition, is love, I suppose? How have our young loves prospered? Are we going to change our condition and give up our chambers? Are you going to divorce me, Arthur, and take unto yourself a wife?”

“I suppose so,” Pen said. His voice had the careful steadiness of a man slicing thin. “She’s very good-natured and lively. She sings, and she don’t mind smoking. She’ll have a fair fortune—I don’t know how much—but my uncle augurs everything from the Begum’s generosity, and says she’ll come down handsomely. And I think Blanche is dev’lish fond of me.”

Warrington nodded, as if tasting and recognising the ingredient. “That means we accept her caresses and her money.”

“Haven’t we said before life was a transaction?” Pen answered. “I don’t pretend to break my heart about her. I’ve told her pretty fairly what my feelings are—and—and have engaged myself to her. And since I saw her last—these last two months especially, while I’ve been in the country—I think she’s been growing fonder and fonder of me. Her letters to me, and especially to Laura, show it. Mine have been simple enough—no raptures, no vows—looking on the thing as an affaire faite; not desirous to hasten or defer the completion.”

“And Laura?” Warrington asked, plainly.

Pen looked at him hard, as if he would cut through the smoke and see what lay beneath it. “Laura, George—by heaven, Laura is the best, and noblest, and dearest girl the sun ever shone upon.”

His own voice sank; the words came as if each cost him. He stretched out his hand. Warrington took it, and only nodded—an English benediction.

“Have you only found out that now, young un?” Warrington said, after a pause.

“Who has not learned things too late?” Pen burst out, heat rising in him as if some old kettle had begun to boil again. “Whose life is not a disappointment? Who carries his heart entire to the grave without a mutilation? I never knew anybody quite happy; or who hasn’t had to ransom himself out of Fate’s hands by paying some dearest treasure. Lucky if we are left alone after we’ve paid our fine, and the tyrant visits us no more. Suppose I’ve found out I’ve lost the greatest prize in the world now that it can’t be mine—that for years I had an angel under my tent and let her go—am I the only one?—ah, dear old boy, am I the only one? And do you think my lot is easier because I own I deserve it? She’s gone from us. God’s blessing be with her! She might have stayed, and I lost her. It’s like Undine: isn’t it, George?”

“She was in this room once,” Warrington said.

That was all; but the room filled at once with what was not there. The past came back with the simplest relics: a glass that had held a nosegay—empty now, yet still seeming to remember its perfume; a Bible with Helen’s handwriting. Warrington heard again, in his mind’s ear, that sweet low voice—so different from the Temple’s perpetual din, so unlike the city’s grinding accompaniment. It is a poor exchange we make: skylarks for street-cries; the hush of fields for the clank of commerce. And when a voice like hers is lost, the very noise of London seems louder, more insolent, as if modern life could not endure being reminded there are tones it cannot counterfeit or sell.

They sat silent a while, each aware of the other’s thoughts, as men are aware of a neighbour’s fire through a wall—feeling the heat, not naming it.

Pen rose first. He said he must go and seek his uncle and report progress. The Major had written in a very bad humour; the Major was getting old.

“I should like to see you in Parliament,” the old gentleman had scrawled, “and snugly settled with a comfortable house and an heir to the name before I make my bow. Show me these, and then let old Arthur Pendennis make room for younger fellows; he has walked the Pall Mall pave long enough.”

“There’s a kindness about the old heathen,” Warrington said. “He cares for somebody besides himself—at least for some other part of himself besides that which is buttoned into his own coat—for you and your race. He’d like to see the progeny of the Pendennises multiplying and increasing, and hopes they may inherit the land. The old patriarch blesses you from the Club window of Bays’s, and is carried off and buried under the flags of St. James’s Church, in sight of Piccadilly, the cabstand, and the carriages going to the levee. It is an edifying ending.”

“The new blood I bring into the family,” Pen mused, “is rather tainted. If I had chosen, I think my father-in-law Amory would not have been the progenitor I should have desired for my race; nor my grandfather-in-law Snell; nor our Oriental ancestors. By the way, who was Amory? Lieutenant of an Indiaman. Blanche wrote some verses about him—storm, mountain wave, seaman’s grave, gallant father, and that sort of thing. Amory was drowned commanding a country ship between Calcutta and Sydney. Amory and the Begum weren’t happy together. She’s been unlucky in her selection of husbands, the good old lady; for, between ourselves, a more despicable creature than Sir Francis Clavering of Clavering Park, Baronet, never—”

“Never legislated for his country,” Warrington broke in; at which Pen coloured, despite himself.

“By the way,” Warrington continued, as if turning over an afterthought like a slice of bread to see if it had browned, “at Baden I found our friend the Chevalier Strong in great state, wearing his orders. He told me he had quarrelled with Clavering—of whom he seemed to have almost as bad an opinion as you have; and in fact, though I won’t be certain, he pretty much confided to me that Clavering was an utter scoundrel. That fellow Bloundell—who taught you card-playing at Oxbridge—was with Strong; and time, I think, has brought out his valuable qualities, and rendered him a more accomplished rascal than he was during your undergraduateship. But the king of the place was the famous Colonel Altamont—carrying all before him, giving flies to the whole society, and breaking the bank, it was said.”

“My uncle knows something about that fellow—Clavering knows something about him,” Pen replied. “There’s something louche regarding him. But come! I must go to Bury Street, like a dutiful nephew.”

He took up his hat. Warrington rose too. “I will walk,” he said.

They went down together, and stopped at Pen’s rooms—which now were on the lower story. Here Arthur began to sprinkle himself with eau-de-Cologne and to scent his hair and whiskers with a minuteness usually reserved for chefs with truffles: dab, pat, smooth—an anxious little ritual, as if he meant to season himself before presenting to company.

“What is the matter?” Warrington growled. “You’ve not been smoking. Is it my pipe that has poisoned you?”

“I am going to call upon some women,” Pen said, still arranging his neckcloth as carefully as if he were folding napkins for a grand dinner. “I’m—I’m going to dine with ’em. They are passing through town, and are at an hotel in Jermyn Street.”

Warrington watched with good-natured interest as the young fellow completed his transformation into a polished dish for society’s table: gorgeous shirt-front and neckcloth, fresh gloves, boots that caught light as if they had been glazed like pastry. Warrington himself had thick high-lows; his old shirt was torn about the breast and ragged at the collar, where his beard had worried it like a file.

“Well, young un,” he said simply, “I like you to be a buck, somehow. When I walk about with you, it is as if I had a rose in my button-hole. And you are still affable. I don’t think there is any young fellow in the Temple turns out like you; and I don’t believe you were ever ashamed of walking with me yet.”

“Don’t laugh at me, George,” Pen said.

“I say, Pen,” Warrington added, and there was a sudden awkward tenderness in the way he handled the words, as if they were too hot, “if you write—if you write to Laura, I wish you would say ‘God bless her’ from me.”

Pen blushed; then looked at him; and then—his composure giving way with the comic relief of a man who has been holding in steam too long—burst into an uncontrollable fit of laughing.

“I’m going to dine with her,” he said. “I brought her and Lady Rockminster up from the country to-day—made two days of it—slept last night at Bath. I say, George, come and dine too. I may ask any one I please, and the old lady is constantly talking about you.”

Warrington refused. Warrington had an article to write. Warrington hesitated; and—strange to say—at last he agreed. Perhaps the thought of Laura’s voice, set against the Temple’s grim acoustics, was too tempting; perhaps he wanted to sit near that sound again, as a man in a city craves a mouthful of clean country air.

So it was agreed they should go and call upon the ladies; and they marched away in high spirits to the hotel in Jermyn Street, where London’s roar was softened by carpets and curtains into a respectable purr, like a great animal taught to behave indoors.

Once more the dear face shone upon Warrington; once more the sweet voice spoke; once more the tender hand pressed a welcome. There still wanted half an hour to dinner—half an hour in which hearts, like sauces, thicken of their own accord if you only keep them warm and do not stir them too much.

“You will go and see your uncle now, Mr. Pendennis,” old Lady Rockminster said, with the airy despotism of a woman who expects the men to obey and is amused when they do. “You will not bring him to dinner—no. His old stories are intolerable; and I want to talk to Mr. Warrington; I daresay he will amuse us. I think we have heard all your stories. We have been together for two whole days, and I think we are getting tired of each other.”

So, obedient to her ladyship’s command, Arthur went downstairs and walked to his uncle’s lodgings.
