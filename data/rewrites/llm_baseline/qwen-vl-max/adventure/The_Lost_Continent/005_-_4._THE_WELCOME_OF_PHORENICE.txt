Until the enemy fleet appeared in the gulf’s mouth, I had given little thought to my role as a recruit for the Empress. But their ambush—waiting in silence, then charging with such reckless fury that they seemed determined to drag me into their grasp—was no accident. It was a sign. Even the blind could see it: I was meant to play a vital part in Atlantis’s fate.

Yet if enemies watched our approach, so too did Phorenice. Scouts from her capital had spotted us from the mountain peaks and raced to deliver the news. The sea arm at the head of Atlantis varies wildly in width. Where mountains have erupted and cooled into stone, the channel narrows to barely a river’s breadth. Then, for half a day’s sail, it flares open like a lake, its shores barely visible. The path winds through marshes, smoking hills, and treacherous flats—where fire rivers or beasts might claim a man. Still, a swift runner who knew the land could carry word faster than any galley could sail.

Of course, such a journey carried danger. A lone courier might not survive. But Phorenice spared no effort. She sent twenty messengers to the headland overlooking the strait, each taking a different route. Seven reached the city unharmed; three others were known to have made it. The rest? We didn’t know. At the time, we believed our arrival was entirely unexpected.

The slaves rowing our galley were mostly savages from Europe—rough men whose stench turned every breath sour. And the wind carried grit from a distant volcano, filling the air with dust and ash. We wanted nothing more than to reach land quickly.

And yet, despite my priestly training in stoicism—despite knowing that all things are in the hands of the gods—I couldn’t help but wonder what awaited me in Atlantis. Would I be taken prisoner upon landing? Tried for healing the Yucatan colony? Or would I slip ashore unnoticed, only to be summoned later by the Empress?

I had no idea how I’d be received.

We sighted the sacred mountain at dawn, its eternal fires glowing behind the city. The boatswains cracked their whips harder, urging the rowers to push on. No sail could be set—the wind was against us—but under oars alone, we gained speed. The mountain loomed larger. Soon, the pyramids and towers of Atlantis rose above the water like visions floating in the light.

It had been twenty years since I last walked those streets. My heart swelled with memory. I saw the temple where I first became a man. The pyramid where I was initiated into the mysteries. Then, as we drew closer, I caught sight of the house where I was raised. My eyes blurred with emotion.

We anchored outside the harbor walls, as law required. The slaves fell silent, panting over their oar-looms. Usually, ships wait hours for port officials to deign to notice them. But this time, there was no delay. A signal rang out from the twin castles at the entrance. The chain between them dropped. A ten-oared boat shot forward from behind the wall, racing to meet us.

“Is that Dason’s galley?” the port captain called.

“It was,” said Tob.

“I saw Dason’s emblem on your prow,” he replied.

“You were Tatho’s captain?”

“And still am. Tatho’s fleet was destroyed by Dason and his allies. This galley was all that remained.”

The captain scanned our group. “I fear you’ll face a dangerous reception. I don’t see Lord Deucalion. Has he arrived with another force? Gods, if he died under your command, the Empress will flay you alive.”

Tob smirked. “With Phorenice and Tatho both so eager for his safety, my lord seems a risky passenger. But I’ll save my skin this time.” He jerked his thumb toward me. “He’s here. He’ll speak for me.”

The port captain stared, then bowed low. “I beg pardon, my lord, for not recognizing your nobleness. I expected… more regal attire.”

“Pish,” I said. “If I choose to dress simply, I won’t complain if I’m mistaken for a commoner. I do not wear finery to advertise my rank. If you feel you owe me compensation, let this inquiry end.”

He bowed again, nearly touching the deck. “I am your humblest servant. It is my honor to guide your galley to its berth.”

Our boat led the way. Tob watched me with a dry grin. “You’ll remember me only as a rough fellow when you step into this world of fine manners,” he said.

“I don’t think so,” I replied.

“Then I’ll prove myself refined,” he said, gripping my hand in his massive fist. “By the gods, Deucalion, you may be a prince, but I’ve known you as a man. You’re the greatest fighter alive—beast or man—and I love you for it. That spear strike of yours on the lizard? They’ll sing about it in taverns for generations.”

We entered the harbor swiftly. Soldiers atop the gate castles blew trumpets in greeting. The port captain hoisted my banner—a symbol of recognition—on his boat. From the quays, music drifted across the water, mingling with the glow of welcoming fires dancing in the sunlight.

I felt humbled. An Empress—Phorenice herself—had arranged this welcome for a mere subject returning home.

The port captain’s boat led us. We followed, our galley brought alongside the royal quay and secured with golden rings—symbols sacred to the throne.

“If Dason could see this honor,” Tob muttered, “he’d have ordered silk ropes instead of hemp. I’m sure he’d frown now—if the sun hadn’t already baked his face stiff.” He looked at me. “My lord, shall you tread carefully over this ship, or walk straight onto the carpet they’ve laid for you?”

The port captain heard and turned pale. I sighed softly. Colonial freedom had no place here. I would have to relearn the rules of ceremony.

Still, I would not surrender myself entirely. When I stepped into the crimson pavilion erected for me, I accepted the shelter—but no further. Three chamberlains, dressed in dazzling robes, offered me clothes—garments covered in jewels, silks, and frills. They insisted I change.

I refused.

One stammered, “But the Empress prefers…”

“Does she?” I asked sharply. “Have you received an order from her on this matter?”

He hesitated. Then admitted: no.

“Then,” I said, “Phorenice commanded Deucalion the man to appear before her. She said nothing about his clothing. This outfit suits me. It keeps me warm, shields me from wind and sun, and is clean. Your interference borders on insult.”

They drew swords—three of them—and thrust the hilts toward me.

“It would be a kindness,” their leader said, “if you took your revenge now, rather than leave us to torture.”

“Poof,” I said. “Forget it. You make too much of nothing.”

But I understood. They meant it. In this new Atlantis, even a disagreement over clothing could bring torture. The world had changed.

There was a bath in the pavilion. I washed gladly—though some foul scent spoiled the water. Then I waited, calm and composed.

The chamberlains stood silently. I did not speak. We sat in silence beneath the blazing sun, while music blared and fires filled the square beyond.

I’ve always thought it petty for the powerful to make others wait. And Phorenice agreed. As soon as Tob’s galley touched the quay, she left the palace.

The procession was ready—magnificent, bedecked, awaiting only her. She mounted her steed, and the trumpets sounded. The march began.

From the great street, soldiers emerged. They marched across the square, saluted me, then lined up on the far side. Then came the Mariners’ Guild, then merchants, tanners, spear-makers—each guild parading in turn, dressed as if for a festival. Some rode beasts they had tamed into service.

Then came the wonders.

First, a colossal bull mammoth stepped into view. It was enormous—even among its kind. Yet it moved with perfect calm, bearing on its back a golden half-castle adorned with silver snakes and the symbol of the outstretched hand. Its tusks were gilded, its neck garlanded with flowers. It walked like a noble beast, born for ceremony.

And then—Phorenice.

She sat upon the mammoth’s back, high above the crowd. The beast drew my eye at first—its size, its power. But then I saw her.

I rose.

She was slight, supple, perfectly formed. Her hair was short, red-gold, falling in waves over her shoulders. Her face—sharp, intelligent, beautiful beyond words. And her eyes… I could not read them. Not one thought escaped them.

She studied me just as intently. I returned her gaze.

When the people shouted, I stood firm. I did not kneel. One chamberlain whispered, “On your knees, my lord!”

“Bent head at least,” urged another.

But I walked across the square, head high, meeting her stare.

She nodded slightly. When I bowed as befit my rank, she spoke—loud enough for all to hear.

“Never again bow your forehead to the ground for me. For others, it is fitting. But you—Deucalion—are above all men in Atlantis. If anyone refuses to obey you, they are my enemy too. They shall feel my wrath.”

A stair was brought. She gestured. I climbed beside her in the golden half-castle, under the canopy of royal snakes. A girl fanned us with perfumed feathers. At her command, the mammoth turned, carrying us back toward the royal pyramid.

Behind us, the procession moved—soldiers, traders, guards marching on either flank.

She smiled at me. “You piqued me at first.”

“I am overwhelmed by your attention, Majesty.”

“You looked at my steed before me. A woman finds that hard to forgive.”

“I envied you the greatest of conquests,” I said. “I’ve fought mammoths. Killed them. But never dared dream of taming one.”

“You speak boldly,” she said, smiling. “And yet with charm. Faugh! These people fawn on me. It makes me sick. I am not like them. Just because I am a goddess’s daughter, they feed me lies.”

So Tatho was right. The swineherd was forgotten. Well, if she chose to pretend, I wouldn’t challenge it. I was her servant—whether rightly or wrongly.

“I’ve longed for something stronger than this,” she continued. “And so I summoned you. I’ve seen your images—drawn by tongues. I knew you before I met you. I admire a man who rejects foolish clothes, feasts, and women’s flattery.”

She glanced down at her own silks and jewels. “We women like color. But it’s different. So I called you—not for pleasure, but to rule with me.”

“There are better men in Atlantis,” I said.

“There are not,” she replied. “They adore me. They tire me with empty words. Their loyalty is only for themselves. So I chose you—the only strong man in the world. Will you not sigh to be my lover?”

I saw her watching me from the corner of her eye.

“The Empress is my mistress,” I said. “I will serve her honestly. As for Phorenice the woman… I expect little involvement. And I am not one who plays with love.”

“And yet,” she mused, “you are quite attractive.”

“That proves my strength,” I said. “I won’t lose my mind to infatuation.”

She turned to her attendant. “Ylga, stop fanning so hard.”

We passed through the main street—the most magnificent city the world had ever known. I had left it two decades ago. Now I saw its growth: new temples, pyramids, palaces, statues everywhere. Its splendor was greater than I remembered. Imperial. Unmatched.

Yet beneath it all—poverty. Squalor. Gaunt faces, naked bodies, hungry eyes. Traders in flashy clothes wore frightened expressions. They hid their fear behind trinkets.

Phorenice saw where I looked.

“The season has been unhealthy,” she said. “These people refuse to build fine homes. They live in filthy hovels. Fever has spread. Work is hard. Trade has nearly stopped—since rebels have been attacking the gates.”

I blinked. “Rebels? At the gates of Atlantis? Is the city under siege?”

“They’ve granted us a holiday today,” she said lightly. “So your welcome comes undisturbed. If they were fighting, you’d hear it. They’re noisy, these rebels. My spies say they’re building engines to breach the walls. You may go out tomorrow and destroy them, if it amuses you.”

“For today,” she said, “you have me. And I have you. Peace surrounds us. Beauty is displayed. If you want more—I will give it.”

“I didn’t know of this rebellion,” I said. “But now that I am your minister, I must know its full scope. This is serious.”

“And do you think I cannot take it seriously?” she retorted.

She turned to Ylga. “Loosen my dress at the shoulder.”

The girl unclasped the jeweled fastening—reluctantly, it seemed. Phorenice pulled the fabric aside, revealing her bare skin. Just below her left breast, a bandage stained with blood.

“There,” she said, looking at me sideways. “Proof of my seriousness. The arrow struck a rib. It saved me. Had it hit between, you’d be standing beside my funeral pyre now—instead of riding this beast you admire so much.”

Her gaze held mine.

“You seem to look at the mammoth more than me,” she said softly. “Ah, poor me. I am not one of your shaggy creatures. So I suppose I’ll never win your regard.”

She turned to Ylga. “Fasten it back.”

I said nothing. I had seen wounds before. There was nothing else to see.

But the truth was clear: this was not a woman who played games. She was strong. Dangerous. And she had chosen me—not for love, but for power.

And so, beneath the golden sky, riding a tame giant, I became the minister of Atlantis.
