The three or four "To Let" boards had leaned propped against the low paling for so long no living resident of the tiny triangular square could remember a time they stood straight. Hunched at their own uneven, lopsided angles, they looked less like signage than a row of weathered wooden sentinels, perpetually on the verge of toppling down onto any passerby slow enough to linger at the gate—yet for years, not one of them had ever snared a tenant for the old, brooding house tucked away behind them. There was hardly any foot traffic to snare, in any case: the busy main thoroughfare ran a full furlong off, cut off from the square by a maze of warren-like tenements, blind alleys, and forgotten byways that had sprung up long after the old red-brick house was erected, hemming it in completely. It was common local gossip the old place was only spared demolition while a handful of long-held leases ran out, and the whole block would be cleared for new slum housing the second those papers lapsed.

The house’s brickwork bloomed dark with centuries of damp, half-buried in its facades the carved crowns, clasped hands, and forgotten insignia of insurance companies that had collapsed and vanished so long ago no one in the district could name them now. Local children had swung from the low wooden gate at the end of the entrance alley until all that remained was a single thick iron top bar; boarded basement windows lining the narrow path were scrawled over with tramps’ cryptic, looping chalk marks that never quite looked like ordinary hobo code, curving into faint, unrecognizable faces if you squinted at them long enough. Water spilling continuously from the gutter of the encroaching next-door house had worn the stone path into uneven, slimy ruts, and stray cats and dogs had long claimed the unlit alley as their own, leaving scraps of half-eaten carrion and paw prints caked in mud across every flat surface. The agents had never bothered to keep the To Let boards repainted or legible, and no one could blame them—there seemed no chance at all anyone would ever agree to move in.

For six months, the struggling writer Oleron had passed the old house twice a day, on his short walk between his overpriced lodgings and the separate workroom he rented an easy ten minutes away. All that time he had kept strictly to the far side of the square, never once brushing close enough to see the peeling paint on the lurching notice boards. Then one gray, rain-damp morning, he took the near side by accident, and found himself pausing right in front of one of the tilted boards. Beneath the agent’s smudged name, the faded lettering—scrawled in ink that had yellowed to the color of an old, healing bruise, seemingly around the time Oleron himself was a small boy—stated the key to the house could be collected at Number Six.

Oleron was already paying far more than a writer with no private income, who all but ignored his dwindling public, could afford for two separate spaces: a bedroom at his lodgings, and the workroom half a mile away. He paid extra, too, to store most of his grandmother’s heavy old furniture in a warehouse across town. Worse, he was constantly plagued by small, infuriating inconveniences: the book he wanted to read in bed was always at his workroom, the urgent note he needed mid-day was always tucked in the pocket of a coat hanging behind his bedroom door. The split domicile was a constant, quiet thorn in his side, and it had never occurred to him to fix it until that very second.

He leaned over the low privet hedge, glancing first at the chalk-marked boarded basement windows, then up at the smudged, grimy first-floor panes, then higher still to the flat stone leads on the roof, pale and blank as an upturned face staring up at the overcast sky. He stood for a minute, thumbing his lean, clean-shaven jaw, then darted one last look at the notice board and walked quickly across the square to Number Six.

He knocked, waited two or three minutes. The front door stood ajar, but no one answered. He raised his hand to knock a second time when a long-nosed man in rolled shirt sleeves appeared, his mouth still half-full of food. “I was saying a blessing over our dinner,” he snapped, severe and affronted that Oleron had interrupted his grace. Oleron asked for the key to the old empty house, and the man vanished back inside. Five more minutes passed before he reappeared, still masticating, and announced flatly the key had been lost years ago. “But you won’t need it,” he added. “The front door’s never shut, and a hard push’ll pop every other one. I’m Barrett, the agent for the place, if you’re thinking of taking it.”

Oleron walked back across the square, stepped down the two broken steps at the splintered gate, and followed the damp alley to the old wide front doorway. When he pushed it open, the house breathed out at him: a thick, cold smell of damp rot, mildew, and the faint, sickly sweet tang of something long left to fester under a floorboard. To his right, dark steps led down to gaping, cavernous cellars, and the broad, handsome main staircase stretched before him, its carved handrail crusted with decades of black grime. Oleron climbed carefully, avoiding all contact with the rail and the grimy walls, and stopped on the first landing. The door facing him was boarded over top to bottom, but he pushed at the door to his right, and a rusted old staple gave with a high, squealing crack that echoed through the empty rooms. He stepped across the threshold into the empty first-floor flat.

He spent a full quarter hour wandering the bare, sun-dappled rooms, then stepped back out and crossed the square again to Barrett’s house. “How much is the rent?” he asked. The sum Barrett named was absurdly, insultingly low, the price easily explained by the neglected neighborhood and the house’s total state of disrepair. When Oleron asked if he could rent only the single first floor, Barrett hesitated, said he didn’t know, it might be possible. The lawyers who owned the whole property were a firm in Lincoln’s Inn, he added, and Oleron ought to mention his name when he called on them.

Oleron was swamped with work that afternoon, so he made the trip to Lincoln’s Inn the next day. The lawyers tried immediately to push him into buying the entire house for fifty pounds down, with the rest of the purchase price left on mortgage. It took him half an hour to dissuade them, to make them understand he had no interest in owning the whole rotting building, he only wanted to rent the one first floor flat. The lawyers hemmed and hawed, claimed at first they had no authority to make that kind of arrangement, before finally caving—on three conditions: the lurching old To Let boards had to stay in place by the paling, no removal permitted; the second they found a tenant for the full house, Oleron’s lease would terminate automatically with no notice required, no questions asked. Oleron laughed off the risk of the whole ever house letting out, promised them his final decision within a week.

The next day he visited the house again, wandering all the way to the top floor, the floorboards creaking under his feet as if something inside the walls was shuffling silently out of his path. He went home giddy, ran a bath and daydreamed nonstop about the space. Scrubbed clean, repainted, filled with his grandmother’s old furniture, it would be perfect. He stopped by the storage warehouse to refresh his memory of the half-forgotten pieces, take measurements, then called on a local decorator. He was busy with a half-finished novel, frustrated that he’d stumbled on the house at such a busy time of year, but decided the fastest solution was to put all writing work on pause until he was fully moved in.

A fortnight later, every wall on his first floor was painted a soft, tender elderflower white, the paint fully cured. Oleron was halfway through moving in, light with giddy delight, rubbing his hands as he polished and arranged his grandmother’s belongings: the tall lattice-paned china cabinet holding his grandmother’s Derby, Mason, and Spode ware, the big folding Sheraton table, the low long bookshelves (he’d ordered two more exact copies to match), the carved dining chairs, the polished Sheffield candlesticks, the dented riveted rose-bowls. All set against the pale white panelled walls, moulded and coffered around the deep window recesses with a soft, quiet warmth no modern builder could ever replicate. The ceilings were lofty, faintly patterned with a faded old design of stars; even the tapered mouldings of his cast iron fireplace were carved as delicately as fine jewellery. Oleron drifted from room to room rubbing his hands, stopping every few steps just to savor the soft, glowing sight of one bright white room opening onto the next.

“Charming, absolutely charming,” he murmured to himself, grinning. “I can’t wait to show Elsie Bengough all this.” He fitted a heavy new bolt and a Yale lock to his entry door, sealing his first-floor quarters off completely from the rest of the empty, rotting house. Now, if he wanted to read in bed, his book was only a few steps away, no half mile walk needed. He thought over and over how absurdly lucky he was, to stumble on this perfect little flat for next to nothing. He hung a heavy hat-rack in his tiny square entry hall, strung up all his coats and hats, and late at night, passersby cutting through the quiet triangular square would glance up past the hunched, waiting line of wooden To Let boards, and see the warm glow of his red blinds glowing against the dark old brick, or the shadow of his candlestick moving as he drifted from room to room, still arranging his things, getting ready to go back to the writing work his move had interrupted. None of them could see the second, fainter shadow that stretched out behind his own, moving slow along the white panelling every time the candle flame dipped and flickered.

As far as the chief business of his life—his writing—was concerned, Paul Oleron had always treated the world far better than it treated him. At forty-four, he rarely bothered to tally the losses: the half-finished manuscripts left to gather dust, the dismissive rejection slips stacked higher than his published work, the decades he’d sunk into exigent, noble ideals that no one else had ever cared enough to reward. Totting up the deficit would do no good anyway, he told himself. He was too far down his chosen path to turn back now. Only lately, a faint, unspoken fear had begun to niggle at the edge of his mind: what if, when he finally hit the peak of his powers and started to decline, he looked back and realised he’d wasted every single day holding himself to a standard no one else had ever valued?

The old house, the one with the faded fire-insurance marks pressed deep into its weathered brick, had interrupted his work on *Romilly Bishop* right at the fifteenth chapter. Tall, lean, his face gaunt and ascetic from decades of sparse meals and long, unbroken hours at his desk, he moved through his new rooms arranging his things with the fastidious, almost spinsterish precision of a man who’d spent twenty years drifting between a hundred different garrets, lodgings, and half-furnished flats. Method, he’d learned, saved you from losing your temper over a teacup that vanished or a page of manuscript that slipped down behind a skirting board. He’d struck a deal with Barrett’s wife, the stout Welsh woman with a falsetto Merionethshire lilt that even decades in London hadn’t worn down, to pop over from the other side of the square each morning to make his breakfast, and give the place a full turn-out every Saturday. For the rest, he welcomed the small, mindless chores: they were a soft reprieve from the burning strain of dragging words out of his head one by one.

His kitchen, and the tiny adjacent room fitted with a rickety old modern bath, looked out over the narrow alley running down the side of the house. At the far end of the kitchen, tucked away, was a heavy closet door, with a square sliding hatch cut high into its upper half. It took him a minute to place it: a powder closet, leftover from the days when men would lean their elaborate wigs through that hatch, and a servant would puff talcum powder through the little opening to dust the curls clean. A faint, unnameable prickle ran down his spine as he stared at it. All those necks, all those faces, pushed through that little gap, unseeing, unguarded. He shook the feeling off with a thin smile. It would make a perfect larder, that was all.

It was rummaging on the deep upper shelf of that very closet that he found them: two old, mushroom-shaped wooden wig stands, crusted with decades of dust. He couldn’t imagine how they’d ended up there, left behind by the decorators no doubt. The five rooms were shockingly short of cupboard space, and he spent the next hour wrangling his linens, his old boxes, his stacks of tattered, irreplaceable manuscript drafts into every nook he could find.

He’d moved in at the very start of spring, and he was desperate to finish *Romilly Bishop* in time for an autumn release. This book was make or break, he knew—his big chance, the one that would finally prove all those years of stubborn, noble poverty hadn’t been wasted. He wouldn’t force it, though. The book had its own life, it needed its own time. Back in his old workroom it had been flowing freely, the characters speaking for themselves, and he was sure once the disruption of moving settled, the words would come just as easily here.

He told himself the distraction was almost over, so on a crisp March morning he stepped out, came back clutching two great bunches of golden daffodils, set one on his mantelpiece between the polished Sheffield fire irons, the other right on the table in front of his desk, and dragged out the half-finished *Romilly* manuscript. But before he could put pen to paper, he dug out his chequebook and passbook from the little rosewood cabinet in the corner, tallied the numbers, and his ascetic face fell quiet. The move had cost him far more than he’d budgeted for. He had less than fifty pounds left in his account, and no cheques due to come in for months.

“Damn rugs and chintz curtains,” he muttered to himself. “Spent too much on the place, but I couldn’t bear to make it look shabby. Well, *Romilly* has to be done by autumn. No way around it.”

He pulled the manuscript closer. But the words wouldn’t come. The square outside hummed with new, unplaceable noises his ears hadn’t learned to filter yet: the raucous cries of hawkers pushing their carts past the gate, the tramping of schoolchildren who swung back and forth on his iron gate hinges at midday, and after they trundled back to class, a street musician with a mandolin who posted himself right under his window and strummed a wobbly, meandering tune. He tossed the man a penny out the window, then sat back down, but his gaze drifted. He found himself staring at the bare walls, wondering what the room had looked like a hundred and fifty years before. Had a settee upholstered in buttercup yellow satin stood under the far window? Had a crystal chandelier glimmered from the centre of the high ceiling? Where had the ladies of the house set their tambour frames, their picquet tables for evening card games? No work was getting done here. He decided he’d go for a walk, then instead dropped into his chair and dozed off, waking at half past four to find the light had drained almost entirely out of the room.

“Useless,” he yawned, stretching, his limbs heavy with a strange, cloying laziness that made him half want to skip the evening meeting he’d arranged with his editor.

The next morning he sat down determined, ignoring three letters piled by his door—two tradesmen’s bills, one a note from Miss Bengough forwarded from his old lodgings. The day was bright, white cloud and blue sky, a sharp giddy wind whipping around the square, and the light kept washing over the room in soft, shifting waves, turning the polished tabletop silver, gilding the scuff marks on the ancient floor. He doodled little dot patterns on his blank sheet of paper, then stood up to pace, struck by a thought. The whole place was too pale, he realised. Too soft, too wan, like an old woman’s face that had no blood left under the skin. It needed more colour, more life. He grabbed his two-foot rule to measure the window recesses for new cushion fabric, and when he stooped to peer under the lip of the sill, his breath caught.

Beneath the layers of peeling paint, he could make out little rusted nail heads. A puttyed-up keyhole glinted faintly under the paint, too.

“Oho,” he murmured, rubbing his hands together, a little giddy. “Nailed-up window boxes. This is a surprise.”

There were three windows total on that wall: two in his sitting room, one peeking through the open bedroom door. Every single windowsill had been painted over so many times the original wood was completely hidden. He pulled out his penknife, picked at the putty for five minutes, then trundled to the kitchen to fetch a hammer and chisel.

The first tap of the chisel under the sill lid made the whole house ring. The panelling hummed like the sounding board of a piano, echoes bouncing up from the deep cellars all the way to the garrets above, a whole swarm of small, skittering noises that he told himself were just loose plaster shifting. The sound made his skin crawl. He grabbed a duster and wrapped it around the head of his mallet, muffling the blows, until the edge of the lid lifted high enough for him to get his fingers under. Paint flaked off in sharp shards, the old nails squealed like something in pain, and he heaved the lid open.

Beneath, there was nothing but two inches of black mould, dust, and cobwebs strung thick as gauze. No hidden letters, no old keepsakes, no treasure. He laughed, a little sheepish. “Well, *Romilly* still has to carry us through, then. Let’s check the others.”

He pried open the second sitting room window box, then the one in the bedroom, working right through the afternoon. The bedroom one was just as empty as the first, but when he lifted the second sitting room sill lid, his fingers closed around something soft, thick, dust-caked. He carried it out to the kitchen, brushed the thick coat of grime off over a bucket, and unfolded it. It was a huge, irregular triangle of ancient frieze-like cloth, furred with dust, the folded top faded to a sickly yellowish brown, the rest dyed a deep, rich crimson that glowed faintly where the sun hit it. It had wide flaps, the rotting remains of leather straps and brass buckles hanging off the edges. He couldn’t for the life of him work out what it had ever been used for.

“Whatever this is, it’s killed any chance of work today,” he said, and carelessly stuffed it into the far corner of the kitchen. He spent the rest of the afternoon scrubbing the window boxes clean, lining them with paper, stowing his spare boots, his old books, his loose manuscript pages inside, shutting the lids back down. He was amused by the silly little mystery, but under it all, a faint, unplaceable tension hummed in his bones. He was eager for the day he finally settled down to work, and stopped finding strange, forgotten things tucked away in the walls of his new, old house.

It niggled at Oleron, sharp as a splinter under the skin, that his old friend Elsie Bengough should dismiss the house he’d come to think of as his private, secret miracle with nothing more than a flick of her fair lashes. She barely lifted her eyes to the high carved cornices, to the way afternoon light pooled thick as cream on the floorboards where no one else had trod for decades. But that was Elsie all over: indifferent to quiet graces, careless of fripperies, far more at home gnawing biscuits straight from a crinkly paper bag than sitting through stuffy dinner parties where everyone pretended to care about social niceties. At thirty-four, she was an unapologetically freelance journalist, broad-shouldered and blooming, butter-gold hair, cheeks flushed the soft pink of a wild dog-rose, like a prize bloom plump straight from a florist’s display. She moved in sweeping, unselfconscious gestures, her voice booming warm and sudden, trailing veils and scarves that left little whirlwinds of displaced air spinning in her wake. She “hauled a better living out of the print pool” as she liked to say, than Oleron ever had, penning sly glowing little mentions of drapers and haberdashers in the society pages in exchange for nearly the entirety of her vast, ever-changing wardrobe.

Oleron heard the rustle of her skirts on his creaking staircase, the single loud unapologetic knock at his door, exactly one month to the day after he’d moved in. She brought the sharp cold tang of the city’s autumn streets in with her, dumping a stack of glossy ladies’ journals unceremoniously on his best chair.
“Don’t stop work for me,” she mumbled around a mouthful of giant hatpins, working them loose one by one to yank off her heavy fur hat and trailing veil. “I had no idea if you were even settled in yet, so I brought ham sandwiches for lunch. You’ve got coffee, right? No, don’t you dare get up – I know where kitchens are, I’ll rummage around and find the kettle myself.”
“Honestly, I’m glad for the interruption,” Oleron told her, half-relieved, gathering up the half-scrawled, half-blank pages of his manuscript scattered across his desk to tuck them out of sight. He’d been staring at the same half-written line for the better part of three hours, words slipping through his fingers like water every time he tried to pin them down.

She was already in his tiny kitchen; he could hear her twisting the tap on, water gurgling into the dented old kettle. He joined her, and ten minutes later they were back in the sitting room, coffee and sandwiches balanced on a rickety little table between them.
“So,” Elsie said, pouring him a cup that steamed thickly, “what do you think of this new hole of yours? Anybody’d swear you were about to get hitched, Paul, the way you’ve been gushing about it.”
He huffed a laugh. “Hardly. But it’s a sight better than the last few dumps I lived in, isn’t it?”
“Is it? I guess. I liked the last one, even with the soot-black ceiling and no running tap. How’s *Romilly* coming along?”
Oleron rubbed at the stubble on his chin, the empty leaden weight in his chest he’d been pretending wasn’t there for weeks. “Ah. I’m almost too embarrassed to tell you. I haven’t exactly made great headway. But it’ll all sort itself out, like you always say the night before a deadline.”
“Stuck?”
“More than stuck.”
“Got any bits you’d let me read?”

For years, he’d made a habit of reading snippets of his work out loud to Elsie. Her feedback was sharp, unvarnished, practical, sometimes directly useful, sometimes sparking ideas he’d never have landed on alone. She never let him see a line of her own work, though – said his was “real, proper writing,” while hers was just filler, barely even grammatically correct half the time.
“I don’t have anything new to show you,” he admitted, still stroking his chin, then blurted the truth before he could stop himself. “Elsie, I haven’t written a single new line of it, not for weeks. But – it’s not like I haven’t been thinking. I’ve been thinking so hard it’s almost alarming. I’m planning to rebuild the whole story from the ground up.”
Elsie gasped so loud her coffee sloshed over the edge of the cup. “Rebuild it?!”
“Make Romilly a different kind of woman entirely. I swear, I’m not pulling every last bit of potential out of her the way she is now. I’ve lost almost all interest in the version of her I had written already.”
“But Paul,” she protested, round-eyed, “she was so real you could almost touch her! You made her breathe.”
Oleron smiled faintly. He’d fully expected her to hate the idea. Of course she’d love the old Romilly – half the fictional woman was based on Elsie herself, whether she’d ever noticed it or not. Of course she’d think that version was alive.
“Are you actually serious about this?” she asked, after a long pause.
“Dead serious.”
“You’re going to scrap those fifteen whole chapters? That lush gorgeous love scene? That bit where she’s standing on the shore, the wind tangling her hair, you wrote it so well I could taste the salt?”
Oleron shifted in his seat, suddenly uneasy, the faint cloying rose-mold scent in the air thickening all at once. “They wouldn’t have to go to waste, exactly.”
Elsie threw up her hands in that big dramatic gusty gesture of hers, and let loose. “You are impossibly infuriating sometimes! I wish you’d remember you’re just a human man who lives in the real world! I’d never ask you to drop your standards an inch, but this isn’t raising them – it’s making the book so obtuse no normal person will ever care to parse it. You’re being far too much of a self-absorbed god about this. Destroying those fifteen chapters would be a criminal waste of the talent you’ve spent twenty years nurturing. You’re almost to the finish line, Paul. I don’t need you to tell me your bank account’s running dry, I can read the papers the same as anyone else. This book was supposed to make your name, and now you’re throwing it away to write something ten to one no one will ever want to publish? You’re testing every last scrap of patience I have.”

Oleron shook his head slowly. This was an old familiar fight between them. The loud brilliant practical journalist was a wonderful friend, up to a very specific point – past that point, every last one of us is entirely alone. Elsie liked to say if she had a tenth of Oleron’s talent she could conquer the entire industry, like talent was some kind of measurable powder you could spoon out into recipes. She never understood that it wasn’t something you could split, or add, or subtract – it was something that lived in your bones, indivisible, and no amount of sharp practicality could manufacture. Their two spirits diverged right there, and Oleron knew it. She never seemed to.
“Alright, alright,” he said, a little tired. “You’re completely right, every word. If I could hand *Romilly* over to you, you’d turn it into a massive bestseller. But I can’t. And for my part? I’m starting to seriously wonder if she’s even worth my time at all. You know what that means.”
“What does it mean?” she demanded bluntly.
He smiled, thin and weary. “When you’re certain a thing isn’t worth doing? You stop doing it.”
Elsie rolled her eyes up at the water-stained ceiling, as if begging it for patience with this impossible man. “That’s utter garbage! The last time I saw you, you were practically dripping *Romilly*. You were cranking out four chapters a week. If you’d never moved into this weird old house you’d be three quarters done by now. What possessed you to pick up and relocate right in the middle of the most important work of your life?”

Oleron tried to brush her off, making jokes about the drafty old lodgings he’d lived in before, but she wouldn’t buy it. He was twenty years deep in garrets, and roof-chambers, and dingy little flats, and he was bone tired of the shabbiness. The reward he’d chased for decades was as far out of reach as it ever was – and lately, he didn’t even have the urge to reach for it. Telling a man at the end of his rope to just make one more effort does nothing if he no longer has the strength to move.
“Anyway,” he said, wrapping the argument up, “I’m happier here than I’ve been in ten years. That’s justification enough, isn’t it?”
“And you’re doing no work,” she said, sharp as a tack.
That was the last straw, and the petty irritation he’d been holding back spilled over. “Why shouldn’t I get to be happy instead of working every waking minute? What has all this work ever gotten me? I don’t claim I don’t love it, once it’s done – but the act of writing it? It’s a burden, an intolerable weight I want to throw off half the time. Once every few weeks I get that one spark, that thrill of creation, and I remember when the whole of it used to feel that way. I’m forty-four now. It’s drudgery. No one buys it, I don’t even want to write it anymore, and if any normal sensible man asked me if I wasn’t a fool to keep going, I’d agree with him.”

Elsie’s pink blooming face went soft and serious. “But you knew all that decades ago, Paul. You chose this life.”
“Did I, though?” he snapped back. “I was told I knew. My heart, if you want to call it that, told me, and youth always thinks it knows everything. Then one day you wake up and you’re almost fifty –”
“Forty-four, Paul.”
“Forty-four, then. And you find out all the magic you thought was waiting for you up ahead was all behind you. I thought I knew what I was choosing, but no one tells you how expensive that choice is, when you’re young and stupid.”
Elsie stared down at the scuffed floorboards, her voice quiet. “You’re not regretting it, are you?”
“Am I not?” he said, and the words were out before he could think. “Lately? I absolutely think I am. What have I ever gotten in return for all this sacrifice?”
“You know exactly what you’ve gotten,” she said, soft, and suddenly he realized he could hear what she wasn’t saying. That if he’d asked her to marry him any time in the last ten years, she would have said yes, no questions, no hesitation. He’d never once thought to ask. “Your work is real. People like me, the hacks, we couldn’t do our jobs without people like you. You and a handful of others carry all the real art on your shoulders.”

They sat in silence for a long minute, and Oleron’s face heated up. He was never the type to mope and grumble like this. He stood up abruptly, stacking the coffee cups back onto the tray.
“Sorry you had to catch me in such a sorry mood, Elsie,” he laughed, a little shaky. “I’ll carry these out to the kitchen, then we can go for a walk, if you like, get some fresh air.”
He dumped the tray in the kitchen sink, then led her on a tour of the rest of the empty flat. She barely commented on any of it, until they got back to the small back bedroom where the window seat sat, its old cushion still propped against the wall. She pointed at the faded reddish scrap of frieze Oleron had found tucked under the seat the first week he moved in, that Mrs. Barrett used as a little footrest for her old wooden chair.
“That thing? I’ve been trying to figure out what it even is,” Oleron told her, unfolding it for her to see, recounting how he’d dug it out from under the splintered old wood of the window seat.
Elsie’s face went pale, suddenly. “I know what that is. It’s wrapping cloth, for a small harp. People used to sew frieze linings to wrap their harps in before tucking them away in their cases to keep the wood from scratching.”
“By God, that makes sense,” Oleron said, a little taken aback. He’d never thought of that. The faint distant harp note he swore he heard drifting through the empty halls after dark made a horrible new kind of sense all at once.

They wandered back to the sitting room, and Elsie leaned back against the mantel. “Who else lives in the rest of the big empty house, then?”
“Hardly anyone. A tramp sometimes crashes in the cellar, I think. No one else.”
“Mm. Let me tell you what I think of this place, Paul.”
“Go on.”
“You will never finish *Romilly* here. You will never write a single good thing in this house. I can’t explain why, but I know it. You’re going to have to leave, before you ever get that book done.”
He blinked, thrown off. “That’s a little prejudiced, isn’t it? There’s no logic to that at all.”
“Not a single leg to stand on, logically,” she agreed, already shoving the big hatpins back in her hat to fasten it in place.
Oleron reached up to the hook by the door to grab his own hat and coat, laughing to play off the cold prickle that had just crawled down his spine. He could swear, for half a second, he felt a warm soft breath against the back of his neck, just over his collar, as if something standing behind him was reading the words off Elsie’s lips.
“I do hope you’re completely wrong,” he said, and his voice shook just a tiny bit, too quiet for her to notice. “If *Romilly* isn’t out by autumn, I’m going to be in a very serious mess indeed.”

That evening, Oleron huddled close to his crackling hearth, low fire painting the walls in streaks of honey and amber, and seethed a quiet, fuming resentment at Miss Elsie Bengough’s afternoon prognostication. Difficulties waited for him here, she’d said; he would never finish his life’s work, the novel *Romilly*, within these walls. What utter nonsense. No man’s confidence thrives when someone snuffs it out before he’s even struck the first spark of the day’s writing, and wasn’t there an old, dark superstition that to speak a warning out loud was to summon the very thing you warned of? Some unnameable, hostile little seed had been planted in the room that afternoon, all because Elsie could not keep her foolish, unasked-for opinions to herself. Worse, she had tied that hostile influence not to some external bad luck, but to the house he’d only just moved into, the quiet, sunlit flat he’d fallen in love with at first viewing.

He snorted, shifting his chair to squint around the space that seemed to positively glow, soft and welcoming, in the firelight. Even that faint, sallow, drained tinge he’d half-noticed in the cold light of midday was gone, washed away by the orange flames. The flowered chintz curtains, printed with trellises and wicker baskets and little carved oaten pipes, fell in long, still folds to the window seats, the spines of his old leather-bound books glowed rich as melted honey, and for the life of him he could not see a single thing amiss. It was Elsie, he realized with a jolt, who had looked out of place that afternoon. The quiet, sparse, unassuming room had made her look garish, overfull, all florid pink skin and overripe laughter, like a prize rose left out too long in a damp summer storm, oozing moisture from every petal, already half-soured at the core.

He was horrified at the thought as soon as it landed. He and Elsie had been close friends for ten whole years, she was the most loyal, steadfast companion he’d ever had, the one person he would have run to if every other door in the world slammed shut. The hot flush of shame burned up his neck and over his cheeks, he sat staring into the fire for ten long minutes, mortified that he could dissect his oldest friend so cruelly in the privacy of his own head, even briefly.

All was dead quiet in the house save for one tiny, tinkling sound from the direction of the kitchen: a tap he’d failed to twist fully shut, dripping, plink-plunk, into a chipped earthen bowl beneath it. He began tapping his index finger on the arm of his chair in perfect time to the drops, the little regular motion slowly chasing the shameful heat from his face, and before he knew it, his unkind train of thought picked right back up where he’d dropped it.

Elsie’s mind, he realized now, had never lined up with his, not really. For years he’d read his rough draft chapters to her, and she’d nodded along, spoken like she understood every line of his vision, but now he saw it for what it was: empty, performative praise, no actual comprehension behind it. And if Elsie was that shallow, then so was the protagonist of his novel, Romilly Bishop, the woman he’d modeled entirely on her.

The realization hit him like a cold fist to the chest. He hated her. That character he’d spent three years nurturing, the woman who was supposed to be his Beatrice, his perfect ideal of all the soft, glorious things a woman could be, was coarse-fibred, over-saturated, rank, bleeding Elsie’s overripe, common traits through every one of the fifteen chapters he’d finished. He’d been stuck on the sixteenth for weeks, had no idea why he couldn’t bring himself to write a single line further, and now he saw it all: the whole thing was rotten at the root.

He dragged himself to his feet, fetched the thick stack of manuscript pages from his desk, and held them over the flames to burn them all to ash. But before he could drop the pages, the dripping tap caught his attention again. The little plinking sound had shifted, ringing out four or five distinct dulcimer notes in irregular little phrases, so faint he almost thought he was imagining it. He found himself waiting, holding his breath, for the little trilling four-note phrase to repeat, and his eyelids grew heavy, the fire shrinking lower and lower in the grate, the candle flames guttering and licking at the edges of their Sheffield silver holders. He stumbled to his bed half-asleep, locked the doors and windows on autopilot, and dropped into a deep, unshakable slumber.

The oddity hit him the very next morning. His landlady, Mrs. Barrett, knocked the usual gentle tap on the thin wooden wall beside his bed, and he called her in for his breakfast tray. The moment he opened the door, he realized he was humming something soft, under his breath. Mrs. Barrett paused, hand still on the doorknob, her face turned away, a strange, dimpling little smile playing across her lips.
“Dear me, that’s a very old tune you’re humming there, Mr. Oleron!” she trilled in her soft falsetto. “I haven’t heard that one in near forty years.”
Oleron froze, his thumb stuck halfway into the seal of an unopened letter. “I was humming? Sing it for me, please.”
Mrs. Barrett protested that she had no voice, that her sister Ann Pugh was the family singer, but she hummed the air anyway, thin and wobbly. “They do say it was only ever played on a harp, hundreds of years back,” she added. “They call it *The Beckoning Fair One*.”
Oleron stood stock still, the little tune looping in his head. It was exactly the four-note phrase he’d heard dripping from the tap the night before. He’d never heard the song in his life. He’d never even known it existed. But the house already knew it, and it had taught it to him, while he sat half-dozing by the fire.

Far stranger than the way a common, banal dripping tap had fallen into perfect sync with a ghostly, unplaceable draft snaking through the walls was the effect that rhythm left on Oleron: it pried open the edges of his perception, and suddenly his ears hummed with a terrible, sharpened sensitivity to every small sound the hundred-year-old house exhaled.

It is an old truth that the deepest, most suffocating silence in the world is not the absence of noise, but a quiet held taut by a thousand almost-too-small-to-hear disturbances. This house was never still. Maybe the soft, damp spring air seeped into its waterlogged, torpid old timbers, waking them from decades of frost-numbed sleep. Maybe the fires Oleron lit each evening stoked heat into its knobbly, jointed skeleton, making it stretch. Every joist and oak baulk was riddled with burrowing, chittering insect life, carving tiny tunnels through the old dark wood like so many secret scribbles. Sit still long enough in his armchair, and Oleron’s hearing would shift, the same way a hiker who thinks the midsummer woods are dead quiet suddenly realizes the air thrums with the whir and creak of ten thousand tiny hidden lives.

He found himself smiling, half uneasy, at the foolish arbitrary line mankind draws between things that live and things that do not. Never mind the pattering of mice behind the wainscot, the soft thud of crumbling plaster falling down inside the walls, the little popping gasps from burning logs in his hearth: the whole house was talking, in a language he almost thought he could learn if he just waited long enough. Beams settled into their mortises with long, tired human sighs. Things ticked slow, rhythmic counts inside the walls, as if the wood itself had a heartbeat. Joints cracked, floorboards whined soft complaints under his feet, and sash windows shifted in their frames with a soft, deliberate thud even when not a breath of wind stirred the curtains.

Whether the house was truly alive or not, it had a personality, soft and winsome, that curled around him like a well-loved coat. After an hour of dozy musing he hit on the fancy that if his own soul and body fit so easily together, he might slip so far into the bones of this house that he would bleed into its walls. It amused him to picture some future tenant, years from now, walking in and feeling that faint, unnameable prickle of a presence. A harmless hack of a novelist, whose only sin was deciding he had to burn through his half-finished novel and start it all over, laying the groundwork for a new ghost. A ridiculous joke. Almost.

But the deeper his quiet, creeping attachment to the house grew, the more Elsie Bengough recoiled from the place, her aversion sharpening into something that made her skin prickle the second she stepped over the threshold. She did not bother hiding it.

“It doesn’t belong to this century, not even a little bit,” she said, sharp as a shard of broken glass, staring at the panelled walls. “And it’s poison for you, Paul. You already drift too far away from the real world, slip right into apathy if no one yanks you back. You should be in a flat with concrete floors, a cheap patent gas meter, a tradesman’s lift, somewhere loud and busy that forces you to rub elbows with other people. I could get you a little part-time work, three days a week, plenty of time left for your writing—would you say yes?”

Oleron felt a petty, unnameable resentment rise in his throat, as if the words were being put in his mouth before he could think. He thanked her, no smile on his face. “No, I don’t think so. Everyone has to live their own life, after all.”

“Live your own life?” She huffed, crossing her arms. “When was the last time you went out for more than a stamp run? When was the last time you took a proper walk, stretched your legs?”

“Some time, I suppose. I haven’t kept track.”

“Since I was last here?”

“I haven’t gone out much.”

“And is *Romilly* turning out better for you being locked up in here, wrapped in all this quiet?”

“I think she is. I’m laying her foundations. I’ll start the proper draft soon.”

She seemed to have forgotten their fight over the first, abandoned draft of *Romilly*. She frowned, turned half away, then snapped back around. “Oh, you’re still clinging to that ridiculous idea?”

“If you mean I threw out the old version and started a new one from scratch, yes,” Oleron said slow, his voice colder than he meant it to be.

That uncordial edge pricked her, but Elsie was a fighter. His unaccountable stiffness made her firmer. She huffed a sharp, exasperated pshaw. “Where is the old manuscript? I want to see it. I want to read you parts of it, knock this wool-gathering nonsense out of your head before you throw years of work in the bin.”

This time Oleron turned his back to her. When he faced her again, his tone was softer, but unyielding. “It’s no use, Elsie. I get to decide what path I take, even if you think it’s wrong. I’ve thought this all through. The manuscript? I almost burned it, but I didn’t. It’s in that window seat, if you absolutely have to look.”

Elsie stepped quick to the window seat, lifted the wooden lid. Then she gave a sharp, gasping little cry, clapping the back of her free hand to her mouth. She spoke over her shoulder, voice tight. “You were supposed to knock those nails flat, Paul.”

Oleron was at her side in two strides. “What? What’s happened?”

“I did pull every last one of them out,” he said, staring.

“Left one sticking far enough to gut someone,” she said, holding up her hand. A raw, welling red gash ran all the way from her wrist to the knuckle of her little finger, the rust from the nail already leaving dark brown streaks in the blood.

“Good God,” Oleron breathed. He grabbed her uninjured hand, tugging her toward the bathroom. “Come on, we need to bathe that right now.”

He ran warm water over the gash, scrubbing away the dirt and rust, then switched to cold, holding her hand under the stream as he rambled in shock. “How did I miss it? I ran my whole palm over the edge of that box a dozen times, there was nothing there. Is the water too cold? Does that hurt? I swear I pulled every nail out of that wood.”

“Leave it a little longer,” she murmured, eyes half closed. “I can bear it.”

He led her back to the sitting room after a few minutes, wrapped the gash tight in one of his clean linen handkerchiefs, but his face was still twisted with bewilderment. He had spent half a whole day restoring those three window seats, prying out every old nail and patching the wood, he knew he had. He’d even opened each of the lids a dozen times after that, no sharp edge, no snag, no nail sticking up an inch and a half like a tiny waiting fang. But there it was.

“I’ll yank the damned thing out right now,” he muttered, grabbing a pair of pincers. He got it in one clean pull, no mistakes this time.

Elsie had sunk into his armchair, her face as pale as parchment, but she still had the crumpled old *Romilly* manuscript clutched in her uninjured hand. She wasn’t done arguing.

“Paul, if you throw this manuscript away and start over, it will be the worst mistake you ever made,” she said, voice thick with earnest worry.

Oleron hung his head. He couldn’t stop seeing that gash, that impossible nail. For once, *Romilly* was the last thing on his mind. But Elsie kept pressing, until he spoke, voice soft, almost apologetic. “What can I say? I have to follow what feels right to me, Elsie. When you read the new version, you’ll understand. And if you still don’t like it, well…” He made a hopeless little gesture. “You have to let me go by my own lights.”

She went quiet.

“Come on,” he said gently. “We’ve gotten along so well up to now. Don’t let this tear us apart.”

The words had barely left his mouth before he regretted them. Her lips and eyelids quivered at the same time, and her voice shook when she spoke. “I can’t help saying this, Paul—you’ve changed. So much.”

“Hush, you’re still in shock,” he soothed. “Rest a minute. How could I change?”

“I don’t know, but you have. You haven’t been the same since the day you moved into this house. I wish you’d never even seen the place. It’s ruined your work, it’s turned you into someone I barely recognize, I’m so scared for you.” She winced, shifting her bandaged hand. “Oh, God, it’s starting to throb.”

“Poor thing,” he said. “Let me walk you to a doctor to get that dressed properly.”

“No, I’ll be fine,” she said. She propped her elbow on the back of the chair, and her bandaged hand rested light on his shoulder.

At that touch, a sudden, strange, sharp new anxiety coiled tight in his chest. A hundred times before, on their walks and trips, she’d slipped her hand into his arm like a sister, and he’d never thought twice about it. But now, staring at her, her eyes half closed, her head tilted back, that soft, secret smile on her parted lips, the truth hit him like a blow to the chest. Good God. He’d never noticed. She was in love with him.

And the strangest thing of all was that instead of softening him, filling him with guilt or warmth, a hot, ugly surge of resentment bubbled up, unbidden, completely alien to him. What was she doing here? Why wasn’t she minding her own work? Why was she poking into his life, trying to drag him out of the only place that had ever felt like home? She was the one who had changed, not him.

By the time she opened her eyes again, he’d pushed the ugly feeling down, but his voice was distant, careful. “Let me take you to the doctor, Elsie.”

She stood up. “No, thank you, Paul. I’ll go now. I’ll stop by the chemist if it needs a new dressing. Take my other arm, please. Goodbye.”

He didn’t try to stop her. He walked her to the bottom of the stairs. Halfway down the narrow alley outside, she turned back. “No point me trekking all the way out here if you’re not home,” she called. “I’ll send you a postcard before I come next time.”

At the front gate, she turned one last time, her face mournful. “Leave this house, Paul. Everything about it is wrong.”

Then she was gone.

Oleron walked slowly back into his silent sitting room. He went straight to the window seat, lifted the lid, and stood there staring down at the patch of wood where the nail had been, the faint dark stain of old, old dried blood already seeping back into the grain, no trace of Elsie’s fresh red wound on the splintered surface.

A cold chill ran down his spine.

“That’s not right,” he whispered to the empty, quiet room. “It’s impossible. I know I pulled every last nail out of this wood.”

Somewhere deep inside the walls, a soft, slow sigh echoed. The house was smiling.

Oleron knew exactly what Elsie had meant when she said her next visit would come with a postcard first. She had finally woken to the truth: he knew of her quiet, longing affection for him, and he did not want it. The sharp, guilty twist of pity in his chest when he heard her unannounced knock on the landing within the week was sharp enough to make him flinch. She called through the door crack that she had no intention of staying, that she only popped by with a tip. He had to all but force her over the threshold, as if she could feel the house's sharp, hungry edges nipping at her coat hem before she even crossed the sill.

Her pretext was a newspaper notice for a short story contest with a generous prize he could not afford to miss. He thanked her, his throat tight, and the second she had said her piece she was already turning for the door, desperate to be gone. Oleron did not move to detain her; he saw through the thin excuse as clearly as he saw the dust motes hanging in the hallway dimness, and he walked beside her down the stairs to see her out.

Elsie Bengough had no luck at all in that house. Halfway down the flight, there was a crack like a gunshot, sharp splinters of old pine exploding upward, and she bit back a shriek so hard her jaw bulged. Oleron knew that wood had been sound when he tested it, two days prior—he had bounced his full weight on every stair to check for rot when he moved in. But her foot was through the plank, splinters digging deep into her ankle through the thin leather of her boot.

He lunged for her, his hands shaking. "Good God, Elsie—are you hurt?"

She laughed in thin, hysterical gasps, tears spilling over onto her cheeks. "It's my weight, I know I'm getting fat—"

"Stay still, don't twist, you'll gash it worse," he snarled through his teeth, prying at the jagged planks around her trapped foot. The work was messy, the edges of the wood sharp as broken glass, and when he finally pulled her foot free, he saw the dark blood seeping through the tear in her boot, the skin of her ankle already raw and ragged.

"Good God," he whispered, over and over, horrified.

"I'll be too heavy for anything soon," she sobbed, still half laughing, trying to hobble backward toward the front step. She refused every plea to come back inside and wash the wound. "No, let me go quickly. I'm not wanted here."

The words hit him like a blow to the skull, and he reeled back a step. "Elsie, that's not—"

She made a sharp, violent gesture, swatting at the air as if pushing a crawling thing away from her face. "Paul, no. Don't say it. Don't give me soft, useless half-truths. You know exactly what I mean. If the thing I want can't be, don't make me sit through the pity. I didn't even want to come. I tried so hard to stay away."

It was heartbreaking, unbearable, and he had nothing to give her. He did not love her, not the way she needed. "Let me get my coat, I'll walk you to the doctor—"

"No." She pushed his offered arm away, her face streaked with tears. "Don't offer me anything less than what I came for. Leave me a scrap of my pride, at least." She gave another wobbly, bleeding laugh as she limped toward the step. "Sorry I broke your stair. And you will enter that story contest, won't you?"

He groaned, glancing out the alley to see their neighbor Barrett loitering on the curb, staring up at the house with a long, pale, unreadable face. But the second Oleron opened his mouth to call for help, Barrett turned on his heel and vanished down the street, gone without a word. Elsie would not be deterred, insisting she would go straight to the doctor on her own, and hobbled off down the sidewalk. Oleron stood in the doorway watching her until she vanished past the row of sharp, hatchet-edged To Let signs nailed to the fence, half convinced the jagged boards would lean off their nails and fall on her to finish the job.

He did not dine that night. He wandered from empty room to empty room, her words ringing in his ears so loud he could not drown them out: I'm not wanted here. Don't offer me anything less. Let me take away what's left of me. He ached for her, so fiercely he almost thought he could force himself to return her love, if it would take that pain off her face. But it was impossible. He had been a fool, ignoring the quiet looks, the light touches to his arm during their walks, never realizing what she was offering him. It was too late now. She had to stay away, for both their sakes.

Twilight bled over the windows. He did not light a candle, just stirred the dying embers in the hearth and dropped into his armchair, until the fire guttered low to nothing but embers. The house creaked around him, tiny, secret little tickings and scratchings in the panelling, as if a hundred quiet presences were holding their breath. A morbid, icy dread curled in his gut: what if Elsie had not gone to the doctor? What if she was leaning over the dark embankment of the river right now, or tying a noose to her door hook? He pictured the inquest, the questions, Barrett testifying that he had seen her leave Oleron's house limping and bleeding, and he shook the thought off as foolish, sick fantasy.

Until he stopped, and thought of the two accidents.

Impossible. The nail that had gashed her hand weeks prior—he had pulled it out himself, wrapped it in newspaper, thrown it in the bin. This broken stair? It had been perfectly sound two days before, no rot, no give. There was no explanation. The whole house was alive, he realized, staring into the dark. Every beam, every jamb, every loose nail was thrumming with some kind of hungry, waiting life. The noises he had always brushed off as settling wood were not creaks—they were movements. The house was listening.

He sat quiet, picking apart the little sounds one by one, assigning each a logical, mundane cause, until he realized there was one he had been deliberately avoiding, for minutes now. It came again: a slow, silky sweeping rustle, with a tiny, almost inaudible crackle tangled in its wake, moving back and forth, back and forth.

He half paid it no mind at first, still thinking of Elsie, of how little all the petty flaws of the world mattered next to the raw, soft pain of a hurting person. He was closer to loving her in that moment than he had ever been. But the rustle kept coming. It quickened. It got louder. It did not sound like anything that belonged in an empty room.

Oleron bolted upright in his chair, every muscle coiled tight as wire. The chair tipped over behind him, clattering to the floor and sending the fire irons skittering across the hearthstone. He knew that sound. There was no other noise on earth like it.

He backed slowly, his hands fumbling behind him for purchase, until his shoulder hit the cold wall. His breath had stopped in his throat. "God in heaven," he whispered.

The sound cut off.

He screamed. "Who's there? What are you?" A small scuttle skittered across the floorboards by his feet, and his knees almost buckled—just a mouse. That was not the sound that had turned his stomach to ice, that had made his mind reel too terrified to think. The brushing noise was gone. He called again, and again, until the sound of his own voice echoing off the walls spooked him so badly he fell silent.

His shaking hand dug in his pocket for matches, found none. He worked his way along the wall, groping around the recess to the mantelpiece, until his fingers brushed the wood. He fumbled along its edge, knocked a full box of matches down onto the hearth. He scraped his raw knuckles across the stone, chasing the box until he cornered it inside the fender, then struck a light.

The candle on the table flared, guttered, then burned steady. The room was empty. No one was there. Nothing was out of place. But it had been there. He knew it. It was still lingering, somewhere in the dark corners.

He had laughed once, at his own silly fancy, that the time he spent merging his whole identity with this beautiful, fair house might leave a ghost of him here one day, long after he was gone. It had never once occurred to him that the house had already done that merging, long before he moved in. That it already had a ghost.

The realization hit him like a fist to the chest. The house had a tenant, and it was not him. That other presence, the one that had been luring him closer every day, had just stood right behind his chair, brushing its long, soft hair.

Without quite knowing how he came to be there, Oleron found himself striding over the loose pine board he’d jammed temporarily across the step Miss Bengough had cracked when she fell. His scalp prickled with the house’s sharp, unwholesome draft, and he realized he was hatless, bare headed, descending the stairs without any clear memory of deciding to stand up from his desk. Only hours later did the hazy fragments drift back: he’d left his candle sputtering on the table, its wick melted long and drooling wax in thick, rivulet-like trails; he’d edged the front door open no wider than his own shoulders, as if terrified something cooped up inside might slip past him before he could escape; he’d sidled out, then pulled the door shut so softly the latch barely clicked.

At the foot of the stairs a shock tore a thin, high sob out of his throat. Something burst from the dark of the disused cellars in a flurry of fur and a shriek, its eyes glowing molten green for half a second before it darted out the open gate. It was only a cat, but his hands were shaking so hard he could barely curl his fingers around the cold iron of the gatepost. He stood for a minute under the crookedly nailed “To Let” sign, plucking at his lower lip until he drew blood, staring up at the smudge of candlelight behind one of his red blinds. For a heartbeat he swore he saw a long, loose shadow drift across the glass, slow as honey, as if someone standing inside was brushing their hair over their shoulder. He did not wait to confirm it. Stumbling, he moved up the square, craning to glance back over his shoulder every few steps.

A tiny, grubby public house huddled around the corner, a spot he’d walked past a hundred times and never once entered. He burst through the door now, fumbling a shilling out of his coat pocket that clattered and missed the sawdust-dusted counter by three full inches. “B-brandy,” he stammered, teeth chattering so hard the word split apart, and he ducked to grope for the coin under a sticky stool. The little bar compartment he’d stumbled into was empty save for him; the regulars—carters, labourers, the neighbourhood tradesmen who knew everyone’s business before it happened—huddled in the far end of the space, behind the white-haired landlady who moved slow between taps and dust-furred bottles.

Oleron sank onto a perforated hardwood settee that dug sharp little holes into the back of his thighs, drank half the brandy in one burning swallow, then drained the rest before he could think to spill it. He sat there, staring at the blurred shapes of the men across the bar, and wondered idly which of them would volunteer to haul his corpse out of that cursed house come morning. He waved for a second brandy. There was no way he was going back to that room where the candle still guttered. Not back to the broken step, not back to that pith-white, breathless, watchful space that felt like it had been waiting for him to leave. He’d beg his old landlady to put him up for the night, he decided, he’d move his work and his sleeping quarters back to the familiar flat first thing tomorrow, never step foot in this house again—

His glass was empty. He ordered a third. The grievances bubbled up hot and sharp in his chest: nails that drove themselves back into solid oak just to gash his palm, stair steps that rotted themselves hollow overnight to trip people, invisible presences that drifted through his rooms in the dark brushing their hair until the air crackled with static like trapped summer lightning. He’d paid good money for this place, for his work on *Romilly*, not for some nameless thing to make itself at home uninvited. That lawyer in Lincoln’s Inn would get a piece of his mind before the sun was high the next day. It was outrageous, trapping a man in a tenancy with a ghost.

A cut-glass partition separated his little nook from the landlady’s station, but it stopped eight inches above the counter top. He glanced up, and saw three shadowy faces pressed to that gap, staring at him. They vanished the second his eyes met theirs, as if they’d never been there at all. He shifted to a corner out of their line of sight, and found himself face to face with the landlady, who’d been pretending to wipe down the same tap for ten minutes. She made a small, meaningless comment about the lingering winter cold, the influenza that had laid half the neighbourhood low, that spring was bound to break soon. The mundanity of it steadied him a little, and he crushed down a stupid, creeping thought: what if the landlady brushed her hair every night in front of her own mirror, and what if it made that same sharp, fizzing crackle?

The fourth glass of brandy hit, and suddenly he was furious at himself for running. What was he, a skittish child? He was a grown writer, halfway through a major novel, not to be chased out of his own home by tricks of the dark. He laughed, loud enough that the patrons at the far end went quiet for a beat. He’d just been overworking, that was all. He’d let his head fill with moonshine about rewriting *Romilly*, even stupidly toyed with burning fifteen whole finished chapters. An empty head gives the devil leave to play, that was the whole of it. He drained the last sip of brandy and stumbled out the door to walk the fumes off.

The fresh cold air made his head swim at first, then cleared, and the false bravado seeped right out of him, leaving a cold, heavy dread in its place. He wasn’t scared for himself anymore. He was scared for Elsie. She’d said it, hadn’t she? I’m not wanted here. She’d seen the rot in the house long before he had, and now he knew it was true: whatever was living in those walls meant her harm. He could not, under any circumstance, let her step foot on that property again. That was non-negotiable. But he, he was not going to run. He would not let a shadow chase him out of his own home, not if he could help it. He turned on his heel and headed back toward the square.

By the time he reached the short street leading to the square, the pub was shuttered for the night, the loitering patrons clustered on the kerb. Their conversation cut dead the second he passed. Long-nosed old Barrett, the handyman who’d been bringing him breakfasts and cleaning his rooms, glared at him under the streetlamp, and pointedly did not return his goodnight. Oleron slipped through the broken gate, hesitated only a heartbeat in the dark alley between the houses, then climbed his stairs. The candle in his Sheffield stick was no more than an inch long, guttering fitfully. He did not light a new one. Deliberately, he forced himself to walk through all five rooms, slow, peering into every dark corner, before he headed for his bedroom.

As he crossed the hall, he spotted a letter on the floor, slid under the front door by hand, no stamp, clumsy scrawl that ran from edge to edge without a single comma or period. It was from Barrett. The man wrote that he would no longer prepare Oleron’s breakfasts, no longer step foot in his house, and Oleron would have to make other arrangements entirely. The sting was in the postscript: a single, pointed line of Scripture, its implication about Elsie and Oleron so lewd and unearned his face burned hot enough to singe. The whole nosing, foul-minded neighbourhood had been whispering filth about them, all because Elsie had tripped on a rotten stair. Oleron crumpled the letter up, held it to the tiny guttering candle flame until it burned to black ash, then ground the flecks under his heel. The rage burned off the last of his frayed unease.

He was undressing, pulling his shirt over his head, when he glanced down at his bed. The coverlet he’d smoothed perfectly before he left that night bore a clear, deep indentation, as if someone heavy had been lying on it for a full hour, right where his chest would rest. He could not remember lying down on the bed at any point that day. But the fumes of the brandy, and his white-hot anger over Elsie’s name, crowded the oddity right out of his head. He blew out the last of the candle, fell back onto the mattress, and sank into a deep, black, dreamless sleep that lasted nearly a full day—so deep he never heard the Barretts knock at his door the following morning.

To the man who heeds that small, shrill voice under his skin warning him that twilight and rot are creeping over the edges of his soul, terror feels like something absolute: a chasm yawning at his feet, that will swallow him whole in a blink if he does not throw every possible barrier between it and the soft, unprotected core of who he is. Mercifully, the materials to build those barriers are never far to hand. He piles up tiny, ordinary things against the dark: the exact time he brews his morning tea, the familiar nod to the baker on the corner, the old rituals, unspoken conventions, small unexamined habits that shore up the line between the world of the living and the hungry things that lurk just outside it. He is even willing to box joy and terror both as absolute, uncrossable forces, for to blur those lines, to tear down the landmarks that tell you what is real and what is not, is the worst treason a man can commit—not just against himself, but against the safety of every soul that still walks in the light.

It was that treason Oleron began to commit, step by unthinking step. He did it insensibly, brushing off the safeguards Elsie Bengough had tried to lay down for him as nothing more than impertinent overcaution. Two months prior, the phrase “haunted house” applied to his beloved, bewitching dwelling would have turned his marrow to slush; now, his sense of scale warping thin, he only found himself asking Haunted by what?—blind to the truth that horror loses none of its awful, unyielding power just because you convince yourself it can be measured, classified, held in the palm of your hand. The landmarks blurred. Mists of confusion wrapped around him thicker by the day, and the only thing he felt sharp and bright beneath it all was a ravenous, gnawing curiosity. He had to know. He would know. Nothing short of unravelling the secret of the house would sate him.

He need not have schemed for it. Answers came easier than breath. The same way the words of his old writing once used to pour out of him fully formed, no edits needed, before he could even fully form the thought behind them, now the questions he posed to the dark empty rooms were answered in the exact same moment they crossed his mind. There was a giddy, heady exhilaration to the speed of it, a thrill he had not felt since the first days his writing flowed fresh and unforced, as if some hidden guide was leaning over his shoulder, dictating every step he needed to take.

First, define the problem, he thought, and his mind sorted it into cold, clear math. He was not alone in the house. The old place had already sunk its teeth deep into his spirit. That shared bond, the common denominator of the four walls, tied whatever his unknown co-tenant was directly to him. All he needed to do was identify the other half of the equation. The answer was obvious: the key lay in his half-written novel, not just the new Romilly he had been drafting, but the old, discarded one he had abandoned the day he first walked through the front door. A month prior he would have thought himself mad to entertain such an idea; now he accepted the dizzying hypothesis without a single tremor.

He reviewed the history of the first, fifteen-chapter *Romilly* in a rush. He remembered now, clear as day, that he had found her stiff, lifeless, wrong on the very first morning he sat down to work at his new desk. He remembered the night he had almost hurled the entire manuscript into the kitchen fire, and the morning after that when he had begun to sketch out the new *Romilly* from scratch. That was the very same morning Mrs. Barrett, overhearing him hum a stray little melody he had picked up from the dripping kitchen tap the night before, had told him he was singing an old air he had never heard of in his life: *The Beckoning Fair One*.

The name hung in the air of the silent room like cobwebs strung between rafters. Oleron did not pause. The first Romilly, cast off entirely, the new one had latched onto him immediately, screaming to be born out of his skull. Now, looking back, he could almost feel the hate, the ravenous passion that had fuelled the supplanting of the old character. More than once, a stray, lazy thought directed at his discarded creation had not just felt wrong—it had felt like an insult, directed at the new thing that had taken her place. The idea that this jealous, almost homicidal malice could extend past fictional characters to Elsie, his old friend, made a strangled “Good God!” tear out of his throat, loud in the hush. The thought of Elsie derailed his cold abstract inquiry entirely, made the back of his neck prickle.

He still could not think of the fight he’d had with Barrett the landlord a few days prior without his face flushing hot and his jaw tightening. He’d marched over to the man’s doorstep first thing the next morning, ready to beard him and demand he stop spreading his garbage rumours, but the man had been nothing but vague, blustering, repeating scraps of lines over and over: “Certain things… Mrs. Barrett… respectable house… if the cap fits… proceedings that shall be nameless.” Oleron had shouted, warned him if a single breath of the slander reached Elsie he would make him pay, but he’d left the square knowing he had only made matters far worse. Now he kept catching glimpses of Barrett’s face pressed to windows across the way, peeking around curtains, lifting blinds, always watching, as if waiting to catch him in some unnameable act to use against him. The neighbours stared at him when he walked past. He started doing his grocery shopping on the far side of town, avoiding the corner shops near the square entirely. He told himself he was fine living alone, bachelor habits were nothing new, that he was deep in research he did not want disturbed.

He was staring out the window one raw, midday, tired and faintly feverish, glad he did not have to go out, when he saw Elsie crossing the square straight for his front door. The wind whipped her skirts out like sails, her veil streaming behind her, rain spitting at her cheeks.

Panic seized Oleron so hard he did not even grab his overcoat. He grabbed his hat, sprinted down the stairs three at a time, his eyes darting up to the sharp slate edges over the eaves unbidden, as if he already knew if she stepped over his threshold, something would slip and fall, something would break. He met her at the gate, words pouring out of him fast, desperate.
“This is terrible timing, Elsie! I’ve been called away urgently, I’m so sorry, you must think me the worst host alive.”
He lied that he was heading to town to see a man named Chambers, that Chambers was leaving for Brighton that afternoon, that he’d just got the letter that morning. He even led her the wrong direction away from the bus stop first, before she gently reminded him the route to town ran the other way. He laughed too loud at his own stupidity, and walked her towards the terminus, shivering so hard his bones ached, all the while thanking every god he could name that he had stopped her before she crossed the line of the house gate.

No one on the top of the open bus would have guessed that the pale, coatless man sitting beside Elsie was fighting not to pass out from relief that he had kept her out of the danger waiting inside his four walls. He even insisted the raw, gusty wind was too warm, that he was not cold at all.
He tried to ease the conversation to the lie he had been turning over in his head, that he was leaving town the next day for a rest, that he would send for her when he got back.
She only looked straight ahead, past the bus driver, and said, “I know.”
He jolted. “You know what?”
“You’re not going anywhere.” Her voice was even, steady. “You never were planning to go out this morning. You only ran out the second you saw me turn into the square. Don’t act like we’re strangers, Paul.”
He had no words. He noticed the wind had stained her cheeks the bright pink of forced rhubarb, and he could not meet her eyes.
“You ought to leave, you know,” she went on, quiet. “You don’t look in the mirror much these days, do you? People keep turning to stare at you in the street. You look half gone. But you won’t go. I know why.”
He shivered, coughed to hide the tremor in his chest, snapped that there was no use discussing it if she already thought she knew everything.
“You’re right about the gossip, about Barrett,” she said, “but you could handle Barrett. This is bigger than him. I promised you I wouldn’t name it, so I won’t. That’s why I came out today, though. To force your hand. We can’t keep going on like this. It’s over now.”
“Over?” he repeated stupidly.
“I’m not going to pester you. I won’t show up uninvited, I won’t hover, I won’t make you uncomfortable with whatever expectations you think I might have.” She held out a cold hand to him, as the bus slowed to his stop. “I only have one rule. If I need you, you don’t have to spare me a single thought. But if you need me, Paul? You will need me, sooner than you think. I will know. And I will come for you, no matter what it costs.”
He could only groan, numb, as he climbed down onto the kerb. The bus pulled away a second later. He watched Elsie’s figure shrink into the distance through the smudged window, and for the first time in all the years he had known her, she did not smile, did not wave her long arm at him as she left. He stood in the gusting wind, shivering, and turned back towards the square. The empty old house was waiting for him, hungry.

He stood planted on the curb, chest stuffed full of a raw, throbbing misery so heavy it had felt like it would pin him to the asphalt forever, staring after her retreating figure until she vanished around the far bend. The second she was out of sight, that weight slid off his ribs like a dead thing sloughing off its skin. She had handed him his freedom back — and though some prickle of his old sense argued he’d never truly surrendered it, he had no patience for fine parsing now. The road ahead lay clear, no tangled strings of obligation, no half-written manuscript weighing on his shoulders, no clinging demands that he be a person he wasn’t.

That lightness swelled fast, turning from relief to a giddy, unruly joy. By the time he was halfway to the parish church, he already knew exactly what he had to do next. The vicar of his district lived less than ten minutes from the square, and Oleron turned his steps straight there. He needed every scrap of information he could dig up on that old house with the weathered insurance marks, the slanted *To Let* boards that always looked like they were tipping over on purpose, and the vicar was the only person in the neighbourhood likely to have all the unspoken, dark little local history no one else cared to remember. Once that last chore was done — Oleron chuckled under his breath, sharp and hungry — the real fun could start.

He got less than he’d hoped for, at first. The house was old, the vicar told him, as if Oleron needed a man of the cloth to point that out. It had a reputation for being haunted, he added primly, and Oleron leaned in, ears pricked. But most old houses collected that kind of silly gossip among the uneducated, the vicar hurried to add, and the modern world’s sad lack of true Faith only fed those stupid superstitions. He was careful, soft-spoken, the kind of man who would never say a thing he couldn’t take back if his listener reacted badly, and Oleron smiled, seeing right through it.

“Don’t worry about my nerves,” he said. “How long has the place been empty?”
“Must be a dozen years now, at least,” the vicar replied.
“The last tenant — did you know them? Man or woman?” A strange, cold tingle crawled up Oleron’s spine as he spoke.
“Man,” the vicar said. “Name was Madley, if I recall right. Artist. Total recluse, barely ever left the four walls. And —” he hesitated, then spilled the words fast, like ripping off a bandage “ — since you’ve clearly come looking for the truth, better you hear it straight than get it all twisted from the gossips: he died there. Circumstances were very strange. Post-mortem found not a single crumb of food in his stomach, even though he had more than enough money in his pockets to buy a feast. His whole body was just worn to nothing. People talked suicide, but you’ll agree, starving yourself deliberately like that? It’s almost unheard of. They brought in an open verdict.”

“Ah,” Oleron said, his mouth gone dry. “Is there any full history of this parish?”
“Nothing comprehensive. I’ve only compiled notes on our ecclesiastical records, the old registers and so on — I’d be happy to show you, if you’d like, but the parish is huge, I’ve only one curate to help me, and my spare time is terribly limited…” The rest of their visit was spent making small talk about the size of the parish and the shortage of church staff, and Oleron thanked him, took his leave, and walked slowly home.

He walked so slow, in fact, that twice when he was within a stone’s throw of his own gate, he turned right back around and tramped another twenty minute loop around the square. He had delicate, dangerous work ahead of him, work that would take every scrap of his focus: he needed to scrub every last leftover thought of his ex, every half-finished line of *Romilly*, every scrap of mental clutter out of his head, get his mind so empty and receptive he could recapture that exact first impression of the house, the one he’d had the day he moved in, before the slow, creeping saturation of its presence had snuck up on him. Back then, the house’s influence had had to fight through all the stale, overwritten chapters he was dragging around. Now he was light. Unburdened. Free of both the book and the woman who had inspired its tedious main character.

Now he could meet the new, unknown, coy, jealous, bewitching thing that called to him — the Beckoning Fair One.

At half past two that afternoon, he turned his key in the Yale lock, stepped over the threshold, and pulled the door shut behind him.

The trick worked instantly, so perfectly he almost shouted out loud with triumph. It felt like he’d escaped *into* the house, not into his own home. That old rush of ease, mastery, exhilaration he hadn’t felt since his writing used to hum with fresh, unbroken promise flooded his bones. The air tasted thicker, sweeter, like it held more oxygen; his steps felt light, almost weightless, as if his own gravity had shifted. The flowers in the bowls glowed, the meadowsweet-coloured wood panels and mouldings stretched out fair and warm to greet him, the polished floor shone, the high ceiling dotted with faint, strange old star marks seemed to lean down to meet him. Oleron laughed right back, spoke out loud to the empty room. “Oh, you’re pretty,” he murmured, soft as a lover. “So pretty.”

He laid down on his couch then, and spent the whole afternoon like a convalescent waiting for a beloved visitor, drifting in a delicious empty haze, smiling to himself half-asleep, his eyes lifting every few minutes to drink in the alluring walls around him. He stayed there until dark fell, and the old house’s nocturnal creaks and whispers started up. But nothing happened. No sign, no manifestation, no trace of the thing he was chasing. He waited the whole next day too, holding his mind as still and sensitive as a photographer’s waiting plate, but still nothing. The thing he was wooing was shy. Exacting.

On the third day, he thought he got it. A droll, cunning little smirk tugged at his mouth, and he chuckled. “Oho, so that’s how you want to play it, is it?” He stood musing, thumbing his lean jaw, gaze slanted off to the side, and suddenly crossed to the hall, took down his hat, and headed for the door. “No, I won’t go fetch Elsie. No need to break a butterfly on a wheel, my pretty thing. You want to play hard to get? I’ll play hard to ignore right back.”

He tramped to the railway station, caught a train, and spent the rest of the day wandering the countryside, not a single thought spared for the house. Oleron thought he knew exactly how to handle coy Fair Ones that beckoned you in then hid away. He didn’t get back until after eleven that night. “Now, my Beckoning Fair one,” he murmured, fumbling for his keys as he walked down the dark alley.

Inside the flat, he was perfectly calm, deliberate, careful not to give away how eager he was. To pretend he was half-asleep, ready to collapse straight into bed, he only lit one single candle. He yawned big, fake, as he set off on his usual nightly circuit, stocking feet making no sound on the floorboards. First the kitchen: the full moon slanted through the uncurtained window, leaving a lozenge of almost peacock-blue moonlight on the planks. He could see the faint reflection of his candle, of his own face, in the glass. The door to the little powder closet stood ajar, so he shut it, then sat on the old chair with the cushion made of a folded harp-bag to take off his boots. He moved through the bathroom next, where another slice of that blue moonlight cut across the windowsill and the exposed pipes on the wall, then the rarely used study, where he stood for a minute staring out at the silvered roofs across the square. He walked through the sitting-room noiselessly, stepped into his bedroom, and set the candle down on the chest of drawers. His face was blank, slack, the picture of a tired man ready to sleep — but he had never been more alert, more sharp, more cunning.

He drew down the blind, took off his coat, then stooped to reach for his slippers under the low bed. He had no proof, no reason to know for certain, but he was utterly, unshakably sure the manifestation he’d been waiting for three days for was right there, close enough to touch. He felt no fear, only thrumming, electric curiosity. It might startle him, but that was all. His hand groped under the bed, back and forth, searching for the slippers.

And then his heart gave a leap so hard it stopped beating for a horrible, suspended second. His hand closed around the slippers, but he would have pitched forward face-first into the floor if he hadn’t already been on his knees. Something soft, silky, the texture of long loose hair, brushed slow across the top of his scalp. He held his position, head tilted to the side like he was still just groping for his shoes, until he could breathe again. When he stood up a moment later, there was a bead of bright blood on his lower lip where he’d bitten right through it, and his watch had jolted clean out of his waistcoat pocket, dangling at the end of its short leather chain, swinging back and forth.

Before the watch had even stopped swaying, he was back in control. He stepped to the mantelpiece, where his grandmother’s portrait stood, and positioned himself so he could see the steady flame of the candle on the chest of drawers reflected in the portrait’s glass. He watched the little glints of light bounce off the facets of the trinkets around the mirror, all of them still — except one faint, moving gleam, bobbing slowly up and down through the air. And with every downward sweep of that gleam came a soft, silky, crackling rustle, exactly the sound of a comb being pulled through long hair.

Oleron didn’t flinch. He reached up, caught his dangling watch, and started winding it slow, deliberate. Then he started emptying the change out of his trouser pockets, lining every penny and halfpenny up in a neat little row on the mantelpiece, like he was nothing more than a boring, tired man winding down for the night. The soft, electric rustle of the comb filled every inch of the small bedroom. When he thought he was ready, he feigned another yawn, and spun around so fast his shoulders jolted.

The thing whose hair was being combed was still invisible. But the comb didn’t stop moving. It had tilted a little, shifted a few inches to the left, gliding in slow, even sweeps from five feet above the floor straight down to a point just a few inches under the top of the chest of drawers.

Oleron kept up his act perfectly. He walked to the little washstand in the corner, poured a glass of water, and washed his hands. He took off his waistcoat, kept going through the motions of getting ready for bed, and then smiled to himself, sharp and sly. “Hm,” he said out loud, loud enough for every corner of the room to hear. “Think I’ll go read for a quarter of an hour.”

He stepped out of the bedroom, and was gone for two full minutes. When he came back, the room was dead quiet. He glanced at the chest of drawers. The black vulcanite comb was lying perfectly still, right between his discarded collar and a pair of gloves. Oleron picked it up. It was just a cheap eighteenpenny comb he’d bought off a chemist’s display card, solid, ordinary, no more capable of defying the laws of physics than the planets were of veering off their orbits. He set it back down, then glanced at the bundle of papers he was clutching in his other hand — the full fifteen completed chapters of the old *Romilly* manuscript. He tossed it into the armchair in the corner.

“Just as I thought,” he muttered, and his lips curled. “She’s blindly, ragingly, murderously jealous.”

Night after night after that, then day after day, for so long he stopped being able to count how many had passed, Oleron chased his unseen housemate. He courted it, cajoled it, deliberately neglected it, whispered threats, begged soft, eaten alive by a curiosity that burned hotter every hour, until his whole life was nothing but that single consuming hunger, that single all-encompassing desire to finally meet the Beckoning Fair One face to face. He didn’t notice his own appetite fading, his work falling by the wayside, the rest of the world slipping further and further away. He thought he was the hunter. He never once considered he was the one being lured.

As the weeks bled one into the next, almost no one besides the postman bothered to climb Oleron’s creaking, dust-furred stairs. And since a man who answers no letters receives almost none in turn, even that heavy, familiar tread grew so rare that he might go four or five days at a stretch without hearing the thin thud of mail sliding through his door slot. First came a letter from his publishers, crisp and formal, asking after his long-overdue new manuscript. He left it face-down on the mantel for three days, then forgot it entirely. A second arrived, stained with rain, which he did not even break the seal on. No third ever came. The world outside had already half-forgotten he existed.

Spring bloomed bright and unforgiving. White privet foam spilled over the edges of the chopper-edged notice boards down the lane, and the flower women’s wicker baskets lined the kerbs, heavy with hyacinth and daffodil, their sweet heady scent drifting three flights up to Oleron’s windows. He bought armfuls every single day; the rooms seemed to hunger for fresh, continuously renewed blooms, and he did not stint their demands. Even so, the chore of stepping outside to buy them began to gnaw at him more and more, and every time he stumbled back over his own threshold, the relief was so sharp it made his knees go weak.

His senses had warped again, not sharpened back to their old normal, but stretched thin, stitched through with a new, unnameable terror: agoraphobia. The name barely mattered next to the raw, animal fear of open air, of empty space that could stretch on for miles, of the unseen thing that might pounce at his unguarded back when he rounded a street corner. Soon he arranged for a shop boy to leave bread, tea, and fresh blooms propped against his outer door at midday. He rubbed his hands giddily when the arrangement was settled. That was better. Now he never had to step out again if he didn’t want to.

He threw himself into the seclusion, locking the outer bolt twice, and for a while he thought he might be happy—though his happiness curdled into something soft and sick, a dull ache that made him weep weak, unprovoked tears for no reason he could name. He would not have traded that strange, thrumming sadness for all the loud, raucous mirth of the world outside, not for anything. Noise of any kind had become unbearable: cart rumbles on the cobblestones, distant children’s shouts, even the creak of a floorboard a room away cut into his skull like a shard of glass. He padded around the flat in thin slippers, closing every door so slowly the hinges never even whined, pressing a finger to his lips for long minutes at a time to soak in the perfect, humming stillness.

Sundays grew intolerable. The fine weather had drawn the noisy sect that long-nosed Barrett belonged to out into the square below his windows each Sunday morning, their drum thudding like a diseased second heartbeat, their brass instruments braying, men and women screaming raw, anguished prayers up at the sky. Barrett himself would stand below Oleron’s window, face tipped up, eyes squeezed shut, brow working, praying for the sound of his voice to pierce every unbeliever’s skull—including Oleron’s. One Sunday mid-rant, Oleron sprang to his window, hauled the crimson blind down hard, and heard, clear as day, Barrett call his name out mid-rhapsody, weaving Oleron’s supposed sin into his prayer for all the square to hear.

Sometimes, standing stock-still in the hush of his flat, Oleron would call out soft, not quite expecting a reply. Once he whispered “Romilly” and waited, breath held, but more often his murmurs were nameless, directed at the empty air around him.

He soon began haunting one spot more than any other: the threshold of his bedroom, just inside the open door. He had discovered that if he left every inner door unlatched (and the outer one bolted tight, unbudging), he could stand in that exact strategic nook and see into every one of his five rooms without shifting his feet an inch: the full sweep of his sitting room, all of his bedroom bar the small patch hidden behind the door, and narrow, peeking glimpses of the kitchen, the bathroom, and the little study he rarely used. He would linger there for ages, breath caught, finger pressed to his lip, watching the shadows drift. One day, the thought struck him: had dead Madley, the former tenant the vicar had warned him about, ever figured out this perfect vantage point too?

Light, he soon learned, troubled him far more than darkness. Direct sunlight seeping through the windows burned like a flame pressed to the back of his skull; even soft, diffused daylight left a dull, numbing ache in his bones. He made short, darting excursions around the flat hour by hour, yanking down each crimson blind one by one, sealing off the sun entirely until every window was shrouded. The whole flat soon drowned in a thick, blood-warm half-light, the pale pith-white walls stained red as if they oozed their own slow fluid, the air thick with the cloying rot of overripe blooms. He snickered to himself as he pulled the last blind down: that would baffle nosy Barrett, the fool, he couldn’t see a single thing that went on behind those crimson curtains. He laughed about it for an entire hour.

A few days later, a fright left him shaking so hard his teeth chattered for the same length of time. He had grabbed the cord to draw the blind over the window seat where he’d found the old harp bag, his back pressed safe to the window embrasure, when he saw the flash of a black-and-white check skirt vanish around the far edge of the house wall. He could not be sure—if he’d run to the other blinded wall, it would have been gone before he got there—but he was almost certain it was Elsie. He waited, frozen, ears ringing, for the sound of her tread on the stairs. Nothing came. After three long, agonized minutes, he let out a shaky breath. “God, that would have compromised me horribly,” he muttered over and over, half to himself, half to the quiet dark. No woman would stand for this red-lit, flower-choked lair, he thought. Any woman would drag him back out to the grey, boring world, and that would ruin everything.

Still he was not quite happy, nor quite unhappy. He was waiting for something to be granted to him, and nothing had been taken away yet, not a single thing. He paid no mind that his money was dwindling to almost nothing, no mind that strangers who’d known him once whispered he’d stopped writing work entirely. They knew nothing. He was just beginning to work now. He was gestating something far greater than any book he had ever penned: the Fair One, the Beckoning Spirit, weaving her out of the stillness, the flower scent, the red gloom of the flat. Lovely radiant creations did not pop into the world easy. Their creators had to weep wretched tears, chase their slippery, mocking, ever-eluding quarry until their lungs burned, and that was exactly what he was doing. Let no one say he had stopped working. He was far closer now than he had ever been.

All the rest of the world was falling away from him, and he let it go gladly. When your Fair One keeps waving you on, you drop every other trivial thing to follow. So he kept his post at the bedroom door frame, watching, waiting, smiling, finger pressed to his lip. This was his worship, his troth, all the love he had ever ached for. The hot flare of jealousy he felt for dead Madley, for the hours Madley had spent in this flat before him? That was devotion too, acceptable service to his coming Bride.

Then he made a discovery he should have seen weeks earlier, spurred on by that jealousy of Madley. Since the night he’d stupidly tried to play hard to get, using the Fair One’s jealousy to banish her, he had forgotten all about the fifteen discarded chapters of *Romilly*, the ones full of Elsie, the flesh-and-blood rival. How stupid he had been! Of course the Fair One would never show herself, not while the evidence of his divided allegiance was still hidden in the window seat! She had almost killed her rival twice, after all, the vicar had said. He owed her that sacrifice.

He hauled the manuscript out of the window seat, carried it to the fire that burned day and night in his sitting room, its smoke curling to bring out every last cloying note of the flowers lining every shelf. His clock had run down weeks earlier, a stupid little contraption that could not measure the colossal, world-altering thing about to happen to him, so he had no idea what the hour was, only that it was very late. He knelt by the hearth, fumbling to untie the string binding the chapters together, when a soft tap-tap-tap pricked the silence.

It was faint, three small raps, but it filled him with a cold, crawling alarm that made his blood run thick. Then Elsie’s voice drifted through the wood of the outer door, soft, trembling: “Paul! Paul, I know you’re in there. You’re in trouble—you’re in danger. At least come to the door.”

He cursed her under his breath, did not move an inch. He would not let her in. He snickered quiet, at the thought that she was standing out there talking about him being in danger, when she was the one trespassing on the threshold of a place no living human should dare enter. Her sobs seeped through the wood, soft and pitiful, begging that she had promised no such thing to stay away if he needed help. He ground his teeth, shook his fist at the closed door, and dropped the first page of *Romilly* into the flames. It curled black, crumbled to ash. He fed the rest in, sheet by sheet, while she called his name, over and over. Then the voice cut off. He heard her slow, dragging footsteps descend the stairs. He waited for the sound of her tripping, of a cry, of the handrail splintering as she fell, but nothing came. She was spared, let crawl away unharmed. The Fair One had shown her mercy.

Oleron dropped the last charred scrap of paper into the embers, stood up with a low, giddy laugh. “Lucky to get away like that,” he murmured to the empty rooms. “If I’d so much as cracked the door an inch, she never would have left this building alive.”

Now his reward was here. He crossed to his post at the bedroom threshold, eyes shining bright in the red dimness. The whole flat was full of juggled, deceptive shadows: the faint streetlamp glow seeping through the nearest blind, the pale blue glimmer of moonlight leaking down the hall from the kitchen, the sullen smoulder of the fire under the ash, the soft pale glimmer of narcissi and tulips in their bowls. He would know her, his Fair One, no matter how the dark tricked him. He hung his head for a second, apologetic, for delaying their shadowy wedding for so long. He did not even dare try to name her, for fear of tainting her with the name of the discarded rival Elsie.

A wisp of shadow, light as lamb’s wool, bloomed in the kitchen. The streetlamp glow by his elbow dimmed all at once, as if some invisible hand had pinched its flame to nothing. The fire settled in the hearth, puffing out a cloud of fine black ash. A heavy daffodil head snapped off its stem, fell to the carpet with a soft thud. A cold draught slipped through the old house, brushing Oleron’s cheek, scented with nothing he had ever smelled before, not flowers, not rain, not any thing that belonged to the living world.

Oleron trembled so hard his whole body shook. The draught had nudged the half-open bedroom door, making it creak soft on its hinges. He reached out, his fingers ice-cold, and pulled the door all the way shut, slow, so no sound broke the stillness. He sank down onto the nearest chair, breath caught in his throat, waiting. He was a man waiting for his name to be called, the summons that would admit him to a weighty, secret, unholy audience he had spent months pining for.

No human heart, no matter how soft or how steeped in love, can hold compassion for the anemia of the soul. When life’s pitch drops so far it snaps, when the spirit is twisted inside out until all that once tasted sweet, bright, and ordinary curdles into loathing, the ties that bind people to each other dissolve. The sane recoil, appalled, lest their own sanity bleed away too. We are not exorcists. We cannot cast out devils. We are selfish, and right to be, to make sure those devils do not crawl under our own doors—even when love swells so hot in our veins we fool ourselves into thinking we are half divine. Talk of honor, of duty, is useless. The note slipped under the dark threshold will lie unread in the dust, or if it is glanced at for a single, fumbling second between two bouts of unspeakable oblivion, it will be dropped and forgotten. The telegram will never be answered. The whistling messenger, trundling down the steps afterward, will never notice the drawn blind creaking open an inch behind him, a trembling finger prodding its edge before snatching it back to hide.

Let the lost man wrestle his own shadows. Let him, if his madness runs that far, cling to the succubus he has conjured, curl around her, feed her. But let him do it in a house no breath of heaven ever touches, no ray of sun ever pierces to cut through its filthy, thick twilight. The lost belong lost. Humanity has other, brighter things to tend to.

The handwriting on the two envelopes Oleron had found kicked across his kitchen floor, the day he crept in to dump an armful of fetid, rotting blooms he’d stashed in his sitting room, meant less to him than the characters of some half-remembered dream. When the telegraph boy rapped on the front door, only a few feet from the bed he was sprawled in, Oleron gnashed his teeth and stuffed his knuckles in his ears. He pictured the boy standing just outside his partition, between stacks of forgotten parcels and bundles of flowers already dead and oozing brown slime. His whole outer landing was heaped with them. He’d been too afraid to open the door and drag them in. After a week, the errand runners marked his address down as a mistake, and never came back. Inside, in that thick red twilight, the old blooms turned brown, crumbled, and rotted where they fell.

His strength was draining away, drop by drop. The Abomination fastened to him, feeding on every spark, and the steady sapping left him prone for hours at a time, staring vacantly up at his rose-tinged ceiling, letting random, unbidden fancies crawl all over him. Even his sharpest memories could barely cling to his attention. Sometimes a half-flitting memory teased him: the novel he was supposed to write, the one his contract demanded he finish, flickering for a few seconds before winking out entirely. Sometimes whole perfect, timeless novels bloomed in his head out of nowhere, vivid enough to touch. Sometimes the memories were tiny, trivial, decades old: the garret he’d lived in at twenty-two, the moldy lodgings he’d rented after his first book got a bad review. He’d once known how to fight these slow, creeping blurs, but that was all over now.

He’d found a place he never intended to leave until they pried his unconscious body out by force. A place some might call fever-sick, reeking too hard of long-dead, morbid things for a living man to lock himself inside— but God, it was irresistible. It had a will of iron, a pull no one could fight, a companion no sane man would ever trade away. What delights it held, once you stopped straining against its inexorable tug! A novel? Somebody ought to write a novel about a house like this! There was a million stories tangled in its walls, if you just dug deep enough. That painter Madley, who’d died here, had probably painted every inch of it… but Oleron didn’t know Madley, and he was sure he’d hate the man. Hated the very thought of him, in fact. (Aha, that was a fine joke!) He was half tempted to march over and tell that long-nosed prude of a curate next door what a rotten, ungodly life Madley had led— but he suspected the curate already knew, that he’d been praying loud through the thin walls for Madley’s soul all these years. He and Oleron had even fought once, about some girl… Elsie Bengough, that was her name.

He had moments of deep, jittery unease about Elsie. Not unease for her, exactly— restless, that she kept shoving herself uninvited into his head. The second she popped up, he tried to kick her out. She wasn’t just a bore, never had been. Now, her very image in his mind was poison, ruining the bright, burning raptures the house gifted him. She had no tact. She ought to know no one wants every random thought parading through their head at all hours. She ought to leave him alone for hours at a time, when his veins hummed with fire and power, when he had every right to shut out prying fools the way he’d shut that nosy Barrett out. But no— there she was, popping up, and the towering, glorious visions he was on the cusp of grasping vanished instantly, as if a fog had snuffed out a star, as if a pit had gaped open right on the threshold of a golden door, as if a flapping bat shadow had snuffed out the dawn and dragged everything back to murk. He fought to snuff even the first faint spark of any thought of her.

But one night, he couldn’t. He didn’t know what hour it was— only the faint glimmer of a streetlamp seeping around his drawn blind told him it was dark, that it had been weeks since he’d thought of her at all. She was just there, unannounced, her face fixed in his mind no matter how hard he tried to scrape it away. She was haunting him, and of all moments to show up, when he was right on the edge of the triumph of his rival— the fair, dark succubus who called to him from the walls— Good God, it was monstrous! How dare she stand there, peering in on it? The blood rushed to the roots of his hair, hot with rage. “Damnation take her!” he choked.

The next second, his petty anger dissolved into a cold, shearing sweat of unnameable fear. Panic coiled in his chest. He knew he’d done something fatal, irreparable. That blaze of fury flooding his murky twilight wasn’t his. That white, searing infernal light that split the dark wasn’t his. That rift yanking open to hell wasn’t his. His rage had been no bigger than a child’s fist, ready to throw a tiny, harmless blow— but that other, horrific hand, pulling back to strike in the exact same spot? Had he set that in motion? Had his tiny spark lit the fuse on the full, accumulated power of this relentless, hungry house? He didn’t know. He only knew his own small spark had been blown out, that the soft, clinging kiss he’d been chasing for months had curdled on his lips to a ravenous, tearing bite, and that for the first time he felt terrible pity for the woman he’d just been screaming about. He had to warn her. He had to.

“LOOK OUT!” he shrieked aloud.

The revulsion hit like a cold, weighty wave crashing over his head. He jolted awake, flat on his back in his bed, the thick mist of horror that had wrapped around him for months suddenly stripped away. He was Paul Oleron. Tired novelist. Past the peak of his talent. Sick, naked, helpless, unutterably alone. His mind was clear, for the first time in longer than he could remember, and he understood he was a failure. He’d missed every prize: the easy, warm domestic happiness other men loved, and the shining, wild prize he’d chased that was supposed to justify all his reckless choices. There was no do-over. Fate gives no second morrows. His future stretched out, empty, nothing but day after day of grinding out bad, unwanted work, paying forfeit to his publishers for the half-finished novel he’d never finish. He was spent. Old age was creeping close. The rest of his life would be nothing but sad, quiet wisdom, if he was lucky. If only he’d chosen the wife, the children, the friend at the fireside, and let the will-o’-the-wisp he’d chased burn itself out without him.

It struck him then how weak he was, how the whole room reeked of rot, how the scratch of a thick, weeks-grown beard rasped against his palm when he dragged a hand over his face. “Extraordinary,” he muttered. “Have I been ill? Why did no one call a doctor? Why am I left alone?”

He thought he heard a soft sound from the kitchen. He propped himself up on one elbow, listening. He wasn’t alone, then. Of course they wouldn’t leave him sick all by himself. If everyone else abandoned him, Elsie Bengough would never leave him. His dearest, most faithful friend. Bless her heart.

A short, stifled, spluttering cry rang out, sharp as a cracked bell, from the other side of the door. “Paul!”

In that same second, a tiny, forgotten sound clicked into place in his memory, one he’d ignored minutes earlier: the soft scrape of metal on metal, exactly the sound his key made turning in his front door lock.

“Hello? Who’s there?” he called, his voice hoarse. No answer. He called louder. “Hello? Who is it? Elsie? Is that you?” He thumped on the wall beside his bed. “Elsie! You called, didn’t you? Come in, whoever you are!”

He heard the soft thud of a closing door, then dead silence. Alarm pricked at him. Elsie must have hired a nurse for him, that made sense. She’d stay as long as she could, then leave the nurse to watch over him. But that voice had been exactly hers. “I have to get up,” he mumbled, throwing one leg over the edge of the bed. His legs were like water. He reached out for the wall to prop himself up.

He froze before he could put his other leg down. He was suddenly, horribly certain he dared not step foot in the kitchen. It was miles away. Any sort of unnameable horror could leap on his back the second he crossed the threshold. When every bone in your body screams to crawl back to bed, you listen. Besides— why should he go? If that spoilsport Elsie had gotten herself into trouble, that was her problem. He wasn’t going to drag some clinging, hungry horror back onto his defenceless self for her sake. Let her find her own way out. He didn’t care. He had more important things to do. Tomorrow, he was going to write a novel with a heroine so beautiful, so wicked, so hungry and adoring and cruel, every man who read it would lose their mind. He could feel her near now, the air in the room thrumming with that soft, giddy bliss that only came when she was there,beckoning,beckoning.

He let go of the wall, and fell back limp into the bed, as the other half of that ravenous kiss the bite had interrupted pressed to his lips, stealing every last breath from his chest.

The June sun blazed white-hot over the cobblestone square, gilding the tops of the crowd’s bowed, craning heads as every pair of eyes fixed on the old red brick house at the alley’s end. Its facade was pocked with antique dented fire-insurance marks that looked like old, faded bruises, and the wooden “To Let” notice boards hung over the top of the surrounding paling like sharp, axe-edged choppers, glinting threateningly in the light. Two constables planted their boots in front of the broken alley gate, chests puffed to hold the gawkers back.
The women clustered at the throng’s fringes, shifting every few steps to angle for a better view of the house’s drawn, blood-red blinds, their voices low, breathless whispers that never carried far enough for anyone else to overhear. Not a single child was out in the square; every front door stood shut, and you could hear faint, muffled curious cries from behind them, smothered by mothers’ hands over small mouths.
A sharp-nosed man named Barrett had a knot of listeners huddled around him, reeling off his story for the dozenth time, his tone thick with self-righteous drama, while a tiny, wide-eyed fat man darted from group to group trying to steal his audience, rambling about a key no one would stop to listen to him about.
“Revealed to me that something was very wrong that very afternoon, it was,” Barrett was saying, chest puffed. “I was standing right where Constable Saunders is now, minding my own business, when they came out that gate. No fooling me, no. I saw her face… white as bleached writing paper, just like the woman our Lord told to go and sin no more. No lying to me! I marched straight home to Jane, and I said ‘Jane, this stops right now. We’re commanded to shun evil, and this ends today. You don’t set foot in that house again.’ And what did she say? ‘John, it’s four-and-sixpence a week for the cleaning.’ I told her if it was forty-six thousand pounds it wouldn’t be worth it. She hasn’t crossed that gate from that day to this.”
A short, strained silence fell. “Did your Jane… ever see anything queer, then?” someone asked, half-hesitant.
Barrett rounded on him, face austere. “What Mrs. Barrett did or did not see stays with her. The Good Book says to keep thy tongue from speaking ill, and I abide by that.”
Another man cut in before anyone could press him further. “That poor actor fellow Oleron was three sheets to the wind in the Waggon and Horses the night before last, weren’t he?”
“Right miserable state he was in. Sat in the bar alone, wouldn’t stand anyone a drink. Ranting to himself about walls breathing.”
The fat, jittery man lunged forward again, hands waving. “She got that key from me! Knew the number off by heart, she did, came into my shop on a Tuesday evening—”
No one paid him any mind.
“Shut your traps,” a gruff labourer rumbled, nodding towards two men in peaked inspector caps pushing through the crowd. “She still ain’t turned up. Now we’ll get the truth.”
The inspectors conferred briefly with the two constables posted at the gate, while the fat man scrambled to their side, spitting his story so fast the words tripped over each other. “I remember the key number! Three ones and three threes, 111333! She bought it off me, I swear it!” One of the inspectors brushed him gently aside.
“Nobody’s gone in the house?”
“No, sir.”
“Right. Brackley, you come with us. Smith, you stay on the gate. The rest of the squad’s five minutes out.”
The three men tramped down the narrow, sun-dappled alley and pushed through the house’s front door. They climbed the wide oak staircase, its carvings crusted with decades of grime, and the lead inspector kicked aside a drift of dead brown leaves and yellowed half-written manuscript pages piled right outside Oleron’s bedroom door.
“Looks like he’s not been sweeping up much lately. Don’t bother knocking. Break one of the glass panels, Brackley.”
A sharp crack of breaking glass echoed through the empty halls. Brackley thrust his arm through the shattered pane, fumbled the latch open, and the second they stepped across the threshold both men choked.
“Christ, that stench—like a closed hearse that’s been sitting in the sun. Flung all the windows open, quick, let the light in.”
Out in the square, the crowd saw the red blinds go whipping up and every sash of the old house’s windows thrown wide. The inspector leaned out, gasping for fresh air. “Simms, you check the rest of the house, I’ll tend to the man on the bed.”
They hauled the bedroom blind up next, and Oleron, gaunt, waxy-white, half starved, threw a shaking hand over his eyes to shield them from the brutal June sunlight that felt like needles stabbing straight into his skull. His hearing felt stuffed with cotton; he could barely process the two uniformed men leaning over him, asking over and over where Elsie Bengough was, the woman everyone knew as Miss Fair One. He only shook his head, too foggy to speak.
“Not getting anything out of him right now. Get him on his feet, easy, careful with him. I’m going to go check the kitchen.”
The second inspector tramped through Oleron’s study, found nothing out of the ordinary at first, then pushed open the kitchen door. His boot sank into ankle-deep muck of rotting vegetable scraps and slimy, blackened dead flower petals that covered the whole floor. The kitchen window had no blind, blocked out almost entirely by the blank brick wall of the house across the alley, leaving the room dim even in full midday sun. At first glance it looked empty—until he spotted a shuffling, smeared track through the refuse, leading straight to a corner cupboard.
Set high in the cupboard door was a small square sliding hatch, running on old wooden runners. The door itself was pulled shut. He reached for the tiny brass knob, slid the hatch open, and stumbled back a full step, his breath catching in his throat.
Framed in the little square opening, bulging so large it jammed halfway out, was a thing that looked exactly like a misshapen, overstuffed plum pudding, wrapped tight in a bag of faded, browny-red frieze. A dark, sticky stain seeped through the cloth. He couldn’t bring himself to touch it, to shove it back through the hatch with his bare hands. Instead he twisted the main cupboard door’s handle and pulled. The weight behind it was impossible, so heavy he could only heave it open four inches before he had to shove his whole shoulder to the wood to force it closed again. A triangle of Elsie’s old black-and-white check apron skirt caught in the door crack, sticking out a few inches above the floor.
He stumbled out into the front hall, calling out. “We’ve got her.”
Oleron was half dressed by then, still holding his hands over his eyes, his brain too muddled to make sense of any of it. He couldn’t understand the piles of dead flowers everywhere, couldn’t understand why policemen were tramping through his house, why one of them was yelling for a four-wheeler cab and a stretcher to be sent for. He could hear heavy thuds from the kitchen, like something big was being dragged across the floor.
“What’s happening?” he mumbled, half asleep.
A murmur rose up from the square outside, and the clatter of cab wheels on the cobblestones. An officer leaned over him, whispering something about his statement being used as evidence, and before he knew it they were lifting him to his feet, guiding him down the stairs. The crowd outside roared, angry, baying for blood, and he caught sight of the little fat key-seller babbling his story into a constable’s notebook, repeating the key number 111333 over and over.
The two inspectors flanked him as they walked him to the cab at the alley gate, trying to block his view of what was being carried out behind him—but they were too slow. Oleron caught a flash of a hooded canvas stretcher, bulging far too lumpy, far too big for a normal human body, being trundled out of the house. A stone whizzed through the air and thudded against the back of his cab.
The inspector climbed in after him, pressing his back to the window facing the crowd to shield Oleron from the flying debris. Through the open cab door, Oleron could see the edges of those wooden chopper-sharp “To Let” boards sticking up through the privet hedge, one of them still painted with the notice that the key was kept at Number Six.
All at once the roar of the crowd went dead quiet. Slow, shuffling footsteps echoed down the alley, and the second inspector climbed into the cab, slamming the door shut and pressing his back to the other window to block it off. Trapped between the two uniformed men, Oleron’s eyelids slid shut, and he drifted off into a heavy, dreamless sleep as the cab trundled out of the square. The other wagon, the one carrying the stretcher, turned and trundled up the hill, towards the mortuary.
