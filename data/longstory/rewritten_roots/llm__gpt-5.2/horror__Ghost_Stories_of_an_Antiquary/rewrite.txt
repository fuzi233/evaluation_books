If there is any reader who likes to set the tongue of a tale upon the roof of his mouth by tasting the places it speaks of, let him know at once that there are names in these pages which are not concocted like a sauce to deceive the palate. St Bertrand de Comminges exists, with its stone that keeps the cool of centuries as faithfully as a cellar keeps wine; Viborg exists too, with winds that carry the sound of far bells across flat country the way a long simmer carries savour through a house. And when, elsewhere, I let a coastal town stand behind the curtain, it was Felixstowe whose salt air and railway-whistle I had in my mind.

But the rest—the little pinches of scholarship I scatter as a cook scatters herbs over a dish, for aroma rather than nourishment—do not deceive yourself into thinking them gathered honestly from some old garden-bed. They are, almost to the last leaf, grown in no soil but my own invention. There was never any such volume as the one I profess to quote in the matter of Abbot Thomas’s treasure. It is a dish plated to look like antique fare, but made in a modern kitchen; the crust may crack convincingly under the knife, yet no medieval hand ever kneaded that dough.

For those who keep their own cupboards of publication dates, and like to mark the seasons of a writer’s making as carefully as one marks the ripening of apples or the curing of bacon, I will set down what I remember. “Canon Alberic’s Scrap-book” I wrote in 1894, and it was printed not long afterward in the _National Review_. “Lost Hearts” found its way into the _Pall Mall Magazine_. As for the next five stories—most of them first served up, piping hot, to friends at Christmas in the rooms of King’s College, Cambridge—I can only clearly recall that I wrote “Number 13” in 1899, while “The Treasure of Abbot Thomas” itself was composed in the summer of 1904, in that peculiar light when the days are long and the nights, though brief, can yet hold a chill that makes old stones speak if you listen.

M. R. JAMES

St Bertrand de Comminges lies on the Pyrenean spurs like a loaf gone stale—its crust of grey stone hard, its inner streets hollowed by time. The place had once been a bishop’s table, with its own library and chapter and discipline; now it lived on a thin broth of pilgrims and a few summer travellers who came to look up at the cathedral and then descend again to Toulouse or Auch as if nothing here could cling to them.

In the spring of 1883 an Englishman arrived alone for the day: Dennistoun, a Cambridge man with the neat, hungry patience of a scholar who does not so much “see” old things as taste them—testing their age the way one tests wine on the tongue, by scent and after-notes and the faint rasp of dust. He had left two friends in Toulouse, promising to rejoin them next morning. They would give St Bertrand half an hour, they said, like men swallowing a dry crust; but Dennistoun meant to spend the entire short day in the cathedral, filling a notebook and exposing plate after plate until every stall and screen had yielded its shape and story.

To do that he needed the sacristan with him. The landlady of the Chapeau Rouge—a brisk woman with a voice like iron ladles on copper pots—sent for the man. When he came, Dennistoun found not the expected harmless guardian of keys, but a small, dried creature whose very shoulders looked as if they were listening behind him. He stood as one stands in a wind that might suddenly turn. Again and again his head twitched, half glancing over his shoulder; his back was hunched and tense, the muscles in a continual recoil.

Dennistoun, watching with the cool curiosity of a collector, tried to settle the matter in his mind. Was the fellow mad with a fixed delusion? Or guilty? Or merely mastered by a wife? The last seemed likeliest, and yet the air about him was not of domestic nagging. It had the weight of something older and less nameable—something that did not speak in ordinary words.

But the church drew Dennistoun down its long stone throat. The day was a jar of chill air scented with damp mortar, wax, and the faint sourness of old woodwork. Every sound had a long tail. His pencil scratched like a small animal in the silence; his camera click seemed too loud; and now and then, from far above, the building answered with noises as if it were remembering other occupiers: muffled footfalls where no feet walked, a murmur as of distant talk swallowed by arches.

Once—Dennistoun told me later—he would have sworn he heard a thin metallic laugh high in the tower, the kind of laugh that has no chest behind it, only hard edges like coin struck on stone. He looked sharply at the sacristan. The little man had gone white to the lips.

“It is he—that is—it is no one,” he stammered. “The door is locked.”

They stared at each other for a full minute, as if both had heard the same note and neither dared to name it.

Another time Dennistoun paused before a large, dark, almost unreadable painting behind the altar—one of the series of St Bertrand’s miracles. The figures were a swamp of browns and blacks; the varnish had yellowed and the centuries had smoked it. Only the Latin legend below stood clear:

_Qualiter S. Bertrandus liberavit hominem quem diabolus diu volebat strangulare._

He turned, with a half-smile forming—ready with some light remark about devils and stranglings—then stopped short. The sacristan was on his knees. He stared at the picture not as at painted wood but as at a living threat, hands clasped till the knuckles shone, tears running over his cheeks with the helplessness of pain. Dennistoun, embarrassed, pretended he had not seen. Yet the question clung to him: why should a bad daub strike a grown man into such supplication? It was as if the man’s fear had a flavour, sharp and unmistakable, and the picture had just touched it.

By late afternoon the short day was dimming. Shadows pooled beneath the stalls and along the choir-screen; the dilapidated organ crouched in darkness like a broken animal. As the light thinned, the ear grew greedy. Those small disturbances—the scuff of something on stone, the suggestion of whispering—became more frequent, more insistently placed, as if the building were trying to draw Dennistoun’s attention away from his notes and toward the unseen.

The sacristan began at last to show hurry. When camera and notebook were packed, he beckoned Dennistoun quickly to the western door under the tower. It was time for the Angelus. He took the rope in both hands and pulled. The bell resisted, then yielded with a vast reluctant swing, and Bertrande’s voice climbed into the pines and dropped down to the valleys, where streams ran loud. The sound was a kind of blessing on the air: it cleared the day’s uneasy murmurs, and for the first time there seemed to be quiet, not merely absence of noise but the proper, living quiet of a place that hears its own rhythms.

They stepped out. The sacristan, breathing easier with the bell’s speaking behind him, ventured into talk.

“Monsieur seemed to interest himself in the old choir-books.”

“Indeed. Is there a library in the town?”

“No, monsieur. Perhaps there used to be one for the Chapter, but now—so small a place—” He hesitated, his face working as though he were chewing something bitter. Then, with a sudden plunge, as of a man stepping off a bank into cold water, he said: “But if monsieur is _amateur des vieux livres_, I have at home something that might interest him. Not a hundred yards.”

For a moment Dennistoun’s mind leapt with those collector’s dreams that are almost physical—hands on vellum, eyes on uncial strokes, the scent of old glue and dust like a spice one should not crave. Then scepticism cooled him: some printed missal, surely, perhaps Plantin, perhaps nothing. Yet to refuse would be to starve one’s own curiosity. He agreed at once.

As they walked, Dennistoun felt again the sacristan’s earlier hesitation and resolve, and, shamefacedly, the thought occurred to him that he might be being drawn into some crooked alley to be robbed as a rich Englishman. He began talking to make his presence known to the air, and contrived to mention—clumsily, but with intent—that two friends would join him early next morning. To his surprise, the sacristan seemed relieved.

“That is well,” he said, almost brightly. “That is very well. Monsieur will travel in company. It is a good thing to travel thus in company—sometimes.”

The last word fell like a stone into water. The brief light in him dimmed again.

They came to a house slightly larger than its neighbours, stone-built, with the shield of Alberic de Mauléon carved over the door: a canon of Comminges, 1680 to 1701, collateral kin, Dennistoun later learned, to Bishop John de Mauléon. The upper windows were boarded. The building had that shut, unused look of things not lived in but merely endured.

On the doorstep the sacristan paused.

“Perhaps,” he said, “perhaps, after all, monsieur has not the time?”

“Plenty of time,” Dennistoun answered. “Nothing to do till tomorrow. Let us see it.”

The door opened, and a young face looked out—his daughter’s. She was handsome enough, but the same distress marked her as her father: not quite fear for herself, rather a strained anxiety on behalf of someone else, like a person listening for a child’s cough in the next room. She brightened when she saw her father with an able-bodied stranger. A few quick words passed between them; Dennistoun caught the sacristan saying, “He was laughing in the church,” and the girl answered with a look of terror that seemed to put out the little warmth in the room before they had even entered it.

The sitting-room was small and high, its stone floor cold underfoot. A wood-fire flickered on a great hearth, sending up a smell of resin and smoke that mixed with the house’s older scent of damp stone and stored cloth. Moving shadows ran up the walls. A tall crucifix stood on one side, nearly reaching the ceiling: the figure painted in natural colours, the cross black, severe as a nail. Beneath it stood an old chest, heavy and solid as if it had been made to keep things not merely safe but shut away.

A lamp was brought. Chairs were set. The sacristan went to the chest and, with a nervous excitement that seemed to rise from his stomach to his hands, drew out a large book wrapped in a white cloth. On the cloth a cross had been crudely embroidered in red thread, the sort of red that suggests dried berries—or dried blood. Even before the wrapping came away Dennistoun felt the size and shape and knew it was no common service-book.

The cloth fell back. He looked down into a folio, bound, he guessed, late seventeenth century, with Canon Alberic de Mauléon’s arms stamped in gold. The paper leaves were thick, and on almost every one was pasted a leaf from an illuminated manuscript. The sight struck Dennistoun like strong food after fasting. The mind, greedy by nature, began at once to count and catalogue.

Here were ten leaves from Genesis, with pictures that could not be later than A.D. 700—colour laid on like ground spices, still bright in places, the tiny strokes fresh as if the hand that set them had only just lifted its brush. Further on, a complete set of pictures from a Psalter, English work, the finest thirteenth century could do: saints and beasts and vines curling with a life that did not belong to paper at all. And best—perhaps best—twenty leaves of uncial Latin from some very early unknown patristic treatise. A few words were enough to give Dennistoun a jolt of recognition and longing. Could it be Papias, “On the Words of Our Lord,” known to have existed at Nîmes as late as the twelfth century? Whatever it was, his mind made its decision with the cold, quick certainty of appetite: this book must go back to Cambridge, even if he drained his account and waited here for the money to arrive.

He glanced up, trying to read whether the treasure was offered or merely shown. The sacristan’s lips worked soundlessly; his face was drawn pale.

“If monsieur will turn on to the end,” he said.

Dennistoun turned, meeting marvel after marvel as each leaf rose. Then, at the end, two sheets of more recent paper lay like a bad aftertaste among sweet things. He took them for contemporary with the canon himself—Alberic, the unprincipled, who had surely plundered the Chapter library to make this scrap-book.

The first sheet bore a plan—carefully drawn—of the south aisle and cloisters of St Bertrand’s. In the corners were curious signs like planetary symbols, and a few Hebrew words. In the north-west angle of the cloister a cross had been drawn in gold paint. Beneath were lines of Latin:

_Responsa 12mi Dec. 1694. Interrogatum est: Inveniamne? Responsum est: Invenies. Fiamne dives? Fies. Vivamne invidendus? Vives. Moriarne in lecto meo? Ita._

Dennistoun smiled, the scholar’s smile that tries to domesticate the uncanny by naming it familiar. “A good specimen of the treasure-hunter’s record,” he said—thinking of romances and minor-canons and buried pots of coin. He turned the leaf.

What he saw then—he told me often—was beyond what any drawing ought to do to a man. The original is gone now, but I possess a photograph, and it bears out his horror.

It was a sepia drawing, late seventeenth century, an interior with semi-classical architecture, as artists of that time gave to Biblical scenes. On the right sat a king on a throne raised by twelve steps, canopy overhead, soldiers flanking him: King Solomon, one would say at first glance. He bent forward, sceptre outstretched, in an attitude of command. His face held horror and disgust and yet that confident imperiousness of a man who believes his word is law even in nightmare.

But the left side dragged the eye as a wound drags the tongue. On the pavement before the throne four soldiers stood grouped around a crouching figure—such a figure as makes the mind recoil and yet, horribly, insist on looking again. A fifth soldier lay dead, neck twisted, eyes bulging from his head with the obscene surprise of sudden death. The four living guards stared toward the king. Their terror was sharpened by discipline: they looked ready to flee but held by trust in their master, as if kingship were a charm.

And in their midst crouched the thing.

At first one saw only a mass of coarse matted black hair. Then the hair resolved into covering for a body of fearful thinness, almost skeletal, but with muscles standing out like wire. The hands—dusky, pale in a way that suggested skin stretched too tight over bone—were covered with long coarse hairs; the fingers ended in nails that rose and curved sharply down and forward, grey and horny and wrinkled. The eyes were touched with burning yellow; the pupils black and intense; and the gaze fixed on the king was beast-hate, intelligent and deliberate. There was an impression—common to all who saw it—that the artist had not invented. It looked drawn from life.

Dennistoun, his stomach turned as if by rancid fat, stole a glance at his hosts. The sacristan had his hands pressed over his eyes. His daughter looked up at the crucifix and told her beads with frantic speed, as if the clicking prayers might fill the air and leave no room for other sounds.

At last Dennistoun asked, steadying his voice as one steadies a knife hand, “Is this book for sale?”

Again the sacristan hesitated; again that plunge of resolve.

“If monsieur pleases.”

“How much?”

“I will take two hundred and fifty francs.”

Dennistoun was struck dumb. Even a collector’s conscience can wake; his was tenderer than most.

“My good man—your book is worth far more than that. Far more, I assure you.”

The sacristan did not move from his price. “Two hundred and fifty francs. Not more.”

There was no refusing such a thing. Dennistoun paid. A receipt was signed. They drank a glass of wine over the transaction—ordinary country wine with a scent of cask and sour cherries; the kind that warms the throat but does not cheer the mind. And as soon as the money was in the sacristan’s hands, a remarkable change came over him. He straightened. The hunted backward glances ceased. He even laughed, or tried to; the sound was thin, like a pot tapped with a spoon.

Dennistoun rose to go.

“I shall have the honour of accompanying monsieur to his hotel?” the sacristan asked quickly.

“Oh no, thanks. It isn’t a hundred yards. I know the way. There’s a moon.”

The offer was pressed, refused, pressed again, refused again, as if the older man were trying to place his body between Dennistoun and something that might leap from a doorway. At last he said, “Then monsieur will summon me if—if he finds occasion. He will keep the middle of the road; the sides are so rough.”

“Certainly,” Dennistoun answered, impatient now to be alone with his prize, and stepped into the passage with the book under his arm.

There the daughter met him. Her face held that same strained earnestness, and she spoke as one offering not a gift but a remedy.

“A silver crucifix and chain for the neck,” she said. “Monsieur would perhaps be good enough to accept it?”

Dennistoun protested he had no use for it; what did she ask?

“Nothing—nothing in the world. Monsieur is more than welcome.”

The sincerity in her tone left him with nowhere to stand but gratitude. He thanked her profusely. She placed the chain around his neck, the metal cool against his skin, its weight oddly insistent, as if it wished to be noticed. It seemed as though he had done them some great service without knowing it. When he set off with his book, father and daughter stood in the doorway watching him as if they were counting his steps. They were still watching when he turned on the inn steps and waved good night.

Dinner at the Chapeau Rouge was done. Dennistoun went upstairs to his room and shut the door. He set the folio on the table as carefully as one sets a rising loaf away from drafts. The landlady had been unusually attentive since hearing he had bought a book from the sacristan; her laugh, too, had grated on him—an uncheerful sound, as though someone were dead in the house and she were determined not to speak of it. He thought he had caught a hurried dialogue in the passage between her and the sacristan: words like “Pierre and Bertrand will be sleeping in the house,” and then a silence that thickened.

A discomfort had been creeping over Dennistoun all evening—perhaps mere reaction from excitement, perhaps the building’s late-hour noises still vibrating in him. It settled into a conviction that someone stood behind him. He found himself more comfortable with his back to the wall, like a man eating in company he does not trust. Still, the book’s richness overruled all. He opened it, leaf by leaf, and the room filled with the dry fragrance of old paper and paste, the faint sweet rot of centuries, and the ghost of pigments ground long ago. His eyes fed on the little scenes; his fingers itched to trace the strokes he dared not touch.

“Bless Canon Alberic,” he muttered—Dennistoun had the habit of speaking to himself as some men hum while they work. “I wonder where he is now? Dear me, I wish that landlady would learn to laugh in a more cheering manner; it makes one feel as if there was someone dead in the house. Half a pipe more, did you say? Perhaps you’re right. And that crucifix the girl gave me—last century, I suppose. It’s rather a nuisance, just too heavy. I might clean it up before I put it away.”

He lifted the chain over his head and laid the crucifix on the table. The metal made a small, dull sound on the cloth. As he did so his attention snagged on an object on the red table-cover near his left elbow. For a fraction of time his mind offered harmless names, as it does when it cannot yet accept what the senses have delivered.

“A penwiper? No—there’s no such thing here. A rat? No—too black. A large spider? God forbid—no. Good God—a hand like the hand in that picture!”

In the next instant he saw it fully: pale dusky skin stretched over bone and tendon of appalling strength; long coarse black hairs; nails rising and curving down, grey, horny, wrinkled. A hand—resting as casually as a guest’s hand might rest—on his table.

He sprang up with a terror so violent it seemed to seize his heart in a fist. Behind his chair something was rising to a standing posture. Its left hand lay on the table; its right hand crooked above his scalp as if testing the air for the place to strike. Black tattered drapery hung about it. Coarse hair covered it as in the drawing. The lower jaw was thin—shallow, almost bestial; teeth showed behind black lips; there was no nose. The eyes were fiery yellow, the pupils black and intense, and in them burned an exulting hate and thirst for life’s destruction that no animal could hold. Yet there was intelligence in the gaze—intelligence above a beast and below a man, as if it had learned enough of us to despise us.

Dennistoun’s fear was physical, like nausea; his loathing was mental, like a stain spreading across thought. What could he do? Later he could not be certain what words he spoke—only that he spoke, that his hand went blindly to the silver crucifix, that the thing moved toward him, and that he screamed with the voice of an animal in hideous pain.

Pierre and Bertrand—the two sturdy little serving-men—burst in. They saw nothing. Yet something passed out between them, thrusting them aside as if by a body that had weight and purpose. They found Dennistoun on the floor in a swoon.

They sat up with him that night. His friends arrived by nine next morning. He was shaken and nervous still, but near himself enough to tell them what had happened. At first they listened as friends do, half believing because they must, half resisting because they fear what belief would mean. Then they saw the drawing at the end of the folio, and their faces changed. After that, they spoke with the sacristan.

The little man came to the inn almost at dawn on some pretence and listened with intense interest to the landlady’s account of the night’s alarm. He showed no surprise.

“It is he—it is he! I have seen him myself,” was all he said; and to every question he returned only the same words, stubborn as a refrain: “_Deux fois je l’ai vu; mille fois je l’ai senti._” Twice I have seen him; a thousand times I have felt him.

He would say nothing of where the book had come from, nor of what had happened in his own house, nor of what Canon Alberic had done to earn such attendance. Only once, when pressed, did he answer with a weary gentleness that made his daughter begin to sob in the next room.

“I shall soon sleep,” he said, “and my rest will be sweet. Why should you trouble me?”

He died that summer. His daughter married and settled at St Papoul, never understanding the true circumstances of her father’s obsession.

What the canon suffered, or what bargains he struck in a language he could not wholly speak, we shall never know. Yet even in Dennistoun’s hands, the scrap-book kept a final hint of its own history: on the back of that fateful drawing were lines of writing—lines that might have explained the power and the price, had they not seemed to retreat from meaning the more one stared, like a dialect one has almost forgotten, whose sounds stir memory but refuse translation.

In September of 1811, when the year was already tipping toward its brown-and-gold decline, a post-chaise rolled up the drive and halted before Aswarby Hall. The boy who stepped down—Stephen Elliott, lately orphaned in Warwickshire—stood a moment on the gravel and looked as children do when they have been uprooted: with the whole face turned outward, as if the eyes could drink enough of a place to make it less strange.

Aswarby was a tall, square, red-brick house of Queen Anne time, wearing, like an older body forced into a newer fashion, a later classical porch set on stone pillars. Many narrow windows, each divided into small panes by thick white woodwork, caught the evening light and held it, so that the house seemed full of slow, contained fires. Wings stretched right and left, linked by glazed galleries on colonnades, and each wing carried an ornamental cupola topped with a gilded vane that spoke, in its quiet glitter, of expense spent for the sake of being seen.

Beyond the front lay the flat park—oaks scattered like deliberate punctuation, firs fringing the horizon. The church clock, half-hidden by trees at the park’s edge, struck six; the sound travelled as if it had weight, beating gently down the wind. Even the air seemed to have a flavour: damp earth pricked with fallen needles of pine, and the faint smoke-sweetness that rides from chimneys at supper time. It was a pleasant impression, and yet one touched with the melancholy proper to early autumn, when the light is kind but brief and everything audible seems to be retreating.

Stephen had been brought here because Mr Abney—an elderly cousin of whom he had heard little—had offered him a home. The offer had surprised those who knew anything of Aswarby’s master. Mr Abney’s neighbours pictured a thin, austere recluse, a man folded into his library, unlikely to tolerate a child’s footsteps and questions. Scholars spoke of him with another sort of awe: the Professor of Greek at Cambridge had once said no one knew more of the religious beliefs of the later pagans than the owner of Aswarby. His shelves held the Mysteries, the Orphic poems, Mithras, the Neo-Platonists—books that smelled of old calf and dust and the sour-sweet of ink long dried. In the marble-paved hall stood a group of Mithras slaying the bull, shipped at great cost from the Levant: carved muscle and stone violence under a ceiling that echoed each step.

Yet when the front door opened, it was not a cold figure who received Stephen. Mr Abney came out of his study with a brisk, almost eager energy, rubbing his hands as though delighted to feel his own warmth.

“How are you, my boy—how are you? How old are you?” he said; and then, as if the question were a point of taste that required insisting upon, he asked it again within minutes. He peered, smiling thinly, like a man checking the ripeness of fruit.

“I’m twelve years old next birthday, sir,” Stephen answered.

“And when is your birthday, my dear boy? Eleventh of September, eh? That’s well—that’s very well. Nearly a year hence, isn’t it? I like—ha, ha!—I like to get these things down in my book. Sure it’s twelve? Certain?”

Stephen assured him it was. Mr Abney’s satisfaction had something clipped and private about it, like a cook pleased with the weight of a joint but reluctant to show it.

“Well, well! Take him to Mrs Bunch’s room, Parkes, and let him have his tea—supper—whatever it is.”

The butler, Mr Parkes—steadily spoken, steady-footed—conducted Stephen down into the lower regions where heat lived more comfortably than it did in the high rooms. There, in the housekeeper’s room, he met Mrs Bunch.

Mrs Bunch was everything the Hall’s marble and scholarly coldness was not: broad-hipped, warm-handed, rich with the aromas of domestic work—soap, starch, stored apples, and the faintly greasy comfort of a kettle that has boiled a hundred times. She set Stephen down as if setting a child in the best chair were as natural as laying bread on a table. Tea came—strong, properly drawn, with milk warmed so it did not shock the cup; bread and butter spread thick; a bit of cold meat sliced with the careful thrift of a woman who knows how far provisions must stretch. The simple ceremony of it eased him. His appetite, stirred by travel, found in that hot drink and those plain, well-kept things a kind of anchoring.

Within a quarter of an hour they were friends. Within a week Stephen had discovered that friendship with Mrs Bunch meant access to Aswarby’s living memory. She had been born near the place, had been at the Hall twenty years, and knew the corners of it as one knows the shelves of one’s own pantry. She was not unwilling to tell what she knew.

Stephen, adventurous and inquisitive, plied her with questions while she worked—polishing, mending, portioning, sorting the day into its proper stacks. Who built the temple at the end of the laurel walk? Who was the old man in the picture on the staircase, seated with a skull beneath his hand? Mrs Bunch supplied answers as readily as she might supply a spoonful of jam to a hungry child. But there were subjects on which her explanations softened into unease, as milk does when it is near to turning.

One November evening, with rain whispering at the windows and the fire making a soft crackle that seemed to say the house was alive and listening, Stephen asked suddenly—trusting, as children do, that elders hold the keys to heaven and hell.

“Is Mr Abney a good man, and will he go to heaven?”

“Good?—bless the child!” said Mrs Bunch. “Master’s as kind a soul as ever I see! Didn’t I never tell you of the little boy as he took in out of the street, as you may say, this seven years back? and the little girl, two years after I first come here?”

Stephen leaned forward at once. “No. Do tell me all about them, Mrs Bunch—now, this minute!”

“Well,” she said, drawing her knitting closer as though it were a net she could keep safe things in, “the little girl I don’t seem to recollect so much about. I know master brought her back with him from his walk one day, and give orders to Mrs Ellis, as was housekeeper then, as she should be took every care with. And the pore child hadn’t no one belonging to her—she telled me so her own self—and here she lived with us a matter of three weeks it might be; and then, whether she were somethink of a gipsy in her blood or what not, but one morning she out of her bed afore any of us had opened a eye, and neither track nor yet trace of her have I set eyes on since.

“Master was wonderful put about, and had all the ponds dragged; but it’s my belief she was had away by them gipsies, for there was singing round the house for as much as an hour the night she went, and Parkes, he declare as he heard them a-calling in the woods all that afternoon. Dear, dear! a hodd child she was, so silent in her ways and all, but I was wonderful taken up with her, so domesticated she was—surprising.”

Stephen imagined that singing—human voices in the dark, the kind that carry farther than they ought, as if the trees themselves were throats. The idea frightened him and pleased him at once.

“And what about the little boy?” he asked.

“Ah, that pore boy!” Mrs Bunch sighed, and in the sigh the room seemed to cool. “He were a foreigner—Jevanny he called hisself—and he come a-tweaking his ’urdy-gurdy round and about the drive one winter day, and master ’ad him in that minute, and ast all about where he came from, and how old he was, and how he made his way, and where was his relatives, and all as kind as heart could wish. But it went the same way with him. They’re a hunruly lot, them foreign nations, I do suppose, and he was off one fine morning just the same as the girl. Why he went and what he done was our question for as much as a year after; for he never took his ’urdy-gurdy, and there it lays on the shelf.”

Stephen’s eyes went at once to the hurdy-gurdy—an object that looked absurdly cheerful in its promise of music, like a loaf that refuses to stale. He spent the remainder of the evening cross-examining Mrs Bunch, and then turning the crank and pressing the keys, trying to coax out a tune. The instrument gave him thin, stubborn noises, as if the wood remembered hands other than his and resented the substitution.

That night he dreamed.

At the top of the house, near his bedroom, there was an old disused bathroom. It was kept locked. The upper half of the door was glazed, and the muslin curtain that once softened that pane had long been removed. If you passed in the daytime you could look in and see the lead-lined bath fixed to the wall, its head toward the window.

Stephen dreamed he stood in the passage, looking through the glass. Moonlight lay in the bathroom like cold milk. In the bath lay a figure.

It was a thing of dreadful thinness, the sort of thin that does not suggest hunger alone but a stripping-away of everything that makes a person solid in the world. The skin looked dusty and leaden, as though it had been coloured by the bath itself; the body was wrapped in a shroud-like garment. The thin lips were crooked into a faint smile that seemed both pleading and mocking. The hands were pressed tight over the heart.

As Stephen stared, a distant moan—almost too quiet to be sound—seemed to form at the figure’s mouth, and the arms began to stir. Terror shoved him backward, and he woke—to find he was indeed standing on the cold boards of the passage, in the full moonlight, his feet bare, his throat dry, his skin prickling as if the night air had salted him.

He forced himself to go to the bathroom door. The lock held. He peered through the glass. The bath was empty. Only the moon’s pallor and the faint metallic smell of old plumbing met him.

He went back to bed.

In the morning he told Mrs Bunch, who was so impressed that she replaced a muslin curtain over the glazed door, as if a layer of cloth could keep out what glass had admitted. At breakfast Stephen confided his experience to Mr Abney as well. Mr Abney did not laugh it away. He grew interested—too interested—and made notes in a book he kept, as though Stephen’s fear were a specimen to be pinned and labelled.

As the spring equinox approached, Mr Abney spoke of it often. He said it had been considered by the ancients a critical time for the young; Stephen would do well to take care of himself and shut his bedroom window at night; Censorinus, he added, had valuable remarks on the subject. His counsel might have sounded like kindness, but the way he looked at Stephen when he said it made the boy feel, unreasonably, as if he were being measured.

Two incidents in March deepened that sensation into something worse.

After a night that left him oppressed and uneasy—though no dream clung to him in the morning—Mrs Bunch, the next evening, was mending his nightgown. She held it up to the lamplight, and her comfortable face tightened.

“Gracious me, Master Stephen!” she broke out, fretful. “How do you manage to tear your nightdress all to flinders this way? Look here, sir, what trouble you do give to poor servants that have to darn and mend after you!”

On the left side of the chest were long, parallel slits, six inches in length, as if something had raked and scored the linen. Some had not quite pierced through. They looked not like a tear from snagging on a nail but like marks made on purpose—patient, repeated, hungry.

Stephen stared, then felt his stomach sink with the strangeness of it. “Mrs Bunch, I don’t know. I’m sure they weren’t there last night. But—they’re just the same as the scratches on the outside of my bedroom door; and I’m sure I never had anything to do with making them.”

Mrs Bunch’s mouth fell open. She snatched up a candle and hurried out, her steps quick and heavy on the stairs. Stephen sat and listened to the house’s noises—old wood shifting, the distant faint rush of wind in a chimney—sounds that, on ordinary evenings, belonged to safety. Now they seemed like a language he did not know.

She returned in a few minutes, candle guttering.

“Well,” she said, trying to sound solid, “Master Stephen, it’s a funny thing to me how them marks and scratches can ’a’ come there—too high up for any cat or dog to ’ave made ’em, much less a rat: for all the world like a Chinaman’s finger-nails, as my uncle in the tea-trade used to tell us of when we was girls together. I wouldn’t say nothing to master, not if I was you, Master Stephen, my dear; and just turn the key of the door when you go to your bed.”

“I always do, Mrs Bunch, as soon as I’ve said my prayers.”

“Ah, that’s a good child: always say your prayers, and then no one can’t hurt you.”

She set to mending, drawing her needle in and out with the firm, domestic rhythm of someone stitching a tear in cloth because that is what one does. Yet even as she worked, she fell into pauses—moments where the needle hovered and her eyes went far away, as if she were listening not to Stephen but to something else in the house.

This was a Friday night in March, 1812.

On the following evening Stephen and Mrs Bunch were together again when the butler, Mr Parkes, came in abruptly. Parkes as a rule kept to his pantry; now he was flustered, and his voice had lost its slow steadiness.

“Master may get up his own wine, if he likes, of an evening,” he said at once. “Either I do it in the daytime or not at all, Mrs Bunch. I don’t know what it may be: very like it’s the rats, or the wind got into the cellars; but I’m not so young as I was, and I can’t go through with it as I have done.”

“Well, Mr Parkes, you know it is a surprising place for the rats, is the Hall.”

“I’m not denying that, Mrs Bunch; and, to be sure, many a time I’ve heard the tale from the men in the shipyards about the rat that could speak. I never laid no confidence in that before; but tonight, if I’d demeaned myself to lay my ear to the door of the further bin, I could pretty much have heard what they was saying.”

Mrs Bunch bristled. “Oh, there, Mr Parkes, I’ve no patience with your fancies! Rats talking in the wine-cellar indeed!”

“Well, Mrs Bunch, I’ve no wish to argue with you: all I say is, if you choose to go to the far bin, and lay your ear to the door, you may prove my words this minute.”

“What nonsense you do talk, Mr Parkes—not fit for children to listen to! Why, you’ll be frightening Master Stephen there out of his wits.”

“What! Master Stephen?” Parkes said, suddenly aware. “Master Stephen knows well enough when I’m a-playing a joke with you, Mrs Bunch.”

Stephen did know, and that knowledge made him colder. Parkes had not been joking at first. Yet no amount of questioning could coax a fuller account from him. Parkes retreated into silence, as if speech itself might invite something nearer.

Then came March the twenty-fourth, 1812.

It was a windy, noisy day that seemed to worry the very walls. Air rushed and worried at the house’s corners; branches scraped and sighed; the cupolas’ vanes turned and flicked with metallic impatience. In the gardens, the laurel leaves rattled like dry scales. Even indoors the sound pressed in—restless, infiltrating—so that the Hall felt less like shelter and more like a shell held to the ear, full of distant, moving sea.

Stephen, standing by the fence and looking out into the park, had a queer sensation that the wind was crowded. It felt as if an endless procession of unseen people were being swept past him—borne resistlessly, aimlessly—forever reaching out to catch at something solid, something living, and forever failing. It was not a thought he could have put into fine words, but it struck him in the body: a tightening in the chest, a prickle at the scalp, a longing to run inside and bolt doors against something that had no hands.

After luncheon Mr Abney said, in a tone that tried to sound casual and did not succeed: “Stephen, my boy, do you think you could manage to come to me tonight as late as eleven o’clock in my study? I shall be busy until that time, and I wish to show you something connected with your future life which it is most important that you should know. You are not to mention this matter to Mrs Bunch nor to anyone else in the house; and you had better go to your room at the usual time.”

To a twelve-year-old, permission to sit up late is its own illicit feast. Stephen’s fear and curiosity mingled until he could not tell one from the other. That evening, going upstairs, he looked in at the library door. The room’s familiar smells—dust, leather, cold stone—were overlaid by another: incense, resinous and sharp, like crushed pine and bitter herbs.

A brazier, usually in the corner, had been dragged out before the fire. On the table stood an old silver-gilt cup filled with red wine—dark, viscous-looking in the lamplight, like blood warmed to a civilized gloss. Near it lay written sheets of paper. Mr Abney, intent, sprinkled incense from a round silver box onto the brazier’s coals. The smoke rose in deliberate curls, and Stephen had a sudden, absurd thought of kitchens: how certain spices bloom when heated, releasing what they hold. This was like that, and not like that at all.

Mr Abney did not seem to notice Stephen’s step.

The wind fell as night came. A full moon made the gardens and terraces look bleached and too clean. At about ten, Stephen stood at his open bedroom window and looked out over the country. The air was cold and had the scent of water. Across the mere the woods were a darker band, their outlines cut sharp by moonlight.

Still as the night was, it did not sound empty. From time to time cries rose from across the water—thin, despairing, like lost wanderers calling without hope of answer. They might have been owls. They might have been water-birds. Yet they did not quite resemble either. The sounds did not sit properly in the ear; they seemed to strike the mind directly, a kind of acoustic bruise.

Were they coming nearer?

Now they sounded from the nearer side of the water. Then, in a few minutes, they seemed to float about among the shrubberies beneath Stephen’s window. He could not locate them, could not pin them to any throat or branch. The house, with its many rooms, seemed to hold its breath.

The cries ceased. Stephen, shivering, began to think of shutting the window and returning to his book—he had been reading *Robinson Crusoe*, and the thought of an island with clear edges and ordinary hunger appealed to him—when he caught sight of two figures on the gravelled terrace along the garden side of the Hall.

A boy and a girl, it seemed. They stood side by side, looking up at the windows as if they knew exactly which one belonged to him.

Something in the girl’s shape recalled, with a sick certainty, the figure from the bath. She stood still, half smiling, with her hands clasped over her heart, as though guarding it. The boy beside her inspired sharper fear: a thin shape with black hair and ragged clothing, raising his arms in a gesture not of greeting but of demand, menace, and an unappeasable hunger. The moon shone through his hands—almost transparent—and Stephen saw the nails: fearfully long, catching the light, seeming to let it pass through them like glass.

When the boy lifted his arms, he disclosed a horror in his own body. On the left side of his chest there was a black, gaping rent. It was not a wound that had bled and closed; it was an absence. And into Stephen’s mind—not so much into his ear—fell the impression of one of those desolate cries he had heard over the woods, as if the sound had been searching for a mouth and had found his thoughts instead.

In another moment the pair moved swiftly and noiselessly over the dry gravel, and were gone.

Stephen’s fear became a single command: go to Mr Abney. Whatever waited at eleven, it was at least in a room with a living man—if Mr Abney were living. Candle in hand, he hurried down the passages. The Hall’s sounds changed with his movement: each stair creak, each soft rattle of flame in the candle, each distant tick of a clock seemed magnified, as though the house were listening to him go.

The study door was not locked; the key, as usual, sat on the outside. Stephen knocked. No answer. He knocked again, harder.

Inside, someone was speaking—Mr Abney’s voice, but not in the tones Stephen knew. It was urgent, strained. And then—what was that?—as though a cry had tried to break free and been choked, pressed back into the throat. The sound made Stephen’s hair lift at the nape of his neck.

Then everything went quiet.

He pushed at the door. It yielded.

What Stephen saw that night, he could not fully understand then. Later, when he was older, certain papers found on Mr Abney’s table explained the chain of events with a clarity that turned memory into nausea.

On the table were written sheets—Mr Abney’s hand, neat and cold. There were sentences Stephen would come to know by heart against his will:

“It was a belief very strongly and generally held by the ancients—of whose wisdom in these matters I have had such experience as induces me to place confidence in their assertions—that by enacting certain processes, which to us moderns have something of a barbaric complexion, a very remarkable enlightenment of the spiritual faculties in man may be attained: that, for example, by absorbing the personalities of a certain number of his fellow-creatures, an individual may gain a complete ascendancy over those orders of spiritual beings which control the elemental forces of our universe.

“It is recorded of Simon Magus that he was able to fly in the air, to become invisible, or to assume any form he pleased, by the agency of the soul of a boy whom, to use the libellous phrase employed by the author of the *Clementine Recognitions*, he had ‘murdered’. I find it set down, moreover, with considerable detail in the writings of Hermes Trismegistus, that similar happy results may be produced by the absorption of the hearts of not less than three human beings below the age of twenty-one years. To the testing of the truth of this receipt I have devoted the greater part of the last twenty years, selecting as the *corpora vilia* of my experiment such persons as could conveniently be removed without occasioning a sensible gap in society.

“The first step I effected by the removal of one Phoebe Stanley, a girl of gipsy extraction, on March 24, 1792. The second, by the removal of a wandering Italian lad, named Giovanni Paoli, on the night of March 23, 1805. The final ‘victim’—to employ a word repugnant in the highest degree to my feelings—must be my cousin, Stephen Elliott. His day must be this March 24, 1812.

“The best means of effecting the required absorption is to remove the heart from the *living* subject, to reduce it to ashes, and to mingle them with about a pint of some red wine, preferably port. The remains of the first two subjects, at least, it will be well to conceal: a disused bathroom or wine-cellar will be found convenient for such a purpose. Some annoyance may be experienced from the psychic portion of the subjects, which popular language dignifies with the name of ghosts. But the man of philosophic temperament—to whom alone the experiment is appropriate—will be little prone to attach importance to the feeble efforts of these beings to wreak their vengeance on him. I contemplate with the liveliest satisfaction the enlarged and emancipated existence which the experiment, if successful, will confer on me; not only placing me beyond the reach of human justice (so-called), but eliminating to a great extent the prospect of death itself.”

On the table, too, stood the silver-gilt cup of red wine. The incense smell lay thick, coating the air like a spice meant to mask another odour. The brazier’s coals glowed dull and steady. Everything had the careful arrangement of a ritual—and yet something in the room had been violently interrupted, overturned without moving a single object.

Mr Abney was in his chair. His head was thrown back. His face—so often dry, measured, interested—was stamped now with an expression Stephen could not have imagined on it: rage, fright, mortal pain, all contorted together as though the man had tried to keep his composure to the last and been torn out of it.

In his left side was a terrible lacerated wound, exposing the heart. There was no blood on his hands. A long knife lay on the table, perfectly clean.

It looked, in the simplest telling, as though some savage wild-cat might have done it—some creature from the woods, driven in through the open window by hunger or chance. The study window indeed was open, and the cool night air came in with the damp smell of the gardens.

That was the coroner’s opinion when the body was found: death by the agency of some wild creature.

But Stephen—who had seen, in moonlight, two children on the terrace, and the black rent in the boy’s chest, and the long, shining nails—carried within him another conclusion. The Hall’s domestic sounds, which had once promised safety—the kettle’s murmur in Mrs Bunch’s room, the tick of clocks, the familiar creak of stairs—were afterward never quite the same to his ear. In quiet places, long after, he would find himself listening for cries that did not belong to any bird, and for footsteps that crossed dry gravel without making a sound.

I dare say you remember that I once spoke of Dennistoun’s experience—how a respectable pursuit, carried on among catalogues and glass cases, may suddenly find itself breathing air that is older, colder, and not meant for scholars. He was not a man to print such things in the newspapers; but news of it travelled, as news does in colleges, along dinner-tables and through common-rooms, until it reached the ears of another custodian of art: Mr Williams, who had charge of a museum at a different University.

Williams comforted himself, as men will, by arranging his apprehensions according to departments. Dennistoun had been hunting manuscripts on the Continent; Williams dealt, in the main, with English topographical engravings—parish churches, market towns, manor-houses—homely things, the sort that hang in inn parlours above the settle where the bacon scent lives in the wood, and no one expects trouble beyond a cracked pane and a draught. His days were paced by ordinary sounds: the soft scrape of paper in drawers, the distant bell for chapel, the clink of cups at tea, and, now and again, the unlovely shriek of some modern contrivance from the street outside—omnibuses and carts that seemed, in their clatter, to drown out the older, kinder acoustics of the place. It annoyed him more than he admitted; there are noises that do not merely offend the ear but thin the mind, as too much heat thins a sauce.

Among those who collected such views there was one London dealer whose catalogues were as necessary as salt: Mr J. W. Britnell. He issued, at intervals, neat lists of engravings and old sketches—mansion fronts, bridges, spires—always shifting stock, always the insinuation that this or that would never be seen again. Williams was a steady customer rather than an eager one. His museum already possessed mountains of prints; he bought to fill gaps, not to chase rarities.

One February morning, however, a Britnell catalogue lay on his desk, and with it a typewritten note:

DEAR SIR, We beg to call your attention to No. 978 in our accompanying catalogue, which we shall be glad to send on approval.

Yours faithfully,  
J. W. BRITNELL.

No. 978 read:

978.—Unknown. Interesting mezzotint: View of a manor-house, early part of the century. 15 by 10 inches; black frame. £2 2s.

Two guineas for an “unknown” was impudence, and for a mezzotint—worse, an indifferent one—little short of a practical joke. Yet Britnell knew his men; and curiosity, like a strong kitchen smell, slips under doors you believed shut. Williams sent a postcard asking that No. 978 be forwarded on approval, along with a handful of other trifles.

The parcel arrived, as parcels do, one day later than it should. It came on the Saturday afternoon after Williams had left the museum, and was carried round to his rooms in college by the attendant so he need not wait through Sunday before sorting and returning what he did not want. He found it there when he came in to tea with a friend.

I must speak of tea, because it matters more than one would think. In Williams’s rooms the kettle’s rising hiss and the small percussion of spoon against cup were the domestic counterpoint to everything that followed; and the smell—India leaf opened by boiling water, a slice of bread toasted to the edge of bitterness, butter softening—made the place feel immune, for a while, from any intrusion that had not paid at the buttery. The room had the safe familiar noises of an old building: a tick from the clock, a faint settling in the woodwork, the muted rumble of footsteps in the stair beyond.

Among the contents of Britnell’s parcel the mezzotint dominated—large, black-framed. At first glance it was precisely the kind of thing you find in country passages where the air smells of polish and damp wool: a full-face view of a not very large manor-house of the last century, three rows of plain sashed windows set in rusticated masonry, a parapet with balls or urns at the corners, and a small portico in the centre. Trees flanked it; lawn spread before it. The narrow margin bore the legend A. W. F. sculpsit, and there was no further inscription.

Indifferent mezzotints have a peculiar deadness: the velvety blacks are muddy, the half-tones bruised. This one had that amateurish look, as if the hand that rocked the plate had done so with more enthusiasm than skill. Williams turned it over contemptuously. On the back was a paper label torn away on the left; two line-ends remained, like a name half-chewed: the first showed …ngley Hall; the second …ssex.

“Somethingley Hall,” Williams said, and felt the irritation of it—like a recipe missing its chief spice. “Either Sussex or Essex.”

He meant to identify it by gazetteer, if only to write to Britnell with remarks on his judgement. He lit candles, because dusk had thickened outside, and the candle-flames made their own gentle sound, a soft prickling in the wick. He poured tea, set out bread and something of cold meat—college fare, serviceable, but Williams took a certain pleasure even in that: the edge of salt, the grain of mustard, the reassuring chew.

His companion, a golfing man, looked about and let his eye be caught by the black frame. Let us call him Professor Binks.

“What’s this place, Williams?”

“Just what I’m going to try to find out,” said Williams, reaching for a gazetteer on the shelf. “Look at the back. Somethingley Hall, in Sussex or Essex. Half the name’s gone. You don’t happen to know it?”

“It’s from Britnell, I suppose?” said Binks. “Is it for the museum?”

“If it was five shillings I should buy it,” said Williams; “but for some unearthly reason he asks two guineas. It’s a wretched engraving, and there aren’t even any figures to give it life.”

Binks, still holding his cup, tilted the picture to the candlelight.

“It’s not worth two guineas,” he said, “but I don’t think it’s so badly done. The moonlight seems rather good to me; and I should have thought there were figures—at least a figure—just on the edge in front.”

“Let’s look,” said Williams, annoyed at having missed anything.

And indeed there was—hardly more than a black blot at the extreme edge: the head and shoulders of a person muffled up, back turned to the spectator, looking towards the house.

“Still,” said Williams, “I can’t spend two guineas of museum money on a view of a place I can’t name.”

Binks went off to his work, and Williams, for the better part of the evening, wrestled with his gazetteer. Names ending in -ngley proliferated like weeds: if only the vowel before ng had survived on the label he could have pinned it at once. As it was, it might be anything—Guestingley, Langley, Annesley—counties and parishes sliding past with no index of terminations to help him. The book itself seemed to resist, its pages making that dry whisper like old flour sifted through fingers.

Hall in his college was at seven; and there, amid the clatter of knives and the polite roar of talk, he met colleagues who had also been out on the links. Later, in common-room, there was the usual migration—some to one set of rooms, some to another. A few came back with Williams for whist and tobacco. The soundscape thickened: cards flicked, chairs shifted, a match struck with a brief sulphurous smell, then the steady rasp of smoke drawn through pipes. The college, with its old stones, held those noises and warmed to them as a kitchen warms to the low stir of pots.

During a lull Williams picked up the mezzotint without really looking at it and handed it to one of the party, a man mildly interested in art, supplying the few particulars.

The gentleman glanced, then straightened a little.

“It’s really a very good thing, Williams. Quite a romantic feeling. The light is admirably managed, and the figure—though rather grotesque—is somehow very impressive.”

“Yes, isn’t it?” said Williams, who at that moment was mixing whisky-and-soda and could not cross at once to examine it again.

The visitors went. Williams wrote letters, tidied papers, performed the small rituals by which a day is shut away. At last, some time past midnight, he carried his bedroom candle across from the sitting-room after putting out the lamp. The mezzotint lay face-up on the table where the last man had left it. The candlelight fell on it, and what it showed made Williams’s hand jerk so that the candle guttered, and he all but dropped it. He swore afterward that if the flame had gone out then, he would have had a fit; for there was no room in him at that moment for darkness.

He set the candle down and looked again, close, with the stubborn thoroughness of a curator who knows how easily the eye invents.

In the middle of the lawn—where there had been nothing at tea-time—there was now a figure.

It was crawling on all fours towards the house, muffled in a strange black garment, and on its back was a white cross.

Nothing in the ordinary life of a college trains a man for this. One may read of miracles and frauds, study pigments and plates, and still be unready for an object on your own table deciding to alter itself. Williams did what he could do. He took the framed print by one corner and carried it across the passage to a second set of rooms he possessed. There he locked it in a drawer, sported the doors of both sets of rooms, and went to bed. But before he slept he sat down, while his hands still shook, and wrote out and signed an account of the change he had seen.

Sleep came late. Yet there was a comfort in remembering the other man’s remark—he had spoken of the figure, too. So it was not merely Williams’s eyes, nor his mind, beginning to sour. In the morning two matters pressed on him as insistently as hunger: he must examine the picture again with a witness; and he must, at any cost, find the house’s name.

He asked his neighbour, Nisbet, to breakfast.

Nisbet arrived about half-past nine. Williams was not fully dressed—an omission that would have troubled him on any other Sunday. Breakfast in college rooms has its own sensory decency: the snap of a crust, the steam from a dish, the clink of a pot lid, and, if the windows are shut, the muffled quiet that makes the world outside seem momentarily irrelevant. They talked as two Fellows will—of games, of people, of the tiresome modern habit of importing noise into places built for voices. Yet Williams’s attention kept pulling away, as a tongue keeps worrying a sore tooth, to the other room where the mezzotint lay face down in the drawer.

At last pipes were lit; the smell of tobacco rose, dry and sweet, and the moment had come.

With an excitement that made him almost tremble, Williams ran across, unlocked the drawer, took out the picture—still face down—and hurried back to Nisbet. He put it into his hands.

“Now,” he said, “I want you to tell me exactly what you see. Describe it. Minutely. I’ll tell you why afterward.”

Nisbet studied it, calm in the manner of a man who expects only art.

“Well. I have here a view of a country-house—English, I presume—by moonlight.”

“Moonlight? You’re sure?”

“Certainly. The moon is on the wane, and there are clouds.”

“All right. Go on.” Williams muttered aside, “There was no moon when I saw it first.”

“There’s not much more,” Nisbet continued. “Three rows of windows, five in each row, except the bottom where the porch takes the middle, and—”

“But what about figures?” Williams broke in.

“There aren’t any,” said Nisbet. “Not a one. But there is something else.”

“What?”

“One of the ground-floor windows—left of the door—is open.”

“Open?” Williams stared, then blurted, with a kind of horrid exhilaration: “My God—he must have got in.”

He snatched the print, went behind the sofa to look for himself. It was true. No figure on the lawn. The window stood open, a square of deeper black.

For a moment he could not speak. Then he went to his writing-table and wrote rapidly. He brought two papers to Nisbet: the first, Nisbet’s description written out by Williams as he spoke; the second, Williams’s signed statement from last night.

Nisbet read, and looked up.

“What can it mean?”

“Exactly,” said Williams. “Three things, then. I must find out from Garwood”—the visitor who had last praised the figure—“what he saw. We must get it photographed before it changes again. And we must find the place.”

“I can photograph it,” said Nisbet. “But it looks uncommonly as though we’re watching a tragedy being worked out somewhere. The question is, has it happened already, or is it to happen?”

“I’ll take it to old Green,” Williams said. “He’ll know the house. We have property in Essex and Sussex; he’s been all over.”

Nisbet hesitated. “Green’s gone down for the Sunday. I didn’t see him in Hall.”

“Yes—Brighton,” said Williams, remembering. “Then do the photograph now. I’ll go to Garwood and get his statement. Keep an eye on it while I’m gone.”

“I’m beginning to think,” he added grimly, “that two guineas is not an exorbitant price.”

He returned shortly with Garwood. Garwood’s recollection was that when he had seen the figure it had been clear of the edge but not far across the lawn. He remembered a white mark on the back, though he could not swear it was a cross. This also was written down and signed; and Nisbet set up his apparatus and photographed the engraving.

“What now?” Nisbet asked. “Are you going to sit and watch it all day?”

“No,” Williams said. “I rather imagine we’re meant to see the whole thing. Between last night and this morning there was time enough for a dozen changes, yet the creature only got into the house. It might have done its business and gone; but the open window suggests it’s there now. And I doubt it will change much in daylight. We’ll go for a walk this afternoon. Come in at tea—when it’s dark. I’ll leave it on the table and sport the door. My skip can get in, no one else.”

They agreed. Also, for prudence: if they kept together they would be less likely to let the matter slip to others, and any rumour of such a thing would fetch the whole Phasmatological Society buzzing like flies to a butcher’s stall.

So we may leave them till five.

At that hour—or near it—they climbed Williams’s staircase. They were mildly vexed to see the door unsported, until they remembered that on Sundays the skips came earlier for orders. Then they entered and found something that struck them silent.

The mezzotint stood as they had left it, propped against books. Opposite it, seated in Williams’s chair, was the skip himself—Mr Filcher (I do not invent the name)—staring at the picture with naked horror.

Filcher was a servant of standing, the man who set etiquette like a recipe sets proportions. Nothing could be more foreign to his practice than taking his master’s chair, or appearing to scrutinize his master’s belongings. He seemed, at their entry, to recall this with a jolt. He sprang up, pale under his respectable years, and made an effort at composure.

“I ask your pardon, sir, for taking such a freedom as to set down.”

“Not at all, Robert,” said Williams, quickly. “I’ve been meaning to ask what you thought of that picture.”

Filcher glanced, as if unwilling, toward the black frame.

“Well, sir—of course I don’t set up my opinion against yours—but it ain’t the pictur I should hang where my little girl could see it, sir.”

“Wouldn’t you? Why not?”

“No, sir. The pore child—I recollect once she see a Doré Bible, with pictures not half what that is, and we had to set up with her three or four nights after, if you’ll believe me. And if she was to ketch sight of this skelinton here—or whatever it is—carrying off the pore baby, she’d be in a taking. Children’s that nervish, sir; a little thing lays hold of ’em. And what I should say, it don’t seem a right pictur to be laying about, sir, not where anyone that’s liable to be startled could come on it. Should you be wanting anything this evening, sir? Thank you, sir.”

He went out, restored as best he could to duty; and the three men were already round the table.

The house stood as before under a waning moon and drifting clouds. But the window that had been open was shut. And the figure was on the lawn again—only not crawling now.

It was upright and stepping swiftly with long strides toward the front edge of the picture, as if returning the way it had come. The moon lay behind it. The black drapery hung down over its face so that only hints could be seen—enough to make the watchers grateful for shadow. There was a white, dome-like forehead, and a few straggling hairs. The head bent down; the arms were clamped around an object held tight to the chest: a child. Whether living or dead no one could tell in that murk of inked night. The legs, however, were plain, and horribly thin.

From five to seven they took turns watching, as if attention itself were a charm; and all that time the mezzotint did not change. At last they agreed it was safe to leave it and go to Hall, and return afterward to see what the night would bring.

When they assembled again at the earliest possible moment, the figure was gone. The house lay quiet, the lawn empty under the moonbeams, as though nothing had ever crossed it.

There remained only the ugly labour of books. They sat late with gazetteers and guide-books, turning pages that smelled of dust and glue, chasing the torn label’s ending. At half-past eleven Williams, stubborn with fatigue, struck luck. In Murray’s Guide to Essex he read:

“16½ miles, Anningley. The church has been an interesting building of Norman date, but was extensively classicized in the last century. It contains the tomb of the family of Francis, whose mansion, Anningley Hall, a solid Queen Anne house, stands immediately beyond the churchyard in a park of about 80 acres. The family is now extinct, the last heir having disappeared mysteriously in infancy in the year 1802. The father, Mr Arthur Francis, was locally known as a talented amateur engraver in mezzotint. After his son’s disappearance he lived in complete retirement at the Hall, and was found dead in his studio on the third anniversary of the disaster, having just completed an engraving of the house, impressions of which are of considerable rarity.”

There was a silence after that, the kind in which one becomes aware again of every small sound: the clock’s tick, the coal’s faint settling in the grate, the far-off, softened rumour of town life—wheels, voices, a modern din that seemed suddenly indecent beside the old, quiet horror they had uncovered, as if commerce and noise could sell anything and drown any memory, even one written in a dead child’s vanishing.

Mr Green, when he returned, identified the house at once as Anningley Hall.

“Is there any kind of explanation of the figure, Green?” Williams asked, for now the name had made it worse, not better; now the thing had a parish and a date, and therefore a place in human language, which is never quite large enough for what it is asked to hold.

“I don’t know, I’m sure, Williams,” said Green. “What used to be said there when I first knew it—before I came up here—was this. Old Francis was always very much down on these poaching fellows, and whenever he got a chance he’d have a man he suspected turned off the estate, and by degrees he got rid of them all but one. Squires could do a lot then that they daren’t think of now. Well, the man that was left was what you find pretty often in that country—the last remains of a very old family. I believe they were Lords of the Manor at one time. I recollect just the same thing in my own parish.”

“What,” Williams put in, “like the man in Tess of the D’Urbervilles?”

Everyone who has crossed the low counties of the east knows the sort of house I mean: the modest country-seat that looks as if it had always been a little cold in the joints; a pale, square block in the Italian manner, set down amid eighty or a hundred acres of park and mere, with reed-beds whispering at the edges and a belt of woods holding the horizon like a dark crust on the sky. The approach is commonly bordered with split-oak paling silvered by weather; there are noble trees that taste of rain when you pass beneath them; there is, often enough, a pillared portico grafted onto an older English core, as though some late-eighteenth-century hand had tried to stucco over the accent of the past and make the whole building speak a smoother, imported tongue.

Inside, such houses keep their own domestic music: the hollow boom of a hall that goes up to the roof, the thin, ecclesiastical voice of a little organ no one plays properly, the soft scuff of house-shoes on flags, and—if you are fortunate—the deeper, kinder rhythm of kitchen life. The day’s bread comes up like an answer to damp walls; the roast turns, and the fat hisses; herbs are bruised on a board, and for a while the air forgets it belongs to a fenland and smells instead of warmth, pepper, and browned butter. It is in these places, where the everyday routine of meals and the keeping of households persists against weather and age, that old tales lodge most stubbornly—like a word in a dialect you can still taste on the tongue, though no one round you speaks it any more.

Castringham Hall in Suffolk was such a house. There have been alterations since the time of my story, but the essential features remain: the Italian portico, the square whitened front, the park and fringe of woods, and the mere. One thing, however—one particular note in its soundscape—has gone. In the years with which I am concerned, there stood, on the right of the house as you looked from the park, a great old ash-tree, within half a dozen yards of the wall, its branches almost touching the building. It had likely been there since Castringham ceased to be fortified, since the moat was filled and the Elizabethan dwelling rose; and by 1690 it had nearly reached the full reach of its limbs and roots.

In that year the district was busy with witch-trials. It is fashionable, now, to speak of such terrors as though they were mere smoke without fire, a contagious superstition that can be swept away with a modern broom. Yet there is a difference between dismissing fear and explaining it; and there are events—set down soberly in parish papers and family notes—that do not submit easily to either. I offer what follows, not as a vindication of old cruelties, but because the tale will not lie flat under the foot.

Castringham contributed a victim. Mrs Mothersole was her name; and though she differed from the usual village witch in being rather better off and holding a more influential place, she was still, to the parish, a woman whose speech did not quite match the dominant lexicon of church and hall. A few reputable farmers did what they could to save her, testifying to her character, anxious for the verdict. It was not village malice that finished her, but the evidence of the proprietor of Castringham Hall—Sir Matthew Fell.

He swore that on three different occasions, at the full moon, he had watched her from his window gathering sprigs “from the ash-tree near my house.” She had climbed among the branches clad only in her shift, and was cutting small twigs with a peculiarly curved knife. As she cut she seemed to be speaking to herself—muttering in that low, inward way which, in the ears of those who cannot catch the words, sounds like conjuration even when it is only a private dialect of thought. Each time Sir Matthew had tried to capture her; each time, some accidental noise—his own tread, the click of a latch, the protest of a board—had made her start, and when he reached the garden all he saw was a hare running across the path towards the village.

On the third night he followed at speed and went straight to Mrs Mothersole’s cottage. He battered at her door for a quarter of an hour. At last she came out, very cross, very sleepy, like one dragged from bed; and he had no decent explanation for his visit.

Mainly on this evidence, with much more of a less striking kind from other neighbours, Mrs Mothersole was found guilty and condemned. A week after the trial she was hanged with five or six other unhappy creatures at Bury St Edmunds.

Sir Matthew Fell, then Deputy-Sheriff, attended the execution. It was a damp, drizzly morning in March: the sort of wet that soaks sound as well as cloth, turning voices dull and hooves into a thick, sucking percussion on the rough grass as the cart went up the hill outside Northgate where the gallows stood. The other victims were apathetic or broken down; but Mrs Mothersole, as in life, was of another temper. Her rage—so an account of the time has it—worked on the bystanders, even on the hangman, until people said she showed “the living Aspect of a mad Divell.” She offered no resistance; she only looked at those who laid hands on her with a venomous intensity that, one of them said later, gnawed at him for six months.

Of words, she gave very few, and those few had the air of nonsense. “There will be guests at the Hall,” she said—more than once, and under her breath.

Sir Matthew was not unimpressed. On the journey home from the assize he spoke of it with the vicar of the parish, Mr Crome, who travelled with him. Sir Matthew’s evidence had not been given willingly; he was not a man infected with the witch-finding mania, and liked to be on pleasant terms with those about him. Yet he declared, then and afterwards, that he could not give any other account than he had given, and could not have been mistaken in what he saw. There had been a duty; he had done it. Mr Crome applauded him, as any reasonable man of the time would have done, employing the approved vocabulary with which law and pulpit justified their weight upon the powerless.

A few weeks later, when May’s moon was full, vicar and squire met again in the park and walked to the Hall together. Lady Fell was away with her mother, dangerously ill, and Sir Matthew, alone, pressed Mr Crome to take a late supper.

There are small domestic details that persist in the record because they are always present when life is present: the lamp-smell in a passage, the faint sourness of yesterday’s fires, the way cold meat tastes when it has been carved too early and left to the pantry air. Sir Matthew had his evening drink, too—small ale, in a silver vessel of about a pint measure. It stood later by his bedside like a humble sacrament of habit.

He was not good company that evening. They talked of family and parish matters. Sir Matthew made, as it happened, a written memorandum of certain wishes regarding his estate—one of those practical pieces of housekeeping which men believe will secure continuity, as though ink could bind fate as well as lawyers.

At about half past nine, when the vicar prepared to go, the two took a turn on the gravel walk behind the house. The air had that May softness which lets you smell leaves even in darkness; and somewhere out on the mere a bird gave a single, thick note and stopped, as if listening to itself. They came in sight of the ash-tree near the windows. Sir Matthew halted.

“What is that,” he said, “that runs up and down the stem of the ash? It is never a squirrel? They will all be in their nests by now.”

Mr Crome looked. He saw the moving creature—something that clung and darted—but in the moonlight he could make nothing of its colour. Only the outline, sharp for an instant, printed itself on his mind. He afterwards said that, foolish as it sounded, squirrel or not, it had more than four legs.

There was no time to make more of it. They parted; and though they may have met again, it was not for nearly twenty years.

Next day Sir Matthew was not downstairs at six, as was his custom; nor at seven; nor at eight. Servants knocked. Anxiety thickened the air of the corridor, and the hush between knocks seemed to have weight. At last the door was opened from outside, and they found their master dead and black. There were no obvious marks of violence at first glance; but the window stood open.

A man was sent for the parson; another rode for the coroner. Mr Crome came quickly and was shown into the chamber where the corpse lay. Among his papers he left notes that show genuine respect and sorrow; and one passage is worth setting down because it throws light not only on what was seen, but on how people’s minds of the time sought for meaning—snatching at phrases and making them bear burdens they were never meant to carry, as though scripture were not a language of consolation but a code.

“There was not any the least Trace of an Entrance having been forc’d to the Chamber: but the Casement stood open, as my poor Friend would always have it in this Season. He had his Evening Drink of small Ale in a silver vessel of about a pint measure, and tonight had not drunk it out. This Drink was examined by the Physician from Bury, a Mr Hodgkins, who could not, however, as he afterwards declar’d upon his Oath, before the Coroner’s quest, discover that any matter of a venomous kind was present in it. For, as was natural, in the great Swelling and Blackness of the Corpse, there was talk made among the Neighbours of Poyson. The Body was very much Disorder’d as it laid in the Bed, being twisted after so extream a sort as gave too probable Conjecture that my worthy Friend and Patron had expir’d in great Pain and Agony. And what is as yet unexplain’d… was this, that the Women which were entrusted with the laying-out of the Corpse and washing it… came to me… saying… that they had no sooner touch’d the Breast of the Corpse with their naked Hands than they were sensible of a more than ordinary violent Smart and Acheing in their Palms… so immoderately… that… during many weeks they were forc’d to lay by the exercise of their Calling; and yet no mark seen on the Skin.

“Upon hearing this, I sent for the Physician… and we made as carefull a Proof as we were able… by the Help of a small Magnifying Lens… but could not detect… any Matter of Importance beyond a couple of small Punctures or Pricks… concluded… the Spotts by which the Poyson might be introduced…

“…There was on the Table by the Beddside a Bible… in which my Friend… used nightly… to read a sett Portion. And I taking it up… it came into my Thoughts… to make trial of that old… Practice of drawing the Sortes… I made, then, three trials… which gave in the first… from Luke xiii. 7, Cut it down; in the second, Isaiah xiii. 20, It shall never be inhabited; and upon the third… Job xxxix. 30, Her young ones also suck up blood.”

That is enough. Sir Matthew Fell was coffined and buried. Mr Crome preached his funeral sermon the following Sunday under the title “The Unsearchable Way; or, England’s Danger and the Malicious Dealings of Antichrist,” for it was his view—and the neighbourhood’s—that the squire had fallen to a recrudescence of the Popish Plot. When a community loses the finer words that might hold a private dread, it reaches for the grand, approved terms of public fear. The result is always the same: a huge explanation that does not fit the small, intimate facts—an imported, dominant vocabulary laid over something local and untranslatable.

Sir Matthew’s son—Sir Matthew the second—succeeded to title and estate. Thus ends the first act of the Castringham tragedy. It is not surprising that the new baronet did not occupy the chamber in which his father died. Indeed it was seldom slept in during his time, save by the occasional visitor. He died in 1735; and nothing particular is recorded of his reign except a curiously constant mortality among his cattle and live-stock, which tended to increase slightly as the years went on.

Those who care for such details may find a statistical account in a letter to the Gentleman’s Magazine of 1772, drawn from the baronet’s own papers. He ended it at last by a simple expedient: he shut all his beasts in sheds at night and kept no sheep in the park. Nothing that slept indoors was attacked. After that, the disorder confined itself to wild birds and beasts of chase. As we have no good medical account of symptoms and the all-night watching that was tried yielded no clue, I say no more of what the Suffolk farmers called the “Castringham sickness.” Only this: there is a kind of terror that moves by night and chooses, with an uncanny precision, whatever is left outside the house’s circle of human sound—the latch, the hearth, the sleepy clatter of a bucket, the comfort-noise of living bodies under a roof. It is as though the boundary between domestic routine and myth were marked not by walls but by the ordinary acoustics of shelter.

In 1735, Sir Matthew the second was succeeded by his son, Sir Richard. In his time the great family pew was built on the north side of the parish church. His notions were so large that several graves on that unhallowed side had to be disturbed. Among them was Mrs Mothersole’s, the position of which was exactly known from a note on a plan of the church and yard made by Mr Crome.

The village took a morbid interest in the exhumation of the famous witch, still remembered by a few old people in half-suppressed turns of phrase, as though even her name demanded a lower volume. Surprise rose into disquiet when it was found that, though the coffin was fairly sound and unbroken, there was no trace within it of body, bones, or dust. It is a curious phenomenon. At the time of her burial no one dreamed of resurrection-men; and it is hard to imagine a rational motive for stealing a body except for the dissecting-room.

The incident woke, for a while, the old stories of witch-trials and witches’ exploits that had lain dormant for forty years. Sir Richard’s orders that the coffin should be burned were thought by many to be foolhardy, but they were carried out.

Sir Richard, in other matters too, was an innovator. Before his time the Hall had been a fine block of mellow red brick. Sir Richard had travelled in Italy and brought back not only marbles but a taste that sought to make everything speak with a foreign accent. Having more money than his predecessors, he determined to leave an Italian palace where he had found an English house. So stucco and ashlar masked the brick; indifferent Roman marbles were planted about hall and gardens; and a reproduction of the Sibyl’s temple at Tivoli was erected across the mere. Castringham took on a new aspect—admired by neighbours, less engaging to those who felt the house had lost its old, local voice.

One morning in 1754 Sir Richard woke after a night of discomfort. The wind had been troublesome, his chimney smoked persistently, and yet it was so cold he must keep a fire. Something had rattled at the window so that no man could get a moment’s peace. There were guests expected that day—persons of position who would demand sport—and the inroads of the distemper among his game had lately been so serious that he feared for his reputation as a preserver. But what touched him most nearly was the matter of his sleepless night. He could not sleep in that room again.

At breakfast he brooded on it, and afterwards began a systematic examination of bedrooms to find one to suit. One had an eastern aspect that would wake him too early; another a northern that seemed too cold; in a third the servants would always pass; in a fourth he disliked the bedstead. No. He must have a western outlook so the sun would not trouble him, and it must be out of the way of the house’s bustle. The housekeeper, Mrs Chiddock, was nearly at the end of her resources.

“Well, Sir Richard,” she said at last, “you know there is but the one room like that in the house.”

“Which may that be?” Sir Richard asked.

“And that is Sir Matthew’s—the West Chamber.”

“Well, put me in there, for there I’ll lie tonight,” said her master briskly. “Which way is it? Here, to be sure,” and he hurried off.

“Oh, Sir Richard, but no one has slept there these forty years. The air has hardly been changed since Sir Matthew died there.”

Thus she spoke and rustled after him.

“Come, open the door, Mrs Chiddock. I’ll see the chamber, at least.”

It was opened. The smell was close and earthy, as though the room had been kept in a shut mouth too long—stale linen, old wood, a faint mould that sat on the back of the throat like bad flour. Sir Richard crossed to the window and, with his usual impatience, threw the shutters back and flung open the casement. For this end of the house the alterations had barely touched; it had grown up, as it were, with the great ash-tree and was otherwise concealed.

“Air it, Mrs Chiddock, all today, and move my bed-furniture in in the afternoon. Put the Bishop of Kilmore in my old room.”

“Pray, Sir Richard,” said a new voice from the doorway, “might I have the favour of a moment’s interview?”

Sir Richard turned. A man in black stood there and bowed.

“I must ask your indulgence for this intrusion, Sir Richard. You will, perhaps, hardly remember me. My name is William Crome, and my grandfather was vicar here in your grandfather’s time.”

“Well, sir,” said Sir Richard, “the name of Crome is always a passport to Castringham. I am glad to renew a friendship of two generations. In what can I serve you? for your hour of calling—and, if I do not mistake you, your bearing—shows you to be in some haste.”

“That is no more than the truth, sir. I am riding from Norwich to Bury St Edmunds with what haste I can make, and I have called in on my way to leave with you some papers which we have but just come upon in looking over what my grandfather left at his death. It is thought you may find some matters of family interest in them.”

Sir Richard led him to the study and offered wine. The packet—young Mr Crome was then lately made a Fellow of Clare Hall, Cambridge, and later produced a respectable edition of Polyaenus—contained, among other things, the old vicar’s notes on the death of Sir Matthew Fell. For the first time Sir Richard confronted the enigmatical Sortes Biblicæ. They amused him.

“Well,” he said, “my grandfather’s Bible gave one prudent piece of advice—Cut it down. If that stands for the ash-tree, he may rest assured I shall not neglect it. Such a nest of catarrhs and agues was never seen.”

The parlour—where the family books were kept until Sir Richard’s Italian collection should have a proper room—held not many volumes. Sir Richard looked up from the paper to the bookcase.

“I wonder,” he said, “whether the old prophet is there yet? I fancy I see him.”

He took out a dumpy Bible that bore on the flyleaf: “To Matthew Fell, from his Loving Godmother, Anne Aldous, 2 September, 1659.”

“It would be no bad plan to test him again, Mr Crome. I will wager we get a couple of names in the Chronicles. H’m! what have we here? ‘Thou shalt seek me in the morning, and I shall not be.’ Well, well! Your grandfather would have made a fine omen of that, hey? No more prophets for me! They are all in a tale.”

He thanked Mr Crome warmly and urged another glass; the offer was genuine, for he liked the young man’s manner. They parted, and Mr Crome rode on.

In the afternoon came the guests: the Bishop of Kilmore, Lady Mary Hervey, Sir William Kentfield, and others. Dinner at five; wine; cards; supper; and then dispersal to bed. The house did what it always does when it entertains: it made a show of continuity. Plates warmed by the fire breathed out a faint iron-and-oat smell; sauces were sharpened with wine; the roasted birds carried their clean, gamey steam into corridors that otherwise tasted of plaster and damp. Laughter rose and fell in rooms newly faced to look Italian, while, outside, the mere kept its older language of reeds and water, and the ash-tree stood close enough to the West Chamber to make its own private weather.

Next morning Sir Richard was disinclined to take his gun with the rest. He walked and talked with the Bishop of Kilmore along the terrace, discussing alterations and improvements. The Bishop, unlike many Irish prelates of his day, had visited and resided in his see and knew what the peasantry said when they spoke among themselves, in words that did not translate neatly into the drawing-room.

Pointing to the window of the West Room, he said, “You could never get one of my Irish flock to occupy that room, Sir Richard.”

“Why is that, my lord? It is, in fact, my own.”

“Well, our Irish peasantry will always have it that it brings the worst of luck to sleep near an ash-tree, and you have a fine growth of ash not two yards from your chamber window. Perhaps,” he added with a smile, “it has given you a touch of its quality already, for you do not seem, if I may say it, so much the fresher for your night’s rest as your friends would like to see you.”

“That, or something else, it is true, cost me my sleep from twelve to four, my lord. But the tree is to come down tomorrow, so I shall not hear much more from it.”

“I applaud your determination. It can hardly be wholesome to have the air you breathe strained, as it were, through all that leafage.”

“Your lordship is right there, I think. But I had not my window open last night. It was rather the noise that went on—no doubt from the twigs sweeping the glass—that kept me open-eyed.”

“I think that can hardly be, Sir Richard. Here—you see it from this point.” The Bishop drew him a little aside. “None of these nearest branches even can touch your casement unless there were a gale, and there was none of that last night. They miss the panes by a foot.”

Viborg lies low and old in Jutland, a town that wears its dignity like a well-used coat: the bishop’s seat, the handsome cathedral rebuilt on ashes, the lake that catches the sky and gives it back, the storks clicking their bills on chimneys as if keeping time for the whole place. The streets smell, depending on the hour, of damp brick cooling after sun, of peat-smoke, of yeast from bakers who still knead by hand, and of the sour-sweet tang that rises from canals and cobbles when evening mist comes in. A traveller can be comfortable there. Comfort, however, is not always the same thing as safety.

My cousin—Mr. Anderson—went, on his first visit, to the Golden Lion.

It stood among newer façades like an elder at a harvest table: broad-shouldered, red-bricked, corbie-stepped, with a pious text above the door that had outlasted fashions and fires. The omnibus rolled through into a courtyard of black-and-white timber and plaster; somewhere a bucket clinked, a broom rasped over boards, and the storks overhead made their dry, domestic percussion. The sun, lowering, struck the front full on, drawing out every warm tone in the old brick as if the house itself had been basted in light.

Anderson, who had the eye of a man trained to read old hands and older lies, delighted in its antiquated look. He told himself that a fortnight—perhaps three weeks—spent in so typical an inn would be not merely tolerable but a positive pleasure.

He had come to Viborg for papers. In the Rigsarkiv, so he had learned, there survived—snatched from the great fire of 1726—documents touching the last, stubborn days of Roman Catholicism in Denmark. He meant to copy and collate them, and he wanted a room large enough to serve as bedchamber and study: a table for his notes, space to lay out bundles, daylight that would not die too early.

The landlord, a solid, courteous man—Kristensen by name—considered, pursed his lips, and proposed that the gentleman should see the larger rooms and choose for himself. It was, of course, the sensible arrangement; and in a Danish house, where rooms are allotted with the same plainness as portions of bread, it seemed almost a kindness.

The top floor Anderson rejected at once: too many stairs after a day’s work; he was no boy. The second floor offered nothing quite of the right size. On the first floor there were two or three that might do.

Kristensen pressed for Number 17. “Quiet,” he said, as if quiet were the only seasoning a room required. But Number 17 looked onto a blank wall and would be dark in the afternoon, and Anderson—who liked his work as he liked his food: with colour, with air, with something living at the edge of it—preferred a street view, even with the noise.

Either Number 12 or Number 14, then. Both faced out, caught the bright evening light, and looked upon the town.

Number 12 won. It had three windows on one side, was high-ceilinged, and unusually long—like a corridor turned into a chamber. There was no fireplace, only a handsome old cast-iron stove, and upon it, in relief, Abraham poised above Isaac with the knife; above the scene the words “1 Bog Mose, Cap. 22.” There was nothing else remarkable: a bed, a wardrobe, a writing table that had known ink-spills and scraped quills, and one old coloured print of Viborg, about 1820, the lake and roofs laid out as calmly as if fire and time had never touched them.

Supper approached. Anderson washed off the day’s travel in the brisk, clean-smelling way of Danish water—cold enough to sting wakefulness into the skin—then went down before the bell rang and read the blackboard of lodgers. As usual, names were chalked in columns beneath the painted room numbers: an advocate (Sagförer), a German, a few bagmen from Copenhagen.

What caught his attention—only as a small oddity, at first—was that the list leapt over thirteen. Twelve, then fourteen. He had seen the omission before; the superstition was common enough. Still, he wondered whether it truly bit so hard that a hotelkeeper could not let a room simply because of the number nailed to its door. He decided to ask Kristensen, if the chance came.

He had nothing of note to tell me of supper. One may infer it was decent: Denmark does decent suppers as naturally as it does clean sheets. Butter with the sharp scent of pasture, rye bread cut thick, perhaps a slice of cured meat that tasted faintly of smoke and juniper, and beer that left a bread-crust bitterness on the tongue. The dining-room would have held its own small ecology of sound: knives against plates, chairs shifting, the low hum of talk, the occasional hard laugh of a commercial traveller who measures the world in profit and mischance. Anderson, attentive to paper and ink even as he ate, went upstairs later to unpack, set out his books, align his pens as if order could be a charm.

Towards eleven he prepared for bed. Yet, like many men who spend their days bent over script, he could not sleep without a few pages of print to settle the mind. He remembered, with irritation, that the very book he wanted—the one he’d read on the train, the only one that would do at that moment—was in the pocket of his great-coat, hanging on a peg outside the dining-room.

He went down and retrieved it in a minute. The passages were not dark; the house had those little lamps that cast more shadow than light, but enough to show the grain of the woodwork, the scuffed edges where trunks had bumped for generations. On his way back he found his door—or thought he did—by habit and by the feel of the corridor underfoot.

He turned the handle.

The door refused to open. From within came a quick, furtive movement, as if someone had sprung towards it in alarm.

He had tried the wrong room. He stood still, listening to the hush that followed, and with the smallest prickling at the back of his neck—the bodily sensation, not yet fear, but the skin’s first warning. Was his room to the right or left? He looked up.

The number on the door, clear enough, was 13.

A little disconcerted, he moved to the left, and yes—there was his own room. He went in, shut the door, and for some minutes read as usual. The street-lamp outside cast a watery half-light through the curtains, giving the furniture a curious, flattened look. Only when he had put out his candle and turned over, ready to sleep, did it occur to him that the blackboard downstairs had shown no Number 13 at all, and yet he had most certainly seen, and tried, a door so numbered.

He felt, absurdly, a moment’s regret that he had not chosen it. A well-born English gentleman’s occupancy, he thought drowsily, might do the landlord a service; such things are repeated, and repetition is a kind of advertisement. But no doubt it was a servant’s room, or a closet. In any case, it was probably smaller.

He glanced about once more. In that dim light the room seemed to have altered—contracted in length and grown higher, as if the far end had slid towards him and the ceiling had lifted away. It was a fleeting impression, easy to dismiss as the mind’s trick in low light.

Sleep mattered more. He slept.

The next day he attacked the Rigsarkiv. He was received with Danish kindness—quiet, practical, without fuss—and given what he asked for. The documents were more numerous, more lively, than he had expected: not only official papers but correspondence—bundles of letters with their seals broken long ago—concerning Bishop Jörgen Friis, the last Roman Catholic holder of the see. In those letters private life crept out between the lines as it always does: petty spite, fear of scandal, and that peculiar relish with which men describe what they think is wicked.

A certain house in Viborg, owned by the Bishop but not inhabited by him, recurred. Its tenant, the letters said, was a stumbling-block to the reforming party: a disgrace to the city, a practiser of secret and wicked arts, a soul sold to the Enemy. They called him a viper, a blood-sucking Troldmand, sheltered by the Babylonish Church’s superstition. The Bishop answered boldly: he abhorred such “secret arts,” he said; let the accusers bring the matter properly before the spiritual court. If evidence proved Mag Nicolas Francken guilty, no one would condemn him more readily than Friis.

Anderson had time only to glance at the next letter—written by the Protestant leader, Rasmus Nielsen—before closing. Its tenor was plain enough: Christian men were no longer bound by Roman bishops; the Bishop’s Court was no fit tribunal for so grave a cause.

Leaving the office, Anderson walked a little with Herr Scavenius, the elderly archivist. His voice had the soft rasp of an old man who has spent a lifetime among paper and dust; even his footsteps seemed careful, as if he trod always between columns of brittle parchments. Their talk naturally turned to Friis and to the mysterious house.

Scavenius admitted he was no specialist in that period. Yet the mention of it stirred him. He spoke of old Viborg’s topography, of how the Bishop’s terrier of 1560 survived almost entire—except, maddeningly, the very piece that listed the town properties. “Most unlucky,” he said. “Perhaps one day I shall find him.”

Anderson took exercise after—he could not recall precisely whether he walked by the lake or along the garden paths—but the air did him good. He returned to the Golden Lion for supper, for a game of patience, for bed. Life settled into routine: archive, meal, sleep; as regular as kneading dough, folding letters, cleaning a table.

Yet on his way to his room that evening he remembered the missing thirteen and his intention to ask the landlord. He also thought—because he had begun, against his will, to itch at the oddness—that he might as well make sure, once for all, that Number 13 truly existed.

There, in the passage, was the door with its number plain enough. And something was going on within: footsteps, and voices—or a voice—muffled by the wood. He slowed as he neared it; the very act of confirming what he thought he knew made him feel faintly foolish.

During the few seconds he paused to read the number, the footsteps ceased—very near the door. Then he heard it: a quick hissing breathing, like someone in strong excitement trying to keep silent, drawing air through teeth.

It was not loud, but it had a texture to it, wet and urgent, that struck the ear the way a sour smell strikes the nose. Sound, in a quiet house, is never merely sound; it occupies space, it alters it. Anderson moved on, unwilling to be caught listening like a servant.

In his own room he was again surprised at its air of having shrunk. The far wall seemed closer than it should be; the ceiling higher. A slight disappointment—nothing more—yet it pricked his methodical mind.

He wanted something from his portmanteau—his handkerchief, if I recall. The porter had placed the trunk on a trestle at the farthest end, against the wall. Anderson glanced, and—

The portmanteau was not there.

He stood, staring at the blank space, and felt a flare of vexation. Theft he dismissed at once; Denmark was not London. Some officious servant must have moved it, perhaps unpacked it. He checked the wardrobe: nothing. The idea of ringing and scolding the stuepige rose in him, then subsided; the thing he wanted could wait till morning.

He went to the right-hand window and looked out.

The street lay quiet under a dark sky. A tall building opposite presented large dead surfaces of wall; there were no passers-by, only the occasional far-off sound of wheels grinding over atrocious cobbles. The air carried a faint odor of horse and wet stone. Somewhere a dog barked once, then stopped, as if corrected.

The light in his room was behind him. He could see his shadow cast on the opposite wall: a clear black cut-out of his head and shoulders. Also the shadow of his neighbour in Number 11 to the left—a bearded man, moving to and fro in shirtsleeves, first brushing his hair, later visible in a nightgown. And also—this caught him—another shadow: the occupant of the room to the right.

Number 13.

The figure leaned on elbows at the window-sill, looking out, as Anderson was. Tall and thin—unless, perhaps, it was a woman; the outline was ambiguous. The head seemed covered with some drapery, as if the person wrapped it before bed. And there was a red light—dull, flickering—playing up and down on the opposite wall, as though a lamp with a red shade were being moved or fluttered by a draught.

Anderson craned out to see more. Beyond a fold of pale—perhaps white—material lying on the sill, he could make out nothing.

A distant footstep sounded in the street, and the effect was immediate. The shadow swept aside from the window with unnatural quickness; the red light went out as if pinched between fingers. Anderson, suddenly conscious of his own cigarette, laid its end on the sill and went to bed.

Morning came with the stuepige’s knock and the smell of hot water, soap, and clean linen aired in a passage. Anderson roused, and, when the maid set down the jug, he said, in careful Danish:

“You must not move my portmanteau. Where is it?”

She laughed—a small, meaningless laugh, such as servants sometimes use to cover incomprehension or impatience—and went away without answering.

Irritated, Anderson sat up to call her back—and remained sitting up, staring straight ahead.

There, upon the trestle at the far end, exactly where the porter had put it on the day of his arrival, was his portmanteau.

The shock was disproportionate, yet real. Anderson prided himself on accuracy of observation; he had not imagined a whole trunk vanishing. How could he have missed it the night before? He could not pretend to understand. Yet there it was, solid, square, indisputable.

Daylight made other things plain. The room, with its three windows, looked again of proper proportion; his choice seemed good enough. He dressed almost entirely, then went to the middle window to look out at the weather.

Another jolt.

On the sill of the middle window lay the end of his cigarette.

He could have sworn—ten times over—that he had smoked at the right-hand window and left it there. He picked it up as one might pick up a spoon that has appeared in the wrong drawer: a small thing, made heavy by implication.

He went down to breakfast, late enough to feel the mild reproach of Danish punctuality. In the passage he noticed, outside the next door, a pair of boots—gentleman’s boots—set out to be cleaned. So Number 13, he told himself, was indeed a man, not a woman. He glanced, automatically, at the number on the door.

It was 14.

A moment’s coldness went through him, as if the corridor air had turned to cellar damp. He thought he must have passed thirteen without noticing it, and the fact that he had made three stupid mistakes in twelve hours angered him more than the strangeness did. Anger is, for some temperaments, the first defence against fear.

He turned back to make sure.

The next number to 14 was 12—his own. There was no Number 13 at all.

He stood, looking from door to door. The woodwork, the numbers, the grain of the panels—all ordinary, all final. Yet his memory retained, with uncomfortable vividness, the feel of the wrong handle under his hand, the sound of movement inside, the hissing breath. A man does not invent such textures without knowing he has done so.

After some minutes—during which he forced himself to review everything he had eaten and drunk, as one might review ingredients after a dish turns out wrong—he decided to give the matter up. If his eyes or brain were failing, the fact would reveal itself soon enough. If not, then he was being offered, unwillingly, an experience worth watching.

He went about his day. In the archive he continued with the episcopal correspondence. To his disappointment it was incomplete. Only one other letter concerning Mag Nicolas Francken could be found: from Bishop Friis to Rasmus Nielsen. The Bishop wrote, in substance, that though he did not assent to Nielsen’s judgement of the episcopal court and was prepared to withstand him, nevertheless, since their “trusty and well-beloved Mag Nicolas Francken,” against whom false and malicious charges had been dared, had been “suddenly removed from among us,” the question fell for the time. Yet Nielsen’s further allegation—that St John’s Apocalypse described the Holy Roman Church as the Scarlet Woman—would be answered, and so forth.

Anderson searched for a sequel, for any clue to the cause or manner of Francken’s “removal.” There was none. Only the stark phrase remained, like a knife mark in a cutting board with no memory of what meat it once dressed. The dates troubled him: a gap of only two days between Nielsen’s last mention of Francken as living and the Bishop’s statement that he had been removed. If death, it was sudden indeed.

In the afternoon Anderson went out to Hald and took his tea at Baekkelund. The journey steadied him. The countryside had its own benign soundscape: wind combing through reeds, a cart creaking at a distance, the soft, innumerable noises of living land that modern engines had not yet fully drowned. Even in his nervous frame of mind he could detect no sign of mental collapse. He saw straight, heard straight. He returned to Viborg with the taste of tea and bread in his mouth and the faint comfort of having moved through a world that obeyed its own rules.

At supper he found himself seated next to the landlord.

“What,” he asked, after some indifferent talk, “is the reason why in most hotels here the number thirteen is left out? I see you have none.”

Kristensen looked amused, as if Anderson had remarked upon the shape of the salt-cellar.

“To think you should notice that!” he said. “I have thought about it once or twice myself. An educated man has no business with such superstitions. I was brought up here in the high school, and our old master—fine upstanding man—always set his face against that kind of thing. He’s been dead these many years. I recollect us boys, one snowy day—”

And with that he plunged into reminiscence, the way an old innkeeper will: stories rising like steam from a pot, thickening the air, delaying what one actually asked.

“And you don’t think there’s any objection to a Number 13?” Anderson pressed.

“Ah! to be sure. Well—listen. I was brought up to the business by my poor father. He kept an hotel in Aarhuus first. Then, when we were born, he moved back to Viborg—his native place—and had the Phœnix until he died, in 1876. I began in Silkeborg myself, and only the year before last I moved into this house.” And then, inevitably, details of leases and repairs and how the roof had been and what the trade was like when he took over.

“And when you came here,” Anderson asked, “was there a Number 13?”

“No, no. That is what I was going to tell you. You see, here it is the commercial class—the travellers—we must provide for. Put them in Number 13? They would sooner sleep in the street. It would make no penny difference to me what number my room had, and I have said so. But they stick to it that it brings bad luck. Quantities of stories among them—men that slept in a Number 13 and were never the same again, or lost their best customers, or—one thing and another.” He hunted for a more graphic phrase and failed, as men do when the truth is ugly but unnamed.

“Then what do you use your Number 13 for?” Anderson asked, and felt—absurdly—anxiety tighten in him, as if the question itself might summon an answer.

“My Number 13? Why, don’t I tell you there isn’t such a thing in the house? I thought you might have noticed. If there was, it would be next door to your own room.”

“Well, yes,” Anderson said, carefully, “only I happened to think—indeed, I fancied last night I had seen a door numbered thirteen in that passage. And I am almost certain, because I saw it the night before too.”

Kristensen laughed it away, as Anderson expected. He insisted, with a kind of hearty impatience, that no Number 13 existed or ever had existed since he had been in the house.

Anderson was in some ways relieved by the certainty; yet the puzzle remained, and with it a sense that his own perceptions were being handled—shifted about like crockery by unseen hands. He decided that the simplest way to test it was to bring the landlord up later for a cigar. He had photographs of English towns in his luggage; they would serve as an excuse.

Kristensen, flattered, accepted readily. He was to come about ten.

Before that Anderson had letters to write. He went upstairs, and—he was almost ashamed of the confession even to himself—he found he had become nervous about the non-existence of Number 13, to the point that he approached his room by way of Number 11, so as not to pass the place where the door ought to be. The avoidance was childish; that did not make it less real.

Inside, he looked about quickly, suspiciously. There was nothing. Only that indefinable air of the room being smaller than it should be—like a loaf that has failed to rise, leaving the same crust but less substance.

There was no portmanteau trouble tonight; he had emptied it himself and pushed it under the bed. He forced his mind away from the question and sat at the table to write.

The house was quiet enough. Now and then a door opened in the passage and a pair of boots was thrown out for cleaning, the leather striking the boards with a blunt little thud. A bagman went past humming to himself—some jingle half-swallowed, as if even his tune belonged to the corridor and not to him. Outside, from time to time, a cart thundered over the cobbles, the sound shaking the window-glass and then receding, leaving the ear slightly bruised by its passage. In those moments Anderson thought—without meaning to—how such violence of noise, repeated daily, eroded a man: not only nerves but memory itself, the inner speech by which one names experience. The town’s older sounds—the storks, the wind, the human footstep—seemed frail against it.

He finished his letters, ordered whisky and soda, and went to the window to study the dead wall opposite and the shadows that the rooms cast upon it.

As far as he knew, Number 14 belonged to the lawyer—the Sagförer—Herr Anders Jensen: a staid man who said little at meals, intent on papers beside his plate as if he chewed clauses instead of meat. Yet the shadow thrown from the next room suggested that, alone, he became someone else entirely.

He was dancing.

Again and again his thin form crossed the lit rectangle of window. Arms waved. A gaunt leg kicked up with surprising agility. He appeared barefoot, and the floor must have been sound, for no thump or creak betrayed his movements—only the silent, absurd pantomime on the opposite wall, like a puppet show played for no audience but Anderson.

Sagförer Herr Anders Jensen, dancing at ten o’clock at night in a hotel bedroom, struck Anderson as so fitting a subject for a grand historical painting that his thoughts, despite himself, began to arrange themselves—like a familiar recipe returning to hand—in jingling lines, as if to ward off the unease with a little doggerel:

“When I return to my hotel,
At ten o’clock p.m.,
The waiters think I am unwell;
I do not care for them.
But when I’ve locked my chamber door,
And put my boots outside,
I dance all night upon the floor.
And even if my neighbours swore,
I’d go on dancing all the more,
For I’m acquainted with the law,
And in despite of all their jaw,
Their protests I deride.”

It is not my purpose to delay the reader with the whole history of how these papers came to me. That point, if it matters at all, is the last I learned myself, and it may lie quietly until the close. Yet a word must be said at the outset, because the shape of what I possess explains the shape of what follows.

The bundle is, in part, what one would expect from a gentleman of the eighteen-forties who had conceived the project of “a volume of travel.” There are the beginnings of that agreeable, gossipy kind of book—half itinerary, half anecdote—in which the inn-parlour and the post-chaise are as important as the cathedral. There are also, as the papers advance, signs that the writer’s hand was drawn away from mere description and towards something narrower and more personal, until the record ceases to be a guide and becomes an account of a single experience, set down with an exactness which has the look of necessity.

The writer names himself: Mr Wraxall.

From his pages I collect that he was past middle age; that he had private means, though the management of them made demands upon him; that he lived for long stretches in hotels and boarding-houses, always on the brink of “settling,” never settled; and that he was alone in the world in the particular way in which a man may be surrounded by waiters and fellow-guests and yet have no one to speak to whose silence would matter. He had been, he hints, near a Fellowship at Oxford (Brasenose, if the scraps of allusion are to be trusted). He had once published a book on Brittany—very likely anonymously, for I have searched in the catalogues and cannot bring it to light. His fault, if it may be called so in a traveller, was curiosity; and it is a fault which in his case took on the force of appetite: once he had tasted a subject, he must needs have the whole dish, and would go on seeking flavour long after prudence would have put down the spoon.

In the early summer of 1863 he set out for Sweden, with the notion of writing another book—travel enlivened by episodes from the history of great families. In Stockholm he remained some weeks, and there, by the help of a learned resident, he was directed to a collection of family papers belonging to the owners of an old manor-house in Westergötland. Permission was obtained; introductions were written; and so, after the long and light days had established themselves, he came to the district and to the house which, for the sake of convenience, I shall call Råbäck.

It is not its true name. But any one who knows Dahlenberg’s great volume—_Suecia antiqua et moderna_—will recognize the place in the engraving of 1694, for the building stands now much as it stood then: red brick with stone facings, broad and sober in the manner of our own early seventeenth-century houses. It was built soon after 1600 by a member of the De la Gardie family, and his descendants possess it still. De la Gardie is the name I shall keep to.

They received Mr Wraxall with a courtesy which, even on paper, seems to have had warmth in it: that effortless, domestic kindness by which a stranger is made at once an expectation and an obligation. They pressed him to stay under their roof while he worked. But he preferred to be “independent”; he distrusted his Swedish in long conversation; and he installed himself at the village inn, a mile or so away, which proved comfortable enough during the summer.

That daily walk, repeated there and back, drew him into the country’s small and intimate geography. The manor-house stood in a park grown up with great old timber. Near it lay the walled garden, where the air was close with leaf-mould and the warm, bruised green smell of stems snapped for no reason but idleness. Beyond the garden one went into a strip of wood which fringed a little lake—one of many, for the land is pitted with them like a loaf with holes. Then came the demesne wall, and after it a steep rocky knoll lightly clothed in soil. On the top of this rose the church, fenced in with tall, dark trees whose branches took and held the sound: the place had that curious hush that comes when needles and leaves drink the wind, so that even your own footfall seems impertinent.

The church itself struck English eyes as strange. The nave and aisles were low, packed with pews and galleries. In the western gallery stood a handsome old organ, gaily painted, its silver pipes catching what light there was and giving it back coldly. The ceiling was flat and had been painted in the seventeenth century with a Last Judgement that was less theology than nightmare: lurid flames, falling cities, burning ships, brown demons smiling with an almost domestic satisfaction at the souls that cried and twisted. Brass coronae hung from the roof; the pulpit was like a carved doll’s-house overrun with little painted cherubs and saints; a threefold hour-glass stood hinged to the preacher’s desk, as if time itself were measured out in sermon-lengths.

But what distinguished the church at Råbäck was an addition to it. At the eastern end of the north aisle the builder of the manor-house had raised a mausoleum for himself and his family: an eight-sided building with oval windows, a domed roof, and above that a pumpkin-like swelling tapering into a spire—one of those shapes in which Swedish taste seems to have delighted. The copper roof was painted black; the walls, like the church’s, were startlingly white; and there was no access to it from within. Its portal, steps, and door were on the north side.

Past the churchyard the path ran down to the village, and within three or four minutes one stood at the inn door and smelled, under whatever cooking there was, the steady odour of old boards, tarred rope, and ale. There is a kind of life that repeats itself in those places—bread cut, plates scraped, shutters put up and down—so that a man may almost forget what day it is, and the house will still remember for him by habit.

On his first day, finding the church open, Mr Wraxall went in and made notes such as I have condensed. Into the mausoleum he could not force his way. He put his eye to the keyhole and could just descry the pale gleam of marble and the duller sheen of copper sarcophagi, with a lavishness of armorial ornament which made him eager to spend time there.

The family papers at the manor-house were precisely of the kind to feed his project. There were journals and correspondence and account-books from the earliest owners—neatly kept, clearly written, full of those small picturesque particulars which make a dead time seem as busy as one’s own. The first De la Gardie who appears in them—Count Magnus—stands out as a strong and capable man; and not a lenient one. Shortly after the building of the house there had been distress in the district, and peasants had risen and attacked châteaux. The owner of Råbäck had taken a leading part in suppressing the trouble, and the papers refer to executions of ringleaders, to severe punishments inflicted in the manor-yard with no sparing hand.

In the hall of the house hung a portrait of this Magnus de la Gardie, which Mr Wraxall studied after his day’s work. He does not describe it in detail, but the impression is clear: a face remarkable not for beauty, still less for goodness, but for force—indeed he notes that Count Magnus was almost phenomenally ugly.

That evening Mr Wraxall supped with the family and walked back late—late by the clock, though the northern summer still kept the light. He notes, as a man notes some small errand connected with household comfort:

“I must remember to ask the sexton if he can let me into the mausoleum at the church. He evidently has access to it himself, for I saw him tonight standing on the steps, and, as I thought, locking or unlocking the door.”

Early next morning he had talk with his landlord. The conversation is written down at length, and at first the minuteness surprised me; but it soon becomes plain that, in those opening pages, he is still gathering the chatty stuff of the travel book—innkeeper’s talk, peasants’ tales, those local judgements which can be set beside the description of a church ceiling or an organ-case. His purpose was to discover whether any tradition of Count Magnus lingered, and whether the popular estimate of him was favourable.

It was not.

If tenants came late to work on the days owed to their lord, they were set on the wooden horse, or flogged and branded in the manor-yard. There were stories—told with that careful vagueness which makes them difficult to contradict—of men who had occupied land encroaching on the demesne, and whose houses were mysteriously burnt on winter nights with the whole family inside.

Yet what most dwelt on the innkeeper’s mind—he returned to it, as a tongue returns to a sore tooth—was that Count Magnus had been on the Black Pilgrimage and had brought something, or someone, back with him.

Mr Wraxall asked, as any one would, what the Black Pilgrimage was. The landlord grew unwilling—held back, fenced, looked about as if the very boards had ears. Then he was called out for a moment and went with a too-ready alacrity, putting his head back in only to say he must go to Skara and would not return till evening.

So Mr Wraxall went to his work at the manor-house unsatisfied, and occupied himself with other matter. He was set that day among the letters of Sophia Albertina in Stockholm to her married cousin Ulrica Leonora at Råbäck (1705–1710), letters of exceptional interest for the light they throw upon the culture of the time. When he had done with these and returned their boxes to the shelf, he took down other volumes near them to choose his next day’s labour.

The shelf held mostly account-books in the first Count Magnus’s writing. But one volume among them was different: a compilation of alchemical and other tracts in a sixteenth-century hand. Mr Wraxall, not being deeply read in that literature, takes pains to set down names and beginnings—book of the Phoenix, book of the Thirty Words, book of the Toad, book of Miriam, _Turba philosophorum_, and so on—and then comes to the point which quickened him. On a leaf left blank near the middle, he found some writing of Count Magnus himself, headed:

_Liber nigræ peregrinationis._

Only a few lines had been copied; but enough to show that the landlord’s phrase was no new coinage, and that the belief was at least as old as Count Magnus, and very likely shared by him. Mr Wraxall translates thus:

“If any man desires to obtain a long life, if he would obtain a faithful messenger and see the blood of his enemies, it is necessary that he should first go into the city of Chorazin, and there salute the prince….”

Here one word had been erased, not very thoroughly, so that Mr Wraxall felt sure he read it as _aëris_—“of the air.” There was no more of the text, only the note:

_Quære reliqua hujus materiei inter secretiora._  
(Seek the rest of this matter among the more private things.)

It threw, he admits, a lurid light upon the Count’s tastes and beliefs. Yet to him, divided from Magnus by nearly three centuries, it served rather as spice than poison: it made the grim noble more picturesque. He spent some time in the hall looking up at the portrait, and then set off toward the inn with his mind full of Count Magnus, as if he had been reading a recipe and could still taste it on the tongue. Of the country he saw nothing: no evening sheen on the lake, no resinous scent of the woods, no shift in the wind among the treetops.

All at once he pulled up short and found himself at the churchyard gate, within minutes of his dinner. His eyes fell on the mausoleum—white walls, black roof, oval windows catching what remained of day like blind eyes.

“Ah,” he said aloud, “Count Magnus, there you are. I should dearly like to see you.”

“Like many solitary men,” he writes, “I have a habit of talking to myself aloud; and, unlike some of the Greek and Latin particles, I do not expect an answer. Certainly, and perhaps fortunately in this case, there was neither voice nor any that regarded: only the woman who, I suppose, was cleaning up the church, dropped some metallic object on the floor, whose clang startled me. Count Magnus, I think, sleeps sound enough.”

That evening, the landlord—who had heard Mr Wraxall say he wished to see the parish clerk or deacon—introduced him to that official in the inn parlour. The room had its accustomed domestic soundscape: the soft, repetitive scrape of a knife on wood, a chair-leg complaining, a fly rattling at the pane, and beneath all that the steady tick of a clock that seemed to insist on the ordinary passage of minutes even while strange subjects were spoken of. A visit to the tomb-house was arranged for the next day, and then there was a little general talk.

Mr Wraxall, remembering that Scandinavian deacons often teach candidates for Confirmation, chose to refresh his own memory on a Biblical point, as a man might turn to a familiar flavour to steady himself.

“Can you tell me,” he said, “anything about Chorazin?”

The deacon started—so Mr Wraxall notes the movement of his head and the quick change in his look—and then, with readiness, reminded him of the denunciation pronounced upon that village.

“To be sure,” said Mr Wraxall; “it is, I suppose, quite a ruin now?”

“So I expect,” replied the deacon. “I have heard some of our old priests say that Antichrist is to be born there; and there are tales—”

“Ah! what tales are those?” Mr Wraxall put in at once.

“Tales, I was going to say, which I have forgotten,” said the deacon; and soon after that he said good night.

Then the landlord was alone with Mr Wraxall; and Mr Wraxall’s curiosity, once roused, was not a polite thing.

“Herr Nielsen,” he said, “I have found out something about the Black Pilgrimage. You may as well tell me what you know. What did the Count bring back with him?”

Swedes are slow, perhaps; or perhaps the man’s reluctance made him slower. Mr Wraxall notes that the landlord looked at him at least a full minute before he spoke. Then he came close—as if the words could not cross even the short breadth of a table safely—and with effort said:

“Mr Wraxall, I can tell you this one little tale, and no more—not any more. You must not ask anything when I have done.

“In my grandfather’s time—that is, ninety-two years ago—there were two men who said: ‘The Count is dead; we do not care for him. We will go tonight and have a free hunt in his wood’—the long wood on the hill that you have seen behind Råbäck. Well, those that heard them say this, they said: ‘No, do not go; we are sure you will meet with persons walking who should not be walking. They should be resting, not walking.’ These men laughed. There were no forestmen to keep the wood, because no one wished to live there. The family were not here at the house. These men could do what they wished.

“Very well, they go to the wood that night. My grandfather was sitting here in this room. It was the summer, and a light night. With the window open, he could see out to the wood, and hear.

“So he sat there, and two or three men with him, and they listened. At first they hear nothing at all; then they hear someone—you know how far away it is—they hear someone scream, just as if the most inside part of his soul was twisted out of him. All of them in the room caught hold of each other, and they sat so for three-quarters of an hour. Then they hear someone else, only about three hundred ells off. They hear him laugh out loud: it was not one of those two men that laughed, and, indeed, they have all of them said that it was not any man at all. After that they hear a great door shut.

“Then, when it was just light with the sun, they all went to the priest. They said to him:

“‘Father, put on your gown and your ruff, and come to bury these men, Anders Bjornsen and Hans Thorbjorn.’

“You understand that they were sure these men were dead. So they went to the wood—my grandfather never forgot this. He said they were all like so many dead men themselves. The priest, too, he was in a white fear. He said when they came to him:

“‘I heard one cry in the night, and I heard one laugh afterwards. If I cannot forget that, I shall not be able to sleep again.’

“So they went to the wood, and they found these men on the edge of the wood. Hans Thorbjorn was standing with his back against a tree, and all the time he was pushing with his hands—pushing something away from him which was not there. So he was not dead. And they led him away, and took him to the house at Nyköping, and he died before the winter; but he went on pushing with his hands.

“Also Anders Bjornsen was there; but he was dead. And I tell you this about Anders Bjornsen, that he was once a beautiful man, but now his face was not there, because the flesh of it was sucked away off the bones. You understand that? My grandfather did not forget that.

“And they laid him on the bier which they brought, and they put a cloth over his head, and the priest walked before; and they began to sing the psalm for the dead as well as they could. So, as they were singing the end of the first verse, one fell down, who was carrying the head of the bier, and the others looked back, and they saw that the cloth had fallen off, and the eyes of Anders Bjornsen were looking up, because there was nothing to close over them. And this they could not bear. Therefore the priest laid the cloth upon him, and sent for a spade, and they buried him in that place.”

The tale, written down in Mr Wraxall’s plain hand, has a peculiar effect: it is made of sounds—scream, laughter, the shutting of a door—and it is the sounds, more than the sight of the poor man’s face, that seem to have gnawed at the hearers and to have been passed on like a family taste for bitterness. The wood on the hill becomes, in the telling, not merely a place but an instrument: a dark body that takes in human noise and gives back something else.

The next day the deacon called soon after breakfast and took Mr Wraxall to the church and the mausoleum. Mr Wraxall noticed that the key of the latter hung on a nail just by the pulpit. The church door, he had observed already, was commonly left unfastened; and it occurred to him at once how easy it would be to return alone if there proved to be more among the monuments than could be digested at a first visit.

The tomb-house, when he entered it, was not unimposing. The air had the dry, contained smell of stone shut away from weather, with a faint metallic coldness from the copper. The monuments—large erections of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries—were dignified if luxuriant. Heraldry and epitaphs crowded the walls in abundance. In the central space under the dome stood three copper sarcophagi, their surfaces engraved with fine ornament. Two had, as is common in those countries, a great metal crucifix upon the lid. The third—the Count Magnus, as it appeared—had instead a full-length effigy engraved upon it; and round the edge ran several bands showing scenes.

One band was a battle: cannon belching smoke, walled towns, troops of pikemen. Another showed an execution. In a third, among trees, a man was pictured running at full speed, hair flying, hands outstretched. After him followed a strange form. It was hard to tell whether the artist had meant a man and failed, or whether he had meant a monstrosity and succeeded. Considering the skill of the rest, Mr Wraxall inclined to the latter. The figure was unduly short, muffled in a hooded garment sweeping the ground; and from within that shelter there projected something not shaped like any hand or arm. Mr Wraxall compares it to the tentacle of a devil-fish.

“On seeing this,” he writes, “I said to myself, ‘This, then, which is evidently an allegorical representation of some kind—a fiend pursuing a hunted soul—may be the origin of the story of Count Magnus and his mysterious companion. Let us see how the huntsman is pictured: doubtless it will be a demon blowing his horn.’”

But there was no such sensational figure. Only the semblance of a cloaked man on a hillock, leaning on a stick, watching the hunt with an interest the engraver had tried to express in his posture.

Mr Wraxall also noted the three steel padlocks—massive and finely worked—which secured the Count’s sarcophagus. One of them lay detached on the pavement. Then, unwilling to delay the deacon or to waste his own working time, he went on to the manor-house.

“It is curious,” he wrote that evening, “how, on retracing a familiar path, one’s thoughts engross one to the absolute exclusion of surrounding objects. Tonight, for the second time, I had entirely failed to notice where I was going (I had planned a private visit to the tomb-house to copy the epitaphs), when I suddenly, as it were, awoke to consciousness, and found myself (as before) turning in at the churchyard gate, and, I believe, singing or chanting some such words as, ‘Are you awake, Count Magnus? Are you asleep, Count Magnus?’ and then something more which I have failed to recollect. It seemed to me that I must have been behaving in this nonsensical way for some time.”

He found the key where he expected it and returned alone to the mausoleum. He copied the greater part of what he wanted, staying until the light began to fail him at the windows. He notes, in the same matter-of-fact tone in which a man might remark that a latch does not catch:

“I must have been wrong in saying that one of the padlocks of my Count’s sarcophagus was unfastened; I see tonight that two are loose. I picked both up, and laid them carefully on the window-ledge, after trying unsuccessfully to close them. The remaining one is still firm, and, though I take it to be a spring lock, I cannot guess how it is opened. Had I succeeded in undoing it, I am almost afraid I should have taken the liberty of opening the sarcophagus. It is strange, the interest I feel in the personality of this, I fear, somewhat ferocious and grim old noble.”

The day following was, as it turned out, the last of Mr Wraxall’s stay at Råbäck. Letters reached him—those practical, unpoetic papers by which money makes itself felt—that made it desirable he should return to England. His work among the manuscripts was, in the main, done; travelling was slow; and he decided therefore to take his leave, set a few finishing touches to his notes, and be off.

“Soon off, I suppose, now term’s done, Professor?” said a voice from beyond the clink of glasses and the warm, yeasty breath of the college hall.

Professor Parkins—young, correct, a man who could pare a sentence as neatly as a cook skins an apple—inclined his head. “Yes. My friends have been making me take up golf, and I mean to go to the East Coast—Burnstow—for a week or ten days. I hope to be away tomorrow.”

At his other side an antiquary (one of those men who always smell faintly of dust and calf-leather, though they have just come from dinner) leaned in. “If you’re going to Burnstow, I wish you’d look at the site of the Templars’ preceptory. Tell me if it would be any good for a dig in summer.”

Parkins’s mouth tightened—usefulness, to him, wore the gown of a professor, not the shovel of a romantic. Still, he was punctilious. “Certainly. Tell me where it lies.”

“Down close to the beach, I should think. The sea’s eaten into the land there disgracefully. About three-quarters of a mile from the Globe Inn, north end of the town. Where will you stay?”

“At the Globe,” said Parkins. “I’ve taken a room. Winter lodgings are mostly shut, so they can only give me a double-bedded room. They can’t stow the other bed anywhere. I must have space for my books and notes. I don’t quite like having an empty bed—two, rather—in what will be my study, but it’s only for a little while.”

Across the table Mr Rogers—bluff, loud, fond of making his fun with a heavy hand—threw out, “If it’s only an empty bed that troubles you, Parkins, I’ll come down and fill it. Keep you company.”

Parkins gave the smallest quiver, which with him did duty for alarm. “By all means, Rogers,” he said, courtesy first as always. “Only—you would find it dull. You don’t play golf?”

“No, thank Heaven!”

“Well, then, when I’m not writing I shall be on the links.”

“Oh, I’d find someone,” Rogers said. “Unless you don’t want me. You always say truth’s never offensive.”

It put Parkins in a corner: the truth, that sacred, sharp instrument he insisted on handing others by the blade. After a pause—just long enough for everyone to hear him being honest—he said, “If you press me: I was considering whether the room would accommodate us both comfortably. And whether you might, perhaps, be—(I should not say it if you had not asked)—a hindrance to my work.”

Rogers roared. “Well done. All right, old man. I only thought I’d keep the ghosts off.” A wink went travelling round the table like a dropped coin.

Parkins coloured, as if heat had risen straight from the wine to his cheeks. “I do not like careless talk of—of that kind,” he said, with the anxious firmness of a man policing a boundary. “A man in my position cannot appear to sanction current beliefs in such matters. Any concession—any semblance of it—is a renunciation of what I hold most sacred.”

Rogers, to do him justice, softened. “Yes, yes. We’ll have it out at Burnstow.”

That, in brief, was Parkins: neat as folded linen, brave in his own fashion, a little hen-like in his caution, and without the least appetite for a joke unless it could be footnoted.

The next day he got away, and by afternoon the Globe Inn had swallowed him into its winter quiet. The landlord made him welcome; the fire in the public room had the smell of salt-damp wood; and in his own chamber, large and draughty, the double beds stood like two pale hulks moored side by side. Parkins arranged his books and papers with a cook’s exactness laying out knives and bowls: everything squared, everything in its place, as if order itself were a charm against intrusion.

His table sat at the outer end of the room, drawn up before three windows looking seaward. The middle pane gave him the straight stare of the sea; the side windows let the coast run away north and south like a strip of grey cloth. Southward lay Burnstow with its clustered roofs; northward, nothing but beach and a low cliff. Between the Globe and the tide there were only rough grass dotted with old anchors and capstans, then a broad path, then shingle and sand. Sixty yards at most—near enough that the sea’s breath salted the glass.

The inn’s winter company was mostly golfers. The loudest among them was Colonel Wilson, an old soldier turned club secretary, with a voice that could strip bark and opinions that came out Protestant as a trumpet-call—especially after he had endured the vicar’s service, the vicar being one of those decent men who liked his ritual picturesque but kept it down out of respect for East Anglian plainness.

Parkins, who possessed pluck of the unshowy sort, spent most of the following day improving his game in company with the Colonel. Whether improvement was possible under that barrage of command and commentary is doubtful; but by late afternoon the Colonel’s face had taken on such a heated and combative colour that Parkins, catching sight of moustache and flushed cheek, decided it would be wise to let tea and tobacco cool the man before dinner forced them together again.

“I might walk back along the beach,” he thought, tightening his scarf against the northern wind. “And look for Disney’s ruins. There will be light enough.”

He found them with his foot. Coming down from the links, picking his way to the shingle, he caught it in a gorse-root and a stone and went over, hands in the sand like a man bowing to a stern altar. When he rose and dusted himself, he saw he had fallen among broken ground—little depressions and mounds, some of them no more than turf hummocks, others hard with flints set in old mortar and half-swallowed by grass. Foundations, then; the preceptory. He walked about with the slow care of someone testing pastry for doneness—tapping the place with eye and heel, measuring in his mind.

He remembered (vaguely, but enough to please his sense of pattern) that the Templars built round churches, and indeed a group of humps did seem to arrange itself into a rough circle. Parkins paced it, wrote down dimensions in his pocket-book, gratifying his friend Disney and, in secret, himself. Then he went to an oblong rise to the east which might have been a platform or altar base. At its north end the turf had been torn away by boys or some other small vandal appetite. It seemed reasonable to probe there, and he took out his knife and began scraping.

The soil gave, unexpectedly, and fell in. Beneath lay a small cavity. He struck match after match, but the wind bullied each flame into nothing. Still, by tapping the sides with his knife and feeling the surfaces, he made out the hole was artificial: rectangular, with smooth, regular walls, a little box built into masonry. Empty, of course—until, as he drew back the knife, there came a clear metallic clink that cut through the wind like a spoon struck against a cup.

He put in his hand and found, lying on the bottom, a small cylinder. He brought it out into the fading light: a metal tube, about four inches long, aged and dulled, but unmistakably made by a human hand.

Darkness pressed in too quickly for more. He examined the cavity once more, found nothing else, and pocketed his find with a pleased quickness, as if he had slipped away a rare spice. Tomorrow, he resolved, he would give a little more daylight to this.

Before turning home he looked out over the coast. Bleak and solemn: links dimming under a faint yellow strip of westering light; a squat martello tower; far lights of Aldsey; the sands like a pale ribbon, stitched at intervals with black wooden groynes; and the sea murmuring in a low, steady voice, the kind that sinks into a man’s bones. The north wind had teeth, but at least it was at his back as he clashed over shingle and gained the sand, where his steps fell quieter.

Groynes forced him every few yards to climb and drop again. It was at one such pause that he glanced behind and saw, far back along the shore, a figure—indistinct, but with the troubling air of someone hurrying. There was a look of running about the movements, and yet the distance between them did not seem to lessen.

“It is absurd to wait,” Parkins decided. He did not know the man; and on a lonely shore the wrong companion is worse than none. Still, the sea’s voice, the wind’s thin whine over the sand, the harsh thud of his shoes on timber, made him aware of how hungry the mind can become for a second human sound. He found his thoughts dragging up old tales read in childhood—meetings on waste places, things seen against a yellow sky. If he looked back and saw a black figure sharply defined, with horns and wings—should he stand, or should he run? He ran, not from any horned fancy but from the very practical nearness of dinner-time.

He dressed in haste, and at table found the Colonel restored, so far as tea and tobacco could restore any man—Peace, or something tolerably like it, holding in the military bosom. There was bridge after dinner; Parkins played well enough to keep even the Colonel from exploding; and so by midnight Parkins went upstairs feeling, with the complacency of a person who has eaten and behaved correctly, that a fortnight—even three weeks—at the Globe might be borne “especially,” he added to himself, “if I continue to improve my game.”

In the passage he met the boots, who stopped, respectful and breathless in that way of men bringing small news like a dish they fear to spill. “Beg pardon, sir, but as I was a-brushing your coat there was somethink fell out of the pocket. I put it on your chest o’ drawers, sir, in your room, sir—a piece of a pipe or somethink, sir.”

The reminder quickened Parkins’s curiosity. In his room, by candlelight, he turned the object over. Bronze, now that he could see it properly—greenish where age had bitten, smooth where hands had worn it. It was shaped exactly like a modern dog-whistle. In fact it was a whistle: no more, no less.

He put it to his lips, but it was clogged with fine caked sand or earth. Knocking did nothing; the obstruction sat as stubbornly as dried dough in a crack of a board. Tidy to the last fibre, Parkins scraped the sand out with his knife onto a piece of paper, as carefully as if he were salvaging ground spice, and carried it to the window to empty. When he opened the casement the cold salt air rushed in; the night was clear, bright with hard stars, and the sea lay black and glossy. On the shore below the inn he noticed a belated wanderer standing as if listening. People kept late hours at Burnstow, it seemed. He shut the window, mildly surprised, and returned to his find.

There were marks on it. Letters. A rub with his thumb made the cuts stand out stark in the candle’s yellow: one inscription on the front, one on the back.

FLA FUR BIS FLE

And:

QUIS EST ISTE QUI VENIT

Parkins frowned, annoyed at himself as if he had discovered a stain on a clean cuff. “I ought to make it out,” he thought. “I must be rusty at my Latin. And I don’t even know the word for whistle. The longer seems plain enough: ‘Who is this who is coming?’”

His mind, always eager to prove a point by experiment, answered itself with a little thrill. “The best way to find out is evidently to whistle for him.”

He blew tentatively.

The note that came was soft, yet it held distance inside it, as if it had travelled a long way before reaching his own ears. It was not loud; it was worse than loud. It seemed made to slip through space without spending itself, to settle into every hollow of the coast. And it did something else—something Parkins could not have explained to a colleague without feeling foolish: it painted pictures in the brain the way certain smells do when you lift a lid and the steam rises—pictures so vivid you taste them.

For a moment he saw, with an odd clearness, a wide dark expanse at night, wind moving fast, and in the midst a lonely figure. How it was occupied he could not tell—only that it was there, and that the wind knew it. The image broke when a gust hit the casement with such sudden force that he looked up, just in time to catch the white glint of a seabird’s wing beyond the dark panes.

Fascination tugged him. He raised the whistle again and blew more boldly. The note was no louder, and this time no picture came—only the wind, which had sharpened, rising so abruptly that the house seemed to take it in the chest.

“There!” Parkins exclaimed, startled into speech. “What force it can get up in a few minutes! That fastening’s no use—ah!”

The casement burst inward with a violence that made the candles gutter and die. Cold air poured into the room like water into a pan, flattening the bedclothes, worrying the papers, making the unused bed seem suddenly less like furniture and more like something waiting. Parkins lunged for the window and fought it, straining as if he were pushing back a sturdy burglar. For twenty seconds he wrestled with it, fingers numb on the latch, the wind pressing with a deliberate strength that felt—unreasonably—like intention. Then, all at once, the pressure slackened. The casement banged shut and latched itself, as if someone outside had let go.

He relit the candles. No glass was broken. Nothing looked out of place, though everything looked more aware of him. Above, the Colonel was roused; Parkins heard heavy feet and a growl, the sound of a man whose sleep has been cut across like a loaf.

The wind did not drop at once. It went on moaning past the house and rushing along the eaves, sometimes rising to a cry so thin and desolate that it made the inn’s ordinary domestic noises—the settling of boards, the distant clink of some late glass—seem insultingly small. Parkins told himself, in the dry tone he used for correcting papers, that fanciful people might be made uncomfortable by it, and that even the unimaginative might be happier without such a sound.

He lay awake. Whether it was wind or golf or the day’s small archaeology he could not say; but wakefulness took him and kept him, and with it came the usual miseries of a neat mind in a dark room: the counting of heartbeats, the suspicion of lungs and brain and liver, all those doom-thoughts that daylight always laughs at and night will not release. He found a thin comfort in imagining someone else in the inn was as restless; nearby, someone—direction uncertain in the darkness—was tossing and rustling too.

At last he shut his eyes and resolved to give sleep its chance.

Then the pictures came.

Not the harmless drifting of half-dreams, but a continuous scene, like a lantern show that refused to be dismissed. When he opened his eyes it vanished at once; when he closed them again it framed itself afresh and played through at the same pace, neither faster nor slower, as if it belonged to time outside his own body.

He saw a long stretch of shore: shingle edged by sand, crossed at short intervals by black groynes running down to the water. It might have been the very beach he had walked that afternoon—no landmark to say otherwise. The light was wrong: dim, as of late winter evening thickening into storm, with cold rain like fine grit on the face.

At first, no one. Then, far off, a black bobbing thing. It resolved itself into a man running—jumping, clambering over the groynes—every few seconds twisting back to look behind him. The nearer he came the more terrible was his fear, though the face remained indistinct. He was almost spent. Each obstacle took more from him than the last, as if the beach itself were draining him.

“Will he get over the next?” Parkins found himself thinking, absurdly compelled to take part. The next groyne was higher. The runner half climbed, half flung himself, and did get over—and collapsed in a heap on the side nearest the watcher. There he stayed, crouched under the timbers, looking up with the cramped, trembling posture of an animal cornered.

Still no sign of what pursued him.

Then, far up the shore, there appeared a flicker of something pale moving with swift, irregular motion, like a sheet caught by sudden gusts. It grew larger quickly and became a figure in fluttering draperies, ill-defined, its edges never still. It stopped, raised arms, bent down towards the sand, then ran stooping to the water’s edge and back, as if searching by scent; then it rose upright and continued forward at a speed that made the mind recoil.

The pursuer hovered from left to right only a few yards beyond the groyne where the runner hid. After two or three castings this way and that, it halted, stood upright with arms raised high, and then darted straight toward the groyne.

At that instant Parkins always broke—eyes flying open, heart hammering as if it had run the beach itself. He told himself, with the frantic rationality of the terrified, about incipient failure of sight, overworked brain, too much smoking. But the persistence of the vision—its exactness, its refusal to behave like imagination—made him at last choose a simpler defence: light.

He struck a match. The first flared and died. In the dark, something small scurried across the floor from the side of the bed—rats, or whatever makes that dry, secretive sound, the kind that seems to carry the whole building’s history of crumbs and refuse. The second match caught properly. He lit a candle, fetched a book, and sat propped against the pillows, reading with fierce attention as if words could barricade him.

At some point sleep came—wholesome, heavy—and for the first time in his orderly life he forgot to blow the candle out. When he was called at eight, there was still a feeble flicker in the socket and a sad spill of guttered grease on the little table, like cold fat hardened after careless cooking.

After breakfast he was in his room putting the final touches to his golfing clothes—fortune had again paired him with the Colonel—when one of the maids came in.

Verum usque in præsentem diem—so ran the crabbed Latin—multa garriunt inter se Canonici: even now the canons gossipped, tongues wagging as steadily as ladles in a refectory, about Abbot Thomas’s buried gold. Mr. Somerton, who had been bent over the page till the light of his lamp made a little pool of honey on the paper, put down his pen and flexed his cramped fingers as if he had been trussing a fowl.

“I suppose I shall have to translate this,” he told the silence; and he did, quickly, as one pares an apple while the kettle boils.

Up to this day, he wrote, the men of Steinfeld had hunted—often, vainly—for a hidden treasure Abbot Thomas was said to have put away while he was still strong and laughing. When questioned, he answered with a smile: “Job, John, and Zechariah will tell you or your heirs.” He had no malice for the finder, he had said. He had set up a great window at the east end of the south aisle, filled with painted figures; he had rebuilt the abbot’s lodging and dug a well in its court, adorning it with carved marble. He died suddenly in 1529, in his seventy-second year.

Somerton’s true business—his sober, daylight one—was glass. Like a patient cook tasting sauces by the teaspoon, he had been tracing the provenance of sixteenth-century painted windows that had drifted to England after the Revolution: panels that had crossed the sea like contraband spices, ending in parish chancels and private chapels where their colours still kept something of candlelight in them. Steinfeld’s glass had scattered far; its cycles could often be recognized by inscriptions and repeated stories. Yet three figures he had seen in Lord D——’s chapel had teased him for months. Their German hand was plain, their date plain, but their companionship was perverse: JOB PATRIARCHA, JOHANNES EVANGELISTA, ZACHARIAS PROPHETA—each filling an entire light, each holding a book or scroll with a sentence that was almost Scripture and not quite, as if some careful tongue had altered a familiar recipe by a single herb.

Job’s scroll read, not as the Vulgate had it, but: Auro est locus in quo absconditur—There is a place for gold where it is hidden. John’s book: Habent in vestimentis suis scripturam quam nemo novit—They have on their raiment a writing which no man knoweth. Zechariah’s was unaltered: Super lapidem unum septem oculi sunt—Upon one stone are seven eyes.

Why those three together? No doctrine stitched them; no story chained them. Somerton had assumed they must once have been part of a long procession of prophets and apostles. Then the Steinfeld passage put a new sound into the air, like a bell heard through fog: those very names had been Abbot Thomas’s answer. Abbot Thomas had commissioned a great window in precisely the place these figures might have belonged. Somerton returned to the chapel, and in another panel—glass bought with the rest—found the Abbot’s arms. The identification clicked shut like a well-latch.

From that moment, he could not help tasting the treasure in the words. “Job, John, Zechariah will tell.” The altered Job text pointed to hoarded gold. John promised writing on vestures. Zechariah muttered of a stone with seven eyes—something to be sought only where the stone lay in its proper bed.

Somerton was not a man who ate merely to stave off hunger. He had the scholar’s palate: the desire to know what a thing had been, who had first seasoned it, how it should properly sound on the tongue. He laid out his notes and tracings in his Berkshire study as if arranging ingredients for a complicated dish—every letter, every scratch, every faint accidental mark on the glass. Many nights he burned through oil and patience alike, while outside the house the old rural quiet held its own against distant modern clatter. Yet the thought of Steinfeld and its gutted church and its court-well pulled at him steadily, like the smell of woodsmoke caught in a traveller’s coat.

At last he told Brown to pack for a short journey abroad.

II

Mr. Gregory, Rector of Parsbury, stepped out before breakfast on a fine autumn morning, intending only to meet the postman at the gate and breathe the clean air that comes with fallen leaves. His children scampered about him, asking bright, foolish questions in bright, foolish voices. Then the postman appeared, and among the letters was one with foreign stamps—coveted at once by small hands—and an address written in a rough English fist.

It was from William Brown, Somerton’s valet.

HONOURED SIR, it began, Has I am in a great anxiety about Master I write at is Wish to beg you Sir if you could be so good as Step over…

The misspellings came thick as crumbs, but the meaning was plain enough: Somerton had suffered a nasty shock, was in bed, and would have no one but Gregory. The “Short Way” was to drive to Coblince and take a trap. The village was called Steenfeld.

A Berkshire parsonage in 1859 did not receive such letters without upheaval. By evening Gregory was on his way: train to town, boat to Antwerp, railway to Coblentz, and from there—by arrangements he hardly remembered—on to Steinfeld.

He would later confess that Steinfeld itself remained in his mind as a place of grey, wind-worn sound: a small village, a large church stripped of its old furnishings, and hulking seventeenth-century buildings leaning about it like tired oxen. There was little worth seeing, he thought—except one thing he prayed he need never look upon.

The only “possible” inn stood near the church. Brown waited at the door. In Berkshire he was the picture of whiskered composure; here, in light tweeds that seemed wrong for the damp air, he was sharp-edged with anxiety, almost snappish, and grateful beyond words to see a familiar face that spoke his own vowels.

“Well, I ham pleased, I’m sure, sir, to see you. And so I’m sure, sir, will master.”

“How is your master, Brown?”

“I think he’s better, sir, thank you; but he’s had a dreadful time of it. I ’ope he’s gettin’ some sleep now, but—”

“What has been the matter? An accident?”

“Well, sir, I ’ardly know whether I’d better speak about it. Master was very partickler he should be the one to tell you. But there’s no bones broke—that’s one thing we ought to be thankful—”

“And the doctor?”

They were outside Somerton’s door, voices dropped. Gregory reached for the handle; his fingers brushed the panels—old wood, worn smooth, smelling faintly of stale smoke and soap. Before Brown could answer, a cry burst from within, raw and sudden, as if a man had been jerked awake by drowning.

“In God’s name, who is that?” Somerton’s voice—high with fear—then: “Brown, is it?”

“Yes, sir—me, sir, and Mr Gregory.”

A groan followed, not pain but relief.

They entered. The room was darkened against the sun. Somerton sat up in the curtained bed, his face drawn tight, damp with fear in beads like sweat from fever. He stretched out a shaking hand, and Gregory felt a shock of pity at the sight—his friend, usually so neat and self-commanding, brought down to this trembling, listening creature.

“Better for seeing you, my dear Gregory,” Somerton said; and it was true. Five minutes of talk steadied him like warm broth. He ate a respectable dinner, and by candlelight spoke of travelling to Coblentz within a day.

“But there’s one thing,” he said, agitation returning like an ill-timed draught through a door. “I must beg you to do something for me, my dear Gregory. Don’t ask what it is, or why. I’m not up to explaining it yet; it would throw me back—undo the good you’ve done by coming. I will only say you run no risk whatever in doing it, and Brown can show you tomorrow. It’s merely to put back—to keep—something—No; I can’t speak of it yet. Do you mind calling Brown?”

Gregory, uneasy but loyal, went to the door. “Somerton, I won’t question you until you choose. If it’s as easy as you say, I’ll do it first thing in the morning.”

“I was sure you would. I knew I could rely on you.” Somerton’s gratitude was fierce, almost desperate. Brown came in at the summons, pale around the mouth.

“The first thing tomorrow morning,” Somerton said—“you don’t mind early hours, Gregory—you must take the Rector to—there, you know.” Brown nodded, swallowing hard. “And he and you will put that back. Don’t be alarmed; it’s perfectly safe in the daytime. You know what I mean. It lies on the step, you know, where—where we put it.” Brown could not speak; he only bowed. “And one other word. If you can manage not to question Brown about this, I shall be still more bound to you. Tomorrow evening, if all goes well, I shall tell you the whole story from start to finish. And now—good night. Brown will be with me—he sleeps here—and if I were you, I should lock my door. Yes, be particular to do that. They—they like it, the people here, and it’s better.”

Gregory did as he was told. Yet in the small hours he woke more than once with the distinct impression of sound at the lower part of his locked door: not the wind, not rats, but a fumbling, careful pressure, as if something nosed and tested the wood, listening with it. The inn, too, had its own voice: boards speaking softly under their own weight, a far-off drip somewhere that counted time, and now and then—he could not have sworn to it—a faint, wet sound like cloth drawn across stone.

He rose with the sun. Brown was waiting, hollow-eyed. They went out into the morning, when village noises—the scrape of a pail, a shutter unhooked, a cock’s brief metallic complaint—lay like thin crust over a deeper hush that seemed to belong to the church and its enclosing buildings. Whatever service Somerton had demanded was done within half an hour. It was neither difficult nor, in full daylight, plainly alarming; and yet Gregory found his stomach tightening as if he had swallowed cold fat.

Later that morning Somerton, much restored, set out from Steinfeld. That evening—whether at Coblentz or somewhere between—he kept his promise. Brown was present. How much Brown truly understood he never said.

III

Somerton told his story with the strained precision of a man reciting ingredients after a burn, afraid that one missing detail might bring the pain back.

“You know roughly,” he said, “that I came here over painted glass. It begins with those lines from the Sertum about Abbot Thomas—his treasure, his window, and the well in the court. Let me go over it quickly.”

He did—names and dates, Job and John and Zechariah, the laughter of the Abbot, the well carved in marble—then leaned forward, his hands working together as if he were shaping dough.

“On my second visit to Lord D——’s chapel I determined to take every possible note: lettering, scratches, even accidental marks. The first problem was John’s phrase: ‘They have on their vestures a writing which no man knoweth.’ Naturally I asked myself whether there was an inscription on the robes. I could see none. Each figure wore a mantle edged with a broad black border—conspicuous, ugly, a heavy stripe. I was baffled. If it had not been for sheer chance, I believe I should have left the matter as the canons left it.

“There was dust on the glass. Lord D—— came in, saw my hands blackened with it, and sent for a broom to clean the window. As the broom passed over the border of one mantle, it left a long scratch—and in that scratch a yellow stain flashed up, as if someone had scored through soot to reveal saffron beneath. I made the man stop, climbed the ladder, and saw at once what it meant. The black was not stained in the glass at all; it was pigment laid on after firing, and so it could be scraped away. I scraped—and beneath it, in yellow stain on clear ground, were capital letters. I confess I almost laughed aloud.

“I told Lord D—— I had found an inscription that might be interesting and asked to uncover the whole. He left me to it. The pigment came away easily, time having loosened it; within two hours I had cleaned the borders in all three lights. And there was indeed ‘a writing on their vestures which nobody knew.’

“This made me certain I was on the right track. And what did the writing say? I almost tried not to read it while I worked—saving the taste, as it were. When the last black smear was gone and the letters lay bare, my dear Gregory, I could have cried from disappointment. It was nothing but a jumble:

Job: DREVICIOPEDMOOMSMVIVLISLCAVIBASBATAOVT  
St John: RDIIEAMRLESIPVSPODSEEIRSETTAAESGIAVNNR  
Zechariah: DREVICIOPEDMOOMSMVIVLISLCAVIBASBATAOVT

“Hopeless—until I realized it must be a cipher. Simple, I thought, given the date. I copied it with the greatest care. And then another thing confirmed it. After copying Job’s line, I counted the letters—thirty-eight—and at that moment I noticed a scratching on the edge of the border: xxxviii. The glass-painter had marked the number as a check. The other lights had similar notes. Abbot Thomas had insisted on accuracy.

“After that I searched the window for further guidance. I did not ignore Zechariah—‘Upon one stone are seven eyes’—but I soon concluded that referred to something on a stone that could only be found where the treasure lay. I took tracings and sketches and returned to Parsbury to work at leisure.

“You can imagine what I went through. I thought the key would be in old books on secret writing. I hunted Trithemius’s Steganographia—he was Abbot Thomas’s contemporary—Selenius’s Cryptographia, Bacon’s de Augmentis, any number of others. Nothing. Then I tried the ‘most frequent letter’—Latin first, then German—still nothing. I came back to the window, reread my notes, hoping the Abbot had supplied the key in some pictorial hint. No landscape details. No objects. No clue in the canopies. Only the figures’ attitudes remained.

“Job: scroll in left hand, forefinger of right hand extended upward. John: book in left, right hand blessing with two fingers. Zechariah: scroll in left, right hand up like Job but with three fingers. One. Two. Three.” Somerton’s eyes fixed on Gregory’s as if begging him to hear not just words but the sound behind them. “That was the key. I didn’t make it fit at first; then I saw. After the first letter you skip one, after the next you skip two, after that you skip three, and so on. Look what it produced—these are the letters that formed words:

Decem millia auri reposita sunt in puteo in at…

“Ten thousand pieces of gold are laid up in a well in at—an unfinished word.

“At once I remembered the Sertum: puteo in atrio—a well in the court. Atrio. There was my missing piece. The next step was to copy out the remaining letters, omitting those already used. This is what remained:

RVIIOPDOOSMVVISCAVBSBTAOTDIEAMLSIVSPDEERSETAEGIANRFEEALQDVAIMLEATTHOOVM CA.H.Q.E.”

He stopped there, and for a moment the room held only the sounds of travel settling—some distant wheel on cobbles, the thin hiss of a lamp, Brown’s breath. Outside, modern life somewhere far away might have been grinding and hammering, but here the quiet seemed older, as if the very air remembered Latin spoken with a regional mouth now long dead. And in that hush, Somerton’s last fragment of cipher lay like a crusted dish set aside—promising sustenance, and something fouler underneath, that could not be translated into any common tongue without loss.
