Our Virginian heroes, displaying a wisdom far more common in youth than in age, had adopted staunch Jacobite sentiments and professed a profound devotion to the exiled Stuart dynasty. The Banished Prince had recognized Madam Esmond's father as the Marquis of Esmond, and she felt no anger toward an unfortunate lineage so eager to acknowledge the merits of her own family. As for the petty scandals surrounding her sister, Madame de Bernstein, and the Old Chevalier, she dismissed them with scornful indifference, declaring with perfect truth that the first two monarchs of the House of Hanover were scarcely superior to any Stuarts regarding domestic morality. Yet, the *de facto* King was the King, just as his Majesty was the *de jure* one. Having been solemnly crowned and anointed within the church, and having utterly defeated his rival in battle for the crown, the reigning sovereign held undeniable power. In Madam's clear judgment, her sons owed it to both themselves and the sovereign to present themselves at his royal court. If His Majesty chose to confer a lucrative office or a blue or red ribbon upon either lad, she saw no reason for surprise; she was convinced the King knew the Virginian Esmonds as well as any member of his nobility.

Consequently, the lads were strictly commanded to attend court. One dares say their mother would have been furious had she known that George had taken Harry's laced coat on the very day he went to make his bow at Kensington. A century prior, the King's drawing-room was accessible nearly daily to his nobility and gentry; loyalty—especially since the war began—could satisfy itself a dozen times a month with the august sight of the sovereign. A prudent avoidance of the enemy's warships and a gracious acknowledgment of the catastrophic loss Britain would suffer if the royal personage were seized at sea caused the monarch to forego his cherished visits to his native Hanover, compelling him to remain, albeit reluctantly, among his loving subjects. A Hanoverian lady, whose virtues had endeared her to the prince, strove to console him for his enforced absence from Herrenhausen. Through the Countess of Walmoden, on whom imperial beneficence had graciously bestowed a high title of British honor, the revered Defender of the Faith could still hear the accents of his homeland.

Mr. Warrington requested his uncle, an assiduous courtier, to present him to the King. Since Mr. Lambert also needed to visit court to thank His Majesty for his promotion, the two gentlemen traveled together to Kensington, hiring a hackney-coach as Lord Wrotham's carriage was required by its rightful owner, who had returned to his townhouse. Alighting at the Kensington Palace Gate, where the sentries knew and saluted the esteemed General, they proceeded modestly on foot to the sovereign's summer residence. Walking beneath the portico, they entered the gallery leading to the great black marble staircase—a structure richly decorated and painted by Mr. Kent—and passed through several rooms hung with tapestry and adorned with pictures and busts until they reached the King's great drawing-room. Here resided the famous "Venus" by Titian and, among other masterpieces, the depiction of "St. Francis adoring the infant Saviour" by Sir Peter Paul Rubens. Alongside the other court visitors, the gentlemen waited until His Majesty emerged from his private apartments, where he was in conference with certain personages referred to in the newspapers of that era as His Majesty's Ministers.

George Warrington, having never visited a palace before, took leisure to admire the surroundings and the people around him. For the first time, he beheld fine paintings, delighting particularly in a charming work by Sir Anthony Van Dyck representing King Charles the First, his Queen, and Family, as well as the magnificent "Esther before Ahasuerus" by Tintoretto, wherein all figures wore splendid Venetian attire. So captivated was he by these artworks that he barely heard the whispers of his good friend the General, who was murmuring into his young companion's nearly heedless ear the names of the surrounding dignitaries.

"Yonder," said Mr. Lambert, "are two of my Lords of the Admiralty, Mr. Gilbert Elliot and Admiral Boscawen—the latter being your Boscawen, whose fleet fired the first gun in your waters two years ago. That stout gentleman, heavily laden with gold, is Mr. Fox, formerly Minister and now content to serve as Paymaster with a substantial salary."

"He carries the *auri famem* on his person," observed George. "Why, his waistcoat is a perfect Potosi!"

"Aliena appetens—how goes the text?" continued General Lambert. "He loves to acquire wealth and squander it."

"That is my Lord Chief Justice Willes, conversing with my Lord of Salisbury, Doctor Headley. If he serves his God as faithfully as he serves his King, he will be translated to some very high promotion in Heaven. He belongs to your grandfather's time and was loved by Dick Steele yet hated by the Dean. With them stands my Lord of London, the learned Doctor Sherlock. My lords of the lawn sleeves have lost half their honors now. I recall when I was a boy in my mother's hand, she made me kneel to the Bishop of Rochester, the man who crossed the water to become Minister to someone who shall remain nameless—Perkin's Bishop. That handsome fair man is Admiral Smith. He presided over poor Byng's court-martial and strove in vain to save him from his penalty; 'Tom of Ten Thousand,' they call him in the fleet. The French Ambassador had him broken when he was merely a lieutenant for ordering a French man-of-war to lower its topsails to him, yet the King made Tom a captain the very next day. That tall, haughty-looking man is my Lord George Sackville; now that I am a Major-General myself, he will treat me somewhat better than a footman. I wish my stout old Blakeney were here; he is the soldier's darling, as kind and brave as that poker-faced nobleman is brave and... I am your lordship's very humble servant. This is a young gentleman just arrived from America, who was involved in Braddock's sad business two years ago."

"Oh, indeed!" exclaimed the poker-faced nobleman. "I have the honor of speaking to Mr.—?"

"To Major-General Lambert, at your lordship's service, and who served in His Majesty's army some time before you entered it. That, Mr. Warrington, is the first commoner in England, Mr. Speaker Onslow. Where is your uncle? I shall have to present you personally to His Majesty if Sir Miles delays much longer."

As he spoke, the worthy General addressed himself entirely to his young friend, paying no attention whatsoever to his colleague, who stalked away with a startled expression, seemingly amazed by the other's audacity. A hundred years ago, a nobleman was a nobleman and expected to be admired precisely as such. Sir Miles's red waistcoat soon appeared in sight, and many cordial greetings exchanged between him, his nephew, and General Lambert. After all, we have described how Sir Miles was the most affectionate of men. Had the General truly departed Lord Wrotham's house? It seemed timely, given that his lordship wished to occupy it. Very well; but consider what a loss for the neighbors!

"We miss you, we positively miss you, my dear General," cried Sir Miles. "My daughters were in love with those lovely young ladies—upon my word, they were! My Lady Warrington and my girls debated endlessly how they might find an opportunity to make the acquaintance of your charming family. We feel as if we were old friends already, indeed we do, General, if you permit me the liberty of saying so; and we love you, if I may speak frankly, on account of your friendship and kindness to our dear nephews. Though we were a little jealous, I confess a little jealous of them, because they visited you so frequently. Often and often have I told my Lady Warrington, 'My dear, why don't we make acquaintance with the General? Why don't we invite him and his ladies to dine with some other plain country gentlemen?' Carry my most sincere respects to Mrs. Lambert, I pray, sir; and thank her for her goodness to these young gentlemen. My own flesh and blood, sir; my dear, dear brother's boys!" He passed his hand across his manly eyes, choking almost with generous and affectionate emotion.

While they discoursed—George Warrington meanwhile restraining his laughter with admirable gravity—the doors of the King's apartments opened, and pages entered, preceding His Majesty. He was followed by his burly son, His Royal Highness the Duke, a very corpulent Prince clad in a coat and face of blazing scarlet. Behind them came various gentlemen and officers of state, among whom George immediately recognized the famous Mr. Secretary Pitt by his tall stature, eagle eye, sharp beak, and grave, majestic presence. As I behold that solemn figure passing, even a century removed, I protest I feel a present awe and a desire to remove my hat. I am not frightened by George the Second, nor are my eyes dazzled by the portentous appearance of His Royal Highness the Duke of Culloden and Fontenoy; but the Great Commoner, the terrible Cornet of Horse! His figure seems to stride our narrow island of a century past like a Colossus. I hush as he passes in his gouty shoes, his thunderbolt hand wrapped in flannel.
