“I have found that holy place of rest
Still changeless.”

When Mr. Thornton left the house that morning, he was consumed by a storm of emotion so fierce it nearly overpowered him. He felt as though he had been struck blind—not by the sun or by dust, but by something far more devastating: the sight of Margaret Hale, so graceful and composed, yet standing before him like a stranger who had shattered his world. His head throbbed with pain; his pulse beat erratically, as if his body refused to keep pace with the chaos inside. The noise of the city, the glare of the daylight, the constant rumble of carriages and foot traffic—all of it grated on his nerves. He cursed himself for being so weak, for allowing such a woman to wound him so deeply. Yet even as he spoke the words “I hate her,” a sharp, undeniable surge of love pierced through his anger like lightning, leaving him breathless.

His only solace was in clinging to his torment—the stubborn certainty that no matter what she said or did, he would not change. She could scorn him, reject him, treat him with cold indifference; none of it would alter his heart. He loved her. He always would. And he would defy her, and the pain, and the shame.

He paused for a moment, gathering himself, trying to fix this resolve in his mind. An omnibus passed by, heading out into the countryside. The conductor, thinking he wanted a ride, stopped near the curb. There was no point in explaining—so Thornton climbed aboard without a word. As the vehicle rolled forward, he watched the city fade behind him—row after row of houses, then elegant villas with neat gardens, until at last they reached open fields and hedgerows. Soon they arrived at a quiet country town, where all the passengers disembarked. Thornton stepped down too, not because he had a destination, but because everyone else did.

He walked into the fields, moving briskly, as if motion might clear the fog from his mind. Now, in the stillness, the memory came back in full force—the absurdity of his behavior, the way he had thrown caution aside, done exactly what he had sworn never to do, and reaped the consequences he had long predicted. Was he bewitched by those eyes? By the softness of her lips, pressed against his shoulder just yesterday? He couldn’t shake the memory of her arms around him—even if it were only once.

He saw her now in fragments: bold one moment, timid the next; tender, then proud as a queen. He recalled every encounter, each glance, each word, searching for meaning, for a clue to her true nature. He saw her in different clothes, in different moods—each version beautiful, each one unattainable. Even that morning, how fiercely she had looked at him, her eyes blazing with defiance when she thought he believed her affection was born of shared danger. But it wasn’t. She didn’t care for him. Not really.

If he was a fool in the morning, he was no wiser by afternoon. All the journey had done was deepen his conviction: there was no one like Margaret Hale. She would never love him. But neither would anyone—no matter how powerful or persuasive—ever make him stop loving her.

He returned to the market square and boarded another omnibus, heading back to Milton. It was late when he stepped off near his warehouse. The familiar surroundings brought back routine and duty. He had much to do—more than usual, thanks to the unrest of the previous day. He needed to meet with the magistrates, finalize arrangements for the new Irish workers, ensure they were isolated from the discontented locals. And finally, he had to face his mother.

Mrs. Thornton had spent the entire day waiting in the dining room, her hands folded tightly in her lap, her eyes fixed on the door. Every sound—a footstep, a knock, a carriage passing—sent her heart racing. She would snatch up her embroidery, begin stitching again, though her hands trembled and her vision blurred. Then the door would open—and it would be only the servant, or a tradesman, or some minor visitor. Her face would slacken, the rigid mask of composure slipping away, replaced by a look of quiet despair.

She tried to push aside thoughts of the future—the changes that would come with her son’s marriage. Instead, she focused on practicalities: the linen, the household goods. She sorted through baskets filled with tablecloths and napkins, some marked with her initials, G.H.T., others with John’s. Some of the older ones were Dutch damask, fine as silk, things she had cherished since her wedding. She stared at them long, remembering the pride she’d once taken in them. Then, with sudden determination, she unpicked the old initials. She searched for the Turkey-red thread to sew in new ones—but it was gone. She didn’t have the strength to order more. So she stood, staring blankly ahead, seeing visions instead: visions of her son, always at the center, always hers.

Still, he did not come. She assumed he was with Miss Hale. Already, she felt displaced—her place in his heart shrinking. A sharp pang, half physical, half emotional, gripped her chest. She sank into her chair, unable to breathe. But within seconds, she rose again, straighter than ever, a grim smile playing on her lips. She was ready—ready to greet the triumphant news, to pretend joy, while hiding the sorrow she felt deep within.

There was little room in her thoughts for Margaret as a person. She was merely John’s wife-to-be. To become mistress of the house, to inherit all the comforts and honors that came with it—that was the prize. Wealth, status, obedience, friends—all would follow naturally, like jewels adorning a king’s robe. To be chosen by John was enough to elevate any woman above the rest. And Miss Hale—well, she was not entirely unsuitable. If she had been a local girl, Mrs. Thornton might even have liked her. She had spirit, character, a certain fire. True, she was ignorant, prejudiced—typical of southern upbringing. But that could be managed.

A strange comparison formed in her mind—between Fanny and Margaret. For the first time, she spoke harshly to her daughter, scolding her bitterly. Then, as if to atone, she picked up Henry’s Commentaries and forced herself to read, abandoning the linen inspection she usually took pride in.

Then—his step.

She heard him before she registered the sentence she was reading. Her senses sharpened, interpreting every movement: the clatter of his boots on the hall floor, the pause at the hat stand, the slow approach toward the door. Why did he hesitate?

Her head remained bent over the book. She did not look up.

He stood beside her, close enough to feel her presence. He waited for her to finish the paragraph, as if it mattered more than anything else in the world.

With effort, she raised her eyes.

“Well, John?”

He knew what that simple question meant. He had prepared himself. He wanted to jest, to deflect, but the bitterness in his heart could have produced mockery—yet he owed his mother better.

He moved behind her, so she could not see his face, and gently tilted her chin upward. He kissed her gray, stern forehead, whispering, “No one loves me—no one cares for me but you, mother.”

Then he turned away, leaning against the mantelpiece, tears welling in his eyes.

She rose—then staggered. For the first time in her life, the strong woman faltered.

She placed her hands on his shoulders—she was tall—and forced him to look at her.

“Mother’s love is given by God, John,” she said, voice steady. “It holds fast forever. A girl’s love is like smoke—it vanishes with the wind. And she would not have you, my own lad, would she?”

She clenched her teeth, showing them like a dog baring its fangs.

He shook his head slowly.

“I am not fit for her, mother. I knew I was not.”

She ground out words between her teeth. He could not hear them clearly, but the fire in her eyes spoke volumes—curse, perhaps, though not in words. Still, her heart lifted. He was hers again.

“Mother!” he said quickly, “I cannot bear to hear a word against her. Spare me—spare me! I am weak, my heart is broken. I love her—I love her more than ever.”

“And I hate her,” she replied, low and fierce. “I tried not to hate her when she stood between us. I told myself she would make you happy—and I would give my blood to see that. But now… I hate her for your suffering. Yes, John, don’t hide it from me. I am your mother. Your pain is mine. And if you won no hatred for her, I will.”

“Then you make me love her more,” he said softly. “She is unjustly treated by you. I must balance the scales. But why speak of love or hate? She does not care for me. That is enough. Let us never mention her name again. That is the only kindness you can give me.”

“With all my heart,” she answered. “I wish she—and all of her kind—were sent back to where they came from.”

He stood silent, gazing into the fire. She watched him, her dry eyes suddenly filling with unexpected tears. But when he spoke again, she was as calm as ever.

“Warrants are out for three men—conspiracy charges,” he said. “Yesterday’s riot helped end the strike.”

And so, Margaret’s name was never spoken again between them.

They fell back into their usual conversation—facts, business, matters of the day. Their voices were quiet, their tones detached. A stranger might have walked in and thought them strangers—so distant, so cold in their manner. Yet beneath the surface, something had shifted—something irrevocable.
