Edward, buoyed by his gratitude toward Colonel Brandon, hastened to share his joy with Lucy. By the time he arrived at Bartlett's Buildings, his elation was so profound that Lucy could confidently report to Mrs. Jennings, who visited the following day with her warmest congratulations, that she had never witnessed him in such high spirits. Lucy's own happiness was equally assured; she eagerly joined Mrs. Jennings in anticipating a comfortable reunion with the entire party at Delaford Parsonage before Michaelmas. Far from hesitating to acknowledge the debt owed to Elinor, Lucy praised their mutual friendship with heartfelt warmth, readily admitting their obligations to her. She declared openly that no effort undertaken by Miss Dashwood for their benefit, past or future, would ever surprise her, for she believed Elinor capable of anything for those she truly cherished. Regarding Colonel Brandon, Lucy went so far as to venerate him as a saint, earnestly wishing he were treated as such in all worldly matters. She anxiously desired his tithes be maximized and secretly resolved to make full use of his servants, carriage, cattle, and poultry whenever she resided at Delaford.

It had been over a week since John Dashwood visited Berkeley Street, and during that interval, the Dashwoods had shown little regard for his wife's illness save for a single verbal inquiry. Consequently, Elinor felt compelled to pay her a visit. However, this errand clashed with her inclinations and lacked support from her companions. Marianne, not only refusing to accompany her sister but actively urging her against the trip, proved unhelpful. Mrs. Jennings, despite offering her carriage freely, harbored such a strong aversion to Mrs. John Dashwood that neither her curiosity regarding the recent revelations nor her desire to champion Edward's cause could overcome her reluctance to endure another encounter with the woman. Thus, Elinor departed alone to face a visit she held little desire for, risking a private confrontation with a woman whom none of the others wished to tolerate.

Upon arrival, Mrs. Dashwood was initially absent. Before the carriage could depart, Mr. Dashwood emerged unexpectedly. He expressed delight at meeting Elinor, claiming he had intended to visit Berkeley Street himself. Assuring her that Fanny would welcome her presence, he invited her inside. They ascended to the drawing-room, where no one was present. "Fanny is in her own room, I suppose," Mr. Dashwood remarked. "I shall go to her shortly; I am certain she will have no objection to seeing you. Indeed, she wouldn't. Especially now, though you and Marianne were always favorites. Why didn't Marianne come?" Elinor offered excuses for her sister's absence. "I am not sorry to see you alone," Mr. Dashwood replied, "for I have much to discuss. This living of Colonel Brandon's—is it true? Did he actually grant it to Edward? I heard rumors yesterday and came specifically to inquire further."

"It is perfectly true," Elinor confirmed. "Colonel Brandon has presented the rectory of Delaford to Edward."
"Truly!" Mr. Dashwood exclaimed, feigning astonishment. "No relation, no connection whatsoever! And considering the current value of livings, what was the worth of this position?"
"Approximately two hundred pounds per annum."
"Well then," he mused, "if the previous incumbent were elderly and infirm, likely to resign soon, one could secure fourteen hundred pounds for the right of presentation. How did he fail to settle this prior to the donor's death? Now, it would be too late to sell. Yet, a man of Colonel Brandon's discernment! I marvel at his imprudence regarding such a fundamental matter. Well, I am convinced there is inconsistency in almost every human character. Perhaps, upon reflection, the situation is this: Edward holds the living merely until the Colonel's actual buyer reaches maturity."
Elinor firmly contradicted this theory. Having personally conveyed the offer from Colonel Brandon to Edward, she possessed definitive knowledge of the terms, compelling Mr. Dashwood to accept her correction.
"It is truly astonishing!" he cried. "What could be the Colonel's motive?"
"A very simple one: to assist Mr. Ferrars."
"Well, well; regardless of Colonel Brandon's nature, Edward is a fortunate man." He lowered his voice, treating the subject with grave importance. "Do not mention this to Fanny. Although I have broken the news to her and she bears it remarkably well, she dislikes hearing it discussed repeatedly. Mrs. Ferrars knows nothing of it currently, and it is best to conceal it entirely until the marriage occurs. Then, I fear, she must learn everything."
"But why such caution? While it is unlikely Mrs. Ferrars finds joy in her son's financial security, surely she feels nothing given her recent behavior? She cast him aside forever and influenced others to reject him as well. After such actions, she cannot be expected to feel sorrow or joy concerning him. She would not be foolish enough to discard parental comfort while retaining parental anxiety!"
"Oh, Elinor," Mr. Dashwood interjected gently, "your reasoning is sound, but it stems from ignorance of human nature. When Edward's unfortunate marriage happens, rest assured his mother will feel exactly as if she had never rejected him. Therefore, every detail accelerating this calamitous event must be hidden from her. Mrs. Ferrars can never forget that Edward is her son."
"You surprise me; I would think her memory of this must have faded by now."
"You underestimate her excessively. Mrs. Ferrars is one of the most affectionate mothers in the world." Elinor remained silent.
"We are now considering," Mr. Dashwood continued after a pause, placing his hand kindly on hers in a hushed, solemn tone, "Robert's marriage to Miss Morton."
Elinor, amused by the gravity in her brother's demeanor, calmly responded, "I suppose the lady has no choice in the matter."
"Choice? What do you mean?"
"I merely imply that, judging by your tone, it seems indifferent to Miss Morton whether she marries Edward or Robert."
"Certainly, there is no difference. Robert is now effectively the elder son. As for other qualities, they are both pleasant young men; I know not which surpasses the other." Elinor said no more, and John fell silent briefly. His thoughts concluded thus: "On one thing, my dear sister, I can assure you—and I shall, for I know it will please you. From the best authority available, I have learned that while there may have been objections to a certain connection, it would have caused Mrs. Ferrars far less vexation than this current arrangement. 'It would have been incomparably preferable,' she reportedly stated, 'the lesser of two evils.' Nevertheless, that is irrelevant; such attachments are impossible. Still, I deemed it proper to inform you, knowing how much it would gratify you. Do not regret your situation, my dear Elinor. There is no doubt you fare exceedingly well—perhaps better, all things considered." He paused, then asked, "Has Colonel Brandon visited you recently?"

Elinor had absorbed enough information to stir her nerves and inflate her self-regard, finding relief in avoiding further conversation when Mr. Robert Ferrars entered. After a brief exchange, John Dashwood, remembering Fanny's unawareness of Elinor's presence, excused himself to seek her out. Left alone with Robert, Elinor observed how the younger Ferrars cultivated an air of cheerful indifference, deriving self-satisfaction from an unequal distribution of his mother's favor and generosity. This attitude, earned solely through his dissolute lifestyle in contrast to his banished brother's integrity, reinforced Elinor's unfavorable judgment of his intellect and character.

Scarcely two minutes passed before Robert turned the conversation to Edward, eager to discuss the newly acquired living. Elinor recounted the details as she had shared them with John. The impact on Robert, though distinct, was equally pronounced. He erupted into uncontrollable laughter. The notion of Edward becoming a clergyman, residing in a modest parsonage, amused him immensely. Adding to this the whimsical image of Edward reading prayers in a white surplice and announcing the banns for ordinary couples like John Smith and Mary Brown, he found the prospect utterly ridiculous. Elinor, maintaining a stoic silence, watched him with a gaze brimming with contempt. Fortunately, this expression served only to alleviate her own feelings without revealing her disdain.

Robert was brought back from levity to seriousness not by Elinor's rebuke, but by his own sudden sensitivity. "We may treat it as a jest," he finally said, recovering from his prolonged, forced laughter, "but, upon my soul, it is a grave matter. Poor Edward! He is ruined forever. I am deeply sorry; he is a good-hearted creature, perhaps the most well-meaning fellow in the world. Do not judge him lightly based on our limited acquaintance. His manners are certainly not the most engaging. We are not all born with equal talents or social grace. It was pitiable to see him among strangers, yet I swear I never felt so shocked in my life as when the truth revealed itself. I could hardly believe it. My mother was the first to tell me, and feeling compelled to act decisively, I immediately declared, 'Madam, whatever you intend, I must state that if Edward marries this young woman, I shall never see him again.' I was profoundly shocked! Poor Edward! He has sealed his fate, shutting himself out from decent society. Yet, as I told my mother, I am unsurprised; given his upbringing, it was inevitable. My poor mother was half-frantic."
