A stillness was settling over Miss Twinkleton’s establishment, the sort of hush that comes not from obedience but from the long inhalation before a door is opened and a household breathes out into the wider world. Christmas recess was at hand. What had been “the half” within living memory—Miss Twinkleton herself once saying it with a classical air, as if the phrase had been minted at Athens—had, by degrees of fashion and the tyranny of labels, become “the term,” for the sound of it was more collegiate, and the sound, in Cloisterham, mattered nearly as much as the sense.

For a few days past, discipline had loosened like a ribbon untied and hurriedly tucked away. There had been secret club-suppers in bedrooms; contraband sustenance smuggled up the stairs under the very noses of Propriety’s handmaids. A dressed tongue—pink, yielding, and scandalously savoury—had been carved not with a knife but with scissors, and passed round in slices pinched between curling-tongs, the metal warmed from hair and candle-flame and leaving faint metallic kisses on the meat. Marmalade had been portioned out, not upon china but upon plates ingeniously constructed of curlpaper—sticky, citrus-bright, and perfumed, so that the whole room smelt for an hour afterwards of orange peel and hair powder. Cowslip wine, pale as diluted sunlight and faintly grassy in its sweetness, had been quaffed from the squat little measuring-glass in which Rickitts—weakly, much dosed, and always spoken of in a whisper of pity—took her steel drops of a morning. The very acoustics of the house altered: stifled giggles behind doors, the hush of feet on the landings, the quick, conspiratorial clink of the measuring-glass set down upon a washstand, and then, somewhere below, the heavy regularity of the town’s traffic beyond the walls, hoarse as if the High Street never learned refinement.

The housemaids had been bribed with odd fragments of ribbon and with shoes more or less down at heel, to know nothing of crumbs in beds. Airiest costumes appeared on these festive occasions, as if girls could dress themselves into invisibility; and the daring Miss Ferdinand had even performed a sprightly solo on the comb-and-curlpaper—an impromptu orchestra of teeth and tissue—until two flowing-haired executioners, laughing breathlessly, smothered her in her own pillow. Modern life had not yet brought machinery into those corridors, but Cloisterham had its own kind of noise pollution: the constant public clatter outside, the gossip that rang like a bell, and the way a word—once let loose—could vibrate through a town for years. The girls, in their tiny rebellion, made a private soundscape, soft and human, as if instinct told them the soul required gentler noises to thrive.

Nor were these the only signals of dispersal. Boxes appeared in bedrooms—excellent pieces of luggage at other times—and a surprising quantity of packing took place, far outweighing the quantity packed. Largess was distributed among attendants: odds and ends of cold cream and pomatum, hairpins, little remnants of scent that carried, in their very grease and sweetness, the domestic memory of another girl’s dressing-table. Under vows of secrecy, confidences were exchanged about “golden youth of England” expected to call at home at the earliest chance. Miss Giggles, deficient in sentiment and proud of it, declared she would meet such homage by making faces; but she was outvoted by an immense majority whose hopes, like their secret suppers, were best enjoyed in company.

On the last night before recess, it was always a point of honour that nobody should go to sleep, and that ghosts should be encouraged by every possible means. The compact invariably broke down: the girls fell asleep very soon and woke very early, because the body will not be bullied for long, no matter what myths the heart tries to conjure. Still, in those early hours, when the house quieted and the Cathedral’s shadow lay long and cold, it was easy to imagine old voices lodged in the stone, speaking a dialect no longer understood—language dying not in one dramatic moment, but in the daily replacement of a word by a smoother one, a phrase by a more “elegant” phrase, until the past could no longer tell its story in its own tongue.

The concluding ceremony occurred at noon on the day of departure. Miss Twinkleton, supported by Mrs. Tisher, held a drawing-room in her own apartment—the globes already wrapped in brown holland, as if knowledge itself were being put away for the holiday—where glasses of white wine and plates of cut pound-cake sat ready upon the table. The cake was dense, buttery, and crumbling at the edge where it had been sliced; it smelled of sugar warmed and cooled again, a scent that clings to napery and fingers. Miss Twinkleton, in her measured voice, addressed them.

“Ladies, another revolving year has brought us round to that festive period at which the first feelings of our nature bounded in our—” She would have said “bosoms,” as she always nearly did, and as she always checked herself. “Hearts. Hem! Again a revolving year, ladies, has brought us to a pause in our studies—let us hope our greatly advanced studies—and, like the mariner in his bark, the warrior in his tent, the captive in his dungeon, and the traveller in his various conveyances, we yearn for home. Did we say, on such an occasion, in the opening words of Mr. Addison’s impressive tragedy: ‘The dawn is overcast, the morning lowers…’? Not so. From horizon to zenith all is couleur de rose, for all is redolent of our relations and friends. Might we find them prospering as we expect; might they find us prospering as they expect! Ladies, we would now, with our love to one another, wish one another good-bye and happiness, until we meet again. And when the time should come for our resumption of those pursuits which—” here the general depression came like a collective sigh “—pursuits which, pursuits which; then let us ever remember what was said by the Spartan General, in words too trite for repetition, at the battle it were superfluous to specify.”

The handmaidens, in their best caps, brought the trays. The young ladies sipped and crumbled. White wine, sharp and faintly appley, cut through the sweet fat of the cake; the small sound of glasses touching teeth and the soft fall of crumbs made a domestic music, while outside the bespoken coaches began to choke the street with their iron rims and impatient horses. Leave-taking followed quickly. Miss Twinkleton kissed each cheek and, with a practised air of delicate surprise, slipped into each hand a neat letter addressed to the next friend at law, “with Miss Twinkleton’s best compliments” in the corner—as if the missive were an unexpected pleasure entirely unconnected with the bill.

So many times had Rosa seen such dispersals, and so little did she know of any other Home, that she was content to remain where she was; and more content than ever now, because her latest friend remained with her. Yet even this friendship had a blank space in it that Rosa could not help but touch, as the tongue touches a missing tooth. Helena Landless—having been party to her brother’s revelation about Rosa, and having entered into a compact of silence with Mr. Crisparkle—shrank from any mention of Edwin Drood. Why she avoided his name was mysterious; but Rosa heard the avoidance as plainly as if it were sounded aloud. If Helena had not flinched from it, Rosa might have unburdened her perplexed little heart; as it was, she could only turn her doubts over and over, and wonder why this omission should continue now that Helena had said there was to be a good understanding re-established between the two young men when Edwin came down.

It would have made a pretty picture: so many pretty girls kissing Rosa in the cold porch of the Nuns’ House, and that sunny little creature peeping out—unconscious of sly faces carved upon spout and gable peeping at her—and waving farewells as if she were the spirit of rosy youth left behind to keep the deserted place warm. The hoarse High Street became musical with silvery voices calling, “Good-bye, Rosebud darling!” Even the effigy of Mr. Sapsea’s father over the opposite doorway seemed to invite the world to admire the last bright lot left behind. Then the sparkle ran dry, and Cloisterham became itself again—staid, stone-heavy, and loud in its habitual way, as if it could not help grinding its own human sounds into something coarser.

If Rosebud, in her bower, now waited Edwin Drood’s coming with uneasiness, Edwin was uneasy too. With far less force of purpose in him than in the childish beauty crowned fairy queen by Miss Twinkleton’s household, he had a conscience, and Mr. Grewgious had pricked it. That gentleman’s steady convictions of right and wrong in such a case could not be frowned aside or laughed aside. But for the dinner in Staple Inn, and but for the ring he carried in his breast-pocket, Edwin would have drifted into his wedding-day without stopping to think—trusting, loosely, that all would go well. Yet that solemn putting him on his truth to the living and the dead had checked him like a hand on the chest. He must either give the ring to Rosa, or take it back.

Once narrowed into so plain a choice, it was curious how he began to consider Rosa’s claims more unselfishly than ever before, and to be less sure of himself than he had been in all his easy-going days.

“I will be guided by what she says, and by how we get on,” he decided as he walked from the gatehouse to the Nuns’ House. “Whatever comes of it, I will bear his words in mind, and try to be true to the living and the dead.”

Rosa was dressed for walking, and she expected him. The day was bright and frosty; the air had that clean, sharp smell that pricks the nostrils and makes the mouth taste of cold iron. Miss Twinkleton had graciously sanctioned fresh air, and thus the two young people slipped out together before either Miss Twinkleton or Mrs. Tisher could lay so much as a finger upon the shrine of Propriety.

“My dear Eddy,” said Rosa, once they had turned out of the High Street and had reached the quieter walks near the Cathedral and the river, “I want to say something very serious to you. I have been thinking about it for a long, long time.”

“I want to be serious with you too, Rosa dear. I mean to be serious and earnest.”

“Thank you, Eddy. And you won’t think me unkind because I begin, will you? You won’t think I speak for myself only, because I speak first? That wouldn’t be generous, would it? And I know you are generous.”

“I hope I am not ungenerous to you, Rosa.”

He called her Pussy no more. Never again.

“And there is no fear,” pursued Rosa, clasping her hand upon his arm, “of our quarrelling, is there? Because, Eddy, we have so much reason to be very lenient to each other!”

“We will be, Rosa.”

“That’s a dear good boy! Eddy, let us be courageous. Let us change to brother and sister from this day forth.”

“Never be husband and wife?”

“Never!”

Neither spoke for a little while. The river ran beside them with a low, continual voice, as if it had been speaking this sentence for centuries in a language that the banks understood better than the people. After the pause, Edwin said, with some effort:

“Of course I know this has been in both our minds, Rosa, and of course I am in honour bound to confess freely that it doesn’t originate with you.”

“No, nor with you, dear,” she returned, with pathetic earnestness. “That sprung up between us. You are not truly happy in our engagement; I am not truly happy in it. Oh, I am so sorry, so sorry!” And there she broke into tears.

“I am deeply sorry too, Rosa. Deeply sorry for you.”

“And I for you, poor boy! And I for you!”

This gentle forbearance—each thinking first of the other—brought its own reward. A softening light fell upon their position. Their engagement no longer looked wilful or capricious, or a mere failure; it became something more self-denying, honourable, affectionate, and true.

“If we knew yesterday,” said Rosa, drying her eyes, “and we did know yesterday, and on many, many yesterdays, that we were far from right together in those relations which were not of our own choosing, what better could we do to-day than change them? It is natural that we should be sorry, and you see how sorry we both are; but how much better to be sorry now than then!”

“When, Rosa?”

“When it would be too late. And then we should be angry besides.”

Another silence fell, with only the river’s steady undertone and, farther off, the town’s ruder noises drifting in—the grind of wheels, the shout that never quite becomes a song.

“And you know,” said Rosa, innocently, “you couldn’t like me then; and you can always like me now, for I shan’t be a drag upon you, or a worry to you. And I can always like you now, and your sister won’t tease or trifle with you. I often did when I was not your sister, and I beg your pardon for it.”

“Don’t let us come to that, Rosa; or I shall want more pardoning than I like to think of.”

“No indeed, Eddy; you are too hard—my generous boy—upon yourself. Let us sit down, brother, on these ruins, and let me tell you how it was with us. I think I know, for I have considered about it very much since you were here last time. You liked me, didn’t you? You thought I was a nice little thing?”

“Everybody thinks that, Rosa.”

“Do they?” She knitted her brow, musing, and then flashed out with her bright little induction: “Well, but say they do. Surely it wasn’t enough that you should think of me only as other people did; now, was it?”

It was not enough. The point held.

“And that is just what I mean; that is just how it was with us,” said Rosa. “You liked me very well, and you had grown used to me, and had grown used to the idea of our being married. You accepted the situation as an inevitable kind of thing, didn’t you? It was to be, you thought, and why discuss or dispute it?”

It was new and strange to Edwin to see himself held up so clearly in her glass. He had always patronised her a little, in his sense of superiority; and now, walking in the thin cold sunlight, he felt the patronage look shabby—like a grand word that, when repeated, has lost its meaning.

“All that I say of you is true of me as well, Eddy. Unless it was, I might not be bold enough to say it. Only the difference between us was that, by little and little, there crept into my mind a habit of thinking about it instead of dismissing it. My life is not so busy as yours, you see, and I haven’t so many things to think of. So I thought about it very much—and I cried about it very much too, though that wasn’t your fault, poor boy—when all at once my guardian came down to prepare for my leaving the Nuns’ House. I tried to hint to him that I wasn’t quite settled, but I hesitated and failed, and he didn’t understand me. But he is a good, good man. And he put before me so kindly, and yet so strongly, how seriously we ought to consider, in our circumstances, that I resolved to speak to you the next moment we were alone and grave. And if I seemed to come to it easily just now, because I came to it all at once, don’t think it was so really, Eddy, for oh, it was very, very hard, and oh, I am very, very sorry!”

Her full heart broke into tears again. Edwin put his arm about her waist, and they walked on by the river-side together, their breath visible now and then like words exhaled and lost.

“Your guardian has spoken to me too, Rosa dear. I saw him before I left London.”

His right hand sought, by habit, the little case in his breast. The ring was there—cold even through cloth, as if it kept the temperature of a past intention. He checked his hand. If he was to take it back, why speak of it?

“And that made you more serious about it, didn’t it, Eddy? And if I hadn’t spoken as I have, you would have spoken to me? I hope you can tell me so. I don’t like it to be all my doing, though it is so much better for us.”

“Yes, I should have spoken; I should have put everything before you; I came intending to do it. But I never could have spoken to you as you have spoken to me, Rosa.”

“Don’t say you mean so coldly or unkindly, Eddy, please, if you can help it.”

“I mean so sensibly and delicately, so wisely and affectionately.”

“That’s my dear brother!” She kissed his hand in a little rapture, as if the new name had taste and warmth in it. “The dear girls will be dreadfully disappointed,” she added, laughing with tears still bright upon her lashes. “They’ve looked forward to it so, poor pets!”

“Ah! but I fear it will be a worse disappointment to Jack,” said Edwin Drood, with a start. “I never thought of Jack!”

Her swift, intent look at him—gone as quickly as lightning—was so vivid that it seemed to leave a mark. At once she looked down, confused, and breathed quickly.

“You don’t doubt its being a blow to Jack, Rosa?”

She answered evasively and hurriedly: why should she? She had not thought about it. He seemed to have so little to do with it.

“My dear child! can you suppose that anyone so wrapped up in another—Mrs. Tope’s expression, not mine—as Jack is in me, could fail to be struck all of a heap by such a sudden and complete change in my life? I say sudden, because it will be sudden to him, you know.”

She nodded two or three times, lips parted as if assent were on them, yet no sound came. Her breathing did not slow.

“How shall I tell Jack?” Edwin ruminated, his thoughts busy with the practical difficulty. If he had been less occupied, he must have noticed her singular agitation. “I never thought of Jack. It must be broken to him, before the town-crier knows it. I dine with the dear fellow to-morrow and next day—Christmas Eve and Christmas Day—but it would never do to spoil his feast-days. He always worries about me, and moddley-coddleys in the merest trifles. The news is sure to overset him. How on earth shall this be broken to Jack?”

“He must be told, I suppose?” said Rosa.

“My dear Rosa! who ought to be in our confidence, if not Jack?”

“My guardian promised to come down, if I should write and ask him. I am going to do so. Would you like to leave it to him?”

“A bright idea!” cried Edwin. “The other trustee. Nothing more natural. He comes down, he goes to Jack, he relates what we have agreed upon, and he states our case better than we could. He has already spoken feelingly to you, he has already spoken feelingly to me, and he’ll put the whole thing feelingly to Jack. That’s it! I am not a coward, Rosa, but to tell you a secret, I am a little afraid of Jack.”

“No, no! you are not afraid of him!” cried Rosa, turning white and clasping her hands.

“Why, sister Rosa, sister Rosa, what do you see from the turret?” said Edwin, trying to rally her. “My dear girl!”

“You frightened me.”

“Most unintentionally, but I am as sorry as if I had meant to do it. Could you suppose for a moment, from any loose way of speaking, that I was literally afraid of the dear fond fellow? What I mean is, he is subject to a kind of paroxysm, or fit—I saw him in it once—and I don’t know but that so great a surprise, coming upon him direct from me whom he is so wrapped up in, might bring it on perhaps. Which—and this is the secret I was going to tell you—is another reason for your guardian’s making the communication. He is so steady, precise, and exact, that he will talk Jack’s thoughts into shape in no time; whereas with me Jack is always impulsive and hurried, and, I may say, almost womanish.”

Rosa seemed convinced. Perhaps because her own point of view of “Jack” was altogether different, she felt comforted—protected—by the interposition of Mr. Grewgious between herself and him.

Again Edwin’s hand closed upon the ring-case and again he checked it with the same thought: it was certain now he must give it back; why speak of it? That pretty sympathetic nature, so sorry for him in the blight of their childish hopes, so ready to stand alone in a new world and weave fresh wreaths from whatever flowers it might bear, would only be grieved by those sorrowful jewels. To what purpose? They were a sign of broken joys and baseless projects; in their very beauty they made a cruel satire on human loves and plans that forecast nothing and crumble like dust. Let them be. He would restore them to her guardian when he came down; he in his turn would restore them to the cabinet from which he had unwillingly taken them; and there—like old letters, old vows, records of aspirations come to nothing—they would lie disregarded until, being valuable, they were sold into circulation again, to repeat their former round. Let them be, and lie unspoken of, against his heart.

Among the countless chains that time and circumstance forge day and night, there was one chain forged in the instant of that small conclusion—riveted to the foundations of heaven and earth, and gifted with invincible force to hold and drag.

They walked on by the river, speaking now of separate plans: he would quicken his departure from England; she would remain where she was, at least as long as Helena remained. The poor dear girls should be prepared gently, and first of all Miss Twinkleton should be confided in by Rosa, even before Mr. Grewgious reappeared. It must be made clear everywhere that she and Edwin were the best of friends. There had never been so serene an understanding between them since they were first affianced. Yet each held a reservation: on Rosa’s side, that she intended—through her guardian—to withdraw herself immediately from the tuition of her music-master; on Edwin’s, that he had begun to entertain wandering speculations whether it might ever come to pass that he would know more of Miss Landless.

The bright frosty day declined. The sun dipped into the river far behind them, and the old city lay red before them, as their walk drew to a close. The moaning water cast its seaweed duskily at their feet when they turned from its margin, and rooks hovered above with hoarse cries—dark splashes in the darkening air, a harsh chorus that seemed part of Cloisterham’s permanent sound, older than any spoken promise.

“I will prepare Jack for my flitting soon,” said Edwin, in a low voice, “and I will but see your guardian when he comes, and then go before they speak together. It will be better done without my being by. Don’t you think so?”

“Yes.”

“We know we have done right, Rosa?”

“Yes.”

“We know we are better so, even now?”

“And shall be far, far better so by-and-by.”

Still, that lingering tenderness for what they relinquished made them prolong the parting. When they reached the elm-trees by the Cathedral, where they had last sat together, they stopped as if by consent, and Rosa raised her face to his as she had never raised it in the old days—for they were old already.

“God bless you, dear! Good-bye!”

“God bless you, dear! Good-bye!”

They kissed each other fervently.

“Now, please take me home, Eddy, and let me be by myself.”

“Don’t look round, Rosa,” he cautioned her, drawing her arm through his. “Didn’t you see Jack?”

“No! Where?”

“Under the trees. He saw us, as we took leave of each other. Poor fellow! He little thinks we have parted. This will be a blow to him, I am much afraid.”

She hurried on without resting, and hurried still until they passed under the gatehouse into the street. Once there, she asked, panting as if the very air had become difficult:

“Has he followed us? You can look without seeming to. Is he behind?”

“No. Yes, he is! He has just passed out under the gateway. The dear sympathetic old fellow likes to keep us in sight. I am afraid he will be bitterly disappointed!”

She pulled hurriedly at the handle of the hoarse old bell—the sound of it rough and antiquated, as if the metal itself spoke an old dialect—and the gate soon opened. Before going in, she gave Edwin one last wide, wondering look, imploring as if it would force understanding into him: Oh! don’t you understand?

And out of that look he vanished from her view.
