There exists within the human soul a latent faculty capable of manipulating circumstances. What might otherwise obscure or dim one's essence can be transformed into mere contingencies of grandeur, serving to elevate one's innate brilliance. Consider the moon on a silent summer evening, rising behind a dense, towering forest. Its light burns like an unconsumed fire through the green canopy, igniting the foliage until the dark shadows merge into a splendor indistinguishable from the moonlight itself, woven together by a power vast and serene.

Daniel Deronda emerged from his narrow Chelsea residence in a mood that demanded vigorous physical exertion to dispel what he termed the "fumes" of his irritation. Heading toward the city, the sight of the Chelsea Steps and the waiting boats instantly resolved his dilemma: he would abandon the passive confinement of a carriage. Instead, he hailed a wherry and took an oar.

His destination was Ram's bookshop. He had visited yesterday only to arrive too late for Mordecai's afternoon vigil, having been informed that the remarkable resident invariably appeared between five and six. Deronda felt a pressing need to deepen his acquaintance with this enigmatic inhabitant of the Cohen household. This familiarity was a prerequisite for reclaiming his ring; he feared their previous conversations ended too abruptly, leaving Mordecai's interest waning like a retractable drawbridge, threatening to seal off any future ease of communication. As the rhythm of rowing warmed his limbs and focused his mind on his errand and his intentions regarding Mirah, Deronda underwent his customary shift in perspective. He mentally stepped into the shoes of the man he had previously viewed merely as a tool for his own ends. He chided himself for resembling a recruiting sergeant who secures soldiers without caring for the dramas that bring them to him.

*"Suppose I obtain the information I crave from this man,"* Deronda mused, *"and he remains indifferent to revealing more of himself—or why he seems to harbor expectations of me that have gone unfulfilled? Such curiosity would fade. Yet, perhaps they passed each other like two ships crossing paths, each carrying an exile who would have recognized the other had they gazed face-to-face. Though there is little chance of a unique bond between me and this poor fellow, whose journey, I suspect, is nearly over... Still, I wonder if there is much of that profound mutual longing between strangers who exchange vacant glances, or even those who secretly desire one another's absence in a crowd. Regardless, one creates opportunities for connection by adopting the recruiter's strategy."*

As the wherry approached Blackfriars Bridge, where Deronda intended to disembark, the clock struck half-past four. The gray daylight was dissolving gloriously; the western clouds fractured into narrowing bands of purple before a sweeping expanse of saffron clarity. The sky held a monumental calm, but on the river, this light danced as a luminous motion—the alternating flashes of ripples, the sudden glow of a brown sail, the transition of laden barges from shadow to color—all responding actively to the brooding beauty above.

Now sufficiently heated, Deronda set down the oar and draped his Inverness cape over his shoulders. Just as he raised his head to fasten the final button, his eyes caught a familiar face peering over the bridge parapet. Bathed in the western light, the visage appeared with startling distinctness and brilliance—a striking emblem of physical emaciation and spiritual urgency. It was Mordecai. Having watched the westward horizon, Mordecai had spotted the approaching boat. Initially drawn by its movement, his gaze sharpened into a trembling anticipation, a presentiment seizing him until the near figure finally lifted its face toward him—the face of his visions. Immediately, Mordecai raised a white hand, beckoning repeatedly.

Anticipating recognition, Deronda had signaled without delay, and the reply was instant. Mordecai tipped his cap and waved it, feeling in that moment that his internal prophecy was realized. Obstacles, contradictions, all dissolved into a sense of completion flooding his soul with the satisfaction of a longed-for reunion. His exultation mirrored that of an experimenter witnessing the first stirrings of change corresponding to what his feverish concentration had foreseen. The anticipated friend emerged from the golden backdrop and signaled; this was reality; the remainder was destiny.

Within three minutes, Deronda had landed, settled his fare, and joined Mordecai, who stood perfectly still, waiting for him.

"I was delighted to see you standing here," Deronda said. "I intended to proceed to the bookshop to find you again. I was there yesterday—did they mention it?"

"Yes," Mordecai replied with simple gravity. "That was precisely why I came to the bridge."

To Deronda, this straightforward answer was profoundly mysterious. Did the peculiarities of this man truly stem from some form of mental alienation, as Cohen had hinted?

"You knew nothing of my presence in Chelsea?" Deronda asked after a pause.

"No; but I expected you to descend the river. I have been waiting for you these five years." Mordecai's sunken eyes locked onto Deronda's with a look of affectionate dependence, simultaneously pathetic and solemn. Deronda's sensitivity responded fully, despite his inability to accept that this strangely revealed relationship was founded on illusion.

"It will please me greatly if I can be of genuine use to you," Deronda answered earnestly. "Shall we take a cab to wherever you wish? You have likely walked enough with your short breath."

"To the bookshop," Mordecai said, turning back toward the river. "It will soon be time for me to be there. But first, look upstream. See the sky slowly fading. I have always loved this bridge; I stood upon it as a child. It is a meeting place for spiritual messengers. It is true what the Masters said: every order of existence has its angel—that is, the full message of each from afar. Here, I have listened to the messages of earth and sky. When I was stronger, I would linger watching the stars in the deep heavens. But this time, just about sunset, was what I loved best. It sank into me, dwelling with me—fading, slowly fading. It was my own decline. It paused, it waited, until finally it brought me my new life, my new self—one who will live when this breath is spent."

Deronda remained silent, deeply affected. His initial suspicion—that Mordecai might suffer from hallucinations or monomania due to a strained constitution—yielded to a more submissive expectancy. His nature was too expansive, too willing to conceive realms beyond his immediate experience, to settle quickly on the easy diagnosis of "madness" whenever a consciousness displayed such fullness and conviction where his own was empty. Consistent with his disposition, he preferred to meet claims of need rather than resist them. This claim radiated a solemnity from Mordecai that seemed to nullify his outward poverty, elevating him into authority as if he were a supernatural guide shedding a mean disguise to reveal a manifest Power. This impression was reinforced by a resolved quietude in Mordecai's demeanor, born of his conviction of fulfillment. After a moment of silence, Mordecai said, "Let us go now." Once seated in the cab, he added, "We will alight at the end of the street and walk to the shop. You may examine the books; Mr. Ram is departing shortly and will leave us alone."

It appeared this enthusiast was as cautious and attuned to the judgments of others as if he were the antithesis of enthusiasm known as "a man of the world."

As the cab rattled along, Mirah remained present in Deronda's mind amidst this strange experience, though he foresaw that the conversation would be dictated by Mordecai, not himself. He was no longer certain what questions he could pose. Reacting internally to his own mood, he thought, *"I suppose I am in a state of complete superstition, akin to awaiting a destiny that interprets an oracle. Yet, there must be a strong bond between us, for he feels it so intensely. Great heaven! What relation has proven more potent in the world than faith, even when mistaken? Than expectation, even when perpetually disappointed? Will my side of this relation prove disappointing or fulfilling? Well, if it is ever possible for me to fulfill, I will not disappoint."*

Ten minutes later, the two men entered the small, gas-lit bookshop. Possessing an intensity of consciousness as if they were undeclared lovers, they faced one another, each removing their hats instinctively, wishing to see the other fully. Mordecai leaned his back against the small counter, while Deronda stood against the opposite wall, barely four feet away. *If I could capture those two faces,* the narrator reflects, *as Titian captured contrasting types in 'The Tribute Money,' it would suffice. Imagine—the pathetic mark of consumption, paired with a brilliant glance, set upon a sharply defined structure of features reminiscent of a forsaken temple, lending an air of reluctant distance. Now imagine this on a Jewish face, naturally expressive of an eager mind—the face of a man little past thirty, yet bearing the weight of an age prolonged by suffering. His black hair and beard throw out the yellow pallor of his skin; his labored breathing marks his mobile nostrils; his wasted yellow hands are conspicuous on his folded arms.
