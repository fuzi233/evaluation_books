The tiny hilltop village of Bohun Beacon clung to a slope so steep the sharp spire of its church looked less like manmade stonework than the jagged peak of some small, natural mountain. At the church’s base hunkered a smithy, its forge glowing red at all hours, its earthen floors littered with heaps of scrap iron and stacks of hammers of every size. Across a rough cobblestone crossroads directly opposite the shop stood *The Blue Boar*, the village’s only inn. It was at this crossway, in the pale, leaden hush of pre-sunrise, that two brothers crossed paths: one starting his day, the other only just wrapping up his night.

The Reverend and Honourable Wilfred Bohun, a man of fierce, unyielding piety, was making his way toward the church to carry out a brutal dawn ritual of private prayer and contemplation. His elder brother, Colonel the Honourable Norman Bohun, was not devout in the slightest. He sat slouched on the bench outside *The Blue Boar* in crumpled evening dress, sipping a drink that any impartial observer could fairly label either the last glass of his Tuesday debauch or the first of his Wednesday morning. The colonel did not care to draw the distinction.

The Bohuns were one of the vanishingly few English aristocratic families whose line stretched unbroken back to the Crusades, their pennon once flown in the streets of Jerusalem. But the old myth that ancient noble houses hold fast to chivalric tradition is a lie. It is only the poor who guard their old stories; aristocrats chase fashion, not legacy. The Bohuns had been wild, lawless Mohocks under Queen Anne, vain, foppish Mashers under Victoria, and over the last two centuries the line had rotted down into a string of drunkards, dandified degenerates, and whispered rumours of inherited madness.

Norman Bohun embodied that decay. He was a tall, powerfully built man, greying in age but still with shockingly bright yellow hair that made him look half leonine, half predatory wolf. His blue eyes had sunk so deep into their sockets they looked black, set too close together above a pair of drooping, waxed yellow moustaches. Deep furrows ran from his nostrils down to his jaw, carving a permanent sneer into his face. Over his evening tails he wore a pale yellow coat that looked more like a silk dressing gown than an overcoat, and perched on the back of his head was an absurdly wide-brimmed bright green hat, some random oriental curio he’d picked up on a drunken trip abroad. He was proud of how ridiculous the outfit looked, prouder still of how he managed to carry it off like it was formal court wear.

His younger brother the curate shared his blonde hair and fine-boned elegance, but he was buttoned up to the chin in severe black clerical cloth, clean-shaven, scholarly, and perpetually taut with nervous energy. He seemed to live for nothing but his faith, though some of the villagers – most notably the Presbyterian blacksmith Simeon Barnes – grumbled that his devotion was to Gothic architecture rather than God, that his constant ghostly hauntings of the nooks and crannies of the church were just a more polished, purer version of the same morbid hunger for beauty that drove his older brother to chase wine and strange women. The charge was unfair, a misreading of his love for secluded private prayer, and it was true he was often found kneeling not before the main altar, but hidden away in the crypts, the gallery rafters, even the belfry.

On that dawn, he was about to cut across the smithy yard to enter the church when he paused, frowning, to see his brother’s sunken black eyes fixed on the exact same spot. He knew Norman had no interest in the church itself. The only other thing to catch his attention was the smithy – and Wilfred had heard the village rumours about Simeon’s famously beautiful, much-admired wife. He shot a suspicious glance across the open shed, and the colonel stood up laughing to greet him.

“Good morning, Wilfred,” he drawled. “Being a good landlord, I’m watching over my people sleeplessly. I’m just about to pop in to call on the blacksmith.”

Wilfred stared hard at the cobblestones at his feet. “Simeon’s out. He’s over in Greenford.”

Norman laughed silently, eyes glinting. “I know. That’s exactly why I’m calling.”

“Norman,” the curate said, still not looking up, “are you ever afraid of being struck down by thunderbolts?”

The colonel raised an amused eyebrow. “What, is your new hobby meteorology now?”

“I mean,” Wilfred said, “do you never stop to think God might strike you dead right here in the street?”

“Ah, I see. Your new hobby’s folk lore.”

“I know your hobby is blasphemy,” Wilfred snapped, stung to the core of his devout being. “And if you won’t fear God, you’d do well to fear man. Simeon Barnes is the strongest man for forty miles in any direction. He could pick you up and throw you clean over the church wall.”

The jab landed true. The sneer lines on Norman’s face darkened, deepened, for a long, tense second – before he recovered his lazy cruel good humour and laughed, showing two pointed dog-like front teeth under his moustache. “Smart of the last of the Bohuns to head out wearing a little bit of armour, then, isn’t it?”

He plucked the absurd green hat off his head to show Wilfred it was lined all over with polished steel, a light Japanese war helmet he’d ripped off an old trophy hanging in the family hall. “First hat that came to hand,” he said airily. “Always grab the nearest hat – and the nearest woman, too.”

“Barnes is still in Greenford,” Wilfred said quietly. “No one knows for certain when he’ll be back.” He turned and walked into the church with his head bowed, crossing himself like a man desperate to flee the presence of something unclean.

He hoped to leave his brother’s gross wickedness behind him in the cool, dim twilight of the Gothic nave, but the morning was set to throw nothing but small shocks at his quiet routine. The church was always empty at this pre-dawn hour – but today, a kneeling figure scrambled hastily to its feet as he stepped through the door, and stumbled towards the light of the doorway. Wilfred froze in surprise. It was Mad Joe, the village idiot, Simeon Barnes’ nephew, a surly, strong slouch of a lad who never cared for church or anything else, who no one had ever seen pray in his life. His face was pale and round as the moon, his mouth hanging perpetually half open, and he gave no sign of what he’d been doing kneeling there in the dark.

Wilfred stood rooted long enough to watch Joe trudge out into the sun, then watch his dissolute brother hail the halfwit with cruel jocularity, even throwing pennies at his gaping open mouth as if he was trying to score a direct hit. The ugly, sunlit image of that cruelty and stupidity drove the ascetic curate deeper into the church to find purification in prayer. He climbed the stairs to his usual favorite pew in the gallery, right beneath the vivid blue stained glass window he loved, the one painted with an angel carrying white lilies. He sank into the soft, cool blues and silvers of the glass, forgetting his brother and the halfwit both, until half an hour later, a hand on his shoulder pulled him back to the real world.

It was Gibbs the cobbler, the village’s famous atheist, who had come rushing to fetch him. His presence inside a church was more of an anomaly than Mad Joe’s earlier visit, and he spoke to Wilfred in a hoarse, huskily sympathetic whisper he would never have used normally.

“I’m sorry to disturb you, sir, but we thought we had no right to wait to tell you. There’s been a dreadful accident – your brother, sir, he’s dead. You’d best come down at once.”

Wilfred scrambled to his feet, his frail hands clenching. “What fresh devilry has he gotten himself into now?” he snapped, half expecting another scandal.

“Nothing, sir, that he can get himself out of,” the cobbler coughed awkwardly. “He’s done for.”

The curate followed him down the winding short stairwell that led out to a landing a few feet higher than the street, and he saw the whole tragedy laid out beneath him like a map. Half a dozen men clustered in the smithy yard: the local doctor, the Presbyterian minister, a police inspector in uniform, and the small quiet Roman Catholic priest Father Brown, who was talking in low, gentle tones to Simeon’s wife – a magnificent red-haired woman sobbing blindly on a wooden bench. Splayed face down between the groups, right beside the heap of spare hammers, was Norman Bohun, spread-eagled in his crumpled evening dress. From the height of the landing Wilfred could make out every detail of his clothes, even the Bohun family signet rings on his fingers – but his skull was nothing but a hideous, spattered mess of black blood and crushed bone.

Wilfred ran down the steps into the yard at once. “My brother’s dead!” he stammered. “What does this mean? What horrible mystery is this?”

The cobbler spoke up bluntly before anyone else could reply. “There’s horror enough, sir, but no real mystery at all. There’s only one man for forty miles around strong enough to strike a blow like that, and he’s the man with more reason to do it than anyone else.”

“We can’t prejudge anything,” the black-bearded local doctor cut in nervously, adjusting his glasses. “But I can corroborate that about the blow. It’s impossible. Metaphors can’t describe it. His skull wasn’t just smashed like an eggshell – fragments of bone were driven straight down into the ground like bullets fired into mud. It was the hand of a giant. No normal man alive could have done this. It clears almost every person within a hundred miles of suspicion at once. If you or I were accused, we’d be acquitted as fast as a baby accused of stealing Nelson’s Column.”

“That’s what I’m saying!” Gibbs the cobbler insisted. “Only Simeon Barnes could have done this. Where is he, anyway?”

“He’s still in Greenford,” Wilfred faltered.

“More likely he’s already halfway to France,” Gibbs muttered.

“No, he’s not,” came a quiet, colourless voice from little Father Brown, who had been hovering at the edge of the group. “He’s walking up the road right now.”

Everyone turned to stare at the winding path that cut across the fields below the village, and sure enough, the giant blacksmith Simeon Barnes was striding up it at his long, powerful pace, a heavy sledgehammer slung over his shoulder, walking and chatting calmly with two other men at his side. He was never a cheerful man, but he looked completely at ease.

“Good God!” Gibbs yelled. “And he’s carrying the very hammer he used to kill him!”

“No,” the sandy-moustached inspector said, speaking for the first time, nodding towards a spot near the church wall. “The weapon’s over there. We left the body and all evidence exactly as we found it.”

Everyone turned to look. The hammer lying on the grass was tiny, the smallest lightest claw hammer in the whole collection of smith’s tools, easy to miss among all the heavy sledges – but its iron edge was crusted dark with dried blood and flecks of the colonel’s yellow hair.

Father Brown knelt down to stare at it for a long silent moment, then spoke without looking up, his dull quiet voice carrying a new sharp note. “Mr. Gibbs was wrong to say there’s no mystery. There’s at least one very big one: why would a giant of a man like Simeon Barnes use a tiny little hammer like this, when there are ten far heavier, far more powerful hammers stacked right at his feet?”

“Forget that!” Gibbs fumed, bouncing on his heels. “What do we do about Barnes?”

“Leave him be,” Father Brown said calmly. “He’s coming here of his own accord. Those two men with him are from Greenford, solid members of his revival mission.”

Even as he spoke, the blacksmith rounded the church corner and strode into his own yard. He froze, the sledgehammer slipping out of his grip to clatter on the cobblestones. The inspector walked up to him formally.

“I won’t ask you to explain yourself, Barnes. You don’t have to say a word. I hope you’re innocent, and we can prove it. But I am duty bound to arrest you in the King’s name for the murder of Colonel Norman Bohun.”

“Don’t say anything!” Gibbs the atheist yelped, dancing around in giddy admiration of the British legal system. “They have to prove everything! They haven’t even proved that smashed mess on the ground is the colonel!”

“That won’t fly,” the doctor said, half smiling aside to Father Brown. “I was his personal physician for twenty years. His hands were very fine, but very distinct: his second and third fingers were exactly the same length. That’s Norman Bohun, no doubt about it.”

The blacksmith’s dark eyes followed the doctor’s glance down to the body on the ground. “Is he dead?” he said, completely calm. “Then he’s damned.”

“Don’t speak, don’t say a single word!” Gibbs hissed at him.

The blacksmith turned on him, his face hard with the fervour of a fanatic. “You infidels can dodge and hide like foxes because the world’s laws favour you. But God guards his own, and you’ll see that today. When did this dog die in his sins?” he asked, turning back to the group.

“I saw him alive at six o’clock this morning,” Wilfred stammered.

“God is good,” Simeon said. “Inspector, I don’t mind being arrested at all. You might mind arresting me, though. I’ll walk out of the courtroom with my name completely cleared. You might walk out having completely wrecked your career.”

The inspector’s eyes went wide with sudden surprise. Simeon nodded over to his two friends standing at the gate. “Those two men with me are respected tradesmen from Greenford. They, and twenty other people from our Presbyterian revival mission, can swear I was sitting in our all-night committee room from before midnight, all through to sunrise, and long after. I never left Greenford at all. If I were a heathen I’d let you arrest me and make a fool of yourself. But I’m a Christian, so I’m giving you a choice. Do you want to hear my alibi now, or do you want to look like a fool in court?”

The inspector quickly called the two witnesses over. Every word they said was impossible to disbelieve, and minutes later Simeon Barnes’ innocence stood as solid and unshakable as the stone church spire towering over the village. A heavy, awkward silence settled over the whole group. To break it, Wilfred turned to the small priest beside him.

“You seem very interested in that little hammer, Father Brown.”

“Yes,” Father Brown said, tilting his head. “I can’t stop wondering why it’s so small.”

“By Jove, you’re right!” the doctor exclaimed, spinning around. “Who would pick up a tiny little hammer when ten bigger, heavier ones are right there?” He leaned in to whisper in Wilfred’s ear, voice hissing with excitement. “Only someone who can’t lift a big hammer. It’s nothing to do with courage – it’s simple shoulder strength. A woman could bash out ten men’s brains with a light hammer and never break a sweat. She couldn’t even kill a beetle with a heavy sledge.”

Wilfred stared at him, hypnotised with horror, as the doctor went on. “Why is everyone so stupidly assuming only a husband hates his wife’s lover? Nine times out of ten, it’s the wife who hates him far more. Who knows what insults, what threats Norman Bohun levelled at her – look!”

He nodded towards the red-haired woman on the bench. She had lifted her head, tears drying on her face, and her eyes were fixed on the corpse with a wild, electric glare that looked almost insane.

Wilfred waved a limp hand as if he wanted to wave the whole thought away, but Father Brown brushed a fleck of ash from the smithy furnace off his sleeve and shook his head. “Your logic is very clever, doctor. But your physics are impossible. I agree a woman would always reach for the smallest hammer. But no woman born could have crushed that man’s skull so flat – especially when he was wearing a solid steel helmet under that stupid green hat of his, the kind that would shatter like glass if hit hard enough to crush the skull beneath. That level of force is impossible for any woman.”

The doctor sulked, crossing his arms. “Fine. Then no sane man would ever pick up that little hammer when he could use a big one.”

Wilfred Bohun’s hands flew up to clutch at his own pale yellow hair. He dropped them a second later, his face lit up with a feverish, excited light. “That’s it!” he cried. “You said the exact word I was looking for. No man but an idiot would pick up that little hammer.”

He was shaking all over with the thrill of his realisation. “I’m a priest,” he said unsteadily, “and a priest should never be the one to send a man to the gallows. But I thank God I’ve found the killer now – a killer who can never be hanged. This morning when I walked into the church I found Mad Joe kneeling there praying, the village idiot who never prayed a day in his life.
