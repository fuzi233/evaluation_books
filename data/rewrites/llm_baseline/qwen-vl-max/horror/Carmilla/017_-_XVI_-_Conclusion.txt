I write this not with calm, but with a trembling hand and a heart still haunted by the horror that once gripped my soul. You may think I am composed, but nothing could be further from the truth. The mere act of recalling these events sends shivers through my bones, as if the shadows of the past have returned to clutch at me once more. Only your persistent request—repeated so earnestly—could have brought me to this task, one that has left me unnerved for months, reviving the dread that followed me long after I believed myself free.

Let me speak briefly of Baron Vordenburg, the eccentric scholar whose knowledge led us to the tomb of Countess Mircalla. He had settled in Gratz, living in modest poverty, his family’s once-great estates in Upper Styria reduced to little more than memory. Yet he devoted himself entirely to the study of vampirism—an ancient, terrifying phenomenon he approached with meticulous rigor. His library was filled with obscure texts: *Magia Posthuma*, *Phlegon de Mirabilibus*, *Augustinus de cura pro Mortuis*, and *Philosophicae et Christianae Cogitationes de Vampiris* by John Christofer Herenberg. He had compiled an exhaustive digest of judicial records, from which he distilled a chilling system of rules governing the vampire’s existence.

One myth he dispelled: the notion that vampires appear deathly pale. In truth, they bear the look of healthy life—whether in their graves or among humans. When uncovered, they exhibit all the signs that confirmed the vampire nature of the long-dead Countess Karnstein. But how they emerge from their coffins each night, returning without disturbing the earth or leaving a trace, remains a mystery beyond reason. Their dual existence depends on nightly sleep in the grave, while their waking hours are sustained by the insatiable hunger for living blood.

Vampires often fixate upon individuals with an intensity akin to love—a passion that drives them to endless patience and cunning. They will pursue their chosen victim relentlessly, using charm and deceit to draw them closer, prolonging their torment like a perverse artist savoring every stroke. In such cases, they seem to crave some form of complicity, a twisted intimacy. But when no such connection exists, they strike swiftly—overpowering their prey, draining them in one brutal feast.

There is a peculiar law governing their names. In Mircalla’s case, her identity could only be revealed through anagrams—words formed from her true name, letter for letter, without addition or omission. “Carmilla” and “Millarca” both fulfilled this condition. My father told the Baron of the tale involving the Moravian nobleman and the vampire at Karnstein churchyard. Then he asked how the Baron had found the hidden tomb.

The Baron smiled—a thin, secretive smile—and stared down at his worn spectacles, fumbling with them. Then, lifting his eyes, he said:
“I possess many journals and papers from that remarkable man—the most curious being one detailing the visit to Karnstein. The legend distorts the truth slightly. While he may have been called a Moravian nobleman, he was actually born in Upper Styria. He had been deeply in love with Mircalla, Countess Karnstein, in youth. Her death shattered him.

“Vampires multiply according to a dark and fixed law. Begin with a land untouched by their curse. It begins when a person—usually one of wickedness—commits suicide. Under certain conditions, such a soul becomes a vampire. That spirit then haunts the living in dreams, causing their deaths. And those who die thus often rise again as vampires themselves. This happened to Mircalla. She was tormented by one of these demons.

“My ancestor, Vordenburg—whose title I now hold—discovered this early on. Through his studies, he learned much. He feared that suspicion would eventually fall upon the dead Countess, whom he had once adored. Though she might have become a monster, he could not bear the thought of her remains desecrated by a posthumous execution. He believed that expulsion from the vampire state sent the creature into an even more terrible existence. So he devised a plan: a false journey to remove her body, a real destruction of her monument.

“In later years, as he aged, he looked back with horror at what he had done. He made careful notes and sketches—guides that led me directly to her resting place. He also wrote a confession of his deception. Had he lived longer, he might have acted further—but death took him before he could. Now, through the hand of a distant descendant, justice has finally reached the beast.”

We spoke a little longer. Among other things, the Baron mentioned a telltale sign: the strength of the vampire’s hand. Mircalla’s delicate fingers had closed like iron vices around the General’s wrist as he raised his axe. But it was not just grip—it left a numbness in the limb, a lingering paralysis that might never fully fade.

The following spring, my father took me on a journey through Italy. We remained away over a year. Still, the terror did not leave me. To this day, the image of Carmilla returns—not always as the gentle, enchanting girl I once knew, but sometimes as the writhing, monstrous thing I saw in the ruined church. Often, from deep in thought, I start awake, convinced I hear her light footsteps at the drawing-room door.

And though I try to believe I am free… I am not.
