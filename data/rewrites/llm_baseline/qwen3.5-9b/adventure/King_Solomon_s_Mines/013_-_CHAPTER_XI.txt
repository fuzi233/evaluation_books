For nearly two hours, we sat in a heavy silence, paralyzed by the memory of the horrors we had witnessed. As twilight deepened toward dawn, a sound broke the stillness: the crunch of footsteps. A sentry challenged at the kraal gate, though his voice was lost in the distance; nonetheless, the approach continued. Moments later, Infadoos entered the hut, followed by a dozen stately chiefs.

"My lords," Infadoos announced, "I have returned as promised. Before you stands Ignosi, the rightful King of the Kukuanas, accompanied by these great men. Each commands three thousand soldiers loyal only to the crown. I have told them of my visions and your warnings. Now, let them see the sacred snake coiled upon your body and hear your tale, Ignosi, so they may decide whether to join you against Twala."

In response, Ignosi removed his girdle, revealing the intricate snake tattoo wrapping his torso. Under the dim lamplight, each chief stepped forward to inspect the mark, then silently moved to the next. When they had all viewed it, Ignosi resumed his *moocha* (a traditional drink) and recounted the morning's events once more.

"Now you have heard," Infadoos declared when Ignosi finished. "Will you stand by this man and aid him in reclaiming his father's throne, or will you not? The land screams against Twala; the blood of our people flows like spring water. You have seen tonight. Two other chiefs I intended to persuade lie dead, torn apart by hyenas. Soon you will share their fate unless you act. Choose, my brothers."

An elder, a short, thick-set warrior with white hair, stepped forward. "Your words are true, Infadoos; the land cries out. My own brother perished tonight. Yet this is a grave matter, difficult to accept blindly. How can we trust that raising our spears is not inviting a thief and a liar? Blood will flood the rivers before this ends. Many will remain loyal to Twala, for men worship the sun that shines above rather than one that has not yet risen. Furthermore, these 'white men from the Stars' possess great magic, shielding Ignosi. If he is truly the rightful king, let them give us a sign—a miracle visible to all. Only then will the people follow us, knowing the white man's power stands with us."

"The snake is the sign!" I interjected.

"My lord," the elder replied, "that is insufficient. The snake could have been placed there since childhood. Show us a sign, and it will suffice. But we will not move without proof."

The others nodded in agreement. Turning to Sir Henry and Good, I explained the deadlock.

"I think I have a solution," Good exclaimed triumphantly. "Ask them to wait a moment."

Once the chiefs had withdrawn, Good rushed to his medicine chest, retrieved a notebook containing an almanac, and opened it. "Tomorrow is the fourth of June," he noted. "According to this, a total lunar eclipse begins at 8:15 Greenwich Mean Time, visible in Tenerife and South Africa. That is our sign. We will darken the moon tonight."

The plan was brilliant, though fraught with peril. If Good's calculations were wrong, our credibility—and Ignosi's claim to the throne—would vanish forever.

"Are you certain?" Sir Henry asked skeptically.

"Eclipses adhere strictly to schedule," Good insisted, scribbling notes. "Based on our approximate location, it should start here around ten tonight and last until half-past twelve, bringing nearly total darkness for an hour and a half."

"We must risk it," Sir Henry conceded reluctantly.

Doubtful myself, knowing that clouds or date errors could ruin everything, I summoned Umbopa to recall the chiefs. They returned shortly, and I addressed them gravely.

"Greatest men of the Kukuanas, listen. We rarely display our powers, for doing so disrupts nature and breeds fear. However, outraged by the slaughter we witnessed and the treachery of *Isanusi* Gagool, who sought to murder Ignosi, we have resolved to break our rule. Tomorrow night, about two hours before midnight, we shall cause the moon to be devoured for an hour and a half. Deep darkness shall cover the earth as a sign that Ignosi is indeed your king. Will this satisfy you?"

"Yes, my lords," the elder chief smiled, "if you perform this feat, we shall be content."

"It shall be done," I proclaimed. "Incubu, Bougwan, and Macumazahn have spoken, and it shall happen. Hear this, Infadoos."

"I hear, my lord. It is wondrous that you promise to extinguish the moon, the mother of the world, at her fullest."

"Yet we shall do it."

"Very well. Today, two hours after sunset, Twala will summon you to witness the annual Dance of the Girls. An hour after the dance begins, the girl deemed fairest will be slain by Scragga, the king's son, as a sacrifice to the Silent Ones watching from the distant peaks. Then, let my lords darken the moon, saving the maiden's life, and the people will believe. Two miles from Loo, on a hill shaped like a crescent moon, my regiment and three others are stationed. This morning, we will coordinate moving additional troops there. If you truly can darken the moon, I will guide you safely out of Loo into the darkness, where we can wage war on Twala."

"That is excellent," I agreed. "Leave us now to rest and prepare our magic."

After the chiefs departed, Ignosi turned to us. "Can you truly do this, or were you merely flattering the captains?"

"We believe we can, Umbopa—Ignosi," I assured him.

"It is strange," he mused. "Had you not been English gentlemen, I would not have believed it. But I have learned that Englishmen do not lie. If we survive, you shall be repaid."

"Ignosi," Sir Henry interrupted, "promise me one thing."

"I will promise, Incubu, even before hearing it."

"This: if you ever become king, abolish the hunting of wizards and ensure no man dies without trial."

Ignosi pondered this. "The ways of black men differ from white men; we do not value life as you do. Yet, I promise. If I can stop them, the witch-finders shall cease, and no man shall die without judgment."

"A bargain," Sir Henry said. "Rest now."

Exhausted, we slept until Ignosi roused us at eleven. After washing and eating a hearty breakfast, we wandered outside, observing the Kukuana huts and the women's customs.

"I hope the eclipse succeeds," Sir Henry remarked.

"If it fails, we are doomed," I replied somberly. "Some of those chiefs will tell Twala the truth, leading to a far worse kind of eclipse—one we cannot control."

We spent the afternoon receiving ceremonial visitors. As dusk fell, a messenger arrived from Twala, inviting us to the Great Annual Dance of the Girls. Hastily donning the chain shirts the king had gifted us, we grabbed our rifles and ammunition, preparing to flee if necessary, and marched boldly toward the palace despite our inner tremors.

The courtyard before the king's kraal looked vastly different from the previous night. Instead of grim rows of warriors, hundreds of Kukuana girls filled the space. Though simply dressed, each wore a floral wreath, holding a palm frond and a white arum lily. In the center sat Twala, the one-eyed king, with Gagool at his feet, flanked by Infadoos, the boy Scragga, and twelve guards. Several of the chiefs from earlier were present.

Twala welcomed us with feigned cordiality, his single eye fixing viciously on Umbopa.

"Welcome, white men from the Stars," he boomed. "This scene differs from last night's moonlight view, but it is not so pleasant. Girls are nice, but without them, we wouldn't be here today. Women offer kisses and sweet words, but the clash of spears and the scent of blood are far sweeter! Do you wish wives from our people? Choose the fairest, and you shall have as many as you desire."

Good, an elderly sailor prone to romantic entanglements, seemed tempted. Recognizing the potential disaster, I quickly declined.

"Thank you, O King, but white men marry only white women. Your maidens are beautiful, but not for us!"

The king laughed. "Well said. Our proverb says, 'Women's eyes are always bright, regardless of color,' and 'Love the one present, for the absent is false.' Perhaps this holds true in the Stars. Since all men are white there, anything is possible. Welcome again! And welcome, black one. Had Gagool succeeded, you would be stiff and cold by now. Lucky for you that you also hail from the Stars! Ha! Ha!"

"I can kill you before you kill me, O King," Ignosi replied calmly. "You will be stiff before my limbs cease to bend."

Twala bristled. "You speak boldly, boy. Presume not too far."

"He speaks boldly because truth is a sharp spear that finds its mark. It is a message from the Stars, O King."

Twala scowled, his eye gleaming, but said nothing else.

"Begin the dance!" he commanded.
