A fortnight had passed when a gaunt, weary figure crossed the threshold of the Dominican convent on the outskirts of Gouda. His face was pallid, his shoulders bent—this was Gerard, the vicar of Gouda, come not to seek refuge, but to surrender himself to the quietude of the cloister, to live out his last days as he had begun them: in devotion.

He came as a novice, though in truth, he was a man already broken, his body failing, his spirit worn thin. The weight of the world had grown too heavy, and his own home—once a sanctuary—had become a prison of memories, each corner whispering of *her*. Here, at least, he might find peace, near Brother Ambrose, his steadfast friend, and among the brothers who had long welcomed him with open arms.

Ambrose, ever compassionate, saw the ruin grief had wrought upon him and harbored a quiet hope: that with time and care, Gerard might yet be restored—not just to health, but to his parish, to his calling. To that end, he placed him in a comfortable cell above the gateway, forbidding fasts or harsh penances. Let him rest. Let him heal.

But fate was unkind.

The new prior arrived soon after, a man of iron will and little patience. His first days were spent in rooting out laxity, in reforging discipline where it had slackened. And then he learned of Gerard’s presence—a vicar turned novice. His lip curled.

“A fat parson in my house?” he scoffed. “No. Let him renounce his vicarage or leave.”

Summoned before the prior, Gerard obeyed. When the two men faced one another, recognition struck them like a thunderclap.

The air between them crackled with the weight of the past, with old wounds and older grudges. And in that moment, Gerard knew—his refuge had vanished. The battle was not yet over.
