George Warrington did not let his legal studies eclipse the pleasures of life or endanger his health. In letters, Madam Esmond had reminded him that though he wore a student’s gown and dined among nameless scholars at hall-commons, he carried with him a name of ancient lineage—one that entitled him to rank among the foremost in his own country. She urged him to study as a gentleman, not as a mere drudge of the law. He followed her counsel faithfully. Thus, while others toiled in the ranks of legal apprenticeship, George stood apart—more like a volunteer in the service of justice than a conscript. Though less rigid in those days than she would later become, the law even then demanded devotion; it was a jealous mistress who asked for near-exclusive attention. Murray, they say, might have been an Ovid, but chose instead the ermine of Lord Chief Justice. Perhaps George Warrington could have risen to a peerage, seated on the woolsack, had he pursued the law with greater diligence, had he mastered courtly ways and won favor among attorneys. But such was not his nature. He honored Themis with respect, yes—but his heart belonged to letters. The black-letter verse of Chaucer stirred him far more than the Gothic pages of Hale and Coke.

In those times, letters were truly revered. Authors were authorities. Gentlemen cited Virgil or Lucan in Parliament or the courts. What said Statius? What thought Juvenal? Let alone Tully or Tacitus—what did they declare on this matter? Their influence has faded now. The good old pagan gods are no longer worshipped—no more than their poetry or ethics. The age of economists and accountants has taken over, and Tooke’s Pantheon stands deserted, absurd. Now and again, perhaps, a Stanley sacrifices a kid, a Gladstone waves a laurel wreath, a Lytton burns incense—honoring the Olympians. But what do they care at Lambeth, Birmingham, or Tower Hamlets for ancient rites or divinities? Who, indeed, are the Muses? And what use is there in all that Greek and Latin nonsense? What is Helicon? Who cares? Who was Thalia, and how long is her *i*? Is Melpomene pronounced in three or four syllables? And from whose design did I borrow that figure of Tragedy which graces the initial G of this chapter?

It has already been told how, in his youth, and during the long idle hours at home and in his imprisonment within the French fort on the banks of Monongahela, George Warrington passed his time courting Melpomene—the Muse of Tragedy. The result of their union was a play, one omitted from Bell’s Theatre, though surely no worse than many printed there. Most young men, when first drawn to literature, turn first to tragedy—just as they often fall in love with women older than themselves. Let the honest reader admit, if ever he felt a spark of literary ambition, that his dreams were once grand—so lofty that only epic or tragic works seemed worthy of his hand. As years passed, he may have lowered his expectations, thinking that translating a Horace ode or rendering a Waller song into decent alcaics was about the height of his talent. But in youth, nothing less than the highest prize would suffice.

Thus, upon arriving in London, George frequented the theatres at both Drury Lane and Covent Garden. He visited theatrical coffee-houses, listened to critics, and could often be seen between acts at the Bedford, or supping at the Cecil with actors and wits after performances. There, he gradually made friends with players and writers known to the public. The tough old Macklin, the lively Foote, the vivacious Hippisley, and the sprightly Mr. Garrick himself sometimes appeared at these gathering places. George, with his wit and modest charm—and perhaps also because of his well-known wealth—grew popular among them. Actors willingly shared a bowl of punch with him, and critics dined at his expense with great ease. To be close to an author or actor has always been a dream for many young men. To hobnob with Bobadil or Henry V, to accept a pinch from Aristarchus’s own box, to help Juliet into her coach or guide Monimia to her chair—these were privileges any poet-hearted youth would cherish. No wonder George loved the theatre. Moreover, he took secret delight in knowing that his mother disapproved of plays and playhouses, making them feel like forbidden fruit.

He gave several elegant dinners for the players, and it was whispered that one or two distinguished geniuses had even borrowed money from him.

As he refined his masterpiece, he sought advice from trusted friends, who offered praise and guidance. Mr. Spencer, his new acquaintance from the Temple, hosted a breakfast in his chambers at Fig Tree Court, where George read part of his play. The gentlemen present declared it possessed unusual merit. Even the learned Mr. Johnson, invited as guest, acknowledged the piece showed talent. “True,” he added, “it violates the unities—but others have done so before. You may break them as well as another.” He noted that George’s tragedy bore echoes of both Coriolanus and Othello. “And two very fine plays, sir!” George replied. “Well,” said Mr. Spencer, “there can be no doubt. Your catastrophe is terrible, just, and since it draws on truth, it is all the more awful.”

The plot of George Warrington’s tragedy was rich with battle and bloodshed. One of his grandfather’s favorite books had been the life of George Frundsberg of Mindelheim, a colonel in the Imperial army during the Battle of Pavia and the wars of Constable Bourbon. Among Frundsberg’s comrades was a certain Carpzow—or Carpezan—as George named his hero. The first act, as preserved in Sir George’s manuscript, unfolds before a convent on the Rhine, besieged by Lutherans under Carpezan’s command. These Lutherans were godless: they’d torn the beards of Roman friars, stripped veils from religious women. Inside the walls, a score of nuns tremble, awaiting surrender unless reinforcements arrive by midday. For now, there is armistice. The sentries watch hungrily as soldiers and camp followers gamble outside the gate.

Twelve o’clock strikes—ding, ding, dong!—on the convent bell. No relief has come. Open the gates, warder! Admit the famed Protestant hero—the terror of Turks on the Danube, of Papists in Lombardy—Colonel Carpezan! Here he comes, clad in full armor, his war hammer slung over his shoulder—once used to shatter infidel fortifications. His banners fly, his trumpets blare.

“Hold your tongues, my men,” says Carpezan. “The wine is yours, and the convent’s larder and cellar are fair game. The church plate shall be melted. Any soldier who wishes to join Gaspar Carpezan will be paid well. No harm to the ladies! I have promised them safe passage. Anyone who touches them hangs—mind that, Provost Marshal!”

The Provost Marshal—a massive man in a red doublet—nods.

“We’ll see more of that Provost Marshal,” Mr. Spencer remarks to the guests.

“A most agreeable companion, I’m sure,” says Mr. Johnson, wagging his head over his tea. “This scene—soldiers, camp followers, their wild games—is fresh and exciting, Mr. Warrington. My compliments. The Colonel has entered the convent, I believe? Let us hear what he intends.”

The Abbess and a few elder nuns appear before the conqueror. Though defeated, they heard him in their sacred halls. They know of his past violence in religious houses. That hammer—he carries it always—has shattered countless holy images. They’ve heard of his melting of convent silver—the sacrilegious plunderer! No wonder the Abbess-Princess of St. Mary’s, noble-born, outspoken, and deeply prejudiced, resents the lowborn heretic now ruling her house. She speaks plainly to Carpezan, scolding him with boldness. This scene—where the woman gains the upper hand—was particularly admired by the audience at the Temple. Though terrifying in war, Carpezan stumbles at first under the Abbess’s sharp words. Conqueror though he is, he seems momentarily conquered by his prisoner.

But such a hardened soldier could not be bested forever by any woman. “Madam,” he says calmly, “how many sisters remain here? I will ensure transport for all.”

The Abbess, troubled and angry, replies, “Besides myself, twenty-three—twenty-three noble sisters of Saint Mary’s House.” She hesitates—had she nearly said twenty-four?

“Ah,” asks Captain Ulric, one of Carpezan’s liveliest officers, “why the hesitation?”

Carpezan pulls a letter from his coat. “I demand,” he says sternly, “the body of Lady Sybilla of Hoya. Her brother was my favorite captain, slain beside me in Milan. With his death, she inherits his lands. It is rumored her greedy uncle brought her here and imprisoned her against her will. The lady shall decide—remain cloistered, or return to freedom as Baroness of ———.”

The Abbess is shaken. “There is no Lady Sybil here,” she retorts. “Every soul in this house is under your protection and sworn to go free. Sister Agnes was a professed nun. Her lands and wealth belong to the Order.”

“Give me the body of Lady Sybilla of Hoya!” roars Carpezan. “Or I give the signal to my Reiters—and this convent goes to war!”

“Faith,” murmurs Captain Ulric, “if I led the charge, I wouldn’t choose your Lady Abbess—but rather some plump, smiling, red-lipped maid like—like—” He glances beneath the veils of two attendant nuns.

“Silence!” cries the Abbess, furious. “The lady you seek has passed beyond sin, temptation, vanity. Three days ago, our Sister Agnes—died.”

At this, Carpezan is thrown into turmoil. The Abbess calls upon the chaplain to confirm. Pale and trembling, he admits that Sister Agnes was buried three days prior.

Enough! In Carpezan’s mail coat lies a letter from Sister Agnes herself—confessing she would be entombed alive in a dungeon, kept on bread and water, or left to starve. He seizes the Abbess by the arm, while Ulric grabs the chaplain by the throat. The Colonel blows his horn. His Lanzknechts rush in—crash, bang! They batter the walls. Flames rise, screams echo, slaughter ensues. And then—Carpezan enters, carrying a fainting woman on his shoulder. It is Sybilla herself. A young nun (that same red-lipped girl) had pointed out the way to Sister Agnes’s dungeon—and revealed her fate to the Lutheran leader.

“The convent is crushed,” says George Warrington. “We end Act I with its burning, the soldiers’ shouts, the nuns’ shrieks. They’d best change their robes soon—they’ll be court ladies in Act II, as you’ll see.” The gentlemen discuss casting. Mrs. Pritchard might not relish playing the Abbess—she appears only in Act I. Miss Pritchard could play Sybilla, Miss Gates the nun. Mr. Garrick, though not tall enough, might still dominate as Carpezan—especially when roused, when no one sees him as anything less than a giant. Mr. Johnson agrees Woodward would make a fine Ulric, given his flair for Mercutio roles. Gradually, the audience imagines the play staged, the parts assigned.

In Act II, Carpezan has married Sybilla. Wealthy, ennobled by the Emperor, he lives in splendor at his castle on the Danube.

Yet despite riches and title, Lord Carpezan is not happy. Perhaps his wild life—leading mercenaries for both sides—has left him haunted by guilt. Or perhaps his rough soldier ways clashed too sharply with his highborn bride. She tormented him—mocking his humble origins, his vulgar companions whom he loved around him. At times, he wished he’d never rescued this lovely, quarrelsome vixen from the dungeon where he pulled her out. After the chaos of Act I, this act is quieter—centered on the bickering between Baron and Baroness Carpezan—until horns sound: the young King of Bohemia and Hungary approaches, hunting nearby.

Act III takes place in Prague, where the King invites Carpezan and his wife, offering nobility to her—Baron to Count, Colonel to General-in-Chief. Sybilla becomes the most dazzling lady at court. And as for Carpzoff—

“Oh, wait—I know your tale now,” interrupts Mr. Johnson. “It’s in ‘Meteranus,’ in the Theatrum Universum. I read it at Oxford—Carpezanus, or Carpzoff—”

“That’s Act IV,” says George. In Act IV, the King’s attentions toward Sybilla grow increasingly evident. Carpezan fights jealousy, refusing to believe until proof overwhelms him—until Sybilla’s guilt is undeniable. Then, convinced, he summons the executioner who follows his regiment and orders her killed in her own palace. The curtain falls just after the deed—inside a chamber lit by moonlight through a great oriel window, beneath which the King arrives with his lute, singing the tune meant to signal their tryst.

This song—written in the old style and repeated earlier in Act III at a royal festival—Mr. Johnson praises as a clever imitation of Waller’s manner. Its cheerful repetition amid murder and horror deepens the tragedy.

“But what happens next?” he asks. “In the Theatrum, Carpezan is later welcomed back by Count Mansfield—perhaps he kills others on the reformed side?”

Here, George departs from history. In Act V, King Louis of Hungary and Bohemia—deeply shaken by the bloody end of his affair—learns that Sultan Solyman invades his Hungarian lands. Two nobles enter, reporting that at the King’s council, Carpezan stormed in, broke his sword, flung it at the King’s feet—along with a glove, daring him to wear it, swearing he’d claim it one day. Then, enraged, he fled Prague. None have heard from him since—except rumors that he joined the Turkish invaders, donned a turban, and now serves in the Sultan’s camp, whose white tents gleam across the river. The King prepares to march against him.

Now the King enters his tent with generals, issuing battle orders. He dismisses them, keeping only an aged knight—his master of the horse—to whom he confesses regret for past sins, admiration for his wronged queen, and resolve to face battle like a man.

“What is this field called?”

“Mohacz, my liege,” answers the old warrior. “Before sunset, Mohacz will witness a battle bravely won.”

Trumpets sound—alarms ring. These are the cymbals and barbaric music of the Janissaries. We are in the Turkish camp. Yonder, surrounded by turbaned chiefs, walks the Sultan’s friend—the conqueror of Rhodes, the dreaded Grand Vizier.

Who is that warrior in Eastern dress, yet with a glove tucked in his cap? It is Carpezan. Even Solyman knows his courage and ferocity. He understands the Hungarian army’s weaknesses—how their cavalry, powerful as it is, should be lured into a marsh where death awaits. He begs for command in the front, near where King Louis will fight. “Well,” says the grim Vizier, “our invincible Emperor surveys the battle from yonder tower. At day’s end, he will reward your valor.”

Signal-guns fire. Trumpets blow. Turkish captains retreat, swearing death to the infidel, eternal loyalty to the Sultan.

Battle begins. Christian knights clash with Turkish warriors. Alarms continue. Troops advance and retreat. Carpezan, glove in cap, hammers all before him, roaring for King Louis. About to strike down a warrior, he recognizes young Ulric—his former captain. He lowers his hammer. “Fly, Ulric. Think of Carpezan.” Moved by seeing his old friend, he remembers their shared victories for Protestantism. Ulric urges him to return—but that is impossible. They fight. Ulric falls beneath the hammer. The renegade weeps at his wounded comrade—then sees King Louis, plumes torn, sword hacked, shield battered by a thousand blows.

Ha! Who is this? The guilty king tries to flee—but Carpezan is upon him. All softness vanishes. He rages like a fury. “A fair fight!” he roars. “Traitor against traitor! Stand, King Louis! False king, false knight, false friend—by this glove in my helmet, I challenge you!” He tears it from his cap and hurls it at the monarch.

They duel. The king falls under Carpezan’s mighty mace. He dies, muttering words of repentance. Carpezan leans on his weapon, delivering a heartbroken soliloquy over the corpse. Turkish warriors gather. The day belongs to them. The dark Vizier stands, surrounded by Janissaries—bows and swords weary from drinking death. He watches the renegade over the king.

“Christian renegade,” he says, “Allah has granted us victory. The Sublime Emperor’s arms triumph everywhere. The Christian king fell by your hand.”

“Peace to his soul,” gasps Ulric, dying on the field. “He died like a good knight.”

“In this battle,” continues the Vizier, “no man fought more bravely than you. You are made Bassa of Transylvania. Archers—fire!”

An arrow pierces Carpezan’s chest.

“Bassa of Transylvania,” the Vizier declares, “you were a traitor to your king—now slain by your hand. Yet you contributed more than any soldier to our victory. Thus, my sublime Emperor rewards you. Sound trumpets—we march for Vienna tonight!”

The curtain falls as Carpezan crawls toward his dying comrade, kisses his hands, and whispers:

“Forgive me, Ulric.”

When George finishes reading, he turns modestly to Mr. Johnson and asks, “What say you, sir? Is there any chance for me?”

But the critic cannot reply—for Mr. Johnson has been asleep for some time. He admits frankly he missed the final scenes.

The small audience hums and stirs as the reading ends. George may have been nervous at first, but all agree he read the last two acts superbly. Compliments are exchanged on both content and delivery. Perhaps everyone is in good spirits simply because the play is over. Mr. Spencer’s servant brings refreshments. The Templars sip negus and voice their opinions.

Mr. Fountain suggests the Vizier should not say “Fire!” when ordering archers—since bows don’t “fire.” A note is made.

Mr. Figtree, sentimental, regrets Ulric couldn’t survive and marry the comic heroine.

“Nay,” says Mr. Johnson, “there was a total annihilation at Mohacz. Ulric must die with the rest. He could only live by fleeing—and we wouldn’t want a hero run away. Sentence him to death, but let him die with honor.”

Messrs. Essex and Tanfield exchange glances at the odd, impertinent guest Spencer has invited—someone who contradicts everyone. They suggest a boat ride up the river for some fresh air after the drama.
