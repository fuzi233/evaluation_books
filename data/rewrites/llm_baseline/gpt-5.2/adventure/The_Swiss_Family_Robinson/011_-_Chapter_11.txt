“Now for the finishing up of this dirty job,” I cried at daybreak, forcing cheer into my voice as we rose and set ourselves again to the whale.

We built a rough stand of poles and crosspieces, a sort of scaffold where we could heap the tubs of blubber high. Then, with all the weight we could contrive—stones, levers, and our own hands—we pressed it hard, and the first pure oil came obediently, slipping over the lip in slow, glossy runnels into vessels set beneath. It was the only pleasing part of the business: that clean, pale flow, like clarified butter when one has skimmed it carefully and kept the fire low.

Afterward there was nothing but stench and labour. We carried what remained to the cauldron we had placed well away from our home, for the very air around it seemed to turn greasy. The fire had to be steady, not raging—too much heat scorches and spoils, too little leaves the fat cloudy—so we fed it patiently, and as the blubber loosened and melted we skimmed, strained through coarse cloth, and watched the oil settle clearer with each careful pass. The vapour that rose was thick and sickening, an oily breath that clung to hair and clothes and tongue; even the sea-wind could not wholly drive it off. The shore birds kept their distance, and the usual friendly chorus of our little world—goats bleating, hens clucking, the soft busy chitter of the boys—seemed muted beneath that oppressive smell, as though the island itself were holding its breath.

My poor wife bore it with her usual quiet courage, though I saw how it tried her. When at last we had filled casks and even bags stitched from intestines—tight, tough vessels, unpleasant to handle but useful beyond price—we stowed them in what the children called our “cellar,” our roughest store-room. Only then did she speak her mind plainly.

“This new island ought to be the headquarters for another little colony,” said she, wiping her hands and looking toward the water. “Any animals left there would be safe from apes and other plunderers—and you would find it so convenient to boil whale-blubber and strain train oil and the like, without poisoning the air of our home.”

The boys welcomed the scheme at once, as they welcomed every plan that promised new ground and new doings. Yet when I reminded them of the carcass still lying there—rank, enormous, a thing that would turn the breeze foul for days—they agreed it was wiser to let wind and storms, birds and insects cleanse the place, to strip it down to a bare, harmless skeleton before we made it our outpost.

All that day, even while the smell still seemed lodged in my nostrils, my thoughts kept returning to an idea that had seized me: a rowing-machine, something to spare our arms and lengthen our range. I set to work with an iron bar laid across the middle of the boat, projecting a foot each way. In the centre I fitted ribbed machinery; at each end I contrived a kind of nave, like that of a cart-wheel, and fixed into it four flat paddles set obliquely, meant to bite the water as oars do.

Then I arranged the jack so that its strong cogwheel would catch firmly upon those ribs. When wound, it drove the cross-bar round with speed, and with it the paddles at both ends, beating the sea in quick, regular strokes. The contrivance was rough, and in many ways begged improvement, but when Fritz and I wound it and pushed off for a trial, the boat splashed forward at such a rate that the very shores rang with the family’s cheering and clapping. Their delight rolled across the bay and came back to us on the clean morning air—sound as nourishing as any meal, a reminder that even here our well-being depended not only on food and shelter, but on the living music of those we loved.

Everyone clamoured to go at once, yet the day was already declining, and I would not risk a late return. I promised instead that on the morrow we should cruise to Cape Disappointment and the little settlement at Prospect Hill.

That evening became a small household festival of preparation: clothes laid out, arms examined, food packed. My wife’s hands moved with the calm precision of one who can make a table out of a rock ledge and a feast out of scant stores, yet she never let our meals become mere fuel. She wrapped what was needed, chose what would travel, and spoke of flavours as if naming them preserved something of home that no shipwreck could take. We went early to rest.

At dawn we left the house in good order, meaning to be away all day. Besides our usual stores we took spades and mattocks, for I wished to bring back young cocoanut trees and shrubs; and on our return, to land on Whale Island and begin planting there.

We steered toward the opposite side of the bay. The sea lay smooth as poured oil—cleaner, blessedly, than any we had rendered—and the rowing-machine did its duty without complaint. Safety Bay and Shark Island slipped behind us; the coast spread out in a wide, bright panorama, every headland and belt of trees set in clear light, and the surf making its own constant low hymn along the outer rocks.

Near Prospect Hill we moored the boat and walked through the woods toward our little farm, gathering fresh cocoanuts and selecting young plants as we went. The grove had its own sound: leaves rubbing softly, insects ticking in the undergrowth, a bird’s call falling into the pauses like a drop into a bowl. Then, before we saw the cottage, we heard the cocks crow.

That sharp homely cry went through me with a force I had not expected. In an instant it carried me—painfully, vividly—into the memory of other mornings, of rides and walks in our former life, when such familiar sounds would greet us near some friendly house. There, that crowing would have meant people, warmth, talk, bread in an oven, a pot simmering. Here it rose from unconscious animals into a solitude so complete that the sound itself seemed like a ghost of language: recognisable, yet unable to translate the whole of what it once contained. We, who would have been welcomed with joy in so many homes, came upon our own cottage unnoticed by anything but beasts.

So long had we been absent that our arrival threw the place into panic. The older animals had forgotten us; their young—the lambs, kids, and chickens who had never seen a human face—took us for an invading troop.

The boys could not milk the goats at first. The creatures sprang away, wild-eyed, stamping and tossing. At last, using the lasso, they captured them one by one, bound their legs, and then—with a touch as old as shepherding—offered them salt to lick. The change was immediate; the goats, busy with the savour, stood quiet enough. Soon we had a supply of excellent milk, warm and fragrant, which we poured from the cocoanut shells we used into calabash flasks, to carry away what we would not drink at dinner.

The fowls were coaxed more gently, with handfuls of grain and rice. My wife, patient and quick, caught as many as she wished, her hands firm but not cruel. She moved among them like someone who understood that food is not merely taken but negotiated, even in the simplest farmyard economy.

We were ready for dinner. The cold provisions were set out, and the chief dish—prepared at the boys’ insistence with great ceremony—was a piece of the whale’s tongue, cooked specially for this entertainment, as if the very boldness of the meal could turn our recent filthy labour into triumph.

But when it was tasted, triumph collapsed into misery. One after another, faces fell; each pronounced it “horrid stuff.” They begged for pickled herring to chase away the taste of train-oil that clung to it, and at last, with a unanimity that would have been comic if it had not been so sincere, they bestowed the cherished dainty upon Fangs. The dog accepted it without scruple, proving again that there are palates which survive any history.

Fortunately we had enough of other eatables; and the fresh cocoanuts—cool, sweet, faintly nutty, with that clean scent of split husk—and the goat’s milk restored everyone’s humour. My wife, who could not bear waste, carefully packed up what remained, while Fritz and I cut sugar-cane shoots I wished to plant. Then we returned to the shore and embarked again.

Before turning back for Whale Island, I felt a strong wish to round Cape Disappointment and survey the coast beyond. Yet the promontory earned its name: a long sandbank, hidden reefs, and treacherous rocks stretched far out. Fritz saw breakers ahead; we put about at once, and with the help of a light breeze made for Whale Island instead.

On landing, I began immediately to plant the sugar-cane shoots. The work was plain and satisfying—soil loosened, shoots laid at the right depth and angle, earth pressed firm, water carried where it would do the most good. For a little while the boys helped, then, as boys will, grew weary of steady labour and wandered off to hunt shells and coral. Their mother stayed with me, and together we finished the row.

Soon Jack came running back, shouting as if he had discovered an age of the world.

“Father! Mother! Do come and look. There is an enormous skeleton lying here—the skeleton of some fearful great beast—a mammoth, I should think!”

“Why, Jack!” I answered, laughing. “Have you forgotten our old acquaintance, the whale? What else could it be?”

“Oh no, father, it is not the whale. This thing has not fish bones, but real good, honest, huge, beast bones. I don’t know what can have become of the whale—floated out to sea most likely. This mammoth is ever so much bigger. Come and see!”

I was about to follow him when, from another direction, a cry broke out sharper and more urgent.

“Father! Father! A great enormous turtle! Please make haste. It is waddling back to the sea as hard as it can go, and we can’t stop it!”

That mattered more than Jack’s marvel. Snatching up an oar, I ran.

Sure enough, a huge turtle was scrambling toward the water and was within a few paces of it, though Ernest—brave beyond his strength—clung to one hind leg and was dragged along in the sand. I sprang down the bank; using the oar as a lever, we managed with difficulty to turn the creature on its back. It was an immense specimen, fully eight feet long; once overturned it was helpless, legs beating the air like slow paddles.

We left it sprawling and went to inspect Jack’s “mammoth,” which proved, of course, to be nothing but the whale’s skeleton. I convinced him by pointing out our own footmarks still impressed in places and the broken jaws where we had hacked out the whalebone.

“What made you take up that fancy about a mammoth, my boy?”

“Ernest put it into my head, father. He said there seemed to be the skeleton of an antediluvian monster there, so I ran to look closer, and I never thought of the whale when I saw no fish bones. I suppose Ernest was joking.”

“Whales are often called fishes by those little acquainted with the animal kingdom,” said I, “but they belong to the class of mammals—man, monkeys, bats, dogs and cats, all hoofed animals, whales and their allies; the last on the list being the sloth. Their name comes from the Latin ‘mama,’ a breast, because all in this class nourish their young with milk. The whale’s bones differ chiefly in being hollow and filled with air, making the carcass more buoyant; birds’ bones are hollow for a similar reason.”

Fritz stared at the immense pale framework half buried in sand.

“What a marvellous structure it is, father! What a ponderous mass of bones! Can we not make use of any of them?”

“Nothing strikes me at this moment,” I said. “We will let them bleach awhile. Perhaps later, sawn up, they may serve for chairs, or a reading-desk for the museum. But now we must return home. Bring the boat round to where the turtle awaits his fate; we must settle how to deal with him.”

It was soon decided that he must swim—for our convenience as much as for his. I fastened an empty water-cask to a long line; one end I made fast to the bow of the boat, the other I passed carefully around the turtle’s neck and fore-paws. Then we lifted him enough to let him regain his feet. The instant he could, he made for the sea, plunged in, and the cask floated after him, preventing him from sinking beneath the burden he now towed.

We were all aboard in a moment. The worthy fellow, after trying vainly to dive, set himself doggedly to swim straight forward, towing us comfortably. I kept ready to cut the line at the least sign of danger and guided him by striking the water with a boat-hook right or left when he was disposed to turn too far one way. The boys delighted in it, shouting comparisons of me to Neptune in his car, drawn by dolphins and attended by Amphitrite and Tritons. Their laughter skittered over the water, and even the turtle’s steady splashing became part of a strange little procession-song, as if myth had decided to make itself useful for once and haul a boat.

We landed safely near Rockburg. The turtle was condemned and executed soon afterward. The shell—eight feet long and three broad—once cleaned and prepared, was destined to become a trough for our water supply at the cave. The meat we salted carefully and stored for many a savoury meal. My wife oversaw the salting with the gravity of a keeper of records: thickness of cut, proportion of salt, the order of layering—these were not small details to her but a kind of domestic history, a way of making the future taste dependable.

It had been my intention to bring a piece of land under cultivation before the next rainy season and sow it with different sorts of grain. But unforeseen circumstances had delayed us, and our animals, unaccustomed to the yoke, could not yet serve for the plough. I gave up the idea for the present and applied myself, with Ernest’s help, to completing the loom. Though clumsy in workmanship, it became fit for use. In my youth I had spent many hours in the workshops of weavers and other artisans; that half-forgotten knowledge, once a mere curiosity, now returned like a saved language—imperfect, yet priceless.

We needed paste or size to smear over the threads, but we could not spare flour. I used isinglass instead, which kept the warp moist perfectly well and spared us the necessity of setting the loom in some damp uncomfortable place merely to prevent overdrying. From the same isinglass I made thin plates to serve as window-panes. They were at least as transparent as horn, and when fixed deep in the rock and beyond the reach of rain they admitted light faithfully, a quiet improvement that changed the very tone of our days inside the cave.

Encouraged, I took up harness-making next. Our hunts had provided plenty of leather. I covered light wooden frames with it, stuffing them with hairy moss or lichen, and before long our animals were equipped with saddles, stirrups, bridles, yokes, and collars, to the great satisfaction of their youthful riders and drivers.

This was followed by busy days with the annual return of the herring shoals, and after them, as before, shoals of other fish and many seals. We had learned too much of want to let such wealth pass unused. We captured large numbers and salted, dried, and stored, turning the sea’s brief generosity into lasting security. The shore, for a time, was full of sound: the slap of fish, the cries of birds wheeling overhead, the wet shuffle of seals, the boys calling to one another, knives tapping boards. It was noisy work, but it was living noise—sound that meant food, warmth, continuance—not the harsh, pointless clatter of machines made only to drown thought.

When the boys began to beg for another shooting expedition, I asked for patience. Before we went, we needed baskets; our sacks were failing, and there was constant demand for strong, airy containers to carry and keep our roots and fruits. We tried our hands at basket-making. The first attempts were clumsy, the weave uneven, the rims weak, but perseverance brought improvement. We produced a good supply of all sorts and sizes. One very large basket I furnished with openings so a strong stick could pass through; thus, when laden, it could be carried by two persons without tearing the sides.

The moment the children grasped the idea, they fetched a bamboo pole, set it through the openings, popped little Franz into the basket, and carried him about in triumph. His laughter, rising and falling with the swing, made my wife shake her head and smile despite herself.

That sport suggested a new notion to Fritz.

“Oh, father,” he cried, “don’t you think we might make something like this for mother, and carry her much more comfortably than jolting along in the cart?”

The boys shouted with glee. Their mother admitted the plan might be feasible, but confessed she did not much like the thought of sitting in the middle of a basket and only peeping over the rim now and then.

I promised it should be a well-shaped, comfortable sedan-chair—or litter. Then came the question of how it should be carried, since the boys could not play at palanquin-bearers without danger to their mother and misery to themselves.

“The bull and the buffalo!” cried Jack. “Why not use them for it? Let’s go and try them now!”

Off they ran. Soon the great basket was securely hung between Storm and Grumble. Fritz and Jack sprang into their saddles; Ernest, with much caution, deposited himself into the “cradle,” as Franz called it. They set forth at a sober pace. The animals were perfectly docile, only a little surprised at their new burden.

“Oh, it is so pleasant, mother—it is a delightful motion,” Ernest called as they passed us. “It swings and rocks really soothingly. Quicker, Fritz! Go quicker!”

The trot pleased him too, and the pace quickened. Before long Storm and Grumble were moving fast enough to shake and jolt the basket fearfully. Ernest screamed for them to stop, but his brothers thought it excellent sport to “shake up” the professor. They made a circuit of the level ground near Rockburg and finally pulled up before us like performers stopping for applause.

It was impossible not to laugh; the sight was too ridiculous. But Ernest was angry, his reproaches sharpened, his brothers answered with high words, and a quarrel began to rise like a sudden gust. I stepped in at once and showed them how easily a joke carried too far becomes bad feeling, and how dangerous any breach of good fellowship and brotherly love would be to us all—our true strength in this strange land.

Good humour returned quickly. Ernest himself helped to unharness the beasts and rewarded them with handfuls of salt and barley, adding—half dignified, half forgiving—that they must have more palanquin practice another day.

Later I sat with my wife and Fritz under the verandah’s shade, our hands busy with wicker-work, our talk easy. The reeds by Jackal River made their soft continual whisper, the kind of sound that settles the mind. Suddenly Fritz stood up, peering down the avenue that led from the river.

“I see something so strange in the distance, father! What in the world can it be? First it seems drawn in coils on the ground like a cable, then it rises as it were a little mast, then that sinks, and the coils move along again. It is coming towards the bridge.”

My wife turned pale at once. Calling the other boys, she retreated into the cave. I told them to close the entrances and keep watch with firearms at the upper windows—openings we had made in the rock, reached inside by steps and a gallery that ran along the front of our rooms.

Fritz stayed by me while I raised my spy-glass.

“It is, as I feared, an enormous serpent!” I cried. “It advances directly this way, and we shall be in the greatest possible danger, for it will cross the bridge to a certainty.”

“May we not attack it, father?” Fritz asked, brave and eager.

“Only with the greatest caution,” I said. “It is too formidable and too tenacious of life for rash attempts. Thank God we are at Rockburg; we can keep safely in retreat while we watch for a chance to destroy it. Go to your mother and help prepare the firearms. I will join you directly, but I must observe its movements.”

Reluctantly he went. Alone, I watched the monster advance—gigantic, already too near the bridge for us to remove it in time. I remembered with dread how easily such a creature might force its way through walls. It moved with slow, writhing undulations, and from time to time reared its head fifteen or twenty feet, turning it gradually as if listening for life. Even its motion seemed to have a sound in it: a faint sliding whisper through grass and earth, a dry rubbing that made the skin tighten on one’s arms.

When it crossed the bridge with a suspicious slowness, I withdrew and joined my family, who were ready at the upper openings—our little fortress garrisoned, though their faces showed fear. My presence steadied them somewhat.

We waited, unseen, hearts beating. The serpent soon became visible below. It seemed puzzled by the signs of habitation, turning and coiling in the space before the cave, repeatedly lifting its head. Then, as if the sight were too much to bear, the boys fired one after another, and even their mother discharged her gun. The shots did little beyond startling it; its movements quickened. Fritz and I fired too, with steadier aim, yet without effect. With a gliding rush it entered the reedy marsh to the left and disappeared.

A weight lifted from us all at once. We spoke eagerly of its awful length and magnificent terror. I recognized it as a boa constrictor—an immense specimen, upwards of thirty feet.

I explained that in South America it is called Boaguacu; the first syllable, with the Latin addition indicating its manner of killing by pressure—constriction—gives the name by which it is commonly known.

But knowledge did not bring comfort. The nearness of such a reptile filled me with anxiety. I forbade anyone to leave the house on any pretence without my express permission.

For three whole days we lived in suspense, not daring to go more than a few hundred steps from the door. The enemy showed no sign; yet the blockade felt real, as if our lives had shrunk to the cave’s dim chambers and the narrow strip of safe ground outside. Even our soundscape changed: fewer wandering footfalls, fewer calls across open air; the animals’ restlessness sounded sharper in confinement, hooves clicking, wings fluttering in tight spaces.

We might have persuaded ourselves the boa had crossed the swamp and found some cleft through the cliffs beyond, if not for the behaviour of our geese and ducks. They were accustomed to rest at night among the reeds, and now they swam anxiously, clapping wings and cackling in disturbed bursts. At last, in a panic of instinct, they took wing, crossed the harbour, and settled on Shark Island. Their fear was a message written in sound: the serpent still lurked there.

My perplexity deepened. To attack such a monster in dense reeds amid dangerous swamps with insufficient force would be madness; yet to live shut up, cut off from our work, enduring the cave’s unnatural light, our minds strained by constant alarm—this was scarcely less dreadful.

We were delivered at last, and by none other than our good old donkey Grizzle—not by any virtue, but by sheer stupidity.

Our stores were low; hay for the animals failed on the evening of the third day. I resolved to send them, under Fritz’s guidance, across the river at the ford where they could feed in safety. Fritz was to ride Lightfoot, and the animals were to be fastened together until all were over.

Next morning we began to tie them in a line. While we worked, my wife opened the door. Grizzle, fresh and frolicsome after long rest and regular feeding, suddenly broke away, cut a few absurd capers, and bolted out at full gallop straight for the marsh.

We shouted after him. Fritz would have dashed in pursuit, but I held him back. In another moment the ass was near the reeds—and then, with a cold shudder, we saw the snake rise from its lair. Its fiery eyes swept; its dark jaws opened wide; the forked tongue darted out greedily. Grizzle’s foolish gaiety died in a single beat of horror.

Aware of danger at last, he stopped short, braced all four legs, and let out the most piteous, discordant bray that ever wrung echo from rocks—an ugly sound, yet heartbreakingly alive, like a complaint that could not be translated into anything but pain.

Swift as a fencer’s thrust, the destroyer was upon him. It wound round him—entangled, enfolded, compressed—cunningly avoiding the convulsive kicks of the agonized animal.

A cry of horror rose from my family.

“Shoot him, father! Oh, shoot him—do save poor Grizzle!”

“My children, it is impossible!” I cried. “Our old friend is lost to us for ever. I have hope, however, that when the snake is gorged with his prey, we may be able to attack him with some chance of success.”

“But the horrible wretch is never going to swallow him all at once, father?” Jack cried. “That will be too shocking!”

“Snakes have no grinders, but only fangs,” said I, forcing myself to speak steadily. “Therefore they cannot chew and must swallow their food whole. The idea is startling, but not more truly horrible than the tearing and bloodshed when lions or tigers seize their prey.”

“But,” said little Franz, clinging to his mother, “how can the snake separate the flesh from the bones without teeth? And is this kind of snake poisonous?”

“No, dear child,” I answered. “Not poisonous—only fearfully strong and ferocious. And it has no need to separate flesh from bones. It swallows them—skin, hair, and all—and digests everything in its stomach.”

“It seems utterly impossible,” Fritz exclaimed, staring as if reason itself had been offended, “that the broad ribs, the strong legs, hoofs and all, should go down that throat.”
