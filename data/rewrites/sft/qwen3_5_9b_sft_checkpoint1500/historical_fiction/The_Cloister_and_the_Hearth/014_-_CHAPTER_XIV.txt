The back door gave way with a violent crash, the sound echoing through the quiet house like a gunshot. Immediately, heavy blows battered against the front entry, sealing them inside. Yet, amidst the chaos, Margaret found a fragment of composure. With a whispered command—"Tell them he was here, but he is gone"—she seized Gerard and nearly dragged him up the rough stone steps leading to her father's chamber. Her own room lay just beyond.

"Who disturbs this hour?" her father's voice called out from within, thick with sleep.

"Open, or you shall answer for it!" the intruders shouted.

"I open not to thieves! Honest folk are all asleep!"

"Open to the law, Martin Wittenhaagen, or you will regret it!"

"That is Dirk Brower's voice," Martin muttered, recognizing the tone. "What brings you so far from Tergou?"

"Open, and you will know!"

Slowly, deliberately, Martin drew the bolt. In rushed Dierich and four burly companions, letting in their leader who had slipped through the rear entrance.

"Now, Martin, where is Gerard Eliassoen?" Dierich demanded, his face grim.

"Gerrard Eliassoen?" Martin feigned surprise. "Why, he was here but moments ago!"

"Here?" Dierich's expression darkened. "And where is he now?"

"The rumor is he fled to Italy," Martin lied smoothly. "But why the alarm?"

"It matters not. When did he leave? Do not tell me he departed in such a storm!"

A sneer curled Martin's lips. "There is quite a fuss over Gerard Eliassoen." He lit a candle and sat calmly by the fire, taking a piece of fine silk and whittling it around his bowstring where the arrow notch fretted it. "Let me inform you," he said casually. "Do you know his brother, Giles? A little malformed imp, all head and limbs? Well, he tore over here on a mule, screaming something I could not hear—only the noise. Whatever he said, he frightened Gerard. Before Giles vanished, there was much weeping and kissing, and then Gerard left. They say he has gone to Italy—you may know where that is, though I do not."

Dierich's countenance sank deeper with every word. Martin's lie was flawless; a less cunning man might have spun too many threads, raising suspicion, but Martin adhered strictly to his orders, inventing nothing of his own.

"Folks," Dierich murmured, "I fear he tells the truth. I warned the burgomaster how this would play out. He spotted the dwarf galloping Peter Buyskens's mule from Sevenbergen. 'They sent that imp to Gerard,' he said, 'so Gerard must be at Sevenbergen.' 'Ah, master!' I replied, 'too late now. We wasted time hunting corners of Tergou for parchments we shall never find without the man who holds them. If he was at Sevenbergen and they sent the dwarf, it was to warn him. He is leagues away.'" He spat on the floor. "Confuse that chalk-faced girl! She has outwitted us, bearded men that we are. So I told the burgomaster, but he would not listen. A wet jerkin apiece, that is all we gain from this job."

Martin returned a cool grin to Dierich.

"However," Dierich added, "to satisfy the magistrate, we shall search the house."

Martin's demeanor shifted instantly to gravity. Dierich noticed the change immediately. After a brief reflection, he ordered, "Two of you watch outside, flanking the house, lest anyone jump from the upper windows. The rest follow me." Taking the candle, he ascended the stairs, followed by three comrades, leaving Martin alone.

The sturdy soldier bowed his head. It had begun so well, and now this fatal twist. Suddenly, hope sparked. Gerard must be in Peter's room or Margaret's; neither was high enough to preclude a leap. Gerard would jump. Dierich had left a guard below, but what then? For thirty seconds, Gerard and Martin would stand two to one, and in that fleeting span, anything might happen.

Martin propped the back door ajar and peered into Peter's room. Light spilled from within. "Curse the fool!" he cursed. "Will he let them capture him like a damsel?" As the light shifted to Margaret's chamber, it revealed no open window. If Gerard intended flight that way, he would not wait until men crowded the room. Seeing this, Martin retreated from the door, climbed down to the foot of the stairs, and listened.

Silence stretched, growing heavier with each passing second. Martin grew convinced Gerard had already slipped out the window while the men occupied the rooms. Then, the illusion shattered. Faint, desperate cries drifted from the inner bedroom—Margaret's.

"They have taken him," Martin groaned. "They've caught him."

Instantly, a terrible resolve crystallized in his mind. If they removed Gerard, his life would be forfeit, and Margaret's heart would shatter. His gaze darted wildly around the room, like a wild beast seeking an exit, before forming a plan characteristic of iron times and a soldier backed into a corner. He stepped to each door, mimicking Dierich's voice to shout sharply, "Watch the window!" Then, quietly, he locked and bolted both doors. He grasped his bow and six arrows, fitting one to the string and stowing the rest in his quiver. He laid his knife on a chair behind him, handle facing inward, and settled at the foot of the stairs. His eye burned with a cold, steady light, prepared to slaughter four men or fall by their hands. He calculated that two could be dispatched with arrows before they reached him; Gerard and he would face the remaining pair hand-to-hand. Moreover, he knew men panicked easily at a sudden ambush. If Brower hesitated even an instant, Martin would loose three shafts, tipping the odds.

He need not wait long. Heavy footsteps echoed in Margaret's room, approaching steadily. The light followed, accompanied by voices.

Martin's heart, though iron-forged, hammered violently. To hear men marching to their deaths—and perhaps to his own? More likely the latter, given four versus one. Gerard might be bound, powerless to aid. Yet, the man who had trembled at the mere mention of Giles-o'-lantern stood now, muscles coiled, breath held, equally ready to give and receive death. A desperate gamble! Victory meant instant exile for life; defeat meant immediate death upon the floor beneath his feet.

Dierich Brower and his men entered Peter's chamber, finding him in deep slumber. They ransacked his cupboards, slashed at an alligator skin he had nailed to the wall, and lifted the mattress from his bed. It was a spacious room, full of potential hiding spots, yet Gerard was nowhere to be found.

Next, they entered Margaret's room. The sight of it was disheartening—it was small, stark, devoid of furniture save for a large fireplace and chimney. Dierich's gaze fixed immediately on the chimney. There, lying upon an old chest barely a foot high, uncovered and unhidden, slept the beauty of Sevenbergen. Her sheets were snow-white, matching her own linens. She lay there, looking like a lily dropped into a muddy ditch.

Soon, she awoke, sitting up in bewildered amazement. Upon seeing the men, she began to scream faint prayers for mercy.

Her plight humiliated Dierich Brower. "Here is a mess," he stammered, slightly flustered. "We mean no harm, fair maiden. Lie still, close your eyes, and dream of your wedding night while I inspect the chimney for Master Gerard."

"In my room?" she gasped.

"Why not? Rumors say you and he..."

"Cruel! You know they have driven him away, exiled him from his native land! This is a trick. You are thieves, wicked men, not of Sevenbergen. If you truly knew Margaret Brandt, you would not seek her lover in this room above all others in the world. Oh, brave! Four great hulking men, armed to the teeth, to insult one poor, honest girl! The women in your own homes must be nothing, or you would hold them in too high regard to defile a maiden of good standing."

"There! Depart, before we hear further nonsense!" Dierich urged hurriedly. "He is not in the chimney. Plaster can repair what a cudgel breaks, but a woman's tongue is a double-edged dagger, and a girl remains a girl with her mother's milk still warm." Beating a hasty retreat, he muttered to his men, "I told the burgomaster precisely how this would unfold."
