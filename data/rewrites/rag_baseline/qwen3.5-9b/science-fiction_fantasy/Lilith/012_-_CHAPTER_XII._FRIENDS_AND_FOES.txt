Coming across what appeared to be a solitary shrub—the vanguard picket of a hidden force—I dropped to one knee to inspect it. It bore a small fruit; unfamiliar and suspicious, I hesitated to consume it. Unaware that I was being scrutinized by hundreds of eyes from behind the rock face, waiting to see if I would succumb to instinct or discipline, I moved on. I encountered larger specimens, then clusters of similar growths, until the illusion shattered: these were not shrubs but dwarf-trees. By the time I reached the second channel of the riverbed, the terrain was choked with them, forcing me to leap gaps I otherwise could not cross. In one channel, a deafening rush echoed like a flock of birds fleeing an ivied wall, though no wings were visible. Further along, massive fruit-bearing trees lined the edge of a vast hollow—once a lake basin. While the surrounding forest displayed botanical diversity, the hollow was dominated exclusively by these fruit-laden giants.

Descending the moss-grass slope, exhaustion claimed me. I collapsed onto the earth, only to find a tiny tree nearby laden with rosy apples no larger than cherries, within arm's reach. I plucked one and ate it. The flavor was exquisite. Just as I reached for a second, a chorus of clear, crystalline laughter cut through the air—"clear and sweet as the music of a brook"—startling me with unexpected delight.

"He likes our apples! He likes our apples!" shrieked a dozen small voices. "He's a good giant! He's a good giant!"

"He's a giant!" one objector declared.

"He *is* rather big," another conceded pragmatically, "but size isn't everything! Neglect will turn you big and stupid unless you exercise caution."

I rose on my elbow, scanning the scene. Surrounding me was a multitude of children, ranging from toddlers learning to walk to adolescents of twelve or thirteen. A select group of four or five stood slightly apart, observing with a calm intensity that suggested maturity beyond their years. The majority chattered in animated clusters, debating and contradicting one another with the fervor of adults in a marketplace, yet possessing superior manners, sharper wit, and greater joy. I deduced that their collective approval stemmed from my acceptance of the first apple, though the logical leap remained opaque to me. Fear of alarming them kept my mouth shut; observation yielded more data than interrogation ever could. Understanding language is impressive, but comprehension without surprise suggests a deeper connection—a shared frequency.

A subtle shift occurred in the crowd. A diminutive figure, possessing an innocent visage marred by a roguish glint, stepped forward holding a massive green apple. The noise died instantly. All eyes fixed on me, expectant.

"Eat, good giant," the boy commanded softly.

I sat upright, accepted the offering, and offered a smile of gratitude. I prepared to bite, but the moment the flesh touched my teeth, I spat it out and hurled the fruit far across the clearing.

Instantly, a roar of triumph erupted. The children surged forward, nearly suffocating me in a tidal wave of affection. Kisses rained on my face and hands; limbs wrapped around my legs; they scrambled up my arms and shoulders, clutching my head and neck. Overwhelmed by the sheer density of these lovely little goblins, I collapsed to the ground.

"Good, good giant!" they chanted. "We knew you'd come! Oh, you dear, good, strong giant!"

Their chatter resumed, a cacophony of jubilation rising from hundreds of young throats. Suddenly, silence descended again. The circle receded; those climbing me dismounted, attempting to stabilize my posture. Concern replaced mirth on their youthful faces.

"Get up, good giant!" a little girl urged urgently. "Hurry! Much haste! He saw you throw his apple away!"

Before her sentence concluded, I was on my feet. She pointed upward, toward the ridge. Perched on the brow stood a grotesque figure, a few inches taller than myself, radiating hostility. He wielded no weapon, yet the threat was palpable. My allies had vanished into the undergrowth.

He began his descent. Anticipating poor footing, I ascended to meet him. At a level plateau, I halted, awaiting his approach. As he neared, he extended a hand. Instinctively, I reached to shake it, but he snatched it back, brandished a threatening gesture, and offered it again. The message was clear: he demanded possession of the discarded apple. I responded with a grimace of disgust and a sharp wave of rejection.

His reaction was a howl of pure rage, conveying a singular sentiment: *"Do you dare insult my judgment?"*

"One bad apple may grow on the best tree," I retorted.

Whether he grasped the metaphorical weight of my words remains unknown, but he closed the distance aggressively. I braced myself. However, his attack was delayed until a second brute emerged from the treeline behind me. Seizing the opening, the first attacker lunged. I delivered a solid blow to his face, but the rearward assailant struck me squarely on the crown of the head. Between them, I was swiftly neutralized.

They dragged me into the woods above the valley, the domain of their tribe. Here, wretched huts constructed of fallen branches and scavenged stones marked their settlement. Forced into one, I was cast onto the dirt floor and subjected to a barrage of kicks. A woman observed my suffering with detached indifference. During my captivity, gender distinctions blurred; the inhabitants possessed barely enough cognitive function to manifest anger and greed, resembling a fungoid species driven by base impulses. Their diet of tubers, bulbs, and fruits was repulsive to my palate, yet nothing enraged them more than witnessing my refusal to consume it. Cuffed by the females and beaten by the males for my fastidiousness, I lay paralyzed on the floor that night. Yet, amidst the pain, sleep claimed me, leaving me partially restored upon waking.

Morning brought a new torment. Dragged back to the valley, they tied my feet to a tree with a rough rope and placed a flat stone with a serrated edge in my left hand. When I shifted it to my right, they kicked me and returned it to the left. The command was explicit: strip the bark from every branch devoid of fruit. Another kick followed, and I was abandoned to the task.

I commenced this dreary penance, hoping that compliance would grant me solitude—to observe, to plan, to escape. Fortunately, a dwarf-tree grew mere paces away. Every sixty seconds, I plucked a small fruit, consuming it with the efficiency of a soldier rationing supplies. The sustenance proved remarkably revitalizing, strengthening my resolve and replenishing my reserves.
