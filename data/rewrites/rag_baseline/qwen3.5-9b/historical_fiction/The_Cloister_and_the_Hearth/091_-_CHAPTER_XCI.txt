The news arrived swiftly, carried on the wings of rumor and sorrow: Margaret Van Eyck was dead, her spirit extinguished and interred beneath the earth. Yet, in the ledger of her passing, death was not the final period; rather, it was a comma preceding a testament of profound generosity. A will, drafted a year prior to her demise, stipulated that after the settling of funeral rites and the distribution of specific gifts to Reicht Heynes, her entire estate devolved to her beloved daughter, Margaret Brandt. With maternal foresight, she instructed her child to maintain Reicht in the household until such time as he chose to wed, binding him to her legacy as long as he remained unmarried.

Through this instrument, Margaret Brandt inherited not merely a furnished dwelling, but a repository of art that, viewed through the lens of modern valuation, would constitute a fortune. Among the canvases hung within those walls stood one painting that held a supremacy over any gallery collection. It depicted "A Betrothal." The composition captured a moment suspended in eternal gravity: the groom's face bore the solemn weight of the covenant, while the bride wore a demure, placid expression of quiet acceptance. Though the bulk of the masterpiece was executed by Margaret Van Eyck herself, the remaining elements were the handiwork of Jan. Every accessory was rendered with exquisite precision, a technical marvel that continues to astound observers centuries later.

Margaret Brandt dispatched a message to Reicht, instructing him to remain in the house until she felt the courage to re-enter a space haunted by the ghost of a lost lover—the face and voice that once filled its corridors. She charged him with a singular, sacred duty: the protection of the painting, specifically the diptych hidden within a small cupboard.

Before the dust of the funeral had fully settled, Luke Peterson returned home, bearing the shocking tidings that Gerard had taken the habit of a monk. The revelation nearly unhinged his reason. Overcome with a chaotic surge of joy mixed with disbelief, he rushed to Margaret.

"Heed, mistress," he declared, his voice trembling. "If he cannot marry you, I can."

Margaret recoiled slightly. "You?" she queried, confusion clouding her features. "Why, I have seen him."

"But he is a friar," Luke insisted, desperation edging his tone.

"He was my husband, and the father of my boy long before he donned the grey robe," Margaret countered, her voice firm despite the tremor in her hands. "And I have seen him. I have seen him."

Puzzled by her conviction, Luke proposed a solution born of legal pragmatism. "Let me tell you what," he suggested. "I possess a cousin who is a lawyer. I shall inquire of him regarding your marital status."

"Nay," Margaret retorted sharply. "I shall ask my own heart, not a lawyer. Thus, this is your regard for me? To drag me into the town's gossip? Oh, fie!"

"That discussion has already occurred without a word from me," Luke muttered defensively.

"But not by those who seek my respect," she snapped, her eyes flashing. "And if you persist in this path, never approach me again."

"Ay," Luke sighed, the fight draining from his shoulders. "You are like a dove to everyone else, yet a hard-hearted tyrant to me."

"'Tis your own fault, dear Luke, for courting me," she explained, her tone shifting from anger to a weary pity. "That is the barrier preventing me from treating you with the kindness I desire, Luke, my bonny lad, listen closely. I am wealthy now; I can bring happiness to my friends, though not to myself. Cast your eyes upon the street, upon the parish. There are countless women fairer than I, twice told, untouched by the spoils of weeping. Look higher; choose freely. Speak to the maiden yourself, and I shall speak to her mother; they shall not refuse you. Take my word for it."

"I perceive your intent," Luke said, flushing crimson. "But if I cannot secure your affection, I accept none of your coin. I served you when you were as destitute as I, and poorer. No; if you would rather be a friar's paramour than an honest man's wife, you are not the woman I believed you to be. Let us part ways without malice. Seek your comfort on that distant road, where no woman has yet found solace. As for me, I shall live and die a bachelor. Good evening, mistress."

"Farewell, dear Luke," she whispered, her voice thick with emotion. "May God forgive you for speaking thus to me."

For several days, Margaret lived in a liminal state, dreading the inevitable confrontation with Gerard almost as intensely as she craved it. She reasoned internally that his absence was understandable; perhaps he needed time to process the shock. Surely, the knowledge that he was a father would eventually override his monastic vows. "And," she confessed to her own reflection, "if this meeting does not destroy me, I feel I shall emerge stronger afterward than I am now."

However, as day succeeded day without a sign of him, a chilling suspicion began to infiltrate her thoughts. Was his absence deliberate? Had he retreated to the cold, calculating minds of his fellow monks, who had commanded him to sever all ties with her forever? The convent, she feared, proved as merciless to true lovers as the grave itself. At the mere thought, the vitality seemed to drain from her veins.

Now, for the first time, a deep, burning indignation intertwined with her grief and terror. "Could he have truly loved me?" she wondered, the accusation stinging. "To flee from me and his child without a single word? Why does this poor Luke value me more than he does?"

Amidst this turmoil, Giles burst in, roaring with triumph. "I have hit the mark! Our Gerard is the Vicar of Gouda!"

A brief chronicle of the dwarf's rise at court serves to contextualize this extraordinary achievement. Months prior to his ascent, his intellect had begun to bud. He traced the transformation to a specific date, the eighth of June, when, balancing on a tightrope laden with a week's worth of laundry in the drying yard, a sudden crack echoed within his skull. Lo! Intellectual chains were shattered. At court, his sharp wit, blunt speech, cavernous voice, and diminutive stature forged him into a figure of immense power; without his small size, he feared they would have consigned him to the gallows. Both the young Duchess of Burgundy and Marie, the heir apparent, doted upon him, as great ladies have always adored dwarves. The court poet melted butter by the six-foot rule, pouring it down his back in quantities sufficient to stew Goliath. Even the poet's rough dictations were amplified, versified, and softened by Giles's touch.

The resulting verbosity traveled to Eli via letter, titled: *"The high and puissant Princess Marie of Bourgogne her lytel jantilman hys complaynt of y' Coort, and praise of a rusticall lyfe, versificated, and empapyred by me the lytel jantilman's right lovynge and obsequious servitor, etc."*

Yet, Giles reached his zenith through a fortunate alchemy of mind and muscle. The day preceding a grand tournament, he challenged the Duke's giant to a contest of strength. The challenge elicited a grimace of amusement and raised expectations.

Giles erected a lofty pole. At the appointed hour, he ascended it like a squirrel, using sheer arm strength to form a right angle with his body, holding the position effortlessly. Then, he slid down with such velocity that the High and Puissant Princess squealed, burying her face in her hands to avoid witnessing the demise of her pocket-Hercules.

The giant managed only about ten feet before looking ruefully upward and downward, descending in a sweat-drenched retreat to explain the discrepancy. "It was not the dwarf's superior strength, but his smaller frame," he argued.

The spectators met this excuse with raucous derision. The fact remained: the dwarf excelled at climbing poles; the giant excelled at excuses. In essence, Giles had gauged their intellects, likely with his own body as the measuring stick.

"Come," Giles declared, "if you resort to that, I shall wrestle you, my lad, provided you allow me to blindfold your eyes."

Smarting from defeat and hoping to reclaim victory through deception, the giant readily agreed.

"Madam," Giles announced, addressing the court, "do you see yonder blind Samson? Upon my signal, he shall perform a low obeisance and remove his cap for me."

"How can that be possible, being blinded?" a lady-in-waiting inquired.

"I wager on Giles for one," the princess proclaimed confidently.

"That is my affair."

As bets were placed on both sides, Giles struck the giant squarely in the breadbasket. The giant doubled over—a perfect obeisance—and his bonnet fell off.

The company erupted in delighted cheers at this delicate stroke of wit. Giles seized the opportunity to flee. The giant, recovering his breath and tearing off his bandages, gave chase. But it was too late. Giles had prepared a narrow door in the wall, sized perfectly for a man but impassable for a giant, camouflaged so artfully it resembled solid masonry.
