As we emerged from our hut, the warm sunlight cast a golden glow over the landscape, illuminating the lush foliage of our nascent orchard. My sons, Franz, Jack, Ernest, and Fritz, gathered around me, their faces aglow with anticipation. Today, we would tend to the avenue of fruit trees, those resilient saplings that had weathered the tempests and now stood in need of our gentle ministrations.

Our buffalo, its wound still tender, grazed placidly nearby, its services temporarily relinquished to us. I assigned tasks to each of my sons, instructing them to collect sturdy bamboo props and strong fibres to reinforce the trees' fragile trunks. As they worked, their banter filled the air – a testament to their boundless energy and curiosity.

"Papa," Franz asked, handing me a bundle of fibres, "are these wild or tame trees?" His eyes sparkled with a hunger for knowledge, and I smiled, recognizing the glint of intellectual inquiry that had always driven our family's explorations.

"Oh, these are wild trees, most ferocious trees!" Jack chimed in, his voice laced with mirth. "And we're taming them, Papa! They'll trot about after us, bearing fruit wherever we go! Just like our buffalo, with a ring through their nose!"

Franz's expression turned thoughtful, and he asked, "But, Papa, are there really wild and tame trees? The ones in the woods are like crab-apples, while those in the garden are like pears and peaches."

I nodded, pleased to share my knowledge with my son. "Ah, yes, Franz. Wild trees grow unfettered in the woods, whereas cultivated trees, like the ones we have here, are the result of careful grafting. You see, grafting involves inserting a slip or twig of a tree into a knot or hole in the branch of another. This allows the new tree to grow and produce fruit that would otherwise be unknown to the original stock."

A puzzled look creased Franz's brow, and I hastened to clarify. "Imagine, my son, taking a slip from an apple tree and inserting it into a crab-apple tree. In time, the branch will bear juicy apples, not the tart fruit of the crab-apple tree. This art of grafting has allowed us to introduce new varieties from distant lands, where the climate is more hospitable to these trees."
