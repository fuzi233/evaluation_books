Deronda had gone out from England as one goes from a familiar kitchen into a street full of unknown smells—aware of hunger, yet not knowing what food could satisfy it; aware of an inward longing, yet frightened by the chance that longing might be fed into something unmanageable. He had left with his will still held back, like a hand hovering over the seasoning: ready to give itself, yet afraid of spoiling the whole by one rash pinch. He came back as if he carried a title-deed written in his own blood and sealed by time—no mere liberty to drift, but a bond he could honor; and the bond, instead of tightening him into helplessness, gave him the strange ease of a purpose found.

There had always been, beneath his careful speech and his trained patience, a secret selection—quiet as a taste that returns again and again to the same bread though the table is spread with other things. Since the day he had left the little house at Chelsea, Mirah’s farewell—those few words and that look, tender as a hand laid on a bruise—had worked in him with the steadiness of daily nourishment. It did not intoxicate; it sustained. It had kept him from any self-indulgence in word or deed when circumstances had pressed him hard, and his habitual sympathy might have slid into weaker compliances. He could not, he thought, ever woo her; the very associations that had made her dear forbade it. Yet she had taken her place in him as a beloved type—so that other fascinations seemed thin, like dishes prettily served but without salt, without scent, without the slow warmth that rises from honest broth.

And poor Gwendolen’s dependence, however it stirred him, had roused in him not the heat of personal love but the stern ardor of pity—the kind of feeling that makes a man stand by a fireless hearth and give away his own cloak. Toward Mirah, unlike in everything, his tenderness flowed without being dragged, as a clear stream takes the deeper channel. His friendship with Mordecai had drawn Mirah nearer still, not by any open change, but by that inevitable closeness which comes when two lives are bound to the care of a third. He had watched his own feeling as one watches a craving it would be safer to starve; yet Hans’s pretensions had flung out the jealous flame, and when his mother, with her piercing imperiousness, accused him of being in love with a Jewess, any evasion felt like treachery. He had been forced, in one hour, both to name his love and to give shape to his resolve.

That new decisiveness acted on him like the sudden freeing of a wind that had long been held back by walls. Energy that had been spent in self-checking rushed forward; and as he travelled home from Mainz the remaining miles became an offence to his blood, as if the very rails were hindering him. It was as though, finding his ancestry, he had found an added soul: his judgment, once wandering in a wide impartiality that soothed itself by not choosing, now chose with that partiality which is man’s strength—the power to draw shoulder to shoulder with those whose inheritance is his own, and to turn sympathy into action. He longed to be again with Mordecai, to speak without that perpetual restraint, to agree, to dissent, to plan; and, all the while, to have Mirah’s presence without seeming to seek it—to see her with a new possibility before him, and test her words and looks by a new light.

Yet with all his impatience, there was in him a delicate fear. He was not greatly alarmed about Hans; but he had a presentiment that Mirah’s feeling toward himself had always flowed in a channel that would not easily be turned into love. To become a lover when a woman has been thinking of you as a grave protector—something like a judge, a guardian—seemed to him a violent transformation, and he shrank from startling her into pain.

Still, what could he do but go straight from the station to the small square in Brompton? He had promised to run down next day to the Abbey to see Lady Mallinger, and the sunset was already lowering into that London haze which drinks up the light and gives it back dimly, like a smoky lamp. Besides, he carried with him the precious chest, heavy not only with papers but with the sense of hands long dead that had packed and guarded them. He wished to deposit it with Mordecai at once—let him study its contents, alone and with him; and he knew that such swiftness of coming would gratify Mordecai’s heart. It gratified his own too. His nature rushed in one current: the eager affection that delighted in meeting the wishes of those he loved, and the imaginative need of a far-reaching relation that would make even daily acts part of a horizon.

He entered as noiselessly as possible, like one who comes into a sickroom, and, though his dress was only the drab contemporary cloth of a gentleman, there was a radiance in him—an added depth of color in the eyes, a quick returning glow under the skin—born of the thing he carried within, not of any thought about becomingness.

It was the evening of the same afternoon in which Mirah had had her interview with her father. The house held a hush that seemed to have thickened all day, as steam thickens under a lid. Papers Mordecai had been sifting lay scattered where they had fallen at the first shock of anxiety; neither he nor Mirah had had the will to stoop and gather them, as if even that small ordering would be a lie against disorder in the heart. They had sat still together—not counting minutes—while the mantel-clock ticked with that dry persistence which can make sorrow feel mocked, and the light sank away.

Mirah had not eaten; the very thought of food, that should have been comfort, had seemed to her like something belonging to other lives. She had thrown off her dust-cloak and sat close to her brother, her hand in his, while he leaned back with closed eyes and difficult breathing. To her he looked as he might look when the soul could no longer endure its narrow lodging. The fear of his death visited her again and again, and now her grief had a cruel doubling: the father’s shadow had returned, and with it the sense of being bereft not only of the dead but of the living—of losing one by death and one by disgrace.

She watched Mordecai’s emaciated face—so lately vivid with inner fire—now like a lamp turned low, as if a veil had been drawn over it. Her own cheeks were pallid; her eyes held that sorrowful brightness young tears leave behind; her curls were in the disorder of a child roused from sleep.

Then the door opened; no one entered at first; but a voice, known as one knows the sound of a loved foot on the stair, said, “Daniel Deronda—may he come in?”

“Come! come!” Mordecai answered, rising instantly, his face irradiated as if the name itself were light. He looked not surprised, but as if he had been expecting him all day. Mirah started up too, blushing with confused, half-alarmed expectation.

When Deronda came in, his presence was like clearness after rain—no matter what clouds might gather again, the beam of that moment warmed them. Mirah stood close by her brother’s left. Deronda held out his right hand to her, and at the same time laid his other hand on Mordecai’s shoulder; he stood thus a moment, holding them both, saying nothing, taking in their faces with anxious swiftness, until he asked Mirah, “Has anything happened?—any trouble?”

“Talk not of trouble now,” Mordecai said, saving her. “There is joy in your face—let the joy be ours.”

Mirah thought, with a quick ache, It is for something he cannot tell us. But they sat, Deronda drawing his chair close in front of Mordecai, as if he could not bear distance between them.

“That is true,” he said, his voice firm with restrained delight. “I have a joy which will remain to us even in the worst trouble. I did not tell you, Mordecai, the reason of my journey abroad, because—never mind. I went to learn my parentage. And you were right. I am a Jew.”

The clasping of the two men’s hands was like a flash—swift, involuntary, inevitable. It passed through Mirah as an electric shock—startling every nerve awake. But Deronda went on, not pausing for their amazement, speaking as if he were uttering what had long been prepared in Mordecai’s own mind as well as his.

“We have the same people. Our souls have the same vocation. We shall not be separated by life or by death.”

Mordecai’s answer came in Hebrew, loud only as a whisper, yet weighty as liturgy: “Our God and the God of our fathers.”

The feeling was too strong for his ready speech; the words that usually sprang winged and quick seemed pressed down by awe.

Mirah fell on her knees beside him and looked up at his illuminated face—so unlike the deathly look of moments ago. The movement was her one possible outlet: despair reversed into gladness so sudden and so solemn it felt like a rite. For that moment she thought of herself only through him—through what this meant to Ezra.

“And it is not only that I am a Jew,” Deronda continued, in one of those rare minutes when yearning and action are one, and the real seems to match the ideal. “I come of a strain that has ardently maintained the fellowship of our race—a line of Spanish Jews that has borne many students and men of practical power. And I possess what will give us a sort of communion with them. My grandfather, Daniel Charisi, preserved manuscripts and family records far back, hoping they would pass into the hands of his grandson. That hope is fulfilled, in spite of attempts to thwart it by hiding my parentage from me. I have the chest containing them, with his own papers; it is down below in this house. I mean to leave it with you, Mordecai, that you may help me study them. Some I can read easily—Spanish and Italian. Others are in Hebrew, and, I think, Arabic; but there seem to be Latin translations. I could only look at them hastily at Mainz. We will study them together.”

He ended with that bright smile which, shining out from his habitual gravity, seemed a revelation—the rare smile that has meaning because it is not cheap. But when his glance passed from Mordecai and rested on Mirah, it was like sunshine too direct: it made her shift as if she had been warmed and wounded at once. She had knelt under an impulse in which personal embarrassment had no place; now thoughts rushed in that were discordant—Mrs. Grandcourt; the altered aspect of Deronda’s life; her own position; and, as his eyes lingered, she colored and rose, taking her seat again with hands and feet crossed, trying to look quietly composed.

Deronda, keenly sensitive, imagined that the feeling he could not wholly hide had looked out too much and had repelled her. He was ready to believe that any sudden revelation might spoil her feeling toward him; and if Mirah could not love him, any advance from him would make her wretched in the very communion that must continue while Ezra lived. The fear pricked him sharply—fear born not of pride but of tenderness, the dread of being a claim upon her gratitude.

Mordecai, seeing only fulfillment, began to speak again, his voice enlarging as if his breath were freer.

“Daniel, from the first I have said to you: we know not all the pathways. Is there not a meeting among them, as in one soul when an idea is born and breathing draws the elements toward it and is fed and glows? All things are bound in that Omnipresence which is the place and habitation of the world; and events are of a glass whereby our eyes see some of the ways. And if it seems that erring and unloving wills have helped to prepare you, as Moses was prepared, to serve your people the better, that belongs to another order than the law by which our footsteps must be guided. For evil makes not a people’s good except by stirring the righteous will. Beneath all clouds with which our thought encompasses the Eternal, this is clear—that a people can be blessed only by counsellors, and by a multitude whose will moves in obedience to justice and love. See now: it was your loving will that made a chief pathway and resisted evil. In doing brotherhood to my sister, and seeking out her brother in the flesh, your soul has been prepared to receive with gladness the message—‘behold the multitude of your brethren.’”

“You and Mirah have been my teachers,” Deronda said. “If this revelation had come to me before I knew you, I think my mind would have rebelled. Perhaps I should have felt—‘If I could have chosen, I would not have been a Jew.’ What I feel now is—that my whole being consents to the fact. It has been the gradual accord between your mind and mine that has brought about that full consent.”

As he spoke, the first evening in the book-shop came back to him with vividness—the dusty air, the quiet murmurs of the place, the sense of being addressed from a height of prophetic certainty that had then made him struggle to keep aloof. Now, the delight of satisfying the eager watcher before him—like one who has long scanned the mountains for a signal-flame—made his fervor fuller.

“It is through your inspiration that I have discerned what may be my life’s task. You have given shape to what I believe was an inherited yearning—the effect of brooding passionate thoughts in many ancestors—thoughts that seem to have been intensely present in my grandfather. Suppose the stolen offspring of a mountain tribe brought up in a city of the plain; or one with an inherited genius for painting and born blind—the ancestral life would lie within as a dim longing for unknown objects and sensations, and the inherited frame would be like a cunningly wrought instrument never played on, quivering throughout with uneasy meanings that, under the right touch, give music. Something like that has been my experience. Since I began to read and know, I have longed for an ideal task in which I might feel myself the heart and brain of a multitude—a social captainship that should come as duty, not be striven for as personal prize. You have raised the image of such a task—to bind our race together in spite of heresy. You said to me, ‘Our religion united us before it divided us—it made us a people before it made Rabbanites and Karaites.’ I mean to try what can be done with that union. I mean to work in your spirit. Failure will not be ignoble; but it would be ignoble for me not to try.”

“Even as my brother that fed at the breasts of my mother,” Mordecai said, falling back with an exultant repose, like one who has finished his day’s labor and hears, through the quiet, the safe sounds of home.

For Mirah, Deronda’s outpouring had the force of a sudden dawn. His nearness to them, so frankly declared, was startling; it broke the old forebodings that had lain on her like night. She felt as if new facts might show themselves where she had expected only repetition of sorrow. Yet with the relief came another trembling awareness—an unspoken shifting of the air between her and Deronda, as if some note had been added to the familiar soundscape, and she did not know yet whether it would soothe or wound.

After a pause, Mordecai spoke again.

“It has begun already—the marriage of our souls. It waits but the passing away of this body; then they who are betrothed shall unite in a stricter bond, and what is mine shall be thine. Call nothing mine that I have written, Daniel; for though our masters taught rightly that all should be quoted in the name of him that said it—and their rule is good—yet it excludes not the willing marriage which melts soul into soul, and makes thought fuller as clear waters are made fuller, where fullness and clearness are inseparable. I have judged what I have written, and I desire the body I gave my thought to pass away as this fleshly body will pass; but let the thought be born again from our fuller soul which shall be called yours.”

“You must not ask me to promise that,” Deronda said, smiling with affectionate firmness. “I must be convinced first of special reasons in the writings themselves. I am too backward a pupil yet. That blended transmission will go on without any choice of ours; but what we cannot hinder must not become our rule for what we ought to choose. Our duty is faithful tradition where we can attain it. You would insist on that for any one but yourself. Do not ask me to deny my spiritual parentage when I am finding the clue of my life in the recognition of natural parentage.”

“I will ask for no promise till you see the reason,” Mordecai answered. “You have spoken truth: I would obey the Master’s rule for another. But for years my hope—nay, my confidence—has not been that the imperfect image of my thought, the ill-shaped work of a youthful carver who has seen a heavenly pattern and trembles in copying, should live; but that my vision and passion should enter into yours—yea, into yours. For he whom I longed for afar—was he not you whom I discerned as mine when you came near? Nevertheless, you shall judge. My soul is satisfied.”

He paused, then began, in a changed tone, “What moved your parents—” but checked himself and added, with a gentle restraint, “Nay, I ask nothing of others, unless it is your pleasure.”

“Some time—gradually—you will know all,” Deronda said. “But now tell me more of yourselves, and how time has passed since I went away. I am sure there has been trouble. Mirah has been in distress about something.”

He looked at Mirah; but she turned at once to her brother, silently begging him to answer for her. She could not bear that Deronda—on such an evening, when he had drawn himself so close and named himself one with them—should hear of the shame that clung about them, as if it might smear even his newly claimed heritage. To relieve her own overstrained heart, she rose and took up her hat and cloak, thinking to go to her room; perhaps they would speak more freely when she had gone.

Mordecai said, slowly, choosing his words as one chooses what may be spoken without profaning joy: “To-day there has been a grief. A duty which seemed far off has come back and turned its face upon us, raising no gladness—raising a dread we must submit to. But for the moment we are delivered from any visible yoke. Let us defer speaking of it—as if this evening deepening about us were the beginning of a festival in which we offer first fruits of our joy, and mingle no mourning with them.”

Deronda divined enough to be silent. He rose as Mirah rose.

“Are you going?” he said softly. “I must leave almost immediately—when Mrs. Adam and I have brought up the precious chest, and I have given the key to Mordecai—no, Ezra. May I call him Ezra now? I have learned to think of him as Ezra since I have heard you call him so.”

“Please call him Ezra,” Mirah answered faintly. His nearness made her timid in a new way. Was the difference in him—or only in herself, now shaken by the last hours’ violent alternations? The day’s distress had drained her; hunger and fatigue made her tremble. Deronda saw her pallor and the slight unsteadiness of her hands; he longed to show more feeling, to do something that would be like putting warm food before one who has forgotten to eat. But he dared not. She put out her hand, forcing a small smile; he took it gently, then opened the door for her. That was all.

Yet in him, when she had gone, the difficulty deepened instead of easing. Men shrink from approaching as lovers when their addresses may seem to have low motives, prompted by wealth or rank; but Deronda found a more delicate obstacle in the opposite case. For to reverential love, the loved woman always has a kind of wealth and rank that makes presumption possible in any station; and here, besides that awe, there was the peculiar tenderness of his position as benefactor. Mirah’s gratitude was strong; he knew she might feel bound, and any wish of his might seem to her like a claim. To be unable to satisfy it would be a pain renewed daily by the inevitable communion in their care of Ezra.

So he stood, with joy like newly kindled fire and fear like a cold draught crossing it—harder to bear because it was born of love itself—while outside the small square sank into evening, and the house held, with its scattered papers and its ticking clock, the sense of lives being written over by older scripts, in languages half-known and wholly felt.
