There is no such thing as a true, unarguable starting line for any story we live or tell. Even the strictest, most exact science fudges the zero point on its star-calibrated clocks, picking an arbitrary spot in the endless drift of the constellations to call the first second. Poetry, that wilder, older grandmother of science, has always leapt straight into the thick of things, and it turns out both do much the same: look back far enough, and you find Science, too, splitting its arbitrary starting unit into a billion tiny fractions, and the moment you thought was zero is already halfway into the messy, lived-in thick of life. No prologue, no matter how grand, can wrap up all the unspoken history that tugs at every person we meet. So we begin right here, mid-breath, mid-rattle of the roulette wheel, with the question looping in Daniel Deronda’s chest the second his eyes landed on her: Was she beautiful? And what was that strange, thrumming pull in her gaze that made it impossible to look away? Was the fire in her glance bright, or sharp-edged, the kind that burns more than it warms? Probably sharp, he thought, when every glance back at her left him restless, not softened—when the urge to look again felt less like a gentle longing, and more like something he could not quite resist.

The woman who held all his attention was gambling, not barefoot on sun-warmed ruins in the south of France, but in one of those gilded, heavy pleasure palaces centuries of wealth had built for the exact same thrill: all gilded moldings, dark polished wood, plump marble nudes on every mantel, thick enough to trap the breath of all the high society guests inside, far away from the ordinary world outside. It was nearly four o’clock on a soft September afternoon, and the air had thickened into a visible, hazy glow, the only sounds the light rattle of the roulette ball, the chink of coins, the soft sweep of the croupier’s stick, and the flat, mechanical French patter rolling off their tongues like it came from a perfectly built automaton.

Fifty or sixty people huddled around the two long tables, every single one of them fixed on the spinning wheel save one: a sad little boy in a fancy costume, his bare calves peeking out under frills, standing right behind the woman absorbed in her game, his face turned toward the doorway with the blank, glazed stare of a child hired to stand outside a traveling show. The crowd at the table was a patchwork of every European type: Livonian and Spanish, Graeco-Italian and half a dozen kinds of German, English aristocrats and English tradespeople, all rubbing elbows the way they never would anywhere else. An English countess’ jewel-encrusted white fingers nearly brushed a bony, yellow, crab-like old hand that stretched out to clutch a pile of gold coins, attached to a gaunt square-faced woman with deep-set eyes and grizzled eyebrows who looked like she was one step away from turning into a vulture. A sleek, soft-handed London tradesman sat right beside the countess, his hair parted perfectly front and back, holidaying in high society on the back of his successful shop that counted half the nobility as clients. A statuesque handsome Italian leaned over him to set down a whole pile of napoleons fresh from a new money bag, his face so impassive you would never guess he was chasing a win, certain his foolproof system could beat chance. Even the worn-out old libertine squinting through a single monocle, his hand shaking as he asked for change, moved through the game with the same vacant, mask-like look on his face as everyone else, as if they had all eaten of some root that narrowed every thought down to the same single narrow track.

Deronda’s first idle thought was that ragged Spanish shepherd boys gambling coppers on a stone wall looked far happier than this crowd of gilded zombies—for once, Rousseau might have been right that art and progress had done the human race no favors. Then suddenly the whole room felt like it shifted into a scene from a play. His gaze landed on the young woman standing not far from him, and he could not look away. She bent to murmur a sentence in English to the middle-aged lady sitting beside her at the table, then turned back to the game, and he got the full view of her graceful, tall figure, and a face no one on earth could pass without a second glance. His scrutiny deepened, no longer the vague warm glow of casual admiration, but sharp, curious, trying to untangle what made her so unignorable. She bent to place her bet, her pale grey gloved fingers moving with quiet, sure confidence, then leaned back to watch the wheel, her face completely unguarded. She was winning, stacking the coins pushed toward her with steady fingers before sliding them right back onto her chosen spot, then glanced around the room with a cool, neutral survey that barely hid the bright spark of triumph thrumming under her skin.

Then her eyes locked straight onto Deronda’s. She meant to look away, to pretend she had not seen him, but she could not. A hot, stinging resentment flooded her chest: he was not just looking, he was weighing her, seeing past all the charm that made everyone else fall over themselves to please her, as if he existed in some higher, separate space outside the crowd here, and she was just a curious, lesser specimen to him. The color did not rise to her cheeks, but it drained all warmth from her lips. She turned back to the game, defiantly, and her stake vanished. No matter—she had been winning ever since she first sat down with a handful of napoleons, she had a big reserve, she was sure her luck could not break. She had even started imagining a little following of people who would watch her play like she was a luck goddess, predicting their wins for them.

But every stake she placed after that was swept away. Her eyes felt hot, and she did not even need to look to know that Deronda was still standing there, watching her, that his gaze was pressing down on her like a weight that was starting to hurt. The more reason not to flinch. When her chaperon touched her elbow and suggested they leave, she answered by slamming ten louis down on the exact same spot. She was past caring about winning or losing; all she cared about was defying both the bad luck she was sure his gaze had cursed her with, and him. She doubled her bet every time the croupier swept it away, no tremor in her hands, no shake to her lips.

“Faites votre jeu, mesdames et messieurs,” the croupier droned, and she stretched out her arm to put down her last poor heap of napoleons. “Le jeu ne va plus.”

Five seconds later, every last coin was gone. She turned from the table, and instead of hurrying away in shame, she turned directly to face Deronda, and stared right back at him. There was a faint, ironic smile in his eyes when their glances met, and it was far better than being ignored like she was just one tiny nameless insect in the swarm of gamblers. She did not even mind the superciliousness in his face; he was young, handsome, clearly from a good family, not one of those sour boring prudes who gave the casino a disgusted look before they hurried past. She had always taken for granted that everyone found her admirable, that she knew exactly what was worth liking in the world, and that little unshakable belief had wobbled, but it was not broken.

By evening, the same room was even more stifling, glowing bright with gaslight, full of ladies sweeping past in their ballgowns, trains trailing on the carpet. Gwendolen Harleth was the Nereid of the hour, dressed in sea-green silk dotted with silver ornaments, a pale sea-green feather tucked into the green hat on her light brown hair, falling back over her shoulders. She drifted along on the arm of her chaperon, the baroness she called her cousin, next to the stiff, white-mustached German baron, and every seated group turned to stare and whisper as she passed.
“Striking girl, that Miss Harleth. Unlike anyone else here.”
“Dressed up like a serpent tonight, all green and silver, holding her neck like she’s winding around something.”
“I swear she always does something outrageous. You think she’s pretty, Mr. Vandernoodt?”
“Very. A fool might risk hanging for her, easily.”
“It’s that Lamia, cold fairy beauty she’s got, no color in her cheeks at all.”
“Nonsense, that warm pale skin is her best feature, that little turned-up nose, that perfectly curved mouth—”
“Ugh, I hate that mouth. It looks so self-satisfied, like it knows exactly how pretty it is.”
“I think she’s odious,” huffed an old dowager. “And who even are these Langens?”
“They’re perfectly respectable. The baroness is English, you know, and Miss Harleth is the cleverest girl you’ll ever meet.”

Gwendolen held herself extra straight that night, craning her neck every chance she got to spot Deronda, still wincing a little under the memory of that measuring gaze of his. She found her chance with the famously well-connected Mr. Vandernoodt, leaning in with a lazy, clear soprano that did not sound nearly as eager as she felt. “You know everyone,” she said. “Who is that dark-haired young man by the door, the one with the dreadful expression?”
“Dreadful? He’s one of the handsomest men in the room. That’s Deronda, Mr. Deronda, here with Sir Hugo Mallinger, they say they’re very close.”
Gwendolen colored faintly. She knew Sir Hugo’s estate was not far from her own family’s home, though he never visited it. “Deronda,” she said, soft. “What a lovely name. He’s nothing like the usual boring young men I meet. I can never guess what they’ll say next, but I can’t begin to imagine what he’d say.”
“Mostly he says nothing,” Vandernoodt laughed. “I sat with his party on the terrace last night for an hour, he never spoke a single word, didn’t even smoke. Looked half bored to death.”
“All the more reason I want to meet him,” Gwendolen said. “I’m always bored.”

But Vandernoodt never managed to pull Deronda over to their group that night, and when Gwendolen finally slipped back to her private room, there was a sealed letter waiting on her dresser, summoning her home immediately, before she had ever gotten the chance to speak to the strange, dark-eyed stranger who had already burned himself into her thoughts.
