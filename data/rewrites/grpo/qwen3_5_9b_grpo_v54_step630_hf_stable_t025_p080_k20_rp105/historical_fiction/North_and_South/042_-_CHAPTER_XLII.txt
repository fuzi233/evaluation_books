"When some beloved voice that was to you
Both sound and sweetness, faileth suddenly,
And silence, against which you dare not cry,
Aches round you like a strong disease and new,—
What hope? what help? what music will undo
That silence to your sense?"

The blow had struck with such force that Margaret sank into a state of total prostration. It was not a collapse marked by sobs or tears, nor did she find solace in words. She lay upon the sofa, eyes closed, speaking only in faint whispers when addressed, and remaining otherwise utterly still. Mr. Bell sat beside her, perplexed and paralyzed by his inability to act. He dared not leave her side, yet he dared not suggest returning to Oxford; her physical exhaustion was absolute, rendering any journey impossible, and the prospect of the sights awaiting her there was a horror he could not impose. Sitting by the fire, he wrestled with the silence of the room. Margaret lay motionless, almost breathless, beside him. He refused to depart even for the dinner Dixon had prepared downstairs. With a sobbing hospitality that seemed desperate, Dixon attempted to tempt him to eat, bringing a plate of food up to his chair. Usually particular and discerning about flavor, Mr. Bell now found the devilled chicken tasting of sawdust. He minced some of the fowl for Margaret, seasoning it heavily, but when Dixon, following his instructions, tried to feed her, the languid shake of her head made it clear that in her condition, food would only choke her rather than nourish her.

Mr. Bell sighed deeply, lifted his stiff, travel-worn limbs from their easy position, and followed Dixon out of the room.

"I cannot leave her," he muttered. "I must write to them at Oxford to ensure preparations are underway; they can proceed until my arrival. Can Mrs. Lennox not come? I will write to insist she must. The girl needs a woman-friend nearby, if only to talk her into a good, cathartic fit of crying."

Dixon was weeping enough for two, but after wiping her eyes and steadying her voice, she informed Mr. Bell that Mrs. Lennox was too close to her confinement to undertake any journey.

"Well! I suppose we must summon Mrs. Shaw; she has returned to England, has she not?"

"Yes, sir, she has returned; but I do not think she would wish to leave Mrs. Lennox at such a critical time," Dixon replied, harboring a distinct disapproval of a stranger entering the household to share in her ruling care of Margaret.

"Critical time be—" Mr. Bell cut himself off with a cough. "She could be content to remain in Venice or Naples, or some of those Popish locales, during the last 'critical time' which occurred in Corfu, I believe. And what does that little prosperous woman's 'critical time' signify, in comparison with that poor creature there—that helpless, homeless, friendless Margaret—lying as still on that sofa as if it were an altar-tomb, and she the stone statue upon it. I tell you, Mrs. Shaw shall come. See that a room, or whatever she requires, is prepared by tomorrow night. I will ensure she arrives."

True to his command, Mr. Bell penned a letter. Mrs. Shaw, with many tears, declared it so reminiscent of the dear General when he was about to suffer a fit of gout that she would always treasure and preserve it. Had he offered her the choice, urging her as if refusal were a viable option, she might have declined—her sympathy for Margaret was genuine and sincere. It required the sharp, uncourteous command to overcome her inertia and allow herself to be packed by her maid after the boxes were completed. Edith, dressed in cap, shawl, and tears, emerged onto the top of the stairs as Captain Lennox assisted her mother down to the carriage:

"Do not forget, Mamma; Margaret must come and live with us. Sholto will go to Oxford on Wednesday, and you must send word via Mr. Bell when we are to expect you. And if you require Sholto, he can travel on from Oxford to Milton. Do not forget, Mamma; you are to bring Margaret back."

Edith retreated into the drawing-room. Mr. Henry Lennox was there, cutting open the pages of a new review. Without lifting his head, he remarked, "If you do not wish Sholto to be absent for so long, Edith, I hope you will permit me to descend to Milton and offer whatever assistance I can."

"Oh, thank you," said Edith. "I am sure old Mr. Bell will manage everything; perhaps more help is not needed. One does not expect much *savoir-faire* from a resident Fellow. But, dear, darling Margaret; won't it be nice to have her here again? You were both great allies years ago."

" Were we?" he asked indifferently, feigning interest in a passage of the review.

"Well, perhaps not—I forget. I was so consumed with thoughts of Sholto. But isn't it fortunate that if my uncle were to die, it should be just now, when we have returned home, settled in the old house, and are fully prepared to receive Margaret? Poor thing! What a change it will be for her from Milton! I shall have new chintz for her bedroom, make it look new and bright, and cheer her up."

In the same spirit of kindness, Mrs. Shaw journeyed to Milton. She occasionally dreaded the first meeting, wondering how it would be endured, but more frequently planned how soon she could extract Margaret from "that horrid place" and return her to the pleasant comforts of Harley Street.

"Oh dear!" she told her maid, gazing out the window. "Look at those chimneys! My poor sister Hale! I do not think I could have rested at Naples if I had known what it was! I must have come and fetched her and Margaret away." To herself, she acknowledged that she had always considered her brother-in-law somewhat weak, but never so weak as now, seeing the lovely Helstone home he had exchanged for such a desolate location.

Margaret remained in the same state: white, motionless, speechless, tearless. They had told her that her Aunt Shaw was coming, yet she expressed neither surprise, pleasure, nor dislike. Mr. Bell, whose appetite had returned and who appreciated Dixon's efforts to satisfy it, vainly urged her to taste some sweetbreads stewed with oysters. She shook her head with the same quiet obstinacy as the previous day, and he was obliged to console himself for her rejection by consuming them all himself. Yet, Margaret was the first to hear the cab stopping outside, bringing her aunt from the railway station. Her eyelids quivered, her lips colored and trembled. Mr. Bell went down to meet Mrs. Shaw; and when they ascended, Margaret was standing, trying to steady her dizzy frame. Upon seeing her aunt, she moved forward into the open arms waiting for her, and first found the passionate relief of tears on her aunt's shoulder. All thoughts of quiet, habitual love, of tenderness for years, of relationship to the dead, all that inexplicable likeness in look, tone, and gesture that seemed to belong to one family, and which reminded Margaret so forcibly at this moment of her mother, came to melt and soften her numbed heart into an overflow of warm tears.

Mr. Bell slipped out of the room and descended into the study, where he ordered a fire and attempted to divert his thoughts by examining the various books. Each volume brought a remembrance or suggestion of his dead friend. It might have been a change of employment from his two days of watching Margaret, but it was no change of thought. He was glad to catch the sound of Mr. Thornton's voice making inquiries at the door. Dixon rather cavalierly dismissed him; for with the appearance of Mrs. Shaw's maid came visions of former grandeur, of the Beresford blood, of the "station" (as she pleased to term it) from which her young lady had been ousted, and to which she was now, please God, to be restored. These visions, which she had dwelt upon with complacency in her conversation with Mrs. Shaw's maid—while skilfully eliciting all circumstances of state and consequence connected with the Harley Street establishment for the edification of the listening Martha—made Dixon inclined to be supercilious in her treatment of any inhabitant of Milton. Thus, though she always stood in some awe of Mr. Thornton, she was as curt as she dared be in telling him he could see none of the inmates of the house that night. It was rather uncomfortable to be contradicted in her statement by Mr. Bell opening the study door and calling out:

"Thornton! Is that you? Come in for a minute or two; I want to speak to you."

So Mr. Thornton entered the study, and Dixon had to retreat into the kitchen, reinstating her own esteem with a prodigious story of Sir John Beresford's coach and six when he was high sheriff.

"I do not know what I wanted to say to you after all. Only it is dull enough to sit in a room where everything speaks to you of a dead friend. Yet Margaret and her aunt must have the drawing-room to themselves!"

"Is Mrs.—is her aunt come?" asked Mr. Thornton.

"Come? Yes! Maid and all. One would have thought she might have come alone at such a time! And now I shall have to turn out and find my way to the Clarendon."

"You must not go to the Clarendon. We have five or six empty bedrooms at home."

"Well aired?"

"I think you may trust my mother for that."

"Then I will only run upstairs to wish that wan girl good-night, make my bow to her aunt, and go off with you straight."

Mr. Bell remained upstairs for some time. Mr. Thornton began to think it long, for he was full of business and had hardly been able to spare the time for running up to Crampton to inquire how Miss Hale was.

When they had set out upon their walk, Mr. Bell said:

"I was detained by those women in the drawing-room. Mrs. Shaw is anxious to get home—on account of her daughter, she says—and wants Margaret to go off with her at once. Now she is no more fit for traveling than I am for flying. Besides, she says, and very justly, that she has friends she must see—that she must wish good-bye to several people; and then her aunt worried her about old claims, and was she forgetful of old friends? And she said, with a great burst of crying, she should be glad enough to go from a place where she had suffered so much. Now I must return to Oxford tomorrow, and I do not know on which side of the scale to throw in my voice."

He paused, as if asking a question; but he received no answer from his companion, the echo of whose thoughts kept repeating—

"Where she had suffered so much." Alas! and that was the way in which this eighteen months in Milton—to him so unspeakably precious, down to its very bitterness, which was worth all the rest of life's sweetness—would be remembered. Neither the loss of father, nor the loss of mother, dear as she was to Mr. Thornton, could have poisoned the remembrance of the weeks, the days, the hours, when a walk of two miles, every step of which was pleasant as it brought him nearer and nearer to her, took him to her sweet presence—every step of which was rich, as each recurring moment that bore him away from her made him recall some fresh grace in her demeanor, or pleasant pungency in her character. Yes! Whatever had happened to him, external to his relation to her, he could never have spoken of that time, when he could have seen her every day—when he had her within his grasp, as it were—as a time of suffering. It had been a royal time of luxury to him, with all its stings and contumelies, compared to the poverty that crept round and clipped the anticipation of the future down to sordid fact, and life without an atmosphere of either hope or fear.

Mrs. Thornton and Fanny were in the dining-room; the latter in a flutter of small exultation as the maid held up one glossy material after another to try the effect of the wedding dresses by candlelight. Her mother really tried to sympathize with her, but could not. Neither taste nor dress were in her line of subjects, and she heartily wished that Fanny had accepted her brother's offer of having the wedding clothes provided by some first-rate London dressmaker, without the endless troublesome discussions and unsettled waverings that arose from Fanny's desire to choose and superintend everything herself. Mr. Thornton was only too glad to mark his grateful approbation of any sensible man who could be captivated by Fanny's second-rate airs and graces, by giving her ample means for providing herself with the finery, which certainly rivaled, if it did not exceed, the lover in her estimation. When her brother and Mr. Bell came in, Fanny blushed and simpered, fluttering over the signs of her employment in a way that could not have failed to draw attention from anyone else but Mr. Bell. If he thought about her and her silks and satins at all, it was to compare her and them with the pale sorrow he had left behind him, sitting motionless, with bent head and folded hands, in a room where the stillness was so great that one might almost fancy the rush in one's straining ears was occasioned by the spirits of the dead, yet hovering round their beloved. For when Mr. Bell had first gone upstairs, Mrs. Shaw lay asleep on the sofa; and no sound broke the silence.

Mrs. Thornton gave Mr. Bell her formal, hospitable welcome. She was never so gracious as when receiving her son's friends in her son's house; and the more unexpected they were, the more honor to her admirable housekeeping preparations for comfort.

"How is Miss Hale?" she asked.

"About as broken down by this last stroke as she can be."
