The hermit's gaze darted frantically between the child in his arms and the woman standing before him, his eyes wide with a mixture of shock and agitated disbelief. Only moments prior, he had overpowered a pale, terrified girl; now, kneeling at his side, stood an imperious beauty. Her cheeks flushed with vitality, her eyes sparkled with triumph, and her lips parted in a sweet, victorious smile. Yet, beneath this radiance lay a new expression he had never witnessed on her face before—a profound, glowing maternal pride. He stared, transfixed, shifting his gaze from the sleeping infant to the mother, his heart pounding with bewildered awe.

"Us?" he finally gasped, his voice cracking. His wonder-struck eyes continued to wander between them, unable to reconcile the reality before him.

Margaret, equally stunned by the magnitude of the moment, cried out, "What! Does not a father recognize his own child? And a man of God, too? Fie, Gerard, to pretend! Nay, you are too wise, too good not to know—look at you both! 'Tis your own flesh and blood held to your heart!"

Clement trembled violently. "What words are these?" he stammered, clutching the sleeping figure. "Is this... is this my angel?"

"Whose else? Since he is mine."

Turning toward the slumbering child, Clement wore an expression beyond description, his entire frame shaking as his eyes seemed to drink in the tiny life. Margaret followed his gaze. "He bears no resemblance to me," she declared proudly. "Yet, at times, he is your very image in miniature. See this golden hair; yours was exactly this color at his age. Ask your mother. And notice the mole on his little finger. Now look at your own! There! Your mother once told me you bore that mark before him. Oh, Gerard, it was this child who found you for me! By that small mark on your finger, I recognized you as his father when I watched from above your window and saw you feeding the birds."

Seizing the child's hand, Margaret kissed it fervently, pressing half of it to her lips. "Ah! Bless thee! Thou didst find thy poor daddy for her, and now thou hast made us friends again after our little quarrel—the first and the last. Thou wast very cruel to me but now, my poor Gerard, but I forgive thee; for I love thy child."

"Ahh! Ahh! Ahh!" sobbed Clement, choking on his emotions. Lowered by prolonged fasting and unnerved by solitude, the once-strong man succumbed to hysteria, teetering on the brink of fainting.

Margaret felt alarm, yet her experience tempered her fear with deep pity. "Nay, do not distress yourself so," she murmured soothingly, placing a gentle hand upon his brow. "Be brave! So, so. Dear heart, thou art not the first man to journey abroad and return richer by a lovely little companion than he departed. As a man of God, take courage. Say that Heaven sends thee this child to comfort thee for what thou hast lost in me—and that is not so grievous, my lamb. For surely the better part of love shall never cool toward thee, though it may in thine, and ought, being a priest and parson of Gouda."

"I? Priest of Gouda? Never!" Clement whispered faintly. "I am a friar of St. Dominic. Yet, speak on, sweet music. Tell me all that has befallen thee before we part again."

Some might have protested against parting at all, raising the eternal question of separation. But women like Margaret do not repeat their mistakes; it is exceedingly difficult to defeat them twice when their hearts are set on a thing. She assented, turning her back on the Gouda manse as a place to be avoided, and began her tale. She dwelt especially on the kindness shown to her by his parents. As she recounted her troubles, Clement's hand instinctively sought hers. Often she felt him wince and tremble with righteous anger, and often he pressed her hand, sharing her grief in every vein.

"Oh, pitiable tale of a true heart battling alone against such bitter odds," he said. "It all seems small now that I see thee here again, nursing my boy. We have received a warning, Gerard. True friends like you and I are rare, and it is madness to part before death divides us."

"And that is true," Clement admitted, caught off guard.

Then she urged him to recount his sufferings for her sake. He initially begged pardon, asking her to excuse him, but she quietly revoked his request through gentle questioning until he yielded. Many a sigh she heaved for him, hiding her face in her hands with terror at the perils he had endured, though they were now past. To console him, she knelt, sliding her arms under the little boy to lift him gently.

"Kiss him softly," she whispered. "Again, kiss thy fill if thou canst; he is sound. 'Tis all I can do to comfort thee until thou art out of this foul den and in thy sweet manse yonder."

Clement shook his head.

"Well," she said, "let that pass. Know that I have been sorely affronted for the loss of my lines."

"Who dared affront thee?"

"No matter. Those who will do it again if thou hast lost them, which the saints forbid."

"I lose them? Nay, there they lie, close to thy hand."

"Where? Where, oh, where?"

Clement hung his head. "Look in the Vulgate."

"Heaven forgive me," she breathed, realizing her mistake. "I thought thou wert dead, a saint in heaven."

She looked, and on the blank leaves of the poor soul's Bible, she found her marriage lines.

"Thank God!" she cried, tears springing to her eyes. "Oh, bless thee, Gerard, bless thee! What is here, Gerard?"

On the other leaves were pinned every scrap of paper she had ever sent him: their two names written together in jest, the lock of her hair she had given him, half a silver coin broken with him, and even a straw she had used to suck her soup the first day he saw her. When Margaret beheld these proofs of love and signs of a gentle heart bereft, her exultation at recovering her lines was overwhelmed by surging tenderness. She almost staggered, her hand flying to her bosom, leaning her brow against the stone cell wall. She wept so silently that he did not perceive it; indeed, she refused to let him see, knowing that to befriend him now required her to be the stronger, for emotion weakens resolve.

"Gerard," she said, "I know you are wise and good. You must have a reason for what you are doing, however unreasonable it may seem. Let us talk like old friends. Why are you buried alive?"

"Margaret, to escape temptation. My impious rage against those two sprang from the heart; that heart I must deaden, and, *Dei gratia*, I shall. Shall I, a servant of Christ and the Church, court temptation? Shall I pray daily to be led into it, and walk into it with open eyes?"

"That is good sense anyway," Margaret remarked with feigned candor. "'Tis unanswerable," Clement sighed.

"We shall see. Tell me, have you escaped temptation here? I ask because, when I am alone, my thoughts grow wilder and more foolish than in company. Nay, speak truth; come!"

"I must confess I have been more severely tempted here by evil imaginings than in the world."

"There now."

"Ay, but so were Anthony and Jerome, Macarius and Hilarion, Benedict, Bernard, and all the saints. 'Twill wear off."

"How do you know?"

"I feel sure it will."

"Guessing against knowledge. Here, men are sillier than we women. Wise in their own conceits, they refuse to see; their stomachs are too proud to be taught by their eyes. If a woman went into a hole in a bank to escape temptation and found it there, she would simply lift her skirt and step out, unaware of her wisdom until she watched a man in similar plight."

"Nay, I grant that humility and a teachable spirit are the paths to wisdom; but when all is said, here I wrestle only with imagination. At Gouda, she whom I loved as no priest or monk must love any but angels, she will tempt a weak soul, unwilling yet not loath to be tempted."

"Ay, that is another matter; I should tempt thee then? To what, in God's name?"

"Who knows? The flesh is weak."

"Speak for yourself, my lad. Why, you are thinking of some other Margaret, not Margaret a Peter. Was ever my mind turned to folly and frailty?
