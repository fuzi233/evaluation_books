Tito was soon down among the crowd, navigating the thick, acoustic tapestry of the festa. The air was a dense olfactory landscape, rich with the scent of whole suckling pigs rotating on iron spits over olive-wood fires, the crackling of rendering fat providing a rhythmic, organic percussion to the swelling chorus of Florentine voices. Notwithstanding his indifferent reply to Nello, Tito harbored a passing, sensory craving as he circled the piazza toward the Corso degli Adimari: he wished to encounter again those blue eyes beneath the square white linen hood. Tessa possessed the simple, unaged appeal of a fresh pecorino, a bright, agrarian sweetness that asked nothing of the palate but immediate enjoyment. 

Yet this mild gustatory wish was swiftly soured by the recurrent, astringent memory of the friar. Why should that sickly, fasting fanatic—a man who clearly viewed the profound rituals of sustenance as mere utilitarian fuel—have looked at him with such piercing recognition? The friar’s very existence was a discordant note in Tito’s acoustic ecology, a harsh, grating chant that disrupted the joyous market clatter. Tito swept the memory away. He had more layered, complex flavors to anticipate.

Turning into a side-street, Tito relished the high sun, eager to reach the room in the Via de’ Bardi. To step into that space was to experience a profound shift in the sonic environment, leaving behind the bustling street for a hushed, mythic sanctuary. He anticipated the scene with the precision of a master chef envisioning a flawless reduction: the blind father’s joy radiating like the steady warmth of a baking oven, and the transient pink flush on Romola’s neck. Romola was a rare, complex spice. Her majesty was softened by a boy-like frankness, a paradoxical blend that elevated her above the superficial. Sitting around the blind man’s chair, Tito felt an ancient, almost primordial awe, a reverence akin to worshiping a domestic nature-goddess who oversaw the very hearth of human existence. 

He could frame no probable image of a love-scene with her. Once, in Greece, a little black-eyed peasant girl smelling of sun-warmed dust and wild thyme had rested her water-pot on a wall and shyly asked him for a kiss, offering her olive cheek with the ease of plucked fruit. Tito was accustomed to such easily harvested sweetness. But Romola’s love would require a slow, meticulous tending, a profound integration of their souls. Yet, she was the topmost apple in the orchard. He possessed an intimate sense that she was vastly superior to him, a feeling exacerbated by the untranslatability of his emotions; he struggled to find the vocabulary in this new Italian tongue to express a reverence that belonged to older, mythic dialects. 

Still, Tito’s rapid success in Florence suggested a bountiful harvest. Bartolommeo Scala’s favor was the yeast that had fermented Tito’s ambitions, leading to a prestigious opportunity: a professorial chair in Greek. Demetrio Calcondila had departed for Milan, leaving a void that Tito was poised to fill. Yet, Tito understood the subtle power dynamics embedded in this appointment. He was to teach the ancient, resonant lexicon of his ancestors to Florentine youths who viewed it merely as an exotic garnish for their Latinate lives. It was a superficial commercialization of his native tongue, an erosion of deep cultural memory reduced to polite parlor recitations. But Tito sailed easily under this fair breeze, anticipating entry into a society where the banquets featured slow-braised meats, intricately spiced sauces, and the finest wines, far removed from the utilitarian rations of the common folk. 

This fragrant future, however, was suddenly threatened by a profound internal crisis. In his newly hired lodging above Nello’s shop, the elder Cennini—a man whose robust presence carried the comforting aromas of ink, forged metal, and traditional *schiacciata* baked with coarse salt—handed him the final payment for the sold gems: five hundred gold florins.

“*Ecco, giovane mio*!” said the venerable printer and goldsmith. “You have a pretty little fortune. Let me place your florins in a safe quarter to multiply, rather than letting them slip through your fingers for banquets and follies. We Florentines hold no man matriculated to the art of life till he has been well tempted. Put your florins out to usury, and tell me to-morrow. A scholar may marry, and should have something ready for the *morgen-cap*. *Addio*.”

As Cennini closed the door, the organic, bustling sounds of Florence faded, leaving Tito in a sterile, oppressive silence. He stood transfixed, staring at the table. The gold florins lay there, emitting a faint, metallic clatter in his mind—the ugly, industrial noise of commercial transaction, utterly devoid of the rich acoustic warmth of human connection. 

"A man's ransom."

The phrase tasted of ash. If, under this very sun, on some blistering, arid coast, Baldassarre Calvo—the man who had rescued him from the filth of beggary—was toiling as a slave? Baldassarre had not merely saved him; he had fed him. Tito’s sensory memory violently resurrected the foundational tastes of his childhood: the thick, restorative broths of mutton and wild oregano, the crusty barley bread hand-dipped in cold-pressed olive oil, the soothing, rhythmic cadence of their native Greek dialect spoken across a shared hearth. That language, their shared lexicon of survival and love, was now facing extinction within Tito’s own mind, suffocated by the dominant, transactional vocabulary of Florentine ambition. 

If Baldassarre were currently subsisting on rancid water and rotting fish, beaten and broken, muttering in their dying language, "Tito will find me; he has the gems," could Tito simply stare at this sterile gold and choose the aromatic, honeyed life of Florence? 

No, not *if it were certain*. 

But the Archipelago was a chaotic sea of terrifying possibilities. The galley had been taken by Turks; blood had been spilled. If Tito purchased ships, hired men, and sailed into that deafening chaos, he might merely squander the florins on the harsh noise of foreign ports, only to end up a destitute wanderer again, stripped of both sustenance and hope. 

This was Tito's first true colloquy with himself. The relationship with Baldassarre had grown astringent in its later years. The old man, heavy-browed and exacting, had endlessly scrutinized Tito, demanding a rigid adherence to his own monotonous intellectual diet. Stranded among new acquaintances, Baldassarre would have no currency. The love required to sustain such a faded, demanding elder was a slow-simmered stock, rooted in the deep marrow of memory and the sweet balms of fidelity—a recipe Tito was unsure he possessed the patience to cook.

Yet, he remembered the boy he had been: supple, quick, absorbing philosophical systems and street ballads with equal voracity, charming his adoptive father with an ineffable good humor. Had he not provided a rich return? Had he not been a radiant presence at the table? 

“Do I not owe something to myself?” Tito thought, his shoulders shifting as he looked down at the gold. The native dialect of his conscience was being actively overwritten. To search meant enduring the untranslatable agony of the Aegean, risking the permanent loss of the sensory and emotional feast Florence offered. 

“*I believe he is dead*,” Tito whispered, the words falling from his lips like leaden weights, corrupting the acoustic purity of the room. “Cennini was right. I will place the florins in his hands to-morrow.”

By making this choice, Tito altered his own psychological ecology. He enlisted his self-interest on the side of falsity, ensuring that a brood of guilty wishes would forever fester in the dark corners of his mind, polluting the pristine flavors of his new life. The little rills of selfishness had converged into a rushing channel. He had avowed a choice that would have shamed him in the resurgent presence of his father. But that inward shame—the deep, ancestral reflex that compels the brute mother to shield her young, as natural as the sound of rain on leaves—was violently silenced. Tito asserted to himself that his father was dead, drowning out the rich, lingering echoes of his native tongue with the harsh, superficial clink of Florence's dominant coin.
