The cycle of their days in London had returned, as inevitably as the turning of an ancestral harvest, to the drawing-rooms of Park Lane. It was only the third evening after their arrival, and Gwendolen Grandcourt moved through the white, gold, and pale crimson chambers of Lady Mallinger’s musical party, draped in pale green velvet and poisoned diamonds. For a palate newly sensitive to the bitter, astringent herbs of moral compromise, the room offered only a sterile feast. To Gwendolen, whose spirit was quietly starving, her own marriage was an act of brutal consumption—a utilitarian arrangement in which she was merely fuel for Grandcourt's tepid fires, leaving her devoid of any true, slow-simmered sustenance. 

The sonic environment of the gathering—a clattering, industrial hum of superficial chatter and the clinking of commercialized high-society—grated against her psychological well-being. But Herr Klesmer was there, a passionate chef of acoustic frequencies who understood that music was not a mere commodity to be served to the highest bidder, but a profound, nourishing broth for the human soul. He was at the piano, meticulously preparing to accompany Mirah Lapidoth in singing Leo’s *O patria mia*. Gwendolen exchanged a bow with Klesmer, sensing the untranslatable gulf between her artificial existence and the deep, agrarian honesty of the young woman standing by the instrument. 

As Gwendolen scanned the room, the olfactory landscape of her memory was suddenly pierced by the stale, oppressive scent of her husband’s secrets. She spotted Mr. Lush. He was a human pollutant, a man whose very presence violated the acoustic and moral sanctity of her world. Her husband, standing nearby and listening to Lord Pentreath, had broken his promise to banish Lush. The dominant lexicon of their marriage was one of silent, coercive power dynamics, and the sudden reappearance of this half-caste among gentlemen felt like a toxic emission in her previously carefully curated space. 

Daniel Deronda stood near the door with Hans Meyrick, acting as a silent guardian of the room's acoustic ecology. Deronda’s senses were finely tuned to the cultural environment, and he cringed inwardly when Lady Pentreath, her violoncello voice polluted by the careless commercialization of art, referred to Mirah as "your Jewess." To Deronda, this dominant lexicon was an act of violence, a superficial label that threatened to erode the nuanced, multi-generational sagas and native dialects of Mirah’s heritage. Deronda’s mind was anchored to Mordecai—a traditional elder and meticulous historian of their people's spiritual sustenance, who was starving as his ancestral language faced the quiet tragedy of death. 

When Mirah sang, the Italian words of Leopardi’s ode washed over the room, restoring an acoustic purity that cut through the smog of the elite. *Non ti difende Nessun de’ tuoi! L’armi, qua l’armi: io solo Combatterò, procomberò sol io.* To Deronda, the notes tasted of dark, mythic wine, carrying the scent of ancestral hearths and the slow-cooked marrow of heroic passion. It was the untranslatable subjective experience of a dying man crying out for a defender, seamlessly integrating a mythic, historical weight into the everyday domestic routine of a London party. 

Gwendolen, moved by a desperate hunger for this kind of authenticity, approached Mirah as the applause faded. "You must let me say how much I am obliged to you," she said, navigating the awkwardness of the dominant social lexicon. "I had heard from Mr. Deronda that I should have a great treat in your singing, but I was too ignorant to imagine how great."

Mirah, who viewed her own artistry through the lens of pure, uncommercialized sustenance, replied with native earnestness. Her speech was flavored with the deep gratitude of someone who knew what it was to truly starve. "I was poor and needed help," Mirah said softly. "And Mr. Deronda has given me the best friends in the world... I was in distress. I owe everything to him."

Gwendolen felt the sharp sting of her own malnourishment. She relocated to a settee to draw Deronda near. When he sat, the torrent-like confluences of Klesmer’s bass and treble played again, a mythic convulsion of sound that reduced the petty, superficial chatter of mortals to insignificance. But Mr. Lush lingered nearby, an olfactory and acoustic pollutant making true communication impossible.

"Miss Lapidoth is everything you described her to be," Gwendolen said, her voice constrained by the artifice required in Lush’s proximity.

"You have been very quick in discovering that," Deronda replied ironically. 

The restraint was a physical pressure, like the heavy, cloying scent of overripe fruit. They remained silent until Lush finally drifted away. Gwendolen immediately dropped the artifice. "You despise me for talking artificially."

"No," Deronda said, looking at her coolly. "I think that is quite excusable sometimes. But I did not think what you were last saying was altogether artificial."

"There was something in it that displeased you. What was it?"

"It is impossible to explain such things," Deronda answered, touching upon the inherent untranslatability of subjective experience. "One can never communicate niceties of feeling about words and manner."

"You think I am shut out from understanding them," Gwendolen said, a tremor in her voice. There was a look of suppressed tears in her eyes. She hungered for his moral sustenance, terrified of being left to consume only the ash of her own selfishness. "If you despair of me, I shall despair. Your saying that I should not go on being selfish and ignorant has been some strength to me. If you say you wish you had not meddled—that means you despair of me and forsake me... It is you who will decide; because you might have made me different by keeping as near to me as you could, and believing in me."

Before he could answer, she rose and returned to her former place. The room fell into quietude as Mirah’s voice gave forth *Per pietà non dirmi addio*. The melody was the pure, unpolluted extract of emotion. Deronda felt the agonizing pull of two interwoven timelines: the ancient, multi-generational saga of Mordecai pulling him toward a native identity, and the desperate, modern starvation of Gwendolen, whose trustful effort to lean on him felt like a drowning cry across a vast sea.

The circular pacing of the night inevitably returned Gwendolen to the suffocating enclosure of her husband's carriage. The sonic space was brutally confined. 

"Lush will dine with us among the other people to-morrow," Grandcourt said, his voice a harsh, utilitarian noise devoid of any musicality. "You will treat him civilly."

Gwendolen’s heart beat violently. Her native dialect of protest—*You are breaking your promise to me*—rose in her throat, but the power dynamics of his dominant lexicon crushed it into an untranslatable, silent plea. To Grandcourt, her submission was merely utilitarian fuel, requiring no sensory appreciation or spiritual communion. 

"I thought you did not intend him to frequent the house again," she managed in a tone of utter defeat.

"I want him just now. He is useful to me; and he must be treated civilly."

Silence fell. It was the moment when a husband introduces the olfactory pollution of his cigar-smoke into the carriage, an oppressive, suffocating atmosphere signaling the absolute erosion of her agency, establishing a toxic environment she would simply have to breathe.
