A low murmur rippled through the throng packed tight around the stone altar, and swelled fast into a rabid roar. “Kneel,” a man breathed in my ear, his palm digging into my shoulder, “kneel now, or they’ll tear you apart.” Another shrieked over the din: “Bow your head, beardless foreign dog, worship the one true Goddess, or by the old sun gods I’ll claim priesthood of her rites and butcher you on these flagstones!” The shouts climbed higher, thick with fanatic fury, and spears were lifted point-first in my direction.

But then the name “Deucalion” passed from lip to lip, and the frenzy drained out of them like water through cracked clay. They knew the name: the exile who’d left Atlantis twenty years prior to rule the rough colonial holdings of Yucatan, the man who’d ridden only days before at the Empress’s side under the gold-canopied litter on the back of her war mammoth. When they weighed the vengeance I could call down, the roars died limply away, every last zealot dropping his gaze to the dust.

Silence pooled around the circle then. Phorenice turned from the altar where she’d been receiving her people’s worship, and saw me standing tall, the only soul in the throng not prostrate in the dirt. I stepped between the two great weathered megaliths that marked the ritual circle’s edge, crossed the trampled grass to her side of the altar, and did not bow. At the exact formal distance her own edicts had set for her chief minister, I gave the crisp salute she herself had ordained, and hailed her plainly by her imperial title, no line of god-worship on my lips.

“Deucalion, man of ice,” she purred, eyes glinting with that sharp, half-amused edge only she carried.

“I still give my fealty to the old Gods.”

“I did not speak of that,” she said, and flicked me a sidelong, knowing smile.

Ylga, her favourite handmaiden, came hurrying over then, face bleached bone-white, hands shaking so hard her ceremonial fan slipped from her fingers. “If he will not turn yet,” she pleaded, bobbing a deep curtsy, “surely you can pardon him? He is a strong man, slow to renounce the beliefs he has held all his life. You may win him over in time. But Phorenice—he is Deucalion. Slay him here over this one slight, and there is no man left in all the marches of Atlantis worthy of serving you half so well—”

The Empress cut her off, voice cracking like a whip. “You little slut. I kept you near me to tend my wardrobe and wave my fan, not to meddle in matters of state. Get out of this circle, now, or I’ll have you flogged within an inch of your life. And more than that: I’ll do to you what Zaemon did to my former captain Tarca. Shall I point my finger, and smite that pretty skin of yours with a creeping, disfiguring leprosy?”

Ylga bowed her shoulders, cowed, and scurried past the stone markers out of sight. Phorenice turned back to me, the cold venom melting out of her face into something soft, bright, reckless. “My lord,” she said, “I am like a fledgling that hatches and suddenly finds it has full-grown wings. There are a hundred flights I want to take, before I even know what they are.”

“May every one of them bring prosperity to Atlantis,” I said formally.

She laughed, eyes flashing. “Oh, I know what you care for most. We will ride back to the pyramid, and talk this all through at leisure. For now, my lord, give me your arm, and conduct me back to my mount.”

It was clear then I would face no punishment for refusing to worship her, no public execution to cast doubt on her new claim to divinity. Ylga’s ill-timed interference had earned her only a lashing that was yet to come. I did not waste time untangling all the whys of it; I accepted the reprieve, for it pushed forward the secret mission Zaemon and the high priest council had laid on my shoulders.

When she rested her fingers in my palm—warm, delicate, fingers that had still wielded a war axe with deadly skill a few days prior—I led her gravely out of the ritual circle, the acrid stench of the goat sacrifice clinging thick behind us. I had expected the old Sun God to strike her down for her sacrilege right there, but no vengeance fell. I knew He would choose His own time, His own instrument, and my puny mortal arm did not need to be the one to defend His honour.

I led that lovely, dangerous woman to the great red war mammoth, standing placidly at the edge of the crowd, its shaggy hide glinting red in the sun. She climbed the carved steps up to the golden litter lashed to its back, the canopy woven with live royal snakes, and bade me sit beside her on the plush silk cushion. Her fan-girl’s empty spot sat at our backs; there was no one else within earshot to hear what we said as the mammoth rumbled slowly toward the city streets.

She was hungry to hear how I had fared after the rebel attack on the north gate. I told her the full tale, making sure to speak of Nais, the rebel girl I had saved from the circus tigers, as sharp, loyal, and someone who could be won back to the empire’s side with gentle handling. Only the secret rites Zaemon had shared with me in the sacred priest tongue did I hold back—those words are never spoken outside the High Council, gathered before the Ark of Mysteries.

“You speak very warmly of this rebel Nais,” Phorenice observed, tilting her head.

“She is far sharper, far more thoughtful than the common run of foot soldiers,” I said.

She sighed, and it sounded genuine. “An Empress loses all the small joys that even the poorest woman takes for granted.”

“What joys?”

“Honest wooing, from a man who is her equal, not a toady bowing and scraping for favours.”

“If you insist on styling yourself a Goddess to the people,” I said, “that is a hard line to walk.”

“Pah! I have no wish to be a Goddess to you, Deucalion. That trick of calling down fire from the skies? All seven high priests know how to do it. I filched the ritual from your stores to give me more power over the common folk. Cannot you be generous enough to admit a woman can figure out those natural laws just as well as your stuffy old elder priests?”

“Even so, you are still Empress of Atlantis.”

“Then do not treat me like one, not right now. Think of me only as a woman sitting here beside you on this cushion. Drop all the ceremonies, all the statecraft. Do you want to sit around until all your manhood has withered away for the sake of formalities? No one is saying we need to rush. But only a fool waits for the fruit to ripen, then leaves it hanging until it rots.”

I looked at her, at that stunning, famous beauty, and by all the old Gods it left me cold. But I remembered the command laid on me by the council, and forced a soft smile. “I have been fastidious all these years away in Yucatan,” I said, “but I do not regret waiting for this long.”

“Nor I. I have played the innocent maid long enough. I am a woman now, full-blooded, ready for more than empty ceremony.”

I closed my hand gently over hers. “Take me as your husband then. I will be a good, loyal man to you. But I will speak plainly, as one equal to another: I will never be your puppet.”

She flicked me that sidelong smile again, amused. “I have been the one giving orders for so long, the idea of being mastered sometimes sounds like a delight. You will never be a puppet, Deucalion. No one could force you to dance to anyone else’s tune, if you did not want to step.”

“Then we will rule Atlantis together, side by side. All these stupid divides will be swept aside: the rebels will go back to their farms and their hunting grounds, the priest clan will be pacified, and we will build an empire that outshines every other nation on the face of the earth, peaceful and prosperous for all.”

She pulled her hand away from mine, petulant, her pretty face souring. “Ugh. Your conditions, your endless Atlantis talk. That rough colonial bluntness of yours grows tiresome very fast. Am I the one doing all the wooing? Is there not a single spark of passion under all that ice of yours?”

“I have spent twenty years fighting border wars and administering colonies. I had barely any time to speak to women, let alone learn what ‘love’ is supposed to be.”

“We were both fools when you first landed, then,” she said, laughing. “Every bearded lord in Atlantis has sighed and grovelled at my feet. Some of them were fun for an hour, but none of them ever touched me. I never knew what that grand passion felt like, either. But you are going to teach me, Deucalion—if you keep being this stubborn and cold, I will make you.”

“From what I have seen, love is for the poor, the weak, the flighty.”

She flung her head back, red curls flying, voice thick with longing. “I wish I was not Empress. I wish I was some ragged gutter girl, so a strong man could pick me up, throw me over his shoulder, and carry me home for pure, unthinking passion. I would revel in that. I would give everything I have just to feel that, for once.” She snickered. “Though I would make his life a living hell if he did not please me, of course.”

“We cannot change the bones of who we are,” I said.

Her face darkened, sullen. “If I cannot melt that ice of yours, Deucalion, there is going to be so much trouble for this Atlantis you love so much. Our whole household will burn, if I grow to love you, and your heart stays cold and dead.”

I could see right then she would have had me kiss her, hold her, right there on the mammoth’s back, in full view of the crowded streets thronging with gawking citizens. She never cared for privacy. But I could not force that familiarity. The Gods know I was dead serious about my mission, and the whole charade made my skin crawl. I was a priest, first and last, and I had set aside all personal desire to fulfil the council’s orders. I was trained in diplomacy, sharp enough of tongue on all matters of state, but I had never wasted time learning to trifle with women. If I had tried to spout the flowery, fake romantic lines the rest of her court tossed at her, she would have seen through the fraud in a heartbeat. She had lived on that empty flattery for years, and could weigh the worth of a pretty speech down to a single copper coin. Honesty would have ruined everything. So I said nothing, and let her dismiss me as a clumsy colonial oaf.

“I always heard the Yucatan colonists were far more polished than the lot stuck in Egypt,” she jeered. “You do your viceroyalty no credit. Even the exiled Egyptian lords that come through my court know how to speak to a woman.”

“They must have improved drastically in twenty years,” I said. “The last time I was sent to report on Egypt, the native black tribes ruled the whole land, and our people lived there only on their sufferance. Their pyramids were tiny, their cities no more than mud forts.”

“Oh, they are still exiles, but they never forgot their manners. They all raved about my face, loud enough to make the palace walls ring. One of them struck a deal with me: ten pretty, flattering words, and he cut off his own right hand to give me as a gift. It sits dried, on a shelf in my private chambers to this day.”

A streak of good luck saved me from her teasing right then. The rebels besieging the outer walls had stepped up their attacks, and switched from throwing stones to lobbing fire-hardened wood carcasses stuffed with throwing oil. The missiles bounced off stone buildings harmless enough, though a few unlucky passers-by were scorched by the splattering flames. But when those fire bombs hit the rough wooden booths and rush huts in the poor quarters, they exploded into roaring walls of flame that no bucket of water could put out.

Neither Phorenice nor I would have cared for that small siege chaos—until the great red mammoth caught the scent of smoke on the wind. The huge beast lifted its trunk high, swaying its great shaggy head uneasily. It had been trained to ignore shouting, drums, arrows, stones, but the ancient, racial terror of fire ran in its blood. Since the first days of man and mammoth hunting each other in the wild northern steppes, fire had been the great mammoth’s only unbeatable enemy.

The guards at its haulers’ rings wrenched at the heavy iron chains bolted to its spiked neck collar, driving the sharp metal points deep into its flesh. Blood dripped down its red fur, but the beast’s panic overpowered every lesson it had ever learned. It gave a deafening, screaming trumpet, and bolted backward down the street it had come, scattering the spearmen like straw, mowing a swath through the crowd so thick we could hear the screams of the trampled civilians over the din. The golden litter swayed so hard the snake canopy whipped back and forth, and we were nearly flung off like pebbles from a slingshot. I braced my feet against the front rail, wrapped one arm around the central wooden pillar, and pulled Phorenice tight against my chest to cushion her from the jolts.

She settled there, her soft cheek resting just under my chin, the scent of her jasmine hair filling my nose, as the mammoth charged on blindly. The smoke and fire were far behind us now, but the beast was too far gone in panic to care. It left the entire royal procession far in the dust, thundering through the artisan quarters, the merchant districts, down a long open lane that ended at the glint of the city harbour.

I braced for the beast to skid to a halt, then spin around and charge right back into the city. But it never slowed. It trumpeted loud enough to rattle the dock pilings, and ran straight off the shore into the dark water of the harbour. The ancient city sewers emptied their waste right there, and the bottom was thick with soft, sucking black sludge. The mammoth kept running until its feet sank deep into that mire, and then it stopped, bellowing in terror, unable to pull itself free no matter how hard it strained. The whole litter lurched sideways, and we were both flung far out into the cold harbour water.

Phorenice laughed as she surfaced, her soaked red curls sticking to her face, her fine silk gown heavy with water. “You move well for a clumsy colonial, Deucalion. You made a very fine cushion for me back there. You have more gallant in you when the chips are down than you let on. How was that ride? Better than any boring royal procession, I wager.”

“I would rather have walked,” I said, treading water beside her.

She snickered. “You will never make a courtier. You are supposed to say you wished the ride had gone on forever, so you could hold me in your arms longer. Ho! The boat there! Hold your arrows! Deucalion, hail them, tell them not to hurt my mammoth. Let it wear itself out thrashing for a day or so, to teach it a lesson, then we will haul it out with windlasses and break it all over again. I would never feel like Phorenice again if I did not have my great red shaggy mammoth to carry me around.”

A ten-oar galley, belonging to the port captain, was rowing as fast as it could toward us. When I hailed them, the soldiers on the foredeck lowered their bows, and the steersman turned to pick us up. The port captain himself, a simpering, fawning little man who lived only to lick the boots of his superiors, knelt grovelling on the deck the second we were hauled over the side, wailing that he wished he had gone blind rather than see the great Empress put in such peril.

“Peril?” Phorenice said, flicking water off her sleeve. “Nothing mortal will ever kill me. I am only annoyed my favourite gown is ruined, and I lost a few pretty jewels.”

The fool immediately offered to blind himself to spare her future discomfort, and she snapped at him that if he was that eager to lose his eyes, she would have them plucked out right then and there, for daring to suggest he kill her beloved mammoth over a little “frolic.” The port captain grovelled lower, and said he had two prisoners locked in his cabin, caught sneaking into the harbour under cover of night, and he would sacrifice both of them at once to the trapped mammoth, to appease its rage.

“Prisoners?” Phorenice said, tilting her head. The galley had bumped against the royal quay now, and she was stepping onto the stone docks, water still dripping from her hem. “Bring the woman out first. I am in the mood for romance today. She was caught sneaking into the city to find the man she loves, if your story is true.”

The port captain scurried into the tiny cabin, white-faced, and dragged the bound female prisoner out into the sunlight.

It was Nais. The same rebel girl I had saved from the cave tigers in the circus, the one who had sat with me all night in the woods and told me stories of the free rebel lands outside the city walls. Phorenice knew her at once. She did not even bother hiding the recognition, a sharp, mocking smile spreading across her face as she called Nais by name, and taunted her loudly about her rebel friends, and the lover she had snuck into the city to find.
