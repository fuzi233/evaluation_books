Jorian Ketel pressed directly into Margaret's chamber, expecting silence, but instead encountered a scene of suspended animation. In the center of the room sat the man he sought, Gerard, pale and utterly still, his head cradled in Margaret's lap. She knelt over him, her countenance a mask of petrified grief; her eyes were wide yet glassy, fixed on the white visage above her, blind to the candlelight, deaf to Jorian's approach, and devoid of any care for the world save for this dying figure.

Jorian stood frozen, the flickering flame of his taper trembling in his grip, awestruck by the stillness around him. "Where was he, then, all this time?" he whispered, his voice breaking the heavy silence.

Margaret offered no answer, her spirit locked in a trance of despair. Jorian stepped forward to inspect the empty chest where the stolen goods had supposedly been hidden. As he looked, the girl's silent, paralyzing agony began to weigh heavily upon his heart. "A pitiful sight," he murmured, his tone shifting from curiosity to compassion. "A black night's work, all for a few skins! Had it been better to let us go with them." He paused, looking at the unresponsive girl, then back at Gerard. "She is past answering me, poor wretch. Stay!"

With sudden resolve, Jorian reached into his tunic and produced a small, circular mirror, no larger than his palm. He pressed it gently against Gerard's lips and nose, holding it steady. When he drew it away, the surface remained dull, reflecting no breath.

"There is life in him!" Jorian exclaimed to himself, a spark igniting within his chest.

The word struck Margaret like a thunderclap. Though spoken softly, it shattered her stupor. Like a statue brought suddenly to motion, she sprang up, throwing her arms around Jorian's neck with a fervor born of desperate relief. "Oh, bless the tongue that speaks this truth!" she cried, clutching the burly stranger tightly, her embrace fierce and trembling.

"Here, here! We must lay him warm," Jorian urged, gently disentangling her. With practiced ease, he lifted Gerard and laid him carefully upon the bedclothes. From a flask at his belt, he poured a generous measure of Schiedamse brandy into his open hand and dashed it sharply onto Gerard's face. The sharp, burning liquor seemed to wake the dormant spirit within; Gerard let out a faint, ragged sigh. To the ears of those listening, it was a sound of profound, miraculous joy. Margaret rushed toward him, but halted abruptly, her body quivering with a fresh wave of terror, afraid that even her touch might harm him. She had lost all faith in her own strength.

"That is right—leave him alone," Jorian advised kindly. "Do not cling to him as you clung to me, or you will drive the breath back into the earth. Let him rest; he will surely recover. He is no old man."

Deep within Gerard's chest, a sigh resonated, and a faint flush crept into his lips, returning color to his wan cheeks. Seizing the moment, Jorian turned toward the door. Hardly had he crossed the threshold when hands seized his legs from behind.

It was Margaret. She coiled around his ankles like a frightened serpent, kissing his hand and fawning upon him with tear-stained eyes. "You will not speak? You have saved his life! You lack the heart to cast him back into the grave, to undo your own noble deed?"

"No, no!" Jorian assured her, his voice firm. "It is not the first time I have served you both well; I told you in the church where we intended to take him. Besides, what concern does Dierich Brower hold for me? I would see him hang before I reveal his secret. Yet, I pray you tell me where the parchments lie! A hundred crowns are offered for them—a fortune for Joan and the children, you understand."

"They shall have those hundred crowns," Margaret promised, her voice thick with emotion. "Are the items in the house?" she asked eagerly.

"No," Jorian said, leaning closer. "But I know where they are. By God and Saint Bavon, I swear you shall possess them tomorrow. Come to me whenever you wish, but come alone."

"I am bound to do so," Jorian declared, his conscience clear. "What! Share the windfall with Dirk Brower? And may my bones rot in my skin if I allow a soul to learn the boy is here."

Before lingering further and risking suspicion, Jorian fled the room. Behind him, Margaret knelt, her frame shaking from head to toe, praying fervently beside Gerard and *for* Gerard.

Outside, Jorian met Dierich Brower, dismissing the urgency of the situation. "What is to be done?" Jorian asked casually. "We have simply scared the girl into a fit. She has been in a state."

"We ought to visit her and tend to her," Dierich suggested.

"Oh, yes!" Jorian laughed lightly, his offhand manner disarming all suspicion. "And frighten her further into the churchyard? Her father is a physician, and I have already roused him to bring her back to sense. Shall we view the fire?"

Soon the group agreed that the kitchen of the "Three Kings" offered far more warmth than Peter's dwelling, and they departed, first releasing Martin from his bonds. At the door, Dierich chuckled, nodding at Jorian. "Note well, friend, that I was correct and the Burgomaster mistaken. I said we were too late to catch him, and indeed, we were."

Thus, Gerard escaped the jaws of prison and the shadow of the grave, not through cunning concealment or the girl's quick wit, but through a sudden impulse of mercy in the heart of one of his captors, a reckless fellow whose greed for gold had been tempered by a sliver of humanity. How strangely intertwined are human motives, how short-sighted the sharpest strategies! Those whose gentle fates spare them the fierce extremes of joy and anguish may be the happiest, yet to them, I cannot fully convey the unspeakable bliss that descended upon these persecuted lovers.

To watch a beloved face return from the abyss to health and beauty, to see the roses bloom once more on a pale cheek, to hear the words of love return to the lips—this was Margaret's joy, balancing a lifetime of sorrow. For Gerard, it was awakening from a trance to find himself cradled in the arms of the woman he adored, hearing her murmur words of eloquent devotion, showering him with tears and tender kisses. Only in this sweet hour did he truly understand the depth of her love. He thanked his enemies, for they had led him here. Their arms wound around each other, and the terrors of danger and imprisonment seemed worlds away, an ancient dream. They called themselves husband and wife; were they not solemnly betrothed? Had they not stood before the altar, blessed by the Holy Church, cursed by it all who would divide them?

Yet, as no woman's nerves could withstand such terrible strain without cost, Margaret presently grew faint and sank onto Gerard's shoulder, her smile feeble, her spirit unstrung. Gerard felt immediate anxiety and sought to call for aid, but she held him fast with a gentle grasp, imploring him not to leave her side for even a moment. "As long as I may lay my hand upon you, I know you are safe," she whispered, her voice trembling. "Foolish Gerard! Nothing ails me. I am weak, dearest, but so happy!"

Now it was Gerard's turn to support that fragile head, with its waves of hair flowing loosely over his shoulder, to nurse and soothe her quivering form with soft, encouraging words and murmurs of affection. The sweetness of a woman's weakness to a manly heart knows no equal. Poor souls! They were happy. Tomorrow, however, they must part. Yet, compared to the glimpse of death they had shared, separation for a year or two was nothing. While there was life, there was hope; while there was hope, there was joy. The future shone bright, the present was heaven, and so passed these blissful hours.

Alas! Their innocence faced dangers beyond the prison walls or the grave. Now that visible peril had vanished, they were in utmost jeopardy from their own hearts and their youth, from the fires of passion and the naivety of love.
