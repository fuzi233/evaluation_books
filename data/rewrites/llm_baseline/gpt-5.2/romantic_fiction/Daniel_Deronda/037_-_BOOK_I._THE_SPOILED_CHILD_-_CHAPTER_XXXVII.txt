Deronda’s wish to be in town again had grown roots in more than one soil: the quick, anxious care he could not shake for Mirah, and the restless curiosity that Mordecai had kindled with a few words and a look. Yet his steps were checked by the simplest of hindrances—days slipping away, arrangements never quite made—until Sir Hugo had already gone up before the household, eager to be in readiness for Parliament’s opening on the sixth of February. When Deronda at last took his old quarters in Park Lane, he went in expecting only what was reasonable: that Hans Meyrick had made free with rooms left in friendly trust.

He had made free, indeed—yet not as Deronda had imagined.

The sitting-room had been turned into a painter’s den that looked less like a bachelor’s lodging than the back-room of some small Italian cookshop where everything—tools, scraps, jars, odd treasures—had been pulled out and set near at hand because life was hungry and work must be fed. Two Rome chests had been unpacked to their last wrinkled paper; plaster casts leaned their pale mouths and noses against the wall; the lower halves of the windows were muffled with baize so that the daylight came subdued, like a voice quieted for a sick-room. The very air held a mingled odor—damp wool from the baize, the sweetish bite of oil paint and turpentine, and a faint dustiness of old paper, like spice left too long in a cupboard.

And in the midst of it Hans—blond as ever, but more wildly unfinished, his hair longer, his face more whimsically puckered, his high voice lifted higher by haste and excitement—stood presiding as if the whole clutter were a banquet he had laid for his own ambition.

“I knew you would like to see my casts and antiquities,” he cried, after the first grip of hands and affectionate questions. “So I didn’t scruple to unlade my chests here. But I’ve found two rooms at Chelsea, not many hundred yards from mother and the girls—soon as they’ve scraped the walls and put in some new lights, I shall be ready to hang out there. That’s all I’m waiting for. But you see I don’t wait to begin work: you can’t conceive what a great fellow I’m going to be. The seed of immortality has sprouted within me.”

“Only a fungoid growth, I dare say—a growing disease in the lungs,” said Deronda, with the familiar brotherly irony that had always been their safe bridge.

He had moved toward some drawings propped along the ledge of his bookcases—five quick-sketched heads, all different angles of one face. He took his stand at a distance convenient for judging, and said nothing.

Hans, too, was silent a moment. He picked up palette and pencil and began adding touches to the half-finished work on his easel with that absorbed briskness which made his fingers seem to have their own mind.

“What do you think of them?” he asked at last, without looking round.

“The full face looks too massive; otherwise the likenesses are good,” Deronda returned—more coolly than his habit with Hans.

“No, it is not too massive,” said Hans decisively. “I have noted that. There is always a little surprise when one passes from the profile to the full face. But I shall enlarge her scale for Berenice. I am making a Berenice series—look at the sketches along there—and now I think of it, you are just the model I want for the Agrippa.” Then, as if he felt he had blundered, he added quickly, “No, no—I forgot; you don’t like sitting for your portrait, confound you! However, I’ve picked up a capital Titus. There are to be five in the series. The first is Berenice clasping the knees of Gessius Florus and beseeching him to spare her people; I’ve got that on the easel. Then, this—where she is standing on the Xystus with Agrippa, entreating the people not to injure themselves by resistance.”

“Agrippa’s legs will never do,” said Deronda.

“The legs are good realistically,” Hans insisted, his face creasing with droll gravity. “Public men are often shaky about the legs—‘Their legs, the emblem of their various thought,’ as somebody says in the Rehearsal.”

“But these are as impossible as the legs of Raphael’s Alcibiades.”

“Then they are good ideally,” said Hans, triumphant. “Agrippa’s legs were possibly bad; I idealize that and make them impossibly bad. Art, my Eugenius, must intensify. But never mind the legs now: the third sketch is Berenice exulting in the prospects of being Empress of Rome, when the news has come that Vespasian is declared Emperor and her lover Titus his successor.”

“You must put a scroll in her mouth, else people will not understand that. You can’t tell that in a picture.”

“It will make them feel their ignorance then—an excellent æsthetic effect,” Hans said lightly. “The fourth is Titus sending Berenice away from Rome after she has shared his palace for ten years—both reluctant, both sad—*invitus invitam*, as Suetonius hath it. I’ve found a model for the Roman brute.”

“Shall you make Berenice look fifty? She must have been that.”

“No, no; a few mature touches to show the lapse of time. Dark-eyed beauty wears well—hers particularly. But now, here is the fifth: Berenice seated lonely on the ruins of Jerusalem. That is pure imagination. That is what ought to have been—perhaps was. Now, see how I tell a pathetic negative. Nobody knows what became of her—that is finely indicated by the series coming to a close. There is no sixth picture.”

He spoke as if he were tasting his own conceit and finding it delicious. He even threw his head back with a mock-sublime frown, searching Deronda’s face for admiration.

“I break off in the Homeric style,” he went on, gasping theatrically. “The story is chipped off, so to speak, and passes with a ragged edge into nothing—*le néant*; can anything be more sublime, especially in French? The vulgar would desire to see her corpse and burial—perhaps her will read and her linen distributed. But now come and look at this on the easel. I have made some way there.”

Deronda moved nearer. The figure on the easel—Berenice in supplication—had that poignant bend of the body which is like a prayer made visible: the arms gathered their pleading around another’s knees as if clasping could keep cruelty from happening. It was the kind of posture one remembers with one’s own muscles, as if one had once knelt so.

“That beseeching attitude is really good,” Deronda said, after a moment. “You have been very industrious in the Christmas holidays; for I suppose you have taken up the subject since you came to London.”

Neither had yet spoken Mirah’s name; and Deronda felt the omission like a plate left uncovered on a table.

“No,” said Hans, touching in a line with quick certainty. “I made up my mind to the subject before. I take that lucky chance for an augury that I am going to burst on the world as a great painter. I saw a splendid woman in the Trastevere—the grandest women there are half Jewesses—and she set me hunting for a fine situation of a Jewess at Rome. Like other men of vast learning, I ended by taking what lay on the surface. I’ll show you a sketch of the Trasteverina’s head when I can lay my hands on it.”

“I should think she would be a more suitable model for Berenice,” Deronda answered, choosing his words as if they were knives he did not wish to draw blood with.

“Not a bit of it. The model ought to be the most beautiful Jewess in the world, and I have found her.”

Deronda’s throat tightened. “Have you made yourself sure that she would like to figure in that character? I should think no woman would be more abhorrent to her. Does she quite know what you are doing?”

“Certainly. I got her to throw herself precisely into this attitude. Little mother sat for Gessius Florus, and Mirah clasped her knees.”

Hans stepped back to judge the effect of his last touches, as serene as if he had said, *We had tea*.

“I dare say she knows nothing about Berenice’s history,” Deronda said, indignation rising in him faster than he could justify it.

“Oh, yes, she does—ladies’ edition,” Hans replied, with airy certainty. “Berenice was a fervid patriot, but was beguiled by love and ambition into attaching herself to the arch-enemy of her people. Whence the Nemesis. Mirah takes it as a tragic parable, and cries to think what the penitent Berenice suffered as she wandered back to Jerusalem and sat desolate amidst desolation. That was her own phrase. I couldn’t find it in my heart to tell her I invented that part of the story.”

“Show me your Trasteverina,” Deronda said, mainly to stop himself saying something harsher.

“Shall you mind turning over that folio?” Hans answered. “My studies of heads are all there. But they are in confusion. You will perhaps find her next to a crop-eared undergraduate.”

Deronda began turning the sheets. Paper whispered against paper; charcoal smudged his fingertips with a dry, soot-like stain. Face after face—quickly caught, variously loved—rose from the pages like people met in a crowd and remembered for one striking feature.

“These seem to be all Cambridge heads and bits of country,” he said, after a minute. “Perhaps I had better begin at the other end.”

“No; you’ll find her about the middle. I emptied one folio into another.”

“Is this one of your undergraduates?” Deronda asked, pausing over an unusually agreeable face—an honest brow, a mouth that looked as if it would rather laugh than sneer.

“That! Oh, that’s a man named Gascoigne—Rex Gascoigne. An uncommonly good fellow; his upper lip, too, is good. I coached him before he got his scholarship. He ought to have taken honors last Easter. But he was ill, and has had to stay up another year. I must look him up. I want to know how he’s going on.”

Deronda found the Trasteverina: a bold head, coarse in the way of robust living—strong jaw, frank mouth, a gaze made to meet sun and street noises without flinching.

“Here she is, I suppose.”

“Ah,” said Hans, glancing and shrugging with contempt. “Too coarse. I was unregenerate then.”

Deronda closed the folio. He left the Trasteverina sketch lying outside, like something exiled.

For a moment he only stood there, his hand at his coat-collar, the words he wanted to say gathering and pressing at him. Outside, the street carried its winter London sounds—wheels, hoofbeats, a call—broken and hurried; within, Hans’s brush made a soft rasp, a private industrious sound like a knife sharpening against stone. Deronda felt, with sudden vividness, how much of human peace depends on what reaches the ear: a home can be made by gentle familiar sounds, and unmade by harsh ones. The city’s roar, even muffled, seemed an enemy to the delicate new life being coaxed into safety at Chelsea.

“I dare say my scruples are excessive, Meyrick,” Deronda said at last, “but I must ask you to oblige me by giving up this notion.”

Hans flung himself into a tragic pose as if struck. “What! my series—my immortal Berenice series? Think of what you are saying, man—destroying, as Milton says, not a life but an immortality. Wait before you answer, that I may deposit the implements of my art and be ready to uproot my hair.”

He laid down palette and pencil with ceremonious care, threw himself backward into a great chair, let his long hair fall over his face, lifted his fingers like talons beside his head, and stared up in comic terror at Deronda.

Deronda could not help smiling. “Paint as many Berenices as you like, but I wish you could feel with me—perhaps you will, on reflection—that you should choose another model.”

“Why?” Hans asked, springing up, suddenly serious.

“Because she may get into such a position that her face is likely to be recognized. Mrs. Meyrick and I are anxious for her that she should be known as an admirable singer. It is right—and she wishes it—that she should make herself independent. And she has excellent chances. One good introduction is secured already, and I am going to speak to Klesmer. Her face may come to be very well known, and—well, it is useless to attempt to explain, unless you feel as I do. I believe that if Mirah saw the circumstances clearly, she would strongly object to being exhibited in this way—to allowing herself to be used as a model for a heroine of this sort.”

Hans listened with thumbs hooked in his blouse belt. Surprise spread on his face and melted into amusement, until a laugh broke out; but when he saw Deronda’s grave offence, he checked himself.

“Excuse my laughing, Deronda,” he said. “You never gave me an advantage over you before. If it had been about anything but my own pictures, I should have swallowed every word because you said it. And so you actually believe that I should get my five pictures hung on the line in a conspicuous position, and carefully studied by the public? Zounds, man! cider-cup and conceit never gave me half such a beautiful dream. My pictures are likely to remain as private as the utmost hypersensitiveness could desire.”

He turned back to his easel as if painting could plaster over an awkward pause. Deronda stood still, recognizing his own mistake about publicity and yet feeling no ease; his repugnance had roots deeper than practical fear. He had expected Hans would be trouble; he had not expected the trouble would sting him with a private soreness.

Hans went on, brushing in color, talking as he worked.

“But even supposing I had a public rushing after my pictures as if they were a railway series including nurses, babies, and bonnet-boxes, I can’t see any justice in your objection. Every painter worth remembering has painted the face he admired most, as often as he could. It is a part of his soul that goes out into his pictures. He diffuses its influence in that way. He puts what he hates into a caricature. He puts what he adores into some sacred, heroic form. If a man could paint the woman he loves a thousand times as the *Stella Maris* to put courage into the sailors on board a thousand ships, so much the more honor to her. Isn’t that better than painting a piece of staring immodesty and calling it by a worshipful name?”

“Every objection can be answered if you take broad ground enough, Hans,” Deronda said, with a peremptory edge he did not quite mean. “No special question of conduct can be properly settled in that way. I might admit all your generalities, and yet be right in saying you ought not to publish Mirah’s face as a model for Berenice. But I give up the question of publicity. I was unreasonable there.”

He paused, then went on more slowly, as if feeling his way along a narrow path.

“Still, even as a private affair, there might be good reasons for your not indulging yourself too much in painting her from the point of view you mention. You must feel that her situation at present is a very delicate one; and until she is in more independence, she should be kept as carefully as a bit of Venetian glass, for fear of shaking her out of the safe place she is lodged in. Are you quite sure of your own discretion? Excuse me, Hans. My having found her binds me to watch over her. Do you understand me?”

“Perfectly,” said Hans, turning a good-humored smile on him. “You have the very justifiable opinion of me that I am likely to shatter all the glass in my way, and break my own skull into the bargain. Quite fair. Since I got into the scrape of being born, everything I have liked best has been a scrape either for myself or somebody else. Everything I have taken to heartily has somehow turned into a scrape. My painting is the last scrape; and I shall be all my life getting out of it. You think now I shall get into a scrape at home. No; I am regenerate. You think I must be over head and ears in love with Mirah. Quite right; so I am. But you think I shall scream and plunge and spoil everything. There you are mistaken—excusably, but transcendently mistaken. I have undergone baptism by immersion. Awe takes care of me. Ask the little mother.”

“You don’t reckon a hopeless love among your scrapes, then,” Deronda said; and his voice, without his willing it, sank deeper.

“I don’t mean to call mine hopeless,” Hans returned with provoking calm, laying down his tools and stepping back as if to contemplate his picture.

“My dear fellow, you are only preparing misery for yourself,” said Deronda, decisively. “She would not marry a Christian, even if she loved him. Have you heard her—of course you have—heard her speak of her people and her religion?”

“That can’t last,” said Hans, with a quick shake of the head. “She will see no Jew who is tolerable. Every male of that race is insupportable—‘insupportably advancing’—his nose.”

“She may rejoin her family. That is what she longs for. Her mother and brother are probably strict Jews.”

“I’ll turn proselyte, if she wishes it,” Hans said, with a shrug and laugh.

“Don’t talk nonsense, Hans. I thought you professed a serious love for her,” Deronda answered, heat rising in him.

“So I do. You think it desperate, but I don’t.”

“I know nothing; I can’t tell what has happened. We must be prepared for surprises. But I can hardly imagine a greater surprise to me than that there should have seemed to be anything in Mirah’s sentiments for you to found a romantic hope on.”

He felt the contempt in his own words and disliked himself for it; yet he could not recall them.

“I don’t found my romantic hopes on a woman’s sentiments,” Hans retorted, perversely growing merrier under gravity. “I go to science and philosophy for my romance. Nature designed Mirah to fall in love with me. The amalgamation of races demands it—the mitigation of human ugliness demands it—the affinity of contrasts assures it. I am the utmost contrast to Mirah—a bleached Christian, who can’t sing two notes in tune. Who has a chance against me?”

“I see now; it was all *persiflage*. You don’t mean a word you say, Meyrick,” Deronda said, laying his hand on Hans’s shoulder, relief softening him.

“Upon my honor I do mean it, though,” Hans answered, turning and setting his left hand on Deronda’s shoulder so their eyes were close and level, as if he spoke at a confessional. “I meant to tell you as soon as you came. My mother says you are Mirah’s guardian, and she thinks herself responsible to you for every breath that falls on Mirah in her house. Well, I love her—I worship her—I won’t despair—I mean to deserve her.”

“My dear fellow, you can’t do it,” Deronda said quickly.

“I should have said, I mean to try.”

“You can’t keep your resolve, Hans. You used to resolve what you would do for your mother and sisters.”

“You have a right to reproach me, old fellow,” Hans said gently.

“Perhaps I am ungenerous,” Deronda returned—without apology in his tone. “Yet it can’t be ungenerous to warn you that you are indulging mad, Quixotic expectations.”

“Who will be hurt but myself, then?” said Hans, pushing out his lip like a boy. “I am not going to say anything to her unless I felt sure of the answer. I dare not ask the oracles: I prefer a cheerful caliginosity, as Sir Thomas Browne might say. I would rather run my chance there and lose, than be sure of winning anywhere else. And I don’t mean to swallow the poison of despair, though you are disposed to thrust it on me. I am giving up wine, so let me get a little drunk on hope and vanity.”

“With all my heart, if it will do you any good,” Deronda said, loosening Hans’s shoulder with a little push, forcing kindness into his tone.

But inwardly he was silenced.

There is a peculiar irritation that comes sometimes to the man others trust as mentor: they speak to him as if he were set on a higher shelf, outside the reach of desire and temptation—like a book too clean to have been handled. Deronda had long been indulgent to Hans’s egotism; it had been part of the boy’s charm, and Deronda had been content to be the listener. But now he had felt, with a sting he must not show, Hans’s easy assumption that for any danger of rivalry where Mirah was concerned Deronda was as impossible as an angel. It is one thing to decide to put oneself out of the question; it is another to have others do the excluding for you, without a thought.

And Hans’s hope—however irrational—raised an image of Mirah altered by that hope, changed by the very fact of being hoped for; an image Deronda rejected and yet could not keep from returning. He had expected to have to guard Mirah from the world’s roughness, from accident, from the loud vulgar press of London life; he had not expected to have to guard his own inner quiet from the sound of another man’s longing.

When he went to Chelsea, Mrs. Meyrick’s evident release from anxiety about her beloved but incalculable son ought to have made him feel easy; and in truth the little house had a homely music that always steadied him—the friendly clink of cups, the hush of sewing, the soft footsteps on the stair, the voices that did not strive to outshout one another. It was as if that modest domestic soundscape were a sheltering dialect of kindness, spoken daily without thinking, and therefore all the more powerful.

Mirah, too, seemed livelier than he had yet seen her; and for the first time Deronda saw her laugh. It was when Hans became, as ever, Mrs. Meyrick’s first subject.

Mirah wanted to know whether Deronda had seen Mr. Hans pass through a sort of character-piece without changing his dress.

“He passes from one figure to another as if he were a bit of flame where you fancied the figures without seeing them,” Mirah said, eager in her description; “he is so wonderfully quick. I used never to like comic things on the stage—they were dwelt on too long; but all in one minute Mr. Hans makes himself a blind bard, and then Rienzi addressing the Romans, and then an opera-dancer, and then a desponding young gentleman—I am sorry for them all, and yet I laugh, all in one—”

And then she gave a little laugh that might have been the bright run of a voice in song—light, shy, and suddenly present, like warmth felt through a door that has opened a crack.

“We hardly thought that Mirah could laugh till Hans came,” said Mrs. Meyrick, and Deronda saw that she too was watching the picture—watching it as one watches a hearth flame one fears may go out.

“Hans seems in great force just now,” Deronda said, in a tone meant for congratulation. “I don’t wonder at his enlivening you.”

“He’s been just perfect ever since he came back,” said Mrs. Meyrick; and she kept to herself what her heart added—*if it will but last*.

“It is a great happiness,” Mirah said softly, “to see the son and brother come into this dear home. And I hear them all talk about what they did together when they were little. That seems like heaven, and to have a mother and brother who talk in that way. I have never had it.”

“Nor I,” said Deronda, involuntarily; and the words came out with a plainness that surprised him, as if some older, half-buried part of himself had spoken in its own tongue.
