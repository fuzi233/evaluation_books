Rough travel has a charm for us English—and so do its tales, even when the wanderer is no lovelorn pilgrim. And ancient friendship holds at least the fascination of a relic. Yet the heart of this story lies in Holland, and it is time to return there, to the simple lives and quiet dramas that have filled the days of all ages.

Jorian Ketel arrived at Peter’s house to claim Margaret’s promise, but she lay ill in bed. Peter, upon hearing his demand, turned him away with harsh words, and a few bystanders even threatened to duck him in the nearest ditch. The town of Sevenbergen was aflame with sympathy for father and daughter, and in such moods, justice is swift and indiscriminate. Jorian left in bitter anger, regretting his kindness—a remorse not uncommon, and at least sincere.

Dierich Brower, caught at *The Three Kings* plying a drunkard with drink to pry loose Martin Wittenhaagen’s whereabouts, was seized and flung into a horsepond, with warnings of worse should he ever show his face in the town again. At last, roused by civic pride, the burgomaster of Sevenbergen sent a stern message to Ghysbrecht of Tergou, reminding him he had overstepped his authority and must in future address complaints through proper channels.

Ghysbrecht, swallowing his rage, replied smoothly that the man he pursued was a Tergovian, one Gerard, accused of stealing town records. Since Gerard had fled abroad—likely with the documents—the matter was closed. Yet this calm was but a mask. Foiled in Sevenbergen, he turned his spies elsewhere, seeking to learn from Gerard’s family where he had gone. To his dismay, they did not know. This gnawed at him. If Gerard lurked nearby, he might yet uncover the truth and return for vengeance. From that day, those near Ghysbrecht marked a change in him—a brooding unease, a furtive watchfulness, as if he expected a blow from the dark.

The little household at Tergou, once so harmonious, now wore the same faces but not the same spirit. Kate, knowing her brothers’ part in Gerard’s exile, shuddered at their sight, though she kept silent for fear of deepening the strife. Catherine, with a woman’s sharp eye, noted this but said nothing, watching and waiting. Cornelis and Sybrandt played their parts well, feigning sorrow to deceive their father and Giles, but their hidden satisfaction could not escape the women’s gaze—

Mistrust now sat at the table, a poor substitute for Gerard’s bright face, whose absence was felt only when he was gone.

The old hosier’s pride had been wounded by his son’s defiance, and he bore himself stiffly, refusing to speak Gerard’s name. Yet beneath the stern front, nature tugged at his heart. "If I but knew where the boy is, and that he is safe," he would sigh, "small would be my care." One could not help thinking that had Gerard walked in then, there would have been embraces, not reproaches.

The family was taken aback when Gerard’s tale spread through the town within a week. Worse still, the people’s hearts sided with the lovers, blaming the harsh parents and the meddling burgomaster.

"Mother," Kate ventured one day, "they say Margaret is down with fever—a burning fever. Her father fears for her."

"Margaret? What Margaret?" Catherine asked, with forced calm.

"Gerard’s Margaret."

Catherine’s voice rose sharp. "How dare you name that girl in this house? She is the ruin of my boy—the flower of my flock! Because of her, he is not a priest among us but a wanderer in the wide world, and I a broken-hearted mother." Then, softening: "Don’t weep, child. I do wrong to speak harshly. But oh, Kate, you cannot know a mother’s fears. I bear up by day, but at night I see him in dreams—torn by beasts, or in robbers’ hands, their knives raised over him. And all for that girl! Her very name is poison to me."

"I’ll not grieve you more, mother," Kate murmured, though her heart ached.

Yet while Margaret’s name was forbidden in one house, it was often spoken in pity elsewhere. Sevenbergen whispered of the village beauty "dying for love," though this did her little justice. Gerard was not dead, and she never doubted his faith. Her father needed her, and but for her failing strength, love would not have kept her from her duties—though she might have moved through them with a heavy heart.

The fever left her at last, but in such weakness that life seemed a burden. Love and friendship might have been healing hands, but she had to mend alone. Many days she lay weeping, listless, until one afternoon she woke to find two women in her room. One was a servant; the other, by the rich fur at her collar and the silver streak in her hair, was a lady of standing. Their faces were kind.

Margaret tried to rise, but the elder woman gently pressed her back. "Lie still, child. We come not to trouble you, but to cheer you, God willing. Now, guess who we are?"

Margaret’s eyes brightened. "You are the demoiselle Van Eyck, and this is Reicht Heynes. Gerard spoke often of you—of your goodness to him. Madam, he has no truer friend now." The thought brought fresh tears.

Reicht, tender-hearted, wept in sympathy, but her mistress chided her. "A fine nurse you make!" Then, with gentle art, she drew Margaret from her sorrow, coaxing her head onto her shoulder, stroking her hair.

"Ah, such hair!" the old lady admired.

Reicht joined in praising it, and Margaret, though frail, was not past the reach of such kindness. "Once I thought it plain," she confessed, "but he praised it, and since then I’ve been vain of it—saints forgive me."

"The greater fools are those who do not love," said the old lady briskly.

Margaret looked up, wondering.

This was but the first of many visits. Either Margaret Van Eyck or Reicht came nearly every day, and under their care, the girl grew stronger—Reicht crediting her broths, Margaret the kind words that gave her heart a reason to heal.

Meanwhile, Martin Wittenhaagen had ridden to Rotterdam, seeking the bull by the horns—the bull being Philip the Good, Duke of Burgundy. Learning the court was at Ghent, he pressed on, but found himself thwarted by lackeys and pages. At last, he waylaid the duke on a hunt, roaring for mercy.

"Why, what danger threatens?" laughed the duke.

"Grace for an old soldier hunted by burghers!"

Kings, for all their pride, often show kindness to lowly men, who neither rival nor flatter them. Philip reined in, listening as Martin recounted Gerard’s imprisonment, the escape from the tower, and the bloody chase through the woods. The tale so gripped the duke that when courtiers interrupted, he swore and threatened to lop off the next head that broke his pleasure.

"By St. Luke!" he cried when Martin finished. "Here’s sport in my own lands, and I miss it! A man-hunt! I’ve never seen one."

"My luck was poor," said Martin dryly. "I was on the wrong side of the dogs."

The duke chuckled. "What do you ask?"

"A pardon—for Gerard and me."

"For prison-breaking? Birds will fly. For striking the burgomaster? A boar turns at bay. For killing the hounds?" Here the duke frowned.

"They were not yours, sire—the ranger’s."

Philip relented. "I’ll not sacrifice an old soldier to a ranger’s dogs. You shall have your pardon—and Gerard’s. And tell that burgomaster his high-handed ways displease me. I’ll have no petty kings in Holland."

The next day, the pardon was signed. Martin returned in triumph to find Margaret sitting pale by the fire. Waving the parchment, he cried, "A free pardon! Send for Gerard when you will—no burgomaster dare touch him now!"

She trembled, reading it, kissing it, then flung her arms around Martin’s neck. When her joy had settled, she spoke of her new friend, the demoiselle Van Eyck. "I long to show her this—but I’ve not strength to walk so far."

Martin laughed. "Take my mule."

"*Your* mule?"

He admitted he had grown fond of the beast, forgetting it was Ghysbrecht’s. "Tomorrow I’ll return her—but tonight she shall bear you to Tergou."

At dusk, Margaret rode to her friend’s house, her cheeks flushed with unaccustomed color, the pardon safe in her bosom.
