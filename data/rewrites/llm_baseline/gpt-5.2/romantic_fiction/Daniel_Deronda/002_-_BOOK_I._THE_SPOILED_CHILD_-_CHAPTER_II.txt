“This man contrives a secret ’twixt us two,
That he may quell me with his meeting eyes
Like one who quells a lioness at bay.”

The letter lay on Gwendolen Harleth’s table as if it had been set there by a hand that understood how paper can weigh more than silver. She broke the seal, and the room—its pale curtains, its bright appointments, the faint perfume clinging to her traveling things—seemed to draw in its breath while she read.

“DEAREST CHILD.—I have been expecting to hear from you for a week….”

Her mother’s words hurried on, anxious, practical, trembling with the effort to keep steady. The Langens might be leaving; how could Gwendolen have let her address be uncertain? And then, the change of key—no longer a domestic fret, but the crack of a great thing broken: Grapnell & Co. had failed for a million; they were ruined, all of them. Offendene must be given up. There would be no money to send. There must be no borrowing. There was talk of tradesmen unpaid, servants’ wages, a “hut or other” to shelter them; a life, in short, suddenly pared down to bare necessity, like a household forced to live on crusts after banquets. There was even—sharpest of all—a name for the cause: Mr. Lassman’s wicked recklessness.

“Summon up your fortitude… resign ourselves… it rends my heart… it is hard…”

The signature—FANNY DAVILOW—looked like a familiar hand shaken by cold.

For a little while the first effect was not tears, not any flooding grief, but a kind of stunned resistance, as if her mind refused to admit the new texture of the world. The certainty she had carried like a birthright—that her destiny must be cushioned, that even trouble would come draped and mannered, well-fed and kept in its place—stood for an instant staring at its own eviction.

She remained motionless. Then, with a movement that was almost automatic, she tossed off her hat and looked into the glass.

Her hair was still coiled smooth and exact—ball-room perfection surviving midnight and agitation. On other nights she would have lingered over the image with that innocent pleasure youth takes in its own bloom; but now she stared past her reflection as if some ugly noise had jarred her and she waited, rigid, for its source to show itself.

She dropped into the corner of the red velvet sofa and read the letter again—then a third time, slower, deliberate, as if by pressing each sentence she might wring out its falsehood. At last it slid from her fingers to the carpet. She sat with clasped hands on her lap, very still, dry-eyed.

Her impulse was not to wail. It was to measure, to defy, to look for leverage.

“Poor mamma” did not rise in her with any tender cry. Her mother, in Gwendolen’s view, had always seemed made for anxious endurance rather than enjoyment; and self-pity—hot, immediate—had the first claim. Was she not the chief object of her mother’s dread? Was not this calamity, in its most intolerable form, aimed at her?

And then came anger—anger with a gambler’s sting in it. She had lost at roulette. If only luck had held for this one day she might have gone home with a handsome sum; she might even have stayed and won enough to keep them all. The thought flared—half hope, half rebellion. Even now, was it impossible?

In her purse were four napoleons. But there were ornaments: things that could be turned to money as easily, in these watering-places, as a ribbon is changed. It was common enough—no need to speak of shame. She had an Etruscan necklace she was not wearing; she could sell it and feel, perversely, that she had escaped the dullness of ordinary necessity by living “intensely.” With ten louis—more, perhaps—and the return of that earlier luck which had seemed so natural to her, what could be wiser than to play a few days more, and come home not helpless but triumphant? Let the rectory, the aunts, the moralists at home disapprove of the manner of getting it: the money would be there.

Yet her imagination, though it dwelt on this course and made it glitter with agreeable consequences, did not gather the blind heat of mania. She had gone to the roulette-table not because passion drove her, but because she wished to be driven—to feel what she had been told of, that intoxicating compulsion. Her mind could still set the scales even while it wished to tip them: the chance of winning allured her, and the chance of losing rose up and chilled her pride with an image of herself—stripped of resource, exposed, dependent.

For she was resolved on one point as firmly as if it were written in her bones: she would not tell the Langens of any family disaster, and she would not be beholden to their pity. If she began to part with her jewelry in a way they could observe, there would be questions, interferences, tedious compassion.

The least intolerable course—the one with the smallest risk of humiliation—was swift and simple: raise money early, tell the Langens only that her mother required her return at once, and take the train for Brussels that same evening.

So instead of going to bed she made as brilliant a light as she could, and began packing. She worked with quick diligence, folding and placing each garment as if order itself might be a kind of defense. All the while her mind was haunted by tomorrow’s scenes—explanations and farewells, then the whirling journey toward a home changed into something meaner; and, like an alternative devil whispering at her elbow, the picture of “just one more day,” and her standing again at the roulette-table.

But in that latter scene, always, inevitably, there was Deronda—present as a watcher, his look seeming to weigh and measure her with an exasperating irony. And with him came the other keen experience, welded to his presence: the moment of forsaken luck, the turn of the wheel, the sense of eyes seeing her defeated. That image helped drive her toward departure, helped her pack until changing her mind would be inconvenient.

It was past midnight when she had entered her room. Before she finished, a faint dawn stole through the white blinds, paling the candle-flames, making the gilt edges of her little possessions look suddenly cheap.

What was the use of lying down? A cold bath was refreshment enough; and if there were a slight fatigue about her eyes, she could tell herself it made her face only more touching, more interesting—like a heroine on the edge of some splendid trouble.

Before six she was completely equipped in her gray traveling dress, even to her felt hat. She meant to walk out as soon as she could be sure other ladies would be on their way to the springs. She sat sideways before the long strip of mirror between the windows and looked at herself—elbow on the chair-back, an attitude fit for a portrait.

She had a strong self-love, and it carried no inward torment with it. It was a simple delight in her fortunate self, fed daily by flattering voices and by the looking-glass’s faithful praise. And even now—at the very beginning of what she called “troubles”—she gazed until the growing light seemed to cheer her into complacency. Her lips curled into a smile that deepened, softened, asserted itself; and at last she took off her hat, leaned forward, and kissed the cold glass that had looked so warm.

How could she believe in sorrow? If it attacked her, she would crush it, defy it, or run away from it—as she had done already. Anything seemed more possible than a long, patient bearing of misery.

Madame von Langen never went out before breakfast. Gwendolen could safely end her early walk by going through the Obere Strasse where the needed shop was, sure to be open after seven. At that hour, the few eyes she might care about would be elsewhere—near the springs—or still behind bedroom doors.

And yet there was one grand hotel—the Czarina—where eyes might follow her up to Mr. Wiener’s door. Deronda’s hotel.

It was a chance to be risked. Might she not be going to buy some trifle that had struck her fancy? The implicit falsehood glided through her mind like a thin veil held up to the world. She walked on with her usual floating movement, her figure and drapery falling in those soft curves that drew attention without asking for it. She looked neither right nor left, and when she entered the shop she transacted her business with cool composure.

Little Mr. Wiener had nothing to remark—nothing he would have dared to remark—except the proud grace of her manner, and the superior size and quality of the three central turquoises in the necklace she offered him. They had belonged once to a chain that was her father’s; she had never known her father, and the ornament, in every sense, was the one she could most conveniently surrender.

It is not impossible to be superstitious and rationalizing at once. Roulette had encouraged in her a romantic superstition about chances, and at the same time a practical hardness about the sentiments that obstruct the raising of money. Her dominant regret, as the dealer counted out her sum, was that she had only nine louis to add to the four napoleons already in her purse. These jewelry men—so ready to profit by a Christian’s bad luck at play!

But she was the Langens’ guest; she had nothing to pay in their hired apartment. Thirteen louis would do more than take her home. Even if she risked three—if she yielded to the temptation of one last trial—the remaining ten would suffice, for she meant to travel on, day and night.

When she turned homeward, when she entered and seated herself in the salon to wait for the Langens and breakfast, she still wavered. She had decided to say only that her mother desired her immediate return—and to keep the moment of departure, for the present, hovering.

It was already the usual breakfast-time. She leaned back, tired and hungry, eyes shut for a moment, listening for footsteps that would be familiar. The words she might speak—words that could permit her to linger another day—were ready in her throat.

The door opened.

Not Madame or Herr von Langen, but the servant, bringing a small packet left at the door for Miss Harleth.

Gwendolen took it and, with a sudden inward start, hurried into her own room. Her face was paler now than it had been over her mother’s letter; her agitation had a new quality, sharper, personal, as if her pride had been touched by a finger.

She never quite knew what it was that foretold the contents before she opened it. She only knew that when her fingers began to tear the paper, her heart sank with certainty.

Underneath the wrapping lay the necklace—her necklace—folded in a cambric handkerchief. Inside was a scrap of torn note-paper, with penciled words in clear but rapid handwriting:

“A stranger who has found Miss Harleth’s necklace returns it to her with the hope that she will not again risk the loss of it.”

The blood rushed to her face with the heat of wounded pride. The handkerchief had a corner torn off—recklessly torn, as if to destroy a mark. And at once, without argument, the first image of “the stranger” rose and occupied her mind like a tyrant: Deronda.

He must have seen her enter the shop. He must have followed, repurchased the necklace, and sent it back. The liberty of it! The insolence! He had dared to place her in a position she loathed—helpless, obliged, preached at. And if she sent it back to him, she would have to risk being mistaken; even if she were right, she would be letting him know she had divined him, would have to meet him again with that recognition alive between them.

He knew perfectly what he was doing: entangling her in humiliation, smiling again with that quiet superiority, taking the tone of mentor over her as if she were a child caught stealing sugar.

Bitter tears of mortification rose and rolled down her cheeks. No one had ever before dared to treat her with irony and contempt.

One thing became instantly clear, as if the room itself had spoken it: she must leave this place at once. She could not go back into the public salon; she could not—above all—stand again at the gaming-table with the risk of encountering Deronda’s eyes.

A knock came at her door—importunate. Breakfast was ready.

With a passionate movement she thrust necklace, cambric, scrap of paper and all into her nécessaire, pressed her handkerchief against her face, and waited a minute, summoning back her proud self-control as one summons a servant who must not see one disordered.

When she joined the Langens, the traces of tears and fatigue that remained seemed easily accounted for by what she said at once—that she had sat up packing rather than wait for help from her friend’s maid. There were protests, as she expected: she must not travel alone; there must be some arrangement, some escort, some propriety observed.

She refused them all with quiet peremptoriness. She would be put into the ladies’ compartment and go right on. She could rest perfectly well in the train. She was afraid of nothing.

And so it happened that Gwendolen never reappeared at the roulette-table. That Thursday evening she left Leubronn for Brussels; and on Saturday morning she arrived at Offendene—the home to which she and her family were soon to say a last good-bye.
