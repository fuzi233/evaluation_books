Marianne, though initially resistant, eventually relented to her sister's gentle urging and agreed to venture out with them and Mrs. Jennings. However, she placed a firm condition on the excursion: they were to avoid social calls, limiting their outing strictly to a brief stop at Gray's in Sackville Street, where Elinor was managing the sale of some of her mother's antique jewelry.

As they approached the shop, Mrs. Jennings remembered a lingering duty to visit a neighbor down the street. Since she had no pressing business at Gray's, it was arranged that she would attend to this errand first, leaving her younger charges to conduct their trade while she returned promptly.

Inside, the atmosphere was stifling with activity. The counters were crowded, and the staff overwhelmed, forcing the Dashwood sisters to wait. They took a seat at the counter end that promised the swiftest turnover, hoping to catch the eye of the sole gentleman currently attending to a customer. This man, however, possessed a sharpness of vision and a fastidiousness of taste that rendered him entirely incapable of courtesy. For nearly twenty minutes, he scrutinized toothpick cases, debating their shapes, sizes, and ornamentations with an obsessive inventiveness, offering the sisters not a word, but merely three or four wide, blank stares. These glances, devoid of warmth, left a sting of profound insignificance upon Elinor, marking her as someone of negligible consequence despite her fashionable attire.

Marianne, shielded from the humiliation of seeing her sister's distress, remained oblivious to the scene, her mind withdrawn into a quiet interior space where she could ignore the world around her. Finally, the transaction concluded. The ivory, gold, and pearls were weighed and valued. With a final, self-satisfied look at the sisters—one that demanded rather than offered admiration—the gentleman donned his gloves with deliberate slowness and departed, radiating a smug, affected indifference.

Elinor wasted no time in resuming her business, but just as she neared completion, another gentleman stepped beside her. Turning, she met the gaze of her own brother, John Dashwood. The reunion was warm, though the setting—a bustling jewel shop—rendered their affection somewhat theatrical. John, surprisingly content to see them, treated his mother with due respect and inquiry.

"I wished greatly to call yesterday," John explained, "but we were detained by Harry's desire to see the wild beasts at Exeter Exchange. The remainder of the day was spent with Mrs. Ferrars. Harry was delighted." He mentioned his intention to visit them later that morning, noting the busy nature of arriving in town. "I am here to commission a seal for Fanny, but I hope to be introduced to Mrs. Jennings tomorrow. I've heard she is a woman of substantial means. And regarding the Middletons... you must introduce me to them. As relatives of my wife's mother, I intend to treat them with the highest respect. They are, I understand, excellent neighbors."

"Excellent indeed," Elinor replied, masking her inner unease. "Their attention to our comfort and friendship knows no bounds."

John beamed with relief at her praise. "It is exactly as it should be. People of large fortune, related to you, surely owe every civility to ensure your happiness. And yet, you are settled so comfortably in your cottage! Edward spoke so highly of it—a model establishment, he said, where you all flourish. It was a true delight to hear."

A flush of shame warmed Elinor's cheeks at her brother's casual assessment of their modesty, but she was relieved from further conversation by the arrival of Mrs. Jennings's maid, who summoned them to the carriage.

Downstairs, John accompanied them to the vehicle, where he was formally introduced to Mrs. Jennings. After promising to return the following day, he took his leave. True to his word, he visited again shortly after, bringing an apologetic letter from his sister-in-law, Fanny, claiming she was too engrossed in domestic matters to join them. Yet, Mrs. Jennings, quick-witted and perceptive, dismissed the excuse, reminding John that they were cousins and asserting her intent to visit Mrs. John Dashwood soon, bringing the girls along. John's demeanor toward them was polite, if slightly distant, and his civility toward Mrs. Jennings was exemplary. When Colonel Brandon entered, John's curiosity flared, his eyes searching for signs of wealth with a calculating intensity that suggested his politeness was contingent solely on financial status.

Half an hour later, John proposed a walk to Conduit Street to meet Sir John and Lady Middleton. The weather was fair, and Elinor accepted. Stepping outside, John's inquiries became immediate and probing.

"Who is Colonel Brandon?" he asked, his tone sharpening. "Is he a man of fortune?"

"Yes," Elinor confirmed, "he holds significant property in Dorsetshire."

"A most gentlemanlike man," John mused, turning to his sister with a conspiratorial glint. "And I must congratulate you, Elinor, on the prospect of a respectable establishment."

"My brother!" Elinor protested, amused and exasperated simultaneously. "What do you mean?"

"He appreciates you," John declared, his conviction absolute. "I watched him closely; I am certain of it. What is his annual income?"

"Estate estimates suggest roughly two thousand pounds a year."

"Two thousand!" John exclaimed, his voice rising to a crescendo of enthusiastic benevolence. "Elinor, I pray with all my soul that it were *double* that amount, for your sake!"

"You speak kindly, but I am firmly convinced Colonel Brandon harbors no thought of marrying me."

"Nonsense, Elinor! You are grievously mistaken. A trifling effort on your part would secure him. Perhaps he hesitates momentarily, perhaps his friends advise caution, or perhaps your fortune seems slight. But a lady's attentiveness, her encouragement, can overcome any obstacle. You should try for him. It is obvious there is no prior attachment on your side—in fact, the notion is absurd, the barriers insurmountable—and you possess too much sense not to recognize this. Colonel Brandon is the man; and I pledge no lack of civility to win him over to your family. It is a union destined for universal satisfaction. In short, it is a matter—" he lowered his voice to a dramatic whisper—"'that will be exceedingly welcome to *all parties*.' Though, I hasten to correct myself, I mean to say: your friends are genuinely eager to see you settled, especially Fanny, whose heart beats for your interests, and her mother, Mrs. Ferrars, who would be thrilled."

Elinor offered no response.

"It would be a delightful irony," John continued, "if Fanny and I were both securing matches simultaneously. And yet, it is not improbable."

Turning the subject abruptly, Elinor asked with resolve, "Is Mr. Edward Ferrars to be married?"

"Not definitively settled, yet there is talk," John admitted. "An excellent match, I assure you. Mrs. Ferrars, with generous spirit, will provide a settlement of a thousand pounds a year if the union proceeds. The lady is the Honorable Miss Morton, daughter of the late Lord Morton, possessing thirty thousand pounds. A connection of high quality, and I foresee no delay."

"And the expense?" Elinor pressed gently.

"The costs of establishing ourselves here are heavy," John confessed. "My father's bequest of the Stanhill effects to your mother meant we must replace the linens and china removed from Norland. Then, there is the enclosure of Norland Common, a draining project, and my recent purchase of East Kingham Farm. I felt compelled to acquire it, adjacent to my lands, to preserve the estate's integrity. It has cost me a fortune."

"You purchased it above its intrinsic value," Elinor noted softly.

"Why, I trust not! I could have sold it the next day for a profit. But the stock market was volatile; had I not held the funds in hand, I would have suffered a catastrophic loss. Other expenses, too, have arisen since moving to Norland. My father left the remaining effects to your mother, obliging us to replenish the household goods. Consider, after all these drains, how far from riches we remain, and how vital Mrs. Ferrars's kindness becomes."

"Certainly," Elinor agreed. "With her support, I hope you will achieve ease."

"One or two years may alter things significantly," John replied gravely. "Yet, much remains undone. Not a stone is laid for Fanny's greenhouse, and the flower garden exists only on paper."

"Where will the greenhouse stand?"

"On the knoll behind the house. The ancient walnuts fell to clear the path. It will be a striking feature, viewed from across the park, with gardens sloping gracefully below."

Elinor suppressed her criticism and pity, grateful that Marianne was absent to witness John's grandiose visions. Having exhausted his list of grievances, John's mood lifted. He turned to compliment Elinor on her friendship with Mrs. Jennings.

"She is a woman of immense value," John observed. "Her lifestyle speaks of ample income. This acquaintance has already benefited you and promises to prove advantageous in the long run. Her invitation to town is a gesture of high regard; when she passes, you will surely inherit her estate."

"Unlikely," Elinor countered. "She leaves only her jointure to her daughters."
