A fortnight slipped by, sharp with the first frosty bite of late autumn, when a pale, stooped figure in a threadbare cleric’s cloak limped through the heavy oak gates of the Dominican convent huddled on Gouda’s mist-fringed suburban edge. He asked the porter for audience with Brother Ambrose, the convent’s acting head: the old prior had died only weeks prior, and the prelate appointed to succeed him had not yet arrived to take up his post.
That ailing visitor was Gerard. He had come full circle, to lay down what remained of his days in the quiet order he had once dreamed of as a boy, long before years of wandering, loss, and grief wore him thin. He formally presented himself to enter as a novice on standard probation, but no one who looked at his sunken cheeks, or the tight hollow cough that shook him unannounced, could miss the hard truth: he was a failing man, and he knew it. He had come here to die in peace, within reach of the kind, gentle Ambrose his oldest friend, and the rest of the monks who had been welcome at his hearth, his larder, and his open heart for decades. The empty vicarage he had left behind was a torture he could bear no longer—every beam, every windowsill, every climbing rose by the threshold was stitched through with memories of her.
Ambrose, who knew full well both Gerard’s worth and the quiet, unshakable sorrow that was eroding him faster than any bodily ailment, clung to a soft, secret hope that he might nurse his friend back to strength and restore him to the parish that loved him. To that end, he assigned Gerard a bright, draft-free cell set above the convent’s main arched gateway, with a clear view over the flat green meadows stretching down to the river, and strictly forbade him from joining the other novices in fasts or harsh bodily austerities, ordering the lay brothers to bring him rich broth, warm buttered bread, and spiced cider each day.
But barely a handful of days later, the new prior arrived, and he proved a hard, unyielding man of the sort that prized rigid discipline above all mercy. For the first weeks he threw himself entirely into rooting out small, long-tolerated laxities in the convent’s routines, tightening rules for every brother under his roof, until word reached him that the former vicar of Gouda had joined the house as a novice. His lip curled. “That will not do. Let him first formally renounce his vicar’s post, or leave these walls. I will not have soft, overfed parsons lingering in my house taking ease no other brother is permitted.”
He sent a lay brother to summon Gerard to his quarters at once, and Gerard came, his steps slow, leaning a little on a gnarled oak staff. The moment the two men crossed the threshold and laid eyes on one another, both froze, their faces blanching with unnameable shock.
“Clement!”
“Jerome!”
