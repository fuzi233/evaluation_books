Despite the previous friction between my Lord Castlewood and Harry, the two men had reconciled, meeting with a cordiality that belied their past. George Warrington, finding in his cousin a willing patron, eagerly accepted the promise of assistance in securing a position within the House of Lords. This offer was particularly appealing to George, who had grown increasingly weary of the stifling atmosphere of Virginia. There, he felt less a master and more a subject, bound by the rigid hierarchy of his mother's household. The prospect of returning to a life of tedious subordination, marked by petty jealousies and domestic friction, held no allure for him. In contrast, London offered him the freedom he craved, a society that matched his wit, and the potential for distinction. He preferred the uncertainty of poverty in England to the certain weariness of his plantation home.

My Lord Castlewood, in turn, proved surprisingly gracious upon learning of George's engagement to Miss Lambert. He visited the Lamberts with such charm and wit that he effectively erased his notorious reputation in their eyes. To the good-hearted Mrs. Lambert and her family, he appeared as a man transformed, leaving his worldliness behind like a heavy cloak until he departed. He spoke openly of his admiration for Miss Theo, claiming it was a genuine sentiment, albeit one that did not trouble his sleep or threaten to break his heart. Yet, in a moment of vulnerability, he confessed that he wished he had met such an innocent soul in his youth, suggesting that her purity might have spared him from the harms of his own nature. When Mrs. Lambert gently urged him that it was never too late to repent, he responded with a melancholy grace, acknowledging the weight of his past while expressing a profound, if distant, respect for her virtue. He saw himself as a man bound by fate, unable to change his nature, yet he found comfort in the kindness of those who remained unaware of his true depths.

While the Lambert family viewed him with hopeful charity, seeing in him a sinner capable of redemption, the reality of his character remained complex. Their kindness, however, did no harm, and an intimacy developed between the households. My Lord Castlewood even hinted at future ecclesiastical opportunities for the Lambert family, hoping to secure a living for their son, thereby cementing the alliance. The ladies of Castlewood became frequent visitors to the Lamberts, and Mrs. Lambert, perhaps influenced by worldly considerations regarding her son's future, welcomed the connection with open arms.

Meanwhile, the machinations of Mrs. Lambert regarding her other grandchildren continued unabated. With Harry away in the army, her sights turned to Jack and Hetty, and she actively encouraged George to consider Miss Lydia Van den Bosch. Despite George's firm declaration of his love for Theo, Mrs. Lambert pressed the advantages of Lydia's beauty and immense fortune, dismissing George's loyalty as mere obstinacy. She argued that Lydia was superior in every regard—richer, handsomer, and cleverer—and that George should not be jealous of these attributes in another. When George retorted that he would not trade his Theo for any combination of wealth and beauty, Mrs. Lambert laughed it off, convinced that her plans would eventually prevail.

The scene shifted to the Van den Bosch household, where the dynamics were equally intricate. Mr. Van den Bosch, a wealthy merchant of Dutch extraction, had initially kept his granddaughter, Lydia, in a narrow circle of city acquaintances. However, upon her return from school, he resolved to indulge her, moving to a grander residence in Bloomsbury and allowing her to command a carriage and social circle of her own choosing. Lydia, a spirited and somewhat spoiled young woman, quickly adapted to her new status, treating the city with the confidence of a queen. She frequented the homes of the wealthy and the theaters, her movements watched with a mixture of amusement and concern by Theo and Hetty.

It was in the privacy of the Van den Bosch home that Lydia revealed her true feelings to George. When they were alone, she spoke with an artless frankness that startled him. She confessed her admiration for his wit and charm, contrasting her own "country-bred" honesty with what she perceived as the calculated hypocrisy of English girls. She admitted that she found his sarcasm and cleverness intoxicating, wondering if she might have been his first choice had she met him sooner. Yet, in a sudden burst of unguarded emotion, she declared that she hated Miss Theo and wished her dead, fearing that if George knew her true thoughts, he would reject her. She begged him to keep this secret, promising that she would be a fool to speak such things, yet unable to contain them.

George, true to his nature, did not reveal Lydia's confession to Miss Lambert. He recognized the innocence of her outburst, understanding that it stemmed from a warm, unformed heart rather than malicious intent. He treated her with increased courtesy and kindness, repaying her artless affection with a gentle gratitude. He remained unaware, however, of the deeper currents swirling around him. Rumors circulated of boxing matches between suitors like Mr. Lutestring and Mr. Drabshaw, and of shifting alliances involving Madame de Bernstein. George walked through Kensington Gardens, observing the chariots of the Van den Boschs visiting Lady Castlewood, oblivious to the fact that he was merely a minor player in a larger drama. He wondered if he had made an impression on Lydia's heart, unaware that he was simply one of many admirers, a Rosencrantz or Guildenstern in a tragedy written by forces beyond his comprehension. The sun shone for everyone, and the flowers bloomed for all, indifferent to whether George understood the full scope of the comedy unfolding around him.
