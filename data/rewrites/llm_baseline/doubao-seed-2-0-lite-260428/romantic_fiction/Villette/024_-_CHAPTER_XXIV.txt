Those who live walled away from the rush of the wider world—schoolgirls tucked behind high iron gates, recluses of quiet ordered lives—can slip unawares right out of the memory of their friends who move in freer, faster lanes outside. Without warning, often right after a stretch of bright, frequent meetings, a string of small exciting moments that you thought would only draw you closer, a hush falls. A wordless, unaccountable silence settles, a long blank stretch of nothing. No letters come, no unexpected knocks at the door, no little books, pressed flowers or small tokens that used to mark someone was thinking of you. The gap never makes itself clear, never offers an explanation.

If the quiet, solitary soul waiting there is wise, they tuck their restlessness out of sight, lock their aching hopes away through these weeks of inner winter. They learn to be like the dormouse, tucking themselves neatly into a small warm nook, letting the drift of life pile up over them, holding still through the cold season. It will pass, they tell themselves, or if it does not, if the frost seeps so deep it never thaws, that is no more than mortal life promised anyway.

The seven weeks that followed that bright, fateful night at the theatre were as bare and blank as seven unmarked sheets of paper, not a single word or visit or small gift scrawled across any of them. Halfway through, my fraying nerves began to spin dark fancies: what if something terrible had happened to the Brettons, to the man whose memory had lit up my quiet days? The long strain of waiting turned nights cruel, sleep slipping away from me, chased by bad dreams full of calamity, the sick cold dread that I had been abandoned entirely. I clung on as best I could, but I grew pale, and thin, and worn.

By the end of those seven long weeks, I finally accepted the silence as fate, not a wrong done to me. I busied my hands with elaborate lacework, pored over German textbooks, waded through the driest, heaviest tomes the school library held, but all of it felt like gnawing on a file to fill my hunger, drinking brine to quench my thirst. The hour the postman knocked was my worst torture: I would wait for that familiar ring, my chest tight with painful expectation, then slump in hollow disappointment when no letter came. I was no better than a caged creature, half-starved, waiting for the scrap of comfort that never came. Those seven weeks, despair breathed cold and sharp right through me, so close it made my heart stutter under its weight. The letter I ached for, the one I thought held all the sweetness left in my life, never arrived.

In the depths of that longing, I returned again and again to the small packet tucked in my case: the five letters I had hoarded, the five little stars that had lit up one perfect month. I only dared read them after dark, too shy to beg the kitchen for extra candles, so I bought my own little wax taper and matches, slipping up to the dormitory after the study hour to feast on that thin, ghostly bread. It never nourished me. I pined on it, growing thin as shadow, though I was never properly ill.

One night, I sat up late poring over those over-read letters, their ink fading to nothing in my sight, my gold crumbling to dead leaves right before my eyes, when I heard quick, tripping footsteps running up the stairs. It was Ginevra Fanshawe, back from a dinner in town, coming to stow her shawl in the wardrobe. I barely had time to tuck my precious letters back into their case and lock it before she was at my side, her bright silk dress askew, her curls drooping damp with the night rain, her mood sour.

“Stupidest evening I ever spent,” she huffed, dropping onto my bed without invitation. “Those people are dreadful.”
“Not Mrs. Cholmondeley’s, then? I thought you loved her parties.”
“I didn’t go there. My uncle de Bassompierre has arrived.”
“Your uncle? I thought you were fond of him.”
“Fond of him? He’s horrid, a great surly bear. He’s English really, you know, just changed his name a few years back when his foreign mother’s family left him a title and estates. Mamma says he killed my aunt Ginevra with his cruelty. Fancy walking into a room all alone, and this great fifty year old man turns his back on me right after we say hello and storms out. They all say I look just like the aunt he treated so badly, I suppose his conscience pricks him.”
“Were there no other visitors?”
“Only his silly spoiled little daughter, missy. She was nearly crushed to jelly in that silly theatre hubbub weeks ago, you know.”
“Ah. Do they live in the big hotel on Rue Crécy?”
“Wait—how do you know that? Oh, right, you’ve been there. The Brettons, of course, Mother Bretton and her son John have free run of the place. That’s who was there tonight, too. John Bretton tended the little girl after her accident, and now they’re all thick as thieves over old times. The little girl got to play hostess, and all the attention that should have been on me was lavished on her. And John—great lout—was being all attentive to her, frowning at excitement and making her lie on the couch. Ugh, it was sickening.”

My temper snapped. “You are lying about him. I’ve had quite enough of your nonsense. Get off my bed and leave this room at once.”

Ginevra cackled, teasing me about how red my face had gone, how cross I got over “gros Jean”, the big oaf. Too proud to rise to her petty taunts, I blew out my taper, locked my bureau, and walked out, leaving her to her sour little vapours.

The next day was Thursday, our half-holiday. The dreaded post hour drew near, and I waited in the first classroom, feeling as tense as someone waiting to see a ghost. The east winter wind howled outside, sharp enough to make every ache sharper, every sorrow heavier. I could not bear to hear the postman’s ring only to face the hollow silence of no letter for me, so I slipped out of the room without my bonnet, ran down to the end of the bare winter garden, and hid among the stripped shrubs, muffling my ears in my apron to block out that cruel clang.

When I dared slip back inside, the classroom was still empty, no pupils yet arrived. On my black desk sat a small flat white letter. The post had come and gone, and I had not heard a thing. Rosine had slipped in and left it there. My heart soared—at last, the letter I had waited seven long weeks for! I stumbled close, only for my hope to crumble: the handwriting was not the firm, bold masculine script I ached for, but a pale, looping female scrawl. The disappointment was so sharp I spoke out loud, unthinking: “This is cruel.”

But I picked it up anyway, and soon recognised the familiar hand of Mrs. Bretton. The letter was dated La Terrasse, and it ran:
> DEAR LUCY,—I’ve been wondering what on earth you’ve been keeping busy with these last few months! I am sure you’ve been as happy and occupied as we have here. Graham’s medical practice is growing by leaps and bounds, everyone in Villette is begging to see him, though I tease him constantly to keep his head from getting too big. He is such a good, dear boy, though—after rushing all day from patient to patient, dealing with every sort of temper and grief, he comes home to me every night as warm and gentle as ever.
> I’ve been busy with old business, trying to win back some of the fortune his father left him for him, though he laughs at my fuss, teases me about wanting sky-blue turbans and diamond jewelry and a grand hotel of my own. Speaking of that turban, you missed the funniest sight last week! Graham came home so tired he fell asleep in my chair, and I snuck that great blue turban of mine onto his head, and draped my best cashmere shawl over his shoulders. He looked like the handsomest pasha you ever saw! He woke up and caught a glimpse of himself in the mirror above the fireplace, and now he swears he will take revenge on me for the prank.
> That brings me to the point of my note: I know you have a half-holiday today. Be ready by five o’clock, and my carriage will come to fetch you out to La Terrasse. Be sure to come—you might meet an old acquaintance you haven’t seen in years. Good-by, my dear, quiet little god-daughter.
> Yours truly,

That letter softened every sharp edge of my grief. My friends were well, they had not forgotten me, the seven weeks of silence had been nothing but the rush of their busy lives. By late afternoon, the sky had turned thick and heavy with snow, a great wild white tempest blowing in from the north, and I was sure no carriage could make it through. But Mrs. Bretton never broke a promise. At six o’clock, the carriage pulled through the drifts, and I was lifted over the snow-blocked steps of the chateau, and carried inside to La Terrasse.

The drawing room was so warm and bright, it felt like stepping into summer. Mrs. Bretton’s warm kiss, the great crimson fire blazing in the hearth, the soft plush carpets and velvet curtains made the cold school rooms I lived in seem like a distant, grey dream. She fussed over me, chiding me for growing too thin, then declared the snow wind had messed my hair, and sent me up to my old little sea-green bedroom to fix myself.

My room had a bright fire burning too, tall wax candles lit on either side of the great mirror. Between those two flames, before the looking glass, something was dressing itself: a small, slight, airy figure, all in white, dotted with tiny scarlet drops, a red sash at its waist, a little wreath of glossy evergreen in its dark curls. For a second I thought of Graham’s old spectral illusions, but there was nothing frightening here. The figure turned, and a pair of large dark eyes under long, soft lashes fixed on me.

“Ah! You are come,” she breathed, in a soft quiet voice, smiling slow and warm.

I stared, recognising that fine, delicate face. “Miss de Bassompierre,” I said, at once.

“No,” she said, shaking her head, “not Miss de Bassompierre to you. You have forgotten that once, long ago at Bretton, I sat on your knee, cried to you when I was a lonely little girl, and you let me share your pillow? You don’t remember little Polly Home?”

All the pieces clicked into place. It was her. The tiny, serious child I had known all those years ago, grown into this soft, lovely young woman.

“I am Paulina Mary Home de Bassompierre,” she said, and her smile lit up the whole warm candlelit room.
