"Good evening," I began, addressing my audience with the practiced ease of one recounting a tale of antiquarian curiosity. "Some time ago, I shared with you the harrowing adventures of a friend named Dennistoun, a collector of rare manuscripts for Cambridge. Upon his return, he kept his findings close, yet whispers inevitably reached the ears of his peers. One such peer was a gentleman presiding over an art museum at another university—a man whose professional obsessions mirrored Dennistoun's, making him acutely sensitive to the supernatural implications of such stories. For this man, the prospect of acquiring ancient manuscripts was a distant nightmare reserved for the Shelburnian Library; his domain was the pragmatic expansion of English topographical collections. Yet, even within this seemingly mundane sphere of maps and etchings, darkness lurked, and Mr. Williams, the museum's curator, was soon drawn into its grasp."

Mr. Williams was a man of routine, accustomed to the predictable rhythms of academic administration. His primary supplier was Mr. J.W. Britnell, a London dealer whose catalogs served as the definitive lexicon for collectors of old engravings and architectural sketches. While Britnell's stock was vast, Williams purchased sparingly, seeking only to fill specific gaps in his extensive holdings rather than chasing rarities. It was in the quiet hum of February that a catalog from Britnell arrived, accompanied by a terse typed note requesting approval for Item No. 978.

Williams glanced at the entry with mild indifference. *Unknown artist. Mezzotint: View of a manor-house, early part of the century. 15 by 10 inches; black frame. Price: Two guineas.* The valuation struck him as absurdly high for what appeared to be a mediocre print. Nevertheless, adhering to protocol, he requested the item on approval alongside several other pieces.

The package arrived via the Saturday afternoon post, delivered to the museum after Williams had departed for the day. An attendant transported it to his college quarters, intending for Williams to inspect it before the weekend concluded. When Williams returned to his rooms, accompanied by a colleague, the contents awaited inspection. Among the various prints, the mezzotint commanded attention. It depicted a modest manor house of the previous century, characterized by three rows of sashed windows, rusticated stone detailing, and a central portico flanked by trees and a sprawling lawn. The plate bore the inscription *A.W.F. sculpsit*, lending it the air of an amateur's work. The price tag of two guineas remained inexplicable, prompting Williams to flip the frame over. A paper label clung to the reverse, partially torn away, revealing only fragments: *...ngley Hall* and *...ssex*.

"It is merely a matter of identification," Williams muttered, resolving to consult a gazetteer and return the item with a polite rebuke regarding the dealer's judgment.

He lit his candles, prepared tea, and joined his companion, Professor Binks, who had recently finished a game of golf. Their conversation drifted through the technicalities of the sport until Binks, noticing the framed print, asked, "What is this place, Williams?"

"Just what I intend to determine," Williams replied, reaching for a reference book. "Somethingley Hall, located in either Sussex or Essex. Half the name is missing. Do you recognize it?"

Binks examined the image. "From Britnell, I assume. Is it for the museum?"

"If the price were five shillings, I would purchase it instantly," Williams admitted. "But for two guineas? It is baffling. The composition is wretched, lacking any figures to animate the scene."

"The lighting is actually quite effective," Binks countered. "And I thought I saw a figure, perhaps just on the edge."

Williams leaned closer. "Indeed. There, barely visible on the extreme periphery, is a dark shape. A head, heavily muffled, facing the house."

Williams had missed it initially. "Admittedly, the craftsmanship is superior to my initial assessment," he conceded, though he maintained his stance against spending museum funds on an unidentified location.

Binks eventually departed for his duties. Alone once more, Williams struggled to decipher the truncated name, frustrated by the gazetteer's lack of an index for suffixes. By seven o'clock, the hall session commenced, filled with the banter of fellow academics discussing their afternoon rounds. Following dinner, the common room buzzed with activity. As the evening wore on, guests retreated to Williams's quarters. Amidst the smoke of tobacco and the clinking of glasses, Williams casually handed the mezzotint to a guest with a passing interest in art.

"It possesses a distinct romantic atmosphere," the guest remarked, examining the print with genuine appreciation. "The chiaroscuro is masterfully handled, and the figure, though slightly grotesque, adds a layer of profound unease."

"Yes, doesn't it?" Williams responded, distracted by serving drinks.

By midnight, the visitors had dispersed. Williams cleared his desk, extinguished his lamp, and blew out the candle in his bedroom. As he settled into bed, the mezzotint lay face-up on the table, illuminated by the dying glow of the hallway. Suddenly, a detail seized his vision, causing him to nearly drop the candlestick. Had he been plunged into total darkness, he feared a seizure would have overtaken him. Instead, he reignited the flame and scrutinized the image. The alteration was undeniable, bordering on the impossible yet absolute in its certainty. In the center of the lawn, where the scene had previously been empty, a figure now crawled on all fours toward the manor. Clad in a tattered black garment bearing a stark white cross on its back, the entity moved with a disturbing, mechanical precision.

Williams did not hesitate. He grabbed the frame, carried it to a secondary suite of rooms, secured it in a locked drawer, barred the doors, and retired to bed. Before sleep claimed him, he drafted a sworn account of the phenomenon, noting that the consistency of the visual evidence—witnessed by the previous guest—rendered madness an untenable explanation.

The following morning, he summoned his neighbor, Nisbet. Breakfast proceeded with the usual collegiate chatter, ranging from lawn tennis to local politics, though Williams's demeanor betrayed a simmering anxiety. Once the coffee was consumed, Williams produced the mezzotint, still face-down, and placed it in Nisbet's hands.

"Nisbet," he instructed, his voice trembling slightly, "describe exactly what you see. Minutely."

"A rural estate, presumably English, viewed under moonlight," Nisbet observed. "The moon appears waning, obscured by scattered clouds."

"There was no moon last night," Williams interjected sharply.

Continuing, Nisbet noted the architectural details: three rows of windows, a central porch replacing the lower-middle pane. "However, regarding figures..."

"Are there none?" Williams demanded.

"Not a single one," Nisbet confirmed. "Except for one discrepancy."

"What?"

"One of the ground-floor windows, to the left of the door, is open."

Williams rushed to verify the claim. The observation was accurate. The window stood ajar, inviting intrusion, while the lawn remained devoid of the crawling specter. Williams quickly drafted two documents: Nisbet's fresh description and his own record of the previous night's discovery.

"We are witnessing the unfolding of a tragedy," Nisbet murmured, studying the print. "The question remains whether the event has occurred or is imminent. Someone has entered. If my instincts serve me correctly, there is chaos awaiting us upstairs."

"I shall take the print to Old Green," Williams decided. "As former Bursar, he knows the properties of Essex and Sussex intimately."

"Green is in Brighton," Nisbet corrected gently. "But I can photograph the print immediately. Keep it safe while I arrange the exposure."

Agreeing, Williams resolved to visit Garwood, the previous night's guest, to corroborate the sighting. As he prepared to depart, a chilling realization struck him: the price of two guineas, once deemed excessive, suddenly seemed justified by the value of the information concealed within the ink.
