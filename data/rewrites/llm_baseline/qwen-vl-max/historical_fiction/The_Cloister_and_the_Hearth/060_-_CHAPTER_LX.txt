Fra Colonna had free access to the Pope’s library, and on occasion he would finish his work at the same hour as Gerard, walking through the city together. On these excursions, the young artist saw Rome in all its grandeur—its churches, palaces, and ancient ruins bathed in golden light, each stone whispering of empires long past. To Gerard, every street was a poem, every archway a portal into history.

But Fra Colonna, ever the scholar, tempered this awe with sober reflection. “This place,” he said, gesturing around, “is not Rome. It is the tomb of Rome.”

He pointed out how the old triumphal arches sank twenty or thirty feet beneath the modern streets, how palaces and columns lay buried under footpaths and homes. The narrow boundaries of today’s city, he noted, were dwarfed by the colossal remnants that still rose from the ground—the Temple of Peace, Monte Savello, once the Theatre of Marcellus, and Testacio, a hill formed from centuries of discarded pottery and household waste. “The women of old Rome threw their broken pots here,” he mused, “and lo—a mountain.”

“*Ex pede Herculem; ex ungue leonem*,” he quoted. “From the foot, we know the giant; from the claw, the lion.”

Gerard listened, captivated, though when the friar began to suggest that the moral greatness of pagan Rome matched its physical might, he hesitated. “Then has the world lost by Christ’s coming?” he ventured, flushing slightly—aware he was questioning his benefactor.

“Saints forbid!” replied the friar. “That would be heresy.” Yet even as he said it, he deftly sidestepped the truth with subtle language, circling toward forbidden ground like a man creeping up a spiral staircase.

They came upon a church where a crowd gathered in the porch. Inside, a demon was being exorcised. At such moments, Fra Colonna would emit a soft, sorrowful moan—one that carried more than disapproval: it spoke of pity for human credulity, for the blindness that clung to superstition even in the face of reason.

He sighed now. “Come, let us go.”

“No, father, please! I’ve never seen a devil cast out!”

Reluctantly, the friar followed Gerard inside. There, they found a man bound on his knees before the altar, a scarf tied tightly around his neck, held like a dog on a leash by the priest who conducted the rite.

Few people were present—fear had spread that the last exorcism had ended badly: “as a rabbit driven from one burrow runs to another.”

When Gerard and the friar arrived, the priest seemed satisfied with the audience. He turned to the possessed man, breviary in hand, and began to call forth the spirit.

“Come out, Ashtaroth. Oho! Not you. Come out, Belial. Come out, Tatzi. Come out, Eza. No—he trembles not. Come out, Azymoth. Come out, Feriander. Come out, Foletho. Come out, Astyma. Come out, Nebul. Aha! Found you, you serpent! At your old tricks. Let us pray!”

“Oh, Lord, we beseech You to drive the foul fiend Nebul from this Your creature—out of his hair, eyes, nose, mouth, ears, gums, teeth, shoulders, arms, legs, loins, stomach, bowels, thighs, knees, calves, feet, ankles, fingernails, toenails—and soul. Amen.”

The priest rose, turned to the crowd, and said with quiet amusement, “Gentlemen, we have here as stubborn a devil as you may see in a summer’s day.”

Then, facing the man again, he addressed him with fierce authority—sometimes speaking to the man, sometimes to the fiend. The answers came through the same mouth: denials of faith, complaints of inner torment.

It was the priest who first blurred the line between victim and villain. He struck the man hard, kicked him, spat repeatedly in his face. Then he lit a candle, held it until it burned his fingers, dropped it hastily. Next, he displayed the Host, showing the sacred body to the afflicted man. He burned another candle, more carefully this time. Then, suddenly gentle, he spoke kindly to the man in his human form, dismissed him, and received the thanks of the onlookers.

“Good father,” said Gerard, “you know their names so well. Our northern priests know little of the infernal host.”

“Ay, young man,” replied the priest. “Here we know them all—their names, their ways. This Nebul is a hard one to banish.”

He then recounted several of his own exorcisms, ending with one from the previous day: a great, hulking fiend expelled through a woman’s mouth, leaving behind nails, pins, and a tuft of hair. The demon cried out in anguish: *“It is not you who conquer me. See that stone on the windowsill. Know that the angel Gabriel once alighted there—’tis that which has undone me.”*

Fra Colonna moaned. “And you believed him?”

“Certainly! Who but an infidel would doubt such a precise revelation?”

“You believe the father of lies? That is beyond belief.”

“Oh, a liar does not always lie.”

“Yes, he does—especially when he tells an improbable tale and shows you a holy relic, arming you against the Satanic host. Fiends, if any, are not so simple. You should have answered him with antiquity: *‘I fear the Greeks even when bearing gifts.’*”

“A rogue chopped his wife’s head off on that stone,” said the friar sharply. “Take my word for it.” And he pulled Gerard away.

“Alack, father,” said Gerard, “you embarrassed the good priest.”

“By Pollux,” chuckled the friar, “I blistered him with a single touch of Socratic interrogation. What modern can resist the weapons of ancient wisdom?”

One afternoon, after finishing his work, Gerard was summoned to the Palace Cesarini by a fine lackey. He was ushered into a noble chamber where a girl sat embroidering tapestry. She rose and left, saying she would inform her mistress.

Gerard waited over an hour in the silent room, growing restless. “These nobles care nothing for a poor man’s time,” he muttered. Just as he decided to leave, the door opened, and a magnificent beauty entered, followed by two maids. It was the young Princess Cesarini.

She spoke loudly and haughtily to her attendants, but upon seeing Gerard, softened her tone. “Are you the writer, messer?”

“I am, signora.”

“Good.”

She seated herself on a high-backed chair—almost a throne—while Gerard and the maids stood.

“What is your name, young man?”

“Gerard, signora.”

“Gerard? By Bacchus, is that the name of a human being?”

“It is a Dutch name, signora. I was born in Tergou, Holland.”

“A harsh name for such a handsome youth,” she said to her maids, who nodded eagerly.

“What did I send for him for?” she asked, half-languid, half-ironic. “Ah, yes—I remember. Be seated, Ser Gerardo. Write me a letter to Ercole Orsini, my lover—at least, he says so.”

Gerard sat, took out paper and ink, and looked up expectantly.

She gazed down at him, her eyes searching. “Well, Gerardo?”

“I am ready, your excellence.”

“Write, then.”

“I await your words.”

“And who, think you, will provide them?”

“Who but your grace? It is your letter.”

“Gramercy! Do you writers not find the words? What use is your art without them? I suspect you’re an impostor.”

“No, signora. I could transcribe your speech into proper grammar and writing. But I cannot read your silence. Speak what is in your heart, and I shall put it on paper before your eyes.”

“But there is nothing in my heart. Sometimes I think I have no heart at all.”

“What is in your mind, then?”

“Nothing. Nor in my head.”

“Then why write at all?”

“Why, indeed? That is the first sensible thing either of us has said, Gerardo. Pestilence take him! Why doesn’t he write first? Then I could say ‘no’ to this and ‘yes’ to that—without headache. Is it not a lady’s part to speak first?”

“No, signora. The last.”

“Well spoken, Gerardo. Ha! ha! You shall have a gold piece for your wit. Give me my purse!” And she paid him immediately, in the fashion of the age. Money never cooled zeal. Gerard, having earned a gold coin so easily, felt compelled to help her out.

“Signorina,” he said, “these things are difficult only because people try too hard. They make them artificial. Imagine the gentleman you love—”

“I do not love him.”

“Well, then, the gentleman you do not love—seated here at this table. Dictate to me what you would say to him.”

“If he were here,” she said, “I’d say, ‘Go away.’”

Gerard, who had been preparing his pen, laid it down with a sigh.

“And when he was gone,” added Floretta, “your highness would say, ‘Come back.’”

“Likely enough, wench. Now, silence. Let me think. He begged me to write, and I promised—my honor is engaged. What lie shall I tell Gerardo to tell the fool?” She turned her head away, chin resting on her clenched hand, deep in thought.

She was so beautiful, so statuesque, so inspired—yet crafting a falsehood—that Gerard forgot everything except art. He began to sketch her profile with feverish intensity.

He was nearly finished when she turned abruptly.

“Nay, signora,” he said, a little impatient, “for Heaven’s sake, don’t move—your pose was perfect. Look, you’re almost done.”

All eyes turned to the drawing. “How lifelike! Done in a minute! Though perhaps her chin isn’t quite right.”

“Oh, a touch will fix it.”

“What a shame it’s not colored. I’m all for color. Black and white? Bah! And her skin—so fair. Take away her skin, and half her beauty vanishes.”

“Peace. Can you paint, Ser Gerardo?”

“Aye, signorina. I’m no master at oils—Pietro shines there—but in small works, I can tint you to life—if you have time to spare.”

“Call this vanity? And time? It hangs on me like lead. Send for your colors—now—this very moment—for the love of all saints!”

“Nay, signorina, I must prepare them. I could come later.”

“So be it. And you, Floretta, see that he is admitted at all hours. Alack! Leave my head! Leave my head!”

“I beg pardon, signora. I thought to prepare it at home for coloring. But I’ll leave it. Now, let us finish the letter.”

“What letter?”

“To Signor Orsini.”

“And shall I waste my time writing letters—to that empty creature, whom I regard as cold as the moon? Nay, not indifferent—I’ve just discovered my true feeling. I hate him. I despise him. Girls, I hereby forbid you to mention his name again—or I’ll whip you till blood flows. You know how I strike when angered.”

“We do. We do.”

“Then provoke me not,” she snapped, her eye flashing like a blade. Then, instantly sweet, she turned to Gerard. “Addio, il Gerardo.” And he bowed himself out of the velvet tiger’s den.

The next day, he returned to color her portrait. Soon he was commissioned to paint her full-length, then a large-scale likeness. He was granted two hours daily to draw and paint her—her beauty and vanity vast, her admirers many. Thus, Gerard found a new and profitable source of income.

Margaret seemed closer than ever.

It was Holy Thursday. No work was done. Fra Colonna and Gerard sat in a window, watching the religious processions. The number and fervor of the crowds stirred Gerard deeply—devotion had replaced earthly pleasures.

Soon the Pope appeared, majestic at the head of his cardinals, wearing a red hat, white cloak, and a red velvet capuchin, riding a white Neapolitan barb adorned with red velvet and golden tassels. Behind him rode a hundred horsemen, fully armored, lances raised, the shafts resting on their thighs. Every fifteen steps, the Pope paused briefly to bless the people, then continued.

They descended and walked through side streets to reach the portico of one of the seven churches. It was draped in black. Soon the Pope and cardinals emerged from another entrance, standing on the steps with torches, separated from the crowd by barriers. A canon read a Latin Bull, excommunicating named individuals—especially princes who denied the Church temporal power.

Gerard trembled. So did the people. But two cardinals ruined the solemnity by laughing openly throughout.

When it ended, the black cloth was removed, revealing a bright display. The Pope blessed the crowd and tossed his torch among them. Two cardinals did the same. Instantly, a brawl erupted—people fought for the torches, tearing them apart, gaining fragments at the cost of bruises, bloody noses, and burnt fingers. Then, amid the chaos, the Pope and his retinue withdrew in peace.

A cry went up. The crowd surged toward a square where a stage stood. Priests prayed, then donned red gloves. One knelt, reverently drew from a shrine a square frame resembling a mirror—and within it, a faint impression of a face.

It was the *Verum Icon*, the true image of Christ’s face taken at His agony. Received without question, it moved every heart.

People fell to their knees when the priest raised it. Tears flowed. Cries of sorrow echoed. After a while, the priest circled the platform, showing it briefly to those near—each glimpse met with fresh cries of grief.

Later, they encountered a procession of Flagellants—bare-chested men whipping themselves until blood ran down their backs, yet smiling, jesting, unaffected by pain. Bystanders offered wine; they accepted it, but rarely drank. Instead, they used it to clean the lashes, making the next blow sharper. Most were boys. A young woman pitied one fair urchin.

“Alas, dear child,” she said, “why wound your fair skin so?”

“Basta,” he laughed, “it’s for your sins I do it—not mine.”

“Hear that?” said the friar. “Show me the whip that can beat vanity from a man’s heart! The young monkey—how does he know a stranger’s sin is greater than his own?”

“Father,” said Gerard, “surely this is not pleasing to our Lord. He was merciful.”

“Our Lord?” said the friar, crossing himself. “What does He have to do with this? This custom existed in Rome six hundred years before He was born. Boys used to lash themselves during the Lupercalia. Married women even tried to get struck—believing the blows brought fertility. A foolish ritual, but interesting. It reminds us of the great heathen past. We forget too much of what we owe them.”

Next, they entered one of the seven churches to witness the Pope celebrate Mass. The ceremony was impressive—but spoiled by prelates who sat about the altar with hats on, chattering like geese.

Before the Pope took communion, an official tasted the Eucharist. Gerard was stunned. “Who is that man? What is he doing?”

“The *Preguste*,” said the friar. “He tastes the sacrament as a precaution. Poison is common here—Popes fall often.”

“But after consecration,” argued Gerard, “the bread and wine become Christ’s body and blood. Poison cannot remain. The substance is gone.”

“So says Faith,” replied the friar. “But experience tells otherwise. Scores have died in Italy poisoned in the host.”

“And I tell you, father, even if both elements were laced with poison before consecration, I would take them without fear afterward.”

“So would I—but for the fine arts.”

“What mean you?”

“I would gladly leave this world, were it not for the arts. They beautify life, make it precious to the educated and thoughtful. No—so long as the Nine Muses scatter roses along my path, Apollo may inspire me with wisdom and caution. Knowing the tricks of my countrymen, I will avoid poison—whether at God’s altar or a friend’s table. For wherever I eat it, it will surely end my mortal thread. And I am writing a book—my soul in it—*The Dream of Polifilo*, the man of many arts. Do not speak of poison to me until it is finished and copied.”

Suddenly, the great bells of St. John Lateran rang in short, sharp peals. The people rushed to see the heads of St. Peter and St. Paul.

Gerard and the friar secured a good view. A heavy curtain hung across the altar. After tense silence, it was drawn back in jerks. At thirty feet high, two bearded faces appeared—lifelike, almost alive.

St. Peter had a pale, oval face, grey forked beard, crowned with a papal mitre. St. Paul was dark-skinned, thick-bearded, square-jawed, resolute.

Gerard was awestruck. The friar approved, in his own way.

“This exhibition of *imagines*—wax effigies of heroes—is an ancient custom. It inspires the common folk with virtue through powerful, invisible examples.”

“Wax images?” said Gerard. “But are they not the apostles themselves, embalmed?”

The friar sighed.

“They did not exist in 800. Roman families always displayed *imagines* at funerals—portraits of ancestors, linking past and present. I find nothing more moving or instructive. But those effigies were made during life or at death. These of Peter and Paul are pure invention.”

“Ah, but say not so, father.”

“The worst is the way they are shown—on a shelf, half in darkness, in snatches, with that mountebank trick of the drawn curtain.”

Enough. Men of today are not men of old. Let us leave these modern farces and return to the Pope’s books—there we shall find wisdom we cannot find in Rome’s streets.”

And so, once the idea took root, the friar pushed through the crowd, heedless of direction, until he reached the quiet sanctuary of the Vatican Library, among the dead and the wise.

Later, entering Gerard’s room, he found the young man in heated debate with Jacques Bonaventura. The soldier had just returned from a ceremony he found absurd—the Pope’s blessing of beasts of burden.

“It is ridiculous!” Jacques declared. “A Pope blessing donkeys?”

“Nothing a Pope does can be ridiculous,” Gerard insisted.

The argument grew intense. The friar stood silent, waiting—like the stork who eats the frog and mouse after their fight—to grind both sides in the jaws of antiquity.

Then, softly, the curtain was drawn. An old man stood there, wearing a purple skullcap, a beard like white silk, his face kind but frail.

“Happy youth,” he said, “that can heat yourselves over such matters.”

They all knelt. It was the Pope.

“Rise, my children,” he said, almost irritated. “I came not to be in state. How goes Plutarch?”

Gerard brought his manuscript, knelt, and presented it. The Pope sat; the others stood.

His Holiness examined it closely.

“Excellent,” he said.

Gerard’s heart swelled.

“Ah, this Plutarch—what art he had, Francesco. Each character stands alive on his page. Full of nature, yet unlike any other. He sees men as they are.”

Jacques Bonaventura spoke up. “Give me Signor Boccaccio.”

“An excellent storyteller, capitano,” said the Pope. “He writes exquisite Italian. But his spirit is too monotonous. Monks and nuns were not all unchaste. A few tales are diverting, but fifty paint his age falsely and sadden the heart. Moreover, he lacks skill in character. This Greek—Plutarch—is supreme. He carves men with his pen. Turn his pages, and enter a real world—of war, politics, business, love in its place. With him, men are not all chasing women. Compare this broad field to Boccaccio’s narrow garden—his little round of dishonest pleasures.”

“Your holiness,” said Jacques, “they say you wrote a novel.”

“My holiness has done more foolish things than one. When I wrote novels, I never dreamed I’d be head of the Church.”

“I search in vain for a copy to add to my library.”

The Pope smiled faintly. “You shall not find it.”
