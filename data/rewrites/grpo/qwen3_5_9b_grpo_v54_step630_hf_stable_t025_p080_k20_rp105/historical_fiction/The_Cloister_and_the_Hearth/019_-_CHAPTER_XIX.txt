The dawn broke gray and cold over the Dutch countryside, but Gerard Willems was already awake. The terror of the previous night's narrow escape had seared itself into his mind, a warning that silence was no longer enough. He roused Martin, the old soldier, with a urgency that startled the veteran. Martin, expecting to find Gerard fleeing through the window as he had last night, found instead a man driven by a fresh, desperate resolve.

"Listen to me," Gerard urged, his voice tight with anxiety. "We cannot stay. The net is closing. There is but one path of safety: the great forest. I know a bridle-road within its depths that leads swiftly to a hostelry. From there, a swift horse will carry us out of Holland before the sun has fully risen."

They moved quickly, leaving the safety of Peter's home behind. The distance to the forest edge was short, mere furlongs across the wet, churned earth. Margaret pinned up her kirtle and farthingale against the damp chill, her movements sharp with worry. Peter accompanied them only as far as his garden hedge, where he paused. With an emotion rarely seen on his weathered face, he placed a hand on Gerard's shoulder and offered a blessing, his gaze lingering on the young man who had become his son-in-law.

As they crossed the stony field toward the treeline, the sun was just beginning to peek over the horizon. Halfway across, Margaret stopped. Her eyes darted nervously toward the village, then toward the woods. A cry tore from her throat—a raw sound of instinctual terror—and she turned, running blindly toward the tree line, screaming.

Behind them, the ground trembled with the approach of Ghysbrecht and his men. The burgomaster was in hot pursuit.

Resistance would have been suicide. Without hesitation, Martin and Gerard followed Margaret's lead, plunging into the darkening shadows of the forest. The pursuers gained ground, their boots crunching loudly on the dry leaves, but Martin's voice cut through the panic, ringing out with a fierce command: "Only win the wood! Only win the wood!"

Their hearts leaped with hope. The great oaks seemed to stretch their branches like protective arms, their dense foliage forming a living screen between them and the world. For a moment, the forest was their sanctuary.

But the sanctuary was breached by an unforeseen danger. The fiery old burgomaster, Ghysbrecht, flung himself onto his mule, spurring the beast into a gallop. He did not merely chase his own men; he turned his mount directly toward the fugitives, aiming to cut them off from the deeper woods. The old man rode in a wide semicircle, positioning himself squarely in front of Gerard, while the others scrambled past.

Margaret shrieked, throwing her arms around Gerard in a futile attempt to shield him. He shook her off, his face grim and set.

In his blind rage, Ghysbrecht forgot the natural instinct of the hunted animal to turn on the hunter. He forgot that hatred breeds hatred. Instead of dodging the charging mule as the burgomaster expected, Gerard flew straight at him. With a savage, exulting cry that echoed through the trees, he raised his stout oak staff and struck with every ounce of his strength and soul.

The impact was catastrophic. The oak staff crashed down upon Ghysbrecht's face with a sound like thunder, felling the old man instantly. The burgomaster tumbled beneath his mule, his face streaming with blood, his collar spattered red, as the beast beat a frantic tattoo of hooves against the earth.

In the next heartbeat, the three fugitives were deep within the tree line. The yells of dismay and vengeful fury that erupted from Ghysbrecht's men signaled that the game had changed; it was now a race for life or death.

"Why run?" Gerard gasped, his chest heaving. "You have your bow, and I have this." He brandished the bloody staff.

"Boy!" Martin roared, his voice cracking with adrenaline. "The gallows! Follow me!" He vanished into the undergrowth.

Soon, the baying of hounds filled the air, a pack of hellhounds opening on the scent of the prey. The men were in the wood, their eyes scanning the trunks as they spotted the figures flitting among the trees. Margaret moaned, her breath coming in ragged gasps, while Gerard clenched his teeth, gripping his weapon until his knuckles turned white.

They reached a stiff hazel coppice. Martin dashed inside, shouldering aside the young wood as if it were standing corn. Within fifty yards, they faced a fork of four blind paths. Martin chose one, whispering, "Bend low." They glided along, half-creeping, until their path intersected with a labyrinth of tortuous trails. Taking another, they found themselves led back briefly before turning sharply into a thick pine grove. Here, the walking was difficult, the young firs so dense that visibility dropped to mere yards.

After some time, Martin sat down. Having learned in war to lose all impression of danger amidst the danger itself, he calmly produced a piece of bread and a slice of ham from his wallet, beginning to eat his breakfast.

The younger ones stared at him with dismay. Martin met their looks with a steady gaze.

"All Sevenbergen could not find you now," he said, chewing slowly. "You will lose your purse, Gerard, long before you reach Italy. Is that the way to carry a purse?"

Gerard looked down. Entangled by its chains to the buckle and strap of his wallet was a large triangular purse.

"This is none of mine," Gerard said, trying to detach it. In the struggle through the coppice, it had become inextricably bound. "It seems loath to leave me," he muttered, finally cutting it loose with his knife.

Upon examination, the purse was well-provided with silver coins of all sizes, though its bloated appearance was largely due to numerous pieces of brown paper folded and doubled inside. A light dawned in Gerard's eyes. "Why, it must be that old thief's! And see! Stuffed with paper to deceive the world!"

The mystery of how the burgomaster's purse ended up on Gerard was soon solved. It must have hung at Ghysbrecht's saddle-bow. In his rush to strike his enemy, Gerard had unconsciously torn it away, felling the man and robbing him in a single, fatal gesture.

Gerard was delighted by this feat of fortune, but Margaret was uneasy.

"Throw it away, Gerard, or let Martin take it back," she pleaded. "Already they call you a thief. I cannot bear it."

"Throw it away? Give it back? Not a stiver!" Gerard protested fiercely. "This is spoil lawfully won in battle from an enemy. Is it not, Martin?"

"Of course," Martin replied. "Send him back the brown paper, and you will; but the purse or the coin—that would be a sin."

"Oh, Gerard!" Margaret cried, her voice trembling. "You are going to a distant land. We need the goodwill of Heaven. How can we hope for that if we take what is not ours?"

But Gerard saw the matter in a different light.

"It is Heaven that gives it to me by a miracle," the pious youth declared. "I shall cherish it accordingly. Thus the favored people spoiled the Egyptians and were blessed."

"Take your own way," Margaret said humbly, her voice dropping to a murmur. "You are wiser than I am. You are my husband—is it for me to gainsay you?"

These humble words, coming from a woman who had until now held the whip-hand in their household, surprised Martin for a moment. They would recur to him later, surprising him even less then. Gerard kissed her tenderly in return for her wife-like docility, and they continued their journey hand in hand, with Martin leading the way into the depths of the huge forest. The farther they went, the more absolutely secure they felt. Indeed, the townspeople never ventured so far into the trackless part of the forest.

Yet, impetuous natures repent quickly. As soon as Gerard was out of immediate danger, his conscience began to prick him.

"Martin, would I had not struck quite so hard," he whispered.

"Whom? Oh! Let that pass; he is cheap served," Martin dismissed.

"Martin, I saw his grey hairs as my stick fell on him. I doubt they will not be from my sight this while."

Martin grunted with contempt. "Who spares a badger for his grey hairs? The greyer your enemy is, the older; and the older the craftier, and the craftier the better for a little killing."

"Killing? Killing, Martin? Speak not of killing!" Gerard shook all over, his face pale.

"I am much mistaken if you have not," Martin said cheerfully.

"Now Heaven forbid!"

"The old vagabond's skull cracked like a walnut. Aha!"

"Heaven and the saints forbid it!"

"He rolled off his mule like a stone shot out of a cart. Said I to myself, 'There is one wiped out,'" and the iron old soldier grinned ruthlessly.

Gerard fell to his knees and began to pray fervently for his enemy's life.

At this, Martin lost his patience. "Here's mummery. What! You that set up for learning, know you not that a wise man never strikes his enemy but to kill him? And what is all this coil about killing of old men? If it had been a young one, now, with the joys of life waiting for him—wine, women, and pillage! But an old fellow at the edge of the grave, why not shove him in? Go he must, today or tomorrow; and what better place for greybeards? Now, if ever I should be so mischancy as to last so long as Ghysbrecht did, and have to go on a mule's legs instead of Martin Wittenhaagen's, and a back like this—striking the wood of his bow—instead of this—striking the string—I'll thank and bless any young fellow who will knock me on the head, as you have done that old shopkeeper. Malison on his memory."

"Oh, culpa mea! Culpa mea!" cried Gerard, smiting his breast.

"Look there!" Martin cried to Margaret scornfully. "He is a priest at heart still—and when he is not in ire, St. Paul, what a milksop!"

"Tush, Martin!" Margaret reproached. Then she wreathed her arms round Gerard, comforting him with the double magic of a woman's sense and a woman's voice.

"Sweetheart," she murmured, "you forget: you went not a step out of the way to harm him, who hunted you to your death. You fled from him. He it was who spurred you on. Then did you strike; but in self-defense and a single blow, and with that which was in your hand. Malice had drawn knife, or struck again and again. How often have men been smitten with staves, not one but many blows, yet no lives lost! If then your enemy has fallen, it is through his own malice, not yours, and by the will of God."

"Bless you, Margaret; bless you for thinking so!"

"Yes; but, beloved one, if you have had the misfortune to kill that wicked man, the more need is there that you fly with haste from Holland. Oh, let us on."

"Nay, Margaret," said Gerard. "I fear not man's vengeance, thanks to Martin here and this thick wood: only Him I fear whose eye pierces the forest and reads the heart of man. If I but struck in self-defense, 'tis well; but if in hate, He may bid the avenger of blood follow me to Italy—to Italy? Ay, to earth's remotest bounds."

"Hush!" said Martin peevishly. "I can't hear for your chat."

"What is it?"

"Do you hear nothing, Margaret; my ears are getting old."

Margaret listened, and presently she heard a tuneful sound, like a single stroke upon a deep, ringing bell. She described it to Martin.

"Nay, I heard it," he said.

"And so did I," Gerard replied. "It was beautiful. Ah! There it is again. How sweetly it blends with the air. It is a long way off. It is before us, is it not?"

"No, no! The echoes of this wood confound the ear of a stranger. It comes from the pine grove."

"What! The one we passed?"

"Why, Martin, is this anything? You look pale."

"Wonderful!" said Martin, with a sickly sneer. "He asks me if it is anything? Come, on, on! At any rate, let us reach a better place than this."

"A better place—for what?"

"To stand at bay, Gerard," Martin said gravely. "And die like soldiers, killing three for one."

"What's that sound?"

"It is the avenger of blood."

"Oh, Martin, save him! Oh, Heaven be merciful! What new mysterious peril is this?"

"Girl, it's a bloodhound."
