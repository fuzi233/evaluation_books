In the course of the next day the first of the usual betrothal visits were exchanged, as punctiliously as courses in a dinner whose order had been agreed upon generations ago and never altered without scandal. Newland Archer went first, as propriety required, with his mother and his sister to call on Mrs. Welland; and afterward he drove with Mrs. Welland and May out to old Mrs. Manson Mingott’s to receive that venerable benediction which, in New York, had the weight of a family deed and the permanence of a recipe copied in a grandmother’s hand.

A visit to Mrs. Manson Mingott always amused Archer, and not only because she was formidable. Her house itself had the air of an historical document that had been handled too much, thumbed and annotated, and yet preserved because no one dared throw it away. It was not as venerable, of course, as the pure 1830 interiors still lingering farther down—those solemn rooms whose grim harmony matched cabbage-rose garlands on carpets to the dark shine of rosewood consoles, to round-arched fire-places in black marble, to immense mahogany book-cases glazed like confectioners’ cases. Mrs. Mingott, who had built later, had swept out the massive furniture of her prime and mixed the Mingott heirlooms with the frivolities of the Second Empire, as if a sober old broth had been brightened—against all rules—by an unexpected dash of foreign spice.

She sat, as she always did, in the ground-floor window of her sitting-room, planted there like a great domestic landmark, calmly watching for life and fashion to flow northward to her solitary doors. The street outside had its own orchestra: the clatter of omnibuses bumping over cobble-stones, the iron-shod impatience of hoofs, the rasp of wheels, and, above it, the thin calls of vendors that rose and fell like birds over a field. There were hoardings and quarries, one-story saloons, wooden green-houses in ragged gardens, and rocks where goats stood and stared; but she seemed in no hurry to have them vanish. Her patience equalled her confidence. Some day, she was certain, all that raw, noisy frontier would be pushed back by residences as stately as her own—perhaps, in her impartiality, even statelier—and the rude percussion of the street would be softened under smooth asphalt, such as people reported having heard their footsteps on in Paris. Meanwhile, every one she cared to see came to her. She could fill her rooms as easily as the Beauforts and, without adding a single item to the menu of her suppers, make people feel they had been fed—if not by dishes, then by importance.

The immense accretion of flesh which had descended on her in middle life like lava on a doomed city had transformed her from a plump active little woman with a neatly turned foot and ankle into something as vast and august as a natural phenomenon. She had accepted this submergence with the same obstinate composure with which she accepted every trial; and now, in extreme old age, was rewarded by presenting to her mirror an almost unwrinkled expanse of firm pink-and-white flesh, in the centre of which the traces of a small face survived as if awaiting excavation. A flight of smooth double chins led down to the dizzy depths of a still snowy bosom veiled in snowy muslins, held in place by the miniature portrait of the late Mr. Mingott. Around and below, wave after wave of black silk surged away over the edges of a capacious armchair, while two tiny white hands, their wrists ringed with rolls of aged fat like ivory bracelets, poised above the billows with the lightness of gulls.

Long since, the burden of that flesh had made it impossible for her to go up and down stairs. With characteristic independence—and in flagrant violation of New York propriety—she had moved her reception rooms upstairs and established herself on the ground floor, so that, seated with her in the sitting-room window, you caught, through a door that was always open and a looped-back yellow damask portière, the unexpected vista of a bedroom: a huge low bed upholstered like a sofa, and a toilet-table dressed in frivolous lace flounces beneath a gilt-framed mirror. Visitors were startled and fascinated by the foreignness of the arrangement, which recalled the settings of French novels and those architectural incentives to immorality the simple American imagination had never dared to furnish. That, Archer told himself with secret amusement, was how women with lovers lived in wicked old societies: apartments with all the rooms on one floor, and all the indecent propinquities novels described. It entertained him to picture Mrs. Mingott’s blameless life led on a stage-set meant for adultery; but he admitted, with real admiration, that if a lover had been what she wanted, the intrepid woman would have had him too.

To the general relief the Countess Olenska was not present when the betrothed couple arrived. Mrs. Mingott said Ellen had gone out; which, on a day of glaring sunlight, at the shopping hour, seemed in itself a questionable thing for a compromised woman to do. But at least it spared them the embarrassment of her presence and the faint shadow that her unhappy past might seem to cast across their radiant future—like a sour note drifting in from the street just as a domestic harmony had been established.

The visit went off successfully, as every one had foreseen. Old Mrs. Mingott was delighted with the engagement; it had been long expected by watchful relatives and carefully passed upon in family council, like a proposed change to a household menu. And the engagement ring—a large thick sapphire set in invisible claws—met with her unqualified admiration.

“It’s the new setting; of course it shows the stone beautifully, but it looks a little bare to old-fashioned eyes,” Mrs. Welland explained, with a conciliatory side-glance at her future son-in-law.

“Old-fashioned eyes? I hope you don’t mean mine, my dear? I like all the novelties,” said the ancestress, lifting the stone to her small bright orbs, which no glasses had ever disfigured. “Very handsome,” she added, returning it. “Very liberal. In my time a cameo set in pearls was thought sufficient. But it’s the hand that sets off the ring, isn’t it, my dear Mr. Archer?” She waved one of her tiny hands, as if offering it for appraisal the way she might offer a pastry to see if it had been properly browned. “Mine was modelled in Rome by the great Ferrigiani. You should have May’s done: no doubt he’ll have it done, my child. Her hand is large—it’s these modern sports that spread the joints—but the skin is white. And when’s the wedding to be?” She broke off and fixed her eyes on Archer’s face with the directness of a cook demanding the date of a feast.

“Oh—” Mrs. Welland murmured; and Archer, smiling at May, answered: “As soon as ever it can, if only you’ll back me up, Mrs. Mingott.”

“We must give them time to get to know each other a little better, mamma,” Mrs. Welland interposed, with the proper affectation of reluctance.

“Know each other? Fiddlesticks! Everybody in New York has always known everybody,” the old lady returned. “Let the young man have his way, my dear; don’t wait till the bubble’s off the wine. Marry them before Lent; I may catch pneumonia any winter now, and I want to give the wedding-breakfast.”

The successive declarations were received with the correct measure of amusement, incredulity, gratitude. The air in the room had that faint domestic perfume of warmed textiles and old pot-pourri, and through it threaded the distant scrape of wheels and the intermittent clang of street life—a reminder that the city’s rougher music went on while families measured out their joys by ritual. The visit was breaking up in a vein of mild pleasantry when the door opened and the Countess Olenska entered in bonnet and mantle, followed by the unexpected figure of Julius Beaufort.

There was a cousinly murmur of pleasure among the ladies, and Mrs. Mingott held out Ferrigiani’s modelled hand to the banker.

“Ha! Beaufort, this is a rare favour!” She had an odd foreign way of addressing men by their surnames.

“Thanks. I wish it might happen oftener,” he said, in his easy arrogant way. “I’m generally so tied down; but I met the Countess Ellen in Madison Square, and she was good enough to let me walk home with her.”

“Ah—I hope the house will be gayer, now that Ellen’s here!” cried Mrs. Mingott with glorious effrontery. “Sit down—sit down, Beaufort: push up the yellow armchair; now I’ve got you I want a good gossip. I hear your ball was magnificent; and I understand you invited Mrs. Lemuel Struthers? Well—I’ve a curiosity to see the woman myself.”

She had forgotten her relatives, who were drifting out into the hall under Ellen Olenska’s guidance. Old Mrs. Mingott had always professed a great admiration for Julius Beaufort; there was a kind of kinship between them, in their cool domineering way and their talent for taking short-cuts through conventions. Now she was eagerly curious to know what had decided the Beauforts to invite, for the first time, Mrs. Lemuel Struthers—the widow of Struthers’s Shoe-polish—who had returned the previous year from a long initiatory sojourn in Europe to lay siege to New York’s tight little citadel.

“Of course if you and Regina invite her the thing is settled,” Mrs. Mingott declared, with a relish like that of someone tasting a new dish and deciding it will do. “Well, we need new blood and new money—and I hear she’s still very good-looking.”

In the hall, while Mrs. Welland and May drew on their furs, Archer saw the Countess Olenska looking at him with a faintly questioning smile, as though she were listening not just for words but for what lay underneath them—the private dialect that never translated well into public speech.

“Of course you know already—about May and me,” he said, answering her look with a shy laugh. “She scolded me for not giving you the news last night at the Opera: I had her orders to tell you that we were engaged—but I couldn’t, in that crowd.”

The smile passed from Ellen’s eyes to her lips. She looked younger suddenly, more like the bold brown Ellen Mingott of his boyhood, before Europe had added its complicated seasonings to her. “Of course I know; yes. And I’m so glad. But one doesn’t tell such things first in a crowd.”

The ladies were on the threshold. She held out her hand. “Good-bye; come and see me some day,” she said, still looking at Archer, as if asking him—without saying it—to remember that there were ways of speaking and being spoken to that did not belong to the loud public rooms of New York.

In the carriage, on the way down Fifth Avenue, they talked pointedly of Mrs. Mingott—of her age, her spirit, her wonderful attributes—as families will talk of a great table laid safely behind them, enumerating the dishes they have survived and praising the cook. No one alluded to Ellen Olenska; but Archer knew Mrs. Welland was thinking: It is a mistake for Ellen to be seen, the very day after her arrival, parading up Fifth Avenue at the crowded hour with Julius Beaufort. And Archer himself mentally added, with the complacence of a man who believed he lived in a quieter, better-ordered soundscape: She ought to know that a man who’s just engaged doesn’t spend his time calling on married women. But I daresay in the set she’s lived in they do—they never do anything else.

And, in spite of the cosmopolitan views on which he prided himself, he thanked heaven that he was a New Yorker, and about to ally himself with one of his own kind.
