Gerard stirred from his slumber to find Denys gazing down at him, concern etched across his weathered face.

“You sleep like the dead,” Denys remarked, nudging him. “The sun’s already climbed past noon.”

Gerard sat up, blinking as if emerging from a dream. “A blessed sleep indeed,” he murmured. “It has drawn a veil between me and last night’s horrors. To think we sit here now, whole and breathing—it feels like some dark vision, not a thing that truly happened.”

Denys clapped him on the shoulder. “A wise way to see it, lad. What’s past is but a shadow. Now, break your fast, and let it fade.” He hesitated, then added, “I promised to bring you before the magistrate by midday.”

Gerard tore a piece of rye bread, soaking it in wine before eating. The taste grounded him, pulling him back to the present. Still, when they retraced their steps to the scene of the ambush, his stomach twisted. The trampled earth, the splashes of dried blood—it was real. He shuddered, quickening his pace until they reached the safety of the town.

Denys led him to *The White Hart*, a sturdy inn with whitewashed walls and a swinging sign. “No cutthroats here,” he assured Gerard. “The landlord’s a burgess, an honest man. This was where I meant us to lodge last night, but—” He shrugged. “Nightfall had other plans.”

Gerard’s jaw tightened. “And the woman at the door—the one Jeanneton warned us against.”

Denys gave a dry chuckle. “Aye, and yet it was a woman who saved our necks, risked her own to do it.”

Gerard’s chest tightened. “She’s worth more than gold. I’d give every coin in my purse to thank her.”

“*Parbleu*, so would I.”

“Then where is she?”

“The alderman may know. But first, we must face his questions.”

The magistrate received them with an odd expression—first a flicker of surprise, then a slow, knowing smirk. He listened as Gerard recounted the night’s events, scribbling notes with a quill. When he finished, he delivered his verdict.

“You’ll remain in town until the trial,” he said sternly. “To leave would mean fines—or worse, a cell.”

The two exchanged uneasy glances.

The alderman softened slightly. “It won’t be long. The thieves were caught red-handed.” Then, with a cryptic note, he added, “Besides, you couldn’t leave this week even if you wished.”

Denys frowned at that, but Gerard barely registered the words. His mind was elsewhere.

“Your worship,” he pressed, “we’ve searched the town for the girl who saved us—the one who came to you in the night. Do you know where she might be?”

The alderman raised a brow. “And who might this benefactress be?”

Gerard leaned forward, urgency sharpening his voice. “The brave soul who risked her life for ours. Where is she? We must thank her—*please*.”

The question hung in the air, unanswered.
