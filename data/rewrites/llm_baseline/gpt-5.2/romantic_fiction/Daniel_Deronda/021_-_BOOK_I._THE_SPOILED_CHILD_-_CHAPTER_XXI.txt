People repeat, with an air of finality, that knowledge is power. They forget how swiftly ignorance can unmake what patience took centuries to cure and season. Knowledge keeps its stores like a careful housekeeper—records stacked as neatly as jars of preserved fruit in a cool pantry, each label a promise to the future. Ignorance, hungry for the day’s heat and unwilling to walk to the woodpile, will tear down the shelves and feed the fire with the very labels—burning the names, the dates, the old dialect words no one thought to save, just to give a moment’s sharp flavor to one careless roast. Knowledge raises a slow, scrupulous architecture; ignorance—great, blind, noisy—lumbers in and, for sport, shakes the pillars, leaving only soot and a ringing emptiness where there used to be rooms full of remembered voices.

And in a single life, too, one may watch it happen: the dull, fatal ignorance of how one event binds itself to another, the wilful belief that consequences can be forced like a sauce rushed on too fierce a flame—boiling over, scalding, spoiling everything with haste. There are days when the smallest, most contemptible detail—an ugly station, a sour smell, a voice that grates—tips a mind toward ruin as surely as a stone tips a cart into a ditch.

It was half-past ten when Gwendolen Harleth, after her leaden journey from Leubronn, arrived at the station from which she must drive to Offendene. No carriage waited; no familiar face lifted a hand. From Dover she had telegraphed naming a later train, and in her impatience—her hatred of being kept under the station roof and the eyes of strangers—she had come on without imagining what it would be to arrive alone at that desolate point of rail that seemed chosen because it belonged to no place in particular, being equally far from everywhere.

So she was set down a solitary woman with her large trunks, and the waiting-room received her like an insult: dirty paint flaking under her gaze, a dusty decanter of water that looked as though it had stood there since last winter, and great printed texts calling on her to repent—texts that felt, to her, like the cracked voice of a preacher heard through a wall. She fled out to the door that looked toward the lane and fields, yet even there the day offered no comfort. Autumn sunlight fell with a kind of tired pity; the leaves and grass shivered under a wind that worried at everything—turning up the feathers of a cock and two croaking hens that seemed, in their lonely fussing, to have lost their whole brood and not know what to do with the remainder of life. The very air had a thin, raw scent, as if the earth had been rinsed and left to dry in draught.

The railway official hovered, resource-less and incurious, watching her and her luggage with an innocent stare made unbearable by a cast in his eye. He was new; he did not know her; and in his not knowing her there lay the humiliation that he must class her with any other stranded traveller, not “high in the world,” not guarded by attendance. That ignorance in him—mere blankness—had power enough to sting her.

At last the vehicle came within sight: a dirty old barouche, slowly being made ready by an elderly laborer. Such details look wretchedly small beside the word “history”; yet most turns in life cannot be accounted for without just these specks of annoyance that lodge, accumulate, and harden into motive. A soul will take its theory of the world from a draught, a stain, a look; and to be dropped alone at an irrelevant place, with the new gnawing consciousness of no income, might prompt even a thinker to bitterness about the origin of things. How much more must such trifles tell on a young woman with fastidious taste, an Indian shawl over her arm, twenty cubic feet of trunks by her side, and a mortal dread of the poverty that was suddenly giving her imagination a thousand disagreeables to chew upon like grit between teeth.

They told heavily on Gwendolen. Her spirit, which had set itself up in resistance all through the journey, began to droop under this first sample of “home.” What was the good of living if the future meant ugliness, hardship, humiliation? The drive in the uneasy barouche made those questions repeat themselves like the clatter of wheels over ruts. One trunk squeezed the meek driver; another was lashed with rope on the seat in front of her. The old leather smelled stale and damp; the road’s dust rose and settled, tasting of chalk. And there was sound—too much sound—iron-shod hooves striking flint, a loose panel rattling, the wind worrying the hedges, the barouche groaning as if it complained aloud of the task of carrying her.

All the way from Leubronn her ruling vision had been of going abroad again. Surely, surely, there must be some little income left—her mother could not have meant they would literally have nothing. The picture she had accustomed herself to was dreary but bearable: some dull place on the Continent, living poorly; and she had seen poor English people abroad—pale, pinched, trying to make a little gentility out of thin soup and worn gloves—and she imagined herself plunged among them, despised and bored, with Alice and Bertha and Fanny and Isabel all growing up in tediousness around her while she drifted toward thirty and her mother sank deeper into melancholy. She did not mean to submit. She had not quite believed in the misfortune.

Yet weariness and disgust, fed by this miserable arrival, began to act on her like an uncomfortable waking—worse, even, than uneasy dreams, because now the air was cold and the dust real. The self-delight with which she had once kissed her image in the glass had faded. What was the good of being charming, clever, resolute, if events could turn out anyhow? Men were hateful—yes, hateful. And in these last hours, hatred had shifted its object: she was beginning to be angry with Grandcourt not only as a man to be feared, but as the man whose existence—whose hideous flaw—had hindered her from marrying him and escaping this very lot. It is one thing to despise a thing in itself, and another to despise it the more because it blocks your way.

The barouche lumbered into the avenue; the windows of Offendene showed themselves. Then under the portico a figure appeared, and the hard selfishness in Gwendolen’s breast gave way as quickly as a crust breaks when a warm knife presses it. She sprang from the carriage; she saw the dear beautiful face—her mother’s—now with fresh lines of sadness traced upon it as if grief had been doing needlework in the skin. Gwendolen flung her arms round Mrs. Davilow’s neck, and for the moment all sorrow existed only as something that touched her mother.

Behind were the four “superfluous girls,” the sad, quiet faces; each had her own hidden world, important to herself and to no one else, and all of them felt—poor things—that where Gwendolen was, something interesting might happen, some decisive movement would be made. Even her hurried submission to their kisses, even her “Now go away, girls,” carried that harsh comfort which weakness takes from decision in another.

Miss Merry, with her meek depressed air—which had once been puzzling in a governess attached to the family—was now only on a level with the rest of the circumstances. She did not expect greeting; she busied herself with trunks and coachman’s pay. Mrs. Davilow and Gwendolen hastened upstairs and shut themselves in the black-and-yellow bedroom.

“Never mind, mamma dear,” Gwendolen said, tenderly pressing her handkerchief where tears rolled down Mrs. Davilow’s cheeks. “Never mind. I don’t mind. I will do something. I will be something. Things will come right. It seemed worse because I was away. Come now—you must be glad because I am here.”

She felt every word. Compassion stirred in her like sudden heat, and the vague projects that had glanced through her mind on the journey gathered into a sharper shape, as if now she could see how to become “something.” It was one of her best moments—one of those moments when even self-love turns into a kind of brave devotion—and her fond mother, forgetting everything below that high tide mark, looked at her with something like adoration.

“Bless you, my good, good darling!” Mrs. Davilow said. “I can be happy, if you can!”

But the tide ebbed later. Misfortune ceased to be a telegram and began to sit down in the house like a tyrannical inmate, breathing its cold breath into corners. At first, it was still Offendene: the large solid rooms; the quiet; the familiar order that secured her ease. Her toilet luxuries were still there, still ministered to without her lifting a finger. A little tray with her favorite food was brought to her in private, because she had said, “Keep them all away from us to-day, mamma. Let you and me be alone together.”

When she came down into the drawing-room, fresh as a newly-dipped swan, and leaned against the cushions of the settee beside her mother, the worst had not yet fully struck her. She felt prepared; she began with deliberate intention.

“What have you thought of doing, exactly, mamma?”

“Oh, my dear, the next thing is to move away from this house. Mr. Haynes is most fortunately as glad to have it now as he would have been when we took it. Lord Brackenshaw’s agent is to arrange everything with him to the best advantage for us—Bazley, you know; not at all an ill-natured man.”

“I cannot help thinking Lord Brackenshaw would let you stay here rent-free, mamma,” said Gwendolen. Her mind, trained more in reading admiration than in reading accounts, moved naturally to that sort of hope.

“My dear child, Lord Brackenshaw is in Scotland, and knows nothing about us. Neither your uncle nor I would choose to apply to him. Besides, what could we do in this house without servants, and without money to warm it? The sooner we are out the better. We have nothing to carry but our clothes, you know.”

“I suppose you mean to go abroad, then?” Gwendolen said. At least that future had been rehearsed.

“Oh no, dear—no. How could we travel? You never did learn anything about income and expenses.” Mrs. Davilow tried to smile, and laid her hand on Gwendolen’s, adding more mournfully, “That makes it so much harder for you, my pet.”

“But where are we to go?” The question came sharp, as if fear had edged her voice.

“It is all decided. A little furniture is to be got in from the rectory—all that can be spared.” Mrs. Davilow hesitated, dreading less for herself than for the shock she must give. “It is Sawyer’s Cottage we are to go to.”

At first Gwendolen said nothing. She paled; anger rose in her throat like something bitter. Then she spoke with haughtiness.

“That is impossible. Something else ought to have been thought of. My uncle ought not to allow that. I will not submit to it.”

“My sweet child, what else could have been thought of? Your uncle is as kind as he can be—but he is suffering himself; he has his family to bring up. And do you quite understand? We have nothing—absolutely nothing except what he and my sister give us.” Mrs. Davilow spoke timidly, yet forced herself on, because how else to make the fact real? “They have been as wise and active as possible, and we must try to earn something. I and the girls are going to work a table-cloth border for the Ladies’ Charity at Winchester, and a communion cloth the parishioners are to present to Pennicote Church.”

She offered details as one offers a thin broth—hoping it will go down, knowing it is poor nourishment. Gwendolen, caught as in a nightmare by the image of that house where an exciseman had lived, persisted.

“But surely somewhere else than Sawyer’s Cottage might have been found.”

“No indeed, dear. Houses are scarce, and we may be thankful to get anything so private. It is not so very bad. There are two little parlors and four bedrooms. You shall sit alone whenever you like.”

But the sympathetic care for her mother had gone low; the sound of “two little parlors” came to her like the clink of a cheap spoon against a tin cup. She did not answer the tenderness.

“I cannot conceive that all your property is gone at once, mamma. How can you be sure in so short a time? It is not a week since you wrote to me.”

“The first news came much earlier, dear. But I would not spoil your pleasure till it was quite necessary.”

“Oh, how vexatious!” Gwendolen colored with fresh anger. “If I had known, I could have brought home the money I had won; and for want of knowing, I stayed and lost it. I had nearly two hundred pounds, and it would have done for us to live on a little while till I could carry out some plan.” She paused, and then broke out more impetuously. “Everything has gone against me. People have come near me only to blight me.”

Among those “people” her mind included Deronda. If he had not interfered—if his voice, his look, his insistence had not stood between her and the gaming-table—she might have gone again with a few napoleons and won back her losses. Even now, the memory of that moment brought a strange soreness, as if a word had been taken from her mouth and she could not translate the feeling into any language that would make others see it as she saw it.

“We must resign ourselves to the will of Providence, my child,” said Mrs. Davilow. The confession of gambling startled her; but she dared not rebuke. She believed “people” meant Grandcourt, and on that subject her lips were sealed.

“But I don’t resign myself,” Gwendolen returned quickly. “I shall do what I can against it. What is the good of calling the people’s wickedness Providence? You said in your letter it was Mr. Lassman’s fault we had lost our money. Has he run away with it all?”

“No, dear, you don’t understand. There were great speculations: he meant to gain. It was all about mines and things of that sort. He risked too much.”

“I don’t call that Providence. It was his improvidence with our money, and he ought to be punished. Can’t we go to law and recover our fortune? My uncle ought to take measures and not sit down by such wrongs. We ought to go to law.”

“My dear child, law can never bring back money lost in that way. Your uncle says it is milk spilled upon the ground. Besides, one must have a fortune to get any law: there is no law for people who are ruined. And our money has only gone along with other people’s. We are not the only sufferers: others have to resign themselves besides us.”

“But I don’t resign myself to live at Sawyer’s Cottage and see you working for sixpences and shillings because of that. I shall not do it. I shall do what is more befitting our rank and education.”

“I am sure your uncle and all of us will approve of that, dear, and admire you the more for it,” Mrs. Davilow said, grateful for an opening. “I didn’t mean that you should resign yourself to worse when anything better offered itself. Both your uncle and aunt feel that your abilities and education are a fortune for you, and they have already heard of something within your reach.”

“What is that, mamma?” Interest softened some of Gwendolen’s sharpness, and imagination stirred—always ready to gild any possible exit.

“There are two situations. One is in a bishop’s family—three daughters—and the other is in quite a high class of school. In both, your French, and music, and dancing—and then your manners and habits as a lady—are exactly what is wanted. Each is a hundred a year—and—just for the present”—Mrs. Davilow grew frightened and hesitating—“to save you from the petty, common way of living that we must go to—you would perhaps accept one of the two.”

“What! Be like Miss Graves at Madame Meunier’s? No.”

“I think, myself, Dr. Monpert’s would be more suitable. There could be no hardship in a bishop’s family.”

“Excuse me, mamma. There are hardships everywhere for a governess. I don’t see that it would be pleasanter to be looked down on in a bishop’s family than in any other. Besides, you know very well I hate teaching. Fancy me shut up with three awkward girls something like Alice! I would rather emigrate than be a governess.”

Mrs. Davilow fell silent, seeing no outlet, thinking with dread of the collision that might come when Gwendolen met her uncle and aunt. There was something withheld in Gwendolen’s resistance—as if she had a plan in reserve. And though Gwendolen’s ignorance of practical life showed itself at every step, the mother’s faith in her force remained: that daring will which had mastered her so often must surely find a way.

“I have some ornaments, mamma, and I could sell them,” Gwendolen said. “They would make a sum. I want a little sum—just to go on with. I dare say Marshall at Wanchester would take them. I know he showed me some bracelets once that he said he had bought from a lady. Jocosa might go and ask him. Jocosa is going to leave us, of course—but she might do that first.”

“She would do anything she could, poor dear soul. I have not told you yet—she wanted me to take all her savings—her three hundred pounds. I tell her to set up a little school. It will be hard for her to go into a new family now she has been so long with us.”

“Oh, recommend her for the bishop’s daughters,” Gwendolen said, and for a moment a gleam of laughter lit her face. “I am sure she will do better than I should.”

“Do take care not to say such things to your uncle,” Mrs. Davilow begged. “He will be hurt at your despising what he has exerted himself about. But I dare say you have something else in your mind that he might not disapprove, if you consulted him.”

“There is some one else I want to consult first. Are the Arrowpoints at Quetcham still, and is Herr Klesmer there? But I daresay you know nothing about it, poor dear mamma. Can Jeffries go on horseback with a note?”

“Oh, my dear, Jeffries is not here, and the dealer has taken the horses. But some one could go for us from Leek’s farm. The Arrowpoints are at Quetcham, I know. Miss Arrowpoint left her card the other day: I could not see her. But I don’t know about Herr Klesmer. Do you want to send before to-morrow?”

“Yes—as soon as possible. I will write a note,” said Gwendolen, rising with sudden alacrity.

“What can you be thinking of, Gwen?” Mrs. Davilow asked, relieved despite her wonder, because energy and better humor were like a window opened.

“Don’t mind what—there’s a dear, good mamma,” Gwendolen said, reseating herself to give atoning caresses. “I mean to do something. Never mind what until it is all settled. And then you shall be comforted. The dear face—it is ten years older in these three weeks. Now, now, now—don’t cry.” Holding her mother’s head with both hands, she kissed the trembling eyelids. “But mind you don’t contradict me or put hindrances in my way. I must decide for myself. I cannot be dictated to by my uncle or any one else. My life is my own affair. And I think”—her tone sharpened with scorn—“I think I can do better for you than let you live in Sawyer’s Cottage.”

She rose and went to a desk. The room was quiet in the way country rooms can be quiet, with a softness of distant sounds—the faint tick of a clock, a far door closing—so that the scratch of her pen seemed loud, like a small animal worrying at wood. She wrote to Klesmer:

Miss Harleth presents her compliments to Herr Klesmer, and ventures to request of him the very great favor that he will call upon her, if possible, to-morrow. Her reason for presuming so far on his kindness is of a very serious nature. Unfortunate family circumstances have obliged her to take a course in which she can only turn for advice to the great knowledge and judgment of Herr Klesmer.

“Pray get this sent to Quetcham at once, mamma,” she said as she addressed the letter. “The man must be told to wait for an answer. Let no time be lost.”

For the moment, sending the letter absorbed her like a fixed hunger: as if, if she could only get it into the right hands quickly enough, her life might yet be saved from the mean, thin fare of humiliation. But once she had been assured it would go, another anxiety rose and set her nerves jangling.

If Klesmer happened not to be at Quetcham—what then?

Her belief in her star had been bruised. A splendid marriage, shining within reach, had shown its hideous flaw. Roulette had refused to arrange itself to her deserts. A man of whom she knew nothing had thrust himself between her and her own intentions, stopping her with a look and a few words that still seemed to sound in her memory with an intolerable distinctness. The conduct of those dull people who managed the world’s business had been culpable precisely in the points most injurious to her. Now, for the first time, the conditions of this world felt to her like a hurrying, roaring crowd—too loud, too indifferent—where she had got astray and no one cared to shelter her more than any other girl, though to her it seemed a peculiar cruelty.

If Klesmer were absent, it would be all of a piece with the rest. The negative pressed itself on her imagination, and her mind began to dart toward desperate alternatives—anything that would save her from Sawyer’s Cottage, from the phrase “taking a situation,” which summed up for her the whole bitterness of being ranked, spoken to, and listened to in a diminished key, as if her very voice must be translated into smaller meanings.

And yet—Klesmer might be there. The thought of that possibility brought a hopefulness that threw a strange light even over her trouble, as if this misery were only the necessary dark background for the biography of the remarkable. For Gwendolen, with all her beauty and conscious force, could not imagine herself unremarkable; and if any one she knew had been cross-examined as to whether she was “remarkable,” the first who said “No” would have astonished her like a word spoken in a dialect she had never learned.
