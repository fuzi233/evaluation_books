My conscience, weighed down by the heavy burden of my aunt's suffering, forbade me to whisper the truth I had uncovered until she chose to speak it herself. I sat in silent vigil, my heart prepared for whatever duty lay ahead, however painful, trusting that a few sustaining words would find their proper moment.

"I have been seriously ill, Drusilla, for some time past," my aunt began, her voice trembling slightly. "And, strange to say, without knowing it myself."

I thought of the countless souls perishing in spiritual darkness, unaware of their own decay, and a deep dread seized me that my poor aunt might be among them. "Yes, dear," I replied softly, my eyes filling with tears. "Yes."

"I brought Rachel to London for medical advice," she continued, her gaze fixed on the fire. "I deemed it right to consult two physicians."

Two doctors! And, oh, the thought that not a single clergyman had been summoned for Rachel's state! "Yes, dear?" I asked again, my voice thick with emotion. "Yes?"

"One of the two medical men was a stranger to me," my aunt explained, her composure slowly fracturing. "The other had been a close friend of my husband's, a man who had always held a sincere affection for me. After examining Rachel, he requested to speak with me privately. I expected specific instructions for my daughter's care. Instead, he took my hand with grave solemnity and said, 'Lady Verinder, I have observed you with both professional and personal concern. You are, I fear, in far more urgent need of medical aid than your daughter.' He questioned me, and though I initially treated his inquiries lightly, I saw the distress they caused him. He arranged to return the following day, accompanied by a colleague, at a time when Rachel would be absent."

"The result of that visit," she whispered, tears finally spilling over, "was delivered to me with kindness, yet it shattered my world. Both physicians agreed that precious time had been lost, irretrievable. My condition has now passed beyond the reach of their art. For over two years, I have suffered from a silent, insidious heart disease that has slowly broken me down without alarming symptoms. I may live for months, or I may die before tomorrow dawns; the doctors dare not be more precise. It would be cruel to say I have not suffered miserably since learning the truth, but I am more resigned now. I am doing my best to settle my worldly affairs. My greatest fear is that Rachel should learn of this. If she knew, she would blame herself for my decline, attributing it to worry over the Diamond, when it is entirely her mother's fault. Both doctors agree the illness began two, perhaps three years ago. I trust you will keep this secret, Drusilla—I see genuine sorrow and sympathy in your face."

Sorrow and sympathy! Oh, what pagan emotions to expect from a Christian Englishwoman so firmly anchored in her faith!

Yet, a torrent of devout gratitude surged through me as she concluded her tragic tale. Here was a field of usefulness opened before me! Here was a beloved relative, a dying soul, utterly unprepared for the Great Change, yet providentially led to reveal her plight to *me*. How could I describe the joy that flooded my heart, remembering that the clerical friends I could call upon were counted not by ones or twos, but by tens and twenties? I drew my aunt into my arms, my overflowing tenderness demanding more than mere words. "Oh!" I cried fervently, "the indescribable interest you inspire in me! Oh, the good I mean to do you before we part!"

After offering earnest warnings, I presented her with a choice of three devoted friends, all engaged in works of mercy within her neighborhood, all inexhaustible in exhortation, all ready to serve at my command. Alas, the response was discouraging. Lady Verinder looked puzzled and frightened, rejecting my offers with the worldly objection that she was too weak to face strangers. I yielded—for the moment. My extensive experience as a Reader and Visitor, guided by fourteen beloved clerical friends, taught me that this required preparation through scripture. I possessed a small library of works tailored to this emergency, designed to arouse, convince, prepare, enlighten, and fortify her. "You will read, dear, won't you?" I pleaded in my most winning tone. "You will read if I bring my own precious books? Turned down at the right passages, aunt. Marked in pencil where you must pause and ask, 'Does this apply to me?'" Even this simple appeal, so thoroughly heathenizing in its worldly influence, seemed to startle her. She replied, "I will do what I can, Drusilla, to please you," with a look of surprise that was both instructive and terrifying.

Not a moment was to be wasted. The clock on the mantelpiece indicated I had just enough time to rush home, select a dozen essential readings, and return to witness the signing of Lady Verinder's Will. Promising to be back by five o'clock, I left the house on my mission of mercy.

When my own interests are not at stake, I am content to travel by omnibus. Yet, to demonstrate my devotion to my aunt's welfare, I committed the extravagance of hiring a cab.

I drove home, carefully selected and marked my first series of readings, and returned to Montagu Square with a carpet-bag containing a dozen volumes, a collection I firmly believed unrivaled in European literature. I paid the cabman his exact fare. He received it with an oath; instantly, I handed him a religious tract. If I had drawn a pistol to his head, this abandoned wretch could scarcely have shown greater consternation. He leaped onto his box, shouting profane exclamations of dismay, and drove off furiously. Fortunately, it was useless! I had sown the good seed, despite him, by throwing a second tract through the cab window.

The servant who answered the door was not the footman with the cap-ribbons, to my great relief, but the footman himself. He informed me that the doctor had called and remained with Lady Verinder. Mr. Bruff, the family solicitor, had arrived a minute prior and was waiting in the library. I was shown into the library to wait.

Mr. Bruff looked surprised to see me. As the family solicitor, we had met several times under Lady Verinder's roof. He was a man grown old and grizzled in the service of the world, a prophet of Law and Mammon in business, yet equally capable of reading a novel or tearing up a tract in his leisure.

"Have you come to stay here, Miss Clack?" he asked, glancing at my carpet-bag.

To reveal the contents of my precious bag to such a person would have invited an outburst of profanity. I lowered myself to his level and stated my business.

"My aunt has informed me she is about to sign her Will," I answered. "She has kindly asked me to be one of the witnesses."

"Aye? aye? Well, Miss Clack, you will do. You are over twenty-one, and you have not the slightest pecuniary interest in Lady Verinder's Will."

Not the slightest pecuniary interest! Oh, how thankful I felt! If my aunt, possessing thousands, had remembered poor me, to whom five pounds is a significant sum, and left me a comforting legacy, my enemies might have doubted the motive behind loading me with the choicest treasures of my library and drawing upon my failing resources for the prodigal expense of a cab. Not the cruellest scoffer could doubt my motives now. Much better as it was! Oh, surely, much better!

I was aroused from these consoling reflections by Mr. Bruff's voice. My meditative silence seemed to weigh upon his spirits, forcing him to talk against his will.

"Well, Miss Clack, what's the last news in the charitable circles? How is your friend Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite, after the mauling he received from the rogues in Northumberland Street? Egad! They're telling a pretty story about that charitable gentleman at my club!"

I had overlooked his remark regarding my age and lack of financial interest, but the tone in which he alluded to Mr. Godfrey was too much for my forbearance. Bound to assert the innocence of my admirable friend whenever questioned, and feeling compelled to include a stinging rebuke for Mr. Bruff, I spoke up.

"I live very much out of the world," I said; "and I lack the advantage of belonging to a club. But I happen to know the story you allude to, and I also know that a viler falsehood than that story was never told."

"Yes, yes, Miss Clack—you believe in your friend. Natural enough. Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite won't find the world quite so easy to convince as a committee of charitable ladies. Appearances are dead against him. He was in the house when the Diamond was lost. And he was the first person to go to London afterwards. Those are ugly circumstances, viewed by the light of later events."

I should have corrected him immediately, for he was speaking in ignorance of testimony offered by the only person competent to speak from positive knowledge. Yet, the temptation to lead the lawyer artfully to his own discomfiture proved too strong. I asked what he meant by "later events," feigning the utmost innocence.

"By later events, Miss Clack, I mean events involving the Indians," Mr. Bruff continued, growing increasingly superior. "What do the Indians do the moment they are released from Frizinghall prison? They go straight to London and fix on Mr. Luker. Mr. Luker, alarmed for the safety of a 'valuable of great price' he possesses, lodges it privately in his bankers' strongroom. Wonderfully clever of him; but the Indians are just as clever. Suspecting the 'valuable' is being moved, they devise a bold plan to clear their doubts. Whom do they seize and search? Not only Mr. Luker, but Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite as well. Why? Mr. Ablewhite claims they acted on blind suspicion after seeing him speak to Mr. Luker. Absurd! Half-a-dozen others spoke to Mr. Luker that morning. Why weren't they followed home too? No! The plain inference is that Mr. Ablewhite had a private interest in the 'valuable,' just as Mr. Luker did. The Indians, uncertain which held the disposal, searched them both. Public opinion says this, Miss Clack. And public opinion, on this occasion, is not easily refuted."

He spoke with such worldly conceit that I, to my shame, could not resist leading him further before overwhelming him with the truth.

"I don't presume to argue with a clever lawyer like you," I said. "But is it fair to Mr. Ablewhite to ignore the opinion of the famous London police officer who investigated this case? Not the shadow of a suspicion rested upon anyone but Miss Verinder in the mind of Sergeant Cuff."

"Do you mean to tell me, Miss Clack, that you agree with the Sergeant?"

"I judge nobody, sir, and offer no opinion."

"And I commit both those enormities, ma'am. I judge the Sergeant to have been utterly wrong; and I offer the opinion that, if he had known Rachel's character as I do, he would have suspected everyone in the house but *her*. I admit she has faults—she is secret, self-willed, odd, and wild. But true as steel, high-minded, and generous to a fault. If the plainest evidence pointed one way, and only Rachel's word of honor pointed the other, I would take her word before the evidence, lawyer as I am! Strong language, Miss Clack; but I mean it."

"Would you object to illustrate your meaning, Mr. Bruff, so I may understand? Suppose you found Miss Verinder unaccountably interested in what has happened to Mr. Ablewhite and Mr. Luker? Suppose she asked strange questions about this scandal and displayed ungovernable agitation upon discovering its direction?"

"Suppose anything you please, Miss Clack; it wouldn't shake my belief in Rachel Verinder by a hair's-breadth."

"She is so absolutely to be relied upon as that?"

"So absolutely to be relied upon as that."

"Then permit me to inform you, Mr. Bruff, that Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite was in this house not two hours ago, and his entire innocence of any concern in the disappearance of the Moonstone was proclaimed by Miss Verinder herself in the strongest language I have ever heard from a young lady."

I enjoyed the triumph—the unholy triumph, I fear I must admit—of seeing Mr. Bruff utterly confounded. He started to his feet and stared at me in silence. I remained seated, undisturbed, and recounted the entire scene. "And what do you say about Mr. Ablewhite *now*?" I asked with utmost gentleness.

"If Rachel has testified to his innocence, Miss Clack, I don't scruple to say I believe in his innocence as firmly as you do. I have been misled by appearances, like the rest of the world; and I will make the best atonement I can by publicly contradicting the scandal assailing your friend wherever I meet it. In the meantime, allow me to congratulate you on the masterly manner in which you have opened the full fire of your batteries on me at the moment I least expected it. You would have done great things in my profession, ma'am, if you had happened to be a man."

With those words, he turned away, pacing irritably up and down the room.

I could plainly see that the new light I had thrown on the subject greatly surprised and disturbed him. Certain expressions dropped from his lips as he became absorbed in his own thoughts, suggesting the abominable view he had previously taken of the mystery. He had not scrupled to suspect Mr. Godfrey of stealing the Diamond and attribute Rachel's conduct to a generous resolve to conceal the crime. Now, on Miss Verinder's own authority—an unassailable authority in Mr. Bruff's estimation—that explanation was shown to be utterly wrong.
