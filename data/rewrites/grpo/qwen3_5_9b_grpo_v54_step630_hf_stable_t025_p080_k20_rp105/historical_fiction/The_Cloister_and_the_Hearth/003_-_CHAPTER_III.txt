A grave, white-haired seneschal approached their table, his demeanor courteous as he inquired if Gerard Eliassoen was among their party. Upon receiving confirmation, he announced that the Princess Marie wished to confer with him and would conduct him to her presence. Instantly, every face within hearing turned sharply toward the man destined to meet royalty, etched with a mixture of curiosity and envy.

Gerard rose to obey.

"I wager we shall not see you again," Margaret said calmly, though a faint flush colored her cheeks.

"That you will," the seneschal replied, leaning close to whisper, "This is my good princess; but you are my queen." Turning to the group, he added aloud, "Wait for me, I pray you; I will presently return."

"Ay, ay!" Peter exclaimed, waking from his reverie and speaking in unison with the others.

Once Gerard was gone, the pair, whose attire was so plainly homely compared to the man the Princess had summoned, became the center of neighboring gazes. Observing this shift in attention, William Johnson stepped forward, feigning surprise to claim his relation.

"And to think that there was I at your backs, and you saw me not," he remarked.

"Nay, cousin Johnson, I saw you long since," Margaret replied coldly.

"You saw me, and spoke not to me?"

"Cousin, it was for you to welcome us to Rotterdam, as it is for us to welcome you at Sevenbergen. Your servant denied us a seat in your house."

"The idiot!" Johnson muttered.

"And I had a mind to see whether it was 'like maid like master,' for there is sooth in bywords." William Johnson blushed a deep purple. He sensed Margaret's keenness and suspected her motives. Under the circumstances, he chose the wisest course: trusting to deeds rather than words, he insisted they accompany him home immediately, promising to show them whether they were truly welcome to Rotterdam.

"Who doubts it, cousin? Who doubts it?" the scholar interjected.

Margaret thanked him graciously but demurred to leave at once, claiming she wished to hear the minstrels again. After a quarter-hour, Johnson renewed his proposal, noting that many guests had departed. Then her true reason surfaced.

"It were ill manners to our friend; and he will lose us. He knows not where we lodge in Rotterdam, and the city is vast, and we have parted company once already."

"Oh!" Johnson responded. "We will provide for that. My young man—ahem! I mean my secretary—shall sit here and wait, and bring him on to my house. He shall lodge with me and with no other."

"Cousin, we shall be too burdensome."

"Nay, nay; you shall see whether you are welcome or not, you and your friends, and your friends' friends, if need be; and I shall hear what the Princess would with him."

Margaret felt a thrill of joy that Gerard might be lodged under the same roof as her, yet a slight misgiving followed. "But if your young man should be thoughtless, and go play, and Gerard miss him?"

"He go play? Leave that spot where I put him, and bid him stay? Ho! Stand forth, Hans Cloterman."

A figure clad in black serge and dark violet hose arose, taking two steps to stand before them without moving a muscle. He was a solemn, precise young man, the very statue of gravity and starched propriety. At his appearance, Margaret, overwhelmed by happiness, could hardly maintain her composure. Yet she whispered to Johnson, "I would put my hand in the fire for him. We are at your command, cousin, as soon as you have given him his orders."

Hans was instructed to sit at the table and wait for Gerard, conducting him to Ooster-Waagen Straet. He replied not in words, but by calmly taking the indicated seat. Margaret, Peter, and William Johnson then departed together.

"And, indeed, it is time you were abed, father, after all your travel," Margaret said, a thought that had occupied her mind all along.

Hans Cloterman sat waiting for Gerard, solemn and businesslike. Minutes flew by, yet excited no impatience in that perfect young man. Johnson did him no more than justice when he laughed to scorn the idea of his secretary leaving his post or neglecting his duty in pursuit of sport or youthful hilarity.

As Gerard remained long in coming, the patient Hans, with his employer's eye no longer upon him, improved the time by quaffing goblets of Corsican wine at solemn, silent, and accurately measured intervals. The wine was strong, and so was Cloterman's resolve. Gerard had been gone nearly an hour ere the model secretary imbibed the notion that Creation expected him to toast the health of all good fellows and the Duke of Burgundy present. With this view, he filled a bumper nine times, then rose gingerly but solemnly and slowly. Having reached his full height, he instantly rolled onto the grass, goblet in hand, spilling the cold liquor on more than one ankle—whose owners frisked—but not disturbing a muscle in his own long face. In the total eclipse of reason, his gravity, primness, and infallibility remained intact.

The seneschal led Gerard through several passages to the door of the pavilion, where young noblemen, embroidered and feathered, sat sentinel guarding the heir-apparent, playing cards by the red light of torches held by their servants. A whisper from the seneschal caused one of them to rise reluctantly, stare at Gerard with haughty surprise, and enter the pavilion. He presently returned, beckoning the pair, and led them through a passage or two into an ante-chamber. There sat three more young gentlemen, feathered, furred, and embroidered like pieces of fancy work, deeply engrossed in that instructive and edifying branch of learning known as dice.

"You can't see the Princess—it is too late," one declared.

Another followed suit: "She passed this way but now with her nurse. She is gone to bed, doll and all. Deuce—ace again!"

Gerard prepared to retire. The seneschal, with an incredulous smile, replied, "The young man is here by the Countess's orders; be so good as conduct him to her ladies."

On this, a superb Adonis rose with an injured look and led Gerard into a room where eleven ladies sat or lolloped, chattering like magpies. Two, more industrious than the rest, played cat's-cradle with fingers as nimble as their tongues. At the sight of a stranger, all tongues stopped like one piece of complicated machinery, and all eyes turned on Gerard, as if the same string that checked the tongues had turned the eyes. Gerard was ill at ease before, but this battery of eyes discountenanced him further, and his gaze dropped to the ground. Then, finding, like hares running by ponds and frogs scuttling into water, that there was a creature they could frighten, the cowards giggled and enjoyed their prowess. A duenna then said severely, "Mesdames!" and they were all abashed at once, as though a modesty string had been pulled. This same duenna took Gerard and marched before him in solemn silence. The young man's heart sank, and he had half a mind to turn and run out of the place.

"What must princes be," he thought, "when their courtiers are so freezing? Doubtless they take their breeding from him they serve." These reflections were interrupted by the duenna suddenly introducing him into a room where three ladies sat working and a pretty little girl tuned a lute. The ladies were richly but not showily dressed. The duenna approached the one hemming a kerchief and spoke a few words in a low tone. This lady then turned toward Gerard with a smile and beckoned him to come near. She did not rise, but she laid aside her work, and her manner of turning toward him, slight as the movement was, was full of grace, ease, and courtesy. She began a conversation at once.

"Margaret Van Eyck is an old friend of mine, sir, and I am right glad to have a letter from her hand, and thankful to you, sir, for bringing it to me safely. Marie, my love, this is the gentleman who brought you that pretty miniature."

"Sir, I thank you a thousand times," the young lady said.

"I am glad you feel her debtor, sweetheart, for our friend would have us to do him a little service in return."

"I will do anything on earth for him," the young lady replied with ardor.

"Anything on earth is nothing in the world," the Countess of Charolois said quietly.

"Well, then, I will—What would you have me to do, sir?"

Gerard had just discovered the nature of the high society in which he stood. "My sovereign demoiselle," he said gently, a little tremulously, "where there have been no pains, there needs no reward."

"But we must obey mamma. All the world must obey."

"That is true. Then, our demoiselle, reward me, if you will, by letting me hear the stave you were going to sing and I did interrupt it."

"What! You love music, sir?"

"I adore it."

The little princess looked inquiringly at her mother and received a smile of assent. She then took her lute and sang a romaunt of the day. Although but twelve years old, she was a well-taught and painstaking musician. Her little claw swept the chords with courage and precision, striking out the notes of the arpeggio clear, distinct, and bright, like twinkling stars; but the main charm was her voice. It was not mighty, but it was round, clear, full, and ringing like a bell. She sang with a certain modest eloquence, though knowing none of the tricks of feeling. She was too young to be theatrical, or even sentimental, so nothing was forced—all gushed. Her little mouth seemed the mouth of Nature. The ditty, too, was as pure as its utterance. There were none of those false divisions, those whining slurs now sold so dearly by Italian songsters, though every jackal in India delivers them gratis to his customers all night, and sometimes gets shot for them, and always deserves it. So there were no cadences and fiorituri, the trite, turgid, and feeble expletives of song, the skim-milk with which mindless musicians and mindless writers quench fire, wash out color, and drown melody and meaning dead.

While the pure and tender strain flowed from the pure young throat, Gerard's eyes filled. The Countess watched him with interest, for it was usual to applaud the Princess loudly, but not with cheek and eye. So when the voice ceased and the glasses left off ringing, she asked demurely, "Was he content?"

Gerard gave a little start; the spoken voice broke the charm and brought him back to earth.

"Oh, madam!" he cried, "surely it is thus that cherubs and seraphs sing, and charm the saints in heaven."

"I am somewhat of your opinion, my young friend," the Countess said, with emotion, and she bent a look of love and gentle pride upon her girl—a heavenly look, such as, they say, is given to the eye of the short-lived resting on the short-lived.

The Countess resumed: "My old friend requests me to be serviceable to you. It is the first favor she has done us the honor of asking, and the request is sacred. You are in holy orders, sir?"

Gerard bowed.

"I fear you are not a priest; you look too young."

"Oh no, madam; I am not even a sub-deacon. I am only a lector; but next month I shall be an exorcist, and before long an acolyte."

"Well, Monsieur Gerard, with your accomplishments you can soon pass through the inferior orders. And let me beg you to do so. For the day after you have said your first mass, I shall have the pleasure of appointing you to a benefice."

"Oh, madam!"

"And, Marie, remember I make this promise in your name as well as my own."

"Fear not, mamma: I will not forget. But if he will take my advice, what he will be is Bishop of Liege. The Bishop of Liege is a beautiful bishop. What! Do you not remember him, mamma, that day we were at Liege? He was braver than grandpapa himself. He had on a crown, a high one, and it was cut in the middle, and it was full of oh! such beautiful jewels; and his gown stiff with gold; and his mantle, too; and it had a broad border, all pictures; but, above all, his gloves; you have no such gloves, mamma. They were embroidered and covered with jewels, and scented with such lovely scent; I smelt them all the time he was giving me his blessing on my head with them. Dear old man! I dare say he will die soon, most old people do, and then, sir, you can be bishop, you know, and wear—"

"Gently, Marie, gently: bishoprics are for old gentlemen; and this is a young gentleman."

"Mamma! He is not so very young."

"Not compared with you, Marie, eh?"

"He is a good birth, dear mamma; and I am sure he is good enough for a bishop."

"Alas! mademoiselle, you are mistaken."

"I know not that, Monsieur Gerard; but I am a little puzzled to know on what grounds mademoiselle there pronounces your character so boldly."

"Alas, mamma," said the Princess, "you have not looked at his face, then;" and she raised her eyebrows at her mother's simplicity.
