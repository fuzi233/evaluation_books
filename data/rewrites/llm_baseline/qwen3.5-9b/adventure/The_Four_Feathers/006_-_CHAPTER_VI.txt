It was the night of August 30. A month had elapsed since the ball at Lennon House, yet the quiet countryside of Donegal remained abuzz with the sensational topic of Harry Feversham's vanishing act. Townsfolk in the steep lanes and gentlemen at their dinner tables gossiped with relish. Rumors flew thick and fast: Harry had allegedly been spotted the very morning after the dance, around six o'clock—though Mrs. Brien insisted it was ten past—but dressed in formal attire, his face pale as a suicide's, rushing along the causeway near Lennon Bridge. Some whispered that only a drag-net could unravel the mystery. Mr. Dennis Rafferty, residing on the road to Rathmullen, reportedly refused salmon on the grounds that he was no longer a cannibal, a sentiment that gained widespread traction. Speculation ran rampant regarding the cause of his disappearance, yet the truth remained locked behind two doors, guarded by those who carried on their daily lives with a steely resolve, their heads held slightly higher than usual. Ethne had softened somewhat, Dermod grown more irritable, but otherwise, the household stood unchanged against the tide of rumor.

While gossip reigned supreme in Donegal, reality unfolded elsewhere. Harry Feversham was in London, a fact Lieutenant Sutch confirmed on the night of the 30th. That day, the city had trembled under rumors of a massive clash at Kassassin, east of Ismailia. Messengers sprinted through the streets, heralding tales of triumph and defeat alike. News circulated of General Drury-Lowe's cavalry charging under the moonlight, rolling up Arabi's flank and capturing his artillery. Whispers claimed an English general had fallen and the York and Lancaster Regiment had been decimated. London grew uneasy; by eleven o'clock, a hushed, expectant crowd had gathered beneath the gaslamps of Pall Mall, staring upward at the illuminated blinds of the War Office. Silence hung heavy, broken only by the fleeting shadow of a figure crossing the glass, sending a ripple of anticipation through the throng.

Lieutenant Sutch, mindful of his injured leg, stood near the edge of the crowd, his back to the railing of the Junior Carlton Club, when a touch on his arm startled him. Beside him stood Harry Feversham. Harry's face was agitated, unnaturally white, his eyes burning with a feverish intensity. Sutch initially struggled to recognize the man speaking to him.

"I might have been out there in Egypt tonight," Harry said, his voice quick and troubled. "Think of it! I could have been sitting by a campfire in the desert, debating the battle with Jack Durrance—or dead. What difference would it have made? I might have been in Egypt tonight!"

Feversham's sudden appearance and erratic demeanor signaled to Sutch that his friend's situation had deteriorated severely. Questions surged in Sutch's mind, but he asked none. Instead, he took Harry's arm and guided him away from the press.

"I saw you in the crowd," Harry continued. "I wanted to speak to you because... do you recall, some time ago, you gave me your card? I've kept it safe, fearing I'd eventually need it. You said that if one was in trouble, reaching out might help."

Sutch halted. "Let us go inside. We can find a quiet corner in the upper smoking-room." As Harry looked up, he realized they were at the entrance of the Army and Navy Club.

"Good heavens, not there!" Harry cried sharply, pulling away and stepping into the dimly lit roadway where shadows obscured his features.

Sutch limped after him. "Not tonight. It's too late. Tomorrow, in a secluded spot, after dark. I avoid the daylight."

Once again, Sutch withheld his questions. "I know a quiet restaurant," he offered. "If we dine there at nine, we won't encounter anyone we know. I'll meet you just before nine tomorrow night at the corner of Swallow Street."

As planned, they met the following evening in the corner of the Criterion grill-room. Feversham scanned the room anxiously upon entering.

"I frequent this place when in town," Sutch noted calmly. "Listen."

The rhythmic thrumming of the electric motors powering the lights vibrated through the floor, audible and palpable.

"It sounds like a ship," Sutch smiled. "I can almost imagine myself back in the gunroom. Let us eat. Afterward, you may tell me your story."

"You haven't heard anything?" Harry asked, suspicion coloring his tone.

"Not a word," Sutch replied. Relief washed over Harry; he had feared everyone knew. He imagined disdain etched onto every passing face. Sutch appeared even more preoccupied than the previous night. In the better light, Harry looked gaunt and exhausted, black circles framing his eyes. He breathed heavily, moving with a restless, feverish energy, his nerves seemingly stretched to snapping point. Twice between courses, Harry attempted to begin his tale, but Sutch waited until the dishes were cleared.

"Now," Sutch said, offering his cigar case. "Take your time, Harry."

Harry recounted the entire truth, omitting nothing and exaggerating nothing. He forced himself into a slow, deliberate, factual narration, creating an illusion that he was merely sharing a stranger's misfortune to pass the time. He began with the Crimean night at Broad Place and concluded with the ball at Lennon House.

"I returned across Lough Swilly early that morning," Harry finished. "I traveled immediately to London. Since then, I have confined myself to my rooms all day, listening to the bugles calling in the barracks below my window. At night, I wander the streets or lie awake waiting for the Westminster clock to strike. On foggy evenings, I can hear the steam-sirens on the river. Do you know when the ducks begin to quack in St. James's Park?" he asked with a dry laugh. "Precisely at two o'clock."

Sutch listened intently, uninterrupted. However, midway through the narrative, his posture shifted significantly. Until Harry mentioned concealing the telegram, Sutch had sat with his arms folded, eyes fixed on his companion. Now, he raised a hand to shield his forehead, keeping his face partially hidden as the final details emerged. Harry sensed the reason instantly: Sutch was struggling to suppress the scorn he felt, unable to control the tension in his facial muscles. Yet, Harry revealed nothing, continuing steadily and honestly to the end.

Even after Harry finished, Sutch remained silent, his hand still raised. When he finally spoke, his words struck Harry with shock. There was no contempt in his voice; instead, it trembled with profound remorse.

"I am deeply at fault," Sutch admitted. "I should have spoken that night at Broad Place, and I held my tongue. I shall hardly forgive myself."

The realization that this was Muriel Graham's son, whose actions had brought ruin and shame, weighed heavily on the lieutenant. He felt he had failed a duty he had imposed upon himself, yet one that was undeniably real.

"You see, I understood," Sutch continued, his voice filled with regret. "I'm afraid your father never did."

"He never will," Harry interjected.

"No," Sutch agreed. "Your mother, had she lived, would have seen clearly; but few women, I believe, save your mother. Brute courage! Women deify it. That girl, for instance..."

"You must not blame her," Harry interrupted. "I was deceiving her into marriage."

Sutch removed his hand from his forehead abruptly. "Suppose you had never met her. Would you still have submitted your resignation?"

"I think not," Harry answered slowly. "I wish to be fair. Disgracing my name and the memory of those dead men in the hall—I would have risked it. But I could not risk disgracing her."

Sutch slammed his fist despairingly onto the table. "If only I had spoken that night! Harry, why didn't you let me speak? I could have spared you countless years of torment. Good heavens, what a childhood you must have endured, carrying that fear entirely alone. It makes me shiver to think of it. I might even have prevented this latest catastrophe. For I understood. I understood."

Sutch perceived the dark corners of Harry Feversham's psyche more clearly than Harry did himself. Because he saw so clearly, he felt no contempt. The long years of isolation at Broad Place, living under the gaze of an incomprehensible father and the relentless portraits of the dead, had caused the damage. There had been no confidant. The paralyzing fear of cowardice had eroded his heart relentlessly. He had carried it everywhere, into his bed, haunting his dreams, acting as a constant, menacing shadow. It had driven him away from friends, terrified that an impulsive word might expose him. Sutch did not marvel that this had culminated in an irreversible error; he comprehended it fully.

"Have you ever read *Hamlet*?" Sutch asked.

"Of course," Harry replied.
