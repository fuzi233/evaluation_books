As Lady Castlewood and her children withdrew through one door of the saloon, Lord Castlewood made his exit by another, leaving Harry Warrington standing beneath the ancestral portrait above the fireplace, his face flushed with a boy’s fervor. The demure eyes that had been fixed upon a tambour frame now lifted, regarding him with a playful scrutiny.

"Why did you refuse to accompany our aunt, cousin?" Lady Maria inquired, her needle pausing mid-stitch.

"Because you bade me stay," Harry replied, his earnestness undimmed by her teasing tone.

"Bade you stay? La, child! Must every idle word a lady speaks be taken as law? If all Virginian gentlemen are so obedient, your country must be a paradise for our sex!"

"You said—" Harry’s voice trembled, "that night on the terrace—"

"Ah, that sweet evening!" she sighed, feigning innocence.

"—when you gave me this rose," he burst out, producing a wilted bloom from his waistcoat, "that if I left, I would forget you. But I swear by heaven, I never could!"

"All men forget," she murmured, casting her eyes down.

"Not in Virginia," Harry declared, his passion undeterred. "I would sooner lose a limb than deny my aunt—yet I could not bear to leave you. If you bade me leap from yonder window, I would do it. If you asked for my blood, I would spill it gladly."

"La! Must you crush my hand so, you foolish boy?" she protested, though she did not pull away.

Harry, heedless of decorum, pressed on. "Ever since that night, I have scarcely slept. I long to prove myself—to fight giants, to conquer kingdoms—if only for your sake."

She laughed, a practiced, musical sound. "Men always speak so when they are near. And when they are gone? They forget."

"Not I," he vowed. "I have tired of games, of books—of everything but you. Before I knew you, I longed for glory in battle. Now, there is only one thing I desire."

"You silly creature! Do you not realize I am nearly old enough to—"

"I know your age," he interrupted. "I’ve seen the family Bible. I know the dates, the names. Nothing changes what I feel."

At this, her composure faltered. "Who showed you?" she demanded, her voice sharp. "Was it my mother-in-law, that grasping harpy? Or my aunt, the moralist who—"

"It was no one," Harry interjected, startled by her vehemence.

She checked herself, smoothing her skirts. "Well, no matter. I only wish you to think well of me, Harry."

His hand found hers, and he pressed it fervently. "Angel," he breathed, lost in the depths of her gaze—those practiced, calculating eyes that mirrored back his own devotion.

Had he been wiser, he might have seen the artifice beneath the charm. But youth is ever blind. To Harry, she was divinity; to her, he was but another conquest, a fleeting amusement in the long, dull game of her life.

---

Later, in the solitude of his chamber, Harry penned a letter home—a carefully edited account of his days at Castlewood, omitting certain tender interludes.

*"The Baroness de Bernstein has shown me every kindness,"* he wrote, *"and I pray my mother will reconcile with her. Lady Maria is beloved by all, especially the poor, whom she visits often..."*

He paused, recalling the rose, now crumbling in his pocket. How had it come to him? The details scarcely mattered. Such stories were as old as time—a glance, a whispered word, a trinket given and cherished.

The ink dried on the page. Some truths were best left unwritten.
