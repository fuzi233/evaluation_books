I’ve been trying to decide how best to begin this story, and I’ve tried two methods: first, scratching my head—useless. Second, asking my daughter Penelope—for which I’m now profoundly grateful. Her idea is simple: lay out the events day by day, starting from the moment we heard that Mr. Franklin Blake was coming to visit. It’s astonishing how memories surface when you fix your mind on a particular date. The only problem is finding those dates in the first place. Fortunately, Penelope offers to help—she keeps a diary, started at school and continued ever since. When I suggested she might tell the story herself from her own entries, she gave me a fierce look and said, with such conviction, “Fiddlesticks!” I asked what she meant. She replied, “Sweethearts.” So there it is.

Following her plan, I’ll start with Wednesday, May 24th, 1848. That morning, I was summoned to my lady’s private sitting-room. She sat calmly, a letter in hand.

“Gabriel,” she said, “here is news that will surprise you. Franklin Blake has returned from abroad. He’s been staying with his father in London and will be joining us tomorrow—staying until next month, to celebrate Rachel’s birthday.”

Had I been holding a hat, I’d have thrown it straight into the air—not from disrespect, but sheer astonishment. I hadn’t seen Mr. Franklin since he was a boy, growing up in our house. As I recall him, he was the most delightful child—one of the nicest boys ever to spin a top or break a window. Miss Rachel, who was present, immediately corrected me. “Oh, no,” she said, “I remember him as the most tyrannical boy alive—the cruellest driver of an exhausted little girl in string harness England has ever produced.” She added, “I burn with indignation, and I ache with fatigue, just thinking of Franklin Blake.”

Now you may ask: why had he been away so long? The answer lies in his father’s misfortune. My lady’s eldest sister married Mr. Blake—a man famed for both his vast wealth and his endless legal battle for a dukedom. For years, he wore down the courts, draining countless lawyers’ pockets and dividing families over whether he had any rightful claim. His wife died. Two of their three children died. Still, the courts refused to act. In the end, they upheld the Duke in possession. And then Mr. Blake realized the only way to repay his country for its injustice was to deny it the privilege of educating his son. “How can I trust my native institutions,” he declared, “after the way they’ve treated me?” Add to that his deep dislike of all boys—including his own—and the result was inevitable. Young Franklin was taken from England and sent to Germany, where his father believed education could be trusted. Mr. Blake himself remained in England, serving in Parliament and publishing a statement about the Duke—which remains unfinished to this day.

Thank God, that’s settled. We need not dwell on Mr. Blake senior. Let us focus instead on the Diamond—and the role Franklin played in bringing it into our home.

Our young master never forgot us after leaving. He wrote often—sometimes to my lady, sometimes to Miss Rachel, sometimes to me. Before he left, we’d had a small transaction: he borrowed a ball of string, a four-bladed knife, and seven-and-sixpence—of which I’ve seen neither the money nor the coin again. His letters mostly concerned further borrowing. From my lady, I learned of his progress abroad. After mastering German institutions, he turned to France, then Italy. Together, they shaped him into something of a universal genius—writing, painting, singing, composing—though I suspect he borrowed skills as readily as he borrowed money. When he came of age, he inherited his mother’s fortune—seven hundred pounds a year—but it vanished as quickly as water through a sieve. The more he had, the more he wanted. There was a hole in Mr. Franklin’s pocket nothing could mend. Wherever he went, his charm made him welcome. He lived here, there, everywhere. His address, as he put it, was “Post Office, Europe—to be left till called for.” Twice he decided to return to England to see us; twice, some unmentionable woman blocked his path. The third time succeeded—just as my lady told me.

On Thursday, May 25th, we were to meet the grown man who had once been our dear boy. He was of good blood, brave, and twenty-five years old by English reckoning. Now you know as much about Mr. Franklin Blake as I did before he arrived.

That Thursday was one of the finest summer days imaginable. My lady and Miss Rachel, expecting Franklin not until dinner, drove out to lunch with friends nearby. Once they were gone, I inspected the bedroom prepared for our guest—everything in order. As butler and steward (a position I insisted upon, especially to keep control of the late Sir John’s cellar), I fetched a bottle of our fine Latour claret and set it in the warm air to warm before dinner. Then, reasoning that if wine benefits from warmth, so might an old man, I took my beehive chair to sit in the back court. But just as I rose, I heard a soft, rhythmic beat—like a drum—on the terrace facing the house.

I walked around and saw three men of mahogany complexion, dressed in white linen frocks and trousers, staring up at the house. Closer inspection revealed each carried a small hand-drum. Behind them stood a delicate, light-haired English boy with a bag. I assumed they were traveling conjurors, the boy carrying their tools. One of the men, who spoke English and behaved with surprising elegance, confirmed my guess. He politely requested permission to perform for my lady.

Now, I am not a grumpy man. I enjoy amusement and have no prejudice against those darker-skinned than myself. But even the most open-minded have weaknesses—and mine is this: when I know a family plate-basket sits uncovered on a pantry shelf, the sight of a stranger with refined manners instantly reminds me of that basket.

I informed the man that my lady was out—and warned them all to leave the premises. He bowed beautifully and they departed.

I returned to my chair, sat in the sun, and soon drifted into a state close to sleep. Then—my daughter Penelope burst out of the house as if fire had broken out. What did she want?

She demanded the immediate arrest of the Indian jugglers. Why? Because, she claimed, they knew Franklin was coming—and intended harm.

Franklin’s name woke me. I asked her to explain.

She had just returned from the lodge, where she’d been chatting with the lodge-keeper’s daughter. They had watched the Indians leave after I’d dismissed them. Seeing the boy—so pale and fragile—they suspected he was mistreated. So they crept along the hedge and observed from the other side.

What they saw stunned them.

The three men scanned the road, ensuring they were alone. Then they faced the house, stared hard, whispered among themselves, looked at each other uncertainly, and finally turned to the boy—as if seeking answers from him.

Then the chief, speaking English, said: “Hold out your hand.”

Penelope said she didn’t know what kept her heart from leaping from her chest. I thought privately it might have been her stays. All I said was, “You make my flesh creep.” (*Nota bene:* Women appreciate such remarks.)

When the Indian said, “Hold out your hand,” the boy hesitated, shook his head, and said he didn’t like it. The Indian gently asked if he’d prefer to be sent back to London—where they’d found him, sleeping in an empty basket in a market, hungry and abandoned. That ended the resistance. The boy reluctantly held out his hand.

The Indian pulled a bottle from his chest and poured black liquid—like ink—into the boy’s palm. He touched the boy’s head, made signs in the air, and said: “Look.”

The boy froze, statue-still, staring into the ink.

So far, I thought, mere trickery—wasteful of ink, perhaps, but nothing more. I began to doze again—until Penelope’s next words jolted me awake.

The Indians checked the road once more. Then the chief asked: “See the English gentleman from foreign parts?”

The boy replied: “I see him.”

“Is it on the road to this house, and no other, that he will travel today?”

“It is on the road to this house, and on no other.”

A pause. Then: “Has the English gentleman got *It* about him?”

After another pause, the boy answered: “Yes.”

“Will he come here, as promised, at the close of day?”

“I can’t tell.”

“Why not?”

“I am tired. The mist rises in my head, and puzzles me. I can see no more today.”

Then the chief muttered something in his own tongue, pointed at the boy and toward town, blew on the boy’s forehead, and awakened him with a start. They walked off toward the town—and were never seen again.

Most things have a moral, if you look closely.

My interpretation? First, the chief juggler had heard talk of Franklin’s arrival among the servants—and saw a chance to earn money. Second, they planned to wait for my lady’s return, then reappear and “predict” Franklin’s arrival through magic. Third, Penelope had witnessed their rehearsal—like actors preparing a play. Fourth, I should watch the plate-basket that evening. Fifth, Penelope should calm down and let her father rest in the sun.

That seemed sensible. But young women, as you know, rarely accept reason.

To Penelope, the moral was grave. She reminded me of the third question: “Has the English gentleman got *It* about him?”

“Oh, father!” she cried, clasping her hands. “Don’t joke about this. What does ‘It’ mean?”

“We’ll ask Mr. Franklin,” I said, winking. “If you can wait.”

She took it seriously. I found her earnestness amusing. “What on earth would Mr. Franklin know about it?” I asked.

“Ask him,” she said. “And see if *he* thinks it a laughing matter too.”

With that, she left.

Once she was gone, I decided—I would indeed ask Mr. Franklin. Not to mock, but to reassure her. What passed between us later that day is recorded fully elsewhere. But I warn you now: do not expect humor. You’ll find none. To my great surprise, Mr. Franklin, like Penelope, took it seriously. So seriously, in fact, that when I asked him what “It” could mean, he answered without hesitation: “The Moonstone.”
