The hospital room was a vessel of suspended animation, filled with the sterile rhythm of healing. Sunlight, sharp and uninvited, sliced through the curtains, illuminating dust motes dancing in the stillness. Harry Warrington lay beneath the sheets, his body a map of recent violence, ravaged by blood loss and fever. Hunger gnawed at him, a primal demand that superseded pain.

"Chicken," the doctor's wife announced, her voice carrying the weight of domestic authority. "Prepared by my own daughter."

A flash of memory, jagged and fleeting, pierced the haze. Two figures. Red cheeks, dark hair, a smile that vanished as quickly as it appeared. Then, an older face, stern and distant. The contrast struck him with the force of a physical blow. Pearls were glass. Gold was base metal. Time had stretched into a canyon, separating the boy he once was from the man he feared he had become. He writhed, a silent scream trapped in his throat, the realization settling over him like a shroud.

"What troubles you, my dear?" Mrs. Lambert asked, her tone softening the edges of his distress.

"A cramp," Harry lied, his voice raspy. "Just a spasm in the shoulder."

"You speak to your hosts," she corrected gently. "We are old friends, Mr. Warrington. Your father and my husband served together. We were schoolgirls at Kensington. You were never a stranger to us."

"They are here?" Harry asked, his eyes searching the room.

"At Tunbridge Wells," she replied. "They sent a rider from Reigate yesterday."

He looked down at his bandaged arm, the white cloth stark against his pale skin.

"I have done well by you," the Colonel stated, his presence filling the corner of the room. "Now, Mrs. Lambert and the cook will attend to you."

"Nonsense," the lady interjected. "Theo prepared the chicken and rice herself. Shall Mr. Warrington rise after breakfast? We shall send your valet."

"If whining is proof of loyalty," the Colonel mused, "your servant is a devoted creature indeed."

"He allowed your luggage to depart in your aunt's carriage," Mrs. Lambert noted. "You must wear my husband's linens. They are coarse, I admit."

"Pish!" the Colonel scoffed. "These shirts suit any Christian."

"They are Theo's and Hester's work," the lady added, glancing at her husband with a knowing tilt of her head. "And Theo stitched your sleeve to accommodate your injury."

Harry's gaze drifted to a porcelain vase on the table, overflowing with crimson roses. "Beautiful blooms."

"My daughter cut them this morning," the Colonel laughed. "She did cut them!"

Perhaps the Colonel felt his wife had overstayed her welcome in the narrative of the sickroom, perhaps he simply nudged her toward propriety with a subtle shift of weight.

"And I thought I heard someone singing the Evening Hymn last night," Harry murmured. "Or was it a dream?"

"Theo again," the Colonel chuckled. "Our servants claim your negro began humming it in the kitchen as if he were a church organ."

"Our household sings it," Harry said, his voice dropping. "My grandfather loved it. His wife's father was a friend of Bishop Ken, who composed it. And my brother..." He trailed off, the name hanging in the air.

Mrs. Lambert leaned forward, compelled by the sudden vulnerability in the boy's posture. Her heart, hardened by the cynicism of the last six weeks, softened under the warmth of gratitude and the comfort of a warm meal. The air around him cleared, shedding the taint of selfishness and corruption that had followed him from England. Fate, or perhaps a simple change of circumstance, had placed him in the company of the wise, whose lamps were trimmed and whose hearts kept a modest watch.

The matron withdrew, leaving Harry to consume the rice and chicken, while the Colonel settled beside the bed. Gratitude and satiety aligned his thoughts. He spoke more freely now, breaking his customary silence. He was not a scholar of books, nor did he offer profound commentary on literature, but he possessed a keen eye for dogs, horses, and the mechanics of daily life.

Lambert, a man of shrewd observation, quickly gauged his guest. For the first time, Harry learned that Mrs. Lambert had been a childhood friend of his mother, and that the Colonel's father had fought alongside Harry's grandfather, Colonel Esmond, during Queen Anne's wars. Harry felt the ground shift beneath him; he was no longer an outsider.

"My sons are away," the Colonel explained. "So you have the company of my wife and her daughters. Mrs. Lambert mentioned Theo—the broth, the roses, the mending of your coat. She is not the miracle her mother claims, but she is a competent housekeeper and a cheerful soul."

"It is very kind of Miss Lambert," Harry said.

"She does no more than her duty," the Colonel replied, smiling. "I mock my wife for praising our children, yet I am equally foolish. God has blessed us with dutiful offspring, and I feel no need to hide my thanks. Do you have a sister?"

"No, sir. I am alone."

"Indeed. I apologize for my intrusion. Your valet informed us of your misfortune last year. I served with Braddock in Scotland; I trust he recovered before passing. He was a wild man, yet truthful beneath his rough exterior. Your black servant speaks freely of you, assuming you allow such liberties because he saved your life."

"Saved me?" Harry exclaimed.

"From numerous Indians on that very expedition," the Colonel continued. "Molly and I were unaware we were hosting a man of such wealth. He claims half of Virginia belongs to you. Even if all of North America were yours, we could offer only our best."

"Negroes lie like the father of all lies," Harry countered sharply. "They exaggerate our riches to flatter us. My mother owns a vast estate in England, sufficient for our needs. Our splendor exists only in Mr. Gumbo's delusion. He never rescued me from an Indian; he would flee at the sight of one, just as my brother's boy did on that fatal day."

"The bravest men falter at unlucky moments," the Colonel observed calmly. "I witnessed elite troops rout before a ragged Highland mob at Preston."

"That was because the Highlanders fought for a righteous cause," Harry insisted.

"Do you believe the French Indians fought for a just cause in last year's conflict?" the Colonel asked.

"The scoundrels!" Harry shouted, his fist tightening. "They invaded British territory. But the Highlanders fought for their king."

"We fought for our king, too," the Colonel said, laughing. "And we won."

"Ah!" Harry cried. "If His Royal Highness the Prince had not retreated at Derby, your king and mine would now be King James the Third!"

"Who instilled such Tory sentiments in you, Mr. Warrington?" Lambert asked.

"The Esmonds have always been loyal," Harry answered. "Had we stayed home, twenty years earlier, my brother and I would have wagered our lives for the crown."

"Your neck remains safer on your shoulders than on a pike at Temple Bar," the Colonel remarked grimly. "I have seen them there; they do not look pleasant."

"I shall remove my hat and salute them whenever I pass the gate," Harry declared. "Provided the king and the entire court stand by!"

"I suspect your relative, Lord Castlewood, is less steadfast across the water," the Colonel said, his expression sobering. "Nor is your aunt, the Baroness of Bernstein, who entrusted you to our care. Though her old loyalties may have faded, she has rallied to our side, placing her nephews in the Household and seeking matches for her nieces. If you hold Tory views, Mr. Warrington, heed an old soldier's counsel: keep them to yourself."

"Why, sir, I do not believe you would betray me!" Harry protested.

"Not I, but others might. You did not speak thus at Castlewood, did you? The old Castlewood you came from?"

"I would be safe among my own kin, surely!" Harry cried.

"Doubtless. Yet even kin can play slippery games. Lacquies possess ears as well as masters, spreading tales wherever they go. Consider your black servant; he is eager to recount everything he knows, and much more besides."

"Did he mention the broken-kneed horse?" Harry blurted out, flushing red.

"To be frank, my groom seemed aware of the tale, remarking it was shameful for a gentleman to sell such a beast, especially a cousin. I am not here to act as a mentor or judge servants' gossip. Once you have seen more of your cousins, you will form your own judgment. Until then, follow an old soldier's advice: be cautious with whom you deal and what you say."

Shortly thereafter, Harry rose with the help of Gumbo, his valet. For the hundredth time, Harry promised a severe beating if Gumbo ever repeated his master's affairs in the servants' hall—a vow Gumbo swore to uphold eternally, even as he likely chattered every detail of the Castlewood secrets to the Colonel's kitchen staff. The hostess had undoubtedly heard fragments of these stories, though one cannot blame a lady of her station for curiosity regarding neighbors. After all, how does one stop servants from talking, or from listening when the faithful ones speak directly to you?
