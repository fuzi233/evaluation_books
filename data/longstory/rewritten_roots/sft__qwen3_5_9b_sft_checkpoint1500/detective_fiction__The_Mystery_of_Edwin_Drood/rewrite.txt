"A cathedral!" cried the man, his voice a jagged tear through the fog of his return. "An ancient English Cathedral Tower! But how can it be here?"

His vision swam, fractured by a nightmare that defied geography. Before him stood the massive, gray square tower of St. Swithin's, looming where it could not possibly exist. Between his eyes and the spire hung a single, grotesque spike of rusted iron, piercing the impossible air. Was it a torture device set up by the Sultan to impale a horde of Turkish robbers? Cymbals clashed in the distance, and a long procession wound through the haze, led by the Sultan himself. Ten thousand scimitars flashed in the sunlight, and thirty thousand dancing girls strewed flowers over carpets of white elephants caparisoned in colors too gorgeous for earth. Yet, despite the grandeur, the Cathedral Tower remained stubbornly fixed in the background, a ghost of a place that did not exist. And upon the grim spike, no writhing figure was to be seen.

"Stay!" he thought, the illusion shifting. "Is this spike merely the twisted, rusty nail atop a post of an old bedstead that has tumbled awry?" A vague, drowsy laughter seemed to bubble within him, a sound dedicated solely to considering this absurdity.

Then, reality crashed down. Shaking from head to toe, the man—whose scattered consciousness had just been pieced together by fantasy—at length rose. He supported his trembling frame with his arms and looked around. He was in the meanest, most stifling of small rooms. Through a ragged window-curtain, the pale, miserably thin light of early day stole in from a foul courtyard. He lay, fully dressed, across a large, unseemly bed, upon a bedstead that had indeed given way under the weight pressing upon it. Also lying, fully dressed and stretched across the mattress not lengthwise but sideways, were a Chinaman, a Lascar, and a haggard woman.

The first two were in a deep stupor, lost to the ether. The third, however, was alive. She blew into a clay pipe, concentrating the red spark of burning coal against the bowl, using the dim glow as a lantern to illuminate the scene. Her lean hand shaded the flame, casting long, dancing shadows.

"Another?" she whispered, her voice querulous and rattling like dry leaves. "Have another?"

He sat up, placing a hand to his throbbing forehead.

"You've smoked as many as five since you came in at midnight," the woman continued, her tone chronic and complaining. "Poor me, poor me, my head is so bad. Those two came in after you. Ah, poor me, the business is slack, is slack! There are few Chinamen about the Docks, and even fewer Lascars, and no ships coming in, they say! Here is another ready for you, deary. You'll remember like a good soul, won't you, that the market price is *dreffle* high just now? More'n three shillings and sixpence for a thimbleful! And you'll remember that nobody but me—and Jack Chinaman over yonder, though he can't do it half as well as me—has the true secret of mixing it? You'll pay up accordingly, deary, won't you?"

She blew at the pipe as she spoke, occasionally bubbling deeply before inhaling great clouds of smoke.

"Oh me, oh me, my lungs are weak, my lungs are bad!" she lamented. "It's nearly ready for you, deary. Ah, poor me, my poor hand shakes like it's gonna drop off! I saw you coming-to, and I said to myself, 'I'll get another ready for him, and he'll bear in mind the market price of opium, and pay accordingly.' Oh, my poor head! I make my pipes out of old penny ink-bottles, you see, deary—this is one—and I fit in a mouthpiece this way, and I take my mixture out of this thimble with this little horn spoon; and so I fill, deary. She handed him the nearly emptied pipe and sank back, turning her face onto the pillow.

He rose unsteadily, laid the pipe upon the hearth-stone, and drew back the ragged curtain. With a look of profound repugnance, he surveyed his three companions. He noted that the woman had smoked herself into a strange, uncanny likeness of the Chinaman beside her. The curve of his cheek, the slant of his eye, the pallor of his temples—all were repeated in her distorted features. The Chinaman convulsively wrestled with one of his many gods or devils, perhaps, snarling horribly. The Lascar laughed, saliva dripping from his lips. The hostess lay still, a statue of smoke and misery.

"What visions can *she* have?" the waking man mused, turning her face toward him and standing over it. "Visions of butcher shops and public houses? Of mountains of credit? Of an increase of hideous customers, and this horrible bedstead set upright again, and this horrible court swept clean? To what height can she rise, under any quantity of opium, higher than that!—Eh?"

He bent his ear close to catch her mutterings.

"Unintelligible!"

Watching the spasmodic jerks and darts that broke out of her face and limbs, like fitful lightning flashing from a dark sky, a contagion seized upon him. It was as if an unclean spirit of imitation had infected him. Driven back, he retreated to a lean armchair by the hearth—a seat placed there, perhaps, for emergencies—and sat down, clutching his sides until he had conquered the feverish urge to mimic her.

Then, with a sudden resolve, he returned. He pounced on the Chinaman, seizing him by the throat with both hands and throwing him violently backward onto the bed. The Chinaman clawed at the aggressive hands, resisting with a guttural gasp and frantic protests.

"What do you say?" the man demanded, his voice cutting through the haze.

A watchful pause ensued.

"Unintelligible!"

Slowly loosening his grip as he listened to the incoherent jargon with an attentive frown, he turned to the Lascar and fairly dragged him forth onto the floor. As the Lascar fell, he started into a half-risen attitude, glaring with wild eyes and lashing about him fiercely with his arms, drawing a phantom knife. There had been enough chattering and clattering between them, yet it amounted to nothing. When any distinct word had been flung into the air, it possessed neither sense nor sequence. There are reasons, obvious to those who follow the thread of this tale, why the ancient cathedral town must wear a mask. In these pages, it shall be known as Cloisterham. To the Druids it bore another name; to the Romans, another still; and the Saxons and Normans added their own layers to the pile. Yet, through centuries of shifting tides, the dust of the chronicles remains largely unchanged.

Cloisterham is an ancient city, ill-suited for anyone with a hunger for the noisy world. It is a monument to silence, deriving a heavy, earthy flavor from the vast crypt of its Cathedral. Here, children grow small salads in the dust of abbot and abbess, fashioning dirt pies from the memories of nuns and friars. In the outlying fields, every ploughman pays a grim tribute to the Lord Treasurer, the Archbishop, and the Bishop, grinding their bones into bread much like the Ogre in the nursery tales who ground the bones of his uninvited guests.

It is a drowsy city, inhabited by souls who seem to believe, with a strangeness that defies explanation, that all change lies behind them and none ahead. The streets are so silent that on a summer's day, the blinds of the shops dare not flap in the gentle south wind. Sun-browned tramps, passing through, quicken their limp merely to escape the confines of its oppressive respectability—a feat easily achieved, for the streets of Cloisterham amount to little more than one narrow artery leading in and out, flanked by disappointing yards with pumps and no thoroughfare, save for the Cathedral Close and a paved Quaker settlement that resembles a Quakeress's bonnet in a shady corner.

In essence, Cloisterham is a city of a bygone era. Its hoarse cathedral bell, the rooks circling the tower, and the darker, indistinct birds nesting in the stalls below speak of times long past. Fragments of old walls, saint's chapels, chapter-houses, and convents have been clumsily incorporated into modern houses and gardens, much like the jumbled, obsolete ideas that clutter the minds of its citizens. Everything here belongs to the past. Even the sole pawnbroker, who has taken no pledges for years, vainly offers an unredeemed stock of dim, pale old watches sweating in the heat, tarnished sugar-tongs with useless legs, and volumes of dismal books. The only evidence of progress in Cloisterham is vegetable life in the gardens; even its drooping, despondent little theatre possesses a poor strip of garden where the devil ducks from his stage into the underworld, hidden among scarlet beans or oyster shells depending on the season.

Dominating the center of this stagnant town stands the Nuns' House. A venerable brick edifice, its current name likely derived from a forgotten legend of its conventual origins. Upon the gate enclosing its courtyard shines a resplendent brass plate declaring: "Seminary for Young Ladies. Miss Twinkleton." The house-front is so weather-worn, and the brass plate so glaringly modern, that the result reminds the imaginative stranger of a battered old beau sporting a large, modern eyeglass in his blind eye.

Whether the nuns of yore, being of a submissive rather than stiff-necked temperament, habitually bowed their contemplative heads to avoid colliding with the low beams of their chambers; whether they sat in long, low windows counting beads for mortification rather than adorning themselves with them; or whether they were ever walled up alive in the building's odd angles and jutting gables due to some ineradicable spark of Mother Nature within them—all these are matters of interest perhaps to the building's ghosts, if any exist. They are, however, absent from Miss Twinkleton's half-yearly accounts. Neither the poetical department nor the other extras of the establishment care to dwell on such unprofitable questions.

Much as there are two states of consciousness associated with drunkenness or animal magnetism that never clash, each pursuing its separate course as if continuous, so Miss Twinkleton possesses two distinct and separate phases of being. Every night, the moment the young ladies retire, Miss Twinkleton smooths her curls, brightens her eyes, and transforms into a sprightly version unseen during the day. At the same hour, she resumes the topics of the previous night, comprehending the tender scandals of Cloisterham and references to Tunbridge Wells—which she airily calls "The Wells"—notably the season wherein a certain finished gentleman, whom she calls "Foolish Mr. Porters," revealed a homage of the heart. Of this, Miss Twinkleton in her scholastic state knows nothing; she is as ignorant as a granite pillar. Her companion in both states is Mrs. Tisher, a deferential widow with a weak back, a chronic sigh, and a suppressed voice, who tends the wardrobes and leads the girls to believe she has seen better days. It is an article of faith among the servants, handed down from generation to generation, that the departed Mrs. Tisher was a hairdresser.

The pet pupil of the Nuns' House is Miss Rosa Bud, universally known as Rosebud. She is wonderfully pretty, wonderfully childish, and wonderfully whimsical. An awkward, romantic interest attaches to her in the minds of the other girls, for it is known that a husband has been chosen for her by will and bequest, and her guardian is bound to bestow her upon him when he comes of age. Miss Twinkleton, in her seminary state, attempts to combat the romantic aspects of this destiny by feigning melancholy behind Rosebud's dimpled shoulders, brooding over the unhappy lot of the doomed little victim. Yet, this effort evokes only a unanimous bedchamber cry from the students: "Oh, what a pretending old thing Miss Twinkleton is!"

The Nuns' House is never in such a state of flutter as when this allotted husband arrives to see little Rosebud. It is understood by the girls that he is lawfully entitled to this privilege, and if Miss Twinkleton disputed it, she would be instantly taken up and transported. When his ring at the gate-bell is expected, every girl who can look out of a window does so; those "practising" practise out of tune; and the French class becomes so demoralized that the mark passes around as briskly as a bottle at a convivial party of the last century.

If one were to categorize the various forms of self-assurance and unshakeable conceit that populate Cloisterham, there exists a specific breed, perhaps more conventional than equitable, which we shall term "the Jackass." Within this taxonomy, the purest specimen, the most unadulterated embodiment of this spirit, is undoubtedly Mr. Thomas Sapsea, the Auctioneer.

Mr. Sapsea lives a life performed entirely in the grandest register. He "dresses at" the Dean, a title he wears with the confidence of a king, despite the frequent, humorous errors of the populace who mistake him for the prelate himself. He has been bowed to as "My Lord" in the streets, an honor he accepts with a stiff nod, convinced that the confusion proves his inherent nobility. He prides himself not merely on his status, but on the timbre of his voice and the cadence of his style. In the art of selling landed estates, he has even attempted to mimic the solemn intonation of a true ecclesiastical figure, believing that the pulpit is merely a variant of the auction block. Thus, when concluding a sale by public auction, Mr. Sapsea invariably finishes with a flourish that resembles a benediction bestowed upon the gathered brokers, leaving the actual Dean—a modest, competent gentleman—to fade into obscurity by comparison.

Mr. Sapsea commands a vast following. Indeed, the proposition, supported by a significant local majority that includes even those who privately doubt his wisdom, is that he is a credit to Cloisterham. He possesses the dual virtues of being portentous and dull, moving with a rolling gait and speaking with a rolling tongue. His hands move with a grave, flowing grace, constantly gesturing as if confirming the souls of his interlocutors. Though nearly sixty, he maintains the illusion of youth through a bloated outline of stomach and the horizontal creases of his waistcoat, which he believes signify prosperity. Reputed wealthy, he votes strictly in the interests of respectability and is morally certain that nothing in the world has grown except himself. How, then, could a man of such monumental ego fail to be considered a pillar of society?

His premises, situated on High Street directly across from the Nuns' House, date from roughly the same era as the convent itself. Irregularly modernized by successive generations who, weary of fever and plague, increasingly prioritized air and light, the building stands as a testament to gradual deterioration. Above the doorway hangs a wooden effigy, roughly half-life-size, depicting Mr. Sapsea's father in a curly wig and toga, captured mid-sale. The concept is so pure, and the execution of the little finger, hammer, and pulpit so natural, that it remains a subject of local admiration.

Inside, on the dull ground floor, Mr. Sapsea presides over a room that faces first upon his paved backyard and then upon his railed garden. Before the fire—an early luxury, yet comforting on this chilly autumn evening—rests a bottle of port wine. The scene is characteristically adorned by his portrait, an eight-day clock, and a weather-glass. These objects serve a symbolic function: the portrait defends him against mankind, the clock against time, and the weather-glass against the elements themselves.

Beside Mr. Sapsea on the table lies a writing desk. Christmas Eve in Cloisterham. The streets held a peculiar mixture of strangers and returnees, those whose childhood memories of the town had faded into the vague recollections of adults visiting a place they knew only by reputation. To these visitors, the cathedral bell and the cawing of the rooks sounded like the distant, comforting voices of a nursery long forgotten. To others, standing in the shadows where the city seemed to shrink under the weight of time, the rustling leaves and the scent of the Close stirred dreams of a final, peaceful reunion between the beginning and the end of life.

The festive tokens were abundant. Red berries glinted from the lattices of Minor Canon Corner, and Mr. and Mrs. Tope diligently pressed sprigs of holly into the carved stalls of the Cathedral, treating them with the reverence due to the coats of the Dean and Chapter. The shops overflowed with lavish provisions: currants, raisins, spices, candied peel, and moist sugar hung in profusion. Neville Landless had risen with the first grey light, his boots kicking up dust as he pushed forward through the mist. By the time the ancient bells of Cloisterham tolled for morning service, marking the start of the day, he was already eight miles down the road. With nothing but a stale crust of bread to sustain him, he knew he required sustenance soon. He pulled his coat tight against the chill and sought refuge at the nearest roadside inn, "The Tilted Wagon."

The tavern was a sprawling, untidy affair perched atop a hill, a place where the earth outside seemed permanently mired in the churnings of horse hooves and stray straw. Inside, the atmosphere was thick with the scent of damp woodsmoke and unwashed linens. A scolding landlady, her face flushed with irritation, slapped a baby wrapped unevenly in socks—one red, one missing—in the corner of the bar. Elsewhere, cheese sat abandoned on a shelf beside a mold-stained cloth and a green-handled knife resting in a makeshift iron canoe, while pale loaves of bread appeared to weep crumbs into similar vessels nearby. It was a chaotic scene, lacking the polish promised by its sign, yet Neville, weary and hungry, accepted whatever comfort it offered. He sat in a sand-scattered parlor, gazing at the dying embers of the hearth, wondering how long it would take for the wet fuel to finally spark warmth for someone else.

Feeling restored, Neville stepped out, pausing at roughly a quarter-mile from the inn to decide his path. The main road stretched straight ahead, but parallel to it lay a cart track squeezed between two towering hedgerows, cutting across a windswept heath before presumably rejoining the highway. Choosing the latter, he set off, his steps heavy against the steep incline and the deep, rutted soil.

As he labored upward, he sensed movement behind him. Turning slightly, he saw four figures approaching at a brisk pace, while another four slowed to a loitering stop, clearly intending to follow. Then, abruptly, the remaining members of the party—a dozen souls in total—turned and fled back at a frantic gallop.

Neville froze, exchanging a sharp glance with the four advancing and the four lingering. They returned his gaze, unblinking. Resuming his trek, he watched the four ahead constantly checking over their shoulders, while the four behind steadily closed the distance. When the group emerged onto the open slope of the heath, maintaining this formation, Neville realized with a jolt that he was surrounded. He halted, testing them. They halted instantly.

"Why do you attend upon me in this way?" Neville called out, his voice ringing in the thin air. "Are you a pack of thieves?"

"Don't answer him!" a voice boomed from the crowd. "Better be quiet."

"Better be quiet?" Neville repeated, his anger flaring. "Who said so?"

Silence answered.

"It's sound advice, whoever the coward was," Neville growled, pacing back and forth. "I refuse to be penned in between four men yonder and four men yonder. I intend to pass the four in front, and I shall!"

They stood immovable, including Neville, trapped in a circle of eight.

"If eight men, or four, or two set upon one," Neville shouted, his face flushing red, "the victim has no choice but to fight back! And by the Lord, I will, if I am harassed further!"

Clutching his heavy walking stick, he surged forward, aiming to dash past the leading pair. Instantly, the largest and strongest man among them pivoted, intercepting him with practiced speed. Before Neville could fully commit his blow, the stranger brought him crashing to the grass. A heavy strike landed, knocking Neville flat.

"Let him be!" the man commanded, his voice low and urgent as they rolled together in the dirt, their faces splattered with blood. "Fair play! Look at him—he looks like a girl compared to me, and he carries extra weight strapped to his back. Leave him alone. I'll handle him."

For a moment, they grappled fiercely, a tangle of limbs and curses, until the larger man finally gained the upper hand. He knelt on Neville's chest, then rose, panting. "Now, take him arm-in-arm, any two of you!"

"As to us being thieves, Mr. Landless," the leader spat, wiping blood from his lip, "you know better than that by noon. We wouldn't have touched you if you hadn't provoked us. When his face was cleaned, Neville recognized the speaker. It was Joe, the driver of the Cloisterham omnibus, whom he had glimpsed only once on his arrival day.

"Alone, deary!" a raspy voice replied. "Better for you, worse for me. Light a match, whoever you be."

As the tenant lit a candle, revealing a gaunt, suffering woman, Jasper introduced himself. She, trembling with a severe cough, spoke of her lungs as "wore away to cabbage-nets," yet her eyes sharpened instantly upon recognizing him. "Why, it's you!" she cried, tears welling. "I thought you were dead, and gone to Heaven."

"I'm alive," Jasper said calmly. "And I am in mourning."

"Mourning?" she echoed, her grief momentarily overshadowed by business acumen. "Who died, lovey?"

"A relative."

"Probably Death," Jasper muttered dryly.

"We're short tonight!" she declared, laughing propitiatorily. "But we're out of sorts for lack of smoke. We've got the 'all-overs,' haven't we? This is the place to cure 'em."

Jasper removed his shoes and loosened his cravat, lying down on the squalid bed. "Now you begin to look like yourself," she approved. "Been trying to mix for yourself this long time, poppet?"

"I have been taking it now and then in my own way."

"It ain't good for trade, and it ain't good for you," she warned, producing an ink-bottle, a thimble, and a spoon to prepare the mixture. "He's going to take it in an artful form now, my deary dear!"

As she bubbled and blew at the faint spark in her hands, Jasper, eyes distant and dreamy, drifted into a monologue. "I've got a pretty many smokes ready for you... When you first come, you was quite new to it... But you got on in the world, and was able by-and-by to take your pipe with the best of 'em."

He accepted the pipe, and after a few silences, questioned its potency. "Is it as potent as it used to be?"

"Just the same," she assured him. "Always the identical same."

"It doesn't taste so. And it's slower."

"You've got more used to it, you see."

Jasper grew thoughtful, then suddenly intense. "Suppose you had something in your mind; something you were going to do."

"Yes, deary; something I was going to do?"

"But had not quite determined to do."

"Yes, deary."

"Should you do it in your fancy, when you were lying here doing this?"

She nodded, stirring the contents of the bowl with a needle. "Over and over again."

"Just like me!" Jasper interrupted, his voice rising with a savage edge. "I did it over and over again. I have done it hundreds of thousands of times in this room."

"It's to be hoped it was pleasant to do, deary."

"It *was* pleasant to do!" he snarled, a wolfish quality emerging in his tone. "It WAS one! It WAS one!"

"There was a fellow-traveller, deary."

"Ha, ha, ha!" Jasper erupted into a ringing, hysterical laugh. "To think how often fellow-traveller, and yet not know it! To think how many times he went the journey, and never saw the road!"

The woman knelt beside him, watching him with a mixture of curiosity and caution. "I'll warrant you made the journey in a many ways, when you made it so often?"

"No, always in one way."

"In the way in which it was really made at last?"

"Ay."

"And always took the same pleasure in harping on it?"

"Ay."

For some time, Jasper could only offer a lazy, monosyllabic assent. Then, the woman probed deeper. "Did you never get tired of it, deary, and try to call up something else for a change?"

Struggling upright, Jasper snapped, "What do you mean? What did I want? What did I come for?"

Laying him back gently, she coaxed, "Sure, sure, sure! You was too quick for me. I see now. You come o' purpose to take the journey."

"Yes, I came on purpose," Jasper confirmed, his teeth clenched passionately. "When I could not bear my life, I came to get the relief, and I got it."

"There was a fellow-traveller, deary."

"How could the time be at hand unless the fellow-traveller was? Hush! The journey's made. It's over."

"So soon?"

"That's what I said to you. So soon. Wait a little. This is a vision. I shall sleep it off. It has been too short and easy. I must have a better vision than this; this is the poorest of all."

Wild, meaningless gestures followed his incoherent ramblings, trailing off into a progressive stupor. Jasper lay like a log upon the bed. The woman, however, remained vigilant. Sitting in a chair by the hearth, she whispered to him, recalling his own words about unintelligible forces. "Don't ye be too sure always; don't be ye too sure, beauty!"

As the candles burned down and daylight crept into the room, Jasper sat up, chilled and shaking. He recovered his consciousness, paid the woman a grateful sum, and departed. But she did not stay behind. As soon as his footsteps faded, she glided after him, muttering, "I'll not miss ye twice!"

Following him through the labyrinthine alleys, she tracked him to a temporary lodging near Aldersgate Street, waiting patiently for hours. When Jasper emerged at noon, having changed his dress but carrying nothing, she followed him to Cloisterham High Street. Standing near the Nuns' House, she waited until nine o'clock, observing the arrival of an omnibus.

Among the passengers, she spotted the gentleman from Cloisterham. "Go on!" she murmured, addressing the air as if he could hear. He walked swiftly to an arched gateway and vanished inside. Quickening her pace, the woman entered the gateway herself, confronting a gray-haired gentleman writing in an ancient vaulted room.

"Halloa!" the man cried, seeing her halted. "Who are you looking for?"

"There was a gentleman passed in here this minute, sir."

"Of course there was. What do you want with him?"

"Where do he live, deary?"

"Up that staircase."

"What's his name, deary?"

"Surname Jasper, Christian name John. Mr. John Jasper."

"He sings in the choir?"

"Yes. In the spire?"

"Choir."
