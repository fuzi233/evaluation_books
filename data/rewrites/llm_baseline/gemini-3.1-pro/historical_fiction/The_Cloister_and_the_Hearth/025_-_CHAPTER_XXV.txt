The heavy, suffocating weight of the bear collapsed onto Gerard, a mountain of coarse fur and feral musk that pinned him to the damp earth. Denys hauled the massive, dying creature off his friend. The beast’s final, shuddering breaths faded into the mossy acoustics of the ancient woods, a mythic life force extinguishing itself into the soil, bound to the eternal, circular rhythm of the forest. Gerard, overwhelmed by the sharp, metallic tang of hot blood and the primal, visceral scent of ruptured organs, rolled away and retched violently into the ferns. 

Denys, a man who viewed the bounty of the wild with a quiet, culinary reverence, merely chuckled, wiping the rich, ruby-dark blood from his tunic. "Courage," he said, his French rolling smooth and bright. "Le diable est mort."

Gerard shivered, his senses assaulted by the overwhelming copper stench. "She sent her nasty blood all over me. I hate it."

"But it is not yours," Denys replied, dropping to his knees. A sudden rush of affection for the fragile youth overtook him. When Gerard insisted on kneeling in the loam to offer a prayer of gratitude, Denys joined him. Their murmured devotions blended with the rustling canopy, a fleeting harmony in the vast acoustic memory of the timberland. 

But as they resumed their trek, the muscles in Gerard’s wounded leg began to seize, stiffening like salt-cured venison. When he finally collapsed on the forest path, unable to bear his own weight, Denys pressed his crossbow into Gerard's hands. Taking Gerard's axe, the soldier vanished into the timber to scout the road backward, leaving the youth to the woodland's deep, resonant silence.

Left alone, the quiet of the woods soothed Gerard—until the soundscape was violently torn apart. It began with shouts, discordant and sharp, shattering the natural acoustic ecology of the trees. Then came a chaotic storm of noise: the drumming of hooves, the shrieks of dying men, and above it all, a terrifying, unnatural thunder. It was the brutal crack of early gunpowder, a harsh, mechanical violence that seemed to pollute the very air, leaving a sulfurous, acrid smoke drifting through the branches. It was an assault not just on the flesh, but on the sonic sanctity of the woods. Then, as quickly as the cacophony arose, the forest swallowed it back into silence.

When Denys returned at sunset, burdened under a freshly flayed bearskin—a heavy, insulating mantle of survival—Gerard babbled frantically about the battle of men and fiends. 

"Fever is a great spur to the imagination," Denys said indulgently, dismissing the chaotic symphony of violence as a hallucination. The subjective terror of the sounds remained profoundly untranslatable to the older soldier, even when Gerard pointed out the undeniable physical proof: a three-foot English broad arrow quivering in a nearby trunk. Denys simply shrugged off the disruption. They scraped together a bed of dried leaves, pulled the thick hide over themselves with the fur inward, and slept warmly under the canopy's reclaimed quiet.

Dawn brought a gnawing emptiness. For Denys, hunger was never a mere lack of fuel, but a call to the sacred duty of sustenance. He meticulously butchered the bear's ears, rolling the dense, collagen-rich cartilage into a strip of skin for careful preservation, and propped Gerard up to walk. 

The moon rose, painting the edge of the woods in silver, but the tranquil night was soon disrupted by a new, oppressive noise: a rhythmic, agonizing creak.

Two white pillars rose in the gloom. Between them, dark cords hung tight as drawn wire, and as the wind swept through the valley, fifteen corpses gently gyrated. The straining of the ropes was a sickening, mechanical drone that wore heavily on Gerard’s nerves, a grim acoustic marker of human cruelty disrupting the night. Directly beneath this swaying harvest of death sat a lone figure, huddled over a crackling fire.

While Gerard recoiled from the macabre sight, Denys saw the glowing embers and recognized the timeless ritual of the hearth. "Courage, comrade," Denys whispered. "Here is a fire."

They approached the local rustic, a man wearing a heavy Brunswicker hat that folded his ears down like a hound’s. Denys didn't hesitate. Where there was fire, there must be roasting meat; the sacred act of cooking transcended the gruesome surroundings. Denys retrieved the bear ears, skewered them on sharpened sticks, and angled them over the hot coals. As the intense heat met the meat, the fat began to render, hissing and spitting, releasing an intoxicating, earthy aroma of wild game and charred wood that fought against the cold, metallic smell of death swinging above them.

"Eat, Denys, if you can," Gerard shuddered, the creaking ropes making his skin crawl. "I am cold and sick; there is no room for hunger in my heart after what mine eyes have seen."

Denys ignored the squeamishness, his focus locked on the alchemy of the flame. The watcher, speaking a thick, guttural regional dialect, was coerced by Denys’s leveled crossbow to share the warmth. Denys, ever the gracious host even under the gallows, offered the watcher a beautifully blistered piece of bear's ear. 

In return, the rustic dug into his pile of leaves and produced a heavy leathern bottle. Denys uncorked it, and the rich, fermented scent of Rhenish wine filled the air. Denys drank, his palate immediately recognizing the profound complexity of the vintage—the crisp, acidic bite of grapes grown on slate-steeped riverbanks, a liquid history of the region's agrarian mastery. "Mort de ma vie!" Denys cried. "It is Rhenish wine, fit for the gullet of an archbishop!" The warming, golden liquor flowed through their veins, a miraculous burst of life and terroir amidst the creaking sepulcher. 

Warmed by the wine, the watcher shared his history. His native, rough-hewn dialect conveyed a grim narrative of power and betrayal, a vernacular history untranslatable to the courts of kings. His comrades—now dangling above—had lived off the forest's bounty, only to be ambushed by soldiers disguised as merchants, men wielding the very diabolical engines of war whose deafening cracks had polluted the woodland's acoustics earlier. The watcher pointed to his dead friends one by one with the bottle, his memories a tangled, subjective history of the woods that was slowly dying out with each swaying corpse.

Denys, pragmatic and fortified by the sublime wine, demanded the watcher act as a crutch for Gerard. In a strange, tense formation—Gerard leaning on the thief while gripping the haft of his knife beneath his cloak—they walked until the watchtower of Dusseldorf broke the horizon.

At the city's edge, they released the outlaw, for the man knew his neck would be in jeopardy near the gates. Denys, paying respect to the profound sustenance the man had provided under the gallows, tossed him silver groschen. Yet the thief lingered, asking for a final transaction: a prayer to protect him from the brutal laws of the land, laws that, as he explained in his coarse tongue, drove men to slit throats simply to avoid the noose. 

Gerard, his patience eroded by the man’s crude justifications and his own throbbing pain, weaponized his education. He looked at the rustic—a man tied intimately to the soil and the fading local dialect—and spoke in the dominant, unyielding lexicon of the Church.

"'Obsecro Deum omnipotentem, ut, qua cruce jam pendent isti quindecim latrones fures et homicidae, in ea homicida fur et latro tu pependeris quam citissime, pro publica salute, in honorem justi Dei cui sit gloria, in aeternum, Amen.'"

The outlaw smiled, deeply satisfied. "That is Latin," he muttered, assuming he had been blessed by a man of learning. "And more than I bargained for."

He turned back to the woods with a mind at ease, entirely oblivious to the lethal curse laid upon him. The deep, untranslatable chasm of language sealed his fate, leaving him deaf to the fatal power dynamics embedded in Gerard's scholarly words. As the thief vanished into the trees, Gerard and Denys moved onward into the night, the memory of the wild bear, the rich Rhenish wine, and the creaking timber settling into the long, cyclical tapestry of their journey.
