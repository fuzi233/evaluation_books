"Have you ever truly contemplated the sheer audacity, the brazen cheek, or, if you prefer, the gall with which Woman, as a species, operates?" I have, by Jove. But then, I've had it thrust upon my notice, by George, in a manner I suspect has occurred to very few men. The limit was finally breached by the business of the Yeardsley 'Venus.'

To comprehend the full gravity of the situation, I must elucidate the state of affairs between Mrs. Yeardsley and myself.

When I first encountered her, she was Elizabeth Shoolbred. An ancient Worcestershire lineage; vast fortunes; pretty as a picture. Her brother, Bill, was at Oxford alongside me.

I adored Elizabeth Shoolbred. I loved her, don't you know. There was a brief interlude, lasting approximately one week, during which we were betrothed. However, just as I began to adopt a serious perspective on life, scrutinize furniture catalogs, and feel appropriately somber when the restaurant orchestra played 'The Wedding Glide,' I'm hanged if she didn't dissolve the engagement. A month hence, she was wed to a fellow named Yeardsley—Clarence Yeardsley, an artist.

Amidst golf, billiards, horse racing, and colleagues rallying at the club to distract me from my own melancholy, I recovered. I came to regard the affair as a closed chapter in the annals of my existence. It seemed improbable we would cross paths again, as she and Clarence had retreated to the countryside, eschewing London entirely. By the time her letter arrived, the wound had largely healed; I was, to a degree, upright and nourished. Frankly, I was immensely relieved the matter concluded as it did.

This epistle descended upon me one morning from a clear blue sky. It read thus:

'MY DEAR OLD REGGIE,—How ages it seems since I last beheld you. Pray, how are you? We have established ourselves in a magnificent ancestral home, surrounded by a ravishing garden, nestled within delightful rural environs. Could you not descend for a few days? Clarence and I would be exceedingly pleased to receive you. Bill is present and most eager to reunite with you; he spoke of you only this morning. Do come. Telegraph your arrival, and I shall dispatch the carriage to greet you.

—Yours most sincerely, ELIZABETH YEARDSLEY.

'P.S.—We can provide you with fresh milk and pristine eggs. Consider the value!'

'P.P.S.—Bill asserts our billiard table ranks among the finest he has ever encountered.'

'P.P.S.S.—We reside merely half a mile from a golf course. Bill declares it surpasses St. Andrews.'

'P.P.S.S.S.—You MUST come!'

A gentleman rises for breakfast, slightly disoriented, to discover a missive from a woman capable of ruining his life! It rattled me considerably, I must confess.

Nevertheless, the reference to golf solidified my resolve. I trusted Bill's judgment; if he deemed the course exceptional, it must be extraordinary. Thus, I departed.

Old Bill awaited me at the station with the automobile. We had not crossed paths in months, yet I rejoiced in his presence, and he, seemingly, in mine.

'Thank heavens you've arrived,' he remarked as we accelerated. 'I was moments from total collapse.'

'What afflicts you, old scout?' I inquired.

'If I possessed the requisite artistic temperament,' he continued, 'if the mere mention of canvases did not induce the pips, it might not be so dire. As it stands, it is ruinous!'

'Artworks?'

'Paintings. Nothing else is discussed in this household. Clarence is an artist. So is his father. And you are aware of Elizabeth's disposition when permitted to pursue her whims?'

I recalled then—a memory previously eluding me—that the majority of my time with Elizabeth involved art galleries. During the period when I allowed her to dictate my movements, I followed her like a hound through gallery after gallery, though paintings are poison to me, as they are to old Bill. It never occurred to me she would persist in this obsession post-marriage to an artist. One assumes the sight of art would eventually satiate her. Not so, according to Bill.

'They discourse on art at every repast,' he stated. 'I tell you, it renders a man alienated. For how long are you staying?'

'A few days.'

'Take my counsel and have me wire you from London. I depart tomorrow. I pledged to compete against the Scots. The plan was for me to return post-match. But you cannot retrieve me with a lasso.'

I attempted to highlight the bright side.

'But, Bill, old scout, your sister mentions a superb links nearby.'

He turned, staring at me, nearly driving us into the bank.

'Do you honestly mean she claimed that?'

'She stated you declared it superior to St. Andrews.'

'I did. Did she imply that was all I said?'

'Wasn't it sufficient?'

'She omitted that I appended the phrase, "I don't think."'

'No, she neglected to inform me of that.'

'It is the most abysmal course in Great Britain.'

I felt somewhat stunned. Whether this constitutes a pernicious habit, I cannot say, but I simply cannot function without my daily ration of golf when away from London.

I seized another opportunity to spin a silver lining.

'We shall compensate with billiards,' I proposed. 'I am delighted the table is excellent.'

'It depends on your definition of excellent. It is half-size, and there exists a seven-inch cut just out of baulk where Clarence's cue slipped. Elizabeth repaired it with pink silk. It appears dashing and fashionable, yet it degrades the utility of the table.'

'But she said you said...'

'Must have been jesting.'

We entered the driveway gates of a substantial estate, situated well back from the road. It loomed black and sinister in the twilight, evoking the sensation of one of those Johns described in tales, lured to isolated dwellings for nefarious ends, hearing a shriek precisely upon arrival. Elizabeth knew me sufficiently to recognize that a premier golf course served as a potent lure. She had deliberately exploited this knowledge. What was the objective? This I sought to ascertain. Then, a sudden thought struck me, inducing a cold perspiration. She harbored a paramour below and intended to attempt a matrimonial maneuver involving me. I have frequently heard that young wives engage in such schemes. Certainly, she had asserted only Clarence, herself, Bill, and Clarence's father resided there, yet a woman capable of invoking St. Andrews in vain would not hesitate at trifles.

'Bill, old scout,' I said, 'are there any dreadful females or other rotting elements residing here?'

'Wish there were,' he replied. 'No such fortune.'

As we halted before the front door, it swung open, revealing a female silhouette.

'Have you secured him, Bill?' she queried. In my current mental state, this struck me as a dreadfully ominous phrasing. The sort of declaration Lady Macbeth might utter to Macbeth, don't you know.

'Do you refer to me?' I asked.

She descended into the illumination. It was Elizabeth, appearing identical to her former self.

'Is that you, Reggie? I am so gratified you managed to arrive. I feared you might have forgotten entirely. You know your nature. Come inside for tea.'

Have you ever been rejected by a suitor who subsequently married, only to be introduced to her spouse? If so, you comprehend my sentiment when Clarence emerged. You possess the feeling. Initially, upon hearing of the union, you muse, 'I wonder what he resembles.' Upon meeting him, you conclude, 'There must be an error. She could not have preferred THIS to me!' Such was my reaction upon setting eyes on Clarence.

He was a slight, nervous specimen of approximately thirty-five. Grey strands peppered his temples; his hair was straggly atop. He wore pince-nez and sported a drooping moustache. I am no Bombardier Wells myself, yet in Clarence's presence, I felt utterly inadequate. And Elizabeth, mind you, is one of those towering, splendid maidens resembling princesses. Honestly, I believe women act out of pure stubbornness.

'How do you do, Mr. Pepper? Hark! Can you discern a mewing cat?' Clarence exclaimed, all in one breath, don't you know.

'Eh?' I responded.

'A mewing cat. I am convinced I hear a mewing cat. Attend!'

While we listened, the door opened, admitting a white-haired elderly gentleman. Constructed on similar lines to Clarence, albeit an earlier iteration, I correctly identified him as Mr. Yeardsley, senior. Elizabeth introduced us.

'Father,' Clarence said, 'did you encounter a mewing cat outdoors? I feel positive I heard a cat mew.'

'No,' the father replied, shaking his head; 'no mewing cat.'

'I cannot abide mewing cats,' Clarence declared. 'A mewing cat aggravates my nerves!'

'A mewing cat is so trying,' Elizabeth observed.

'_I_ detest mewing cats,' old Mr. Yeardsley affirmed.

That concluded the discussion on mewing cats for the moment. They seemed satisfied they had covered the terrain adequately and returned to art.
