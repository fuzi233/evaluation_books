Once upon a very long vacation, a strapping young Cambridge tutor named the Reverend Francis Bell rolled up to his aunt’s tiny village cottage, where his cousin Helen Thistlewood (whose dad had copped it at Copenhagen, poor lieutenant) lived on a pension so small you could tuck it behind a teacup. Bell was a hotshot college fellow, famous for churning out prize-winning students faster than the village dairy churned butter, and he’d brought three or four pupils along to camp out in the spare rooms for the summer.

Everybody figured out within 48 hours that the reverend lad was thoroughly trapped in an engagement he’d stumbled into as a gawky 17-year-old. Picture it: he was helping Miss Martha Coacher, daughter of his old childhood tutor, pick peas one sunny afternoon, got so flustered he dropped his basket, and flopped straight onto his knees in the dirt to swear eternal undying devotion. Martha was 12 years his senior, and her love life up to that point read like a list of every bad 18th century romance trope: three of her dad’s pupils had trifled with her affections and vanished, the local apothecary had dumped her via a note left on his shop counter, and a dragoon officer she’d danced with every night at Bath had ridden off one morning mid-flirt, never to be seen again. No wonder she jumped at the chance to lock down a earnest, clever boy who actually seemed to mean what he said.

She sent him back a lock of her auburn hair tied with blue ribbon as her answer – never mind she’d snipped off three identical locks for the three prior faithless lovers, the hair grew back, after all. Young Bell was so over the moon he decided he’d grind his way to top Wrangler, secure a fat college living, and make her his wife no matter what. Term after term he brought home honours, sent her prize books and silver declamation trophies, corresponded constantly, and truly believed their love was the stuff of legend.

By the time he turned 26, though? The pea garden magic had well and truly worn off. Martha was 34, sharp-tempered, coarse-mannered, and forever picking fights. Bell realized he was bound by hundreds of gushy old love letters to a woman he couldn’t stand the sight of. So rather than spend the summer at her father’s parsonage, he legged it down to his aunt’s village, where he hadn’t set foot since little Helen was small enough to sit on his knee.

Helen was now a 20-something beauty, and for four months straight they walked at sunset, read from the same book while old Mrs. Thistlewood dozed by the candles, and slowly fell head over heels for each other. She knew all about his messy engagement, even saw the hard, snappish letters Martha sent him. But both of them were far too noble for their own good. They decided duty came first: Bell would honour his silly teenage promise, they’d go their separate ways, and they’d both be miserable for the rest of their lives. That’s 19th century romance for you.

The living fell in far too soon. Bell was already grey and worn out by the time he got installed as parson. Helen sent him a polite “My dear Cousin” note for his wedding, returned all his old letters and the tiny lock of his hair she’d kept tucked in her desk, and married Arthur Pendennis senior soon after. Then Bell finagled a faraway chaplainship on the godforsaken Coventry Island, dragged Martha out there with him, and she died of tropical fever a few months later, still cackling about how she’d stopped him from running off with Helen. Bell married a nice colonial lady for a bit, but she died in childbirth too, and the poor man gave up on joy entirely. He shipped his tiny newborn daughter back to Helen, with a note begging her to raise the girl for him, then kicked the bucket himself not long after.

Little Helen Laura showed up at Fairoaks dressed in black, clinging to her nurse’s hand and sobbing her eyes out, and Helen Pendennis took her in instantly as a piece of the old love she’d never forgotten. Her new husband John Pendennis, though, was a moody old stick-in-the-mud who hated the very sight of her – she reminded him of all that old romantic history he’d rather pretend never happened – so he insisted they skip the “Helen” name and just call her Laura. Only once he was in the ground did Helen dare shower the little girl with all the affection she’d been holding back. That’s the origin story of your little Laura: two hundred pounds in the bank, no fancy noble relatives, and a whole heap of quiet softness that neither grumpy John Pendennis nor his snobby brother the Major thought was worth a tuppence.

Alright, enough of that. Let’s let Pen back in, the poor lad who’s been stood hovering outside the door rehearsing his dramatic duel speech for ten whole minutes.

He’d puffed out his chest, practiced his most stern, battle-ready scowl, and convinced himself he was about to have a screaming knock-down drag-out fight with his terrifying uncle, defending his great romantic love like a knight of old. He flung the door open so hard it banged against the wall, marched in with his “lance couched and plumes flying” expression, ready to snap “come on then, I’m ready.”

Major Pendennis, that old smooth operator, surveyed the boy’s ridiculous pompous posturing and almost snort-laughed out loud. He’d already sussed out that Helen was half ready to give Pen everything he wanted, so yelling and grand speeches would do no good at all. So he dropped all his stern guardian act, beamed the friendliest smile you ever saw, held out both his hands, shook Pen’s limp, confused hand gaily, and said “Well Pen my boy, tell us all about it.”

Poor Pen was gutted. He’d geared up for full Greek tragedy, and the script had gotten swapped for a friendly tea party. His big grand entrance was completely wasted. He blushed bright red, fumbled for words, and nearly burst into tears. “I – I didn’t know you’d arrived till just now,” he stammered. “Is – is London very busy, then?”

The Major had to turn away to hide his cackle, darting a comical little glance at Helen, who was equally half-amused half-melted at the sight of her son flustered. She stepped forward and kissed his forehead, and Pen’s last scrap of tragic hero dignity dissolved entirely.

“What a pair of soft silly things they are,” the Major thought to himself. “If I’d waited one more day, Helen would have hitched up the carriage to go and beg blessing on this nonsense marriage herself.”

“Come on, no need for all the over-sentimental stuff,” he said, still grinning. “Pen, old chap, spill the whole story.”

Pen immediately snapped back into his tragic heroic pose. “The story is sir, that I’ve met the most beautiful, virtuous woman of noble birth who’s fallen on hard times. My whole life’s happiness is tied up in her. I know there’s a gap in our ages and all sorts of obstacles, but I’m ready to surmount them all. She’s agreed to marry me, to share my life and my fortune.”

“Eh, how much fortune is that exactly, lad?” the Major said, raising an eyebrow. “Last I checked, you don’t own a single penny in your own right.”

“Every penny he has is mine, and I’d give it all to him,” Helen burst out immediately.

The Major bit back a very sharp “for heaven’s woman, shut up!” and kept his temper. “Right, right, of course you would. Everyone knows you’d do anything for him. But at the end of the day, that fortune you’re offering the young lady is your mother’s, Pen, and you’re only 18. Why on earth didn’t you have the decency to consult the lady who runs the house, pays the bills, and feeds you three meals a day before you went and pledged all her assets to a stranger? I’m not yelling at you, mind – the law and your dad’s will give me full authority over you for another three years, but I’m talking man to man here. Isn’t it a bit selfish to make all these huge decisions for the woman who’d give you the shirt off her back, without even asking her opinion?”

Pen’s head dropped. All that supposed grand, selfless romantic gesture he’d been so proud of suddenly looked an awful lot like a selfish, headstrong bit of silliness. “I did it in a fit of passion,” he mumbled. “But I said it now, and I stand by it. I won’t go back on my word, not for anything. I’ll die first. I’ll work for her! I’ll go on the stage and act with her, she says I’d be great at it.”

“Fair enough,” the Major said, utterly unflappable. “But no gentleman breaks his word when he’s given it freely. We’re not asking you to jilt her. But you owe your mother a little courtesy, don’t you? Your family, me as your father’s representative?”

“Oh of course,” Pen said, already half-relieved.

“Great. Then give us your word on one other thing, Arthur. No sneaking off for a private marriage. No trips to Gretna Green in the dead of night, you understand?”

“Arthur would never lie to his mother!” Helen said, shocked.

Pen’s face burned with shame. He’d already been sneaking around behind his mother’s back this whole time, hadn’t he? He held his hand out to his uncle. “On my word of honour as a gentleman, sir, I will never marry without my mother’s full consent.” He gave Helen a bright, loving smile, and trotted off to his study.

“He’s an absolute angel!” Helen gushed.

“Good stock, that boy, on both sides,” the Major said, very pleased with how easily his little trick had worked. He even bowed over the tip of her glove, and slipped into that posh drawl he only used when he was feeling extra clever with himself. “My dear, I’m very glad I came down. That last little promise I got him? Learned that trick from Lady Ferrybridge, three years back. Her son had been secretly married to a Scotch clergyman’s daughter for three whole months before Lord Ferrybridge found out, all because he tried to yell and threaten the boy instead of getting his word of honour. That’s why I wasn’t taking any chances.”

“Arthur would never do that,” Helen said, completely unshaken.

The Major didn’t press the boy any harder that day. He knew time was on his side, and Pen’s over-inflated romantic honour was the perfect lever to work him with. Over dinner, when they were sipping the good claret, he chatted away casually, pointing out how Pen’s marriage would shut every fancy possible career path he could have walked right into: no diplomatic postings to Pumpernickel, no army commission to follow their ancestor who fought at Ramillies, no entry into good society, no bright future at the bar outside the most low-rent circles. “You’ll be stuck down here in Fairoaks forever, my boy,” he said, sighing fondly at the thought of his beloved Pall Mall clubs. “No county gentry will call on you, and even I’ll be too embarrassed to come visit. You’ll spend your whole life drinking rum punch with grumpy farmer gents, dragging your poor mother down into your new wife’s low social circle. It’s no skin off my nose, of course, but it’s a crying shame for you and her. But of course, you’re a man of honour, you’ve given your word, you’ll do the right thing by her. Just promise us there’ll be no sneaking around, you’ll keep up your studies, and you’ll write to her openly, with your mother’s full knowledge, alright? You do write to her a fair bit, I suppose?”

Pen blushed bright crimson, and stammered out that yes, he had written quite a lot.
