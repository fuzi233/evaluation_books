Helen Pendennis would sooner have let Pen eat three full pickled onions before breakfast than let him so much as think about little Fanny Bolton, post-fever. Not a single word about the girl ever crossed her lips after her chat with the Major, and she acted so thoroughly like Fanny had been erased from the face of the Earth you could be forgiven for assuming she’d waved a magic broomstick and swept her right out of Clavering. All the same, she kept Pen under a tighter watch than a theatre manager over a drunk leading man, claiming his health was too fragile to leave her sight for even five minutes, and insisting he didn’t have to waste his strength answering letters, ever.

Poor Pen, still wobbly as a new-born lamb, scanned every piece of post that landed on the breakfast table like it was a secret spy dispatch, half-convinced any day he’d spot Fanny’s little wobbly handwriting on an envelope. He was never sure if he was more relieved or annoyed when no such letter turned up. We all know how it goes, don’t we? When your little “romance startup” is clearly going under, you don’t want to immediately hang the “closed for business” sign. You pawn the fancy candlesticks, drag out the sad partner meetings, put off telling all your friends you’re bankrupt, just to avoid the humiliation of a sudden total collapse. Pen was indignant, frankly: could Fanny really just walk away without a single “wait, come back”? Not even a little wave over her shoulder? Turns out most people who sink on those romantic trips just get a little wet, flail a bit, and scramble back to shore dry enough, and anyone who knows Arthur Pendennis knows he was never the sort to drown over a girl, no matter how sweet she was.

Helen couldn’t very well lock up George Warrington too, though. She couldn’t stop him trotting off to London on business whenever he liked, and once she caught herself hoping he’d just stay there permanently, she scolded herself for being selfish — after all, George had stuck by her boy through the worst of the fever, so she treated him like honorary family, all soft sad smiles and extra jam at tea. But the morning he set off for town, she knew exactly what he was up to: he wasn’t going to the market for turnips, he was off digging up news of Fanny for Pen.

Pen had already spilled the whole messy Fanny saga to George, mind. He was so grateful he’d dodged the massive, life-ruining bullet of eloping with her he could have kissed Warrington, but he still insisted he just wanted to say a proper “God bless you” and send her off well, no hard feelings. He spoke so seriously, his voice all wobbly over his toast, that George started to panic his mate wasn’t nearly as over the girl as he claimed. “The smartest thing a fool like us can do when temptation’s hanging about is run as fast as we can,” George told him glumly. “I didn’t do that once, and look at the mess I turned out.” Pen immediately perked up, nosy as ever, begging for the full story. “Some dumb mistake I made when I was your age shattered all my chances at happiness before I turned twenty,” Warrington said. “I’ll tell you the full tale someday, but not now. Just take the warning, kid — don’t go hitting that same rock I wrecked my whole ship on.”

Now, over at the Back Kitchen pub, our favorite over-enthusiastic med student Huxter was having the time of his life. He’d already turned old Irish Captain Costigan into the pub’s main headline act, all for the low, low price of sixpence’ worth of brandy and water. Huxter could mimic literally anything, from a hammy tragic actor overacting Hamlet to a cock crowing on a dunghill to a cork popping out of a stout bottle, and he’d turned getting Costigan sloshed into an art form. Give the old man a sip of the good stuff, and he’d spin you yarns for hours: how he brawled with a jeweler in Phoenix Park, how he once had a private audience with the Duke of York, how he was practically best friends with every nobleman in the country back in the old days, and how his ungrateful daughter Emily had left him to drink alone now, woe is him. The pub landlord was delighted: all the young med students and clerks poured in just to listen to Costigan’s nonsense, spending money left and right, so he pretended not to notice the old man was three sheets to the wind by 2pm most days. Costigan was such a happy, harmless old drunk the regulars even started making up wild stories about where he lived, since he’d never tell anyone his address. Huxter started the silliest rumor of all: he said the old man slept in a watch box by the Bank of England, or tucked into the side of the Duke of York’s column.

Then one bright September morning, after Huxter had spent the whole night dancing at Vauxhall and was nursing his hangover over a twopenny coffee at a Covent Garden stall, he spotted Costigan reeling down Henrietta Street, with a troop of ragged little street urchins hooting after him. The old boy was too far gone to care what the kids thought, too far gone to even get annoyed when the cabmen jeered at him, too far gone to notice he was heading straight for a stray slice of orange peel someone had dropped on the pavement.

Down he went, flat on his back, with a thud that shook the cobblestones.

Huxter sprinted over to help him up. Costigan was too woozy to remember where he lived at first, trying to wave Huxter off and pretend he could make it home on his own, but he was lurching so bad the kid insisted on escorting him, half-carrying the old fellow past Drury Lane’s gin shops and costume warehouses until they stumbled through the gates of Shepherd’s Inn. He dragged Costigan up his rickety staircase, helped him out of his boots and trousers, and spotted the big, raw scrape on his shin from the fall. Turns out old drunks’ cuts don’t heal very fast: the leg swelled up, got all inflamed, and Costigan was stuck in bed feverish and miserable for days.

Huxter immediately volunteered to be his personal doctor, showing up two or three times a day to mess around, tell jokes, and make sure no one gave the old boy too much whisky (to Costigan’s great annoyance). He quickly made friends with everyone in the inn: the porter Bolton, his wife, the Bolton’s little kids, and of course Fanny, who was helping nurse the old General back to health. Fanny was a shadow of her old self by that point, pale and quiet, dropping tears straight into Costigan’s gruel half the time, staring at the postman every time he walked through the gate waiting for a letter from Pen that never, ever came. Huxter brought the kids brandy balls and silly masks to scare each other with, made Fanny laugh once or twice even through her gloom, and Mrs. Bolton, who thought stuck-up old Pen was a cruel stuck-up prat who’d tricked her poor girl, spilled her whole biased version of the story, making Pen sound like the worst cad to ever walk London. Huxter, who still remembered Pen sneering at him like he was something he stepped in on the street, was all ready to hop on a coach to Clavering and punch the convalescent’s lights out, until old Bows the musician pulled him aside and told him the real tea: none of it was Pen’s fault, Costigan was the one who’d messed up the whole meeting, Pen had been nothing but decent, and Helen was the one who’d turfed Fanny out of the sick room. Huxter, decent sort he was, immediately felt like a total git, and called off his planned brawl.

All the same, he kept hanging around the inn trying to cheer Fanny up, which made sour old Bows seethe with jealousy. “Of course it happens again,” he moped to himself, bitter as pickled limes. “Every single time I start to care about anyone, some younger, nicer bloke comes along to steal their attention. I’ve been playing second fiddle my whole sixty years of life, why did I ever think that was going to change?” Fanny, for her part, was so wrapped up in her own sad thoughts she barely noticed Huxter’s kind attentions at all — she was convinced Bows was hiding letters from Pen from her, and treated him so coldly the poor old musician’s life was more miserable than ever.

That was exactly when George Warrington turned up at the Shepherd’s Inn gate, sent by Pen as official, low-key envoy. He asked for Bows by name, and the musician met him at the top of the stairs, surprised to see him. They shook hands, and Warrington passed on Pen’s thanks for Bows running all the way to the country to fetch the Major when Pen was half-dead with the fever. Bows just sneered back at him, all sarcasm. “Lucky him, having friends to run after him. The General up there only cares about his whisky bottle. I know exactly why you’re here, though. It’s Fanny, isn’t it? You lot just sent that poor girl packing out of Pen’s sick room after she sat up three days and three nights watching him rave through the fever, and now you’ve waltzed back to see what you can do with her? My compliments to your fancy lady Mrs. Pendennis, by the way — an apothecary’s daughter who acts so grand? She deserves a double-gilded pestle and mortar stuck to the side of her carriage, that’s how posh her behaviour was.”
