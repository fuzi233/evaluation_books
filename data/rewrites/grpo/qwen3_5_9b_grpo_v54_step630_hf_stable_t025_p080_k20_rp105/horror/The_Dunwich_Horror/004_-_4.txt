For ten years, the Whateley name dissolved into the grim, indifferent fabric of Dunwich, a town hardened by the sight of their kin's peculiarities and accustomed to the orgiastic rites of May Eve and All-Hallows. Twice a year, fires blazed atop Sentinel Hill, triggering earth-shaking tremors that grew more violent with each passing season, while the isolated farmhouse remained a theater of portents. Neighbors claimed to hear whispers drifting from the sealed upper floors even when the entire family gathered below, and rumors swirled about the speed of cattle sacrifices. Proposals to alert the Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals were quietly dropped; Dunwichers preferred to let the outside world remain ignorant of their local horrors.

By 1923, when Wilbur Whateley was merely ten years old yet possessed the bearded visage, deep voice, and imposing stature of a man, a second siege of construction engulfed the old house. This work occurred entirely within the sealed upper section. From scraps of discarded timber, villagers deduced that the boy and his grandfather had demolished every partition and stripped away the attic floor, carving a single, cavernous void between the ground level and the peaked roof. They had also razed the great central chimney, replacing the rusting range with a flimsy exterior tin stove-pipe.

In the spring following this transformation, Old Whateley noted a proliferation of whippoorwills emerging from Cold Spring Glen to chirp beneath his window at night. He regarded this phenomenon with profound foreboding, telling the loafers at Osborn's that his end was imminent.

"They whistle in perfect tune with my breathing now," he rasped, "and I reckon they are preparing to catch my soul. They know it is coming and calculate to take it. You will know, boys, once I am gone, if they succeeded or failed. If they did, they will keep singing and laughing until dawn. If they did not, they will settle down quietly. I expect these birds and the souls they hunt engage in rather brutal struggles sometimes."

On Lammas Night, 1924, Dr. Houghton of Aylesbury was urgently summoned by Wilbur, who had tethered his lone horse and ridden into the darkness to telephone from Osborn's. He found Old Whateley in a critical state, his heart failing and breath stertorous, signaling death was near. Beside the bed stood the shapeless albino daughter and the oddly bearded grandson. From the empty abyss above, a disturbing rhythmical surging emanated, like waves lapping against a shore. Yet, it was the chattering of night birds outside that most unsettled the doctor—a seemingly endless legion of whippoorwills crying a diabolical message timed precisely to the dying man's wheezing gasps. It felt uncanny, unnatural, an excess that mirrored the reluctant horror of the region itself.

Around one o'clock, Old Whateley regained consciousness, interrupting his labored breathing to choke out instructions for his grandson.

"Make more room, Willy, more space soon. You grow—and *that* grows faster. It will be ready to serve you shortly. Open the gates to Yog-Sothoth with the long chant found on page 751 of the complete edition, and *then* set fire to the prison. Fire from earth cannot burn it!"

He appeared utterly mad. After a pause, during which the flock of whippoorwills outside shifted their cries to match the altered tempo and distant hill noises began to rumble, he added a final warning.

"Feed it regularly, Willy, and mind the quantity; but do not let it grow too fast for the place. If it bursts the quarters or escapes before you open to Yog-Sothoth, it is all over and useless. Only those from beyond can make it multiply and work... Only them, the old ones who wish to return..."

Speech gave way to gasps, and Lavinia screamed as the whippoorwills seemed to react to the change. This cycle repeated for over an hour until the final throaty rattle ceased. Dr. Houghton covered the glazing gray eyes with shrunken lids as the bird tumult faded imperceptibly into silence. Lavinia sobbed, but Wilbur only chuckled, a low, heavy sound, as the hill noises rumbled faintly in the distance.

"They didn't get him," he muttered.

By this time, Wilbur was a scholar of terrifying, one-sided erudition, known by correspondence to librarians in distant lands guarding rare and forbidden texts. He was increasingly hated and feared in Dunwich due to vague suspicions surrounding youthful disappearances, yet he silenced inquiries through terror or by dispensing old-time gold for cattle purchases. Now, his aspect was tremendously mature; his height, having reached the normal adult limit, seemed poised to exceed it. In 1925, when a correspondent from Miskatonic University visited and left pale and bewildered, Wilbur stood fully six and three-quarters feet tall.

Throughout the years, Wilbur had treated his half-deformed albino mother with growing contempt, eventually forbidding her from joining him on the hills during May Eve and Hallowmass. By 1926, the poor creature confessed to Mamie Bishop that she feared him.

"There is more about him than I can tell you, Mamie," she whispered, "and nowadays there is more than I even know myself. I vow by God, I don't know what he wants or what he is trying to do."

That Halloween, the hill noises roared louder than ever, and fires burned on Sentinel Hill as custom dictated, but the villagers paid closer attention to the rhythmic screaming of vast flocks of unnaturally belated whippoorwills gathered near the unlit Whateley farmhouse. After midnight, their shrill notes erupted into a pandemonium of laughter that filled the countryside, quieting only at dawn. Then they vanished, hurrying southward, a full month overdue. No one could be certain what this meant until later. None of the countryfolk seemed to have died—but poor Lavinia Whateley, the twisted albino, was never seen again.

In the summer of 1927, Wilbur repaired two sheds in the farmyard and began moving his books and effects out to them. Shortly thereafter, Earl Sawyer reported to the loungers at Osborn's that more carpentry was underway in the Whateley farmhouse. Wilbur was sealing all doors and windows on the ground floor while removing partitions, just as he and his grandfather had done upstairs four years prior. He lived in one of the sheds, appearing unusually worried and tremulous. The townspeople generally suspected him of knowing something about his mother's disappearance, and very few ventured near his neighborhood. His height had increased to more than seven feet, showing no signs of stopping its relentless growth.
