The sky was overcast, but in the Piazza del Duomo, the atmosphere was anything but somber. A festive canopy of blue drapery stretched overhead, adorned with golden lilies and noble coats of arms, transforming the square into a stage for celebration. Banners fluttered from the corners of the Baptistery, though no carpet yet lay upon the steps of the cathedral—its marble worn smooth by countless feet, none of them particularly distinguished. It was the hour of the Advent sermons, and just as the city’s streets had been painted with holiday colors, so too did the spiritual needs of the people demand the presence of preaching in the Duomo. Yet not all who moved through the piazza were bound for worship. Men of every rank hurried about their business, their strides brisk and purposeful. Clusters of talkers stood scattered across the open space—some indifferent to the sermon, others deliberately late, or simply uninterested. Their faces bore no trace of idle pleasure; instead, they carried expressions of tension, concentration, or quiet vigilance. Many turned their heads sharply at the sound of approaching footsteps, scanning strangers with wary eyes.

At the edge of the piazza, where the bright artificial light gave way to the dull grey of the Via de’ Cerretani, a group of working men gathered—men whose clothes bore the marks of their daily toil. Most carried tools or weapons: hammers, axes, knives, or staffs that could double as cudgels. Standing in the dimness, their bare arms and grimy garments formed a stark contrast to the splendor of the square. They listened intently to Ser Cioni, a thin notary on his way to the Duomo. Once scorned in the Mercato, he now spoke with the confidence of one who knew his party held power—and who sensed influence among the common folk.

“Never speak to me,” he declared in his sharp, cutting voice, “of bloodthirsty Swiss or fierce French infantry. Let them march through mountain passes if they like—but not our streets. Peasants have shattered the finest armies of our condottieri when trapped between cliffs. I say this plainly: Florentines need fear no army within their own walls.”

“That’s true, Ser Cioni,” replied Oddo, a dyer whose hands and arms were stained crimson—like blood, some might think. A small hatchet hung at his belt. “And those French cavaliers, strutting in their fine doublets the other day, got a taste of what we could serve them. I was carrying cloth near Ognissanti when I saw them pass—looking around as if the houses of the Vespucci and the Agli were beneath them, eyeing us like proud roosters pitying the poor. ‘Yes, my fine *Galli*,’ I said, ‘puff out your chests—I’ve got a meat-axe here that’ll slip right inside you.’ Then came the lowing of the cow—the great bell of Palazzo Vecchio—so I dropped my cloth, grabbed my axe, and ran after them toward the Vigna Nuova. ‘What is it, Guccio?’ I asked when he caught up. ‘I think it’s the Medici returning,’ he said. *Bembè!* I expected nothing less! We raised a barricade. The Frenchmen looked back and saw they were trapped. Our *Ciompi* surged forward—one man swung a scythe and sliced off a knight’s plume. True as truth! And the women threw stones to frighten them. But Piero de’ Medici didn’t come after all. A shame—because we would have left him no legs or wings to flee with.”

“Well said, Oddo,” chimed in a young butcher, knife at his side. “And I believe Piero won’t be back soon—he looked like a hunted chicken when we chased and stoned him in the piazza. Cowardly, or he’d have fought harder with his horsemen. But no more Medici, whatever the French king wants to force down our throats.”

“But I don’t like those French cannons they’re talking about,” said Goro, still stout despite two years of hardship. “San Giovanni protect us! If Messer Domeneddio truly cares for us, as your friar claims, Ser Cioni, why didn’t he send the French another way—to Naples?”

“Ay, Goro,” said the dyer. “That’s a fair question. You’re not such a fool as I thought. Why couldn’t they go through Bologna? Or Arezzo? We wouldn’t mind them going to Arezzo.”

“Fools!” Ser Cioni snapped. “It will be for Florence’s good and glory!”

But before he could continue, a cry rose from several voices: “Look there!”

All heads turned toward the Via de’ Cerretani, where a procession was emerging. “Lorenzo Tornabuoni,” Ser Cioni muttered, “and one of the French noblemen staying in his house.” He added with disdain, “He pretends contentment—that deep Tornabuoni—but he’s a Medicean at heart. Remember that.”

The group was indeed impressive: Lorenzo Tornabuoni, dignified and composed; a French nobleman in dazzling white linen and rich embroidery; two Florentine gentlemen in fine attire prepared for the coming procession; and beside the Frenchman, a figure whose presence commanded attention without needing adornment. He wore only black—mourning dress—but would soon don a red mantle as Latin Secretary to the Ten. This was Tito Melema.

Tito had become indispensable in dealings with the French, thanks to his knowledge of Southern Italy and fluency in French, learned in youth. He had visited the French camp at Signa multiple times. Now, fortune shone upon him. He smiled, listened, interpreted—his usual grace and ease undimmed. Yet even the keenest observer might have noticed a subtle shift in him—not due to time alone, but to a deeper transformation: the final shedding of moral youth, the conscious embrace of a role in life. His face remained soft, his eyes clear, but something vital had gone—like the fleeting glow of dawn before full day. It was the quiet certainty of self, now fixed in place.

The Frenchman sought last-minute guidance on ceremony before returning to Signa. He wished to survey the piazza, where the royal procession would pause for religious rites. As they entered, all eyes followed them—but not with uniform admiration. Murmurs arose about the Frenchman’s hoof-shaped shoes—mocking references to royal excesses. Slight sneers slipped out, barely disguised: “Mediceans,” they whispered, with venomous undertones.

Yet Lorenzo Tornabuoni mastered the art of masking annoyance—a necessity for one seeking favor. Tito, meanwhile, felt no such passion. As an outsider, he viewed local feuds with detachment, though he still responded with charm. When they reached a vantage point near the Duomo, they paused. The decorations above the central door stirred debate. Tornabuoni summoned Piero di Cosimo, who, as usual at this hour, lounged outside Nello’s shop. Soon, a lively exchange erupted—amusing to the Frenchman, who found Piero’s blunt wit astonishing. Tito translated faithfully, and laughter spread, even among those who had earlier glowered. Snarling onlookers softened into half-smiles, humbled by missing the joke. For Tito, it was a moment of pure joy—not triumph, but simple delight in being liked. He basked in the approval, glowing under the rainbow light that now seemed to crown the laughing group. Inside, the churchgoers had vanished. The piazza shimmered with the illusion of a real Florentine festival.

But beyond the decorated square, in the shadowed alleys, another procession approached—without finery, without cheer. Here, too, French dress and strange footwear stood out—but now they were surrounded by an ever-growing crowd of angry Florentines. Leading the group were three men, stripped nearly bare, hands bound together with rope, another looped around neck and body, allowing the captors to choke them if needed. The guards were French soldiers, using broken Italian phrases and blows from knotted ropes to force the prisoners to beg.

Two complied. To every passerby, they stretched their bound hands and pleaded in trembling voices:
“For the love of God and the Holy Madonna, give us something for our ransom! We are Tuscans—we were taken in Lunigiana.”

But the third man refused to speak. He was unlike the others—older, perhaps sixty-five or more. His beard and hair, long and unkempt, were almost white. Though gaunt, his frame remained strong, upright—an echo of strength still clinging to age. His dark eyes and thick brows stood out vividly against a face pale, wrinkled, and yellow, marked by exhaustion and neglect. Yet his gaze flickered—fierce one moment, lost the next. But his lips stayed sealed. His hands, clenched at his sides, refused to lift.

The sight enraged the Florentines. Some at doors or windows tossed coins—out of habit, or fear of the French soldiery, whose cruelty had bred a silent dread. But as the group advanced deeper into the city, resistance grew. The soldiers found themselves flanked by a swelling mob of men and boys, shouting insults loud enough to reach foreign ears.

“French dogs!”
“Bullock-feet!”
“Snatch their pikes!”
“Cut the cords and let them run! They’ll flee faster than geese—look at their webbed feet!”

The soldiers understood these as jeers—and possibly threats. They wanted to draw swords, but dared not, lest they lose control of their prisoners. So they hurried, hoping to find shelter in an inn.

“Santiddio! Look!” the dyer exclaimed, recognizing the scene. “And the fools just hoot. Come on!” He yanked his axe from his belt and charged, followed by the butcher and others—except Goro, who fled up a narrow lane.

The sight of the dyer rushing forward, axe raised, sent a spark through the crowd. He didn’t fight—just pushed ahead, joining the throng and waving his weapon like a banner of rebellion. And then, like a sudden flame, Lollo, the impish apprentice of the conjuror, darted forward. He had no pity for the prisoners, but he possessed a sharp knife—and a sense of mischief. From the start, he’d imagined leaping forward, slicing the rope, and retreating before the soldier could react. Now, as the crowd surged, he saw his chance. He was close to the oldest prisoner. In a flash, he cut the cord.

“Run, old man!” he cried into the prisoner’s ear, then bolted as if winged.

The prisoner reacted instantly. Escape had been his constant dream. The crowd’s fury had given him new hope. He ran—though not fast enough, had it not been for the Florentines who surged between him and his captor. He raced into the piazza, but behind him came the tramp of boots—the other two prisoners freed, the soldiers struggling forward, hindered by their clumsy shoes and the mob.

One younger prisoner veered into Borgo di San Lorenzo, easing the chaos. But the main clash headed toward the Duomo, drawing all eyes with alarmed curiosity. The cause was hidden—only glimpses of French uniforms visible through the press.

“An escape!” Lorenzo Tornabuoni said, turning as a prisoner sprinted past. “The people aren’t satisfied with emptying the Bargello yesterday. If no authority appears, they’ll attack the sbirri and free thieves. Ah—there’s a French soldier. That’s more serious.”

The soldier struggled along the north side of the piazza, but his quarry had taken another path. The eldest prisoner had circled the Baptistery and now raced toward the Duomo, determined to seek refuge within its sacred walls.

As he climbed the steps, his foot caught. He stumbled forward, crashing into a group of signori whose backs faced him. He grasped one man’s arm to steady himself.

It was Tito Melema.

Tito turned. And there, inches from his face, was the visage of Baldassarre Calvo—his adoptive father.

They stared at each other. No words. Only silence—deep, suffocating. Baldassarre’s eyes burned with dark intensity, his grip tightening on Tito’s velvet-clad arm. Tito’s face drained of color, frozen in horror. Time stretched—yet it lasted only a heartbeat.

Then, a short laugh broke the stillness. Piero di Cosimo, standing nearby, was the only one who saw Tito’s face.
“Ha, ha! Now I know what a ghost looks like.”

“This is another escaped prisoner,” said Tornabuoni. “Who is he?”

“_Some madman, surely_,” Tito murmured.

He didn’t know how the words had escaped. In moments like this, passion speaks before reason. And sometimes, such words carry poison—poison that acts like lightning, fulfilling what years of thought could never achieve.

Their eyes locked. And when Tito spoke, it felt as though a venomous glance had pierced him, racing through his veins.

Then—relaxation. The grip loosened. Baldassarre vanished into the church, leaving only silence and shock behind.
