Universities, ancient public schools, and the Inns of Court are stubbornly stuck in the 1600s, bless their stuffy old hearts, clinging to all the weird customs our great-great-great grandpas dreamt up that every sensible person dumped decades ago. You know what’s far better set up for clean, comfortable living than a venerated centuries-old college or a learned Inn of Court? A properly run workhouse, that’s what. Over in the Temple, grown men happily pay the price of an entire nice suburban villa just for two tiny rooms: a sitting space, and a dingy little closet they call a bedroom to crash in. A random mechanic in Spitalfields gets unlimited running water right out of a tap any time he wants, but the high-and-mighty barristers? They make their laundresses haul water up three flights of stairs in dented jugs, like running water’s some unholy new fad for peasants.

Plenty of grumpy rich gents out there love sneering at the “unwashed masses” like their own ancestors weren’t literally nicknamed the Great Unwashed back in the day. The Temple in particular? You’d have had to fight tooth and nail to practice that virtue everyone says comes right after godliness (that’s cleanliness, in case you missed the hint). Take Old Grump, of the Norfolk Circuit, who’s lived under Warrington and Pen’s chambers for 30 whole years. He gets woken up at 7 a.m. every morning by the roar of their shower bath, and half the time the water leaks straight through his ceiling onto his law papers. He’s been cussing showers every single day as a ridiculous new-fangled dandy fad, and yells at their laundress every time she drips water on the staircase he has to walk down. The man is pushing 60, and he’s never once set foot in a shower in his whole life, convinced all his ancestors did just fine going their whole lives without washing too. All those fancy knights and lords with their coats of arms painted on the Upper Temple hall walls? Not a single one of them thought to install a public bathhouse for their lawyer pals, did they? The local Temple history nerd never wrote a word about it. Sure there’s Pump Court and Fountain Court, but no one’s ever seen a senior judge splashing around in the fountain. Shocking, how many old barristers could’ve used a good soak.

For all their weird grime and backwards ways, though, those Inns with their Lamb and Flag and Winged Horse emblems are weirdly charming: that rough, no-rules bachelor comfort you still think about fondly years later. Law students might be too busy cramming torts to get poetic, but any book lover wandering past those old chambers gets starry eyed thinking about the greats who lived there: Eldon scribbling case notes, Coke obsessing over Littleton, Fielding pounding out newspaper articles at 2 a.m. with ink all over his cuffs while his printer’s kid naps on the landing, Dr. Johnson tramping through the fog to visit Goldsmith in Brick Court, even Sir Roger de Coverley strolling the Temple Gardens gawking at ladies in their huge hoop skirts. If some mischievous Temple Asmodeus could sneak us a full day’s play-by-play of one of those four-story dingy court houses where Pen and Warrington live, you’d end up with one very chaotic, very funny book.

Take the ground floor: there’s a hotshot parliamentary barrister who makes tens of thousands a year, drives out to Belgravia for fancy dinners every night. Not long ago, though, he was a starving unknown stuck in a garret, scribbling anonymous penny-a-line literature just to eat, waiting years for a single client, dodging angry debt collectors, convinced he was ruined forever before he hit that lucky jackpot on the Bar’s weird lottery wheel. There are hundreds of better lawyers than him who make less than his old clerk, who a year ago couldn’t even get shopkeepers to extend him credit for boot blacking for his boss’s unpaid shoes.

First floor? There’s that ancient famous scholar Mr. Doomsday, who’s lived in the same chambers for 50 years, his shelves crammed full of classics and legal books, all alone, hoarding knowledge and money like a tiny book dragon. He trundles home from his club after dinner every night, to his empty chambers, no wife, no kids, no mates, nothing but his books. When he kicks the bucket, the Inn will put up a fancy little plaque honoring him, and his ungrateful heirs will toss half his library straight into the fire. Sounds like a great old age plan, right?

We can’t hang around his door too long, though. Right above him lives our old mate Grump, who’s holed up with three other equally ancient barristers for their regular weekly rubber of whist, after polishing off three full bottles of port between them. You can spot all four of them fast asleep in the pews at Temple Church every single Sunday, no attorney has bothered them for 20 years, they’ve all got little nest eggs enough to never work a day again.

On the other side of that third landing, where Pen and Warrington live, is Mr. Paley, the super-achiever college fellow who got all the top honours back in his university days. He reads and scribbles case notes until 2 a.m. every night, wakes up at 7 a.m. sharp, works at the pleader’s offices straight through until an hour before dinner, then comes home to cram right up until dawn the next day. Half the time he’s just blowing out his lamp to head to work when Pen and Warrington are stumbling home from some late-night pub adventure. Everyone praises Paley as the hardest working bloke alive, everyone says Warrington’s wasting his whole life lazing about. But who’s actually using his time better? Paley never has a spare minute to think, never makes a friend he doesn’t think will advance his career, never stops to look at a pretty sunset, read a poem, listen to a nice song. His whole world ends at the edge of his reading lamp. Art, love, nature? He’s locked all of it out, just so he can memorize thousands of pages of tedious legal jargon to make a living. He still shivers when he runs into Warrington on the stairs, like Warrington’s a hellbound sinner who’s throwing his whole life away.

That very pale, pinched, ambition-crazed look on Paley’s face is half the reason Pen never had the energy to grind his way to becoming a judge. He was having way too much fun living the easy Temple bachelor life. His uncle Major Pendennis was writing gushing congratulatory letters back to Pen’s widowed mom at Fairoaks, swearing up and down that Pen had sown all his wild oats, was a changed hardworking law student now. The truth was, Pen had dropped all the stuffy fancy-gentleman airs he’d picked up around his aristocratic college mates, and the rough, cheap fun of London bachelor life was brand new and thrilling to him. He didn’t even care anymore that he couldn’t afford fancy horses to ride in Rotten Row, he was just happy strolling through the park looking at everyone else’s. His days were perfect: a few hours of law reading in the morning, a walk in the park, a row on the Thames, a hike up to Hampstead, a cheap hearty tavern dinner, some nights out with the boys having innocent fun (Pen thought women were so great he refused to hang around any he didn’t think was proper and good), quiet nights at home with Warrington, a couple of pipes, a bottle of cheap gin that their laundress Mrs. Flanagan always insisted on taste-testing first to make sure it wasn’t swill. No wonder he never wanted to go back to boring stuffy high society parties. The Major would’ve had a full-on heart attack if he found out how little actual law studying Pen was doing.

The only law-related thing Pen was actually perfectly diligent about was showing up to eat his required dinners in Hall, like every law student has to. The Upper Temple Hall is a delightfully weird time capsule, the kind of place you sit down to dinner and feel like you’re eating a 17th century meal. The judges sit up on the raised high table under all those portraits of old kings and dead chief justices, the working barristers have their own messes, and the students sit at their own tables, a mixed bunch of everyone: 60-year-old grey-haired attorneys working to qualify as barristers, dandies who just want the “barrister” title for clout, sunburnt colonial students who’ve come over from the Caribbean to qualify before they go home, a whole herd of Irish lads hanging around Middle Temple Lane before they go back to Ireland. There are the nerds who talk nothing but law the whole dinner, the rowing boys who only chat about sculling matches and the Opera, the political nerds who yell at each other about debating club meetings. Pen made friends with every single group except the law nerds, whose whole conversation was gibberish to him.

The Temple charges you practically nothing for a full dinner: soup, roast meat, tarts, a glass of port or sherry. You sit in messes of four, each quartet gets a whole roast joint, an apple pie, a full bottle of wine. The veteran students have all kinds of silly little schemes to score better food than the basic roast, what they call “dodges.” One of those veteran food schemers was a bloke named Lowton, who grabbed Pen’s gown on his very first night. “Hang on a minute, mate,” Lowton said, eyes glued to the benchers’ high table, “there’s only three old judges up there facing 12 silver covered dishes. They’re never going to eat all that. If we hang back a minute, we might get their leftover turtle soup!”

Lowton was the undisputed captain of every mess he joined, always fighting to get the prime cut of meat, hoarding the gravy, making sure he was the one to grab the 13th glass from the bottle so he got an extra pour. His greatest dream in life never cost more than 18 pence, bless his heart. He immediately latched onto Pen, who looked like a proper well-bred gent way too posh to hang around the other scruffy students. “It’s boiled beef day today, innit, sir?” he said to Pen, starting the conversation. Pen, who had no idea what day it was, laughed and said it was his very first term. Lowton immediately gave him a full guided tour of the room, pointing out every notable: the bald bencher Boosey who’s probably eating turtle soup, old Grump who’s been eating dinner here 40 years, the fancy rich lads at the opposite table — the Bishop of Ealing’s son, Lord Cinqbar’s little brother, who go to all the fancy society parties that get written up in the Morning Post. “You think those blokes are gonna eat this dinner?” Lowton snickered. “Their carriages are waiting right outside Essex Street this second. They’re gonna sit here for ten minutes, then bounce off to a fancy banquet. If I get put in their mess? I get the entire bottle of port all to myself, free!”

Then he pointed out the Irish contingent: the flashily dressed Molloy Maloney who brags nonstop about his uncle the Major-General Sir Hector O’Dowd, who showed up to Hall once in silver striped trousers the day he got presented at court, the fiery radical who gives rants at the debating club that make everyone’s blood run cold, and Jack Finucane the newspaper reporter. Pen was already delighted with the chaotic Lowton, when a ragged-looking barrister in a faded old gown and a shirt that was clean but definitely had frayed cuffs trundled over, couldn’t find a seat anywhere else, and plopped down right next to Pen. He glanced at Pen’s name scribbled on the mess slip, and grinned. “Hey, we’re both Boniface College boys, aren’t we? I’m Warrington.”

Pen’s face lit right up. He’d heard stories about the legendary Warrington, the college hero who’d thrashed the abusive bargee Bill Simes back at Oxford. They immediately fell to chatting about old college pranks, Pen even stopped pretending he hated port wine when Warrington poured himself a big glass, much to Lowton’s disappointment. After dinner, Pen said he was planning to go dress up and hear Grisi sing in *Norma* at the opera. “Nah, that’s boring,” Warrington said. “Come back to my place in Lamb Court, smoke a quick pipe with me, we can gush about old college times for hours.”

They left, Lowton staring after them wistfully, reverent that Pen was mates with a baronet’s son. That night Pen and Warrington became best friends for life. Pen liked hanging around the cheap pubs with Warrington way more than he ever liked stuffy fancy parties, and within a few weeks he’d given up his expensive St. James’s lodgings, moved straight in with Warrington at Lamb Court, and their two staff — Pidgeon the errand boy and Mrs. Flanagan the laundress — now split their work looking after the two of them.
