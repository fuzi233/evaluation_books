Mrs. Jennings, though accustomed to spending much of the year amidst the homes of her children and friends, maintained a permanent residence of her own. Since the passing of her husband, a successful merchant of a less fashionable quarter, she had made a house near Portman Square her winter sanctuary. As January approached, her thoughts turned inevitably toward this retreat, and one day, she abruptly—and to the surprise of all present—invited the elder Miss Dashwoods to join her.

Elinor, failing to notice the stormy shift in her sister's expression or the fervent light in her eyes that betrayed a deep desire to accept, offered an immediate and polite refusal. She spoke for both sisters, citing their firm resolve not to abandon their mother during the winter season. Mrs. Jennings received this rejection with mild astonishment, only to renew her plea with renewed vigor.

"Oh, Lord! I am certain your mother can spare you quite easily," she declared warmly. "I truly beg you to honor me with your company; I have quite set my heart upon it. Do not imagine you will be a burden; I shall make no special accommodations for you. It will simply mean sending Betty by coach—a sum I can certainly afford. The three of us shall travel comfortably in my chaise. And once in town, if you prefer not to follow my exact footsteps, you are welcome to stay with one of my daughters. I am sure your mother will not object; having successfully dispatched my own children, she will likely view me as the ideal guardian for you. And if I fail to secure a marriage for either of you before I am done with you, it shall not be my fault. I shall speak highly of you to every eligible young man you meet."

"I suspect," interjected Sir John, "that Miss Marianne would welcome such an arrangement, provided her elder sister agreed. It is a pity she is denied even a small measure of pleasure simply because Miss Dashwood objects. I would advise you both to depart for town the moment you tire of Barton, without informing Miss Dashwood of your plans."

"Nay!" cried Mrs. Jennings. "I shall be overjoyed to have Miss Marianne's company, regardless of whether Miss Dashwood accompanies her. The more the merrier, as they say. It would be far more comfortable for them to be together; should they grow weary of me, they could confide in one another and laugh at my peculiarities behind my back. But I must have one, if not both of them. Good heavens, how can I endure poking around alone, accustomed as I have been until this winter to the company of Charlotte? Come, Miss Marianne, let us seal this bargain with a handshake. If Miss Dashwood changes her mind later, so much the better."

"I thank you, ma'am, most sincerely," Marianne replied with evident emotion. "Your invitation guarantees my eternal gratitude. To accept it would bring me such happiness—perhaps the greatest joy I am capable of feeling. Yet, my mother, my dearest and kindest mother... I acknowledge the validity of Elinor's argument. Were her happiness or comfort diminished by our absence? Oh, no! Nothing could tempt me to leave her. It must not, and shall not, become a struggle."

Mrs. Jennings reiterated her assurance that Mrs. Dashwood could easily spare them. Elinor, now fully understanding her sister's state of mind and recognizing how her eagerness to reunite with Willoughby had rendered her indifferent to nearly everything else, ceased her direct opposition. She deferred the decision to her mother, though she doubted she would find support in her efforts to prevent a journey she disapproved of for Marianne, and one she herself wished to avoid for personal reasons. Whatever Marianne desired, her mother was eager to facilitate; she could not expect to sway a woman who had never learned to distrust the affections of a man like Willoughby. Furthermore, Elinor dared not reveal her own reasons for hesitating to visit London. That Marianne, so fastidious in nature and thoroughly acquainted with Mrs. Jennings' mannerisms, should overlook every discomfort and disregard anything that might wound her sensitive feelings in pursuit of a single object, was a testament to the intensity of her desire that Elinor found difficult to witness, despite everything that had transpired.

Upon learning of the invitation, Mrs. Dashwood, convinced that the trip would prove delightful for both her daughters and perceiving through her own affectionate attentiveness just how deeply Marianne's heart was involved, refused to hear of their declining the offer on her account. She insisted they both accept immediately and began to anticipate, with her characteristic cheerfulness, the various advantages such a separation would bring.

"I am delighted with the plan," she exclaimed. "It is precisely what I could have wished. Margaret and I shall benefit as much as you. Once you and the Middletons depart, we shall settle into a quiet, happy routine with our books and music! You will find Margaret greatly improved upon your return. I have even planned alterations to your bedrooms that can now be executed without inconvenience. It is right that you should visit London; every young woman of your station ought to be familiar with its manners and amusements. You will be under the care of a kind, motherly woman, and I have no doubt of her goodness. Moreover, you will likely see your brother. Regardless of his faults—or those of his wife, considering his lineage—I cannot bear the thought of you remaining so estranged from one another."

"Though you act with your usual concern for our happiness," Elinor remarked gently, "there remains one objection which, in my opinion, cannot be so easily dismissed."

Marianne's countenance fell instantly.

"And what," asked Mrs. Dashwood, "is my dear, prudent Elinor suggesting now? What formidable obstacle is she bringing forward? Do not speak of the expense."

"My objection is this," Elinor continued calmly. "While I hold Mrs. Jennings' heart in high regard, her society offers us little pleasure, and her protection affords us no social standing."

"That is entirely true," her mother replied. "However, apart from the company of others, you will have little interaction with her anyway, and you will almost always appear in public accompanied by Lady Middleton."

"If Elinor is deterred by her dislike of Mrs. Jennings," Marianne interjected, "it need not prevent ME from accepting. I have no such scruples, and I am certain I could endure every unpleasantness with minimal effort."

Elinor could not suppress a smile at this display of indifference toward the manners of a woman she had often struggled to persuade Marianne to treat with basic politeness. Resolving internally that if her sister persisted, she would go as well—deeming it improper to leave Marianne solely to her own judgment or to subject Mrs. Jennings to Marianne's capricious whims for the sake of domestic comfort—she found her determination reinforced by the knowledge that Edward Ferrars, according to Lucy, would not arrive in town until February. Their visit could thus be concluded without unreasonable delay.

"I will have you BOTH go," Mrs. Dashwood declared. "These objections are nonsensical. You will find great pleasure in London, especially in each other's company. If Elinor would ever condescend to anticipate enjoyment, she would foresee it from many sources; perhaps she expects some from improving her acquaintance with her sister-in-law's family."

Elinor had long wished for an opportunity to subtly weaken her mother's dependence on the attachments of Edward and herself, hoping the eventual revelation of the truth would be less shocking. Seizing this moment, though she felt the attempt was nearly hopeless, she forced herself to begin her design with as much composure as possible. "I like Edward Ferrars very much and shall always be glad to see him," she said. "But regarding the rest of the family, it is a matter of perfect indifference to me whether I am ever known to them or not."

Mrs. Dashwood smiled and said nothing. Marianne raised her eyes in astonishment, and Elinor realized she might as well have remained silent.

After little further discussion, it was settled that the invitation would be fully accepted. Mrs. Jennings received the news with immense joy, offering countless assurances of kindness and care; nor was it merely a pleasure for her. Sir John was equally delighted; for a man whose greatest fear was solitude, the addition of two companions to his London household was a significant gain. Even Lady Middleton managed to feign delight, a feat requiring considerable effort on her part. As for the Miss Steeles, particularly Lucy, they had never been happier in their lives than this intelligence made them.

Elinor submitted to the arrangement that contradicted her wishes with less reluctance than she had anticipated. Regarding herself, it became a matter of indifference whether she traveled to town or not. Seeing her mother so thoroughly pleased and her sister so visibly exhilarated—restored to her usual animation and elevated to a gaiety exceeding her normal self—Elinor could not be dissatisfied with the cause, nor did she allow herself to doubt the consequences.

Marianne's joy surpassed mere happiness; her spirits were so perturbed and her eagerness to depart so intense that only her unwillingness to leave her mother served as a balm to her calm. At the moment of parting, her grief was excessive. Her mother's sorrow was hardly less, and Elinor was the only one of the three who seemed to regard the separation as anything short of eternal.

Their departure occurred in the first week of January. The Middletons were to follow within a week, while the Miss Steeles remained at the park, intending to leave only with the rest of the family.
