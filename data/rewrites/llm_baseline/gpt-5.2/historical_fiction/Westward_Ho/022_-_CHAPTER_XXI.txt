“Follow thee? Follow thee? Wha wad na follow thee? Lang hast thou looed and trusted us fairly.”

If ever a man might have been forgiven for falling sick, it was Amyas Leigh. The hot land had a way of seeping through cloth and skin alike, creeping in as noiselessly as damp into a ship’s biscuit. Yet he did not take the yellow fever then, and the one reason he gave Cary was reason enough for those who have carried others when their own knees shook.

“I have no leisure to be ill while my men lie ill.”

So long as there was work—lifting, hauling, ordering, watching—his blood kept its pace like a drum that will not slacken for pain. But he knew, as all who have driven themselves hard know, that the moment the labor ceased, the body might claim its due with interest.

Next morning he called, not so much a council of war as a desperate inquiry into how to keep men alive. Below-decks the air seemed thickened—close, sour, tainted as if a great pot had been left to stand and turn, and all the ship were forced to breathe its spoiled steam. Above, the crew were wild with fear, that sharp-edged fear which makes men talk of mutiny as readily as they talk of supper.

Amyas faced them first, plain and fierce.

“I cannot see what profit can come to you by killing me,” he told them, “or”—and he let his hand fall meaningly to his sword—“being killed by me.”

Then he went below, where the voices of his chief men rose and fell like different instruments playing tunes that would not meet.

The doctor had his learning ready, as if learning were broth that could be ladled hot into a dying man. He spoke of humors and complexions and animal spirits, naming invisible things while the visible men shook with ague.

Jack Brimblecombe, who could find a text for any disaster, called it God’s visitation.

Cary smiled and jested with a dryness that was almost despair; a man will sometimes crack a walnut on the edge of his own coffin.

Yeo stood like an old oak, fatal as winter, buttressing himself with Scripture as if words could nail down fate.

Drew, the master, would not be drawn beyond his own craft.

“My business is to sail the ship,” said he, “and not to cure calentures.”

Amyas, at his wits’ end, caught at his hair till he seemed like to tear it out, and then flung his anger round the room in a blaze.

“Doctor! a fig for your humors and complexions! Can you cure a man’s humors, or change his complexion? Can an Ethiopian change his skin, or a leopard his spots? Don’t shove off your ignorance on God, sir. I ask you what’s the reason of this sickness, and you don’t know.

“Jack Brimblecombe, don’t preach God’s visitation at me; this looks more like the devil’s visitation. We are doing God’s work, and He is not likely to hinder us. Down with the devil, say I.

“Cary, laughing killed the cat, but it won’t cure a Christian.

“Yeo, when an angel tells me it’s God’s will we should all die like dogs in a ditch, I’ll call it God’s will; but not before.

“And you, Drew—if your business is to sail the ship, then sail her out of this infernal poison-trap this very morning, if you can—which you can’t. The mischief is in the air, and nowhere else. I felt it run through me coming down last night, and smelt it like any sewer: and if it was not in the air, why was my boat’s crew taken first, tell me that?”

Silence answered him. There are moments when even the boldest tongues grow blank.

Then Amyas, as if he had seized the truth by the throat, spoke on, quick and hard.

“I’ll tell you why they were taken first: because the mist only rose five or six feet above the stream, and we were in it, while you on board were above it. And those that were taken on board this morning—every one slept on the main-deck; and every one was in fear of the fever. Whereby I judge two things—Keep as high as you can, and fear nothing but God, and we’re all safe yet.”

“But the fog was up to our round-tops at sunrise,” Cary answered.

“I know it. But we on the half-deck were not in it so long as those below, and we had freer air. Besides—I suspect the heat in the evening draws the poison out more, and when it gets cold toward morning, the venom goes off somehow.”

How that venom “went off” no man there could tell; they had no neat rule for it, only the harsh schooling of suffering. Yet common sense sat on Amyas’s side; and old Yeo, remembering words he had heard long ago, rose at last.

“As I have said before,” quoth he, “the Lord hath sent us a very young Daniel for judge. I mind me now how Spaniards say these calentures lie always in the low ground, and never come more than a few hundred feet above the sea.”

“Then let us go up those few hundred feet.”

They stared at him—at one another. They had expected orders about swabbing decks and boiling vinegar and bleeding men. They had not expected to be turned loose upon a jungle.

“Gentlemen,” said Amyas, “look the devil straight in the face, if you would hit him in the right place. We cannot get the ship to sea as she is; and if we could, we cannot go home empty-handed; and we surely cannot stay here to die of fever. We must leave the ship and go inland.”

“Inland?” the voices rose together, save only Yeo’s, who kept his assent in his eyes.

“Up to the mountains; stockade a camp, and get our sick and provisions thither.”

“And what next?” someone demanded.

“And when we are recruited, march over the mountains, and surprise St. Jago de Leon.”

Cary let fly an oath, half admiration, half incredulity.

“Amyas! You are a daring fellow!”

“Not a bit,” said Amyas. “It is the plain path of prudence.”

“So it is, sir,” Yeo replied, steady as a ploughman measuring a field, “and I follow you in it.”

“And so do I,” squeaked Brimblecombe, eager as if salvation lay up-hill.

“Nay then, Jack, thou shalt not outrun me. So I say yes too,” said Cary.

Amyas turned to Drew.

“At your service, sir, to live or die,” said the master. “I know naught about stockading; but Sir Francis would have given the same counsel, I verily believe, if he had been in your place.”

“Then tell the men we start in an hour. Win over the Pelicans, Yeo and Drew; the rest must follow like sheep over a hedge.”

The Pelicans, and the liberated galley-slaves, came in at once; they had tasted Spanish chains and feared fever less than another hand on the collar. But the Rose’s crew gave Amyas a stormy hour, fretting and accusing in the same breath. The mangrove thicket, dense as woven rope, shut out all sight, and the great question arose: where were the hills at all?

“The hills are not three miles to the south-west of you at this moment,” said Amyas. “I marked every shoulder of them as we ran in.”

“I suppose you meant to take us there?” one cried; and that question, like a spark, lit all the hidden tinder of suspicion.

Amyas stamped it out with sheer force.

“Fools! If I had not wit enough to look ahead farther than you do, where would you be? Are you mad as well as reckless, to rise against your own captain because he has two strings to his bow? Go my way, I say, or, as I live, I’ll blow up the ship and every soul on board, and save you the pain of rotting here by inches.”

They knew the tone; Amyas was not a man to threaten air. He did not mean to do it—he meant them to believe he would—and belief was enough. The sight of the Pelicans setting to work with willing hands shamed the rest into obedience.

So they went. And if there is any mercy in leaving misery undescribed, let it be here. For five or six hours under a sun that did not merely burn but stewed—like a cauldron kept always at the boil—they stumbled over mangrove roots slick as wet bone, hewed through thorn that caught clothes and skin alike, dragged sick men whose sweat had turned sour with fear, hauled casks and sacks that grew heavier with every step, and fought the small torments of that land—snakes that slid away with a whisper, mosquitoes that sang triumph at each bare wrist, false alarms of Spaniards that made the heart leap and the breath go thin. The forest was never silent: it clicked and creaked and murmured, a thousand little lives rasping and droning, and beneath them the steady, thick hush of heat, like a great ear pressed close to listen for weakness.

By sunset, they had gained a level place a full thousand feet above the sea, backed by a cliff so sheer it might have been God’s own wall, and guarded below by steep wooded slopes. A few trees felled in the right place would make it, to all but artillery, unassailable.

Amyas settled the sick under the arched roots of an enormous cottonwood—roots not so much roots as buttresses, like the ribs of a cathedral turned inside-out. The air up there was different; it moved. It tasted of green things crushed fresh, of water over stone, instead of the lowland reek that had clung to the men like an unwashed garment.

He made a second journey down toward the ship for hammocks and blankets, while Yeo’s quiet strength held the company together. It had been Yeo who found the brook, and then, like a man who has followed streams in many countries, drove them to keep its course, arguing they would find water and flatter ground where water chooses to linger. Again and again, when men begged to be let lie down and be done, he went back, speaking to them as one speaks to oxen that must plod another furrow: not cruelty, but necessity.

Cary brought up the rear like a cheerful tyrant—bullying, rallying, snatching burdens from faint hands, and singing on halts in a voice that made the weary lift their heads as if sound itself could feed them. Once or twice he drove back at sword-point those who tried to slink away downhill, and then laughed at them, calling them cowards to keep them from thinking themselves dying men. There is a kind of hopefulness which is not blindness but defiance, and Cary had it.

That night, with the camp quieting to groans and low talk, Amyas, beside himself with a fierce joy that the two men he trusted most were true, leaned close to Yeo and Cary and whispered what had grown in him all day like a seed in hot soil.

“Cortez burnt his ships when he landed. Why should not we?”

Yeo sprang upright as if struck by lightning, then sank down again, whispering, eyes alight under his grey brows.

“Do you say that, captain? ’Tis from above then, that’s certain; for it hath been hanging on my mind too all day.”

“There’s no hurry,” Amyas answered. “We must clear her out first, you know.”

Cary said nothing. He stared into the dark as if seeing ships burn there already, and other, stranger fires beyond.

By dawn Amyas had them fortifying. The place was strong by nature; high cliffs hemmed it, and the mountain-range behind was so vast and tangled that no enemy would come over it, and if he did, he would never find a handful of men hidden under that roof of trees. Attack must come from below.

Against that, Amyas had smaller trees felled and laid with boughs outward over the crest of the slope, a snarling hedge of timber that would stop man or beast in two steps. Trunks were sawn into logs, set lengthwise, steadied by stakes and packed earth; and in three or four hours’ savage labor they had a stockade that would laugh at anything but cannon.

When it was done, Amyas scrambled up into the boughs of the huge ceiba-tree that dominated the place and took his seat there like a watchman in a living tower. From that height the world spread out—forest like a green sea, mangrove-swamps like a darker stain, and beyond them the bright blue of open water, sheeted with white. The sounds came up too: the steady insect-hum like an unseen wheel, bird-calls sharp as whistles, distant surf with its unending breath. For a moment he let it fill him, as if sound itself might keep his thoughts from tearing him.

To stay long was impossible; to avenge himself on La Guayra was impossible; to go without knowing whether Frank was alive seemed at first impossible too. But was he to sacrifice Brimblecombe, Cary, and eighty men again to his private grief? That question wrung him until rage and prayer came one upon the other, and he wept for both.

At last he cried aloud, as if the forest must hear him: “God help him! for I cannot!” And his mind turned like a compass needle, fixing on duty.

He went down calm, and drew Cary and Yeo aside. They could not refit the ship without dying of fever in the doing; neither dared deny it. Even if they refitted her, they would likely fight Spaniards again; the Indian’s warning had been true enough, and there was no doubting now that the enemy had been forewarned. Nor was there any doubt—though Amyas spoke it with a bitterness that tasted like iron—that Eustace had been the traitor.

“Let us try St. Jago then,” said Amyas. “Sack it, come down on La Guayra in the rear, take a ship there, and so get home.”

“Nay, Will,” Cary answered, and for all his daring his voice had the tone of a man tasting a broth and finding poison. “If they have strengthened themselves against us at La Guayra, where they had little to lose, surely they have done so at St. Jago, where they have much. I hear the town is large, though new; and besides, how can we get over these mountains without a guide?”

“Or with one?” said Cary, with a sigh, looking up at the wall of wood and rock range upon range. “But it is strange to find you, at least, throwing cold water on a daring plot.”

Amyas’s eyes narrowed, and he spoke lower.

“What if I had a still more daring one? Did you ever hear of the golden city of Manoa?”

Yeo gave a grim, joyful laugh.

“I have, sir; and so have the old hands from the Pelican and the Jesus of Lubec, I doubt not.”

“So much the better.” And Amyas told Cary what he had learned from the Spaniard, while Yeo, like an elder repeating half-forgotten harvest-lore, set rumor upon rumor beside it—tales gathered in taverns and on decks at night, names passed mouth to mouth until the sound of them carried more weight than truth. Cary sat half aghast as that glittering phantasm grew—golden city, far rivers, strange nations—as if the jungle itself were making promises.

At last Cary shook his head, half laughing, half sober.

“So that was why you wanted to burn the ship. Well—after all, nobody needs me at home, and one less at table won’t be missed. So you want to play Cortez, eh?”

“We shall never need to play Cortez,” said Amyas, “because we shall have no such cannibal fiends’ tyranny to rid the earth of as he had. And I trust we shall fear God enough not to play Pizarro.”

So it dropped for the time; but none of them forgot.

In that mountain nook they lay ten days and more. Several died—some from fever laid atop wounds, some, likely, because the surgeon would have had blood for everything, as if a man were a cask to be tapped till the bad liquor ran out. He was all but mutinous when Amyas stayed his hand; but Amyas would not let him drain what little strength remained.

The others mended steadily, helped by certain herbs Yeo administered with the air of an old husbandman who knows what leaf cools a swelling and what bark steadies a shaking stomach. The doctor scowled, for it galled him to see plants do what his lancet could not.

By daily trips down to the ship, provisions were fetched up, so that they ate better than despair might have warranted. There were raccoons and monkeys and other small beasts which Yeo and Hawkins’s veterans knew how to snare. There was fruit, and vegetables; above all the tender mountain cabbage of the Areca palm, cut with care lest the heart be bruised, then boiled till it yielded like marrow, and eaten with salt like something stolen from a king’s own table. And there was the fresh milk of the cow-tree, white and thick, drawn not from beast but from bark, and warming in the mouth with a faint wild sweetness, as if the forest itself had learned the craft of dairying. They paid well for what they took, and the land, if it cannot love, at least did not refuse them.

All day a watch was kept from the branches of the mighty ceiba. And what a tree it was—an empire of wood. The hugest English oak would have looked a stunted hedge beside it. Its roots rose like twisted boards, walls twelve feet high, between which the whole crew might shelter with ammunition and stores. Above that, the trunk, forty feet in girth, soared smooth for a hundred feet, then burst into boughs so vast that each limb was itself a stately tree. The topmost twigs were two hundred and fifty feet from the ground.

Yet it was easy to climb, for the lianes hung down like ropes set there for men’s hands—smooth cords trailing to earth, often without knot or leaf. Once up, you were in a world between earth and heaven. Cary joked that it was like Jack’s beanstalk: somewhere amid that tangle a giant might keep his castle and his park.

Gardens there were, truly enough. Pendent cactuses drooped like chandeliers; orchises flamed in strange colors; wild pines clung and flowered. One half of the tree was in rich foliage; the other half, leafless, carried brilliant yellow blossoms on every twig, about which hummingbirds whirred like living sparks. Parrots peeped and screamed in the hollows. Lizards basked on bark like jewels made flesh. Gaudy finches flitted and chirruped. Butterflies hovered on the upper air. Insects hummed from morn till eve with that ceaseless music of the tropics—so lovely at first, and then so pressing, so inescapable, that a man might long for one honest silence. At sunset tree-toads came out to croak and snore till dawn, their chorus thick and throaty, as if the night itself were breathing.

There was more life round that one tree than in a whole square mile of English soil; and Amyas, lounging among its branches, felt sometimes he could stay there forever, fed by sound and sight alone. Then he would start, remembering that a few days might bring the foe, and force him to a choice at which the stoutest heart might falter without shame.

So he watched—often taking the scout’s place himself—over the forest at his feet, the flat mangrove-swamps below, and the blue sea beyond. No sail appeared for days. As fear of fever ebbed, the men began to ask when they would go down and refit the ship. Amyas put them off as best he could, keeping his deeper purpose in his own breast.

Until, one noon, he saw a large ship creeping along the shore from the westward under easy sail. He recognized her—or believed he did—the vessel they had passed on their way.

If it were she, she must have run past them to La Guayra by night and now returned, perhaps searching the coast.

He watched, hoping she might glide past the river mouth and be gone. But she lay-to close to shore. After a while two boats pulled in from her and vanished behind the mangroves.

Sliding down a liane, he told the camp. The men, sick of waiting, shouted with joy and set to work as if battle were a feast promised after fasting.

Four brass swivels they had brought up were mounted and fixed in logs to command the path. Musketeers and archers clustered round with matches and tackle ready. Half a dozen good marksmen volunteered up into the cotton-tree with their arquebuses, bragging that from there “a man might have very pretty shooting.” Prayers followed as a matter of course; dinner too, as if war and eating were the two hinges on which their days turned. Yet two weary hours dragged by with no sign.

Then a wreath of white smoke curled up from the swamp, and the crack of a caliver followed. Amid English growls, the Spanish flag rose above the trees—and floated, horrible to behold, at the masthead of the Rose. They were signaling their ship for more hands; and indeed a third boat soon pushed off and vanished into the forest.

Another hour of waiting, during which men paced, muttering loud enough to betray themselves. Amyas hushed them sharply. The Spaniards might never find the track; they might pass the stockade within yards and see nothing; if they missed it, they would likely return for the present. He exacted a promise: perfect silence until he gave the word.

Hardly had the promise been wrung from them when a helmet glanced in the path below—then another, then another.

“Fools,” whispered Amyas to Cary. “They come up in single file, rushing on their own death. Lie close, men!”

The path was narrow; two could seldom walk abreast. It was steep enough that men stumbled as they climbed, their armor clinking softly, the sound swallowed by leaves. They seemed half unwilling, halting more than once; but Amyas heard an authoritative voice behind. Presently a figure pushed forward, sword in hand, and at the sight of him both Amyas and Cary started as if the air had turned suddenly cold.

“Is it he?”

“Surely,” whispered Cary. “I know those legs among a thousand, though they be in armor.”

“It is my turn for him now, Cary, remember. Silence—silence, men!”

The Spaniards reached within forty yards of the stockade and paused, suspecting a trap, puzzled by the complete stillness. Amyas sprang atop the stockade with a white flag in his hand; but his heart hammered so fiercely at the sight of that hated man that the words fought him.

“Don Guzman! The quarrel is between you and me, not between your men and mine. I would have sent you a challenge at La Guayra, but you were away. I challenge you now to single combat.”

Don Guzman’s answer came like a lash.

“Lutheran dog, I have a halter for you, but no sword! As you served us at Smerwick, we will serve you now. Pirate and ravisher, you and yours shall share Oxenham’s fate, as you have copied his crimes, and learn what it is to set foot unbidden on the dominions of the king of Spain.”

“The devil take you and the king of Spain together!” shouted Amyas, laughing loud. “This ground belongs to him no more than it does to me, but to Queen Elizabeth, in whose name I have taken lawful possession of it as you ever did of Caracas. Fire, men! and God defend the right!”

Both sides answered with steel and flame. Amyas dropped behind the stockade just in time; a bullet whistled over the timber. The English line burst into one blaze of muskets and swivels, raking the Spaniards from front to rear. In that cramped file the front ranks fell over each other in heaps; the rear turned and ran, tumbling down the steep path, chased by bullets and arrows that struck men into sudden silence.

“Out, men—charge them! See, the Don runs like the rest!” Amyas cried.

He scrambled over the abatis with about thirty at his heels, for he hoped to snatch a prisoner and wring from him news of Frank.

But Amyas was unjust in that bitter taunt. Don Guzman, by what seemed miracle, had been only slightly wounded. When his men broke, he rushed back to rally them, but the flood of fugitives bore him away.

The Spaniards vanished into thick bush before the English could overtake them. Amyas, fearing a rally and an ambush against so small a party, withdrew against his will.

In the path lay fourteen Spaniards—dead. One of the wounded had fired as he lay, and Amyas’s men, maddened by desperation and by the ghastly tales of the rescued galley-slaves, had slain them all before their captain could stop them.

“Are you mad?” cried Amyas, striking up a sword as it rose again. “Will you kill an Indian?”

He dragged from the bushes an Indian lad of sixteen, slightly wounded, crawling away like a copper snake along the ground. Someone cursed.

“The black vermin has sent an arrow through my leg—poisoned too, most like.”

“God grant not,” said Amyas. “But an Indian is worth his weight in gold to us now.”

He tucked the lad under his arm like a bundle. The boy, seeing escape was gone, resigned himself with a stoic stillness that seemed older than his years. They brought him in and treated him kindly enough, but he refused to eat. After much questioning he gave his reason in broken signs and fearful words: he meant to make them kill him at once, for fatten him they should not.

Little by little they understood. The Spaniards, it seemed, had told him that the English always fattened and ate their prisoners like the Caribs. And until he saw the English go out and bury the Spanish corpses, nothing would persuade him that those bodies were not meant to be cooked for supper.
