—*The Wife of Usher's Well*

The evening of February 15, 1587, found Mrs. Leigh standing upon the terrace at Burrough, her gaze fixed upon the distant sea. Three long years had passed—three years of silence, of unanswered prayers, of hollow eyes in Bideford and Appledore, watching for those who had vanished into the West. Once, hope had flared when news came of Drake’s return from the Spanish Main—but no word of the *Rose*, nor of the souls aboard her. And so, with the quiet resolve of one who has learned to endure, Mrs. Leigh bowed her head and whispered, *"The Lord gave, and the Lord hath taken away; blessed be the name of the Lord!"*

Her hair had silvered; her cheeks, once full of warmth, were hollowed by grief. Yet every evening, when the tide was high, she walked the terrace, scanning the horizon for a sail that never came.

Tonight, however, the air was alive with clamor. Bells pealed from Northam’s tower; bonfires blazed in the streets. The news had spread like wildfire: Mary, Queen of Scots, was dead, her head struck from her shoulders at Fotheringay. England breathed again, freed from the shadow of seventeen years of dread.

Mrs. Leigh neither cheered nor wept. She merely murmured a prayer—for even a queen’s soul might yet find mercy.

Then—a sound from the sea. A gunshot. Then another.

The crowd hushed, rushing to the churchyard that overlooked the river. A foreign ship, large and swift, was making for the harbor, her sails full beneath the fading light. No English vessel, this—yet she flew no flag of surrender, no veiled topsails in deference to the realm.

The mystery deepened as she passed the Hubbastone, music drifting from her decks—drums, fifes, trumpets. And then, as she rounded into full view of Burrough, a mighty cheer erupted from her crew, rolling like thunder across the water.

Mrs. Leigh did not wait. She turned to her maid.

Bideford’s quay was chaos—sailors, women, apprentices, all shouting, weeping, laughing. And then, towering above them, Amyas Leigh.

He fell to his knees before his mother. She clasped him to her, her fingers tightening in his hair. No words were needed. She knew, even before he whispered:

*"You need not tell me that,"* she answered.

A pause. Then, softer:

*"A martyr,"* Amyas said. *"In the Inquisition."*

A shudder passed through her, but she lifted her chin. *"Come home, Amyas."*

Then her eyes fell upon the girl standing behind him—dark-eyed, uncertain.

Mrs. Leigh extended a hand. *"Then she is my granddaughter. Come, child."*

That night, by the hearth, Amyas told his tale—of battles, of treasure, of Frank’s fate. Sir Richard Grenville arrived, breathless from haste, to clasp his godson and demand every detail.

*"Would God I had been with you!"* the old warrior sighed.

But Mrs. Leigh sat composed, her hands folded, her grief long since tempered into steel. When the story was done, she kissed Amyas’s brow.

Then she rose, leading Ayacanora away—to teach her, to comfort her, to weave her into the fabric of their lives.

For though one son was lost, another had returned. And in that, there was grace enough.

The next morning, Amyas sought out old Salterne. The merchant listened, stone-faced, to the tale of Rose’s fate. At the end, he merely nodded.

And when Amyas spoke of his resolve—to hunt Spaniards wherever they might be found—Salterne’s smile was grim.

For the past was dead. But the sea still called.
