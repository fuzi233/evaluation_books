When Romola mentioned that someone awaited her, she referred to her cousin, Brigida. Yet, she had no inkling of the profound crisis in which that kindred spirit found herself. As they walked back toward the Piazza, they spotted a gathering of youths halting before Tessa. Upon seeing the simple peasant girl's distress, Romola stepped closer and said, "Wait a moment, cousin."

Monna Brigida, however, reacted with haste. "Ah, I cannot proceed," she exclaimed breathlessly. "Come with me to Boni's shop; I shall return there."

The truth was, Monna Brigida was torn between two forces. On one hand, she clung to the "vanities" adorning her person; on the other, a growing terror seized her that the Piagnoni—the penitent friars—were correct in their assertion that rouge, false hair, and pearl embroidery damaged the soul. Their stern worldview hung in the air like a pervasive odor; nothing tasted or felt as it once had. Here stood her dear child, Romola, youthful and beautiful, living a life that demanded rigorous self-denial. For a widow of fifty-five whose happiness had long been derived from her appearance and the admiration of others, maintaining buoyancy required immense imagination. Monna Brigida had recently begun to struggle violently with her toilette. Was it truly worthwhile to apply rouge and braid her hair if her soul needed to flourish without them?

But when she raised her hand-mirror, she saw a sallow face with sagging cheeks and crow's-feet that no amount of lip-smirking could conceal. When she parted her grey hair to wear it simply in the Piagnone style, her courage evaporated. Monna Berta, though only fifty-two, possessed a sharp tongue and a superior demeanor that would surely reduce her to laughter, calling her an old hag. Furthermore, every non-Piagnone woman would sneer, and men would address her as if she were their grandmother. At fifty-five, she reasoned, she was not ancient; she merely needed a touch of makeup. Thus, the rouge, the braids, and the embroidered *berretta* remained, and she was pleased with the familiar effect. Her neck, usually hidden because she feared it looked too aged, appeared finer against the backdrop of necklaces, surpassing even Monna Berta's.

That very day, while preparing for the Piagnone Carnival, these internal conflicts had become unbearable. Driven by fear and longing, she had turned back to seek refuge in the druggist's shop rather than face the collectors of the Anathema without Romola's support. However, her retreat was not swift enough. Even before she turned, she had been spotted by the white-robed boys trailing behind the crowd that had circled Tessa. Perhaps their eagerness to seize Tessa stemmed partly from the fact that they had already cornered another figure bearing the visible weight of the Anathema.

Several of these youths were among the youngest recruits, trained in peculiar fervor. A small boy of ten, wearing an olive wreath over cherubic cheeks and wide brown eyes, possessed an imagination overwhelmed by the looming consequences of morality. Despite his desire for mischief, he was the first to intercept Monna Brigida, placing himself squarely in her path. She felt anger rising, searching for an exit, but none was available; fleeing now would only worsen her plight.

Yet, it was not the cherubic boy who spoke first. It was a youth of fifteen gripping the handles of a wide basket.

"Venerable mother!" he declared. "The Blessed Jesus commands you to surrender the Anathema you carry. That cap embroidered with pearls, those jewels securing your false hair—give them up and sell them for the poor. Cast the hair itself away, for it is a lie fit only for the fire. Doubtless, beneath your silk mantle, you harbor other treasures."

"Yes, lady," agreed the youth at the opposite handle, reciting Fra Girolamo's phrases with practiced ease. "They are too heavy for you. Heavier than a millstone, weighing you down toward perdition. Will you decorate yourself with the hunger of the poor and boast of carrying God's curse upon your brow?"

"In truth, you are old, *buona madre*," chimed in the little boy, his voice a sweet soprano. "You appear frightfully ugly with the red on your cheeks, that glossy black hair, and those fine trinkets. Only Satan delights in your sight. Your Angel grieves; He wishes you to wipe away the red."

Snatching a soft silk scarf from the basket, the boy held it out for her to use as her guardian angel commanded. Monna Brigida's anger and humiliation rapidly gave way to spiritual panic. Monna Berta and the cloud of fashionable onlookers were nowhere to be seen. Instead, she was surrounded by young monitors in white robes, wreaths, and red crosses, their terrifying candor radiating an awful solemnity. Her Franciscan confessor, Fra Cristoforo, was absent to bolster her skepticism of the Dominicans, leaving her helpless before a vague, supreme warning. With no other option presenting itself, she accepted the offered scarf and trembled as she wiped her cheeks in submissive obedience.

"It is well, madonna," said the second youth. "A holy beginning. Once you remove these vanities from your head, the dew of heavenly grace shall descend upon it."

As the spirit of mischief grew within him, the boy reached for one of the jeweled pins fastening her braids to the *berretta*. He pulled it free. The heavy black plait fell forward, dragging the rest of her headgear down over Monna Brigida's face. This became the final catalyst; she raised her hands frantically, undone the remaining fastenings, and tossed her beloved crimson-velvet *berretta*, with its unparalleled embroidery of seed-pearls, into the basket of doom. She stood revealed as an unrouged woman, grey hair swept back from a face where the deep lines of age had triumphed over her former fullness.

However, the *berretta* was not permitted to rest in the basket. With impish zeal, the youngsters lifted it and held it up mercilessly, letting the false hair dangle.

"Behold, venerable mother," proclaimed the taller youth, "the ugly lies from which you have freed yourselves! Now you resemble the Blessed Saint Anna, mother of the Holy Virgin."

Thoughts of immediate entry into a convent, never again to show her face to the world, raced through Monna Brigida's mind. Nothing remained but the care of her soul. Of course, spectators laughed; she did not need to glance around to confirm it. Perhaps it would be better to be forced to contemplate Paradise. Yet, at the realization that her cherished, accustomed world was no longer an option, hard tears gathered in her elderly eyes, blurring her vision. She saw only vaguely a large, rough hand clutching a red cross, thrust suddenly before her over the boys' shoulders, accompanied by a strong, guttural voice:

"Only four *quattrini*, madonna, blessing included! Purchase it. You will find comfort in it now that your wig is gone. *Deh!* What do we sinners do all our lives? Make soup in a basket and receive only the scum for our bellies. Better buy a blessing, madonna! Only four *quattrini*; the profit is less than the scent of a single coin, and it goes to the poor."

Dim-eyed and confused, Monna Brigida moved toward the further submission of extracting money from her embroidered purse, currently concealed by her silk mantle. Just then, the circle around her—which she had not yet considered escaping—parted before a figure as welcoming as an angel releasing prison bolts.

"Romola, look at me!" cried Monna Brigida in a piteous tone, extending both hands.

The white-robed troop was already drifting away, aware that their excessive zeal regarding the headgear warranted a dispensation from demanding further penitential offerings.

"My dear cousin, do not be distressed," said Romola, struck with pity yet unable to suppress a smile at the sudden transformation of her kinswoman into a genuine, natural guise, so strikingly different from her usual appearance. She removed the black drapery from her own head and draped it over Monna Brigida's.

"There," she soothed. "No one will notice you now. Let us turn down the Via del Palagio and go straight home."

They hurried away, Monna Brigida clutching Romola's hand tightly, seeking a firmer assurance of her presence.

"Oh, my Romola, my dear child!" called the short, stout woman, hurrying with frequent steps to match the majestic stride of her relative. "What an old scarecrow I am! I must be good—I mean to be good!"

"Yes, yes; buy a cross!" echoed the guttural voice. The rough hand thrust the red cross forward once more, for Bratti was not daunted by Romola's presence into abandoning a potential customer. He had quietly trailed their retreat.

"Only four *quattrini*, blessing and all—and if there were any profit, it would all go to the poor."

Even in a less submissive mood, Monna Brigida would have been compelled to pause. Raising one hand deprecatingly to stop Romola's protest, she reached out with the other, offering a *grosso* worth many white *quattrini* in an entreating tone.

"Take it, good man, and depart."
