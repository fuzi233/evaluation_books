The Major and Captain Costigan were seasoned soldiers, used to facing down enemies in the field—so it’s safe to say they kept their heads when Pendennis burst into the room like a man who’d just escaped from a duel with destiny. The rest of the party, however, might have been forgiven for losing their composure slightly. Miss Fotheringay, bless her slow heart, flushed up like a rose after a summer shower—probably because Sir Derby Oaks was glaring at her as if she’d stolen his last biscuit. And poor little Mr. Bows, the crooked old gentleman in the window seat (who had been trying to write theatre music until the fencing match upstairs made him give up and mutter about “dancing fools”), looked up eagerly, as though he’d just witnessed a miracle.

Then came the Major—well-booted, well-bowled, and full of the grace that only a man who’s spent decades pretending to be noble can master. “Me daughter—me friend, Mr. Bows—me gallant young pupil and friend, I may call ’um, Sir Derby Oaks,” announced Costigan, waving his hand like a general addressing a parade ground. “In one moment, Meejor, I’m your humble servant!”

And off he went—into the tiny bedroom next door—to perform the most dramatic grooming ritual since Cleopatra applied her makeup. With the speed of a man whose life depends on fashion, he brushed his lank hair (a relic of ancient times), tore off his old stock, slipped on a new one Emily had sewn herself, added a clean collar, and donned the brand-new coat ordered especially for Miss Fotheringay’s benefit night. All this took less than a minute. Then Sir Derby emerged, freshly encased in his shell-jacket, which clung to his broad frame like armor made for a giant baby. He looked magnificent—though perhaps not as magnificent as he thought he did. Miss Fotheringay probably admired him; Pen certainly did; and even the Major couldn’t help but notice.

Meanwhile, polite conversation had begun between the actress and the visitor. They exchanged the usual pleasantries about the weather—“Rather damp today,” said the Major, “but not without charm.” Then Costigan reappeared, resplendent in his new attire (which he called his “Shoot”).

“I needn’t apologoise to ye, Meejor,” he declared, “for receiving ye in me shirt-sleeves.”

“A soldier’s best duty is teaching a young one how to handle a sword,” replied the Major, bowing so low you could almost see the ceiling. “I remember hearing that you were quite skilled with one, Captain Costigan.”

“What? Ye’ve heard of Jack Costigan, Major?” the Irishman exclaimed, puffing out his chest like a rooster.

Indeed, the Major had. He’d asked his nephew Arthur about the fellow, and whether he knew anything else beyond what he’d been told. Whether he actually remembered Costigan from past days or not—well, that’s a mystery. But Major Pendennis was a man of honor and truth, and he insisted he’d met Captain Costigan before—at Sir Richard Strachan’s table in Walcheren—and had even heard him sing.

At this, Bows blinked. “Well now,” he muttered, “that’s a surprise.”

But the Major wasn’t done. “We’ll talk of these things another time,” he said, clearly wanting to avoid any deeper entanglement. “Today, I came to pay my respects to Miss Fotheringay.”

He bowed again—a bow so grand, so courtly, so full of theatrical flair, that if she’d been a duchess, she wouldn’t have gotten better treatment.

“I’ve heard of your performances from my nephew,” the Major continued, “who raves about you—though, of course, he’s only a boy, wild and enthusiastic, and we must take his opinions with a grain of salt. Still, I confess I wanted to judge for myself. And I must say—your performance delighted and astonished me. I’ve seen our finest actresses, and I truly believe you surpass them all. You are as majestic as Mrs. Siddons.”

“Faith, I always said so,” Costigan chimed in, winking at his daughter. “Major, take a chair.”

Milly sprang up, removed an unripped satin garment from the only available seat (as though it were sacred), and presented the chair with a curtsey so deep it nearly knocked her over.

“You are as pathetic as Miss O’Neill,” the Major went on, seating himself with great dignity. “Your singing reminded me of Mrs. Jordan in her prime—when we were young men, Captain Costigan. And your manner—ah, your manner reminds me of Mars.”

“Ah,” interjected Miss Emily, “there were two Mahers in Crow Street. Fanny was decent enough, but Biddy was no great shakes.”

“Sure, the Major means the god of war, Milly, my dear,” Costigan corrected gently.

“No, not exactly,” the Major replied, smiling warmly at Sir Derby Oaks, who had just returned in his shell-jacket. “Though Venus, I suppose, may forgive him for thinking about him.”

But Miss Fotheringay didn’t catch the joke. Nor did Sir Derby. In fact, he scowled harder than ever—like someone who’d just discovered the soup was lukewarm. His expression seemed to say: *What in the name of all that’s holy is this man doing here?*

The Major, however, wasn’t fazed. On the contrary—he found it delightful. *A rival!* he thought. *Excellent.* And he silently vowed that Sir Derby should not only be a rival—but a winner too—in this love affair that had already claimed both him and Pen.

“I fear I interrupted your fencing lesson,” the Major said, smoothing his way forward. “But my stay in Chatteris is brief, and I wished to greet my old campaigner, Captain Costigan, and meet a lady who has enchanted me from the stage.”

He paused. “I wasn’t the only one charmed last night, Miss Fotheringay—though I must call you so, though your family name is ancient and noble. There was a reverend friend of mine who went home raving about Ophelia. And I saw Sir Derby Oaks fling a bouquet—now, that’s a gesture no actress ever deserved more. Did you know those flowers are still sitting in a glass of water on the mantelpiece?”

“I am very fond of flowers,” Miss Fotheringay replied, giving Sir Derby a look that would have melted stone—had he been made of anything softer than granite.

But he remained unmoved. “Sweets to the sweet—isn’t that the line from the play?” the Major asked, trying to be charming.

“’Pon my life, I don’t know,” Sir Derby grumbled. “Very likely it is. I ain’t much of a literary man.”

“Is it possible?” the Major asked, feigning shock. “You donー’t inherit your father’s love of letters, then? He was a remarkable scholar—I had the honor of knowing him well.”

“Indeed,” Sir Derby muttered, giving a stiff nod.

“He saved my life,” the Major added.

“Did he now?” Miss Fotheringay gasped, rolling her eyes first at the Major, then at Sir Derby—with gratitude.

But Sir Derby barely blinked. If anything, he looked annoyed. As though the idea of his father saving the Major’s life was somehow an insult. “My father,” he said coldly, “was a very good doctor. I’m not in that line myself. I wish you good morning, sir. I’ve got an appointment—Cos, bye-bye—Miss Fotheringay, good morning.”

And with that, he stormed out of the room—despite the young lady’s pleading glances and sweet smiles. The clatter of his sabre echoed down the stairs, followed by furious curses directed at little Tom Creed, who was playing with a peg-top in the hallway. Sir Derby kicked it into the street with a curse.

The Major didn’t smile. Not even a little. “Monstrous handsome young man,” he said to Costigan. “As fine a soldier as I’ve ever seen.”

“A credit to the army and human nature in general,” Costigan agreed.

“A young man of refined manners, polite affabilitee, and princely fortune. His table is sumptuous—he’s adawr’d in the regiment—and he rides sixteen stone.”

“A perfect champion,” laughed the Major. “I have no doubt all the ladies admire him.”

“He’s very well,” Milly said, “in spite of his weight, while he’s young. But he’s no conversation.”

“He’s best on horseback,” Mr. Bows observed.

“Oh yes,” Milly replied, “he rode third in the steeplechase on Tareaways.” The Major began to wonder how such a woman could act so brilliantly yet speak so plainly.

Costigan, true to Irish hospitality, immediately offered refreshments. The Major, though not hungry—having just survived a Lord Mayor’s dinner—declared he’d like a biscuit and a glass of wine, “for I feel faint from long fasting.” He knew, of course, that small kindnesses flatter donors immensely.

“Some of the old Madara, Milly, love,” Costigan said, winking at his daughter.

She nodded, left the room, descended the stairs, and quietly summoned Tommy Creed. She gave him a coin and instructed him to buy a pint of Madara wine from the Grapes and sixpennyworth of sorted biscuits from the baker. And if he returned quickly, he’d get two biscuits for himself.

While Tommy was gone, Miss Costigan sat downstairs with Mrs. Creed, telling her landlady all about the Major being upstairs—a “nice, soft-spoken old gentleman; butter wouldn’t melt in his mouth”—and how Sir Derby had stormed out in a jealous rage. What should they do?

“She keeps the keys of the cellar, Major,” Costigan said, as Milly left.

“Upon my word, you have a beautiful butler,” the Major replied, gallantly. “No wonder the young fellows go mad for her. When we were their age, Captain Costigan, I think plain women would have done us just fine.”

“Faith, and ye may say that, sir,” Costigan said. “And lucky is the man who gets her. Ask my friend Bob Bows here whether Miss Fotheringay’s mind isn’t superior to her person, whether she doesn’t possess a cultivated intellect, a refined understanding, and an emiable disposition?”

“O of course,” Bows replied, rather dryly.

“Here comes Hebe blushing from the cellar,” Costigan said. “Don’t you think it’s time to go to rehearsal, Miss Hebe? You’ll be fined if you’re late.”

He gave her a look that said, *Let’s leave these two alone.*

Miss Hebe, looking lovely and cheerful, picked up her bonnet and shawl. Bows gathered his papers, hobbled across the room, and fetched his hat and cane.

“Must you go?” the Major asked. “Can’t you stay a few minutes longer, Miss Fotheringay? Before you leave, allow an old fellow to shake your hand. I am proud to have met you, and sincerely hope to be your friend.”

She curtsied deeply. The Major followed her to the door, squeezed her hand with paternal warmth, and watched her go.

Bows stared. *The lad’s relatives can’t really want to marry him to her,* he thought.

And so they departed.

Now, thought Major Pendennis, *time to negotiate.*

And Captain Costigan, once his daughter was gone, immediately seized the opportunity to drink the rest of the wine—gulp after gulp of Madeira from the Grapes, his hand shaking with eagerness.

The Major joined him at the table, lifted his glass, and drained it with a satisfied smack. “Capital Madeira, Captain Costigan,” he said. “Where do you get it? I drink to that charming creature in a bumper. Faith, I don’t wonder the men are wild about her. I’ve never seen such eyes—or such a grand manner. I’m sure she’s as intelligent as she is beautiful. And I have no doubt she’s as good as she is clever.”

“A good girl, sir—*a good girl, sir*,” Costigan repeated, delighted. “And I pledge a toast to her with all my heart. Shall I send for another pint? It’s handy by. No? Well, indeed, sir, ye may say she is a good girl—the pride and glory of her father—honest old Jack Costigan. The man who gets her will have a jewel for a wife, sir. And I drink his health, sir—you know who I mean, Major.”

“I’m not surprised young or old fall in love with her,” the Major said. “And frankly, I must tell you, though I was angry with my nephew Arthur when I heard of his passion—now I’ve seen the lady, I can forgive him anything. By George, I’d enter the race myself—if I weren’t an old fellow and poor.”

“And no better man, Major,” Costigan cried, enraptured. “Your friendship delights me. Your admiration for my girl brings tears to my eyes—*tears, sir—manlee tears*—and when she leaves me humble home for your more splendid mansion, I hope she’ll keep a place for her poor old father—poor old Jack Costigan.”

He spoke with such emotion that tears welled in his bloodshot eyes.

“Your sentiments do you honour,” the Major said. “But, Captain Costigan, I can’t help smiling at one thing you just said.”

“And what’s that, sir?” Jack asked, still in heroic mode.

“You mentioned our splendid mansion—my sister’s house, I mean.”

“I mane the park and mansion of Arthur Pendennis, Esquire, of Fairoaks Park,” Costigan declared, with dignity. “Whom I hope to see a Member of Parliament for Clavering, when he is of age to take that responsible station.”

The Major smiled. He recognized the arrow—it was one he’d shot himself. He’d planted the idea in Pen’s head about running for Parliament. And now the boy had been bragging about it to Costigan and his beloved.

“Fairoaks Park, my dear sir,” the Major said. “Do you know our history? We’re of ancient lineage, certainly—but I began life with scarcely enough money to buy my commission. My eldest brother was a country apothecary—who made every shilling he died with from his pestle and mortar.”

“I have consented to waive that objection, sir,” Costigan said, magnificently, “in consideration of the known respectability of your family.”

“Curse your impudence,” the Major thought—but only smiled and bowed.

“The Costigans have met with misfortunes too,” Costigan continued. “Our house of Castle Costigan is by no means what it was. I’ve known honest apothecaries, sir—and there are some in Dublin who’ve dined at the Lord Lieutenant’s table.”

“You’re very kind to give us the benefit of your charity,” the Major said. “But let me say—this isn’t the issue. You spoke of my nephew as heir to Fairoaks Park and all that goes with it.”

“Funded property, I’ve no doubt, Meejor—and something handsome eventually from yourself.”

“My good sir,” the Major said, rising, “I tell you the boy is the son of a country apothecary. When he comes of age, he won’t have a shilling.”

“Pooh, Major, you’re laughing at me,” Costigan said. “Me young friend, I make no doubt, is heir to two thousand pounds a year.”

“Two thousand fiddlesticks!” the Major exploded. “Has the boy been humbugging you? It’s not his habit. Upon my word and honour, as a gentleman and executor of my brother’s will, he left little more than five hundred a year.”

“And with aconomy,” Costigan insisted, “a handsome sum too. Faith, I’ve known a man drink his clar’t and drive his coach-and-four on five hundred a year and strict aconomy in Ireland. We’ll manage on it—trust Jack Costigan for that.”

“My dear Captain Costigan,” the Major said, “I give you my word—my brother left nothing to his son. Not a shilling.”

“Are ye joking with me, Meejor Pendennis?” Costigan roared. “Are ye thrifling with the feelings of a father and a gentleman?”

“I am telling you the honest truth,” the Major said calmly. “Every shilling my brother had, he left to his widow—with a partial reversion to the boy. But she’s young and may marry if he offends her—or she may outlive him. And I ask you, as a gentleman, what allowance can my sister make to her son from five hundred a year—enough to maintain himself and your daughter in the rank befitting such an accomplished young lady?”

“Am I to understand,” Costigan thundered, “that the young gentleman—your nephew—whom I have fostered and cherished as the son of me bosom—is an imposther who has been thrifling with the affections of me beloved child?”

“Have you yourself been working upon the young man’s susceptible nature to injuice him to break off an engagement—and with it me adored Emily’s heart? Have a care, sir, how you thrifle with the honour of John Costigan. If I thought any mortal man meant to do so, by heavens, I’d have his blood—were he old or young.”

“Mr. Costigan!” the Major cried.

“Mr. Costigan can protect his own and his daughter’s honour,” the other snapped. “Look at that chest of drawers—it contains heaps of letters that that viper has addressed to that innocent child. Promises enough to fill a bandbox. And when I drag the scoundrel before the Courts of Law, and show up his perjury and dishonour, I have another remedy in yondther mahogany case—ye mark me words, Major Pendennis—with any individual who has counselled your nephew to insult a soldier and a gentleman. What? Me daughter to be jilted, and me grey hairs dishonoured by an apothecary’s son? By the laws of Heaven, I’d like to see the man who dares do it.”

“I am to understand,” the Major said, cool as ice, “that you threaten first to publish the letters of an eighteen-year-old boy to a woman of twenty-eight—and then to do me the honour of calling me out?”

“You have described my intentions perfectly, Meejor Pendennis,” Costigan replied, pulling his whiskers over his chin.

“Well, well,” the Major said, “these shall be matters for future arrangements. But before we come to powder and ball, my good sir—do have the kindness to consider: in what earthly way have I injured you? I’ve told you my nephew is dependent on his mother, who has barely five hundred a year.”

“I have my own opinion of that assertion,” Costigan said.

“Will you go to my sister’s lawyers, Messrs. Tatham, and satisfy yourself?”

“I decline to meet those gentlemen,” Costigan said, disturbed.

“If it’s as you say, I’ve been atrociously deceived—and I’ll be revenged.”

“Is it my nephew?” the Major asked, standing up and putting on his hat.

“Did he ever tell you he had two thousand a year? If he did, I’m mistaken in the boy. Lying is not in our family. I don’t think my brother’s son has learned it yet. Try to consider—have you not deceived yourself? Or adopted exaggerated reports from hearsay?”

“As for me, sir,” the Major said, “you are at liberty to understand I am not afraid of all the Costigans in Ireland. I know how to defend myself against any threat. I come here as the boy’s guardian to protest against a marriage—absurd and unequal—that would bring poverty and misery. And in preventing it, I believe I am doing your daughter’s honour as much as my own family’s. And prevent it I will, by every means in my power. There. I have said my say.”

“But I have not said mine, Major Pendennis,” Costigan said, eyes blazing. “And ye shall hear more from me.”

“’Sdeath, sir, what do you mean?” the Major asked, turning back at the door.

“Ye said,” Costigan said, slowly and gravely, “that ye were at the George Hotel, I think.”

“A friend shall wait upon ye there before ye leave town, sir.”

“Let him make haste, Mr. Costigan,” the Major shouted, almost beside himself. “I wish you a good morning, sir.”

And Captain Costigan bowed a magnificent, defiant bow over the landing as the Major retreated down the stairs—leaving behind a silence thick with threats, tears, and the lingering scent of Madeira.
