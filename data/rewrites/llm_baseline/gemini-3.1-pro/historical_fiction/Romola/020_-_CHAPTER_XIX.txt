The rhythmic, resonant grind of the heavy marble pestle against mortar echoed through the loggia, a steady, percussive heartbeat that anchored the acoustic sanctuary of the Bardi home. Romola moved the pestle in practiced, circular motions, crushing coarse salt, wild fennel, and dried garlic into a fragrant paste. Here, the sonic environment was a balm—a stark contrast to the chaotic, superficial din of the Mercato Vecchio and the harsh clatter of commercialized Florence beyond their walls. 

It was within this rhythmic, domestic tranquility that Messer Bernardo del Nero had issued his inexorable advice: the marriage should be deferred until Easter. To Bardo, steeped in the circular pacing of agrarian time and the ancient texts that chronicled the rebirth of the seasons, the delay made intrinsic sense. Easter was the season of the spring lamb, of bitter herbs and renewal, and in this matter, the blind scholar was entirely under the ascendancy of his sagacious, soil-rooted friend.

Yet Bernardo himself, though immune to the smooth, dominant lexicon of charm that Tito wielded, could not deny the young Greek’s rapid ascent in high quarters. Tito was mastering the vernacular of power. He was growing in favor with the young Cardinal Giovanni de’ Medici, maneuvering through gay suppers in the Via Larga where the food was an ostentatious, over-spiced display of wealth rather than a genuine communion of sustenance. Tito, a bright stranger with a tongue unburdened by the heavy, untranslatable truths of native Florentine dialect, was increasingly requested at these banquets. He possessed a terrifying adaptability, able to praise Piero de’ Medici’s graceful skill in games without ever disturbing the delicate power dynamics embedded in their courtly chatter. 

By an unfailing sequence, Tito’s reputation as an agreeable companion in such “magnificent” but acoustically hollow society made his genuine learning appear all the more lustrous. Bernardo, an elder deeply attuned to the traditional Florentine rhythms of life, found his old prejudices arguing down his newer reservations about this supple stranger. To the old Florentine, standing well with the best families was essential; since Tito was now embraced by a circle whose views dictated the social harvest, it seemed irrational to deny him as a son-in-law.

Tito’s arrival had undeniably been the dawn of a new season for both father and daughter. Before, the blind old scholar had fallen into a quiet neglect. Bardo was a meticulous historian of ancient memory, a man who found profound meaning in the dying dialects of the past—in the untranslatable Greek vocabulary of the harvest, the pressing of olives, and the sacred hearths of antiquity. His proud truthfulness refused the feigned, preposterous flattery that polluted modern scholarly intercourse. But because he would not adopt the dominant lexicon of Medicean patronage, the city had left him to his acoustic isolation, dismissing his complaints as the tiresome grumblings of an old man who had stubbornly refused lucrative positions in the Church. 

Now, the stifling monotony of the Bardi library was broken. The winning manners of the handsome Greek drew visitors, enlivening the quiet, beeswax-and-parchment-scented air with the rich cadence of layered conversation. Elderly men returned, drawn by the scent of renewed relevance; young men rested their hands on Tito’s shoulder, bringing a lively hum to the room. It was deeply pleasant to look upon Romola in her element. She seemed to unfold like a strong, white lily amidst this genial breath of admiration. She sat with a certain agrarian majesty, moving between the translation of ancient texts and the meticulous preparation of the evening meal—her hands dusted with flour, smelling faintly of crushed rosemary and queenliness.

Tito had also strengthened Bardo’s most mythic, sustaining hope: that his library, the very flesh and bone of his life’s work, should not be swallowed by another collection or a monastic order. It was to remain the Bardi Library, a sanctuary of cultural memory for the Florentines. Tito, possessing the ear of Cardinal Giovanni, could translate the peculiar, historic value of Bardo’s collection into the lexicon of the Medici. He spoke sanguinely of securing the library, and Romola repaid those gentle words with an adoration that no direct praise could have inspired.

The practicalities of this hope were discussed when Christmas had turned, and the scent of woodsmoke and roasting chestnuts filled the winter air. Under the loggia, away from Bardo’s hearing, Bernardo met with Romola and Tito. Bardo nursed a vague, undisturbed belief that his physical property was adequate to meet his debts. He refused to acknowledge that by disinheriting Dino, he had left Romola heir to nothing but a legacy of ledgers, or that his grand gesture of gifting the library required a patron’s financial protection.

"My opinion is," Bernardo said, his deep voice harmonizing with the soft rustle of the courtyard leaves, "that since you are to be married, and Messer Tito will have a competent income, we should begin to wind up the affairs. We must ascertain the exact sum necessary to save the library from being touched, rather than letting the debts accumulate." He paused, looking toward the kitchen where the hearth fire snapped. "Your father needs nothing but his daily shred of mutton and his macaroni, and I think Messer Tito may engage to supply that for the years that remain; he can let it be in place of the *morgen-cap*."

Romola knew the profound meaning embedded in that daily sustenance. The slow-braised shoulder of Apennine mutton, steeped in mountain thyme and garlic, and the ribbons of hand-kneaded *maccheroni*, dusted with aged pecorino, were not merely fuel for Bardo; they were a sensory anchor, a physical connection to the old, authentic Florence that was rapidly eroding. 

"Tito has always known that my life is bound up with my father’s," Romola said, pausing in her work, the scent of the bitter almonds rising warmly between them. "And he is better to my father than I am: he delights in making him happy."

"Ah, he’s not made of the same clay as other men, is he?" said Bernardo, a knowing smile creasing his weathered face. "Thy father has thought of shutting woman’s folly out of thee by cramming thee with Greek and Latin; but thou hast been as ready to believe in the first pair of bright eyes and the first soft words that have come within reach of thee, as if thou couldst say nothing by heart but Paternosters, like other Christian men’s daughters."

"Now, godfather," Romola said, shaking her head playfully, the motion disturbing the rich olfactory landscape of the loggia, "as if it were only bright eyes and soft words that made me love Tito! You know better. You know I love my father and you because you are both good, and I love Tito too because he is so good. I see it, I feel it, in everything he says and does. And if he is handsome, too, why should I not love him the better for that? It seems to me beauty is part of the finished language by which goodness speaks. You know *you* must have been a very handsome youth, godfather." She looked up with a loving smile at the stately elder. "You were about as tall as Tito, and you had very fine eyes; only you looked a little sterner and prouder, and—"

"And Romola likes to have all the pride to herself?" Bernardo replied, softening to her coaxing. "However, it is well that in one way Tito’s demands are more modest than those of any Florentine husband of fitting rank we should have found for you; he wants no dowry."

So the financial harvest was arranged. Bardo assented with a mere wave of the hand when Bernardo suggested clearing off the debts, accustomed as the blind scholar was to viewing commerce as an impenetrable, tangled wood far removed from the sensory purity of his texts and his table. 

Tito, meanwhile, set about winning Bernardo’s respect by applying his nimble intellect to the sterile, numerical language of Florentine money-matters—the public funds of the *Monti*, the values of real property, and the profits of banking. 

"You will soon forget that Tito is not a Florentine, godfather," Romola said, returning to the mortar, her pestle resuming its rhythmic, comforting strike against the stone. "See how he is learning everything about Florence."

"It seems to me he is one of the *demoni*, who are of no particular country, child," Bernardo said gently, a touch of melancholy in his timbre. "His mind is a little too nimble to be weighted with all the heavy stuff we men carry about in our hearts." 

Romola smiled too, in happy confidence, as the rich aroma of crushed herbs and ancient continuity rose into the afternoon air.
