"At the court of the Tuileries," said Mr. Sillerton Jackson, his smile curving with a nostalgic warmth that suggested he was recalling a distant, sunnier kingdom. "Such matters were treated with a refreshing openness."

The setting was the formidable black walnut dining room of the van der Luydens on Madison Avenue, illuminated by the soft, heavy glow of evening lamps. It was the night following Newland Archer's fateful visit to the Museum of Art. Mr. and Mrs. van der Luyden had hurried back from Skuytercliff, fleeing the shockwaves of Beaufort's scandal, convinced by the social machinery of the city that their presence was now vital to stabilize the shaken foundations of high society. To the minds of the Archers, this was a duty; as Mrs. Archer often phrased it, it was simply a matter of "owing it to society" to appear at the Opera and keep their own doors ajar for the stragglers.

"Do not let anyone, my dear Louisa, imagine that Mrs. Lemuel Struthers has earned the right to step into Regina's shadow," Mrs. van der Luyden admonished, her voice sharp with the defensiveness of a fortress under siege. "These are precisely the moments when newcomers push in, seeking purchase. Remember the epidemic of chicken-pox last winter, when the married men abandoned their duties to flee to Mrs. Struthers' house while their wives were occupied in the nursery? You and dear Henry, Louisa, must stand firm in the breach as you have always done."

Bound by the unwavering code of their class, Mr. and Mrs. van der Luyden could not ignore this summons. They had reluctantly returned to town, unveiled their residence, and issued invitations for two dinners and a grand reception. That specific evening, they had extended an invitation to Sillerton Jackson, Mrs. Archer, Newland, and his wife to accompany them to the Opera, where Gounod's *Faust* was making its debut for the season. Under the van der Luyden roof, nothing occurred without ritual; although there were only four guests, the meal commenced promptly at seven, allowing the proper sequence of courses to unfold with unhurried grace before the gentlemen retired to smoke their cigars.

Newland had not seen his wife since the previous evening. He had departed early for the office, drowning himself in a mountain of trivial business, and later, a senior partner had made an unscheduled incursion into his time. By the time he reached home, May had already preceded him to the van der Luydens' and returned the carriage. Now, sitting across from him, separated by a landscape of Skuytercliff carnations and a massive silver plate, she appeared pale and languid. Yet her eyes burned with an exaggerated animation, and she spoke with a fervor that seemed to demand attention.

The topic that had inspired Mr. Sillerton Jackson's favorite metaphorical comparison had been broached—Archer suspected it was done with deliberate intent—by their hostess. The aftermath of the Beaufort failure, or perhaps the attitude adopted by the disgraced couple following it, remained a fertile ground for the drawing-room moralist. After thoroughly dissecting and condemning the affair, Mrs. van der Luyden turned her scrutinizing gaze upon May Archer.

"Is it truly possible, dear, that what I have heard is accurate? I was informed that Grandmother Mingott's carriage was observed standing at Mrs. Beaufort's door." It was notable that she no longer addressed the offending lady by her first name.

May's color flushed, and Mrs. Archer interjected quickly, "If it was, I am certain it was there without Madame Mingott's knowledge."

"Ahh, you think..." Mrs. van der Luyden paused, sighed, and cast a sidelong glance at her husband.

"I fear," Mr. van der Luyden offered, "that Madame Olenska's benevolent heart may have led her into the imprudence of paying a call on Mrs. Beaufort."

"Or perhaps her affinity for peculiar individuals," chimed in Mrs. Archer in a dry, detached tone, her eyes resting innocently on her son.

"I regret to think it of Madame Olenska," said Mrs. van der Luyden. And Mrs. Archer murmured softly, "Ah, my dear—and after you had entertained her twice at Skuytercliff!"

It was at this juncture that Mr. Jackson seized the opportunity to deploy his favored analogy.

"At the Tuileries," he repeated, sensing the expectant turn of every eye toward him, "standards were remarkably lax in certain respects; and if you had asked where Morny's fortune originated—or who bore the debts of some of the Court's most celebrated beauties..."

"I trust, dear Sillerton," said Mrs. Archer, "that you are not implying that we ought to adopt such standards?"

"I never suggest," returned Mr. Jackson, imperturbable. "But Madame Olenska's foreign upbringing may render her less particular—"

"Ahh," the two elder ladies sighed in unison.

"To have kept Grandmother's carriage at a defaulter's door!" Mr. van der Luyden protested. Archer surmised that he was remembering, and harboring resentment toward, the hampers of carnations he had sent to the modest dwelling on Twenty-third Street.

"Naturally, I have always maintained that she views matters quite differently," Mrs. Archer summarized.

A blush rose to May's forehead. She looked across the table at her husband and said abruptly, "I am sure Ellen meant it kindly."

"Imprudent souls are often kind," remarked Mrs. Archer, as if the observation were hardly an extenuation. Mrs. van der Luyden murmured, "If only she had consulted someone..."

"Ahh, she never did!" Mrs. Archer replied.

At this precise moment, Mr. van der Luyden glanced at his wife, who bowed her head slightly in the direction of Mrs. Archer. The shimmering trains of the three ladies swept out of the room as the gentlemen settled down to their cigars. Mr. van der Luyden provided short cigars for opera nights, yet they were so superior that they caused his guests to lament his inexorable punctuality.

After the first act, Archer detached himself from the party and made his way to the rear of the box. From there, peering over the shoulders of various Chivers, Mingott, and Rushworth, he beheld the same scene he had witnessed two years prior, on the night of his first encounter with Ellen Olenska. He had half-expected her to reappear in old Mrs. Mingott's box, but it remained vacant. He sat motionless, his eyes fixed upon it, until suddenly Madame Nilsson's pure soprano voice erupted into *"M'amour, non m'amour..."*

Archer turned his gaze to the stage, where, amidst the familiar backdrop of giant roses and pen-wiper pansies, the same tall, blonde heroine succumbed to the charms of the same diminutive, brown-suited seducer. His eyes drifted to the apex of the horseshoe shape where May sat nestled between two older ladies, exactly as she had positioned herself on that former evening, between Mrs. Lovell Mingott and her newly arrived "foreign" cousin. Just as on that night, she was dressed entirely in white. Archer, having not noticed her attire earlier, recognized the blue-white satin and antique lace of her wedding gown.

In old New York, it was customary for brides to wear this opulent garment during the first year or two of matrimony. His mother, he knew, preserved hers in tissue paper, hoping Janey might one day don it, though poor Janey was nearing an age when pearl-gray poplin and the absence of bridesmaids would be deemed far more appropriate.

It struck Archer that May, since their return from Europe, had rarely worn her bridal satin, and the surprise of seeing her in it compelled him to compare her appearance with that of the young girl he had watched with such blissful anticipation two years ago. Although May's silhouette was slightly fuller, as her goddess-like constitution had promised, her athletic erectness of carriage and the girlish transparency of her expression remained unchanged. Save for the slight languor Archer had recently perceived in her, she would have been the exact image of the maiden playing with a bouquet of lilies-of-the-valley on her betrothal evening. This resemblance seemed an additional appeal to his pity: such innocence was as moving as the trusting clasp of a child. Then he recalled the passionate generosity latent beneath her seemingly indifferent calm. He remembered her look of understanding when he had urged that their engagement be announced at the Beaufort ball; he heard the voice in which she had declared in the Mission garden, "I could not have my happiness constructed from a wrong—a wrong inflicted upon someone else." An uncontrollable longing seized him to confess the truth, to throw himself upon her generosity, and to ask for the freedom he had once denied.

Newland Archer was a quiet, self-controlled youth. Conformity to the disciplines of a small society had become almost his second nature. It was deeply distasteful to him to engage in anything melodramatic or conspicuous, anything Mr. van der Luyden would have deprecated and the club box would condemn as poor form. Yet, he had become suddenly unconscious of the box, of Mr. van der Luyden, of everything that had so long enclosed him within the warm shelter of habit. He walked along the semi-circular passage behind the house and opened the door of Mrs. van der Luyden's box as if it were a gateway into the unknown.
