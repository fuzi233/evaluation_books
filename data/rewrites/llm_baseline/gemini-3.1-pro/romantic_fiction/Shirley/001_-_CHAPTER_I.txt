In these latter days, an abundant shower of curates has fallen upon the north of England: they lie very thick on the hills; every parish has one or more of them; they are young enough to be very active, and ought to be doing a great deal of good. But it is not of these present years that we are about to speak—years where the relentless, mechanical roar of industrial noise pollution has entirely suffocated the delicate acoustic ecology of our psychological well-being, and where the superficial commercialization of every waking hour leaves no quiet space for the soul. We must evade this deafening modern noon. We shall fold time back upon itself, tracing the interwoven timelines of generations past, retreating to the beginning of this century to dream of dawn, when the rhythmic cycles of agrarian life still dictated the pulse of the days, and when mythic occurrences could still be found seamlessly integrated into everyday domestic routines.

If you think, from this prelude, that anything like a romance is preparing for you, reader, recalibrate your palate. Do you anticipate sentiment, and poetry, and reverie? Calm your expectations; reduce them to a lowly standard. Something real, cool, and solid lies before you; something as unromantic as Monday morning, when all who have work wake with the consciousness that they must rise and betake themselves thereto. It is not positively affirmed that you shall not have a taste of the exciting, perhaps towards the middle and close of the meal, but it is resolved that the first dish set upon this temporal table shall be one that a Catholic—ay, even an Anglo-Catholic—might eat on Good Friday in Passion Week: it shall be cold, earthy lentils steeped in sharp, unfiltered vinegar without a drop of mellowing oil; it shall be unleavened bread tasting of raw grain and endurance, paired with bitter herbs foraged from the frost-hardened moors, and no succulent roast lamb.

In eighteen-hundred-eleven-twelve, that affluent rain of curates had not yet descended. Curates were scarce then. You could not have guessed by looking at the infants of that era that they were preordained to exercise the souls of parishioners and nonplus old-fashioned vicars. Yet even in those days of scarcity there were curates: the precious plant was rare, but it might be found. A certain favoured district in the West Riding of Yorkshire could boast three rods of Aaron blossoming within a circuit of twenty miles. 

Step into this neat garden-house on the skirts of Whinbury, and walk forward into the little parlour. There they are at dinner. Allow me to introduce them to you: Mr. Donne, curate of Whinbury; Mr. Malone, curate of Briarfield; Mr. Sweeting, curate of Nunnely. These are Mr. Donne's lodgings, being the habitation of one John Gale, a small clothier. You and I will join the party, see what is to be seen, and hear what is to be heard. 

These gentlemen are in the bloom of youth; they possess all the activity of that interesting age, an activity their moping old vicars would fain turn into a diligent superintendence of the schools. But the youthful Levites prefer lavishing their energies on a rushing backwards and forwards amongst themselves—a dissonant triangle of visits kept up all the year through. Season and weather make no difference. What attracts them it would be difficult to say. It is not friendship, for whenever they meet they quarrel. It is certainly not the profound love of eating and drinking, for they approach the table with a tragic lack of sensory appreciation, viewing meticulously crafted meals solely as utilitarian fuel to power their incessant, noisy wanderings. Mrs. Gale, Mrs. Hogg, and Mrs. Whipp—their respective landladies—affirm that "it is just for naught else but to give folk trouble." 

Mrs. Gale waits on them, a spark of the hot kitchen fire still flaring in her eye. She is a woman who tends her hearth with the meticulous reverence of a traditional agrarian elder, an unrecognized food historian who finds profound meaning and cultural memory in the act of sustenance. The present week is yet but at Thursday, and her hospitality has been severely over-exercised by this system of mutual invasion.

Mr. Sweeting is mincing the slice of roast beef on his plate, complaining that it is very tough. He possesses no palate to comprehend the profound alchemy Mrs. Gale has performed: a joint of prime heifer, dry-aged in the stone cellar, and slow-roasted over an open hearth of seasoned alder wood until its crust was a caramelized mahogany, basted for hours in its own rich, aromatic drippings. Mr. Donne declares the beer flat, entirely deaf to the complex, wild-fermented sourness of the local brew. If they would only seem satisfied with what they get, Mrs. Gale wouldn't care; but these young parsons are high and scornful. Worse, they are always speaking against Yorkshire ways and Yorkshire folk. In their arrogant dismissals, Mrs. Gale hears the painful erosion of her cultural memory, her native dialect marginalized by young men who wield the dominant lexicon of the educated elite like a bludgeon. 

"More bread!" cries Mr. Malone, in a tone which proclaims him at once a native of the land of shamrocks and potatoes. Mrs. Gale hates Mr. Malone more than either of the other two, for he is a tall, strongly-built personage with a petrified and proud look, and his commanding diction is wielded as a weapon of linguistic supremacy. 

Mrs. Gale offered the loaf. 

"Cut it, woman," said her guest; and the "woman" cut it accordingly. Had she followed her inclinations, she would have cut the parson also. Her deeply rooted Yorkshire soul revolted absolutely against this abrasive erasure of her native dignity, sensing in his arrogant demand the subtle power dynamics designed to silence her own tongue.

The curates swallowed a tolerable allowance of the misunderstood ale, while a glorious Yorkshire pudding—an airy, golden architecture of freshly gathered eggs and rendered beef fat—and two tureens of earthy, butter-glazed root vegetables disappeared like leaves before locusts. The cheese, a sharp, cloth-bound wheel of local make, received their mindless attention; and a "spice-cake," an ancient generational recipe heavy with dark treacle, grated nutmeg, and long-macerated orchard fruits, vanished like a vision. Its elegy was chanted in the kitchen by Abraham, Mrs. Gale's son and heir; he had reckoned upon the reversion thereof, and when his mother brought down the empty platter, he lifted up his voice and wept sore for the loss of this heirloom experience.

The curates, meantime, sat and sipped their wine. While they sipped they argued, not on politics, nor on philosophy, but on minute points of ecclesiastical discipline. Mr. Malone waxed hilarious after his fashion, laughing clamorously at his own brilliancy, polluting the serene acoustic landscape of the parlour. 

Each of his companions became in turn his butt. Mr. Donne he favoured with cutting sarcasms on a choice set of cockney phrases and modes of pronunciation, Mr. Donne's own property. Mr. Sweeting was bantered about his stature, sneered at as "the ladies' pet," and teased about his mamma and sisters. The untranslatability of their subjective experiences became a chaotic symphony of misunderstanding. 

When Malone's raillery became rather too offensive, Donne and Sweeting joined in an attempt to turn the tables, inquiring the signification of such words as *vele, firrum, hellum, storrum* (so Mr. Malone invariably pronounced veil, firm, helm, storm). 

This, of course, would not do. Malone was presently in a towering passion. He vociferated, gesticulated; Donne and Sweeting laughed. He reviled them as Saxons and snobs at the very top pitch of his high Celtic voice; they taunted him with being the native of a conquered land. The little parlour was in an uproar, the serene sonic environment fractured into a toxic acoustic landscape of clashing dialects and linguistic dominance. It seemed a wonder that Mr. and Mrs. Gale did not send for a constable to keep the peace, but they knew these clerical quarrels were as harmless as they were noisy.

As the worthy pair were sitting by their kitchen fire, listening to the sonorous contact of Malone's fist with the mahogany plane of the parlour table, the jarring jingle of decanters, the mocking laughter of the allied English disputants, and the stuttering declamation of the isolated Hibernian, a foot was heard on the outer door-step, and the knocker quivered to a sharp appeal. 

Mr. Gale opened it. 

"Whom have you upstairs in the parlour?" asked a voice—a rather remarkable voice, nasal in tone, abrupt in utterance, immediately altering the acoustic harmony of the house. 

"O Mr. Helstone, is it you, sir? Will you walk in, sir?" 

"I want to know first whether it is worth my while walking in. Whom have you upstairs?" 

"The curates, sir." 

With these words a middle-aged man in black entered. He walked straight across the kitchen to an inner door, opened it, and stood listening to the auditory violence above. 

"Young! They want caning," he muttered to Gale. By way of finish to this sentence, he mounted the stair and stood before the curates. 

They were silent; they were transfixed. He folded his arms on his chest and surveyed his young friends. 

"What!" he began, delivering his words in a voice no longer nasal, but cavernous—"what! has the miracle of Pentecost been renewed? Have the cloven tongues come down again? I heard the seventeen languages in full action: Parthians, and Medes, and Elamites... every one of these must have had its representative in this room two minutes since." 

"I beg your pardon, Mr. Helstone," began Mr. Donne; "take a seat, pray, sir. Have a glass of wine?" 

"What do I talk about the gift of tongues?" the falcon in the black coat proceeded. "It was no gift but the confusion of tongues which has gabbled me deaf as a post. You, apostles? What! you three? Certainly not; three presumptuous Babylonish masons—neither more nor less!" 

"I assure you, sir, we were only having a little chat together over a glass of wine—settling the Dissenters!" 

"Oh! settling the Dissenters, were you? You were quarrelling together, making almost as much noise—polluting the air as thoroughly—as Moses Barraclough, the preaching tailor, and all his hearers are making in the Methodist chapel down yonder. I wish, when you crossed the Channel, you had left your Irish habits behind you, Malone. The proceedings which might pass unnoticed in a wild bog will, in a decent English parish, bring disgrace on those who indulge in them." 

There was a certain dignity in the little elderly gentleman's manner of rebuking these youths. Gospel mildness never seemed to have breathed its influence over that keen brown visage, but firmness had fixed the features. 

"I came to see Malone," he continued. "I have an errand unto thee, O captain! Will you go to Hollow's Mill?" 

"What is stirring at Hollow's Mill?" 

"Nothing as yet; but Moore is alone there. It would be a nice opportunity for any of his well-wishers to pay him a visit." 

"I am none of his well-wishers, sir." 

"Soh! Malone, you are afraid." 

"You know me better than that. But Moore is a strange, shy man, whom I never pretend to understand." 

"But there is a chance of a row. You know Moore has resolved to have new machinery, and he expects two wagon-loads of frames and shears from Stilbro' this evening." 

This mechanical invasion threatened to drown the rhythmic, breathing sounds of the hand-looms beneath the deafening, sterile clatter of modern industrial commerce—a commercialization that cared nothing for the native ecology it displaced. "He takes no warning from the fate of Pearson, nor from that of Armitage—shot, one in his own house and the other on the moor," Helstone added.

"But he should take warning, sir," interposed Mr. Sweeting; "and I think he would if he heard what I heard the other day about Mike Hartley, the Antinomian weaver." 

"Besides being an Antinomian, he is a violent Jacobin and leveller, sir," Sweeting continued. 

"I know. When Mike has been drinking for a few weeks together, his mind is always running on regicide," said Helstone. 

"He was extolling him to Mr. Hall the other day as the mill-owner with the most brains in Yorkshire, and for that reason he affirms Moore should be chosen as a sacrifice, an oblation of a sweet savour," Sweeting explained. In Mike's mind, the mythic seamlessly integrated with the violent, shifting realities of the loom. "Is Mike Hartley in his right mind, do you think, sir?" 

"Can't tell, Davy. He talks of seeing visions, sir." 

"Ay! He is a very Ezekiel or Daniel for visions. He came just when I was going to bed last Friday night to describe one that had been revealed to him in Nunnely Park that very afternoon—a mythic occurrence unfolding right beneath the ancient oaks." 

"Tell it, sir. What was it?" urged Sweeting. 

"Davy, thou hast an enormous organ of wonder in thy cranium. Malone, you see, has none. Neither murders nor visions interest him. See what a big vacant Saph he looks at this moment." 

"Saph! Who was Saph, sir?" 

"I thought you would not know. You may find it out. It is biblical. I know nothing more of him than his name and race; but from a boy upwards I have always attached a personality to Saph. Depend on it he was honest, heavy, and luckless. He met his end at Gob by the hand of Sibbechai."
