I descended into that Darkness with extreme caution, moving quietly and slowly. You must understand that I was now enveloped in a night so profound it seemed to press directly against my soul—a darkness unlike anything you have ever witnessed or felt. In its grip, I felt untethered from myself, drifting as though walking in an unreal state, passing endlessly through an eternal void. At times, my mind wandered so far astray that I believed I stepped no longer upon Earth, but drifted off into the Nothingness itself. Yet, whenever this delusion seized me, reality would abruptly correct course. I would kick against a protruding rock or stumble onto a massive, unseen boulder, jolting me instantly back to the hard, undeniable truth that I was treading solid ground, bound firmly to the physical world.

My only guide was the relentless descent itself. However, blinded by the absolute blackness, my progress was agonizingly slow—scarcely a mile per hour, perhaps taking two full hours to cover such distance. The inability to stride freely bred a bitterness in my spirit.

Determined to illuminate my path, I began to spin the Diskos intermittently. Its strange, glistening glow revealed just a fraction of the slope ahead, allowing me to anchor my direction in memory before plunging forward again. Soon, the darkness would overwhelm me anew, driving me to seek the blessedness of light once more to regain my bearings. Truly, the Diskos's beam appeared astonishingly bright, not because it was powerful, but because the surrounding darkness was so monstrous and infinite. I would march on until the pain of stumbling compelled me to summon that sweet, shining light again.

Witness this journey: it was sore and miserable to the heart, threatening to shake the very courage of the spirit. Yet, in truth, I had endured much and refused to yield to foolish thoughts. I used the Diskos only when absolutely necessary, for it was unwise to waste its power except in extremity.

After six long, bitter hours—and it was now past the twentieth hour since my last sleep—I sat upon the Mighty Slope in the endless dark. I ate two tablets, mixed water, and hesitantly checked my actions to ensure they were right. Once fed and hydrated, I wrapped my cloak tightly around me, placed my satchel and pouch beneath my head, and took the Diskos close. Sleep claimed me swiftly, though my thoughts remained earnestly, albeit vaguely, fixed on Naani.

I slept nearly six hours before waking suddenly, as if something had brushed against me. Propping myself up on an elbow, I listened keenly, gripping the Diskos tight. Silence answered from all sides of that night. Convinced I was safe, I sat upright, retrieved my supplies, and ate and drank again, fumbling slightly in the gloom. Soon, I was dressed, the Diskos in hand, and moving forward once more.

Throughout the day, a strange unease settled over my spirit. I stopped frequently to listen, convinced my soul sensed something following me silently nearby. My ears, however, detected nothing, so I continued downward into the night-holding slope.

Early in the seventh hour, after eating and drinking, disaster struck. Stepping suddenly into a small hole, I pitched violently against a sharp rock. The fall shook me to my core; I lay motionless for a time, my body ripped open despite the protection of my armor. When strength returned, I realized standing was impossible. Instead, I crawled on hands and knees. This method allowed me to feel the terrain directly, reducing my reliance on the Diskos, which I now used sparingly, trusting my intuition more than my vision.

Crawling proved a bitter mode of travel, yet I had covered many painful miles in the Night Land before. After eighteen hours of descent, eating and drinking three times, I sought a level spot for rest. Finding a tolerable patch, I cleared away small, irritating boulders. I ate, drank, and prepared for sleep, haunted by thoughts of Naani and a lingering, half-formed fear that something constantly hovered near me in the dark. Twice I rose to listen, hearing nothing but silence, convincing myself it was merely imagination. Still, my sleep was restless; I listened even as I dreamed.

Waking again after only six hours, startled by the same sensation of proximity, I gripped the Diskos and listened. Again, there was no sound, nothing my spirit could identify. The next day mirrored the previous one, except around the eighth hour, when I nearly plunged into a monstrous pit in the Great Slope. Catching myself on the edge, I crawled around it in the dark to reach the lower side safely. Shaken and terrified, I questioned whether I had stumbled upon something terrible or merely imagined the danger.

For a long while, I traveled with excessive caution in that utter dark. Eventually, I devised a plan to move with greater certainty and speed. I needed a cord, but I had none. If a boy cannot be called a boy without a rope, surely the same applies to a man! Searching frantically, I found a solution: I buckled my satchel and pouch together, removing a long, thin strap from the pouch. I fastened a stone to one end, secured it tightly, and cast the weighted stone ahead of me as I crawled. Holding the other end of the strap, I could gauge the depth of the ground before me, ensuring I did not fall into a hidden chasm.

Thus I proceeded, throwing the stone continuously before me down the slope. It was a cumbersome fashion of travel, yet I felt safer than I had since beginning my descent into the everlasting darkness.

At the eighteenth hour, I slept, only to wake strangely before the sixth hour, exactly as before. This recurring pattern filled me with fresh wonder and unease. No harm befell me, and I strove to keep my mind clear, though I truly believed something was always near in the dark—not necessarily evil, for it had not harmed me.

Three more days passed, and I never ceased crawling wearily on hands and knees. The Diskos rested at my hip. By now, I had spent six days in the Great Slope's utter dark, heading inevitably toward some dire destination, having descended a monstrous distance.

Here, I must note a significant change in the environment. The biting cold had vanished from the air on the slope; instead, the atmosphere felt heavy against my chest. I recall mentioning the air of the Night Land and the Mighty Pyramid earlier, but I focused primarily on my adventures. Now, I must clarify: the air of that distant, chill era was not like ours. It was thin and keen, hovering low near the earth rather than extending high above. There was a stark difference between the air inside the Mighty Pyramid and that outside its base. Beyond the upper levels, no breathable air existed; the Pyramid was effectively sealed from the outside world above. Whether it was completely sealed at the base remains uncertain in my memory, though I doubt I ever pondered such details deeply. We likely drew air from the Underground Fields, but whether those fields received fresh air from the Night Land, I do not know. I lament this lack of certainty, for I could write a hundred books on this wonder of the future and still fail to capture half the truth.

It seems logical that the people of the Pyramid possessed larger chests than we do today. Perhaps the reasoning of our age clouds my recollection of theirs. Surely, it is natural to assume they had broad chests to cope with the thin air of their time. Yet, striving to explain this clearly, I distrust my own reason, fearing it might distort my knowledge. Even a fool would accept what I have stated, yet the truth may differ.

However, I am certain that the people of the *Upper* Cities had large chests. This was common knowledge, just as we recognize the skin color of Africans or the stature of Patagonians today. One could distinguish a citizen of the Upper Cities from a Lower City dweller by this single trait. Due to this physiological difference, historical records indicate a plan was once proposed to transport people vertically through the immense height of the Pyramid, moving from city to city. The idea met with great disfavor and was abandoned, reflecting the natural resistance of the human heart to such upheaval.

Perhaps this vertical migration was intended as a health regimen and mental exercise. As previously noted, every youth and maiden was required to travel through all the cities of the Mighty Pyramid, a journey lasting three years and 225 days. Through this ordeal, they breathed the air of every altitude, presumably benefiting their development and learning which air suited them best.

Regarding the Night Land's air, the records confirm there were no flying creatures, for the atmosphere had become too thin. Long ago, however, monstrous flying brutes roamed the land in great swarms, though we can only suppose the records speak the truth.

When the Monstruwacans learned of my intention to traverse the Night Land seeking Naani, some offered foolish but well-meaning advice: they suggested I take a small flying-ship from the Great Museum, housed beside the models of the Great Ships. That machine was reportedly sound, crafted from the indestructible grey metal of the Pyramid. Yet, in truth, I lacked the skill to pilot it. No one had flown it in a hundred thousand years; the mastery of flight required constant practice and was fraught with dangers that often wrecked the machines, as detailed in the Book of Flying. Furthermore, the Night Land's air had grown too weak to support such a craft. Doubtless, the people of the Pyramid had ceased flying not only due to the air's weakness but also out of fear of the Forces of Evil lurking in the night.
