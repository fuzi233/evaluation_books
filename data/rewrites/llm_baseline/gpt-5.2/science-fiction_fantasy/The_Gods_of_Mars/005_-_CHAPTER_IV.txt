The first thing that hauled me back from the soft blackness was not light, nor pain, but sound—an ugly stew of it, thick as overcooked porridge. Feet scuffed and dragged beyond the blank golden wall at my side; chains whispered and answered one another; metal harness rang with a dull, tired clink; and underneath it all came the animal music of the place—banths coughing in their throats, a low snarl that vibrated through stone, and a man’s breath forced out in hard, wet pulls as though the air itself were something to be chewed.

For a heartbeat I could not place where I lay. Then the chamber resolved around me: the stench of old blood dried into metal; the sour reek of beasts kept too long without clean water; the copper taste that always rose in my own mouth after fighting. Along the opposite wall the prisoners and the brutes were chained in a line like goods hung in a market that sold only despair. Eyes followed me—some dull with resignation, some bright with hatred, some with the sharp, starving hope that a change in the wind can bring.

One face among them held my attention, not because it pleaded, but because it watched as a skilled hand watches a fire. She was young, a red Martian woman of that clean-limbed, noble type whose beauty seems almost indecent in such a place—skin like light copper warmed at the forge, eyes steady, mouth set as if she had learned to ration her words the way one rations spices when the pantry is bare. She wore no ornaments by which to read her rank, only the plainness of captivity; yet there was a composure about her that did not fit chains.

The sounds beyond the wall sharpened and I knew them at last. Tars Tarkas. Not the measured tread of the Jeddak of Tharks issuing orders, but the brutal cadence of a warrior alone, making his own body a barricade.

I threw myself at the secret door, shoulder and hip driving into the smooth gold. It might as well have been the cliff itself. I clawed along the seam, searching for the trick of it with fingertips that still trembled from the earlier struggle, but the wall gave me nothing—no latch, no handle, no mercy. I had just lifted my longsword, rage ready to ruin good steel against senseless metal, when the chained girl spoke.

“Save thy sword, O mighty warrior,” she called, her voice low and certain, like an elder in a farm-kitchen warning a youth not to waste flour on a failed batch. “Thou shalt need it where it will bite. That panel yields to a finger when one knows its secret.”

“You know it?” I demanded.

“Yes. Release me, and I will open to thee. The keys are upon the first dead of thy foemen.” She hesitated only long enough to glance toward the other side of the wall, where the struggle rasped on. “But why return to face the banth again—or whatever ruin they have loosed? None are meant to go back.”

“Because my friend fights there alone.”

I found the ring of keys on the carcass of the dead custodian—cold metal slick with blood—and carried it to her. There were many keys, each shaped to some particular cruelty. Without fumbling she chose one, the right one, and the great lock at her waist sprang with a heavy click. The sound was as loud in that room as a ladle struck against an empty pot: freedom announced.

She moved to the blank wall as if she had done it a thousand times in her mind. From the ring she selected a slender, needle-like key and slid it into an almost invisible puncture in the metal. A soft internal catch sighed, and the panel swung on its pivot.

The floor beneath my feet moved with it—an ingenious treachery. Before I could even shift my weight, the revolving section carried me through the wall and set me down in the adjoining chamber.

Tars Tarkas stood there, backed into an angle, holding the corner with his body because the room had become the enemy. Six banths crouched in a half circle before him—huge, corded things, heads low, shoulders ridged and smeared with their own blood and his. The beasts’ wariness was written in the slices and bruises across their muzzles. It was also written on him: his glossy green hide was torn as though someone had tried to carve him like meat from the bone. Talons and teeth had opened his leg, his arm, his breast. Blood—dark and copious—had run and dried and run again, the way a cooking pot bubbles over and crusts at the rim and then boils anew.

He should not have been standing. The wall did the work his failing strength could not. Yet his one hand held his sword as though the steel were an extension of will, not muscle. He looked up and, impossibly, a grim smile touched his mouth—whether relief at seeing me or amusement at my own battered state I could not tell.

I sprang forward, ready to throw myself into the circle, when a hand—light as a brush of flour from a baker’s wrist—touched my shoulder. I turned. The girl had followed.

“Wait,” she whispered. “Leave them to me.”

She stepped past me—unarmed, unarmored—into the animal stench of the banths as calmly as someone stepping into a dairy yard at dawn. The beasts’ snarls swelled; their claws flexed on the floor with a sound like knives on a board. She spoke a single Martian word, soft but edged with command.

The six great heads snapped toward her. I saw, in my mind’s eye, her flesh torn, her voice cut off mid-syllable. Instead, the monsters slunk to her feet like punished hounds, shoulders rolling low, ears flat, tails dragging. She spoke again, too low for me to catch, and began to walk toward the far side of the chamber. The banths rose and padded after her in obedient file, their heavy breathing now subdued, the rhythm of their paws on metal oddly domestic, like animals led from pen to trough.

One by one she sent them through another secret panel. The last banth vanished; she turned back and smiled at us—brief, almost weary—and then slipped through herself, leaving the chamber abruptly empty of everything but blood, breath, and astonishment.

For a moment neither Tars Tarkas nor I spoke. The absence of snarling was so sudden it rang in the ears like a silence after a factory’s engines cut out. In that hush the only living sound was the Thark’s harsh breathing and the distant, muffled clatter of the underground.

At last he said, “I heard fighting beyond the partition. I did not fear for thee, John Carter, until I heard the report of a revolver. There lives no man on Barsoom who may face thee with naked steel and live; but a shot—” He shook his head slowly. “Thou hast no firearms. Tell me.”

I told him quickly—of the therns, the trap, the girl’s warning, and the bullet that had been meant for me. While I spoke, his eyes stayed on my face with that relentless attentiveness his kind gives to a scent on the wind.

When I finished, we went together to the panel I had entered by, the one opposite the passage through which she had led the banths away. We searched for its secret with bruised fingers, pressing, tapping, testing seams. It did not yield. We crossed the chamber to the other golden door and tried it as well. Nothing. The place was a jar sealed without a lid.

Prisoners were kept chained here, which meant the therns had no fear of them reaching the outside. Somewhere there must be passages—means to move beasts, slaves, food, weapons, the whole machinery of terror. Yet the metal refused us. Again and again we turned between the two panels like men walking between two ovens, feeling the heat but unable to find the handle.

Then, as though mocking our ignorance, one panel turned of its own accord, silent as a thought. The girl stood there once more.

“Who are you,” she asked, “and what your mission, that you have the temerity to attempt escape from the Valley Dor and the death you have chosen?”

“I have chosen no death,” I said. “I am not of Barsoom, nor have I taken the voluntary pilgrimage upon the River Iss. My friend here is Jeddak of all the Tharks. Whether he longs to return or no, I am taking him from the living lie that lured him to this place.”

I straightened, and in that foul chamber spoke the titles that tied me to another world, another life. “I am John Carter, Prince of the House of Tardos Mors, Jeddak of Helium. Perhaps a rumor of me has reached even here.”

She smiled, but without warmth. “Yes. Naught that passes in the world we have left is unknown here. I have heard of you—many years ago. The therns wondered whither you had flown, since you took neither pilgrimage nor could be found upon Barsoom.”

“Then tell me who you are,” I said, “and why you are prisoner—yet hold power over the beasts as one who knows their breeding and their bridles.”

“Slave I am,” she replied. “Fifteen years a slave in this terrible place. Now they tire of me—and fear what I know—so I am condemned to die the death.” She shuddered, and the sound was like cloth drawn across skin.

“What death?”

“The Holy Therns eat human flesh,” she said simply, as if naming a recipe learned unwillingly. “But only that which has died beneath the sucking lips of a plant man—flesh from which the defiling blood of life has been drawn. To this end I have been condemned. It should have been within hours, had your coming not interrupted them.”

I felt a coldness behind my ribs. “Then those I killed—were they Holy Therns?”

“Oh, no. Lesser therns. Of the same cruel race.” She nodded toward the unseen reaches of the mountain. “The Holy Therns abide upon the outer slopes, facing the broad world from which they harvest victims and spoils. Labyrinthine passages connect these caves with their palaces, and through them pass lesser therns, slaves, prisoners, beasts—the inhabitants of this sunless world.”

She spoke on, and as she did her voice took on the steady cadence of one reciting a history that the conquerors keep alive while the conquered forget their own words. “There are men and women born within these passages who have never seen the light of day—nor ever shall. They are kept to do the bidding of the therns; to furnish their sport and sustenance.

“Now and again a pilgrim drifting from the Iss escapes the plant men and the great white apes that guard the Temple of Issus and falls into thern hands; or, as was my misfortune, is coveted by a Holy Thern who watches from the balcony above the river where it issues from the mountains through the cliffs of gold to empty into the Lost Sea of Korus.”

The names—Iss, Korus—fell from her tongue with the weight of old belief, syllables polished by repetition until their edges cut. I wondered how many dialects had died down here, how many native songs had been replaced by the therns’ words for fear and obedience. Even the sounds of this place—chains, claws, metal—seemed designed to drown out memory.

“All who reach the Valley Dor are, by custom, rightful prey of plant men and apes,” she continued, “while arms and ornaments become portion of the therns. But if one escapes the valley’s denizens even for hours, the therns may claim him. And a Holy Thern on watch, should he see a victim he covets, often tramples upon the rights of the valley brutes and takes his prize by foul means if he cannot gain it by fair.

“It is said that occasionally some deluded victim of Barsoomian superstition so far escapes the enemies that beset him as to reach the walls of the Temple of Issus. But what fate awaits within—none know. None who pass those gilded walls return.”

“The Temple is to the therns what the Valley Dor is imagined by the outer world to be to them,” she finished, and for an instant contempt flashed across her face like a knife catching light. “Their ultimate haven—delights of the flesh for a race of mental giants and moral pygmies.”

“A heaven within a heaven,” I said. “Let us hope it measures to them what they measure to others.”

She murmured, “Who knows? The therns are spoken of by all Barsoom with awe, as of gods.”

“They are mortal,” she said. “They die as we do, save that their allotted span is one thousand years. Those who live it may take their way, by custom, through the long tunnel that leads to Issus. Those who die before are supposed to spend the balance of their time in the image of a plant man. For this reason plant men are held sacred by the therns, since they believe each hideous creature was formerly a thern.”

“And if the plant man dies?” I asked.

“Then the soul passes into a great white ape—if the thousand years are not yet complete. If the ape dies before the exact hour that ends the thousand years, the soul is lost for ever and passes into the carcass of the slimy silians whose wriggling thousands seethe the silent sea beneath the moons when the sun is gone and strange shapes walk through the Valley Dor.”

Tars Tarkas, despite blood loss and exhaustion, gave a low laugh. “We sent several Holy Therns to the silians to-day, then.”

“And so thy death will be the more terrible when it comes,” she said, as though the therns’ theology were a ledger where each act accrued interest. “And come it will—you cannot escape.”

“One has escaped, centuries ago,” I reminded her. “What has been done may be done again.”

“It is useless even to try,” she answered, hopelessness slipping into her words like ash into bread dough. Then she looked at me, and something like pride fought its way through. “But try, if you must.”

“Try we shall,” I said, “and you shall go with us, if you wish.”

She drew herself up. “To be put to death by mine own people—and render my memory a disgrace to my family and nation? A Prince of the House of Tardos Mors should know better than to suggest such a thing.”

I felt Tars Tarkas’s gaze on me—heavy, expectant. My answer would be more than counsel to her; it would be the shape of our own fate. If I bowed to superstition, we would remain and be consumed by this mockery of paradise.

“We have the right to escape if we can,” I said. “Our moral sense will not be offended if we succeed, for we know the fable of love and peace in Dor is a wicked deception. The valley is not sacred. The Holy Therns are not holy. They are cruel mortals who know no more of the real life to come than we.

“Not only is it our right—it is our duty. Even if our peoples revile and torture us when we return. Only thus may we carry the truth to those without. And though mortals cling to superstition like a child to a familiar word even when the meaning is empty, we should be cowards to shirk what lies before us. With the testimony of several, perhaps the truth may be believed—or at least doubted enough that an expedition will be sent to investigate this hideous mockery.”

Silence settled. In it I could hear, faintly, the distant living machinery of the underground—doors moving, steps, the far-off echo of a chain drawn through a ring—sounds that, over years, must have rewritten the minds of those trapped here. Sound shapes thought; noise can become a master as cruel as any thern.

At last the girl spoke. “Never had I considered it thus,” she said softly. “Indeed would I give my life a thousand times if I could save one soul from the awful life I have led. Yes. You are right. I will go with you as far as we can go—though I doubt we shall escape.”

I turned to Tars Tarkas.

“To the gates of Issus or to the bottom of Korus,” he said, voice steady. “To the snows north or the snows south, Tars Tarkas follows where John Carter leads. I have spoken.”

“Come, then,” I said. “We cannot be further from escape than we are now—in the heart of this mountain and within four walls built for death.”

“Come,” the girl answered, and there was a grim humor in it. “But do not flatter thyself there is no worse place within the therns’ territory.”

She swung the panel open and led us back into the chamber where the chained prisoners waited. There were ten red Martians in all, men and women, their faces hollowed by captivity yet still carrying the stamp of their homes and tribes—features like remembered recipes, distinct and local, stubborn against the therns’ uniform cruelty. When we explained—briefly, for time was a knife at our throats—they chose to join us. I saw their misgivings: the old superstition still tugged at them, not because it was true, but because it was the language their world had used to name death for centuries.

The girl—Thuvia, we learned—moved from prisoner to prisoner, selecting keys with the same surety she had shown at the door. Locks sprang, chains dropped. The sound of each shackle hitting the floor was a small percussion of rebellion.

Tars Tarkas and I stripped the two dead therns of their weapons: swords, daggers, and two of the red Martian revolvers—those compact, deadly things that snap like a whip crack and leave only silence. We distributed what we could. Two of the women took the firearms; Thuvia was one of them. The weight of the revolver in her hand looked less like a weapon than like a long-denied utensil finally returned to its rightful owner.

With Thuvia guiding, we moved out swiftly but warily into the maze—winding corridors, steep inclines, great chambers hewn from the metal cliff itself. The air changed from passage to passage: sometimes close and warm with beast heat, sometimes cold enough to prickle the skin; always carrying the mineral tang of the gold walls and the stale breath of too many bodies. Now and again we pressed into shadowed recesses as footsteps approached, letting patrols pass—lesser therns and slaves moving with the blind obedience of those who have forgotten there is any other rhythm to life.

Our first destination, Thuvia told us, was a storeroom where arms and ammunition could be found. From there she would lead us toward the summit of the cliffs, and then—if we had both wit and steel enough—through the very heart of the Holy Therns’ stronghold to the world outside.

“And even then, O Prince,” she whispered to me as we went, “the arm of the Holy Thern is long. It reaches every nation. Secret temples lie hidden in the heart of every community. If we escape, word of us will run before us, and death will wait—lest we pollute the air with our blasphemies.”

We had been moving perhaps an hour when we entered a great chamber and found a man there—alone, waiting as if he had stepped from the wall itself. He was thern, but more than the common sort: besides leather trappings and jeweled ornaments, a great circlet of gold encircled his brow, set in its center with an immense stone.

The sight of it struck me like the smell of a long-remembered food—instant, vivid, dragging memory with it. I had seen such a gem once before, nearly twenty years ago, upon the breast of a little old man at the atmosphere plant. Two such jewels were known to exist, worn as insignia by the two old men entrusted with the engines that pump artificial air across Mars. That plant—and its secret—had given me the power to save a world from suffocation.

This stone matched the other in size—an inch across, perhaps—and in the way it broke light into nine rays: the seven familiar colors, and two beyond Earth’s seeing, colors that seemed to be felt more than looked at, like a tone too deep for the ear yet heavy in the chest.

The thern’s eyes narrowed to slits. “Stop!” he cried. “What means this, Thuvia?”

For answer she lifted her revolver and fired point-blank. The crack of it slammed through the chamber, harsh and final, the kind of sound that leaves the ears ringing and the heart leaping. The thern sank without a sound, dead before his body finished accepting what had happened.

“Beast,” Thuvia hissed, the word spat as if it burned her tongue. “After all these years—I am at last revenged.”

She turned toward me, explanation rising, but her eyes widened as they fixed on my face. She stepped closer with a sudden, fierce energy.

“O Prince,” she cried, “Fate is kind to us. The way is still difficult, but through this vile thing upon the floor we may yet win to the outer world. Notest thou not the resemblance between this Holy Thern and thyself?”

I looked down. The man was my stature. His features—eyes, nose, mouth—echoed mine unsettlingly, like a reflection seen in troubled water. Only his hair differed: a mass of flowing yellow locks, while mine is black and close-cropped.

“What of it?” I asked. “Would you have me, with my black hair, pose as a yellow-haired priest of this infernal cult?”

Thuvia smiled—a quick flash of practicality. She knelt beside the corpse, removed the golden circlet, and then, to my utter astonishment, lifted the entire scalp bodily from the dead man’s head. It came away like a cap, the yellow hair attached—a wig made of skin and tradition.

She rose and set it on my head, then crowned me with the circlet and its impossible gem.

“Now don his harness, Prince,” she said. “You may pass where you will in the realms of the therns. Sator Throg was a Holy Thern of the Tenth Cycle—mighty among his kind.”

As I bent to strip the dead man’s gear, I saw his skull beneath—smooth and bare as an egg.

“They are all thus from birth,” Thuvia said, seeing my surprise. “The race from which they sprang were crowned with luxuriant golden hair, but for many ages the present race has been entirely bald. The wig has become part of their apparel—and so important that it is deepest disgrace for a thern to appear in public without it.”

In another moment I stood garbed as a Holy Thern, wearing his harness and the circlet with the priceless jewel, the dead man’s false hair settling over my own like an adopted language—powerful, sanctioned, and rotten at the root. Two of the freed prisoners lifted Sator Throg’s body and bore it with us, as though we carried our own lie on our shoulders.

We continued toward the storeroom. Thuvia’s keys opened it without struggle, and within we found what we needed: arms, ammunition, the cold tools by which men convince other men to obey. We outfitted ourselves quickly, hands moving with the brisk economy of those who know they may not have time to correct mistakes.

Only then did my body demand its due. Exhaustion came down like a shutter. The air of the room seemed suddenly thick, and my limbs felt filled with sand.

I threw myself upon the floor, bidding Tars Tarkas to do likewise, and set two of the freed prisoners to keep careful watch. The last sound I remember, before sleep took me, was the soft, vigilant shifting of feet at the doorway—an attempt at quiet in a world that had forgotten what true quiet sounded like. In an instant I was asleep.
