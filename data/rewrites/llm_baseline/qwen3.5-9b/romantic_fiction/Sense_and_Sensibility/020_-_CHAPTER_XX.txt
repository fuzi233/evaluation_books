The following day, as the Dashwood sisters entered the drawing-room of the Park at one door, Mrs. Palmer burst in at the opposite entrance, radiating a cheerful merriment that seemed entirely restored. With affectionate warmth, she greeted them all, expressing profound delight at their return. "How glad I am to see you!" she exclaimed, settling herself between Elinor and Marianne. "For it has been such a dreadful day, I feared you wouldn't come, which would have been truly shocking, considering we depart again tomorrow. We simply must go; the Westons arrive next week, you know. It was quite impulsive on our part—we knew nothing until the carriage pulled up to the door, and then Mr. Palmer merely asked if I would accompany him to Barton. He is so whimsical! He rarely tells me anything beforehand. I am terribly sorry we cannot stay longer; however, I trust we shall meet again in London very soon."

They felt compelled to dampen this hopeful anticipation. "Not go to town?" Mrs. Palmer cried, laughing. "I shall be utterly disappointed if you do not. I could secure the finest house imaginable for you, right next door to ours in Hanover Square. You must come, indeed. I am certain I would be delighted to act as your chaperone at any time until I am confined, should Mrs. Dashwood prefer avoiding public society." They offered thanks but firmly resisted her persistent entreaties.

"Oh, my dear," Mrs. Palmer called out to her husband, who had just entered the room, "you must assist me in persuading the Miss Dashwoods to spend the winter in London." Her husband offered no reply; after a slight bow to the ladies, he launched into a complaint about the weather. "How horrid everything is!" he declared. "Weather like this renders everyone and everything repugnant. Dullness is generated indoors just as readily as outdoors by the rain; it makes one detest all acquaintances. What on earth does Sir John mean by lacking a billiard room in his house? How few people understand true comfort! Sir John is as obtuse as the weather itself."

Shortly thereafter, the remainder of the party arrived. "I fear, Miss Marianne," Sir John remarked, "that you were unable to take your customary walk to Allenham today." Marianne appeared deeply somber and remained silent. "Oh, don't be so secretive before us," Mrs. Palmer teased. "We know all about it, I assure you. Besides, I admire your taste immensely; I find him extremely handsome. We do not reside far from him in the countryside, you know—not more than ten miles, I daresay."

"Much closer to thirty," her husband interjected.

"Ay, well! There isn't much difference. I have never visited his home, but they say it is a lovely, pretty place."

"As vile a spot as I have ever seen in my life," Mr. Palmer countered sharply.

Marianne remained perfectly silent, though her expression clearly betrayed her intense interest in the conversation. "Is it very ugly?" Mrs. Palmer pressed. "Then it must be some other location that is so charming, I suppose."

When they moved to the dining room, Sir John noted with regret that there were only eight of them present. "My dear," he said to his wife, "it is exceedingly vexing that we are so few. Why didn't you invite the Gilberts to join us today?"

"Didn't I tell you, Sir John, when you first suggested it, that it wasn't possible? They dined with us yesterday."

"You and I, Sir John," Mrs. Jennings remarked, "should not stand on such formalities."

"Then you would be dreadfully ill-bred," Mr. Palmer retorted.

"My love, you contradict everyone," his wife replied with her characteristic laughter.

"Do you realize you are being quite rude?"

"I did not know I was contradicting anyone by calling your mother ill-bred."

"Ay, you may abuse me as you please," the kindly old lady said. "You have taken Charlotte off my hands, and you cannot give her back. So, there, I hold the upper hand over you." Charlotte laughed heartily at the thought that her husband could not escape her, exclaiming triumphantly that she cared not how cross he became, for they were bound to live together forever.

It was impossible to find someone more thoroughly good-natured or more determined to cultivate happiness than Mrs. Palmer. Her husband's calculated indifference, insolence, and dissatisfaction caused her no distress; indeed, when he scolded or insulted her, she found it highly diverting. "Mr. Palmer is so droll!" she whispered to Elinor. "He is perpetually in a bad mood."

Elinor, after observing him closely, was disinclined to accept his claim of genuine, unfeigned ill-nature or rudeness. Perhaps his temperament had become somewhat sour due to discovering, like many men, that through some inexplicable bias toward beauty, he had married a very foolish woman. Yet, she recognized that such errors were too common for any sensible man to suffer lasting harm from them. Rather, she believed it was a desire for distinction—a wish to appear superior—that drove his contemptuous treatment of others and his general denigration of everything around him. The motive was commonplace enough not to warrant wonder, but the methods, however successful they might be in establishing his superiority through bad breeding, were unlikely to attract anyone to him save his wife.

"Oh, my dear Miss Dashwood," Mrs. Palmer resumed shortly afterward, "I have a favor to request of you and your sister. Would you consider spending some time at Cleveland this Christmas? Please, do—and come while the Westons are still with us. You cannot imagine how happy I shall be! It will be absolutely delightful!—My love," turning to her husband, "do you not long to have the Miss Dashwoods visit Cleveland?"

"Certainly," he replied with a sneer—"I came to Devonshire with no other purpose."

"There now," she said, "you see Mr. Palmer expects you; therefore, you cannot refuse."

Both sisters eagerly and resolutely declined her invitation.

"But indeed you must and shall come. I am sure you will enjoy it above all else. The Westons will be with us, making it quite delightful. You cannot conceive what a charming place Cleveland is; moreover, we are so lively now, for Mr. Palmer is constantly traveling the country campaigning for the election, and so many people have come to dine with us that I have never seen before—it is simply enchanting! But, poor fellow! It is very exhausting for him, for he is forced to make everyone like him."

Elinor could scarcely maintain her composure as she acknowledged the hardship of such an obligation. "How charming it will be," Charlotte added, "once he enters Parliament! Won't it? How I shall laugh! It will be so absurd to see all his letters addressed to him with 'M.P.' appended. But do you know, he insists he will never frank them for me? He declares he won't. Don't you, Mr. Palmer?"

Mr. Palmer ignored her completely.

"He cannot bear writing, you know," she continued. "He says it is quite shocking."

"No," he replied, "I never said anything so irrational. Do not attribute all your misuse of language to me."

"There now; you see how droll he is. This is always his manner! Sometimes he refuses to speak to me for half a day, and then suddenly bursts forth with something so witty—about anything in the world."

She greatly surprised Elinor as they returned to the drawing-room by asking if she did not like Mr. Palmer excessively.

"Certainly," Elinor answered. "He seems very agreeable."

"Well, I am so glad you feel that way. I thought you would; he is so pleasant. And Mr. Palmer is excessively pleased with you and your sisters, I can tell you. You cannot imagine how disappointed he will be if you do not come to Cleveland. I cannot fathom why you should object."

Elinor was once again compelled to decline, putting a halt to her entreaties by shifting the topic. She hoped that, living in the same county, Mrs. Palmer might provide a more detailed account of Willoughby's general character than could be gleaned from the Middletons' limited acquaintance. Eager to obtain confirmation of his virtues sufficient to alleviate Marianne's fears, she began by inquiring if they saw much of Mr. Willoughby at Cleveland and whether they were intimate with him.

"Oh, dear, yes; I know him extremely well," Mrs. Palmer responded. "Though I never actually spoke to him, indeed; I have merely seen him frequently in town. Somehow, I never happened to be staying at Barton while he was at Allenham. My mother saw him here once before, but I was with my uncle at Weymouth. However, I suppose we should have encountered him often in Somersetshire if it hadn't unfortunately occurred that we were never in the country simultaneously. He is very seldom at Combe, I believe; but even if he were there more often, I doubt Mr. Palmer would visit him, for he is in the opposition, you know, and besides, it is so remote. I know precisely why you are inquiring about him; your sister intends to marry him. I am monstrously glad of it, for then I shall have her as a neighbor."

"Upon my word," Elinor replied, "you seem to know much more about the matter than I do, if you have grounds to expect such a union."
