Spring advanced, turning the surface of England a pleasant green, yet the heart of the nation remained frozen. While fields bloomed and gardens opened, the country's soul was still tormented by a war that drained her blood and wealth for ends that seemed increasingly inadequate. News of victories in the Peninsula arrived in slow, sparse drips, interrupted by long silences filled only with the arrogant boasts of Bonaparte. For those who bore the brunt of the conflict, this struggle felt endless and despairing; they clamored for peace on any terms whatsoever. Men like Sir John Moore and the countless merchants trembling on the edge of bankruptcy fought for this deliverance with the desperate energy of men holding back a collapsing dam.

They held meetings, delivered speeches, and circulated petitions, caring little for the specific conditions of peace. After all, men taken individually are naturally selfish, and when gathered in bodies, that selfishness intensifies into a roaring tide. The British merchant class illustrated this rule with striking clarity. Too obsessed with the accumulation of gold, they were blind to every national consideration save the expansion of commerce—*their* commerce. Chivalry, honor, and disinterested patriotism had died within them. A nation ruled solely by traders would likely submit ignominiously to a conqueror, not from noble conviction, but from the seduction of Mammon. During the recent wars, English tradesmen would have borne blows to both cheeks, handed their cloaks to Napoleon, and politely offered their coats and waistcoats if pressed. They would pray only to keep their final garment, solely to protect the purse within it. It was only when the Corsican thief's hand closed around that beloved purse that these "British bulldogs" would spring at the robber's throat, hanging there, insatiable and furious, until the treasure was returned. When speaking against war, these merchants always claimed to hate it for its brutality, painting themselves as uniquely civilized and kind. In reality, many were narrow, cold-hearted, and viewed anyone outside trade as a parasite breathing air they didn't deserve. Long may it be before England truly becomes a nation of shopkeepers!

Sir John Moore, despite his lack of sacrificial patriotism and his tendency to prioritize personal interest, fought fiercely when pushed to the brink of ruin. He agitated in the North, instigating others with greater influence than his own. Occasionally, flashes of reason pierced his foggy mind regarding his party's demands on government. When he heard of Europe arming to resist Bonaparte, when he saw Russia rising to defend her frozen soil and serfs from foreign tyranny, he realized that a free realm like England could not delegate its sons to make concessions to an unjust, grasping leader. Yet, amidst the news of Lord Wellington's steady, unhasting, and unresting advances in the Peninsula, Moore confessed a deep-seated admiration. He saw a vigilance and endurance in the British troops that promised ultimate victory. But victory lay in the "end"—a horizon still far off. Meanwhile, as an individual, Moore feared he would be crushed, his hopes ground to dust. His focus shifted entirely to himself, to fulfilling his own destiny.

He fulfilled it so vigorously that a decisive rupture ensued with his old Tory friend, the Rector, Mr. Helstone. They quarreled publicly and exchanged biting letters in the newspapers. Mr. Helstone denounced Moore as a Jacobin, severed all ties, and strictly forbade Caroline's niecehood communications with Hollow's Cottage. He insisted she cease taking French lessons, dismissing the language as frivolous and injurious to the weak female mind. He questioned who first made it fashionable to teach women French, comparing it to feeding a rickety child chalk and water gruel. Caroline, obedient and quiet, agreed immediately. There was no vexed flush or gathering tear; the shadowy thoughtfulnes that had covered her features remained undisturbed. Indeed, the mandate coincided with her own judgment. Going to Hollow's Cottage had become a source of pain, meeting only disappointment. Hope and love had fled that little tenement; Robert seemed to have deserted its precincts. Whenever she asked after him—a rarity, as uttering his name made her face grow hot—the answer was invariably that he was from home or consumed by business. Hortense feared he was killing himself with application.

At church, Caroline rarely looked at him, for it was both too painful and too pleasurable to do so, and she had learned that such emotion was ultimately wasted. On a particularly dark, wet Sunday, when few attended and the observant ladies were absent, she allowed her eye to seek Robert's pew. He sat alone, arms folded, eyes cast down, looking sadder and more abstracted than usual. Depressed, the hue of his face seemed darker, his olive skin devoid of color. By instinct, Caroline knew his thoughts were running in channels far removed from her comprehension, wrapped in interests and responsibilities from which she was excluded. Nothing they had ever discussed was in his mind.

Meditating on this separation, Caroline realized the vast disparity between their conditions. "Different, indeed," she thought. "I think only of him; he has no room, no leisure, to think of me. Love has been the predominant emotion of my heart for two years... Quite other feelings absorb his reflections." When service ended and Robert rose, he did not turn his head toward her. He gave her no look. "Strange that grief should almost choke me," she lamented, "because another human being's eye has failed to greet mine."

That evening, after tea, Caroline retired to her chamber. Mute and solitary, she paced the carpeted floor, her head drooped, hands folded. The study door muffled the voices of Mr. Helstone and Mr. Malone, who were discussing theology. The servants were quiet, engrossed in books Miss Helstone had lent them for "Sunday reading." Caroline, too, had a book open, but she could not read it. Its theology was incomprehensible, and her mind teemed with images of Moore, winter firesides, and the lush landscape of Nunnely Wood. She remembered sharing September treasures of blackberries with him, feeding them to him berry by berry like a bird feeding a fledgling. But these joys, hollow and illusory, were quickly crushed. The pictures faded, the voice failed, and the warm seal of lips on her forehead felt now like a cold, sleety raindrop. Returning from an enchanted region to the real world, she saw her narrow chamber instead of Nunnely Wood and heard the rain on her casement instead of the south wind.

Turning from the pale phantom of her reflection, she sat down, resolving to live. "As far as I know, I have good health," she mused, "half a century may lie before me. How am I to occupy it?" She reflected that she would likely never be married, for Robert did not care for her. She would probably be an old maid, watching Robert marry some wealthy lady, while she remained unmarried. "What was I created for?" she wondered. "Where is my place in the world?"

She saw then the puzzle that plagued old maids: the question of purpose. Others solved it by saying, "Your place is to do good to others." But she sensed a terrible hollowness in that existence, a craving for something of one's own to bestow. Was virtue merely abnegation of self? Did undue humility create tyranny? She suspected not. Every human being had a share of rights, an allotment to be held tenaciously. Life was short, a vapour like a dream, terminating inevitably in the grave, where the body decays and springs anew, while the soul winged its flight upward to gaze upon the vision of the Trinity.

The next day, Mr. Helstone was away dining, leaving Caroline alone. Fanny, observing her mistress's stillness, noted that she sat bent industriously over sewing, refusing to lift her head or accept advice to walk. "You are very diligent at that sewing, Miss Caroline," Fanny remarked. "Put it down. Read, or do something to amuse you."

"It is solitary in this house, Fanny," Caroline replied. "Don't you think so?"

"I don't find it so, miss. You should visit more. Go and take tea with Miss Mann or Miss Ainley."

"But their houses are dismal: they are both old maids. I am certain old maids are a very unhappy race."

"Not they, miss. They take such care of themselves. They are all selfish."

"Miss Ainley is not selfish, Fanny. She is always doing good. How devotedly kind she was to her stepmother..."

"She shouldn't let gentlemen think only of ladies' looks," Fanny interjected.

"I'll go and see her," Caroline exclaimed, starting up. "And Miss Mann too. How wrong it is to neglect people because they are not pretty!"

Fanny helped her dress, smoothing her soft curls and tying her sash. "_You_'ll not be an old maid, Miss Caroline," Fanny said confidently. "There are no signs about you."

Caroline looked into the mirror. She saw signs nonetheless. Her complexion was paler, her eyes ringed with a wan shade, her countenance dejected. She was not the fresh, pretty girl she used to be. Fanny assured her that falling away at her age meant nothing, and wrapped her warmly in shawls, nearly smothering her.
