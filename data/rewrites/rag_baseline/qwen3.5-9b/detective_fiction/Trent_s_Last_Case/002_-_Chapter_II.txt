To comprehend the immediate impact of the murder of Sigsbee Manderson, one must first analyze the structural integrity of the media ecosystem in which he operated. Sir James Molloy, the managing director and editor-in-chief of *The Record*, represents the apex of this industry—a towering figure of fifty years, possessing the swart complexion and black moustache characteristic of his Irish heritage, yet devoid of the charlatanism often associated with his race. His leadership style is defined by a pragmatic synthesis of meritocracy and strategic patronage; where gifts cannot be secured, he relies on solid talent, employing a workforce that commands genuine respect rather than feigned reverence.

The narrative precipitates in the only comfortably furnished room of the *Record* offices, where the sudden intrusion of technology disrupts the routine. The telephone, a buzzing artifact of modern communication, interrupts Sir James's work. Upon receiving a call from Calvin Bunner, Manderson's right-hand man, the atmosphere shifts instantly. Bunner, speaking from the vicinity of Bishopsbridge, conveys information described as the "gravest piece of news," necessitating a direct connection. When Sir James answers, the transmission reveals a reaction of profound shock: a murmur of "Good God!" followed by a physical ascent to his feet, ears bent intently to the device. The realization of the event's magnitude triggers an immediate operational response. Sir James dispatches Mr. Silver to summon reporters Figgis and Williams, demonstrating the hierarchical command structure essential for crisis management in journalism.

Mr. Figgis, a hard-featured man with spectacles, joins Mr. Silver and the youthful Williams, forming a triad of investigative assets. Sir James, regaining his composure, issues precise directives with rapid calmness. He instructs Figgis to compile factual data for a special edition of the *Sun*, the evening counterpart to the *Record*. Simultaneously, he orders urgent telegraphic communication to their local correspondent regarding Marlstone, mandating absolute secrecy until the publication hits the streets. The strategy involves a coordinated assault on information dissemination: Williams is tasked with briefing the lead editor, Mr. Anthony, to prepare a two-column opening designed to "knock the town endways"—a metaphorical expression indicating a sensationalist approach intended to dominate public discourse. Miss Morgan, a pale and graceful figure embodying imperturbable efficiency, enters the scene to manage the biographical and visual assets required for the coverage. She notes that while the *Record* possesses extensive cuttings, much of it is of low quality ("mostly rubbish"), whereas the *Sun* retains a superior sketch from two years prior concerning Manderson's settlement of the potash difficulty in Berlin. Furthermore, she identifies high-quality photographic portraits created by Mr. Trent, acknowledging the public's paradoxical preference for inferior photography over artistic drawings despite the latter's superior fidelity.

As the logistical preparations unfold, Sir James engages in a meta-commentary on journalistic fallibility, jokingly requesting that Miss Morgan commit an error to maintain professional humility. The focus then shifts to the core of the investigation via a telephone link with Mr. Trent. Trent, currently engaged in painting, initially resists the summons, citing a strong creative impulse and a desire to avoid interruption. However, Sir James leverages the gravity of the situation—"something very serious has happened"—to override Trent's reluctance. The details provided confirm the death of Sigsbee Manderson: he was shot through the brain at his residence, White Gables, near Bishopsbridge. The body was discovered by a gardener around ten o'clock the following morning, showing no signs of robbery but evidence of a struggle on the wrists. Local authorities remain without a suspect.

During the conversation, Trent offers a preliminary assessment grounded in logical deduction. He posits that the lack of theft suggests the perpetrator may have been someone Manderson knew, perhaps a tramp he attempted to eject, who acted as a form of social protest. Trent explicitly states he would not participate in executing a man merely for exposing social injustices, highlighting a moral dimension to the potential crime. Despite his initial hesitation, the allure of the case proves irresistible; he admits to wanting to investigate, noting the ambiguity of whether the matter constitutes a complex mystery or a straightforward incident. Arrangements are made for Trent to travel to the scene, utilizing either a specific motor vehicle or the last available train, depending on timing constraints. Sir James ensures accommodation is secured near the site of the tragedy.

The sequence concludes with the external manifestation of the internal preparation. A shrill outcry erupts from the street below, revealing a band of excited boys carrying bundles of newspapers bearing the headline: "MURDER OF SIGSBEE MANDERSON." These youths rush toward Fleet Street, physically manifesting the spread of the news. Observing this spectacle, Sir James smiles and rattles coins in his pocket, remarking to Mr. Silver that the event "makes a good bill." This final observation underscores the dual nature of the profession: while the murder is a tragic reality, its reporting serves as a lucrative commodity, cementing Manderson's legacy not solely through his life's work, but through the sensationalized epitaph printed on the front pages of the city.
