Morning came to Shador with a thin, cold light that smelled of wet stone and brine, and with it the soft, steady hush of Korus beyond the walls—a living sound, like a giant kettle kept just under simmer. Even in a cell, you could tell when the sea was calm; its breath laid itself along the corridors and slipped beneath doors the way steam finds a crack.

Xodar and I put our minds to escape the way old women put their hands to kneading: with patience, with repetition, with the certainty that rough material can be made workable. I had him scratch a map of the south polar regions across the cell’s stone floor. We had no stylus—only the buckle from my harness and the keen edge of the strange gem I had taken from Sator Throg. The gem bit the stone cleanly, leaving pale grooves that looked like chalk lines until you brushed them and felt the grit under your fingertips.

He drew the curve of the Golden Cliffs; he marked the general lay of the ice and the southward wastes; he indicated the hidden opening that led into Omean. While he worked, I listened—not only to his voice but to the place itself: the shuffle of distant guards, the metal tick of harness fittings, the far-off clank that came from Shador’s docks. Sound carried here as if the air were a drumskin. Even the smallest noise announced a life, a duty, a threat.

From his crude cartography I computed direction and distance—Helium out there somewhere beyond the frozen desolation, as real and unreachable as a remembered taste. Then I had him draw Omean itself, the bowl of the buried sea, and the prison’s position within it; he marked plainly the opening in the dome that led to the outer world, and where Shador sat by the edge like a scabbed wound on the shore.

I studied the lines until they no longer lay in the floor but in my mind. If the body must move in darkness, the memory must be a lamp.

From Xodar I learned the guard customs. During the sleep hours, only one man patrolled at a time, pacing a slow circuit around the prison at a distance of about a hundred feet. It took him close to ten minutes to complete a round—so that for nearly five minutes any given side lay unwatched while his snail’s pace carried him to the opposite face.

“This information you ask,” Xodar said at last, voice low with the sour caution of a man who has watched too many hopes spoiled, “will be valuable after we are out. But nothing you have asked touches the first matter: how do we pass these walls?”

“We will get out,” I told him, and could not keep the laugh from my throat. The sea’s hush steadied me, and the rhythm of the guard’s distant step set a pace to my thoughts. “Leave that to me.”

“When?” he asked.

“The first night a small craft lies moored near Shador,” I said.

“But how will you know?” He jerked his chin toward the high windows, far above reach. “The openings are beyond us.”

“Not beyond,” I answered, and sprang.

Earth-bred muscles on Barsoom are a sort of sorcery; I caught the iron bars, climbed as lightly as if the grating were a ladder, and lifted myself to glance out.

Below, in the pale morning, several small craft and two large battleships lay within a hundred yards of the prison.

To-night, I thought—had the word on my tongue—when the door opened without warning and a guard stepped in.

If he saw me at the window, he would not merely scold. He would put me in irons, and once iron has a man, it makes him a theory instead of a threat.

The black entered and stood facing the center of the cell. His back was toward me. Between my boots and the floor was the whole weight of his suspicion. Five feet above me ran the top of the partition wall dividing our cell from the next.

There was no graceful way down. If I dropped, I would strike him. If he turned, I was done.

“Where is the white man?” he bellowed at Xodar. “Issus commands his presence.” He began to turn his head, just enough for one eye to search.

I did not descend. I sprang.

My foot found the sill for the briefest heartbeat; I let go and launched for the partition top. Metal rasped on stone as I slid over—an ugly, betraying scrape.

“What was that?” the guard snarled, the sound of his voice deep as a drum.

I dropped into the adjacent cell and landed soft, knees bent, breath held. From the other side came his renewed roar.

“Where is the white slave?”

“I know not,” Xodar answered, calm as a man stirring embers. “He was here even as you entered. I am not his keeper—go find him.”

The black muttered something I could not catch; then came the click and grind of keys, a door opening in the corridor beyond, then another. He was searching cells. I listened to the pattern of his movements until his steps passed into the next compartment and the door shut behind him.

Then I was over the partition again and down into my own cell beside Xodar, who stared as though he had seen a conjurer pull smoke from a fist.

“Do you see now how we will escape?” I whispered.

“I see how you may,” he replied, bitterness and wonder tangled together. “But I am no wiser how I am to pass these walls. Certain it is I cannot bounce over them as you do.”

We heard the guard returning, keys jangling. He came in at last, and when his eyes fell on me they bulged with a mixture of rage and relief, as though his mind could not decide whether he had been tricked or simply shamed.

“By the shell of my first ancestor!” he roared. “Where have you been?”

“In prison,” I said blandly. “Since you put me here yesterday. I was in this room when you entered. You had better look to your eyesight.”

He glared a moment longer—anger searching for purchase—and then the order overcame the uncertainty.

“Come. Issus commands your presence.”

He took me out, leaving Xodar behind. Outside the prison other guards waited, and with them the red Martian youth from another cell. The sight of him tightened something in me like a knot drawn suddenly. His face had haunted my thoughts. There was a familiarity in the set of his mouth, in the way his shoulders carried their strength, in the way his gaze moved—alert, appraising, full of a contained heat that was more than youth.

I knew too well that I had never seen him before. And yet my memory kept insisting I had—like a word you cannot translate but recognize in your bones because it belonged once to your mother’s tongue.

We made again the journey I had taken the day before toward the Temple of Issus. The guards kept the youth and me apart, so we could not finish the conversation that had been cut short the previous night. The world around us wore its false beauty like spiced sweetness laid over spoiled meat: exquisite gardens, the air rich with perfume, water murmuring in hidden channels. But beneath it all I heard the harsher soundtrack of Omean—chains, shouted orders, the slap of sandals on stone, the sharp clack of harness buckles. This place tried to sell holiness the way cities sell entertainment, with spectacle and noise; yet the sea beyond still kept its own truth, steady and unbought.

When we reached the gardens of Issus we were led away from the temple rather than toward it. The path wound through parks that felt staged—trees too perfectly spaced, flowers too lavishly arranged, the air heavy with scent as if to drown out the stench of fear. We came at last to a mighty wall a hundred feet high, with massive gates that opened upon a small plain rimmed by the same gorgeous forests I had seen below the Golden Cliffs.

Crowds of blacks strolled toward the same destination. Among them moved plant men and great white apes, as casually as a man might walk dogs. The blacks shoved the beasts aside or struck them with the flat of a sword; the apes slunk away, cowed by habit. It was not courage that ruled here, but ownership.

Half a mile beyond the garden wall stood a great amphitheatre at the edge of the plain. Through a massive arched gateway the blacks poured in to take their seats while our guards led us to a smaller entrance. We passed into an enclosure beneath the stands where other prisoners were herded, some in irons, most simply held by the knowledge that every exit was guarded.

Here, in the caged gloom, the guards’ attention softened. I slipped to the youth’s side.

“What is the object of this assembly?” I asked. “Are we to fight for their amusement, or something worse?”

“It is a part of the monthly rites of Issus,” he said. His voice carried the flatness of repeated horror, as though he had learned to speak of it the way a butcher speaks of slaughter—without letting the taste of blood rise into the mouth. “Black men wash sins from their souls in the blood of men from the outer world. If the black is killed, it proves his disloyalty to Issus—the unpardonable sin. If he lives, he is held acquitted of the charge that forced the rites upon him.

“The forms vary. Sometimes we fight in groups against equal numbers, or twice our number. Sometimes singly against beasts, or some famed black warrior.”

“And if we win?” I asked. “Freedom?”

He laughed—short, sharp.

“Freedom. The only freedom is death. None who enters the domains of the First Born ever leave. If we fight well, we are permitted to fight often. If we fight poorly—” He lifted a shoulder and let it fall. “Sooner or later we die in the arena.”

“And you?” I asked. “You have fought often.”

“Very often,” he replied, and for the first time something like pleasure warmed his eyes. “It is my only pleasure. Some hundred black devils have I accounted for during nearly a year of the rites. My mother would be proud could she know how well I have maintained the traditions of my father’s prowess.”

“Your father must have been a mighty warrior,” I said. “I have known most of Barsoom’s great fighters. Doubtless I knew him. Who was he?”

“My father was—”

“Come, calots!” a guard barked. “To the slaughter with you.”

We were hustled down the steep incline to the chambers that let out upon the arena. The amphitheatre, like others on Barsoom, had been built into an excavation so that only the uppermost seats formed a low wall above ground. The arena lay far below, a pit of sand and blood.

Just beneath the lowest tier of seats ran a series of barred cages level with the arena floor. Into these we were herded. The youth—my strange familiar—was placed elsewhere, not in my cage.

Directly opposite stood the throne of Issus. The creature squatted upon it, surrounded by a hundred slave maidens glittering with jewels. Brilliant cloths of many hues covered the dais like a feast of color meant to distract from the hunger that sat at its center.

Below the throne, in rigid ranks, stood heavily armed soldiers. In front of them lounged the dignitaries—gleaming blacks weighted with precious stones, their foreheads marked with circles of gold that announced rank the way a chef’s knife announces his station: by what it can cut.

On both sides of the throne the seats were packed—women and men in elaborate harness, each attended by one to three slaves drawn from therns and from the outer world. Among the First Born there was no peasantry; even the lowest soldier was “noble” and kept slaves as casually as one keeps cups.

They did no work. The men fought as a sacred privilege and duty; the women did nothing at all. Slaves washed them, dressed them, fed them. Some had slaves who spoke for them. I saw one woman sit with eyes closed while a slave narrated the arena’s events into her ear, turning slaughter into a purchased story.

The first event was called the Tribute to Issus. It ended the year-long service of ten young women—beauties taken from courts of jeddaks and from the temples of the Holy Therns. For a year they had attended Issus. To-day they were to pay for the “honor” with their lives. Tomorrow they would grace the tables of court functionaries.

A huge black entered the arena with them. He inspected them as a buyer inspects carcasses: pressing flesh, prodding ribs, testing limbs as though judging tenderness. At last he chose one, led her before the throne, spoke words I could not hear. Issus nodded. The black raised his hands in salute, took the girl by the wrist, and dragged her through a small doorway below the throne.

“Issus will dine well to-night,” said a prisoner beside me, his tone the exhausted cynicism of a man who has been forced to learn the vocabulary of cannibals.

“What do you mean?” I asked, though the smell of the place—perfume over sweat, incense over fear—made the answer sour on my tongue already.

“That was her dinner Thabis is taking to the kitchens. Did you not note how carefully he selected the plumpest and tenderest of the lot?”

The words struck like a fist. In my mind I saw hands at a chopping block, but not hands that cook with reverence; hands that butcher to display power. The First Born had turned hunger into doctrine and called it divinity.

I growled my curses on the monster squatting opposite.

“Fume not,” my companion said. “You will see far worse if you live even a month among them.”

Then a gate near by was thrown open and three monstrous white apes sprang into the arena. The girls shrank together at the center like a clutch of doves under hawk shadow. One fell to her knees and stretched imploring hands toward Issus; the goddess leaned forward in anticipation, as though the screams were music and the rush of the beasts a drumroll.

The apes spied the huddled knot and charged with shrieks that tore through the amphitheatre. The sound hit the ear not as mere noise but as weapon—industrial in its brutality, like machines designed to grind nerve and hope into silence. The crowd’s roar rose in waves, a manufactured ecstasy, the commercialization of agony dressed as rite.

A blood-red haze swam before my eyes.

The guard lounged at my cage gate, unbarred. Why bar it, indeed? Where would any victim flee, when the world itself had been built as their pen?

One blow dropped him unconscious. I tore his long-sword from his grasp and sprang into the arena.

For an instant the amphitheatre went silent—the sudden hush that comes when a kitchen sees a knife lifted against the master. Then a wild shout rose from the cages of the doomed.

My blade cut the air with a clean, singing arc. One ape fell headless at the feet of the fainting girls. The other two whirled on me.

From the corner of my eye I saw guards rushing across the sand, and then a figure broke from a cage behind them. It was the red youth. He stood a moment with sword raised high.

“Come, men of the outer world!” he shouted. His voice carried clear over the arena, cutting through the din the way a bell cuts through market clamor. “Let us make our deaths worth while! At the back of this unknown warrior, turn this day’s Tribute into revenge that will echo through the ages—so black skins blanch at every telling of the rites of Issus! Come! The racks without your cages are filled with blades!”

He did not wait for an answer. He bounded toward me.

From every cage that held red men a thunderous response rose. Guards went down beneath howling mobs. Cages vomited forth their inmates, hot with the lust to kill. Swords were ripped from racks—blades meant to stage controlled contests now taken for uncontrolled revolt.

While the charging guards were still distant, my sword put the great apes down. They towered fifteen feet, but flesh is flesh; and the will behind my arm that day was not merely anger but refusal—refusal to let cruelty become routine, refusal to let the cries of girls be treated as background noise for a crowd’s pleasure.

I stayed with the maidens behind me. I had sprung for them, and if I died it would be in that service.

The youth raced after the guards with a speed I had never seen in any Martian. His leaps and bounds were nearly akin to my own, and the sight of him stirred in me that same untranslatable certainty: that I knew him, that some part of my life was speaking through him in a dialect I could not name aloud.

He fell upon the guards from behind with such ferocity that they turned as though a dozen men attacked them. I rushed them from the front.

Then there was only fighting—metal purring and snarling, sand turning dark, breath burning the throat. Now and again I glimpsed the youth beside me: a lightly springing figure of sinewy steel, a grim smile on his handsome face, taunting his foes as he cut them down. There were movements in him that echoed my own—an old habit of stance, a familiar timing of step—and it filled me with a yearning so sharp it was almost pain, and with a pride that had no explanation.

We fought as men do when the world has stripped them of everything but the right to choose how to die.

For half an hour it was as though all hell had broken loose. Within the arena’s walls we became an inextricable mass—howling, cursing, blood-streaked demons. Slowly, by shouted command and sheer will, I drew the prisoners into a rough formation, a circle in whose center we kept the doomed maids. Many fell on both sides, but more guards died than prisoners. Messengers ran through the audience; noble blacks unsheathed their swords and leaped down. They meant to crush us with numbers.

I saw Issus leaning far forward, her face twisted with hate and rage—and beneath it, fear. That fear was the crack in her godhood, the proof she was not a deity but an appetite wearing jewels.

It inspired what followed.

I ordered fifty prisoners to fall back and form a tighter ring around the girls.

“Remain and protect them until I return,” I commanded.

Then I turned to those holding the outer line.

“Down with Issus!” I cried. “Follow me to the throne. We will reap vengeance where vengeance is deserved.”

The youth at my side took up the cry first, his voice ringing like struck metal: “Down with Issus!”

From behind and all around rose the hoarse answer: “To the throne! To the throne!”

We moved as one, a fighting mass, over dead and dying toward the dais. The strongest warriors of the First Born poured down to stop us. We cut through them as though they were paper men. The sound in the amphitheatre became a storm—steel on steel, screams, the crowd’s roar breaking into panicked shrieks as the spectacle turned on its spectators.

“To the seats, some of you!” I shouted as we neared the barrier wall. “Ten of us can take the throne!” For I saw most of Issus’ guards had already joined the arena fight.

Prisoners broke left and right, vaulting the low wall with dripping swords and rushing into the stands. In another moment the amphitheatre was filled with the shrieks of the dying and the wounded, with triumphant shouts, with the hideous chorus of a world overturned.

Side by side the youth and I, with a dozen others, fought our way to the foot of the throne. Remaining guards, reinforced by dignitaries and nobles, closed between us and Issus. She leaned forward on her carved sorapus bench, screaming high-pitched commands, hurling curses as though words alone could restore the hierarchy.

The slave girls around her trembled, wide-eyed, not knowing whether to pray for our victory or our defeat. Some—proud daughters of great warriors, I had no doubt—snatched swords from fallen hands and struck at Issus’ guards. They fell quickly, martyrs to a hope that had only seconds to live, but in those seconds they had reclaimed their own language of action.

The men with us fought well, but soon they were gone. One by one they fell until only the youth and I remained pressing toward the bench. Never, not even beside Tars Tarkas against Warhoon, had I seen two men fight to such good purpose and unconquerable ferocity as we did there before the throne of Issus.

Man by man those who stood between us and the sorapus wood went down. Others swarmed in to fill the gap, and still we advanced—inch by inch, foot by foot.

Then a new cry rose from the stands, at first in one section, then rolling outward in waves like sound across water:

“Rise slaves! Rise slaves!”

For an instant the fighting slackened as men turned to understand this new note in the storm. It took only a heartbeat to see: everywhere the female slaves were falling upon their masters with whatever came to hand. Daggers snatched from harnesses, swords pulled from corpses, heavy ornaments swung as clubs. Those who found nothing else used fingers and teeth. It was both shuddering and glorious—a long, pent vengeance finally given voice, a chorus rising against the dominant lexicon that had named them “property” until even they nearly believed it.

“Rise slaves! Rise slaves!” echoed again, but already the sound began to thin under the answering screams. Even revolt has its acoustics: first the clean bell-tone of hope, then the ragged tearing as it is cut down.

We returned to our own struggle. Only a thin rank now stood between us and Issus. Her face had gone blue with terror; foam flecked her lips. She looked less like a goddess than like a frightened animal trapped on a table.

A black Dator swung a long-sword in a nasty cut meant to split my skull. I would have died—my own blade wedged in a breastbone at the worst moment—had not a hand from behind my adversary shot out and clutched his elbow as he struck. The blow faltered. The youth sprang to my side and ran his sword through the man before he could recover.

My sword came free as the Dator fell. I snatched the dying man’s weapon and looked, over his prostrate body, into the eyes of the one whose quick hand had saved me.

Phaidor—daughter of Matai Shang.

“Fly, my Prince!” she cried, breathless with terror and urgency. “It is useless to fight longer. All within the arena are dead. All who charged the throne are dead but you and this youth. Only among the seats are there any of your fighting-men, and they and the slave women are fast being cut down. Listen—you can scarce hear the battle-cry of the women now for nearly all are dead. For each one of you there are ten thousand blacks within the domains of the First Born. Break for the open and the sea of Korus. With your mighty sword arm you may yet win to the Golden Cliffs and the templed gardens of the Holy Therns. There tell your story to Matai Shang, my father. He will keep you, and together you may find a way to rescue me. Fly while there is yet a bare chance for flight.”

But that was not my mission. Nor, in the bitter knowledge of what these hidden heavens did to flesh and memory, could I see much to be preferred in the cruel hospitality of the Holy Therns over that of the First Born.
