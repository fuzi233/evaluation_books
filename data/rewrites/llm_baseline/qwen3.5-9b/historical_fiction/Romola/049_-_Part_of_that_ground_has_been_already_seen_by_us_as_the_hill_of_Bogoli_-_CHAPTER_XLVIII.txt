The afternoon sun was beginning to fail as Tito returned home. In her small chamber, Romola sat opposite the cabinet, her fingers moving over parchment to copy documents. Seeing the light dimming, she prepared to cease her work when her husband entered. He did not go to the hall or the garden; he came straight to her room with a singular, defined purpose. As he stepped inside, Romola noted a profound shift in his demeanor. Without removing his cap or mantle, he leaned an elbow on the cabinet and stood directly before her, gazing at her in silence.

Throughout the day, Romola had been reassured regarding the Friar's safety, yet she now felt the heavy recoil of her own earlier actions. A pang of penitence struck her for the distrust and indignation that had driven her to confront her husband publicly on a matter he deemed private. She convinced herself she had been wrong. The calculated duplicity inherent in Florentine factionalism—a trait even her godfather acknowledged—could easily explain Tito's connection with Spini without implying that Tito intended to advance the conspiracy. She sought to atone for her impulsiveness, hoping that a confession of her haste might break the two-year silence that had locked their hearts away. That silence was absolute; Tito did not know she had fled and returned, nor had they ever dared to speak of the youthful love that had once existed between them, or the violent shock that had shattered it. They treated that past like a banquet hall where death had abruptly ended the feast.

She looked up at him with the submissive gaze born of self-reproach, ready to speak, but a subtle transformation in his face halted her words. For several moments, they stared at one another in a charged quiet. Tito felt, acutely, that a crisis had arrived in his marriage. The husband's latent determination to dominate, usually buried beneath layers of blandness and pleasantry, had surged to the surface. It altered his visage, giving him the appearance of a man secretly strangling something weak yet dangerous within his grip.

"Romola," he began, his voice a cool, liquid tone that sent a shiver through her, "it is time that we should understand each other."

"That is what I most desire, Tito," she replied faintly. Her pale, sweet face, stripped of anger and filled only with the timidity of self-doubt, seemed to highlight the overwhelming dominance of his dark strength.

"You took a step this morning," Tito continued, "which you must now perceive to have been futile—one that exposes you to scrutiny and may entangle me in grave practical difficulties."

"I acknowledge that I was too hasty," Romola said, finding a fuller, firmer tone in her voice. She hoped that expressing her regret would soften his glare, allowing her to say what truly mattered. "I wish you to understand once and for all," Tito added, his voice unchanged, "that such clashes are incompatible with our station as husband and wife. Reflect on how you were led to that step, lest it happen again."

"That depends chiefly on you, Tito," Romola retorted, catching a spark of defiance. It was not exactly what she intended to say, but the distance between them left little room for careful navigation.

"You would say, I suppose," Tito answered smoothly, "that nothing in the future should provoke your unreasonable suspicions. You were frank enough last night to declare you hold no faith in me. I am not surprised by the exaggerated conclusions drawn from slight evidence, but I must warn you of the consequences of using such distortions to interfere in affairs you know nothing of. Are you paying close attention?"

"Yes," Romola flushed, resenting the cold superiority in his tone.

"Well, then, sooner or later, some stray word or incident may set your imagination racing to devise crimes against me. You might rush to the Palazzo Vecchio to alarm the Signoria and stir the entire city. Shall I tell you the outcome? Not merely the disgrace of your husband—which you anticipate with such courage—but the arrest and ruin of many leading men in Florence, including Messer Bernardo del Nero."

Tito had meditated a decisive blow, and he had delivered it. The flush drained from Romola's face until her lips were pale—a rare sight, for she was seldom afraid. Tito sensed his victory.

"You might flatter yourself," he pressed on, "that you are performing a heroic act of deliverance. You might as well attempt to pick locks with flowery language as apply such notions to the politics of Florence. The question is no longer whether you can believe in me, but whether, now that you are warned, you will dare to rush, like a blind man brandishing a torch, into intricate matters beyond your comprehension."

Romola felt her mind gripped in a vice by Tito's logic; the dangers he outlined rose before her with terrifying clarity.

"I am too rash," she admitted. "I will strive not to be rash."

"Remember," Tito insisted relentlessly, "that your act of distrust this morning could have had far more fatal consequences than the sacrifice of your husband, a fate you have learned to contemplate without flinching."

"Tito, it is not so!" Romola burst out, rising and approaching him with a desperate resolve to speak her truth. "It is false that I would willingly sacrifice you. It has been the greatest struggle of my life to remain by your side. I fled in anger two years ago, yet I returned because I was bound to you more than to anything on earth. But it is useless. You shut me out from your thoughts. You treat me as too irrational to share in your affairs. You will reveal nothing."

She appeared like a guardian angel pleading with him, bending toward him with wide, imploring eyes and laying her hand upon his arm. Yet, Romola's touch and gaze no longer stirred a single fiber of tenderness in her husband. The good-natured, tolerant Tito, capable of little hatred or impatience, accustomed to gentleness toward the world, felt himself growing strangely hard toward this wife who had once been his strongest influence. Beneath his soft disposition lay a masculine intellectual power and purpose, sharp-edged and energetic, working steadily without needing great momentum. Romola possessed her own energy, one that thwarted his, and no man of ordinary weakness endures being thwarted by his wife. Marriage, he seemed to feel, must be either a union of sympathy or a contest of conquest.

No emotion crossed Tito's face as he heard her mention her departure two years prior. Only his lips tightened slightly as he offered a faint smile.

"My Romola, when certain conditions are established, we must accept them. No amount of wishing can fill the Arno river, as your people say, nor turn a plum into an orange. I have observed that prayer holds little efficacy in such matters. You are constituted to possess strong impressions inaccessible to reason; I cannot share these impressions, and consequently, you have withdrawn your trust from me. You have changed toward me, and thus I have changed toward you. There is no use looking backward. We must simply adapt ourselves to these altered conditions."

"Tito, it would not be useless for us to speak openly," Romola said, her voice trembling with the frustration of pushing living flesh against a lifeless, immovable wall. "It was the sense of deception in you that drove me away, keeping us apart. And it is untrue that I changed first. You changed toward me the night you first donned that chain-mail armor. You had a secret from me—concerning that old man—and I saw him again yesterday."

In a tone of agonized entreaty, she pleaded, "Tito, if you would once tell me everything, whatever it may be, I would not mind the pain. Let there be no wall between us! Is it not possible that we could begin a new life?"

For the first time, a flash of emotion crossed Tito's face. He stood utterly still, the flash seeming to whiten him. Ignoring Romola's appeal, he paused briefly before speaking quietly.

"Your impetuosity regarding trifles, Romola, possesses a freezing influence that would chill the baths of Nero."

At these stinging words, Romola recoiled, drawing herself up into her customary pose of self-possession. Tito continued.

"If by 'that old man' you mean the mad Jacopo di Nola, who attempted my life and made a strange accusation against me—which I withheld from you because it would have alarmed you to no avail—he, poor wretch, has died in prison. I saw his name listed among the dead."

"I know nothing of his accusation," Romola stated. "But I know he is the man I saw with a rope around his neck in the Duomo—the man whose portrait Piero di Cosimo painted, clutching your arm just as he saw him seize it the day the French entered, the day you first wore the armor."

"And where is he now, pray?" Tito asked, remaining pale but controlling himself.

"He was lying lifeless in the street from starvation," Romola replied. "I revived him with bread and wine. I brought him to our door, but he refused entry. I gave him some money, and he departed without telling me anything. However, he discovered that I was your wife. Who is he?"
