On a picturesque common near Tunbridge Wells—where the air is so pure it makes one sneeze, and the scenery so charming it nearly ruins your digestion—Lady Clavering had secured a villa that looked like a fairy-tale cottage if you squinted just right. After her latest marital skirmish (a mere scratch compared to the war of attrition waged during the London season), she fled there with all the grace of a disgraced queen escaping a mob. Miss Amory, naturally, tagged along like a well-bred shadow. Master Clavering, the family’s only male heir, arrived for the holidays, bringing with him the usual chaos of schoolboy energy—though Blanche’s primary occupation was not to play with him, but to quarrel with him as though they were rivals in a bad farce. But this was merely domestic theatre, and the young gentleman, being a boy of thirteen and therefore constitutionally opposed to home life, preferred cricket, horses, and the general mayhem of Tunbridge Wells society.

The Begum’s house, a sort of gilded pigsty of hospitality, was perpetually overrun by a pack of thirteen-year-old gentlemen who ate tarts like they were sacred relics, drank champagne like it was water, raced on the lawn like their lives depended on it, smoked cigars indoors like they were in a Turkish bath, and generally made such a mess that poor Mrs. Smirke could barely breathe without choking on secondhand pipe smoke and juvenile idiocy. Blanche, however, found this company utterly unbearable. She did not like young gentlemen of thirteen—unless they were particularly good at singing or had nice hair.

For Blanche, change was the spice of life—any change, even poverty, a cottage, bread and cheese, or a dungeon with bread and water. A night in a dungeon might have been delightful, provided there was a moonbeam and a romantic prisoner. So the move to Tunbridge Wells was not unwelcome; indeed, it felt like liberation from a world of dullness and duty.

She wandered through the woods, sketching trees and farmhouses with the solemnity of an artist preparing for a posthumous exhibition. She read French novels—mostly ones about unrequited love and tragic misunderstandings—and drove into Tunbridge Wells whenever something interesting appeared: a play, a ball, a conjurer, or a musician. She slept a great deal—more than any woman should, especially one who claimed to be “so full of spirit.” She quarreled with her mother and brother in the mornings, then attended the village school, where she first charmed the girls and tormented the mistress, then scolded the girls and laughed at the teacher. She was constant at church, of course—because everyone must be seen somewhere, and church was less likely to involve drunkenness than the local tavern.

The church itself was a tiny Anglo-Norman bijou, built “the day before yesterday” according to local legend, decorated with painted windows, carved saints’ heads, gilt scripture texts, and open pews that looked like they’d been designed by someone who’d never heard of privacy. Blanche immediately took up needlework and began crafting a high-church altar cover with the precision of a woman planning a royal coronation. She became a saint in the eyes of Mr. Smirke, the clergyman, whom she coaxed, wheedled, and fondled with such artful charm that poor Mrs. Smirke—wife of our old friend Smirke, Pen’s former tutor and Helen’s would-be suitor—was reduced to near madness with jealousy.

Smirke, once a man of modest piety and even more modest neckties, had undergone a spiritual metamorphosis after his wife’s death. He cut off his coat collar, let his hair grow long enough to qualify as a wig, abandoned his forehead curl (a relic of youth), and went tieless—like a man who’d lost faith in fashion and civilization alike. He fasted on Fridays, read the Roman Hours, and announced he was ready to hear confessions in the vestry. The most harmless creature alive, he was denounced as a Jesuit spy by Muffin of the Dissenting chapel and Mr. Simeon Knight of the old church. “Lord! Lord!” people whispered. “What would his mother have said if she’d seen a table called an altar? Candlesticks? Letters signed ‘On the Feast of Saint So-and-so’?” All these things Smirke did—his faithful wife following him like a loyal shadow.

When Blanche held a two-hour conference with him in the vestry, Belinda paced outside on the grass, where only two little gravestones stood. She wished there were a third—one for herself. “Only,” she thought, “he’d probably offer it to *her*—that creature who has bewitched him in less time than it takes to say ‘Grace.’ No, I’ll retire. I’ll go into a convent. I’ll profess and leave him.”

Such were the thoughts of Smirke’s wife and neighbors—some believing him in direct correspondence with the Pope, others mourning the errors of his ways. Yet Smirke meant no harm. The post-office never delivered letters from Rome. He truly believed Blanche to be pious, gifted, and angelic—at least until she lost her temper, rapped a child’s knuckles, and ruined the schoolmistress’s dignity. Then he began to doubt. And then he grew tired.

Blanche had a way of inspiring admiration and then boredom in men. She’d fling out all her graces at once—jewels, smiles, coquettish glances, sweet words—then drop them like hot potatoes when she got bored. And men, equally easily bored, dropped her too. It was a happy night for Belinda when Blanche left. Smirke, blushing and sighing, confessed he’d been deceived. “I thought her endowed with precious gifts,” he said. “Now I fear they’re mere tinsel. I thought she was serious about religion—now I suspect she treats it like a game. She lost her temper with the schoolmistress, and beat Polly Rucker’s knuckles cruelly.”

Belinda flew into his arms. No more talk of graves or veils. He tenderly kissed her forehead. “There is none like thee, my Belinda,” he declared, gazing upward with dramatic intensity, “precious among women!”

As for Blanche, she forgot them both the moment she turned the corner. But when Arthur Pendennis came down to spend a few days at Tunbridge Wells, the stage of indifference had not yet set in.

Smirke still believed Blanche to be an angel. He sat listening to her sing in summer evenings, mouth agape, tea forgotten, butter uneaten. He’d heard opera once—on the day he’d accompanied Helen and her son to a play in Chatteris—and while he admitted it was impressive, he now swore nothing compared to Blanche’s music. She was gifted. She had a soul. She had talents. She had, outwardly, the most heavenly disposition. He spoke of her thus to Arthur, who arrived with a cordial greeting and a heart full of affection for anyone who loved his mother.

Arthur, always amused by eccentricity, found Smirke endlessly entertaining—his airs, his simplicity, his long hair, his blank stock, his genuine kindness. And Smirke’s praise of Blanche surprised and pleased him. He began to regard her with new respect.

Truthfully, Blanche was delighted to see Arthur. Like any sensible woman in the country, she welcomed a man from the city who brought news, stories, and the sound of London slang—“the darling jargon,” as she called it, “so dear and indispensable to us Londoners, so incomprehensible to those outside the world.” The first evening Arthur arrived, he kept her laughing for hours after dinner. She sang with renewed vigor. She didn’t scold her mother; instead, she kissed her, much to the Begum’s astonishment. At bedtime, she said, “Deja!” with the prettiest expression of regret, squeezed Arthur’s hand warmly, and he returned the pressure with equal warmth.

Arthur, being of a nature easily dazzled by moderately bright eyes, thought, “She is very much improved. Very much. I suppose the Begum won’t mind my smoking with the window open. She’s a jolly good old woman, and Blanche is immensely improved. I liked her manner with her mother tonight. I liked her laughing way with that stupid young cub of a boy—though they oughtn’t to let him get drunk. She sang those little verses beautifully—they were devilish pretty verses, though I say it who shouldn’t say it.” And he hummed a tune she’d set to some of his own lines.

“Ah! What a fine night!” he mused. “How jolly a cigar is at night! How pretty that little Saxon church looks in the moonlight! I wonder what old Warrington’s doing? Yes, she’s a dayvlish nice little thing, as my uncle says.”

“Oh, heavenly!” came a voice from a clematis-covered casement nearby—the voice of the author of *Mes Larmes*. Arthur burst into laughter.

“Don’t tell about my smoking,” he said, leaning out of his window.

“Oh! Go on! I adore it,” the lady of *Mes Larmes* replied. “Heavenly night! Heavenly, heavenly moon! But I must shut my window—not to talk to you on account of *les moeurs*. How droll they are, *les moeurs*! Adieu.”

And Arthur began to sing the Goodnight to Don Basilio.

The next day, they walked together through the fields, laughing and chattering—the gayest pair of friends imaginable. They talked about their youth, and Blanche grew prettily sentimental. They discussed Laura, dearest Laura—Blanche had loved her like a sister. Was she happy with Lady Rockminster? Wouldn’t she come and stay? Oh, what walks they’d take! What songs they’d sing—the old, old songs! Laura’s voice was splendid. Did Arthur—she must call him Arthur—remember the songs they sang in the happy old days, now that he was such a great man and had such a *succès*?

The day after, they took a ramble through the woods to Penshurst, admired the park and hall, and then came the conversation with the curate—which made Arthur think more and more.

“Is she all this perfection?” he asked himself. “Has she become serious and religious? Does she tend schools and visit the poor? Is she kind to her mother and brother? Yes, I’m sure of that—I’ve seen her.”

Walking with his old tutor over the little parish, visiting the school, Arthur found Blanche seated among the children, instructing them with patient dignity. He imagined her as simple, kind, unspoiled—truly innocent of the world’s corruption.

“And do you really like the country?” he asked her.

“I should like never to see that odious city again. O Arthur—that is, Mr.—well, Arthur, then—one’s good thoughts grow up in these sweet woods and calm solitudes, like those flowers which won’t bloom in London, you know. The gardener comes and changes our balconies once a week. I don’t think I shall bear to look London in the face again—its odious, smoky, brazen face! But, heigho!”

“Why that sigh, Blanche?”

“Never mind why.”

“Yes, I do mind why. Tell me, tell me everything.”

“I wish you hadn’t come down,” she said—and a second edition of *Mes Soupirs* burst forth.

“You don’t want me, Blanche?”

“I don’t want you to go away. I don’t think this house will be very happy without you, and that’s why I wish you’d never come.”

*Mes Soupirs* were laid aside. *Mes Larmes* began.

Ah! What answer is given to those in the eyes of a young woman? What method is used to dry them? What happened?

O ringdoves and roses, O dews and wildflowers, O waving greenwoods and balmy airs of summer! Here were two battered London rakes, taking themselves in for a moment, and fancying they were in love with each other—like Phillis and Corydon!

When one thinks of country houses and country walks, one wonders that any man is left unmarried.
