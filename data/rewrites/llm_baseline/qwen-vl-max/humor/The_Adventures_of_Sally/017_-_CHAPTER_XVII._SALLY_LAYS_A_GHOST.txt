The blood crept back into Sally’s cheeks like a shy visitor returning from a long trip abroad, and her heart—just moments before galloping around her chest like a startled pony—settled down to its usual business of beating, one beat at a time, as if nothing had happened. Which was odd, because something *had* happened: Gerald Foster had just walked through the door, looking like a man who’d lost both his dignity and his lunch in a single night.

And yet, here she stood, perfectly calm. Not a tremor. Not a sigh. Not even a flicker of the panic she’d imagined would consume her when this moment finally arrived. She’d spent months rehearsing this scene in her mind—Gerald appearing, dramatic music swelling, tears falling, hearts breaking—but now that it was real, it felt… anticlimactic. Like watching someone else’s movie while you’re supposed to be in it.

“Hullo, Sally!” he said, voice thick as molasses and smile wider than a barn door. He leaned against the frame, swaying slightly, shirt-sleeves rolled up, collar gone AWOL, face pale and puffy, smelling faintly of regret and cheap gin. Around him hovered an aura so disreputable it could’ve been bottled and sold as “Despair Essence.”

Sally didn’t say a word. She simply looked at him. Coolly. Quietly. As if he were a stray dog with bad breath and questionable intentions. Her nerves, exhausted by the previous night’s drama, had entered what psychologists might call “the Indian summer of over-tiredness”—a state where you’re too drained to feel anything, but also strangely alert, like a robot running on fumes.

“Hullo again,” Gerald repeated, as though repeating himself would make it more convincing.

“What do you want?” Sally asked, flatly.

“Hear your voice,” he slurred. “See door open. Thought I’d come in.”

“What do you want?” she repeated.

His smile cracked. A single tear slipped down his cheek. The alcohol had reached its final stage: maudlin melodrama. “Sally… S-Sally… I’m very miserable.” He struggled with the syllables like they were foreign languages. “I see what a fool I’ve been.”

Sally’s mind flashed back—*this* was exactly how Mr. Reginald Cracknell had behaved when he’d tried to win her over with a combination of weeping and whiskey. It was almost funny. Almost.

“I think you should go to bed, Gerald,” she said, voice steady as a stone.

He blinked. “What’s the use? Can’t sleep. No good. Couldn’t sleep. Sally, you don’t know how worried I am.”

She held up a hand. Not to stop him—though she really wanted to—but to prevent the inevitable apology about how he’d wrecked her life, which she wasn’t remotely interested in hearing. But apparently, that wasn’t what was weighing on his soul.

“I was a fool ever to try writing plays,” he continued, voice cracking like old leather. “Got a winner first time, but can’t repeat. It’s no good. Ought to have stuck to newspaper work. I’m good at that. Shall have to go back to it. Had another frost tonight. No good trying any more. Shall have to go back to the old grind, damn it.”

He sobbed softly, a picture of pathos. “Very miserable,” he murmured.

He took a step forward, wobbled, and retreated to the door like a man clinging to the last raft in a stormy sea.

For a split second, Sally’s composure cracked. Contempt flared—sharp, sudden, and hot. Then it passed. She was back in her armor of indifference.

“Go to bed, Gerald,” she said. “You’ll feel better in the morning.”

Gerald winced. “May not be alive in the morning,” he declared solemnly. “Good mind to end it all. End it all!” He raised a hand dramatically—then dropped it, clutching the door like it was his only friend.

Sally sighed. “Oh, go to bed,” she snapped. “Honestly.”

Her icy detachment began to thaw, replaced by a growing resentment—not just at Gerald for making a spectacle of himself, but at herself for ever thinking this man was anything other than a walking cliché. How had she once allowed him to dominate her thoughts, her dreams, her entire emotional landscape? It humiliated her. And humiliation bred hardness. She felt cold, pitiless, almost amused.

Then came the sound.

A muffled crash.

Not loud. Not terrifying. Just… *wrong*. Like something heavy hitting the floor in a room full of fragile things.

Sally froze. Then, ears pricked like a fox, she moved toward the door.

From across the passage, a perfect fusillade of crashes erupted. One after another, each louder than the last, echoing through the stillness of the night. It sounded like a china shop being dismantled by a disgruntled badger.

And suddenly, the detached Sally vanished. Panic surged—brief, sharp, real.

Gerald had mentioned wanting to end it all earlier. What if he’d decided to act?

She ran across the hall and pounded on the door.

---

A few seconds later, the door creaked open.

Gerald stood there, weak smile back in place, eyes half-lidded, hair tousled like a bird’s nest after a hurricane.

“Hullo, Sally!”

She stared. He was unharmed. Unscathed. Still wearing the same clothes. Still reeking of sorrow and spirits.

Her fear evaporated. Replaced by annoyance. And a fresh wave of irritation—because he’d scared her for no reason.

“What was *that* noise?” she demanded.

“Noise?” he said, blinking slowly, as if the concept was new to him.

“Yes, noise!” she snapped.

“I’ve been cleaning house,” he announced, voice slow and serious, like a man delivering a eulogy to a dead toaster.

Sally pushed past him.

Inside, she found a scene straight out of a disaster movie. The apartment was a wasteland. Every surface was littered with shattered glass, broken vases, splintered frames, and fragments of porcelain that looked like they’d been chewed by dinosaurs. A niblick—golf club—lay in the corner, as if casually tossed aside after a particularly violent round of destruction.

The walls were stripped bare. Pictures had been torn off or slashed. Lampshades lay crushed underfoot. Plates were stacked like tiny tombstones against the wall. It was chaos. Utter, glorious chaos.

Gerald had collapsed onto an occasional table—now reduced to kindling—and was weeping again. “Wasn’t going to stand for it anymore,” he muttered, voice thick with self-pity. “A fellow’s wife goes and lets him down… ruins his show by going off and playing in another show… why shouldn’t I smash her things? Why should I stand for that?”

Sally gasped. “Well! You’ve certainly made a good job of it!”

Just then, the table gave way with a sharp crack. Gerald tumbled into a pile of watercolors, and Sally burst out laughing.

It wasn’t cruel laughter. It was relief. Relief that the drama was over. Relief that the man she’d once adored was now nothing more than a sad joke. She laughed until her sides hurt, imagining the absurdity of it all—the man who’d once claimed to be a genius, now reduced to smashing things with a golf club and crying about his career.

Gerald picked himself up, wiping paint from his sleeve. “No sympathy,” he said, sternly.

“I can’t help it,” Sally choked out. “It’s too funny.”

“Not funny,” he corrected, brain already clouding again. “What did you do it for?”

He paused. Then, with sudden fire: “Wasn’t going to stand for it anymore! A fellow’s wife goes and lets him down…”

“Yeah, well,” Sally interrupted, “you haven’t. So there’s no need to discuss it. You seem to have acted in a thoroughly manly and independent way.”

“That’s it,” Gerald said, wagging a finger. “Manly independent. Don’t care what she says. Don’t care if she never comes back.”

Sally had heard enough. She got up. “And now,” she said firmly, “I’m going to tidy up.”

“No!” Gerald insisted, shaking his head. “Leave it. Let her find it. Leave it as it is.”

“Don’t be silly,” she said. “All this has to be cleaned up. I’ll do it. You go sit in my apartment. I’ll tell you when you can come back.”

“No!” he said again.

Sally stamped her foot. “Do as I tell you!”

He hesitated. Then shuffled off, muttering something about “not needing to be bossed around,” and disappeared into her room.

She exhaled. Relief washed over her. Now she could clean up the mess—both literal and metaphorical.

She grabbed a broom from the kitchen and set to work. Methodically. Efficiently. In half an hour, the floor was clear, the wreckage stacked neatly against the walls. She opened the window, letting in the crisp dawn air.

Outside, the city stirred. A cold breeze whispered. The sky turned a soft blue. The night was gone.

And suddenly, she felt it—a wave of exhaustion so deep it knocked her off her feet. She stumbled to a chair and fell asleep before her head hit the cushion.

---

She woke to sunlight streaming through the window and the city waking up around her. Horns honked. Feet clattered. Streetcars clanked. She stretched, groaned, and went to the bathroom. Water on her face. Fresh air. A headache throbbed behind her eyes, but it was fading.

She crossed the hall.

Gerald was sprawled in a chair by the window, legs outstretched, mouth open, snoring like a bear in hibernation. Ugly. Unimpressive. A sight she could barely stomach.

But beneath the disgust, something else stirred—relief. A sense of closure. This chapter was over. Forever. She could look at him now and feel only pity and contempt. The glamour was gone. The illusion shattered.

She shook him gently.

He sat up, blinking wildly. “Oh, my God!” he moaned, pressing his hands to his temples. “I’ve got a headache!”

“You earned it,” she thought—but didn’t say. Instead: “You’d better go wash.”

“Yeah,” he mumbled, staggering toward the bathroom.

She sat in his chair. Everything felt dreamlike. The splashing in the bathroom. The quiet street. The sun. It all seemed unreal.

Then the bell rang.

She opened the door.

Bruce Carmyle stood there, smiling like a man who’d just won a lottery he didn’t know existed. He wore a light motor coat, and his face—usually stern—was softened by effortful cheerfulness.

“Well, here I am!” he said brightly. “Are you ready?”

He’d come to make amends. Overnight, he’d realized his abrupt exit from the Flower Garden had been less than gentlemanly. He’d let his nerves get the better of him. Now, he was determined to be charming. To be *perfect*.

Sally stared blankly. She’d completely forgotten he was coming.

Before either of them could speak, the bathroom door opened.

Gerald stepped out, looking like a ghost who’d been dragged through a mud puddle.

Bruce Carmyle froze.

Gerald froze.

They stared at each other.

Gerald looked like death warmed over—pale, unshaven, eyes red, hair wild. Bruce Carmyle saw not a man, but a symbol. A warning. Proof that Uncle Donald had been right all along.

At his elbow, the phantom of Uncle Donald puffed proudly. “I told you so!”

Bruce Carmyle’s eyes narrowed. A cold fury lit within him. He pointed at Gerald.

“Get out,” he said.

Gerald tried to stand tall. Tried to assert his dignity. But a fresh wave of pain shot through his skull, and he collapsed back into a defensive crouch.

“Get out,” Bruce repeated.

Gerald shuffled toward the door.

Bruce watched him go, hands clenched. For a moment, the man inside him—rarely seen—almost rose to violence. Then dignity whispered: *Don’t be ridiculous.*

Gerald was gone.

Bruce turned to Sally.

“So…” he said.

Sally met his gaze. Steadily. Calmly. As if she were reading a menu.

“This,” Bruce said, voice cold, “is very amusing.”

He waited. She said nothing.

“I might have expected it,” he added, bitter laugh escaping.

Sally forced herself awake. “Would you like me to explain?”

“There can be no explanation,” Bruce said.

“Very well,” she said.

Pause.

“Good-bye,” Bruce said.

“Good-bye,” she replied.

He walked to the door. Stopped. Looked back.

Sally stood by the window, sunlight catching her hair.

For one fleeting second, something in him stirred—something warm, something human. He hesitated.

Then he remembered who he was. Who she was. What she’d become.

The door slammed shut.

Outside, he climbed into his car. Glanced up. She was gone.

Back inside, Sally was on the phone.

“Ginger Kemp?” she said.

“Who?” came the sleepy voice.

“It’s Sally.”

There was a pause. Then, a burst of joyous, riotous energy. “SALLY! YOU’RE ALIVE! I KNEW IT!”

Five minutes later, Ginger was in the bath, singing off-key, fully convinced that life had just taken a turn for the spectacular.
