“My Romola,” Tito said on the second morning after he had stepped up to speak to the crowds gathered in the Piazza del Duomo, “I am expecting very grand visitors today. The Milanese Count will call again, alongside the Seneschal de Beaucaire, that great favourite of the French king Charles VIII, whom they name the Cristianissimo. I know you have no taste for going through all the smiling, bowing ceremonies with these rustling, pompous magnates who will likely never set foot in Florence again; and since they are sure to demand a tour of our collection of antiquities and the library, it would be far more pleasant for you to set aside your work for the day and pay a visit to your cousin Brigida.”

Romola caught the quiet, unspoken wish behind his suggestion at once, and agreed without hesitation. But a few minutes later, when she returned wrapped in her hooded mantle ready to go, she exclaimed, “Oh, what a long, relieved breath Florence will draw the moment the city gates are flung wide, and the very last French soldier marches out! Even you are growing tired of it all, for all your endless patience, my Tito—confess it. Ah, your head is burning hot.”

He was leaning over his desk, pen flying across a sheet of parchment, and she rested her hand lightly on his head, meaning to give him the soft parting caress she had offered him a hundred times before. In all their months of marriage, he had always responded to that small touch by lifting his head, leaning back into her space, and looking up to meet her eyes. But now he felt as incapable of raising his head as if her hand had been heavy as a leaden cowl pulled down over his face. He kept writing, speaking in a deliberately light, careless tone. “The French are as eager to leave Florence as wasps are to abandon a ripe pear the moment they have drained every drop of its juice.”

Romola, keenly attuned to every small shift in his behaviour, felt the absence of their familiar, easy ritual sharp as a pinprick. She pulled her hand away. “I am going, Tito.”
“Farewell, my sweet one. I must wait here to receive our guests. Take Maso as your escort, to keep you safe on the streets.”
Still he did not lift his eyes from his page. Romola walked out of the house without saying another word.

The smallest, unremarkable moments can mark quiet turning points in a marriage, and that morning, for the first time, Romola admitted fully to herself not just that Tito had changed, but that he had changed *towards her*. For a fleeting second she wondered if the fault lay in some failing of her own—if she had been too cold, too stubborn, too wrapped up in her father’s old books to notice what he needed. But then she thought of the half-hidden suit of armour she had stumbled on in their store rooms, the strange, veiled painting he refused to explain, and she knew there was some secret external event twisting him away from her, a riddle she had no way of solving.

The moment Tito was certain Romola was well out of the house, he dropped his pen at once and lifted his head, breathing out in relief that he no longer had to face anything but stacks of parchment and fragments of broken antique marble. He felt faintly angry with himself that he had not been able to school his features enough to meet her gaze and act as normal. He had wanted, if anything, to be warmer and more affectionate than usual—he just could not shake the involuntary, deep-rooted recoil from her that had seized him the second she laid her hand on his head. That shrinking was tied directly to the very part of him that most wanted to preserve her love: for he was about to take a step he knew would rouse her deepest, fiercest indignation, and he dreaded every unpleasant, angry moment he would have to weather before he could possibly win her forgiveness. Tito had never been the sort of man who revelled in defiance or impudence. Every part of his nature leaned away from conflict, away from harsh words, away from the sight of the tenderness he cherished in Romola curdling into anger. He had talked himself out of all the old sentimental scruples he had once held, reasoning that devotion to Bardo’s dying wish to keep the library intact was nothing but foolish, impractical sentiment that deprived both him and Romola of real, tangible comfort. But being free of moral scruples did not make him free of the pain of discomfort. Unscrupulousness can save a man from many burdens, but it cannot cure a toothache, or soothe a bruised vanity, or chase away the sharp fear of loneliness—none of which have any remedy but a sound body, and a just, loving soul. At that moment, Tito felt acutely that no amount of smooth words or charming persuasion could cushion him against the shock of the collision coming with Romola. He felt like a timid hare, forced to make a desperate leap over a chasm because the fangs and claws of a hunter were snapping at its heels behind it.

That quiet, long-held thought that clinging to Bardo’s library was foolish folly would never have pushed him to action if not for the events of the past week, which had brought both a sharp new urgency to make money, and a once-in-a-lifetime opportunity to sell his collection to wealthy visiting French and Milanese nobles. It was only when he had spotted Baldassarre in the streets a few days prior, looking far more lucid and self-possessed than the raving beggar he had seemed before, that he had fully resolved to use his legal right as Bardo’s son-in-law to sell the entire library before this rare chance slipped through his fingers. He could not rule out that he might have to flee Florence at any moment, and he had no wish to run away a destitute wanderer. He had grown used to ease, to respect, to pleasant comforts, and he intended to carry all the resources he could to keep that ease wherever he went. More than anything, he wanted to take Romola with him, and if at all possible, to carry no stain of infamy that would ruin his social standing. He had no desire to sneak away under a cloud of dishonour, even with a bag of jewels, to live the rest of his life as a nameless, rootless adventurer. He could not bear to lose the goodwill of the Florentine citizens who now greeted him with respect, and far less could he bear to lose Romola. She had been the great love of his youth, he still loved her now, and she was part of the gentle, comfortable furniture of his life that he shrank from tearing himself away from. The quiet sense of power most husbands feel over their wives, the boldness that lets them risk betrayals no lover would ever dare attempt, was not enough to still his unease. That was the heavy leaden weight that had settled on him that morning, and made him incapable of lifting his head to meet her pure, clear gaze, a gaze that shone too bright a light on the painful struggle he knew was coming.

There was no way around it: if they had to flee Florence, they would need gold. Tito could not imagine arranging his life to suit himself without a large sum of money in hand, and that single, endless desire to build a perfectly comfortable, trouble-free life for himself was the root of every wrong he had done. He would have eagerly made any sacrifice that did not come with pain or discomfort.

The grand, rustling dignitaries came and went, the bargains were fully struck, and by the time Romola returned home all the deals were signed. No grave words were exchanged that night. Tito was unusually gay and chatty, pouring out a flood of stories and vivid descriptions of everything he had seen during the French court’s visit, as he had not done in months. Romola thought she could see the strain of effort underpinning that forced liveliness, and assumed it was his way of making amends for the coldness he had shown her that morning. She accepted it as a quiet act of penitence, her heart aching a little at that small new sign of the growing distance between them—that there was now a small offence hanging between them that neither of them dared name.

The next day, Tito stayed out of the house until long after dark. It was a day that stood out sharply in Romola’s memory: Piero di Cosimo, spurred to finish the work faster by the fear that he had caused her pain the week before, had sent home the finished portrait of her father Bardo. She had propped it up against the back of his old, worn favourite chair, and had been staring at it for a long time as the light faded, when the door opened behind her and Bernardo del Nero stepped inside.
“It is you, godfather! I only wish you had come a little sooner, it is growing quite dark in here,” Romola said, hurrying to meet him.
“I only popped in to bring you good news, for I knew Tito had not come home yet,” Bernardo said. “The French king marches out tomorrow, and not a moment too soon. There was another scuffle between our citizens and his soldiers this very morning. Now there is every chance the city will return to order, and trade will start moving again.”
“That is such happy news,” Romola said. “But it is very sudden, is it not? Tito told me only yesterday that there was little chance of the king leaving anytime soon.”
“He got a good loud barking-to, that is why,” Bernardo said, smiling. “His own generals shouted at him quite fiercely, and at last our Signoria sent the city’s fiercest mastiff: Fra Girolamo. The Cristianissimo got thoroughly frightened by that thunder of preaching, and gave the order to depart at once. I fear we will have plenty of infighting among ourselves once he is gone, but every party in the city is agreed on being glad to no longer have Florence stifled under a swarm of foreign soldiers. The Frate’s barking has done some good at last. Ah, what is this?” he added, as Romola slipped her hand through his arm and led him over to stand in front of the portrait. “Let me have a look.”
He began to unwind his long woollen scarf while Romola pulled a chair out for him.
“Do you not want your spectacles, godfather?” Romola asked, anxious that he should see every detail of the portrait exactly as she saw it.
“No, child, no,” Bernardo said, uncovering his grey head as he sat down, straight and firm in his seat. “For seeing at this distance, my old eyes are likely better than your young ones. Old men’s eyes are like old men’s memories: they are strongest for things that happened a very long time ago.”
“It is better than having no portrait of him at all,” Romola said apologetically, after Bernardo had sat silent for several minutes. “It does not look quite like him now as the image I carry in my head, but that memory might fade with the passing of years.” She rested her arm on the old man’s shoulder as she spoke, drawn close to him by their shared love for the dead man they both missed.
“I do not know,” Bernardo said. “I almost think I see young Bardo, all fiery and bright-eyed, in that face more than I see the old blind man we knew in his last years. Your father had a great deal of fire in his gaze when he was young. I never could understand it: a man with that much passionate, impatient spirit, far more fiery than my own, could spend his whole life hunched over old books, living in the company of shadows. Still, he put his whole heart into that library of his.” Bernardo gave a small, faint shrug as he said the last words, but Romola could hear the warm affection in his tone that matched her own.
“And he was disappointed right to the very end,” Romola said, before she could stop herself. Afraid her words might sound like an implied accusation against Tito, she hurried on. “If only we could see his longest, dearest wish fulfilled exactly as he wanted it!”
“Well, there is every chance we will,” Bernardo said kindly, rising to his feet and settling his cap back on his head. “The times are cloudy right now, but fish are always caught by waiting. Who knows? When the wheel of fortune has turned enough times, I may yet be elected Gonfaloniere, the highest magistrate of Florence, before I die, and no foreign buyer could ever lay a finger on those books. I have made sure of it.” He glanced around the walls of the library as he spoke, then turned back to her and patted her cheek gently. “And you need never fear me dying and leaving any trouble behind, my ghost will not come to claim anything. I have written all that out clearly in my will.”
Romola seized the hand resting against her cheek, and pressed it to her lips in silence.
“Have you been scolding your husband for being away from home so much lately? I see him everywhere I go but here,” Bernardo said, eager to change the subject to something lighter.
Romola felt a hot flush creep up her neck and across her face. “He is very much needed by the city, you know. He speaks so well in the councils, and I am glad everyone is finally recognising how valuable he is.”
“So you are content then, Madonna Orgogliosa—my proud girl?” Bernardo said, smiling as he stepped toward the door.
“Assuredly.”
Poor Romola. There was one thing that would have made the sharp pain of disappointment in her husband even harder to bear, and that was having anyone else guess that he had given her cause to be disappointed. It might seem like a foolish woman’s weakness, but it is a weakness that sits very close to a woman’s greatest nobleness. The wife who willingly flings wide the veil that shrouds her married life has profaned what should be a quiet sanctuary, and turned it into just another vulgar, public place for strangers to gawk at.
