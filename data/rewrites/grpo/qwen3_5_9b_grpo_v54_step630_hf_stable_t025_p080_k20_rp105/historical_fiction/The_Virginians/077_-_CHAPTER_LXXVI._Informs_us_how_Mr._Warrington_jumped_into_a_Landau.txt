Once the initial shock of our reunion had settled, the little maid, Hetty, launched immediately into her tirade.

"So you have finally arrived to inquire after Theo, and you feel remorse that your neglect has rendered her so ill? For six weeks she has languished, and you have uttered not a single word regarding her! How very kind of you, Mr. George, I am certain!"

"Kind!" Mr. Warrington gasped, the word tearing from his throat.

"I suppose you deem it kind to attend her day and night for a year, only to vanish without a whisper?"

"My dear child, you recall my pledge to your father?" I replied.

"Pledge!" Miss Hetty scoffed, rolling her eyes. "A magnificent pledge, indeed, to render my darling sick, and then, on a sudden, one fine morning, to bid her 'Good-bye, Theo,' and depart forever. Gentlemen make such vows, I suppose, because they intend to honor them. I would not toy with a poor child's heart and abandon her afterward if I were a man. What has she ever done to you but be foolish and too fond of you? Pray, sir, by what right do you seize her from all of us, and then desert her because an elderly woman in America disapproves of her? She was content with us before your arrival. She adored her sister—there was never such a devoted sibling—until she saw you. And now, because your mother believes a young gentleman might fare better elsewhere, you must leave her, forsooth!"

"Heavens, child!" I cried, exasperated by her misguided logic. "Was it I who retreated? Am I not the one forbidden your household? Did not your father demand, upon my honor, that I cease seeing her?"

"Honor!" Hetty exclaimed. "You are the men who claim superiority, and we are the ones meant to revere you! I declare, George Warrington, you ought to return to your schoolroom in Virginia, have your black nurse tuck you in, and beg permission from your mother to step outside. Oh, George! I little imagined my sister was surrendering her heart to a man lacking the spirit to stand by her; yet, at the first obstacle, he fled! When Doctor Heberden mentioned attending to you, I resolved to visit, and you do appear quite ill, which brings me relief. I assume it is your mother you fear. But I shall not inform Theo of your sickness. She cannot cease caring for you. She cannot abandon her solemn engagements and stride into the world the next day as if nothing occurred! That is reserved for men, our superiors in courage and wisdom; to desert an angel—yes, an angel ten thousand times too pure for you; an angel who loved me until she saw you, and was the blessing of our lives—is what you term honor? Do not tell me, sir! I despise you all! Are we to worship and wait upon you? I care not for your wit, your tragedies, or your verses; I find them often tedious. I will not spend nights copying your manuscripts nor waste hours gazing from a window to catch a glimpse of your worship walking with your hat cocked! If you are departing, and welcome, give me back my sister, I say! Return my darling of the old days, who loved every one of us until she saw you. And you leave her because your mother thinks she can secure a richer match for you! Oh, brave gentleman! Go marry the person your mother chooses, and let my dear die here deserted!"

"Great heavens, Hetty!" I cried, stunned by the ferocity of her reasoning. "Is it I who wish to leave your sister? Did I not offer to uphold my promise, and was it not your father who refused me, compelling me to vow never to seek her again? What remains to me but my word and my honor?"

"Honor, indeed! You keep your word to him, yet break it to her! Pretty honor! If I were a man, I would soon reveal my thoughts on your honor! Only I forgot—you are bound to maintain the peace and must not... Oh, George, George! Do you not see the agony I endure? I am distraught, scarcely knowing what I speak. You must not leave my darling. They do not know at home. They do not believe it, but I know her best of all, and she will perish if you abandon her. Say you will not! Have pity upon me, Mr. Warrington, and restore my dearest to me!" Thus the warm-hearted, frantic creature oscillated between scorn and supplication, from anger to tears. Was my little physician correct in describing the plight of her dear patient? Was there no other remedy than what Hetty cried out for? Had not others felt the cruel pain of amputation, endured the subsequent exhaustion and fever, lain hopeless of anything but death, and yet recovered, limping through life thereafter? Why, but that love is selfish, heedless of others' griefs and passions, or that ours was so intense and unique that we deemed no other lovers could suffer as we did; here, in this passionate young advocate for her sister, we might have witnessed an instance that a fond heart could be struck by the love malady, silently endure it, and eventually recover. What had transpired in Hetty's own case? My sister and I, in our easy triumph and confidential chatter, had often discussed this matter, and, egotists as we were, perhaps derived a secret zest and security from her less fortunate attachment. It was like sitting by the fireside while winter howled without; it was like walking beside the great river, watching a ship toss at sea. We clung to each other more tightly, and wrapped in our own happiness, viewed others' misfortunes with complacent pity. Be the truth as it may. Grant that we might have been sundered and, after a while, survived the separation—so much my skeptical old age may admit. Yet, at that time, I was eager to share my ardent little Hetty's terrors and apprehensions, willingly choosing to believe that the life dearest to me in the world would be sacrificed if separated from mine. Was I wrong? I would not say so now. I may doubt myself (or not doubt, I know), but of her, never; and Hetty found in me a quite willing sharer in her alarms and terrors. I was inclined to impart some of these feelings to our doctor; but the good gentleman shut my mouth. "Hush," he said, with a comical look of fright. "I must hear none of this. If two people who happen to know each other chance to meet and talk in my patients' room, I cannot help myself; but as for match-making and love-making, I am your humble servant! What will the General do when he returns to town? He will have me behind Montagu House as sure as I am a living doctor, and alive I wish to remain, my good sir!" And he skipped into his carriage, leaving me there to meditate. "And you and Miss Hetty must have no further meetings here, mind you," he had previously warned.

Oh, no! Of course we would have none! We are gentlemen of honor, and so forth, and our word is our bond. Besides, to have seen Hetty was not that an inestimable boon, and would we not be forever grateful? I was so refreshed by that drop of water I had consumed that I thought I could endure for a very long time. I walked away with Hetty to Soho, never once considering arranging a new meeting with her. But the little emissary was more thoughtful, and she asked me if I intended to go to the Museum to read. I replied, "Oh yes, sometimes, my dear; but I am too wretched for reading now; I cannot discern what is on the paper. I do not care about my books. Even Pocahontas is wearisome to me. I..." I might have continued far longer when, "Nonsense!" she said, stamping her little foot. "Why, I declare, George, you are more stupid than Harry!"

"How do you mean, my dear child?" I asked.

"When do you go? You depart at three o'clock. You strike across the road to Tottenham Court. You walk through the village and return by the Green Lane that leads back toward the new hospital. You know you do! If you walk for a week there, it can do you no harm. Good morning, sir! Please do not follow me any farther." And she dropped a curtsey and walked away, her face veiled.

That Green Lane, lying to the north of the new hospital, is now built over with houses. In my time, when good old George II. was yet king, it was a shabby rural outlet of London; so dangerous that the City folk who visited their villas and junketing houses at Hampstead and the outlying villages would return in parties at night, escorted by waiters with lanterns to defend them from the footpads who prowled the town outskirts. Hampstead and Highgate churches, each crowning its hill, filled the background of the view as you turned your back to London; and for one, two, three days Mr. George Warrington had the pleasure of gazing upon this landscape, walking back in the direction of the new hospital.

Along the lane stood sundry small houses of entertainment; and I remember at one place, where they sold cakes and beer, at the sign of the Protestant Hero, a decent woman smiling at me on the third or fourth day, curtseying in her clean apron as she said, "It appears the lady does not come, sir! Your honor had best step in and take a can of my cool beer."

At length, as I returned through Tottenham Road on the 25th of May—an O day to be marked with the whitest stone!—a little way beyond Mr. Whitefield's Tabernacle, I saw a landau before me, and on the box-seat by the driver sat my young friend Charley, who waved his hat and called out, "George! George!" I ran up to the carriage, my knees knocking together so violently I thought I should fall by the wheel; and inside I saw Hetty, and by her my dearest Theo, propped with a pillow. How thin the little hand had become since last it was laid in mine! The cheeks were flushed and wasted, the eyes strangely bright, and the thrill of the voice when she spoke a word or two smote me with a pang, I know not of grief or joy, so intimately were they blended.

"I am taking her an airing to Hampstead," Hetty said demurely. "The doctor says the air will do her good."

"I have been ill, but I am better now, George," Theo said. There came a great burst of music from the people in the chapel hard by as she spoke. I held her hand in mine. Her eyes looked into mine once more. It seemed as if we had never been parted.

I can never forget the tune of that psalm. I have heard it throughout my life. My wife has touched it on her harpsichord, and her little ones have warbled it. Now, do you understand, young people, why I love it so? Because it was the music played at our *amoris redintegratio*. Because it sang hope to me during the period of my existence the most miserable. Yes, the most miserable: for that dreary confinement of Duquesne had its tendernesses and kindly associations connected with it; and many a time in after days I have thought with fondness of the poor Biche and my tipsy jailor, and the reveille of the forest birds and the military music of my prison.

Master Charley looked down from his box-seat upon his sister and me engaged in beatific contemplation, and Hetty listening too to the music. "I think I should like to go and hear it. And that famous Mr. Whitfield, perhaps he is going to preach this very day! Come in with me, Charley—and George can drive for half an hour with dear Theo towards Hampstead and back."

Charley did not seem to possess a very strong desire for witnessing the devotional exercises of good Mr. Whitfield and his congregation, and proposed that George Warrington should take Hetty in; but Hetty was not to be denied. "I will never help you in another exercise as long as you live, sir," cries Miss Hetty, "if you don't come on,"—while the youth clambered down from his box-seat, and they entered the temple together.

Can any moralist, bearing my previous promises in mind, excuse me for jumping into the carriage and sitting down once more by my dearest Theo? Suppose I did break them? Will he blame me much? Reverend sir, you are welcome. I broke my promise; and if you would not do as much, good friend, you are welcome to your virtue. Not that I for a moment suspect my own children will ever be so bold as to think of having hearts of their own, and bestowing them according to their liking. No, my young people, you will let papa choose for you; be hungry when he tells you; be thirsty when he orders; and settle your children's marriages afterwards.

And now, of course, you are anxious to hear what took place when papa jumped into the landau by the side of poor little mamma, propped up by her pillows. "I am come to your part of the story, my dear," I said, looking over to my wife as she plied her needles.

"To what, pray?" said my lady. "You should skip all that part, and come to the grand battles, and your heroic defense of—"

"Of Fort Fiddlededee in the year 1778, when I pulled off Mr. Washington's epaulet, gouged General Gates's eye, cut off Charles Lee's head, and pasted it on again!"

"Let us hear all about the fighting," said the boys. Even the Captain condescended to own he would listen to any military details, though only from a militia officer.
