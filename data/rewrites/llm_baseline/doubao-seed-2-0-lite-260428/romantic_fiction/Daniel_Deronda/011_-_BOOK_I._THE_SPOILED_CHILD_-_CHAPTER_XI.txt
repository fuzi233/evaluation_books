Every new acquaintance, whether with a person or a thing, begins by carving a sharp, clear edge around the vast blank of what we do not yet know.

Gwendolen had braced herself to meet Mr. Grandcourt for weeks, his anticipated arrival no sudden surprise at all—until Lord Brackenshaw stepped aside to make way for the man she’d pictured a dozen different ridiculous, posturing ways, and suddenly she was face to face with him. A hot little shock flooded her cheeks, and her vexation at the flush only made it burn deeper. All her imagined portraits of him were useless, he was nothing like any of them. He stood just a hair taller than her, their eyes meeting almost exactly level: there was not a flicker of a smile on his face as he looked at her, no trace of self-consciousness, no anxious currying of favor. When he lifted his hat, she saw a broad, smooth expanse of baldness ringed only by a thin fringe of reddish-gold hair, and a hand so perfectly formed it might have been carved from polished ivory. His features, unhidden by any full beard, were unapologetically handsome, the line from his brow to his chin almost perfectly straight, his faint side-whiskers trimmed to match that same unyielding perpendicular. No living man could have been freer of grimaces, of fawning little shifts of posture—and no wide-awake, breathing man could have looked less animated. The stiff, correct Englishmen one usually met, drawing themselves rigid after a bow and assenting with a severe nod, always gave off the air of holding back some riotous energy that would burst free the second they were off parade. But Grandcourt bore no trace of that rigid restraint; his posture leaned soft, unhurried, no tension coiled under his skin. His complexion was a faded, fair translucence, like an actress scrubbed clean of stage paint, his long, narrow gray eyes holding nothing but calm, unapologetic indifference.

It is foolish to try to sum a human being up in a single glance: when we first meet someone, we only spot the letters of their alphabet, with no idea of the full story they spell out. All Gwendolen knew for certain, in those first bright minutes under the archery tent, was one sharp, unshakable thought: He is not ridiculous.

Lord Brackenshaw drifted away almost at once, and conversation began, every pause between their lines stretching out longer than the last, Grandcourt’s gaze fixed steadily on her with a faint, unreadable curiosity, no shift of expression to give away what he thought. Gwendolen only dared flick a look back at him now and then, her observation softened a little by her usual coquettish charm.

“I used to think archery was a terrible bore,” Grandcourt said first. His accent was refined, effortless, but marked by a slow, broken drawl, as if some distinguished nobleman had been laid low by a very elegant chest cold.

“Have you converted to it today?” Gwendolen teased.
A long pause followed, in which Gwendolen’s mind raced, running through every possible judgment he might be forming of her.
“Yes. Since I saw you shoot. All you usually see at these things is people missing their targets and simpering to cover it up.”
“I suppose you’re a first-rate shot with a rifle, then?” Another pause, while Gwendolen darted a rapid, assessing look over him, composing a sharp, vivid little sketch of him in her head for some imaginary audience.
“I’ve given up shooting.”
“Oh, that makes you a terribly formidable person. People who master a thing and then toss it aside make the rest of us feel utterly trivial, like we’re still trotting out last year’s out-of-date fashions. I hope you haven’t given up all follies, because I practice quite a great many of them.” She paused again, turning her own last line over in her head, wondering if she’d sounded too bold.
“What do you call a folly?”
“Oh, anything that’s enjoyable, I suppose. But I hear you haven’t given up hunting, at least.” Another lull, and Gwendolen recalled all she’d heard of his vast estates, his fortune, and decided he was the most aristocratic-looking man she had ever laid eyes on.
“One has to do something to pass the time.”
“Do you care for the turf, then? Or is that another thing you’ve left behind?” This pause found her speculating that a man with such a calm, cold, steady bearing might make a far less tiresome husband than most, unlikely to poke his nose into every little whim his wife wanted to indulge.
“I run a horse now and then, but I don’t throw myself into it the way some men do. Are you fond of horses?”
“More than anything. I never feel half so alive as when I’m out on a great gallop, flying across the fields. My mind goes completely empty, I just feel strong, and happy.” She lingered in the silence that followed, wondering if he’d approve of that admission, but stubbornly resolved not to soften her own tastes for anyone’s sake.
“Do you like danger?”
“I don’t know. When I’m on horseback I never think about it. It feels like if I broke every bone in my body I wouldn’t even notice. I’d ride straight at anything that crossed my path.” For a second she let herself daydream through a whole hunting season, two perfect hunters stabled for her to ride whenever she pleased.
“You’d probably love tiger hunting, or pig-sticking. I did a season or two of that out in the East once. All the sport here feels like child’s play after that.”
“So you *are* fond of danger, then?” Gwendolen wondered if the coldest, most unflappable men always turned out to be the most wildly adventurous, and preened a little at her own obvious cleverness for spotting that.
“One has to have something to fill the hours. And you get used to anything, after a while.”
“I think I’m very fortunate, then—everything is still new to me. I never get enough of any of it. The only thing I’m used to is being bored, and I’d like to give that up just the same way you gave up shooting.” It occurred to her then that a man of such distinguished, chilly manners might make a dreadfully dull companion—but then, most people were dull, weren’t they? She’d never seen any of the marriages around her where the husband doubled as a proper entertaining companion, anyway. And besides, she was certainly not going to accept Mr. Grandcourt, no matter how well he looked.
“Why are you bored?”
“This whole neighborhood is dreadful. There’s nothing at all to do here. That’s why I took up archery, for lack of anything better.” She thought ahead, to the years stretching before her as an unmarried woman, with no money of her own, no power to go where she pleased, and the boredom looming larger every day.
“You’ve made yourself its undisputed queen. I’d put good money on you winning the first prize.”
“I wouldn’t be so sure. I have very worthy rivals. Didn’t you see how well Miss Arrowpoint shot?” Gwendolen found herself musing, in the lull that followed, that even the most admired men often picked someone other than the woman everyone assumed they would choose, she’d read that plot a hundred times in novels.
“Miss Arrowpoint? No—wait, yes, I noticed her.”
“Shall we go over to the scoring board now? Everyone’s heading to the other end of the grounds. My uncle’s looking over at me, he probably needs me.”

Gwendolen fled the tête-à-tête with quiet, unacknowledged relief. It wasn’t that talking to him was unpleasant, exactly—but for the whole duration of their conversation, she’d been unable to shake that strange, unwonted flush in her cheeks, the sense that she was less in control of herself than she usually was. This Mr. Grandcourt, who seemed far more wrapped up in his own importance than in hers—an absurdity almost no one else dared inflict on her—must never get the idea that he mattered to her, just because all the local gossips had marked him down as a desirable match, that she would ever dance attendance on his whims. What Grandcourt thought about in all those long silences would make itself clear soon enough.

“You just missed winning the gold arrow, Gwen,” Mr. Gascoigne told her when they reached the board. “Miss Juliet Fenn is eight points ahead of you.”
“I’m very glad to hear it,” Gwendolen laughed easily. “I’d hate to make myself thoroughly disagreeable by taking every prize for myself.” No one could possibly be jealous of Juliet Fenn, a girl as perfectly middling as a midday market stall in every way except her archery skill and her staggering plainness, which outdid even her father’s, with his underhung jaw and receding brow that put one in mind of a particularly clever fish. It was a wonder, Gwendolen thought, that all these “intending bridegrooms” never stopped to look at themselves in the mirror before choosing a pretty wife, as if their own ugly features wouldn’t pop up to surprise them in their daughters no matter how lovely the mother was.

The groups of guests began to mill about more lively then, chatter bouncing from one cluster to the next. Gwendolen, now fully herself again, watched as Grandcourt was introduced to the sharp, energetic composer Klesmer by a strange stout, dark man with fat, nimble hands who seemed on familiar, easy terms with both of them, before leading the pair over to join the Arrowpoints, whose acquaintance he and Grandcourt had already made. She didn’t much care who the stranger was, but she was curious to see how Grandcourt behaved around people other than herself. Exactly the same, it turned out: he barely glanced at Miss Arrowpoint, his gaze resting mostly on Klesmer, who was talking with wild, vivid animation, stretching his long fingers out horizontally, jabbing his forefinger down at the ground, folding his arms and tossing his shaggy dark mane as he hopped from one topic to the next. Grandcourt listened, face impassive, narrow eyes half-lidded, his left forefinger tucked into his waistcoat pocket, his right hand brushing idly at his thin whisker.

I wonder which of those two men Miss Arrowpoint likes best, she thought, a faint mocking smile tugging at her lips. But she refused to gawk like she was curious, turning all her bright charm on the people around her, determined not to care if Mr. Grandcourt ever came near her again.

He came to her a few minutes later, offering to escort Mrs. Davilow to her carriage. “Shall we see each other in the ballroom later?” Gwendolen asked as he lifted his hat to take his leave. His “yes” came out with that same slow drawl, perfectly grave, no extra warmth at all.

“For once you were wrong, Gwen,” Mrs. Davilow said, as their carriage rattled toward the castle where the evening’s ball was to be held. “You were convinced he’d be absurdly ridiculous, and there’s nothing the least bit silly about him.”
“I suppose I could find something ridiculous if I tried hard enough, but I don’t feel like it today,” Gwendolen said, a little pettishly, and her mother thought better of pressing the subject.

It was custom for the ladies and gentlemen to dine apart at these archery gatherings, so both groups could relax without having to perform for each other. The gentlemen all had a stock of old archery ball jokes about how shockingly greedy the ladies were, rumors even flying that some of them had the audacity to ask for the fat off their venison, proof of how far women would sink without strict social rules to keep them in line. Every year Lord Brackenshaw, a devoted gourmet, would lean in conspiratorially and share Byron’s opinion that a woman should never be seen eating at all, as if he was revealing a brand new secret he’d just thought up himself.

In the ladies’ dining room, it was obvious Gwendolen was no universal favorite among her own sex. None of the other girls tried to strike up a close friendship with her, and they all watched what she said far more than they talked back to her freely. She didn’t care much for them, after all, and when she was left alone in their company, it felt like she was performing to a half-empty theater. Mrs. Vulcany once remarked that Miss Harleth was far too fond of gentlemen—but the truth was, Gwendolen wasn’t fond of them at all. She was only fond of their homage, and no woman in the room was offering her that. The only exception was Miss Arrowpoint, who would drift unobtrusively to stand at her side, talking to her with quiet, unforced friendliness. She knows as well as I do that all our parents are ready to claw each other apart to get us a husband, Gwendolen thought, and she’s decided not to join the fight.

“I think Miss Arrowpoint has the most perfect manners I’ve ever seen,” Mrs. Davilow said later, when they were in the dressing room with Mrs. Gascoigne and Anna, far enough away from the other ladies to talk privately.
“I wish I was like her,” Gwendolen said.
“Why? Are you getting discontented with yourself all of a sudden, Gwen?”
“No, I’m discontented with everything else. She looks like she’s perfectly happy with what she has.”
“You ought to be happy today. You clearly loved the archery, I saw how bright you were out there.”
“That’s all over now. I don’t know what’s coming next.” Gwendolen stretched, throwing her bare arms over her head with a soft little moan. It was the fashion at these events to dance in the archery dress, ditching the heavy velvet jacket, and her simple white cashmere gown edged with pale green flattered every line of her figure. A thin gold chain around her neck, and a small gold star pinned to her breast, were her only ornaments. Her soft, smooth hair was piled high in a gleaming crown, framing her brow so perfectly Sir Joshua Reynolds would have begged to paint her portrait. He would have had an easy task of it, too—he only had to capture one perfect, beautiful moment, not capture the messy, messy truth of all the change that was coming for her.
“Dancing is what’s next,” Mrs. Davilow said. “You’re bound to love that.”
“I’m only dancing the quadrilles. I told Mr. Clintock that already. I won’t waltz or polka with anyone.”
“Whatever makes you say that, out of nowhere?”
“I can’t stand having ugly people pressed so close to me.”
“Who do you mean by ugly people?”
“Oh, half the men in that room.”
“Mr. Clintock isn’t ugly, for example.” Mrs. Davilolen didn’t dare name Grandcourt out loud.
“I hate the feeling of woolen cloth rubbing up against me.”
“Would you believe it,” Mrs. Davilow said, drifting over to her sister a moment later, “Gwendolen says she won’t waltz or polka at all tonight?”
“She’s far too given to silly whims, if you ask me,” Mrs. Gascoigne said gravely. “It would be far more proper for her to behave like every other young lady here, especially when she’s paid for first-rate dancing lessons all these years.”
“Why should I dance if I don’t enjoy it, aunt? It’s not written in the catechism somewhere, is it?”
“My *dear*!” Mrs. Gascoigne said, her tone sharp with rebuke, and Anna stared, wide-eyed, at Gwendolen’s audacity. No one pressed the subject further.

Something had shifted in Gwendolen’s mood since that bright, triumphant hour out on the archery range, but she looked more stunning than ever under the chandeliers in the ballroom, the soft gold light spilling over the polished floors, the sweet scent of roses drifting in from the conservatory wrapping around the room like silk. Hardly a single unmarried man there wasn’t desperate to secure her as a partner, and every one of the men she did accept greeted her refusal to waltz or polka with mournful little protests. “Are you under some sort of vow, Miss Harleth?” “Why are you being so cruel to all of us?” “You waltzed with me back in February!” “And you’re so very, very good at it!” Their frustration was far from unpleasant for her. The other young women who were waltzing freely all whispered that Miss Harleth was just making a nuisance of herself to draw extra attention—but when her uncle overheard her refusals, he defended her at once. “Gwendolen always has a good reason for what she does.” He thought refusing the waltzes made her look far more distinguished, and he wanted her to shine. The archery ball was meant to keep a decorous, subdued tone, suitable for clergymen and elders alike, not a wild romp for unruly young people, and he’d always thought the fast, close fashionable dances were far too unseemly anyway.

Mr. Grandcourt, though, never joined the crowd of men begging her for a dance. He stood up for one quadrille with Miss Arrowpoint, and then seemed to decide he didn’t want any other partners at all. Gwendolen spotted him lingering near the Arrowpoints’ small group again and again, never making any move to approach her. Her uncle spoke to him sometimes, but her uncle was everywhere, talking to everyone. She found herself relaxing: he probably didn’t care about her even a little, she decided. He was far too used to every possible pleasure in the world to think of her as anything more than one random pretty local girl who’d been invited to the party. Of course the elders were all fools, gossiping about who he would marry without even speaking to him. He was probably planning to marry Miss Arrowpoint, with all her family’s fortune. No matter what happened, Gwendolen told herself, she would not be disappointed. The whole situation was a joke, no matter how it turned out, because she’d never even let herself silently hope for anything from Mr. Grandcourt. Still, she noticed that every now and then, he would shift his position slowly, subtly, so he could get a clear line of sight to her, watching whenever she danced. If he didn’t admire her, well, that was his loss.
