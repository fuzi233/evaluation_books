The narrative begins not with a hero, but with a confession of familial rot. A narrator recounts his intimacy with a man from an unnamed island—a place deemed a jewel by impartial critics, yet populated by a lineage of pure depravity. This family tree was a forest of crimes: ten commandments shattered, murders committed with the casual efficiency of Thurtell or Turpin, and forgeries so brazen they defied invention. Brother Tim had inherited his father's gray hair along with his sorrow; Brother Mick had desecrated parish churches; Sister Annamaroia had jilted a captain, stolen a grandmother's will, and pilfered silverware until a knife-boy named Larry paid the ultimate price. Yet, amidst this catalogue of hellish acts, the family maintained a grotesque, amicable correspondence. They wrote to one another as "dearest brother" and "dearest sister," sharing drinks tainted by patricide and kisses stained by perjury. They wept at reunions, clutching memories of a castle that no longer existed, their grief masking the fact that they could hang each other if they chose.

This capacity for forgiveness struck the narrator as a peculiar, angelic virtue. After hurling insults, they apologized; after flinging decanters, they embraced. It was a performance of peace, a rationalization of chaos. The aunt and niece at Tunbridge Wells exemplified this. They played cards, shared wings of chicken, and scratched neighbors' eyes while sparing each other's. Their reconciliation was a theatrical display, a way to ignore the loaded firelocks resting quietly beside them. Lady Maria, holding Harry's unpaid bond in her pocket, treated him not as a debtor but as a pet, granting him leave of absence on parole. She allowed him to frisk through London like a mousekin, while her cat, Grimalkin, watched with green, judgmental eyes.

While Harry wandered, the household sought diversion. Jack Morris, a man who delighted in the company of the titled and the loss of his coin, became a convenient distraction. Lady Maria, eager to win money, secured permission to play cards with him at the Countess of Yarmouth's assembly. For forty-eight hours, the atmosphere at the Wells was cheerful, a suspension of tension until Friday arrived. That day demanded a sermon from Mr. Sampson, a preacher renowned for his eloquence and ability to entertain. Knowing the power of Lady Yarmouth—who held the keys to bishoprics and livings—the congregation waited expectantly. If there were no concerts or dancing bears, they would endure the word.

Sampson prepared his finest discourse, aware that the Great Man might attend. In the church, the dynamic shifted instantly upon the arrival of the aristocracy. The curate, Trotter, faltered, hoping for a living from the Great Pew. His wife and daughters watched anxiously, gauging the effect of his words on the assembled dignitaries. But the moment the Right Honourable Lord Naseby began to snore in the pew by the fire, the illusion of piety evaporated. The visionary mitre dissolved into the rhythm of sleep.

Sampson, the domestic chaplain to Madame Bernstein, entered the scene rosy and handsome, wearing a fresh wig and a new cassock bought on credit. He marched from breakfast to church, flanked by lacqueys carrying gilt prayer books. The crowd was a mix of high fashion and ragged desperation. Among them were two men in worn coats, one with carrot-colored hair peeking from a scratch-wig. As the organ ceased, the stranger crossed himself mechanically, whispering, "Bedad, Tim, I forgawt!" revealing his origin from the same cursed island. Nearby, two men with hooked noses and sallow complexions, smelling of spirits, managed to slip past the beadle. One whispered, "We wants to be conwerted, gov'nor!" and slipped a coin into the beadle's palm. The barrier fell, the doors closed, and the service commenced.

Sampson ascended the pulpit, buoyant and jolly. Then, disaster struck. He turned pale as death, his eyes darting to the western door where the two Hebrew strangers stood like silent sentinels. Elsewhere, two perfumed Irishmen slipped from their seats and positioned themselves near the vestry door, casting meek glances at the floor. The sermon continued, but its content was irrelevant; the preacher was already consumed by panic.

Afterward, the ladies praised the sermon effusively. Madame Walmoden claimed it made her tremble; Madame Bernstein called it excellent. Lady Maria waved to Sampson, seeking acknowledgment, but he barely registered her gesture. His gaze remained fixed on the font, where the two Hebrew men still stood alone as the tide of humanity swept past them. The congregation dispersed in a rush, a throng, a dribble of individuals. Only the two strangers remained by the door.

Lady Maria, distressed by the smell of spirits emanating from the figures at the exit, voiced her outrage to her aunt's maid. Meanwhile, Madame Bernstein engaged in polite small talk with a gentleman, ignoring the impending storm. As the old lady insisted that her niece, the earl's daughter, precede her from the pew, the two "horrible men" advanced. One produced a long strip of paper. Lady Maria started, turning pale, and fled toward the vestry, hoping to seal the door. But the men were faster. One seized her shoulder, introducing himself as Costigan, a poor gentleman of Ireland forced into a disagreeable profession.

"At the suit of Mistress Pincott... I have the honour of arresting your leedyship," Costigan declared.

Lady Maria shrieked three times and collapsed. "Keep the door, Mick!" Costigan shouted, politely instructing Madame Bernstein to exclude others. "Her ladyship has fallen in a feinting fit."

"Unlace her, Brett!" cried the Baroness, her eyes twinkling with a strange, predatory light. As the maid loosened the necklace, the Baroness snatched a small bag hanging around Lady Maria's throat and severed the chain. "Dash some cold water over her face!" she commanded. "You stay with her, Brett. How much is your suit?"

Costigan replied, "The debt we hold against her leedyship is one hundred and thirty-two pounds."

Amidst this chaos, where was Mr. Sampson? Like the opossum who surrenders to the hunter rather than fight, he yielded to his pursuers. "At whose suit, Simons?" he asked, resigned.

"Buckleby Cordwainer," came the reply.
