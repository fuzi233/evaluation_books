Oleron did not remember choosing to leave, yet suddenly he was there—striding over the makeshift plank on the broken stair, descending into the gloom below. His head was bare, his movements stiff, automatic. Only later would fragments return to him: the candle still burning on the table, the door opened just wide enough to let him slip through, then closed again with unnatural quiet.

At the foot of the stairs, another shock. Something darted from the cellar shadows—a streak of movement, gone before he could blink. Just a cat, but the sight wrung a choked sob from him, thin and childish. He staggered out into the night, pausing beneath the *To Let* sign, fingers plucking absently at his lip as he glanced back at the dim red glow behind his window. Then, with another uneasy look over his shoulder, he stumbled up the square.

There was a pub around the corner—a place Oleron had never entered before. Tonight, he did. His hand shook as he slapped a shilling onto the counter; it missed, clattering to the floor.

"B-brandy," he muttered, bending to retrieve the coin.

The bar was nearly empty. A few laborers and tradesmen murmured in the far room, but here, Oleron sat alone on a hard settee, the sawdust grit beneath his shoes. He drank the first glass in two swallows, then ordered another. The liquor burned, but he welcomed it. He would not go back. Not tonight. Not to that room with its waiting candle, its hollow silence, its unseen things that moved when he turned his back.

No, he would return to his old lodgings, just for tonight. His fingers tightened around the glass. He had reason enough to leave. Nails that worked themselves back into the wood, stairs that crumbled beneath his feet, *her*—that figure in the dark, brushing out her long, unseen hair. The thought made his skin prickle.

A flicker of movement caught his eye. Faces peered through the gap above the glass partition, watching. When he turned, they vanished. He shifted, putting his back to them, but now the white-haired landlady stood before him, wiping a glass.

"Cold night," she remarked.

Oleron mumbled something—he hardly knew what—and she nodded, prattling about the weather, the late winter, the coming spring. The mundane words steadied him, if only for a moment. His mind flitted, absurdly, to whether her hair crackled when she brushed it at night—then he crushed the thought.

Another drink. Another resolve. He *would* go back. He wasn’t some superstitious fool to be chased from his own home by shadows. He’d walk first, clear his head, then return. He had work to do—*Romilly* wouldn’t write itself.

But as he stepped into the cold air, the bravado faded. The brandy’s warmth dulled, and in its place crept a slow, creeping dread. Not for himself—for *her*. For Elsie.

She had sensed it before he had. And she was right. Whatever lurked in that house, it hated her. That was reason enough to keep her away—not his own shame, not his fear, but *hers*.

He turned back toward the square, jaw set. If fear rose again, he would drink once more. But by the time he reached the pub, it was shuttered, the last stragglers lingering outside. They fell silent as he passed. Barrett, the long-nosed caretaker, did not return his greeting.

Oleron stepped through the broken gate, hesitated only a breath in the dark alley, then climbed the stairs.

The candle had burned low. He forced himself to walk through every room, though his pulse hammered in his throat. As he crossed the hall, a letter on the floor caught his eye—no stamp, slipped under the door by hand. The writing was crude, sprawling without punctuation.

Barrett’s work.

The message was plain: he would no longer serve Oleron’s meals or clean his rooms. And then—the postscript. A scrap of Scripture, twisted, insinuating. About Elsie.

Oleron’s hands clenched. The paper crumpled, then blackened in the candle’s flame. He ground the ashes beneath his heel.

Good. Let Barrett go. Let them all go.

But as he undressed, another disquieting sight: the bedclothes were rumpled, as if someone had lain there. Had he? He couldn’t recall.

No matter. The brandy and his fury blotted out the unease. He blew out the candle, sank into the bed, and slept—deep, dreamless, and far longer than he meant to.
