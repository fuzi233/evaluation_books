When Georgette recovered, Madame sent her away to the countryside. I felt a pang of loss—she had been a small light in my quiet life, and her absence left me more alone than before. But I could not complain. I lived in a house full of energy, laughter, and noise; companionship was within reach, yet I chose silence. One by one, the teachers offered me friendship, each in their own way. I tested them all. The first was honest but narrow-minded, coarse in feeling, and utterly self-absorbed. The second—a Parisienne—was outwardly refined, but beneath the surface lay emptiness: no beliefs, no principles, no true affection. Her soul seemed stripped bare, like a shell without a creature inside. She craved gifts above all else, and the third teacher, though otherwise dull and unremarkable, shared that same hunger. This woman, however, had another trait: an almost animal greed. Money itself stirred her deeply—the sight of gold brought a strange green gleam to her eyes, as if she were drawn to it like a moth to flame. Once, as a sign of favor, she took me upstairs, opened a hidden door, and revealed a hoard of crude, heavy coins—about fifteen guineas’ worth in five-franc pieces. She cherished it like a bird guards its nest. These were her savings. She would speak of them with a kind of obsessive devotion, staring at them for hours, even though she was barely twenty-five.

The Parisienne, on the other hand, spent recklessly—though not necessarily in action, only in spirit. Her profligacy showed itself once, cautiously, like a snake peeking from under a leaf. I glimpsed it briefly, and found it unsettlingly new. If it had come forward boldly, I might have stood firm, observed it coldly from head to tail. But it retreated quickly when I reacted with sudden anger, hissing into hiding. From that moment, she hated me.

She was always in debt. Her salary vanished long before payday—not just on clothes, but on perfumes, cosmetics, sweets, and spices. What a cold, detached hedonist she was! I can still see her: thin face, sallow skin, sharp features, perfect teeth, lips like a thread, a broad chin, wide-open eyes that held no warmth—only a craving light that never gave thanks. She despised work and adored what she called pleasure—meaning idle, heartless, thoughtless distraction.

Madame Beck knew her well. Once she spoke of her to me, mixing insight with indifference and disdain. I asked why she kept such a woman in her employ. “Because it suits my interest,” she replied plainly. Then she pointed out something I’d already noticed: Mademoiselle St. Pierre had a rare talent for controlling unruly students. A silent, chilling authority surrounded her—no shouting, no force, yet she held them fast, like frost halting a rushing stream. She taught little, but enforced discipline perfectly. “I know she has no principles, perhaps no morals,” Madame admitted, “but her presence in class is always proper, even dignified. That’s all that matters. Neither the girls nor their parents look beyond that—and so, neither do I.”

This school was a lively, noisy, almost giddy place. Efforts were made to mask chains with flowers. A subtle current of Roman Catholicism ran through every custom. Physical indulgence was allowed—almost encouraged—as a balance to strict spiritual control. Minds were shaped in servitude, but to keep them from questioning, every chance for bodily enjoyment was seized. Here, as everywhere, the Church aimed to raise children strong in body, weak in soul—plump, rosy, cheerful, ignorant, passive, unquestioning. “Eat, drink, and live!” she says. “Care for your bodies; leave your souls to me. I hold their fate—I guide their path—I guarantee their end.” A bargain many Catholics accept gladly. Lucifer offers the same: “All this power and glory shall be yours—for it is mine, and I give it to whom I will. If you worship me, all shall be yours.”

At this time—when summer reached its fullest bloom—Madame Beck’s house became as festive as any school could be. All day, the folding doors and casement windows stood open. Sunlight settled into the air like a resident guest. Clouds drifted far off, beyond the sea, perhaps resting over misty islands like England—our beloved land of fog—but gone entirely from this sun-drenched continent. We lived more in the garden than indoors. Classes were held and meals eaten in the “grand berceau.” And there was a growing sense of celebration, nearly tipping into freedom. The autumn vacation was two months away, but before that came a grand event: Madame’s fête.

Organizing it fell mostly to Mademoiselle St. Pierre. Madame pretended ignorance, as if she were unaware of the festivities being prepared in her honor. Especially, she never suspected the secret subscription collected each year from the entire school to buy her a gift. Let us quietly omit mention of the brief, hushed meeting in her own room about this.

“What would you like this year?” asked her Parisian assistant.

“Oh, nothing. Leave it. Let the poor children keep their francs,” Madame said, looking gentle and modest.

St. Pierre would then thrust out her chin. She knew Madame well. She called her displays of kindness “grimaces”—nothing more. She never pretended to respect them.

“Quick!” she’d snap. “Name the item. Jewels or porcelain? Haberdashery or silver?”

“Well… two or three spoons, and the same number of forks, in silver.”

And so it was: a fine case containing 300 francs worth of silverware.

The fête’s program included: presentation of the silver, a garden meal, a play performed by pupils and teachers, followed by dancing and supper. To me, it seemed magnificent—especially since Zélie St. Pierre understood these things and managed them with skill.

The play was central—requiring a month of rehearsal. Casting demanded care, then lessons in voice, posture, and expression, followed by endless practice. For all this, St. Pierre wasn’t enough. Another talent was needed—someone with different strengths. That person was M. Paul Emanuel, professor of literature.

I never saw him during rehearsals, but often caught sight of him crossing the *carré*—the square hall between the house and school. I heard him in warm evenings, lecturing with open doors, and his name, along with stories about him, echoed through the halls. Miss Ginevra Fanshawe, our former acquaintance, who had been cast in a leading role, often filled my ears with tales of him during our talks. She found him hideously plain and claimed she nearly fainted at the sound of his footsteps or voice.

He was indeed a dark little man—sharp, severe. Even to me, he seemed harsh: black hair cropped close, a broad, pale brow, thin cheeks, flaring nostrils, piercing eyes, and a restless stride. Irritable, they said, especially when addressing the clumsy group under his direction. He would burst out in fury at their flat emotions, hollow expressions, weak delivery. “Listen!” he’d roar, and his voice cut through the building like a trumpet. When a timid voice—Ginevra’s, Mathilde’s, Blanche’s—mimicked him, a wave of scorn or rage would follow.

“You are nothing but dolls,” I heard him thunder. “You have no passions. You feel nothing? Your flesh is ice, your blood is frozen! I demand fire—life—soul!”

But his demands were futile. When he finally accepted that, he tore apart the tragedy they’d been learning and returned the next day with a short, comic piece. That, they embraced eagerly. Soon, he had it memorized and polished in their minds.

Mademoiselle St. Pierre always presided over M. Emanuel’s classes. I was told her poise, attention, tact, and grace impressed him deeply. She had the gift of pleasing people—briefly. But the charm faded fast, like dew under sun, like gossamer in wind.

The day before the fête was as festive as the fête itself. It was devoted to cleaning, rearranging, and decorating the three classrooms. Inside, there was constant bustle—no corner was quiet, no space untouched. So I fled to the garden. All day, I wandered or sat there alone, warmed by the sun, sheltered by trees, companioned only by my thoughts. I remember exchanging only two words with anyone that day. I didn’t feel lonely—I welcomed the peace. As a spectator, I passed through the rooms several times, watching how they transformed: a green room and dressing room were created, a tiny stage built with scenery, M. Paul Emanuel and Mademoiselle St. Pierre directing everything, while eager students—among them Ginevra—worked happily under their guidance.

The great day arrived. The sun rose hot and clear, and burned steadily until nightfall. Every door and window stood open, giving a sense of summer freedom—freedom, it seemed, was the theme of the day. Teachers and students descended for breakfast in dressing gowns and curlers, enjoying a morning of careless comfort, anticipating the evening’s elaborate dress. Like aldermen fasting before a feast, they relished their slovenliness.

Around nine, the “coiffeur” arrived. It was sacrilegious, some said, that he set up shop in the oratory—right beside the holy water, candle, and crucifix—where he performed his sacred art. Each girl was summoned in turn. Emerging from his hands, her head was smooth as a shell, marked by flawless white lines, braided in Greek-style plaits that shone like lacquer. I joined the line, and when I looked in the mirror afterward, I hardly recognized myself. The mass of woven brown hair amazed me—I feared it wasn’t all mine, and pulled hard to confirm it was. I now acknowledged the coiffeur as a master—someone who could make beauty from mediocrity.

Afterward, the dormitory became the scene of ablutions, dressing, and adornment—elaborate, intricate, prolonged. To me, it remains a mystery how so much time could be spent achieving so little. The process seemed complex, the result simple. A crisp white muslin dress, a blue sash (in the Virgin’s colors), white or straw-colored kid gloves—this was the uniform. Three hours went into putting it on. Yet though simple, the effect was impeccable—perfect in style, fit, freshness. Every head was styled with precision, and the overall look—stiff but elegant—suited the sturdy Labassecourien features, though too rigid for softer beauty. On the whole, it was admirable.

Seeing this sea of white and blue, I felt like a shadow against light. I dared not wear a sheer white dress. The weather and rooms were too hot for heavy fabrics, so I searched shops until I found a material like crape, in a purple-gray hue—the color of mist over a blooming moor. My seamstress made it as best she could, wisely noting it was “so sad—so dull,” so extra care was needed. She was right: I had no flower, no jewel, and no natural flush of color. We ignore such flaws in daily routine, but on bright occasions like this, they stand out.

Still, in this shadowy gown, I felt at ease. I wouldn’t have in anything brighter. Madame Beck also wore a subdued dress—nearly as quiet as mine—except for a bracelet and a large brooch glittering with gold and gems. We met on the stairs, and she nodded and smiled approval. Not because I looked well—something she likely didn’t care about—but because I was dressed “appropriately,” “decently.” Convenance and Decence were the gods she worshipped. She paused, placed her gloved hand—holding a perfumed, embroidered handkerchief—on my shoulder, and whispered a sneer about the other teachers (whom she had just praised). “Nothing so absurd,” she said, “as mature women dressing like fifteen-year-olds. As for St. Pierre—she looks like an old coquette pretending to be innocent.”

Dressed hours before anyone else, I enjoyed the peace of going not to the garden—where servants were setting tables and chairs—but to the empty, cool, clean classrooms. Their walls were freshly stained, floors scoured and still damp, pots of fresh flowers in corners, draperies newly hung.

I withdrew to the first classe—a smaller, neater room—and took a book from the glass-fronted cabinet, whose key I kept. I sat down to read. The glass door opened onto the large berceau; acacia branches brushed the panes, stretching toward a rosebush blooming by the opposite lintel. Bees hummed contentedly among the blossoms. I began reading. Just as the quiet hum, the dappled shade, and the warm solitude began to draw me into reverie, a sharp ring of the front door bell—sharper than any it had ever made—snapped me back.

The bell had rung all morning, as workers, servants, stylists, and seamstresses came and went. It would ring again in the afternoon, with about a hundred outside students arriving by carriage. And it would ring through the evening as parents and friends gathered for the play. So a loud ring was expected. But this one had a special edge—cutting through my dream, startling my book from my lap.

I bent to pick it up when—firm, fast, straight—through vestibule, corridor, courtyard, divisions, grand hall—came a step: quick, regular, determined. The closed door of the first classe—my refuge—offered no resistance. It burst open, and a paletôt and Greek bonnet filled the doorway. Two eyes first glanced at me, then locked on, searching.

“That’s her!” a voice declared. “I know her: the Englishwoman. Too bad. Any Englishwoman—however tongue-tied—will serve my purpose, or I’ll know why.”

Then, with stiff politeness (perhaps thinking I hadn’t grasped his earlier harsh muttering), and in the worst French imaginable: “Meess——, you must play. I am planted there.”

“What can I do for you, M. Paul Emanuel?” I asked. It was him—clearly agitated.

“You must play. I won’t allow you to shrink, frown, or pretend virtue. I read your mind the night you arrived. I see your talents: you can act. You must.”

“But how, M. Paul? What do you mean?”

“There is no time,” he continued, switching to French. “Let us forget excuses, hesitation, nonsense. You must take a part.”

“In the vaudeville?”

“In the vaudeville. You’ve said it.”

I gasped, horrified. What did he mean?

“Listen,” he said. “I’ll explain. Then answer—yes or no. Your answer will determine how I view you forever.”

A storm of irritation glowed in his cheeks, sharpened his gaze. Such a nature—irritated, sentimental, hesitant, stubborn—could turn violent quickly. Silence was the best response. I listened.

“The whole performance is collapsing,” he began. “Louise Vanderkelkov is ill—at least her ridiculous mother claims so. I believe she could perform if she wanted. It’s only willpower missing. She had a role—you know or don’t—it doesn’t matter. Without it, the play fails. There are only hours left to learn it. No girl here would listen, would accept. It’s not a likable role—her petty pride, that base quality women possess, revolts at it. Englishwomen are either the best or worst of their kind. God knows I hate them usually” (he muttered through clenched teeth). “I turn to an Englishwoman to save me. Will she say yes—or no?”

A thousand objections flooded my mind. The language, the time, the exposure… My instincts recoiled, my confidence faltered, my pride—this “vile quality”—trembled. “No, no, no!” said every instinct. But looking up at M. Paul, seeing in his furious, searching eyes a plea beneath the threat, my lips formed the word: “Yes.”

For a moment, his stern face softened with relief. Then, swiftly regaining composure, he said: “To work! Here is the script. Here is your role. Read.”

I read. He didn’t praise. At some lines, he frowned, stamped. He gave me a lesson. I tried hard to imitate. The role was unpleasant—a man’s, a foolish fop’s. I couldn’t put heart or soul into it. I hated it. The play was trivial—a comedy about two rivals vying for a flirtatious woman. One lover was the “Bear”—a good, brave, rough diamond. The other was a butterfly, a talker, a traitor. And I was to be the butterfly, the talker, the traitor.

I did my best—bad, I knew. It angered M. Paul. He seethed. I worked harder, trying to surpass my best. Perhaps he saw my effort. He grudgingly said, “It will do.” As voices began rising from the garden and white dresses fluttered among the trees, he added: “You must be alone to learn this. Come with me.”

Without time to think, I was swept along—like a whirlwind—upstairs, up two flights, then a third. This fiery little man seemed to know every corner. I was carried to a solitary, high attic, shut in, and locked. He took the key and vanished.

The attic was no haven. I doubt he knew how terrible it was, or he wouldn’t have imprisoned me so casually. In summer, it was as hot as Africa; in winter, colder than Greenland. Boxes and clutter filled it. Old dresses hung on the walls. Cobwebs covered the ceiling. Rats, beetles, cockroaches lived here—rumor even said the ghostly Nun of the garden had been seen here. A dimness covered one end, where an old russet curtain hid a row of winter cloaks—each pinned like a criminal to a gallows. From behind that curtain, the Nun was said to emerge. I didn’t believe it, nor was I afraid. But I saw a large, dark rat glide from the alcove. And my eyes landed on countless black beetles dotting the floor. These disturbed me more than I care to admit—along with the dust, clutter, and stifling heat. Had I not opened the skylight and propped it up, I would have suffocated. Beneath it, I placed a large chest, climbed a smaller box, wiped the dust, arranged my best dress carefully around me, and perched on this makeshift throne. Seated, I began to learn my part—while keeping watch on the beetles and cockroaches, which I feared more than the rats.
