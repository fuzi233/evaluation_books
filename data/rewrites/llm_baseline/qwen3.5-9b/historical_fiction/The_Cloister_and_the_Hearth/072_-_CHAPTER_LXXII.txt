The Dominicans, once the most formidable order in Europe, were now fading into obscurity. Their bitter rivals, the Franciscans, were sweeping across the continent, overtaking them everywhere—even in England, that land of wealth and piety where the "Black Friars" had formerly held sway. Consequently, the wise leaders of the Order grew increasingly desperate to recruit able and zealous souls, dispatching them abroad to reclaim the world for their cause.

It became evident that Clement possessed talents of rare distinction, particularly his command of languages—he spoke Latin, Italian, French, and both high and low German fluently. He was destined for travel and preaching in England, serving as a correspondent to the Roman center. Yet, Jerome, who held the superior's ear, obstructed this plan.

"Clement," Jerome argued, "still carries the milk of the world in his veins; he retains its feelings and its weaknesses. Let not his newborn zeal or his humility lead us to discard our ancient wisdom. Test him first, temper him, lest one day we find ourselves leaning on a reed for a staff."

"It is sound advice," agreed the Prior. "Take him in hand yourself."

Thus, following the counsel of antiquity, Jerome undertook the task of testing Clement. One day, he led him to a field where young men played the games of the day—a haunt of Clement's former friends. Soon, Pietro Vanucci and Andrea passed by, casting a casual glance at the two friars. They failed to recognize their deceased companion in the shaven monk.

Clement gave a slight start, lowered his eyes, and recited a *paternoster*.

"Would you not speak with them, brother?" Jerome probed.

"No, brother; yet it was good for me to see them. They remind me of the sins I can never repent enough."

"It is well," Jerome replied, filing a cold report in Clement's favor.

Next, Jerome subjected Clement to the grim realities of death. He took him to numerous dying beds, then dragged him into noisome dungeons where darkness was appalling and the stench loathsome and pestilential. There, men resembling wild beasts lay coiled in rags and filth, consumed by despair. The trials tested Clement's body severely, yet his soul gathered all its strength to comfort the wretched souls who remained capable of solace. In this crucible, Clement shone brilliantly. Jerome reported that Clement's spirit was willing, though his flesh was weak.

"Good!" declared Anselm. "His flesh is weak, but his spirit is willing."

Yet, a far greater trial awaited.

I shall recount it as witnessed by others. One morning, a principal street in Rome was choked with crowds, the avenues blocked by heads pressing together. It was an execution—not for a common crime, nor upon a vulgar victim.

The Governor of Rome had been found slaughtered in his bed at dawn. His hand, presumably raised in self-defense, lay severed at the wrist beside him; his throat was cut, and his temples bruised by a blunt instrument. The murder was traced to his servant, who was to face retribution that very morning.

Italian executions were rarely cruel in general, but this regicide demanded exact and bloody justice. The criminal was brought to the house of the murdered man and fastened to the wall for half an hour, a foretaste of legal vengeance. Then, his left hand was hacked off, mirroring his victim's. A newly killed fowl was slit open and pinned around the bleeding stump—the purpose of this macabre gesture remains obscure, perhaps a quirk of the era's medical mysticism. Thus mutilated and bandaged, the murderer was hurried to the scaffold. There, a young friar prayed with him most earnestly and affectionately, holding the crucifix close to his eyes.

Suddenly, the executioner shoved the friar aside roughly. With a heavy blow of a mallet, he felled the culprit, then slashed his throat from ear to ear. A cry of horror erupted from the crowd. The young friar swooned. A gigantic monk strode forward and carried him off like a child.

Brother Clement returned to the convent deeply discouraged. Confessing to the Prior with tears of regret, he was met with encouragement.

"Courage, son Clement," the Prior said. "A Dominican is not made in a day. You shall undergo another trial. And I forbid you to approach it fasting."

Clement bowed in obedience. He need not wait long. A notorious robber, a monster of villainy who had slain men in wanton cruelty after stripping them, was brought to the scaffold. Clement spent his final night in prison with the convict, accompanying him to the platform. He prayed for the hardened ruffian with such intensity that the man shed tears and embraced him. Though Clement's flesh quivered with repugnance, he reciprocated the embrace, holding the crucifix firmly before the criminal's eyes. As the rope tightened and the dark spirit departed, Clement prayed aloud and earnestly. The moment the man was dead, the hangman raised his hatchet and quartered the body on the spot. The crowd, having watched the living man die with indifference, even satisfaction, uttered piteous cries at each stroke of the axe upon the corpse, which could feel nothing. Clement shuddered but stood firm, like a rock that vibrates yet cannot be toppled.

Suddenly, Jerome's voice rang in his ear: "Brother Clement, mount that cart and preach to the people! Quickly! Strike with all your force against this iron while it is hot, while souls may yet be saved."

Clement's color drained and returned; he breathed heavily. Yet, he obeyed. With uncertain steps, he climbed onto the cart and delivered his first sermon to the first crowd he had ever faced. That sea of upturned faces! His throat felt parched, his heart hammered, his voice trembled.

Soon, however, the magnitude of the occasion, the sight of the eager faces, and the fire of his own zeal ignited the pale monk. He recounted the robber's history, fresh from his own lips in the prison cell, using the example to illustrate the gradations of folly and crime, warning them solemnly not to place their foot on the first rung of that fatal ladder. Alternately thundering against shedders of blood and moving the crowd toward charity and pity, his tremors vanished. He felt strung tight like a lute, gifted with an unsuspected force. He mastered the listening crowd, sensing their very pulse, playing sacred melodies upon them as if they were his psaltery. Sobs and groans testified to his power over the mob, already stirred by the preceding tragedy. Jerome stared, astonished, like one watching a rocket launch. After a while, catching Jerome's look of surprise and finding no approval in it, Clement abruptly stopped and descended.

"It was my first endeavor," he apologized. "Your command came upon me like a thunderbolt. Am I qualified? Did I succeed? Oh, correct me, and aid me with your experience, Brother Jerome."

"Hmph!" Jerome responded dubiously. After long reflection, he added somewhat sullenly, "Give the glory to God, Brother Clement; my opinion is that you are an orator born."

He reported this at headquarters with half-reluctance, for he was an honest friar, albeit a disagreeable one.

Later, Julio Antonelli was accused of sacrilege. Three witnesses swore they saw him emerge from the church where candlesticks had been stolen, at the precise moment of the theft. Other witnesses provided a positive alibi. Caught in this contradiction, Antonelli was granted the trial by water—hot or cold. By the hot trial, he would plunge his bare arm into boiling water fourteen inches deep and retrieve a pebble; by the cold trial, his body would be submerged in eight feet of water. The clergy, believing him innocent, recommended the hot water ordeal, which, to those favored, did not seem as terrifying as it sounded. But Antonelli lacked the nerve and chose the cold ordeal.

This presented Jerome with another opportunity to steel Clement. Antonelli received the sacrament, was then stripped naked on the banks of the Tiber, and tied hand and foot to prevent struggling, which would cause a man to sink. He was gently lowered into the stream, floating momentarily with only his hair above the surface. A simultaneous roar from the crowds on either bank proclaimed him guilty. But the next instant, the ropes—which happened to be new—got wet and slackened, allowing him to settle deeper. Another roar declared his innocence. They left him at the bottom for the appointed time, slightly longer than half a minute, before drawing him up, gurgling, gasping, and screaming for mercy. After the requisite prayers, he was dismissed, cleared of the charge.

Throughout the experiment, Clement prayed earnestly on the bank. When it ended, he thanked God in a loud but slightly quavering voice. Later, he asked Jerome if the man ought not to be compensated.

"For what?" Jerome asked.

"For the pain, the dread, the suffocation. Poor soul, he lives, but has tasted all the bitterness of death. Yet he committed no ill."

"He is rewarded sufficiently in being cleared of his fault."

"But being innocent of that fault, he has nonetheless drunk Death's cup, though not to the dregs; while his accusers, less innocent than he, suffered naught."
