At five o'clock, the shop had already closed, and the Christian servant admitted Deronda. She led him into a chamber behind the counter, revealing a scene of unexpected charm. Though the house was old and the rear extension likely gloomy by day, the interior now glowed warmly under a magnificent antique brass lamp with seven oil-flames suspended above a table draped in snow-white linen. The air within was heavy with the smoke of age, rendering the walls and ceiling dim, yet this shadow played beautifully against the figures gathered there, giving them a luminous, Venetian quality.

The grandmother was dressed in shades of yellow-brown, adorned with a heavy gold chain serving as a necklace; beneath the soft light, her yellow complexion, framed by dark eyebrows and a roll of gray hair, possessed a picturesque beauty. Beside her sat young Mrs. Cohen in red and black, her throat encircled by a thick strand of large artificial pearls. Between them, a baby slept peacefully under a scarlet coverlet. Adelaide Rebekah wore braided amber silk, and Jacob Alexander sat in black velveteen with scarlet stockings. As the four pairs of dark eyes brightened with welcome, Deronda felt a sudden shame for the haughty disdain they had provoked in him during daylight hours. The atmosphere was impossibly cordial; both mother and grandmother seemed to derive a heightened sense of dignity from displaying such warm hospitality on their private hearth.

Deronda glanced around with wonder at the antiquated furnishings. An oak bureau and a high side-table stood nearby, appearing less like objects of taste and more like acquisitions born of necessity. On the side-table rested a large dish of blue and yellow ware, flanked by two tarnished silver vessels. Before them lay a massive volume bound in darkened vellum with a deeply ribbed spine. At the far end, an open door revealed a sliver of light from an inner room.

While absorbing these details through fleeting glances, Deronda focused his attention on Jacob's earnest concern regarding the knife. The boy had gone to some trouble to procure one featuring a hook and a white handle, producing it upon request.

"Is this the sort you wanted, Jacob?" Deronda asked.

The instrument underwent a rigorous inspection; the hooks and blades were tested, and the article intended for barter—a corkscrew—was produced for comparison.

"Why prefer a hook to a corkscrew?" Deronda queried.

"'Cause I can grab things with a hook," Jacob explained seriously. "A corkscrew won't fit anything but corks. But it works better for you, because you can pull corks."

"You agree to trade, then?" Deronda asked, noting the grandmother watching with delighted interest.

"What else do you have in your pockets?" Jacob pressed, his expression grave.

"Hush, Jacob, darling," the grandmother chided gently. Mindful of propriety, Deronda replied, "I suppose I cannot tell you that. Our business concerns the knives."

Jacob gazed up into his face, searching for answers, then, seemingly satisfied, declared solemnly, "I'll swap," passing the corkscrew-knife to Deronda, who tucked it away with equal gravity.

Instantly, the youngest son of Shem darted into the adjacent room, his voice rising in rapid chatter, before scambling back. Spotting his father, he snatched a small velvet hat from a chair and donned it to greet him. Mr. Cohen, however, retained his own hat, ignoring the gesture, and stood still as the two children climbed onto his knees. He laid his hands upon each, uttering a Hebrew benediction. Then, having recently lifted the sleeping infant from the cradle, Mrs. Cohen brought the child forward, holding it beneath her husband's outstretched hands to receive the blessing even in dreams. For a moment, Deronda perceived that this pawnbroker, proud of his calling, was not entirely devoid of spiritual depth.

"Well, sir, you found your welcome in my household, I trust," Cohen said, removing his hat and resuming his usual demeanor. "And you were punctual. There is nothing quite like a bit of pressure here," he added, tapping his side pocket as he sat. "It benefits us all in turns. I remember when I had to arrange payments; I began fitting boxes of every kind. It is invigorating to the mind. Now then! Let us see, let us see."

"That is the ring I mentioned," Deronda said, lifting it from his finger. "I believe it cost a hundred pounds. It should serve as ample security for fifty, I think. I expect to redeem it within a month."

Cohen's glistening eyes seemed to narrow slightly as he met the innocent gaze of this somewhat rough young gentleman, who apparently believed redemption offered a unique satisfaction to pawnbrokers. Taking the ring, he examined it closely before returning it with indifferent grace. "Good, good. We shall discuss it after our meal. Perhaps you will join us, if you have no objection. My wife and I shall feel honored, and so will Mother; do you, Mother?"

The invitation was enthusiastically echoed, and Deronda readily accepted. Everyone turned toward the table. Aside from a single dish concealed beneath a napkin, no food was visible, though Mrs. Cohen had placed a porcelain bowl near her husband for him to wash his hands. However, after putting on his hat again, Cohen paused, then called out loudly, "Mordecai!"

Could this be part of a religious rite? wondered Deronda, unsure what role the ancient hero might play. Yet he heard a "Yes" from the next room, prompting him to look toward the open door. To his astonishment, there stood the enigmatic Jew he had encountered that morning in the bookshop. Their eyes met, and Mordecai appeared as surprised as Deronda; neither offered any sign of recognition. But as Mordecai took his seat at the far end of the table, he merely bowed his head to the guest in a cold, distant manner, as if the disappointment of the morning lingered as an unpleasant association.

Now, Cohen washed his hands, reciting Hebrew words throughout the ritual. He removed the napkin to reveal two long, flat loaves sprinkled with seeds—the remembrance of the manna that sustained the wandering ancestors. Breaking off small portions, he distributed them to each member of the family, including Adelaide Rebekah, who stood on a chair, her entire length displayed in her amber gown, her little Jewish nose comically elongated by the compression of her lips in an attempt to maintain a proper countenance. Cohen then pronounced another Hebrew blessing. Following this, the men uncovered their heads, all seated themselves, and the meal commenced without any peculiarity that particularly engaged Deronda.

He was barely conscious of the dishes before him, preoccupied with a desire to steer the conversation toward a leading question, and constantly thinking of Mordecai, between whom and himself lay an exchange of fascinated, half-furtive glances. Mordecai lacked a handsome Sabbath garment; instead of the threadbare, rusty black coat worn that morning, he wore a loose coat of light drab, which appeared to have once been a handsome paletot now shrunk by frequent washing. This change in attire only accentuated his dark-haired, eager visage, which might have belonged to the prophet Ezekiel—itself likely unfashionable in contemporary eyes. It was noticeable that the thin tails of the fried fish were reserved for Mordecai, a share typically allotted to a poor relative, undoubtedly a "survival" of prehistoric custom, rarely acknowledged today as superstitious.

Mr. Cohen maintained the conversation with lively spirit, introducing subjects always treated with decorum (for the Jew takes pride in his loyalty), namely the Queen and the Royal Family, the Emperor and Empress of the French—topics into which both grandmother and wife entered with genuine zest. Young Mrs. Cohen demonstrated an accurate memory of distinguished birthdays, while the elder assisted her son in recounting events when the Imperial couple visited England and toured the city a decade prior.

"I dare say you know all about it better than we do, sir," Cohen repeated often, prefacing his full information. These interesting statements continued in a spirited trio.

"Our baby is named Eugenie Esther," announced young Mrs. Cohen vivaciously.

"It is wonderful how the Emperor resembles a cousin of mine in the face," remarked the grandmother. "It struck me like lightning when I first saw him. I never could have imagined it."

"Mother and I went to see the Emperor and Empress at the Crystal Palace," Mr. Cohen recounted. "I had a difficult task to ensure Mother's safety; she might have been crushed flat—though she was nearly as robust then as she is now. I said that if I had a hundred mothers, I would never take one of them to see the Emperor and Empress at the Crystal Palace again. You may think a man cannot afford it when he possesses but one mother—not even if he holds a vast insurance policy on her." He stroked his mother's shoulder affectionately, chuckling softly at his own wit.

"Your mother has been a widow for a long time, perhaps," Deronda observed, seizing an opportunity. "That has made your care for her all the more essential."
