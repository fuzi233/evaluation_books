John Jasper walked through the Close on his way home, the moon casting long shadows across the ancient stones. He came to a sudden halt when he saw Stony Durdles leaning against the iron railing of the burial ground, dinner bundle tucked under one arm, as if in deep thought or perhaps simply waiting for something. Beside him, a ragged boy—small, dirty, and with half his teeth missing—was throwing stones at Durdles, who seemed utterly indifferent to the assault.

The stones struck sometimes, missed others, but Durdles did not flinch. The boy, however, was animated by each hit. When one connected, he blew a shrill whistle through the gap in his front teeth—a sound like a tiny horn of triumph. When he missed, he yelped, “Mulled agin!” and adjusted his aim with fierce concentration.

“What are you doing to that man?” Jasper demanded, stepping into the moonlight from the shadowed path.

“Making a cock-shy of him,” the boy replied without pause.

“Give me those stones,” Jasper said, reaching out.

“No, you don’t!” the boy snapped, backing away. “I’ll shove ’em down your throat if you try to grab me. I’ll smash your eye if you come near!”

“Baby-Devil that you are,” Jasper said, voice low and warning, “what has he done to you?”

“He won’t go home,” the boy answered.

“And what is that to you?”

“He gives me a ha’penny to pelt him home if I catch him out too late,” the boy explained, then broke into a chant, stumbling and dancing slightly in his worn boots:

“Widdy widdy wen!
I—ket—ches—Im—out—ar—ter—ten,
Widdy widdy wy!
Then—E—don’t—go—then—I—shy—
Widdy Widdy Wake-cock warning!”

He swept his arm wide on the last word and hurled another stone at Durdles. It was clearly a signal—both a threat and a warning.

Jasper beckoned the boy forward with a slow nod, knowing it was useless to argue or force him. He crossed to the railing where Durdles stood, lost in contemplation.

“Do you know this creature?” Jasper asked, searching for the right word.

“Deputy,” Durdles said, nodding.

“Is that his name?”

“Deputy,” Durdles confirmed.

The boy stepped forward. “I’m man-servant up at the Travellers’ Twopenny in Gas Works Garding,” he declared. “All us man-servants at Travellers’ Lodgings are named Deputy. When we’re chock full and the travellers are all in bed, I come out for my health.”

He stepped back into the road and resumed his aim.

“Widdy widdy wen! I—ket—ches—Im—out—ar—ter—”

“Hold your hand!” Jasper shouted. “Don’t throw while I stand so close—or I’ll kill you! Come, Durdles. Let me walk home with you tonight. Shall I carry your bundle?”

“Not on any account,” Durdles replied, adjusting the pack. “I’ve been making my reflections here when you came up, sir—surrounded by my works, like a poplar author.”

He gestured toward the tombstones within the railings, one by one.

“Your own brother-in-law,” he said, pointing to a sarcophagus gleaming white in the moonlight.

“Mrs. Sapsea,” he added, indicating a monument.

“Late Incumbent,” he said, nodding at a broken column.

“Departed Assessed Taxes,” he announced, pointing to a vase and towel atop what looked like a soap cake.

“Former pastrycook and muffin-maker, much respected,” he said, gesturing to another stone.

“All safe and sound here, sir,” Durdles continued, “and all my work. As for the common folk—bundled up in turf and brambles—the less said, the better. A poor lot. Soon forgotten.”

Jasper glanced back. “That creature, Deputy, is still behind us. Is he following?”

Their relationship was unpredictable. When Durdles turned slowly, as if weighed down by drink, Deputy darted sideways into the road, taking a defensive stance.

“You never cried ‘Widdy Warning’ before you started tonight,” Durdles said suddenly, as though recalling an offense.

“Yer lie, I did,” Deputy shot back, using his only form of polite contradiction.

“Own brother, sir,” Durdles mused, turning again, his anger forgotten as quickly as it had come. “Own brother to Peter the Wild Boy! But I gave him an object in life.”

“At which he takes aim?” Jasper suggested.

“That’s it, sir,” Durdles replied, satisfied. “At which he takes aim. I took him in hand and gave him purpose. Before? A destroyer. What did he do? Destruction. What did he earn? Short terms in Cloisterham jail. Not a soul, not a horse, dog, cat, bird, fowl, pig—nothing he wouldn’t stone, just because he lacked direction. I gave him that direction. Now he earns his honest halfpenny—three penn’orth a week.”

“I wonder he has no rivals,” Jasper said.

“He has plenty,” Durdles replied. “But he stones them all away.”

He paused, considering. “Now, I don’t know what this scheme of mine amounts to. I don’t know what to call it exactly. Isn’t it a sort of National Education?”

“I should say not,” Jasper said.

“I should say not,” Durdles agreed. “Then let’s not give it a name.”

They walked on. Deputy stayed behind, moving in a loose formation, stoning every wall, post, pillar—anything inanimate along the deserted route.

“Anything new down in the crypt, Durdles?” Jasper asked.

“Anything old, I think you mean,” Durdles growled. “It ain’t a place for novelty.”

“I meant any discovery of your own.”

“There’s an old one under the seventh pillar on the left as you go down the broken steps of the little underground chapel. I make him out—so far as I’ve made him out—to be one of them old ones with a crook. From the size of the passages, steps, doors—by which they came and went—those crooks must have been quite common among the old ones. Two of them meeting promiscuously would probably have tangled mitres, I’d say.”

Jasper studied his companion—covered head to foot in mortar, lime, and stone dust—as if sensing a strange romanticism in Durdles’s life.

“Yours is a curious existence,” he said.

Durdles grunted. “Yours is another.”

“Well,” Jasper replied, “since my fate is cast in the same old earthy, chilly, unchanging place—yes. But there’s more mystery and interest in your connection with the Cathedral than in mine. In fact, I’m beginning to think I might ask you to take me on as a student, or free apprentice—let me go with you sometimes, see some of these odd corners where you spend your days.”

Durdles replied vaguely, “All right. Everybody knows where to find Durdles when he’s wanted.”

Which, though not strictly true, was close enough—meaning Durdles could always be found somewhere drifting, somewhere idle.

“What I dwell upon most,” Jasper continued, “is how accurately you seem to locate where people are buried.”

“What’s wrong?” Jasper asked, noticing Durdles had stopped and backed slightly. “That bundle’s in your way. Let me hold it.”

Durdles had been looking for a ledge or corner to set it down. Now relieved, he said, “Just give me my hammer from that bundle. I’ll show you.”

The hammer was handed over. *Clink, clink.*

“Now, lookee here,” Durdles said. “You pitch your note, don’t you, Mr. Jasper?”

“Yes.”

“So I sound for mine. I take my hammer and tap.” (He struck the pavement. Deputy, ever alert, darted further into the road, as if anticipating a blow.)

“Tap, tap, tap. Solid! I go on. Still solid! Tap again. Holloa! Hollow! Tap again, persevering. Solid in hollow! Tap, tap, tap—try it better. Solid in hollow; and inside solid, hollow again! There you are! Old’un crumbled away in stone coffin, in vault!”

“Astonishing,” Jasper breathed.

“I’ve even done this,” Durdles said, pulling out his two-foot rule. Deputy edged closer, now suspecting treasure might be unearthed—and that he might profit from it, or see the discoverers hanged by the neck, on his testimony.

“Say that hammer of mine’s a wall—my work,” Durdles began, measuring on the pavement. “Two, four, and two is six. Six feet inside that wall is Mrs. Sapsea.”

“Not really Mrs. Sapsea?”

“Say Mrs. Sapsea. Her wall’s thicker, but say Mrs. Sapsea. Durdles taps that wall—represented by the hammer—and says, after good sounding: ‘Something betwixt us!’ Sure enough—some rubbish left behind by my men!”

Jasper nodded. “Such accuracy—it must be a gift.”

“I wouldn’t have it at a gift,” Durdles snapped. “I worked it out myself. Durdles comes by his knowledge through grubbing deep for it, and having it up by the roots when it doesn’t want to come.”

“Holloa you, Deputy!”

“Widdy!” the boy shrieked, stepping back.

“Catch that ha’penny,” Durdles said, tossing it. “And don’t let me see you again tonight after we reach the Travellers’ Twopenny.”

“Warning!” Deputy called, catching the coin—his mystic word of agreement.

They crossed what once was the monastery’s vineyard, now overgrown and silent, and entered the narrow back lane where stood the Travellers’ Twopenny—a crooked wooden house of two stories, warped like the morals of its guests. Its porch, once latticed, hung loosely; its garden fence was gone, stripped by travelers who couldn’t bear to leave. The windows bore fragments of red curtains, translucent and muddy in the night, lit by dim rushlights.

A paper lantern above the door announced the inn’s purpose. And as they approached, half a dozen other ragged boys—lodgers or followers—appeared from the shadows, drawn by the scent of Deputy like vultures to carrion. They surged into the moonlight and immediately began stoning each other and Deputy.

“Stop, you young brutes!” Jasper shouted. “Let us pass!”

They ignored him, hurling stones as if it were their duty. This, Jasper knew, was a custom lately accepted by English law—Christians stoned on all sides, as if Saint Stephen’s time had returned.

Durdles merely observed, “They haven’t got an object,” and led the way down the lane.

At the corner, Jasper, furious, stopped and looked back. Silence.

Then—*rattle!*—a stone bounced off his hat. A distant yell echoed: “Wake-Cock! Warning!” followed by a crowing sound, as if a demon-bred rooster had risen from hell.

Jasper turned the corner into safety, and Durdles stumbled into his yard, tripping over rubble as if ready to fall face-first into an unfinished tomb.

Jasper returned by another route to his gatehouse. He unlocked the door quietly and found the fire still burning. From a locked press, he took a peculiar pipe—not for tobacco—but filled it carefully with something else. Using a small tool, he adjusted the contents precisely, then climbed a short staircase to two rooms.

One was his bedroom. The other belonged to his nephew.

Light burned in both.

His nephew slept peacefully, undisturbed.

Jasper stood over him, pipe unlit, staring down with intense focus. Minutes passed. Then, moving silently, he crossed to his room, lit the pipe, and surrendered himself to the visions it summoned—spectres born of midnight smoke and hidden sorrow.
