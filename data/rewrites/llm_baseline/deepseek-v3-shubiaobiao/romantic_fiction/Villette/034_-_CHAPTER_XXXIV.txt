The afternoon sun hung heavy behind a veil of sullen clouds when Madame Beck summoned me to her sitting room.

“Lucy,” she said with that smooth efficiency I knew well, “you have no engagements, I trust? There are a few trifles in town I should like you to procure for me.”

Ever the obliging governess, I agreed at once. A list was pressed into my hands—silks for embroidery, wools for the girls’ needlework—and I had nearly escaped into the humid air when her voice called me back.

“Ah, one more thing!” She emerged from the adjoining room with a basket of hothouse fruit, each piece a jewel nestled among glossy leaves. “A small favor. You will deliver this to Madame Walravens—Rue des Mages, Numéro 3. It is her fête day. And Lucy,” she added, her tone turning deliberate, “you must place it in her hands yourself. She is… particular.”

The commission was peculiar, but I was in no position to refuse. The errands in town passed without incident, though the shopkeepers moved as if weighted by the thickening air. By the time I turned toward the Basse-Ville, the sky had darkened to a molten bronze, the horizon flickering with the promise of storm.

The Rue des Mages was no street at all, but a forgotten square where time had settled like dust. Grass split the flagstones; the houses loomed, their grandeur long since abandoned. At its heart stood the church of the Magi, its spires clawing at the bruised heavens. The place was silent, save for the slow tap of an old priest’s staff as he shuffled away from the very door I sought.

The woman who answered was a relic herself—gnarled, clad in antique lace and sabots, her face a web of resentment. She would have barred my way had the priest not intervened, his voice a dry murmur in the local dialect. Reluctantly, she admitted me.

The salon was a cavern of shadows, its stained-glass windows dulled by the storm’s approach. A smaller chamber opened beyond, its walls lined with the ghosts of opulence. And then—impossible—a painting on the wall shuddered, rolled back like a curtain, revealing a stone passage. From its depths came a tapping, deliberate as a countdown, until *she* emerged.

Madame Walravens.

A dwarf in brocade, her spine twisted, her chin bristling with a silver beard. Jewels crusted her fingers—rings of amethyst and blood-red garnet. But it was her eyes that struck me: pits of malice set in a face that might have been a century old.

*“Que me voulez-vous?”* Her voice was a rasp, more specter than woman.

I offered the basket and Madame Beck’s felicitations.

She sneered. “Tell her I buy my own fruit. As for her pretty words—*je m’en moque!*”

Thunder split the sky as she turned. Lightning seared the room, etching her hunched silhouette against the wall. Then she was gone, her ivory staff clicking like a beetle’s carapace against the floor.

The storm broke in earnest as the priest—Père Silas, I knew him now—guided me to a quieter room. An oratory, where a portrait of a pale young nun watched us.

“Justine Marie,” he said, noting my gaze. “Beloved. Taken too soon.”

The tale unfolded like a litany: a love thwarted, a life abandoned to the cloister, a death that left a wound no years could heal. And the man who bore it—M. Paul Emanuel, his pupil, his benefactor—who even now supported the wretched Walravens, though she had destroyed his happiness.

“He gives all,” Père Silas murmured. “His heart is buried with her.”

The pieces aligned. Madame Beck’s errand. The priest’s presence. The portrait. All threads pulled by unseen hands.

As I left, the rain fell in rods. Père Silas’s parting words followed me—*“Daughter, you shall be what you shall be!”*—an oracle heavy with intent.

I walked fast, the storm at my back. Rome had fingers in every shadow, but M. Emanuel’s grief was real. And I wondered, as the lightning scored the sky, what game was being played—and whose move would come next.
