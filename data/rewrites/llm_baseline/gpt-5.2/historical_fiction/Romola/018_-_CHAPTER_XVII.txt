The loggia on the roof of Bardo’s house rose above the neighbouring tiles and chimneys, a square gallery wrapped round by four walls as if the building wore a crown. On the side that looked toward the street, the roof rested on columns; on the other three sides it was guarded by a wall pierced with arched openings, so that at the back—where one looked over a tangle of mean little dwellings towards the hill of Bogoli—Romola could pace in sunlight and shadow without the eyes of the street finding her.

Near one of those arches, close to the door by which he had come up, Tito waited.

The light fell slantwise across the stones, warming them, drawing out a faint mineral smell from mortar and old rain, and yet it seemed to him a mocking brightness, as if day itself had taken sides against him and made his fears stand out sharply on the pavement. He had never built his hopes on Romola’s passion being strong enough to overbear the revulsion that must follow her discovery of his secret: he was too keen-sighted—too practised in reading the limits of other hearts—for that sort of vanity. Her love, he knew, had grown from her belief in him; and if belief were plucked out by the roots, the flower would wither at once.

And yet, as he pictured her coming towards him—radiant, dignified, made deliciously mortal by the softness of those hazel eyes—there rose in him an ignoble wish that she had been something less than she was, if only that she might have let him clasp and kiss her without judgment, before they parted. He had had no full caress from her—no unmeasured intimacy; only, now and then, a long look that was like a touch, a kiss that was quickly given and quickly withdrawn, a pressure of the hand whose warmth lingered on his palm like spice on a cup. He had so often longed for them to be alone. They were going to be alone now; and he saw her already, in his imagination, standing apart from him with that inexorable purity which was harder to beguile than any anger.

His heart leapt as the door moved. Romola was there.

It was like lightning—too swift for separate seeing. He felt rather than beheld the glory that seemed to hang about her head, the tender, tear-laden appeal of her eyes; he heard rather than understood the cry with which she said, “Tito!” And in that same instant she was in his arms, sobbing with her face hidden against him, as if her grief had found at last the one breast where it might break without shame.

How she had yearned through the night-watches to see that bright face again! The new image of death; the bewildering doubt poured into her by a story of a life so unlike her own that it stood outside her sympathy like a foreign tongue; above all, the haunting vision which she seemed not only to hear in that low, gasping voice, but to live through—as if it had been her own dream—had made her more conscious than ever that it was Tito who had first brought warmth into her days, who had first loosened the iron pain of her brother’s remembrance and let hope run, like a stream unblocked, through her heart.

She would tell Tito everything: there was no other being to whom she could pour it out. She had held herself rigid all the morning before her father, biting back tears as one bites back an outcry; but here the long-pent sob might come forth. Proud and self-contained to the world, she was, in her love, as unguarded as a child.

“My Romola! my goddess!” Tito murmured, and his voice had that rich caressing quality which made words seem like touch. He clasped her gently, and kissed the thick golden ripples where her hair lay warm against her neck. A breath of her came to him—something like violets crushed in the palm with the salt of tears—and he felt himself, for the moment, in paradise. Disgrace, shame, parting—there was no fear of them any longer. This happiness was too strong to be marred even by the knowledge that she was deceived in him; rather, he rejoiced in her delusion, as men rejoice in a well-spiced dish whose ingredients are not questioned. Concealment had been wisdom. If he regretted anything, it was his needless dread—unless, indeed, dread were worth all suffering for the sake of this sudden rapture.

Her sobs spent themselves; she lifted her head. They stood silent, looking at each other with that sweet wonder which belongs to young love when it has, for the first time, no witnesses. Her long white hands rested on his dark-brown curls; his dark fingers were lost in the streaming gold of her hair, as if his touch could not bear to leave it. Each face was to the other a new world of meaning. A cold sadness pressed on Romola’s heart, making her linger the more in that silent nearness, as one lingers in the scent of bread when the mind has been sickened with other odours; and Tito did not even seek her lips, because to change their stillness seemed to risk breaking the spell.

“Tito,” she said at last, “it has been altogether painful, but I must tell you everything. Your strength will help me to resist the impressions that will not be shaken off by reason.”

“I know, Romola—I know he is dead,” said Tito. His long lustrous eyes told nothing of the many wishes that would have welcomed that death long ago, if wishing had been a knife with an edge. Romola read only her own pure thoughts in those dark depths, as in happy dreams we read letters clearly where, waking, we should see only blots.

“So changed, Tito! It pierced me to think it was Dino. And so strangely hard: not a word to my father; nothing but a vision he wanted to tell me. And yet it was so piteous—the struggling breath, and the eyes that seemed to look toward the crucifix, and yet not to see it. I shall never forget it; it seems as if it would come between me and everything I shall look at.”

Her voice faltered; the heart swelled in her again until it forced her to break off. But the need to disburden her mind to Tito urged her to master herself, as one steadies a trembling hand to keep it from spilling something precious.

“It was strange, Tito. The vision was about our marriage, and yet he knew nothing of you.”

“What was it, my Romola? Sit down and tell me,” said Tito, leading her to the bench near by.

A quick fear had shot through him that this “vision” might touch Baldassarre—as if some long-buried taste could suddenly rise again on the tongue. The fear made him crave a change of place, as if moving his body could move the thought away.

Romola told him all that had passed from her entrance into San Marco, leaving out hardly one of Dino’s words, so burned were they into her memory. She spoke with the fidelity of one repeating a chant heard once, where the cadence and the very pauses are remembered; and behind her speech there seemed to linger the hush of that place—the muffled footfalls, the distant murmur of prayer, the oppressive stillness broken now and then by a sound like a drawn breath. Even here under the open arches, the loggia’s air felt to her as if it carried an echo of cloistered walls.

But when she reached the end of the vision she paused. The rest—the dying look, the last gasps—came too vividly to be uttered, and she sat staring outward through the arch as if the hill and the roofs were far away and she saw only the chapter-house again. Tito’s mind, meanwhile, was at ease. That vague monkish warning had swept over him like a white mist and left no stain. He waited in silence, expecting her to speak again.

“I took it,” she said at last, as if he had been following the unspoken thread of her thoughts. “I took the crucifix; it is down below in my bedroom.”

“And now, my Romola,” Tito answered, leaning towards her with entreaty, “you will banish these ghastly thoughts. The vision was an ordinary monkish vision, bred of fasting and fanatic ideas. It surely has no weight with you.”

“No, Tito; no. But poor Dino—he believed it was a divine message. It is strange,” she went on, meditatively, “this life of men possessed with fervid beliefs that seem like madness to their fellow-beings. Dino was not a vulgar fanatic; and that Fra Girolamo—his very voice seems to have penetrated me with a sense that there is some truth in what moves them: some truth of which I know nothing.”

“It was only because your feelings were highly wrought,” said Tito, in the tone he used when he wanted to lay a cool hand over a feverish mind. “Your brother’s state of mind was no more than a form of that theosophy which is the common disease of excitable, dreamy natures in all ages; the same ideas your father’s old antagonist, Marsilio Ficino, pores over in the New Platonists—only your brother’s passionate temper drove him to act out what other men write and talk about. And for Fra Girolamo, he is simply a narrow-minded monk with a gift of preaching, and of infusing terror into the multitude. Any words, any voice, would have shaken you then. When your mind has had a little repose, you will judge of such things as you have always done before.”

“Not about poor Dino,” said Romola. “I was angry with him; my heart seemed to close against him while he was speaking. But since then I have thought less of what was in my own mind and more of what was in his. Oh, Tito! it was very piteous to see his young life coming to an end in that way. That yearning look at the crucifix when he was gasping for breath—I can never forget it. Last night I looked at the crucifix a long while, and tried to see that it would help him, until at last it seemed to me by the lamplight as if the suffering face shed pity.”

“My Romola,” Tito said, and his tenderness took on an edge of impatience, “promise me you will resist such thoughts. They are fit for sickly nuns, not for my golden-tressed Aurora, who looks made to scatter all such twilight fancies.” He added, in a lower tone, as if reminding her of the world beyond their solitude, “Try not to think of them now; we shall not long be alone together.”

He turned her face towards him with a gentle touch. Romola’s eyes had been fixed absently on the arched opening, but she had not seen the distant hill; she had been in San Marco still, among pale images of sorrow and death. His hand and voice recalled her to the warm present. In the sunlight she saw again the rich dark beauty of Tito’s face, which seemed to gather about it all images of joy—vine tendrils and purple grapes in her imagination, the strong corn swelling under the vibrating heat, bright-winged creatures darting and settling among flowers, and, mingled with it all, the remembered rhythm of festival sounds: the beat of feet on hard earth, the ring of cymbals lifted high, the quick sting of laughter, the throbbing of strings as if a heart had been made audible. Even the city’s air came to her as a kind of music—pigeon wings, distant voices, the far-off hammering that Florence never let die, a shrillness in merriment as if joy itself had a keen edge.

What thought could reconcile the worn anguish of Dino’s face—his straining after something invisible—with this satisfied strength and beauty that shone on her like a sun that had never known dusk? Were these indeed in one world, or were there only clashing deities to be worshipped blindfold—first in mad gladness, then in wailing? For an instant Romola felt a dizziness, a sudden need of something to grasp, as if she stood between two languages and could not translate her experience from one into the other. But no answer rose to meet that need; and it vanished before the returning rush of young sympathy with the glad, loving presence before her.

“Your mind lingers apart from our love, my Romola,” Tito murmured, softly reproachful. “It seems a forgotten thing to you.”

She looked at him in silence until the sadness melted from her own face, as frost melts when the sun returns.

“My joy!” she said, and the fullness of her voice sounded like a promise.

“Do you really care for me enough, then, to banish those chill fancies,” said Tito with his bright smile, “or shall you always be suspecting me as the Great Tempter?”

“How should I not care for you more than for everything else?” Romola answered. “Everything I had felt before in all my life—about my father, and about my loneliness—was a preparation to love you. You would laugh at me, Tito, if you knew what sort of man I used to think I should marry—some scholar with deep lines in his face, like Alamanno Rinuccini, and with rather grey hair, who would agree with my father in taking the side of the Aristotelians, and be willing to live with him. I used to think about the love I read of in the poets, but I never dreamed that anything like it could happen to me here in Florence, in our old library. And then you came, Tito, and were so much to my father, and I began to believe that life could be happy for me too.”

“My goddess! is there any woman like you?” Tito said, and there was real wonder in his fondness at the majesty and simplicity mingled in her.

“But, dearest,” he went on, more timidly, as if he feared to touch what might be a sore place, “if you minded more about our marriage, you would persuade your father and Messer Bernardo not to think of any more delays. But you seem not to mind about it.”

“Yes, Tito, I will. I do mind. But I am sure my godfather will urge more delay now, because of Dino’s death. He has never agreed with my father about disowning Dino, and you know he has always said that we ought to wait until you have been at least a year in Florence. Do not think hardly of my godfather. I know he is prejudiced and narrow, but yet he is very noble. He has often said it is folly in my father to want to keep his library apart, that it may bear his name; yet he would try to get my father’s wish carried out. That seems to me very great and noble—that power of respecting a feeling which he does not share or understand.”

“I have no rancour against Messer Bernardo for thinking you too precious for me,” Tito said; and it was true. Messer Bernardo’s caution could not sting him, since it was grounded in Romola’s worth.

“But your father, then, knows of his son’s death?” Tito asked.

“Yes. I told him—I could not help it. I told him where I had been, and that I had seen Dino die; but nothing else; and he has commanded me not to speak of it again. But he has been very silent this morning, and has had those restless movements which always go to my heart; they look as if he were trying to get outside the prison of his blindness. Let us go to him now. I persuaded him to try to sleep, because he slept little in the night. Your voice will soothe him, Tito: it always does.”

“And not one kiss? I have not had one,” said Tito, in the gentle, dependent tone that made his reproach sound like a plea.

A sweet pink blush spread over Romola’s face and neck with the quickness of light. She bent towards him, and their lips met with a reverence as if such a thing could never become common. It seemed to her impossible that kisses could ever be like the daily bread one eats without thinking—yet even bread, she knew, is made of innumerable small acts, patient and repeated, which are forgotten only because they are faithful.

“Let us walk once round the loggia,” Romola said, “before we go down.”

They moved slowly along the front of the house where the city opened itself to sight. Roofs lay piled like broken waves; beyond them the river’s line was guessed rather than seen; on the further side the buildings rose again, and the air carried up a mixture of sounds—voices that struck sharply and died, a cry from a distant vendor, a burst of laughter with a thin metallic ring, the ceaseless undertone of tools and traffic. Even here, in sunlight, Florence seemed to Tito to make its music with hard instruments.

“There is something grim and grave to me always about Florence,” he said, pausing. “And even in its merriment there is something shrill and hard—biting rather than gay. I wish we lived in Southern Italy, where thought is broken, not by weariness, but by delicious languors such as never seem to come over the ingenia acerrima Florentina. I should like to see you under that southern sun, lying among the flowers, subdued into mere enjoyment, while I bent over you and touched the lute and sang to you some little unconscious strain that seemed all one with the light and the warmth. You have never known that happiness of the nymphs, my Romola.”

“No,” she said, and her eyes softened with the confession as with a taste long imagined and never tried. “But I have dreamed of it often since you came. I am very thirsty for a deep draught of joy—for a life all bright like you. But we will not think of it now, Tito; it seems to me as if there would always be pale sad faces among the flowers, and eyes that look in vain. Let us go.”
