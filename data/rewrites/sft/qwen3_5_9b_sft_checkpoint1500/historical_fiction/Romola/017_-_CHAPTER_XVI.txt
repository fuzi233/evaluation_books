A Florentine Joke.

The dawn broke early on the morning following Tito's return from Bratti's shop in the winding alley of the Ferravecchi. The Genoese stranger had departed with the onyx ring, leaving behind fifty florins in Tito's pocket. For a fleeting moment, a shadow crossed his mind: if Fortune, in one of her cunning turns, had spared him the necessity of fleeing Florence, perhaps it would have been wiser to keep the ring. It bore the weight of peculiar memories and predilections, symbols of a past he had tried to bury. Yet, it was a trifling loss, barely worthy of emphasis, and in that instant, Tito's faith in fortune had evaporated. The feverish, desperate excitement of the previous night's alarms had cooled into a heavy, regretful lethargy. He found himself wishing he had never courted disgrace by shrinking from the obligations his neighbors demanded, for he cherished the pleasures that flowed solely from their esteem.

Actions, however, are like children born to us; they grow and move independently of our will. While a child may be silenced, a deed cannot be strangled; it possesses an indestructible vitality, thriving both within and outside our consciousness. This terrible, autonomous life of his actions pressed hard upon Tito for the first time, a weight he had not yet fully anticipated.

Bound for his lodging in the Piazza di San Giovanni, Tito deliberately avoided the Mercado Vecchio, his nearest route, lest he encounter Tessa. He was not in the mood to seek anything, save the first sign of how his circumstances might shift. The square itself, bathed in the warm, golden sunlight of a clear autumn morning where the dew still lingered, invited a languid idleness unburdened by fatigue. It was a festive day; the soft warmth seemed to seep into the bones with an invitation to lounge and observe. Around the octagonal Baptistery, stalls were unfurled with fruits and blossoms, while laden mules stood patiently, absorbed in their feedbags, their drivers perhaps having slipped inside the sacred doors to kneel before the Virgin Mary on her Nativity. Upon the broad marble steps of the Duomo, scattered clusters of beggars and chattering idlers formed a tapestry of daily life: an elderly crone with white hair and a weather-beaten countenance coaxed a round-capped infant to feel the warmth of the stone beneath its tiny bare feet, while a nearby dog sniffed the scene with suspicion; further along, two shaggy-headed boys leaned forward to watch a small, pale cripple carving a visage into a cherry-stone; and atop the wide platform, men knotted and unknotted ribbons in laughter-filled chatter, or stood in tight couples, gesticulating with eager intensity.

Yet, the largest and most vital assembly of loafers drew Tito's attention. It was the peak hour for Nello, and in this balmy season, when clients thronged, most men preferred the shade of the shop's pretty red-and-white awning to the stifling confines of narrow chambers. Though it is hardly a sublime posture—to sit with a lathered chin thrown backward, having one's nose treated as a handle—it remained the fashion of Florentine respectability. Astonishingly, men regarded one another with grave seriousness when all were thus attired. It was also the hour when yesterday's gossip was freshest, and the barber's tongue flourished in tandem with his razor. The deft interplay of blade and sponge seemed propelled by a common spring. Tito foresaw that escape from the circle was impossible; he would have to smile, retort, and maintain perfect composure. Very well, it was merely the trial of swallowing bread-and-cheese pills. To let the mere anticipation of discovery paralyze him was the mark of a man with weak nerves.

Just as he settled into this resolve, a hand descended upon his shoulder. No prior fortification could shield him from the jarring, unpleasant sensation of that sudden contact. As he turned his face, the shock betrayed itself, but the owner of the hand, possessing an aura of such malignant charm, broke into a light laugh. He was a youth of Tito's own age, with keen features, a small, closely clipped head, and a close-shaven lip and chin suggesting a mind stripped of non-essential material. His keen eyes burned with hope and friendliness, qualities that often precede bitter closure for those who later close their eyes to a disappointed world. At this juncture, however, prophecy spoke of Niccolò Machiavelli as a man of boundless promise, destined to repair the shattered fortunes of his ancient lineage.

"Why, Melema, what evil dream plagued you last night that you mistook my light grasp for that of a *sbirro* or worse?"

"Ah, Messer Niccolò!" Tito replied, instantly regaining his poise. "It must have been an excess of sluggish blood in my veins this morning that shuddered at the approach of your wit. But the truth is, I have endured a bad night."

"That is ill fortune, for you are expected to shine without obstruction today in the Rucellai Gardens. I take it you are attending?"

"Messer Bernardo honored me with an invitation," Tito said, "yet I shall be occupied elsewhere."

"Aha! I recall—you are in love," Machiavelli observed, shrugging. "Otherwise, you would never harbor such inconvenient engagements. We are to feast on peacock and ortolans beneath the loggia amidst Bernardo Rucellai's rare trees. There will be the finest spirits of Florence and the choicest wines. Alas, since Piero de' Medici attends, the choice spirits may well drown in the capricious verses of impromptu rhymesters. I detest that game; it is a contrivance for the triumph of petty minds, who are always most inspired by the smallest occasions."

"What of Piero de' Medici and small wits, Messer Niccolò?" asked Nello, whose slender frame currently dominated the Herculean bulk of Niccolò Caparra.

That renowned artisan in iron, last seen with bared muscles and a leathern apron in the Mercato Vecchio, wore holiday attire this morning. Seated submissively while Nello skipped around him, applying lather, seizing his nose, and scraping with magical swiftness, Caparra resembled a lion clad in linen and tunic, preparing to enter society.

"A private secretary shall never ascend in the world if he couples the great and the small in such a manner," Nello continued. "When great men are forbidden to wed their sons and daughters as they please, small men must not expect to marry their words as they please. Have you heard the news, Domenico Cennini, that Pagolantonio Soderini struck Ser Piero da Bibbiena a blow for urging Piero de' Medici to interfere with the union between young Tommaso Soderini and Fiammetta Strozzi? He is to be exiled as ambassador to Venice as punishment?"

"I know not which I envy most," Machiavelli mused, "the offense or the punishment. The offense will render him the most popular man in all Florence, while the punishment will send him among the sole people in Italy who understand how to govern their own affairs."

"Yes, if Soderini remains long enough at Venice," Cennini remarked, "he may catch the Venetian fashion and bring it home. The Soderinis have long been allies of the Medici, but this affair may open Pagolantonio's eyes to the utility of our old Florentine trick: discarding the old harness when it galls, provided we have not utterly lost the knack in these last fifty years."

"Not we," declared Niccolò Caparra, reveling in the freedom of his restored lips. "Eat eggs during Lent, and the snow will melt. That is my counsel when the folk grow noisy over their cups at San Gallo, discoursing of insurrection (*romor*). Never scheme a *romor*; you might as well attempt to fill the Arno with buckets. When there is sufficient water, the river will be full, and that awaits the readiness of the torrent."

"Caparra, that oracular wisdom stems from my excellent shaving," Nello grinned. "You could never have uttered it with that dark rust upon your chin. *Ecco*, Messer Domenico, I am now ready for you. By the way, my learned scholar," Nello continued, noting Tito's movement toward the door, "here has been old Maso seeking you, but your nest lay empty. He returns presently. The old man appeared mournful and hurried. I trust there is no calamity in the Via de' Bardi."

"Doubtless Messer Tito knows that Bardo's son is dead," interjected Cronaca, who had just stepped forward.

Tito's heart leaped—had the death occurred before Romola saw him?

"No, I had not received word," Tito replied, maintaining a composure commensurate with the occasion. He turned and leaned against the doorpost, feigning resignation to staying. "I knew his sister had gone to visit him. Did he pass away before her arrival?"

"No," said Cronaca. "I was in San Marco at the time and witnessed her emerging from the chapter-house with Fra Girolamo. He informed us that the dying man's breath was preserved, almost by miracle, so that he might disclose matters to his sister."
