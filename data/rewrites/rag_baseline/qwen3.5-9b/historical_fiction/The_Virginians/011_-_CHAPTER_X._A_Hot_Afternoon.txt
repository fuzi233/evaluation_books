The heat pressed down on Castlewood like a physical weight, distorting the air above the verandah where General Braddock and his entourage lingered. Once the formalities of the evening were concluded, the boys retreated to their room, the silence of the corridor giving way to the frantic, circular logic of their youth. They could not comprehend the transaction unfolding downstairs. How could the representative of the Marquises of Esmond—their lineage, their history—marry the younger son of a colonial surveyor? To hand over their mother, their home, their entire world to a man of thirty-two, a stranger to their blood, seemed a grotesque error. Harry's mind raced toward immediate action: he imagined bursting into his mother's chamber, where her maids were stripping her of the jewels she had worn for the feast, and demanding they flee to their small estate, living in obscurity forever rather than submit to this unnatural union.

George, however, possessed a colder clarity. He outlined a strategy that felt inevitable, a mathematical solution to a moral problem. His mother could not marry a man connected to the battlefield in such a specific way—a man they had fought, or whom they had killed. Therefore, the obstacle had to be removed physically. "We must have him out, Harry," George stated, his voice carrying the flat certainty of someone solving an equation.

Harry recognized the brilliance of the deduction instantly. "Mother cannot marry our murderer," he agreed, the realization settling over him with the gravity of a verdict. "She won't be that cruel. And if we strike him down, the question is settled. *Cadit quaestio.*" He looked at his brother, the envy and admiration warring in his chest, and asked if he should dispatch his servant with a challenge to Colonel George immediately.

"My dear Harry," the elder replied, a hint of complacency coloring his tone regarding his own affair of honor at Quebec, "you are not accustomed to matters of this magnitude."

"No," Harry admitted, sighing, his gaze fixed on his senior with a mixture of longing and inferiority.

"We cannot insult a gentleman within our own walls," George continued, his posture stiffening into something resembling majesty. "The laws of honor forbid such inhospitality. But we can ride out with him. Once the park gates are shut, we can speak our minds plainly."

"That we can!" Harry cried, seizing his brother's hand. "That we will! Say it, Georgy..." His face flushed violently, and his brother paused, asking what he intended to say.

"This is my turn, brother," Harry pleaded, his voice rising. "If you undertake the campaign, I deserve the other affair. Truly, I do." He prayed for this elevation, desperate to claim his due.

"The head of the house must lead," George said, his demeanor shifting to a superior calm. "If I fall, my Harry will avenge me. But I must fight George Washington, Hal. And it is best I do so; for indeed, I hate him the most. Was it not he who counseled my mother to order that wretch, Ward, to seize me?"

"Ah, George," the younger brother interjected, attempting a note of peace, "you ought to forget and forgive."

"Forget? Never, sir, as long as memory serves. You cannot command remembrance to vanish from a man's mind. A wrong committed yesterday remains a wrong tomorrow. I have never harmed anyone knowingly, and I will not suffer harm if I can prevent it. I think poorly of Mr. Ward, but not so poorly as to believe he will ever forgive the blow with the ruler. Colonel Washington is our enemy, mine specifically. He advised one injustice against me and plots a greater. We must punish him."

The old Bordeaux had ignited George's pale complexion into a flame. Harry, his devoted follower, could only admire his brother's haughty bearing and rapid declamation, preparing himself with his customary obedience to follow his leader. That night, they retired to their beds, the elder issuing special instructions to his junior to maintain civility toward all guests while they remained under their mother's roof.

Decorum and a reluctance to recount scandals forbade naming which of Madame Esmond's guests first succumbed to the weight of her hospitality. The respectable descendants of the Talmadges and Danvers, aides-de-camp to the General, might prefer ignorance of their ancestors' intoxications a century prior; yet the gentlemen themselves bore no shame, and there is little doubt they or their companions were tipsy twice or thrice weekly. Imagine them staggering to bed, supported by sympathetic negroes; imagine their vinous General, too stout a drinker to surrender to only six bottles of Bordeaux, escorted to his chamber by the young gentlemen of the house, quickly succumbing to the sleep that friendly Bacchus grants. The good lady of Castlewood observed the condition of her guests without surprise or horror. She rose early, providing cooling drinks for their parched throats, which the servants distributed to their respective rooms.

At breakfast, one of the English officers rallied Mr. Franklin, who consumed no alcohol whatsoever, refusing the morning draught of toddy. The officer pointed out how the Philadelphia gentleman forfeited two pleasures simultaneously. The young man replied that the disease was pleasant and the remedy delicious, laughing as he proposed continuing the cycle. Colonel Washington, the General's new American aide-de-camp, remained sober and serene. The British officers vowed to intervene, to teach him the ways of the English army, but the Virginian gentleman gravely declined, stating he cared not to learn that particular branch of English military education.

The widow, exhausted by the cares of a grand dinner followed by a substantial breakfast, had scant leisure to scrutinize her sons' behavior closely. Yet she noted that George treated her favorite, Colonel Washington, with scrupulous politeness, extending the same courtesy to all other guests.

Before Mr. Braddock departed, he held a private audience with Madame Esmond, formally offering to adopt her son. When the arrangements for George's departure were finalized between his mother and his future chief, Madame Esmond, though she felt the dread, showed no squeamish terror regarding the dangers of the bottle, recognizing them as among the severest evils her son would face. She knew her boy must take his part in the world, encountering his portion of good and evil. "Mr. Braddock is a perfect fine gentleman in the morning," she declared stoutly to her aide-de-camp, Mrs. Mountain. "Though my father did not drink, it is certain that many of the finest companies in England do." The jovial General shook hands with George, who presented himself to his Excellency after the maternal interview concluded. The General welcomed him and instructed him to attend at Frederick in three days, shortly thereafter setting forth on the expedition.

Now the great coach was requisitioned again. The General's escort pranced around it; the other guests and their servants mounted their horses. The lady of Castlewood accompanied his Excellency to the steps of the verandah. The young gentlemen followed, standing on either side of the coach door. The guard trumpeter blew a shrill blast; the negroes shouted, "Huzzay, and God save de King," as Mr. Braddock graciously took leave of his hospitable entertainers and rolled away on his road to headquarters.

As the boys ascended the steps, the Colonel was once more taking leave of their mother. No doubt she had been urging George into his namesake's care again. Colonel Washington said, "With my life. You may depend on me," as the lads returned to their mother and the few remaining guests in the porch. The Colonel was booted and ready to depart. "Farewell, my dear Harry," he said. "With you, George, 'tis no adieu. We shall meet in three days at the camp."

Both young men were heading toward danger, perhaps toward death. Colonel Washington was taking leave of her, and she would not see him again before the campaign. No wonder the widow was deeply moved.

George Warrington watched his mother's emotion, interpreting it with a pang of malignant scorn. "Stay yet a moment, and console our mamma," he said, his countenance steady. "Only enough time to get ourselves booted, and my brother and I will ride with you a little way, George." George Warrington had already ordered his horses. The three young men were swiftly underway, their negro grooms trailing behind. Mrs. Mountain, knowing she had sown discord between them and trembling for the result, felt a vast relief that Mr. Washington had departed without a quarrel with the brothers, without, at any rate, an open declaration of love to their mother.

No man could be more courteous in demeanor than George Warrington toward his neighbor and namesake, the Colonel. The latter was pleased and surprised by his young friend's altered behavior. The shared prospect of danger, the necessity of future fellowship, the softening influence of the long friendship binding him to the Esmond family, and the tender farewells exchanged with the mistress of Castlewood inclined the Colonel to forget the unpleasantness of recent days, making him unusually friendly with his young companion. George was quite gay and easy; it was Harry who was now melancholy. He rode silently and wistfully beside his brother, keeping his distance from Colonel Washington, to whose side he had previously pressed eagerly. If the honest Colonel remarked on his young friend's conduct, he undoubtedly attributed it to Harry's known affection for his brother and his natural anxiety to remain close to George as the day of their parting approached.
