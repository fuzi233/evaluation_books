Durrance found his body-servant waiting for him when he crossed the fields to his home, "Guessens."
"You can turn out the lights and go to bed," he said, stepping through the hall into his study.

The room was more gun-room than study—though it bore that name. It held relics of a life lived boldly, fiercely, in motion. He sat for a time in silence, then rose and began pacing slowly in the dark. The dimness didn’t trouble him; he knew every inch of this place by touch, by memory. Scattered across shelves and tables were trophies: cups, goblets, medals—each one a chapter in a story only he could read.

He reached out, fingers tracing the contours of a thin-stemmed goblet. He remembered the day he won it—a steeplechase at Colchester, rain threatening, the sky gray as ash, the ploughed fields between hedges dull and flat. He ran his hand over the pewter pen-holder on his desk, the one from his college days at Oxford, when he rowed in the freshman fours. A silver hoof, mounted on the mantel, belonged to a horse he’d loved dearly—its name lost now, but not its spirit.

These objects were not mere decorations. They were records. Each one carried a scent, a sound, a heat of memory. And then came the guns—his true companions. He took them down from their racks, one by one. To him, they spoke. Not aloud, but in whispers only he could hear. A Remington rifle brought back a long, sweltering afternoon in the hills west of Berenice, where he had stalked a lion across sun-baked stone and shot it at three hundred yards just before sunset. Another rifle told of his first ibex in the Khor Baraka mountains, of antelope in the highlands north of Suakin. There was a small Greener shotgun—used on winter nights in a boat on the Salcombe estuary. With it, he’d taken his first mallard. He lifted it now, slid his hand along the barrel, felt the stock settle into the hollow of his shoulder—just as it had all those years ago.

But the voices grew louder. Too loud. Like Ethne’s violin once did after Harry Feversham vanished, echoing with a note too sharp, too piercing for comfort. As he touched the locks, knowing he could no longer see the sights, he felt the full weight of what he had lost—not just sight, but the world beyond the door. He replaced the guns, and something stirred within him: a fierce, almost desperate urge to forget his blindness. To pretend it did not exist. To believe so hard that it would cease to be.

And so he fled—not physically, but in mind—into the East.

The scent of spice and dust filled his senses. The clamor of markets, the domes of mosques against a steel-blue sky, the hot breath of the sun on his skin—it all pulled him forward. He walked through Port Said, marching with the endless line of steamers threading the Suez Canal. He heard the chant of Arab coalers, working in chains under the red glow of braziers at night, their naked figures moving like ghosts up and down the planks between barges and deck. He sailed past the red cliffs of Sinai, into the chill waters of the Gulf of Suez. He zigzagged down the Red Sea, watching the Great Bear dip low in the northern sky, while the Southern Cross blazed in the south. He passed Tor and Yambo, saw the white towers of Yeddah rise from the sea, admired the blackened woodwork of their carved windows. He wandered through the shaded bazaars, heart swelling with homesickness turned joy.

From Yeddah, he crossed the coral reefs into Suakin—where the desert stretched westward, vast and silent. He felt its rhythm—the soft padding of camel feet, the sudden stillness beneath rock-cones rising like islands from a motionless ocean, the fleeting blaze of sunset, the wind rustling through twilight, the sky turning pale green in the west, deep blue in the east. Then the planets dropped from nowhere, sweeping down to earth.

He dreamed himself there—free, strong, alive—until the familiar sounds of birds broke through: blackbirds chattering, swallows piping in the garden. Dawn. The world outside was bright with morning light.

He woke from the dream. No more journeys. His affliction had caged him. A chain was welded around his leg. He made his way up the stairs by feel, found his bed, and fell asleep as the sun climbed above the horizon.

* * *

At Dongola, on the great bend of the Nile south of Wadi Halfa, the sun burned already. The town stirred.

Under the few palm trees before the house of Emir Wad El Nejoumi, sport was prepared. A prisoner—white, captured near the wells of El Agia on the Arbain road—had been brought in during the night. Now he waited, bound and bare, for judgment.

Word spread like fire. Men, women, children flocked to the spectacle. Before the palms, an open space led to the emir’s gate. Behind, sand sloped down to the river. In the center stood a gallows—black and stark against the yellow earth.

Harry Feversham stood beneath the trees. Four Ansar soldiers guarded him. His clothes were gone. Only a torn jibbeh clung to his body, a cotton twist on his head. His shoulders and arms were blistered from sun and travel. His ankles were shackled, his wrists bound with rope, an iron collar locked about his neck, linked to a chain held by one of the guards.

Yet he smiled. He laughed. He danced. He seemed mad.

It was a performance. A gamble. If he could keep them guessing—whether he was truly insane or a spy with secrets for the tribes of Kordofan—then perhaps they would hesitate to kill him. Perhaps they would send him to Omdurman instead.

It was exhausting. But he endured.

Inside the house, the emir and his council debated. Outside, the gallows loomed. Time stretched. The sun climbed higher. No one came from the gate. The hours were worse than any fight. For the first time in weeks, he had nothing to do but wait. And fear crept in.

He remembered Berber—the hope of escape, the sudden lantern behind him, the rush of action. Here, there was only time—too much of it. He could anticipate every moment. He could see the vultures perched on his shoulders, circling his eyes. His heart sank. He felt faint—just as he had once, years before, when hounds broke cover and he sat trembling on his horse.

He glanced toward the gallows. But he also thought of Ethne. Of the fourth feather. Of the future he had dared to believe in. That thought steadied him.

He was glad of the collar. The fetters. They made his helplessness real—and thus, less terrifying. He summoned his strength. He laughed again. He mimicked the old woman who danced before him, singing of torture, of hellfire, of dismemberment. He nodded, bowed, mowed at her as if she sang of paradise.

Others blew war-trumpets into his ears. “Do you hear, Kaffir?” a child cried. “Blow louder! Blow louder!”

Feversham clapped his hands. “Good music!” he shouted.

Then came a tall warrior with a long spear. The crowd roared. He stepped forward, swinging the spear like a bowler preparing to bowl. Feversham looked wildly around—no escape. He thrust his chest forward, ready to meet the blow.

But the spear never struck.

One of the guards yanked the chain from behind. Feversham was thrown backward, half-choked, onto the ground. Three times this happened—each time met with laughter and cheers.

Then a soldier appeared at the gate.
“Bring him in!” he called.

The prisoner was dragged across the courtyard into a dark room.

For a moment, darkness. Then his eyes adjusted. He saw a tall man seated on an angareb—the Sudanese bedstead—two others squatting beside him. The man on the bed was the emir.

“You are a spy from Wadi Halfa,” the emir said.

“No,” Feversham replied, laughing. “I am a musician.”

A zither with broken strings was handed to him. He sat on the floor, fingers fumbling, breathing hard. Slowly, he played. The melody wavered—but it was the same tune Durrance had heard in Tewfikieh, the same Ethne had played the night before at Southpool. The only melody he knew.

When he finished, the emir repeated: “You are a spy.”

“I have told you the truth,” Feversham answered, stubborn.

Nejoumi changed tone. Food was brought—raw camel liver, salted, peppered. Feversham had no appetite. But he ate. He knew refusal would be seen as fear—and fear meant death.

As he chewed, Nejoumi asked questions—about Cairo’s fortifications, Assouan’s garrison, rumors of conflict between the Khedive and the Sirdar.

To each, Feversham replied:
“How should a Greek know such things?”

Nejoumi rose. Gave an order.

The soldiers seized Feversham and dragged him back into the sunlight. Water was poured on the rope binding his wrists—so it swelled, bit deeper into his flesh.

“Speak, Kaffir,” Nejoumi said. “You carry promises to Kordofan.”

Silence.

Feversham clung to his plan. He could not alter it—not now, not in fear. He had thought it through. He would not betray it.

A rope was placed around his neck. He was pushed beneath the gallows.

“Speak, Kaffir,” Nejoumi said. “So shall you live.”

Feversham smiled. Grimaced. Shook his head.

He was astonished—he did not fall to his knees. He did not beg. He felt no temptation to crawl. He wondered if the stories were true—that English prisoners went calmly to the scaffold because they were drugged. He was not drugged. Yet he stood firm. His heart pounded—not with terror, but with a strange excitement.

He did not think of Ethne. Not now. The dread of failing her, of the hope dying unfulfilled—none of that mattered. He had a part to play. He played it. That was all.

Nejoumi stared at him for a long moment. Then turned to the men ready to remove the angareb.

“To-morrow,” he said, “the Kaffir goes to Omdurman.”

Only then did Feversham feel the rope burn his wrists.
