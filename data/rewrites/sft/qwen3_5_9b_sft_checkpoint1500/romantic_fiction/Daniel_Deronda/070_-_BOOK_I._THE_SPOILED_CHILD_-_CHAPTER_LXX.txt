In the intricate tapestry of human life, where every season blends seamlessly into another like the eternal days of myth, joys and sorrows coexist in a single breath. While one hand gathers the ripe fruit, another scatters seeds into the earth; where one cultivates the vine, another crushes the grapes underfoot. Thus, within the span of a single existence, harvest and bloom remain eternally intertwined, until the Great Gardener calls us home and plants us afresh in His unseen fields.

Yet, among all the gifts of affection, none surpasses the profound solace of binding one's beloved life to our own. It grants us the sacred duty to guard their happiness, to transform hardship into comfort, and to turn the bitter wells of past suffering into springs of pure joy. This protective love burned brightly within Deronda for Mirah. Even as a child, her steps had stumbled upon sharp stones, and when he first laid eyes upon her young countenance, it appeared to him as a portrait of utter despair.

But now, she radiated like a flower of deep violet petals edged with pale ivory, blooming in the warm sun of contentment. To her, every shadow of grief belonged to a life shared with Deronda—a union she could name only "good." He marveled at the steady, radiant cheer that illuminated her very movements, at the peaceful stillness of her resting postures, and felt a swelling pride within himself that saving her from pain was a treasure sufficient unto itself. She knew nothing of Hans's internal battle nor of Gwendolen's heartache. Since Deronda's secret devotion to her had been revealed, she interpreted Gwendolen's anxious concern merely as the gratitude of a dependent friend, a perspective reinforced by Deronda's gentle, distant allusions to Mrs. Grandcourt. Mirah was ready to accept his word that he had been a rescuer to many souls; the only mystery to her lay in why destiny had chosen her above all others to walk constantly beside him.

Thus, when the bridal veil descended upon Mirah, it concealed no tremor of doubt, but only a holy awe at receiving a magnificent gift that demanded mighty stewardship. Under the velvet canopy stood perhaps the fairest pair ever united, worthy of prayers for offspring whose hearts beat in truth. Their lips spoke the marriage vows with an unblemished sincerity, and the blessing they invoked promised a fulfillment forged in the iron strength of their mutual pledge. By the ancient rites of their faith, they were wed. And just as no feast is truly grand unless it welcomes the kin of the heart, few would take offense to learn that the guest list included the entire Cohen household, minus the tiny infant who happily chattered through her teething troubles at home. How could Mordecai bear the thought of excluding those who had shared his hardships from sharing his triumph?

Mrs. Meyrick, having long ago conquered her biases, arrived alongside her three daughters, all buoyed by the knowledge that Mirah's marriage to Deronda had turned their family's history into a cherished romance. Each woman, mother and daughter alike, felt a generous swell of pride for having given so freely of themselves to the girl who needed them. They mused that if Hans could have been present, it would have been ideal, but Mab wisely noted that men often pay the price for being inconvenient. Perhaps Kate and Amy had loved Mr. Deronda too? Yet, being women, they spared themselves the ridicule of such folly.

Their sacrifices were honored by a speech from Mr. Cohen, possessing a rare quality of authenticity that defied convention. Jacob, eating with youthful vigor, contributed a string of soft, whinnying laughter to accompany his father's words—not irreverently, but with a lively sense that his family was shining. Adelaide Rebekah, clad in a fresh Sabbath gown, maintained a stately air of solemn responsibility throughout.

Mordecai's brilliant eyes, sunken deeply within their large sockets, swept the room with the benevolent warmth of a spirit already lifted to an aloof plane where selfish desires held no sway, leaving only pure sympathy alive. Yet, inevitably, his gaze drifted back to Deronda, alighting there with a renewed gleam of trusting affection.

The wedding feast was humble, yet Mirah departed with splendid gifts. Friends who had heard of the betrothal had devised gracious tokens. Sir Hugo and Lady Mallinger presented a complete outfit for Eastern travels, along with a precious locket inscribed, *"To the bride of our dear Daniel Deronda all blessings. H. and L. M."* The Klesmers offered a flawless watch, similarly adorned with a tender message.

But far more valuable than gold or jewels arrived for Deronda from the neighborhood of Diplow on the morning of his nuptials. It was a letter bearing these words:

"Do not think of me sorrowfully on your wedding-day. I have remembered your words—that I may live to be one of the best of women, who make others glad that they were born. I do not yet see how that can be, but you know better than I. If it ever comes true, it will be because you helped me. I only thought of myself, and I made you grieve. It hurts me now to think of your grief. You must not grieve any more for me. It is better—it shall be better with me because I have known you.

Preparations for the journey to the East commenced immediately, for Deronda could not refuse Ezra's wish that they depart at once, allowing the old man to travel with them rather than keeping them to watch over him. Deronda harbored little hope that Ezra's life would endure the voyage, for symptoms indicated that the final stage of his illness had arrived. Yet Ezra himself had declared, "Never mind where I die, so that I am with you."

However, Deronda did not set sail with them. One bright morning, he told Deronda, "Do not quit me today. I shall die before it is ended."

He requested to be dressed and seated in his easy chair as was his custom, with Deronda and Mirah flanking him on either side. For several hours, he remained strangely silent, making no effort to speak, though occasionally he looked at them with eyes filled with a restful, meaningful tranquility, as if to assure them that while this remaining fragment of time was difficult, an ocean of peace lay beneath him.

It was not until late in the afternoon, as the light faded, that he took hold of each hand in his own. Looking steadily at Deronda, he whispered, "Death is coming to me as the divine kiss which is both parting and reunion—which takes me from your bodily eyes and gives me full presence in your soul. Where thou goest, Daniel, I shall go. Is it not begun? Have I not breathed my soul into you? We shall live together."

He paused, and Deronda waited, sensing that further words might follow. But slowly, with evident effort, Ezra pressed their hands, raised himself, and uttered the Hebrew confession of the Divine Unity, a prayer that for generations has lingered on the lips of the dying Israelite. Then he sank gently back into his chair, saying no more. It was many hours before he ceased to breathe, cradled between the arms of Mirah and Deronda.

"Nothing is here for tears, nothing to wail
Or knock the breast; no weakness, no contempt,
Dispraise or blame; nothing but well and fair,
And what may quiet us in a death so noble."
