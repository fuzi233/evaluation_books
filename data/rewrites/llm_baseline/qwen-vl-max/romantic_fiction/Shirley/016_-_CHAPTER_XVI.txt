The relief fund had taken root and grown strong. Inspired by Miss Keeldar’s generosity, the tireless efforts of the three rectors, and the quiet yet steadfast support of their unmarried assistants—Mary Ann Ainley and Margaret Hall—a generous sum had been collected. Prudently managed, it offered real comfort to the unemployed poor in the region. For over two weeks now, no cloth had been burned, no mill or manor attacked across the three parishes. The air seemed lighter, the tension easing. Shirley felt a hopeful certainty that the storm she’d feared was passing. With summer approaching, trade would revive—it always did—and with it, peace would return. When peace came, what a surge of energy would ripple through commerce!

These were her usual words whenever she met Gérard Moore, her tenant, in private conversation. He listened quietly—too quietly for her liking. She would meet his silence with an impatient glance, silently demanding more: a deeper thought, a fuller explanation. Then, with a smile that softened his lips but left his brow solemn, he would reply, “I too place my hope in the finite nature of this war. That is the anchor of my faith. My plans depend on it.”

“And you know,” he added, “that I run Hollow’s Mill entirely on speculation. I sell nothing. There’s no market for my goods. I produce only for a future day—preparing to seize the first chance that opens. Three months ago, I couldn’t have done this. I’d exhausted every credit and every penny. You know who saved me—whose hand lent me the loan that kept me from ruin. It is because of that loan that I can now play this bold game I once feared I’d never dare again. Total collapse will follow loss, and profit remains uncertain. Yet I am at peace. As long as I can act, as long as I can strive—as long as my hands are free—I cannot be crushed. One year—perhaps just six months—of peace, and I shall be secure. As you say, peace will stir commerce. You’re right there. But about the calm we see now—the supposed good effect of your fund? I remain doubtful. Charity has never truly calmed the working class. It has never made them grateful. It cannot. Human nature won’t allow it. We ought not to need such humiliating aid if things were rightly ordered—and they feel that. We would feel it too, were we in their shoes. And whom should they thank? You, perhaps, or the clergy—but certainly not us, the mill-owners. They hate us more than ever. Besides, the discontented here are linked with those elsewhere. Nottingham, Manchester, Birmingham—all are hubs of unrest. Orders flow from leaders to followers; discipline is strict. No action is taken without careful planning. Like the sky in sultry weather, threatening thunder daily, yet clearing night after night, the danger isn’t gone—only delayed. The storm will break eventually. There’s a truth in both moral and physical skies.”

“Well, Mr. Moore,” she would say, each time bringing the talk to a close, “take care of yourself. If you believe I’ve ever helped you, repay me by promising to stay safe.”

“I do,” he said. “I’ll watch myself closely. I want to live, not die. The future stretches before me like Eden. And yet, when I gaze into the shadows of my paradise, I see a vision I prize more than any seraph or cherub—faintly glimpsed in distant glades.”

“What vision?” she asked.

“I see—”

The maid burst in with the tea tray.

Early May had begun with fine weather, then turned wet. But by its final week, with the moon’s change, the skies cleared. A fresh wind swept away the deep, rain-laden clouds, carrying them eastward until they vanished beyond the horizon, leaving behind a vault of pure blue, ready for the summer sun. On Whitsuntide morning, the sun rose broad and bright. The gathering of the schools was marked by perfect weather.

Whit-Tuesday was the grand day. In preparation, the two large schoolrooms at Briarfield—built by the current rector, mostly at his own cost—had been cleaned, whitewashed, repainted, and adorned with flowers and evergreens. Some blooms came from the rectory garden, two cartloads from Fieldhead, and a wheelbarrowful from De Walden, the home of Mr. Wynne. Twenty tables, each meant for twenty guests, were laid out with white cloths and benches. Above them hung at least twenty cages, each holding a canary. This was a local custom, cherished especially by Mr. Helstone’s clerk, who loved the birds’ sharp song and believed they sang loudest amid noise and confusion. These tables were not for the twelve hundred children gathered from the three parishes, but for the patrons and teachers. The children’s feast would be held outdoors. At one o’clock, the children would arrive; at two, they would be marshalled; until four, they would parade through the parish; then came the meal, followed by music and speeches in church.

Why Briarfield was chosen as the meeting point should be explained. It wasn’t the largest parish—Whinbury far surpassed it in population—or the oldest—Nunnely’s ancient church and moss-covered parsonage, nestled among age-old oaks, predated it. It was simply because Mr. Helstone wished it so. His will outweighed that of Boultby or Hall. The former could not challenge him, the latter would not. So they let him lead.

This annual event had always been difficult for Caroline Helstone. It forced her into public view, placing her before all the wealthy, respectable, influential people in the district. Without the kind presence of Mr. Hall, she would have felt utterly exposed. She had to stand out—walk at the head of the procession as the rector’s niece and head teacher of the first class—serve tea to a mixed crowd of ladies and gentlemen—all while lacking a mother, aunt, or chaperone. And she was naturally shy, deeply afraid of attention. No wonder she trembled at the thought of Whitsuntide.

But this year, Shirley would be with her. That changed everything. It transformed the ordeal into something almost joyful. Miss Keeldar was worth more than a hundred ordinary friends. Confident, spirited, easy in manner, aware of her standing yet never arrogant—just seeing her gave courage. The only worry was whether Shirley would arrive on time. She often dawdled, and Caroline knew her uncle would wait for no one. At exactly two o’clock, the church bells would ring, and the march would begin. Caroline must make sure Shirley didn’t miss it.

On Whit-Tuesday, Caroline rose with the sun. She and Fanny, Eliza spent the morning preparing the rectory parlours, setting out refreshments—wine, fruit, cakes—on the dining-room sideboard. Then came dressing. Her finest white muslin gown, fitting the occasion perfectly, was put on. Her new sash—a birthday gift from Margaret Hall, which she suspected Cyril himself had chosen—was tied by Fanny’s skilled hands. Her bonnet matched the sash; her simple white crape scarf completed the look. When finished, she looked not dazzling, but gently pleasing—soft in tone, pure in air, graceful in bearing. Her brown eyes and clear forehead revealed a mind modest, gentle, and harmonious, though thoughtful. She seemed harmless—so much so that lambs and doves might trust her, finding in her expression a kinship with their own quiet natures.

Yet she was flawed, imperfect. Fair in form and dress, yes—but not, as Cyril Hall once remarked, as good or great as the withered Miss Ainley, who now dressed herself in her best black gown and Quaker drab shawl in her small cottage room.

Caroline set off across secluded fields and hidden lanes toward Fieldhead. She moved swiftly beneath green hedges and over grassy meadows. No dust, no damp marred her clean hem or delicate sandals. After recent rains, everything was washed clean; under the sun, everything was dry. She walked boldly, through daisies and thickets, reached Fieldhead, and entered Miss Keeldar’s dressing room.

She was fortunate to have come—Shirley was still lying on a couch, lost in a book. Mrs. Pryor stood nearby, urging her to rise. Caroline wasted no words. She took the book, and began dressing Shirley herself. Indolent from the heat, cheerful in spirit, Shirley wanted to talk, laugh, linger. But Caroline, focused on punctuality, worked quickly—fastening strings, inserting pins. Only when the last hook and eye was secured did she pause to scold. “You’re very naughty,” she said, “to be so late. You look like the very picture of careless charm.” And indeed, Shirley did—though a charming picture of it.

She was quite the opposite of Caroline. Every fold of her dress, every line of her figure, spoke of style. Rich silk suited her better than simplicity. Her deep embroidered scarf fell gracefully, worn negligently but with flair. Her bonnet wreath sat well. Her taste in fashion, her skill in adornment, were natural to her. It all fit her—like the light in her eyes, the playful curve of her lips, her upright posture and light step. Caroline took her hand, hurried her downstairs, out into the open air. Together, they raced through the fields, laughing, looking like a snow-white dove and a jewel-hued bird of paradise flying side by side.

Thanks to Caroline’s promptness, they arrived in time. Even before the trees hid the church, they heard the bell tolling—measured, urgent—calling all to gather. The sound of many feet, many voices, drifted through the air. From a hilltop, they saw the Whinbury school approaching—five hundred children. Ahead marched the rector and curate, Boultby and Donne. Boultby loomed large in full canonicals, walking with priestly dignity beneath a shovel-hat, supported by a gold-headed cane. He wore the broadest black coat, the most ample vestments. Now and then, he flicked his cane and nodded with a dogmatic tilt of his hat toward Donne, his aide-de-camp.

Donne, thin and slight beside Boultby’s bulk, still managed to look every inch a curate. Everything about him was precise, self-satisfied—from his upturned nose and raised chin to his clerical gaiters, short trousers, and square-toed shoes.

Walk on, Mr. Donne! You’ve been judged. You think you look well. Whether the figures watching you from the hill agree is another matter.

Those figures—Caroline and Shirley—raced down as the procession passed. The churchyard was filled with children and teachers, all in their best holiday clothes. Despite hardship, they looked neat, even handsome. British pride in decency worked miracles. Poverty might strip an Irish girl bare, but it couldn’t rob an English girl of her sense of dignity. And Shirley, watching them, felt proud. Her timely generosity had eased many families’ worries and provided new frocks and bonnets for children. She knew it—and it lifted her heart. She couldn’t give charity like Miss Ainley—her nature wouldn’t allow it. But she found joy in knowing there was another way, one that suited her, that truly helped.

Caroline too felt glad. She’d given up dresses, ribbons, collars she couldn’t spare to outfit her pupils. Unable to donate money, she followed Miss Ainley’s example—giving time, labor, sewing for the children.

Not only the churchyard was crowded—the rectory garden teemed with people. Couples and groups strolled among lilacs and laburnums. Inside, at open windows, lively clusters of patrons and teachers stood. Behind the rectory, in the parson’s croft, the three parish bands waited with instruments. Fanny and Eliza, in crisp caps and gowns, white aprons, circulated among them, serving strong ale brewed weeks earlier under the rector’s supervision. Nothing he touched was allowed to be shabby. From building churches to cooking meals, he demanded grandeur, generosity, efficiency. Shirley shared this standard, and they admired each other’s ways.

Soon, Caroline and Shirley were among the company. Caroline moved easily—no longer retreating to a corner or hiding in her room. She wandered through the parlours, smiled, spoke freely, even before being addressed. She seemed reborn. It was Shirley’s presence that changed her. Seeing Miss Keeldar’s ease, her confidence, inspired her. Shirley had no fear of people. Unless someone was vulgar or arrogantly coarse, she welcomed them. Most men, unless proven truly bad, she assumed could be good—worth knowing, worth treating kindly. This openness made her beloved. Her wit was warm, not cutting; her laughter infectious. And her true friendships—especially with Caroline—were deeper, rooted in something different. Miss Helstone was her choice, her soulmate. Others, like the Misses Pearson, Sykes, Wynne, benefited from her charm, but weren’t central.

Donne entered the drawing-room just as Shirley sat on the sofa, the center of a circle. She had already forgotten her anger toward him. She bowed, smiled warmly. His reaction revealed his nature. He couldn’t decline gracefully—he lacked pride. Nor could he accept kindly. His punishment hadn’t humbled him; he felt no shame now. He wasn’t malicious enough to be actively cruel—only awkward, scowling, sheepish. He’d never forgive her, but neither could he rage. His sluggish nature prevented passion.

“He wasn’t worth a scene,” Shirley whispered to Caroline. “What a fool I was! To punish Donne for his petty grudge against Yorkshire is like crushing a gnat that bit a rhinoceros. Had I been a gentleman, I might have thrown him out physically. I’m glad I used only words. But he mustn’t come near me again. I don’t like him. He irritates me. There’s no fun in him. Malone is better sport.”

And indeed, Malone seemed determined to prove it. As soon as the words left her mouth, Peter Augustus approached—dressed in full splendor, gloves on, hair oiled and polished, holding a bouquet of cabbage roses, five or six fully bloomed. He presented them to Shirley with a flourish that even the finest artist couldn’t capture. Who now could claim Peter wasn’t a lady’s man? He’d gathered and given flowers—a poetic tribute to Love or Mammon. Hercules with a distaff was a pale image of Peter with roses. He must have known it, for he stepped back, chuckling softly to himself—then paused, turned, and stared to confirm he’d actually given the bouquet. Yes—there they were, six red blooms on purple satin, held by a pale hand with gold rings, ringlets framing a laughing face. Only half-hidden. Peter saw the laughter. He was mocked. His gallantry ridiculed. By two petticoats—Miss Helstone too was smiling. He felt seen through. His face darkened like a storm cloud. When Shirley looked up, his eyes locked onto hers—cold, furious. Malone had fire in his hatred. She saw it.

“Peter *is* worth a scene,” she whispered. “And he’ll get one, if he wants.”

Then, solemn in color but pleasant in expression, the three rectors appeared at the dining-room door. They’d been in church and now came to refresh themselves before the march. A large morocco chair awaited Dr. Boultby. He took it. Caroline, urged by Shirley to act as hostess, rushed forward with wine and macaroons. Boultby accepted, but noticed her not at all. He saw only the sweets. He took a handful. Mrs. Boultby insisted on mixing the wine with hot water, sugar, and nutmeg.

Mr. Hall stood near an open window, breathing in the scent of flowers, talking warmly to Miss Ainley. Caroline turned to him with pleasure. “He mustn’t serve himself—he must be waited on.” She fetched a salver, offering him variety. Margaret Hall joined them, then Shirley. The four women stood around their beloved pastor. They saw in him an earthly angel. Cyril Hall was their pope—infallible to them as Boultby was to his flock. A crowd gathered around old Helstone—twenty or more pressed close. No parson had ever commanded such loyalty. The curates clustered together like minor stars. Young ladies watched from afar, but dared not approach.

Mr. Helstone pulled out his watch. “Ten minutes to two,” he announced. “Time to form ranks. Come.” He grabbed his shovel-hat and marched off. All rose and followed in one sweeping movement.

The twelve hundred children formed three groups of four hundred. Bands stood at the rear of each. Teachers were posted between every twenty children. Helstone called out:

“Grace Boultby and Mary Sykes lead Whinbury.

“Margaret Hall and Mary Ann Ainley guide Nunnely.

“Caroline Helstone and Shirley Keeldar head Briarfield.”

Then he ordered:

“Now—march!”
