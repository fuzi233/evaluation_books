“Quit jittering,” O’Kelly told me, patting my shoulder like I was a spooked cart pony, “and don’t try to blow the rafters off. You’ve got a perfectly serviceable little tenor, but it’s not loud enough to rouse a napping seagull. Keep your head on, open your mouth like you’re yawning at a bad sermon, and you’ll be grand.”

It was 11 a.m., and we were huddled in the mouth of a narrow, smelly alley that led to the theatre’s stage door. For two whole weeks O’Kelly had been drilling me, an experience that had been far more stressful for him than it was for me. His wife, Mrs. O’Kelly, was a thin, sharp-faced woman I’d only caught glimpses of from Belsize Square while loitering waiting for O’Kelly’s secret signal, and she was dead set against any music that wasn’t hymn-adjacent. In a well-meaning attempt to yank O’Kelly away from his “sinful” love of secular tunes, she’d hauled their perfectly good piano out to be sold and replaced it with an American organ that sounded like a grieving walrus being stepped on.

That was the only instrument we had to practice on. And while O’Kelly, who was a proper musical genius, had wrangled that droning beast into accompanying “Sally in Our Alley” without making it sound like a funeral march for a dead goldfish, he’d only barely succeeded. By the end of our two weeks of drills, my version of the sweet old ballad was so mournful you’d leave the room wondering if the groom-to-be had a secret opium habit and Sally was already planning to elope with the village baker. You could practically hear the audience whispering: “This engagement is not going to end well.”

“Lighter! Less woe-is-me, you daft lad!” O’Kelly would beg, as the organ wheezed and groaned like it was begging for a mercy killing.

Once we nearly got caught red-handed, when Mrs. O’Kelly came home early from her district visitors’ committee meeting. O’Kelly stuffed me behind a stack of geranium pots in the tiny first-floor conservatory, and I crouched there shaking while she grilled him like a suspect at a police station.

“William, don’t lie to me. That was not a hymn.”
“Ah, darlin’, so much depends on the rhythm—let me show ye what I mean—”
“William, stop making a mockery of sacred music. And I distinctly heard the word ‘Sally’ when I walked in the door.”
“Salvation, me love! I was singing about Salvation!”
“Your enunciation is never that muddled, William.”
“Bit of a sore throat, pet. All that hymn-singing wears a man out.”
“Your voice sounded perfectly fine. Let’s just drop this subject.”

O’Kelly was only too happy to agree. “She’s a bit of a tough nut when you’re fit as a fiddle,” he explained later, wiping sweat off his brow, “but if you’re sick? She’s the tenderest nurse that ever lived. When I had typhoid three years back, she didn’t sleep more than two hours a night looking after me. Never saw anything like it. Trouble is, I’m such a annoyingly healthy bastard she never gets the chance to dote on me. If I could just be a chronic invalid, she’d wait on me hand and foot. As it is—” He plonked a discordant chord on that wretched organ, and we went back to singing about Sally.

Back to that morning outside the stage door. “Meet me at the Cheshire Cheese at one,” O’Kelly said, shaking my hand. “If this gig doesn’t work out, we’ll find you something else. I already talked to Hodgson, the manager, and he’s half expecting you. Good luck!”

He trundled off, and I pushed through the stage door, where a tiny round-eyed man with a nose curved like a parrot’s beak was stuck in a glass booth, looking like an angry sparrow in a cage. He squawked at me to state my business, I showed him my letter of audition, and he spat out directions so fast I almost choked: “Up the passage, across the stage, down the corridor, first floor, second door right!” and slammed his window shut so hard the glass rattled.

I trundled along the passage, and to my shock I wasn’t even a little bit starstruck. It reminded me of a story my dad once told me, about a young Crimean War soldier he’d met who got asked what he thought about his first ever battle charge. The lad said all he could think about the whole time was that he’d left the canteen beer tap running, and he couldn’t get the thought out of his head. That was me: I was too busy worrying I’d forget the words to Sally to be impressed by backstage glitz. All the theatre magic evaporated the second I stepped onto the boards. The whole thing was just a grown-up kid’s dollhouse: chipped paint, wobbly flats, costumes that looked like they’d been cobbled together out of old curtains. Not that I’m knocking theatre! It’s a lovely thing, but it’s still just a fancy toy, same as dressing dolls and putting them to bed in their little dollhouse rooms. It’s all practice for when we finally grow up, isn’t it?

I got lost in the maze of hallways backstage, but eventually followed the sound of chattering voices to a big bare room full of 20 or 30 people sitting on beat-up settees. They were all different ages and sizes, but every single one of them had that unmistakeable theatrical glow: the men had no facial hair and way too much hair on their heads, the women all had impossibly pink-and-white complexions and eyes so bright they looked lit from inside. Everyone called each other “my love” and “old chappie,” like they were all related, and I nearly apologized for walking in on a family reunion until I realized no one cared. I squeezed into an empty seat next to a tiny, boyish-looking man with big fish-like eyes, and asked him if this was the waiting room for new chorus members for the upcoming comic opera.

He stared at me up and down for so long that a giant broad-shouldered bloke sitting nearby, who’d overheard me, took it upon himself to answer. “Aye, it is. And that arsehole Butterworth who’s supposed to be auditioning us is keeping us waiting for no damn reason. Bloody rude, if you ask me.”

The tiny fish-eyed man turned to him, his voice a deep rumbling contralto. “I think it’s exceedingly bad manners for a gentleman to butt in and answer a question that was directed specifically at me.”
“Sorry, mate. I thought you were asleep.”
“And it’s even worse manners to tell a perfectly awake man you thought he was asleep.”
“Sir,” the big guy said, propping himself up on his giant umbrella like he was Samuel Johnson himself, “I’m not rearranging my entire personality to suit your weird little rules of etiquette.”
“Fair enough,” the tiny man said. “But you’re still making a fool of yourself.”

“Does anyone even know what this comic opera is supposed to be about?” a bright little lady at the other end of the room piped up.
“Has anyone ever known what a comic opera is about?” an older, world-weary woman shot back.
“I once asked the author of the last one I was in,” a tired-looking man in the corner said. “He told me, ‘If you’d asked me at the start of rehearsals I might’ve been able to tell you, but I’ll be damned if I can remember now.’”
“Bet there’s a drinking chorus for blokes somewhere in it,” a good-looking guy in a velvet coat said.
“And if we’re real lucky,” added the thin blonde lady, “the heroine will pour her whole sad love life out to us, and we’ll all gasp and go ‘ooooh’ like we care.”

The door popped open, and a name got called. An older lady everyone called Gertie got up and left. “Poor old Gertie,” the blonde lady said sympathetically. “I heard she once had a lovely voice, back in the day.”
“Reminds me,” the big guy next to me said, “of when young Bond first came to London. He applied to Alexander Lee, the guy who ran the Strand Theatre, for a chorus spot. Lee heard him sing two lines and jumped up, said ‘Thanks, that’s all, goodbye.’ Bond knew his voice was good, so he asked Lee what was wrong. Lee yelled at him: ‘Do you think I hire a chorus to make my principals sound like tone-deaf donkeys?’”
“Given the company we’re in right now,” the fish-eyed man said coldly, “that anecdote is in terrible taste.” Everyone else nodded along like they agreed.

For the next half hour the door kept opening and closing, swallowing people one by one until the room got quieter and quieter, everyone’s nerves getting thicker than cigarette smoke. Finally, a voice yelled: “MR. HORACE MONCRIEFF!” We all glanced around to see who that poor sod was, until it hit me like a brick: that was me.

“Glad you finally remembered who you are,” the pale-faced young man who was calling names said, when I stumbled over to the door. “Try not to lose yourself again, we’re running behind schedule.”

He led me past an empty, sad-looking refreshment bar where the once-fresh sandwiches were all curled up, into a room where a miserable-looking man was slouched at the piano. Next to him was tall, handsome Mr. Hodgson, the theatre manager, who was flipping through a huge stack of letters and never once looking up. A big burly guy who later turned out to be the opera’s “English adapter” (read: guy who translated the French libretto and changed three jokes to make them worse) was trying very hard not to laugh at the theatre’s lead low comedian, who had a weird habit of never looking at the person he was talking to—his eyes were always darting somewhere else, like he was looking for a lost cockroach.

“Moncrieff?” Hodgson said, still staring at his letters. The pale guy nodded for me.
“Go on, then,” Hodgson said.
“What’s your song?” the sad pianist mumbled.
“‘Sally in Our Alley,’” I said.
“What are you?” Hodgson asked, still not looking up.
“Tenor,” I said, remembering O’Kelly’s instructions. “Not a *loud* tenor, though.”
“Terrible idea to ever hire a full tenor for chorus, am I right?” the restless-eyed comedian said, staring right at me while talking to the burly adapter. “Have you ever even tried to fit one?” Everyone laughed, except the sad pianist, who huffed out a single mechanical “ha” without cracking a smile.

“Alright, off you go,” the pianist said, and started the wheezy accompaniment.
When I finished the first verse, Hodgson didn’t even glance up. “Tell me when he’s supposed to start singing, would you?”
“He’s got a nice little voice,” the pianist said, “he’s just nervous.”
“Audiences tend to prefer voices they can actually hear,” Hodgson said. “That’s all I’m getting at.”

I belted out the second verse like I was trying to yell at a ship three miles out at sea. The burly adapter said he could definitely hear me, the comedian joked we’d have to put out a notice for the audience to shut up while I sang. Suddenly my voice decided it was too shy to perform, and it vanished right down into my boots, like a skittish cat hiding under the sofa. I was panicking so hard I thought I’d pass out.

“Your voice is great, it’s fine,” the pianist whispered, patting my arm. “Just belt the last bit out as hard as you can.”

My voice immediately zoomed back up my throat and out my mouth. “Thirty shillings a week, half pay for rehearsals,” Hodgson said, still scribbling notes on his letters. “If that’s alright, Catchpole here will give you your contract. If not, thanks for stopping by.”

I signed the papers in 30 seconds flat, the comedian called me Boanerges (which means “sons of thunder,” apparently) and wished me good luck, and I stumbled back out onto the street grinning like an idiot. My first ever theatre gig!

Our first rehearsal call was the following Monday at 2 p.m. The whole theatre was buzzing. The principals had just finished their own run-through, chatting in a huddle off to the side. We chorus folks milled around center stage: I spotted old Gertie, the thin blonde lady from the waiting room, the big umbrella guy, and the fish-eyed tiny bloke who were still bickering, same as before. The tiny guy was ranting about how chorus performers should be legally required to retire the second their voices went even a little bit off, and the big guy was yelling back about how rude little men who were only 5’2” needed to be sent home to get their spanked by their mums.

Hodgson was still standing by the footlights flipping through letters, exactly the same as he’d been four days earlier. My sad pianist friend was up in the orchestra pit with a dozen other musicians, all armed with violins. The adapter and the French stage manager (who I very carefully pretended was born and raised in Birmingham to keep him happy) huddled over a table under a hissing gas lamp, whispering. After 15 minutes the stage manager suddenly jumped up, rang a little bell so hard his arm almost fell off, and yelled: “EVERYBODY CLEAR OFF THE STAGE!” A tiny page boy who looked like a very important fox terrier echoed the order, and we all herded back into the wings.

The stage manager sorted us into couples based on height, which was hilarious. He spotted the tiny fish-eyed guy, rubbed his hands together, and said: “Oh, I’ve got the perfect leading lady for you! The most gorgeous 6 foot 2 amazon you’ve ever seen!” He darted into the group of chorus ladies, dragged over a woman who was so broad she could’ve carried the tiny man under one arm, and presented her to him like he was handing out a prize at a fair. The big umbrella guy got a tiny sharp-faced lady who looked like she was 16 from a distance, and I got paired up with that thin blonde lady I’d sat next to in the waiting room.

We lined up in the proper semi-circle, the orchestra struck up the opening chorus, and I realized I knew my music cold from all the practicing with O’Kelly—my only problem was figuring out what on earth I was supposed to be doing with my hands.

“Sorry to be a bother, love,” the blonde lady said, winking at me, “but you’re gonna have to put your arm around my waist. I know it’s scandalous, but rent’s due next week, so we’ve got no choice. Think you can manage it?” I blushed so hard my ears burned, but her silly little grin chased all my shyness away, and I did exactly what she asked.

The stage manager zoomed around us like a border collie herding sheep, shoving this couple a foot back, yanking that one a foot forward, shoving people closer together then spreading them out again. “Perfect!” he yelled when we finished the run-through. “One more time, and this time *sing in tune* for God’s sake!”
“And this time make it look like you’re in love, not like a bunch of wooden marionettes!” he added. He grabbed the nearest chorus lady, struck a dramatic pose, one hand over his heart and his head tilted 45 degrees to the side, and demonstrated how a “passionate peasant” was supposed to moon at his lady love.
“If he thinks I’m gonna do Romeo poses for the same 30 shillings a week,” the big umbrella guy muttered under his breath, “I’m demanding a ten bob raise.” The stage manager then grabbed a random chorus guy and showed us ladies how a shy village maiden was supposed to blush and wiggle her shoulders all coquettish. The veteran chorus ladies all snickered, like they’d seen 100 stage managers pull this exact same schtick.

We started the run-through again, and all of us got immediately distracted by the fish-eyed tiny man, who was following every single one of the stage manager’s instructions to the absolute letter. He was overacting so hard the rest of us forgot to sing, the orchestra got out of time, Hodgson actually looked up from his letters for the first time all day, and the leading lady and the comedian stared at him, mouths hanging open. The only people who weren’t cracking up were the comedian, the stage manager, and the tiny man himself, who was just earnestly doing his very best. The three of them huddled for a quick whispered confab, then the comedian called out: “Oi, you! What on earth do you think you’re doing?”
“Making love,” the tiny man said, looking genuinely confused.
“You’re making a fool of yourself, mate!” the comedian snapped.
“That’s very rude,” the tiny man said, looking hurt. “I’m doing the best I can.”
Hodgson snickered behind his stack of letters. “Poor sod, can’t help it. Let him carry on.”
“We’re not doing a pantomime here, for Christ’s sake!” the comedian complained.
“Can’t fire him,” Hodgson whispered. “He’s the only one supporting his widowed mum back in London, plus five little siblings that are all too small to work. Would be cruel to kick him out.”

The comedian didn’t argue, but he frowned the whole rest of the rehearsal. The second act was chaos. We had to dress up as soldiers and vivandieres, the stage manager demonstrating that the only difference between being overcome with patriotism and being overcome with cheap brandy was that patriotism made you slap your chest really hard, and brandy made you slap your forehead really hard. Then we were conspirators, and he draped a tablecloth over his head to show us how to mumble and lurk in corners. Then we were a rioting mob led by a sentimental baritone, and he ruffled his hair up to show us how to wave our fists without actually punching anyone. We ended the act with a big battle scene, the stage manager yanking his coat off to demonstrate how to dramatically die, rolling around on the dusty stage floor until he was covered in grime.
