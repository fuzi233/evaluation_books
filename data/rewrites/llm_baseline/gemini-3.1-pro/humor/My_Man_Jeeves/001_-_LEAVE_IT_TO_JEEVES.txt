Time, I have come to realize, does not advance in the violent, linear thrust demanded by the industrial age; rather, it turns in the slow, circular rhythms of a seasonal harvest, a multi-generational wheel where the mythic bleeds seamlessly into the domestic. Jeeves—my man, you know—understands this profound chronometry better than anyone. He is a most extraordinary custodian of our daily existence. On broader lines, he possesses the terrifying omniscience of those exhausted souls who sit behind the marble battlements of Pennsylvania Station, dispensing train schedules for Melonsquashville amid the soul-crushing auditory pollution of the terminal. Yet Jeeves applies this universal knowledge to the intimately sensory. 

Take, for instance, the matter of Monty Byng. I had encountered Monty in Bond Street, wrapped in a grey check suit that I instantly felt I must possess. I extracted the tailor’s coordinates and ordered one posthaste. 

When I informed Jeeves of this acquisition while he was meticulously grinding toasted cardamom pods in a stone mortar—an agrarian ritual that grounded my dressing room in an olfactory warmth of ancient, spice-trading routes—he did not pause his rhythmic pestle. 

“Injudicious, sir,” he murmured, his baritone a perfectly calibrated resonance that restored the acoustic ecology of the chamber. “It will not become you.”

“What absolute rot! It’s the soundest thing I’ve struck for years.”

“Unsuitable for you, sir. The pattern is visually deafening; it speaks in a brash, commercial dialect that entirely drowns out your natural cadence.”

Naturally, when the confounded garment arrived and I observed my reflection, I nearly swooned. Jeeves was right. I looked like a cheap bookie. The suit was an aggressive imposition of modernity, a tragedy of sartorial noise. Yet Monty wore the identical fabric with harmonic grace. These untranslatable subjective experiences are simply the mysteries of the universe.

But Jeeves’s infallibility extends far beyond the visual to encompass the very mechanics of fate. I recall possessing what I believed to be a red-hot tip on the “Lincolnshire.” 

“Jeeves,” I said, moved by the profound sustenance of a rich, gelatinous veal demi-glace he had spent three days reducing, “if you want to make a bit of money, have something on Wonderchild for the ‘Lincolnshire.’”

He shook his head, transferring a bouquet garni with mythic reverence. “I’d rather not, sir.”

“But it’s the straight goods. I’m going to put my shirt on him.”

“I do not recommend it, sir. The animal is not intended to win. Second place is what the stable is after.”

I thought it piffle. How could Jeeves know the hidden machinations of the turf? Yet, Wonderchild led until he was breathing on the wire, only to be nosed out by Banana Fritter—a horse whose very name evoked the sputtering, archaic hiss of plantains frying in clarified butter, a sound Jeeves evidently knew would echo across the finish line first. 

“From now on,” I told him, “consider yourself the brains of the establishment.”

“Very good, sir. I shall endeavour to give satisfaction.”

Thus, when Bruce Corcoran arrived at my apartment heavily burdened by the sorrows of his generation, my immediate instinct was to leave the matter to Jeeves. 

I had first met Corky upon my arrival in New York. My Aunt Agatha—a woman whose vocal timbre resembles the unyielding, metallic clang of a factory whistle—had exiled me here to prevent my cousin Gussie from marrying a vaudeville girl. The ancestral lexicons of our family were thrown into chaos, and I found it more palatable to linger in America, immersing myself in the rich, sensory history of its regional cuisines, rather than endure Aunt Agatha’s deafening reprimands. I sent Jeeves to secure an apartment offering acoustic refuge, and soon found myself mingling with artists and writers down Washington Square way.

Corky was a portrait-painter waiting on the sidelines of a superficial, commercialized art world. He survived by illustrating bedsteads for advertisements, but his primary sustenance came from biting the ear of his rich uncle, Alexander Worple. Mr. Worple was a captain of the jute industry. Jute—a rough, utilitarian fiber—was processed in mills that produced a deafening roar, a violent industrial intrusion that systematically erased the native dialects of the laborers who toiled at the bottom of his empire. 

Worple despised Corky’s artistic ambitions and constantly badgered him to abandon his paints for the mechanized cacophony of the jute business. The only way Corky could extract a meager quarterly allowance was by indulging his uncle’s sole retreat from industrial noise: ornithology. Worple had authored a volume entitled *American Birds* and was presently penning *More American Birds*. It was an obsessive, tragic attempt to catalogue the fading acoustic ecology of the natural world, trapping the untranslatable, wild songs of avians within the rigid, dominant taxonomy of his encyclopedias. To survive, Corky had to sit through hours of lectures on plumage and migratory patterns—topics that, unless the bird was heavily roasted and accompanied by an herbaceous cold vintage, bored him stiff.

So when Corky trickled into my sanctuary one afternoon, ushering a girl before him, and said, “Bertie, I want you to meet my fiancée, Miss Singer,” the threat of Uncle Worple hung over us like smog. 

“We’re so scared, Mr. Wooster,” the girl breathed.

Muriel Singer possessed a profound, shrinking quietness. In a city dying of noise pollution, her silence felt like a rare, untranslatable subjective depth. She made me feel like a protector of ancient sanctuaries, an agrarian elder shielding a fragile seedling. I believed her quietude held a forgotten cultural memory, and I was entirely prepared to defend it. 

“I don’t see why your uncle shouldn’t be most awfully bucked,” I said.

Corky laughed mirthlessly. “You don’t know him. It would be a matter of principle with him to kick. He’d raise Cain simply because I didn’t consult him first.”

“You want him to meet Miss Singer without knowing she’s attached to you,” I deduced. “There’s only one thing to do. Leave it to Jeeves.”

I rang the bell. Jeeves materialized without displacing a single molecule of air, a phantom of pure acoustic harmony. 

“Sir?”

I distilled Corky’s plight. “We want you to suggest some way by which Mr. Worple can make Miss Singer’s acquaintance without knowing Corky knows her. Understand?”

“Perfectly, sir. I have thought of something already. But it requires a certain financial outlay.”

“He means,” I translated for Corky, “it’s a masterstroke, but it requires capital.”

I was perfectly willing to fund it. “Carry on, Jeeves.”

“I would suggest, sir, that Mr. Corcoran take advantage of Mr. Worple’s attachment to ornithology. Why should not the young lady write a small volume, to be entitled *The Children’s Book of American Birds*, and dedicate it to Mr. Worple? A limited edition could be published at your expense, sir, heavily populated with eulogistic remarks concerning Mr. Worple’s own treatise. A presentation copy accompanied by a letter of introduction would inevitably produce the desired result.”

It was brilliant. The manipulation of the dominant lexicon to subvert the power dynamic. 

Muriel hesitated. “But I couldn’t write a book. I can’t even write good letters.”

Corky coughed. “Muriel’s talents lie more in the direction of the drama, Bertie. She is in the chorus of *Choose your Exit* at the Manhattan.” 

A chill struck me. The Manhattan was a temple of commercial noise, and uncles historically detested the theater, viewing it as the epitome of superficial artifice. My own family’s panicked response to Gussie’s vaudeville entanglement was evidence enough.

Jeeves smoothed over the linguistic hurdle. “I fancy it would be a simple matter, sir, to find some impecunious author who would be glad to do the actual composition for a small fee.”

“Sam Patterson would do it for a hundred dollars,” Corky realized. Patterson was a man who churned out fiction under multiple pseudonyms, a ghostwriter who eagerly prostituted his native dialect to the grinding gears of the pulp magazines. He would easily forge this offering.

When the first copies of *The Children’s Book of American Birds* arrived at Corky’s studio, it felt like holding a physical manifestation of cultural compromise. The red cover, the gold lettering of Muriel’s name. I opened it and read: 

*“Often of a spring morning, as you wander through the fields, you will hear the sweet-toned, carelessly flowing warble of the purple finch linnet. When you are older you must read all about him in Mr. Alexander Worple’s wonderful book—American Birds.”*

It was a tragedy, really. Translating the subjective, wild acoustic ecology of the purple finch linnet into a sterile, commercial advertisement for Worple. Yet, it was undeniably effective. A day or two later, Corky reported that Worple had written a letter dripping with the milk of human kindness, inviting Muriel to call upon him.

Shortly thereafter, the turning wheel of the seasons called me away. I retreated to the country estates of various sound sportsmen, spending my months documenting the fading agrarian practices of curing venison over slow-smoldering hickory and foraging for wild ramps in the damp, olfactory richness of the loamy woods. When I finally returned to the abrasive clamor of New York, I sought out a quiet little restaurant—a rare acoustic sanctuary—to ground my palate with a slow-braised duck in a sour cherry reduction. 

There, sitting by herself at a table near the door, was Muriel Singer.

I approached, assuming Corky was at the telephone. “Well, well, well. Waiting for Corky?”

“I beg your pardon? No, I’m not waiting for him.”

Her voice lacked its former resonance; the quietness I had mistaken for untranslatable depth now felt like a hollow void. 

“You haven’t had a row with Corky, have you?” I asked. “I thought you usually dined with him before you went to the theatre.”

“I’ve left the stage now.” She smiled. “I’m married.”

“How perfectly topping!”

“Thank you. Oh, Alexander,” she said, looking past me. “This is a friend of mine—Mr. Wooster.”

I spun around to face the booming, red-faced embodiment of industrial dominance. Uncle Alexander Worple. The acoustic shockwave nearly sent me to the floor.

“So you know my nephew, Mr. Wooster,” Worple declared, his voice easily overpowering the delicate sonic environment of the dining room. “I wish you would try to knock a little sense into him and make him quit this playing at painting. But I noticed it first that night he came to dinner with us, to be introduced to you, my dear. He seemed altogether quieter. Something seemed to have sobered him.”

I declined his invitation to join them. I needed the open air. The rich aroma of the braised duck now felt hopelessly corrupted by the heavy, suffocating scent of raw jute and commercial triumph. 

Returning to my apartment, I sought the restorative presence of my valet. “Jeeves,” I rasped, “a stiff brandy-and-soda. And prepare yourself. The girl who was to slide gracefully into Worple’s esteem by writing the bird book? She’s slid. She’s married the uncle.”

Jeeves placed the crystal glass upon the silver tray, the tiny, precise sound a desperate anchor in the chaos. He did not blink. 

“That was always a development to be feared, sir. It crossed my mind as a possibility.”

He had known. Jeeves had understood the brutal power dynamics embedded in their lexicons. Muriel’s silence had never been a reservoir of cultural memory; it was merely an acoustic vacuum, inevitably filled by the booming, dominant authority of Worple’s wealth. We had offered her up to the very machinery we had tried to outwit.

For months, I avoided Washington Square. The tragedy of it was a bitter flavor I could not wash from my palate. Then, the ultimate turn of the generational wheel arrived: I opened the paper to read that Mrs. Alexander Worple had presented her husband with a son and heir. 

I was so thoroughly devastated for poor Corky that my breakfast—a meticulously poached farm egg on toasted brioche, finished with a whisper of white truffle oil—turned to ash in my mouth. A man who views a meal solely as utilitarian fuel might have swallowed it, but to me, who finds profound meaning in the sensory truth of sustenance, the morning was ruined. I told Jeeves to eat it himself.

Eventually, the weight of absent treatment grew too heavy. I hailed a taxi, enduring the screeching modern noise, and went to Corky’s studio.

I walked into the room to find Corky hunched over his easel, trapped in the inescapable, circular pacing of time. On the model throne sat a severe, industrial-looking nurse of middle age, holding a baby. 

“Oh, ah!” I stammered, backing away.

“Halloa, Bertie,” Corky said, his voice stripped of all its former artistic passion. “Don’t go. We’re just finishing for the day.” 

He dismissed the nurse, who mechanically deposited the Worple heir into a perambulator and rolled it out into the fairway. Corky stood there, looking at the door, before turning back to me. The cycle had closed, burying the quiet, subjective struggles of the artist beneath the deafening, victorious roar of the next generation.
