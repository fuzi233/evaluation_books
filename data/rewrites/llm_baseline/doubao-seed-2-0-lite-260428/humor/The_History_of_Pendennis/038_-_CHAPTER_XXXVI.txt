The new paper blew up almost instantly. Rumour had it a powerful political party was bankrolling the whole operation, and half the top names in Westminster were secretly writing for its columns. Were the rumours true? We’re not at liberty to call them outright lies, but we can spill the full, far funnier tea: that celebrated foreign policy essay everyone swore was penned by a well-known Foreign Office cabinet minister? It was actually Captain Shandon, scribbling on a wobbly pub table at the Bear and Staff near Whitehall Stairs, where the printer’s boy had tracked him down while he was crashing with his chaotic literary mate Mr. Bludyer. And that lauded series of finance papers everyone credited to a senior Commons statesman? Entirely the work of George Warrington of the Upper Temple, dashed out between cigars and lazy breakfasts.

It wasn’t all nonsense, of course. Percy Popjoy, whose dad Lord Falconet was a big shot in that same ruling party, could be spotted traipsing up to Warrington’s chambers three times a week, and the paper did run scoops no other outlet could get their hands on. Popjoy even had a regular byline of “P.P.” for his loud, overblown poems that had zero going for them besides volume, and his mediocre new novel got such absurdly over-the-top glowing reviews in the *Gazette* that readers were joking the protagonist could walk on water by the third chapter.

Pen never touched the political side of the operation, but he was the paper’s most giddy, hyperactive literary contributor. The *Gazette* offices were tucked in Catherine Street off the Strand, and Pen would bounce over several times a week with crumpled manuscripts stuffed in his coat pocket, buzzing with that new-car-smell thrill of a fresh literary career, where seeing your own name in print still feels like a magic trick and you’re fully convinced every line you write is sending ripples through London high society.

That’s where sub-editor Jack Finucane held court, running his little gossip empire with paste pot and a pair of scissors. No aristocrat’s dinner party, no old dowager’s funeral, no obscure country house fête slipped past his eagle eye. He dug up gossip from the tiniest provincial rags, remote Irish local papers, forgotten Scottish parish newsletters, all to pad his society column with endless accounts of the upper crust’s doings. It was equal parts ridiculous and heartwarming to watch Jack, tattered trousers and stained shirt sleeves, eating a cheap plate of takeaway meat and a pint of porter for lunch, describe a duchess’s glittering masked ball like he’d been seated right next to her at the banquet. Jack had barely ever stepped foot in a nice house, let alone a West End club, but his writing made him sound like he was on first-name terms with every ambassador in town. His bosses wisely kept him mostly on clipping duty rather than letting him write original copy, since half his society anecdotes were so full of errors they’d get the paper sued by every aristocrat in the country.

Pen poured all his energy into his reviews. He’d picked up a whole load of random reading in his youth, had a sharp eye for the funny bits, and his pieces went over a treat with both his bosses and the public. He was dead proud of the money he was earning off his own brain. Naturally, the whole of Fairoaks was a regular subscriber, with Pen’s mom and aunt poring over every issue like it was scripture. Clavering Park got it too, where that literary-minded young lady Helen Pendennis thought Pen’s writing was the cleverest thing she’d ever read, and old Dr. Portman, who got the paper once the widow had memorised all her son’s articles, gave it his full seal of approval, saying the lad had spirit and wrote like a proper gentleman even if he wasn’t a stuffy classical scholar.

The real fun kicked off when Major Pendennis wandered into his club, the Regent, to find Wenham, Lord Falconet, and half a dozen other posh, well-connected gents cackling over a recent issue of the *Gazette*. They were obsessing over Pen’s latest review, a sharp but perfectly polite takedown of the Countess of Muffborough’s new travel book about Spain and Italy, a volume so full of mangled French and butchered English no one could tell which language she was writing half the time. Pen had torn through her mistakes with such playful mock gravity that the whole thing was side-splitting, not a single rude word in sight, but the countess was now the laughing stock of every drawing room in Mayfair. Wenham was grinning so hard his bilious little face was crumpled up—Lady Muffborough had snubbed him and not invited him to any of her parties the year before. Lord Falconet was snorting with laughter too: he and Lord Muffborough had been petty rivals since they were at Eton together. Even the legendary Lord Steyne, who the Major showed the review to later, cackled and swore the Muffboroughs would be squirming under the burn like a harpooned whale.

Suddenly Major Pendennis, who’d been pretending his nephew’s little newspaper gig was far too low-brow for a dignified retired army man, was bragging to everyone in earshot, saying he’d always known the “young rascal had real talent”. He wrote a gushy letter to Pen’s mom at Fairoaks listing every great lord’s praise of her son, then wrote to Pen himself inviting him out for a chop, and saying he’d scored an invite for Pen to dinner at Gaunt House—Lord Steyne loved anyone who could make him laugh, no matter if they were a lord, a hack, or a total oddball.

Pen flung the letter across the table to Warrington, fully expecting his mate to lose his mind over the big news. Warrington just glanced at it, puffed on his cigar, and shrugged like it was no big deal.

That’s the thing about 24-year-old critics: they have zero imposter syndrome at all. Pen would’ve happily reviewed Macaulay’s entire *History of England* or Herschel’s giant astronomy tome after skimming it over one post-work cigar, acting like he was the older, wiser patron of both authors. A couple of months later, when he’d re-read those same reviews, he’d stare at the pages astonished at how learned and authoritative he sounded, and he couldn’t even remember which reference books he’d pulled all the obscure dates and names from. Luckily Warrington roasted him nonstop to keep his ego from ballooning so big it popped—if it were up to Shandon, he’d feed Pen’s delusions all day long, since he loved the kid’s snappy, flashy writing far more than Warrington’s slower, heavier, more thoughtful work.

For all his unearned confidence, though, Pen was a scrupulously honest critic—far too honest for their publisher Mr. Bungay’s liking, in fact. Bungay stormed into the office one morning frothing mad, because Pen had written a glowing, 5-star review of a book from their direct rival, Bacon’s publishing firm. Shandon pulled Pen aside to ask what on earth he was thinking.

“C’mon, kid,” Shandon said, “you think I sunk money into this whole journal just to give free publicity to the bloke who steals half my customers?”

Pen blinked at him, completely bewildered. “You think I should lie and say a perfectly good book is garbage just because the other guy printed it? Ruat coelum, let the sky fall, I’m not going to lie about what I read.”

Shandon laughed. “And what about our original prospectus? That thing was full of overblown nonsense, half the promises we made never happened. Did you lose sleep over that?”

“Nah,” Pen said, grinning. “Me and Warrington talked about that. That’s just a circus poster, making the giant on the bill look a foot taller than the actual giant inside the tent. No harm done. We’re just the fiddlers playing the tunes, we don’t have to own the whole show. But I’m not gonna lie about a book. I’ll hype all our authors as much as you want, I’ll roast a bad book from the other guys as hard as you like, fair and square—but I’m not gonna punch an opponent below the belt. I’d rather starve than write something unfair just to make us a few extra pennies.”

Shandon shook his head, amused. “Alright, kid, from now on we’ll get Bludyer to do all the hatchet jobs on Bacon’s books. Your soft little novice conscience is a luxury, you know. I had one once, too. But that bloom wears off after you’ve been a hack in this game long enough.”

Pen left the conversation feeling very proud of his integrity, vowing he’d never let the cynical world of journalism wear down his commitment to telling the truth. The universe, of course, decided to serve him a perfect little slice of humble pie immediately.

The very next issue of the *Gazette* ran a brutal, no-mercy hatchet job tearing apart the new *Spring Annual* poetry collection, by their savage star critic Mr. Bludyer, a man famous for ripping books to shreds for his own amusement. The most thoroughly eviscerated poem in the whole collection? Pen’s own secret submission, which he’d hidden under a fake pen name. He’d refused to review the book himself, so Shandon had handed it straight to Bludyer, who’d smashed every verse into tiny pieces like a bull rampaging through a flower bed. When he was done tearing the whole volume apart, he sold the tattered, marked-up copy to a used book stall, and spent every penny he got for it on a pint of brandy. Warrington was rolling on the floor cackling so hard he could barely breathe, while Pen stood there red-faced, staring at his own words being roasted to a pulp in the very newspaper he worked for.
