On the first of May, we were roused at dawn—twenty boarders and four teachers—summoned to assemble by six o'clock under the command of M. Paul Emanuel. He had promised us a country breakfast, and though I had initially been excluded from this excursion (quite pointedly, in fact), a sharp tug on my ear and a warning glance silenced any protest.

*"Je vous conseille de vous faire prier,"* he said, with mock severity.

One Napoleonic reprimand was enough. I resolved to join the party.

The morning was serene, the air fragrant with birdsong and the promise of warmth. We shed our winter layers for light print dresses and straw bonnets, each crafted with that effortless French elegance—simple, yet impossibly becoming. At six, the bell chimed, and we streamed through the corridors to the vestibule, where M. Paul stood transformed. Gone was the stern professor in his somber *paletôt*; instead, he wore a youthful blouse and a cheerful straw hat, greeting us with such warmth that even the shyest among us returned his smile.

We set out through quiet streets, the boulevards fresh as meadows. Happiness seemed to radiate from him, an infectious energy that brightened even those he seldom favored. I, however, kept to the edges, using Ginevra Fanshawe as a living shield—her considerable charm proving both a burden and a strategic buffer. Yet M. Paul was not so easily deceived.

*"Qu’est-ce que c’est? Vous me jouez des tours?"* he demanded, his eyes narrowing before landing on the culprit: my pink dress.

*"A-h-h! C’est la robe rose!"* he exclaimed, with all the indignation of a bull spotting red.

I protested—cotton, practical, *washable*—but he only smirked.

The expected lecture never came. Instead, his mood softened.

The countryside unfolded in lush May greenery, a fleeting perfection before summer’s dust settled. At a shaded well, he gathered us like a storyteller of old, his voice weaving tales with such vivid simplicity that even the most restless child stilled. I watched him, struck by the contrast between his usual tempest and this rare tranquility.

When the tale ended, he approached Ginevra and me.

He sighed. *"I could never write it down. But perhaps you would, if I asked?"*

I demurred—his thoughts would outpace my pen—but he only laughed and set me to buttering rolls at the farmhouse where we breakfasted. There, amid the clatter of dishes and the scent of fresh coffee, he presided like a benevolent king, his earlier irritation forgotten.

Yet shadows lingered. As we returned, the children flagging in the noon heat, he arranged carriages to spare us the long walk. The day *should* have been perfect. But when evening fell, I saw him pacing the garden with Madame Beck, his expression troubled. Later, his restless search through the school—*"Où est Mademoiselle Lucie?"*—sent me fleeing like a startled hare, though every nerve in me longed to meet him.

St. Pierre’s lie—*"Elle est au lit"*—sealed my regret. By the time I steadied my pulse, he was gone, leaving me to wonder what words had been lost to my own cowardice.

That night, I counted my wages: not solace, but silence.
