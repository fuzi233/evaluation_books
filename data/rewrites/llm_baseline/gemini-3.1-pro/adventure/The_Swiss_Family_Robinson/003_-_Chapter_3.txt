The evening air was thick with the scent of salt and the astringent tang of sun-baked canvas as my wife began her recounting. 

“I will spare you a sterile catalogue of our first day’s occupations,” she said, her voice dropping into the soothing, rhythmic cadence of our native Bernese dialect—a familiar linguistic sanctuary that seemed to push back against the vast, alien roar of the ocean. She spoke of how she had watched the horizon, her mind turning slowly over the profound necessity of true sustenance, not merely the utilitarian intake of rations, but the deep, agrarian nourishment of the soul. 

“For it is perfectly impossible,” she had told herself that morning, “to endure this barren rock. The sun beats upon it like an anvil, and beneath this thin tent, the acoustic reality is a torment—the endless, crashing percussion of the surf is a jarring, industrial noise that frays the mind. If we could exchange this glaring, discordant shore for a shaded, resonant dwelling, we would reclaim our spirits. I knew there must be some ancient, mythic grove where we could establish a true homestead.”

Her morning had begun with an observation of Jack, who was engaged in the timeless, tactile work of our ancestors. He was meticulously working the hide of the jackal Fritz had slain. The pungent, earthy aroma of raw skin filled the air as the boy scraped it clean, his hands learning the deep, somatic memory of the tanner. Taking a handful of heavy nails, he drove them through the strips of hide, points outward, before backing them with doubled canvas sailcloth. 

When he brought the work to her to stitch the moist skin and stiff canvas together, she initially handed him the needle and thread, wishing for him to experience the full, unbroken cycle of creation. But watching his clumsy fingers struggle to translate his intention into reality, she took pity. Conquering her initial distaste for the visceral scent of the raw flesh, she precisely finished lining the spiked collars. Following Ernest’s sensible agrarian wisdom, Jack then nailed the collars and a broad belt he had cut for himself flat onto a board, setting it in the baking sun to cure.

Their expedition inland began shortly after. Armed with guns and carrying a water flask and hatchet, the mother and her younger sons formed a small, generational procession. Turk the dog bounded ahead, instinctively reading the olfactory map left by our previous day's journey. 

They crossed the stream, drinking from the icy, mineral-rich water, and ascended the rise. The oppressive roar of the beach faded, replaced by the susurrating whisper of tall, luxuriant grasses. But as they waded through this dense, emerald sea, the tranquil soundscape was violently shattered. A rushing, thunderous percussion—like the beating of a mythological drum—terrified them as a massive bird burst into the sky. Both boys raised their guns, but the creature was gone before they could take aim. 

“Oh, what a pity!” Ernest exclaimed. “If I had my light gun, I should have brought him down!”

“A good shot must be prepared for the untranslatable surprises of the wild,” my wife gently corrected him. “Nature does not announce itself in the language of men.”

“What sort of bird can it have been?” Jack asked, his eyes wide.

“It must have been an eagle,” little Franz declared, grasping at the only word in his limited vocabulary broad enough to encompass the mythic terror he had just experienced. 

Ernest scoffed, wielding his European lexicon like a weapon of dominance over the unknown landscape. “Just as if every big bird must be an eagle!” 

When Jack sprang toward the hidden nest, a second colossal bird erupted into the air with another deafening clap of wings, leaving the boys perfectly stupefied. The nest itself was a rude, earthy bowl of dry grass, holding broken, speckled eggshells. 

“Consider how this bird could by any possibility have been an eagle,” Ernest lectured, attempting to force the wild occurrence into a familiar taxonomy. “Eagles do not build on the ground, nor do their young run from the shell. That is a peculiarity of the gallinaceous tribe. Based on the white belly and dull red wing coverts, they are bustards.” It was a stark reminder of how our old-world words struggled, often inadequately, to capture the subjective majesty of this new earth.

They walked onward, eventually reaching the woods my wife had spied. It was not a sprawling forest, but a mythic cathedral of merely a dozen gargantuan trees. The sheer scale of them defied the limits of our language. Their massive, arching roots sprang high into the air like the flying buttresses of an ancient palace, anchoring the central trunks into the rich, loamy earth. 

Here, the acoustic ecology was flawless. The thick foliage absorbed the harshness of the world, leaving only a profoundly peaceful rustle. The ground was a lush carpet of soft green herbage, free of thorns. In this majestic arboreal hall, they ate their midday meal, experiencing the food not as mere fuel, but as a communion with the deep, protective spirit of the grove. It was an environment that begged to be inhabited, to have their daily domestic routines interwoven with its ancient rhythms. 

Returning to the shore, Jack proudly strapped on his sun-cured belt, while Ernest fitted the spiked collars onto the dogs. They found Turk and Juno at the tide pools, cracking the shells of small green crabs, the crisp, briny snapping mingling with the sound of the receding tide. But it was Juno who uncovered the true culinary prize. Scraping furiously at the sand, she unearthed a clutch of round, leathery spheres. 

Ernest examined them. “Turtles’ eggs,” he reported. 

My wife immediately recognized the profound value of the find. Driving the reluctant dog away, they harvested two dozen of the heavy, yolk-dense eggs, stowing them carefully. These were not merely scavenged calories; they were the rich, concentrated essence of the sea, promising deep nourishment. 

“Now,” my wife concluded her tale beneath the flickering light of our tent, “I hope tomorrow you will pack our lives and take us to live among my splendid trees.”

“A tree?” I laughed, my mind initially failing to translate her vision. “A roost for us to perch upon like birds?” 

“It is not absurd,” she insisted, her voice resonating with the agrarian wisdom of our ancestors. “In Switzerland, I knew of a beautiful arbour built strong and true in the branches of a lime tree. We could construct a sanctuary up there, safe from the jackals.”

I promised to consider it, and beneath the canvas, we slept as soundly as marmots until the dawn broke. 

In the morning, the transition from our rocky prison to the Promised Land, as the children quickly dubbed it, became our central focus. I agreed that the heat and the abrasive soundscape of the beach were intolerable. We would make the grove our home, and the rocky cliffs our magazine. 

“I could easily render it more secure by blasting the rock with gunpowder,” I mused. 

“The gunpowder,” she said with a shudder, “must be stored far from us.” 

We both understood this implicitly. Gunpowder was the literal embodiment of the old world’s industrial violence—a deafening, destructive force that threatened to shatter the pure acoustic harmony of our new existence. We would entomb it in the rock. But first, to cross the tidal stream with our animals, a bridge was required.

While my wife set to work stitching heavy canvas panniers for the donkey—a meticulous, tactile labor of love—Fritz, Ernest, and I took the boat out toward the reef to harvest planks from the wreck. 

As we rounded the islet, an auditory assault hit us. A wild, screaming cacophony of gulls and sea-birds rose in a blinding cloud from the sand. It was a chaotic, gluttonous noise, eerily reminiscent of the crowded, commercialized squalor of European fish markets—a jarring rupture in the island’s natural symphony. 

Sailing closer, we discovered the source of their frenzy. A monstrous fish lay stranded, its flesh being violently torn by the ravenous flock. 

“It is the shark!” Ernest cried. “Fritz’s shark from yesterday!”

“Look at those rows of murderous teeth,” I said, marveling at the grim reality of the beast. “Let us take a portion of its skin. When dry, its rough texture will serve us well as a file.”

Ernest charged into the shrieking mass, his ramrod striking left and right, killing several birds and silencing the immediate din. Fritz skillfully detached broad, raspy strips of the shark’s hide, the smell of iodine and decay heavy in the air. 

We gathered our planks, lashed them into a raft, and towed them homeward. On the journey, Fritz nailed the wet shark skin to boards to dry in the sun. 

“Will it be useful if it dries crooked?” he asked. 

“It will become beautiful shagreen,” I told him, “perfect for smoothing joiners' work.” 

“I thought shagreen was made from asses' hides,” Ernest remarked. 

“You thought rightly,” I replied, touching upon the strange ways human language bends to commerce and power. “In the East, they press hard grains of corn into fresh hides to artificially create the texture. But in France, they harvest it naturally from the rough skin of a hideous creature they have named the angel-fish.”

“Angel-fish!” Fritz exclaimed. “What a name for something hideous!”

“There are bad angels as well as good ones,” Ernest noted dryly, highlighting the inherent untranslatability of human labels, where dominant lexicons often mask the visceral truth of the physical world.

Upon our return, the silence of the beach was broken by a cheery halloo. My wife and the two younger boys emerged from the rocks, carrying mysterious, heavy bundles in their handkerchiefs. When opened, they revealed a magnificent, writhing mass of grand crawfish. 

“We found them swarming the jackal’s carcass!” Jack beamed. 

We immediately prepared a feast. As my wife plunged the crawfish into the boiling pot, the air filled with the sweet, oceanic steam of cooking chitin and briny meat. The shells turned a brilliant, coral-red. When we sat to eat, the meat was sweet, succulent, and perfectly firm—a profound, gustatory revelation that bound us deeply to the land’s provisions. We did not eat merely to survive; we ate to understand our place in this new world, finding marrow-deep meaning in the sustenance provided by the cycle of life and death on the island. 

Energized by the magnificent meal, I turned my attention to the stream. Ernest, utilizing the simple, timeless tools of our heritage, suggested tying a stone to a ball of string to measure the gap. It was a perfect, elegant solution, revealing a distance of eighteen feet. Adding allowance for the banks, I calculated we needed twenty-four-foot planks. 

As I sat contemplating the engineering puzzle, my wife proudly displayed her finished canvas bags. Lacking an awl, she had patiently, meticulously bored the hole for every single stitch with an iron nail. It was a stunning piece of traditional craftsmanship, quietly defying the disposable nature of the world we had left behind. 

With dinner finished and the afternoon sun warming the rocks, a scheme finally crystallized in my mind for how we might lever the first great plank across the rushing water.
