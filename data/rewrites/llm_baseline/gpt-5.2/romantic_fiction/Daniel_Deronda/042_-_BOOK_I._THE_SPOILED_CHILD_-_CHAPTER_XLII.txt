Deronda had been reading, only the night before, a passage that clung to his mind with the tenacity of an old refrain—Zunz speaking of a tragedy that had lasted not for one theatre-season but for fifteen centuries, written and acted by its sufferers, carried like a wound from land to land. It came back to him now with a sort of ironical contrast as he walked toward the Cohens’ pawnbroker’s shop; for nothing in that narrow frontage, with its bright little temptations under glass, suggested an aristocracy of sorrow. Ezra Cohen’s prosperity had the self-satisfied sheen of well-rubbed metal; his hands were quick for bargains, his eyes quick for safety, and his life had grown fat in that exasperating way in which greed seems sometimes to flourish most where it has been most condemned.

Yet it was precisely here—among padded counters and the common plenty of a family who talked loud and lived close—that Mordecai’s fragile, burning consciousness had found a nest. The thought had a strange fitness in it, as if history loved to hide its most haunted spirits behind the most ordinary doors.

Their gladness at seeing Deronda again was almost lustrous. Cohen met him with the eager cordiality of a man whose hospitality had the air of being a shrewd investment and yet was not wholly that.

“Why, sir, we’ve done nothing but talk of you since you was here. And as for the ring—let it lie a bit longer, and it would have bred more money, so to speak—but I don’t mind that—not a sixpence—against the pleasure you’ve given the women and the children.”

Young Mrs. Cohen hurried him inward as if the parlour itself were a better proof of affection than the shop.

“Don’t stop here among the tickets and the bundles, Mr. Deronda. Come in—mother and the children are in the parlour. I’m only sorry baby’s asleep—though then again I’m glad, for she’d cry at the door opening. But Adelaide’s up.”

Deronda had come prepared for such welcome. He drew out his little offerings with the quiet pleasure of one who likes to see delight take shape: a packet of paper figures for Adelaide, bright and jointed for dancing; and an ivory cup and ball for Jacob, smooth as bone and cool to the touch.

In the parlour the grandmother sat with a pack of cards before her, making “plates” with the children—little flat constructions that could be tossed and caught in a way that seemed to the young a kind of miracle. One plate had just been flung down and, by a stroke of luck, had kept itself whole.

“Stop!” Jacob cried, darting to Deronda as he entered. “Don’t tread on my plate. Stop and see me throw it up again.”

Deronda paused at once, smiling, and the grandmother gave him a look that said she understood this reverence for a child’s proud moment. Jacob tossed again and again, until the cards at last broke apart and the triumph ended in a crash of paper.

Only then was Deronda allowed to come forward and seat himself. He noticed, with a quick inward note, that the door through which Mordecai had appeared last time was now closed. He let the moment wait. It seemed right first to take his place among these homely affections, to let their warmth reach him before he asked for the man who had drawn him here.

Adelaide, light as a bird, climbed to his knee, and he set up the paper figures on the table, making them bow and turn as if the stiff little dancers had breath. Jacob had seized his ivory toy already and practised, tongue between teeth, the patient rhythm of catching—cup, ball, string taut and slack, his small wrist learning its own precision.

Then, as if speaking only because the room’s merriment could bear it, Deronda said, “Is Mordecai in just now?”

“Where is he, Addy?” Cohen asked, having slipped in from the shop during a lull.

“In the workroom there,” said his wife, nodding toward the closed door.

Cohen’s face altered—business alertness giving place to a perplexed concern.

“The fact is, sir, we don’t know what’s come to him this last day or two. He’s always what I may call a little touched, you know”—he tapped his forehead with an apologetic bluntness—“not quite so rational in all things, like you and me; but mostly he’s wonderful regular and industrious, so far as a poor creature can be. And he takes as much delight in the boy as anybody could. But this last day or two he’s been moving about like a sleep-walker, or else sitting as still as a wax figure.”

“It’s the disease,” said the grandmother, her voice softening like a cloth laid over something fragile. “Poor dear creature. I doubt whether he can stand long against it.”

“No,” said Mrs. Cohen, with the more practical resistance of youth, “I think it’s only something he’s got in his head. He’s been turning over writing continually, and when I speak to him it takes him ever so long to hear and answer.”

“You may think us a little weak ourselves,” Cohen added, half defending himself against an imagined critic. “But my wife and mother wouldn’t part with him if he was a still worse incumbrance. It isn’t that we don’t know the long and short. It’s our principle. There’s fools do business at a loss and don’t know it. I’m not one of ’em.”

“Oh, Mordecai carries a blessing inside him,” the grandmother said, with the obstinate tenderness of an older generation that keeps faith with what it cannot prove.

“He’s got something the matter inside him,” Jacob put in, coming near to correct her with childish exactness. “He said he couldn’t talk to me, and he wouldn’t have a bit o’ bun.”

Deronda’s heart tightened at that small detail—the refusal of a sweet, the turning away from the ordinary bribe of comfort. It meant a mind drawn so far inward that even taste had become distant.

“So far from wondering at your feeling for him,” Deronda said, “I already feel something of the same sort myself. I have lately talked to him at Ram’s book-shop—in fact, I promised to call for him here, that we might go out together.”

“That’s it, then!” Cohen exclaimed, slapping his knee with the sudden pleasure of explanation. “He’s been expecting you, and it’s taken hold of him. I suppose he talks about his learning to you. It’s uncommonly kind of you, sir; for I don’t suppose there’s much to be got out of it, else it wouldn’t have left him where he is. But there’s the shop.” And he hurried away again, pulled by the tide of customers.

Jacob, always ready to be useful, pushed himself near Deronda’s elbow. “I’ll call Mordecai for you, if you like.”

“No, Jacob,” his mother said quickly. “Open the door for the gentleman and let him go in himself. Hush! Don’t make a noise.”

Jacob entered into the importance of silence as if it were part of a game. He turned the handle with exaggerated care, and Deronda followed, standing on the threshold.

The little workroom beyond was lit only by a fire dying into a red dullness and one candle, shaded. The air held that peculiar mingling of things stored too long: faint metal tang, dusty cloth, a suggestion of oil used for mechanisms. On the board beneath the window lay scattered pieces of jewellery—brooches, chains, a few rings—objects that had once been intimate on someone’s skin and were now coldly awaiting redemption. In the corner, beyond them, a small heap of books leaned in a way that suggested they had been taken up and put down with impatience.

Mordecai sat on a high chair at the board, his back to the door, his hands resting on one another and on the wood. A watch stood propped before him like an idol of measured time. His stillness was not calm; it was strained, as if all his being listened.

Deronda spoke, and the sound seemed to cut through the close room like a breath of fresh air.

“I am come for you. Are you ready?”

Mordecai turned instantly, without a word. He seized his furred cap and moved toward Deronda with the swiftness of a man released from a sentence. In another moment they were back in the parlour, and Jacob, catching the change in Mordecai’s face—some tightening loosened, some pallor stirred—caught his arm with affectionate urgency.

“See my cup and ball!” he cried, sending the ball up close to Mordecai’s cheek, as if skill and play were medicine.

It was a sign of the easing within Mordecai that he could smile.

“Fine, fine!”

“You’ve forgotten your greatcoat and comforter,” said Mrs. Cohen, and Mordecai went back obediently, fetching them as if he had been half-dreaming until that reminder tied him again to the outer world.

“He’s come to life again, do you see?” Cohen whispered when he reappeared, triumph warming his undertone. “I told you so: I’m mostly right.” Then, aloud, with hospitable insistence: “Well, sir, we mustn’t detain you, I suppose; but I hope this isn’t the last time we shall see you.”

“Shall you come again?” Jacob demanded, advancing with the earnestness of a small bargainer. “See, I can catch the ball; I’ll bet I catch it without stopping, if you come again.”

“He has clever hands,” Deronda said, glancing toward the grandmother. “Which side of the family does he get them from?”

The grandmother only nodded toward her son, who answered with prompt vanity.

“My side. My wife’s family are not in that line. But bless your soul! ours is a sort of cleverness as good as gutta percha; you can twist it which way you like. There’s nothing some old gentlemen won’t do if you set ’em to it.”

He winked down at Jacob’s back; but the boy, catching only the words, stamped and gave a nasal whinny of laughter, chanting, “Old gentlemen, old gentlemen,” in a little sing-song that made the room ring.

As Deronda went out with Mordecai, the thought returned, persistent as hunger: he would never know anything decisive about these people until he asked Cohen pointblank whether he had lost a sister named Mirah when she was six years old. Yet the decisive question lodged in him like something too sharp to swallow. He could not find the moment for it—not yet.

His first recoil from the Cohens’ commonness had already begun to yield to a gentler judgment. Their speech was coarse, their manners unpolished; and yet there was a kind of moral fineness in the way they kept this consumptive worker under their roof, not as a profitable tool but as a being they had pledged themselves to shelter—loving him, even while understanding him only as a harmless, silent visionary.

Outside, the street had its usual London sound—wheels over stones, calls that rose and fell, a far clink as if the city’s iron nerves were never at rest. Mordecai’s breathing made a thin, inward counter-music, and Deronda instinctively kept his pace fitted to it.

“The Cohens seem to have an affection for you,” Deronda said.

“And I for them,” Mordecai answered at once. “They have the heart of the Israelite within them, though they are as the horse and the mule, without understanding beyond the narrow path they tread.”

“I have caused you some uneasiness, I fear, by my slowness in fulfilling my promise,” Deronda said. “I wished to come yesterday, but I found it impossible.”

“Yes—yes, I trusted you. But it is true I have been uneasy, for the spirit of my youth has been stirred within me, and this body is not strong enough to bear the beating of its wings. I am as a man bound and imprisoned through long years: behold him brought to speech of his fellow and his limbs set free: he weeps, he totters, the joy within him threatens to break and overthrow the tabernacle of flesh.”

Deronda felt those words of reliance fasten round him with a painful tenderness, like cords that bound because they were made of need.

“You must not speak too much in this evening air,” he said, practical care answering the poet’s rapture. “Cover your mouth with the woollen scarf. We are going to the Hand and Banner, I suppose, and shall be in private there?”

“No—that is my trouble that you did not come yesterday,” Mordecai said. “For this is the evening of the club I spoke of, and we might not have any minutes alone until late, when all the rest are gone. Perhaps we had better seek another place. But I am used to that only. In new places the outer world presses on me and narrows the inward vision. And the people there are familiar with my face.”

“I don’t mind the club if I am allowed to go in,” Deronda answered, and he found he was not sorry for the delay that company would bring. It gave him a breathing-space before the private strain that waited between them. “It is enough that you like this place best. If we have not time, I will come again. What sort of club is it?”

“It is called ‘The Philosophers.’ They are few—like the cedars of Lebanon—poor men given to thought. But none so poor as I am. And sometimes visitors of higher worldly rank have been brought. We are allowed to introduce a friend who is interested in our topics. Each orders beer or some other drink, in payment for the room. Most of them smoke. I have gone when I could, for there are other men of my race who come, and sometimes I have broken silence. I have pleased myself with a faint likeness between these poor philosophers and the Masters who handed down the thought of our race—the great Transmitters, who laboured with their hands for scant bread, but preserved and enlarged for us the heritage of memory, and saved the soul of Israel alive as a seed among the tombs. The heart pleases itself with faint resemblances.”

Deronda listened as they walked. Around them the modern city kept up its ceaseless grinding chorus—too many hard edges, too much haste, as if even sound had been commercialised into mere pressure. He had a sudden sense that the little room Mordecai spoke of, with its human voices and chosen silence, might be a refuge not only from cold air but from the vulgar tyranny of noise.

“I shall be very glad to go and sit among them,” Deronda said, “if that will suit you. It is a sort of meeting I should like to join in.”

In a few minutes they had passed through a glazed door with a red curtain and entered a little parlour hardly more than fifteen feet square. Gaslight shone through a slight haze of smoke, and the air was dense with the smell of burning tobacco—acrid and sweet at once, layered with the damp wool of coats and the faint sourness of beer. The room’s sounds were intimate: the small click of pipes against teeth, a match struck and hissed, a chair leg scraping, the low murmur that swelled into argument and dropped again, like waves in a confined harbour.

Half-a-dozen men sat in a tight semicircle round the fireplace and a small table holding glasses, spare pipes, and tobacco. They were shabbily dressed, but their faces had that sharpened look which comes from minds that have had to bite their knowledge out of scant hours. A broad-chested man in pepper-and-salt, fair-haired with a wide forehead, was speaking, his pipe lifted in his left hand while his right beat time on his knee. He was finishing a quotation from Shelley—his voice thickened a little by smoke but clear with conviction.

“As thought by thought is piled, till some great truth
Is loosened, and the nations echo round.”

The entrance broke the fixity of their attention. Seats shifted; bodies made room in the cramped arc. This was the soberest of clubs; and yet the need to smoke and “take something” asserted itself as the common rite of equality, a way of buying one’s place in talk.

Mordecai was greeted with voices that carried, under their cordiality, a cadence of compassion. But the curious glances went instantly to Deronda.

“I have brought a friend who is interested in our subjects,” Mordecai said. “He has travelled and studied much.”

“Is the gentleman anonymous? Is he a Great Unknown?” the broad man asked, with an amused readiness.

“My name is Daniel Deronda,” Deronda said. “I am unknown, but not in any sense great.”

The smile that broke over his gravity made the company murmur approval in that indistinct way which passes for applause among men who will not cheapen their feeling by noise. The broad man nodded.

“You recommend the name, sir, and are welcome. Here, Mordecai, come to this corner against me,” he added, making the warmest place the sick man’s.

Deronda took a seat opposite, where he could easily include them all in his view. Mordecai remained, even among these sharply marked faces, an object that compelled attention—so thin, so eager, with something in his eyes like a lamp burning too near its end.

Deronda noted, too, how little “pure English blood” declared itself here, if such a thing could ever be measured except by the fictions men like to tell. Miller—the fair, broad man—was a second-hand bookseller of an uncommon sort, one who knew books from within, and whose ancestry, by his own hinting, ran back to Germany and perhaps further to a denied Jewishness. Buchan the saddler had the quick, cool Scotch sound in his speech. Pash the watchmaker was small, dark, and vivid—his Jewishness stamped on him like a seal, his mind triple-fired into sharpness. Gideon, who made optical instruments, had red hair and generous features that might have passed anywhere for English cordiality. Croop the shoemaker, dark-eyed, looked as if Celtic blood had left its restless shadow in him. Only a few would have been recognised anywhere as simply English—Goodwin the wood-inlayer, open-faced and well-built; Marrables, florid, an assistant in a laboratory; and Lily, a pale copying-clerk with neat features and a shirt whose freshness seemed almost a national assertion against seediness.

Deronda did not look for comedy in their departures from polished tone. The room’s talk had an earnestness that made laughter feel beside the point. He ordered whisky and water, and, out of habit and a certain wish not to stand apart like a diagram of virtue, offered the contents of his cigar-case. He rarely smoked himself, for reasons he held to; but he liked to give. Matches flared; smoke thickened, and the talk returned to its thread as if his presence had been quietly absorbed.

“This is what I call one of our touch-and-go nights, sir,” Miller said to Deronda, assuming the role of moderator without offence, and nodding toward each man as he named him. “Sometimes we stick pretty close to the point. But to-night our friend Pash there brought up the law of progress; and we got on statistics; then Lily there saying we knew well enough before counting that in the same state of society the same sort of things would happen, and it was no more wonder that quantities should remain the same than that qualities should remain the same—for in relation to society numbers are qualities—the number of drunkards is a quality in society—the numbers are an index to the qualities, and give us no instruction, only setting us to consider the causes of difference between different social states—Lily saying that, we went off on causes of social change; and when you came in I was going upon the power of ideas, which I hold to be the main transforming cause.”

“I don’t hold with you there, Miller,” said Goodwin, leaning forward with the eagerness of a man more hungry for truth than for deference. “For either you mean so many sorts of things by ideas that I get no knowledge by what you say, any more than if you said light was a cause; or else you mean a particular sort of ideas, and then I go against your meaning as too narrow. Look at it one way, all actions men put a bit of thought into are ideas—sowing seed, making a canoe, baking clay; and such ideas as these work themselves into life and go on growing with it, but they can’t go apart from the material that set them to work and makes a medium for them. It’s the nature of wood and stone yielding to the knife that raises the idea of shaping them, and with plenty of wood and stone the shaping will go on. I look at it that such ideas as are mixed straight away with all the other elements of life are powerful along with ’em. The slower the mixing, the less power they have. And as to the causes of social change, I look at it in this way—ideas are a sort of parliament, but there’s a commonwealth outside, and a good deal of the commonwealth is working at change without knowing what the parliament is doing.”

“But if you take ready mixing as your test of power,” Pash said, eyes glittering as if he enjoyed the click and spring of paradox, “some of the least practical ideas beat everything. They spread without being understood, and enter into the language without being thought of.”

“They may act by changing the distribution of gases,” Marrables put in, half serious, half pleased with his own image. “Instruments are getting so fine now, men may come to register the spread of a theory by observed changes in the atmosphere and corresponding changes in the nerves.”

“Yes,” Pash replied, and his tone had a little impish lightness, “there is the idea of nationalities; I dare say the wild asses are snuffing it, and getting more gregarious.”

Deronda, struck by the incongruity between the sarcasm and the strong racial stamp of Pash’s features, let his curiosity take shape.

“You don’t share that idea?”

“Say, rather, he does not share that spirit,” Mordecai said, turning a melancholy glance on Pash. “Unless nationality is a feeling, what force can it have as an idea?”

“Granted, Mordecai,” Pash answered good-humouredly. “And as the feeling of nationality is dying, I take the idea to be no better than a ghost, already walking to announce the death.”

“A sentiment may seem to be dying and yet revive into strong life,” Deronda said. “Nations have revived. We may live to see a great outburst of force in the Arabs, who are being inspired with a new zeal.”

“Amen, amen,” Mordecai murmured, and his delight in Deronda’s words was visible: he sat more upright, as if energy had flowed back into him from that agreement, as if even his breath had found a fuller measure.

“That may hold with backward nations,” Pash said, “but with us in Europe the sentiment of nationality is destined to die out. It will last a little longer in the quarters where oppression lasts, but nowhere else. The whole current of progress is setting against it.”

“Ay,” Buchan cut in, his Scotch rapidity like a cool draught in the thick air, “ye’ve done well to bring us round to the point. Ye’re all agreed that societies change—not always and everywhere—but on the whole and in the long run. Now, with all deference, I would beg t’ observe that we have got to examine the nature of changes before we have a warrant to call them progress, which word is supposed to include a bettering, though I apprehend it to be ill-chosen for that purpose, since mere motion onward may carry us to a bog or a precipice. And the questions I would put are three: Is all change in the direction of progress? if not, how shall we discern which change is progress and which not? and thirdly, how far and in what way can we act upon the course of change so as to promote it where it is beneficial, and divert it where it is injurious?”

But Buchan’s method could not bridle the talk; it broke and ran in livelier channels at once.

“Change and progress are merged in the idea of development,” Lily said, with the clipped assurance of a clerk who has made certainty out of copied sentences. “The laws of development are being discovered, and changes taking place according to them are necessarily progressive; that is to say, if we have any notion of progress or improvement opposed to them, the notion is a mistake.”

Deronda felt his mind resist, not out of love for contradiction but out of dread of that new superstition which wears the mask of reason and asks men to bow without understanding.

“I really can’t see how you arrive at that sort of certitude about changes by calling them development,” he said. “There will still remain the degrees of inevitableness in relation to our own will and acts, and the degrees of wisdom in hastening or retarding. There will still remain the danger of mistaking a tendency which should be resisted for an inevitable law that we must adjust ourselves to—which seems to me as bad a superstition or false god as any that has been set up without the ceremonies of philosophising.”

“That is a truth,” Mordecai said, and his voice, though low, had the ring of something forged in long inward fire. “Woe to the men who see no place for resistance in this generation! I believe in a growth, a passage, and a new unfolding of life whereof the seed is more perfect, more charged with the elements that are pregnant with diviner form. The life of a people grows, it is knit together and yet expanded, in joy and sorrow, in thought and action; it absorbs the thought of other nations into its own forms, and gives back the thought as new wealth to the world; it is a power and an organ in the great body of the nations. But there may come a check, an arrest; memories may be stifled, and love may be faint for the lack of them; or memories may shrink into withered relics—the soul of a people, whereby they know themselves to be one, may seem to be dying for want of common action. But who shall say, ‘The fountain of their life is dried up, they shall for ever cease to be a nation?’ Who shall say it? Not he who feels the life of his people stirring within his own. Shall he say, ‘That way events are wending, I will not resist?’ His very soul is resistance, and is as a seed of fire that may enkindle the souls of multitudes, and make a new pathway for events.”
