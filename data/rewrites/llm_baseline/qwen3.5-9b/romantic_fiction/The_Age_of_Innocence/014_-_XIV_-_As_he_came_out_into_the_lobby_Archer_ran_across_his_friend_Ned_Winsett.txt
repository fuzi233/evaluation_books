As Archer stepped out into the bustling lobby, he collided with Ned Winsett, the sole member of Janey's circle of "clever people" with whom Archer felt compelled to dig deeper than the surface-level chatter typical of the club and high-end dining rooms. Spotting Winsett's familiar, hunched silhouette across the room—his eyes previously noted glancing toward the Beaufort box—the two men exchanged greetings. Winsett immediately suggested they grab a beer at a small German eatery nearby. Archer, however, politely declined, citing pressing work at home. "Oh, well, so do I, for that matter," Winsett replied with a shrug, adopting the persona of the industrious apprentice.

Strolling side by side, Winsett soon pivoted to his true purpose. "Look here," he said, "what I'm really after is the name of the dark lady in that fancy box of yours—the one with the Beauforts, wasn't it? The one your friend Lefferts seems so infatuated with." Archer felt a flicker of annoyance he couldn't quite place. What did Ned Winsett want with Ellen Olenska's name? And why pair it with Lefferts? It was unlike the journalist to show such specific curiosity, yet Archer recalled Winsett's trade. "Is this for an interview?" Archer asked, laughing lightly. "No, not for the press; just for myself," Winsett corrected. "She's actually a neighbor of mine—a strange neighborhood for such a beauty to inhabit. She was incredibly kind to my little boy, who fell in her courtyard chasing a kitten and gave himself a nasty cut. She rushed in bareheaded, holding him in her arms with his knee beautifully bandaged, radiating such sympathy and grace that my wife was completely dazzled and forgot to ask her name."

A warm glow spread through Archer's chest. While the act itself was noble and something any woman might do, it was quintessentially Ellen to rush in bareheaded, cradling the child, and to mesmerize poor Mrs. Winsett into oblivion regarding her identity. "That is the Countess Olenska," Archer revealed smoothly, "a granddaughter of old Mrs. Mingott's."

"Whew—a Countess!" Winsett whistled. "Well, I didn't realize Countesses were so neighborly. The Mingotts certainly aren't." "They would be, if you'd let them," Archer retorted. Their usual debate regarding the stubborn refusal of the intellectual elite to mingle with the fashionable crowd resumed briefly, but both men knew it was futile to prolong it. "I wonder," Winsett interrupted, "how a Countess manages to live in our slums?" "Because she doesn't care a fig about where she lives—or about any of our petty social markers," Archer said, feeling a secret surge of pride in his mental portrait of her. "Hmm—she must have been in bigger places, I suppose," Winsett observed. "Well, here's my corner." He slouched off across Broadway, leaving Archer standing alone, watching him go and pondering his final words.

Ned Winsett possessed flashes of profound insight; they were his most captivating trait, often making Archer wonder why the journalist accepted failure so stoically at an age when most men were still striving. Archer knew Winsett had a wife and child, though he had never seen them. Their paths crossed only at the Century Club or in haunts frequented by writers and theater-goers, like the restaurant where Winsett had proposed their meeting. Winsett had hinted that his wife was an invalid—a claim that could be literal truth or merely a euphemism for lacking social graces, evening attire, or both. Winsett harbored a fierce disdain for social conventions. Archer, who dressed formally simply because he found it cleaner and more comfortable—and hadn't considered that cleanliness and comfort were expensive luxuries in a modest budget—viewed Winsett's attitude as part of a tedious "Bohemian" affectation. Yet, Archer was always invigorated by him. Whenever he spotted Winsett's lean, bearded face and melancholy eyes, he would drag him out of his corner for a lengthy conversation.

Winsett was not a journalist by choice; he was a pure man of letters, tragically born in an era that held no demand for literature. After publishing a single volume of brief, exquisite literary critiques—one hundred and twenty copies sold, thirty distributed, and the rest destroyed by the publisher to make room for profitable content—he had abandoned his true vocation. He now worked as a sub-editor on a women's weekly magazine, *Hearth-fires*, where fashion plates and sewing patterns alternated with New England romance novels and ads for temperance beverages. On the subject of domestic life, he was endlessly entertaining, but beneath his humor lay the sterile bitterness of a young man who had tried everything and given up. His conversations forced Archer to assess his own life and realize its emptiness compared to Winsett's even shallower existence. Despite their shared intellectual curiosities making their talks exhilarating, their exchanges usually remained within the bounds of a thoughtful dilettantism.

"The fact is, life isn't much of a fit for either of us," Winsett had once confessed. "I'm down and out; there's nothing to be done. I have only one product to offer, and there's no market for it here, nor will there be in my lifetime. But you are free and well-off. Why don't *you* get involved? There's only one way: enter politics." Archer threw his head back and laughed. There, instantly, appeared the insurmountable divide between men like Winsett and Archer's class. Everyone in polite society knew that in America, "a gentleman cannot go into politics." Unable to phrase it so bluntly to Winsett, Archer responded evasively, "Look at the career of the honest man in American politics! They don't want us." "Who is 'they'? Why don't you all unite and become 'they' yourselves?" Archer's laughter faded into a slightly condescending smile. It was pointless to extend the discussion; everyone knew the tragic fate of the few gentlemen who had risked their pristine reputations in New York municipal or state politics. The era for such endeavors was gone; the nation belonged to the bosses and the immigrants, forcing decent people to retreat into sports or culture.

"Culture! Yes—if we had it! But there are only a few tiny, dying patches here and there, lacking... well, tending and cross-pollination: the last remnants of the old European tradition your ancestors brought with them. But you remain a pitiful minority: you have no center, no competition, no audience. You are like portraits on the walls of an empty house: 'The Portrait of a Gentleman.' None of you will achieve anything until you roll up your sleeves and dive straight into the mud. Or, emigrate..." Archer mentally shrugged and steered the conversation back to books, where Winsett, if unsure, was invariably fascinating. Emigrate! As if a gentleman could abandon his homeland! One could not do that any more than one could roll up one's sleeves and descend into the filth. A gentleman simply remained at home and abstained. But convincing a man like Winsett of this logic was impossible, which explained why New York's literary clubs and exotic restaurants, initially appearing as a dazzling kaleidoscope, ultimately resolved into a smaller box with a far more monotonous pattern than the scattered elements of Fifth Avenue.

The following morning, Archer searched the city in vain for yellow roses. Arriving late at the office due to this quest, he realized his tardiness affected no one, filling him with sudden fury at the elaborate futility of his existence. Why not be, at that very moment, on the sands of St. Augustine with May Welland? No one suspected his feigned professional diligence. In traditional legal firms like Mr. Letterblair's, which managed vast estates and conservative investments, there were always two or three young men, reasonably wealthy and devoid of professional ambition, who spent hours performing trivial tasks or simply reading newspapers. Although propriety demanded they hold occupations, the crude reality of money-making was still viewed as beneath them; the law, being a profession, was deemed a more gentlemanly pursuit than commerce. Yet, none of these youths harbored serious hopes or desires for advancement, and the green mold of perfunctoriness was visibly spreading over many. The thought that it might spread over him too made Archer shudder.

He certainly had other passions: European travels during vacations, cultivating the "clever people" May mentioned, and generally trying to "keep up," as he had wistfully admitted to Madame Olenska. But once married, what would happen to this narrow margin of life where his genuine experiences occurred? He had witnessed other young men dream his dreams, perhaps less fervently, only to sink gradually into the placid, luxurious routines of their elders. From the office, he dispatched a note via messenger to Madame Olenska, requesting permission to call that afternoon and asking her to leave a reply at his club. However, he found nothing there, nor did he receive a letter the next day. This unexpected silence mortified him beyond reason. Although the following morning revealed a magnificent cluster of yellow roses behind a florist's window, he walked past them without stopping.

It was only on the third morning that a postal line arrived from the Countess Olenska.
