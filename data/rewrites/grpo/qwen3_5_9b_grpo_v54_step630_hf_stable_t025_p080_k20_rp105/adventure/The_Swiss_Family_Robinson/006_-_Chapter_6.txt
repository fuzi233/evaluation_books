The discovery of bread had solved our immediate hunger, but my mind drifted inevitably back to the wreck, specifically the magnificent pinnace that still lay within its hull. I knew that reclaiming her would require the combined strength of all the boys, save for Franz. I approached my wife with a plea to allow me to lead this expedition. After some reluctance, she consented, only after I solemnly promised never to spend a night aboard the ship. We parted with a mutual sense of unease, though the boys were thrilled at the prospect of such a grand venture, marching off with bags of cassava-bread and potatoes.

Upon reaching Safety Bay, we tended to the geese and ducks before donning our cork belts and stepping into the tub-boat, towing the raft toward the wreck. Once aboard, I ordered the boys to gather whatever they could carry onto the raft for our return journey, while I conducted a thorough inspection of the pinnace. It was clear that the vessel was trapped in an impossibly confined space deep within the hold. The parts were too heavy to move individually, and the area was too narrow to assemble them.

I dismissed the boys to rummage for salvage and sat in the dim light, observing how meticulously the ship's components were numbered and arranged. If only I could create space, success seemed within reach. "Room! We need room to work!" I shouted as my sons gathered around, their faith in my leadership absolute. "Bring axes!" I commanded. We hacked away at the surrounding woodwork, but as evening fell, our progress was negligible. The task ahead was immense, requiring a perseverance that bordered on the superhuman.

Returning to shore, we found my wife and Franz waiting at Tentholm. She had moved her quarters there to be closer to our work, a gesture that filled me with gratitude. The boys returned with casks of butter, flour, and other provisions, a welcome addition to our stores. For days, we labored tirelessly, clearing a workspace around the pinnace and attempting to assemble her. Finally, the vessel stood ready, yet she remained imprisoned by the massive wooden walls of the wreck.

Despair threatened to overwhelm me until a sudden idea struck. I retrieved a large cast-iron mortar, packed it with gunpowder, and secured it atop a block of oak with a fuse inserted. I positioned this petard to blast open the side of the wreck where the pinnace lay. Securing it with chains to prevent recoil damage, I told the boys I was returning to shore early and urged them into the boat. With a pounding heart, I lit the slow-burning match and hurried after them.

From the beach, we watched anxiously as the explosion tore through the air—a flash, a roar, and a cloud of smoke. My family, terrified, turned to me for explanation. Calmly, I reassured them that a fire during our caulking work had likely triggered the blast. Without waiting, we raced back to the wreck.

What awaited us was a scene of devastation. The side of the ship was torn open, debris floated in the water, and the compartment was exposed. Yet, nestled safely within the breach, the pinnace sat untouched. The boys realized my cunning plan, cheering at the sight of their freedom. We examined the damage; the vessel was unharmed. With rollers placed beneath the keel and levers ready, we worked together to slide the pinnace gently into the water. She floated effortlessly, a testament to our industry.

We returned to Tentholm, claiming the explosion was merely a means to access the remaining cargo. Over the following days, we finished rigging the pinnace and mounting her two brass guns. The boys' imaginations ran wild with tales of naval battles and savage encounters, though I reminded them that our true victory would be living in peace.

Before setting sail, we surprised my wife with a salute from the guns as we entered the bay. Her initial terror gave way to boundless joy as she boarded the vessel. We then ventured inland to show her the garden she had cultivated in our absence—rows of vegetables, fruit trees, and a neat layout that spoke of her diligence and love.

The following Sunday, we gathered for worship and instruction. In the afternoon, I demonstrated athletic exercises to the boys, emphasizing that true courage required both moral strength and physical prowess. The next day, I introduced them to the lasso, a versatile tool for hunting and survival. Fritz quickly mastered the technique, proving himself adept with the new weapon.

As a gale approached, we prepared for a family excursion to the Calabash Wood. Loaded with provisions and accompanied by our pets, we set out. The journey led us through lush landscapes where Fritz shot a magnificent bustard, a feat that initially caused alarm for the bird's offspring but ultimately resulted in the rescue of the wounded mother. We encountered a coconut crab descending a tree, which Jack captured with a clever trick using his jacket.

Our trek revealed the wonders of the island, including the precious liane rouge, a vine providing fresh water. We harvested gourds from the calabash trees, carving them into bowls and cups. However, our cooking plans were thwarted by a lack of water, prompting a search that led to a startling encounter with a wild boar. To our amusement, the beast turned out to be our own runaway sow, feasting on potatoes we had inadvertently disturbed.

Continuing our journey, Jack mistook a large iguana for a crocodile. I patiently demonstrated how to capture the harmless lizard using a noose and a whistle, a method that fascinated the boys. Carrying the iguana on my back, we returned to Falconhurst, where we enjoyed a hearty supper of roasted iguana, acorns, and the fruit we had gathered. The day's adventures, filled with danger, discovery, and teamwork, reinforced our bond and our resolve to thrive in this new world.
