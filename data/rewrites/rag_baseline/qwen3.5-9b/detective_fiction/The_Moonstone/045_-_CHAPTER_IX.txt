The morning sun poured through the open street door, flooding the hall with a brilliance that seemed to mock the shadowed interior of the doctor's residence. Standing there, a picture of forced cheerfulness, was the physician's pretty housemaid, her hand resting on the latch as if holding back the light itself. She greeted me with a smile that didn't quite reach her eyes, a mask of amiability worn thin by the weight of secrets she carried.

When I turned to face the man awaiting me, the warmth of the sunlight caught the contours of Ezra Jennings' face, casting sharp, dramatic shadows across features that defied easy categorization. It was undeniable, as Betteredge had claimed, that his very presence worked against him in the court of public opinion. His complexion bore the sallow hue of the gypsy tribes; his cheeks were hollowed out, revealing the stark geometry of his gaunt facial bones. His eyes held a perpetual, dreamlike vacancy, and his hair was a chaotic tapestry of disparate colors, a visual paradox that rendered him simultaneously ancient and youthful. This dissonance between his visage and his frame created an aura of unsettling mystery, likely designed to repel the casual observer. Yet, despite the instinctive wariness that prickled along my spine, an inexplicable pull anchored me to him. There was a profound, inscrutable resonance in his demeanor that bypassed logic and spoke directly to my sympathies, making resistance impossible.

While my worldly experience screamed caution—warning me to acknowledge the gravity of Mr. Candy's decline and depart immediately—this magnetic attraction to Ezra Jennings held me fast. It granted him the opening he had clearly anticipated, allowing him to address me privately regarding his employer.

"Are you walking my way, Mr. Jennings?" I asked, noting the hat he clutched in his hand. "I am heading to pay a call on my aunt, Mrs. Ablewhite."

Jennings confirmed he had a patient to attend to, but that his path coincided with mine.

Together, we departed the house. As we stepped out, I witnessed the transaction of dismissal. The maid, previously radiant with greeting, received a brief note from Jennings concerning his expected return time. Her response was a tight-lipped refusal, her eyes darting deliberately away from his face, refusing to meet his gaze. The poor soul was manifestly an outcast within the household. Outside its walls, according to Betteredge's testimony, his unpopularity extended universally. "What a life," I murmured to myself as we descended the steps, the weight of his isolation settling heavily upon me.

Jennings, having already alluded to Mr. Candy's ailment from his own perspective, now seemed resolved to pass the baton, leaving the narrative to me. His silence was eloquent, a silent prompt signaling, *Your turn.* I, too, possessed compelling reasons to initiate the discussion. Accepting the mantle willingly, I broached the subject.

"Judging by the transformation I observe in him," I began, "Mr. Candy's illness must have been far more severe than I initially suspected?"

"It is nearly a miracle," Jennings replied, his voice low and weary, "that he survived it at all."

"Does his memory ever clear, even momentarily? He has been attempting to speak to me..."

"Regarding events prior to his taking ill?" Jennings interrupted, sensing my hesitation.

"Yes."

"The memory of those past events is hopelessly enfeebled," Jennings stated, his tone tinged with pity. "It is a tragedy, truly, that even the wreckage of it persists. He retains dim, fragmented impressions of plans he formulated—actions he intended to take—but he is utterly incapable of retrieving the substance of those plans or the nature of the tasks themselves. He suffers acutely from this deficit, painfully aware of his own failure, and equally painfully anxious to conceal it from others. Had he been allowed to sink into a total oblivion of the past, he would undoubtedly be a happier man. Perhaps we all would be," he added, offering a sorrowful smile, "if we could simply choose to forget!"

"There are surely certain events in every man's life," I countered, "whose memory he would be reluctant to surrender entirely?"

"That, I hope, applies to most men, Mr. Blake. I fear it cannot be said of *all*. Do you have any reason to suspect that the lost memory Mr. Candy struggled to reclaim during our conversation just now was one of particular importance to *you*?"

With those words, he had inadvertently touched the very nerve I sought to probe. The curiosity I felt for this enigmatic figure had compelled me to grant him the floor, carefully reserving my own disclosures until I was convinced of his integrity and discretion. What little he had revealed thus far was sufficient proof that I was conversing with a gentleman of refinement. He possessed what I might term *unsought self-possession*, a hallmark of true breeding recognized across civilized nations. Regardless of his ulterior motives in posing that question, I felt fully justified in answering without reservation.

"I believe I possess a strong stake," I admitted, "in recovering the lost memory Mr. Candy was unable to access. Might you suggest any method by which I could aid his recollection?"

Jennings regarded me, a sudden spark of intense interest igniting in his dreamy brown eyes.

"Mr. Candy's memory lies beyond the reach of assistance," he declared firmly. "I have endeavored to revive it repeatedly since his recovery, and I can speak with certainty on that point."

Disappointment washed over me, and I confessed it openly.

"I confess you led me to expect a less disheartening prognosis," I said.

Jennings smiled gently. "It may not be a final verdict, Mr. Blake. It is conceivable that Mr. Candy's lost recollection can be traced without the necessity of appealing to Mr. Candy himself."

"Indeed? Is it improper of me to inquire how?"

"Not in the slightest. My sole difficulty lies in articulating the explanation. May I rely on your patience if I revisit Mr. Candy's illness, and if I disclose certain professional details I have thus far withheld?"

"Please, continue. You have already captivated me with the mere mention of the details."

My evident eagerness seemed to amuse him—or perhaps, more accurately, to please him. He smiled again. We had by now passed the last row of townhouses, moving into the quiet outskirts. Jennings paused briefly, bending to pick a cluster of wildflowers from the roadside hedge. "How beautiful they are!" he exclaimed simply, presenting the small bouquet to me. "And how few people in England seem to appreciate them as they deserve!"

"You have not always resided in England?" I asked.

"No. I was born, and raised in part, in one of our colonies. My father was English; my mother... Well, we are drifting from our topic, Mr. Blake, and it is my fault. The truth is, I hold deep associations with these humble hedgerow blooms. It does not matter; we were discussing Mr. Candy."

Connecting the sparse fragments of his biography with the melancholic worldview that equated human happiness with the erasure of the past, I felt a growing conviction that the story written on his face was authentic. He had endured suffering rare among men, and there was undoubtedly a strain of foreign ancestry mingling within his English blood.

"You have heard, I presume, of the root cause of Mr. Candy's illness?" he resumed. "The night of Lady Verinder's dinner party was marked by torrential rain. My employer drove home through the storm in his gig, arriving drenched to the bone. He discovered an urgent summons from a patient and, disastrously, proceeded immediately to visit the sick individual without changing his attire. I was professionally detained that evening by a case located some distance from Frizinghall. Upon returning the following morning, I found Mr. Candy's groom waiting in a state of high alarm to escort me to his master's chamber. By then, the damage was irreversible; the illness had taken hold."

"The illness has been described to me merely as a fever," I noted.

"I can offer no clarification that renders the description more precise," Jennings answered. "From onset to conclusion, the fever assumed no specific character. I dispatched two of Mr. Candy's distinguished colleagues, both physicians, to render their opinions. They concurred with my assessment of the severity but vehemently disagreed with my proposed treatment. We diverged completely in our interpretation of the patient's pulse. Arguing from its rapidity, the two doctors insisted that a lowering regimen was the only viable option. On my side, I acknowledged the rapidity but highlighted its alarming feebleness as evidence of a depleted system, necessitating stimulants. The two physicians advocated for gruel, lemonade, and barley-water. I advocated for champagne, brandy, ammonia, and quinine. A grave divergence of opinion, as you perceive! A conflict between two established local authorities and a stranger serving merely as an assistant. For the initial days, I was compelled to yield to my seniors; the patient sank steadily.
