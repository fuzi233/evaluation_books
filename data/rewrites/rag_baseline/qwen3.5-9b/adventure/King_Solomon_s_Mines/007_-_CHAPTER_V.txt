The dust of the veldt settled over our camp like a shroud, heavy with the memory of death. Nine elephants had fallen to our bullets, their massive carcasses silent witnesses to our triumph. For two days, we had labored under the scorching sun to extract the ivory, a task that demanded both strength and precision. When the tusks were finally severed, we buried them beneath the gnarled roots of a great acacia tree, marking the spot with a cairn of stones visible for miles. It was a harvest of unparalleled quality; the ivory averaged between forty and fifty pounds per tusk, a sheer, pearlescent perfection. Only the tusks of the great bull that had claimed poor Khiva surpassed even this, weighing in at a staggering one hundred and seventy pounds the pair.

Khiva himself received a burial fitting for a warrior. We interred his remains within an anthill, a fortress of chitin and earth, alongside an assegai to guard him on his passage to whatever realm awaited him beyond the veil. By the third day, we broke camp, marching forward with a grim resolve. We hoped to survive this trek and return to reclaim our buried treasure, a dream that would eventually become a cruel irony. After a long, weary tramp punctuated by dangers too numerous to recount, we reached Sitanda's Kraal, nestled near the Lukanga River. This was the true genesis of our quest, the point from which the map of civilization frayed into the unknown.

I recall our arrival with vivid clarity. To our right lay a scattered settlement of the locals, dotted with stone cattle kraals and patches of cultivated grain growing along the riverbank. Beyond this fragile oasis stretched vast tracts of waving veld, carpeted in tall grass where herds of smaller game wandered aimlessly. To our left, however, the landscape shifted abruptly into the vast, unyielding desert. This boundary appeared to be the outpost of fertility, a stark line where the rich soil gave way to arid wasteland. The cause of such a sudden geological rupture remained a mystery to me, yet the transition was absolute.

Just below our encampment trickled a narrow stream. On the far bank rose a stony slope, the very same incline down which, twenty years prior, I had watched poor Silvestre crawl back in defeat after his futile attempt to breach Solomon's Mines. Beyond that slope began the waterless desert, a sea of barrenness dominated by a hardy species of Karoo shrub.

Evening fell as we pitched our tents, casting the camp in twilight. The great orb of the sun sank into the horizon, spilling glorious, multi-hued rays across the endless expanse of sand. I left Good to organize the camp's affairs and accompanied Sir Henry to the crest of the opposite slope. From there, we gazed across the dunes. The air was crystalline, allowing my eyes to pierce the distance until I could discern the faint, azure silhouette of the Suliman Mountains, their peaks capped with eternal snow.

"There," I pointed, my voice barely rising above the wind. "That is the wall surrounding Solomon's Mines. Whether we shall ever scale it, God alone knows."

"My brother should be there," Sir Henry replied, his tone steady and unshakeable. "And if he is, I shall find him."

"I hope so," I answered, turning back toward the flickering fires of the camp. As I moved, I noticed we were not alone. Standing behind us, staring intently at the distant peaks, was Umbopa, the great Zulu warrior who had attached himself to Sir Henry's service.

When Umbopa realized I had seen him, he stepped forward to address Sir Henry directly.

"To that land do you intend to journey, Incubu?" he asked, using the native term for elephant that the Kafirs had bestowed upon their master. He gestured toward the mountains with the butt of his broad assegai.

My irritation flared instantly. "How dare you address your master in such a familiar manner?" I snapped, translating my outrage. "It is acceptable for natives to share names amongst themselves, but it is undignified to call a white man by their heathen appellations in his presence."

Umbopa chuckled, a soft, dry sound that only deepened my annoyance. "How do you know I am not the equal of the Inkosi whom I serve?" he countered calmly. "His royalty is evident in his stature and bearing; perhaps mine is as well. At the very least, I am a man of consequence. Speak for me, O Macumazahn, and convey my words to the Inkoos Incubu. I wish to speak to him, and to you."

Anger surged within me; I was not accustomed to being lectured by a Kafir. Yet, something in his demeanor commanded respect, and curiosity pricked at my defenses. I translated his challenge, adding my own assessment that he was an impudent fellow whose arrogance bordered on the offensive.

"Yes, Umbopa," Sir Henry said, ignoring my translation of my anger. "I intend to journey there."

"The desert is vast and devoid of water," Umbopa continued, his voice carrying a strange cadence. "The mountains are high, crowned with snow, and no man can predict what lies beyond them where the sun sets. How will you reach them, Incubu, and why do you go?"

I relayed the question again.

"Tell him," Sir Henry instructed, "that I go because I believe a man of my blood, my brother, has ventured there before me. I journey to seek him."

"That is so, Incubu," Umbopa acknowledged. "A Hottentot I encountered on the road informed me that a white man entered the desert two years ago, heading toward those mountains with a single servant, a hunter. They never returned."

"How do you know it was my brother?" Sir Henry asked, his brow furrowing slightly.

"Nay, I know not the truth of it," Umbopa admitted. "But when I asked the Hottentot to describe the white man, he said he possessed your eyes and bore a black beard. Furthermore, he stated that the hunter accompanying him was named Jim, a Bechuana tracker clad in European clothing."

"There is no doubt about it," I declared, the realization settling cold in my stomach. "I knew Jim intimately."

Sir Henry nodded slowly. "I was certain," he murmured. "George, once he set his mind upon a goal, invariably achieved it. It was his nature from boyhood. If he intended to cross the Suliman Berg, he crossed it, barring some catastrophic accident. We must search for him on the other side."

Umbopa, who understood English despite his reticence, interjected softly. "It is a far journey, Incubu."

"It is far," Sir Henry agreed, his voice gaining strength. "Yet there exists no journey upon this earth that a man cannot undertake if his heart is resolved. Nothing stands in his way, no mountain too steep, no desert too dry, save perhaps those specific barriers you are spared the knowledge of. If love guides him, and he holds his life lightly, counting it as nothing, ready to surrender it or keep it according to Heaven's decree, then he shall prevail."

I translated these stirring words.

"Great words, my father," Umbopa responded, adopting the title I had unconsciously assigned him. "Grand, swelling phrases worthy of a king. You are correct, my father Incubu. Listen! What is life? It is a feather, the seed of grass, blown hither and thither. Sometimes it multiplies and dies in the act; sometimes it is carried aloft into the heavens. But if that seed is good and heavy, it may travel a little way along the path it chooses. It is well to try one's road and to fight the air. Man must die. At worst, he merely dies a little sooner. I will accompany you across the desert and over the mountains, unless fate claims me before we reach the end, my father."

He paused, gathering his thoughts, before launching into one of those peculiar bursts of rhetorical eloquence characteristic of the Zulu people. To my mind, replete though they were with repetition, these speeches revealed a poetic instinct and intellectual depth that belied their reputation.

"What is life? Tell me, O white men, who claim wisdom, who know the secrets of the world, the stars, and the realms above and around them; who project your words across distances without uttering a sound. Tell me, white men, the secret of our existence—where it goes and whence it comes!"

"You cannot answer," Sir Henry noted.

"You know not," Umbopa insisted. "Listen, I will answer. From the darkness we emerged, and into the darkness we return. Like a storm-driven bird at night, we fly from the Nowhere. For a fleeting moment, our wings catch the light of the fire, and lo! we vanish back into the Nowhere. Life is nothing. Life is everything. It is the Hand that wards off Death. It is the glow-worm shining in the night, black in the dawn; it is the white breath of oxen in winter; it is the tiny shadow racing across the grass to disappear at sunset."

"You are a strange man," Sir Henry observed when the warrior finished.

Umbopa laughed. "It seems to me we are much alike, Incubu. Perhaps *I* seek a brother over the mountains."

I regarded him with suspicion. "What do you mean?" I asked. "What do you know of those mountains?"
