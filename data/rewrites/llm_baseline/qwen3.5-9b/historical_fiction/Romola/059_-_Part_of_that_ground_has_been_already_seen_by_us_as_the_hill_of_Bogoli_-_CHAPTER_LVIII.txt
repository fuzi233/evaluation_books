Tito returned swiftly from Siena, only to embark immediately upon another journey, absent until the seventeenth of August. Nearly two weeks had elapsed since the arrest of the accused, yet they remained incarcerated, their destiny hanging in a precarious balance. During this interlude, Romola felt as though all worldly cares had ceased to exist save for the singular, consuming task of monitoring the shifting probabilities surrounding their fate. At times, the odds appeared heavily weighted in their favor; the passage of time itself acted as a shield, heightening the stakes for those seeking to intervene, while the authorities' reluctance to bear the stigma of a hasty execution fostered an indefinite horizon of delay.

On one side, a clamorous voice declared the Republic perilously near collapse, warning that clemency toward the prisoners would serve as a beacon inviting attack from all enemies. Conversely, there existed a grim certainty: a verdict of death and confiscation against five citizens of such distinguished standing would inevitably breed a rancorous hatred from their kin against anyone deemed complicit in such a sentence. Ultimately, the power of judgment rested with the Eight, the body overseeing criminal justice, where a majority of six votes—the *set fave*, or "six beans," the political pulse of Florence—was required. Yet, the Eight recoiled from this heavy burden, requesting in this extraordinary instance that the Signoria share the responsibility. The Signoria, in turn, shrugged off the weight, proposing an appeal to the Great Council. According to a law championed by Savonarola three years prior, any citizen condemned to death by the fatal six votes possessed the right to appeal to the Great Council. However, at this juncture, the friends of the accused resisted the formal appeal, driven primarily by a desperate need for further delay; strictly speaking, a sentence must be pronounced before an appeal can be lodged. Prevailing in their strategy, a compromise was struck: the case was referred to a grand assembly convening on the seventeenth, comprising all higher magistrates, the Senate of Eighty, and a select group of citizens.

On this fateful day, Romola, her anxiety sharpened by the fear that her godfather's doom might be sealed before the proceedings concluded, secured permission to visit him for the second time, albeit only in the presence of witnesses. She returned to the Via de' Bardi accompanied by her cousin, Monna Brigida, still unaware of whether the council had reached a definitive conclusion. Brigida departed once more to await news at the residence of a friend within one of the magistracies, promising to return with authentic tidings the moment they became available. Exhausted and shattered, Romola collapsed onto the first seat in the sunlit salon, too overwhelmed by grief to mind her surroundings or the dissonance of the room's decor. With her back to the door, she rested her head in her hands, waiting. Time stretched endlessly until the door swung open, revealing not Brigida, but someone else.

Since her departure from Tito on that unforgettable night, Romola had possessed no tangible proof that he had secured his safety through treachery. To the contrary, evidence suggested he remained trusted by the Medici, ostensibly carrying out missions in Romagna under the guise of official government commissions. The fog surrounding the conspirators allowed for the interpretation that Tito had escaped all implication. Yet, Romola's suspicion refused to dissipate. Her horror at his treatment of Baldassarre cast a shadow over every perception of his actions; it was as if she witnessed him commit murder, leaving her with a permanent, diseased conviction that his hands were stained with fresh blood.

As his footsteps echoed on the stone floor, a cold shiver seized her. Unable to turn, unable to rise to greet him, she remained frozen. He spoke not a word initially, pausing briefly before sitting opposite her across the table. When she finally lifted her eyes to meet his gaze, she found herself mute. He showed no irritation, merely stating calmly, "This meeting corresponds with our parting, Romola. Yet I understand it is a moment of terrible suspense. I have come, if you will listen, to bring you the relief of hope."

She startled, adjusting her posture but regarding him with deep doubt. "It will not be unwelcome to hear—even though it is I who tell you—that the council has been prorogued until the twenty-first," he continued smoothly. "The Eight have finally succumbed to fear and passed a sentence of condemnation, but a demand has now been made on behalf of the condemned for an Appeal to the Great Council."

Romola's doubtful expression vanished, replaced by eager inquiry. "And when is it to be made?"

"It has not yet been granted, but it *may* be," Tito replied. "The Special Council reconvenes on the twenty-first to decide whether the appeal shall be allowed. In the interim, there is a three-day window wherein favorable chances may arise, wherein interest may be mobilized on their behalf."

Romola sprang from her seat. Color flooded her cheeks, and her hands began to tremble. In that instant, her feelings toward Tito receded completely. "Possibly," Tito observed, rising as well, "your own intuition anticipated what I was about to say. You are thinking of the Frate."

"I am," Romola admitted, surprised. "Has he done something? Is there anything to tell me?"

"Only this," Tito explained. "Messer Francesco Valori's bitterness and violence largely dictated the council's course today. Half the members who voted against the prisoners were frightened into compliance. Furthermore, there are numerous friends of Fra Girolamo within and outside this Special Council who strongly oppose the death sentence—such as Piero Guicciardini, a member of the Signoria who offered stout resistance, and Giovan Battista Ridolfi, a *Piagnone* who will not easily forgive the death of his brother Niccolò."

"But how can the appeal be denied?" Romola exclaimed indignantly. "When it is the law! Was it not one of the chief glories of our popular government to enact this very law?"

"They label this an exceptional case," Tito countered. "Of course, clever arguments abound, but there is much louder posturing about the danger to the Republic. Still, no opposition could prevent the assembly from adjourning. A powerful influence, rightly applied over these next three days, might sway the wavering courage of those wishing to grant the appeal, or even check the headlong fury of Francesco Valori. I have learned that the Frate has already intervened, sending a message to Valori in favor of Lorenzo Tornabuoni. Given that you occasionally have access to the Frate, it would surely be worthwhile to exercise your privilege now."

"It is true," Romola murmured, her gaze distant. "I cannot believe the Frate would approve of denying the appeal."

"I heard it whispered by several people in the Palazzo courts before I left," Tito said. "To allow a government composed almost entirely of his own party to deny the appeal without protest, especially after boasting in his books and sermons that he secured the law, would be to the everlasting discredit of Fra Girolamo. Moreover, between ourselves—and with all due respect for your Frate's abilities—he has fallen into the habit of preaching a form of human sacrifice known as 'killing tyrants.' Some of his followers might find this inconsistent with the leniency required in this specific case."

*[Footnote: The most recent and esteemed biographer of Savonarola, Signor Villari, argues that the Law of Appeal, ultimately enacted, was wider than Savonarola originally intended, serving as a source of bitter annoyance to him as a tool of the aristocracy to attach the injurious results of license to popular measures. However, this view overlooks the fact that Savonarola himself, in his deliberately prepared work, the Compendium Revelationum, written long after the law became statute, enumerated among the benefits secured to Florence 'the Appeal from the Six Votes, advocated by me, for the greater security of the citizens.']*

"I know, I know," Romola said, her voice and countenance betraying profound pain. "But he is driven into those excesses of speech. Things used to be different. I *will* request an audience. I cannot rest without seeing him. I trust in the greatness of his heart."

Her eyes were fixed on the floor, vacant and unfocused, lacking clear awareness that the words issuing from her mouth were being spoken by her husband.

"Do not waste any time, then," Tito urged, his tone smooth and untroubled, twirling his cap as if preparing to depart. "Now, Romola, you will perhaps be able to see, despite your prejudices, that my wishes align with yours in this matter. You will not regard the misfortune of my safety as an offense."

A sensation akin to an electric shock coursed through Romola; it was the sudden, full realization of her husband's presence returning to her. She stared at him, silent.

"At least," he added, his tone hardening slightly, "you will endeavor to base our interactions on reasoning other than the notion that because an evil deed is possible, *I* have committed it. Am I the sole individual excluded from the reach of your extensive charity?"

The emotion that had been suppressed from Romola's lips two weeks prior surged forth again with the gathering force of a tidal wave. She spoke with a decisiveness that signaled her disregard for consequences. "It is too late, Tito. No amount of denial can kill the suspicion that deceit has once been born.
