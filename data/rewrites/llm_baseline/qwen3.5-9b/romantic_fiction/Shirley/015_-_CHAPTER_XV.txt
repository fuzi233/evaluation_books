The following day, Shirley confided to Caroline just how delighted she was that the recent gathering had concluded so successfully.

"I rather enjoy entertaining a circle of gentlemen," she remarked. "There is something amusing in observing how they relish a meal that has been wisely prepared. As for us, you see, these fine wines and scientifically arranged dishes hold little significance; yet gentlemen seem to retain a certain *naïveté* regarding food, akin to that of children. One simply wishes to please them—provided, of course, they display the proper, decent self-restraint characteristic of our excellent rectors. I often watch Moore to discern what might please him, but he lacks that childlike simplicity. Have you ever discovered his weak point, Caroline? You have known him longer than I."

"It is certainly not the same as my uncle or Dr. Boultby," Caroline replied, smiling. She always felt a shy pleasure in following Miss Keeldar's lead when discussing her cousin's character. Alone, she would never broach the subject, but when invited, the temptation to speak of the man she constantly thought of became irresistible. "However," she added, "I truly cannot say what it is, for whenever I have ever watched Robert, my scrutiny was immediately thwarted by discovering that he was watching me."

"There it is!" Shirley exclaimed. "You cannot fix your gaze upon him without his flashing back instantly. He is never off his guard; he refuses to give you the upper hand. Even when he isn't looking directly at you, his thoughts appear to be busy among your own, tracing your words and actions back to their source, contemplating your motives with perfect ease. Oh! I know that type of character, or something very similar. It peculiarly piques me. How does it affect you?"

This question exemplified one of Shirley's sharp, sudden shifts in topic. Caroline had initially been flustered by them, but she had since learned to deflect these home-thrusts with the skill of a little Quakeress.

"Pique you? In what way does it pique you?" she asked.

"Here he comes!" Shirley suddenly cried, breaking off mid-sentence. She jumped up and darted to the window. "Here comes a diversion. I never mentioned a superb conquest I've recently secured—one made at those parties I can never persuade you to attend—and, I swear, it required no effort or intention on my part. There is the bell! And, by all that's delicious! There are two of them. Do they never hunt except in pairs? You may have one, Lina, and you may choose whichever you prefer. I hope I am generous enough. Listen to Tartar!"

The black-muzzled, tawny dog, previously glimpsed in the chapter introducing his mistress, now gave voice in the hall. Amidst the hollow space of the entrance, his deep bark resonated with formidable power. It was followed by a growl more terrifying than the bark itself, menacing like muttered thunder.

"Listen!" Shirley cried again, laughing. "You'd think this was the prelude to a bloody assault. They'll be terrified. They don't know old Tartar as I do. They aren't aware that his uproars are merely sound and fury, signifying nothing!"

Bustle erupted nearby. "Down, sir, down!" came a high-toned, imperious voice, accompanied by the snap of a cane or whip. Instantly, there was a yell, a scramble, and a genuine tumult.

"Oh, Malone, Malone!"

"Down! Down! Down!" the high voice commanded.

"He is actually worrying them!" Shirley exclaimed. "They've struck him. A blow is something he isn't accustomed to taking."

She ran out. A gentleman was fleeing up the oak staircase, seeking refuge in the gallery or upper chambers with frantic haste; another was backing rapidly toward the bottom of the stairs, wildly waving a knotty stick while repeatedly shouting, "Down! Down! Down!" Meanwhile, the tawny dog bayed, bellowed, and howled at him, while a group of servants bundled out from the kitchen. The dog leaped forward; the second gentleman turned tail and raced after his companion. The first was already safe inside a bedroom, barring the door against his friend—nothing so merciless as terror. Yet the other fugitive struggled valiantly; the door was on the verge of yielding to his strength.

"Gentlemen," Shirley said, her silvery but vibrating tones cutting through the chaos, "spare my locks, if you please. Calm yourselves! Come down! Look at Tartar; he wouldn't harm a cat."

She was caressing the beast. He lay crouched at her feet, forepaws stretched out, tail still twitching in threat, nostrils snorting, his bulldog eyes burning with a dull fire. He was an honest, phlegmatic, somewhat stupid, yet stubborn canine. He loved his mistress and John—the man who fed him—but was largely indifferent to the rest of the world. Quiet enough unless struck or threatened with a stick, in which case a demon seized him instantly.

"Mr. Malone, how do you do?" Shirley continued, lifting her mirth-lit face toward the gallery. "That is not the way to the oak parlour; that is Mrs. Pryor's apartment. Please request your friend Mr. Donne to evacuate. I shall be delighted to receive him in a lower room."

"Ha! Ha!" cried Malone, in hollow laughter, stepping away from the door and leaning over the massive balustrade. "Truly, that animal alarmed Donne. He is a little timid," he proceeded, straightening his posture and walking trimly to the top of the stairs. "I thought it better to follow, in order to reassure him."

"It seems you did. Well, come down, if you please.—John" (turning to her servant), "go upstairs and liberate Mr. Donne.—Be careful, Mr. Malone; the stairs are slippery."

Indeed, they were, made of polished oak. The warning arrived a moment too late for Malone. He had already slipped during his stately descent, saving himself only by clutching the banisters, causing the entire structure to groan anew.

Tartar seemed to view the visitor's dramatic arrival with unwarranted *éclat*, and thus he growled once more. Malone, however, was no coward. The dog's spring had surprised him, but he now passed him in suppressed fury rather than fear. If a glance could have strangled Tartar, Malone would have breathed no more. Abandoning all pretense of politeness in his sullen rage, he pushed into the parlour before Miss Keeldar. He glanced at Miss Helstone, barely bringing himself to bow. He glared at both ladies. He looked as though, had either been his wife, he would have made a glorious husband at that very moment. In each hand, he seemed poised to clutch one and squeeze her to death.

Nevertheless, Shirley took pity. Her laughter ceased; Caroline, a true lady, refrained from smiling even at someone in such mortification. Tartar was dismissed, and Peter Augustus was soothed—for Shirley possessed looks and tones capable of calming even a bull. He had the sense to realize that, unable to challenge the dog's owner, he ought to be civil. And civil he strove to be; his efforts being well-received, he soon became *very* civil and returned to his usual self. He had indeed come expressly to charm and fascinate. Though rough portents awaited him upon entering Fieldhead, he resolved to overcome them. Like March, having entered like a lion, he intended to depart like a lamb.

For the sake of air, or perhaps to ensure a quick exit should a new emergency arise, he took his seat—not on the sofa where Miss Keeldar offered him a throne, nor near the fireside to which Caroline gently invited him with a friendly gesture, but on a chair close to the door. No longer sullen or furious, he grew, in his own fashion, constrained and embarrassed. He spoke to the ladies in fits and starts, selecting topics that were intensely commonplace. He sighed deeply and significantly at the end of every sentence; he sighed in every pause; he sighed before opening his mouth. Finally, finding it necessary to add ease to his other charms, he produced an ample silk pocket-handkerchief. This was to serve as the graceful toy with which his idle hands would dally. He worked with considerable energy. He folded the red-and-yellow square cornerwise; he snapped it open with a wave; he refolded it into a narrower shape; he fashioned it into a handsome band. What purpose would he apply this ligature? Would he wrap it around his throat—or his head? Should it be a comforter or a turban? Neither. Peter Augustus possessed an inventive, original genius. He was about to demonstrate graces of action possessing at least the charm of novelty. Sitting on the chair with his athletic Irish legs crossed, he circled them with the bandana and bound them firmly together. Evidently feeling this device worthy of an encore, he repeated it more than once. The second performance sent Shirley to the window to laugh her silent, irrepressible laugh unseen; it turned Caroline's head aside so her long curls might screen the smile blooming on her features. Miss Helstone, indeed, was amused by several points in Peter's demeanor. She was edified by the complete, albeit abrupt, diversion of his homage from herself to the heiress.
