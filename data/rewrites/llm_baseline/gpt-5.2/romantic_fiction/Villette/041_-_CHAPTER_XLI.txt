Was I to set down, before I shut the book, an account of those two loud companions I had brought home from the fête—Freedom and Renovation—those sturdy fellows who had strode at my side under lanterns and fireworks, and sworn they would deliver me from the old servitude?

I put them to proof the next day, as one tests flour by touch and scent before risking the whole loaf. Freedom—who had strutted most—proved, on a nearer view, a beggar in fine clothes: he had excuses ready, and empty hands. Renovation never answered at all. In the night he had died—quietly, without even the decency of a struggle—leaving me to wake to the same narrow room, the same close air, the same rack of suspense tightened anew.

Then came back that corrosive vigil: Shall I yet see him before he goes? Will he remember? Will he come? Will this hour, this half-hour, bring him—or must I again endure the wrench of a farewell unspoken, the uprooting that tears out both hope and doubt, leaving only the raw cavity where the heart has been?

It was the Feast of the Assumption. No class was held. After mass, the boarders and teachers streamed away on holiday, to take their goûter at some farm-house; one could almost taste beforehand the country bread and milk, the brown eggs, the fruit warm from hedges, and hear, in fancy, the pleasant rural hush broken only by cart-wheels and birds. I did not go. Two days only remained before the Paul et Virginie sailed, and I clung to my last chance as a castaway to his last plank.

Some joiners’ work had been set down for the first classe—a bench or desk to mend—such things done best when the rooms are empty. I sat solitary, intending to escape to the garden when the men came, but too listless even to obey my own plan. At length I heard footsteps.

Labassecourien work is seldom solitary; they do in pairs what one could do alone, as if each nail demanded witnesses. So, while I was tying my bonnet—its ribbons hanging idle from my fingers—I noted, with the dreary leisure of one imprisoned, that only one man approached. I noted, too, he wore shoes, not sabots. It must be the master-carpenter, I thought, come to inspect before sending his journeymen. I drew my scarf about me. He opened the door. My back was toward it. A quick, light thrill—too swift for thought—ran through me. I turned.

The doorway was filled; my eyes stamped the image upon my brain: M. Paul.

How many prayers fall back from Heaven as dry leaves from a closed gate! Yet once—perhaps once in a life—some golden thing is dropped full into the lap, complete, flawless from Fruition’s mint.

He wore travel-dress—a surtout guarded with velvet—such as a man chooses when he means to set out. He looked well, cheerful, and, above all, kind; he came in with eagerness, and in one second was close, all amity, all sunshine. It might be the brightness of a bridegroom; it might be merely his own brave habit of carrying light where he can. Whatever the cause, I could not meet his warmth with coldness. If this were indeed the last moment, I would not waste it in proud distance. I loved him too well to let Jealousy—thin witch—stand between us and a humane farewell. A cordial word, a gentle look, would be provision for a long famine; I would take it and taste it, and not let pride spill the cup.

It would be short—so I told myself. A hand held for two minutes; a word as he had said to the assembled pupils; perhaps—only perhaps—the lightest touch of his lips on my cheek, once, and then the gulf: the wide separation I could not cross to go to him, and across which he might never glance back.

He took my hand. With the other he pushed back my bonnet. He looked into my face—and his luminous smile went out, as if someone had laid a wet cloth upon a flame. His lips moved into a mute language—almost maternal—such as one uses over a child suddenly found altered by illness or want.

“Paul, Paul!” cried a woman’s hurried voice behind. “Paul, come into the salon; I have yet a great many things to say to you—conversation for the whole day—and so has Victor; and Josef is here. Come, Paul, come to your friends.”

Madame Beck—drawn by vigilance or by that instinct which never slept in her—pressed near, so near she almost thrust her person between him and me.

“Come, Paul!” she reiterated; her eye grazed me with its hard ray, keen as a steel point. She pushed against her kinsman. I thought he would recede; I thought he would go.

Something in me—held down, strained long—snapped. What broke loose was not decorum, but pain.

“My heart will break!”

It seemed literal heart-break; yet, under the strain, another fountain yielded. One breath from him—only the whisper, “Trust me!”—lifted a weight and opened an outlet. I wept: deep sobs, icy shivers, strong trembling—yet relief in the very storm.

“Leave her to me; it is a crisis: I will give her a cordial, and it will pass,” said Madame Beck, with her calm.

To be left to her and her cordial was to be left to the poisoner and her bowl. When M. Paul answered—deep, harsh, brief—

“Laissez-moi!”

In that grim sound there was a music—strange, strong, life-giving.

“Laissez-moi!” he repeated; his nostrils widened; the muscles of his face quivered.

“But this will never do,” said Madame, stern.

More sternly he returned—“Sortez d’ici!”

“I will send for Père Silas: on the spot I will send for him,” she threatened, pertinacious.

“Femme!” cried the Professor—no longer in his deep tones, but high, sharp with excitement. “Femme! sortez à l’instant!”

He was roused. I loved him then in his wrath with a passion beyond what I had yet known—because the wrath was for me, and because beneath it was something steadfast that would not be handled.

“What you do is wrong,” she persisted; “an act characteristic of men of your unreliable, imaginative temperament; a step impulsive, injudicious—”

“You know not what I have of steady and resolute in me,” said he; “but you shall see. Modeste,” he added, less fiercely, “be gentle, be pitying, be a woman; look at this poor face, and relent. You know I am your friend, and the friend of your friends. Of sacrificing myself I made no difficulty; but my heart is pained by what I see; it must have and give solace. Leave me.”

There was such bitterness, such command, in that leave me, that I marvelled she delayed. Still she stood—stone against fire—meeting his eye with her own fixed forbiddance. She opened her lips to retort. I saw a light leap up over his face; he moved—how, I scarcely know, for it seemed still within courtesy; he gave his hand; it scarcely touched her—and she ran, whirled from the room, was gone, and the door shut, in a second.

The flash passed. He smiled, told me to wipe my eyes, waited quietly till the storm in me sank, dropping now and then a word like cool water on heated stone. Soon I sat beside him again—not desperate now, not desolate; not friendless, not sick of life and craving death.

“It made you very sad then to lose your friend?” he said.

“It kills me to be forgotten, Monsieur,” I answered. “All these weary days I have not heard from you one word; and I was crushed with the possibility—growing to certainty—that you would depart without saying farewell.”

“Must I tell you again, as I told Modeste Beck, that you do not know me? Must I show you my character? You will have proof that I can be a firm friend? Without proof, this hand will not lie still in mine, it will not trust my shoulder? Good. The proof is ready. I come to justify myself.”

“Say anything, teach anything, prove anything, Monsieur. I can listen now.”

“Then, first, you must go with me a good distance into the town. I came on purpose to fetch you.”

I did not question, did not bargain with fear, did not ask for explanations. I retied my bonnet. I was ready.

He took me by the boulevards. Under the lime-trees the air was greener and cooler; their leaves made a roof that held the day’s heat in check, and the trunks—pale, columnar—stood like sentinels in calm. On benches set along the walk, he made me sit more than once. He did not ask if I was tired; he looked, and drew his conclusion with that practical tenderness of his.

“All these weary days,” he repeated, with a gentle mimicry of my voice and foreign accent—playful, never cruel. “‘All these weary days’ I have not for one hour forgotten you. Faithful women err in this: they think themselves the sole faithful. On a very fervent truth to myself, I, too, till lately scarce dared count on faith from any quarter; but—look at me.”

I lifted my eyes. They were happy now; they could not help betraying the heart.

“Well,” he said, after scrutinizing me a moment, “there is no denying that signature: Constancy wrote it. Her pen is of iron. Was the record painful?”

“Severely painful,” I said. “Withdraw her hand, Monsieur; I can bear its inscribing force no more.”

“Elle est toute pâle,” he murmured to himself; “cette figure-là me fait mal.”

“Ah! I am not pleasant to look at—?”

The question slipped out as if it had been waiting long behind my teeth. I had always carried, like a hidden bruise, the dread of outward deficiency; at that moment it throbbed and made itself heard.

A great softness came over his face. His violet eyes glistened under their dark lashes. He started up. “Let us walk on.”

“Do I displease your eyes much?” I urged, driven by a necessity that was not vanity alone, but hunger for some assurance.

He stopped and gave me a short, strong answer—one that subdued and satisfied. From that hour I knew what I was for him; and what I might be for the rest of the world, I ceased painfully to care.

Where we wandered I could scarcely tell. The walk was long and yet seemed brief. M. Emanuel spoke of his voyage—he thought to stay away three years. On his return from Guadaloupe he looked for release from liabilities and a clear course. And what would I do in the interval? Had I dropped the idea of keeping a little school of my own?

“Indeed I had not,” I said. “I was doing my best to save what would enable me to begin.”

He did not like leaving me in the Rue Fossette; he feared I should miss him there too much, feel desolate, grow sad. That was true; yet I promised to endure.

“Still,” he added, speaking low, “there is another objection. I should wish to write to you sometimes. It would not be well to have uncertainty about letters. And in the Rue Fossette—in short, our Catholic discipline in certain matters—though justifiable and expedient—might, under peculiar circumstances, become liable to misapplication—perhaps abuse.”

“But if you write,” I said, “I must have your letters; and I will have them. Ten directors, twenty directresses, shall not keep them from me. I am a Protestant: I will not bear that kind of discipline. Monsieur, I will not.”

“Doucement—doucement,” he replied. “We will contrive a plan. We have resources. Soyez tranquille.”

He paused.

We were returning now, and had reached the middle of a clean faubourg. The houses were small, but had a look of decency and domestic hope—white steps scrubbed, windows bright, little vines trained where there was room to grow. Before a neat white door-step M. Paul halted.

“I call here,” he said.

He did not knock. He took a key from his pocket, opened, and entered at once. He ushered me in and shut the door. No servant appeared. The vestibule was small but freshly painted; at the end a French window stood, with vines trained about its panes—tendrils and green leaves kissing the glass. The house held a silence like a held breath.

He opened an inner door and showed me a parlour—a tiny salon—pretty as a shell. The walls were tinted like a blush; the floor waxed to a mild shine; a bright carpet lay square in the centre. A little round table gleamed like the mirror above the hearth. There was a small couch, a chiffonnière half-open to show porcelain, a French clock, a lamp, some biscuit ornaments. In the deep recess of the single ample window stood three green pots of plants, each in bloom, as if the room kept a private summer. In one corner a small marble-topped guéridon held a work-box, and beside it a glass of violets in water. The lattice was open; the air breathed in fresh; the violets lent their sweet, cool scent—clean and living, like a promise.

“Pretty, pretty place!” I said; and he smiled to see me pleased.

“Must we sit down here and wait?” I whispered, half-awed by the hush.

“We will first peep into one or two other nooks of this nutshell,” he answered.

“Dare you take the freedom of going all over the house?” I asked.

“Yes, I dare,” he said quietly.

He showed me a little kitchen: a small stove and oven; bright brasses—few, but scoured till they held the light; two chairs and a table; a cupboard with a diminutive but well-chosen set of earthenware. The place smelled not of grease, but of clean metal and dry wood—as if it waited to be awakened by honest cooking.

“There is a coffee service of china in the salon,” he observed, as I looked at the green-and-white plates and the cups and jugs to match.

Up a narrow, clean staircase he let me glimpse two little sleeping-rooms—pretty cabinets, modest, orderly. Then he led me down again, and we halted, with a certain ceremony, before a larger door than any yet opened.

He produced a second key, fitted it, and opened.

“Voici!” he cried.

I stood in a good-sized room—scrupulously clean, though barer than the rest. The boards were well scoured and left without carpet; two rows of green benches and desks ran with an alley between, ending in an estrade with a teacher’s chair and table; behind them a tableau. Two maps hung on the walls; in the windows a few hardy plants stood like patient pupils. In short, a miniature classe—complete, neat, pleasant.

“It is a school then?” I said. “Who keeps it? I never heard of an establishment in this faubourg.”

“Will you have the goodness to accept a few prospectuses for distribution in behalf of a friend of mine?” he asked, drawing from his pocket some quires and placing them in my hand.

I looked. I read. In fair print:

“Externat de demoiselles. Numéro 7, Faubourg Clotilde. Directrice, Mademoiselle Lucy Snowe.”

And what did I say to M. Paul Emanuel?

There are junctures one cannot recall—crises that whirl in the mind like a wheel spun too fast, leaving only a blur of light. I cannot remember the thoughts or words of the ten minutes that followed; but the first thing distinct is this: I was speaking very fast, repeating—

“Did you do this, M. Paul? Is this your house? Did you furnish it? Did you get these papers printed? Do you mean me? Am I the directress? Is there another Lucy Snowe? Tell me—say something!”

He would not speak. His pleased silence, his laughing downward look, his attitude—those are clear to me still.

“How is it? I must know all—all!” I cried.

The packet of papers fell on the floor. He had extended his hand; I had fastened on it as if it were a hold above deep water.

“Ah! you said I had forgotten you all these weary days,” he said. “Poor old Emanuel! These are the thanks he gets for trudging about three mortal weeks—from house-painter to upholsterer, from cabinet-maker to charwoman. Lucy and Lucy’s cot—the sole thoughts in his head!”

I hardly knew what to do. I caressed the soft velvet of his cuff, then stroked the hand it surrounded. It was his foresight—his goodness—his silent, strong, effective goodness—that overcame me. The assurance of his sleepless interest broke on me like sudden day; and his look—tender, fond, daringly tender—shook me in a way I cannot translate into any language but trembling.

Yet in the midst of that sweetness, the practical in me rose—like a housewife measuring flour even while her heart sings.

“The trouble!” I exclaimed. “And the cost! Had you money, M. Paul?”

“Plenty of money!” he said heartily. “The disposal of my large teaching connection put me in possession of a handsome sum; and with part of it I determined to give myself the richest treat that I have known or shall know. I like this. I have reckoned on this hour day and night lately. I would not come near you, because I would not forestall it. Reserve is neither my virtue nor my vice. If I had put myself into your power, and you had begun with your questions of look and lip—‘Where have you been, M. Paul? What have you been doing? What is your mystery?’—my solitary first and last secret would presently have unravelled itself in your lap. Now,” he pursued, “you shall live here and have a school; you shall employ yourself while I am away; you shall think of me sometimes; you shall mind your health and happiness for my sake, and when I come back—”

There he left the future blank, as if it were too sacred to fill with common ink.

I promised. I promised to work hard and willingly. “I will be your faithful steward,” I said; “I trust at your coming the account will be ready. Monsieur, monsieur, you are too good!”

The words were poor for what I felt—speech is brittle and cold; it shivers when one would mould it. He watched me still, and gently lifted his hand to stroke my hair; it touched my lips in passing. I pressed it close, paid it tribute. He was my king; and that hand—so bountiful, so human—made homage both joy and duty.

The afternoon slipped away; evening shaded the quiet faubourg. M. Paul claimed my hospitality: he had been afoot and occupied since morning and needed refreshment. He said I should offer him chocolate in my pretty gold-and-white service. He went out and ordered what was needful from the restaurant; he placed the guéridon and two chairs on the balcony outside the French window, under the screening vines. With what shy joy I accepted my part as hostess, arranged the salver, served the benefactor-guest—feeling, in that small domestic ritual, the beginning of an identity I had never dared name.

Behind the house were gardens, and beyond them fields. The air was still, mild, and fresh. Sound, too, was blessedly clean there: no harsh roll of heavy carts, no clatter of commerce, no iron shouting—only a light breeze making a soft, leaf-borne speech; and, in a garden near, the steady rise and fall of a jet from a well—water talking to itself in silver syllables. That gentle acoustic world soothed like a hand laid on a fevered brow; it made the heart’s listening less frantic. Over poplars, laurels, cypresses, and roses, the moon looked up—so halcyon that the spirit trembled under her quiet. A star shone subject beside her with the unemulous ray of pure love. Near us a pale statue leaned over the play of waters.

M. Paul talked—his voice modulated so it did not bruise the evening. It mingled with the whisper of vines, the hush-sigh of foliage, the continual thin music of the fountain—nature’s unbought art, unadvertised, unexhibited, healing because it asked no audience.

Our meal was simple: chocolate, rolls, and a plate of fresh summer fruit—cherries and strawberries bedded in green leaves. Yet it was what we both liked better than a feast. The chocolate had that deep, dark fragrance—warm, faintly bitter, sweetened into comfort; the rolls broke with a tender crack; the berries, cool and perfumed, stained the tongue with June. I found an inexpressible delight in tending him—placing, pouring, watching the steam lift, hearing the small sounds of cup and spoon that, in such quiet, become intimate as whispers.

I asked whether his friends—Père Silas and Madame Beck—knew what he had done, whether they had seen my house.

“Mon amie,” he said, “none knows what I have done save you and myself. The pleasure is consecrated to us two—unshared and unprofaned. To speak truth, there has been to me in this matter a refinement of enjoyment I would not make vulgar by communication. Besides,” he added, smiling, “I wanted to prove to Miss Lucy that I could keep a secret. How often has she taunted me with lack of dignified reserve and needful caution! How many times has she saucily insinuated that all my affairs are the secret of Polichinelle!”

It was true; I had not spared him. Magnificent-minded, grand-hearted, dear, faulty little man—he deserved candour, and from me had it.

Still I questioned—practical again: to whom did the house belong? Who was my landlord? What was the rent? He gave me these particulars at once in writing; he had foreseen all.

The house was not his—of that I had been sure. He could earn, but he was not made to hoard; he needed, by nature, a treasurer. The tenement belonged, he said, to a citizen in the Basse-Ville—a man of substance; and then he startled me by adding, “a friend of yours, Miss Lucy, a person who has a most respectful regard for you.”

To my pleasant surprise I found the landlord was none other than M. Miret—the short-tempered, kind-hearted bookseller who had found me a seat that night in the park. He was rich in his station, much respected, and owned several houses in this faubourg. The rent was moderate—scarce half what it would have been nearer the centre of Villette.

“And then,” observed M. Paul, “should fortune not favour you—though I think she will—I have the satisfaction to think you are in good hands. M. Miret will not be extortionate. The first year’s rent you have already in your savings; afterwards Miss Lucy must trust God, and herself. But now—what will you do for pupils?”

“I must distribute my prospectuses,” I answered.
