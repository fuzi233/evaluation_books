Within the drawing-room at The Pool, Durrance bid farewell to Ethne, orchestrating the parting with such precision that there was scant time for lingering adieus. Already, the carriage waited at the steps of Guessens, its roof burdened with luggage, a silent sentinel of departure, while his servant stood poised at the threshold. Ethne emerged onto the terrace to join him, finding Mrs. Adair positioned at the summit of the stone flight. Durrance extended his hand toward her, but she deflected the gesture, turning her gaze solely to Ethne. "I require a moment with Colonel Durrance before he departs," she declared. "Very well," Ethne replied, her tone steady despite the gravity of the situation. "Then let us exchange our farewells here," she added to Durrance, signaling the finality of their separation. "You will write from Wiesbaden? Promptly, please!" "The instant I arrive," Durrance assured her. Descending the steps alongside Mrs. Adair, he left Ethne standing solitary upon the terrace. The theatrical facade had finally collapsed; the months of tense surveillance and calculated maneuvering had dissolved into dust, leaving both parties grateful for the sudden, unburdened release. Durrance moved with a brisk energy, crossing the lawn beside Mrs. Adair, a physical manifestation of his relief. Yet she trailed behind him, her step heavy, her voice laced with a profound despondency. "So you depart," she murmured. "In two days, you shall be in Wiesbaden, and Ethne at Glenalla. We shall be scattered like leaves in a gale. This place will become a hollow shell." She had achieved her objective; she had severed the bond between Ethne and Durrance, sparing herself the torture of witnessing their intimacy, of hearing the cadence of their voices intertwined. Yet, paradoxically, her victory tasted like ash. "The house will feel terribly vacant once you are gone," she observed, turning to follow him into the garden. "We shall undoubtedly return," Durrance reassured her, attempting to soothe the air with his presence. Mrs. Adair scanned the grounds. The vibrant blooms were gone, extinguished by the season; the sunlight had fled, replaced by a canopy of gathering clouds. The grass beneath their feet was a dull, muted green, and the stream wound through the tree line in shades of slate-grey. Russet and yellow leaves drifted across the lawns, dancing in a somber rhythm. "For how long will you remain in Wiesbaden?" she inquired. "I cannot say precisely. As long as it remains prudent," he answered evasively. "That tells me nothing," she snapped, her composure fracturing slightly. "I suppose it was intended to withhold information." Durrance offered no reply, and her silence bred resentment. She knew nothing of his itinerary; she was ignorant of whether he planned to dissolve his engagement with Ethne or bind it tighter. Curiosity gnawed at her, a persistent hunger for answers that threatened to consume her sanity. "Do you distrust me?" she demanded, her voice rising with a defensive edge. Durrance responded with gentle firmness. "Is there no cause for distrust? Why did you inform me of Captain Willoughby's arrival? Why did you intervene?" "I deemed it your right to know." "Yet Ethne desired secrecy. I am relieved to learn this, truly glad. But ultimately, you revealed it, and you were Ethne's confidante." "Yours, too, I trust," Mrs. Adair countered, her eyes flashing. "How could I continue to guard a secret? Do you not comprehend?" "No." Durrance might have understood, but he had rarely afforded her the depth of consideration she craved, and she sensed this acutely. The realization rankled within her, and his curt denial stung her pride beyond endurance. "I spoke brutally, did I not?" she accused. "I delivered the truth as harshly as I could. Does that not illuminate your confusion?" Again, Durrance uttered a single "No," and the monosyllable shattered her remaining restraint. Suddenly, she found herself spilling the contents of her tormented mind, unable to halt the torrent once started. She stood detached from herself, observing her own unraveling logic, yet she pressed on. "I spoke brutally on purpose," she confessed, her voice trembling. "I was seared by your refusal to perceive what lay naked before you, had you merely the capacity to see. I sought to wound you. I am a wicked woman, I suppose. There you and she were in the room, conversing in the shadows; there I stood alone upon the terrace. It is the same pattern today. You and Ethne inside, I isolated outside. I wonder if this dichotomy will persist forever. But you will not say—you will not admit it." She clasped her hands together in a gesture of sheer despair, but Durrance remained silent, walking steadily along the garden path toward the stile. He quickened his stride, a non-verbal signal of dismissal, yet Mrs. Adair was no longer deterred. Madness had seized her grip. "I would scarcely have cared so greatly," she ranted, "had Ethne truly loved you; but she never loved more than a friend loves—a mere, trivial friend. And what value holds friendship?" "Some value, surely," Durrance stated calmly. "It prevents Ethne from recoiling from her friend!" Mrs. Adair cried out. "She withdraws from you. Let me explain why. Because you are blind. She fears. Whereas I—I will speak plainly—I rejoice. When the news first arrived from Wadi Halfa declaring you blind, I rejoiced; when I beheld you in Hill Street, I rejoiced; ever since, I have rejoiced—utterly rejoiced. For I witnessed her retreat. From the outset, she retreated, calculating how her life would be shackled and fettered," and the scorn in her voice sharpened, though her volume dropped to a poisonous whisper. "I am not afraid," she insisted, repeating the phrase with passionate fervor. "I am not afraid. I am not afraid." To Durrance, this eruption from the woman who professed to be Ethne's friend appeared the most horrific anomaly of his experience, entirely unforeseen. "Ethne wrote to you at Wadi Halfa out of pity," she continued relentlessly. "Nothing else. She wrote from pity; and having written, she feared the consequences of her action; and fearing, she lacked the courage to confess her fear. You would not have condemned her had she honestly admitted it; you would have remained her friend. But she lacked the fortitude." Durrance knew there was another explanation for Ethne's hesitations, one that was undeniably true. Yet tomorrow he would depart the Salcombe estuary, and Ethne would journey toward the Irish Channel and Donegal. Arguing against Mrs. Adair's slanderous fabrications served no purpose. Moreover, he neared the stile separating the garden of The Pool from the open fields. Once across, he would be free of her influence. Content with a quiet rebuke, he said softly, "You are unjust to Ethne." At these simple words, the feverish delirium of Mrs. Adair evaporated. She recognized the futility of her boasts, her claims of courage, her denigration of Ethne. Whether her words were true or false, they achieved nothing. Durrance remained perpetually in the room with Ethne, never upon the terrace with Mrs. Adair. She became acutely aware of her degradation and began to offer apologies. "I am a bad woman, I suppose. But after all, my life has not been particularly fortunate. Perhaps there is merit in my circumstances." It sounded pathetic and weak, even to her own ears; yet they had reached the stile, and Durrance turned to face her. She noticed the sternness fading from his expression. He stood quietly, prepared to listen. He recalled that in his sighted days, he had always associated her with a dignified carriage and a reticent speech. It seemed inconceivable that this was the same woman addressing him now, and the violent contrast rendered him willing to consider that there might be truth in her plea. "Will you tell me?" he asked gently. "I was wedded almost immediately after leaving school. I was a child, the merest girl. I knew nothing, and I married a man of whom I knew nothing. It was my mother's design, and no doubt she believed she acted for my highest good. She secured for me a position of sorts, comfort, and protection from the specter of poverty. I accepted her dictates blindly, ignorantly. I could hardly have refused, for my mother was an imperious woman, and I was accustomed to obedience. I did as she commanded and dutifully married the man she selected. The case is common enough, certainly, but its prevalence does not ease the burden of endurance." "But Mr. Adair?" Durrance interjected. "After all, I knew him. He was older, undoubtedly, than you, but he was kind. I believe, too, he cherished you." "Yes. He was kindness incarnate, and he cared for me.
