Did I say I wondered sadly? No. A new influence had begun to reshape my life, holding sadness at bay for a time. Imagine a secluded dell, deep-hidden in forest secrecy, lying in dimness and mist. Its turf is damp, its grass pale and humid. But imagine a storm or an axe creating a wide gap among the oaks; the breeze sweeps in, the sun looks down, and the sad, cold hollow transforms into a deep cup of radiance. High summer pours her blue glory and golden light from that beautiful sky, which the starved hollow had never known before.

A new creed became mine: a belief in happiness.

Three weeks had passed since the adventure in the garret. I now possessed, in that upstairs case, box, and drawer, four companions like the first letter I received. Traced by the same firm pen, sealed with the same clear wax, they were full of the same vital comfort. To me, at that time, they seemed truly vital. Reading them in later years, I found them kind and pleasing, written by someone well-pleased themselves. In the final two, there were a few closing lines, half-gaily, half-tenderly, "touched by feeling, but not subdued." Time, dear reader, has mellowed them into a mild beverage; but when I first tasted their elixir, fresh from the honored fountain, they seemed the juice of a divine vintage—a draught that Hebe might fill and the very gods approve.

Perhaps you recall what was said earlier and wish to know how I answered these letters: did I write under the dry restraint of Reason, or the full impulse of Feeling?

Truthfully, I compromised. I served two masters: I bowed in the houses of Rimmon and lifted my heart toward another shrine. I wrote two answers to these letters—one for my own relief, the other intended for Graham's perusal.

First, Feeling and I turned Reason out of the room, barred the door, and sat down to pour out our hearts with eager pens. Two sheets were covered with the language of a strongly adherent affection, a rooted and active gratitude. (Once, for all, I disclaim with scorn any sneaking suspicion of so-called "warmer feelings": women do not entertain them where, from the start of an acquaintance, they have never been cheated of the conviction that to do so would be a mortal absurdity. Nobody launches into love unless they have seen or dreamed the rising of Hope's star over Love's troubled waters.) When I had expressed this closely-clinging, deeply-honoring attachment—an attachment that wanted to absorb all the pain of its object's destiny, to conduct away all storms and lightning with passionate solicitude—the doors of my heart shook, bolts yielded, and Reason leaped in vigorous and revengeful. She snatched the sheets, read them, sneered, erased, tore them up, rewrote, folded, sealed, directed, and sent a terse, curt missive of a single page. She did right.

I did not live on letters alone. I was visited, cared for, and taken out to *La Terrasse* weekly. I was treated with great kindness. Dr. Bretton openly explained why: "To keep away the nun," he said. "He was determined to dispute with her for her prey. He had developed a thorough dislike for her, chiefly on account of that white face-cloth and those cold grey eyes. The moment he heard of those odious particulars, consummate disgust incited him to oppose her; he was determined to try whether he or she was the cleverest, and he only wished she would visit him again when he was present:" but she never did. In short, he regarded me scientifically as a patient, exercising his professional skill and natural benevolence through a course of cordial treatment.

One evening, the first of December, I walked alone in the *carré*. It was six o'clock; the class doors were closed, but inside, the pupils, rampant in the license of evening recreation, created a miniature chaos. The *carré* was dark, save for a red light beneath the stove; the wide glass doors and long windows were frosted over. A crystal sparkle of starlight, here and there, spangled this blanched winter veil, proving it a clear, moonless night. My daring to remain alone in the darkness showed my nerves were regaining health. I thought of the nun but hardly feared her, though the staircase loomed behind me, leading up through blind, black night to the haunted garret. Yet, I admit, my heart quaked and my pulse leaped when I suddenly heard breathing and rustling. Turning, I saw in the deep shadow of the steps a deeper shadow—a shape moving and descending. It paused at the class door, then glided past me. Simultaneously, a clangor of the distant doorbell echoed. Life-like sounds bring life-like feelings: this shape was too round and low for my gaunt nun; it was only Madame Beck on duty.

"Mademoiselle Lucy!" cried Rosine, bursting in with a lamp from the corridor, "they are waiting for you in the salon."

Madame saw me, I saw Madame, Rosine saw us both; there was no mutual recognition. I headed straight for the salon. There I found exactly what I anticipated: Dr. Bretton, but in evening dress.

"The carriage is at the door," he said. "My mother has sent it to take you to the theatre. She was going herself, but an arrival prevented her. She immediately said, 'Take Lucy in my place.' Will you go?"

"Just now? I am not dressed," I cried, glancing despairingly at my dark merino gown.

"You have half an hour to dress. I decided to go only after five, when I heard there was to be a genuine feast in the presence of a great actress."

He mentioned a name that thrilled me—a name that, in those days, could thrill Europe. It is hushed now; its once-restless echoes are still. She who bore it went years ago to her rest; night and oblivion have long since closed above her. But *then*, her day—a day of Sirius—stood at its full height, light and fervor.

"I'll go; I will be ready in ten minutes," I vowed. Away I flew, never once checked by the thought that perhaps stops you now: namely, that going anywhere with Graham and without Mrs. Bretton could be objectionable. I could not have conceived, much less expressed to Graham, such a scruple without risking a tyrannous self-contempt, kindling an inward fire of shame so quenchless and devouring that I fear it would soon have licked up the very life in my veins. Besides, my godmother, knowing her son and knowing me, would as soon have thought of chaperoning a sister with a brother as of keeping anxious guard over our movements.

The present required no showy array; my dun mist crape would suffice. I sought it in the great oak wardrobe in the dormitory, where hung no less than forty dresses. But changes and reforms had occurred; an innovating hand had pruned this crowded wardrobe, carrying divers garments to the garret—my crape among them. I had to fetch it. Getting the key, I went aloft, fearless and almost thoughtless. I unlocked the door and plunged in. You may believe it or not, but when I entered, that garret was not wholly dark as it should have been. From one point shone a solemn light, like a star but broader. So plainly did it shine that it revealed the deep alcove with a portion of the tarnished scarlet curtain drawn over it. Instantly, silently, before my eyes, it vanished; so did the curtain and alcove. All that end of the garret became black as night. I ventured no research; I had neither time nor will. Snatching my dress, which hung on the wall near the door, I rushed out, relocked the door with convulsed haste, and darted downward to the dormitory.

But I trembled too much to dress myself; impossible to arrange hair or fasten hooks-and-eyes with such shaking fingers. I called Rosine and bribed her to help. Rosine liked a bribe, so she did her best, smoothing and plaiting my hair as well as a coiffeur would, placing the lace collar mathematically straight, tying the neck-ribbon accurately—in short, doing her work like the neat-handed Phillis she could be when she chose. Giving me my handkerchief and gloves, she took the candle and lit me downstairs. After all, I had forgotten my shawl; she ran back to fetch it, and I stood with Dr. John in the vestibule, waiting.

"What is this, Lucy?" he said, looking down at me narrowly. "Here is the old excitement. Ha! The nun again?"

I utterly denied the charge, vexed to be suspected of a second illusion. He was skeptical.

"She has been, as sure as I live," he said. "Her figure crossing your eyes leaves on them a peculiar gleam and expression not to be mistaken."

"She has _not_ been," I persisted; for indeed, I could deny her apparition with truth.

"The old symptoms are there," he affirmed. "A particular pale look, and what the Scotch call a 'raised' look."
