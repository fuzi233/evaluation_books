My parents' situation was, to put it mildly, irregular. They had been estranged for several years, yet despite their advanced ages—my father approaching forty, my mother somewhere past thirty—they possessed the vital energy of adolescents trapped in geriatric bodies. To the uninitiated eye, particularly that of an inquisitive maid or a lonely bachelor, they presented a distorted vision of matrimony. I would have described a husband as a creature incapable of finding peace unless his spouse hovered nearby, someone who would burst from his office door twenty times a day to inquire, "Maggie, are you busy? I need to discuss a matter of business," or "Maggie, are you alone? I'm descending immediately." Conversely, I would have defined a wife as a woman whose gaze was perpetually illuminated with affection when fixed upon her partner, someone delighted by his presence and distressed by his absence. However, in cases like theirs, such definitions were woefully inadequate.

Furthermore, living with any married couple required specific tactical maneuvers. One must raise one's feet high above the table surface lest they be mistaken for others and subsequently crushed. Furthermore, entering a room without prior auditory signaling—a practice known in Stageland as "noise without"—proved highly inadvisable. The nervous intruder, clutching the door handle, would be met with the sudden, spring-like eruption of occupants into the air, followed by a bizarre scuttling of limbs, only to discover the residents seated rigidly in opposite corners, deeply engrossed in books or embroidery. Yet, regarding certain households, such precautions were superfluous.

I, unfortunately, exerted little control over my parents in these matters. My interruptions were quickly dismissed with the weary sigh of, "Oh, it's only Spud," usually accompanied by the removal of a sofa cushion. My aunt, however, commanded a different kind of reverence. Though she rarely spoke, her efforts to spare their feelings bordered on the excessive. She traversed the house solely to the rhythm of a sustained, piercing cough. As my father once noted, albeit ungraciously, the volume of barking generated by my aunt in a single day would have earned accolades from a hospital ward full of dying consumptives; for a robust and healthy lady, the expenditure of nervous force must have been astronomical. Moreover, ensuring that her eyes had witnessed nothing unintended, she invariably entered rooms backward, slamming doors loudly and laboriously before turning around. Through dark passages, she ambulated while singing. Few women could have displayed greater consideration, yet—such is the nature of humanity—neither my father nor my mother extended gratitude to her, as far as I could discern.

Indeed, paradoxically, the more sympathy she showed, the more irritable they became.

"I believe, Fanny, you hate seeing Luke and me happy together," my mother declared one day, emerging from the kitchen to intercept my aunt, who was preparing to invade the drawing-room by dropping teaspoons at precise five-second intervals outside the door. "Do not make such a spectacle of yourself." Her tone was genuinely unkind.

"Hate it!" my aunt replied. "Why should I? Why not allow a pair of turtle-doves to coo, when their combined age barely exceeds seventy?" The gentle candor of her responses often took me aback.

As for my father, he turned positively vindictive. I recall the occasion vividly; it was the first, though certainly not the last, time I witnessed him lose his temper. Whatever sparked the subject, my father halted abruptly. We were strolling along the canal bank.

"Your aunt"—my father likely did not intend it, but his tone and manner invariably conveyed to me that he regarded me as personally responsible for her existence. This burden weighed heavily on me. "Your aunt is the most cantankerous, the most—" he broke off, shaking a fist toward the setting sun. "I wish to God," he cried, "your aunt had a comfortable little income of her own, with a freehold cottage in the countryside, by God I do!" But the next moment, shame washing over him: "Not but what sometimes, of course, she can be very nice, you know," he added hastily; "do not tell your mother what I said just now."

Another observer of this domestic farce was Susan, our maid-of-all-work, the first of a long and varied lineage extending until the advent of Amy, to whom heaven sent blessings. Susan was a stout, elderly female prone to sudden, inexplicable fits of sleepiness, allegedly caused by "trouble." Yet her heart, she proudly boasted, was always in the right place. She could never witness my parents sitting near one another without collapsing into tears, the sight of conjugal bliss serving as a painful reminder of the past glories of her own failed marriage.

Though an earnest seeker, I could never fully grasp the intricacies of Susan's marital history. Whether her answers were deliberately evasive or the result of a naturally incoherent mind, I could not say; they tended toward confusion. On Mondays, I observed Susan weeping regretfully into Brussels sprouts because she had been prevented from watering "his" grave at Manor Park due to other duties. By Tuesdays, I listened, blood chilling, to her recitals of intentions to seize "him" by the scruff of his neck if she ever regained the luxury of touching him.

"But, I thought, Susan, he was dead," I ventured naturally.

"So did I, Master Paul," she rejoined. "That was his artfulness."

"Then he isn't buried in Manor Park Cemetery?"

"Not yet; but he'll wish he was, the half-baked monkey, when I get hold of him."

"Then he wasn't a good man?"

"Who?"

"Your husband."

"Who says he ain't a good man?" It was Susan's ability to leap from tension to tension that most bewildered me. "If anybody says he ain't, I'll gouge their eye out!"

I hastened to assure Susan that my remark was an inquiry, not an assertion.

"He brings me a bottle of gin—for my headaches—every time he comes home," Susan continued, citing evidence, "every blessed time."

At such junctures, I would retreat to the safer atmospheres of German grammar or mixed fractions.

We suffered greatly from Susan in various ways. Regarding the admirable position of her heart, we all felt it our duty to overlook mere failings of the flesh—all but my aunt, who never feigned sentimentality.

"She's a lazy hussy," my aunt declared one morning while rinsing her mouth; "a gulping, snorting, lazy hussy, that's what she is." There was some justification for my aunt's indignation. It was eleven o'clock, and Susan was still asleep off an attack of what she termed "new-ralgy."

"She has seen a good deal of trouble," said my mother, who was drying dishes.

"And if she were my cook and housemaid," replied my aunt, "she would see more, the slut!"

"She's not a good servant in many respects," my mother admitted, "but I think she's good-hearted."

"Oh, drat her heart," was my aunt's retort. "The proper place for that heart is on the doorstep. And that's where I'd put it, along with her and her box, if I had my way."

Susan's departure did occur shortly thereafter. It happened one Saturday night. My mother ascended the stairs looking ghastly.

"Luke," she said, "please run for the doctor."

"What's the matter?" asked my father.

"Susan," gasped my mother, "she's lying on the kitchen floor breathing in the strangest fashion and quite unable to speak."

"I'll go for Washburn," said my father; "if I am quick, I shall catch him at the dispensary."

Five minutes later, my father returned panting, trailed by the doctor. This was a large, black-bearded man who possessed the uncanny knack of appearing larger than he actually was. He descended the kitchen stairs two at a time, shaking the entire edifice. He brushed my mother aside and bent over the unconscious Susan, who lay on her back with her mouth agape. Then he rose, looked at my father and mother, who watched him with troubled expressions, and opened his mouth. From it erupted a roar of laughter unlike any sound I had ever heard.

The next instant, he seized a pail half-full of water and flung it over the woman. She opened her eyes and sat up.

"Feeling better?" asked the doctor, still holding the pail; "have another dose?"

Susan began to gather herself, intending to express her feelings; but before she could utter the first word, he had pushed the three of us outside and slammed the door behind us.

From the top of the stairs, we heard Susan's thick, rancorous voice raging fiercer and fiercer, periodically drowned by the man's savage roars. Whenever she flagged for breath, he would yell encouragement, shouting, "Bravo! Go it, my beauty, give it tongue! Bark, bark! I love to hear you," applauding her, clapping his hands, and stamping his feet.

"What a beast of a man," said my mother.

"He is really a most interesting man when you come to know him," explained my father.

Replied my mother, stiffly: "I don't ever mean to know him." But knowledge belongs only to the past.
