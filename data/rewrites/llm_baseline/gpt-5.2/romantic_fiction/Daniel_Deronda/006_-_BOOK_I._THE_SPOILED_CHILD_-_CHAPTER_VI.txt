That high-handed judgment from Herr Klesmer had left Gwendolen with a bruise she could not show. She would not have owned—not even to the looking-glass—that she envied Miss Arrowpoint for anything so unromantic as disciplined practice: the slow, daily surrender to scales, the sure ear trained by patient correction, the confidence that comes from having learned a language of sound thoroughly enough to dispute a master’s taste. Yet it stung her that this pale, slight young woman—no one’s notion of a triumph to look at, with a sallow skin and small, tidy features that seemed made for economy rather than conquest—should possess an authority that could not be laughed away: a certainty in music, a fineness of choice, a quiet refusal to be bullied into admiration. And the worst cruelty was that it never declared itself; it stayed wrapped in perfect kindness, as if superiority could afford to be gentle.

Gwendolen’s mind, when it was hurt, did not like to sit still with the hurt. She pushed it aside as one brushes crumbs from a dress—lightly, decisively, without examining what it means to be eating on the wrong occasion. Herr Klesmer, that sudden magician who had widened her horizon with a word, was not always there; his coming and going between London and Quetcham itself became a sort of promise. A man who moved could be intercepted. A judgment that had once been severe might, with the right performance, be lured into admiration.

Besides, her singing was applauded everywhere the neighborhood had any notion of brilliance—at Brackenshaw, at the Firs, at the little assemblies where tea steamed and candles made every cheek look younger. Praise was warm, immediate, and—she preferred to think—trustworthy. She was not one of those thirsting spirits who must have a perfection no one asks of them; she could live on approval as comfortably as on well-made broth, without demanding that it be clarified to the last impurity.

And yet, for all her pride in being unlike the “ordinary young ladies,” her rebellion had no daring map. It was a hunger for supremacy that wore the ornaments of breeding: it could not consent to look like the sordid struggle for income, could not admit itself as mere appetite. She mocked “theoretical women” and would have separated herself with laughter from anything that smelled of reform; still, she resented the wirework of family obligation as if she had built a whole philosophy against it. Her dreams were the dreams of genteel romance—souls in journals, power in vague phrases, a satin shoe wandering into a swamp and making the tragedy doubly refined.

Offendene, in that first winter, began to feel like a handsome backdrop waiting for an event that would not arrive. She knew, with a sharp clarity, what she did not want: the smooth, predictable days of other girls. What she could not find was the door into a different life, or the particular act that would feel like freedom instead of mere caprice. The neighborhood, on the whole, was too well behaved to supply a plot.

After the earliest invitations had exhausted their small stock of novelty, she returned from these little social raids with a few bright arrows of satire—some of which had already offended Mrs. Arrowpoint—and then filled the long, wet days with devices so girlish that they were almost pathetic in their ingenuity. Her strongest assertion of personal empire was to neglect Alice’s lessons—on the principle that Alice might achieve distinction by not knowing what everyone else knew—and to set Miss Merry, along with the maid who attended “all the ladies,” to work with her on a wardrobe of dramatic costumes. There were Greek draperies, Oriental folds, odd composite inventions that borrowed from everywhere without submitting to any one law; there was the smell of starched linen warmed near the fire, of lavender tucked into drawers, of paste and paint, of candle-grease and the faint metallic tang from pins held between lips. The house became a little workshop where domestic routine—tea-trays, sewing-baskets, the crackle of logs—was threaded through with the stagey labor of hemming and fastening, as if the making of an illusion were as ordinary as mending.

Gwendolen, who had never acted beyond posing in school tableaux, was certain she could act well; certainty rose in her like yeast without requiring proof. She had seen the Théâtre Français once or twice, had heard her mother speak of Rachel, and at times her mind played with the thought of becoming an actress—because the world would be compelled to look. She believed herself more beautiful than that thin Jewess; beauty, in her private creed, was a kind of destiny.

One afternoon, when rain was drawing grey lines down the window and the room smelled of wool drying near the hearth, she stood in her Greek dress before Anna and declaimed scraps with a tragic intention that made the common furniture look like properties.

“Do I look as well as Rachel, mamma?” she asked, turning her arms a little, letting the lamplight skim them as if skin were a material to be displayed like satin.

“You have better arms than Rachel,” Mrs. Davilow said, lazily kind. “Your arms would do for anything, Gwen. But your voice is not so tragic as hers; it is not so deep.”

“I can make it deeper, if I like,” said Gwendolen, as if voice were merely a ribbon to be chosen. Then, with the swift obstinacy of a girl arranging the world to her taste, she added, “I think a higher voice is more tragic. It is more feminine; and the more feminine a woman is, the more tragic it seems when she does desperate actions.”

“There may be something in that,” Mrs. Davilow returned, half amused, half indifferent. “But I don’t know what good there is in making one’s blood creep. And if there is anything horrible to be done, I should like it to be left to the men.”

“Oh, mamma, you are so dreadfully prosaic! As if all the great poetic criminals were not women! I think the men are poor cautious creatures.”

“Well, dear—and you, who are afraid to be alone in the night—I don’t think you would be very bold in crime, thank God.”

“I am not talking about reality, mamma,” Gwendolen said, impatient, as if reality were an ill-bred intruder. And when her mother was called out, she turned instantly to Anna, seizing the moment like a cook snatching the exact second to lift a soufflé before it falls.

“Anna, do ask my uncle to let us get up some charades at the rectory. Mr. Middleton and Warham could act with us—just for practice. Mamma says it will not do to have Mr. Middleton consulting and rehearsing here. He is a stick, but we could give him suitable parts. Do ask, or else I will.”

“Oh, not till Rex comes,” Anna pleaded, brightening. “He is so clever, and such a dear old thing, and he will act Napoleon looking over the sea. He looks just like Napoleon. Rex can do anything.”

“I don’t in the least believe in your Rex, Anna,” Gwendolen laughed, pinching at Anna’s worship with a light cruelty. “He will turn out to be like those wretched blue and yellow water-colors of his which you hang up in your bedroom and adore.”

“Very well, you will see,” Anna said stoutly. “It is not that I know what is clever, but he has got a scholarship already, and papa says he will get a fellowship, and nobody is better at games. He is cleverer than Mr. Middleton—and everybody but you call Mr. Middleton clever.”

“So he may be—in a dark-lantern sort of way. But he is a stick. If he had to say, ‘Perdition catch my soul, but I do love her,’ he would say it in just the same tone as, ‘Here endeth the second lesson.’”

“Oh, Gwendolen!” Anna cried, shocked by the way Gwen’s irreverence threw sacred and profane into the same pan. “And it is very unkind of you to speak so of him, for he admires you very much. I heard Warham say one day to mamma, ‘Middleton is regularly spooney upon Gwendolen.’ She was very angry with him; but I know what it means. It is what they say at college for being in love.”

“How can I help it?” said Gwendolen, with contempt that covered a flicker of triumph. “Perdition catch my soul if I love him.”

“No, of course; papa, I think, would not wish it. And he is to go away soon. But it makes me sorry when you ridicule him.”

“What shall you do to me when I ridicule Rex?” Gwendolen said, wickedly, tasting the power of making Anna tremble.

“Now, Gwendolen, dear, you will not?” Anna’s eyes filled. “I could not bear it. But there really is nothing in him to ridicule. Only you may find out things. For no one ever thought of laughing at Mr. Middleton before you. Every one said he was nice-looking, and his manners perfect. I am sure I have always been frightened at him because of his learning and his square-cut coat, and his being a nephew of the bishop’s, and all that. But you will not ridicule Rex—promise me.”

The beseeching look, so simple and so loyal, touched Gwendolen like a soft hand on a bruise.

“You are a dear little coz,” she said, lifting Anna’s chin with thumb and forefinger. “I don’t ever want to do anything that will vex you. Especially if Rex is to make everything come off—charades and everything.”

When Rex came at last, he brought an immediate stir as fresh as opening a window onto fields after too many days of indoor air. Offendene and the rectory felt suddenly peopled; plans had a partner. Rex was open-hearted, handsome in the same family fashion as his father and Anna—only softer than the one and larger than the other—healthy as if he had been made out of sunlight and good appetite. Life pleased him so naturally that vice lay outside his path like some foul ditch he had no need to step into; “what some fellows did” seemed to him “stupid stuff,” scarcely worth noticing.

His affection for Anna was full and easy; but he had never known a stronger love than the one that took him, almost at once, for Gwendolen. Within a fortnight it had settled into him like a fate. He did not so much observe her as receive her: the sound of her voice, the sway of her movement, the very air of the room shifting when she entered—his senses were her servants. The back of his head seemed enough to tell him whether she was near. He could not imagine obstacles; his love itself felt like a guarantee of hers. It never occurred to him that perfection might demand a grand match; he was without conceit except that small necessary firmness that lets a man call his own life real. He took Gwendolen’s beauty as part of the world’s goodness, as if it were as natural as ripening fruit.

She, for her part, liked him. She sang and played when he wished—music thickening the rooms like warm scent from a kitchen where something sweet is baking—she rode with him willingly, though his borrowed horses were sometimes comical, and she was ready for any sport that amused him. She showed the proper appreciation of Anna. No mark of sympathy seemed absent. And Rex, poor boy, read every kindness as devotion.

They were continually together, more often at Offendene where there was a freer reign—meaning, chiefly, that Gwendolen’s wishes took the place of law. The charades were arranged as she meant, and rehearsals began; even little scenes outside the planned ones occurred, in which Gwendolen’s acting was quicker than her design.

Mrs. Davilow, seeing no great objection now that Rex was there, allowed even Mr. Middleton to be asked and pressed into service. Warham, who was preparing for India under a Wanchester coach, had little time; he moved about as if packed with facts and yet starving for any joy not required by the coming examination, which threatened to prove that the welfare of the Empire depended on the ability to quote Browne’s _Pastorals_ on demand. Mr. Middleton, persuaded to take grave parts, did them well chiefly by not attempting to be amusing. Gwendolen flattered him on his enviable immobility of countenance, and he, who had at first felt a jealous pinch at her easy comradeship with Rex, began to soothe himself with the notion that cousinly familiarity excluded serious passion. Indeed, her more formal manner with himself sometimes seemed like favor; he felt encouraged to think he might speak before leaving Pennicote, though he had meant to wait until his position was surer.

Gwendolen knew, perfectly, that this pale-whiskered curate in his square collar adored her; she felt only that she had no objection to being adored. She looked at him with calm mercilessness and gave him mild hopes by avoiding any direct dramatic contact—as if distance were meaning. For meanings, after all, depend on who translates them, and in what dialect of desire.

If anyone had reasoned beforehand, they might have concluded that a young man with Anglican leanings—scrupulous about small sacrednesses, seldom laughing except from politeness, shrinking from naked names as if they were indecent—would not choose a bride so daring in ridicule, so little shaped into the special softness expected in a clergyman’s wife. Or they might have thought that a young man trained by theological reading would foresee his taste would not suit a restless girl like Miss Harleth. But love, when it comes, does not ask permission from other people’s logic.

Rex, absorbed, would have been sorry for Middleton if he had known of the curate’s inward conflict; but Rex saw nothing except what belonged to Gwendolen. His passion made the whole household’s soundscape—the clink of teacups, the rustle of scripts, the piano’s scales, laughter in the hall—feel like accompaniment to one central melody: her presence. And, sensitive though he was to noise and rhythm in the ordinary way of the young, he had no defense against the sharper noises that sometimes lacerate the mind: a slammed door, an unexpected cry, a chord struck too hard. Those sounds, which later would matter, were not yet part of his foresight.

At last, after much rehearsal, it was decided that a select party should be invited to Offendene. The actors were pleased with themselves; the audience would be chosen to be indulgent. Anna surprised everyone by the neatness of her playing; there was something almost sly in how well she managed her small parts, as if her simplicity had been listening and storing away. Mr. Middleton answered perfectly by remaining solemn.

The chief delay had been caused by Gwendolen’s insistence on appearing in her Greek dress. She could find no charade-word—waking or dreaming—that would justify her favorite costume and its statuesque possibility. Racine was no use; the others could not declaim French verse, and improvised speeches would drop the scene into burlesque. Besides, Mr. Gascoigne prohibited acting scenes from plays. He usually protested against the idea that what was fit amusement for others was unfit for a clergyman; but in this matter he would not step beyond the decorum of that part of Wessex. Charades in his sister-in-law’s house were one thing; private theatricals, fully so called, were another.

Since everyone wished to satisfy Gwendolen, Rex proposed a tableau at the end, where her “majesty” could be presented without speech to mar it. This delighted her; only the choice remained.

“Something pleasant, children, I beseech you,” Mrs. Davilow said. “I can’t have any Greek wickedness.”

“It is no worse than Christian wickedness, mamma,” said Gwendolen, remembering her earlier talk of poetic criminals.

“And less scandalous,” Rex added. “One thinks of it as all gone by and done with. What do you say to Briseis being led away? I would be Achilles, and you would be looking round at me—after the print we have at the rectory.”

“That would be a good attitude for me,” Gwendolen said, accepting the thought of her own posture as the primary argument. But then, later, she rejected it. “No. It will not do. There must be three men in proper costume, else it will be ridiculous.”

“I have it,” Rex said after a pause. “Hermione as the statue in _The Winter’s Tale_. I will be Leontes, and Miss Merry Paulina, one on each side. Our dress won’t signify,” he added, laughing; “it will be more Shakespearian and romantic if Leontes looks like Napoleon, and Paulina like a modern spinster.”

Hermione was chosen; everyone agreed that age did not matter. But Gwendolen insisted there should be just enough acting to introduce the moment: the cue, the music, her stepping down and advancing. Leontes, instead of embracing her, was to kneel and kiss the hem of her garment, and then the curtain would fall. The antechamber with folding doors made an admirable stage; the whole household, with Jarrett the village carpenter added like another instrument to the orchestra of labor, was absorbed in preparations. Paint, wax, wood, cloth—all the homely materials took on that peculiar excitement of becoming something else. Wax-lights were tested and set; their smell—honeyed and faintly animal—mixed with the sharper odor of tallow and the dust disturbed by moving furniture. Boards creaked under hurried feet; someone’s hammer tapped; the piano was opened and shut. The house, which on other days held its silence like a well-bred person, now hummed with purposeful sound.

Gwendolen had a special exultation, for she knew Herr Klesmer was again at Quetcham, and she had made sure to include him among the invited. His presence would be the true audience: not the friendly faces that applauded out of habit, but the one ear she feared and wanted.

Klesmer came. He was in one of his quiet moods, placid and mostly silent, replying with benignant syllables more or less articulate, as if he were meekly bearing a cross in a world overgrown with amateurs—or as if, being a lion among mice, he must take care where he set his paws.

The evening went smoothly—so smoothly that the small accidental things seemed part of the plan, as they often do when people want to believe in success. The charades were received with polite laughter; Anna was praised; Mr. Middleton managed his gravity. The air of the room thickened with warmth from bodies and lamps; the rustle of dresses and whispering of little comments made a soft, continuous undertone. And then the tableau of Hermione came, striking in its stillness after the earlier movement. It answered perfectly. A murmur of applause had begun and was gradually suppressed as Leontes gave permission that Paulina should exercise her utmost art and make the statue move.

Hermione—Gwendolen—stood with her arm resting on a pillar, elevated by about six inches, chosen with care so that her foot and instep would show when she advanced and descended. The wax-lights, placed to flatter her, made her white drapery glow; her hair and eyes looked darker by contrast. She waited for the cue with a triumphant inward steadiness.

“Music, awake her, strike!” said Paulina—Mrs. Davilow, who by special entreaty had consented, wrapped in a white burnous and hood, looking like a gentle specter.

Herr Klesmer, seated at the piano, struck a thunderous chord.

In that same instant—before Hermione had put forth her foot—the movable panel on a line with the piano flew open to the right, opposite the stage, and revealed, in the pale definiteness of the candlelight, a picture: a dead face, and a fleeing figure. The effect was like a wound suddenly uncovered. A startled movement ran through the company; eyes turned toward the opening.

But in the act of turning, all eyes were jerked back by a piercing cry from Gwendolen.

She had not changed her attitude; only her expression had transformed, and the transformation was terrifying in its terror. She looked like a statue into which a soul of Fear had entered: lips gone pallid and parted, eyes—usually narrowed under long lashes—dilated and fixed, as if the sight before her had broken some inner barrier. Her mother, less surprised than alarmed, rushed toward her; Rex, too, was at her side instantly.

Mrs. Davilow’s touch had the effect of an electric shock. Gwendolen dropped to her knees and covered her face with her hands. Her whole body trembled, but no sound came after that first cry. Even in that collapse there was self-consciousness; she seemed to be trying to control the signs of what had mastered her. Presently she allowed herself to be raised, and she was led away while the company, relieved to have something else to do with their fear, began to explain.

“A magnificent bit of plastik that!” Klesmer said to Miss Arrowpoint, his voice almost approving, as if the shock were part of art.

A quick fire of undertoned question and answer ran round, like crackling brushwood.

“Was it part of the play?”

“Oh, no—surely not. Miss Harleth was too much affected. A sensitive creature!”

“Dear me! I was not aware there was a painting behind that panel; were you?”

“No; how should I? Some eccentricity in one of the Earl’s family long ago, I suppose.”

“How very painful! Pray shut it up.”

“Was the door locked? It is very mysterious. It must be the spirits.”

“But there is no medium present.”

“How do you know that? We must conclude that there is, when such things happen.”

“Oh—the door was not locked; it was probably the sudden vibration from the piano that sent it open.”

This last conclusion came from Mr. Gascoigne, who asked Miss Merry, if possible, to fetch the key. His readiness to explain was thought by Mrs. Vulcany unbecomingly rational for a clergyman; she observed in an undertone that Mr. Gascoigne was always a little too worldly for her taste. But the key was produced. The rector turned it in the lock with an emphasis that seemed to say, “it will not fly open again,” and he put the key in his pocket as a security.

The panel was shut; the room’s ordinary noises—small laughs, chair-legs shifting, the soft tap of a fan against fingers—returned cautiously, as if testing whether the air was safe again.

Before long Gwendolen reappeared. She showed her usual spirits with a visible effort, determined to ignore, as far as she could, the terrible interruption she had made in her own Hermione. Her cheeks had color again; her mouth curved; she spoke as if nothing had happened. But there was a strained brightness in it, like music played a little too fast to outrun a memory.

When Klesmer said to her, “We have to thank you for devising a perfect climax: you could not have chosen a finer bit of plastik,” she flushed with pleasure. It was easier to accept his words as belief than to face them as the delicate feigning they were. He had divined that her betrayal into fear must be mortifying to her, and he offered her an escape: he would treat it as acting. Gwendolen, hungry for his admiration, cherished the idea that now he was struck with her talent as well as her beauty, and the uneasiness his earlier criticism had planted in her was half turned to complacency.

But too many knew what had been rehearsed and what had not. No one besides Klesmer took the trouble to soothe her imagined humiliation. The general instinct was to let the incident drop—like a dish that has spilled, wiped away quickly so the meal may go on, while the stain remains in the mind long after the cloth looks clean.
