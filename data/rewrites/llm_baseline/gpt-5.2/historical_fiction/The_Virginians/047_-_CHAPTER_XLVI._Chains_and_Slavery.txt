Harry Warrington, caged in that sour little lock-up off Cursitor Street, had leisure enough for remorse and for rage; remorse—yes, for the follies that had led him here; rage—hotter still, at the indignity of a sheriff’s hand on a gentleman’s shoulder. Yet he would not grant the thing its full weight. It must be a passing inconvenience. He had twenty friends in London who would unlatch any door for him: Mr. Draper, all bows and deference; his kind baronet-uncle, who had offered him hearth and home; his cousin Castlewood, lately so lucky at cards; the great men at White’s and the Chocolate-House; good Aunt Bernstein herself. Only—let it be done quietly. Let no buzzing town-mouths make a dish of it, spicing his name for their amusement.

As if London were not a kitchen where every whisper is minced fine and tossed from table to table. As if the world cannot smell out a man’s mischance through closed shutters, the way hungry folk smell roast-meat down a passage. Still, Harry, with the vanity of youth, imagined all of St. James’s already chewing on his disgrace, Selwyn cracking bone-dry jests over it, March fretting about the bet, the whole club clattering their plates in cruel congratulation at the “Savage” being taken.

No—he would not begin with family, nor with lords. He would send for Sampson. Sampson had been in such scrapes before; Sampson knew the turns of such locks, the price of such keys. So at the first watery light of dawn—so late it seemed that morning, as though the sun had forgotten the alleyways of bondage—Harry got a message carried to Long Acre, begging the chaplain to come with counsel and comfort.

Having done that, his mind settled enough to condescend to breakfast. It came on a tray like a sentence: pale bread, a weak pot, and the smell of yesterday’s grease clinging to everything, as if the very boards had been basted with old misery. The attendant, with the practised civility of a tavern-waiter and the hard eye of a gaoler, asked whether his honour would order dinner, or take his meal at Mrs. Bailiff’s table with other gentlemen. No. Harry would be gone before dinner. The fellow’s mouth twitched as if he had heard that promise from a hundred young blades before.

“Well, if your honour does stay,” he said, shutting the door upon Harry and his pride, “there is good beef and carrot at two o’clock.”

So the morning dragged—hour after hour, each one a spoon scraping the bottom of a pot. A return message came from Sampson: he would attend as soon as might be. Ten o’clock came, eleven, noon; no parson. Instead, near midday, in burst Gumbo with a portmanteau, and with such howling grief as might have raised the dead. He flung himself at Harry’s feet, clasped at his knees, sobbed out oaths of fidelity; he would die, he would sell himself into slavery again, he would do anything to save Master Harry from this calamitous place.

Harry—touched in spite of himself—ordered him up sharply. “Give me my clothes. Dress me. And hold your tongue. Not a word of this business to anybody. Mind you—not a word.”

“Oh no, sir—nebber to nobody!” Gumbo swore, solemn as a judge; and set about his work with the devotion of a man who loved with his hands. He brushed and shook out linen, laid out stockings and waistcoat, and set his master’s coat as carefully as a cook trusses a bird for a great table. He flung a dash of powder through Harry’s hair—too much, perhaps, for a prison room, but Gumbo would have his gentleman look like a gentleman, even among walls that stank of fear. Buttons were fastened, cravat coaxed into place, cuffs set straight; and when the black had done, Harry might have been stepping from his lodgings into a chair for St. James’s, not waiting for a key in a shabby den.

Love and servility did all they could. But there are things no hands can mend once done; and there are truths that crouch away when a man has been trained to fear. Gumbo, for all his warmth, had been schooled in that old lesson of dependence: to slip, to excuse, to lie prettily where plain truth might bring a blow. Such training is a chain of the tongue, whether the neck beneath it be black or white; and the slave’s habit clings like smoke to cloth.

For indeed—his oath to keep silence was the very kind of promise his nature could not keep. Gumbo’s heart might ache honestly enough, but his tongue wagged with its own appetite: for story, for brag, for company. Long before he entered Harry’s room with that portmanteau, he had carried the news as fast as feet and vanity could take him—chiefly to gentlemen with shoulder-knots and worsted lace, who live by other people’s fortunes and misfortunes. He had told Colonel Lambert’s people; he had told Lord Wrotham’s; he had proclaimed it at his footmen’s club over ale, with every repetition adding a fresh garnish; he had repeated it in Sir Miles Warrington’s butler-room, where the tale was swallowed with relish; he had gone on to Madame Bernstein’s household, to those with whom he kept affectionate commerce; and, having nothing to do that evening now his master was “captured,” he had even climbed to Lord Castlewood’s and fed the news to that kitchen too. So when he looked holy-eyed and vowed, “nebber to nobody,” it was already all over town in the way kitchen-savour runs—under doors, up stairs, into drawing-rooms.

Harry sat dressed, breakfast finished, pride propped up as if clothes could armour him. Still no Sampson. At last, after noon, a billet arrived—not the man, but his writing, sealed with a wafer still damp, the ink hardly dry.

It ran—blotted with haste and misery—to this effect: that Sampson had done all that could be done; that Harry little knew what danger he had called his chaplain into; that Sampson, hastening to him with gratitude and zeal, had been seized by two villains with a writ, and was now lodged in Naboth’s, three doors off; so close, he wrote, that they might almost hear each other across the garden walls of their respective prisons. He had much of importance to say, but would not put it on paper. He ended with a prayer that both their cursed fortunes might mend, and signed himself, “Your honour’s afflicted Chaplain-in-Ordinary, J. S.”

So the friend Harry had summoned into the pit had tumbled in beside him.

And since Sampson would not speak plainly on paper, the rest must be told without his voice.

Gumbo’s babble had not reached as far as Long Acre. Sampson had known nothing of Harry’s arrest until Harry’s messenger found him. Still freshly grateful for the service Harry had lately done him, the chaplain’s first impulse was to run to Lord Castlewood—who had, after all, won so much from his cousin—and try to wring pity from that fine-bred hardness.

Sampson spoke with genuine feeling. He pleaded, he painted Harry’s distress, he reminded the nobleman of kinship and of winnings. But Lord Castlewood, whom life had made peevish and inwardly desperate, cut him short.

“Did I not tell you, two days since, when you came for money, that I was as poor as a beggar, Sampson?” he said. “Has any one left me a fortune since? The little sum I won from my cousin was swallowed up by others. I not only can’t help Mr. Warrington, but—upon my word, not knowing his calamity—I had written to him this morning to ask him to help me.”

And he had: a letter to that very effect was even then making its slow, cruel way by penny post to Harry’s lodgings.

“I must get him money, my lord,” Sampson cried. “He had scarcely anything left after relieving me. I’ll pawn my cassock and bands—he must have money!”

“Amen,” Castlewood said with an indifferent sneer. “Go and pawn your bands, your cassock—anything you please. Your enthusiasm does you credit.” And he went back to his paper as though he had dismissed a beggar from his door.

Sampson went out with despair in his throat—and was seized, just beyond that house, by Lady Maria.

She drew him into the dining-room, her face already worked into grief and anger. “Tell me,” she demanded, “what is this that has happened to Mr. Warrington?”

“Your ladyship knows, then?” Sampson asked.

“Have I not been in mortal anxiety since his servant brought the dreadful news last night?” she said. “We had it as we came from the opera—from my Lady Yarmouth’s box—my lord, my Lady Castlewood, and I.”

“His lordship, then, did know?” Sampson pressed.

“Benson told the news when we came from the playhouse to our tea,” she repeated.

The chaplain’s patience snapped. He swore, and told her—hotly—of Castlewood’s refusal; of his pretence of ignorance; of the sums he had taken from Harry; and of Harry’s generosity to himself. Sampson spoke as only a man can speak when gratitude and indignation boil together.

Then Lady Maria—who did not commonly love truth, yet could fling it like stones when rage armed her—burst into a torrent against her own blood. Tears, curses, and words worse than curses; she flung open family histories as if they were cupboards and dragged out the sour contents. Her voice rose so shrill, her gestures so wild, that Sampson, fearing the storm she meant to loose, edged away toward the street.

But Maria dashed to her brother’s library door and broke in upon him.

Castlewood looked up, surprised as a man is surprised when a quiet room is invaded by a sudden hawk-cry. He chose a strong oath, as one chooses a stick, and threw it at her to beat her back.

She did not flinch. Fear of him had been her habit; fury unmade it.

“So, my lord!” she cried. “You sit with him in private to cards and pigeon him! You get the poor boy’s last shilling, and you won’t give him a guinea out of his own winnings now he is penniless!”

“So that infernal chaplain has been telling tales!” Castlewood said.

“Dismiss him—do! Pay him his wages, and let him go. He’ll be glad enough!” she flung back.

“I keep him to marry one of my sisters, in case he is wanted,” he answered, glaring.

“What can the women be in a family where there are such men?” Maria cried.

“Effectivement!” said my lord, with a shrug.

“What can we be, when our fathers and brothers are what they are?” she went on. “We are bad enough—but what are you? You have neither courage—nor honour—nor common feeling. As your equals won’t play with you, you must take this poor lad out of Virginia—your own kinsman—and fleece him! Oh, it’s a shame—a shame!”

“We are all playing our own game,” Castlewood said coolly. “Haven’t you played and won one, Maria? Is it you that are squeamish of a sudden about the poor lad from Virginia? Has Mr. Harry cried off, or has your ladyship got a better offer? If you won’t have him, one of the Warrington girls will, I promise you; and the old Methodist woman in Hill Street will give him the choice of either. Are you a fool, Maria Esmond? A greater fool, I mean, than in common?”

“I should be a fool if I thought either of my brothers could act like an honest man, Eugene!” she cried. “I am a fool to expect you will help a relative in distress; that if you find a victim you won’t fleece him.”

“Fleece him? Psha! What folly! Have you not seen, from the course the lad has been running for months, how he would end? If I had not won his money, some other would. I never grudged you your little plans regarding him. Why fly into a passion because I put out my hand to take what he was offering to the world? I reason with you—I don’t know why. You think this money belonged by right to Lady Maria Warrington and her children? I tell you that in three months every shilling would have found its way to White’s macco-table, and it is much better spent paying my debts. So much for your anger, tears, menaces, and naughty language. See! I am a good brother, and repay you with reason and kind words.”

“My good brother might have given a little more than kind words,” Maria broke in, “to the lad from whom he has taken hundreds.”

“Great heavens, Maria!” Castlewood cried, suddenly changing his tone to one of sly instruction. “Don’t you see that even out of this affair—unpleasant as it seems—a clever woman may make her advantage?”

“I don’t understand you,” she said.

“C’est possible; but sit down, and I will explain in a manner suited to your capacity.” And Maria, who had entered like a raging creature, found herself—by habit, by family training, by the old authority of the elder male voice—sinking into a chair to listen.

Meanwhile, in Clarges Street, Madame de Bernstein had not slept. The news Gumbo brought her the night before—one more bitter draught for a weary spirit—had struck her with a real, unwilling tenderness. Arrests were common enough in her world; she knew the process as she knew the prices at market. But what scrape had her scapegrace nephew fallen into now? How much money would be demanded for his release? Had he spent all his own?

If he had not committed himself beyond remedy, she was disposed to help. She liked even his follies; they were at least alive. For long years he had been almost the only creature upon whom she could lay anything like natural affection; and so, while he lay wakeful behind his prison door and she lay wakeful behind her curtains, their thoughts, in different rooms, kept the same uneasy vigil.

Early in the morning she sent to her man of business, Mr. Draper, commanding him to learn at once for what sums Mr. Warrington had been arrested, and to report to her. Draper’s people were quick. They found Harry locked up close by, and they counted the detainers already laid against him. Others would follow, they warned, as soon as creditors scented the confinement—like flies that find a spilled pot of syrup.

So much to Mr. Sparks, the jeweller, for those unlucky presents; so much to the Bond Street landlord for board, fire, and lodging. These, for the present, were all. Draper stood ready to settle them the moment she gave word. Especially the jeweller’s bill must be paid; for Harry had acted with a rashness that could turn to scandal: taking goods on credit and pledging them with a pawnbroker. It spoke of sudden pressure, not of dishonour; perhaps he meant to redeem at once. Still, in London, appearances are currencies too; and this coin had an ugly stamp unless melted down quickly.

“There cannot be the least difficulty about a thousand pounds more or less,” Madame de Bernstein said, “for a gentleman of Mr. Warrington’s rank and expectations.” She knew very well where funds lay that could be touched at once with her guarantee.

Mr. Draper, ever eager to smooth a disturbance, proposed to go instantly to Messrs. Amos, settle the matter, and have Mr. Harry free in time to dine with her at two—and so confound the club gentlemen “who are no doubt rejoicing over his misfortunes.”

But the Baroness, with that hard practical mercy of hers, had other views.

“I think, my good Mr. Draper,” she said, “that my young gentleman has sown wild oats enough; and when he comes out of prison I should like him to come out clear—and without any liabilities at all. You are not aware of all his.”

“No gentleman ever does tell all his debts, madam,” Draper answered. “No one I ever had to deal with.”
