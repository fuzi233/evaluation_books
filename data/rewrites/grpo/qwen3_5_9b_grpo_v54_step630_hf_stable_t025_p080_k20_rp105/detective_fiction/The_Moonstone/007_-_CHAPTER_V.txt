The moment the carriage doors closed behind us, leaving us alone on the desolate stretch of sand, I rose to my feet, intending to stretch my stiffened limbs. But Mr. Franklin held up a hand to stop me.

"There is one comfort in this wretched spot," he observed, his voice cutting through the damp air. "We have the entire world to ourselves. Stay where you are, Betteredge. I have something important to discuss with you."

As he spoke, I studied him, searching for the ghost of the boy I remembered in the man standing before me. The search was futile. The rosy cheeks and the trim little jacket of my childhood memory were gone, buried under a pallor that suggested illness or worry. Most jarring of all, his face was now framed by a curly brown beard and moustache, a transformation that left me both surprised and disappointed. While he retained a certain lively, engaging manner, it lacked the effortless charm of his youth. Worse still, he had failed to keep his promise of growing taller; he remained neat and slim, but nowhere near the middle height he had boasted. The years had stripped him of almost everything, save for that bright, straightforward gaze in his eyes. There, finally, I found the essence of the old boy, and I decided to rest my investigation there.

"Welcome back to the old place, Mr. Franklin," I said, masking my unease. "It is doubly welcome that you arrived hours ahead of schedule."

"I have reasons for arriving early," Franklin replied, his tone shifting to something more serious. "I suspect I have been followed and watched in London for the past few days. That is why I took the morning train instead of the afternoon; I needed to lose sight of a certain dark stranger."

His words struck a chord deep within me, instantly recalling the three jugglers and Penelope's vague fears that they intended mischief against him.

"Who is watching you, sir? And why?" I pressed.

Instead of answering, Franklin pivoted. "Tell me about the three Indians who visited your house today. It is quite possible, Betteredge, that my pursuer and your jugglers are merely pieces of the same puzzle."

"How do you know about the jugglers, sir?" I asked, stacking questions upon questions—a breach of etiquette, I grant you, but human nature is rarely perfect.

"I saw Penelope," Franklin explained casually. "She told me. Your daughter promised to be a pretty girl, and she has fulfilled that vow. She has a small ear and a small foot. Did the late Mrs. Betteredge possess such inestimable charms?"

"The late Mrs. Betteredge had her share of defects, sir," I replied dryly. "One of them, if I may mention it, was an inability to stay on the subject at hand. She was more like a fly than a woman; she could not settle on anything."

"She would have suited me perfectly," Franklin admitted with a grin. "I never settle on anything either. Your daughter praised your edge to me, saying you express yourself beautifully. Penelope's own words, blushing divinely. My respect for you did not prevent me from noting that she knows you well. Let us be serious, however. What exactly did these jugglers do?"

A flicker of dissatisfaction crossed my mind—not that Franklin had kissed my daughter, for he was welcome to that—but that I had been forced to recount her foolish tale at second hand. Yet, there was no turning back. As I recounted the events, Franklin's merriment vanished. He knitted his brows and twisted his beard, his expression growing grave. When I finished, he repeated two of the questions posed by the chief juggler to the boy, seemingly committing them to memory.

"'Is it on the road to this house, and on no other, that the English gentleman will travel today?' 'Has the English gentleman got It about him?' I suspect," Franklin said, producing a small, sealed parcel from his pocket. "That 'It' refers to *this*. And 'this,' Betteredge, refers to my uncle Herncastle's famous Diamond."

"Good Lord, sir!" I exclaimed. "How do you find yourself in charge of the wicked Colonel's Diamond?"

"The Colonel's will bequeathed the Diamond as a birthday present to my cousin, Rachel," Franklin stated flatly. "And my father, acting as the Colonel's executor, has entrusted it to me to deliver here."

If the sea, slowly creeping over the Shivering Sand, had suddenly transformed into dry land, I doubt I could have been more stunned.

"The Colonel's Diamond left to Miss Rachel!" I stammered. "And your father, sir, the Colonel's executor! I would wager anything that your father would not have so much as touched the Colonel with a pair of tongs!"

"Strong language, Betteredge! What was there against the Colonel? He belonged to your era, not mine. Tell me what you know of him, and I will explain how my father became his executor, along with other matters. I have made disturbing discoveries in London regarding my uncle Herncastle and his Diamond, and I require your confirmation. You just called him the 'wicked Colonel.' Search your memory, my old friend, and tell me why."

Seeing his earnestness, I obliged.

Here is the substance of what I told him, written out for your benefit. Clear your minds of the children, the dinner, the new bonnets, or whatever trivialities occupy your thoughts. Forget politics, horses, city prices, and club grievances. I hope you do not take this direct address amiss; it is simply a method I use to engage the gentle reader. Have you not seen the finest authors in your hands, and do you not know how easily your attention wanders when a book demands it less than a person does?

I began with my lady's father, the old lord known for his short temper and long tongue. He had five children: two sons, followed eventually by three daughters, my mistress being the youngest and the finest of the lot. The eldest son, Arthur, inherited the title and estates. The second, the Honourable John, received a substantial fortune from a relative and entered the army.

They say an ill bird fouls its own nest. I consider the noble family of the Herncastles my own nest, and I shall be grateful if I am spared the details concerning the Honourable John. I honestly believe he was one of the greatest blackguards to ever live. He joined the Guards but was forced to leave before his twenty-second year—the army was too strict for him. He traveled to India to test if discipline was equally rigid there, seeking active service. In terms of bravery, he was a mixture of bulldog and game-cock, with a dash of savagery. He fought at the taking of Seringapatam. Soon after, he transferred regiments twice more, eventually reaching the rank of lieutenant-colonel. It was during this time he suffered a severe sunstroke and returned to England.

He came back with a reputation that barred him from every door in his family. My lady, then newly married, took the lead, declaring—with her brother's approval—that he should never enter her house again. There were numerous slurs attached to his name, but the stain of the Diamond is the only detail I need mention.

It was rumored he had acquired his Indian jewel through means too bold for him to acknowledge publicly. He never attempted to sell it, nor did he need money. He never gave it away, nor did he show it to a living soul. Some claimed he feared military authorities; others, less informed, suggested he feared losing his life. Perhaps there was a grain of truth in the latter. It was false to say he was afraid, yet it was true that his life had been twice threatened in India, and the Moonstone was widely believed to be the cause. Upon his return to England, finding himself shunned by everyone, the Diamond was once again blamed. The mystery of his life became his prison, outlawing him among his own people. Clubs refused him entry; women he sought to marry rejected him; friends and relations could barely bear to look at him in the street.

Most men in such a predicament would seek to clear their names. But to submit, even when wrong and facing universal opposition, was not the way of the Honourable John. He kept the Diamond in flat defiance of assassination in India, and in flat defiance of public opinion in England. This was the portrait of the man: a character who braved everything, with a handsome face that seemed possessed by the devil.

Rumours varied. Sometimes he was said to be addicted to opium and collecting old books; at other times, experimenting with strange chemicals; occasionally, carousing among the lowest slums of London. Regardless, he led a solitary, vicious, underground existence. Once, and only once, after his return to England, did I see him face to face.

About two years prior to our current timeline, and roughly eighteen months before his death, the Colonel appeared unexpectedly at my lady's house in London. It was the night of Miss Rachel's birthday, the twenty-first of June, and a party was in full swing. A footman summoned me to the hall, where I found the Colonel wasted, worn, old, shabby, and as wild and wicked as ever.

"Go up to my sister," he commanded. "Tell her I have called to wish my niece many happy returns of the day."

He had attempted reconciliation by letter on several occasions, presumably only to annoy her. But this was his first actual visit. I nearly mentioned the party, but his devilish look daunted me. I ascended with the message and, at his request, left him waiting in the hall. The servants stared at him from a distance, viewing him as a walking engine of destruction, loaded with powder and shot, ready to explode at any moment.

My lady possessed a dash of the family temper. "Tell Colonel Herncastle," she instructed me coldly, "that Miss Verinder is engaged, and that *I* decline to see him." I tried to plead for a more civil response, knowing the Colonel's constitutional superiority to normal gentlemanly restraints. It was useless. The family temper flared instantly. "When I want your advice," she snapped, "you know I ask for it. I do not ask for it now." I descended with the message, offering a slightly softened version of my own devising: "My lady and Miss Rachel regret that they are engaged, Colonel, and beg to be excused the honor of seeing you."

I expected him to erupt, even at such politeness. To my surprise, he reacted with unnatural quiet. His eyes, a glittering bright grey, settled on me for a moment, and he laughed—not out loud, but inwardly, in a soft, chuckling, horribly mischievous manner. "Thank you, Betteredge," he said. "I shall remember my niece's birthday." With that, he turned on his heel and walked out.

The following year, we heard he was ill in bed. Six months later—that is, six months before the time I am now writing—a letter arrived from a highly respectable clergyman to my lady. It announced two wonderful pieces of family news: first, that the Colonel had forgiven his sister on his deathbed; second, that he had forgiven everyone else and met an edifying end. I hold an unfeigned respect for the Church, despite the bishops and clergy, yet I am firmly persuaded that the devil remained in undisturbed possession of the Honourable John, and that his final abominable act was to take the clergyman in!

This was the sum total of my report to Mr. Franklin. I noted that he listened with increasing eagerness. The story of the Colonel being turned away from his sister's door on the occasion of his niece's birthday seemed to strike Franklin like a bullet hitting its mark. Though he did not acknowledge it, I saw plainly that I had made him uneasy.

"You have said your say, Betteredge," he remarked. "Now it is my turn. Before I reveal the discoveries I made in London and how I became involved with the Diamond, I must ask one thing. You look, my old friend, as if you do not quite grasp the purpose of this consultation. Do your looks belie you?"

"No, sir," I replied. "My looks, on this occasion at least, tell the truth."

"In that case," Franklin continued, "suppose I outline my perspective before we proceed. I see three very serious questions involved in the Colonel's birthday gift to my cousin Rachel. Follow me carefully, Betteredge; count me off on your fingers if it helps," he added with a certain pleasure in demonstrating his clear-headedness, a trait that reminded me wonderfully of his boyhood. "First question: Was the Colonel's Diamond the object of a conspiracy in India? Second question: Has that conspiracy followed the Diamond to England? Third question: Did the Colonel know the conspiracy followed the Diamond, and has he purposely left a legacy of trouble and danger to his sister, through the innocent medium of his sister's child? That is what I am driving at, Betteredge. Do not let me frighten you."

It was all very well for him to say that, but he *had* frightened me.

If he was right, our quiet English home was suddenly invaded by a devilish Indian Diamond, bringing with it a conspiracy of living rogues, set loose by the vengeance of a dead man. This was our situation as revealed by Franklin's final words! Who had ever heard the like in the nineteenth century? In an age of progress, in a country rejoicing in the blessings of the British constitution? Nobody had, and consequently, nobody could be expected to believe it. Yet, I would continue my story regardless.

When one receives a sudden alarm of this magnitude, nine times out of ten the sensation is felt in the stomach. When the stomach reacts, the mind wanders, and one begins to fidget. I fidgeted silently on the sand. Franklin noticed my agitation—whether of body or mind, they are often the same—and, checking himself just as he was about to begin his part of the story, asked sharply, "What do you want?"

What did I want? I did not tell him, but I will tell you in confidence. I wanted a whiff of my pipe and a turn at *Robinson Crusoe*.
