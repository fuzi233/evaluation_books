Once inside the palace I drew Sola straight to the dining hall, because there was comfort in that long room’s familiar noises: the soft clink of service blades laid beside platters, the hush of sandals on polished stone, the low, domestic thunder of a hundred throats swallowing warm broths. The air carried the clean bite of carved fruit and the faint tang of fermented juices—small, ordinary scents that tried, bravely, to stand between us and catastrophe.

Sola greeted her father with the grave, ritual economy of her people. Then she began, and though she spoke of gods and black ships and the Valley Dor, she spoke as one might recite the steps of a bread one must bake to keep a household alive—measured, exact, unwilling to waste a syllable. Her voice had that husky Barsoomian cadence I had come to know, a dialect that held meanings no Heliumetic court phrase could quite translate; each time she named Iss or Dor, the word itself seemed to scrape the room’s acoustics, as though the name carried its own echo.

“Seven days ago,” she said, “after her audience with Zat Arras, Dejah Thoris tried to slip from the palace in the dead of night. I had not been told what passed between them, but I saw what it did to her—like a knife turned inside the mind. When I found her moving like a shadow through the sleeping corridors, I did not need her to name her destination.

“I woke a dozen of her most faithful guards. I told them my fear. They did not argue, not one. They took up their harness and their weapons as quickly and quietly as cooks take up their knives when the fire is already hot and there is no time left for indecision. We swore to follow our Princess wherever she went—even to the Sacred Iss and the Valley Dor.

“We overtook her not far beyond the palace. Woola was with her, and no one else. She tried to drive us back, feigning anger, ordering us home as though we were children under a stern elder’s hand. But for once we disobeyed her. When she saw we would not let her walk the last long pilgrimage alone, she broke—she wept, and then she embraced us. Together we went south into the night.

“The next day we came upon a herd of small thoats. From that hour we were mounted, and the miles fell behind us. We rode hard—so hard the wind rasped in our throats and the world became a thin, whistling line of sand and scrub and bitter, dusty air. We travelled very fast and very far. On the morning of the fifth day we sighted a great fleet of battleships sailing north.”

At that, the dining hall seemed to tighten. Even the little sounds—spoons against bowls, the quiet breathing of the guards at the doors—felt suddenly too loud, as though noise itself might summon dark sails to the windows.

“They saw us before we could find shelter,” Sola went on. “Soon we were ringed by a horde of black men. The Princess’s guard fought to the end. They died as they had lived—close to her. Only Dejah Thoris and I were spared.

“When Dejah Thoris knew she was in the hands of the black pirates, she tried to take her own life. One of them tore her dagger away. Then they bound us so tightly we could not even curl our fingers. They did it with practiced speed, the way a butcher ties a struggling carcass—not for mercy, but for convenience.

“The fleet continued north. There were about twenty large battleships, and many smaller, swift cruisers. That evening one of the cruisers returned from far ahead with another prisoner—a young red woman they had found in the hills. They boasted they had taken her under the very noses of three red battleships.

“We heard scraps of their talk. They were searching for fugitives who had escaped them days before. The commander spoke with the new captive for a long time—long and earnest. When he finished, they bound her and threw her into our compartment.”

Sola’s eyes, when she remembered it, went distant, as though she still smelled that compartment’s stale breath—the sourness of fear sealed up in metal, the rank musk of strangers, the bitter oil of machinery that never quite loses its metallic sting.

“She was very beautiful,” Sola said. “She told Dejah Thoris she had taken the voluntary pilgrimage many years ago from the court of her father, the Jeddak of Ptarth. She was Thuvia—Princess of Ptarth.

“Then she asked Dejah Thoris who she was. When she learned, she fell to her knees and kissed Dejah Thoris’ bound hands. And she said that very morning she had been with John Carter, Prince of Helium—and with Carthoris, his son.”

I felt the words like a blow and a balm at once: my name carried into that prison of air and iron by a stranger’s mouth; my son alive, standing in the same hour of sunlight as a captive Princess. How much of life is nothing but sound—names spoken, vows repeated—until we hang our survival upon them?

“Dejah Thoris could not believe her at first,” Sola continued. “But Thuvia told her everything—strange adventures since she had met you, and what you and Carthoris and Xodar had told her of your time in the Land of the First Born. Then Dejah Thoris said, ‘For who upon all Barsoom other than John Carter could have done the deeds you tell of?’”

Sola paused, and when she spoke again her voice thickened, as though the memory had its own weight.

“And when Thuvia told Dejah Thoris of her love for you—of her own heart, and of your loyalty to the Princess you had chosen—Dejah Thoris broke down. She wept. She cursed Zat Arras and the cruel fate that drove her from Helium only days before your return.

“‘I do not blame you for loving him, Thuvia,’ she said. ‘That your affection is pure I can believe, from the candour with which you confess it.’”

The hall was very still then, but it was not silence so much as listening—everyone straining to hear the next thing that would decide whether the night’s bread would rise or fall.

“The fleet continued north nearly to Helium,” Sola said. “But last night they must have learned that you had escaped them. They turned south again. Not long after, a guard came into our compartment and dragged me out onto the deck.

“‘There is no place in the Land of the First Born for a green one,’ he said. Then he shoved me—hard—over the side.”

A sharp intake moved through the room like a gust. Even the servants, who had no business in councils of war, froze with trays half-lifted, the faint rattle of utensils suspended in air.

“But fate intervened,” Sola said, and there was something grim in the way she said fate, as though it were merely another predator with its own habits. “By a miracle I lived. The ship was moving slowly, and I thought I would fall thousands of feet to my death, for all day they had sailed high above the ground. But I struck soft vegetation—no more than twenty feet below the deck. The keel must have been grazing the surface.

“I lay there all night. When the sun rose I understood: I was on the highest peak of a lofty range. In the darkness the fleet had barely skimmed the crest of the hills, and in that brief moment the guard threw me overboard, thinking to rid the ship of me and kill me in one act.”

She lifted her chin, proud in spite of bruises and exhaustion.

“A few miles west I saw a great waterway. When I reached it I learned it belonged to Helium. There I procured a thoat. The rest you know.”

For many minutes no one spoke. The ordinary sounds of the dining hall—the tame, human noises that make a palace feel like a home—had gone thin, as though the room itself were holding its breath. Dejah Thoris in the clutches of the First Born. The thought made my skin crawl, as if the word Issus were a foreign spice that burned rather than nourished.

Then the old fire rose in me, sudden and absolute. I sprang to my feet. The chair scraped the floor with a raw, protesting cry that seemed too loud in that hushed hall.

With back-thrown shoulders and sword upraised I took a solemn vow: I would reach her. I would rescue her. I would have revenge.

A hundred swords sang from a hundred scabbards—bright, clean sounds that cut through the room’s heavy air. A hundred fighting-men leaped up, some even to the table-top, pledging lives and fortunes as though they were passing bread from hand to hand: not charity, but necessity.

My plans had already formed themselves in my mind, each part fitted to the next like well-made gear. I thanked them—each loyal friend—and left Carthoris to keep them occupied while I withdrew with Kantos Kan, Tars Tarkas, Xodar, and Hor Vastus to my audience chamber.

There, long after dark, we spoke in low voices and listened hard between phrases, because a palace has its own acoustic ecology: the whisper that travels under a door; the footfall that does not belong; the faint click of a listening device or the soft intake of breath behind a curtain. In courts, the wrong sound at the wrong moment can kill more surely than a blade.

Xodar was positive Issus would choose both Dejah Thoris and Thuvia to serve her for a year.

“For that length of time at least they will be comparatively safe,” he said, “and we will know where to look.”

We spoke of equipping a fleet to enter Omean. The details were left to Kantos Kan and Xodar. Kantos Kan would take the vessels into dock as rapidly as possible; Xodar would direct their equipment with water propellers. For years the blacks had refitted captured battleships so they might navigate Omean, and Xodar knew the construction of propellers, housings, and auxiliary gearing.

Six months, we estimated—because secrecy would be as essential as steel. Let Zat Arras scent this plan too soon, and he would poison it as one spoils a pot by slipping in the wrong bitter herb at the last moment.

Kantos Kan’s face was hard as he spoke of Zat Arras. “The man’s ambitions are fully aroused,” he said. “Nothing short of the title of Jeddak of Helium will satisfy him. I doubt he would even welcome Dejah Thoris’ return, for it would place another nearer the throne than he. With you and Carthoris out of the way, there would be little to prevent him from assuming the title. So long as he is supreme here there is no safety for either of you.”

“There is a way,” Hor Vastus cried, “to thwart him effectually and for ever.”

“What?” I asked.

He smiled as though tasting the words before releasing them. “I shall whisper it here,” he said, “but some day I shall stand upon the dome of the Temple of Reward and shout it to cheering multitudes below.”

“What do you mean?” Kantos Kan demanded.

“John Carter, Jeddak of Helium,” Hor Vastus said softly.

Eyes brightened around me—grim, eager. The room’s very air seemed to sharpen with expectation. But I shook my head.

“No,” I said. “I thank you, but it cannot be. Not yet. When we know that Tardos Mors and Mors Kajak are gone to return no more—if I be here then—I shall join you to see that the people of Helium are permitted to choose fairly their next Jeddak. Whom they choose may count upon my loyalty. I will not seek the honour for myself. Until then Tardos Mors is Jeddak of Helium, and Zat Arras is his representative.”

“As you will, John Carter,” Hor Vastus said—and then his head snapped toward the window overlooking the gardens. His voice dropped to a hiss. “But—what was that?”

He pointed. In the same instant he sprang to the balcony.

“There he goes!” he cried. “The guards! Below there—the guards!”

We were close behind him. All of us saw it: the figure of a man running across a patch of sward, vanishing into shrubbery beyond. A fleeting silhouette, but the sound of his movement—too light, too practiced—was unmistakable.

“He was on the balcony when I first saw him,” Hor Vastus said. “Quick—after him!”

We ran to the gardens. We scoured the grounds with the entire guard for hours, but found nothing—not a footprint, not a broken stem, not a cloth snagged on thorn. Whoever had been there moved like a rumor, leaving only the unease of a disturbed night-bird’s cry.

“What do you make of it, Kantos Kan?” Tars Tarkas asked.

“A spy sent by Zat Arras,” Kantos Kan replied. “It was ever his way.”

“He will have something interesting to report, then,” Hor Vastus laughed, but the laughter had no warmth in it.

“I hope he heard only our references to a new Jeddak,” I said. “If he overheard our plans to rescue Dejah Thoris, it will mean civil war. He will try to thwart us—and in that I will not be thwarted. I would turn against Tardos Mors himself, were it necessary. If all Helium is thrown into bloody conflict, I shall still go on. Nothing shall stay me now short of death.”

I looked from face to face—men whose voices I knew, whose steps I could identify in any corridor by the rhythm of their stride.

“And should I die,” I said, “will you take oath to prosecute the search for her and bring her back in safety to her grandfather’s court?”

Each man swore upon the hilt of his sword.

We agreed the battleships to be remodelled should be ordered to Hastor, far to the southwest. Kantos Kan believed the docks there, alongside their regular work, could handle six battleships at a time. As commander-in-chief of the navy he could order the vessels there without raising suspicion, and keep the remodelled fleet in remote parts of the empire until we were ready to assemble it for the dash upon Omean.

Our conference broke up late. Each man left with duties clear as a recipe learned by heart: Kantos Kan and Xodar to oversee the remodelling; Tars Tarkas to communicate with Thark and learn his people’s sentiment toward his return from Dor, and if favourable to go at once and assemble a great horde of green warriors to be transported directly to the Valley Dor and the Temple of Issus while our fleet entered Omean and destroyed the vessels of the First Born; Hor Vastus to organize, quietly and delicately, a secret force of fighting-men sworn to follow me wherever I might lead. Over a million men to man a thousand great battleships and transports and convoys—an undertaking as vast as any harvest, requiring patience, timing, and hands willing to work in darkness.

After they had gone I bade Carthoris good-night. I was exhausted in a way that went beyond muscle and bone, like a man who has listened too long for the wrong sound outside his door.

In my own apartments I bathed and lay down upon sleeping silks and furs, hoping at last for the first full night’s sleep since my return to Barsoom. The fabrics held the faint scent of clean oils and the warm animal musk of the furs—small, intimate assurances of home.

Even that was denied me.

How long I slept I cannot say. I woke suddenly to the crushing weight of bodies. A gag was already in my mouth. Before my mind fully cleared, my arms and legs were bound so securely that struggle was a mockery. They worked with the swift efficiency of men who have rehearsed the task until it is nothing but muscle memory.

Not one of them spoke. The gag made speech impossible. They lifted me and bore me toward the door.

As they passed the window, the farther moon spilled brilliant beams across them, and I saw their faces were swathed in layers of silk. No features—no recognition—only eyes that avoided my own.

In the corridor they turned toward a secret panel in the wall—the one that led to the passage terminating in the pits beneath the palace. That any outside my household knew of it had seemed unlikely; yet the leader did not hesitate. He stepped directly to the concealed button. The panel swung open. His companions carried me through. He closed it behind him and followed.

Down we went through passageways that swallowed sound, the air growing cooler and closer with each turn, until the world was nothing but the scrape of sandals, the faint creak of leather, the muffled beat of my own heart trapped behind the gag.

At last we stopped. The leader rapped upon the wall with the hilt of his sword—three quick, sharp blows, a pause, then three more, another pause, then two. A code, crisp as a kitchen signal between cooks when words would carry too far.

A second later the wall swung inward.

I was pushed into a brilliantly lighted chamber. Three richly trapped men sat within. One turned toward me with a sardonic smile upon thin, cruel lips.

Zat Arras.
