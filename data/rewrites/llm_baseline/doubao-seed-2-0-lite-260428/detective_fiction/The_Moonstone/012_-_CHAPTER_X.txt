One after another, the rest of the party followed the Ablewhite clan through the doors, until we had all twenty-four guests settled in their places around the long dinner table. It was a fine sight, when the Rector of Frizinghall—blessed with a smooth, resonant delivery—stood up to say grace.

I won’t waste your time running through the full guest list. You’ll not cross paths with any of them again for the rest of the story, save for two men who sat flanking Miss Rachel at the head of the table. As the birthday girl, queen of the occasion, she was the natural centre of every eye that night—made all the more conspicuous by the fact that she’d worn the fateful birthday gift her mother had tried to persuade her to leave locked away: the Moonstone Diamond, fixed as a gleaming brooch pinned to the bodice of her white dress, rigged up for her by that universal handy man Mr. Franklin Blake with nothing but a bit of thin silver wire and his neat, clever fingers.

Every guest oohed and aahed over the diamond’s impossible size and colour, as you’d expect. But only the two men sat right beside her said anything that stuck in my memory long after the plates were cleared.

The guest on her left was Mr. Candy, our local Frizinghall doctor. A cheerful, companionable little fellow, he had one stubborn flaw: he could never resist a bad joke, and would plunge headfirst into conversation with total strangers without stopping to test the waters first, often upsetting people by accident before he even realised he’d said something wrong. Only in his actual medical practice did he show good sense—his enemies claimed he picked his discretion by instinct, and he often got results where far more careful, qualified doctors fumbled and failed. His line about the diamond was, true to form, a silly mystifying prank. He leaned in as solemn as a judge, and begged her, “in the name of science, let me take it home and burn it. We’ll heat it to exactly the right temperature, blow a steady stream of air over it, and little by little—puff!—it will evaporate into nothing, and you’ll never have to lie awake worrying about keeping a priceless stone safe ever again.” I saw Lady Verinder, listening from the head of the table, wear such a careworn little look I swear she half-wished the doctor was in earnest, and that Rachel was half as eager to sacrifice her birthday gift for the cause of science.

The man on Rachel’s right was far more notable: the celebrated Indian traveller Mr. Murthwaite, the same man who’d risked his life travelling in disguise through parts of the subcontinent no white European had ever dared set foot before. Tall, lean, wiry, sunbrowned and almost completely silent, he had a permanent look of weary restlessness and a pair of steady, unblinking eyes that missed nothing. Rumour in our neighbourhood ran that he was sick to death of our quiet, humdrum village life, and ached to head back out wandering the wild, ungoverned corners of the East. Over the whole of the three-hour dinner, I doubt he spoke six full words, or drank more than a single glass of wine—all save for what he said to Rachel about her diamond. The Moonstone was the only thing in the whole room that interested him, it seemed. He’d heard tales of it even in those remote, dangerous Indian places he’d trekked through. He stared at it in silence for so long Rachel grew flustered and fidgeted under his gaze, until he spoke in his cool, flat, unshakable tone: “If you ever travel to India, Miss Verinder, leave your uncle’s birthday present behind. A Hindu diamond is sometimes far more than a pretty trinket—it is part of a Hindu’s religion. I know of a certain city, and a certain temple inside that city, where dressed as you are now, your life would not be worth five minutes’ purchase.” Rachel, safe and warm in her English country home, thought the idea of being in such danger perfectly thrilling. The two Bouncer girls, our neighbourhood’s most giddy young misses, were even more delighted: they dropped their knives and forks with a clatter and shrieked in unison, “O! How interesting!” Lady Verinder fidgeted in her chair at once, and steered the conversation hard onto a new topic.

As the dinner dragged on, I slowly realised this feast was not going half as well as all the birthday feasts we’d hosted in the past. Looking back now, with all that happened later fresh in my mind, I’m half convinced that cursed diamond had cast a blight over the entire company. I poured their wine as generously as I could, and used my privilege as the old, trusted family servant to follow even the unpopular dishes around the table, whispering confidentially, “Do try a bite, ma’am/sir, I promise it’ll do you good.” Nine times out of ten they humoured me and took a portion, out of fond old Gabriel Betteredge regard—but it made not a scrap of difference. Long, awkward silences kept gaping open across the table that made me shift uncomfortably at the sideboard. When people did speak, they managed to stumble into the most unfortunate, ill-timed conversations you could imagine. Mr. Candy, for one, said more unlucky, foot-in-mouth things that night than I’d ever heard him say in all his years of practice. Take just one sample, and you’ll understand the torture I was standing through, trying my hardest to keep the party’s spirits up.

One of the ladies at dinner was good Mrs. Threadgall, widow of the late Professor Threadgall. She talked about her late husband constantly, but it never occurred to her to mention to strangers that he was dead—she seemed to think every adult in England would already know that. In one of those long silences, someone brought up the dry, unpleasant topic of human anatomy, and Mrs. Threadgall immediately started gushing about how anatomy was her husband’s favourite hobby in his spare time. As bad luck would have it, Mr. Candy, sitting across the table and having no idea the Professor had been in his grave for ten years, jumped in to be helpful.

“The College of Surgeons got in some remarkably fine new skeletons recently,” he called across the table in a loud, cheerful voice. “I strongly recommend the Professor pay them a visit, ma’am, the next time he has an hour to spare.”

You could have heard a pin drop. The whole table went dead silent out of respect for the Professor’s memory. I was standing right behind Mrs. Threadgall, pouring her a glass of hock, when she dropped her head and mumbled very quietly, “My beloved husband is no more.”

But Mr. Candy, far from suspecting the truth, carried on louder and more polite than ever.

“The Professor may not know,” he went on, “that a member of the College’s card will admit him any day except Sunday, between ten and four.”

Mrs. Threadgall tucked her face right down into her neckerchief, and repeated, even quieter, “My beloved husband is no more.”

I winked so hard at Mr. Candy across the table I almost strained my eye. Rachel tapped him on the arm. Lady Verinder shot him a look that could have curdled cream. It was no use. He kept going, all hearty, unstopping cordiality: “I would be delighted to send the Professor my personal pass, ma’am, if you would just be so kind as to pass along his current address.”

“His current address, sir, is the grave!” Mrs. Threadgall burst out, suddenly flying into a rage so fierce her shout made the wine glasses rattle on the table. “The Professor has been dead these ten years.”

“Oh good Heavens!” Mr. Candy gasped. Save for the two Bouncers, who burst into hysterical giggles, the silence that fell after that was so heavy you’d have thought every last person at the table was already on their way to join the Professor in the grave.

That was Mr. Candy’s contribution to the awkwardness, but the rest of the party were nearly as bad in their own ways. They either clammed up when they should have been chatting, or babbled so hard they talked clean past each other. Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite, that famously eloquent public speaker, wouldn’t exert himself to entertain a soul in private. Whether he was sulking or still embarrassed after that rejection he’d got in the rose garden earlier that day, I can’t say. He stuck strictly to talking to the woman sat right next to him, one of his charity committee members: a pious, spiritual lady who had a fine set of collarbones and an excellent taste in dry champagne. Standing close behind them at the sideboard, I heard every word they exchanged while I pulled corks and carved the roast mutton. They’d long since finished discussing their plans for rescuing fallen women and running charity workshops, and were deep in some high-falutin spiritual debate. “Religion,” I heard Mr. Godfrey say between the pops of corks, “is just love. And love is just religion. The earth is nothing but heaven, a little worn out and scuffed around the edges. And heaven is just the same earth, all polished up to look brand new. There are some very unpleasant people down here on earth, to be sure—but up in heaven, every single woman will be a member of one enormous, perfectly harmonious committee that never argues, and all the men will wait on them hand and foot as ministering angels.” Beautiful words, no doubt—but why on earth was he hoarding all that charming talk for just one lady, and not sharing it with the rest of the party?

Surely, you’ll say, Mr. Franklin Blake would have stepped in and livened things up? Not a bit of it. He was in fine, high spirits—Penelope had no doubt told him all about Godfrey’s humiliating rejection in the rose garden—but no matter how hard he tried to spark conversation, he always landed on the wrong topic, or spoke to the wrong person, managing to puzzle half the table and offend the rest. That mixed foreign training of his, those French, German and Italian quirks I mentioned before, was on full, bewildering display at Lady Verinder’s dinner table.

What do you make of him debating at great length, in his witty, frank French style, exactly how far a married woman could let her admiration for a man who was not her husband go—all to the vicar of Frizinghall’s elderly spinster aunt? What do you say to him, shifting into his German philosophical mode, telling our local lord of the manor, a world famous expert on cattle breeding who was in the middle of ranting about how to raise prize bulls, that all real world experience was worthless, and the only correct way to breed a perfect bull was to sit and meditate until you pulled the ideal idea of a perfect bull out of your own mind? And then, when our county MP got red in the face after the cheese and salad course, sputtering that if we lost our ancient traditional safeguards from the spread of democracy, we’d have nothing left at all—what do you say to Mr. Franklin, leaning over with that cool Italian smile of his, replying, “You are wrong, sir—we would still have three things left: Love, Music, and Salad.” He terrified half the table with those sorts of absurd outbursts, and when his plain English side finally came out, he dropped all his foreign smoothness and started making such scathing jokes ridiculing the entire medical profession that he actually made good-tempered little Mr. Candy fly into a proper rage.

The argument started when Franklin admitted, offhand, that he’d been sleeping very badly lately. Mr. Candy immediately told him his nerves were completely out of order, and he needed a full course of medication right away. Franklin laughed and replied that in his opinion, a course of blind guesswork medicine and stumbling around in the dark were exactly the same thing. Candy snapped back that Franklin was already stumbling around in the dark chasing sleep, and only medicine could show him the path to find it. Franklin shot back that he’d only ever heard the phrase “the blind leading the blind” as an old proverb, and now he was seeing it play out in real life. They went back and forth, trading sharp jabs, until both of them were red in the face—Candy so incensed defending his profession that Lady Verinder was forced to step in and order them to stop arguing at once. That final, public show of authority snuffed out the last remaining flicker of fun the party had left. Conversation sputtered up here and there for a minute or two at a time, but there was no life left in it. The Devil, or that cursed Moonstone, had possessed that entire dinner party—and every last person there was relieved when Lady Verinder rose from her seat, signalling the ladies could leave the gentlemen to their post-dinner wine.

I had just lined all the decanters up in a neat row before old Mr. Ablewhite, who was standing in for the master of the house, when a sound drifted from the terrace that made me drop my serving cloth on the spot. Mr. Franklin and I stared at each other across the table. It was the low thud of an Indian drum. As sure as I live, those wandering jugglers had come back the very second the Moonstone returned to the house!

I hobbled out to the terrace ready to chase them off, but the two Bouncer girls beat me to it. They whizzed out the doors like a pair of fireworks, wild to see the Indians perform their tricks, and the rest of the ladies and gentlemen trotted right after them. Before you could say “good gracious”, the three Indian jugglers were bowing and salaaming to the party, and the Bouncers were fussing over their little boy performer and kissing his cheek. Mr. Franklin moved to one side of Rachel, and I positioned myself right behind her. If our suspicions were correct, she was standing there completely innocent of any danger, showing the Indians the Moonstone glittering on the bodice of her dress for all to see.

I was so flustered by the whole wretched evening and the audacity of those men showing up right when the diamond was out on display that I can barely remember what tricks they performed. The next clear thing I noticed was Mr. Murthwaite, the Indian traveller, gliding around the half-circle of guests to step up quietly behind the jugglers. Without warning, he spoke to them in a low, guttural language none of the rest of us could understand.

If he had driven a bayonet into each of their sides, I doubt the Indians could have spun around to face him with more tiger-quick speed. The very next second, they were bowing and salaaming to him in their most obsequious, snaky polite way, their faces schooled to show nothing. After a minute of quiet back and forth in that unknown tongue, Mr. Murthwaite drifted away as silently as he’d arrived. The lead Indian, who acted as their interpreter, turned back to the party, and I noticed his dark coffee-coloured skin had gone a sickly pale grey from the moment Murthwaite had spoken. He bowed to Lady Verinder, and announced the performance was over. The Bouncer girls were so unutterably disappointed they let out a unified loud, wailing “O!” directed straight at Mr. Murthwaite for cutting their fun short. The little Indian boy went around passing his hat for donations, the ladies trundled back inside to the drawing room, and all the gentlemen save Franklin and Murthwaite headed back in for their wine. I went with one of the footmen to escort the Indians safely off the property.

Heading back to the house through the shrubbery, I caught the sharp smell of tobacco, and found Mr. Franklin and Mr. Murthwaite pacing slowly under the trees, the traveller puffing on a cheroot. Franklin waved me over to join them.

“This is Gabriel Betteredge,” Franklin said, presenting me to Murthwaite. “The old family servant and friend I told you about a little while ago. Tell him, if you please, what you just told me.”

Mr. Murthwaite took his cheroot out of his mouth, leaned wearily against a tree trunk, and spoke. “Mr. Betteredge, those three men out there are no more street jugglers than you or I are.”

That was a shock, of course. I asked the traveller if he’d met these particular men before.

“Never,” he said. “But I know what real Indian street juggling looks like, and what you saw tonight was a clumsy, badly done imitation. Unless decades of wandering the subcontinent has left me completely mistaken, those men are high-caste Brahmins. I accused them of being disguised, and you saw how it shook them—even the most gifted Hindu actors find it almost impossible to hide that kind of shock. They have committed two unforgivable sins against their caste already: first, they crossed the sea, which is strictly forbidden to high caste Brahmins, and second, they disguised themselves as lowly common jugglers. To make that double sacrifice in their home country is unthinkable, unless their motive is extraordinarily serious, and they already have a plan to earn their way back into the good graces of their caste when they return. There is no other reason to do such a thing.”

I was struck completely speechless. Franklin, after fidgeting for a moment, clearly weighing up whether he ought to share the secret family business with a total outsider, broke the silence. “I am loath to share private family matters that are none of your concern, Mr. Murthwaite,” he said, “but after what you just told us, I owe it to Lady Verinder and Rachel to explain the full context, in strict confidence.” He then told the traveller the whole story: the colonel, the diamond stolen from the Hindu temple, the deathbed wish to send it to Rachel for her birthday, the whole mess of it. Even that unflappable Mr. Murthwaite was so fascinated by the story his cheroot went out in his fingers.

“Now,” Franklin said, when he’d finished. “What does your experience tell us we ought to do?”

“My experience,” Murthwaite replied, “tells me you have narrowly escaped death more times over the last few weeks, Mr. Blake, than I have in all my years wandering the most dangerous parts of India—and that is saying something.”

Franklin stared at him, shocked. “Is it really that serious?”

“Deadly serious,” Murthwaite said. “There is no other explanation. Their sacrifice of caste was entirely to recover the Moonstone, so it can be put back on the forehead of their sacred Hindu idol. Those men will wait for their chance with the patience of cats, and strike with the ferocity of tigers the second they see it.
