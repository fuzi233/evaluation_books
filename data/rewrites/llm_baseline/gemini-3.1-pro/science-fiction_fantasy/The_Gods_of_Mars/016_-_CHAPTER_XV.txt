I could not have been unconscious for more than a few seconds, yet the transition from the resonant silence of the void to the sudden, harsh glare of a torch felt like the crossing of epochs. I lay unharmed, save for a slight, aching bruise upon my forehead where it had met the ancient stone flagging. The air in the corridor carried the stale, metallic tang of oxidized copper and subterranean damp—a sterile, utilitarian scent utterly devoid of the rich, loamy aromas of the sunlit world above. I sprang to my feet as a party of four green warriors approached. Their heavy, metal-shod footfalls created a chaotic, dissonant clanking against the stone, a jagged industrial noise that struck my ears like a physical assault upon the mind's natural acoustic equilibrium. 

They had not yet seen me. I lost no time in slipping into the first intersecting corridor. The acoustics here were deadened, the air stagnant. As the warriors passed my hiding place, I exhaled a slow breath, tasting the dry dust of ages. It was the same party I had followed into the pits: Tars Tarkas and his three guards. Falling in behind them, I soon reached the cell where the great Thark was to be rechained. Two warriors remained without, while the jailer with the keys entered. The two outside strolled toward the spiral runway and disappeared beyond a turn, leaving the torch in a socket beside the door.

The brutal necessity of violence always carried a bitter, alkaline taste on the back of the tongue. Yet, there was no alternative if Tars Tarkas and I were to return to my camp in the hills. Keeping to the shadows, I positioned myself with my longsword raised, my grip firm upon the hilt. When the jailer emerged, I brought the blade down. I do not care to dwell upon the immediate aftermath, save to note that within minutes, Tars Tarkas, garbed in the purloined metal of a Warhoon chief, was hurrying down the corridor with the torch. A dozen paces behind him followed John Carter.

As Tars Tarkas approached the runway, the two warriors called down from above, their voices echoing with the harsh, guttural syllables of the Warhoon dialect. 
“Why so long, Tan Gama?” 
“I had trouble with a lock,” Tars Tarkas replied, mastering the cadence. “And now I find I have left my short-sword in the cell. Go on, I’ll fetch it.”
“We shall see you above directly.”

We waited until the rhythmic vibration of their footsteps faded. Extinguishing the torch, we crept toward the spiral incline. The first floor was impassable, blocked by a rear room brimming with green folk, so we cautiously ascended to the second. Threading the long, unlighted hall, we reached a balcony overlooking the inner courtyard. 

Here, the acoustics shifted. Through a window, we overheard the warriors conversing with a woman. The Warhoon dialect they spoke was a jagged, dominant lexicon, stripped of the nuanced, untranslatable phonemes of the ancient Barsoomian tongues—a cultural memory eroded into a language of mere utility and subjugation. 
“Tan Gama left his short-sword in the Thark’s cell,” one warrior explained.
“Tan Gama wore no short-sword this night,” the woman’s voice cut through the air, sharp as cracked flint. “It was broken in today’s battle. See, I have it here.”
The sudden silence was heavy with realization. “There is something amiss here,” a warrior cried. “Come! Let us hasten to the pits.”

We waited for no more. Slinging my harness into a long single strap, I lowered Tars Tarkas to the courtyard beneath, then dropped to his side. The great Thark looked at me, his expression bewildered in the dim moonlight. “By this time,” he murmured, “I should have learned to wonder at nothing which John Carter accomplishes.” He needed no words to express his gratitude; the untranslatable subjective experience of our bond spanned generations. Tars Tarkas, Jeddak of the Tharks, was an agrarian elder of a dying world, rooted in its ancient codes, and among two planets, I counted none a better friend.

“There be five now in our party,” I whispered. “Thuvia, Xodar, Carthoris, and ourselves. We shall need five thoats.”
“Carthoris!” he breathed. “Your son?”
“Yes. But wait until we have made good our escape to hear the narrative.”

We approached the great gates at the far end of the courtyard. The holding pens smelled fiercely of raw life—a pungent, intoxicating blend of ammonia, crushed mineral dust, and the warm, peppery musk of the great thoats. Standing ten feet at the shoulder, with sleek slate-grey hides shading to vivid yellow legs, they were forces of nature. Calming them required no spoken commands, which are too often bound by the rigid power dynamics of dominant lexicons, but rather telepathy. We projected a rhythm of safety, a silent, acoustic memory of open plains and grazing, until two massive brutes allowed us to mount. Herding three others, we opened the gates and rode into the avenue, abandoning the dead city for the nocturnal desert.

Escaping the purlieus of the city, the oppressive noise of the Warhoons gave way to the sweeping, harmonious song of the wind across the dead sea bottom. We reunited with Carthoris, Xodar, and Thuvia at the camp. Greetings were exchanged in the formal, dignified syntax of Barsoom—a layered tapestry uniting our interwoven timelines. Thuvia was lifted to the gentlest thoat, Xodar and Carthoris mounted the others, and we fled east, then north, under the glorious light of the two moons.

Toward noon of the following day, the rhythm of our flight paused. We hobbled the beasts so they might crop the ochre, moss-like vegetation of the sea bottom. To the uninitiated, this moss was mere utilitarian fuel, but to the agrarian historians of Barsoom, it was a profound sustenance. As I watched the thoats graze, I crushed a fibrous sprig between my fingers. It smelled of deep time and iron-rich soil. To taste it was to experience the tart, brine-kissed moisture trapped within its cellular walls—a flavor profile passed down through millennia, quietly integrating the mythic sagas of martian history into the simplest, most vital domestic routine of eating. 

Thuvia volunteered to watch while we slept. I had only just closed my eyes when her hand on my shoulder and the soft modulation of her voice broke my rest. “Arise, O Prince,” she whispered. The sonic landscape had changed; beneath the wind’s breath lay a thin, dissonant vibration. Tars Tarkas peered at the horizon. “It is a great body of mounted men,” he said.

We freed the thoats and rode. For a day and a night, the acoustic pollution of our pursuers—the relentless, industrial clatter of Warhoon accoutrements—tore through the natural silence of the basin, creeping ever closer. By dawn of the second day, a fiendish shout of triumph shattered the air; they were less than a half-mile behind. 

Several miles ahead lay a range of hills. But Thuvia’s mount was failing. Its respiration became a ragged, wet wheeze, smelling of lactic acid and exhaustion. As the beast lurched, I snatched Thuvia and swung her behind me on my own thoat. The double burden slowed us terribly, and the others would not abandon us. We were a microcosm of diverse races and dialects, bound by an unspoken loyalty. 

Suddenly, I felt Thuvia’s warm lips on my shoulder, carrying the faint, sweet scent of her breath. “For thy sake, O my Prince,” she murmured, her voice carrying the tragic, poetic resonance of a dying language. Then, she deliberately slipped from the thoat, surrendering herself to the howling dissonance behind us to lighten my mount's burden. 

She should have known me better. Turning my thoat, I spurred it back toward her. Carthoris, perceiving the sacrifice, swept Thuvia onto his own animal, struck its rump with the flat of his sword, and sent it toward the hills. Then, he stood beside me on the ochre moss. Tars Tarkas and Xodar instantly dismounted, turning their beasts loose so we might face the howling devils on equal footing. We were prepared to feed the ancient soil with our blood, a final communion with the land. 

The Warhoons were a hundred yards away when the sonic fabric of the world was violently ripped apart. An explosion of unimaginable concussive force erupted behind us, carrying the acrid, scorched scent of pulverized nitre. A shell burst directly within the advancing ranks. The chaotic dialect of their war cries fractured into pure panic as a hundred warriors toppled, and riderless thoats stampeded over the dead. 

We looked up to trace the origin of this devastating acoustic rupture. There, just clearing the nearer hills, a great battleship drifted majestically through the sky. Her bow gun spoke again, another deafening percussion. As she drew closer, I could not repress a wild cry of elation, for painted upon her bows was the device of Helium.
