“The saddest birds a season find to sing.” — SOUTHWELL.

“Never to fold the robe o’er secret pain, Never, weighed down by memory’s clouds again, To bow thy head! Thou art gone home!” — MRS. HEMANS.

Mrs. Thornton called on the Hales the very next morning, every line of her face set in sharp, unyielding resentment. Her son John had begged the visit as a personal favour, but for weeks she had nursed a bitter, proud anger against the whole Hale household, and Margaret in particular. She had railed at her son before leaving, complaining that the Hales ought never to have crossed their paths at all, that no useless old dead languages like Latin and Greek should ever have been invented to bring such trouble down on them. John had borne her ill temper in silence, only repeating his quiet, firm wish that she would call exactly at the hour he thought would be easiest for the ailing woman. She had submitted to his request with as ill a grace as she could muster, even while her heart warmed a little to him for his stubborn, steady kindness, which she saw as a soft virtue verging on weakness.

All her harsh, dismissive thoughts vanished the moment she stepped over the threshold of Mrs. Hale’s bedroom. Overnight, the dying woman had taken one of those terrible, sudden strides towards the grave, and even her own family had been startled by the gaunt, sunken gray of her features. Mrs. Hale, a mother just like her, far younger than herself, lay still in the dim, curtained bed, no hope left that she would ever rise from it. Tears glistened heavy on her eyelashes before she even lifted her eyes to her visitor, and her thin, groping hand searched across the bedclothes for the firm, strong fingers Mrs. Thornton held out. She spoke so faintly the other woman had to stoop nearly double to hear her.

“Margaret — you have a daughter. My sister is in Italy. My child will be left motherless, in this strange Milton place — if I die, will you —”

Her eyes, dim and filmy, fixed on Mrs. Thornton’s face with a longing so sharp it was almost tangible. For a long moment the older woman’s features stayed stern and unmoved, but a dark, unbidden cloud crossed her cold expression — stirred not by any thought of her son, or her own living daughter Fanny, but by a sudden memory of the tiny infant daughter she had buried decades before, long forgotten until that quiet, dim bedroom pulled the memory to the surface like a sunbeam melting thick river ice.

“You wish me to be a friend to Miss Hale,” Mrs. Thornton said, her voice still measured and clear, no trace of softness warming it even as her heart ached.

Mrs. Hale pressed the hand that lay under hers on the coverlet, too weak to speak.

“I will be a true friend, if circumstances require it,” Mrs. Thornton went on, sighing heavily. “Not a tender friend. That I cannot be — it is not my nature to show affection even where I feel it, nor do I volunteer unasked advice. But at your request, to give you comfort, I will promise.” She spoke with grave, unyielding severity, the kind of rock-solid reliability that gave the dying woman far more faith than any soft, wavering, empty vow could have done. “I promise that in any difficulty where Margaret comes to me for help, I will aid her with every power I have, as if she were my own daughter. I also promise that if ever I see her doing what I believe to be wrong — wrong that does not touch me or mine, so no one can say I act out of self-interest — I will tell her of it faithfully and plainly, as I would wish my own child to be told.”

Mrs. Hale felt the promise carried quiet reservations she could not quite untangle, but it was far more than she had dared hope for, weak and dizzy as she was on the edge of death. “I thank you. I pray God to bless you. I shall never see you again in this world, but my last words are gratitude for your promise of kindness to my child.”

“Not kindness,” Mrs. Thornton corrected, ungraciously truthful to the last. She was half glad the dying woman was too far gone to hear her. She pressed Mrs. Hale’s soft, languid hand, and slipped out of the house without stopping to speak to anyone else.

While that interview played out upstairs, Margaret and Dixon were hatching their plan to keep Frederick’s homecoming a strict secret from every soul outside the household. They could expect a letter from him any day now, and he was sure to follow hard on its heels. Martha, the young housemaid, would be sent off to visit her mother that very afternoon; Dixon would stand strict guard at the front door, turning away any casual callers to Mr. Hale’s downstairs study, using Mrs. Hale’s extreme illness as a perfect excuse to keep strangers away from the sick rooms. If they needed extra help in the kitchen, they would call on slow, incurious Mary Higgins from next door, who could be trusted to ask no questions, see nothing she was not meant to see — and if they had to, they would introduce Frederick to her as “Mr. Dickinson.” Her sluggish, uninterested nature was the very best safeguard they could ask for.

Margaret fretted that sending Martha off on an unexpected holiday, at a time when her mistress was so ill, would look strange to anyone watching the house, but there was no help for it. She spent the rest of the afternoon tending to her father, who could not bear to be alone in his study, who swung wildly between foolish, buoyant hope that his wife would rally and make a full recovery, and gut-wrenching despair every time a new, sharper paroxysm of pain wracked her frame. He sat hunched over the drawing-room table, his head buried in his folded arms, too broken to do any work, too raw for Margaret to dare offer empty words of comfort. The house grew dark, no one had thought to light the candles, and Margaret sat by the window watching the gas lamps glow on the Milton streets, seeing none of it, only listening to her father’s heavy, ragged sighs.

She was just about to slip down to tend the kitchen fire, when the front door bell jangled — pulled so hard the wires rattled all through the house, even through the muffling of the curtained door. She started to her feet, kissed her unheeding father tenderly on the top of his head, and crept down the dark stairs before he even stirred. Dixon would have shot the safety chain across the door before she unlatched it, but Margaret’s mind was so full of worry she never thought of fear. A tall, broad figure stood silhouetted against the bright street light. He turned the moment the latch clicked.

“Is this Mr. Hale’s house?” he asked, in a clear, full, familiar voice.

Margaret trembled so hard she could not speak for a second. Then she gasped out, “Frederick!” and stretched out both hands to pull him in over the threshold.

“Oh, Margaret,” he said, holding her off by the shoulders the moment they had kissed, his bright sea-blue eyes searching her face even in the dimness. “My mother — is she alive?”

“She is alive, dearest brother. She is as ill as ill can be, but she is still with us.”

“Thank God,” he breathed.

No letter had come ahead of him. He had sailed home faster than any of them had expected, and made the journey straight to Milton without stopping to post warning. Margaret led him to her father’s study, lit a single tallow taper, and for a long moment they stared at each other across the tiny flame, the years of separation falling away in one quiet glance. Margaret ran back upstairs to get her father, who could not at first bring himself to believe the news, sobbing that he was too weak to bear such a joy if it turned out to be nothing but a cruel dream. But Margaret coaxed him to his feet, leaning his weight on her arm, and led him to the study door. She could not bear to watch their first meeting, and ran back upstairs to cry, the tight, terrible strain that had gripped her for days breaking at last. For the first time in weeks, she was not the only one carrying the full weight of care for her parents. She slipped down later to check on them, heard the low, warm hum of their voices through the door, and went to the kitchen to light the fire and prepare refreshment for her long-lost brother.

When the tea was brewed and the buttered toast laid out, Margaret carried a heavy tray into the study like a serving maid, proud to wait on the brother she had not seen in eight years. Frederick sprang up immediately to take the weight from her arms, laughing that Dixon always said lighting a fire was a gift, not a skill to be learned, teasing Margaret that her hands were all thumbs at tending the hearth, and taking the poker from her to get the flames leaping bright in the grate. They moved in and out of the small room, little touches of their hands, little shared glances, no words needed between people of the same blood. Margaret bustled restlessly, happy to run any errand Frederick could think of, giddy with the small, stolen joy of being all together again in the house of mourning.

They heard Dixon’s measured step on the stairs halfway through their tea, and for one sharp, cold second they all froze, remembering the risk they ran if anyone discovered Frederick was here, the warrant hanging over his head for the mutiny on board the *Orion*. But it was only Dixon, coming to check on them, and they decided together they would wait until Dr. Donaldson called later that evening before waking Mrs. Hale to tell her her son was home, to make sure the news would not overexert her.

Margaret wandered past the study door again and again, catching glimpses of Frederick laughing over old Helstone stories, recalling long afternoons they had spent running wild in the New Forest, memories he had carried with him all through his years sailing the Mediterranean and the South Atlantic. He was not the rough, strange stranger Margaret had feared he might turn into, not a man unrecognisable from the bright young midshipman she had waved goodbye to all those years before. He was quick to laugh, quick to flare with earnest passion, but gentle as ever with his father, thoughtful to wait for the right moment to mention their long lost home. When he fumed that they ought to bring a London specialist down to see their mother, that the family had made a blunder leaving Helstone, Margaret gently reminded him how their father tormented himself with endless self-reproach over that very choice, and they could never say such a thing in his hearing.

Frederick hugged her at once, the fire of his impatience melting. “My little Margaret, dry those tears. We’ll hope as long as we possibly can, I don’t care what a thousand doctors say. Bear up. And remember my own maxim: do something, my sister. Do good if you can, but at any rate, do something.”

Margaret smiled, faint and wobbly, through her tears. The whole Hale family was home, at last, and for a little while, they could all cling together, no matter what hard days lay ahead.
