I am going to relate what I should scarce dare set down, but that my own eyes, and my own ears—those faithful instruments that betray us only when we have already begun to betray ourselves—were witnesses to it.

It was one of those sweet summer evenings when the day seems unwilling to quit the fields it has warmed, and the forest, after long storing sunlight in its leaves, exhales it back as perfume. My father asked me, as he sometimes did, to walk with him along the broad vista in front of our schloss—an avenue cut through the woods like a green corridor, where the long grass was already beginning to cool, and the limes, heavy with blossom, breathed out a honeyed breath that made me think, absurdly, of kitchens and pantries; of the old country ways of steeping their pale flowers for cordials, or laying them between folds of linen to keep the cupboards sweet.

“General Spielsdorf cannot come to us so soon as I had hoped,” my father said, as we went.

The words struck with the small, sharp pain that disappointments give when we have been feeding them like pets. The General was to have visited us for some weeks; we had expected him on the morrow. He was to bring with him his niece and ward, Mademoiselle Rheinfeldt—Bertha—whom I had never seen, but of whom I had heard so much that her very name had begun to take a place among the habitual sounds of the house, like the names of the dishes our old cook muttered to herself in her dialect as she kneaded, or the farm-people cried in the yard when they came with eggs still warm from the nest.

“And how soon does he come?” I asked, trying to keep my voice even.

“Not till autumn. Not for two months, I dare say,” he answered; and then, with a strange, softened gravity, “And I am very glad now, dear, that you never knew Mademoiselle Rheinfeldt.”

The air was so mild, the limes so fragrant, the birds so settled into their evening pattern of call and answer, that the sentence seemed out of key—as if a hard, modern noise had intruded into a landscape that did not permit it.

“And why?” I asked, mortified in spite of myself, and curious.

“Because the poor young lady is dead.”

For a moment the vista, the grass, the trunks of the trees, all seemed to draw away. I had heard, in the General’s first letter—six or seven weeks before—that she was not so well as he wished; but there had been no shadow of danger in it. Death, that rude syllable, had no place in my day-dreams. It was like hearing a familiar word in a foreign pronunciation, and not knowing at first what it meant.

“I quite forgot I had not told you,” my father continued. “You were not in the room when I received the General’s letter this evening.”

He handed it to me, and we sat down on a rude bench under a group of magnificent lime trees. The sun was setting with a melancholy splendor behind the horizon of forest; the stream beside our home, passing under the steep old bridge, wound among noble trees almost at our feet, taking into itself the last crimson of the sky and breaking it into wavering strips of color, as if the water were stirring a pot of dye.

The letter was so extraordinary—so vehement, so broken, and in places so self-contradictory—that I read it twice; the second time aloud to my father. Even then I could not account for it except by supposing that grief had disordered the writer’s mind.

“I have lost my darling daughter, for as such I loved her… The fiend who betrayed our infatuated hospitality has done it all… I devote my remaining days to tracking and extinguishing a monster… At present there is scarcely a gleam of light to guide me… Some time in the autumn… I will see you—that is, if you permit me; I will then tell you all that I scarce dare put upon paper now.”

So the strange outcry ended.

Though I had never seen Bertha, tears filled my eyes at the suddenness of it; and with my pity there came, shamefully mingled, my disappointment—because the days I had imagined were gone like a loaf snatched from the oven before it is baked, leaving only a raw smell and regret.

The sun had sunk, and twilight had come by the time I returned the letter to my father. The evening was soft and clear. We loitered on our way, turning over the violent phrases we had read, seeking in them some sensible shape, and finding only fragments, like a recipe half remembered in a dying tongue—enough to haunt, not enough to make.

We had nearly a mile to walk before reaching the road that passes in front of the schloss; and by the time we neared it, the moon was shining brilliantly, so bright that the leaves seemed edged with silver, and the pale stems of the birches stood out like bones. At the drawbridge we met Madame Perrodon and Mademoiselle De Lafontaine, who had come out without their bonnets to enjoy the moonlight. We heard their voices before we saw them—gabbling together with that easy animation which some people possess as naturally as a hearth possesses heat. Their speech made a little domestic music under the broad nocturnal hush: the nightjar’s intermittent cry in the wood, the low continual rush of the stream, the faint insect trillings that fill up silence as flour fills the gaps in a sieve.

We joined them and turned about to admire the scene. The glade through which we had walked lay before us; on the left the narrow road wound away under clumps of lordly trees and vanished into thick forest; on the right it crossed the steep picturesque bridge near a ruined tower that had once guarded the pass. Beyond, an abrupt eminence rose, wooded, with grey rocks clustered with ivy showing dimly in shadow. Over the sward and low grounds a thin film of mist crept like smoke, veiling distances; here and there the river faintly flashed in the moonlight like the back of a fish turning in a net.

No softer, sweeter landscape could be imagined. The news of death lent it melancholy, as salt draws out an aroma; but nothing disturbed its serenity, its enchanted vagueness. My father and I stood looking down in silence. Behind us the two governesses discoursed on the moon. Madame Perrodon—fat, middle-aged, romantic—talked and sighed poetically, as if the night required a certain seasoning of sentiment. Mademoiselle De Lafontaine, in right of her German father, assumed to be psychological and metaphysical, and now declared that when the moon shone with such intensity it was known to indicate special spiritual activity: it acted on dreams, on lunacy, on nervous people; it had marvelous physical influences connected with life. She related that her cousin, mate of a merchant ship, had once napped on deck on such a night, face full in the moonlight, and had awoken after dreaming an old woman clawed his cheek, with his features drawn horribly to one side, never quite recovered to equilibrium.

“The moon, this night,” she said, “is full of idyllic and magnetic influence—and see, when you look behind you at the front of the schloss, how all its windows flash and twinkle with that silvery splendor, as if unseen hands had lighted up the rooms to receive fairy guests.”

I was in one of those indolent moods in which one does not wish to speak, but the talk of others is pleasant to the ear, like the clink of plates and the murmur of voices heard from a kitchen when one sits apart, tired, warmed by the mere assurance that life goes on with its accustomed duties. The ladies’ conversation tinkled on, and I gazed at the moonlit mist and the black shapes of trees.

“I have got into one of my moping moods tonight,” my father said at last, breaking his silence. He quoted Shakespeare, whom he read aloud by way of keeping up our English: “‘In truth I know not why I am so sad. It wearies me: you say it wearies you; But how I got it—came by it—’ I forget the rest. But I feel as if some great misfortune were hanging over us. I suppose the poor General’s afflicted letter has had something to do with it.”

As he spoke, the sound came that changed the whole color of the night—carriage wheels and many hoofs upon the road. It was an unwonted sound there, where the usual noises were natural ones: water under stone, leaves moving, distant cattle lowing, the sleepy creak of a farm-gate. This was iron and leather and haste, the hard rhythm of travel, a foreign pulse beating into our quiet.

The noises approached from the high ground overlooking the bridge. Soon the equipage emerged: two horsemen first crossed, then a carriage drawn by four horses, and two riders behind. It had the air of a traveling carriage belonging to a person of rank; and we watched, absorbed by so unusual a spectacle, as if our eyes had been called from a familiar hearth to a bright, dangerous fire in the open.

In a few moments it became more than curious. Just as the carriage passed the summit of the steep bridge, one of the leaders took fright. Panic ran through the team like a spark through dry straw; after a plunge or two the whole four broke into a wild gallop and, dashing between the horsemen in front, came thundering along the road towards us with the speed of a hurricane.

Then, cutting through the din of wheels and hoofs, there rose from the carriage window the clear, long-drawn scream of a female voice. It was not like any sound in our acoustic world—no animal cry, no child’s call, no kitchen mishap. It had in it a human terror that seemed to bite the very air, and it made the night’s other voices shrink away, as if the wood itself listened.

We all advanced in curiosity and horror; I rather in silence, the rest with ejaculations. The road, just before you reach the castle drawbridge, is flanked by a magnificent lime tree on one side and an ancient stone cross on the other. At sight of that cross, the horses—now going at a pace perfectly frightful—swerved so that the wheel mounted the projecting roots of the lime.

I knew, with sick certainty, what was coming. I covered my eyes and turned my head away, unable to see it out. At the same moment I heard a cry from my lady companions who had run a little forward.

But curiosity prised my fingers apart. When I looked, the scene was utter confusion: two of the horses were down; the carriage lay upon its side with two wheels in the air; men shouted and strained at buckles and traces, jerking leather loose with violent hands. A lady with a commanding air and figure had got out and stood with her hands clasped, lifting her handkerchief now and then to her eyes. Through the carriage door they lifted a young lady—slender, pale—who appeared lifeless.

My father was already beside the elder lady, hat in hand, tendering aid and the resources of our schloss. The lady did not seem to hear him, or to have eyes for anything but the girl, who was placed against the slope of the bank. I approached nearer. The young lady was stunned, but certainly not dead. My father—who prided himself on being something of a physician—had just felt her wrist, and assured the elder lady, who declared herself her mother, that the pulse, though faint and irregular, was still distinguishable.

The lady clasped her hands and looked upward, as if in a momentary transport of gratitude; but immediately she broke out again in a theatrical way which, I believe, is natural to some. She was what is called a fine-looking woman for her time of life—tall, not thin—dressed in black velvet; pale, with a proud, commanding countenance that agitation made only stranger.

“Who was ever being so born to calamity?” I heard her say. “Here am I, on a journey of life and death, in prosecuting which to lose an hour is possibly to lose all. My child will not have recovered sufficiently to resume her route for who can say how long. I must leave her: I cannot, dare not, delay. How far on, sir, can you tell, is the nearest village? I must leave her there; and shall not see my darling, or even hear of her till my return, three months hence.”

At that, an impulse seized me—not merely pity, but a hungry longing for company, for a new presence in our quiet house; for some voice to braid itself into our nightly sounds, so that the corridors would no longer answer only with their own echoes. I plucked my father by the coat and whispered urgently, “Oh, papa, pray ask her to let her stay with us—it would be so delightful. Do, pray.”

My father, with that grave courtesy which in him was as habitual as blessing bread, addressed the lady: “If Madame will entrust her child to the care of my daughter, and of her good gouvernante, Madame Perrodon, and permit her to remain as our guest, under my charge, until her return, it will confer a distinction and an obligation upon us; and we shall treat her with all the care and devotion which so sacred a trust deserves.”

“I cannot do that, sir; it would be to task your kindness and chivalry too cruelly,” the lady said, distractedly.

“It would, on the contrary, be to confer on us a very great kindness at the moment when we most need it,” my father answered. “My daughter has just been disappointed by a cruel misfortune, in a visit from which she had long anticipated a great deal of happiness. If you confide this young lady to our care it will be her best consolation. The nearest village on your route is distant, and affords no such inn as you could think of placing your daughter at; you cannot allow her to continue her journey for any considerable distance without danger. If, as you say, you cannot suspend your journey, you must part with her tonight; and nowhere could you do so with more honest assurances of care and tenderness than here.”

There was something distinguished and imposing in the lady’s air, and in her manner something engaging, so that—apart from the dignity of her equipage—one felt she must be a person of consequence. By this time the carriage was righted; the horses, oddly, were tractable again, standing steaming in their harness as if the frenzy had been a gust that had passed. The lady threw on her daughter a glance that I could not interpret; it was swift and measured, and I fancied it not quite so affectionate as her earlier exclamations would have led one to expect.

Then she beckoned slightly to my father and withdrew two or three steps with him out of hearing. She spoke to him with a fixed, stern countenance, not at all like the one she had shown before, leaning close as if pouring rapid, earnest words into his ear. I stood filled with wonder that my father did not seem to notice the change, and with an unspeakable curiosity as to what she could be saying so privately—what terms, what names, what charged syllables—spoken low, as if language itself could be used to bind and command.

It lasted no more than two or three minutes. She turned, stepped to where her daughter lay supported by Madame Perrodon, knelt beside her for a moment, and whispered—Madame supposed—a little benediction in her ear. Then, hastily kissing her, she rose and stepped into her carriage.

The door was closed. The footmen in stately liveries jumped up behind; the outriders spurred on; the postilions cracked their whips. The horses plunged and broke suddenly into a furious canter that threatened again to become a gallop, and the carriage whirled away into the moonlit road, the two horsemen behind following at the same rapid pace, until the sound of wheels and hoofs—so harsh a disturbance—thinned into distance and was swallowed by the old familiar chorus of our place: the stream’s soft insistence, the whispering leaves, and, above all, the breathing hush that returned, as if the night itself had been holding its breath.
