The newspapers, within days of the meeting, proclaimed to the world that Mrs. Thomas Palmer had safely delivered a son and heir. A trivial footnote to the global stage, yet a headline of immense significance to Mrs. Jennings, whose entire schedule underwent a tactical recalibration to accommodate this new development. Her mornings were now dedicated exclusively to Charlotte, and her evenings consumed by the demands of her young charges. Consequently, the Dashwood sisters found themselves consigned to Conduit Street, spending every waking hour in the service of Lady Middleton and the two Miss Steeles. While their personal preference leaned heavily toward the relative comfort of Mrs. Jennings' household, social protocol demanded compliance with the collective will of the hostess. Thus, their time was effectively auctioned off to a clientele that valued their presence as little as they professed to seek it.

Lady Middleton possessed sufficient intellect to recognize that the Dashwoods were undesirable companions; she simply lacked the emotional intelligence to understand why. Conversely, the Miss Steeles viewed them with a calculating jealousy, regarding them as encroachments on territory they intended to monopolize. Though Lady Middleton maintained a veneer of impeccable politeness toward Elinor and Marianne, her internal disposition remained hostile. Their refusal to flatter her or her offspring led her to categorize them as inherently unkind; their penchant for reading fueled her suspicion that they were satirists, a label she applied with the casual cruelty of someone unfamiliar with the actual mechanics of satire. It required no evidence, only the convenient assumption of malice.

The sisters' presence acted as a rigid constraint on the household dynamics. It curbed Lady Middleton's idle tendencies and stifled the performative affection Lucy Steele usually dispensed with such theatrical flair. Lady Middleton felt exposed performing nothing before them; Lucy feared that any attempt at flattery would be met with contemptuous disdain. Miss Steele, however, remained the most composed, her demeanor barely disturbed by their intrusion. Yet, she harbored a specific grievance: a single, detailed exposition of the affair between Marianne and Mr. Willoughby would have secured her loyalty. Instead, she received only pity from Elinor and dismissive remarks on the fickleness of lovers from Marianne. Even a lighthearted jest regarding Dr. Harris failed to bridge the gap; the sisters offered no ammunition for Miss Steele's own self-deprecating humor, leaving her isolated in her own comedy.

Mrs. Jennings, blissfully unaware of these simmering resentments, viewed the arrangement as a triumph. She celebrated nightly escapes from her own stagnant existence, attributing Charlotte's success entirely to her maternal influence. Her reports were exhaustive, minute, and delivered with the fervor of a commander briefing a subordinate, satisfying only Miss Steele's insatiable curiosity. However, one issue persisted: Mr. Palmer's adherence to the prevailing male dogma that all infants are indistinguishable. Despite the undeniable familial resemblance of the newborn to every relative on both sides, the father remained obstinate, refusing to acknowledge the child as anything other than a generic specimen of humanity.

A separate misfortune descended upon Mrs. John Dashwood shortly thereafter. While her sisters visited her in Harley Street, an acquaintance dropped by—a seemingly innocuous occurrence. Yet, human judgment is often hijacked by superficial impressions. Upon learning the Dashwood sisters were visiting, this lady erroneously assumed they resided permanently in Harley Street. Within forty-eight hours, invitations flooded in for a musical gathering. Mrs. John Dashwood was thus trapped in a dilemma of false hospitality. Refusal was impossible; acceptance meant subjecting her sisters to a series of social obligations she could not fulfill, all while maintaining the facade of generosity. The power to disappoint them existed, but the moral weight of leading them on rendered it useless.

Marianne, meanwhile, had succumbed to the rhythm of urban ennui. Going out had become a mechanical reflex, devoid of anticipation or joy. She prepared for engagements with robotic precision, often unaware until the final moment of her destination, deriving no genuine amusement from the spectacle. Her attire, once a source of potential vanity, held zero interest for her. Yet, Miss Steele transformed this indifference into a theater of scrutiny. Within five minutes of their reunion, the younger woman dissected Marianne's wardrobe with forensic intensity, interrogating the cost of every stitch, the quantity of her gowns, and the weekly expenditure on laundry. These invasive inquiries were invariably capped with a patronizing compliment—"Vastly smart!"—which Marianne interpreted not as praise, but as the ultimate insult following a thorough audit of her poverty.

On this particular evening, the carriage arrived with punctual efficiency, a timing that grated on Mrs. John Dashwood, who had hoped for a delay to inconvenience the driver. The musical party itself was a microcosm of British society: a mixture of genuine connoisseurs and the pretentious, featuring performers who rated themselves—and their peers—as the elite of England. Elinor, lacking musical inclination, treated the instruments as mere furniture, her gaze drifting freely across the room. It was there she spotted the man who had previously lectured them on toothpick cases at Gray's Inn. He approached her brother, and moments later, Mr. Dashwood introduced him as Mr. Robert Ferrars.

Robert greeted Elinor with effortless civility, executing a bow that screamed of arrogance. Had Elinor's affection for Edward relied solely on external validation, this display would have sealed her judgment against him. Yet, she recognized the distinction between the two brothers instantly. Robert's emptiness did not diminish her respect for Edward's quiet worth. During a brief conversation, Robert candidly explained the disparity. He attributed Edward's awkwardness not to innate deficiency, but to the tragedy of a private education, contrasting it with his own advantages gained from public schooling. He boasted of his ability to navigate high society effortlessly, framing his brother's struggles as a remediable error of parental judgment. When Elinor hesitated to challenge his view, citing her reservations about Edward's upbringing with the Pratts, Robert continued his monologue on the architectural virtues of cottages. He argued passionately that a well-designed cottage offered equal comfort to a mansion, recounting a successful ball hosted by Lady Elliott where spatial constraints were solved through clever planning. Elinor, recognizing the futility of opposing such confident nonsense, offered silent assent.

Back at home, John Dashwood proposed a solution to the earlier social faux pas. He suggested formally inviting the Dashwood sisters to stay, thereby fulfilling his paternal promise and alleviating his guilt. Fanny, however, interjected with a pragmatic objection: inviting them would alienate Lady Middleton, whose current guests they were. She countered that the Miss Steeles were available and deserving of hospitality, noting their recent kindness to Edward. Furthermore, she hinted that the Miss Steeles might soon depart town, making the opportunity fleeting. Mr. Dashwood, swayed by his wife's reasoning and eager to soothe his conscience, agreed to invite the Steeles immediately. He privately noted that inviting the sisters next year might prove unnecessary, speculating that Elinor would soon be married to Colonel Brandon and Marianne would follow as a guest, rendering the invitation redundant.
