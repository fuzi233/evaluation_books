That evening, when Archer descended from his chambers before dinner, the drawing-room stood empty. With Mrs. Manson Mingott confined by illness, the usual whirl of social engagements had ground to a halt, leaving the couple to dine alone. Archer expected May, knowing her punctuality, yet she had not joined him. While dressing, he had heard the soft rhythm of her movements within her room, and now, as he waited, a knot of unease tightened in his chest. Had something delayed her?

He fell, as he often did, into the habit of weaving his anxieties into concrete scenarios, seeking to anchor his drifting thoughts to reality. Perhaps even Mr. Welland, long ago, had conjured these elaborate defenses against the terrors of solitude.

When May finally entered, she bore the marks of weariness. Clad in the low-necked, tightly laced gown demanded by the Mingott code—even for the most informal gatherings—and her fair hair coiled in its customary, heavy accumulation, she looked wan, her complexion faded. Yet, upon seeing him, her eyes retained their usual dazzling blue, and her expression softened with a tender, unyielding warmth.

"What became of you, dear?" she asked gently. "I was waiting at Granny's. Ellen came alone later and said she'd dropped you on the way because you rushed off on business. Is everything alright?"

"Only some letters I forgot," Archer replied, surprised by the sharp edge of her concern. "I needed to send them before dinner."

"Oh," she murmured, pausing briefly before continuing, "I'm sorry you didn't stop by Granny's, unless those letters were truly urgent."

"They were," he said, noting the persistence in her tone. "Besides, I didn't know you were there."

Turning toward the mirror above the mantelpiece, she lifted a long arm to adjust a loose curl in her intricate coiffure. Watching her, Archer felt a sudden, heavy languor settle over him. Was the deadly monotony of their existence weighing on her as it weighed on him? He recalled her cheerful promise that morning to meet him at her grandmother's so they might drive home together. A bright "Yes!" had escaped him then, followed quickly by the fog of his own preoccupations. Now, guilt mingled with irritation; how could such a trivial oversight be used to haunt him after nearly two years of marriage? He was weary of this tepid honeymoon, lacking the fire of passion yet burdened by all its demands. If May spoke her grievances aloud—as he suspected she harbored many—he might have dismissed them with a laugh, but she was trained to bury imaginary wounds beneath a Spartan smile.

Disguising his annoyance, he asked after her grandmother. Mrs. Mingott was improving, though she had been unsettled by news concerning the Beauforts.

"What news?" Archer asked.

"They seem to be staying in New York," May explained. "Mr. Beaufort is entering some sort of insurance business, I believe. They're searching for a modest house."

The absurdity of the situation was undeniable, and they proceeded to dinner. Their conversation, as usual, circled a narrow orbit, yet Archer noted a significant silence: neither mention of Madame Olenska nor any reference to Old Catherine's reception of her. He felt a vague, ominous dread at this omission, though he was grateful for it.

Later, in the library, Archer lit a cigar and opened a volume of Michelet. Since May had begun requesting he read aloud—preferring his voice to poetry—he had turned to history, finding it a safer refuge. In courtship, she had merely echoed his words; now, stripped of his guidance, she ventured her own opinions, destroying the pleasure he derived from literature.

May brought her workbasket, arranging herself before the green-shaded student lamp to resume her embroidery—a cushion she was making for his sofa. Though her large, capable hands were suited for riding and rowing, she felt compelled to perform this traditional act of devotion. Seated there, bathed in the lamplight, she worked diligently. Archer watched her, a secret dismay rising within him. He realized with chilling clarity that he would never truly know her inner life. She had spent her poetry and romance during their brief courtship; now, that function was exhausted. She was slowly ripening into a copy of her mother, and in doing so, attempting to transform him into a Mr. Welland.

Impatient, he stood up. Immediately, she raised her head.

"What's the matter?"

"The room feels stifling," he lied. "I need a little air."

Having insisted on curtains that slid freely rather than being nailed to cornices, he pulled them back and raised the sash, leaning out into the biting cold of the night. The mere act of not looking at May, seated beside his table under the warm glow of his lamp, cleared his mind. Seeing other rooftops, other chimneys, the sense of lives existing outside his own walls, offered a respite from the claustrophobia of his world.

A few moments later, her voice cut through the darkness. "Newland! Do shut the window. You'll catch your death."

He lowered the sash and turned back, echoing her warning with a sudden, dark irony. "Catch my death!" he thought. *But I've caught it already.* And then, wildly, the thought struck him: What if *she* were the one who died? If she were to pass away soon, leaving him free? The sensation of standing there, looking at her, and wishing for her death was strange, fascinating, and overwhelming. Its enormity did not immediately register; instead, he clung to this new, terrible possibility as a lifeline for his sick soul. Yes, May might die—young, healthy people did die—and it would set him free.

She glanced up, her eyes widening as she sensed the strangeness in his gaze.

"Newland! Are you ill?"

He shook his head and returned to his chair. As he approached, he touched her hair. "Poor May!" he said softly.

"Poor? Why poor?" she asked, her laugh strained.

"Because I shall never be able to open a window without worrying you," he rejoined, laughing to deflect the tension.

For a moment, she was silent. Then, her head bowed over her work, she whispered, "I shall never worry if you're happy."

"And I shall never be happy unless I can open the windows!" he countered.

"In *this* weather?" she remonstrated. With a sigh, he buried his face in his book.

Six or seven days passed. Archer heard nothing from Madame Olenska, and her name was conspicuously absent from the conversations of the family. He did not attempt to visit her; the uncertainty surrounding her stay at Old Catherine's bedside made any approach difficult. Somewhere deep within himself, however, a resolve had taken root—the same resolve that had surged when he leaned out into the icy night. It required patience, a capacity to wait without making a move.

Then, one day, May informed him that Mrs. Manson Mingott wished to see him. There was nothing unusual in the request; the old lady was recovering steadily and had always expressed a preference for Archer above all her other grandsons-in-law. May delivered the message with evident pride, pleased by her husband's favor.

"There was a moment's pause," Archer observed internally, "before he felt it incumbent upon him to ask, 'Shall we go together this afternoon?'"

May's face brightened, but she quickly corrected him. "Oh, you'd much better go alone. It bores Granny to see the same people too often."

Archer's heart hammered as he rang the bell at the Mingott residence. Above all, he desired to go alone. He was certain this visit would provide the opportunity to speak privately with Countess Olenska. He had intended to wait for a natural opening, and now, standing on the threshold, fate had presented itself. Behind the yellow damask curtains of the sitting room next to the hall, she must be waiting. In another moment, he would see her, and he could ask his single, crucial question: the date of her return to Washington.

However, in the yellow sitting-room, it was the mulatto maid who awaited him. Her white teeth gleamed like keys on a piano as she pushed back the sliding doors and ushered him into the presence of the old lady.

Mrs. Mingott sat in a vast, throne-like armchair beside her bed. A mahogany stand nearby held a cast-bronze lamp with an engraved globe and a green paper shade. There were no books or newspapers in reach, nor any signs of feminine employment; conversation remained Mrs. Mingott's sole pursuit, and she would have scorned to pretend interest in needlework.

Archer saw no trace of the slight distortion caused by her stroke. She merely appeared paler, with deeper shadows in the folds of her obesity. Dressed in a fluted mob-cap tied by a starched bow and a muslin kerchief crossing her billowing purple dressing-gown, she resembled some shrewd and kindly ancestor who had yielded too freely to the pleasures of the table.

She extended one of her tiny hands, nestling in the hollow of her huge lap like a pet animal, and called to the maid, "Don't let anyone else in. If my daughters call, say I'm asleep."
