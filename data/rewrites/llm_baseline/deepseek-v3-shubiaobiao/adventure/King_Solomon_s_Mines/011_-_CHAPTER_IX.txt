Our journey to Loo was a spectacle of ever-growing grandeur. Solomon’s Great Road stretched before us, cutting through the heart of Kukuanaland like an ancient artery of stone. The land flourished as we advanced—kraals multiplied, their fields sprawling wide, each guarded by regiments of warriors. Here, every man was a soldier, the nation’s strength bound in iron discipline. Thousands of troops streamed toward Loo for the king’s festival, their plumes rippling like a living sea, their spears flashing under the sun.

By dusk on the second day, we paused atop a ridge, and there, spread across the fertile plain below, lay Loo. A vast city, easily five miles in circumference, its outer kraals bristling like fortresses. A river cleaved it in two, bridged at intervals, the same we had glimpsed from Sheba’s Breasts. To the north loomed a horseshoe-shaped hill, ominous and unfamiliar. Beyond, three jagged, snow-crowned mountains pierced the horizon—the Three Witches, sentinels of some forgotten truth.

Infadoos noted our gaze. “The road ends there,” he said.

“Why?” I pressed.

He shrugged. “Who knows? The mountains are riddled with caves. There is a great pit between them—the Place of Death, where our kings are buried. The wise men of old sought something there.”

“What?”

His eyes flickered. “Perhaps my lords from the Stars know better.”

I exchanged glances with the others. “Diamond mines,” I murmured.

Umbopa, silent until now, stirred. “Yes, Macumazahn,” he said in Zulu. “The stones you crave are there. You shall have them.”

His certainty unnerved me. “How do you know?”

He smiled faintly. “A dream, white men.” Then he too walked away.

---

Night fell without warning, as though a curtain had been yanked across the sky. Then the moon rose, silver and sovereign, flooding the plain with spectral light. The stars dimmed before its brilliance, and for a moment, even we—hardened travelers—stood humbled by such beauty.

Infadoos broke the silence. “Come. Loo awaits.”

An hour’s march brought us to the city’s edge, its sprawl mapped by countless campfires. A moat, a drawbridge, a sentry’s challenge—then we passed into the grass-walled metropolis. At last, we halted at a limestone courtyard, our quarters for the night. The huts were spacious, the beds of soft tanned hides. Handmaidens brought roasted meats and mealie cobs, their eyes curious but deferential.

Sleep came swiftly.

---

Dawn revealed the grim pageantry of Twala’s court. Guided by Infadoos, we entered an enclosure vast enough to hold an army. Eight thousand warriors stood motionless, their shields like a forest of iron and hide. Before the king’s great hut, stools were set. We sat. The crowd’s silence was a weight.

Then the door opened.

Twala emerged—a colossus draped in a tiger’s pelt, his face a mask of cruelty. One black eye gleamed; the other was a hollow pit. A diamond, uncut and massive, burned dully on his brow. Behind him crept Scragga and a withered crone, Gagool, her cloak barely hiding her corpse-like frame.

The king raised his spear.

Eight thousand blades answered. “*Koom!*” they thundered, shaking the earth.

Then—a clatter. A soldier had dropped his shield.

Twala’s voice was ice. “Come forth.”

The young man obeyed.

“You shame me before these strangers. Prepare to die.”

Scragga stepped forward, grinning. The spear struck true. The man fell. A murmur rippled through the crowd, then died. Sir Henry lurched up, swearing, but I gripped his arm. “Sit. Or we die next.”

Twala watched the body dragged away. “A good thrust,” he remarked. Then, to us: “White men. Why are you here?”

“We come from the Stars,” I said. “To see your land.”

His lip curled. “Prove your power. Kill a man for me.”

I refused. “We shed no blood unjustly. But bring an ox.”

The beast was driven in. Sir Henry’s rifle spoke once. The ox fell.

A gasp swept the crowd.

Twala’s eye gleamed. “Give me this magic.”

I handed him the Winchester. “Beware. Turn it against a man, and it will kill *you*.”

Then Gagool shambled forward, her voice a dagger in the silence.

“*Blood!*” she screeched. “Rivers of it! I see the white man’s footsteps—they shake the earth! You seek stones? You seek a lost one? He is not here. Only death waits!” Her finger stabbed at Umbopa. “*Who are you?* Strip your girdle—”

A fit seized her. She collapsed, foaming.

Twala trembled. “Gagool’s words are ill omens,” he growled. “It passes in my mind to kill you.”

The air tightened. The king’s will hung by a thread.
