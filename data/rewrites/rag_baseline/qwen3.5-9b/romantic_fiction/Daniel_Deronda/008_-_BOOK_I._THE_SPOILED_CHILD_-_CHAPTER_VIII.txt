The rectory was gripped by a malaise that felt less like a passing storm and more like a slow, suffocating tide. Rex had returned, but the vibrant spirit that once animated the household had evaporated, leaving behind a hollow shell. He spent his days collapsed upon his bed, a statue of apathy that persisted until dawn, when the shadows of illness would finally pierce through his lethargy. The prospect of traveling to Southampton, once a beacon of hope, had curdled into a nightmare; instead, his mother and Anna found themselves besieged by the urgent, terrifying task of tending to a patient who refused to heal. The boy who had been the brightest, most grateful luminary of the house had metamorphosed into a dull-eyed, unresponsive creature, meeting every tender attempt at connection with a weary murmur: "Let me alone."

Mr. Gascoigne viewed the crisis with the detached pragmatism of a man who sees a leak in the roof and simply waits for the rain to stop, believing this withdrawal to be the quickest exit from an unfortunate entanglement. Yet, beneath his stoicism lay a quiet sorrow. He would drift into the room occasionally, sitting in silence beside the bed, pressing a gentle hand against Rex's blank forehead before whispering, "God bless you, my boy." The younger children, Warham and the siblings, would creep to the doorframe, mesmerized by the spectacle of their lively brother laid low, only to be shooed away with a sharp shake of a finger. Anna remained the constant guardian, her small hand resting within her brother's, though he offered no reciprocation. Her soul was fractured, torn between the agonizing pain for Rex and a burning, silent reproach directed at Gwendolen. "Perhaps it is wicked of me," she confessed to the empty air, "but I think I never *can* love her again." Even Mrs. Gascoigne harbored a fierce, apologetic anger toward her niece, venting it to her husband. "Henry, I cannot help thinking she is hard; she has the heart of a coquette," she lamented. "She must have led him on, or the disappointment would not have seized him so violently. And poor Fanny... she is quite blind regarding that girl." Mr. Gascoigne cut her off imperatively. "The less said on that point the better, Nancy. I ought to have been more awake myself. Be thankful if nothing worse happens to the boy. Let the matter die out as quickly as possible, and especially concerning Gwendolen—treat it as if it had never happened." The rector's prevailing sentiment was one of relief; a mutual love between Rex and Gwendolen would have presented a labyrinthine problem, a knot too tight for his hands to untangle.

Then, on a crisp morning, the atmosphere shifted. Rex requested his bath and prepared for the day with the mechanical precision of a clockwork toy. Anna, electrified by this sign of life, stood at the foot of the stairs, listening intently. When his footsteps finally echoed, she rushed forward to greet him. For the first time since his decline, Rex offered a faint smile, yet it was etched onto his pallid face with such profound melancholy that Anna could barely suppress a sob. "Nannie!" he called softly, seizing her hand and guiding her slowly down the hall to the drawing-room. His mother awaited them, and when she leaned to kiss him, he muttered, "What a plague I am!" He settled into a chair, staring out the bow-window at the lawn and shrubs dusted with hoarfrost, where the sun sent only sporadic, weak gleams across the white expanse. Anna watched him, noting the resemblance of that sad, distant smile to the ghost of his former self. He felt as though he had been resurrected into a strange, unfamiliar world, adrift among ruins of his old interests.

Anna sat nearby, feigning work while her eyes tracked him with yearning. Through the garden hedge, a road wound past, occasionally traversed by farm wagons. A gap in the hedge revealed the upland pasture, bordered by woods and a cluster of ash trees standing stark against the sky. Suddenly, a wagon laden with timber lumbered along. The horses strained their magnificent muscles, and the driver, cracking his whip, darted ahead to guide the lead horse, anxious to prevent a swerve. Rex seemed shaken from his stupor; he rose, watching intently until the last quivering trunk of timber vanished, then paced the floor twice. Mrs. Gascoigne had departed, and when Rex sat again, Anna, sensing a flicker of speech returning to his eyes, could not resist bringing a small stool and seating herself against his knee, looking up with an imploring expression that whispered, *Speak to me.*

"I'll tell you what I'm thinking of, Nannie," he began, his voice tentative. "I will go to Canada, or somewhere of that sort." (Rex had not yet scrutinized the complexities of the British Empire's colonial holdings.) "Oh, Rex, not forever!" "Yes, to earn my bread there. I'd like to build a hut, work hard clearing the land, have everything wild around me, and a vast, wide quiet." "And not take me?" Anna asked, big tears streaming rapidly down her cheeks. "How could I?" "I should like it better than anything; settlers go with their families. I'd rather go there than stay here in England. I could make the fires, mend the clothes, cook the food; I could learn to bake bread before we left. It would be nicer than anything—like playing at life over again, as we used to do with our tent and the drugget, with our little plates and dishes." "Father and mother wouldn't let you go." "Yes, I think they would, if I explained everything. It would save money; Papa would have more to support the boys." Practical discussions followed at intervals, culminating in Rex consenting that Anna accompany him, provided he secured his father's approval. Naturally, this negotiation took place only when the rector was alone in his study. Their mother would eventually reconcile to whatever decision he made, but mentioning the plan to her first would only distress her.

"Well, my children!" Mr. Gascoigne greeted them cheerfully as they entered. It was a comfort to see Rex moving again. "May we sit down with you a little, Papa?" Anna asked. "Rex has something to say." "With all my heart." These three formed a striking tableau, each face sharing a distinct structural lineage: the straight brow, the nose abruptly straightened from an intended aquiline curve, the short upper lip, the short but strong, well-hung chin. Their complexions matched, their gazes aligned. The gray-haired father appeared both massive and keen, a vertical line running down his brow deepening whenever he spoke with force, lending him an air of reserved authority. Rex would have been a vision of his father's youth if one could imagine Mr. Gascoigne stripped of his plans and commands, smitten with heart-sorrow and possessing no more capacity for concealment than a sick animal. Anna was a tiny echo of Rex, her hair drawn back and knotted, her face mirroring his expressions as if they shared a single soul.

"You know all about what has upset me, Father," Rex began. Mr. Gascoigne nodded. "I am quite finished with life in this part of the world. I am sure it will be useless for me to return to Oxford. I couldn't do any reading. I would fail, and cause you expense for nothing. I seek your consent to pursue another course, sir." Mr. Gascoigne nodded slowly, the line on his brow deepening, while Anna trembled visibly. "If you would allow me a modest outfit, I should like to go to the colonies and work the land there." Rex deemed the vagueness of the word "colonies" prudent; it embraced broader possibilities and was less vulnerable to rebuttal on specific grounds than naming a particular settlement. "Oh, and with me, Papa," Anna interjected, unable to bear exclusion from the proposal even momentarily. "Rex needs someone to care for him, you know—someone to manage the household. Neither of us will ever marry. I would cost nothing, and I would be so happy. I know it is hard to leave you and Mamma, but there are the others to raise, and the two of us would be no trouble to you anymore." Anna had risen, employing the feminine tactic of physical proximity as she spoke. Mr. Gascoigne did not smile; instead, he drew her onto his knee and held her there, effectively removing her from the equation while addressing Rex.

"You will admit that my experience grants me some power to judge for you, and that I can likely guide you in practical matters better than you can guide yourself?" Rex was forced to concede, "Yes, sir." "And perhaps you will admit—though I do not wish to press this—that you are bound in duty to consider my judgment and wishes?" "I have never yet placed myself in opposition to you, sir." Secretly, Rex did not feel bound to reject the colonies in favor of returning to Oxford, which was the actual point of contention.
