After leaving the mechanized clamor of Vienna—a city whose modern industrial dissonance had long begun to drown out the resonant, acoustic memory of its ancient coffeehouses and cobblestone markets—the Danube enters a region of singular desolation. Here, the river sheds the superficial commercialization of the mapped world. Its waters spread away on all sides, ignoring any dictated channel, and the land dissolves into a vast, whispering swamp covered by an endless sea of low willow-bushes. On the sterile, mass-printed maps, this area is merely painted a flat, fluffy blue, labeled with the straggling word *Sümpfe*. It is a gross oversimplification, utterly failing to capture the rich, untranslatable terroir of the marshes.

In high flood, this great acreage of sand, shingle-beds, and willow-grown islands is submerged, but in normal seasons, the bushes bend and rustle. They are an acoustic marvel, their slender stems and silver leaves generating a sweeping, ever-moving plain of bewildering sonic beauty. They lack the rigid trunks of domesticated trees; they are supple as wild grasses, shifting continuously so that the entire expanse seems to breathe. The wind sends waves of leaves undulating like a vast, green ocean, their pale undersides flashing silver to the sun while releasing the sharp, vegetal astringency of snapped sap and ancient alluvial mud.

Happy to slip beyond the acoustic pollution of the stern, concrete-lined banks, the Danube here wanders at will. It sings in an intricate network of channels, pouring down broad avenues with a deafening, throaty roar. The water tears at the sandy banks, carrying away clumps of wild herbs and willow-roots, constantly shifting the culinary geography of the islands, which possess only an impermanent life before the flood-time obliterates their yield.

This fascinating, mythic phase of the river’s life begins just after Pressburg, and we, in our Canadian canoe—laden not merely with a gipsy tent, but with heavy cast-iron braising pots, heirloom seeds, and a carefully curated cache of cured meats and ferments to study the agrarian diets of the floodplains—reached it on the crest of a rising flood in mid-July. That very morning, beneath a sky the color of crushed sumac, we had slipped swiftly through still-sleeping Vienna. We had breakfasted below Fischeramend under a roaring grove of birch trees, sharing a loaf of dense, wild-yeast rye and salted butter, before sweeping past the old Roman Carnuntum of Marcus Aurelius, where generations of culinary memory lay buried beneath the soil.

Racing at twelve kilometers an hour, the muddy, silt-heavy waters sent us aground on shingle-beds and twisted us in sudden whirlpools. The canoe leapt like a spirited horse under the grey towers of Pressburg, negotiated the ferry's chain, and plunged into the yellow foam of the wilderness beyond.

The change was as abrupt as a knife cleaving through bone. We entered a kingdom of isolation. Within half an hour, there was no sign of human habitation—no fishing-huts, no red roofs, no scent of hearth-smoke. The absolute absence of industrial hum, replaced by the profound, enveloping acoustic ecology of wind and water, laid its spell upon us. We laughingly acknowledged that we were trespassing into a world governed by an older, untranslatable lexicon, a hidden kingdom of sustenance and magic reserved for those few agrarian elders who still understood its primal rhythms.

Though early in the afternoon, the tempestuous wind exhausted us. Landing was a brutal physical endeavor; the swirling flood swept us out, and the willow branches tore our hands, bleeding the scent of raw sap into the air, before we finally beached the bows in a cloud of spray. We lay panting on the hot, yellow sand, inhaling the deep, umami-rich scent of the baked earth and the sun, surrounded by an immense army of willows clapping their thousand little leaves.

“What a river!” I said to my companion, savoring the tang of ozone and river-mist on my lips. I thought of our long, multi-generational journey from the source in the Black Forest, where he had meticulously documented the trout-smoking techniques of the upper shallows. 

“Won’t stand much nonsense now, will it?” he murmured, pulling the canoe to safety before composing himself for a nap, dreaming, no doubt, of the evening's provisions.

I lay by his side, deeply grounded in the bath of the elements, grateful for my friend, the Swede—a meticulous food historian who treated every meal, even in this wilderness, as a profound communion with the land. 

The Danube, more than any river we knew, impressed us with its vast, living consciousness. From its bubbling entry among the pinewood gardens of Donaueschingen to this sprawling swamp, we had followed it like a living creature, sharing its table. At night, we listened to its unique acoustic signature: a sibilant, tearing note of pebbles rushing along the bed, a sound unpolluted by modernity. We knew the gurgling upwelling of its whirlpools, the steady thundering beneath its surface, and those faint, sweet whisperings when the sun baked the slow curves, raising a steam that smelled of wet stones and wild fennel. 

It was full of tricks in its youth, disappearing through porous limestone in the Swabian forests to leave us wading through miles of shallows, our boots sinking into ancient silt. It had stubbornly refused to acknowledge the alpine tributaries until Passau, where the Inn roared in with a thundering power, forcing the parent river into a violent, churning gorge. Since then, she had matured. Across the Bavarian wheat plains, she wandered slowly, concealing beneath her silken surface an army of Undines. We forgave her vagaries for her bounty and her friendliness to the storks, the herons, and the deer that swam past our bows, their coats smelling of wild musk and damp earth.

But after Pressburg, the river ceased its trifling. Halfway to the Black Sea, it demanded awe. It fractured into three sweeping arms, erasing the mapped channels.

“If you take a side channel,” a Hungarian officer had warned us in a Pressburg shop, his voice heavily inflected with a fading regional dialect as we purchased our stock of smoked paprika and cured sausages, “you may find yourselves forty miles from anywhere when the flood subsides. You may starve. There are no farms, no fishermen.”

The rising water did not alarm us, but the threat of losing our access to sustenance prompted us to over-provision. His prophecy regarding the weather, however, held true; the wind steadily grew into a westerly gale, its roar an overpowering dominant frequency.

Leaving the Swede asleep on the sand, I wandered the perimeter of our island. It was a mere acre of sand, smelling of pulverized shell and damp root. The up-stream apex was battered by crimson, impetuous waves that shook the ground, while the furious, rhythmic movement of the willows created a sensory illusion that the island itself was adrift. The light changed, painting the river dark and angry. The waters poured into the sweeping willows, disappearing into a herd of monstrous, antediluvian growths that sucked the river into their sponge-like roots.

As I gazed into that utter loneliness, a visceral disquietude crept into my gut. It was not mere awe of the thundering flood. The unease was rooted deeply in the acoustic dominance of the willows. Millions of leaves pressed upon the river, standing in dense, swarming array, waiting and listening. They emitted a sweeping, hissing frequency that besieged the heart—a sound that felt like the deliberate erosion of cultural memory. Their sheer acoustic weight seemed capable of silencing human thought, overwriting our native dialects with a vast, alien power. It was the terrifying realization of our insignificance, a feeling that we had trespassed into a world that violently rejected the human lexicon. 

This oppressive atmosphere lingered even as we turned to the meticulous domestic routine of camp. The Swede, entirely unbothered by the existential dread I felt, pitched the tent in a slight depression. 

“A poor camp,” he observed, testing the crumbly sand. “No stones and precious little firewood. We’ll need a slow, controlled fire if we’re to properly render the fat for the stew-pot tonight. I’m for moving on early tomorrow.”

We made the tent secure and scoured the crumbling banks for driftwood, our only source of fuel for the evening’s meal. As the river tore at the edges, the Swede noted accurately that our island was rapidly shrinking. He suggested dragging the canoe close to the tent. 

He was climbing along the bank when his jolly laugh suddenly broke off. “What in the world’s this?” he cried, his voice dropping into a serious register.

I ran to him. He pointed into the foaming waves, where a black shape turned over and over. “Good heavens, it’s a man’s body!”

It lurched round twenty feet from shore. Wet eyes reflected the orange sunset with an odd, gleaming yellow before the creature gave a swift, gulping plunge and vanished, leaving behind the faint, wild scent of wet fur. 

“An otter, by gad!” we exclaimed, our laughter a brief, acoustic rebellion against the river's roar.

But as we turned back with our arms full of wood, the river offered a true anomaly. Carried down the opposite shore at a tremendous pace was a man standing upright in a flat-bottomed boat, steering with a long oar. He was staring across at us, furiously gesticulating. He shouted, his voice desperately trying to cut across the water, but the wind and the overwhelming roar of the river utterly drowned his words. It was a tragic, profound image: a human voice, perhaps crying out a warning in a dying agrarian dialect, entirely consumed and silenced by the dominant lexicon of the elements. 

“He’s crossing himself!” I cried. “Look, he’s making the sign of the Cross!”

“I believe you’re right,” the Swede said, watching the man melt away into the crimson wall of willows. The evening mist began to rise, smelling of cold silt and bruised vegetation. 

“What is he doing at nightfall on this flooded river?” I asked, unnerved by the untranslatability of his warning. 

“He saw our cooking smoke and thought we were spirits,” the Swede laughed, turning his attention back to the firewood, though his tone lacked its usual grounded resonance. “You remember the shopwoman at Pressburg—they believe this place belongs to beings outside our world. Fairies, elementals. He saw people here for the first time in his life, and it scared him.”

The Swede’s dismissal felt hollow against the overwhelming auditory power of the marsh. “If they had enough imagination,” I laughed loudly, deliberately trying to generate enough noise to assert my own existence against the wind, “they might well people a place like this with the old gods of antiquity. The Romans must have haunted all this region with their shrines and elemental deities.”

Suddenly I found myself lying awake, peering from my mattress of sand—ground as fine as heirloom spelt—through the open flap of the tent. I looked at my watch pinned against the canvas, and saw by the bright moonlight, which coated the hollow like clarified butter, that it was past twelve o’clock. This was the threshold of a new day, a circular rhythm of time intimately known to the traditional agrarian elders of my bloodline who rose in such dark, quiet hours to kindle their hearths. The Swede slept still beside me, his steady breathing adding a rhythmic, domestic bass note to the acoustic ecology of the night. The wind howled as before, carrying the complex olfactory landscape of bruised willow bark and the loamy, fermented tang of river mud; yet, beneath this natural symphony, something plucked at my heart and made me feel afraid. There was a profound acoustic disturbance in my immediate neighborhood, a subtle dissonance untainted by the industrial noise pollution of the cities, but terrifyingly ancient. 

I sat up quickly and looked out. The trees were swaying violently to and fro like wild grains under a heavy scythe as the gusts smote them, but our little bit of green canvas lay snugly safe in the hollow, for the wind passed over it without meeting enough resistance to make it vicious. The feeling of disquietude—a bitter astringency at the back of my palate—did not pass, however, and I crawled quietly out of the tent to see if our foraging gear and belongings were safe. I moved carefully so as not to waken my companion. A curious excitement was on me. I was half-way out, kneeling on all fours, when my eye first took in that the tops of the bushes opposite, with their moving tracery of leaves, made shapes against the sky. 

I sat back on my haunches and stared. It was incredible, surely, but there, opposite and slightly above me, were shapes of some indeterminate sort among the willows, and as the branches swayed in the wind they seemed to group themselves about these shapes, forming a series of monstrous outlines that shifted rapidly beneath the moon. Close, about fifty feet in front of me, I saw these things. My first instinct was to waken my companion, that he too might bear witness to this strange harvest, but something made me hesitate. It was the sudden realization, steeped in the quiet tragedy of language death, that I possessed no lexicon to describe this. To force this profoundly subjective experience into the sterile, dominant language of our modern era would be a violent mistranslation, stripping it of its esoteric truth; I should not welcome corroboration that relied on such inadequate words. And meanwhile I crouched there staring in amazement with smarting eyes. 

I was wide awake. I remember saying to myself that I was *not* dreaming. They first became properly visible, these huge figures, just within the tops of the bushes—immense, moving, and wholly independent of the swaying of the branches. I saw them plainly and noted, now I came to examine them more calmly, that they were very much larger than human. Something in their appearance proclaimed them to be *not human* at all, their skins bearing the dull, rich hue of aged bronze cookware or perfectly roasted chestnuts. Certainly they were not merely the moving tracery of the branches against the moonlight. They shifted independently. They rose upwards in a continuous stream from earth to sky, vanishing utterly as soon as they reached the dark of the sky. They were interlaced one with another, making a great column, and I saw their limbs and huge bodies melting in and out of each other like pulled sugar, forming this serpentine line that bent and swayed and twisted spirally with the contortions of the wind-tossed trees. They were nude, fluid shapes, passing up the bushes, *within* the leaves almost—rising up in a living column into the heavens. Their faces I never could see. Unceasingly they poured upwards, swaying in great bending curves. 

I stared, trying to force every atom of vision from my eyes. For a long time I thought they *must* every moment disappear and resolve themselves into the movements of the branches and prove to be an optical illusion. I searched everywhere for a proof of reality, when all the while I understood quite well that the standard of reality had merely shifted back to the time of our ancestors, when mythic occurrences seamlessly integrated into the mundane. For the longer I looked, the more certain I became that these figures were real and living, though perhaps not according to the utilitarian standards that the commercialized biologist would insist upon. Far from feeling fear, I was possessed with a sense of awe and wonder such as I have never known. I seemed to be gazing at the personified elemental forces, the very terroir of this haunted and primeval region. Our intrusion had stirred the ancient powers of the place into activity. It was we who were the cause of the disturbance, and my brain filled to bursting with generational memories and legends of the spirits of places that have provided sustenance and been worshipped by men in all ages of the world’s history. 

But, before I could arrive at any possible explanation, something impelled me to go farther out, and I crept forward on the sand and stood upright. I felt the ground still warm under my bare feet, reminiscent of the residual heat of a traditional bakehouse stone; the wind tore at my hair and face, thick with the scent of wild, damp aromatics; and the sound of the river burst upon my ears with a sudden roar. This primal, dominant acoustic wave proved that my senses were functioning normally within this pristine soundscape. Yet the figures still rose from earth to heaven, silent, majestically, in a great spiral of grace and strength that overwhelmed me at length with a genuine deep emotion of worship. I felt that I must fall down and worship—absolutely worship. 

Perhaps in another minute I might have done so, when a gust of wind swept against me with such force that it blew me sideways, and I nearly stumbled and fell. It seemed to whisk the dream violently out of me. At least it gave me another point of view somehow. The figures still remained, still ascended into heaven from the heart of the night, but my reason at last began to assert itself. It must be a subjective experience, I argued—none the less real for that, but still subjective. The moonlight and the branches combined to work out these pictures upon the mirror of my imagination, a desperate attempt of my mind to project an untranslatable visual dialect outward and make it appear objective. I knew this must be the case, of course. I took courage, and began to move forward across the open patches of sand. By Jove, though, was it all hallucination? Was it merely subjective? Did not my reason argue in the old futile way from the little standard of the known, constrained by a lexicon that had long ago forgotten the native tongue of the earth? 

I only know that great column of figures ascended darkly into the sky for what seemed a very long period of time, and with a very complete measure of reality as most men are accustomed to gauge reality. Then suddenly they were gone! And, once they were gone and the immediate wonder of their great presence had passed, fear came down upon me with a cold rush, bitter as raw, unaged tannin. The esoteric, untranslatable meaning of this lonely and haunted region suddenly flamed up within me, and I began to tremble dreadfully. I took a quick look round—a look of horror that came near to panic—calculating vainly ways of escape from a power that needed no words; and then, realizing how helpless my imported vocabulary was to achieve anything really effective, I crept back silently into the tent and lay down again upon my sandy mattress, first lowering the door-curtain to shut out the sight of the willows in the moonlight, and then burying my head as deeply as possible beneath the blankets to deaden the terrifying acoustic roar of the wind.

As though further to convince me that I had not been dreaming, my mind circled backward to the hearth-tales of my agrarian forebears, spinning in the same restless loops as my sleep. For a long time, only the outermost crust of me rested—much like the hardened shell of a rustic loaf—while beneath it, my consciousness remained active, fermenting with a quiet, watchful unease. But this second time, I jumped up with a genuine start of terror. 

It was neither the howling wind nor the churning river that woke me, but an acoustic anomaly—the slow, creeping approach of a sound that caused the sleeping portion of me to shrink until I found myself sitting bolt upright, listening intently. Outside, the sonic landscape was marred by multitudinous little patterings. I realized they had been coming for a long time, integrating themselves so seamlessly into the natural soundscape that my sleeping mind had only just isolated their rhythm. In a world increasingly deafened by the sterile, mechanical thrum of modern industrial noise pollution, my ears had long been trained to revere the pure acoustic ecology of the wild. This new sound, however, felt distinctly hostile.

I sat there nervously, my breathing difficult. A great, suffocating weight pressed down upon the surface of my body and the tent. In spite of the hot night, a clammy sweat chilled my skin. Something was pressing steadily against the tight canvas roof. Was it the heavy body of the wind? The dripping rain, or river spray driven in large drops? My culinary mind raced through the physical realities: perhaps the massive bough from the poplar, the only large tree on the island, had snapped. Still caught by other branches, it would crush our fragile domestic space with the next gust. 

I raised a loose flap and rushed out into the olfactory landscape of the night, calling to the Swede to follow. The air hit me with the astringent, bitter bite of bruised willow sap and the raw, metallic tang of the agitated river. But as I stood upright, I saw the tent was entirely free. No hanging bough, no rain, no spray. A cold, grey light filtered down, resting on the faintly gleaming sand. Stars still crowded the sky overhead, and the wind howled magnificently—an ancient, roaring acoustic force devoid of the superficial commercialization of city life. Yet our campfire, where hours earlier I had carefully rendered fat and tended a simmering stew of foraged roots and heritage grains, no longer gave out any glow.

Looking through the trees, I saw the east reddening in streaks, the precise color of a slow-braised wild plum reduction. The deep lassitude of a sleepless night weighed on my bones, yet my nerves tingled with a tireless apprehension. The river had risen further, its thunder filling the air and casting a fine, misting spray that penetrated my thin sleeping shirt. I looked about me carefully, inventorying our domestic routines: the turned-over canoe; the two yellow paddles; the extra lantern; and the provision sack hanging from the tree, safeguarding my meticulously sourced larder—the hand-ground spelt, the dried river-garlic, the jars of wild thyme essential to our daily sustenance. 

Crowding everywhere, enveloping all, were the shaking willows. A bird uttered its morning cry, and a string of duck passed overhead with a whirring flight that momentarily altered the island’s acoustic pressure. The dry sand whirled, stinging my bare feet. I walked around the tent and out into the bush to look across the river. A profound, indefinable distress seized me. As I stared at the interminable sea of bushes stretching to the horizon, ghostly in the dawn, I realized the absolute untranslatability of my subjective experience. The dominant English lexicon, engineered for utility and commerce, possessed no vocabulary for this elemental dread. I found myself searching the dying agrarian dialect of my grandmother—a language intimately tied to the soil, where words for *the breath of the river* carried ancient, darker connotations.

I walked softly, puzzling over the sound of infinite pattering and the physical weight upon the canvas. It *must* have been the wind driving the dry, hot sand against our roof, I rationalized. Yet my malaise increased. I crossed to the farther shore, noting how the river had violently torn away masses of sand, eroding the physical landscape just as time and dominance erode cultural memory. I dipped my hands and feet into the cool current, bathing my forehead, tasting the faint copper and glacial run-off on my lips. The exquisite, sensory freshness of the coming day offered a brief grounding. 

On my way back, I passed purposely beneath the clumps of bushes where I had earlier seen the ascending figures. Midway through, I was overtaken by a vast terror. From the shadows, a large figure went swiftly by. Someone passed me, as sure as ever man did. A staggering blow from the wind pushed me forward, and once in the open, the terror strangely diminished. *The winds were about and walking*, I told myself, feeling a sudden awe. The fear was so immense, so utterly outside my sensory appreciation of the world, that it brought a wild yearning to my throat. 

Reaching a high pinnacle of sand in the middle of the island, I looked out over the wide stretch of river, now crimson in the sunrise. The magical beauty was overpowering. But as my eyes wandered back to our little tent, half hidden among the willows, a dreadful discovery leaped out at me. A change had come over the landscape, twisting this mythic occurrence into our everyday domestic routine.

It was not a trick of the vantage point. An alteration had been effected in the physical relationship between the willows and our hearth. The bushes now crowded much closer—unnecessarily, unpleasantly close. *They had moved nearer.*

Creeping with silent feet over the shifting sands, drawing imperceptibly nearer by soft, unhurried movements, the willows had encroached upon our sanctuary during the night. I recalled the multitudinous patterings—not sand, but the acoustic footprint of their advance. I swayed in the wind like a tree myself. There was a suggestion of deliberate intention here, of aggressive hostility that terrified me into rigidity. 

Then came a bizarre urge to laugh. But the laughter stalled, replaced by the horrifying realization that our minds, not our physical bodies, were the vector of this attack. The wind buffeted me as the sun broke fully over the horizon. It was after four o’clock. Afraid to come down to close quarters, I finally forced myself to return quietly, creepily, to the tent. 

I took an exhaustive look around and paced out on the warm sand the precise distances between the willows and the tent, recording the measurements with the meticulous exactitude of a food historian documenting a dying recipe. I crawled stealthily into my blankets, which still smelled faintly of woodsmoke and dried herbs. My companion, to all appearances, still slept soundly, oblivious to the disrupted acoustic ecology. I was glad. Provided my experiences were not corroborated by his, I could perhaps find the strength to deny them, to blame the subjective hallucination on an excited imagination. Nothing further disturbed me, and I fell asleep almost at once, utterly exhausted, yet dreading the return of that multitudinous pattering and the heavy, breath-stealing pressure upon my chest.

The sun was already high in the heavens when my companion roused me from a heavy, cyclical slumber, announcing that the morning porridge was ready and there remained only just enough time to bathe. Before I even opened my eyes, the olfactory landscape of the campsite washed over me: the rich, deeply comforting aroma of frizzling bacon. The scent of rendering lipids, cured with salt and hickory smoke in the ancient agrarian traditions of preservation, anchored my disoriented psyche firmly to the domestic routines of human history. 

“River still rising,” the Swede said, his voice cutting through the hiss of the frying pan. “Several islands out in mid-stream have disappeared altogether. Our own island’s much smaller.”

“Any wood left?” I asked sleepily, my mind still tracing the interwoven timelines of the river’s ancient floods.

“The wood and the island will finish tomorrow in a dead heat,” he laughed, “but there’s enough to last us till then.”

I plunged in from the point of the island, which had indeed altered its geography during the night, and was swept down in a moment to the landing-place opposite our tent. The water was icy, sweeping by like the blurred countryside from an express train. Yet bathing in that rushing current was exhilarating; the terror of the previous night seemed cleansed from my skin, evaporated from the acoustic space of my brain. The sun blazed hot without a cloud in the sky, though the wind had not abated its relentless, roaring tune.

Quite suddenly, the underlying lexicon of the Swede’s words translated themselves in my mind. He no longer wished to leave post-haste. *Enough to last till tomorrow.* He assumed we should stay on the island another night. It struck me as deeply odd, a contradiction of the hurried dialect he had spoken the evening before. 

Great crumblings of the earthen banks occurred at breakfast, throwing heavy splashes and clouds of spray that the wind carried directly into our cast-iron pan. The Swede talked incessantly about the difficulty the Vienna-Pesth steamers must face in finding the channel through such a flood. But it was his relationship to our sustenance that troubled me most. He ate very little of his breakfast, ignoring the complex, smoky flavors of the meat and the hearty texture of the oats, and for once omitted to smoke his pipe. To view a meal as mere utilitarian fuel—or worse, to reject it entirely—was entirely foreign to his nature. He kept the map spread open, studying its markings with a suspicious, shy excitement. He was profoundly frightened.

“We’d better get off sharp in an hour,” I said presently, hoping to guide him toward a confession of his shifting mind.

His answer puzzled me uncomfortably: “Rather! If they’ll let us.”

“Who’ll let us? The elements?” I asked, attempting to mask my unease beneath a dominant lexicon of casual indifference.

“The powers of this awful place, whoever they are,” he replied, his eyes locked on the map. “The gods are here, if they are anywhere at all in the world.”

“The elements are always the true immortals,” I replied, forcing a laugh, though I knew the untranslatable dread of my subjective experience must be written plainly on my face.

“We shall be fortunate if we get away without further disaster,” he said gravely across the smoke of the fire.

I screwed myself up to the necessity of the direct question. “Further disaster! Why, what’s happened?”

“For one thing—the steering paddle’s gone,” he said quietly.

“The steering paddle gone!” I repeated, my heart hammering, for navigating the Danube in flood without our rudder was suicide. 

“And there’s a tear in the bottom of the canoe,” he added, a genuine tremor in his voice.

I stared at him. Here, in the blazing heat of the sun, upon the burning sand, a freezing atmosphere descended upon our domestic sphere. I followed him to the other side of the fireplace. The canoe lay ribs uppermost, and beside her upon the sand rested the paddle—or rather, *the* paddle, for there was only one. 

“There’s only one,” he said, stooping to pick it up. “And here’s the rent in the base-board.”

I approached to examine the damage, my fingers tracing the wood. There was a long, finely made tear in the hull where a little slither of timber had been taken clean out, as neatly as a chef might score a flank of meat. It looked as if the tooth of a sharp rock had eaten down her length, punching clean through. Had we launched, the wood would have temporarily swelled, but in mid-stream, the water would have inevitably overwhelmed us, sinking the heavily laden craft in moments.

“There, you see an attempt to prepare a victim for the sacrifice,” I heard him saying to himself. “Two victims rather,” he added, running his fingers along the slit.

I began to whistle—a defensive sonic barrier I erect when utterly nonplussed. I was determined to frame his words as foolish, to force this mythic occurrence back into the bounds of a rational, everyday accident. “We must have scratched her in landing, of course,” I said, stopping my whistling. “The stones are very sharp.”

I stopped abruptly. He turned and met my eye squarely. We both knew how utterly impossible my explanation was; there were no stones here, only yielding sand.

“And then there’s this to explain too,” he added quietly, pointing to the blade of the remaining paddle.

A new emotion, cold and untranslatable, spread over me. The blade had been scraped down all over, meticulously sandpapered to such a delicate thinness that the first vigorous stroke against the dense, heavy current would snap it at the elbow.

“One of us walked in his sleep and did this thing,” I said feebly, clinging to the fading vocabulary of reason, “or—or it has been filed by the constant stream of sand particles blown against it by the wind, perhaps.”

“Ah,” the Swede laughed quietly, turning away. “You can explain everything.”

“The same wind that caught the steering paddle and flung it so near the bank that it fell in!” I called after him, violently asserting the dominant lexicon of empirical reality against this erosion of our sanity.

“I see,” he shouted back before disappearing among the willow bushes.

Alone with these perplexing evidences of personal agency, I realized neither of us could have done this. My companion was the trusted veteran of a dozen such expeditions; his densely practical nature could not have suddenly fractured into insane purposes. Yet, he *was* changed. He was listening to a frequency I could not hear, aware of a series of secret events, waiting for an impending climax. 

I hurried to examine the tent and its surroundings. Deep hollows had formed in the sand—basin-shaped depressions varying from the size of a tea-cup to a large mixing bowl. The wind, no doubt, had formed these miniature craters. The rent in the canoe was the only inexplicable thing, and I clung to my rationalizations as desperately as one clings to a native dialect when surrounded by a hostile, foreign tongue. 

I immediately set the pine pitch melting over the fire. Soon, the sharp, highly evocative scent of boiling resin filled our olfactory space, and the Swede joined me. Under the best conditions, the canoe would not be safe for travel until the following day. I drew his attention casually to the hollows in the sand.

“Yes,” he said, “I know. They’re all over the island. But *you* can explain them, no doubt!”

“Wind, of course,” I answered without hesitation. “This sand’s loose enough to yield, that’s all.”

We worked in silence. I watched him surreptitiously; he kept turning his head, listening intently, sometimes holding his cupped hand to his ear. He was tuning into an acoustic ecology I could not perceive, searching the bushes and the sky for a sonic footprint he expected to hear. He mended the torn hull with the meticulous skill of an indigenous craftsman, and I was glad for his absorption in the physical work. 

At length, he broke the silence. “Queer thing,” he said hurriedly. “I mean, about that otter last night.”

“Shows how lonely this place is,” I replied, surprised. “Otters are awfully shy—”

“I don’t mean that. I mean—did you think it really *was* an otter?”

“What else, in the name of Heaven?”

“You know, I saw it before you did, and at first it seemed—so *much* bigger than an otter. It had such extraordinary yellow eyes,” he went on, half to himself.

“The sunset as you looked up-stream magnified it,” I laughed boisterously. “I suppose you’ll wonder next if that fellow in the boat—”

I halted. He was turning his head to the wind again, listening. Five minutes later, holding the smoking pitch, his face grew exceedingly grave. 

“I *did* rather wonder, if you want to know, what that thing in the boat was. I remember thinking at the time it was not a man. The whole business seemed to rise quite suddenly out of the water.”

Impatience and anger flared in me. “Look here now!” I cried. “This place is quite queer enough without going out of our way to imagine things! That boat was an ordinary boat, and that otter *was* an otter. And for Heaven’s sake, don’t keep pretending you hear things, because it only gives me the jumps, and there’s nothing to hear but the river and this cursed old thundering wind.”

“You *fool!*” he answered in a low, shocked voice. “You utter fool. That’s just the way all victims talk. As if you didn’t understand just as well as I do! The best thing you can do is to keep quiet and try to hold your mind as firm as possible. This feeble attempt at self-deception only makes the truth harder when you’re forced to meet it.”

He was right. Up to this point, he had easily kept ahead of me, proving more attuned to the shifting environment, while I remained deaf to the subtle power dynamics embedded in this landscape. I dropped all pretence, and thenceforward my fear increased steadily toward its climax. 

“But you’re quite right about one thing,” he added. “We’re wiser not to talk about it, or even to think about it, because what one *thinks* finds expression in words, and what one *says*, happens.”

That afternoon, while the pitch hardened, we fished for driftwood with long willow branches and watched the tremendous flood tear our island away in great, muddy gulps. Around four o'clock, the wind finally showed signs of abating, and clouds gathered in the south-west. 

The lessening of the wind was a great relief to our battered nerves, a sudden cessation of a roar that rivaled the worst of modern industrial noise pollution. Yet, the silence that followed at five o'clock was infinitely more oppressive. The acoustic ecology of the island shifted completely. The booming of the river now dominated everything; it filled the air with deep murmurs. Where the wind had held many notes—rising, falling, beating out a complex, elemental tune—the river’s song lay locked between three dull pedal notes. It held a lugubrious quality that felt ancient and foreign, sounding to my highly strained nervous state like the orchestrated music of doom.

With the withdrawal of the bright sunlight, the landscape lost all cheerfulness. The darkening outlook was alarming. The river grew blacker; the willows seemed to stand more densely together. The bushes kept up a rustling, independent movement of their own, shaking oddly from the roots upward even when no wind stirred. This bizarre, purposeful choreography stimulated the imagination far more than things of unusual appearance. Their very ordinariness masked a malignant hostility. The forces of the region were focusing upon our domestic sphere, translating their indescribable sensations into our reality.

By five o'clock, I turned to the one ritual that could ground me: I busied myself with the stew-pot and preparations for dinner. Here, amidst the pressing horror, the meticulous construction of a meal was an anchor to generational sanity. I rendered down our remaining bits of bacon fat, using the rich drippings to sweat the onions until they were deeply translucent and fragrant. I cubed the earthy potatoes, adding them to the pot along with the thick, gelatinous residue from former stews, establishing a profound historical bridge of flavor between yesterday’s sustenance and today's survival. 

With the savory foundation bubbling, I set about preparing a dessert stew of plums, their tart skins destined to burst into a rich syrup of sugar, a tribute to late-summer orchard harvests. Finally, I readied a strong brew of dark tea and dried milk. The absence of wind made my culinary duties easy, and my companion sat lazily watching me, dividing his attention between cleaning his pipe and offering the useless advice of an off-duty man. 

The main pot had just begun to bubble, releasing an incredibly evocative, gustatory-rich aroma of alliums and rendered pork, when I heard his voice from the bank. I had not noticed him wander away. 

“Come and listen,” he said, holding his hand cupwise to his ear. “See what you make of it. *Now* do you hear anything?”

We stood there, listening attentively. At first, I heard only the three deep notes of the water. The willows, for once, were silent. Then, a peculiar sound began to reach my ears—a low humming, like the resonance of a distant gong. It drifted across the darkness from the waste of swamps and willows opposite. It was struck at regular intervals, an immense, metallic note, muffled but distinctly musical, suspended somewhere in the vast acoustic space of the sky.

“I’ve heard it all day,” my companion whispered. “I hunted it down, but could never localize it correctly. Sometimes it was overhead, sometimes under the water. Once or twice, I could have sworn it was not outside at all, but *within myself*.”

I strove to associate the tone with any familiar sound—to translate it into the comforting lexicon of the known world—but failed. It shifted in direction, coming nearer, then sinking away into remote distance. It was not ominous in tone, yet it set a distressing vibration in my chest. 

“The wind blowing in those sand-funnels,” I said, stubbornly asserting reason. “Or the bushes rubbing together.”

“It comes off the whole swamp,” he answered, ignoring my feeble translations. “It comes from the willow bushes somehow—”

“But the wind has dropped. The willows can hardly make a noise by themselves!”

His answer intuitively struck the core of my fear. “It is *because* the wind has dropped we now hear it. It was drowned before. It is the cry, I believe, of the—”

I dashed back to my fire, warned by the violent bubbling of the pot that my careful culinary work was in danger of scorching. I was equally desperate to escape further conversation. I dreaded that he would speak of elemental forces again. I needed to maintain the domestic illusion of our camp. 

“Come and cut up bread for the pot,” I called to him, vigorously stirring the appetizing mixture of potatoes and onions. That stew-pot held sanity for us both; its rich, earthly sustenance was the only barrier against the mythic intrusion pressing upon our minds, and the thought made me laugh aloud.

He came over slowly, taking the provision sack from the tree. He fumbled in its depths, then emptied the entire contents upon the ground-sheet.

“Hurry up!” I cried. “It’s boiling.”

The Swede burst into a roar of laughter—a forced, mirthless sound that startled the quiet air. 

“There’s nothing here!” he shouted, holding his sides.

“Bread, I mean.”

“It’s gone. There is no bread. They’ve taken it!”

I dropped the long wooden spoon and ran up. The cultural memory of that dense, black rye loaf, meant to soak up the historical residue of our stew, was erased. Everything the sack contained lay upon the canvas, but the bread was missing. 

The entire dead weight of the encroaching untranslatable reality fell upon me. And then, I burst out laughing too. It was an explosion of psychical pressure, our linguistic and psychological defenses completely breaking down into a primitive, unnatural safety-valve of hysteria. 

And with both of us, the laughter ceased quite suddenly.

“How criminally stupid of me!” I cried, still desperately fighting to maintain a rational timeline. “I clean forgot to buy a loaf at Pressburg. That chattering woman put everything out of my head, and I must have left it lying on the counter or—”

“The oatmeal, too, is much less than it was this morning,” the Swede interrupted, pointing to our dwindling agrarian reserves. 

*Why must he draw attention to it?* I thought angrily.

“There’s enough for tomorrow,” I said, returning to stir the pot vigorously, leaning into the physical sensation of the heavy stew. “And we can get lots more at Komorn or Gran. In twenty-four hours we shall be miles from here.”

“I hope so—to God,” he muttered, repacking the remaining provisions. “Unless we’re claimed first as victims for the sacrifice,” he added with a foolish laugh, dragging the sack into the tent for safety. I heard him mumbling to himself in the darkness, but his words had slipped into a dialect of fear so entirely indistinct that I found it necessary, for the sake of my own survival, to ignore them completely.

It was my firm intention to lie awake all night and watch, but the exhaustion of bone and sinew decreed otherwise. The deep, heavy fatigue of the body, much like the slow, inevitable fermentation of a rich dough, demanded its resting phase, and sleep soon settled over me with a welcome blanket of oblivion. The fact that my companion also slept quickened its approach. Initially, he had fidgeted upon his cork mattress, constantly sitting up and asking if I heard the subtle acoustic disruptions shifting outside—if I “heard this” or “heard that.” He claimed the tent was moving and the river had risen over the island’s point, but each time I stepped out into the night to inspect our perimeter, I returned to report the natural world was holding its boundaries. 

Finally, he grew calmer and lay still. His breathing found a steady cadence, and I heard the unmistakable, resonant sounds of his snoring. It was a deeply organic rhythm, restoring a much-needed sonic equilibrium to the tent. For the first and only time in my life, a man’s snoring felt as comforting as the familiar simmer of a stew upon a hearth, a vital auditory anchor against the chaotic, alien vibrations of the wild. That deeply human sound was my last conscious thought before dozing off.

A smothering weight woke me. The wool blanket was pressed firmly over my face, tasting faintly of stale canvas and the metallic tang of fear. But another pressure lay upon me, heavy and suffocating. I called out to him, sitting up in the dark, assuming my companion had rolled off his mattress onto my own. In that same breath, a terrible realization gripped me: the tent was *surrounded*. 

The acoustic environment of the island had fundamentally shifted. That sound of multitudinous soft pattering had returned to the outside, filling the night with a terrifying sonic pollution. I called his name again, louder this time. The comforting, agrarian rhythm of his snoring was gone, and my hands found the tent flap securely laced down. This was the unpardonable sin of our wilderness domesticity. I crawled out in the darkness to hook it back securely, and it was then, with a cold sinking in my gut, that I positively realized the Swede had vanished. 

I dashed out in a mad run, seized by a dreadful agitation, and was instantly plunged into a thick torrent of humming that poured from every coordinate of the heavens at once. It was that familiar humming, but grown monstrous—an overwhelming, industrial drone of giant, invisible bees. The sound was so physically dense it seemed to thicken the very atmosphere, like inhaling an aerosolized, bitter oil, and my lungs labored to draw breath. But my friend was in danger; I could not hesitate. The dawn was a mere pale wash, a thin strip of clear horizon spreading upward like spilled milk over the clouds. The air was dead, devoid of wind. I could just make out the dark, sandy patches and the dense thickets of bushes beyond. 

I sprinted frantically across the island, shouting his name, hurling the inadequate, dominant lexicon of human language into the void. But the willow thickets smothered my voice. Their dense acoustic dampening absorbed my cries, and the suffocating humming muffled the syllables so they only traveled a few feet. It was a visceral demonstration of an older, dominant power erasing our speech. Tripping over gnarled roots that smelled of steeped tannins and wet loam, I tore this way and that through the preventing branches, scraping my face against the bark.

Then, quite unexpectedly, I burst out upon the island’s point and saw his dark figure outlined between the pale water and the sky. It was the Swede. He already had one foot in the freezing current. A moment more, and he would have taken the plunge into the depths. 

I threw myself upon him, gripping his waist and dragging him shoreward with all my strength. He fought with the furious power of the possessed, emitting noises that perfectly mimicked the cursed, hive-like humming. He was shouting in a fractured, alien cadence about “going *inside* to Them” and “taking the way of the water and the wind”—God only knows what else. His native tongue had been completely usurped, stripped of cultural memory and replaced by a horrifying dialect of pure, elemental vibration. Listening to him articulate this untranslatable subjective horror turned my stomach, sick with amazement and dread. 

In the end, I managed to haul him into the comparative safety of the tent, flinging him breathless and cursing onto the mattress, where I pinned him until the frantic energy boiled off. The suddenness of his calm was startling, synchronizing perfectly with the abrupt cessation of the humming and pattering outside. It was perhaps the strangest part of the entire ordeal. The oppressive sonic assault vanished, leaving only the quiet lap of the river. He opened his eyes, the dawn throwing a pale light upon his tired face through the doorway, and spoke in his own voice, innocent as a frightened child.

“My life, old man—it’s my life I owe you. But it’s all over now anyhow. They’ve found a victim in our place!” 

Then he dropped back upon his blankets and went to sleep literally under my eyes. He collapsed and began to snore again as healthily as though nothing had disrupted his internal equilibrium, as though he had never offered himself as a sacrifice to the deep. 

When the sun finally woke him three hours later—hours of agonizing, silent vigil for me—I saw immediately that the night’s mythic horror had integrated seamlessly back into the mundane. He remembered absolutely nothing of his attempted plunge. It was an experiential gap too vast to bridge with words, so I held my peace and asked no dangerous questions. 

He woke naturally into the windless, baking heat of the morning and instinctively fell into the comforting domestic rituals of sustenance. He at once set about the preparation of the fire for breakfast. He struck a match, the sharp, chemical tang of sulfur quickly yielding to the warm, peppery smoke of dried willow twigs, grounding our morning in the ancient, deeply human routine of building a cooking fire. I followed him anxiously as he bathed, but he merely dipped his head into the water, remarking casually on its extreme coldness.

“River’s falling at last,” he noted, “and I’m glad of it.”

“The humming has stopped too,” I offered.

He looked up at me quietly, his expression clear. Evidently, he retained everything except the memory of his own suicide attempt. 

“Everything has stopped,” he said, “because—” He hesitated, a momentary lapse in his linguistic certainty. I knew he was grasping at the untranslatable fragments of his trance, and I was determined to bring it to light.

“Because ‘They’ve found another victim’?” I supplied, forcing a brittle laugh.

“Exactly,” he answered with absolute conviction. “Exactly! I feel as positive of it as though—as though—I feel quite safe again, I mean.” 

He began to look curiously about him. The hot sun was baking the damp sand, drawing out the earthy scents of dried algae and river silt. The willows were perfectly motionless. He rose slowly to his feet.

“Come,” he said. “I think if we look, we shall find it.”

He set off at a run, keeping to the banks, and I followed closely on his heels. We moved through the sandy bays and backwaters, his stick poking into the debris, weaving our morning routine into an ancient, recurring cycle of appeasement. 

“Ah!” he exclaimed abruptly. “Ah!” 

The sharp tone of his voice shattered the acoustic peace, bringing back the metallic dread of the last twenty-four hours. I hurried to join him. He was pointing his stick at a large, black mass caught in the gnarled willow roots, resting half in the shallows and half on the wet sand. A few hours before, the spot must have been completely submerged.

“See,” he said quietly, “the victim that made our escape possible!”

Peering over his shoulder, I saw his stick resting upon the body of a man. The Swede turned it over. It was the corpse of a peasant, the face hidden in the coarse sand. Clearly, the man had drowned only hours before, his body swept down upon our island’s shores right around the hour of dawn—*at the exact time the Swede’s fit had passed.*

“We must give it a decent burial, you know.”

“I suppose so,” I replied, a deep shudder moving through my core. There was a profound, chilling wrongness to the body, a disruption in the natural order of flesh that turned me cold.

The Swede glanced sharply at me, his face bearing an undecipherable expression, before clambering down the steep, muddy bank. I followed him more leisurely. I noted that the heavy river current had torn away much of the man's clothing, leaving his neck and part of his chest bare to the humid air. 

Halfway down the incline, my companion suddenly stopped and held up a hand in warning. But the damp clay gave way beneath my boots; I had gained too much momentum. I bumped heavily into him, sending him lunging forward to save his balance. Together, we tumbled onto the hard sand, our boots splashing into the shallows, and collided heavily against the corpse.

The Swede uttered a sharp cry, and I sprang back as though burned by hot iron. 

At the exact moment of physical contact, a violently loud humming erupted directly from the surface of the dead man. It was the sound of multiple, layered hummings—an auditory manifestation of a completely foreign ecosystem—bursting upward into the sky with the vast, chaotic commotion of winged things. The intense vibration grew fainter and fainter until it dissolved into the high, hot silence of the distance. It was exactly as though our clumsy human bodies had disturbed a colony of invisible, living organisms at their feast.

My companion clutched me, and I clutched him. Before either of us could recover from the visceral shock, the river current caught the corpse, releasing it from the grip of the willow roots. The body turned completely over, the dead face staring up at the blinding sky, resting precariously on the edge of the main stream. In another breath, it would be gone.

The Swede lurched forward to save it, shouting something untranslatable about a “proper burial”—and then abruptly dropped to his knees on the wet sand, covering his eyes with his hands. 

I was beside him in an instant. I saw what had broken him. 

As the chest and face were exposed to the harsh daylight, I saw plainly how the skin and flesh were intricately scored. The tissue was indented with hundreds of small, beautifully formed hollows, completely identical in their precise, geometric shape to the sand-funnels we had found scattered across the island. 

“Their mark!” I heard my companion mutter under his breath, his voice stripped to an awe-struck whisper. “Their awful mark!”

When I tore my eyes from his ghastly face back to the water, the current had finished its work. The body had been swept into the powerful mid-stream current, already beyond our reach and almost out of sight, tumbling over and over on the waves like an otter, carrying the physical evidence of its consumption into the deep.
