Jorian Ketel went straight as a draught through a broken shutter to Margaret’s room, the candle he carried making a timid, butter-yellow puddle of light that wavered with every step. The house itself seemed to hold its breath. The boards did not creak; the rafters did not settle; even the winter wind at the eaves came muffled, as if the night had laid a thumb upon the mouth of the world. In that hush he found what he had not expected to find at all.

Gerard—whom they had hunted through kitchen and loft as if he were a wolf—lay pale and motionless, his head sunk in Margaret’s lap. She knelt over him, not weeping now, not crying out, but turned to stone: eyes wide and emptied, as though all her sight had been poured into the white face she cradled. She neither saw the candle nor heard the man; the room might have been a chapel after the last bell, the congregation gone, the candles guttering, and one stubborn soul still praying.

Jorian stopped, struck dumb for a heartbeat, the grease of the candle running down his fingers. “Why, where was he, then, all the time?” he said, half to her and half to the baffled night.

Margaret did not so much as flicker.

He looked from Gerard to the empty chest, lid thrown back, its belly gaping and bare. The trick of it began to piece itself together in his mind—hiding, flight, search, the burlap and skins, the false promise of a clever nook. It had all ended here, on a girl’s knees. Her despair, so mute it had no language left to it, pricked him like a fishbone.

“This is a sorry sight,” he muttered. “A black night’s work—all for a few skins! Better have gone with us than so. She is past answering me, poor wench. Stop—let us try whether—”

He took down a little round mirror—no bigger than his hand—from where it hung, and held it close to Gerard’s mouth and nostrils. The candle’s flame trembled in the glass; Jorian’s own breath fogged it a little. He waited, counting the small sounds he could still hear: the wick’s faint hiss; the far-off tick of some settling timber; the blood pounding in his ears like a drum under cloth.

When he drew the mirror away, its surface was dulled.

There was the smallest smear of life upon it, a mist like dawn on cold water.

“THERE IS LIFE IN HIM!” Jorian said to himself—only muttered, only breathed, yet the words fell into that air like a stone into a still pond.

Margaret snatched them at once. It was as if the statue had cracked and a living woman burst out. She sprang up and flung both arms round the great fellow’s neck, clutching him with a fierce gratitude that had no measure.

“Oh, bless the tongue that tells me so!” she cried, and kissed him—cheek, beard, jaw—again and again, as if each touch could nail Gerard to the world. “Bless it! bless it!”

“There, there,” said Jorian, shaken and embarrassed by so much passion, yet softened in spite of himself. “Let us lay him warm.”

He lifted Gerard as if he were a heavy sack of meal—careful, though, with the head—and laid him on the bedclothes. Then he drew from his coat a flask; the sharp smell of Schiedamze leapt out into the room, clean and biting as crushed juniper, like a kitchen suddenly overrun by a spirit too strong for any pot.

He filled his broad hand and flung the liquor sharply in Gerard’s face—once, twice. The cold wet slap, the pungent sting, the violent scent, all conspired like rough nurses.

Gerard gave a faint sigh.

To Margaret that thin sound was richer than any song. It was a bell rung at the right hour; it was a hearth catching after long coaxing. She flew toward him, then stopped short, trembling, afraid her very love might bruise him. Her confidence in her own hands had fled.

“That is right—let him alone,” said Jorian, in a voice that tried to sound careless and came out kind. “Don’t go cuddling him as you did me, or you’ll drive his breath back again. Let him alone: he is sure to come to. ’Tisn’t like as if he was an old man.”

Gerard sighed more deeply; a faint streak of colour crept back to his lips as warmth returns to bread set near the fire. The room, that had been a tomb, began to be merely a room again: four walls, a bed, a candle, two living people.

Jorian turned for the door. If he lingered, someone might wonder where he had gone; suspicion, like smoke, found every chink. He had scarcely reached the latch when something seized his legs from behind.

It was Margaret.

She curled about his knees like a thing that will not be shaken off—no longer statuesque, no longer cold—kissing his hand, pressing her cheek to it, pleading with a body before she could find words.

“You won’t tell?” she whispered, hoarse with fear. “You have saved his life; you have not the heart to thrust him back into his grave—to undo your own good work?”

“No, no!” Jorian answered roughly, as if roughness could hide feeling. “It is not the first time I have done you two a good turn; ’twas I told you in the church whither we had to take him. Besides, what is Dierich Brower to me? I’ll see him hanged ere I’ll tell him.”

He paused; the thought that always sat nearest his tongue pushed forward—his own hearth, his own mouths to fill, his own wife with her practical hands, his children with their hungry mornings. Gain, like a strong spice, will assert itself even in a kindly stew.

“But I wish you’d tell me where the parchments are! There are a hundred crowns offered for them. That would be a good windfall for my Joan and the children, you know.”

Margaret’s fingers tightened on him, not with greed, but with the desperate quickness of one snatching at any rope flung into deep water. “Ah! they shall have those hundred crowns.”

“What! are the things in the house?” Jorian asked eagerly, his voice dropping.

“No; but I know where they are; and by God and St. Bavon I swear you shall have them to-morrow. Come to me for them when you will—but come alone.”

“I were made else,” said Jorian, and a grin flashed and vanished. “What! share the hundred crowns with Dirk Brower? And now may my bones rot in my skin if I let a soul know the poor boy is here.”

With that oath—and the odd blend of mercy and profit that had fathered it—he slipped out quickly, for delay was danger. Margaret sank again to her knees, quivering from head to foot as a flame trembles in a draught, and prayed beside Gerard and for Gerard, as if prayer were another blanket she could tuck about him.

When Jorian returned to the others, Dierich Brower turned on him with the blunt impatience of a man whose blood had been stirred and wanted a conclusion.

“What is to do?” asked Brower. “What saw you?”

Jorian made his face a careless mask. “Why, we have scared the girl out of her wits. She was in a kind of fit.”

“We had better all go and doctor her, then,” said another, with that officiousness that mistakes noise for help.

“Oh, yes!” Jorian replied, in a tone that mocked them without their hearing it, “and frighten her into the churchyard. Her father is a doctor, and I have roused him, and set him to bring her round. Let us see the fire, will ye?”

The words were thrown off as lightly as a man tosses a crust to a dog. Their very ease disarmed suspicion. Men who have been straining their ears for a cry of “Here he is!” will accept a shrug and a hearth as a relief; they are glad to be told there is nothing more to do.

Soon enough, cold and half-ashamed and hungry, the party agreed that the kitchen of the Three Kings was much warmer than Peter’s house. They departed, not without untying Martin first—an afterthought, as if a man bound in a corner were no more than a sack that had been in the way.

At the door Dierich Brower could not resist his little triumph of hindsight. “Take note, mate, that I was right, and the burgomaster wrong,” said he. “I said we should be too late to catch him, and we were too late.”

Thus, in one terrible night, Gerard grazed the prison and the grave.

And how did he get clear at last? Not by the cunning of a hiding-place; not by the sharpness of Margaret’s wits; but by a sudden good impulse in one of his captors—by the scrap of humanity left in a somewhat reckless man’s heart—helped along by that same man’s desire of gain. In such mixed measures are men compounded: mercy and money, pity and profit, kneaded together like dark flour and white, and baked by chance into the loaf that saves or ruins us.

Within the room, as danger receded like footsteps down a lane, joy came in upon them—first timidly, then with a flood that made everything else seem small. Gerard’s colour returned by degrees: a faint warmth in the cheeks, a breath that no longer faltered, an eyelid that fluttered as if waking from a deep, strange sleep. Margaret watched each change as a housewife watches the first sign of rising in dough she has feared would never lift—hope in the smallest swelling, salvation in a simple softness.

At last Gerard’s eyes opened upon her.

He woke from his trance to find his head pillowed on her arm. The scent of her hair was about him, loosened in great waves, warm with the heat of her body and the close air of the room. The candle-smoke lay thin as a veil; the hush that had been terror now felt like safety. He heard her voice—broken, new, eloquent with a love that had no need of fine words because it spoke first in breath, in tears, in the trembling of fingers against his face.

She bent close and kissed him, and then kissed him again, and tears fell—hot drops, each one a proof that she was alive and he was alive and the world had not taken him after all. She murmured endearments in the tongue that belonged to her childhood and her heart; and in those sounds, not to be translated without loss, he understood more than he had ever understood before. It struck him with a sweet shock how ardently, how tenderly, she loved him—how much of her very self was bound up in those familiar syllables no stranger could quite pronounce.

He whispered thanks—not to his enemies as saints might counsel, but to the strange chain of accidents that had let him return to her. Trouble and danger seemed suddenly a far country, a whole age behind them. They drew together, arms twining as naturally as if they had always been so, and called each other husband and wife.

Were they not solemnly betrothed? Had they not stood before the altar together? Was not the blessing of Holy Church upon their union—her curse on all who would part them?

In that hour their words were simple, domestic even: a vow spoken low, like something said at the hearth when the door is barred against the weather. Outside, the night had its own noises—distant feet in the street, a dog somewhere giving one lonely bark, the city’s faint murmur like the sea heard through walls—but in the room the soundscape had changed entirely. No longer the racket of pursuit, no longer the clatter of men; only breath, heartbeats, the soft rustle of linen, the small, sacred creak of the bed as life resumed its ordinary weight.

But no woman’s nerves bear such a strain without recoil. Presently Margaret’s strength ebbed as suddenly as it had flared. She turned faint and sank upon Gerard’s shoulder, smiling—oh, so feebly—yet with a smile that said she had won a battle. She was quite, quite unstrung.

Gerard started up in alarm, seeking help with his eyes, his whole body eager to rise, to call, to do something.

But she held him with a gentle grasp and pleaded, “Do not leave me for a moment. While I can lay my hand on you, I feel you are safe—not else. Foolish Gerard! nothing ails me. I am weak, dearest, but happy—oh! so happy.”

Then it was Gerard’s turn to be steady. He supported that dear head, and her great loose hair fell over him like a cloak. He nursed her as carefully as if she were the precious thing and he the strong one set to guard it—soothing her quivering on his bosom with soft words, with murmurs of love, with gentle caresses that asked no more than that she stay, that she breathe.

Poor things—they were happy.

To-morrow they must part. Yet the thought, hovering at the edge of their bliss, could not sting them now. They had looked into Death’s face; all other troubles seemed light as air. While there is life there is hope; while there is hope there is joy. Separation for a year or two—what was it to two so young, who had caught even a glimpse of the grave?

The future shone. The present was heaven. So the hours passed—sweet, perilous, and unguarded; for innocence, when visible danger has slipped away, runs risks of its own, and they lay not in prisons or pursuers, but in the beating of their own hearts, and in the inexperience that can scarcely tell one kind of hunger from another.
