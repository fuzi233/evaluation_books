The night air felt lighter now, after the suffocating darkness that had clung to the depths of the ancient sea-bed. I stepped onto a stretch of land clearly to the right of where I’d first entered the submerged plain—my path reversed, my journey homeward begun. Around me, fire-holes burned in abundance, their crimson glows casting flickering warmth across the stone. My heart stirred at the sight, but caution held me back. These fires were not merely sources of heat; they were gathering places for life—some of it terrible, some of it cruel.

I looked down at Naani, and she rose to meet my gaze. She came closer, her face pale, her breath shallow. The long walk had drained her, and I felt a pang of guilt—had I pushed her too hard? I was strong, forged by hardship into something almost unyielding, while she remained delicate, tender, human. But she refused my remorse, standing beside me with quiet resolve, her eyes holding mine with a depth of trust that made my chest tighten.

I reached out and pulled her close. We kissed, brief and fierce, a moment of solace before the world demanded again. Then I turned my attention to the horizon.

Before me stretched the blue glow I’d seen from the mouth of the Upward Gorge—a distant shimmer in the black sky, like a wound in the night. It had been my compass across the sea-bed, a beacon guiding me toward the far shore. Though vague and broad, it had kept me from circling blindly in the dark.

Now, studying the terrain, I began to place the Gorge. It lay somewhere to my left, but not too far—too near, and I risked the red fire-pit of the Giants. And too close to the blue shine itself… that I distrusted deeply. Something about that light felt wrong, unnatural. It wasn’t just illumination—it was a presence.

I took Naani’s hand, and pointed westward. “The Gorge is there,” I said, “but we cannot go straight.” I explained our route: around the gas-laden wastes, beneath the old sea-bed, then up again, avoiding both the giants’ pit and the blue glow.

She listened, then asked, “How did you find your way before?”

I told her how I’d walked along the edge of the gas field, unknowingly, guided by instinct or fate. When I finished, she smiled faintly and suggested a new plan: descend into the sea-bed once more, walk south-west beneath the surface until we passed the gas region, then climb back up and make for the Gorge, always watching for the giants.

It was sound. In fact, it was exactly what I would have devised. But it was *her* mind that shaped it—sharp, practical, quick. And hearing her speak, seeing her think, planning beside me, filled me with a quiet joy. Her voice, her presence, the way she saw the world—these things had become part of me. To share thought with her was to be whole.

We agreed on the course. But the hours had taken their toll. Seventeen since our last rest. Naani trembled, her breath ragged. I insisted we stop soon, and she nodded without protest.

We chose a fire-hole nearby, to the north, one glowing fiercely from within a rocky hollow. As we approached, I signaled silence. Drawing the Diskos from my hip, I moved ahead, dropping to hands and knees, beckoning her to follow.

At the edge of the pit, we peered down. A great fire roared in the earth, but no monstrous shapes lurked near it. That eased my fear—slightly. Still, I remembered: all life gathered around fire. It was safer to stay away.

But Naani shivered violently. “We must warm ourselves,” she whispered. “Just for a little.”

And I, too, felt the cold gnawing at my bones. So I relented. We descended into the hollow, drawn by the heat.

As we warmed, I searched the rocks. Three snakes slithered in crevices. Two scorpion-like creatures watched from holes, neither fleeing nor attacking. Their stillness unsettled me. They liked the warmth. And if they liked warmth, they might come for us in sleep.

I didn’t tell Naani. Let her rest. Let her forget the dangers for a moment. I would warn her later.

She ate, slowly, quietly. Her movements were subdued. I knew she remembered the whipping. And yet—she was mine. Utterly.

After dinner, she noticed my tension. She glanced over her shoulder—and saw a snake coil among the stones. Her breath caught. “We must leave,” she said.

We searched for shelter. And found it: a small cave in a rock wall, perhaps a hundred feet from the fire. Dry. Safe. No signs of creeping things.

Inside, we huddled together, wrapped in the cloak. The firelight filtered through the entrance, painting shadows on the walls. For a moment, it felt like refuge.

But the Land never rests.

Twenty hours since sleep. We drifted into exhaustion, our minds alert, ears tuned to the dark.

Then—screams.

Not animal cries. Human. Women. Children. Lost, terrified, pleading.

I sat bolt upright. Naani woke instantly.

More sounds followed—roars, deep and guttural, echoing across the land. Giant voices. Men as big as houses, shouting, chasing.

Naani trembled. I pulled her deeper into the shadow.

Then—a scream cut through the night. A young woman, dying. Brutal. Sudden. My stomach twisted. I wanted to run, to fight, to *do something*. But I couldn’t leave her.

The roarings grew louder. Then—thudding footsteps. Heavy, pounding. Coming closer.

I crept to the mouth of the cave. Outside, figures ran past—the desperate, ragged remnants of a hunted people. Men, women, children, naked or torn, faces streaked with dirt and blood. They fled like animals.

Then—four giants emerged from the dark. Three were massive, thick-furred, brutish. The fourth—pale, livid, its skin blotched with sickness. A monster. A thing beyond nature.

They vanished into the night, leaving behind only echoes and the fading cries of the dying.

When the thunder of their feet faded, I heard them bellow again—far off—then another scream. Then silence.

A wave of fury surged through me. Why didn’t they fight? They could have turned, charged, died together. But they ran. Because they were broken. Starved. Weak. Not by choice—but by generations of suffering.

The Lesser Redoubt had bred weakness into its people. But Naani was different. She was strong. Even now.

Then—movement in the cave.

I turned. Naani was sobbing softly in the back corner. I started toward her—but froze.

A naked woman ran across the rim of the hollow, looking back as she fled. She slipped down, crawled under a ledge—exhausted, terrified. And then, a squat, hairy giant appeared from the shadows, silent as death. He saw her. He moved.

I didn’t hesitate.

I leaped from the cave—twenty feet down—landing hard, but unharmed. I was upon the beast before he could act.

He lunged at the maid. She screamed once—then was gone.

Rage exploded in me. I swung the Diskos. It shrieked as it spun, alive with fury. The giant turned, roaring, but I struck high—too high. He dropped to his hands, catching my legs. I slashed with the Diskos—its single remaining talon bit deep. He threw me across the hollow, metal clanging, the Diskos ringing like a bell.

I rose, unharmed. He bounded at me—silent, frothing at the mouth, teeth like daggers. I leaped, struck—*and severed head and shoulder in one blow.*

The body fell, knocking me back. I staggered, bruised, bleeding, but alive.

I knelt beside the dead woman. Took her body. Threw it into the fire.

Then I turned to the cave.

Naani stood there. Pale. Silent. In her hand—my belt-knife.

She had come to help.

She stepped forward, her eyes locked on me. No words. Just that look—filled with awe, with respect, with something deeper than love.

I didn’t need her to say it. I *knew*. She saw me—not just as a warrior, but as someone worthy of her trust, her courage, her devotion.

And when she finally allowed herself to fall into my arms, trembling, I held her tight. We had both seen horror. And we were both changed.

Later, we ate. Two tablets each. Water. Then, after an hour of listening, we climbed out of the hollow, gear in hand.

We walked slowly, cautiously, toward the sea-bed. The fear of the giants lingered. But no harm came.

Two hours passed. We reached the edge of the old sea-bed.

And the journey home continued.
