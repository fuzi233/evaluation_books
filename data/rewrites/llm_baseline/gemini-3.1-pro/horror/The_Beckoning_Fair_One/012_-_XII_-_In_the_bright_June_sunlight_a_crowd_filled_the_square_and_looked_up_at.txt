In the bright June sunlight, the square was a cauldron of discordant acoustic friction. A dense crowd filled the space, their collective murmur rising like the hum of an ungreased industrial mill, all faces turned upward to the windows of the old red-brick house. The structure, bearing its antique insurance marks like ancestral crests, seemed to breathe in the heavy air, a site where mythic, cyclical tragedies seamlessly wove themselves into the quiet fabric of domestic routine. Now, the agents’ notice-boards hung over the paling like wooden meat cleavers, and two constables stood at the broken gate of the narrow entrance-alley, acting as a dam against the surging tide of onlookers.

The women kept to the fringes, shifting their angles to peer at the drawn red blinds, their whispers forming a steady, rhythmic undertone. The children remained sequestered behind closed doors, hidden from the disruption of their neighborhood’s sonic ecology. At the center of the throng, an agrarian elder of the parish—a long-nosed man named Barrett—was preserving the oral history of the afternoon through the fading, rhythmic dialect of the local working class. 

Another man, little and fat and wide-eyed, was desperately trying to interweave his own timeline into the neighborhood’s collective memory, seeking to capture Barrett’s audience with a frantic story about a key.

"…and it was revealed to me that there’d been something that very afternoon," Barrett was intoning, his voice carrying the rich, unhurried cadence of a man who measured time by harvests and seasons. "I was standing there, where Constable Saunders is—or rather, I was passing about my business, when they came out. There was no deceiving me, oh, no deceiving *me*! *I* saw her face…."

"What was it like, Mr. Barrett?" a man asked.

"It was like hers whom our Lord said to, 'Woman, doth any man accuse thee?'—white as sifted flour, white as paper, and no mistake! Don't tell *me*!... And so I walks straight across to Mrs. Barrett, and 'Jane,' I says, 'this must stop, and stop at once; we are commanded to avoid evil,' I says, 'and it must come to an end now; let him get help elsewhere.' And she says to me, 'John,' she says, 'it's four-and-sixpence a week'—them was her very words. 'Jane,' I says, 'if it was forty-six thousand pounds it should stop'... and from that day to this she hasn't set foot inside that gate."

There was a short silence, a brief lull in the oppressive soundscape. Then, somebody vaguely inquired, "Did Mrs. Barrett ever... *see* anythink, like?"

Barrett turned austerely on the speaker, guarding the subjective experience of his household. "What Mrs. Barrett saw and Mrs. Barrett didn't see shall not pass these lips; even as it is written, keep thy tongue from speaking evil," he said.

Another man spoke, layering the history. "He was pretty near canned up in the *Waggon and Horses* that night, weren't he, Jim?"
"Yes, 'e 'adn't 'alf copped it...."
"Not standing treat much, neither; he was in the bar, all on his own...."
"So 'e was; we talked about it...."

The fat, scared-eyed man made another attempt to insert his truth into the dominant narrative. "She got the key off of me—she 'ad the number of it—she come into my shop of a Tuesday evening...."

Nobody heeded him. The untranslatability of his small, crucial interaction was swallowed by the crowd. 
"Shut your heads," a heavy labourer commented gruffly, his native inflection thick and resonant. "She hasn't been found yet. 'Ere's the inspectors; we shall know more in a bit."

Two inspectors advanced, their authoritative presence immediately asserting a sterile, dominant lexicon over the community’s organic dialogue. The little fat man ran eagerly forward, insisting she had bought the key of him. 
"I remember the number, because of it's being three one's and three three's—111333!" he exclaimed excitedly.

An inspector brushed him aside, nullifying the man's contribution. "Nobody's been in?" he asked one of the constables.
"No, sir."
"Then you, Brackley, come with us; you, Smith, keep the gate. There's a squad on its way."

The two inspectors and the constable passed down the alley and entered the old house. They mounted the wide carved staircase, their heavy boots crushing a brittle, desiccated litter of dead leaves and paper that lay banked outside Oleron's door like the ruined chaff of a failed harvest. 

"This don't look as if he'd been out much lately," one of the inspectors muttered. "I don't think we need knock—break a pane, Brackley."

The door had two glazed panels. The sudden, violent shattering of glass fractured the suffocating acoustic ecology of the house, letting in the harsh dissonance of the modern street. Brackley thrust his hand through the jagged aperture and drew back the latch.

"Faugh!" choked one of the inspectors as they entered. "Let some light and air in, quick. It stinks like a hearse—" 

The olfactory landscape was one of deep, irreversible rot—a cloying, fermented miasma of bruised organic matter, oxidized copper, and the yeasty, sour tang of complete putrefaction that coated the back of the palate. Outside, the assembly saw the red blinds go up, exposing the house's shadowed belly to the bright sun.

"That's better," said the inspector, leaning out of the window to draw a deep, untainted breath. "That seems to be the bedroom in there; will you go in, Simms, while I go over the rest?..."

They drew the bedroom blind. On the bed lay Oleron, a waxy-white, emaciated husk of a man who had entirely forsaken the profound meaning of sustenance. He made a blinker of his hand against the torturing flood of light. His physical decay was a testament to a life stripped of sensory appreciation, a vessel hollowed out by a diet of mere atmospheric starvation. He could scarcely believe his hearing was not playing tricks on him; the intrusive, heavy voices of the policemen bending over him shattered his meticulously cultivated silence. 

"This woman Bengough... goes by the name of Miss Elsie Bengough... d'ye hear? Where is she?" they demanded. 

He shook his head. His subjective descent was inherently untranslatable into their clinical vocabulary.
"No good, Brackley; get him up; be careful with him; I'll just shove *my* head out of the window, I think...."

The other inspector had moved through the study, finding nothing, before entering the kitchen. Here, in the traditional hearth of creation and culinary ritual, lay a desecrated space. The floor was cumbered with an ankle-deep mass of fermenting vegetable refuse—wilted stalks and decaying flowers that gave off the sharp, rancid odor of over-steeped botanicals turning toxic in the dark. The kitchen window, overshadowed by the blank end of the neighboring house, offered no light. 

Though the room appeared empty, the inspector’s eye caught a shuffling track swept through the rotting vegetation, leading to a cupboard in the corner. The upper half of the cupboard door featured a square panel that slid on runners, much like a traditional pantry hatch. The door itself was closed. The inspector advanced, pinched the little knob, and slid the hatch back along its groove. 

He took an involuntary step back.

Framed in the aperture, and falling forward slightly before jamming in its frame, was a horrific perversion of culinary form. It was something that resembled a massive, lumpy pudding, densely packed and bound up in a pudding-bag of faded, browny-red frieze. 

"Ah!" said the inspector. 

To close the hatch, he would have had to press his hand against the yielding, macabre mass of that pudding, and his senses rebelled at the thought of the tactile exchange. Instead, he turned the handle of the main cupboard door. There was immense, dead weight behind it—the heavy settling of a ruined preserve. After opening it mere inches and peering into the dark, he had to put his shoulder against the wood to force it shut again. In doing so, he left a few inches of a black and white check skirt sticking out near the floorboards. 

He stepped back into the small hall. "All right!" he called.

They had wrestled Oleron into his clothes. His mind was adrift, unable to anchor itself in this sudden collapse of his isolated world. He couldn't comprehend the extraordinary mess of dead flora that choked the air with the smell of ruined soil; he couldn't parse why police officers had invaded his acoustic sanctuary; he couldn't grasp why they were sending for a four-wheeler and a stretcher, nor what heavily weighted article they were attempting to dislodge from his kitchen.

"What's the matter?" he muttered sleepily. 

A murmur rose from the square outside, accompanied by the stopping of a four-wheeler. An officer appeared at Oleron’s elbow, leaning in to whisper a rapid string of official phrasing—something about "used in evidence against you." The state’s dominant lexicon was asserting its power, officially erasing Oleron’s complex, multi-layered reality. They lifted him to his feet, steering him toward the door. 

Oleron couldn't understand it at all. They guided him down the carved stairs and out along the alley. The sonic environment outside was a raging sea of angry, disorganized shoutings; the mob's collective voice sought to lynch an offender they barely understood. Amidst the chaos, Oleron’s attention latched onto the little fat man, whose frightened eyes darted about as an officer finally transcribed his native dialect into a notebook. 

"I'd seen her with him ... they was often together ... she came into my shop and said it was for him ... I thought it was all right ... 111333 the number was," the man pleaded, preserving his linguistic fragment of the tragedy.

The crowd pressed in, their fury palpable, but one of the inspectors possessed a voice that Oleron found quite kind—a surprisingly gentle timbre amidst the industrial roar of the mob. The inspector barked orders to load Oleron into the cab before the other burden was brought out. As they hoisted him up, Oleron saw the inspector deliberately position himself to block the view of something positioned behind the cab. But the shield was not quick enough. Oleron caught a glimpse of a hooded stretcher. 

The angry voices swelled like an ocean. Something hard, a stone of raw, unrefined fury, struck the back of the cab. The inspector followed Oleron inside, standing with his broad back to the window nearest the mob. Through the open door, Oleron saw the hatchet-like "To Let" boards rising from the privet-trees. Someone shouted that the key was at Number Six. 

Suddenly, the raging voices hushed. The acoustic assault broke into an eerie, breathless silence. Along the entrance-alley, heavy, shuffling steps approached. The second inspector appeared at the cab door. 

"Right away," he ordered the driver. He climbed in, fastened the door, and blocked the second window with his own back. 

Safeguarded between the two officers, the harshness of the outside world muffled at last, Oleron slept peacefully. The cab moved smoothly down the square, while the other vehicle turned and labored up the hill. The mortuary lay that way. The cycle was complete.
