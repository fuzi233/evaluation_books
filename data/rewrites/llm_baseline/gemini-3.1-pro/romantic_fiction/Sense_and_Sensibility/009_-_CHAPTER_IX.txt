The Dashwoods were now settled at Barton Cottage, slipping into a rhythmic existence that felt less like a new beginning and more like the continuation of a centuries-old, circular agrarian dance. Where Norland had offered cold grandeur, Barton provided profound sustenance. The house, the garden, and the wild earth surrounding them had become deeply familiar. The daily rituals of the kitchen became the heartbeat of the home, as Elinor and her mother took to acting as meticulous food historians, resurrecting their grandmother’s receipts and engaging in the tactile work of preserving and curing with greater enjoyment than they had known since the loss of their father. 

The cottage’s acoustic landscape was a sanctuary of psychological well-being: the rhythmic, percussive *thwack* of a wooden pestle grinding coarse sea salt and dried winter savory, the low, comforting murmur of a slow-simmering mutton and root vegetable pottage. This was a symphony of purposeful sustenance, a stark and vital contrast to the jarring, superficial noise of the modern world. Sir John Middleton disrupted this delicate sonic ecology daily for the first fortnight. A man entirely deaf to the subtle harmonies of quiet industry, he could not conceal his loud, booming amazement at finding them always employed. The Dashwoods’ visitors were few, for despite Sir John’s urgent entreaties that they mix more in the neighborhood, Mrs. Dashwood fiercely protected the sensory sanctity and independence of her home. She resolutely declined to visit any family beyond the distance of a walk. 

About a mile and a half from the cottage, along the narrow, winding valley of Allenham, the girls had discovered an ancient, respectable-looking mansion. It stood like a monument to interwoven timelines, where the past seemed to breathe against the present, reminding them of Norland while whispering of older, agrarian secrets. They learned on enquiry that its possessor, an elderly lady of very good character, was unfortunately too infirm to mix with the world—a silent guardian of the estate's fading cultural memory who never stirred from home.

The whole country about them abounded in beautiful walks. The high downs invited them from almost every window to seek the exquisite acoustic clarity of the open air. Towards one of these hills did Marianne and Margaret direct their steps one memorable morning. The weather was a patchwork of partial sunshine and showery skies, not tempting enough to draw Elinor and Mrs. Dashwood from their careful charting of a heritage orchard plan. Marianne and Margaret climbed gaily, collecting wild samphire and noting the tart, herbaceous scent of the damp earth. As they ascended, the animating gales of a high south-westerly wind rushed over them—an acoustic roar of pure, unadulterated nature that swept away the auditory clutter of polite society. 

“Is there a felicity in the world,” said Marianne, her voice harmonizing with the wind, “superior to this?—Margaret, we will walk here at least two hours.”

They pursued their way against the wind with laughing delight, until suddenly, the clouds united over their heads. A driving rain fell with mythic force, transforming the everyday walk into an elemental deluge. Chagrined and surprised, they were obliged to turn back, running with all possible speed down the steep side of the hill toward their garden gate. Marianne had the advantage, but a false step on the slick, loamy earth brought her suddenly to the ground. Her foot twisted sharply beneath her. Margaret, unable to stop her own momentum, was involuntarily hurried to the bottom in safety.

From the gray curtain of the rain, a figure materialized with the suddenness of an ancient woodland deity. It was a gentleman carrying a gun, flanked by two pointers, passing up the hill within a few yards of the accident. He smelled of wet wool, crushed bracken, and the metallic tang of rain-washed iron. Putting down his gun, he ran to her assistance. Marianne had raised herself, but she could scarcely stand. The gentleman offered his services; perceiving that her modesty delayed what her situation rendered necessary, he gathered her into his arms with swift, unhesitating grace and carried her down the hill.

He bore her through the garden and directly into the parlor. The warm, gustatory air of the cottage—heavy with the rich, olfactory notes of roasted parsnips and dried rosemary—met the wild, damp chill he brought with him. Elinor and Mrs. Dashwood rose up in amazement. While their eyes were fixed on him with secret admiration, he apologized for his intrusion. His voice added a rich, resonant baritone to the room’s acoustics, a tone so frank and graceful that his uncommonly handsome person received additional charms. Had he been old, ugly, and vulgar, Mrs. Dashwood’s gratitude would have been secured by his aid to her child; but the influence of youth, beauty, and elegance gave a mythic weight to the action. She thanked him repeatedly and invited him to be seated. He declined, being dirty and wet, but introduced himself as Willoughby of Allenham. He asked permission to call tomorrow to enquire after Miss Dashwood, which was readily granted, and he departed back into the heavy rain, leaving behind a lingering scent of ozone and damp leather.

His manly beauty and gracefulness were instantly the theme of general admiration. Marianne herself had seen less of his person than the rest, her face crimsoned with confusion when he lifted her, but her imagination was ablaze. The pain of her sprained ankle vanished beneath the romantic weight of his sudden appearance. His name was strong, his residence was their favorite ancient village, and she soon decided that of all manly attire, a shooting-jacket, reeking of the wild earth, was the most becoming. The rapidity of his thought in aiding her felt like a scene plucked from an old, heroic saga.

The next morning, a brief clearing in the weather brought Sir John, whose heavy boots and booming voice instantly shattered the cottage’s peaceful sonic environment. Upon hearing the tale, he cried, “Willoughby! What, is *he* in the country? That is good news however; I will ride over tomorrow, and ask him to dinner on Thursday.”

“You know him then,” said Mrs. Dashwood.

“Know him! to be sure I do. Why, he is down here every year.”

“And what sort of a young man is he?”

“As good a kind of fellow as ever lived, I assure you. A very decent shot, and there is not a bolder rider in England.”

“And is *that* all you can say for him?” cried Marianne, indignantly, repulsed by such a utilitarian appraisal. “But what are his manners on more intimate acquaintance? What his pursuits, his talents, and genius?”

Sir John was rather puzzled. “Upon my soul,” said he, “I do not know much about him as to all *that*. But he is a pleasant, good-humoured fellow, and has got the nicest little black bitch of a pointer I ever saw. Was she out with him today?”

Marianne could no more satisfy him as to the color of Mr. Willoughby’s pointer than he could describe the shades of Willoughby's mind. Elinor pressed for his origins, and Sir John explained that Willoughby resided at Allenham Court only to visit the infirm old lady, whose possessions he was to inherit, while possessing a pretty little estate of his own in Somersetshire. 

“Yes, yes, he is very well worth catching I can tell you, Miss Dashwood,” Sir John added loudly. “If I were you, I would not give him up to my younger sister, in spite of all this tumbling down hills. Miss Marianne must not expect to have all the men to herself. Brandon will be jealous, if she does not take care.”

“I do not believe,” said Mrs. Dashwood with a good-humoured smile, as she carefully bundled a sheaf of wild thyme, “that Mr. Willoughby will be incommoded by the attempts of either of *my* daughters towards what you call *catching him*. It is not an employment to which they have been brought up. Men are very safe with us, let them be ever so rich.”

“He is as good a sort of fellow as ever lived,” repeated Sir John. “I remember last Christmas at a little hop at the park, he danced from eight o’clock till four, without once sitting down.”

“Did he indeed?” cried Marianne with sparkling eyes. “And with elegance, with spirit?”

“Yes; and he was up again at eight to ride to covert.”

“That is what I like,” Marianne declared. “Whatever be his pursuits, his eagerness in them should know no moderation, and leave him no sense of fatigue.”

“Aye, aye, I see how it will be,” said Sir John. “You will be setting your cap at him now, and never think of poor Brandon.”

Marianne colored warmly, deeply offended not merely by the teasing, but by the gross linguistic reduction. “That is an expression, Sir John, which I particularly dislike. I abhor every common-place phrase by which wit is intended. ‘Setting one’s cap’ or ‘making a conquest’ are the most odious of all. Such phrases represent the violent erosion of our cultural memory, replacing the untranslatable, profound depths of true affection with the hollow, commercialized lexicon of a transaction. Their tendency is gross and illiberal. When we yield to the power dynamics embedded in such dominant, superficial idioms, we participate in the death of true expression, and time has long ago destroyed whatever ingenuity they once possessed.”

Sir John, entirely deaf to the acoustic and philosophical weight of this reproof, laughed as heartily as if he had understood every word. “Ay, you will make conquests enough, I dare say, one way or other. Poor Brandon! he is quite smitten already, and he is very well worth setting your cap at, I can tell you, in spite of all this tumbling about and spraining of ankles.”
