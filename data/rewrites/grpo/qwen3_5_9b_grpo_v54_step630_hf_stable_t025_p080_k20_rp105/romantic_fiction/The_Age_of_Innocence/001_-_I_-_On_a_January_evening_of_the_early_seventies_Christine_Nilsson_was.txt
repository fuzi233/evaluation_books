January evening, early seventies. The air inside the Academy of Music was thick with the scent of lilies and the hush of a thousand held breaths. Up on the stage, Christine Nilsson sang, her voice a crystalline instrument cutting through the silence. She was performing *Faust*, though the words were a fluid thing, shifting from German to Italian to suit the ears of the American crowd. *"M'ama... non m'ama..."* she cooed, sprinkling falling daisies with notes as clear as morning dew, before pressing the flower to her lips and lifting her eyes to the man playing the lover—a figure in purple velvet who looked far too serious for his artless role.

Newland Archer leaned against the velvet wall of the club box, watching her. He had arrived late, a deliberate choice born of both metropolitan etiquette and a personal desire to savor the anticipation. He had dined alone with his mother and sister, lingering over a cigar in the gloomy library where smoking was permitted, letting the moment build until it was exquisite. Now, as the prima donna sang her triumphant *"M'ama!"*, his gaze drifted from the stage to the opposite side of the house.

There, in the box of the Mingotts, sat the object of his thoughts. May Welland was there, a young girl in white with eyes fixed ecstatically on the lovers. A warm pink flush climbed her cheeks, mantling her brow and suffusing the slope of her breast, where a single gardenia pinned a modest tucker. Her white-gloved fingers touched the immense bouquet of lilies-of-the-valley on her lap. Newland felt a surge of satisfied vanity, a thrill of possession mingled with a tender reverence for her purity. He imagined them later, reading *Faust* together by the Italian lakes, heaping romance upon her abysmal innocence. He did not wish her to be a simpleton; he intended to refine her, to teach her the social tact necessary to navigate the world, to make her as worldly-wise as the married women he admired, yet without their frailties.

Beside him, Lawrence Lefferts shifted, his opera-glass snapping shut with a sharp click. Lefferts was the arbiter of "form" in New York, a man whose elegance was as innate as his knowledge of when to wear a black tie. "My God!" he murmured, handing the glass to the elderly gentleman beside him.

Newland followed the direction of Lefferts's stare. In the Mingott box, a new figure had appeared. It was a slim young woman, slightly shorter than May, with brown hair curled close to her temples and held by a narrow band of diamonds. She wore a dark blue velvet gown, caught theatrically under her bosom, giving her a distinct "Josephine" look. She stood for a moment, consulting with Mrs. Welland about seating arrangements, then smiled faintly and took her place in the row, settling herself between Mrs. Lovell Mingott and Mrs. Welland.

Old Mr. Sillerton Jackson, the keeper of New York's family trees and its buried scandals, returned the glass. The entire box turned instinctively, waiting for his verdict. Jackson was a man of immense memory, knowing the tangled connections of the Thorleys and the Chiverses, the stinginess of the Leffertses, and the madness that plagued certain generations. He carried the weight of fifty years of secrets in his hollow temples. He looked at the newcomer, his filmy blue eyes scanning the group over his veined lids. He gave his moustache a thoughtful twist, a gesture that spoke volumes to the men around him.

"I didn't think the Mingotts would have tried it on," Jackson said simply.

The words hung in the air, heavy with implication. To the men in the box, it was a comment on propriety, on the breaking of unwritten rules. To Newland, it was a sudden, chilling disruption of the perfect picture he had held in his mind. The pristine world of the Mingotts, the fortress of their reputation, had just been breached by a stranger whose presence felt entirely out of place. The spell of the evening, the romantic haze of the opera, seemed to fracture instantly, replaced by the rigid, terrifying clarity of social consequence.
