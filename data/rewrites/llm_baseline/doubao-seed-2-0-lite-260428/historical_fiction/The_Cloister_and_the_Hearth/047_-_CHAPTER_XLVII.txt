The very next day, Denys seized a quiet moment to pull Catherine and her daughter aside, and told them all the rest of Gerard’s adventures, excusing himself in his gruff, soldierly way for keeping Cornelis and Sybrandt in the dark. “It is not for me to blacken their names—they come of a good, honest stock. But Gerard counts them no friends in this matter, and I am his sworn comrade. Us soldiers live by a rule: we tell the enemy nothing but lies.”

Catherine sighed and said nothing more at first. The stories Denys recounted stirred such a tumult of grief and agitation in the pair that they wept freely at the tale of the two friends’ parting, a moment Denys himself could barely speak of without his voice faltering. By the end of it all, though, their sorrow softened into bright, eager hope that Gerard would soon be home. Denys shared their confidence, but reminded them that hope was no excuse to let Gerard’s last wishes go unfulfilled. “Why, if Gerard walks through that door next week and his Margaret is nowhere to be found, what sort of grief will he fall into?”

Catherine had never felt so tender toward the absent girl as she did now, and was just as anxious to find her and welcome her home before Gerard’s return as Denys was. But she refused to agree they ought to leave the neighborhood to hunt for her across the whole country. “She must have told someone where she was bound. This isn’t a pair of thieves fleeing justice—they owe not a single stiver to any soul in Sevenbergen. Dear heart, Denys, you cannot chase every last corner of Holland looking for her.”

“Can I not?” Denys said, grim and determined. “That we shall soon see.” After a moment’s thought, he outlined their plan: they would split their forces. Catherine would stay close to home, keep her ears pricked and her eyes wide open for any gossip of Margaret’s whereabouts, while he would ransack every town and village in Holland if need be. “She will not be more than a few leagues off, depend on it. Three people cannot flee half so fast, nor half so far, as one alone.”

“That is sensible enough,” Catherine agreed. But she insisted he pay a visit first to the demoiselle Van Eyck. “She and our Margaret were bosom friends. She must know where the girl has gone, if only she will tell us.” Denys was all for going that instant, so Catherine smoothed her gown and tidied her cap in two shakes, and the pair set off together.

The old painter received them graciously in her richly tapestried, sunlit parlor, but the second Catherine laid out their errand, the embroidery slipped right out of Margaret Van Eyck’s hands. “Gone? Left Sevenbergen without so much as a word to me? The thankless girl.”

The visitors were stunned. “You do not know where she went? When was she last here?”

“Mayhap ten days agone. I had dragged my old brushes out of storage, after years of laying them aside, to paint her portrait. I never did, though, for reasons of my own.”

Catherine protested how strange it was that Margaret would vanish bag and baggage without a by-your-leave, no explanation given. “Just when all our hearts are warm to her, too! Here is Gerard’s own comrade come from the ends of the earth with word from him, and we cannot find her, and Gerard himself is on his way home. Sure she did not leave without a reason. Prithee, good demoiselle, can you not give us the clue?”

“I have no clue to give you,” the elder lady said, a little peevish.

“Then I do.” Reicht Heynes stepped into the doorway, her cheeks bright pink.

“So you have been hearkening at the door this whole while, eh?” her mistress said.

“What use are my ears for if not that?” Reicht shot back. “There is no dark riddle here to puzzle over, if you ask me. The clue is a two-ply twine, and both ends are in this very room right now. Oh mistress, I cannot believe you sit there pretending you had no hand in this.”

Marry, the old lady’s cheeks flushed as red as her servant’s. “So I drove the foolish girl away, did I?”

“You did your full share. What sort of greeting did you give her the last time she called? She was all friendless then, you cannot think she missed how cold you were. You turned up your nose at her and said you’d altered your mind about painting her portrait.”

“I did not turn up my nose!” Van Eyck laughed, half indignant. “My nose is not shaped to point heavenward the way yours is.”

“All our noses point where our hearts lead them,” Reicht said, unrepentant. “The poor soul came into my kitchen after that, tears in her eyes, and said she wasn’t to be painted after all. I knew exactly what she meant. I’d seen how you looked at her work.”

Van Eyck sighed, and owned to as much, turning to Catherine to plead her case. “These young girls mimic every last thing their sweethearts do, you know, no thoughts of their own. Your Gerard is handy at the illuminator’s craft, and Margaret was his most diligent pupil—no marvel, with a woman’s eye for bright colour, and a lover to ape. But I have always despised that trick at heart: it takes the great, royal, aspiring art of colour, and chains it to the petty drudgery of writing and printing, shrinks it down to fit between the pages of tiny prayer books, to be stuffed in some rich fool’s pocket at church.
“Still, affection softens us all. When the girl would bring me her pages of little thorn leaves, lilies, ivy, dewberries, ladybirds, and butterfly grubs, all stuck flat in gold leaf like wasps in a honey pot, and show me her journal where she’d spent a hundred, a hundred and fifty, two hundred hours poring over every tiny page, I was wroth. An immortal soul, all that skill and labour, wasted on Nature’s tiniest scraps, barren letters and little bugs! But I held my tongue, and looked kindly at her work, and told her: if Heaven has doomed us to spend our hours painting great gilded letters and little beetles instead of saints and heroes and their grand passions, why not paint those scraps like they live? The grapes you see hanging in the woods hang in the air, not stuck flat to a wall. Those insects crawl and hover free, not wedged in a prison of gold like flies in glue.
“She said she saw what I meant, but that the gold ground was sacred—you could never shade it. All her teachers told her that, she said, even Gerard. So I told her ‘That for Gerard, and all the rest of the lot,’ grabbed my brush, and picked a colour that was false in nature, but true next to that garish glaring gold. In five minutes, I’d painted a bunch of raspberries so juicy you’d think you could reach out and bite them, and that butterfly grub she’d drawn so true it turned your stomach. The girl threw her arms right around my neck and cried out in delight!”

Denys finally wedged a word in. “The little love!” he said, grinning.

Van Eyck stared at him, then smiled, and went on to tell them how she’d been led step by step to promise she would take up her brushes again, the art she had laid aside mourning for her painter brothers, and paint a Madonna—with Margaret as her model. She even told them her trick for turning a living girl into a saint on canvas. “Your hair is adorable,” I told her. “Why, it’s red,” she said. Aye, I told her, but what a red! Brown, glossy, the very hue a painter would kill for. And your violet eyes, that now go languid pining for Gerard, now spark bright with hope of him? I will lift them heavenward, fixed in holy meditation. That resolute chin of yours? I will soften it a little, for a Madonna has no need of a chin that can stand up to the whole wicked world.”

“Softens her chin?” Catherine said, bewildered. “Well I never. A resolute chin, that little darling?” Denys added, soft and fond.

“And that was when the trouble came,” Van Eyck said, her voice falling. “When you told me the news that she was with child, unwed, it gave me such a shock I dropped my brushes. Was I, at my age, to turn a girl who could not keep herself chaste for her lover into the Virgin Mary? I love the silly girl still, but I adore our Blessed Lady. Most male painters would think nothing of it—their saints and virgins are no better than their mistresses, saving your presence, and they see no shame in painting street trollops as angels. But I am no man. My painting is half craft, half devotion. I could not do it. It was foolish, maybe, but I could not help it. I am sorry, though.” She ended despondently, the defiance she had started her speech with all melted away.

“Alas, but you must know that broke the poor girl’s heart,” Catherine said gently.

Reicht bit her lips, impatient, and rounded on Catherine next. “That was not the only reason Margaret and old Peter left Sevenbergen, you know. Nay. She left because Gerard’s own folk slighted her so cruelly. Who would bide among hard-hearted people who drove their own lad off to Italy, and then when he was gone, never once came near the girl he left behind, all alone?”

“Reicht, I was going—” Catherine stammered.

“Aye, going, and going, and going, for months on end!” Reicht cried. “You built her hopes up with soft words, and then let them drop flat with your deeds. The poor soul sighed to me that none of you had ever come to call. I know what that loneliness feels like. I am far from all my kith too, and when I first came to Holland I used to creep off and cry all by myself. Ten times rather be Reicht Heynes, separated from my family by leagues of sea, than be Margaret, right in the middle of the very folk who ought to love her, and yet lonelier than I am.”

“Alack, Reicht, I meant to call only yesterday, but one thing or another always hindered me—”

“Did anything ever hinder you from eating your dinner?” Reicht said, sharp as a tack. “If your heart had been half as set on seeing her as it was set on your meat, nothing would have stopped you. There she sat, day after day, watching the road for you, with your grandchild beating beneath her heart.”

Van Eyck, who had been nettled by Reicht’s frankness, chose that moment to strike. “Since you will be so disrespectful to my guests, you can seek another place to work at once!”

“With all my heart,” Reicht said, stout as anything.

“Nay, mistress,” Catherine put in, good natured as ever. “Truthful folk always speak their minds. Her tongue stings, but it is better she says what she thinks than let it fester in her heart. Do not send her away on my account. I have had my share of sharp, good servants. Once when I was young, I had one that hectoring me so hard I thought I would go mad. Then she married the baker, and I was my own mistress again. After that I only hired silly girls who did not know their left hand from their right, and taught them my ways, for at least a dunce cannot saucy me around. Though it is wearisome, teaching one fool after another—” She then rounded on Reicht, half cross. “And you think you are mistress here, do you?”

“I am no mistress here, and no longer a servant. You turned me off, remember,” Reicht said, indifferent.

“Then hear me!” Denys said, solemn as a judge. All heads turned to him at once. “Mistress Reicht—you are turned off. If a man who barely knows you might give counsel, I will say: now you are no servant, be a mistress. Be a queen.”

“Easier said than done,” Reicht replied, blunt.

“Not a jot,” Denys said, drawing himself up to his full height. “I am half an arbalestrier, still limping a little from that devil Englishman’s arrow, though I left my own bolt in his carcass for my trouble, which is a comfort. I have twenty gold pieces right here, and a strong right arm. In a week I will have twice that. Marriage is not a habit of mine, I will own—but I capitulate to so many virtues. You are beautiful, good-hearted, speak your mind plain, and above all, you stand up for my friend’s Margaret. Be an arbalestress. Be my wife, my mistress, my queen, Denys of Burgundy’s lady.”

A dead silence fell. It broke a moment later in a flood of indignant squawking. “Well did you ever?” Catherine gasped. “Never in all my born days!” Van Eyck cried. “Before our very faces! Of all the absurd, insolent, ridiculous—”

Denys cleared his throat, dry. “The lady I spoke to has not said a word. The rest of you, who had no part in the offer, are chattering overmuch.” The voices stopped, and every eye pivoted to Reicht. She glanced at Denys from under her lashes, half smiling. “I intend to take a good long look at any man before I leap into his arms.”

Denys drew himself up majestically. “Then look your fill. And leap away.”

That was all the spark Van Eyck needed. She pointed a long white finger straight at Denys, her voice breathless. “Stand there! In the name of all the saints, you are beautiful! You are inspired—with folly, but no matter, you are inspired. I must sketch your head at once!” She grabbed her pencil, and screamed at Reicht: “Hussy, come stand right in front of him, keep him looking inspired and handsome. Oh why did they give me that stupid brute of a centurion to paint before, with the low forehead and the shapeless beard, when I could have had this maniac?”

Catherine stood staring, half convinced the old painter had been a secret lunatic all this time. Reicht grumbled that her cauldron of stew was left boiling over on the fire, she had no time to waste playing fool and model both. Her mistress chided her for being rude to the art, so Reicht pouted and obeyed, glowering at Denys, who gazed back at her with unshaken admiration.
Van Eyck drew until her old, disused wrist cramped up, leaving a rough but noble sketch of the pair, and sighed that she could work no more that day.

“Then can I go tend my pot now?” Reicht demanded.

“Aye, go to your pot, get inside it, you will find your soul in there with it,” Van Eyck said, half laughing.

“Boo, that nonsense,” Reicht said, contemptuous. “You said you turned me off? When you want to get rid of me, you can turn yourself off and die—you are old enough for it, and I will not leave you. When that day comes, though, I will need a man to dry my eyes. If the soldier is still of the same mind then, he can say so. If not? It is all one to Reicht Heynes.” And off she went to her kitchen.

Catherine and Denys said their goodbyes not long after, both of them now certain Reicht had read Margaret Brandt aright: the girl had fled Sevenbergen broken hearted, crushed by loneliness. That very same afternoon, Denys set out on his quest. His plan was simple, like all great things: he would wander a hundred towns and villages, ask every burgomaster after new arrivals, inquire at every village fountain after an old physician with a fair young daughter and a longbow soldier. He tramped for months, roaming all of Holland, and never found a trace of her.
But his chivalrous quest was its own reward. For the first time in his life, he was half Spartan, half content: sober, prudent, vigilant, unshaken, happy even through constant disappointment, no time for reckless excess even when he flirted on the road, every step taken in service to his friend.
We leave him there now, tramping the muddy roads under the sun and stars, a pilgrim of friendship, just as Gerard had once been a pilgrim of love.
