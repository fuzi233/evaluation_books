Margaret, who had wept so freely for the sad, slow decline of old Martin, could no more bear his final parting with cold stoicism than a tender sapling can bear the felling of the oak it has leaned on for years. Catherine, her betrothed mother-in-law, did her best to soothe her with well-worn platitudes: she told Martin had gone happy, that he himself had said his life was fully ripe, ready to fall from the bough. But her words curdled to bitterness when she added that now there were fewer mouths to feed, and one heavy burden off Margaret’s shoulders.

“Burden?” Margaret gasped, her face streaked with fresh tears. “Such ‘burdens’ were the only joys I had left. They filled this desolate heart of mine, and now it is not just desolate – it is hollow, empty as that oak chair pulled up to the hearth. Oh, that empty chair! My chest aches so to look at it. Poor old man, how could I have loved him by halves? I used to sit and watch him drowse, and think but for me, you would have starved in your old age, once so learned and sharp, till you grew as helpless as my own sweet babe. I loved him more like my child than my father. Oh, empty chair! Oh, empty heart! Woe is me, woe is me!”

No words of comfort would stem the tide of her pious grief, and at last Catherine fell silent, hanging her head in shame. A day later she spoke her true thought out loud: “I’ve been talking at you out of my head, not my heart. You are right to turn a deaf ear to such empty words. If I was in your shoes, I would mourn the old man just as hard as you do.” Margaret hugged her tight, and that flash of unvarnished sympathy did her more good than a month of pious platitudes.

Catherine’s heart ached for the girl she had come to love as her own daughter. “My poor lass, you were never made to live all alone. Stop wasting your youth pining for my son Gerard. Either he died out on that mad pilgrimage of his to Rome, or he has proven a traitor and forgotten you. It cuts my own heart to say it, but who can deny the plain truth? Your father is gone, and I cannot linger at your side every hour of every day. There is an honest lad who has loved you well these many years – take him, take the comfort he offers. Heaven sends us these souls to torment us and cheer us, for we are nothing at all in the world without someone to lean on.”

When Margaret stared back at her, bewildered, Catherine huffed a laugh. “Surely you are not so blind you have never seen it?”
“Seen what? Who?”
“Why, Luke Peterson, of course.”
“Our Luke? The boy who carries my market basket on market days?”
“Nay, he is turned nineteen, and a fine strapping healthy lad, not a child. I asked around, and every soul in Rotterdam says he is a steady, capable workman, and never touches a drop of strong drink – a rare thing for a Rotterdam lad, most of whom are half man, half drunkard’s sponge.”

Margaret smiled for the first time in days. “Luke loves dried suet puddings more than anything else in the world,” she said. “I make them just the way he likes, I do believe that is the only reason he comes a-courting round here.” Then she flushed bright red. “But if I thought he was daring to come after the wife of your son – the wife that is, and ought to be – I would show him the door so fast his boots would spin.”
“Nay, nay, do not go making mischief for nothing,” Catherine said quickly. “Poor lad! I wormed his secret out of him near on a twelvemonth ago.”
“Oh, mother, and you let him keep coming?”
“I did think of you,” Catherine defended herself. “I said to myself, if the fool is daft enough to be her willing slave for nothing, all the better for her. A lone woman is lost without a man about to fetch and carry her little errands. But now my mind is changed, and I think the best use you can put him to is marry him.”

“So you would wed me off to the first lout that looks my way, even against my own heart?” Margaret said, her voice sharp with hurt. “Gerard has no one in all the world but me. I will not believe he is dead till I see his gravestone with my own eyes, nor that he is false till I see him standing hand in hand with another lover. Foolish boy – I will not be civil to him ever again.”

The poor unsuspecting Luke, used to warm welcomes and extra slices of pudding at Margaret’s hearth, walked straight into a wall of cold shortness. At first he was hardly spoken to, then spoken to sharply, till the big soft-hearted fellow started whimpering like a lost hound, and asked Margaret plain what he had ever done to offend her.
“Nothing. It is all my fault. I am cursed,” Margaret said. “If you take my advice, you will keep out of my way for a good long while.”
“It was all my doing, Luke,” Catherine cut in, before he could spiral further.
“Dame Catherine?” Luke blinked at her, bewildered. “What did I ever do to you, to set her against me?”
“Nay, I meant all for the best. I told her I had seen you were looking at her through a wedding ring, and she will not hear a word of it.”
“There was no need to tell her that,” Luke said, plainspoken. “She knows I have been courting her this twelvemonth.”
“Not I,” Margaret said, wide-eyed. “Else I would never have opened my door to you.”
“Why, I come here every Saturday night,” Luke said, confused. “That is how lads in Rotterdam do court. If you sup with a lass on a Saturday, that is official wooing, everyone knows that.”

“Oh, that is Rotterdam’s custom, is it?” Margaret said, a faint glint of amusement in her tear-reddened eyes. “Next time you come, make it Thursday or Friday. For my part, I was fully convinced you only hung around for my puddings, boy.”
“I do like your puddings well enough,” Luke said tenderly. “You make them far better than my own mother does. But I like you far better than any pudding.”

Margaret’s smile faded, and her voice turned soft but firm as iron. “Then you have seen the last of those puddings. How dare you speak so to another man’s promised wife, when he is far away on his travels? You need not trouble to come back here any more, Luke. I can carry my own market basket well enough.”
“Oh, very well,” Luke moped. He sat stupid and silent for a little while, then rose and said sadly to Catherine, “Dame, I reckon I got the sack,” and trundled out the door.

But the very next Saturday, Catherine found him huddled on their doorstep, blubbering so hard his shoulders shook. He said he had gotten so used to walking the path to their cottage every Saturday night, every other street in Rotterdam felt strange and cold to him. She went inside and told Margaret, who sighed, and relented a little. “Poor Luke. He can come in again, if he will only know his place, and treat me like a wedded wife waiting for her husband, no foolish talk of wooing.”

Catherine ran back to relay the news, then nudged the hesitating Luke hard in the ribs. “Pshaw, boy! Promises are only pie-crust, made to be bent. Promise her all the world, sooner than sit outside on the step like a fool, when one word will get you inside. Humour her every little whim, run all her errands, wait it out. A lone woman is never one to hold out forever. If my poor Gerard never comes home to claim her, you will be sure to win her in time.”
