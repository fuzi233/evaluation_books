The first flush of recovered liberty at Sevenbergen was steeped in the grounding, ancient aromas of the hearth. Margaret stood over a heavy cast-iron pot, gently stirring a slow-braising hare in a rich reduction of dark, rustic ale, wild-foraged juniper berries, and heirloom parsnips pulled from the morning’s frost. The rhythmic scrape of her wooden spoon against the iron created a soothing, domestic cadence—a fundamental acoustic frequency that temporarily stabilized their frayed nerves. Yet, beneath the caramelized scent of rendered fat and roasting root vegetables, a sour note of dread lingered. Gerard was an escaped prisoner, liable to be retaken and punished; their agrarian sanctuary could not hold. 

Gerard had conceived a bitter hatred for Tergou, a town whose cold, mercantile dialect had condemned him. To survive, and to clear the obstacles to their marriage, he must leave for Italy. The demoiselle Van Eyck had provided the gold pieces specifically for this journey, a truth Gerard repeated as they sat by the fire. Italy was a land of new culinary and artistic lexicons, a place where his meticulous craftsmanship would be valued rather than persecuted by boorish magistrates. Margaret, an unselfish girl deeply rooted to the soil of her ancestors, gave her tearful consent. As they held hands until midnight, they mourned the inherent untranslatability of their grief. The nuanced, native dialect of their shared love—spoken through the shared breaking of bread and the quiet hum of their daily routines—was being violently displaced by the dominant, oppressive vocabulary of the law that sought to part them. 

The morning found them resigned to this separation, though neither possessed the courage to name the hour. But about three in the afternoon, the harmonious acoustic ecology of the cottage—the bubbling broth, the rustling oak branches—was brutally ruptured. 

Giles rode up to the door, having made a circuit of many miles. He shattered the sonic landscape of the garden with his tremendous tones, the sheer volume a jarring intrusion of the town’s chaotic noise. “Brother Gerard!” he cried. “Kate bids you run for your life. They charge you with theft; you have given them a handle. Think not to explain. Hope not for justice in Tergou. The parchments you took, they are but a blind. She hath seen your death in the men's eyes; a price is on your head. Fly!”

It was a thunder-clap that drained the blood from their faces. Giles, putting aside the rote words of his sister, added his own urgent counsel. “All the constables were at our house after you, and so was Dirk Brower. Kate is wise, Gerard. Best give ear to her rede, and fly!”

“Oh, yes, Gerard,” cried Margaret wildly, her voice trembling. “Fly on the instant. Ah! those parchments; my mind misgave me: why did I let you take them?”

“Margaret, they are but a blind,” Gerard insisted. “No matter: the old caitiff shall never see them again; I will not go till I have hidden his treasure where he shall never find it.”

After warmly thanking Giles and bidding him carry a farewell back to Kate, Gerard called for Martin. The weathered agrarian elder, whose very posture mirrored the ancient, bending oaks of the forest, listened to the peril. 

“Ay!” Martin said, his voice a low, gravelly timbre that belonged to the woods. “And if I see Dirk Brower or any of his men, I will shoot an arrow into the oak-tree that is in our garden; and on that you must run into the forest hard by, and meet me at the weird hunter's spring. Then I will guide you through the wood.”

With surprise thus provided against, Gerard breathed again. He took a spade to the garden, and while Margaret watched the oak-tree tremblingly, he dug into the earth. The olfactory landscape immediately shifted from the domestic warmth of the kitchen to the sharp, primal scent of dark loam, severed roots, and rich petrichor. One by one, he threw the parchments into the hole. They were records of the burgh—lifeless instruments of a dominant lexicon designed to subjugate the peasantry. But among them was a private deed between Floris Brandt, Margaret’s grandfather, and Ghysbrecht. 

“Why, this is as much yours as his,” Gerard said, brushing the fragrant soil from the seal. “I will read this.”

“Oh, not now, Gerard, not now,” cried Margaret. “Every moment you lose fills me with fear; and see, large drops of rain are beginning to fall, and the clouds lower.”

Gerard yielded, slipping the generational artifact into his bosom before stamping the earth down over the bureaucratic rot. A flash of lightning cleaved the sky, followed by a peal of distant thunder that rolled like the groan of some mythic beast. The rain came down heavily, washing the scent of dirt from Gerard’s hands as he and Margaret ran inside, speedily followed by Martin.

“The road is clear,” Martin said, shaking the wet from his cloak. “And a heavy storm coming on.”

The thunder crashed directly overhead, a deafening obliteration of the world’s quietude. The lightning flashed like the strokes of a whip, and Margaret hid her face. Gerard put up the rough shutter and lit a beeswax candle, blessing the fierce weather that granted him a few stolen hours with his love.

The sun set unperceived behind the tempest. Supper was finally set upon the wooden table. The hare was perfectly tender, the broth thickened with ground barley and fragrant with wild thyme—a meticulous testament to their regional food history. Normally, Gerard and Margaret revered such sustenance, decoding the profound generational memory embedded in every savory bite. But tonight, the sorrow choked them. The sensory richness of the meal stood in agonizing contrast to their constricted throats; the thought that this was their last supper together rendered the food impossible to swallow. 

The storm lulled. Peter retired to rest. Gerard was to leave at the peep of day, and neither lover could afford to lose an hour in sleep. Martin sat with them, meticulously fitting a new hemp string to his yew bow. The tight *thrum* of the cord was a pure, resonant frequency in the quiet cottage, a sound of survival. Beside him, the lovers murmured their sorrows.

Suddenly, the old man held up his hand. 

They fell dead silent, straining their ears. At first, there was nothing. Then, a footstep crackled faintly upon the autumn leaves outside the back door. To an ear dulled by the industrial clatter of the town, such a step would have meant nothing. But within the hyper-attuned acoustic ecology of the cottage, a foot trying to be noiseless was a terrifying violation.

Martin smoothly fitted an arrow to his string and blew out the candle, plunging the room into darkness and heightening their auditory focus. The rich smell of the stew mingled with the acrid smoke of the snuffed wick. At that moment, to their utter horror, they heard more than one footstep approach the other door of the cottage. These were stealthy, arrhythmic, and followed by a dead pause. 

Their blood froze. 

“Oh, Kate, oh, Kate! You said fly on the instant,” Margaret moaned, wringing her hands in anguish and wild remorse for having kept him. 

“Hush, girl!” Martin commanded in a stern whisper. 

A heavy knock fell on the door. And on the hearts within.
