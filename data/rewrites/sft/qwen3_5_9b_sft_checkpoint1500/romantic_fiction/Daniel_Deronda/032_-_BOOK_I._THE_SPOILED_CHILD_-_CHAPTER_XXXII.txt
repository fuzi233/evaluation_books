In every age, it has been a favored maxim that a mighty love possesses the nature of an isolated fate, to which the mind's usual opinions and accustomed resolutions are wholly strangers. Consider Daphnis's madness, where conviction of Heraclitus's doctrines would have availed him little; or Tristan, bred by a philtre, who, though as profound as Duns Scotus, would have had his reasoning shattered by that single cup; or Romeo, seized suddenly for Juliet, where any objections he might have raised against Ptolemy would have made no difference to his discourse beneath the balcony. Yet not all love is thus severed from reason, even when potent. Nay, this passion holds a vast capacity for alliance with every operation of the soul, acknowledging effects from the imagined light of unproven firmaments and scaling itself to the grander orbits of what has been and shall be.

Upon returning to town, Daniel Deronda could assure Sir Hugo that he had lodged in Grandcourt's mind a clear understanding: fifty thousand pounds could be obtained by surrendering a prospect that was likely distant and by no means absolute. Yet, regarding Grandcourt's actual disposition, Deronda saw nothing more than an evident inclination to maintain friendly communications.

"And what did you think of the future bride upon a closer inspection?" Sir Hugo asked.

"I thought better of her than I did at Leubronn," Deronda replied. "Roulette was not a fitting setting for her; it revealed something of the demon within. At Diplow, she seemed far more feminine and attractive—less hard and self-possessed. I perceived a different expression in her mouth and eyes."

"Do not flirt with her too much, Dan," Sir Hugo said, intending a pleasant jest. "If you manage to make Grandcourt savage when they arrive at the Abbey at Christmas, it will interfere with my affairs."

"I can remain in town, sir."

"No, no. Lady Mallinger and the children cannot do without you at Christmas. Only do not make mischief—unless you can engineer a duel and manage to shoot Grandcourt, which might be worth a slight inconvenience."

"I do not think you have ever seen me flirt," Deronda said, unmoved.

"Oh, haven't I, though?" Sir Hugo countered provocatively. "You are perpetually gazing tenderly at women and conversing with them in a Jesuitical manner. You are a dangerous young fellow—a sort of Lovelace who will cause Clarissas to chase you rather than vice versa."

What use was it to be exasperated by a tasteless joke? Only the exasperation precedes reflection on utility. Few friendly remarks are more irritating than the information that we constantly *seem* to do what we never intend to do. Sir Hugo's conception of flirting was, it was hoped, rather peculiar; for Deronda was certain he had never flirted. Nevertheless, he was relieved that the baronet lacked knowledge of the repurchase of Gwendolen's necklace, which might otherwise have fed his appetite for such banter.

From now on, he would exercise vigilance. Specifically, in his conduct at Mrs. Meyrick's, where he was about to pay his first visit since arriving from Leubronn. For Mirah was undoubtedly a creature in whom it was difficult not to display a tender kind of interest, both in gaze and speech.

Mrs. Meyrick had not failed to send Deronda a report of Mirah's well-being. "We are growing fonder of her every day," she had written. "At breakfast, we all look toward the door with expectation to see her enter, watching and listening to her as if she were a native from a new land. I have heard not a word from her lips that causes me doubt. She is quite contented and full of gratitude. My daughters are learning from her, and they hope to secure her other pupils, for she is anxious not to eat the bread of idleness but to work, like my girls. Mab says our life has become like a fairy tale, and all she fears is that Mirah will turn into a nightingale again and fly away from us. Her voice is simply perfect—not loud and strong, but searching and melting, like the thoughts of what has been. That is how old people like me feel a beautiful voice."

However, Mrs. Meyrick did not delve into particulars requiring her to admit that Amy and Mab, who had accompanied Mirah to the synagogue, found the Jewish faith less reconcilable with their wishes in Mirah's case than in Scott's Rebecca. They remained silent out of delicacy, for Mirah's religion was too tender a subject to be touched lightly. Yet eventually, Amy, a practical reformer, could not restrain a question.

"Excuse me, Mirah, but *does* it seem quite right to you that women should sit behind rails in a gallery apart?"

"Yes, I never thought of anything else," Mirah answered with mild surprise.

"And you prefer to see the men with their hats on?" Mab cautiously proposed the smallest point of difference.

"Oh, yes. I like what I have always seen there, because it brings back to me the same feelings—the feelings I would not part with for anything else in the world."

After this, any criticism, whether of doctrine or practice, would have seemed to these generous little people an inhospitable cruelty. Mirah's religion was of one fiber with her affections and had never presented itself to her as a set of propositions.

"She says herself she is a very bad Jewess and does not half know her people's religion," Amy remarked when Mirah retired to bed. "Perhaps it would gradually melt away from her, and she would pass into Christianity like the rest of the world, if she grew to love us very much and never found her mother. It is so strange to be of the Jews' religion now."

"Oh, oh, oh!" cried Mab. "I wish I were not such a hideous Christian. How can an ugly Christian, who is always dropping her work, convert a beautiful Jewess, who has not a fault?"

"It may be wicked of me," said shrewd Kate, "but I cannot help wishing that her mother may not be found. There might be something unpleasant."

"I do not think so, my dear," said Mrs. Meyrick. "I believe Mirah is cut out after the pattern of her mother. And what a joy it would be to her to have such a daughter brought back again! But a mother's feelings are not worth reckoning, I suppose"—she shot a mischievous glance at her daughters—"and a dead mother is worth more than a living one?"

"Well, and so she may be, little mother," said Kate, "but we would rather hold you cheaper, and have you alive."

Not only the Meyricks, whose varied knowledge had been acquired by the irregular foraging to which clever girls are often reduced, but Deronda himself, despite all his masculine instruction, had been awakened by this apparition of Mirah to the consciousness of knowing hardly anything about modern Judaism or its inner history. The Chosen People have commonly been treated as a people chosen for the sake of somebody else; and their thinking as something (whatever it may be) that ought to have been entirely otherwise. Deronda, like his neighbors, had regarded Judaism as a sort of eccentric, fossilized form which an accomplished man might dispense with studying and leave to specialists. But Mirah, with her terrified flight from one parent and her yearning for the other, flashed upon him the hitherto neglected reality that Judaism was something still throbbing in human lives, still providing them with the only conceivable vesture of the world. On the idle excursion he immediately undertook with Sir Hugo, he began to seek out the exteriors of synagogues and the titles of books concerning the Jews.

This awakening of a new interest—this transition from the supposition that we hold the right opinions on a subject we neglect, to a sudden care for it and a realization that our opinions amount to ignorance—is an effective remedy for ennui, which, unfortunately, cannot be secured by a physician's prescription. Yet Deronda had carried it with him, enduring his weeks of lounging all the better. It was during this journey that he first entered a Jewish synagogue—at Frankfurt, where his party rested on a Friday. Exploring the Juden-gasse, which he had seen long before, he recalled well enough its picturesque old houses; what his eyes chiefly dwelt upon now were the human types there. His thoughts, busily connecting them with the past phases of their race, stirred that fiber of historic sympathy which had helped to determine in him certain traits worth mentioning for those interested in his future.

True, when a young man possesses a fine person, no eccentricity of manners, the education of a gentleman, and a present income, it is not customary to feel a prying curiosity about his way of thinking or his peculiar tastes. He may very well settle into life as an agreeable, clever young fellow without passing a special examination on those heads. Later, when he becomes somewhat slovenly and portly, his peculiarities are more distinctly discerned, and it is taken as a mercy if they are not highly objectionable. But anyone wishing to understand the effect of later events on Deronda should know a little more of what he was at twenty-five than was evident in ordinary intercourse.

His early-woken sensibility and reflectiveness had developed into a many-sided sympathy, threatening to hinder any persistent course of action. As soon as he took up any antagonism, though only in thought, he seemed to himself like the Sabine warriors in the memorable story—with nothing to meet his spear but flesh of his flesh, and objects that he loved.
