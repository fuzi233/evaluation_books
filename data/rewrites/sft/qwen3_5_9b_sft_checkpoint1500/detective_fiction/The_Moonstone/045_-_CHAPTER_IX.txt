The doctor's maid stood by the street door, holding it open for me with a bright, welcoming smile. As I stepped forward, however, the morning light illuminated the face of Mr. Candy's assistant, Ezra Jennings, standing just inside the hall. It was undeniable that his appearance was ill-suited to the public eye. With a complexion akin to a gypsy's, hollow cheeks, gaunt features, and eyes that held a perpetual dream, coupled with hair of strange, shifting colors, he presented a jarring contradiction of age and youth. These traits, combined with a slight mismatch between his frame and countenance, were bound to cast suspicion on a stranger. Yet, despite my rational understanding that he was an unwelcome presence, a profound, inexplicable pull drew me toward him, anchoring me to the spot.

While I acknowledged the gravity of Mr. Candy's deterioration and prepared to depart, Jennings had clearly anticipated my hesitation. Seizing the moment, he approached me privately. "Are you walking my way, Mr. Blake?" he asked, noting my hat in hand. "I am heading toward Mrs. Ablewhite's house as well."

"I have a patient to see," Jennings admitted, confirming he was traveling in my direction.

Together, we left the residence. Inside, the cheerful servant girl who greeted me with such warmth received a curt, business-like note from Jennings regarding his return time. Her lips pressed tight, her gaze deliberately avoiding his, betraying that he was a pariah within the household. Outside, Betteredge confirmed his unpopularity throughout the area. "What a life!" I mused as we descended the steps.

Jennings, sensing I wished to broach the topic of the doctor's illness, fell silent, signaling it was now my turn. "Judging by the transformation I observe," I began, "Mr. Candy's affliction must have been far more severe than I initially imagined?"

"It is a miracle," Jennings replied softly, "that he survived it at all."

"Does his memory ever clear, as it seemed to flicker this morning? He attempted to speak to me..."

"Regarding an event prior to his sickness?" Jennings prompted gently.

"Yes."

"The memory of those times is hopelessly weakened," Jennings explained, his voice tinged with sorrow. "Even the fragments remain obscured. He recalls vague intentions, things he meant to say or do, yet the substance of those plans eludes him entirely. He is acutely aware of this deficit and desperately tries to conceal it. If he could have slipped into a total oblivion of the past, he would be a happier man. Perhaps humanity would be wiser if we could all simply forget!"

"There are surely memories in every life," I countered, "whose loss would be a tragedy?"

"That may hold true for most," Jennings conceded, glancing at me with a sad, knowing smile. "But not necessarily for all. Do you suspect that the lost recollection Mr. Candy sought this morning was one specifically important to *you*?"

The question struck the heart of my purpose. I had invited him to speak, trusting that his demeanor revealed a man of discretion. Indeed, he possessed an "unsought self-possession"—a quiet assurance that signaled genuine breeding. Having verified his integrity through his initial caution, I felt justified in answering freely.

"I believe I have a compelling interest," I said, "in uncovering the memory Mr. Candy could not retrieve. Can you suggest any method to aid his recollection?"

Jennings's dreamy brown eyes flashed with sudden interest. "Mr. Candy's memory is, alas, beyond restoration," he stated flatly. "I have attempted to revive it repeatedly since his recovery with no success."

"You led me to hope for a less grim prognosis," I confessed.

Jennings smiled. "It may not be a final verdict, Mr. Blake. It is conceivable to trace Mr. Candy's lost thoughts without consulting him directly."

"How?" I asked, intrigued. "Is it an intrusion?"

"Not at all. The challenge lies solely in articulating the process. May I rely on your patience if I recount the particulars of Mr. Candy's illness, including some professional specifics?"

"Please, proceed. Your account has already captured my attention."

Amusement, or perhaps pleasure, played across his features at my eagerness. As we walked away from the town, Jennings paused beside a hedgerow to gather a small bouquet of wildflowers. "How exquisite they are!" he remarked, presenting them to me. "Few in England appreciate them as they deserve."

"Have you not always resided in England?" I queried.

"No. I was born and partially raised in a colony. My father was English, though my mother..." He trailed off, noticing we had drifted from the subject. "Forgive me, Mr. Blake. These flowers carry associations. Let us return to Mr. Candy."

Reflecting on the melancholy philosophy he espoused—that happiness lay in forgetting the past—and connecting it with the foreign strain in his lineage, I concluded that his face bore the marks of deep, singular suffering.

"You have likely heard the origin of Mr. Candy's malady?" Jennings resumed. "It was the night of Lady Verinder's dinner party. Heavy rains fell. My employer returned home drenched, only to receive an urgent summons from a patient. Driven by duty, he departed immediately to tend to the sick person, foregoing the chance to dry his clothes. That night, I was detained elsewhere by a distant emergency. Upon returning the following morning, I found the groom in a state of alarm. By then, the damage was irreversible; the fever had taken hold."

"To date, the illness has been described merely as 'fever'," I noted.

"I can offer no further precision," Jennings admitted. "Two of Mr. Candy's colleagues, both established physicians, visited me to assess the case. They concurred with my diagnosis of severity but vehemently opposed my treatment strategy. Their interpretation of the patient's pulse diverged sharply from mine. Arguing from its rapidity, they advocated for a lowering regimen of gruels and barley waters. Conversely, I perceived the pulse's feebleness as evidence of a depleted system requiring stimulants—champagne, brandy, ammonia, and quinine. A grave disagreement ensued between the local authorities and an assistant. For several days, I yielded to their judgment, watching the patient sink steadily. Eventually, the disparity became untenable. When I insisted on administering stimulants, they demanded I choose: manage the case alone or step aside. I chose the former, summoning champagne from the cellar and administering it personally. The senior physicians departed in silence, leaving me to bear sole responsibility."

"In your position," I ventured, "I would have feared the consequences."

"Consider the bond you shared with Mr. Candy," Jennings urged gently. "If I were you, seeing him fade hour by hour, I would risk anything to save the man who had befriended me. I understood the gravity of my isolation and the peril I courted. Had I known the comfort of a secure past, I might have succumbed to despair. But devoid of a peaceful history to contrast with my present torment, I held firm. Through twenty-four hours of vigil, battling the fever-induced delirium that raged from dusk till dawn, I staked everything on my treatment. When wine failed, I used brandy; when that faltered, I doubled the dose. Then, amidst a suspension of time that I pray never returns, the pulse slowed. Not merely slower, but stronger, steadier. *Then*, I knew I had saved him. Laid his wasted hand back upon the bed, I wept. A hysterical release, perhaps. Some men, physiology suggests, are born with female constitutions, and I am one."

Apologizing quietly for the display, he continued. "Why have I burdened you with these details? Only to prepare you for what follows. Knowing my desperate need to alleviate the mental weight of that ordeal, I devoted my leisure to writing a treatise on the brain and nervous systems, intended for my professional peers. Though unfinished and unpublished, it sustained me during those agonizing waits. Recalling the nature of Mr. Candy's delirium, I questioned whether the inability to speak coherently implied a cessation of coherent thought. Using shorthand, I transcribed his fragmented wanderings. Treating them like a puzzle, I pieced together the disjointed phrases, filling gaps based on context until a narrative emerged. The result confirmed my hypothesis: while his lower faculties of expression were in chaos, his higher faculty of thought remained active, albeit fractured."

"One word!" I interrupted eagerly. "Did my name appear in his ravings?"

"You shall hear, Mr. Blake." Jennings produced a sheet of paper. "Among my records, there exists a fragment where your name appears. For nearly an entire night, Mr. Candy's mind was preoccupied with something involving you and himself. I have reconstructed the broken words into an intelligible statement detailing an action performed in the past and a contemplation of a future act thwarted by his illness. This, I posit, constitutes the lost memory he sought this morning."

"Not a shadow of a doubt!" I cried. "Lead us back to examine the papers!"

"Impossible, Mr. Blake."

"Why?"

"Imagine yourself in my shoes," Jennings pleaded. "Would you reveal the unconscious confessions of a suffering friend without absolute certainty that justice demands it?"

I felt the force of his argument but struggled to respond. "My actions would hinge on whether the revelation compromised my friend."
