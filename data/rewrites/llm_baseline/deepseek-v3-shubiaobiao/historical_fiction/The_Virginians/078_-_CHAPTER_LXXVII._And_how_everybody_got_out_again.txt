Captain Miles Warrington, you have the honor of winning the favor of a lady—or perhaps many ladies. The Duchess of Devonshire, Mrs. Crew, Mrs. Fitzherbert, even the Queen of Prussia—what matter who? But when a gentleman secures a lady’s regard, does he rush to the mess to boast of it?

"Not such a fool, Squire!" retorts the Captain, admiring his reflection.

"And you, Miss Theo—did you recount every word exchanged with young Mr. Blake in the shrubbery this morning?"

"Joe Blake? Really!" scoffs Theo, flushing.

"And you, mademoiselle? That perfumed letter delivered under Sir Thomas’s seal—have you disclosed its contents? Ah, see how she colors! Crimson as the curtain! No, my friends, we all keep our secrets." (Here the Squire executes his finest French bow.) "The shrubbery held only nuts, my dear. And as for my own tale—what passed in a carriage on the Hampstead Road, the 25th of May, 1760—may the Chevalier Ruspini pluck out every tooth in my head before I reveal it!"

"Tell us, papa!" implores my wife.

"Or summon Jobson from the stables—he drove us that day! I insist!"

"What is this mystery?" asks mademoiselle, her accent charming.

My wife leans close. "What did I say? *Yes*—that was all."

And so it is she who confesses, not I. That single word was the sum of our exchange as the carriage sped toward Hampstead and back again. Theo had not consented to defy her parents—no. But she would marry no other, though we lived as long as Methuselah. Not the Prince of Wales himself could sway her. Her heart had been given with her father’s blessing; it was no longer hers to withdraw. Surely, in time, his kindness would relent. And if I kept my vow—whether now, in twenty years, or beyond this life—she would never break hers.

Hetty’s face glowed when she saw Theo’s serene expression. No physician’s tonic had ever wrought such a change. The sisters returned home, their act of defiance complete. I surrendered my place beside my dear invalid to Hetty, while Charley resumed his post on the box. Later, he admitted the sermon had been dull—too orthodox for his taste.

Hetty was not the only one to notice Theo’s transformation. Their mother embraced them both warmly, praising the virtues of country air. "Another ride tomorrow—and the next day! Lord Wrotham’s horses are at our disposal!"

Did she suspect our meeting? Perhaps. Women have ways of aiding such affairs—little schemes, whispered confidences, averted glances while lovers steal their moments. Or perhaps, like Miss Prim, they snarl and suspect the worst. But Aunt Lambert was no Miss Prim. She was kindness itself, and if she guessed, she gave no sign.

At supper, the General rejoiced at Theo’s renewed appetite. "If a coach-and-six restores her, she shall have it!" he declared.

Yet Theo, ever honest, could not long deceive him. When he marveled again at her improvement, she laid a hand on his. "Papa, it is because I have seen him."

The room stilled.

"Not wrongfully," she pressed. "But I could not hide it from you."

The General paled. "God above—"

"It was my doing!" Hetty cried, rushing to Theo’s side.

Theo clung to her sister. "We are bound, papa. No priest or promise can undo it."

"Women!" the General thundered. "Do you play with my honor?"

Aunt Lambert wept. "Martin—do not speak so!"

But Theo had fainted, her frail form collapsing against Hetty.

---

The next morning, General Lambert stormed into my lodgings.

"Your accomplices did not confess," he said coldly. "But Theo herself told me."

"Accomplices?" I countered. "Your children love you too well to betray justice. Must your pride dictate their hearts?"

"Do not twist my words, George. You swore not to seek her."

"I did not. She came to me."

"You hide behind a girl’s petticoat?"

"Very well—no more evasion. I broke the spirit of my vow, though not the letter. I revoke it now. No promise made in anguish should bind forever. Would you have us part, marry others, while our hearts lie elsewhere? Call that honor? I call it cruelty."

"Dishonor—"

"No!" I rose, fierce. "Were you my own father, I would not endure that word. Theo is mine before God. If she calls, I will answer. If she comes, this house is hers. You gave her to me—you cannot take her back. Must she wither to soothe your pride? Marry her to a prince tomorrow, and it is still betrayal. She is my wife, or she is dead. Choose."

His face darkened. "Then we part as enemies. My family will know you no more."

---

That afternoon, I met Jack Lambert at the Museum. Desperate for solace, I poured out my grief. To my surprise, he sided with me.

"Father questioned me at dinner," Jack said. "I told him: once consent is given, it cannot be revoked. Marriage is sacred, whether before a magistrate or Heaven itself."

He recounted the scene—Theo pale but resolute, Hetty’s whispered joke about sewing shirts, their father’s reluctant surrender.

"*You should have heard Jack’s sermon!*" Hetty had teased.

Theo, too weak for debates, had only wept upon the General’s shoulder.

"*I cited Pausanias,*" Jack said, grinning. "*When Ulysses took Penelope, her father begged her return. She covered her face—as Theo hid in our father’s coat!*"

Laughter had followed, though their mother vowed *she* would have left any suitor on the spot.

"*But I am no woman of spirit,*" Theo had murmured.

---

From despair to hope—how swiftly the tide turned! Jack’s words flooded me with joy. That night, I drank too freely, rhapsodizing beneath Theo’s window (or so Jack claimed). The next morning, though my head throbbed, my heart was light.

I learned later that the General, wounded by my words, had sought solace in prayer. There, kneeling, he saw the truth: Theo’s loyalty was no rebellion, but love. His own pride had blinded him. And so, before Jack had even pleaded, the battle was won.

For a father’s heart, when tender and true, cannot long resist the plea of happiness—especially when it echoes his own youthful ardor.
