"For joy or grief, for hope or fear,
For all hereafter, as for here,
In peace or strife, in storm or shine."

Edith moved through the house on tiptoe, silently checking every word Sholto spoke the following morning. She acted as if even the faintest clatter of a footstep might shatter the serious conference currently underway in the drawing room. By two o'clock, the doors remained shut, and the silence within seemed absolute. Suddenly, the sound of a man's hurried footsteps echoed down the staircase. Edith peeked out into the hallway.

"Well, Henry?" she asked, her expression questioning.

"Well!" he replied, somewhat curtly. "Come in to lunch."

"No, thank you. I've wasted far too much time here already."

"Then it isn't fully settled?" Edith asked, her spirits visibly drooping.

"Not at all," Henry answered firmly. "It never will be settled, if 'it' refers to whatever I suspect you mean. That simply cannot happen, Edith. Please stop worrying about it."

"But it would be so wonderful for everyone," Edith pleaded softly. "I would feel so secure regarding the children if I knew Margaret was settled nearby. As it stands, I'm constantly terrified she'll drift off to Cadiz."

"I promise," Henry said, "that when I marry, I will seek out a young lady skilled in managing children. That is all I can offer. Miss Hale will not have me, and I shall not ask her."

"Then what exactly have you been discussing?"

"A thousand topics you wouldn't comprehend: investments, leases, and the valuation of land."

"Oh, leave if that's all," Edith dismissed. "You and she will seem dreadfully dull if you've spent hours debating such tedious matters."

"Very well. I shall return tomorrow with Mr. Thornton to discuss further with Miss Hale."

"Mr. Thornton? Why does he need to be involved?"

"He is Miss Hale's tenant," Mr. Lennox stated, turning away. "And he wishes to surrender his lease."

"Oh! Very well. If those are the details, I cannot follow them, so please spare me."

"The only detail I require you to grasp is this: ensure the back drawing room remains undisturbed, just as it was today. Generally, the constant comings and goings of the children and servants prevent me from explaining business matters satisfactorily. The arrangements we must finalize tomorrow are of critical importance."

No one ever discovered why Mr. Lennox failed to appear the following day. True to his word, Mr. Thornton arrived punctually. After waiting nearly an hour, Margaret entered, appearing pale and deeply anxious.

"I am terribly sorry, Mr. Thornton, that Mr. Lennox is absent," she began hurriedly. "He could have handled this far better than I can. He serves as my advisor in this matter—"

"I apologize for having come if it causes you trouble. Should I go to Mr. Lennox's chambers to search for him?"

"No, thank you. I wished to tell you how grieved I am to learn that I must lose you as a tenant. However, Mr. Lennox assures me that circumstances are bound to improve—"

"Mr. Lennox understands little of the situation," Mr. Thornton said quietly. "Happy and fortunate in everything a man desires, he fails to comprehend what it feels like to realize one is no longer young, yet thrust back to the starting line requiring the hopeful energy of youth. To feel half a lifetime vanished, with nothing accomplished, save the bitter memory of wasted opportunities. Miss Hale, I prefer not to hear Mr. Lennox's assessment of my affairs. Those who are content and successful themselves are often too quick to dismiss the misfortunes of others."

"You are being unfair," Margaret said gently. "Mr. Lennox merely spoke of the high probability he believes exists for you to recover—and indeed, surpass—the losses you have suffered. Do not speak until I have finished, pray, do not."

She collected herself, then began rapidly flipping through legal documents and account statements in a trembling, hurried fashion.

"Oh! Here it is! He drafted a proposal for me," she continued, her voice steadying slightly. "It shows that if you were to accept a sum of my own—a thousand eight hundred and fifty-seven pounds currently idle in the bank, yielding me only two and a half percent—you could pay me significantly higher interest and continue operating Marlborough Mills."

Her voice had cleared, becoming more resolute. Mr. Thornton remained silent as she searched for the document outlining the security proposals. She was desperate to view the entire situation strictly as a business transaction, ensuring the primary benefit lay with her. Yet, while searching for the paper, her heartbeat faltered at the tone of Mr. Thornton's voice.

His voice was hoarse, trembling with intense emotion as he called out:

"Margaret!"

For an instant, she lifted her gaze, then quickly tried to hide her luminous eyes by pressing her forehead onto her hands. Stepping closer, he implored her with another tremulous, urgent cry.

"Margaret!"

Lower still sank her head; more completely concealed became her face, nearly resting upon the table before her. He approached her closely. Kneeling beside her, he brought his face level with her ear and whispered, gasping the words:

"Be careful. If you do not speak now, I shall claim you as my own in some strange, presumptuous manner. Send me away immediately if I must go... Margaret!"

At this third call, she turned her face toward him, still veiled by her small, white hands, and pressed it against his shoulder, hiding it there entirely. It was too exquisite a sensation for him to desire seeing either deep blushes or loving eyes. He embraced her tightly. They remained in silence together. Finally, she murmured in a broken voice:

"Oh, Mr. Thornton, I am not good enough!"

"Not good enough! Do not mock my own profound sense of unworthiness."

After a brief pause, he gently released her hands from her face and arranged her arms as he had once positioned them to shield him from the rioters.

"Do you remember, love?" he whispered. "And how I repaid you with my insolence the very next day?"

"I recall how wrongly I spoke to you—that is all."

"Look here! Raise your head. I have something to show you!"

Slowly, she faced him, glowing with a beautiful, radiant shame.

"Do you recognize these roses?" he asked, producing his pocketbook containing preserved, dried flowers.

"No!" she replied with innocent curiosity.

"Did I give them to you?"

"No! Vanity prevented you. You likely wore similar roses elsewhere."

She examined them, puzzled for a moment, then smiled faintly.

"They are from Helstone, are they not? I know the deep indentations around the leaves. Oh! Have you been there? When were you there?"

"I wanted to visit the place where Margaret grew into what she is, even during the darkest times, when I held no hope of ever calling her mine. I went there upon my return from Havre."

"You must give them to me," she said, attempting to take them from his hand with gentle force.

"Very well. Only you must pay me for them!"

"How shall I ever tell Aunt Shaw?" she whispered after a long, delightful silence.

"Let me speak to her."

"Oh, no! I owe her—but what will she say?"

"I can imagine. Her first exclamation will surely be, 'That man!'"

"Hush!" Margaret warned softly, "or I shall attempt to demonstrate your mother's indignant tones when she says, 'That woman!'"
