I lay down with my back against the rough flank of a tree whose bark smelled of rain and resin, and watched the day fold itself away. One by one, and in twos and threes, the children slipped from the grass like mice from a hearth-stone and climbed to their nests. Their bare feet made soft, brief sounds—thup of heel on bole, the faint rasp of fingers on lichen—until each small body was swallowed by leaf and shadow.

They were always like that: spent to the bone at nightfall, and at dawn as if poured newly into themselves. Sleep and waking were for them two doors in one house, both opening easily. I was tired too; my limbs had the dulled ache of healing, and my mouth still held the memory of fear like bitter herb. Yet I lay wakeful. Lona had not told me good night; and I knew, with the quiet certainty a hungry man has of bread that is on its way, that she would come.

When first I saw her again, the likeness struck me as sudden as a scent carried on wind. The princess’s face was there—those lines the moon loved—yet in Lona the dazzling edge was blunted into gentleness, and the beauty deepened as water deepens where it runs slower: with childlikeness, and with a mother’s gravity. She looked young, but not untried. How such a child should have been born of such a mother was hard to take in; and yet, happily, she had two parents—say rather three. Whatever had been broken in the blood had been mended somewhere else.

“She is occupied,” I told myself, listening to the forest’s small night-work—the creak of branch, the far cough of some beast, the whispering insects—“with the child of the woman I met fleeing.” Lona had already said the woman was not half mother enough.

At last Lona came. She sat beside me on the roots, and for a little while said nothing. Her delight was in her hands: she stroked my face and the backs of my fingers as if she were calming a dough that had risen too fast, easing it down into obedience. Her touch had the same certainty a good cook has with fire: she knew where pain would flare if she pressed, and where it would quiet if she held.

Then, as the moon lifted herself and began to look through the leaves, lighting Lona’s face in quivering patches, she began to tell me all that had befallen since I left. The moon made her features now silver, now leaf-shadowed green; thought lay in her brow, and care too—yet love redeemed and glorified even that, as salt turns plain food into something that belongs to a table.

After I left the orchard valley, she said, the giants began to believe a little more in the actual existence of their neighbours. The belief did not soften them; it sharpened them. Sometimes the Little Ones would see the huge brutes trampling furiously, perceiving—or imagining—some sign of their presence, while the children stood close by, unseen and safe, and laughed at the blind rage of it. In time, though, the giants’ spite took on a more useful shape: they began to destroy the trees.

They were not clever in malice, but they were strong. A giant would go at a trunk with his clubbing hands until the bark split and the sap bled. Sometimes they tore down branches heavy with fruit and crushed them underfoot, wasting what they could not eat. The orchard’s smells—apple-sweetness, pear perfume, the faint wine-breath of figs—were trampled into mud. The children lived on that fruit; it was their pantry, their market, their morning. To have it spoiled was like having the language of their hunger erased.

This drove the mother of them all—Lona spoke of her so, with the reverence one gives to an elder who knows seed-times and weather—to meditate counteraction. She set the sharpest of the children to listening at night. They were small, but their ears were keen; they could hear the difference between a squirrel’s scuttle and a wolf’s padding, between a branch complaining and a foot intending. And what they heard was this: the giants thought I was hidden somewhere near, meaning, when my strength returned, to come in the dark and kill them as they slept.

So the mother concluded the only way to stop the destruction was to give the giants ground for thinking the Little Ones had abandoned the place. They must remove into the forest—beyond the range of the giants, but within reach of their own trees, which they could visit by night. The plan’s objection was plain: the forest had little undergrowth to hide them. But she reflected that where birds made homes, the Little Ones could learn to live.

“They have eager sympathies,” Lona said softly, as if repeating a phrase she loved. “They can learn of the wildest creatures. Why not take refuge in the tree-tops? Why not, having lain once in low brushwood, seek now the lofty foliage?”

They had watched birds build. They had climbed trees for play. The forest trees were large, and did not look unkind. They went up nearer the sky than the orchard trees of the giants, and spread their arms—some even bent them down—as if inviting habitation. The children made a story of it at once, as children do: perhaps, in the top of the tallest tree, they might find the bird that laid the baby-eggs, sat on them till they were ripe, and then tumbled them down so the little ones could come out. In their mouths, the tale tasted true; in their minds, it was a promise.

Yes, they would build sleep-houses in the branches, where no giant would see, for never by any chance did a giant throw back his dull head to look up. The giants were people of ground and glare; sky made them squint. Then the bad giants would believe the Little Ones gone, and the children would gather apples and pears and figs and mesples and peaches while the giants slept, storing the orchard’s sweetness as carefully as a thrifty household puts by winter jars.

Thus reasoned the Lovers—for so, Lona said, they called themselves, and it was the most accurate name they had—and they adopted her suggestion eagerly. The result was that they became as much at home in the tree-tops as birds, and the giants, ere long, concluded they had frightened them out of the country. Then the giants forgot their trees and almost ceased again to believe in their small neighbours.

“Did you not observe,” Lona asked, “that many of the children are grown?”

I had not. The change had crept like yeast through dough: silent, patient, and suddenly there. But I could believe it easily.

“It is so,” she said. “And their minds have grown too. That has helped me not to fear it as much as I did.”

In the last of the short twilight—and later, when the moon shone—they would go down to the valley and gather fruit enough to serve for the next day. The giants never went out in twilight: to them it was darkness. They hated the moon; had they been able, they would have extinguished her, as a crude man snuffs a candle because it shows him his own ugliness.

But the children soon found that fruit gathered in the night was not altogether good the next day. It held the night’s chill too long. The cut stems wept and the sweetness dulled; bruised peaches turned faintly bitter by morning, and figs, once opened, lost the clean, green scent that makes them feel like a secret. There is a music to fresh food—crispness is a sound as well as a taste—and by midday that music was muted.

So the question rose among them, like hunger itself: might it not be better, instead of pretending to have left, to make the bad giants leave?

By then, exploring the forest, they had made acquaintance with the animals, and with most of them personally. Lona told it with a sort of quiet amusement: how strong as well as wise and docile some were, how swift and manageable others. The children, with their love-first way of speaking, addressed them at once as Brother or Sister—Brother Elephant, Sister Horse—until before long each creature had an individual name. It took longer before they said Brother Bear, but that too came; and the other day, Lona had heard one little fellow cry, “Ah, Sister Serpent!” to a snake that bit him because he played with it too roughly.

Most of them would have nothing to do with a caterpillar except watch it through its changes; but when at last it came from its retirement with wings, all would address it as Sister Butterfly, congratulating it on its metamorphosis with a word that meant something like repentance—turning, returning, becoming otherwise. They treated it as sacred, as if the smallest creature carried in its body the whole sermon of change, though they could not translate it into any other speech than wonder.

One moonlit evening, as they went to gather fruit, they came upon a woman seated on the ground with a baby in her lap—the woman I had met on my way to Bulika. The children took her for a giantess who had stolen one of their babies; for they regarded all babies as theirs, as naturally as they regarded apples as belonging to the tree until gathered. Filled with anger, they fell upon her in a multitude, beating her in a childish manner that was more confusing than cruel. She would have fled, but a boy threw himself down and held her by the feet.

Then, recovering her wits, she recognised them—the very children whose hospitality she had sought—and at once yielded the baby. Lona appeared and carried the child away in her bosom, as one carries warm bread to keep it from cooling.

The woman noted something that changed her mind: in striking her, the children had been careful not to hurt the baby; and as she surrendered her, she hugged and kissed the child just as they themselves longed to do. That sight softened them. They concluded she must be a giantess of the same kind as the good giant. The moment Lona had the baby, they brought the mother fruit—apples that snapped when bitten, pears with the smell of honeyed grass, figs that bled syrup at the stem—and began to show her every sort of childish attention, as if feeding her would prove their pardon.

Now the woman, Lona said, had been in perplexity where to go. She dared not return to Bulika, because the princess would certainly learn who had lamed her leopardess. The friendliness of the little people delighted her; she resolved to remain with them for the present. She would have no trouble with her infant—no crying to hush alone, no milk to coax when fear had dried her—and perhaps she might find a way back to her husband, who was rich in money and gems, and very seldom unkind.

Here, from Lona’s words and my own conjecture, I understood more of the woman. In Bulika, as among many peoples, there was a tradition: the princess lived in terror of the birth of an infant destined to her destruction. The Bulikans knew the story but not the princess’s means of holding on to youth and beauty; they did not know the price she paid in secret. Yet as her body deteriorated and required more of those frightful means, they took the apparent increase of her hostility to children for a sign she felt her doom near. No one dreamed of attempting anything against her, but the rumour nourished a hope of change as famine nourishes a dream of harvest.

In the woman’s mind rose an idea: to further the fulfilment of the shadowy prediction—or at least to use the myth for her own restoration to her husband. What seemed more likely than that fate lay with these very children? They were marvellously brave; the Bulikans were cowards, in abject terror of animals. If she could rouse the Little Ones to the ambition of taking the city, then, in the confusion, she might slip away from their small army, reach her house unrecognised, and there, hidden, await the result.

And should the children first succeed in expelling the giants, she could begin at once—while they were still flushed with victory—to suggest a loftier aim.

By disposition, indeed, the children were unfit for war. They hardly ever quarrelled, never fought. They loved every live thing, and hated either to hurt or to suffer. Still, they were easily influenced, and could certainly be taught any exercise within their strength. So the woman began at once with what looked like play: she set some of the smaller ones throwing stones at a mark. Soon all were engrossed with the new game and growing skilful. The first practical result was their use of stones in my rescue.

While gathering fruit, they found me asleep. They went home, held a council, and came the next day with elephants and horses. They overwhelmed the few giants watching me and carried me off. Lona’s voice altered as she told it—tender, proud, troubled all at once. Jubilant over victory, the smaller boys were childishly boastful, the bigger boys less ostentatious; the girls’ eyes flashed more than usual, but they spoke less. The woman of Bulika, no doubt, felt encouraged.

We talked the greater part of the night, chiefly of the children’s growth and what it might mean. I had long known Lona’s power of recognising truth; now I found myself astonished at her practical wisdom. Perhaps, had I been more of a child, I should have wondered less. Wisdom in that land did not always come by age; sometimes it came by listening—truly listening—to what life said in its own dialects: the crackle of leaf, the hush that warns of an enemy, the way a frightened baby breathes.

It was far from morning when I became aware of a slight fluttering and scrambling. I rose on my elbow. Through the moon-dappled gloom I saw many Little Ones descend from their nests. Their bodies made small noises against bark, like the whisper of a broom on a kitchen floor. They disappeared. In a few minutes all was still again.

“What are they doing?” I asked.

“They think,” Lona answered, “that, stupid as they are, the giants will search the wood. They are gone to gather stones with which to receive them. Stones are not plentiful in the forest, and they must scatter far to find enough. They will carry them to their nests, and from the trees attack as the giants come within reach.”

She spoke as one describing preparations for baking: you must gather fuel, you must fetch water, you must grind. War, in her mouth, sounded like household labour undertaken unwillingly for the sake of the children’s table.

“Knowing their habits, they do not expect them before morning. If they come, it will be the opening of a war of expulsion: one people or the other must go. The result is hardly doubtful.”

“We do not mean to kill them,” she added. “Indeed, their skulls are so thick I do not think we could. Not that killing would do them much harm; they are so little alive. If one were killed, his giantess would not remember him beyond three days.”

“Do the children throw so well that it might happen?” I asked.

“Wait till you see,” she said, and there was in the phrase a touch of mother-pride, like a cook’s satisfaction in a knife well honed. “But I have not yet told you of a strange thing that happened the night before last.”

We had come home from fruit-gathering, she said, and slept in our nests, when horrid noises of beasts fighting roused us. The moon was bright. In a moment our trees glittered with staring little eyes as the children peered down. Two huge leopardesses were below: one perfectly white, the other covered in black spots. They worried and tore each other with a fury that made the night’s air itself seem shredded. To judge by her back, the spotted creature must have been climbing when the other sprang upon her. When Lona first saw them, they were just under her own tree, rolling over and over in the moonlight, their bodies slick with blood.

At first the children enjoyed the spectacle, taking sides as if it were a game, for they had never seen such beasts and thought they played. But by degrees their roaring and growling almost ceased, and Lona saw they were in deadly earnest. She found herself wishing neither would be left able to climb a tree.

Then, when the children saw blood pouring from flanks and throats, what did they do? They scurried down to comfort them. Gathering in a great crowd, they began to pat and stroke the terrible creatures, speaking to them as they spoke to all living things, as if a name could be a salve.

Lona climbed down too, calling to them, but they were too absorbed to heed. Before she could reach them, the white leopardess stopped fighting and sprang among the children with a hideous yell. The children flew up into the trees like birds. Before Lona could regain her branch, the beasts were at it again.

Then Whitey—so Lona called her—had the best of it. Spotty ran away as fast as she could. Whitey came and lay down at the foot of Lona’s tree. A minute or two later she was up again, walking about as if she suspected the spotted one lurked near. Lona woke often, and every time she looked out, she saw the white leopardess keeping watch. In the morning, she went away.

“I know both the beasts,” I said. “Spotty is a bad beast. She hates the children and would kill every one of them. But Whitey loves them. She ran at them only to frighten them away, lest Spotty should seize one. No one need be afraid of Whitey.”

By this time the Little Ones were returning with their stones. They came with much noise, for they no longer cared to keep quiet now that they counted themselves at open war with the giants. Each carried what he could: round river-stones found in some distant bed, angular fragments prised from roots, hard lumps that would fly true. They mounted to their nests with difficulty under the burdens, like small people hauling sacks of grain up to a loft. Once settled, as if all that labour had been only a kind of prayer, they slept at once.

Lona retired to her tree. I lay where I was and slept the better for thinking the white leopardess might still be somewhere in the wood, a silent guardian in the dark acoustics of branches.

I woke soon after sunrise and lay pondering. The day’s sounds came gradually: a far call, a nearer flutter, the thin crack of a twig under some small foot. Two hours passed. Then, in truth, the giants began to appear.

They came in straggling companies of three and four, until I counted over a hundred—great loutish shapes moving between trunks, making noise enough for a world that had forgotten how to listen. The children were still asleep. To call them would draw the giants’ attention; I kept quiet as long as they did not discover me.

But one came blundering upon me. He stumbled, fell, rose again, and began to search about with his dull eyes and duller mind.

I sprang to my feet and struck him in the middle of his huge body. The roar he gave tore the morning. It roused the children like a bell.

At once a storm—hail made of stone—fell from the trees. Not a single stone struck me; not one missed the giant. He fell and lay.

Others drew near, and the storm extended. Each purblind creature, entering the range of a garrisoned tree, became a target for converging stones. The air clacked and cracked with impacts; the forest took on a harsh, percussive music, and then, as giant after giant went down, the rhythm shifted: the children’s throwing became almost like drumming, a fierce defense played on the skulls of those who would not look up.

In a short time almost every giant was prostrate. Then from the tops of fifty trees rose a jubilant paean of bird-song. The children sang as birds sing after a hawk has passed—sound returning to its proper place, the soundscape cleansed of trampling.

Many elephants came hurrying up at their call. The children descended the trees like monkeys; in a moment every elephant carried three or four of them on his back. Thus loaded, the elephants began to walk over the giants, who lay and roared.

The giants’ noise went on and on—gross complaint without meaning. Losing patience with it, the elephants gave them a few blows with their trunks, not to kill but to silence, as one raps a barrel that will not stop booming. Then they left them.

Until night the bad giants remained where they had fallen, silent and motionless at last, as if the forest itself had swallowed their voices.

In the morning they were gone—every one. The children saw no more of them. They removed to the other end of the orchard valley and never after ventured into the forest.
