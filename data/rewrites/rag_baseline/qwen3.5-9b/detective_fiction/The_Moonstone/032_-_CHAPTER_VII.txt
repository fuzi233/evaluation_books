The correspondence preceding this account serves as sufficient justification for my decision to bypass the tragic conclusion of Lady Verinder's life, offering merely the stark, unadorned announcement of her demise that closed my fifth chapter. With a resolve to confine my narrative strictly to the boundaries of my own direct experience, I must now recount the intervening month—a period of suspended animation between my aunt's passing and the inevitable collision of paths with Rachel Verinder. That singular encounter, occurring shortly thereafter, precipitated a brief cohabitation under the same roof and set in motion a sequence of events regarding her betrothal to Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite that demands meticulous documentation. Once this final, agonizing thread of familial circumstance is fully unraveled, my mandate shall be fulfilled; I shall have exhausted every testimony available to me as an unwilling, yet factual, observer of history.

Lady Verinder's mortal remains were transported from the capital to rest within the secluded cemetery adjacent to the church in her ancestral park. Though I received an invitation to attend the interment alongside the extended family, the gravity of the situation rendered attendance impossible. My religious convictions demanded a period of reflection incompatible with the haste required to recover from such a profound shock. Furthermore, the prospect of hearing the service conducted by the rector of Frizinghall presented a moral dilemma; having previously witnessed this ecclesiastical castaway participating in the frivolous games of Lady Verinder's whist-table, I doubted whether I could justify my presence at a holy ceremony led by such a man, regardless of my physical readiness to travel.

Consequently, Lady Verinder's death transferred guardianship of her daughter to her brother-in-law, the elder Mr. Ablewhite. Appointed by the will to act as custodian until Rachel reached maturity or wedded, he promptly informed his son of this newly acquired dominion. Within ten days of the tragedy, the secret of the engagement dissolved into public knowledge within the family circle. The paramount concern for Mr. Ablewhite Senior—another confirmed castaway in the theater of life—became the strategic maneuvering required to render his authority palatable to the wealthy heiress destined for his son.

From the outset, Rachel presented obstacles regarding her place of residence. The estate in Montagu Square was tainted by the calamity of her mother's death, while the Yorkshire home bore the indelible stain of the scandal surrounding the lost Moonstone. Neither location offered refuge. Yet, her presence at Frizinghall would inevitably curtail the revelry of her cousins, the Misses Ablewhite, leading her to defer her visit indefinitely. Thus, the proposal emerged from the elder Mr. Ablewhite to secure a furnished residence in Brighton. His wife, an invalid daughter, and Rachel were to inhabit it together, with the patriarch joining them later in the season. Their social sphere would be restricted to a handful of elderly acquaintances, while Mr. Godfrey would traverse the London-Brighton line at their beck and call.

I chronicle this aimless peripateticism, this insatiable restlessness of the body coupled with the appalling stagnation of the soul, solely to arrive at the pivotal result. The providential instrumentality that reunited Rachel Verinder and myself was none other than the acquisition of the Brighton house.

Mrs. Ablewhite is a figure of considerable stature, possessing a pale complexion and a defining characteristic: from the hour of her birth, she has never undertaken action for herself. She has navigated existence by accepting assistance and adopting the opinions of others. In a spiritual context, she represents a hopeless void; there exists absolutely no obstructive material upon which to work. Mrs. Ablewhite would receive the doctrines of the Grand Lama of Tibet with the same placid receptivity as she does my own teachings. She located the Brighton lodging by lingering in a London hotel, reclining on a sofa, and summoning her son. She procured the necessary domestic staff by dining in bed one morning, granting her maid a holiday contingent upon the girl's promise to "begin enjoying herself by fetching Miss Clack." I found her leisurely fanning herself in her dressing-gown at eleven o'clock. "Drusilla, dear, I require servants. You possess such acumen; please procure them for me." Surveying the untidy chamber, the tolling of church bells for weekday services prompted a gentle admonition. "Oh, aunt!" I replied sorrowfully. "Is *this* befitting a Christian Englishwoman? Must the transition from temporal life to eternity occur in *this* manner?" My aunt responded, "I shall don my gown, Drusilla, if you will kindly assist me." What further discourse was possible? I have achieved wonders with murderers, yet I have never advanced an inch with Aunt Ablewhite. "Where is the list," I inquired, "of the servants you require?" She shook her head, lacking even the energy to maintain one. "Rachel has it, dear," she stated, gesturing to the adjoining room. Entering, I beheld Rachel for the first time since our separation in Montagu Square.

She appeared pitifully diminutive and emaciated, clad in deep mourning. Were I to attach significance to such ephemeral attributes as personal appearance, I might note that her complexion suffers whenever not framed by a border of white linen. Yet, what are our complexions and features? Mere hindrances and pitfalls besetting women on their ascent to higher truths. To my astonishment, Rachel rose as I entered, advancing to greet me with an outstretched hand.

"I am delighted to see you," she declared. "Drusilla, I have frequently spoken foolishly and rudely to you on prior occasions. I beg your forgiveness. I trust you will grant me absolution."

My countenance, I presume, betrayed the shock I felt at this reversal. She flushed momentarily before elaborating.

"In my late mother's lifetime," she continued, "her friends were not invariably my own. Now that I have lost her, my heart seeks solace among the people she cherished. She loved you. Strive to be my friend, Drusilla, if you are capable."

To any mind properly constituted, this motivation appears profoundly disturbing. Here, in Christian England, stands a bereaved young woman with such limited discernment regarding sources of genuine comfort that she expects to find it among her mother's associates! Here is a relative of mine, awakening to her deficiencies toward others not through conviction or duty, but through sentiment and impulse! Deplorable indeed—but suggestive of hope for one experienced in the arduous work of reform. I resolved to ascertain the extent of the transformation wrought by her mother's death. As a practical test, I probed her concerning her engagement to Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite.

After initially receiving her advances with maximal cordiality, I seated myself beside her on the sofa at her request. We discoursed on family affairs and future plans, deliberately excluding the one trajectory destined for matrimony. Despite my efforts to steer the conversation, she resolutely declined the implication. Any overt reference to the topic at this nascent stage of reconciliation would have been premature. Moreover, I had gleaned all requisite intelligence: she was no longer the reckless, defiant entity I had encountered during my ordeal in Montagu Square. This alteration alone encouraged me to assume charge of her future conversion, commencing with earnest warnings against the hasty formation of marital bonds and progressing toward loftier aspirations. Observing her anew with this fresh interest, recalling the precipitous nature of her acceptance of Mr. Godfrey's matrimonial proposals, I felt a solemn duty to intervene with fervor, confident of achieving extraordinary results. Speed was essential. I returned immediately to the matter of the required servants.

"Where is the list, dear?"

Rachel produced it.

"Cook, kitchen-maid, housemaid, and footman," I read aloud. "My dear Rachel, these positions are needed only temporarily, for the duration of your guardian's lease. Securing individuals of character and capacity willing to accept such transient engagements in London presents significant difficulties. Has the house in Brighton been secured?"

"Yes. Godfrey has taken it; and persons in the house urged him to hire them as servants. He deemed them unsuitable for us and returned without settling anything."

"And you possess no experience in these matters yourself, Rachel?"

"None whatsoever."

"And Aunt Ablewhite will not exert herself?"

"No, poor dear. Do not blame her, Drusilla. I believe she is the only truly happy woman I have ever encountered."

"There are gradations in happiness, darling. We must discuss this subject sometime. Meanwhile, I shall address the difficulty regarding the servants. Your aunt will draft a letter to the household staff—"

"She will sign it if I compose it for her; the effect is identical."

"Precisely the same outcome. I shall obtain the letter and depart for Brighton tomorrow."

"How exceedingly kind of you! We shall join you as soon as you are prepared. And you will stay, I hope, as *my* guest. Brighton is so vibrant; you are certain to enjoy it."

In these words, the invitation was issued, and the glorious prospect of intervention unfolded before me.

It was mid-week when this occurred. By Saturday afternoon, the house was ready for occupancy. During this brief interval, I scrutinized not only the characters but also the religious convictions of all unemployed servants applying to me, successfully selecting those whose consciences I could approve. I furthermore identified and visited two serious friends residing in the town, individuals to whom I could entrust the pious objective driving my journey to Brighton.
