Margaret Brandt had always kept herself apart from the people of Sevenbergen. Her quiet dignity, her silence, and her refusal to mingle freely with others had been mistaken for pride—something she heard whispered about in hushed tones across the town. And now, as the truth of her condition began to show, she knew that those same whispers would turn into venomous tongues, eager to wound her beyond repair. The thought haunted her like a shadow: when the world could no longer deny what was plain, how would they mock her? How would they tear her down?

For weeks, she had withdrawn into the house, avoiding daylight, even on rare outings. She did not deceive herself—not in her heart or conscience. Though she knew little of legal fine points, she understood one thing clearly: a betrothal was a binding promise, as sacred as any marriage contract. It could not be broken lightly, not by either party. She knew that such unions had long been recognized by both Church and State, and that children born of such bonds were considered legitimate. But there was no document to prove hers.

Gerard had taken it. And Gerard was gone—far away, across seas and mountains, where her voice could not reach him.

She despised herself for the careless act that had left her defenseless before the world’s judgment. She had never read Horace’s words—“The more you irritate, the more you are hated”—but she lived them. With a mind sharp and clear, untouched by sentimentality, she saw exactly how the world would see her. No marriage lines in hand, only a swelling belly and a child soon to come into the world—what story could she tell that would silence the gossips? “I have my papers,” she might say, “but I cannot show them.” Would any woman believe that? Not one.

And yet, Margaret was not a woman who craved men’s approval. She cared nothing for their praise or scorn. What mattered to her was the regard of other women—the quiet, modest women of Holland, whose eyes saw deeper than mere appearances. To be judged by them as weak, reckless, unchaste—that was her true fear. And now, the slander wore a mask of truth. There was no escape from it.

She was not strong-minded in the way the modern world might expect. Though raised early to manage a household, though firm in some things, she was fragile in others—especially in matters of reputation. Like so many noblewomen of her time, she was a leaf caught in a storm, trembling at every breath of public opinion. And the storm was coming.

Her suffering was not loud, but deep—silent, constant, gnawing at her soul. Hours passed in stillness, her thoughts a torment. Even when her father spoke to her, she replied mechanically, her mind elsewhere. Then, without warning, her face would flush, her body tremble—though he said nothing to provoke it. His words meant nothing. It was the dread within that burned her cheeks red.

It reached its peak after she returned from Margaret van Eyck’s house. There, among women, her secret was discovered. The old artist, reclusive and kind, might keep silent. But Catherine—Catherine lived in the center of a busy family, surrounded by neighbors who knew everything. If she spoke, the whole town would know.

Yet, Catherine had spoken gently. That tiny spark of kindness gave Margaret hope. Perhaps she would be spared. But then came silence. No word. No comfort. And in that silence, Margaret realized: Catherine would not be her ally. Without love or loyalty to balance her instinct to gossip, she would betray her.

Then the desire to flee took root—first a whisper, then a cry, then a desperate longing. But how could she persuade her father? He was old, frail, rooted in the place where he had lived all his life. To move him would be a burden he could not bear.

There was only one path: confess to him. Better that than leave him behind, lying and ashamed. Yet, the thought of speaking to a man—even her own father—about such things filled her with horror. She wavered between flight and confession, torn by agony.

And then, one day, the unthinkable entered her mind: *End it.*

Foolish words, perhaps—but sometimes, the most foolish-sounding thoughts carry the deepest weight. One of Catherine’s fears—those wide, dark canals that ran through the city—now seemed like an invitation to peace. She stood by the water one afternoon, staring into its still, deep surface. It looked calm. Still. Unmoving. And she—so weary, so afraid—felt drawn to it. No more pain. No more shame. No more fear.

But if Gerard had not loved her—if he had not risked his life for her—she might have stepped in. As it was, she knelt beside the canal, hands clasped, heart pounding, and prayed aloud to God. “Oh, selfish wretch,” she whispered, “to leave your father! Oh, wicked wretch, to kill your child, and make poor Gerard’s suffering all in vain!” And she rose, resolved. “I will tell him. Before this sun sets.”

She rushed home, afraid her courage would vanish before she reached him.

Peter Brandt, though learned in medicine and nature, was simple in domestic matters. Since Margaret turned sixteen, she had ruled the house with quiet authority. So it was strange to see her now—hesitant, trembling, speaking in broken sentences to the man who, in household affairs, was less important than Martin Wittenhaagen or even the young girl who washed the dishes each morning.

“Father,” she said, “I must speak to you.”

“Speak on, girl,” he replied.

“Will you listen? And—and not try to excuse me?”

“We all have faults, Margaret. You no more than the rest. Fewer, unless I am blinded by love.”

“Oh, father,” she murmured, “I am a foolish girl. I meant well, but I’ve done wrong—greatly wrong. And now I must bear the shame. But I love you, and I beg you—will you forgive me, and still love your motherless daughter?”

“That you may count on,” Peter said cheerfully.

“Do not smile,” she pleaded. “If you do, I cannot speak. I will only make you sad.”

“Why, what is the matter?”

“Disgrace is coming upon this house. It stands at the door. And I—I am the cause. Turn your head away, father. I—I let Gerard take my marriage lines.”

“Is that all?” Peter asked. “It was an oversight.”

“A madwoman’s deed,” she whispered. “But worse is coming. Oh, father, turn away. Please.”

“Foolish wench,” he muttered, but obeyed.

She trembled, her face aflame, and began to stammer: “I saw Gerard as my husband—we were betrothed—and he was in great danger. I thought I had killed him. And I—oh, if you were my mother, I might find courage. You would question me. But you say nothing.”

“Why, Margaret,” he said, “what is all this? Why are your cheeks so red? You’re speaking to your father, not a stranger.”

“Why am I burning? Because—because—father, kill me! Send me to heaven! Let Martin shoot me with his arrow! Then the gossips will come and tell you why I blush today. And when I am dead, I hope you will love your girl again—for your wife’s sake.”

“Give me your hand, mistress,” Peter said, his voice firm but gentle.

She offered it, trembling. He took it, felt her pulse, and frowned.

“Alas, no,” she said. “It is my soul that burns, not my body. I cannot stay here. I will not.”

She wrung her hands, agitated.

“Be calm,” he said softly. “Do not torment yourself. You wish to leave Sevenbergen? Why not? If it troubles you so much, we can go. You are fevered, admit it.”

“What?” she cried. “You would leave without asking why? Without knowing?!”

And suddenly, she fell to her knees beside him, clutching his sleeve, then his arm, then his shoulder, pleading with tears in her eyes. “Please, father! Leave this place! Do not ask why! I cannot say it. I would rather die.”

“Who seeks your secrets, child?” he asked. “Am I not the man who has spent his life studying nature? And as for leaving Sevenbergen—what keeps me here? Respect? Gratitude? They call me quacksalver, wizard, fool. They give their thanks and honors to those who harm them. And what does it matter to me where we live? Choose your own place, as your mother did before you.”

She threw her arms around him, weeping silently. She was spared—yet in her sorrow, she almost wished she had told him everything.

After a while, Peter insisted she take a draught he brought from his cabinet. She drank it obediently, to please him. It was a calming remedy, given under a mistaken belief—but it helped. She slept deeply, and awoke refreshed. That very day, she began packing.

A week later, they left Sevenbergen, taking all their belongings to Rotterdam. They lodged above a tailor’s shop on Brede-Kirk Straet. Only one person in Tergou knew their destination: the Burgomaster. He kept the knowledge to himself. What use he would make of it, no one could guess—not even he. But time, as it often does, would reveal all.
