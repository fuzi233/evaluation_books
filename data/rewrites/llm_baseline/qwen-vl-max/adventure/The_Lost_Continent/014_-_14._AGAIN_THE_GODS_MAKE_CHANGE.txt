It would be tedious to recount how, with only a handful of seasoned warriors, I charged again and again until we shattered the rebel horde—fifteen times our size. But let it be known that their ranks dissolved into chaos when they realized no one returned from the mine beneath the city walls, a passage they had believed their secret advantage. The initial cries of panic inside the city soon turned to shouts of triumph. And yet, despite their numbers, these rebels were untrained, poorly armed, and leaderless—each man believing himself equal to any other, so no one could rally them.

When fear struck, it spread like a plague through their ragged lines. No commander stood to order a retreat, no voice to inspire resistance. My strategy was simple: never stop, never defend. We attacked one flank, burst through the center, then darted to the opposite side, striking before they could regroup. By keeping them constantly off balance, confused and running, we sowed terror deeper than steel or fire ever could.

The rout began at the edges. Men fled toward the forests and ruined fields, while those still standing longed only for escape. This was not a feat of arms, nor would any disciplined general claim it as such. It was raw instinct, seizing the moment. Had we met them in open battle on their own ground, they might have held fast—brutal, desperate, and countless. They were Atlanteans, just as the citizens within the city were, and their fury burned bright from years of oppression. But now, broken by panic, they scattered.

With the siege lifted, it was clear the rebellion could end—if handled wisely. Harshness only fuels revolt. Mercy, even if hard to bear, may bring peace.

We pursued the fleeing rebels to ensure no pause in their flight, driving home the lesson of defeat. But the chase had taken us far from the city, and after fighting through the relentless heat of the day, my men were spent. I decided to make camp among half-ruined buildings, which offered shelter and a crude fortification. Fortuitously, a herd of small cloven-hoofed horses, spooked during the chaos, wandered into our lines. We killed five before they escaped—and with that, we had supper. Fires were lit, food cooked, and the night settled in.

Sentries paced the perimeter, calling to each other in the dark. Wounded men sat near the flames, tending their wounds. Officers gathered around me, reviewing the day’s charges, analyzing tactics, reliving the fight. Soldiers delight in such talk; to others, it is mere noise.

As sleep drew near, snores rose from every corner. My officers were exhausted, lingering only out of respect. Yet I dreaded silence—dreaded being alone with my thoughts. I pressed them to speak, though their eyes grew heavy. But they were spared the shame of falling asleep mid-sentence.

A sentry approached, saluting. “My lord,” he said, “a woman has come from the city. We caught her trying to enter the bivouac.”

“What is her name?”

“She refuses to say.”

“Does she have business?”

“She claims none. Only demands to see you.”

“Bring her to the fire,” I ordered. Then, thinking better of it—knowing this woman likely carried news meant for my ears alone—I added, “Stay.” I rose and followed the guard to the edge of the camp.

“Where is she?” I asked.

“My comrades hold her. She might be a rebel spy, intent on killing you. The Empress values you highly, and we know the cruelty of her torturers.”

“Your caution is honest,” I replied. Then he showed me the woman.

She wore a hood and cloak, wrapped tightly, but anyone who knew Nais—as I did—could recognize Ylga, her twin sister, even under layers of cloth. I told the guards to release her without question, then led her beyond the camp, out of earshot.

“You’ve come for something urgent, Ylga?”

“You know me, then? There must be more between us than mere acquaintance, Deucalion, if you guessed who walked beneath all these wrappings.”

I let it pass. “What do you want?”

“Aye,” she said, bitterly, “that’s my reward. All your concern is for the message, none for the messenger. Well, good sir, you are no longer husband to Phorenice.”

“This is news.”

“And true. She has divorced you, cast you off, rejected you. After her first rage, pain and punishment follow.”

“The Empress can do no wrong. You must speak respectfully of her.”

“Oh, spare me that old lie! It sickens me. She was mad with love for you once. Now she’s mad with jealousy. She knows you gave Nais some of your priestly magic—enough to keep her asleep until you choose to awaken her, even if centuries pass. And if you wish to know how she learned this… there’s another airshaft beside the one where you spoke. It leads to a cell with another prisoner. He heard everything and thought to gain freedom by telling. But his tale came too late. With the morning ceremonies, he was forgotten. When his keeper finally remembered him, Phorenice had already married another, and poor Nais had likely taken her sleeping draught. Still, the fools tried to sell the story. They paid for it with their heads. And then Phorenice acted as you might expect.”

She paused, then continued, “She declared: ‘Shall I cut off his head? Pah! Shall I hand him over to my tormentors? He wouldn’t flinch. No—he must suffer worse. I will sever his right hand and left foot. Then he’ll be no warrior. I’ll drive him crippled into the wild lands, where he’ll learn Fear. Beasts will hunt him. Volcanic fires will burn his rest. He’ll know hunger, breathe poisoned air. And always, always, he’ll remember that I have Nais near me—alive, locked in stone, to toy with as I please.’ That is what she said, Deucalion. Now I ask you—will you return to face that?”

“No,” I said. “I do not plan to be maimed and left to live.”

“So you’re wise,” she replied. “And since I still care for you, I’ve made a plan. Does the name Tob ring a bell?”

“Captain of Tatho’s navy?”

“That same Tob. A rough, tar-smelling man, but honest. Tonight, he sails for unknown lands—intending to found a kingdom with himself as king. He can’t earn enough in Atlantis anymore, and he won’t let his wife and children starve. He told me this at the harbor, when I claimed I needed transport for you. With me under his control, he spoke freely. You’ve impressed him, De ucalion. He called you—quite frankly—the recruit he’d been waiting for. Whether you join him or not, he said he’d go to hell to serve you.”

“I fought sea beasts with him once.”

“Well, go back to it. Believe me, it’s your only chance. I’d hate to hear the iron sear your flesh. I’ve arranged with Tob to slip past the harbor forts before the chain is raised. He’s bold, fearless in darkness. He’ll wait at a point we agreed upon. Come, Deucalion—let me show you the way.”

“My girl,” I said, “I owe you much for this.”

“Oh, your thanks?” she scoffed. “Keep them. I didn’t come out here in the dark and danger for gratitude. I knew there’d be little else.”

She tugged my sleeve. “Now show me your pace. They’ll miss you soon, and we don’t want questions.”

So we set off, Ylga and I, leaving the campfires behind. I carried my weapons, ready for attack—beast or man. Few words passed between us, only warnings of the path ahead. Once, we crossed a rivulet of scalding water flowing from a hot spring. I asked, “Are you sure-footed?”

“No,” she admitted, reaching for my hand.

I helped her across, then released her. She sighed, slowly pulling away. Then we walked on—hour after hour, league after league—in silence.

At last, we crested a rise. Below, through the trees, I saw the gleam of the great estuary where Atlantis stood. The ground was soggy, the trees bristling with thorns. Ylga’s breath came in ragged gasps. When I offered to support her, she pushed me away. “I’m not weak,” she said sharply. “Don’t think that’s why you want to touch me.”

Soon, we broke through the trees. The worst was behind us. Ahead, the ship lay anchored a mile offshore—a strange sight under the dim stars. We moved along the beach toward her.

Tob kept watch. We were challenged at once, ordered to halt and state our purpose. But when he recognized us, he welcomed us. He called his crew, loosened the anchor rope, and brought the ship close to shore. Two oars were thrust out to help us aboard.

I turned to Ylga. “I shall never forget what you’ve done tonight. If the High Gods allow me to return to Atlantis, you’ll feel my gratitude.”

“I don’t want to return. I’m tired of this life.”

“But you have your palace, your servants, your wealth. Phorenice won’t take those from you.”

“Oh, I could return tomorrow and be fan-girl again. But I don’t want to.”

“I warn you—it’s no place for a refined woman like you. I’ve been viceroy of Yucatan. I know hardship. This will be worse. Few survive the early colonization. Only the strongest endure. They gain new scars every day.”

“I don’t care. And I can work. I can cook, shoot arrows, sew clothes from animal hides with sinew thread. You see only the fine dress, the fan-girl, and think me useless. Bah, Deucalion! Don’t let anyone tell me about your perfection. You know nothing of women.”

“I’ve learned all I need to know about one woman. Because of her memory, I cannot ask her sister to come with me.”

“Aye,” she said, bitter again, “hurt my pride. I knew I’d never be first. But no decent man would remind me.”

“Then I must go alone. I’d rather carry your respect than your hatred.”

“That’s the most humiliating thing. I can’t hate you. I should, after the way you’ve treated me. But I don’t. I grow fonder. If I thought you could stay alive and whole in Atlantis, I wouldn’t mention Tob’s impatience.”

Suddenly, she threw her arms around my neck and kissed me fiercely. “There—that’s farewell, dear. See? I’m reckless. I don’t care what I do now. You can’t despise me more than you already have.”

She ran back into the trees.

“This is madness,” I said. “I must escort you safely to the city gate, then return.”

“You don’t need to worry. The broken-hearted are always safe. Besides, I have my way. At least you’ll remember me now. You’ll never forget that kiss.”

“Farewell, Ylga,” I called. “May the High Gods keep you in their care.”

No answer came. She was gone.

I turned and waded to the ship, climbed aboard via the steering oars. Tob gave the order to haul up the anchor and pull away.

“Greeting, my lord,” he said. “I wished you’d arrived earlier. We’ve few hands and slow speed. The sooner we’re beyond pursuit, the safer my wife and children in the after-castle. I haven’t sailed in a ship like this in ages. She’s no machine, no steering mannikin. Look at her fittings. I suspect rot in her hull. But she’s the best I could buy. If she reaches the land I’ve chosen, we’ll build a home—or die. She’s been in the Egypt trade—known for rotting wood.”

“You had enough crew before I joined?”

“Oh yes. Fifty strong men, eight women—all packed tight. Getting them out of the city was trouble. The port captain nearly stripped us bare before letting so many fighters leave. Times have changed. Sea trade’s dying. Skilled sailors take wives and go elsewhere. I barely got half a dozen to help sail this. The rest are raw landmen. If I land half alive, I’ll be lucky.”

“Still, with luck and good winds, we’ll reach Europe soon.”

Tob slapped his leg. “No savage Europe for me. I once discovered islands north of Europe, separated by a strait. I called them the Tin Islands—tin ore litters the beaches. Storm drove me there. I said nothing when I returned. Now it’s useful. No one in Atlantis knows of them except me. We’ll fight for our ground, build a town, a kingdom.”

“With Tob as king?”

“I’ve dreamed of it for years. But I know my place. I’d gladly serve under you, my lord. Though I thought the lady at the harbor said you’d bring a wife. I’d have mentioned it earlier. The natives on my Tin Islands go naked, stain themselves blue with woad. Filthy, brutish. Strong, though—good slaves. But one grows numb to their looks before wanting children with them.”

“I am still married to Phorenice.”

Tob grinned. “The Gods bless her. But mariners believe marriage doesn’t cross seas. Still, that’s neither here nor there. Back to the Tin Islands—they’ll please you. Kingdoms aren’t hard to carve there. There are beasts—cave-bears, cave-tigers, river-horses, snakes—but the great lizards avoid the land. Birds? None dangerous. It’ll be a desirable realm.”

He sighed. “I’ve imagined myself king, truly. And no one I’d break this journey for but you, Deucalion. But I don’t regret it. This breeze and ebb carry us well. See those fire baskets on the forts? We’re abreast now. By dawn, the city will vanish. The flood tide won’t begin till then. But unless the wind strengthens, we’ll anchor when the tide turns. Tides run fierce here. And there are treacherous rips near the Tin Islands—wait until we reach them.”

At dawn, fearful glances turned backward. The waters sparkled, mountains blazed in the distance. All expected Phorenice’s navy to pursue. I shared their dread. Many swore they’d fight to the ship’s sinking before obeying her again. But their courage was not wasted. During the full tide, we drifted barely forward—no sails, no oars seen. Then, when the current reversed, we surged onward, greatly relieved.

Tob’s wife insisted on bringing drink, urging libations to herself as future queen. Tob cuffed her back into the after-castle, slamming the hatch. “We’re done with that nonsense,” he snapped. “My Lord Deucalion will be king of our new land. A proper king—just ask those who’ve sailed with him.”

Beneath his words, I sensed regret. But I said nothing. My plans were already formed.

We sailed down the great estuary. Others cheered at the empty seas. I mourned the once-thriving shipping lanes.

They’d started with only two days’ provisions. So, even after leaving the estuary, we coasted, stopping at rivers and hidden shores to hunt and fish. “When winter comes,” Tob said, “its gales will be too strong for this ship. I’ll haul her ashore, build a camp, grow grain, thresh it, then resume. That’s the custom. I’ll do it—unless you advise otherwise.”

Two months later, I saw my chance. “Tob,” I said, “I am weak, defenseless, at your mercy. But what if I don’t go all the way to the Tin Islands? What if I abandon your kingship?”

He brightened. “Aye, you’re weak, my lord. We know. But what’s your meaning?”

“I do not wish to leave Atlantis. If you’ll put me down anywhere on this coast facing Europe, I’ll bless you in the eyes of the Gods. I wish I could give you money or supplies—but I have nothing.”

He nodded slowly. “Very well. We’ll find a place.”
