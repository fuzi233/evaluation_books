Carmilla had dug her heels in, refusing point-blank to suffer even a lady’s maid to sleep a single night inside her chamber. To head off any repeat of the unaccountable midnight wanderings that had left the household on edge, my father arranged for a strapping serving girl to drag a straw pallet right up against her door, her shoulder pressed to the oak panels so closely no one could slip out without rousing her. The night passed without a sound. No soft scrabbling of a turning lock, no bare feet pattering down the dark corridor.
By dawn, Father had already sent for the old physician without breathing a word of it to me. I had not the faintest suspicion anything was amiss until Madame escorted me to the library that morning, and there I found the grave little white-haired doctor I’d met once before, his spectacles slipping down his thin nose as he waited, his black medical bag sitting heavy on the table beside him.
I told him everything: the creeping cold that slithered up my calves in the dead of night, the weight on my chest that stole my breath, the sharp, stabbing pricks at my throat that felt exactly like two fine needles driving through my skin. The more I spoke, the stonier his face grew. We stood face to face in the deep stone window recess, the fog of the early morning pressing against the glass, and when I fell silent, he leaned his shoulders back against the cold wall, his eyes fixed on mine so intently I flinched—there was sharp concern there, tangled thick with a flicker of unmistakeable, ancient horror.
He stood silent for a full minute, thinking hard, before he asked Madame if he might speak to my father. Father came in smiling, teasing that he expected the doctor to call him an old fool for dragging him all the way out to the schloss, but that smile died fast, fading to a pale shadow when the doctor waved him over to the window recess, his face still grim.
The two of them talked in low, urgent tones, too quiet to carry across the huge, high-ceilinged library, its shelves crammed with leather-bound tomes that had sat untouched for a hundred years. Madame and I hung back at the far end, burning with curiosity, but the deep hollow of the window niche swallowed their voices whole. We could make out nothing but the faint glint of the doctor’s spectacles, the flash of Father’s arm as he gestured sharply, the toe of his boot tapping the stone floor, no single word distinguishable.
When Father finally stepped out of the recess, his face was as white as chalk, his jaw tight, and I could see his hands trembling at his sides.
“Laura, dear, come here. Madame, we won’t need you for a little while,” he called.
A prickle of cold fear skittered down my spine as I walked towards them. I felt weak, yes, drained of all the bright, easy energy I’d had only a few weeks prior, but I did not feel ill. I’d always assumed strength was something you could reclaim with a good night’s sleep, whenever you chose.
Father held out a hand to steady me, his eyes still fixed on the doctor’s. “It is very strange. I scarcely understand it myself. Laura, pay close attention to Doctor Spielsberg, and try to recall everything he asks.”
“That prick like two needles piercing your skin, the night of your first terrible nightmare,” the doctor said, his voice soft but sharp as a scalpel. “Just below your throat, you said. Is there any soreness there, even now?”
“None at all,” I answered.
“Can you point exactly to the spot?”
I touched the place just an inch or two below the edge of my lace collar, the fabric of my morning dress falling thick over it.
“Your father will only lower the neck of your dress a very little way, I promise,” the doctor said. “It is absolutely necessary, to confirm the symptom of the complaint that has been wearing you down.”
I nodded, acquiescing. I felt Father’s fingers brush my neck as he pulled the fabric down, and he gasped so loudly I jumped back.
“God in heaven—there it is,” he breathed, his face going paler still.
“You see it for yourself now,” the doctor said, a grim, terrible satisfaction in his tone. “A small blue bruise, no bigger than the tip of your little finger, faint at the edges, two tiny pinpricks visible dead at its centre.”
A jolt of pure terror shot through me. “What is it? What’s wrong with me?”
“Nothing that cannot be set right, my dear girl,” the doctor said quickly. “I do not see any reason you should not start recovering immediately. That spot, that is the very point where the choking sensation begins, is it not?”
I nodded.
“And that cold, thrumming feeling you described, like a current of ice-cold water pressing against your skin—was it centred here, too?”
“I think it was,” I whispered.
The doctor turned back to my father, his eyebrows raised. “Now, Madame, if you would step over. My young patient is far more unwell than she knows. It is not fatal, not if we act quickly, but there are steps we must take at once. The first, and most non-negotiable: you must not leave Miss Laura alone for a single moment. Not to fetch a book from the next room, not to light a candle, not even to step outside for a breath of air. She is never to be unwatched.”
Madame assured him she would obey, without a single question. Father added his own thanks, then turned to me. “You will follow the doctor’s instructions, won’t you, my love?”
“Doctor, there is another guest in our house,” Father went on, turning back to the physician. “A young lady, our Carmilla. Her symptoms are far milder, but they are the very same, I would swear to it. You said you would be passing this way again this evening—stay for supper at seven, and you can examine her then. She does not rise and come downstairs until the afternoon.”
The doctor agreed, repeating his strict orders that I was never to be left unaccompanied, before he and Father walked out together, pacing the grassy stretch between the schloss moat and the forest road, heads bowed deep in conversation. I watched him mount his horse a few minutes later, tip his hat, and ride away east into the trees.
Almost at once, our post boy rode up from Dranfield, swinging his leather post bag down from his saddle to hand it to Father.
Madame and I spent the rest of the hour spinning wild theories, none of them touching the truth. She was convinced the doctor feared I would have a sudden, fatal seizure, that no one would be there to save me if I was left alone. I, for my part, thought the orders were only to keep me from overexerting myself, or eating unripe berries from the orchard, the kind of foolish mischief girls my age were always liable to get up to.
Half an hour later, Father came back into the parlour, a crumpled open letter in his hand. “This was delayed on the road,” he said. “It’s from General Spielsdorf. He might have been here yesterday, he might not arrive until tomorrow… he could turn up at any moment now.”
He pushed the letter into my hand, but he was not smiling the way he always did when our dear old friend the General came to visit. He looked, in fact, as if he wished the man was a thousand miles away, sunk to the bottom of the Red Sea. There was something dark and heavy weighing on his mind, something he would not speak of.
“Papa,” I said, laying my hand on his arm, looking up at him as pleadingly as I could. “Is the doctor right? Am I very ill?”
He smoothed my hair back from my face, his touch gentle but distracted. “No, my love. If we do all the right things, you will be well, fully on the road to recovery in a day or two. I only wish the General had chosen any other time to visit. I wish you were well enough to welcome him without strain.”
“Then tell me what is wrong with me,” I begged.
“Stop pestering me with questions,” he snapped, sharper than I had ever heard him speak to me. When he saw my face fall, hurt, he kissed my forehead, softening. “You will know everything, everything that I know, in a day or two. Don’t trouble your pretty head about it for now.”
He turned to leave, then popped his head back in the door to say he was driving out to Karnstein, the ruined village and the old castle, to call on the priest who lived near those abandoned grounds. He told me and Madame to come with him, and said Carmilla could join us later with the governess, who would bring out a picnic hamper to set up in the ruins of the old Karnstein keep.
By twelve o’clock I was ready, and the three of us set off in the carriage, crossing the drawbridge, turning right over the steep old Gothic bridge, heading west towards the overgrown woods that swallowed the path to Karnstein. There could be no lovelier drive. The road wound over soft, rolling hills and hollows thick with unpruned old wood, no formal straight lines, no sign that human hands had ever tended the trees, curving around the sides of mossy hollows and steep slopes, turning so many unexpected bends I never knew what I would see around the next turn.
We rounded one sharp bend, and there he was: General Spielsdorf, riding towards us, his mounted servant trailing behind him, his portmanteaus loaded in a hired cart trundling a little way behind. He dismounted the second we pulled the carriage to a stop, and after we exchanged greetings, he agreed easily to take the empty seat beside us, sending his horse and his servant ahead to the schloss.
