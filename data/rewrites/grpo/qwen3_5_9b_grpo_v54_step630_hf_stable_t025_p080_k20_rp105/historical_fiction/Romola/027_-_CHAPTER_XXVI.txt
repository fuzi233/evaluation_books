The Garment of Fear.

By six o'clock, the hearts of most Florentines had settled into a weary relief that the arrival of the new Charlemagne was finally concluded. When the thunderous roll of drums, the piercing blast of trumpets, and the heavy tramp of hooves along the Pisan road had mingled with the frantic pealing of church bells, it was indeed a majestic spectacle for those perched upon turreted roofs. From their vantage points, they watched the long, winding procession of terrible pomp against the backdrop of green hills and valleys. Though the sky offered no sunshine to illuminate the banners, spears, and silken surcoats, the absence of a thick dust cloud allowed the details to stand out with stark clarity. Tall and tough Scottish archers, ponderous Swiss halberdiers, and nimble Gascons ready to scale walls marched alongside cavalrymen who resembled knight-errants with their indomitable spears and chargers. It was a comforting assurance that this array of strength would injure none but the enemies of God. With such confidence, viewing the nobles and knights, the youthful pages of noble lineage, the jewelled sword-hilts, the satin scarves embroidered with pious symbols, and the transcendent canopy borne above the Most Christian King became a less dubious pleasure. As an old diarist noted, simply halting behind the wonders of the royal visit: "It was great magnificence."

Yet for the Signoria, who had waited on their platform against the gates before marching out to meet the mighty guest, the grandeur of the scene had been obscured by unsettling sensations. Had Messer Luca Corsini's Latin welcome remained legible, it might have been less confusing when the rain began to fall, creating an impatience in both men and horses that shattered his well-studied periods. This forced the scholarly representatives to offer a makeshift greeting in impromptu French. However, this sudden chaos created a golden opportunity for Tito. Stationed behind the Signoria among the secretaries, he found himself thrust forward when the dignitaries were pressed by the approaching horses.

"Somebody step forward and say a few words in French," Soderini urged. But no high-ranking official wished to risk a second public failure. "You, Francesco Gaddi—you can speak." Gaddi, distrusting his own quickness, hesitated and pushed Tito forward, saying, "You, Melema."

Tito stepped forward instantly, speaking the necessary few words in French with an air of profound deference that came as naturally to him as breathing. He then withdrew gracefully, allowing the King to pass. The presence of mind that had deserted him during the terrifying crisis of the morning was now a ready instrument. It was the mark of an excellent livery servant to never forsake his master when danger was not visible. Yet, when complimented on his timely service, Tito laughed it off as trivial, ensuring that Gaddi received the credit for the improvised welcome. No wonder Tito was popular; the touchstone by which men judge us is most often their own vanity.

Other aspects of the day had proven even more disastrous than anticipated. The Florentine procession of clergy and laity had become so choked that they were forced to take a makeshift route through the back streets, meeting the King only at the Cathedral. Furthermore, the young monarch, seated beneath the canopy with his lance resting on his thigh, looked less like a Charlemagne and more like a hastily modeled grotesque. It might have been wished that the scourge of Italian wickedness and "Champion of the honour of women" possessed a less miserable leg, fewer toes, a mouth less like a reptile's slit, and a head of less exorbitant outline. Yet, the thin leg rested upon cloth of gold and pearls, and the face was merely a small interruption amidst black velvet, gold, blazing rubies, and the brilliant tints of the embroidered canopy—"It was great magnificence."

The people cried out "Francia, Francia!" with an enthusiasm proportional to the splendor of the canopy, which they subsequently tore to pieces as their spoil, following immemorial custom. Royal lips duly kissed the altar, and despite the mischances, the King and his retinue were lodged in the Palace of the Via Larga. The nobles were dispersed among the great houses of Florence, and the terrible soldiery were encamped in the Prato and other open quarters. The business of the day was ended.

However, the streets still presented a surprising aspect, unseen by Florentines under the November stars before. Instead of a gloom broken only by feeble lamps before saintly images or streams of redder light from open doorways, lamps were suspended at the windows of every house. Men could now walk along no less securely and commodiously than by day—"It was great magnificence."

Along these illuminated streets, Tito Melema walked at about eight o'clock in the evening on his way home. He had exerted himself throughout the day under the pressure of hidden anxieties and had finally escaped unnoticed from the midst of after-supper gaiety. Now, at leisure to face his circumstances, he hoped to adjust himself to all probabilities and shed his childish fear. If only he had not lacked the presence of mind necessary to recognize Baldassarre during that surprise! It would have been happier for him on all accounts; he still winced under the sense that he was deliberately inflicting suffering upon his father. He would have preferred Baldassarre to be prosperous and happy. But he had left himself no second path now; there could be no conflict anymore. The only thing remaining was to take care of himself.

As these thoughts occupied his mind, he advanced from the Piazza di Santa Croce along the Via dei Benci. As he neared the angle turning into the Borgo Santa Croce, his ear was struck by a music that was not the sound of evening revelry, but of vigorous labor—the music of the anvil. Tito gave a slight start and quickened his pace, for the sounds suggested a welcome thought. He knew they came from the workshop of Niccolò Caparra, the famous resort for all Florentines who cared for curious and beautiful iron-work.

"What makes the giant at work so late?" Tito thought. "But so much the better for me. I can do that little bit of business tonight instead of tomorrow morning."

Preoccupied as he was, he could not help pausing a moment in admiration as he approached the workshop. The wide doorway, standing at the truncated angle of a great block of houses, was surmounted by a loggia roofed with fluted tiles and supported by stone columns with roughly carved capitals. Against the red light framed by the outline of the tiles and columns stood in black relief the grand figure of Niccolò, his huge arms rising and falling rhythmically, first hiding and then disclosing the profile of his firm mouth and powerful brow. Two slighter ebony figures, one at the anvil and the other at the bellows, served to set off his superior massiveness.

Tito darkened the doorway with a very different outline, standing in silence, knowing it was useless to speak until Niccolò should deign to pause and notice him. This did not occur until the smith had beaten the head of an axe to the due sharpness of edge and dismissed it from his anvil. In the meantime, Tito had satisfied himself by a glance around the shop that the object of his search had not disappeared.

Niccolò gave an unceremonious but good-humored nod as he turned from the anvil and rested his hammer on his hip.

"What is it, Messer Tito? Business?"

"Assuredly, Niccolò; else I should not have ventured to interrupt you when you are working out of hours, since I take that as a sign that your work is pressing."

"I've been at the same work all day—making axes and spear-heads. And every fool that has passed my shop has put his pumpkin-head in to say, 'Niccolò, wilt thou not come and see the King of France and his soldiers?' and I've answered, 'No: I don't want to see their faces—I want to see their backs.'"

"Are you making arms for the citizens, then, Niccolò, that they may have something better than rusty scythes and spits in case of an uproar?"

"We shall see. Arms are good, and Florence is likely to want them. The Frate tells us we shall get Pisa again, and I hold with the Frate; but I should be glad to know how the promise is to be fulfilled if we don't get plenty of good weapons forged? The Frate sees a long way before him; that I believe. But he doesn't see birds caught with winking at them, as some of our people try to make out. He sees sense, and not nonsense. But you're a bit of a Medicean, Messer Tito Melema. Ebbene! so I've been myself in my time, before the cask began to run sour. What's your business?"

"Simply to know the price of that fine coat of mail I saw hanging up here the other day. I want to buy it for a certain personage who needs a protection of that sort under his doublet."

"Let him come and buy it himself, then," said Niccolò, bluntly. "I'm rather nice about what I sell, and whom I sell to. I like to know who's my customer."

"I know your scruples, Niccolò. But that is only defensive armour; it can hurt nobody."

"True: but it may make the man who wears it feel himself all the safer if he should want to hurt somebody. No, no; it's not my own work; but it's fine work of Maso of Brescia. I should be loth for it to cover the heart of a scoundrel. I must know who is to wear it."

"Well, then, to be plain with you, Niccolò mio, I want it myself," said Tito, knowing persuasion was useless. "The fact is, I am likely to have a journey to take—and you know what journeying is in these times. You don't suspect *me* of treason against the Republic?"

"No, I know no harm of you," said Niccolò, in his blunt way again. "But have you the money to pay for the coat? For you've passed my shop often enough to know my sign: you've seen the burning account-books. I trust nobody. The price is twenty florins, and that's because it's second-hand. You're not likely to have so much money with you. Let it be till tomorrow."

"I happen to have the money," said Tito, who had been winning at play the day before and had not emptied his purse. "I'll carry the armour home with me."

Niccolò reached down the finely-wrought coat, which fell together into little more than two handfuls.

"There, then," he said, when the florins had been counted out on his palm. "Take the coat. It's made to cheat sword, or poniard, or arrow. But, for my part, I would never put such a thing on. It's like carrying fear about with one."

Niccolò's words held an unpleasant intensity of meaning for Tito. But he smiled and replied,

"Ah, Niccolò, we scholars are all cowards. Handling the pen doesn't thicken the arm as your hammer-wielding does. Addio!"

He folded the armour under his mantle and hastened across the Ponte Rubaconte.
