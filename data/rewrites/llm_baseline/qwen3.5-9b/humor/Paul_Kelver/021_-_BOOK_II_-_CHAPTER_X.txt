I climbed the ladder of my ambition slowly, surely, and with steady resolve. I had banished all idle dreams, focusing strictly on the business at hand. Occasionally, my inner child—little Paul with his sad eyes—would try to lure me away from my work, whispering promises of easier paths. But my determination was ironclad; I refused to rest until I had bought back my honesty. More than that, a hunger grew within me, day by day becoming almost physical, a desperate craving to feel the pressure of Norah's strong white hand in mine again. If I had yielded to these distractions, heaven only knows what I might have become: a politician, a minor poet, or perhaps a competent editor hamstrung by rigid convictions. None of these roles would have served me well.

Occasionally, when my humor needed a holiday and there was nothing urgent to do but wait patiently for its return, I would write stories purely for my own pleasure. They made no mark on the world, yet success in purposeful labor grows far more slowly. Had I persisted in my serious pursuits, I might have achieved greatness. But there was money to be earned. And by the time my debts were cleared, I had already established a reputation.

"Madness!" argued my practical friends. "You are throwing away a certain fortune for, at best, a doubtful competence. Stick to what you know you can do; starting your career anew in a different field is foolish."

"You would find it almost impossible now," explained those who spoke with the air of imparting wisdom born of experience. "The world simply would not listen to you. Once a humorist, always a humorist. It would be like asking a comic actor to play Hamlet. Even if he delivered the finest performance the stage had ever seen, the audience would only laugh—or walk out."

Seeking solace in our mutual need for sympathy, "Goggles" and I would retreat to a quiet corner of the club to pour out our souls. He would spend considerable time detailing his vision of Romeo—an excellent concept, I had no doubt, though I confessed privately that I couldn't visualize him as the tragic lover. Yet, I listened with every sign of encouragement. It was the price I paid for him to listen while I unfolded my grand designs for building monumental literature that would truly aid mankind.

"Perhaps in a future existence," Goggles laughed one evening as the clock struck seven, "I shall be a great tragedian, and you a famous poet. Meanwhile, as your friend Brian puts it, we are both sinning our mercies. After all, to live is the most important thing in life."

I had escorted him as far as the cloakroom, helping him into his coat.

"Take my advice," he said, tapping me firmly on the chest. His funny, fish-like eyes fixed upon me. If I hadn't known his intention was serious, I would have laughed; his expression was so comically earnest. "Marry some dear little woman. One never understands life properly until the babies come along to explain it to you."

I returned to my easy chair before the fire. *Wife, children, home!* Was that not the true work of man—the living man, not the dreamer? I pictured them surrounding me, lending my life dignity and responsibility. The fair, sweet woman, helper, comrade, comforter; the little faces molded in our image, their questioning voices offering the answers to life's riddles. Compared to these realities, all other hopes, ambitions, and dreams were merely phantoms of the morning mist, fading under the sunlight.

One evening, Hodgson approached me. "I want you to write me a comic opera," he announced, holding an open letter. "The public seems tired of these endless translations from the French. I want something English, something new and original."

"The English is easy enough," I replied cautiously. "But I wouldn't clamor for anything 'new and original' if I were you."

"Why not?" he asked, looking up from his letter.

"You might get it," I answered. "And then you'd be disappointed."

He laughed. "Well, you know what I mean—something we can list on the program as 'new and original.' What do you say? It will be a big break for you, and I'm willing to risk it. People are beginning to talk about you."

I had written a few farces and short comedies that had been successful, but this proposal carried a heavy, serious responsibility. A young man might be excused for hesitating; it could make him, or it could ruin him. Above all other arts, a comic opera demands experience.

"I explained my fears," I noted later. "I didn't mention that in my desk lay a four-act drama throbbing with humanity and life—a work I had hoped, though that hope faded daily, would take the theatrical public by storm and establish me as a serious playwright."

"It's very simple," Hodgson urged. "Just provide Atherton with plenty of comic business—you can certainly manage that. Give Gleeson something pretty in waltz time, and give Duncan a part where she changes her frock every quarter-hour, and the job is done."

"I'll tell you what," Hodgson continued. "I'll take the whole cast down to Richmond on Sunday. We'll hire a coach and leave the theater at half-past ten. It'll be a chance for you to study them. You can talk to them and figure out exactly what they can do. Atherton has ideas in his head; he'll explain them to you. Next week, we'll draft a contract and get to work."

It was too good an opportunity to ignore, even though I knew that if it succeeded, I would be pinned down forever to the role of the jester. But, as the saying goes, it pays well to write comic operas.

A small crowd had gathered in the Strand to see us depart.

"Nothing wrong, is there?" the leading lady asked anxiously, alighting a quarter-hour late from her cab. "Isn't it a fire?"

"Merely assembled to see you," Mr. Hodgson replied without lifting his eyes from his letters.

"Oh, good gracious!" cried the leading lady. "Do let us get away quickly."

"Box seat, my dear," Hodgson returned.

Accepting the assistance of myself and three other gentlemen, the leading lady mounted the ladder with charming hesitation. Delays ensued due to our low comedian, who twice pretended to miss his footing, sliding back into the arms of the stoic door-keeper. The crowd, mostly small boys enjoying the spectacle, laughed and applauded. Encouraged, the comedian made a third attempt on hands and knees, gained the roof, and sat down atop the tenor, who smiled somewhat mechanically.

As we passed the first dozen or so buses, our low comedian greeted them by rising to his feet and bowing profoundly, before falling back onto either the tenor or myself. Except for the tenor and me, his performance was highly appreciated. Passing Charing Cross, however, nobody seemed interested in our progress, bringing relief to the tenor and myself, as the comedian finally settled down.

"People sometimes ask me," said the low comedian, brushing dust from his knees, "why I do this sort of thing off-stage. It amuses me."

"I was coming up to London from Birmingham recently," he continued. "At Willesden, when the ticket collector opened the door, I sprang out and ran down the platform. Naturally, he chased me, shouting for everyone to stop me. I dodged them for about a minute. You wouldn't believe the excitement; nearly fifty people left their seats to see what was happening. I explained when they caught me that I was traveling second class with a first-class ticket, which was true. People think I do it to attract attention. I do it for my own pleasure."

"It must be a troublesome way to amuse yourself," I suggested.

"Exactly what my wife says," he replied. "She can never understand the desire that comes over us all, I suppose, at times, to play the fool. As a rule, when she is with me, I don't do it."

"She's not here today?" I asked, glancing around.

"She suffers terribly from headaches," he answered. "She hardly ever goes anywhere."

"I'm sorry." I spoke not out of mere politeness; I genuinely felt sorry.

During the drive to Richmond, this irrepressible urge to entertain got the better of him repeatedly. Through Kensington, he attracted attention by balancing a horn on his nose. At Kew, he stopped the coach to request a change of fare from a young ladies' boarding school for sixpence. At the foot of Richmond Hill, he caused a crowd to gather while trying to convince a deaf old gentleman in a Bath-chair to let his man race us up the hill for a shilling.

At these antics, our party roared with laughter, except for Hodgson, who was busy with his correspondence, and an elegant young lady of high social standing who had recently emerged from the Divorce Court with a reputation worth a hundred pounds a week in cash.

Upon arriving at the hotel a quarter-hour before lunch, we strolled into the garden. Our low comedian spotted an elderly gentleman of dignified appearance sipping vermouth at a small table. Standing rooted to the spot for a moment in astonishment, he then made a bee-line for the stranger, seizing and shaking him warmly by the hand. We exchanged admiring glances.

"Charlie is in good form today," we told one another, trailing behind him.
