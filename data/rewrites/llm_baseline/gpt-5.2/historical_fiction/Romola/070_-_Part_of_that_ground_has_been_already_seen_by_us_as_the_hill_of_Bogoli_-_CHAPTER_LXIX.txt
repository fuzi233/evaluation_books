In the deep, hushed hours of winter—when the valley lay cupped in cold and Romola lay still, mending as one mends after fever—her mind travelled backward and forward with a new steadiness, as if some hand had pressed a fresh seal into softening wax and left its mark clear.

She remembered, with a sort of shame that burned without heat, how she had once felt herself loose from every tie: unmoored, purposeless, sinking into a peevish solitude where even sorrow became a private luxury. There had been a moment when she had let the thought rock her like a lullaby—Life gives me nothing; I am tired; I want to die—and she had carried that inward sob down into sleep.

But the instant after waking in the boat—after that cry had reached her across the water like a bell heard through fog—she had not stopped to argue herself back into endurance. She had simply lived. She had moved as hunger moves a hand toward bread: urgently, without reasoning, answering need because it called. She had not told herself, as she used to do in Florence, that it was good to live in order to lighten sorrow; she had been drawn into the work as naturally as a cook is drawn to a fire when a pot boils over—no speeches, no maxims, only quick hands and attention.

It was a new baptism for her, not of ceremony but of necessity. In Florence, everything simple between human creature and human creature had been knotted for her into special bindings—marriage, the State, discipleship—till affection and duty had become a tangle where one could scarcely breathe. When those bindings had disappointed her trust, the recoil had stunned her sympathy, as if a loud clang had left her ears ringing and made every softer sound seem distant.

Here, among these people whose lives turned on seed and sickness, on the keeping of goats and the curing of a child’s cough, she had found again the plain scale of suffering: it could be measured by a hand laid on a brow, by water warmed, by barley broth stirred until it thickened, by a clean cloth torn and folded. And in the quiet where the only harshness was the wind in the bare branches or the far crack of wood in the frost, she said to herself:

“It was baseness in me to desire death. If everything else is doubtful, this suffering that I can help is certain. If the glory of the cross is an illusion, the sorrow is only the truer. While the strength is in my arm, I will stretch it out to the fainting; while the light visits my eyes, they shall seek the forsaken.”

Then, as soon as the valley’s need no longer pressed upon her from morning to night—when the fevers eased, and the children’s cries grew fewer, and there were hours unfilled with sudden calls—her heart, released from the people nearest at hand, rushed back into its older channels. Florence returned upon her with the insistence of appetite. Familiar faces, familiar lanes, familiar voices—those sounds she had once thought she could escape—came back as if they were the only language her inward self entirely understood. No new kindness, no new gratitude, could feed her as those memories fed her; the living fibre of her feeling took its nourishment from what it had long known.

Complete severance from one’s accustomed life makes a rare space for looking inward, and in that space Romola judged herself as she had never done before. Compunction—born in natures that feel others’ possible pain as keenly as their own actual pain—began to stir and gather force. She questioned the justice of her conclusions, the rightness of her deeds. Had she been rash? arrogant? always impatient that others were not as good as they should be, while she herself had failed to be faithful to what she had once recognised as the best?

Her flight rose before her as something cowardly—self-care disguised as renunciation. Savonarola had once taken her back on grounds that now seemed truer and deeper than those which had driven her out the second time. How could she feel the needs of strangers and not feel, above all, the needs of those nearest by bond and history?

But self-reproach met its own recoil. The memory of her life with Tito—of the conditions that made any real union impossible, while their outward union forced on her a whole daily discipline of false duty, a concealment and a sanctioning of what her whole mind revolted from—told her again that flight had been her only resource. There are minds so dull in sensibility that they never doubt; but all others must suffer this recurring conflict, where life’s many-twisted conditions have forbidden the fulfilment of a bond. For, strictly, there is no replacing of relations: the new does not cancel the old. The breach remains a wound; and until the scar is hard, conscience keeps turning back to look at it with troubled eyes.

Romola shrank with dread from any renewed closeness to Tito, and yet she could not be at ease in ignorance of his fate. The thought would rise unbidden, as persistent as the smell of smoke in hair after one has been near a fire: What if he were in misery? what if he needed her? There was still, within her, a thin thread of pain that testified to Fra Girolamo’s words—that she could not cease to be a wife. Could anything utterly cease for her that had once mingled with the current of her heart’s blood?

Florence returned like hunger; her feelings could not wander forever after the possible and the vague. To have divided herself from all that for ever—was that truly strength? Or was it the pride of one who says, “This world is not good enough for me,” when in truth she had simply been bruised and frightened? If she had been higher, would she so easily have lost trust?

Even her indignant grief for her godfather no longer held sole possession of her. Her sense of debt to Savonarola recovered its predominance. Nothing that had come, or might yet come, could undo the fact that there had been a great inspiration in him—something that had wakened new life in her, as leaven wakes sluggish dough. Who else, in all her experience, could claim from her the same gratitude? His errors—might they not bring calamity? The possibility made her restless, as if some harsh, jarring note had entered the valley’s quiet and she could not find its source.

She could not rest. Perhaps it was the return of strength with the first budding leaves—the sap rising, the air softened, the mornings less iron-cold—that made her active again. Perhaps it was simply longing, keen as thirst, to draw nearer to Florence. She did not picture herself daring to enter the city; but to be near enough to learn what was happening there urged itself with a force that pushed every other purpose aside.

So one morning in March, when the light had a gentler colour and the earth-smell was no longer shut away under hard frost, the people of the valley gathered to see the blessed Lady depart. Jacopo had fetched a mule for her and was to go with her over the mountains. The Padre, too, was to accompany her as far as the nearest town, that he might aid her in learning the safest road by which she could reach Pistoja. The little store of trinkets and money she had carried with her and never touched in this valley was abundant for her needs.

If she had been less drawn away by that inward pull, it would have been a harder moment when she walked along the village street for the last time. The place had its own domestic music now: the soft cluck of hens near doorways, the creak of a pail at the well, the low talk of men whose tongues kept to the valley’s old turns of speech—phrases that did not travel well beyond these slopes, meanings packed in sounds that would be thinned and altered in the city. Romola listened as one listens before leaving a country where the very names of herbs and tools are different—where memory lives in syllables as much as in faces.

Near the well the Padre and Jacopo waited with the mule. She had scarcely reached them before the wailing began. The people knelt and kissed her hands; they clung to her skirts and pressed their lips on the grey folds, crying out between sobs:

“Ah, why will you go, when the good season is beginning and the crops will be plentiful? Why will you go?”

“Do not be sorry,” Romola said, and her voice—so often constrained in Florence—came now with the plain tenderness these people understood. “You are well now, and I shall remember you. I must go and see if my own people want me.”

“Ah, yes—if they have the pestilence!”

“Look at us again, Madonna!”

“Yes, yes,” others cried quickly, as if promising could bind her back. “We will be good to the little Benedetto!”

At last she mounted the mule. But when Benedetto saw her lifted into this new height, turned away from him, he sent up a vigorous screaming—sharp, indignant, the sound of a small creature robbed. It gave all the people an excuse to surge after her, insisting that he must ride too, perched on the mule’s neck as far as the foot of the slope. Someone thrust him up; his little hands gripped, his cries broke into sobs, and Romola steadied him against her with the instinctive care she had practised in these months, as if a child were as natural a burden as a loaf is a natural comfort.

The parting had to come; even the valley’s love could not make the road shorter. When they reached the place where the path began to rise, Benedetto was taken down, and the people’s hands, which had held him and her and the mule’s coarse mane, fell away one by one. Romola turned again and again as she went upward. Each time she looked back she saw them lingering like a little flock, their figures small against the greening, waiting to catch the last waving of her hand, as if that motion could carry a blessing farther than words, beyond the reach of dialect, into whatever strange and louder world lay on the other side of the mountains.
