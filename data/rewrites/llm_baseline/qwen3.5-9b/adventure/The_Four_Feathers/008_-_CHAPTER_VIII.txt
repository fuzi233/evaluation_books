Durrance arrived in London one morning in June. That same afternoon, he took his first walk as an exile, strolling into Hyde Park. He sat beneath the trees, marveling at the grace of his countrywomen and the delicate cut of their dresses. He was a solitary figure—sunburnt and already marked by that indefinable expression in the eyes and face that distinguishes men who have been set apart in the distant corners of the world.

Among the crowd passing him, one woman paused and smiled. As Durrance rose from his chair, Mrs. Adair approached. She scanned him from head to toe with a quick, almost furtive glance that seemed to reveal exactly how deeply he weighed on her thoughts. She was comparing the man before her to the image she held of him from three years prior, searching for the subtle signs of change those years might have wrought, her eyes filled with apprehension.

Durrance merely noted that she was dressed in black. Understanding the silent question in his gaze, she answered quietly, "My husband died eighteen months ago. He was thrown from his horse during a hunt with the Pytchleys. He was killed instantly."

"I had not heard," Durrance replied awkwardly. "I am very sorry."

Mrs. Adair took a seat beside him but offered no further comment. She was a woman of perplexing silences; her pale, placid face, framed by a cold correctness, gave no clue to the turmoil occupying her mind. She sat motionless. Durrance felt embarrassed. He recalled Mr. Adair as a good-natured man whose defining trait was his evident love for his wife, yet he had never truly considered the depth of the wife's regard for him. Mr. Adair had often been a shadowy figure in that small household, and Durrance struggled to summon any full expression of regret from his memories. Giving up the attempt, he asked, "Are Harry Feversham and his wife in town?"

Mrs. Adair was slow to respond. After a pause, she said, "Not yet." Immediately, she corrected herself, speaking a little hurriedly, "I mean—the marriage never took place."

Durrance was not easily startled, and even when surprised, he did not show it in exclamations. "I don't think I understand," he said. "Why did it never happen?"

Mrs. Adair looked sharply at him, as if probing the reason for his deliberate tone. "I don't know why," she replied. "Ethne can keep a secret if she wishes." Durrance nodded his assent. "The engagement was broken off on the night of a dance at Lennon House."

Durrance turned immediately to her. "Just before I left England three years ago?"

"Yes. Then you knew?"

"No. Only you have explained to me something that happened on the very night I left Dover. What has become of Harry?"

Mrs. Adair shrugged. "I do not know. I have met no one who does. I doubt I've encountered anyone who has even seen him since then. He must have left England."

Durrance pondered this mysterious disappearance. It was Harry Feversham, then, whom he had seen on the pier as the Channel boat pulled away. The man with the troubled, despairing face was, after all, his friend.

"And Miss Eustace?" he asked after a pause, with a strange timidity. "Has she remarried?"

Again, Mrs. Adair took her time. "No," she said. "Is she still at Ramelton?"

Mrs. Adair shook her head. "There was a fire at Lennon House a year ago. Do you recall a constable named Bastable?"

"Indeed, I do. He was the one who introduced me to Miss Eustace and her father. I was traveling from Londonderry to Letterkenny. I received a letter from Mr. Eustace—a stranger to me—who knew from my friends in Letterkenny that I was passing his house. He invited me to stay the night. Naturally, I declined, and as soon as I disembarked from the ferry, Bastable arrested me on a magistrate's warrant."

"That is the man," Mrs. Adair confirmed, then recounted the history of the fire. It appeared that Bastable's bond with Dermod relied on his skill in brewing a specific concoction of toddy, which required a single oyster simmered in the saucepan to achieve perfection. Around two o'clock on a June morning, the spirit lamp supporting the pot tipped over. Both drinkers were too intoxicated to react, and the house burned partially before being extinguished by water that ruined the rest.

"There were consequences far worse than the destruction of the house," she continued. "The fire served as a beacon to Dermod's creditors. Already burdened with debt, he collapsed into total ruin. He was soaked by the hoses and caught a chill that nearly killed him; he has never fully recovered. You will find him a broken man. His estates are leased out, and Ethne now lives with her father in a small mountain village in Donegal."

Mrs. Adair had not looked at Durrance while speaking. Her gaze remained fixed straight ahead, her voice devoid of emotion, spoken only because speech was necessary. When she finished, she did not turn to meet his eyes.

"So she has lost everything?" Durrance asked.

"She still has a home in Donegal," Mrs. Adair replied.

"And that means a great deal to her," Durrance said slowly.

"Yes, I think you are right," she agreed. "It means," she added, "that despite her ill fortune, Ethne is envied by many other women."

Durrance did not answer this suggestion directly. Instead, he watched carriages roll past, listening to the chatter and laughter of the people around him. His eyes were refreshed by the women in their light-colored frocks, yet his slow mind worked tirelessly toward a lame philosophical conclusion.

Finally, Mrs. Adair turned to him with a hint of impatience. "Of what are you thinking?"

"That women suffer much more than men when the world turns against them," he answered, his response sounding more like a question than a statement.

"I know very little, of course. I can only guess. But I believe women internalize their suffering far more than we do. To them, the past becomes a literal part of them, as integral as a limb. For us, it remains external—at best a rung on a ladder, at worst a stone on our heel. Don't you think so too? I may phrase it poorly. But consider this: women look backward; we look forward. Therefore, misfortune hits them harder, doesn't it?"

Mrs. Adair responded in her own manner. She did not explicitly agree, but a note of humility entered her voice. "The mountain village where Ethne resides is called Glenalla. A path leads up to it from the road halfway between Rathmullen and Ramelton."

As she finished, she rose and extended her hand. "Shall I see you again?"

"You are still in Hill Street?" Durrance asked.

"I shall be in London for a time."

Mrs. Adair raised her eyebrows. By nature, she sought intricate, concealed motives, often baffling her when actions stemmed from simple, obvious reasons. Now, Durrance's decision to remain in town puzzled her. Why hadn't he traveled immediately to Donegal, since his thoughts clearly went there? Hearing of his constant presence at his Service Club, she couldn't comprehend it. She didn't even suspect his motive when he later informed her he had gone to Surrey and spent a day with General Feversham.

It had been an ineffective visit for Durrance. The General kept him firmly anchored to the history of the campaign from which he had just returned. Only once did Durrance broach the subject of Harry Feversham's disappearance. At the mere mention of his son's name, the old General's face hardened like plaster, becoming void of expression and attention, resembling a mask.

"We will talk of something else, if you please," the General said. Consequently, Durrance returned to London not an inch closer to Donegal.

From then on, he sat under a large tree in the inner courtyard of his club, conversing with various men but remaining unsatisfied. Throughout that June, every afternoon and evening found him at his post. No friend of Feversham passed by without Durrance offering a greeting that inevitably led to a question. Yet, these inquiries yielded nothing but a shrug of the shoulders and a muttered, "Hanged if I know!" Harry Feversham's circle knew him no longer; he had faded even from the speculations of his acquaintances.

Toward the end of June, however, an elderly retired naval officer limped into the courtyard. Upon seeing Durrance, he hesitated, then moved away with remarkable speed. Durrance sprang up.

"Mr. Sutch," he said.

"You have forgotten me?" the Lieutenant replied.
