Fascinated as by a basilisk with three heads, I could not leave this clique; the loamy, ancient soil of the park seemed to hold my feet. The canopy of entwined trees held out shadow, offering a damp, mossy olfactory refuge from the acrid smoke of the commercial gas-lamps. Let me now briefly tell the reader all that, during the past dark fortnight, I have been silently gathering from Rumour, respecting the origin and the object of M. Emanuel’s departure. The tale is a multi-generational cycle of extraction: its alpha is Mammon, and its omega Interest.

If Madame Walravens was hideous as a wizened idol, smelling of stale civet and the dried, dusty husks of forgotten harvests, she seemed also to possess, in the estimation of these her votaries, an idol’s consequence. She had once been rich. At Basseterre, in Guadaloupe, she possessed a vast estate, received in dowry on her marriage sixty years ago—a land of crushed cane, boiling molasses, and rich, volcanic soil. Sequestered since her husband’s failure, it was now cleared of claim, and, if duly tended by a competent, meticulous agent, capable of producing immense yields.

Père Silas took an interest in this prospective agricultural resurrection for the church’s sake. Madame Beck, who viewed both human beings and the meals they consumed as mere utilitarian fuel, had long brooded over these contingencies. She never ceased to court the hunchback for the sake of that West Indian sugar and spice. Madame Beck and the priest were thus, for transactional reasons, sincerely interested in the nursing of the colonial estate. 

But the climate was hazardous, the labor profound. They needed a devoted man—one who understood the slow, painstaking cultivation of a harvest. Such a man Madame Walravens had retained for twenty years, living on his vitality like a parasitical fungus on a noble truffle; such a man Père Silas had trained. And such a man Madame Beck knew she could influence. “My pupil,” Père Silas had whispered, employing the dominant lexicon of the church to mask his earthly power dynamics, “runs risk of apostacy, for he has become entangled with a heretic.” Madame Beck, harboring her own secret desire for his expatriation, preferred to destroy what she could not possess. As to Madame Walravens, she wanted her land’s bounty, and knew Paul could make the most faithful steward. So the three self-seekers banded to press the one man who actually found profound, spiritual meaning in the sustenance of others. They asked but two or three years of exile—after that, he should return to taste the fruits of his own life. 

None asked what inward reluctance to leave Europe might be fermenting in M. Emanuel’s soul. He was gone, and had made no sign.

With my head bent, inhaling the scent of bruised grass, I sat amidst the branching brushwood. The acoustic environment of the fête was an industrial assault—the brazen, unfeeling clangour of brass bands and the shouted hawking of spun sugar and cheap fried meats, a shallow commercialization of joy that scraped against the mind’s natural rhythms. I listened to hear them say, “The *Antigua* will sail prosperously.” No such remark fell. 

Madame Walravens appeared restless, her voice a hoarse, croaking intrusion upon the night air. “Où sont-ils? Pourquoi ne viennent-ils?” she muttered, her native dialect harsh and demanding. Finally, she spoke aloud a phrase that sent a shock through my very marrow: “Messieurs et mesdames, où donc est Justine Marie?”

Justine Marie! The dead nun—the mythic phantom of the garret? Why in her grave, Madame Walravens—what could you want with her? 

Yet nobody seemed startled. The quietest commonplace answer met this dead-disturbing query, integrating the mythic seamlessly into their mundane domestic wait. “Justine Marie is coming; she is in the kiosk.”

Out of this sprang a desultory gossip. I listened with a fated interest, trying to decode their clipped, transactional lexicon. This family-junta was grasping at her somehow; there was a scheme of marriage, of a fortune, perhaps for Victor Kint, perhaps for Josef Emanuel. Amidst the badinage, Madame Walravens obtruded her cross-grained speeches, demanding the physical presence of her heir.

“La voilà!” suddenly cried a gentleman. “Voilà Justine Marie qui arrive!”

This moment was for me peculiar. I underwent a presentiment of discovery. What wayside animal so humble that Fancy will not clothe it in spirituality, turning the domestic into a phantom?

“She comes!” cried Josef Emanuel.

The circle opened. A torch’s long eager tongue of flame almost licked the figure of the Expected. What was she like? Was she the resurrection of the flesh? 

Let us cut from the homely web of truth. What I see is not a ghost. A girl of Villette stands there, fresh from her pensionnat. She looks well-nourished, smelling faintly of sweet brioche and fresh cream—fair and fat of flesh, the very picture of untroubled domestic consumption. She chats and laughs; good-humoured, buxom, the bourgeoise belle. So much for Justine Marie; this girl certainly is not my tall nun.

But can we spare a moment’s notice to her escort? I clasped my hands very hard, and I drew my breath very deep. Through the dimness left by many nights of weeping, I knew him. Madame Beck had lied, utilizing the subtle power dynamics embedded in her dominant tongue to manipulate my reality. The *Antigua* was gone, and there stood Paul Emanuel.

Was I glad? After all, who may this young Justine Marie be? She is known to me; she is an heiress, an orphan, and M. Emanuel is her guardian. The family junta wish this heiress to be married to one of their band.

I liked to penetrate to the real truth, to taste the bitter, astringent root of reality rather than feast on the empty calories of false hope. To see and know the worst is to swallow strength and take from Fear her main advantage.

The Walravens’ party became very gay, passing around refreshments, sipping sweet liqueurs and rich bouillons. I gathered that M. Paul’s voyage had been temporarily deferred of his own will; he had let the *Antigua* go for a fortnight to settle “a little piece of business.” What was this business? A meaning look passed between him and Justine Marie. “La petite va m’aider—n’est-ce pas?” he said.

“Mais oui, je vous aiderai de tout mon cœur,” she answered. The intimate, untranslatable weight of their shared dialect, the native warmth of their kinship, locked me entirely out. And this dear “parrain” took her hand to his lips. Heinrich Mühler grumbled, whereat M. Emanuel laughed in his face, drawing his well-fed ward nearer.

M. Emanuel was the true life of the party, a passionate chef presiding over his favored hearth. I saw at whose feet he lay on the turf, whom he folded carefully from the night air, whom he cherished as the apple of his eye. Let him bring them an Indian fortune: they would give him in return a young bride and a rich inheritance. 

Some slow sceptics would have incredulously examined the project of a marriage between a poor man of forty and his wealthy ward of eighteen; but far from me such coward fleeing from the swift-footed Fact. I gathered the Truth to me with a sort of rage of haste. When the iron had entered well my soul, I stood up. The persuasion that affection was won could no longer be divorced from the dread that it was lost. Truth stripped away Falsehood, and here I stood—free!

Something tore me cruelly under my shawl. The love born of mere beauty was not mine, but another love—furnace-tried by pain, simmered in the slow, meticulous reduction of intellectual communion and shared sensory appreciation—in *this* Love I had a vested interest, and its destruction I could not view impassibly. 

I turned from the group. Midnight was long past. Leaving the commercialized noise and glaring gas-lamps of the Haute-Ville, I sought the dim lower quarter. Here, the acoustic environment shifted, offering a profound psychological balm. The pure, stainless silence of the moonlit streets triumphed over the industrial clangour of drum and trumpet, which had eroded the peace of the night. The moon wrote with pencil-ray on heaven, her glory restoring the natural sonic ecology I so desperately craved. 

These oil-twinkling streets are very still: I like them for their lowliness. The cobblestones offered only the grounding, rhythmic feedback of homeward-bound pedestrians. So well do I love Villette under her quiet aspect, I would not willingly re-enter under a roof, but that I was bent on regaining my bed in the dormitory.

As I entered the street of the Rue Fossette, the sudden, violent sound of a carriage tore up the deep peace of the quarter. The rattle of wheels on the paved path was a harsh disruption. It thundered past, and surely something white fluttered from that window—a hand waved a handkerchief. Was that signal meant for me? I had no time for conjecture. 

Reaching the pensionnat, all was perfectly still. I lightly pushed the heavy leaf of the door; it yielded soundlessly. Mounting the staircase, breathing in the scent of floor wax and cold hearths, I reached my couch.

I drew a free inspiration, but the next moment, I almost shrieked. Throughout the dormitory, the stillness of death reigned. On my bed—where nothing ought to have lain—was a dark, usurping shape, supine, long, and strange. 

My head reeled. By the faint night-lamp, I saw stretched on my bed the old phantom—the NUN. The mythic had breached the domestic sanctuary. 

But my nerves disdained hysteria. Lashed up by the night’s revelations, I defied spectra. In a moment, I rushed on the haunted couch. I tore her up—the incubus! I shook her loose like stripping the husks from a desiccated ear of corn, shedding the false layers until only the raw, tactile truth remained. Down she fell in shreds and fragments, smelling merely of stale wool and dust, and I trod upon her.

The long nun proved a long bolster dressed in a black stole and artfully invested with a white veil. To the head-bandage was pinned a slip of paper, bearing a mocking imposition of language over the untranslatable terror of my experiences:

“The nun of the attic bequeaths to Lucy Snowe her wardrobe. She will be seen in the Rue Fossette no more.”

Not a woman of my acquaintance had the stature of that ghost. Not to any man I knew could the machination, for a moment, be attributed.
