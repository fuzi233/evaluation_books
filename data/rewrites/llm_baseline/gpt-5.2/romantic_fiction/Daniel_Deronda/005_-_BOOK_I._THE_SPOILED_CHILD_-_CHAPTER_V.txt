Gwendolen’s first weeks in the neighborhood fell in with her uncle’s forecasts as neatly as pastry fits the tin it is baked in. From Brackenshaw Castle to the Firs at Wanchester—where Mr. Quallon the banker kept his table as carefully stocked as his ledgers—doors opened to her with a readiness that was less friendship than appetite: society, like a large household, must be fed with novelty, and a striking young woman was a new dish to set down without obliging the hostess to swallow any guest she disliked.

Besides, to ask for Gwendolen did not mean enduring a tedious train. Mrs. Davilow, her mother, sat by with that quiet, watered-color grace which never clamors for attention yet somehow improves a room, like a vase of pale flowers set where the lamplight warms their scent. And Mr. Gascoigne, the clergyman-uncle, was welcomed for his own sake everywhere—like good bread: never too rich, always wanted.

Among the houses where Gwendolen was not quite liked, yet invited all the same, was Quetcham Hall. One of her earliest summonses was to a large dinner-party there, a sort of general introduction—thirty guests arranged with the deliberation of courses, so that hardly any “visitable” family could be left out without making a gap in the composition. It was an evening of long rooms and many lights: the kind of house where one seems to walk through a succession of air—cool at the first doorway, then warmer, then warmest—each chamber carrying its own bouquet of wax, flowers, and fine linen.

Gwendolen had never made that ceremonial promenade before. She floated forward in white drapery, passing under arch after arch into fuller illumination, and felt with a sudden, exulting certainty that this was her element, as if her life had always been measured by galleries and footmen and the soft friction of silk on polished floors. Any one seeing her for the first time might have believed she had been bred among such lights. Her cousin Anna, who had in fact seen more of it, looked as startled and shy as a rabbit set down in an open, lamp-lit square.

“Who is that with Gascoigne?” said the archdeacon, breaking off a discussion of military manoeuvres as if the sight of her had fired a more immediate curiosity.

And his son—young Clintock, the classical scholar who was already at the stage of proposing “not less elegant than ingenious” alterations in Greek texts—uttered, almost in the same breath from the other side of the room, “By George! who is that girl with the awfully well-set head and jolly figure?”

To any mind disposed to wish every one their fair share, it was vexing to see how Gwendolen eclipsed the other young faces, as a bright flame makes even good candles look dull. The handsome Miss Lawe—daughter of Lady Lawe—seemed suddenly broader, heavier, less alive. And Miss Arrowpoint, unhappily in white too, had the air of a small photograph where one imagines the price was mostly for the skirt.

Miss Arrowpoint, Catherine, was generally liked. She wore her great fortune with an amiable absence of ostentation, and served as a soft screen for her mother’s oddities. It struck more than one observer as unfitting that Gwendolen should look, at first glance, the more important person.

“She is not really so handsome if you come to examine her features,” Mrs. Arrowpoint said later to Mrs. Vulcany with the confidential relish of a woman who prides herself on discernment. “It is a certain style she has—very effective at first—but afterwards she is less agreeable.”

For Gwendolen, who could be keenly alive to absurdity in others, was also kindly disposed toward any one who could make life agreeable to her, and she meant to win Mrs. Arrowpoint by giving her an attention beyond what others were likely to show. But self-confidence has its own clumsiness: it talks down to the person it assumes to be dull. Because Mrs. Arrowpoint’s figure was squat, her voice harsh and parrot-like, her head-dress systematically high, it slipped into Gwendolen’s unreflecting mind that ridicule must go with want of penetration. So she played her little scene of pretty deference without suspecting that every shade of her manner was being tasted and judged, like seasoning in a dish by a palate quick to detect mockery.

After dinner, when the company had broken into drawing-room groups and the air had shifted—less savory now, more sweet with hot wax and crushed flowers—Mrs. Arrowpoint approached her for a tête-à-tête.

“You are fond of books as well as of music, riding, and archery, I hear,” she said. In another woman the remark might have come with a low, melodious kindness; in Mrs. Arrowpoint it arrived on a loud twang. “Catherine will be very glad to have so sympathetic a neighbor.”

“It is I who am fortunate,” Gwendolen answered with graceful readiness, the very tone of a girl giving alms of admiration. “Miss Arrowpoint will teach me what good music is. I shall be entirely a learner. I hear she is a thorough musician.”

“Catherine has certainly had every advantage. We have a first-rate musician in the house now—Herr Klesmer; perhaps you know all his compositions. You must allow me to introduce him to you. You sing, I believe. Catherine plays three instruments, but she does not sing. I hope you will let us hear you. I understand you are an accomplished singer.”

“Oh no!—‘die Kraft ist schwach, allein die Lust ist gross,’ as Mephistopheles says.”

“Ah—you are a student of Goethe. Young ladies are so advanced now. I suppose you have read everything.”

“No, really. I shall be so glad if you will tell me what to read. I have been looking into all the books in the library at Offendene, but there is nothing readable. The leaves all stick together and smell musty.” As she spoke she had, in her imagination, the close scent of long-shut bindings—dust, glue, and time—so unlike the clean, appetizing openness of a newly laid table. “I wish I could write books to amuse myself, as you can! How delightful it must be to write books after one’s own taste instead of reading other people’s! Home-made books must be so nice.”

For an instant Mrs. Arrowpoint’s glance sharpened, as if a knife had been laid on the tongue to test whether it was really sweet. But the last phrase could be taken for girlish simplicity, and Gwendolen looked so candid.

“And why should you not?” Mrs. Arrowpoint returned, encouraged. “You have but to begin as I did. Pen, ink, and paper are at everybody’s command. But I will send you all I have written with pleasure.”

“Thanks. I shall be so glad to read your writings.” Gwendolen went on, with the airy eagerness of a child proposing to taste everything on the sideboard. “Being acquainted with authors must give a peculiar understanding of their books: one would be able to tell then which parts were funny and which serious. I am sure I often laugh in the wrong place.”

She felt, even as she spoke, that she had ventured too far, as if she had sprinkled spice into a dish without measuring it; and she added quickly, “In Shakespeare, you know, and other great writers that we can never see. But I always want to know more than there is in the books.”

“If you are interested in any of my subjects I can lend you many extra sheets in manuscript,” said Mrs. Arrowpoint; and Gwendolen had the disagreeable inward consciousness of a young lady who has declared herself fond of potted sprats and is immediately offered a whole jar. “These are things I dare say I shall publish eventually: several friends have urged me to do so, and one doesn’t like to be obstinate. My Tasso, for example—I could have made it twice the size.”

“I dote on Tasso,” said Gwendolen, promptly.

“Well, you shall have all my papers, if you like. So many, you know, have written about Tasso; but they are all wrong. As to the particular nature of his madness, and his feelings for Leonora, and the real cause of his imprisonment, and the character of Leonora—who, in my opinion, was a cold-hearted woman, else she would have married him in spite of her brother—they are all wrong. I differ from everybody.”

“How very interesting!” said Gwendolen. “I like to differ from everybody. I think it is so stupid to agree. That is the worst of writing your opinions; you make people agree with you.”

Again the hostess’s eye took on that momentary, pricking attention; but Gwendolen’s face wore innocence as easily as her dress.

“I know nothing of Tasso except the Gerusalemme Liberata,” she added in a docile tone, “which we read and learned by heart at school.”

“Ah, his life is more interesting than his poetry. I have constructed the early part of his life as a sort of romance. When one thinks of his father Bernardo, and so on, there is much that must be true.”

“Imagination is often truer than fact,” said Gwendolen decisively—words she could not have interpreted any more than if she had spoken them in Etruscan, yet she uttered them with the ease of a singer striking a note she has heard praised. “I shall be so glad to learn all about Tasso—and his madness especially. I suppose poets are always a little mad.”

“To be sure—‘the poet’s eye in a fine frenzy rolling’; and somebody says of Marlowe, ‘For that fine madness still he did maintain, Which always should possess the poet’s brain.’”

“But it was not always found out, was it?” said Gwendolen, with an ingenuous lift of her brows. “I suppose some of them rolled their eyes in private. Mad people are often very cunning.”

A faint shadow passed over Mrs. Arrowpoint’s face, as if she had heard, under the music of the words, an odd, dissonant note. But at that moment the gentlemen entered, and the small, dangerous duet was broken off.

“Ah, here comes Herr Klesmer,” said Mrs. Arrowpoint, rising; and presently she brought him to Gwendolen and left them together.

Herr Klesmer had the kind of presence that seems to bring its own climate: grand features, brown hair floating in an artist’s fashion, and brown eyes behind spectacles—an unlikely veil for brilliance. His English was fluent and scarcely foreign at all; yet there was in him that mingled origin—German, Sclave, Semite—which made his very accent feel like a history. He had, too, the disarming softness that will sometimes come over genius in the act of wishing to be agreeable to beauty; as if the great instrument had, for a moment, played a lighter tune to please the ear nearest it.

Music began.

Miss Arrowpoint and Herr Klesmer played a four-handed piece on two pianos. To the company in general it proved chiefly that the piece was long; to Gwendolen it proved something sharper—that Catherine, with her neutral, placid face, held the instrument under her fingers with a mastery that made Gwendolen’s own much-praised touch and style look like a pretty household trick.

Afterward every one became anxious to hear Gwendolen sing—especially Mr. Arrowpoint, who was the very pattern of an easy, well-bred host, and of whom no one had anything to report except that he had married Miss Cuttler and imported the best cigars. He led her to the piano with a politeness as smooth as well-aged wine.

Herr Klesmer closed the instrument in readiness and smiled as she approached, then placed himself a few feet away so he could see her face as she sang.

Gwendolen was never nervous. What she undertook she did without trembling, and singing was an enjoyment to her, as natural as drawing breath. Her voice was a moderately powerful soprano—some one had compared it to Jenny Lind’s—her ear was good, and she kept in tune. Ordinary hearers were pleased, and she had been used to applause that came like a warm dish set down promptly, with no suspicion of criticism in the aroma. She had even the advantage—rare and enviable—of looking almost prettier when she sang.

Her chosen song was a favorite aria of Bellini’s, one she felt sure of. She sang it through with full confidence, and when she ended Mr. Arrowpoint murmured “Charming!” while the word ran round the room in friendly echoes.

But Herr Klesmer stood mute—statuesque, if a statue can be imagined in spectacles.

Gwendolen was pressed to keep her seat and repeat the general pleasure. She did not wish to refuse; yet before she committed herself she leaned a little toward Herr Klesmer with a smile of appeal.

“It would be too cruel to a great musician. You cannot like to hear poor amateur singing.”

“No, truly; but that makes nothing,” said Herr Klesmer suddenly, and with the words his English changed. The staccato endings came out—an odious German fashion that had not shown itself before, as if his temper had plucked a hidden string and made it sound. “That makes nothing. It is always acceptable to see you sing.”

The sentence struck like a draught of cold air in a warm room: an unexpected assertion of superiority, all the more startling because it was so calmly spoken. Color rushed into Gwendolen’s face; but she had presence of mind enough not to betray resentment by abrupt movement.

Miss Arrowpoint, who had been near enough to overhear—and also to notice that Herr Klesmer’s open admiration of Gwendolen was too conspicuous to be quite in taste—came to her with tact and kindness.

“Imagine what I have to go through with this professor! He can hardly tolerate anything we English do in music. We can only put up with his severity, and make use of it to find out the worst that can be said of us. It is a little comfort to know that; and one can bear it when every one else is admiring.”

“I should be very much obliged to him for telling me the worst,” said Gwendolen, recovering her poise. “I dare say I have been extremely ill taught, in addition to having no talent—only liking for music.”

It was admirably phrased, considering that such a thought had never visited her before.

“Yes, it is true: you have not been well taught,” Herr Klesmer replied quietly. Woman was dear to him, but music dearer. “Still, you are not quite without gifts. You sing in tune, and you have a pretty fair organ. But you produce your notes badly; and that music which you sing is beneath you. It is a form of melody which expresses a puerile state of culture—a dawdling, canting, see-saw kind of stuff—the passion and thought of people without any breadth of horizon. There is a sort of self-satisfied folly about every phrase of such melody; no cries of deep, mysterious passion—no conflict—no sense of the universal. It makes men small as they listen to it. Sing now something larger. And I shall see.”

“Oh, not now—by-and-by,” said Gwendolen. Her heart sank, as if she had been made to taste, in one mouthful, the difference between a sugared confection and a strong, bitter draught whose after-flavor widens the throat. For a girl who meant to lead, this first collision in her campaign was a shock.

Miss Arrowpoint helped her.

“Yes, by-and-by. I always require half an hour to get up my courage after being criticised by Herr Klesmer. We will ask him to play to us now: he is bound to show us what is good music.”

To be quite safe on that point, Herr Klesmer sat down and played a composition of his own—a fantasia called Freudvoll, Leidvoll, Gedankenvoll. It was as if the piano, that moderate instrument of drawing-rooms, had suddenly been made to speak in a language older than parlors and their pretty decorums: a speech of joy and grief and thought braided together. Under his fingers the ivory and wood seemed to acquire nerves; the strings answered in lingering, quivering phrases, as though compelled to confess what they knew of passion.

Gwendolen, wounded in pride, still had nature enough to feel the power of it. Gradually her inward sob of mortification was turned into an excitement that lifted her for a moment into a reckless indifference about her own performance—or rather into a determination to rise above it by laughing at it as if it belonged to some other girl. Her eyes brightened; color stayed in her cheeks; her tongue grew ready for mischief.

“I wish you would sing to us again, Miss Harleth,” said young Clintock, who had had the good fortune to take her down to dinner and now came up eagerly as soon as the playing ended. “That is the style of music for me. I never can make anything of this tip-top playing. It is like a jar of leeches, where you can never tell either beginnings or endings. I could listen to your singing all day.”

“Yes, we should be glad of something popular now—another song from you would be a relaxation,” said Mrs. Arrowpoint, who had come nearer with a careful politeness.

“That must be because you are in a puerile state of culture, and have no breadth of horizon,” Gwendolen said, turning her bright smile up to Clintock and taking no notice of Mrs. Arrowpoint. “I have just learned that. I have been taught how bad my taste is, and am feeling growing pains. They are never pleasant.”

Mrs. Arrowpoint was not insensible to the rudeness—she heard it as plainly as a sour note sounding under a chord—but she merely said, “Well, we will not press anything disagreeably.” Conversation in the room began to bubble up as if a lid had been lifted; guests moved to seek one another; and she stayed seated with the relief of a hostess suddenly unneeded.

“I am glad you like this neighborhood,” Clintock went on, pleased with his position before Gwendolen.

“Exceedingly. There seems to be a little of everything and not much of anything.”

“That is rather equivocal praise.”

“Not with me. I like a little of everything; a little absurdity, for example, is very amusing. I am thankful for a few queer people; but much of them is a bore.”

Mrs. Arrowpoint, hearing, found her earlier doubt revive: this girl’s tone had changed as easily as a mask is shifted, and the suspicion of a satirical doubleness returned like a remembered bad taste.

“I think there should be more croquet, for one thing,” said Clintock. “I am usually away, but if I were more here I should go in for a croquet club. You are one of the archers, I think. But depend upon it croquet is the game of the future. It wants writing up, though. One of our best men has written a poem on it, in four cantos—as good as Pope. I want him to publish it—You never read anything better.”

“I shall study croquet to-morrow,” said Gwendolen. “I shall take to it instead of singing.”

“No, no, not that; but do take to croquet. I will send you Jenning’s poem if you like. I have a manuscript copy.”

“Is he a great friend of yours?”

“Well—rather.”

“Oh, if he is only rather, I think I will decline. Or, if you send it to me, will you promise not to catechise me upon it and ask me which part I like best? Because it is not so easy to know a poem without reading it as to know a sermon without listening.”

“Decidedly,” Mrs. Arrowpoint said to herself, “this girl is double and satirical. I shall be on my guard against her.”

Yet, guarded or not, the Arrowpoints continued to show Gwendolen polite attentions afterward—not merely because invitations and visits have larger foundations than personal liking, but because the little trying scene at the piano had stirred in Miss Arrowpoint a gentle solicitude. Catherine, who managed the invitations and calls while her mother was occupied elsewhere, could not help being touched by the sight of a girl so sure of her charms meeting, for once, a judgment that did not flatter—and by the strange, sharp music of Herr Klesmer’s truth sounding through a room trained to softer harmonies.
