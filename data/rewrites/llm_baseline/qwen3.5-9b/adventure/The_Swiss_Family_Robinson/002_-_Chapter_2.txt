Our night's rest was surprisingly comfortable despite the biting chill that followed the day's intense heat. Exhaustion from our labors had lulled everyone into a deep sleep, undisturbed until dawn. The rooster's crow, heralding the rising sun, was the final sound I heard before waking my wife. Seizing the quiet hour while the children still slept, we conferred privately on our precarious situation. Our immediate priorities were clear: first, to determine the fate of our shipwrecked companions, and second, to assess the resources of this unfamiliar land.

We agreed that after breakfast, Fritz and I would embark on an expedition to investigate, while my wife remained at the camp with the three younger boys.

"Rouse up, my dears!" I called cheerfully, stirring the children. "Help your mother prepare breakfast."

"As to that," she smiled, "we can only boil more soup!"

"You forgot Jack's fine lobster!" I reminded him. "Where is it, Jack?"

"It's safe in this hollow in the rock, Father," Jack replied. "I figured since the dogs love good things, they might covet the lobster just as much as the agouti."

"A wise precaution," I noted approvingly. "Even heedless Jack is learning. It is fortunate the lobster is so large; we shall need it for our journey today."

Mention of an expedition sent the four children into a frenzy of delight. They danced around me, clapping their hands.

"Hold on now," I cautioned gently. "You cannot all come. An expedition like this would be too perilous and exhausting for the younger ones. This area appears secure. Fritz and I will go alone, accompanied by one of the dogs, leaving the other to guard you."

At the word "guns," poor Fritz blushed deeply, struggling futilely to straighten his weapon. After a brief pause, seeing his distress, I permitted him to take a second firearm, pleased to note that his embarrassment had sparked a sense of responsibility.

He immediately tried to restrain Flora, but remembering the recent beating she had endured, the dog snarled and refused to approach. Turk exhibited similar hostility. I was forced to summon them with my own voice. Tears streaming down his face, Fritz begged for a biscuit from his mother, offering to skip the rest of his meal simply to make peace with the animals. Carrying the treats, he stroked and caressed the dogs, seeking forgiveness. Dogs, being creatures of quick affection and slow to hold grudges, responded readily. Flora instantly softened, licking the hands that fed her. Turk, however, remained wary, lacking confidence in Fritz's advances.

"Here, Turk," Jack offered, tossing him a claw of the lobster. "I intended to give it to you for your journey anyway."

With this gesture, Turk forgave Fritz. Armed with guns and game bags—Fritz also sporting a pair of pistols and I a small hatchet—we packed the remaining lobster, biscuits, and a flask of water, ready to depart.

"Wait!" I exclaimed. "We have neglected something vital."

"What?" Fritz asked.

"Yes," I replied solemnly. "We have not yet joined in morning prayer. Amidst life's cares and joys, we too easily forget the God to whom we owe everything."

Having committed ourselves to His protection, I bade farewell to my wife and children, instructing them not to stray far from the boat and tent. Though we parted with mutual anxiety, knowing the dangers of this unknown region, we moved forward.

The stream's banks were so rocky that access to the water was limited to a single narrow passage, with no corresponding path on the opposite side. This was reassuring; it confirmed that my family was situated on relatively inaccessible ground, shielded by steep cliffs on the other side.

Fritz and I ascended the stream until we reached a cascade where large rocks protruded from the water, allowing us to cross safely. On our left lay the sea; to our right, a long ridge of rocky heights dotted with trees stretched inland.

Scarcely fifty yards into the tall, tangled grass—which was partially withered by the sun—we were startled by a rustling sound. Looking around, we saw the grass wave as if disturbed by an animal. Fritz instantly raised his gun, poised to fire the moment a beast appeared.

I admired my son's composure; it demonstrated that he could be relied upon in times of genuine danger. Fortunately, no savage beast emerged. Instead, it was our faithful dog, Turk, whom we had momentarily forgotten in our haste to separate. My wife, ever thoughtful, had likely sent him ahead. I praised Fritz's discretion for withholding his shot until he was certain of the intruder.

This incident highlighted the peril of our position. Should a fierce animal attack us unawares, escape would be nearly impossible. Hastening toward the open seashore, we were greeted by a delightful scene: rolling hills, lush green grass, and clusters of trees reaching the water's edge.

Searching the smooth sand for traces of our missing companions yielded nothing—not even a footprint.

"Should I fire a shot or two?" Fritz suggested. "It might attract our companions, or perhaps any savages nearby."

"That would indeed," I agreed. "But let us search diligently and quietly first."

"Why bother, Father?" Fritz argued. "They abandoned us to fend for ourselves. I wouldn't wish to see them again."

"You are mistaken, my boy," I corrected. "First, we must never repay evil with evil. Second, they might prove invaluable in constructing a dwelling. Third, consider that they left the vessel empty-handed; they may be starving."

"But Father," Fritz countered, "while we waste time searching for them with little hope of success, why not return to the ship and rescue the animals aboard?"

"When faced with competing duties," I explained, "we must choose the action offering the greatest solid advantage. Saving a human life is a higher calling than providing comfort to a few animals, whom we have already stocked with food for days. Moreover, the sea is calm; we need not fear the ship sinking before we can return."

Guided by this logic, we pressed on until we reached a pleasant grove bordering the water. Halting beneath a large tree beside a babbling rivulet, we rested. Above us, thousands of colorful birds twittered. Suddenly, Fritz jumped up.

"A monkey!" he exclaimed. "I'm nearly sure I saw one."

Springing around the tree, he stumbled over a small, round object and handed it to me. "Is this a bird's nest?" he asked.

"You may have read about such nests," I laughed. "But do not assume every round, hairy object is one. This is a coconut. Recall the description: a hard shell encased in a fibrous husk, surrounded by a green hull. The outer layer here has decayed, exposing the twisted fibers. Break the shell, and you will see the nut inside."

Splitting the nut proved difficult, and to our disappointment, the kernel was dry and inedible.

"Hallo!" Fritz cried. "I always imagined coconuts were full of sweet liquid, like almond milk!"

"So they are," I replied. "When young and fresh. As they ripen, the milk congeals and eventually solidifies into a kernel. This kernel dries as you see, but if dropped on fertile soil, the germ within swells, bursts through the shell, and sprouts a new tree."

"How does the germ penetrate such a thick shell?" Fritz wondered. "Unlike almonds or hazelnuts, it doesn't split down the middle."

"Nature provides for all things," I answered, picking up the fragments. "Notice these three small holes near the stalk. That is the exit for the germ."

Seeking a better specimen, we struggled for some time before finding a coconut where the kernel was not fully desiccated. It was slightly oily and rancid, but given our circumstances, it was a welcome refreshment. This allowed us to postpone our midday meal until later, conserving our supplies.

Pushing onward through a dense thicket choked with lianas, we hacked a path with our hatchets and emerged onto the beach. Before us lay an open vista where the forest swept inland, punctuated by solitary trees of unusual appearance.

"Oh, what absurd-looking trees, Father!" Fritz pointed out. "Look at those strange bumps on the trunks!"

Approaching to inspect them, I identified them as calabash trees. Their fruit grew directly on the stems in the form of gourds, from which bowls, spoons, and bottles could be crafted.

"The savages," I remarked, "are known to craft these ingeniously, using them to hold liquids. In fact, they reportedly cook food inside them."

"Oh, that's impossible!" Fritz protested. "I'm sure the rind would burn through instantly if placed on a fire."

"I didn't say they set the whole gourd on the fire," I clarified. "Once halved and emptied, the shell is filled with water containing the fish or meat. Red-hot stones are added to boil the water. The food cooks, and the gourd remains unharmed."

"That is a clever, simple plan," Fritz admitted. "I daresay I would have invented it myself."

"Columbus's friends thought it easy to balance an egg once he showed them how," I mused. "Let us prepare some of these calabashes for our return."
