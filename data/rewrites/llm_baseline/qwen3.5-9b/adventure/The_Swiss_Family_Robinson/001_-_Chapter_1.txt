For seven days and nights, we were battered by a tempest of unprecedented fury. Darkness swallowed us repeatedly, only to reveal scenes of greater terror when the light returned. By the seventh day, all hope had evaporated.

Driven far from our intended course, we knew not where we drifted. The crew, once disciplined, had succumbed to exhaustion and despair. Their masts were splintered, leaks tore through the hull from bow to stern, and the rising sea threatened to swallow us whole. In place of curses, the sailors now cried out to God for mercy, making frantic vows to perform any task if delivered from this nightmare. Each man committed his soul to Heaven, desperately searching for a way to survive.

My heart ached as I surveyed my family amidst the chaos. Our four young sons were paralyzed by terror. "Dear children," I urged, "if the Lord wills, He can save us from this dread; if not, let us calmly surrender our lives to His hands, remembering the eternal joy of being reunited in heaven. Death is not bitter when it keeps us together."

At my words, my weeping wife lifted her head with newfound bravery. Clinging to her, the boys listened as she spoke with calm, loving encouragement. Seeing her fortitude brought me solace, though my own heart nearly broke. Together, we knelt, praying with profound earnestness. Little Fritz, in particular, begged for deliverance for his parents and brothers, seemingly forgetting his own fear. Such childlike trust soothed our souls, making the horrors of our plight feel slightly bearable. *Perhaps*, I thought, *the Lord hears our prayers!*

Suddenly, above the thundering waves, a cry rang out: "Land! Land!" Simultaneously, the ship struck with a bone-jarring force that threw everyone onto the deck, threatening immediate destruction. Shouts of breaking timber and the roar of pouring water signaled the ship's demise.

"Lower the boats!" the Captain shouted over the tumult. "We are lost!"

"Lost!" I gasped, the word piercing my heart like a dagger. Yet, seeing my children's renewed panic, I forced a cheerful demeanor. "Take courage, my boys! We are still above water! Look—the land is near! Let us strive to reach it. Remember, God helps those who help themselves!" I turned to my wife and children, then climbed the slippery deck.

Waves hurled me down, but I regained my footing. The ship was shattering everywhere; a massive hole gaped in the hull. The crew, abandoning the passengers, crowded into the lifeboats. As the last man boarded, he severed the ropes, casting the boats adrift, ignoring my desperate pleas to include us. The wind drowned my voice, and the towering waves made a return impossible.

Despair washed over me until I noticed the ship's stern, where our cabin lay, wedged tightly between two jagged rocks. While the bow was smashed against the breakers, the rear remained elevated. Through gaps in the driving mist, I glimpsed a rugged coastline—a sign of salvation.

Returning to my family, I masked my anguish with a smile. "Courage, dear ones! Though our ship sails no more, our cabin remains dry. If the winds calm by morning, we shall surely reach shore."

My children, accustomed to trusting my judgment, instantly transformed their fear into hope, even enjoying the cessation of the violent rocking. My wife, however, sensed my hidden distress. I confessed our true predicament, fearing the blow to her nerves, but her unwavering faith restored my own resolve.

"We must find food for supper," she declared. "Starving will weaken us for tomorrow."

Night descended, the storm raging as fiercely as before. Crashes echoed from the wreck, signaling further damage. We feared the lifeboats had perished beneath the waves.

"God will help us soon, Father?" asked my youngest.

"You silly child," scolded Fritz, my eldest, "do not presume to dictate God's plans. We must wait patiently."

"I wish you'd spoken kinder," I murmured.

Finally, a meal was ready. The boys ate heartily and fell asleep quickly. Fritz, aware of the danger, stayed awake to keep watch. After a long silence, he suggested, "Father, couldn't we make swimming belts for Mother and the younger boys? You and I can swim."

"That is an excellent idea," I replied. "Let us prepare them tonight, just in case."

Scouring the wreckage, we found empty flasks and tin cans, linking pairs together to create buoyant floats. My wife and the young boys readily accepted theirs. I gathered matches, tinder, knives, and cord, hoping that if the ship broke apart before dawn, we might reach shore with essential supplies.

While the boys slept, my wife and I prayed vigilantly throughout the night, bracing for disaster.

Dawn broke, revealing a moderating gale and a brilliant sunrise. I woke the boys, and we gathered on the remaining deck. To our astonishment, it was empty—no sailors remained.

"Hallo, Papa! Where are they? Did they take the boats? Why leave us?" the children cried in alarm.

"My good children," I reassured them, "despair not. Those we trusted have abandoned us, but God will not. He has not forsaken us. Let us rise and do our best. Who has an idea?"

"The sea will be calm enough for swimming," Fritz noted.

"And that suits you," Ernest retorted, "since you can swim. But what of Mother and the others? Why not build a raft?"

"A raft carrying us all safely seems difficult," I admitted. "But we must improvise. First, let us gather what is most useful."

Dispersing, we scavenged the wreck. My wife tended to the suffering animals with her youngest son, Franz. Fritz retrieved weapons and ammunition from the armory. Ernest collected tools—nails, scissors, axes, hammers, and chisels. Even little Franz, usually timid, produced a box of sharp fishhooks, earning my praise despite his brothers' scoffs. "These hooks may save us more than anything else!" I declared.

My wife shared better news: livestock survived—a donkey, goats, sheep, a ram, a cow, and a pregnant sow. She had saved them with food, though she worried about preserving milk in the heat.

Turning to the dogs, I sighed. "Jack has brought us two huge, hungry beasts who will consume more than we can provide."

"They'll help us hunt later!" Jack argued.

"No doubt, if we ever reach land," I conceded. "But how?"

"Why not ride in big tubs?" Jack suggested, recalling sailing in ponds at home.

"A capital idea!" I cheered. "Ernest, lend me your tools. Fetch any tubs you can find!"

We located four sturdy wooden casks floating nearby. Hauling them aboard, I sawed them horizontally, creating eight shallow tubs. Exhausted from labor, we refreshed ourselves with goat's milk, wine, and biscuits.

Arranged in a row, the tubs looked promising. Yet, my wife hesitated. "I shall never muster the courage to enter one!"

"Do not worry," I promised. "Once you see my design, you may prefer it to this sinking wreck."

Using a long plank as a keel and bending its ends upward, I constructed a narrow boat from the tubs, dividing it into eight compartments. It proved too heavy and clumsy to move manually. Summoning a capstan bar and using makeshift rollers, I applied the principle of leverage to raise the bow, allowing the boys to slide a roller underneath. Explaining Archimedes' law to Ernest, who vowed to study mechanics once safe, we successfully launched the craft.

She slid swiftly into the water, leaning dangerously to one side. Adding weight balanced her, delighting the boys who raced to climb in. Realizing stability was crucial, I attached outriggers—long poles capped with empty brandy casks—to prevent capsizing.

Testing the vessel, I cleared a path from the wreck and prepared oars. Too late to depart, we postponed the journey until morning.

Another night in the precarious wreck was unpleasant, but necessity prevailed. We ate a simple supper and rested, donning float belts again. Persuading my wife to wear a sailor's dress from a midshipman's chest, I assured her it offered greater comfort. Her initial embarrassment faded as she embraced the practicality, and we retired to our hammocks, ready for the day ahead.

Rising early, we knelt in prayer. "With God's help, we escape today," I announced. "Feed the animals we leave behind; perhaps we can return for them later. Gather everything useful."

The boys obeyed joyfully. From their haul, I selected canvas for a tent, carpenter's tools, firearms, fishing gear, pots, and provisions. These items replaced the ballast I had discarded earlier, yet we still had to leave some treasures behind.

Just as we settled into our tubs, roosters crowed and chickens clucked, protesting our abandonment. "Why not take the fowl?" I decided. "If we starve, they can feed us!" Ten hens and two roosters were secured in a tub with netting. Ducks and geese swam freely, while pigeons flew toward the shore.

My wife returned with a large bag, intending it as a cushion for Franz. "Here is my contribution," she said, tossing it to the little boy.

Ready to depart, we pushed off from the wreck
