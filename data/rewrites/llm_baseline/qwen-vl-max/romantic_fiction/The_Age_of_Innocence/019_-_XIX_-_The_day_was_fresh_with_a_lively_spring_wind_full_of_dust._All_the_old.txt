XIX. The day was crisp and bright, the spring wind stirring up clouds of dust as it swept through the city streets. Inside Grace Church, the air was thick with the scent of camphor from the old ladies’ fur coats—faded sables and yellowed ermines—mingling faintly with the sweet perfume of lilies arranged along the altar. Newland Archer stood on the chancel step, his tall hat and pearl-grey gloves held loosely in one hand, waiting for the signal that the bride had arrived. At last, a nod from the sexton confirmed it: the brougham carrying May Welland and her father was approaching.

But there would be delays—always delays. The bridesmaids were already gathered in the lobby like clusters of Easter blossoms, adjusting their trains and whispering nervously. As custom dictated, the groom was expected to stand alone, exposed to the eyes of all, while the guests waited. Archer accepted this ritual with quiet resignation, just as he had once guided others through it, years ago, when he’d been the best man at someone else’s wedding. Now, the roles were reversed, and every detail felt both familiar and strangely distant.

He’d done everything right: the bouquets of lilac and lilies-of-the-valley delivered; the ushers’ sleeve-links and his own scarf-pin carefully chosen; the Bishop and Rector’s fees tucked safely away. His luggage, including the clothes he’d wear after the ceremony, was already at Mrs. Manson Mingott’s house, where the wedding breakfast would take place. A private train compartment had been reserved—part of the unspoken rule that no one should know where the newlyweds would spend their first night together.

“Got the ring?” whispered young van der Luyden, his voice tight with anxiety. He was new to the role of best man and overwhelmed by the weight of tradition.

Archer reached into his waistcoat pocket, fingers brushing the small gold band engraved with *Newland to May, April ——, 187—*. It was there. He returned to his position, heart steady, gaze fixed on the church door.

Above, Handel’s march swelled through the vaulted ceiling, echoing the countless weddings he’d attended before—each one a pageant of elegance and restraint, each bride floating down the aisle like a vision from another world. He watched the faces in the pews, recognizing them all: Mrs. Selfridge Merry with her towering ostrich feathers, Mrs. Beaufort radiant in diamonds. Would they still be here, he wondered, when the Last Trump sounded? And would heaven have its own box seats?

His thoughts drifted to the women in the front rows—their sharp eyes full of curiosity, the men grumbling about having to wear frock coats and fight over food at the breakfast. “Too bad it’s at old Catherine’s,” he imagined Reggie Chivers muttering. “But I hear Lovell Mingott insisted on his chef. Should be good, if you can get to it.”

Sillerton Jackson would likely add: “My dear fellow, haven’t you heard? It’s to be served at small tables, English style.”

Archer glanced toward the left-hand pew, where his mother sat weeping softly beneath her lace veil, her hands tucked into her grandmother’s ermine muff. “Poor Janey,” he thought. “She can only see the people in the front few pews—and most of them are dowdy Newlands and Dagonets.”

Across the white ribbon separating the families, Beaufort sat tall and red-faced, scanning the women with his usual arrogance. Beside him, his wife shimmered in silvery chinchilla and violets. On the other side, Lawrence Lefferts sat stiffly, his polished head guarding the invisible deity of “Good Form” who presided over the ceremony.

Archer smiled faintly. Once, he too had cared deeply about such things—about whether the presents should be displayed, about whether the ritual was perfect. But now, those concerns seemed trivial, almost absurd. A heated debate over whether to “show” the gifts had nearly ruined the final hours before the wedding. Mrs. Welland had put an end to it with tears: “I should as soon let the reporters loose in my house.” And yet, not so long ago, Archer had believed such matters mattered deeply.

Now, he couldn’t help but wonder: “And all the while,” he thought, “real people were living somewhere, real things happening to them…”

Then came the breathless whisper: “There they come!”

But it wasn’t them. Only Mr. Brown, the livery-stable keeper turned sexton, stepped out to survey the scene before ushering the procession forward. The door closed again. Then, slowly, majestically, it opened once more.

A murmur rose through the church: “The family!”

Mrs. Welland entered first, arm-in-arm with her eldest son. Her face was solemn, her plum satin dress with blue panels and ostrich plumes drawing admiration. But everyone strained to see who followed her.

Rumors had flown the day before: would Mrs. Manson Mingott appear, despite her frailty? She had sent her carpenter to measure the pew—could she fit? Could she even walk? The idea of her being wheeled in her massive Bath chair had horrified the family. Too wide to pass under the awning, too visible to the crowd outside—no, it couldn’t happen. And so, the plan was abandoned.

But still, some hoped. When Mrs. Lovell Mingott appeared instead—her cheeks flushed, her eyes glassy from effort—there was a moment of disappointment. Yet her black Chantilly over lilac satin, crowned with Parma violets, made a striking contrast to her mother-in-law’s colors.

Then came the next figure—gaunt, flamboyant, draped in stripes and fringes, floating like a ghost through the nave.

Archer froze.

It was Medora.

He hadn’t expected her. He’d assumed she was still in Washington with Madame Olenska. The sudden appearance of her aunt stirred something deep inside him—a coldness, a tightening in his chest.

For a moment, he forgot everything—the music, the flowers, the voices—straining to see who came behind her. But the procession ended there. The ushers slipped through the side doors, ready to guide the bridal party.

“Newland—she’s here!” the best man breathed.

Archer blinked. Time had stretched. The bride was halfway down the aisle, the Bishop and Rector standing at the altar, the Spohr symphony weaving delicate notes through the air. The scent of lilies, the sight of May’s tulle and orange blossoms drifting closer, the sound of the Rector’s voice—everything was familiar, yet now it felt alien.

“My God,” he thought. “Have I got the ring?”

Again, he reached into his pocket, fingers finding the cool metal.

Then May was beside him, glowing, radiant. Her presence warmed him through the numbness. He straightened, smiled.

“Dearly beloved, we are gathered together here…” the Rector began.

The ring was on her finger. The blessing was given. The bridesmaids poised to return. The organ began to swell toward the Mendelssohn March—the inevitable, unmissable song of every New York wedding.

“Your arm—give her your arm!” the best man hissed.

Archer snapped back into himself. Where had he been? Lost in thought, perhaps. Or in memory. Had he seen, among the crowd, a dark coil of hair beneath a hat—only to realize it belonged to a stranger, a woman with a long nose, laughably unlike the one whose image it had evoked? Was he losing his grip?

Now, he and May walked slowly down the nave, carried forward by the gentle rhythm of the music. Outside, spring beckoned. The doors stood wide open. Mrs. Welland’s chestnuts pranced at the end of the canvas tunnel, their white favours fluttering.

The footman wrapped May’s cloak around her. Archer jumped into the brougham beside her.

She turned to him, smiling triumphantly. Their hands clasped beneath her veil.

“Darling,” he said.

And suddenly, the abyss opened again—deep, dark, endless. He felt himself falling, sinking, while his voice continued smoothly: “Yes, of course I thought I’d lost the ring. No wedding is complete without that little scare. But you did keep me waiting, didn’t you? I had time to think of every horror that might happen.”

She surprised him. In the middle of Fifth Avenue, she turned and threw her arms around his neck.

“But none ever can happen now, can it, Newland? As long as we two are together?”

Everything had been planned perfectly. After the breakfast, they changed into their travelling clothes, descended the grand Mingott staircase between laughing bridesmaids and weeping parents, and climbed into the brougham beneath a shower of rice and satin slippers. They had time to drive to the station, buy the latest weeklies, and settle into their reserved compartment—where May’s maid had already placed her dove-coloured cloak and gleaming London dressing bag.

The du Lac aunts had offered their Rhinebeck house, a gesture welcomed by both couples. Archer was glad to avoid the usual hotel suite. May loved the idea of going to the country, delighted by the bridesmaids’ futile attempts to guess their destination.

It was considered very English to have a country home lent to you. And though no one knew exactly where they were going—not even the parents—the mystery added to the charm.

Once aboard, the train pulled out of the city, shaking off the endless suburbs and entering the pale green landscape of spring. Conversation flowed easier than Archer had expected.

May was still the same girl—eager, innocent, discussing the wedding like a curious observer. At first, he thought her calmness hid fear. But her eyes were clear, her expression tranquil. She was alone with her husband—but he was merely the charming companion of yesterday. There was no one she liked more, no one she trusted more. And the greatest joy of all? Being off with him, alone, like a grown-up, like a married woman.

It amazed him—how such depth of feeling could exist alongside such absence of imagination. He remembered how, even in St. Augustine, she had reverted to girlish simplicity once her conscience was clear. She lived fully in the moment, never looking ahead. Perhaps that was why her eyes were so transparent, her face so timeless—like a statue of civic virtue or a Greek goddess.

Her blood seemed to run close to her skin, not as a fiery force, but as a preserving fluid. Yet she looked neither hard nor dull—just pure, untouched, primitive.

In the midst of this reflection, he found himself staring at her—as if seeing her for the first time.

Then came the moment he’d known was coming.

“Do you know,” she said, “I was surprised—weren’t you—that Aunt Medora came after all? Ellen wrote that neither of them was well enough to travel. I do wish it had been she who recovered! Did you see the exquisite old lace she sent me?”

He had known this moment would arrive. But he’d hoped, by sheer will, to delay it.

“Yes—yes, it was beautiful,” he murmured, eyes unfocused, wondering if hearing those two words would always bring down the walls of his world.

“Aren’t you tired?” she asked, taking his hand. “It will be good to have tea when we arrive. I’m sure the aunts have everything ready.”

Her mind raced to the silver tea service from the Beauforts, which matched uncle Lovell’s trays perfectly.

At dusk, the train stopped at Rhinebeck. They walked to the carriage.

“Oh, how kind of the van der Luydens—they’ve sent their man from Skuytercliff to meet us,” Archer said, as a liveried servant approached.

“I’m extremely sorry, sir,” the man said, “but there’s been a little accident at the Miss du Lacs’. A leak in the water tank. Mr. van der Luyden heard this morning and sent a housemaid to prepare the Patroon’s house. It’ll be quite comfortable, I assure you. And the Miss du Lacs have sent their cook. Everything will be just as if you were at Rhinebeck.”

Archer stared, stunned.

“Exactly the same?” May chimed in, eagerly. “The Patroon’s house? Oh, it will be a hundred thousand times better—won’t it, Newland? How dear of Mr. van der Luyden to think of it!”

As they drove off, the maid beside the coachman, their bridal bags gleaming on the seat, she continued: “Only imagine—I’ve never been inside it. Have you? The van der Luydens don’t show it to many. But Ellen saw it, and she says it’s the only house in America she could truly be happy in.”

“Well—then that’s what we’re going to be, isn’t it?” Archer said, trying to sound cheerful.

She smiled, her eyes bright. “Ah, it’s just our luck beginning—the wonderful luck we’re always going to have together!”
