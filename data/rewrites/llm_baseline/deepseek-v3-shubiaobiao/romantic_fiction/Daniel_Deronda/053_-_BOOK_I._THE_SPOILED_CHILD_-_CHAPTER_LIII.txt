—Shakespeare, *Antony and Cleopatra*

Three days passed before Deronda was summoned again to his mother’s presence. When the message finally came—*"I leave today. Come and see me at once."*—he obeyed without delay.

The room was darker now, the blinds drawn low, the air heavy with the scent of silk and resignation. The Princess entered, draped in dusky orange, her sleeves wide, her arms bare, the black lace about her head like a shadow of the past. She looked at him with the same fierce beauty, but her bearing was different—steadier, as though she had resolved something within herself.

She kissed him, once on each cheek, then settled among her cushions, a queen granting audience rather than a mother welcoming a son.

"You are well?" Deronda asked.

"At ease enough," she said. "Is there more you wish to ask?"

"Only if I might find the house in Genoa where you lived with my grandfather."

"Gone," she said, waving a dismissive hand. "Pulled down. But the chest holds papers—names, places, the dry bones of our history. I left them behind long ago."

Deronda masked his sorrow. "Then I will ask only what you wish to tell me."

"I have told you everything required," she said, her voice cool, distant. She had poured out her confession once; she would not revisit it.

The finality of it struck him like a blow. All his life, he had longed for her. Now, faced with her indifference, he could not hide the ache in his voice. "Then we part, and I am nothing to you?"

"It is better so," she said, softer now. "You would not love me. You think I wronged you. You judge me."

He stood abruptly, stung. "Must you silence me before I speak?"

She studied him, then smiled faintly. "You are angry. Good. It means you are stronger for what I did."

"And what shall I do with that strength?" he asked.

"Will you become what your grandfather wished? A Jew in heart and soul?"

"I cannot undo my upbringing," Deronda said firmly. "But I will honor my blood. If there is work among my people that calls to me, I will answer."

Her eyes narrowed. "You are in love with a Jewess."

He flushed but held her gaze. "My choices are my own."

"You lie poorly," she said, a sharp amusement in her tone. "She will not have you unless you are one of them. I know how such women think."

Deronda said nothing.

"Ah," she murmured. "Just as your father loved me. And now his father wins at last."

"Mother," he pleaded, "do not see it as a battle. You gave me freedom; fate has given me purpose. Can you not find peace in that?"

She shook her head. "I hated what he wanted of me. I will not pretend otherwise."

"Then hate, but do not let it poison you," he said, his voice rough with emotion. "You sought what you thought best for me. Can you not forgive him for doing the same?"

"Not for me," she said bitterly. "For his pride. For his grand vision of a Deliverer sprung from his line. He wanted a grandson with a *true Jewish heart*—and now he has you."

The words struck Deronda like a prophecy. "Then let me be a bridge, not a weapon. Let me mend what was broken."

She laughed, a sound without joy. "You speak like a man who believes in healing. I am past it."

A silence fell. Then, abruptly, she asked, "Is she beautiful?"

He stiffened. "Who?"

"The Jewess you love."

He could not lie. "Yes."

"Ambitious?"

"No."

"Not like me, then," she said, drawing a jeweled miniature from her robe—her own likeness in youth, radiant and defiant. "I had a right to be more than a daughter, more than a mother. I was meant for greatness."

"You were," Deronda agreed, his throat tight.

"Take it," she said, pressing the portrait into his hand. "If she is kind, let her think kindly of me."

He hesitated. "I have no certainty she will ever be mine."

"Tell me of her."

Reluctantly, he did—Mirah’s sorrows, her devotion to a mother she barely remembered, her longing for a people she had been taught to scorn.

The Princess listened, then scoffed. "Sentiment. She clings to a heritage she does not know."

"Perhaps. But it is real to her."

"Like you," she mused. Then, with sudden intensity, she seized his shoulders. "Would you have loved me, had I kept you? Or would we have fought until love turned to hate?"

"I would have loved you always," he said, the words raw.

She released him, folding her arms. "It does not matter. I was not made for love. I took it where it was given, but I did not return it. Men have been subject to me—never I to them."

"Then you never knew the greater happiness," he said softly.

"Perhaps," she admitted. "But I had my own. For a time." She closed her eyes, as if gazing into some distant realm. "This is another life now. One where I am already gone."

Deronda’s composure broke. A sob escaped him.

Her eyes opened. She touched his face. "Goodbye, my son. We will not meet again. Kiss me."

He embraced her, and for a moment, they clung to each other—two souls bound by blood yet divided by fate.

When he left, he was no longer the same man. The boyish yearning for his mother had burned away, leaving behind something harder, deeper. He carried her secrets now, her regrets, her defiance. And he would carry them always.
