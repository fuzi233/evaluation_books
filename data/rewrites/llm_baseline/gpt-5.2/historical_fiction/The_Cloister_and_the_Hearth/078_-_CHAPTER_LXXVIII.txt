Sybrandt—or some other rakehell of that stamp—had once flung out, with a laugh, that a lie is not like a blow from a curtal axe. True enough. A steel edge has its measure: you may guess where it will bite, how deep, and what it will hew. But a lie is a seed you cannot see sprout; it runs under doors, it creeps into beds, it takes root in wills and wedding-lines, and it bears a harvest no man can count.

Here, too, one heartless falsehood—minted lightly between brothers who only wished, as they told themselves, to lessen the number of hands that might dip into their father’s substance—spread farther than their malice had meant. They gained little. Instead they saw their mother’s heart, emptied of one son, fill itself the more fiercely with the name and face of little Gerard. And then, like salt cast upon their secret, there came a day when Eli, before them all, spoke plain as a bell: Gerard being lost, and like dead, he had set it down in his will that the child should be provided for, and Margaret too—Margaret, his poor son’s widow.

At that, a look went like lightning between the black sheep of the house: not triumph, but warning—such as thieves give one another when a door has creaked. Cornelis had the words on his tongue—Gerard is like to be alive—yet he saw his mother’s eyes on him, steady and searching, and he swallowed his speech as a man gulps down something that chokes.

But the other partner in the lie, Ghysbrecht van Swieten, had no such easy swallowing. He was failing; his breath grew short on stairs, his hands trembled at the clasps of his purse. He felt, with a chill that was not of weather, how near the hour came when all his goods would fall from him like clothes, and only the naked weight of his deeds cling fast to his soul.

He had never been able to blind himself altogether; he was too shrewd, too taught by years of reckonings. Remorse came upon him by fits, like a gust through a chink. And so, when Gerard’s letter had reached his hands—the letter meant for Margaret—he had compounded with himself, as men do who would be thought honest and yet mean to keep their hold on earth.

“I cannot give up land and money,” said his giant Avarice, squatting broad across his heart.

“I will cause her no needless pain,” piped his dwarf Conscience, thin-voiced, but not quite dead.

So he tampered with the seal, finger and nail worrying the wax; and, finding no syllable there of deed or property, he carried the letter himself to Margaret, and—strange jugglery of the human mind—took merit to himself for that crumb of right. He set it like a counter on the scale against all he withheld; and because he was both judge and prisoner, he found the weight convenient.

Then Margaret’s child was born; and that small wail in the world shifted the whole matter into another light. If Gerard’s letter should work as Gerard meant—if it should bring him back, or even bring the truth—then Ghysbrecht’s trick would not merely rob her of acres; it would stain her name. Dishonour would touch the daughter of Peter Brandt, the granddaughter of Floris: the child of two old friends that had once succoured Ghysbrecht in his plague-time and his poverty. These thoughts, coming at that season of life when a man’s body wanes and old faces rise up clearer than yesterday’s, filled him with dark horrors. He would start at night as if some dead hand had shaken him; the silence of his chamber seemed full of whispers.

Yet he dared not confess. The cure was an honest man; he would have made Ghysbrecht disgorge, not sentiments, but coin and land. And with Ghysbrecht, Avarice had hardened into habit—like salt meat long kept, tough and stubborn; penitence was but a taste on the tongue, gone as soon as swallowed.

Matters stood thus when one day, coming back from the town-hall to his own house, he found a woman waiting in his vestibule with a child on her arm. She was veiled; and, thinking her one of those who come by stealth to bargain with shame, he addressed her in a magisterial tone, as if he were already her judge.

At that she let fall the veil and looked him full in the face.

It was Margaret Brandt.

Her sudden presence—her pallor, her bright, fevered eyes—struck him like a clap; he could not hide the start nor the quick confusion that ran over him.

“Where is my Gerard?” she cried; and her breast rose and fell as if the words had been torn up from deep water. “Is he alive?”

“For aught I know,” stammered Ghysbrecht. “I— I hope so, for your sake. Prithee come into this room. The servants—”

“Not a step,” said Margaret. Her voice was low, but it had iron in it. She set her hand on his shoulder and held him, not with violence only, but with the sheer strength of a woman driven past fear. “You know the secret of that which is breaking my heart. Why does not my Gerard come, nor send a line this many months? Answer me, or all the town is like to hear me—ay, and thy servants too. My misery is too great to be sported with.”

He persisted, trembling, that he knew nothing; that he had no more knowledge than she.

She told him those who had sent her to him told her another tale. “You do know,” said she, with a dreadful quietness, “why he neither comes nor sends.”

Ghysbrecht turned paler and paler; but he gathered his dignity about him like a cloak, and tried to stand upon it.

“Would you believe,” said he, “those two knaves against a man of worship?”

“What two knaves?” said Margaret sharply; for she caught at every word as a hungry child snatches crust.

He stammered, snared by his own slip. “Said ye not—? There, I am a poor old broken man, whose memory is shaken. And you come here and confuse me so, I know not what I say.”

“Ah, sir,” said Margaret, and now her tone changed; grief and a strange tenderness made it deep, “your memory is shaken—or sure you would not be my enemy. My father saved you from the plague, when none other would come anigh you; and was ever your friend. My grandfather Floris helped you in your early poverty, and loved you, man and boy. Three generations of us you have seen; and here is the fourth of us.”

She shifted the child—little Gerard—so his face came into the light that fell from the vestibule window. He had been fretful, and now, feeling the mother’s quickened pulse, he hushed, staring with the grave wonder of infants; his mouth still shone with milk. The very smell of him—warm skin, clean linen, and that faint sour-sweet breath that clings to babes—went up like an appeal.

“This,” she said, “is your old friend Peter’s grandchild, and your old friend Floris his great-grandchild. Look down on his innocent face, and think of theirs!”

“Woman, you torture me,” sighed Ghysbrecht; and he sank upon a bench as if his knees had been cut.

But she saw her advantage, and she took it—not cruelly, but as drowning folk clutch. She kneeled before him and set the boy on his knees, steadying him with her hands. The child’s small fingers, exploring, caught at Ghysbrecht’s sleeve and the roughness of his furred gown; he made a soft sound in his throat, half coo, half question.

“This fatherless babe,” said Margaret, “is poor Margaret Brandt’s, that never did you ill, and comes of a race that loved you. Nay, look at his face; ’twill melt thee more than any word of mine. Saints of heaven, what can a poor desolate girl and her babe have done, to wipe out all memory of thine own young days—when thou wert guiltless as he is—that now looks up in thy face and implores thee to give him back his father?”

And with her arms under the child she held him up higher and higher, so the little one’s eyes were level with the old man’s. The baby smiled—one of those sudden, careless smiles that are like sunlight slipping through cloud, unbought and undeserved.

Ghysbrecht cast a wild look of anguish on the child, and another on the kneeling mother; and then something in him—wounded and venomous—sprang up. He started to his feet, shrieking:

“Avaunt, ye pair of adders!”

For a moment the stung soul lent the old limbs a cruel vigour. He walked rapidly to and fro, wringing his hands and clutching at his white hair, as if he would tear the thoughts out by the roots.

“Forget those days?” he cried. “I forget all else. Oh, woman, woman—sleeping or waking I see but the faces of the dead. I hear but the voices of the dead. And I shall soon be among the dead. There—there—what is done is done. I am in hell. I am in hell.”

The unnatural force fled as it had come. He staggered; and but for Margaret—who, in spite of her own shaking, flung an arm about him—he would have fallen full length. She cried out for help.

Two servants ran in. They took him, helpless now as an unbaked loaf that collapses when the oven’s heat fails, and carried him away in a swoon, his old furrowed face white and slack, his hair hanging down over a servant’s elbow like spilled flax.

“Heaven forgive me,” Margaret whispered, standing where they left her. “I doubt I have killed the poor old man.”

Yet the visit, meant to pierce the mystery, left it darker. When she turned over each word he had let slip, her suspicion grew teeth. He did know something. And that cry of “two knaves”—who were they? Who had told her, or seemed to tell her, a tale that made him betray himself?

“Oh, my Gerard,” she said, drawing her child close as if the babe were the only warm certainty left, “my poor deserted babe—thou and I are wading in deep waters.”

The journey to Tergou had eaten money like a hungry fire, and she could ill spare it; and then, as if the world watched for the instant her purse was thinnest, a customer ran away owing her. Bread had to be kneaded with less wheat; broth stretched with more water; the little household sounds—the scrape of a spoon in a pot, the clink of a farthing on wood—took on a sharpness that seemed to mock her.

Once more she was driven to unfold Catherine’s angel, that precious piece of gold, and feel its cold weight in her palm. She had it in her hand as she came down-stairs—already tasting in her mouth the shame of parting with it, as if she were selling a memory. And then she stopped.

Loose silver lay on the table, and beside it a written line.

For Gerard his wife.

She fell upon the writing with a little cry, not of pain but of astonished life; and soon it broke into a cry of joy that filled the small room like a sudden song.

“He is alive,” she whispered, and her lips trembled upon the letters. “He sends me this by some friendly hand.”

She kissed the writing again and again, as if she could drink comfort through ink. Then she hid it in her bosom, close to the heat of her heart.

Time rolled on. No news came—no word, no sure sign, no voice calling in the street, no footstep on the stair that made her certain. Yet about every two months a small sum in silver found its way into the house.

Sometimes it lay quietly on the table, as if it had always belonged there. Once it was flung in through the bedroom window in a purse, the leather striking the floor with a dull thud that made her start from sleep. Once she found it at the bottom of Luke’s basket; he had stopped at the public-house to talk to a friend, and swore he knew nothing of it. The giver—or his agent—was never caught. Catherine disowned it with honest surprise. Margaret van Eyck swore she had no hand in it. So did Eli.

And Margaret, whenever it came, would look down at little Gerard—now sturdier, with bread-crumbs on his lips and a way of drumming his heels against her hip when he wanted to be let down—and say, half to him and half to the empty air:

“Oh, my poor deserted child, you and I are wading in deep waters.”

At least half of this modest, useful supply she spent—against prudence, and in defiance of her own station—on clothing the boy beyond what folk deemed fit. She scoured for decent cloth, chose seams that would hold, washed and aired the little garments till they smelled of soap and sun, not of sour damp. If there was to be hunger, let there not be slovenliness; if there was to be loss, let there not be shame upon the child’s body.

“If it does come from Gerard,” she told herself, tightening a stitch, “he shall see his boy neat.”

The mothers in the street began to sneer—especially those whose own brats went with elbows out, and whose envy made a harsher music than their tongues. Their voices, sharp as crows, followed her sometimes; but the sounds that ruled Margaret were other: the hush of her child asleep, the small steady chewing of crust, the crackle of kindling, the distant bell that marked the hours whether they were joyful or not.

Months rolled on; and the keener torments—those that sting and keep you moving—gave way to a dead sickness of heart, heavy as sodden bread. She went back to her first thought, the thought that had never truly left:

“Gerard must be dead.”

She should never see her boy’s father again; never see the marriage lines that would make the world shut its mouth. That last grief—her lack of proof—had been somewhat eased by Eli and Catherine, who, in their own way, had recognized the betrothal. But now it revived in full force; others would not be so gentle, nor would their tongues be bridled by family affection.

Often she moaned over the child’s illegitimacy, the word itself like a stone in her throat.

“Is it not enough for us to be bereaved?” she would whisper in the dimness, rocking the boy when he fretted. “Must we be dishonoured too? Oh, that we had ne’er been born.”

Then a change came over Peter Brandt.

For nearly two years his mind had been clouded since the paralytic stroke; he had lived as if behind a misted window, sometimes smiling at shadows, sometimes staring without seeing. Now the cloud began, by fits, to lift. He had intervals of clear intelligence; and in those hours he and Margaret would speak of Gerard, touching the name softly, as one touches a sore place to see if it still hurts—until his mind wandered again and the door shut.

One day Margaret, returning after some hours abroad, found him conversing with Catherine in a manner he had not done since the stroke—words linked, sense steady, his voice more like the old physician’s than she had heard in long.

“Eh, girl,” said Catherine, “why must you be out? But indeed I have told him all; and we have been a-crying together over thy troubles.”

Margaret stood silent, joy rising in her like leaven. She looked from one to the other, hardly daring to breathe lest the moment should crumble.

Peter smiled on her—a true smile, not the wandering one. “Come,” said he, “let me bless thee.”

She kneeled at his feet. His hand, though weakened, rested upon her head with a gravity that seemed to steady the whole house. He blessed her most eloquently, with the old cadence that had comforted sick-beds and frightened death away for many.

He told her she had been, all her life, the lovingest, truest, and most obedient daughter Heaven ever sent to a poor old widowed man.

“May thy son be to thee,” he said, “what thou hast been to me!”

After that his lids drooped and he dozed, the blessing still warm on her crown like fresh bread from an oven.

Then Catherine and Margaret whispered together, careful of the old man’s sleep; the house itself seemed to listen, the faint creak of timber and distant street-sounds softened, as if the world had stepped on tiptoe.

“All our talk e’en now,” Catherine breathed, “was of Gerard. It lies heavy on his mind. His poor head must often have listened to us when it seemed quite dark. Margaret, he is a very understanding man; he thought of many things. ‘He may be in prison,’ says he; ‘or forced to go fighting for some king; or sent to Constantinople to copy books there; or gone into the Church after all.’ He had a bent that way.”

“Ah, mother,” whispered Margaret back, “he doth but deceive himself as we do.”

She had scarce finished when a strange interruption broke in, as sudden as a shout in a still field.

A loud voice cried out, “I SEE HIM, I SEE HIM!”

Peter Brandt’s eyes flew wide, dilating; he seemed to look not at the wall, but through it—through brick, through distance, through time itself.

“IN A BOAT; ON A GREAT RIVER; COMING THIS WAY,” he cried. “Sore disfigured; but I knew him. Gone! gone! all dark.”

His head sank back; the fire that had flared in him died as quickly as it had blazed.

He asked feebly, “Where is Margaret?”

“Dear father, I am by thy side.” Her hand found his, and held it fast. “Oh, mother—mother—what is this?”

“I cannot see thee,” he murmured, “and but a moment agone I saw all round the world. Ay—ay. Well, I am ready. Is this thy hand? Bless thee, my child, bless thee! Weep not! The tree is ripe.”

The old physician’s own body read the signs aright. These calm words were his last. The next moment he drooped his head, and gently—placidly—drifted away from earth, like an infant sinking into rest.

The torch had flashed up before going out.
