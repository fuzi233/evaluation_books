The scent of cardamom and cloves wafted through the humid air as I sat at my desk, quill in hand, pouring out my thoughts onto the page. To my dear relatives in England, I wrote, seeking to clarify the reasons behind my estrangement from my cousin, John Herncastle. I had long kept my true feelings hidden, lest they misunderstand and jeopardize our family's reputation.

As I dipped my quill into the inkwell, memories flooded back to the tumultuous days of the storming of Seringapatam, where we had fought side by side under General Baird on the fourth of May, 1799. The tales of a legendary treasure hidden within the palace walls had captivated our camp, and I recalled one story in particular – the legend of the Moonstone, a glittering gem said to adorn the forehead of the four-handed Indian god, embodying the mystical power of the moon.

In the earliest recorded annals of India, the Moonstone was said to have been imbued with the deity's essence, its radiance waxing and waning in tandem with the lunar cycles. A similar myth was whispered to have originated in ancient Greece and Rome, though the stone itself was of a lesser quality, a semi-transparent gem influenced by the moon's phases. As I wrote, the aroma of freshly brewed tea wafted into my chambers, transporting me to the distant lands of the East.

Our story began in the eleventh century, when the Mohammedan conqueror, Mahmoud of Ghizni, swept across India, desecrating the revered temple of Somnauth and stripping it of its treasures. Amidst the chaos, the moon-god alone remained untouched, preserved by three devoted Brahmins who spirited it away under the cover of night to the sacred city of Benares. There, within a shrine inlaid with precious stones and supported by pillars of gold, the deity was re-established and worshipped.

On the night of its consecration, Vishnu, the Preserver, appeared to the three Brahmins in a vision, breathing the divine breath upon the Moonstone. The Brahmins, awestruck, knelt and veiled their faces as the deity decreed that the gem should be vigilantly guarded by three priests in succession, night and day, for the duration of human existence.
