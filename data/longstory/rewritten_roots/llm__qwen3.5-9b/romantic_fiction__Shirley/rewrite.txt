In recent times, the northern counties of England have been deluged with a prodigious rainfall of curates. They blanket the hills in dense layers; every parish boasts one or more of these young men, energetic in spirit and seemingly destined to accomplish vast amounts of good. However, our narrative does not concern itself with this modern era. We shall retreat to the dawn of the nineteenth century—those early years. The present day is dusty, sun-scalded, arid, and oppressive; let us avoid the blistering noon, seek refuge in a siesta, drift through the midday hours in slumber, and dream instead of the coming dawn.

Should you, dear reader, infer from this introduction that a romance awaits you, you would be grievously mistaken. Do you anticipate sentiment, poetry, and reverie? Do you crave passion, stimulation, and melodrama? Compose your expectations; lower them to a modest level. Before you lies something real, cool, and substantial; something as unromantic as a Monday morning, when workers awaken with the grim realization that they must rise and attend to their labors. It is not strictly denied that you may later taste excitement, perhaps toward the middle or conclusion of the feast, but it is resolved that the first course served shall be one fit for a Catholic—even an Anglo-Catholic—to consume on Good Friday during Passion Week: cold lentils and vinegar without oil; unleavened bread with bitter herbs, and no roasted lamb.

To return to the past: while recent years saw an abundance of curates, the period between 1811 and 1812 witnessed a drought. There was no Pastoral Aid, no Additional Curates' Society to extend a helping hand to weary old rectors, enabling them to hire vigorous young colleagues from Oxford or Cambridge. The current generation of apostolic successors, disciples of Dr. Pusey and instruments of the Propaganda, were then merely being hatched under cradle blankets or regenerated via nursery baptism in washbasins. One could not have looked upon any of them and recognized the Italian-ironed double frills of their net caps as surrounding the brow of a preordained successor to St. Paul, St. Peter, or St. John; nor could one have foreseen the white surplices hidden within their long nightgowns, garments they would later wield to exercise the souls of their parishioners and confound their traditional vicars by waving them aloft in the pulpit.

Yet, even in those lean days, curates existed. This precious plant was rare, but it could still be found. A favored district in the West Riding of Yorkshire could boast three rods of Aaron blossoming within a twenty-mile radius. You shall behold them, reader. Enter this neat garden-house on the outskirts of Whinbury, proceed into the small parlour. There they sit at dinner. Permit me to introduce them: Mr. Donne, curate of Whinbury; Mr. Malone, curate of Briarfield; Mr. Sweeting, curate of Nunnely. These are Mr. Donne's quarters, belonging to John Gale, a small clothier. Mr. Donne has graciously invited his brethren to dine with him. Join us, you and I, to observe what is seen and hear what is spoken. For the moment, however, they are merely eating; while they eat, we shall converse apart.

These gentlemen are in the prime of youth, possessing all the vigor of that fascinating age—a vitality their moping, elderly vicars would gladly channel into pastoral duties, often wishing to see it spent diligently supervising schools and visiting the sick. But the youthful Levites deem such tasks dull. They prefer to lavish their energies on pursuits that, to outside observers, appear heavier with ennui and more cursed by monotony than the labor of a weaver at his loom, yet which provide them with an inexhaustible supply of enjoyment and occupation.

I refer to their frantic back-and-forth movement among themselves, traveling to and from their respective lodgings—not a simple circle, but a triangular route of visits maintained throughout the year, regardless of season or weather. With incomprehensible zeal, they brave snow, hail, wind, rain, mire, and dust to dine, drink tea, or sup with one another. What draws them remains elusive. It is not friendship, for whenever they meet, they quarrel. It is not religion—the topic is never raised among them; theology may be discussed occasionally, but piety is absent. Nor is it a love of food and drink; each could enjoy equally fine joints, puddings, potent teas, and succulent toast at his own lodging as he receives at his brother's. Mrs. Gale, Mrs. Hogg, and Mrs. Whipp—their respective landladies—assert that "it is just for naught else but to give folk trouble." By "folk," the good ladies naturally mean themselves, for indeed, they are kept in a perpetual state of agitation by this system of mutual intrusion.

Mr. Donne and his guests are currently at dinner. Mrs. Gale serves them, though a spark of the hot kitchen fire burns in her eyes. She believes the privilege of hosting a friend occasionally without extra charge—a benefit included in her letting terms—has been sufficiently exercised recently. It is only Thursday, yet on Monday Mr. Malone of Briarfield came to breakfast and stayed for dinner; on Tuesday, Mr. Malone and Mr. Sweeting of Nunnely arrived for tea, lingered until supper, occupied the spare bed, and visited her again at breakfast on Wednesday morning; now, on Thursday, they are both here for dinner, and she is nearly certain they will remain overnight. "C'en est trop," she would say if she could speak French.

Mr. Sweeting minces his slice of roast beef, complaining of its toughness; Mr. Donne remarks that the beer is flat. Indeed, that is the worst of it: if they would only be civil, Mrs. Gale would not mind so much. If they merely seemed satisfied with what they received, she would not care. But "these young parsons are so high and so scornful, setting everyone beneath their feet." They treat her with less than civility precisely because she does not employ a servant but performs the household work herself, as her mother did before her. Consequently, they constantly criticize Yorkshire ways and Yorkshire people, leading Mrs. Gale to conclude that none of them are true gentlemen or of gentle birth. "The old parsons are worth the whole lot of college lads," she muses. "They know what constitutes good manners and are kind to both high and low."

"More bread!" cries Mr. Malone, in a tone that, though prolonged for only two syllables, identifies him immediately as a native of the land of shamrocks and potatoes. Mrs. Gale dislikes Mr. Malone more than either of the others, yet she fears him as well. He is a tall, powerfully built figure with genuine Irish limbs and a face authentically national—not the Milesian type, nor Daniel O'Connell's style, but the high-featured, North American Indian visage associated with a certain class of Irish gentry, possessing a petrified and proud expression better suited to the owner of slave estates than to the landlord of a free peasantry. His father called himself a gentleman; he was poor and deeply in debt, yet besottedly arrogant, and his son mirrored him perfectly.

Mrs. Gale offers the loaf.

"Cut it, woman," commands her guest. The "woman" cuts it accordingly. Had she followed her inclinations, she would have cut the parson as well; her Yorkshire soul revolts absolutely from his manner of command.

The curates have hearty appetites. Though the beef is "tough," they consume large quantities of it. They swallow a considerable amount of the "flat beer," while a dish of Yorkshire pudding and two tureens of vegetables vanish like leaves before locusts. The cheese receives distinguished attention, and a "spice-cake," served as dessert, disappears like a vision, leaving no trace. Its elegy is sung in the kitchen by Abraham, Mrs. Gale's son and heir, a youth of six summers. Having counted on inheriting the cake, he lifts up his voice and weeps bitterly when his mother brings down the empty platter.

Meanwhile, the curates sit sipping their wine, a liquor of unpretentious vintage, moderately enjoyed. Mr. Malone, indeed, would much prefer whisky, but Mr. Donne, being an Englishman, does not keep the beverage. As they sip, they argue—not on politics, philosophy, or literature (topics entirely uninteresting to them)—nor even on practical or doctrinal theology, but on minute points of ecclesiastical discipline, frivolities that seem as empty as bubbles to anyone except themselves. Mr. Malone, managing to secure two glasses of wine while his companions settle for one, gradually becomes hilarious in his own fashion: he grows slightly insolent, speaks rudely in a hectoring tone, and laughs boisterously at his own brilliance.

Each companion becomes, in turn, his target. Malone possesses a stock of jokes he dispenses regularly on such convivial occasions, rarely varying his wit; indeed, he shows no concern for monotony or the opinions of others. The night was absolute, a heavy blanket of pitch-black silence where stars and moon had been smothered by gray rain-clouds—clouds that looked merely gray in daylight but transformed into somber sable against the night sky. Curate Malone was not a man inclined toward close observation of nature; the shifting moods of the heavens usually passed him entirely unnoticed. He could walk miles on a brilliant April morning without seeing the delicate interplay between earth and sky, failing to notice when a sunbeam kissed a hilltop to make it gleam in green light, or when a shower wept over the landscape, hiding the peaks beneath the disheveled tresses of low-hanging clouds. Consequently, he cared little to contrast the current sky—a muffled, streaming vault of black, broken only by a tremulous, lurid shimmer cast by the furnaces of Stilbro' ironworks on the eastern horizon—with a clear, frosty night. He did not trouble himself to locate the missing constellations or lament the loss of the "black-blue" serenity of the air-ocean, dotted with its white islets, now replaced by a heavier, denser ocean rolling below and concealing it all.

Instead, he doggedly pressed onward, leaning slightly forward in his stride and wearing his hat pushed back over his ears, a habit of his Irish heritage. *Tramp, tramp* went his footsteps along the causeway, where the road allowed for such an accompaniment; *splash, splash* echoed through the mire-filled cart ruts, where flagstones yielded to soft mud. His gaze sought only specific landmarks: the spire of Briarfield Church ahead, and further on, the lights of Redhouse. He longed deeply for a tumbler of whisky-and-water. Malone took a direct line through them, leaping over hedges and walls. He passed only one building here, which appeared large and hall-like despite its irregular shape. A high gable loomed, followed by a long front, then a low gable, and finally a thick, lofty stack of chimneys. Trees stood behind it. It was dark; not a single candle shone from any window. Absolute stillness reigned, broken only by the rain trickling from the eaves and the rather wild, yet very low, whistle of the wind circling the chimneys and threading through the branches.

Having passed this building, the fields, previously flat, began a rapid descent. Clearly, a valley lay below, through which the sound of running water drifted. A single light glimmered in the depths. Malone steered directly for that beacon.

Cheerfulness, it turns out, relies just as heavily on what is happening inside us as on what surrounds us outside. I mention this obvious truth because I observed Messrs. Helstone and Moore stepping out from the mill-yard gates, leading a tiny procession, buoyed by spirits that were genuinely high. When the beam of a lantern—each of the three walkers carried one—fell upon Mr. Moore's countenance, a peculiar, lively spark ignited in his eyes, casting a newfound vitality across his dark features. Similarly, when the light touched the rector's face, his usually severe expression softened into a grin, shining with evident delight. One might assume that a drizzling night and a somewhat dangerous journey were circumstances calculated to dampen such enthusiasm, yet here they were, undeterred. Had anyone working on Stilbro' Moor glimpsed this party, they would have taken great pleasure in taking aim at either leader from behind a wall; and the leaders, knowing this perfectly, were elated precisely because of it.

I am well aware, reader, that it is dreadfully improper for a parson to be warlike; I know he should be a man of peace. I have a vague understanding of a clergyman's mission among mankind, and I recall clearly whose servant he is, whose message he bears, and whose example he should emulate. Nevertheless, if you are a parson-hater, do not expect me to walk every step of your dismal, downward path. Do not expect me to join your sweeping anathemas, your narrow and poisonous rancour against "the cloth," nor to raise my voice in horror alongside a Supplehough or a Barraclough against the diabolical rector of Briarfield. He was not diabolical at all. The fault lay simply in that he had missed his vocation. He was born a soldier, but circumstance had made him a priest. Otherwise, he was a conscientious, hard-headed, hard-handed, brave, stern, implacable, and faithful little man—a figure almost devoid of sympathy, ungentle, prejudiced, and rigid, yet utterly true to principle, honorable, sagacious, and sincere.

It seems to me that one cannot always trim men to fit their professions, nor should one curse them solely because their calling hangs upon them awkwardly. Nor will I curse Helstone, though he was indeed a clerical Cossack. Yet he *was* cursed, and by many of his own parishioners, as well as by others; he was also adored. This is the frequent fate of men who display partiality in friendship and bitterness in enmity, who cling equally to principles and prejudices.

Helstone and Moore, both in excellent spirits and united temporarily in a common cause, might seem destined to converse amicably as they rode side by side. Oh, no! These two men, both of hard, bilious constitutions, rarely met without chafing each other's tempers. Their constant point of friction was the war. Helstone was a staunch Tory (Tories existed in those days), while Moore was a bitter Whig—at least, as far as opposing the war-party went, which directly affected his own interests; on that specific issue alone did he claim any British political stance. He delighted in infuriating Helstone by declaring his belief in the invincibility of Bonaparte, taunting England and Europe with the impotence of their resistance, and coolly arguing that it was better to yield sooner rather than later, since the Frenchman must eventually crush every opponent and reign supreme. Helstone could not endure such sentiments. He tolerated them only because Moore was considered an outcast and alien, possessing but half-British blood to temper the foreign gall in his veins. Furthermore, Helstone harbored a grudging respect for the dogged consistency with which Moore asserted these opinions.

Mr. Yorke was, in every conceivable sense, the quintessential Yorkshire gentleman. At fifty-five, he appeared even older at first glance, his hair a stark, silvery white. His forehead was broad rather than high, his complexion fresh and robust. The harshness of the North was etched into his features and echoed in his voice. Every trait was thoroughly English—devoid of any Norman lineage. It was an inelegant, unclassical, and decidedly non-aristocratic countenance. Those accustomed to fine things might have labeled it vulgar; sensible individuals would have called it characteristic; while the shrewd among us would have delighted in it, recognizing the pith, sagacity, and raw, original intelligence stamped upon every line and furrow. Yet, it remained an indocile, scornful, and sarcastic face—the visage of a man difficult to lead and impossible to drive. He was tall, well-made, wiry, and possessed a stately integrity of posture; there was absolutely no hint of clownishness about him.

To sketch Mr. Yorke's physical appearance is relatively easy; to define his mind is far more arduous. Do not expect to meet a perfection here, nor a benevolent, philanthropic elder. While he has spoken with sense and good feeling to Mr. Moore, do not assume he always thinks or speaks justly and kindly.

First, Mr. Yorke lacked the organ of veneration—a significant deficit that misaligns a man wherever reverence is due. Second, he lacked the faculty of comparison, a deficiency that strips one of sympathy. Third, he possessed too little of the organs of benevolence and ideality, robbing his nature of glory and softness, diminishing those divine qualities across the universe.

This lack of veneration rendered him intolerant toward those above him—kings, nobles, priests, dynasties, parliaments, and all their institutions. To him, their doings, enactments, forms, rights, and claims were abominations, mere rubbish. He found no pleasure in them and believed that razing their high places and crushing their occupants would bring clear gain to the world with no damage whatsoever. Furthermore, this absence of veneration deadened his heart to the electric delight of admiring what is admirable, drying up a thousand pure sources of enjoyment and withering a thousand vivid pleasures. He was not irreligious, belonging to no sect, but his faith was that of a man lacking awe, imagination, and tenderness. He believed in God and Heaven, but his conception of them was stripped of wonder.

His inability to compare made him inconsistent. While professing excellent general doctrines of mutual toleration, he harbored a bigoted antipathy toward specific classes. He spoke of "parsons" and their ilk, of "lords" and their retinue, with a harshness and insolence that was both unjust and insufferable. He could not place himself in the shoes of those he vilified; he could not weigh their errors against their temptations, or their defects against their disadvantages. Unable to realize how circumstances might affect him similarly, he frequently voiced ferocious and tyrannical wishes regarding those he deemed to have acted tyrannically. By his threats alone, one might suspect he would employ arbitrary and cruel means to advance freedom and equality. Yet, "equality!" was a word he spoke, but at heart, he was a proud man. Very friendly to his workpeople, generous to those beneath him, and content to submit quietly to being beneath them himself, he was haughty as Beelzebub toward anyone the world considered his superior. Revolt ran in his blood; he could not bear control. Neither his father, his grandfather, nor his children could endure it.

His lack of general benevolence made him impatient of imbecility and any fault that grated on his strong, shrewd nature, leaving no check to his cutting sarcasm. Being unmerciful, he would sometimes wound repeatedly, oblivious to the pain inflicted or caring how deeply he thrust.

Regarding the scarcity of ideality in his mind, this is scarcely a fault. Possessing a fine ear for music and a correct eye for color and form, he retained the quality of taste. And who cares for imagination? Who does not view it as a dangerous, senseless attribute, akin to weakness, perhaps bordering on frenzy—a disease rather than a gift?

Most likely everyone thinks so, except those who possess it or fancy they do. Listen to them speak, and you would believe their hearts grow cold without that elixir, their eyes dim without that refining flame, and they become lonely without that strange companion. You would suppose it imparts glad hope to spring, charm to summer, tranquility to autumn, and consolation to winter, feelings you yourself do not experience. It is, of course, an illusion; yet the fanatics cling to their dream, refusing to exchange it for gold.

Since Mr. Yorke did not possess poetic imagination himself, he considered it a superfluous quality in others. He could tolerate, even encourage, painters and musicians because he could appreciate the results of their art; he could enjoy a fine picture and good music. But a quiet poet—regardless of the struggle or fire burning within his breast—if he could not function as a man in a counting-house or a tradesman in the Piece Hall, might live despised and die scorned under the gaze of Hiram Yorke.

Given that there are many Hiram Yorkes in the world, it is fortunate that the true poet, though outwardly quiet, often possesses a truculent spirit beneath his placidity. Full of shrewdness in his meekness, he can measure the stature of those who look down on him and accurately assess the value of the pursuits they disdain. It is fitting that he maintains his own bliss and society with Nature, independent of those who find little pleasure in him and in whom he finds none. Just as the world often turns a dark, cold side to him—and rightly so, for he first turns a dark, cold, careless side to them—he should be able to sustain a festal brightness in his bosom, making all bright and genial for him, while strangers might deem his existence a Polar winter never gladdened by the sun. The true poet is not to be pitied; indeed, he is apt to laugh in his sleeve when a misguided sympathizer whines over his wrongs. Even when utilitarians sit in judgment, pronouncing him and his art useless, he receives their sentence with hard derision and a broad, deep, merciless contempt for the unhappy Pharisees who utter it. These, however, are not Mr. Yorke's reflections; we are currently dealing with Mr. Yorke.

Caroline Helstone was just eighteen. At eighteen, the true narrative of life has barely begun. Until then, we sit listening to a marvelous fiction—sometimes delightful, often sad, but almost always unreal. Our world seems heroic; its inhabitants are half-divine or semi-demonic. Its scenes are dreamscapes: darker woods, stranger hills, brighter skies, more dangerous waters, sweeter flowers, and tempting fruits spread across an enchanted globe. What a magnificent moon we gaze upon before that time! How the trembling of our hearts at her sight bears witness to its unutterable beauty! As for the sun, it appears a burning heaven—the domain of gods.

But at eighteen, drawing near the edge of these illusory dreams, Elf-land lies behind us, while the shores of Reality rise before us. These shores still look distant, blue, soft, and gentle; we long to reach them. In the sunshine, we see a greenness beneath the azure, imagining spring meadows, catching glimpses of silver lines, and envisioning the roll of living waters. We think, *If we could but reach this land, we would hunger and thirst no more.* Yet, many a wilderness, and often the flood of death, or some stream of sorrow as cold and black as death itself, must be crossed before true bliss can be tasted. Every joy life offers must be earned before it is secured; and how hard that earning is, only those who have wrestled for great prizes know. The heart's blood must gem the brow of the combatant with red beads before the wreath of victory rustles overhead.

At eighteen, we are unaware of this. When Hope smiles and promises happiness tomorrow, we believe her implicitly. When Love wanders like a lost angel to our door, we admit him, welcome him, embrace him instantly. We do not see his quiver; if his arrows penetrate, the wound feels like a thrill of new life. We fear neither poison nor the barb that no leech can extract. That perilous passion—an agony in some phases, an agony throughout for many—is believed to be an unqualified good. In short, at eighteen, we enter the school of experience, and her humbling, crushing, grinding, yet purifying and invigorating lessons await us.

Alas, Experience! No other mentor has so wasted and frozen a face as yours. None wears a robe so black, bears a rod so heavy, or draws the novice so sternly to his task with a hand so inexorable. By your instructions alone can man or woman find a safe track through life's wilds; without them, how they stumble, how they stray! On what forbidden grounds do they intrude, down what dread declivities are they hurled!

Spring arrived, bringing a deceptive bloom to the English countryside. Fields turned green, hills refreshed, and gardens burst into flower, yet beneath this verdant skin, the nation remained unchanged in its suffering. Poverty persisted among the poor, and hardship weighed heavily on employers. While commerce faltered under the shadow of continuing war—England's blood spilled and wealth squandered for seemingly futile ends—the occasional news of victories in the Peninsula arrived too slowly to offer comfort. Instead, long silences were broken only by Bonaparte's arrogant boasts of triumph. For those bearing the brunt of the conflict, this protracted struggle against an apparently invincible foe felt tedious and hopeless. Desperation drove men like Yorke and Moore, along with countless others teetering on the brink of bankruptcy, to demand peace on any terms whatsoever. They organized meetings, delivered impassioned speeches, and circulated petitions, caring little for the conditions attached to such a boon.

Human nature, whether viewed individually or collectively, leans toward selfishness; the mercantile class exemplifies this trait with striking clarity. These merchants prioritize profit above all national concerns, viewing England's interests solely as extensions of their own wallets. Chivalry, disinterest, and honor have largely died within their hearts. Under their rule, a nation might submit ignominiously—not out of Christian principle, but driven by the greed of Mammon. During recent conflicts, English traders would have endured insults from the French, offering cloak after cloak to Napoleon until only a single garment remained, worn solely to protect the purse within its pocket. Only when that beloved purse was seized would they transform into fierce defenders, biting at the thief's throat until the treasure was restored. Yet, when speaking against war, they claim to despise it for its brutality, presenting themselves as uniquely civilized and kind. In reality, many are narrow-minded and cold-hearted, harboring no affection for anyone outside their own trade, viewing others as burdensome parasites who deserve neither air nor sustenance. To them, anyone not involved in commerce eats the bread of idleness. Long may it be before England truly becomes a nation of shopkeepers!

We have established that Sir Walter Moore was no self-sacrificing patriot, guided instead by a keen focus on personal advancement. Consequently, when pushed once again toward financial ruin, he fought fiercely against forces threatening his stability. While he stirred anti-war sentiment in the North and leveraged the influence of wealthier allies, he occasionally sensed the irrationality of his party's demands. Hearing of Europe arming against Bonaparte, witnessing Russia rise to defend her frozen lands and serfdom from foreign tyranny, Moore realized that a free nation like England could not simply concede to an unjust aggressor. Furthermore, reports of Lord Wellington's steady, unyielding progress in the Peninsula convinced him that a genuine, enduring power resided within the British army—a force destined eventually to secure victory. However, that "end" felt impossibly distant. Meanwhile, as an individual, Moore faced imminent destruction; his hopes were grinding to dust. His priority shifted entirely to self-preservation and fulfilling his own destiny.

His vigorous pursuit of this goal led to a definitive break with his former Tory friend, the rector, Mr. Helstone. Their dispute erupted at a public meeting and escalated into scathing newspaper exchanges. Early May had been fine, the middle wet, but during the last week, coinciding with the change of the moon, the weather cleared. A fresh breeze swept away the heavy, silver-white rainclouds, carrying them mass upon mass toward the eastern horizon until they vanished, leaving the sky a vault of pure blue, ready for the summer sun. That sun rose broad and brilliant on Whitsuntide. The gathering of the schools was marked by splendid weather.

Whit-Tuesday was the grand day. In preparation, the two large schoolrooms of Briarfield—built by the current rector largely at his own expense—were scrubbed, whitewashed, repainted, and adorned with flowers and evergreens sourced from the rectory garden, two cartloads from Fieldhead, and a wheelbarrow-full from the more parsimonious estate of De Walden, home to Mr. Wynne. Inside, twenty tables, each seating twenty guests, were arranged among benches and draped in white cloths. Suspended above were at least twenty cages housing canaries, a tradition cherished by the district and specifically favored by Mr. Helstone's clerk, who loved their piercing songs and knew they sang loudest amidst the cacophony of other tongues. It must be noted that these tables were not for the twelve hundred scholars arriving from the three parishes, but solely for the patrons and teachers. The children's feast was to be held outdoors. At one o'clock, the processions would arrive; by two, they would be marshaled; by four, they would parade the parish, followed by the feast, and finally, the meeting within the church accompanied by music and speeches.

Briarfield was chosen as the rendezvous point not because it was the largest or oldest parish—Whinbury surpassed it in population, and Nunnely's ancient temple and mossy parsonage predated it still—but simply because Mr. Helstone decreed it. His will outweighed that of either Boultby or Hall; the former lacked the power to object, and the latter refused to challenge his brother's authority. They allowed him to lead and rule.

Historically, this anniversary had been a trying ordeal for Caroline Helstone. It forced her into public view, compelling her to face the wealthy, respectable, and influential families of the neighborhood. Without the kindly countenance of Mr. Hall, she would have appeared utterly unsupported. Obligated to be conspicuous, to lead her regiment as the rector's niece and head teacher, to serve tea at the front table for a motley crowd of ladies and gentlemen, and to perform all this without a mother, aunt, or chaperone—while being a naturally timid person who dreaded publicity—it is understandable why she trembled at the approach of Whitsuntide.

However, this year Shirley was to accompany her, transforming the trial entirely. It ceased to be a burden and became almost an enjoyment. Miss Keeldar, alone, was superior to a host of ordinary friends. Self-possessed, spirited, and easygoing, conscious of her status yet never presumptuous, her mere presence inspired courage. The only anxiety was whether the heiress would be punctual. Shirley often lingered carelessly, and Caroline knew her uncle would not wait a second. At the precise moment the church clock tolled two, the bells would clash and the march begin. Caroline realized she must ensure Shirley's arrival, or her companion would fail her.

Whit-Tuesday saw Caroline rise almost with the dawn. Sweetness of tint, purity of air, and grace of demeanor compensated for the lack of rich color or magnificent contours. Most people experience periods in their lives when they feel thus forsaken—when, having long hoped against hope, they see the day of fulfillment endlessly deferred, causing their hearts to truly sicken. This is a terrible hour, yet it is often the darkest point preceding the rising of the day—the turning of the year when the icy January wind sweeps across the waste, carrying simultaneously the dirge of departing winter and the prophecy of coming spring. Only half of Moore's energy and determination had been displayed in his defense of the mill; the other half—a far more terrifying facet—was revealed in his tireless, relentless pursuit of the riot's leaders. The mob itself, the mere followers, he left entirely alone. Perhaps an innate sense of justice guided him, telling him that men misled by false counsel and driven to madness by privation were unfit targets for vengeance. The leaders, however, were different. They were strangers—emissaries from the great cities. Most were not of the working class; rather, they were "down-draughts": bankrupts, men perpetually in debt and often intoxicated, possessing nothing to lose and everything to gain in terms of character, cash, and cleanliness. Moore hunted these individuals with the ferocity of a bloodhound, and he took a genuine pleasure in the occupation. The local magistrates likely dreaded him. They were slow, timid men, whereas Moore enjoyed frightening and rousing them. Sometimes the future whispers a low, ominous warning of the tragedies it brings, like a distant storm gathering on the horizon. Its tones carry the threat of a blast that will scatter wreckage across the sea; its colors hint at a fog carrying the yellow stain of pestilence, dimming the windows of English homes with the breath of Eastern plagues. Caroline Helstone returned from Hollow's Cottage believing herself in good health. Yet, when she woke the next morning, she was overcome by an unfamiliar lethargy. By breakfast, and at every meal throughout the following day, she lost all desire to eat. Food tasted only like ash and sawdust.

"Am I ill?" she asked, staring into the mirror. Her eyes were bright, her pupils wide, her cheeks flushed and fuller than usual. "I look well; why can I not eat?"

A rapid pulse hammered in her temples, and her mind raced with strange, frenetic activity. Her spirits were high, filled with hundreds of brilliant, fragmented thoughts that cast a warm glow over her countenance.

What followed was a hot, parched, restless night. Toward dawn, a terrifying dream seized her like a tiger. When she awoke, she knew with certainty that she was sick.

She could not recall exactly how she contracted the fever—though it was clearly a fever. Perhaps, during her late walk home, a sweet, poisoned breeze, scented with honeydew and miasma, had drifted into her lungs and veins. Finding there a pre-existing fever of mental excitement and the lingering fatigue of long conflict and habitual sadness, that breeze had fanned a mere spark into a roaring fire.

Yet, initially, it seemed a gentle blaze. After two scorching days and anxious nights, the symptoms showed no violence. Neither her uncle, Fanny, the doctor, nor Miss Keeldar, who visited her, harbored any fear. Everyone believed she would recover in a few days.

Those few days passed, and although recovery was expected soon, it did not begin. Mrs. Pryor, who had visited daily, entered her chamber one morning when Caroline had been ill for two weeks. She observed her closely for several minutes, taking her pulse, before quietly leaving to go to Mr. Helstone's study. She remained there, locked away, for nearly half the morning.

When she returned to her sick young friend, she removed her shawl and bonnet. Standing by the bedside, she rocked gently, a motion habitual to her. Finally, she spoke.

Despite this devoted care, the sick girl did not improve. She wasted away like a snowdrift melting in spring, fading like a flower in drought. Miss Keeldar, who rarely feared danger or death, had initially entertained no worries. But witnessing her friend's gradual decline during her visits, alarm gripped her heart. Still, no rumor of serious illness spread through the neighborhood. At Hollow's Cottage, it was believed Caroline merely had a severe cold; she had even written a note to Hortense confirming this. Mademoiselle responded with two pots of currant jam, a recipe for a tisane, and a note of advice.

Mrs. Yorke, upon learning a physician had been called, scoffed at the hypochondriac fancies of the rich and idle. She argued that those with nothing but themselves to think about would summon a doctor even if their little finger ached.

Meanwhile, the "rich and idle"—represented by Caroline—were sinking rapidly into a state of prostration. Their swiftly consuming weakness baffled everyone who witnessed it, except for one person. Only that one understood how easily an undermined structure can collapse into sudden ruin.

Sick people often possess fancies incomprehensible to ordinary attendants, and Caroline had one that even her tender nurse could not immediately explain. The Sympsons were church people. Naturally, the rector's niece was received by them with courtesy. Mr. Sympson proved to be a man of spotless respectability, a worrying temper, pious principles, and worldly views; his lady was a very good woman—patient, kind, well-bred. She had been brought up on a narrow system of views, starved on a few prejudices—a mere handful of bitter herbs; a few preferences, soaked until their natural flavor was extracted, with no seasoning added in the cooking; some excellent principles, encased in a stiff, raised crust of bigotry difficult to digest. Far too submissive was she to complain of this diet or to ask for a crumb beyond it.

The daughters were an example to their sex. They were tall, each possessing a Roman nose. They had been educated faultlessly. Everything they did was done well. History and the most solid books had cultivated their minds. They possessed principles and opinions that could not be mended. Lives more exactly regulated, feelings, manners, and habits, it would have been difficult to find anywhere. They knew by heart a certain young-ladies'-schoolroom code of laws on language, demeanor, etc.; they themselves never deviated from its curious little pragmatical provisions, and they regarded with secret, whispered horror all deviations in others. The Abomination of Desolation was no mystery to them; they had discovered that unutterable Thing in the characteristic others call Originality. Quick were they to recognize the signs of this evil; and wherever they saw its trace—whether in look, word, or deed; whether they read it in the fresh, vigorous style of a book, or listened to it in interesting, unhackneyed, pure, expressive language—they shuddered, they recoiled. Danger was above their heads, peril about their steps. What was this strange thing? Being unintelligible, it must be bad. Let it be denounced and chained up.

"Hast thou entered into the treasures of the snow? Or hast thou seen the treasures of the hail, which I have reserved against the time of trouble, against the day of battle and war? Go your ways. Pour out the vials of the wrath of God upon the earth."

It is done. The earth is scorched with fire; the sea becomes "as the blood of a dead man"; the islands flee away; the mountains are not found.

In this year, Lord Wellington assumed the reins in Spain. They made him generalissimo for their own salvation's sake. In this year he took Badajos, fought the field of Vittoria, captured Pampeluna, stormed San Sebastian; in this year he won Salamanca.
