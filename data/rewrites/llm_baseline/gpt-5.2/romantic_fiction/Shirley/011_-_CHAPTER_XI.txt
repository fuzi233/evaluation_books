Yet Caroline would not lie down and let the sorrow trample her. There was a native stoutness in her, a stubborn, quiet grain of strength that belonged not to counsel or company, but to solitude. When there is no witness to applaud, no hand to steady, and no voice to say, “This will pass,” the heart fights hardest, because defeat then seems not merely pain, but shame.

So she laboured at herself as one labours at a refractory dough: pressing, folding, beating down the air-bubbles of wild thought, trying to make the mass smooth enough to bear handling. She was never so busy. She made duties where none were demanded, and if the house offered none, she went out and sought them on the roads. She walked in all weathers—long, lonely walks—through drizzle that smelled of wet stones and peat, through sharp winds that combed the hedgerows and set the bare twigs clicking like brittle bones, through sunlight that warmed the green rise of Nunnely Common as if it were a hearthstone. Day after day she came home in the evening pale and worn; yet, once her bonnet and shawl were cast aside, she would not rest. She paced her room as if movement were a charm against thinking. Sometimes she did not sit down till faintness forced her.

She said she meant to tire herself into sleep. But night was her enemy. When other heads sank into pillows, hers was lifted, listening—listening to the house’s minute sounds, the settling timbers, the thin stir of a branch against a pane, the far-off dog that answered another across the fields. The silence was not kind silence; it had hollows in it where her own thoughts echoed. Often—unhappy girl—she cried in that pitch of despair which is not dramatic, only intolerable; a rushing helplessness that smote her down and made her childlike, as if all her careful daytime discipline were a crust that broke the instant darkness touched it.

Then temptation came, not with any grand shape, but with the small, plausible whisper of ink and paper. Write to Robert. Say you are unhappy. Say you fear he will forget you. Beg him—only his friendship, of course; she always told herself that word, as though it were a cold cloth to fever—beg him to remember, to write sometimes. One or two such letters she did write. She formed his name with a trembling precision, and the strokes looked to her like a kind of touch. But she never sent them. Shame sealed the envelope, and good sense locked it away.

At last the life she led tightened like a cord. She felt she could bear no more—that she must find change, or her heart and head would fail together under the strain. She wanted to leave Briarfield—far, far away. Another longing, deeper and more secret, grew alongside this: to discover her mother, to know her. Yet dread went with the desire. If she found her, could she love her? No one had ever spoken warmly of Mrs. James Helstone. The few times Caroline had heard her mentioned, the tone had been cool, as of something puzzling and best left untouched. Her uncle’s very silence carried an aversion. An old servant, who had lived with Mrs. Helstone after her marriage, spoke with chilling reserve: “queer,” sometimes; “I never understood her,” often. Those words fell like frost on the daughter’s heart. They suggested that perhaps it was safer never to know a parent than to know and fail to cherish her.

Still, one plan took shape, the only one she could imagine that promised relief: she would take a situation—become a governess. She could do nothing else. A small incident—one of those moments when a soul that has been wavering is suddenly pricked into decision—brought her at last to the courage of speaking.

Her walks were always on solitary roads, but whichever way she went—along the drear skirts of Stilbro’ Moor or the brighter stretch of common—she so contrived her homeward path that it passed near the Hollow. She rarely went down into the den, but almost every twilight found her on its brink, as regularly as the first stars climbed over the hill-crests. Her resting-place was by a certain stile under a certain old thorn. There she could look down on the cottage, the mill, the dark, still dam holding the sky’s reflection as a pan holds broth; the little garden plot lying dewy and quiet. From that point she could see the counting-house window; and at a fixed hour, from its panes, a ray would suddenly shoot—lamp-light. She went to watch for that ray as other women might go to listen for a footstep.

Sometimes it was bright and steady in clear air; sometimes it shivered thin through mist; sometimes rain slanted between her eyes and the lattice, breaking it into winking fragments. She came in all weathers.

There were nights when it did not appear. Then she knew Robert was away, and she went home with sorrow doubled, as if absence were not merely distance but refusal. When the light kindled, her spirits rose on the instant, on no rational ground—only on the sweet, foolish persuasion that where he was, hope must also be. If, while she gazed, a shadow crossed between lamp and lattice, her heart leaped. That shadow was Robert; she had seen him. She would go back comforted, carrying a clearer image of his face, a more distinct recollection of his voice and smile, his carriage—little fragments of him, like crumbs gathered and hoarded because there is no loaf. Along with them often came the thought—too tender to be called expectation, too strong to be called fancy—that if she were near him, he might yet welcome her; that now, at this moment, he might be willing to stretch out his hand and draw her to him and shelter her at his side as once he used. On such a night, even if she wept, her tears seemed less scalding; the pillow felt softer; her temples ached less against it.

The shortest path from the Hollow to the rectory wound near a certain mansion, tenantless by its owner for ten years, yet no ruin—Fieldhead. Mr. Yorke had had it kept in repair; an old gardener and his wife lived there, tended the grounds, and kept the house habitable, so that its emptiness was not squalor but a kind of maintained pause, like a table laid and waiting.

If Fieldhead had few other virtues, it was picturesque. Its regular grey front was softened by time into mossy, pencilled colour; its old latticed windows, stone porch, roof and chimneys had the richness of things long looked at and long weathered. Behind, trees rose bold and spreading; in front, the cedar stood grand and dark. Granite urns on the garden wall and the fretted arch of the gateway seemed made to catch an artist’s eye.

One mild May evening, near moonrise, Caroline passed close by, weary, yet unwilling to go home, where the night’s thorny bed of grief waited. She sat down on the mossy ground near the gate and looked through towards cedar and mansion. The night was still, dewy, cloudless; the westward gables held a clear amber from the horizon. The oaks behind were black; the cedar blacker—its dense boughs like a canopy cut from raven cloth. Under them the sky opened, gravely blue, and there hung the moon, solemn and mild, looking down on Caroline as if it had watched women suffer there before.

She found it mournfully lovely. She wished she could be happy; she wished she could have that inward peace which seems, to those who lack it, as simple as bread and as unattainable. She wondered—half child, half rebel—whether Providence had no pity on her, and would not help or console her. Old ballads rose in her mind—lovers meeting at twilight under trees, vows whispered, hands clasped. Such a tryst, in such a scene, she thought, would be bliss.

Where was Robert now? Not at the Hollow; she had watched long, and his lamp had not appeared. Would she and Moore ever meet and speak again? The question had scarcely formed when the door within the stone porch opened. Two men came out—one elderly and white-headed, the other young, dark-haired, and tall. They crossed the lawn, went out through a portal in the garden wall, crossed the road, passed the stile, descended the fields, and disappeared. Robert Moore had passed her with his friend Mr. Yorke. Neither had seen her.

It was over almost before she could draw breath; but that brief sight ran through her like fire. It found her despairing; it left her desperate—two states, and yet different as numbness from pain.

“Oh, had he but been alone! had he but seen me!” was her cry. “He would have said something. He would have given me his hand. He does—he must—love me a little. In his eye, on his lips, I should have read comfort. But the chance is lost. The wind, the cloud’s shadow, do not pass more silently, more emptily than he. I have been mocked, and Heaven is cruel!”

In utter sickness of longing and disappointment she went home.

The next morning, at breakfast, she appeared white-cheeked and wretched, like one who had walked with a ghost. She asked her uncle, as steadily as she could, “Have you any objection, uncle, to my inquiring for a situation in a family?”

Mr. Helstone, ignorant as the table that held his coffee-cup of all she had undergone, scarcely believed his ears.

“What whim now?” he demanded. “Are you bewitched? What can you mean?”

“I am not well, and need a change,” she answered.

He looked at her then—really looked. He saw, though he did not understand. The rose had dwindled to a mere snowdrop; bloom had gone, flesh wasted; she sat drooping, colourless, thin. Only her brown eyes remained soft, and her hair still flowed in abundance.

“What on earth is the matter with you?” he said. “What is wrong? How are you ailing?”

She could not speak. The brown eyes filled; the lips, faintly tinted, trembled.

“Look out for a situation, indeed! What situation are you fit for? What have you been doing with yourself? You are not well.”

“I should be well if I went from home.”

“These women are incomprehensible,” he broke out. “They startle you with unpleasant surprises. To-day they’re bouncing, buxom, red as cherries, round as apples; to-morrow they’re effete as dead weeds, blanched and broken down. And why? That’s the puzzle. She has her meals, her liberty, a good house, good clothes. A while since that sufficed to keep her handsome and cheery; and there she sits now, a poor little pale puling chit. Provoking! Then comes the question, what is to be done? I suppose I must send for advice. Will you have a doctor, child?”

“No, uncle. I don’t want one. A doctor could do me no good. I merely want change of air and scene.”

“Well, if that be the caprice, it shall be gratified. You shall go to a watering-place. I don’t mind the expense. Fanny shall accompany you.”

“But, uncle, some day I must do something for myself. I have no fortune. I had better begin now.”

“While I live, you shall not turn out as a governess, Caroline. I will not have it said that my niece is a governess.”

“But the later in life one makes a change of that sort, the more difficult and painful it is. I should wish to get accustomed to the yoke before any habits of ease and independence are formed.”

“I beg you will not harass me. I mean to provide for you. I have always meant it. I will purchase an annuity. Bless me! I am but fifty-five; my health and constitution are excellent. There is plenty of time. Don’t make yourself anxious respecting the future. Is that what frets you?”

“No, uncle; but I long for a change.”

He laughed shortly. “There speaks the woman! A change! a change! Always fantastical and whimsical. Well, it’s in her sex.”

“But it is not fantasy and whim, uncle.”

“What is it then?”

“Necessity, I think. I feel weaker than formerly. I believe I should have more to do.”

“Admirable! She feels weak, and therefore she should be set to hard labour—‘clair comme le jour,’ as Moore—confound Moore! You shall go to Cliff Bridge; and there are two guineas to buy a new frock. Come, Cary, never fear. We’ll find balm in Gilead.”

“Uncle, I wish you were less generous and more—”

“More what?”

Sympathizing was the word on Caroline’s lips; she swallowed it. She knew the sound of it would make him laugh the more. He would have taken it for a kind of soft nonsense, a foreign importation into a plain English house. Finding her silent, he concluded, “The fact is, you don’t know precisely what you want.”

“Only to be a governess.”

“Pooh! mere nonsense! I’ll not hear of governessing. Don’t mention it again. It is rather too feminine a fancy. I have finished breakfast. Ring the bell. Put all crotchets out of your head, and run away and amuse yourself.”

“With what? My doll?” she asked herself, as she left the room.

A week or two passed; her health neither worsened nor improved. She had reached that strange middle state when, if the seeds of some fatal malady had been in her constitution, misery might have forced it rapidly to bloom and kill; but if no such seed existed, sorrow could do its worst and still not take life—only its colour, its ease, its spring. She felt no dying sickness; there was no sharp pain—only diminished appetite, which she understood because she wept at night; lessened strength, which she could explain because sleep was shy and when it came brought distressing dreams. In the far future she still believed—dimly, stubbornly—that this passage might be crossed, and that she might one day be calm again, though perhaps never again happy.

Her uncle urged her to visit—to accept the frequent invitations of their acquaintance. She evaded them. She could not be cheerful in company, and she felt observed with curiosity rather than tenderness. Old ladies offered advice and nostrums; young ladies looked at her in a way she understood and shrank from. Their eyes said they knew she had been “disappointed,” as custom phrases it; by whom they were not sure.

Commonplace young ladies can be as hard as commonplace young gentlemen—worldly, selfish, quick to despise what hurts. They seem to regard calamity as a judgment on inferiority. With them, to “love” is to scheme for a match; to be “disappointed” is to have the scheme detected and thwarted. They suppose others feel as they do, and judge accordingly.

Caroline knew this, partly by instinct, partly by observation. She kept her pale face and wasted figure as much out of sight as possible. Living thus in seclusion, she ceased to hear the neighbourhood’s small news.

One morning her uncle came into the parlour where she sat trying to coax pleasure out of painting—a little group of wild flowers she had gathered under a hedge at the top of the Hollow fields. Their scent still clung to her fingers: crushed stem and green bitterness, with a faint honey from the smallest bloom—tiny comforts she could at least translate into colour. Mr. Helstone spoke abruptly.

“Come, child, you are always stooping over palette, or book, or sampler; leave that tinting work. By-the-bye, do you put your pencil to your lips when you paint?”

“Sometimes, uncle, when I forget.”

“Then it is that which is poisoning you. The paints are deleterious. There is white lead and red lead, and verdigris, and gamboge, and twenty other poisons in those colour-cakes. Lock them up! Lock them up! Get your bonnet on. I want you to make a call with me.”

“With you, uncle?”

Surprise made her voice thin. She was not accustomed to accompany him on visits; she neither rode nor walked out with him for such purpose.

“Quick! quick! I am always busy, you know. I have no time to lose.”

She gathered up her brushes and saucers, asked where they were going.

“To Fieldhead.”

“Fieldhead! What—to see old James Booth, the gardener? Is he ill?”

“We are going to see Miss Shirley Keeldar.”

“Miss Keeldar! Is she coming to Yorkshire? Is she at Fieldhead?”

“She is. She has been there a week. I met her at a party last night—that party to which you would not go. I was pleased with her. I choose that you shall make her acquaintance. It will do you good.”

“She is now come of age, I suppose?”

“She is come of age, and will reside for a time on her property. I lectured her on the subject; I showed her her duty. She is not intractable. She is rather a fine girl; she will teach you what it is to have a sprightly spirit. Nothing lackadaisical about her.”

“I don’t think she will want to see me, or to have me introduced to her. What good can I do her? How can I amuse her?”

“Pshaw! Put your bonnet on.”

“Is she proud, uncle?”

“Don’t know. You hardly imagine she would show her pride to me, I suppose? A chit like that would scarcely presume to give herself airs with the rector of her parish, however rich she might be.”

“No. But how did she behave to other people?”

“Didn’t observe. She holds her head high, and probably can be saucy enough where she dare. She wouldn’t be a woman otherwise. There! Away now for your bonnet at once!”

Caroline’s confidence had never been robust; weakness of body and depression of spirits did not strengthen it. As they walked up the broad paved approach from Fieldhead’s gateway to its porch, she quailed, despite all rebukes she tried to give herself. She followed Mr. Helstone reluctantly through the porch into the old vestibule.

It was very sombre—long, vast, and dark; one latticed window admitted a dim, grudging light. The wide chimney held no fire in that warm weather; willow boughs filled it instead, their pale leaves smelling faintly of sap and water. Above, a gallery ran opposite the entrance, lost towards the ceiling in shadow. Carved stags’ heads with real antlers looked down grotesquely from the walls. The house was neither grand nor comfortable: antique, rambling, incommodious. Yet it carried an authority of age. A thousand a year belonged to it, descending for lack of male heirs upon a female; mercantile families nearby could boast twice the income, but the Keeldars, by antiquity and the title of lords of the manor, took precedence.

They were shown into a parlour lined with oak—dark, glossy panels that made the room seem at once rich and chilly, as if it had been polished with patience but warmed by no affection. The furniture was old, solid, sincere. On each side of the high mantelpiece stood antique chairs like little wooden thrones; in one sat a lady.

If this were Miss Keeldar, she must have come of age twenty years ago. She was matronly in form, capless, with auburn hair still undimmed and features small and naturally young-looking; yet her aspect was not youthful, nor did she seem to desire it. Her gown, though of handsome material, was arranged in scanty folds after an obsolete mode; the oddity suggested eccentricity at first glance.

She received them with a mixture of ceremony and diffidence peculiarly English: unsure of her own power to please, anxious to be proper, desirous—if possible—of being agreeable without daring to trust herself. Yet her embarrassment went beyond ordinary shyness. Caroline felt it, and, knowing what is kind to the timid, sat quietly near her and began to talk in a gentle, easy way—an ease that came to Caroline now because she was, for once, the steadier of two.

Alone, they might have suited at once. The lady had the clearest voice imaginable—soft and tuneful, more like a well-kept instrument than could have been expected from forty years—and her form inclined to comfortable fulness. Caroline liked that voice; it made amends for the formal correctness of her language. In ten minutes, liking would have been mutual, and friendship possible.

But Mr. Helstone stood on the rug, looking at both—particularly at the strange lady—with a sarcastic, keen eye that expressed impatience of ceremony and annoyance at her want of boldness. His hard gaze and rasping tone discomfited her more and more. She tried to offer little speeches about the weather and the country, but Mr. Helstone affected not to hear distinctly. Whatever she said he made her repeat; each elaborately-constructed nothing had to be spoken twice, as if words were heavy loads and she had to lift them again. The effort soon overwhelmed her. She rose at last in a flutter, murmuring nervously that she did not know what detained Miss Keeldar and that she would go and look for her—when Miss Keeldar spared her.

A glass door from the garden opened, and in came a girl erect and slight, bringing with her the outdoor air—leaf-coolness, earth-damp, the faint aromatic crush of bruised herbs. She carried in her left hand a little silk apron full of flowers, gathered as if she had been busy among borders and beds, and she gave Mr. Helstone her right hand with pleasant readiness.

“I knew you would come to see me,” she said, “though you do think Mr. Yorke has made me a Jacobin. Good-morning.”

“But we’ll not have you a Jacobin,” returned he. “No, Miss Shirley; they shall not steal the flower of my parish from me. Now that you are amongst us, you shall be my pupil in politics and religion; I’ll teach you sound doctrine on both points.”
