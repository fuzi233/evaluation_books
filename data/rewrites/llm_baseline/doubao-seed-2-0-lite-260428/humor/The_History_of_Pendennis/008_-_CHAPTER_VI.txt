For weeks after Pen got bit by the love bug, Cicero and Euripides didn’t stand a chance. Poor Mr. Smirke, his tutor, had the easiest stretch of work he’d had in his entire curate career—half the lessons Pen just stared out the window, and the other half he’d already snuck out the door.
The real victim of Pen’s lovesick chaos was his mare Rebecca. Whenever she went lame, Pen threw a bigger tantrum than Richard III after his horse got killed under him at Bosworth. He pored over farrier manuals from the Chatteris livery stable until his eyes crossed, dropped so much cash on vet bills and rental horses he was on first-name terms with every stable boy within 10 miles, just so he could gallop over to Prior’s Lane at a moment’s notice.
Half his trips he made up a story to his mom about going to Chatteris for fencing lessons, the other half he lied through his teeth to Smirke, saying he wanted to read a Greek play together, then bolted to catch the Competitor down coach. He’d hang out for two hours with Emily Fotheringay, then hop the 10pm Rival coach back home so no one noticed he was gone. He almost blew his whole secret once, when Mrs. Pendennis asked Smirke if they’d had a productive study night the day before. Smirke’s mouth was already open to admit he hadn’t seen Pen at all, when Pen ground his boot heel so hard onto the poor curate’s toe under the table it made him yelp, and shut his mouth faster than a clam at low tide.
Naturally, two guys who get tangled up in the same love adjacent mess end up talking about it, and there’s no better sport than listening to two besotted fools ramble on about their crush (as long as you’re not the one doing the rambling). Smirke made Pen swear a solemn secret oath to tell him he wasn’t mixed up in some sketchy, improper entanglement—because if he was, the poor flustered curate was convinced he’d have to break his vow and tell Mrs. Pendennis, which would ruin the quiet, secretly pining little life he’d built for himself admiring Pen’s mom from afar.
“Unworthy? Unlawful?” Pen squawked, indignant. “She’s purer than she is beautiful, and I’d never look at another woman. The only reason I haven’t told my family yet is—well, there are heavy, secret reasons I can’t share. And any man who dares say a rude word against her? That’s an insult to me, and dammit, I won’t stand for it.”
Smirke gave a wobbly little laugh. “Whoa, easy, Arthur, don’t challenge me to a duel. You know I can’t fight, I’ve never even held a pistol that wasn’t used for firing off birthday toffees.” That compromise just left the poor curate even more wrapped around Pen’s finger, and the Greek verbs and math problems gathered more dust than ever.
If Smirke had bothered flipping through the Poet’s Corner of the weekly County Chronicle that arrived in Wednesday’s mail, he’d have noticed a gushy new poem popping up every week: lines to Miss Fotheringay of the Theatre Royal, all dramatic, passionate, signed not with Pen’s old “NEP” signature, but a mysterious “EROS”. Even Helen, who clipped every single one of Pen’s amateur verses out of the paper to save in a scrapbook, didn’t connect the two. She just cooed at Pen one day, “This Eros guy writes sort of like you, dear, but yours is far better. Have you met this Miss Fotheringay he’s fawning over?”
Pen said he had, that he’d seen her play Mrs. Haller in The Stranger a few nights prior, and hey, fun fact—she was doing Ophelia for her benefit show next week. Why not all go? It’s Shakespeare, after all! Little Laura jumped up and down so fast her ringlets bounced, begging to come see her first ever play. Pen made sure to drop the Shakespeare name extra heavily, because his late dad had spent his whole life ranting about how the Bard was better than every poet in Johnson’s collection put together, and had always sworn he’d take Helen and Pen to a proper Shakespeare play as soon as Pen was old enough.
Tears welled up in Helen’s eyes as she remembered her husband’s old speeches. She kissed Pen’s forehead and agreed to go. Then on a whim, she invited Smirke along too. The poor curate had been raised by strict Clapham parents who’d banned theatres entirely, so he’d never set foot in one in his life. But Shakespeare! And a whole night riding in Mrs. Pendennis’s carriage, sitting right next to her? He couldn’t say no. He stammered out a messy speech about “temptation and gratitude” while making such a moon-eyed face at Helen that she started feeling distinctly uncomfortable. The man was getting creepier by the day, and she was already half-regretting the invite.
We don’t need to drag you through every second of Pen’s courtship of Emily—you’ve already got a pretty good idea of how it went. He’d sit with her for hours, spilling every dumb 18-year-old thought out of his brain: every line of Byron he’d memorized, every half-baked poem he’d scribbled down, every grand plan he had for their future, rambling on for 20 minutes straight without stopping. Emily never had to do much work at all, except nod prettily, bat her eyes, and gasp “Oh, that’s so beautiful! Say those lines again!” while she thought about something way more practical: how to stretch her old black satin dress into a new gown, what they’d have for dinner that night (hashed mutton, probably), if she could turn her old scarf into a bonnet just like the fancy one Miss Thackthwaite down the street had bought.
It didn’t take long for Emily and her dad to catch onto how head over heels Pen was. The second time he left their house, Captain Costigan winked at his daughter over his glass of grog and cackled, “Faith, Milly darling, you’ve hooked that rich boy for good.”
“Pfft, he’s just a baby, papa,” Milly shrugged. “He’s barely even grown up.” Little did she know, Pen was galloping home on Rebecca at that exact moment, screaming her name at the top of his lungs and grinning so wide his face hurt, and he’d have been thrilled to hear she called him a “child”.
“Baby or not, he’s a great catch,” the Captain pushed on. “I asked the bartender at the George and the grocer where his mom shops—she’s loaded. Drives her own chariot, owns Fairoaks Park, he gets every bit of the property the second he turns 21. You could hunt far longer and not find a better fish than that, Miss Fotheringay.”
“Boys his age are all talk,” Milly said, serious. “Remember back in Dublin when you carried on about Lord Poldoody? I’ve got a whole desk full of verses he wrote me at Trinity, and then his mom shipped him abroad and married him off to some random English heiress.”
“Lord Poldoody was a nobleman, that’s how they are,” her dad huffed. “You’re way better set up now, Milly. But don’t lead the boy on too much, I won’t have anyone trifling with my daughter.”
“Neither will his daughter, you can be sure of that, Captain,” Milly said, pouring him another sip of punch. “I’m old enough to take care of myself, don’t you worry.”
So Pen kept coming back, day after day, rushing in the door all breathless, rushing out again, getting more obsessed every single visit. Half the time Captain Costigan would make a silly excuse to leave the two of them alone, cock his hat over one eye, and strut off to go hang out at the pub, leaving them in the low wainscoted drawing-room with the big window looking out onto the Dean’s garden. Pen would ramble, Emily would sit there looking stunningly pretty doing her needlework, the cathedral bell would boom every hour, the rooks would caw in the elms at sunset, and Pen never wanted to leave. For the record, every Sunday Emily and her dad turned up to church, the Captain bellowing the responses in his thick rich Irish brogue, behaving so perfectly the vicar would have never guessed what their day jobs were.
Little Bows, the crippled acting coach who’d spent three years drilling every single gesture, line, and perfectly timed sob into Emily after she’d flopped horribly in London years prior, was furious. He’d worked himself hoarse screaming at her to nail every part, had mapped out exactly which foot she should stand on, exactly when to roll her eyes, exactly which syllable to cry on to make the crowd weep, and now this teenage squire was wandering in thinking he was gonna marry his star pupil right before a London manager was supposed to discover her and make her a household name? It was a travesty. Emily wasn’t even particularly grateful for Bows’ work—she was just… stupid. But Pen? Pen was so madly in love he couldn’t see a single flaw in her.
The post horses from the Clavering Arms showed up right on time, and the whole crew piled in the carriage to the Chatteris theatre. The audience was packed. Pen spotted his friend Foker and his buddy Spavin in a fancy box, all dolled up in their fanciest evening wear, waving wildly. They approved of the group too: little Laura was round-cheeked and pretty with her pile of shining brown ringlets, Mrs. Pendennis looked absolutely regal in her black velvet gown and the diamond cross she only wore on special occasions, and Smirke sat behind them, his white tie perfectly tied, blushing bright red because he’d never been in a theatre before. The coachman Samuel was posted in the pit, alongside a bunch of dragoon non-commissioned officers whose band was playing in the orchestra, and their colonel, the portly war hero Swallowtail with his Waterloo medal, was sat in a box with his young officers.
“Who’s that odd man in the white gloves bowing at you, Arthur?” Helen asked. Pen blushed bright red. “That’s Captain Costigan, ma’am. A Peninsular war officer.” He didn’t elaborate further, and Helen had no reason to guess the man was Miss Fotheringay’s father.
The night’s Hamlet was a visiting London actor named Mr. Hornbull, and the theatre manager Mr. Bingley was playing Horatio, saving his best performance for the second play of the night, *Black-Eyed Susan*.
The Hamlet performance was fine, but Ophelia? She was stunning. Her dark hair tumbled over her shoulders as she raved through her mad scene, waving her gorgeous white arms, flinging her flowers around, making the whole crowd weep. She was the prettiest corpse the stage had ever seen. When Hamlet and Laertes were fighting in her grave, she was peeking out from backstage, curious to get a look at Pen’s little group in their box.
Everyone was obsessed with her. Helen gushed nonstop about how beautiful she was, Laura cried so hard at the tragic ending her nose ran, Smirke cheered so loud his voice went hoarse. Pen was over the moon his mom liked her so much, it was almost perfect. The whole house roared with applause when the curtain fell, the dragoons were stamping their feet, Colonel Swallowtail waved his cap over his head, and Pen was yelling “Fotheringay! Fotheringay!” louder than anyone else. Even Helen was waving her handkerchief, little Laura was bouncing up and down clapping.
When Emily was led out on stage to take her bows, Pen spotted the little locket he’d bought for her with his last shilling plus a pound he’d borrowed from Smirke glinting on her chest, and he nearly blurted out the whole secret to Helen right then and there. Foker tossed a giant bouquet of roses up at her, Smirke tossed a shy little rose that landed in the pit, and Emily went through her whole practiced routine: glancing gratefully at the whole crowd, pretending to tremble with emotion, pressing the flowers to her chest like she was about to faint.
Then *Black-Eyed Susan* started, and Emily looked just as lovely in her simple russet gown and pink ribbon, making the whole crowd sigh over her sweet, sad story. No one wanted the night to end.
Pen would have spilled his whole secret to his mom on the ride home if they’d been alone in the carriage, but he ended up sitting up on the box next to Smirke, smoking a cigar while Smirke wrapped his comforter tight around himself against the cool night air. Halfway home, Foker’s fast tandem carriage zoomed past them, and Spavin blasted a very wobbly rendition of *Rule Britannia* on his key bugle, making Laura cackle.
Two days later, the Dean of Chatteris was hosting a dinner for his clerical friends. They drank excellent port, gossiped about the bishop over dessert, and Doctor Portman, Pen’s old mentor, snuck out of the dining room doors to go chat with Mrs. Dean, who was walking on the lawn with her kids, pink parasol over her head. The sun had just sunk behind the giant cathedral towers, and the little crescent moon was glowing bright in the golden summer sky.
At the end of the Dean’s lawn, right next to the old open window of the first-floor room in the house that backed onto the garden, were two people: a 26-year-old actress with her eyes wide open, and an 18-year-old boy so blind in love he couldn’t see straight. Poor Pen had just done it—he’d trembled, cried a little, and poured his whole heart out on the floor at Emily’s feet, proposing to her, kissing her hand over and over like his life depended on it.
“Does your mother know about this, Arthur?” Emily said slow, not pulling her hand away. She was already thinking about that pretty diamond cross Helen had worn to the play, and the 200 acres of Fairoaks property, and figured this arrangement could work out very nicely for her.
Pen babbled that his mom had already seen her and loved her, that of course she’d be happy they were getting married, that no one on earth could not adore her. Emily patted his messy hair, gave him a slow pitying little smile, and let him keep kissing her hand.
Down on the lawn, little Master Ridley Roset, the Dean’s 7-year-old son, tugged at the back of his mom’s dress and pointed up. “Ma! Look up there!”
What a sight it was. There was Pen, all dopey and lovesick, kissing Emily’s hand like it was made of solid gold, and Emily sitting there completely calm, perfectly composed. Mrs. Dean’s jaw dropped, little Rosa her younger daughter gaped, and Doctor Portman stared up at the window, frozen, half furious half bewildered, because he’d just caught his prize pupil, the boy he’d spent years mentoring, head over heels tangled up with the local theatre actress, right over the Dean’s rose bushes.
