A Catherine is not a wholly welcome guest in a house not her own. She carries within her a quiet, unyielding force of order. No sooner does she step across the threshold than the hearth seems to brighten, the windows to open wider, and the very air to clear itself as if swept by invisible hands. Yet this same energy, though born of care, is relentless. It sees disorder everywhere—and corrects it without mercy. The kitchen sparkles; the furniture is rearranged; even the silence grows taut with tidiness. But such zeal knows no limits. It does not stop at pots and pans. It reaches for people.

In Margaret’s home, Catherine moved with purpose. Every chair was shifted, every table repositioned—perhaps for the better, but certainly without consultation. And when the time came for Margaret’s confinement, Catherine did not hesitate. She summoned Denys and Martin, not to help, but to remove them. When the two men, honest and gentle, asked what they could do, she smiled kindly and said, “You might go for a walk.” That was fair enough. But then she added, with a quiet edge, “If it lasts until midweek, so much the better.” Her tone made it clear: their presence was not wanted.

And so began their exile. Each morning they slipped out before dawn, returning only after dark like thieves afraid of being seen. If they were caught inside, they moved with the caution of cats on a glass-strewn wall—eyes lowered, steps light, faces drawn with tension. They lived under a silent sentence of banishment. Their mere existence seemed to grate upon Catherine’s nerves. Was it instinct that whispered: *No place for men who diminish in a house where life is growing?*

Words, too, became weapons. A woman’s tongue can pierce armor stronger than steel. Catherine used hers with subtle cruelty. She spoke lightly, yet each remark carried weight. She left no doubt that their stay was temporary, their usefulness ended.

When Margaret finally descended the stairs, she found the kitchen empty of men. Only Martin remained—old, stiff, and stubborn. He had retreated no farther than the street, but he had brought his stool with him, sat upon it, and refused to budge. Margaret saw him in the sun, but did not know he had become part of the landscape. She asked for Denys.

“Good, kind Denys,” she murmured. “He’ll be glad to see me up again.”

Catherine, wiping a bowl with unnecessary vigor, replied, “Denys has gone to his friends in Burgundy.”

“And high time,” she added, as if speaking to herself. “Hasn’t been near them in three years, by all accounts.”

“What?” Margaret gasped. “Gone without saying farewell?”

She raised her eyes—soft, tender, like violets just opened. Catherine flushed. The truth struck her: she had lied. She had not told Denys’s departure because Margaret was sleeping. But now, faced with that innocence, guilt pricked her conscience. She tossed her head and said, “Oh, you were asleep. I didn’t want to wake you.”

“Poor Denys,” Margaret whispered, and tears gathered at the corners of her eyes.

Catherine saw it—not directly, but from the corner of her eye. Without a word, she turned and hurried outside to tend to Martin. She offered him food, warmth, kindness. But Martin, old and set in his ways, shook his head.

“No, thank you, dame,” he said. “I’ve grown used to being out here. I don’t like changing. I don’t meddle with anyone, and no one meddles with me.”

“Oh, you nasty, cross old wretch!” Catherine burst out, switching in an instant from sweetness to sharpness. And she stormed back into the house.

Later, alone, she wept. Then, to soothe her conscience, she vowed to be so kind to Margaret that she would never miss the burdens of having soldiers about. Still, unease lingered. So she did what she always did when troubled: she took the baby.

From head to foot, she washed little Gerard in tepid water. Soap and water became a sacrament. In that moment, all else—the quarrels, the pride, the displaced men—faded into nothing. Heroes and their grievances dissolved like dust in the sea.

Margaret could not remain still. She hovered nearby, eager to participate, even if only in spirit. She dipped a finger into the water—not to test the temperature, but to claim a share in the act. As Catherine bathed the child, Margaret knelt opposite him, chattering softly, carefully avoiding adult speech, as though cherubs could not understand human words.

“I wish you could wash out *that*,” she said, pointing to the boy’s little finger.

“What?” Catherine asked.

“You haven’t noticed? On his finger—a mark.”

Granny looked. There, indeed, was a small brown mole.

“Eh, but this is wonderful!” she exclaimed. “Nature, my lass, you’re strong—and meddling too. Have you seen such a mark on someone else? Tell me the truth, girl!”

“On him? No, mother, not I.”

“Well then, he has. Right there. And you never noticed it. But dear heart, I forgot—you haven’t known him from child to man as I have. I’ve held him hundreds of times on my knee, just like this, and washed him from head to toe in lukewarm water.” She swelled with quiet pride. Margaret looked up, humbled, recognizing the depth of experience before her.

Catherine watched from above, thinking. She was different from other busybodies—because she sometimes thought, though not deeply. “It is strange,” she mused, “how things come full circle. Life is but a whirligig. At least, our lives as women are all cut from the same pattern. Wasn’t I once washing out *my* Gerard’s mole? ‘Oh, fie!’ I said. ‘Here’s a foul blot.’ And I scrubbed and scrubbed till he cried. Then I knew it was time to stop. And now you say to me, ‘Mother,’ you say, ‘Try to wash it out of my Gerard’s finger.’ Think on it!”

“Wash it out?” Margaret cried. “Never! For all the world, no. It’s the sweetest thing on his little body. I’ll kiss it every morning and every night until the one who first owned it returns to us. Oh, bless you, my jewel of gold and silver, for being marked like your father—to comfort me.”

And she kissed the mole, then couldn’t stop. Soon, the child was sprawled across her lap, kissed again and again, gently but fiercely, as if she feared losing him. Catherine watched, smiling. She had seen such scenes many times before.

This, then, was the rhythm of Margaret’s days for months to come. A few small events passed—worth noting, but best saved for later. One thread holds many beads.

Meanwhile, while her son’s father endured storms of soul, ending in the stillness of monastic peace, Margaret’s life unfolded in one great, simple joy—motherhood. You who know its depth, imagine her: nursing, bathing, dressing, undressing, playing, laughing with her firstborn. Then, swift as lightning, turn your gaze to Italy—where cold cloisters stand, monks moving like ghosts, eyes down, hands folded over hearts dead to earthly things. Among them walks one man—cloaked in silence, yet carrying a fire beneath. This man, filled with love and youth and longing, is the one the radiant young mother waits for.

In the valley of Grindelwald, the traveler sees on one side the towering Alps—perpendicular, icy, eternal, piercing the clouds. On the other, gentle slopes, green as emerald, dotted with cows and cottages, alive with ordinary joy. The mighty peaks are sublime, yet lifeless. The humble hills, though plain, stir the heart by contrast.

So too are the two halves of this tale. On the Italian side: adventure, passion, sin, penitence, ice, snow, and a soul soaring toward heaven. On the Dutch side: quiet, modest, domestic—yet ever green, ever living. And between these two worlds, as a path winds through Grindelwald’s ice and sunlit meadows, lies but a single page—between *the Cloister and the Hearth*.
