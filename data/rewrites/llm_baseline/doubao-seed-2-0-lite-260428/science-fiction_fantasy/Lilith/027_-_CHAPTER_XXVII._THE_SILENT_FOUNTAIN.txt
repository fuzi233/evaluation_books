I turned and followed the spotted leopardess, catching only one flash of her tawny, rosette-marked fur before she tore up the brow of the hill and vanished through the palace gate. When I stumbled into the entrance hall, the princess was just snatching her discarded robe off the stone floor, wrapping it tight around her shoulders. The blood from the wounds on her neck had clotted dark, crusted fast by the wind of her headlong flight. She spotted me first: a sharp flare of unmasked anger flickered across her face, and she snapped her head aside like she could not bear to look at me. Then she forced a wobbly, performative smile back onto her lips and turned to face me.
“I met with a trivial little accident,” she purred, smooth as spun honey. “I heard that cat-woman had snuck back into the city, so I went down to drive her off. But she brought one of her horrid abominations with her — it sprang straight for me, sank its claws in my neck before I could strike a single blow!” She shivered so hard her whole frame trembled, and I could not help pitying her, even though I knew every word was a lie. Her wounds were real, though, and her haggard, hunted expression reminded me exactly of how she had looked trapped in that dark cave days before. Guilt twisted my chest that I had let her fight that battle unaided, and I must have worn my compassion plain on my face.
“Child of folly!” she said, with that same wobbly false smile. “You’re not about to cry, are you? Wait for me right here. I’m stepping into the Black Hall for a moment, and you can fetch me something to dress these scratches.” But I stuck close on her heels. I did not dare let her vanish out of my sight.
The instant we crossed the Black Hall threshold, a low thrumming buzz hit my ears, as of hundreds of overlapping muffled voices. Slow wandering light seeped across the vast dark, roving from group to group, illuminating them one after another: cluster after cluster would glow bright for a handful of heartbeats, then sink back into the hazy gloom as the light drifted onward. So many of the scenes that flared to life were familiar to me — moments I had lived through, or watched unfold. And the princess was present in every single one of them, even though she stood right at my side, motionless, her head bowed to her chest. The skull-faced dancers stamped through the grass of the forest hall, and there was the princess peering in through the doorframe at them. The vicious brawl raged deep in the Evil Wood, and there was the princess, yelling to egg the fighters on.
The murmur rolled on, shapes and colours shifting like watercolour bleeding into paper, until the roving light finally settled on that sunken hollow out on the heath. There was the princess, pacing back and forth, snarling as she tried in vain to wrap the clinging mist around her own body. That was when I froze, stunned: the old librarian stepped up to her, stood staring at her for a long beat, and then she crumpled. Her limbs peeled away from her, her whole ghostly eidolon dissolved into thin air. A wild, ringing shriek echoed off the high vaulted ceiling, and the moment her double collapsed, the real princess standing like a statue at my side fell heavily to the stone floor, and went utterly still.
I turned on my heel immediately and walked out. Not again. I would never waste another breath trying to restore her.
I was still shaking so hard my teeth rattled as I stood beside the leopardess’s cage, and it hit me all at once: that shifting, buzzing dark in the Black Hall? We had been standing inside the princess’s own mind. I watched the spotted tip of the leopardess’s tail twitch once. I was still trying to calm my frayed nerves when the princess’s voice rang out right beside my ear.
“Come now,” she said, soft and sweet like nothing at all had happened. “I will show you exactly what I need you to get me for my scratches.” She led me out into the courtyard, and I followed in dazed, unresisting compliance. The moon hung near the zenith, and its silver glow shone brighter than the gold of any absent midday sun I had ever known. She led me through the orchard of trees, straight to the tallest one at the very centre of the grove. It was nothing like the rest: all its topmost branches curved inwards, knitting together to make a tight rounded crown that looked, from where we stood below, exactly like a giant fir cone. The princess stopped right under it, tilted her head back to stare up at its crown, half talking to herself.
“Right at the summit of that tree grows one tiny blossom. One touch of it would heal all my scratches instantly. I could turn myself into a dove for a moment to fetch it, but there’s a little venomous snake coiled in the leaves up there — its bite would be worse to a dove than a tiger’s bite would be to me! How I hate that cat-woman!” She spun around to face me, one of her softest, most persuasive smiles tugging at her lips. “Can you climb?” The smile vanished as fast as it had come, and her face crumpled into a look of raw, aching suffering. I ought to have left her there to suffer, but the way she pressed her wounded hand to her neck went straight to my heart.
I studied the tree: all up its thick trunk, there were rough knobby protrusions, leftover stumps of shed palm fronds, perfect hand and footholds. “I can climb that tree,” I answered.
“Not with bare feet!” she protested. I had run after the vanishing leopardess so fast I had left my sandals back in my chambers.
“It is no matter,” I said. “I have long gone barefoot.” I glanced up the trunk again, my gaze wandering all the way up until it vanished into the shadowed crown of leaves. The moon spilled silver across the rough bark in patches like river froth, and a soft gust of wind slipped through the top of the tree, making a sound as gentle as water falling into still water. I stepped forward to begin my ascent, and the princess grabbed my arm to stop me.
“I cannot let you attempt it with your feet bare!” she insisted. “A fall from the top would kill you!”
“So would a bite from the snake!” I laughed — and I will confess, I did not believe there was any snake up there at all.
“It would not hurt you, not the way it would hurt me,” she replied. “Wait a moment.” She tore the two wide embroidered silk borders off the front of her robe, dropped to one knee, and wrapped one strip around my left foot, the other around my right, tying them tight.
“You have left the ends hanging, princess!” I pointed out.
“I have nothing sharp to cut them off with,” she said. “But they are not long enough to get entangled.”
I turned back to the tree, and began to climb. The cold after sundown in Bulika was never as biting as the chill around the sexton’s cottage, but the higher I climbed, the colder I grew. I shivered hard, until I could no longer feel my hands or my feet. There was barely any wind, the branches did not sway at all, but as I drew near the summit, every single branch I put my foot on or grabbed hold of felt like it was about to snap the second I put pressure on it. When my head poked above the topmost layers of leaves, out into the bright open moonlight, to start searching for the tiny healing blossom, I was suddenly drenched from head to foot. The next instant, I was no longer in a tree at all. I was thrashing in storm-tossed water, heaved wildly about, sinking fast. Tossed up and down, side to side, rolled over and over, dragged deeper every time I caught a gasp of air, I was choking and gurgling, lungs burning, until I slammed down hard on solid ground.
“I told you so!” croaked a familiar cackling voice right in my ear.
