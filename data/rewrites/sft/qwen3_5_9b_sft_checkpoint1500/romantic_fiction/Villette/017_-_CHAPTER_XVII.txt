Those battles against one's inherent nature, the stubborn currents of the soul, may appear to be wasted efforts, yet they yield a harvest. However slight, they steer the outward conduct toward what Reason sanctions, often counteracting the erratic impulses of raw Feeling. They impose a discipline upon life, rendering it more steady, quieter on the surface, and thus acceptable to the casual observer. Yet beneath this veneer lies the chaos; leave that turmoil to God. A fellow human, frail and limited, lacks the authority to judge the depths of the spirit. One must kneel before the Maker, presenting the hidden wounds of the heart, asking for patience in agony and strength in weakness. Surely, at some point, the still waters will move. Though the healer may arrive not in the guise one imagined, nor at the hour of longing, the blind, the lame, and the broken will eventually be led to the pool. Come, Herald! Around the spring, thousands weep and despair, watching it stagnate through endless years. Time moves slowly in heaven; the cycles of angels span epochs, and generations rise and fall like dust in the wind. To countless millions, the first angelic visitor is merely Azrael, the harbinger of silence.

I attempted to rise the following morning, but the effort was thwarted by Mrs. Bretton. While I was lacing my gown and sipping cold water from the carafe on my washstand, hoping to steel my trembling limbs, she burst in. "An absurdity!" she declared immediately, adopting her customary brisk and commanding tone—the same spirited demeanor her son, John, had once admired and fiercely defied. Within moments, she had coaxed me back onto the French bed. "Lie there until afternoon," she ordered. "My boy gave strict instructions before departing that this must happen, and I assure you, Master John speaks law here." Soon after, she returned with breakfast, carrying the tray herself rather than delegating the task to servants. She sat upon the bed while I ate. Not everyone, among our circle of friends and acquaintances, enjoys having a nurse hover so closely, watching every swallow, waiting on every need. Yet, with Mrs. Bretton, it was different. She had always been my comfort. Nothing tasted as sweet as food delivered by her own hands. I do not recall a single instance where her entry into a room failed to brighten the air. Her nature, vibrant and distinct, possessed a peculiar chemistry. Some draw us away despite knowing their virtue; others, flawed as they may be, radiate a warmth that makes their proximity feel like a breath of fresh air. My godmother's striking black eyes, her clear complexion, her prompt and warm hand, and her self-assured bearing acted upon me like the invigorating breeze of a healthy climate. Her son used to call her "the old lady," a title that filled me with a strange delight, for within her frame of twenty-five, I sensed the boundless energy and vigor of youth.

"I would have stayed right here with you," she remarked, removing my empty cup, "working and keeping you company all day, were it not for that tyrannical John Graham. 'Mamma,' he cried when he left, 'do not disturb your goddaughter with chatter.' He insisted I remain in my own quarters and spares me the privilege of my company. He believes, Lucy, that you've caught some nervous fever from your appearance—is that true?"

I admitted that I was unsure of the specific illness but confessed to significant mental distress. I hesitated to elaborate further, for the particulars of my suffering belonged to a realm I did not expect my godmother to inhabit. The contrast between us was stark: hers was a stately ship sailing safely on calm seas, crewed by brave and capable men under a cheerful captain; mine was a lifeboat lying dry and solitary in a dark shed, launched only when storms rage and the abyss threatens. The *Louisa Bretton* never ventured into such treacherous waters, and so the drenched survivor kept his counsel, offering no tales of the wreck.

After she departed, I lay content in bed, grateful for John's consideration. My day was solitary, yet the promise of evening softened its loneliness. As the morning hours faded, bringing with them the vague, pressing sense of obligations unmet, I drifted into a dreamy state. My small, quiet room seemed transformed into a sea cave. Lacking color save for whites and pale greens, it suggested foam and the deep ocean. The cornices, bleached by time, held shell-like ornaments; the ceiling angles featured white moldings resembling dolphins. Even the single splash of color, a red satin pincushion, echoed coral, and the dark, glossy glass mirror seemed to hold the reflection of a mermaid. Closing my eyes, I imagined a gale subsiding, rolling gently over the house like a wave settling upon a rocky base, fading far away into the upper world, sounding there like nothing more than a lullaby.

Evening arrived, and Martha brought a lantern. With her assistance, I dressed and, feeling stronger, descended the stairs unaided into the blue salon. Dr. John appeared to have finished his rounds early. He stood in the recessed window opposite the door, reading a newspaper by the dim twilight. The fire burned brightly, but the lamp remained unlit, and tea had not yet been served. Mrs. Bretton, who had spent the entire day outdoors, lay half-reclined in her deep chair, fast asleep. Seeing me, John approached, careful not to startle her slumber. His voice was soft and melodic, lacking any harsh edge, designed to soothe rather than awaken.

"This is a quiet little château," he observed, inviting me to sit near the window. "Though invisible from the main road, it sits a mile beyond the Porte de Crécy, tucked down a lane that turns into an avenue leading straight through the meadows to the door. It is not a modern estate but built in the old style of the Basse-Ville. More a manor than a château, it is called 'La Terrasse' because its façade rises from a broad turf walk, with steps descending a grassy slope to the path. Look there!" He pointed outside. "The moon is rising. She shines beautifully through the trunks of the trees."

Indeed, where does the moon not shine? Is there any scene, enclosed or vast, that her light does not sanctify? Rosy or fiery, she climbed above a nearby bank, clearing the haze to become pure gold, floating swiftly into a calm sky. Did the moonlight soften or sadden Dr. Bretton? Did it whisper romance? I believed it touched him. Though not given to sighs, he sighed quietly as he watched it ascend. I knew the cause; I knew it was for Ginevra. This knowledge pressed upon me a sense of duty to speak the name he carried in his heart. He seemed eager for the topic, his face brimming with questions and interest, yet restrained by a polite hesitation on how to begin. To relieve him of this awkwardness was my purpose. I needed only to utter the idol's name, and love's gentle prayer would follow. I had almost begun, "You know Miss Fanshawe is traveling with the Cholmondeleys," when he interrupted me with a different subject.

"The first order of business this morning," he said, pocketing his sentiments and turning from the moon, "was to visit the Rue Fossette and inform the cook that you were safe. Did you know she hadn't realized you were missing? She thought you secure in the great dormitory. How carefully you must have been tended!"

"Oh, that is easily explained," I replied. "Goton could do nothing but bring a tisane and a crust of bread. Having rejected them so often during the past week, the kind woman grew tired of the daily trip from the kitchen to the school dormitory and only visited once a day at noon to make my bed. I believe she is a good-natured soul; she would have been delighted to cook me lamb chops if I could have eaten them."

"What did Madame Beck mean by leaving you alone?"

"Madame Beck could not foresee that I would fall ill."

"Your nervous system bore the brunt of the suffering?"

"I am not entirely certain what constitutes my nervous system, but I was dreadfully low in spirits."

"That leaves me unable to assist with pills or potions. Medicine cannot restore good spirits. My art stops at the threshold of hypochondria; she peeks in and finds a chamber of torture, able to do little. Cheerful company would be of use. You must avoid solitude and take ample exercise."

Agreement and a pause followed these words. They sounded sensible, stamped with the authority of custom and experience.

"Miss Snowe," Dr. John resumed, shifting the focus to matters of faith, "may I inquire what your religion is? Are you a Catholic?"

I looked up, surprised. "A Catholic? No! Why suggest such an idea?"

"The manner in which you were entrusted to me last night caused me to doubt."

"You entrust me? Indeed, I hardly recall. Pray, tell me how I ended up in your care."

"It was under circumstances that baffled me. I had attended a patient of singular interest and critical condition throughout the day; the disease was rare, the treatment uncertain. I had seen a similar, even more severe case in a Parisian hospital, though that need not concern you. Eventually, a reduction in the patient's acute pain freed me, and I rode homeward.
