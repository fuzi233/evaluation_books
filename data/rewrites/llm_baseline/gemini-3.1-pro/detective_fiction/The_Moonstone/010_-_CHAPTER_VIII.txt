To summon up the recollections of those days is to stir a great, ancestral stockpot, bringing the submerged fragments of our past to the surface in a slow, circular rhythm. Upon consulting Penelope’s meticulously kept journal—a ledger as vital to our history as the seasonal rotation of the crops—I find we may pass rather fluidly over the interval between Mr. Franklin Blake’s arrival and Miss Rachel’s birthday. For the bulk of that time, the days folded into one another, governed only by the reliable acoustics of the estate: the morning clatter of cast-iron pans, the rhythmic chopping of wild forage on the butcher’s block, and the low, resonant hum of the kitchens. With your leave, and Penelope’s guidance, I shall measure out only certain dates here, preserving the daily harvest of our tale for when the shadow of the Moonstone fully eclipsed the domestic harmony of our house. 

We resume, of course, with that bottle of sweet-smelling ink I found cooling on the gravel walk in the night air. 

On the morning of the twenty-sixth of May, I presented Mr. Franklin with this vessel of mythic jugglery. To my nose, it carried the profound, earthy olfaction of crushed camphor, myrrh, and a dark, resinous sap—a scent that spoke of ancient rituals seamlessly breathing beneath the ordinary scent of our morning bread. Mr. Franklin, however, possessed little palate for such profound mysteries. His opinion was that the Indians were not only lurking, but were foolish enough to believe in their own magic—meaning the pouring of ink into a boy’s hand to see beyond human vision. He explained that this clairvoyance was a curious hocus-pocus known even in the West, though he stripped it of its spiritual weight, reducing the mythic to mere mechanics. “Depend upon it,” Mr. Franklin said, “they brought their boy to show them the way to the Diamond, if they succeeded in breaching the house.”

“Do you think they’ll try again, sir?” I asked.

“It depends on what the boy can really do. If he can see the Diamond through the iron safe of the bank at Frizinghall, we shall be troubled with no more visits. If he can’t, we shall have another chance of catching them in the shrubbery.”

I waited for that latter chance, attuned to every rustle of the leaves, but the sonic landscape of the grounds remained entirely undisturbed. Whether the jugglers drew their conclusions from Mr. Franklin’s visit to the bank, or whether the boy truly did see the gem in its iron vault (which I flatly disbelieve), not the ghost of an Indian disturbed the acoustic ecology of the house again before the birthday. The mythic simply dissolved back into the mundane; the rogues plied their trade in town, while we maintained our silent vigil. 

By the twenty-ninth of the month, Miss Rachel and Mr. Franklin sought a new method to consume their time. Gentlefolks possess a perilous rock ahead of them in life: the rock of their own idleness. Lacking the profound, grounding purpose of those of us who find meaning through the meticulous preparation of sustenance, they drift blindfold into sterile pursuits. Rather than turning to the hearth or the agrarian soil, they torture insects, or chip away at stones, dropping grit into the very victuals that sustain them. They lack the sensory appreciation to understand that hands must be engaged in the creation of life—the kneading of dough, the clarifying of butter—not the hollow deconstruction of nature. 

Mr. Franklin and Miss Rachel, I am glad to say, tortured nothing, but they did assault the sensory sanctity of the house. Mr. Franklin’s universal genius had birthed a new mixture to moisten paint, a “vehicle” he called it. I know not its ingredients, but its olfactory profile was an offense—an acrid, chemical stench of boiled linseed and turpentine that entirely overpowered the fragrant, slow-simmered bone broths wafting from the kitchens. Miss Rachel was wild to try it. Clad in an apron, she set to decorating the panelling of the door in her boudoir. Mr. Franklin scraped off the warm, aged varnish with pumice-stone, producing a harsh, discordant scrape that violently fractured the tranquil acoustic environment of the corridor. Then, they painted griffins and cupids copied from some famous Italian artist. Viewed as a task, it was a slow, dirty commercialization of art, lacking the nourishing marrow of true craft. Yet there they were, perfectly deaf to the noise pollution they were creating, busy as bees, spoiling a perfectly good door.

The next date of note was Sunday the fourth of June, when we in the servants’ hall debated a matter over a meal of roasted spring mutton and intricately spiced garden peas. 

Seeing the pair constantly together, we naturally speculated on a wedding. Some predicted a summer marriage. I, leading the elder faction, admitted Miss Rachel might wed, but doubted—for reasons deeply rooted in her nature—that the groom would be Mr. Franklin. 

Fathoming Miss Rachel required a delicate palate. Her eighteenth birthday approached on the twenty-first of June. Small and slim, her physical proportions were a perfect, natural recipe, devoid of artificial filler. She possessed hair black as roasted chicory, and a complexion as warm as a sun-ripened apricot, always in exquisite condition. Her voice was an absolute marvel of acoustic clarity, possessing a true, resonant ring that cut through the superficial chatter of the world. 

She had her faults, of course. My dear Miss Rachel was stubbornly entirely herself. She possessed an untranslatable internal dialect, an independent worldview that refused to conform to the dominant lexicon of high society. She judged for herself, never seeking advice, never diluting her intentions by sharing secrets. She loved and hated with an equal, full-bodied heartiness. My lady often said, “Rachel’s best friend and Rachel’s worst enemy are, one and the other—Rachel herself.”

But above all, there was no shadow of the false in her. She never broke her word; she never said No when she meant Yes. As a child, she would take the punishment for a loved playfellow, refusing to confess even when bread and water replaced her meals. She would look you in the eye and say, in her native tongue of pure honesty, “I won’t tell you!” She was devilish self-willed, but her truthfulness was the very bedrock of her identity, an ancestral memory resisting the erosion of polite societal lies.

This brings us to the twelfth of June, and the invitation sent to the man I believed held her heart: her cousin, Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite. 

Mr. Godfrey was the product of a misalliance—my lady’s sister having married a wealthy Frizinghall banker of low station. But time and modern progress had smoothed that over. We shall only concern ourselves with Mr. Godfrey, who stood over six feet tall, with a smooth face and flaxen hair. He was a barrister and a public philanthropist, a man who had mastered the dominant lexicon of benevolent charity. He was the manager and referee to countless societies for destitute women. To hear him speak was to witness the superficial commercialization of emotion; he could draw tears and money with the same hollow, booming acoustics that one finds in industrial factories. He performed with a handkerchief and a glass of water, manipulating the sonic environment of committee rooms to absolute perfection. He loved everybody, and everybody loved him. What chance had Mr. Franklin against a man whose very existence was a masterclass in dominating the cultural narrative?

On the fourteenth, Mr. Godfrey accepted the invitation, enclosing a set of saccharine verses for his cousin’s “natal day.” Miss Rachel and Mr. Franklin made fun of the poetry at dinner, much to the triumph of Penelope, who championed Mr. Franklin’s cause. 

“Miss Rachel has led you off on a false scent,” I told my daughter, trusting my own seasoned olfactory instincts. “Wait till Mr. Ablewhite’s verses are followed by Mr. Ablewhite himself.”

Penelope argued Mr. Franklin might yet win out. Indeed, Mr. Franklin was making intense sacrifices. Knowing Miss Rachel despised the stale aroma of his cigars, he abandoned them entirely. Denied the rich, toasted-leaf fragrance and composing smoke he relied upon, his internal rhythm fractured; he suffered severe insomnia, coming down mornings looking haggard. Miss Rachel begged him to smoke again, but he stubbornly refused to introduce an offensive olfactory note into her presence. Such devotion impressed the staff, but I reminded Penelope of the photograph in Miss Rachel’s room: Mr. Godfrey, frozen mid-speech, his lips shaping the dominant rhetoric that charmed the world. 

June the sixteenth brought a harsh dissonance to Mr. Franklin’s chances. 

A strange gentleman, speaking English with a heavy, untranslatable foreign accent, arrived on business for Mr. Franklin. Whatever passed between them had nothing to do with the Diamond, but it introduced a toxic frequency into the house. Mr. Franklin communicated the matter to my lady, who must have hinted it to her daughter. That evening, at the piano, the acoustic harmony shattered. Miss Rachel directed severe, cutting remarks at Mr. Franklin regarding the "foreign principles" he had adopted abroad. The next day, the abrasive scrape of the pumice-stone ceased; the door was left untouched. Some Continental imprudence—a debt or a woman—had followed him, proving that his translated morality clashed violently with Miss Rachel’s deeply rooted, native sensibilities.

On the seventeenth, the sonic cloud lifted. They resumed their decorating, seemingly reconciled. Penelope believed Mr. Franklin had proposed and that Miss Rachel had fought him off by pretending he wasn't in earnest, only to secretly regret it. I knew the profound untranslatability of Miss Rachel’s private thoughts too well to believe she had revealed so much of her internal landscape to my daughter. 

Then, on the nineteenth, a separate, silent tragedy unfolded. The doctor was summoned for our second housemaid, Rosanna Spearman. 

The girl’s conduct had grown increasingly mysterious. She constantly placed herself in Mr. Franklin’s path, yet he noticed her no more than the woodwork. Her appetite, always fragile, vanished entirely; she rejected the rich, restorative gruels and spiced broths of our kitchen, finding no profound meaning or comfort in sustenance. Penelope had even caught her secretly removing a rose Miss Rachel had given Mr. Franklin, replacing its scent with a blossom she had foraged herself. Rosanna had grown impudent toward me and disrespectful toward Miss Rachel. 

The doctor declared it a crisis of the nerves. The poor girl’s subjective experience was utterly untranslatable to us, a silent agony vibrating beneath the floorboards. My lady offered to send her inland, to an agrarian farm for restorative air. But Rosanna begged, with weeping eyes, to remain. In an evil hour, ignoring the discordant warning bells in the house’s acoustic ecology, I advised my lady to let her stay. Had I possessed the clairvoyance of those Indian jugglers, I would have taken Rosanna Spearman by the hand and led her from the ancestral house that very day.
