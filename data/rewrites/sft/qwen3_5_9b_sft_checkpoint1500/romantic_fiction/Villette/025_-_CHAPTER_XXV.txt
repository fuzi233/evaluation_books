There was a cheerfulness in my godmother that was innate, and she endeavored to maintain it for our sakes; yet, despite her efforts, the evening at La Terrasse lacked genuine joy until the wild howl of the winter night carried the signal of their arrival. One wonders how often, while women and girls sit warm beside cozy fires, their hearts and imaginations are severed from that physical comfort, driven forth by the night to traverse dark paths, to brave the stress of the elements, to stand sentinel at lonely gates amidst the wildest storms, waiting for the return of fathers, sons, or husbands.

At last, Father and Son reached the Château. The Count de Bassompierre had arrived that night alongside Dr. Bretton. I could not determine which of us three first heard the horses' approach; the severity and violence of the weather compelled us to descend into the hall to welcome the two riders as they entered. However, they cautioned us to keep our distance. Both were encased in white, resembling two moving mountains of snow. Indeed, Mrs. Bretton, observing their condition, immediately directed them to the kitchen, strictly prohibiting them, under penalty, from stepping onto her carpeted staircase until they had removed the "mask of Old Christmas" they currently wore. Yet, drawn by curiosity, we followed them into the kitchen. It was a large, ancient Dutch kitchen, picturesque and pleasant.

In the midst of this scene, the little White Countess danced in a circle around her equally white sire, clapping her hands and crying, "Papa, papa, you look like an enormous Polar bear!"

The bear shook himself, sending a frozen shower flying, causing the little sprite to flee far away. Yet, she soon returned, laughing, eager to assist in stripping off the arctic disguise. Finally emerging from his "dreadnought," the Count threatened to overwhelm her with the snow, like an avalanche.

"Come, then," she said, bending to invite the fall. When it was playfully advanced above her head, she bounded out of reach like a little chamois.

Her movements possessed a supple softness and velvet grace akin to a kitten's; her laugh was clearer than the ring of silver and crystal. As she took her sire's cold hands and rubbed them, standing on tiptoe to reach his lips for a kiss, a halo of loving delight seemed to surround her. The grave and reverend seigneur looked down upon her as men look upon the apple of their eye.

"Mrs. Bretton," he said, "what am I to do with this daughter or daughterling of mine? She neither grows in wisdom nor in stature. Do you not find her pretty nearly as much the child as she was ten years ago?"

"She cannot be more the child than this great boy of mine," replied Mrs. Bretton, who was currently in conflict with her son regarding a change of dress she deemed advisable, which he resisted. He stood leaning against the Dutch dresser, laughing and keeping her at arm's length.

"Come, mamma," he said, "by way of compromise, and to secure for us inward as well as outward warmth, let us have a Christmas wassail-cup, and toast Old England here, on the hearth."

Thus, while the Count stood by the fire and Paulina Mary danced to and fro—happy in the liberty of the wide, hall-like kitchen—Mrs. Bretton instructed Martha to spice and heat the wassail-bowl. Pouring the draught into a Bretton flagon, it was served round, steaming hot, via a small silver vessel I recognized as Graham's christening-cup.

"Hear's to Auld Lang Syne!" cried the Count, holding the glancing cup aloft. Looking then at Mrs. Bretton, he began:

"We twa ha' paidlet i' the burn
Fra morning sun till dine,
But seas between us braid ha' roared
Sin' auld lang syne.

"And surely ye'll be your pint-stoup,
And surely I'll be mine;
And we'll taste a cup o' kindness yet
For auld lang syne."

"Scotch! Scotch!" cried Paulina. "Papa is talking Scotch; and Scotch he is, partly. We are Home and de Bassompierre, Caledonian and Gallic."

"And is that a Scotch reel you are dancing, you Highland fairy?" asked her father. "Mrs. Bretton, there will be a green ring growing up in the middle of your kitchen shortly. I would not answer for her being quite *cannie*: she is a strange little mortal."

"Tell Lucy to dance with me, papa; there is Lucy Snowe."

Mr. Home (there remained quite as much of plain Mr. Home within him as of the proud Count de Bassompierre) held out his hand to me, saying kindly, "He remembers me well; and, even had his own memory been less trustworthy, my name was so often on his daughter's lips, and he has listened to so many long tales about me, that I should seem like an old acquaintance."

Everyone had now tasted the wassail-cup save Paulina, whose *pas de fée*, or *de fantaisie*, no one thought of interrupting to offer so profanatory a draught. Yet she was not to be overlooked nor balked of her mortal privileges.

"Let me taste," she said to Graham, as he moved the cup to the shelf of the dresser, beyond her reach.

Mrs. Bretton and Mr. Home were now engaged in conversation. Dr. John had not been unobservant of the fairy's dance; he had watched it, and he had liked it. Beyond the softness and beauty of the movements, eminently grateful to his grace-loving eye, it was the ease in his mother's house that charmed him, for it set *him* at ease. Again she seemed a child for him; again, almost, his playmate. I wondered how he would speak to her; I had not yet seen him address her. His first words proved that the old days of "little Polly" had been recalled to his mind by this evening's child-like light-heartedness.

"Your ladyship wishes for the tankard?"

"I think I said so. I think I intimated as much."

"Couldn't consent to a step of the kind on any account. Sorry for it, but couldn't do it."

"Why? I am quite well now: it can't break my collar-bone again, or dislocate my shoulder. Is it wine?"

"No; nor dew."

"I don't want dew; I don't like dew: but what is it?"

"Ale—strong ale—old October; brewed, perhaps, when I was born."

"It must be curious: is it good?"

"Excessively good."

He took it down, administering to himself a second dose of this mighty elixir, expressing in his mischievous eyes extreme contentment with the same, and solemnly replacing the cup on the shelf.

"I should like a little," said Paulina, looking up. "I never had any 'old October': is it sweet?"

"Perilously sweet," said Graham.

She continued to look up exactly with the countenance of a child longing for some prohibited dainty. At last, the Doctor relented, took it down, and indulged himself in the gratification of letting her taste from his hand. His eyes, always expressive in the revelation of pleasurable feelings, luminously and smilingly avowed that it *was* a gratification; and he prolonged it by so regulating the position of the cup that only a drop at a time could reach the rosy, sipping lips by which its brim was courted.

"A little more—a little more," said she, petulantly touching his hand with the forefinger, to make him incline the cup more generously and yieldingly. "It smells of spice and sugar, but I can't taste it; your wrist is so stiff, and you are so stingy."

He indulged her, whispering, however, with gravity: "Don't tell my mother or Lucy; they wouldn't approve."

"Nor do I," said she, passing into another tone and manner as soon as she had fairly assayed the beverage, just as if it had acted upon her like some disenchanting draught, undoing the work of a wizard: "I find it anything but sweet; it is bitter and hot, and takes away my breath. Your old October was only desirable while forbidden. Thank you, no more."

And, with a slight bend—careless, but as graceful as her dance—she glided from him and rejoined her father.

I think she had spoken truth: the child of seven was in the girl of seventeen.

Graham looked after her a little baffled, a little puzzled; his eye was on her a good deal during the rest of the evening, but she did not seem to notice him.

As we ascended to the drawing-room for tea, she took her father's arm: her natural place seemed to be at his side; her eyes and her ears were dedicated to him. He and Mrs. Bretton were the chief talkers of our little party, and Paulina was their best listener, attending closely to all that was said, prompting the repetition of this or that trait or adventure.

"And where were you at such a time, papa? And what did you say then? And tell Mrs. Bretton what happened on that occasion." Thus she drew him out.
