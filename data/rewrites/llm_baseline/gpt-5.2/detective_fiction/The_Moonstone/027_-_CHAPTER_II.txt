Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite entered the drawing-room as punctually as a dish arrives at table when the cook has been trained to respect the clock. Not too soon to throw the household into confusion; not too late to leave us standing with the door yawning open and the air of the corridor leaking in, colder and flatter than the room’s own temper. His whole manner had that finished completeness which, in domestic life, is sometimes mistaken for virtue because it never wrinkles the surface.

“Go to Miss Verinder,” my aunt said to the servant, “and tell her Mr. Ablewhite is here.”

We both asked after his health. We both spoke at once, as women do when they mean to be kind and cannot agree who shall be kind first. And he, with that practised gentleness which seems to hear two voices as neatly as a musician catches two notes, answered both of us in the same moment—my aunt receiving the words, I receiving the smile.

“What,” he cried, with infinite tenderness, “have I done to deserve all this sympathy? My dear aunt! my dear Miss Clack! I have merely been mistaken for somebody else. I have only been blindfolded; I have only been strangled; I have only been thrown flat on my back, on a very thin carpet, covering a particularly hard floor. Just think how much worse it might have been! I might have been murdered; I might have been robbed. What have I lost? Nothing but Nervous Force—which the law doesn’t recognise as property; so that, strictly speaking, I have lost nothing at all.

“If I could have had my own way, I would have kept my adventure to myself—I shrink from all this fuss and publicity. But Mr. Luker made his injuries public, and my injuries, as the necessary consequence, have been proclaimed in their turn. I have become the property of the newspapers, until the gentle reader gets sick of the subject. I am very sick indeed of it myself. May the gentle reader soon be like me!

“And how is dear Rachel? Still enjoying the gaieties of London? So glad to hear it! Miss Clack, I need all your indulgence. I am sadly behind-hand with my Committee Work and my dear Ladies. But I really do hope to look in at the Mothers’-Small-Clothes next week. Did you make cheering progress at Monday’s Committee? Was the Board hopeful about future prospects? And are we nicely off for Trousers?”

His voice—deep, rich, with a soft reverberation like a well-constructed room that gives back what it is given—made even the word Trousers sound like a benevolence. I was on the point of replying that we were, if anything, oppressed by the abundance of that article, when the door opened again, and in came the brisk disturbance of youth—Miss Verinder herself.

She crossed the room at an unladylike speed, as if she had run from one end of the house and brought the rush of it with her; her hair not arranged as it ought to have been arranged, her face flushed as if some inward heat had risen too suddenly—like broth that has been let boil too fast and will not clear.

“I am charmed to see you, Godfrey,” she said, in the off-hand manner of one young man greeting another. “I wish you had brought Mr. Luker with you. You and he—while our present excitement lasts—are the two most interesting men in all London. It’s morbid to say this; it’s unhealthy; it’s all that a well-regulated mind like Miss Clack’s most instinctively shudders at. Never mind that. Tell me the whole of the Northumberland Street story directly. I know the newspapers have left some of it out.”

I grieve to write it; yet truth, like salt, must be used though it stings. Mr. Godfrey—too human, alas—took her hand in both his, and laid it softly against his waistcoat, where one might suppose his heart to be. It was a direct encouragement of her recklessness, and of her insolent reference to me.

“Dearest Rachel,” he said, “the newspapers have told you everything—and they have told it much better than I can.”

“Godfrey thinks we all make too much of the matter,” my aunt remarked. “He has just been saying that he doesn’t care to speak of it.”

“Why?” Rachel asked, and in the single word there was a sharp lift—like a knife’s point catching the light.

He looked down at her with an indulgence which, to my mind, was as injudicious as it was undeserved. I felt called upon—gently—to interpose.

“Rachel, darling,” I said, “true greatness and true courage are ever modest.”

She never so much as glanced at me.

“You are a very good fellow in your way, Godfrey,” she returned, still speaking to him as if they were equals in some masculine club of frank speech. “But I am quite sure you are not great; I don’t believe you possess any extraordinary courage; and I am firmly persuaded—if you ever had any modesty—that your lady-worshippers relieved you of that virtue a good many years since. You have some private reason for not talking of your adventure in Northumberland Street; and I mean to know it.”

“My reason is the simplest imaginable,” he answered, still bearing it with patience. “I am tired of the subject.”

“You are tired of the subject? My dear Godfrey, I am going to make a remark.”

“What is it?”

“You live a great deal too much in the society of women. And you have contracted two very bad habits in consequence. You have learnt to talk nonsense seriously, and you have got into a way of telling fibs for the pleasure of telling them. You can’t go straight with your lady-worshippers. I mean to make you go straight with me. Come, and sit down. I am brimful of downright questions; and I expect you to be brimful of downright answers.”

She actually dragged him to a chair by the window, planting him where the light could fall full on his face, as if she meant to read him as one reads a page held near the glass. I looked to my aunt for intervention; but she sat unmoved, with a heaviness in her stillness that struck me as new—like a harvest field after reaping, when the bustle has gone and the stalks stand blunt and tired. Perhaps it was reaction after her late trials in the country. Perhaps it was something more. At her age, and with her autumnal exuberance of figure, it was not pleasant to observe.

Rachel began at once, taking no more notice of her mother or of me than if we were the furniture.

“Have the police done anything, Godfrey?”

“Nothing whatever.”

“It is certain, I suppose, that the three men who laid the trap for you were the same three men who afterwards laid the trap for Mr. Luker?”

“Humanly speaking, my dear Rachel, there can be no doubt of it.”

“And not a trace of them has been discovered?”

“Not a trace.”

“It is thought—is it not?—that these three men are the three Indians who came to our house in the country.”

“Some people think so.”

“Do you think so?”

“My dear Rachel, they blindfolded me before I could see their faces. I know nothing whatever of the matter. How can I offer an opinion on it?”

Even his angelic gentleness began to fray under such persistent tugging. He made as if to rise. She stopped him—took him by the shoulders and pushed him back into the chair. Do not say immodest; do not say guilty terror; we must not judge. Indeed, indeed, we must not judge.

“I want to know something about Mr. Luker, Godfrey.”

“I am again unfortunate, Rachel. No man knows less of Mr. Luker than I do.”

“You never saw him before you and he met accidentally at the bank?”

“Never.”

“You have seen him since?”

“Yes. We have been examined together, as well as separately, to assist the police.”

“Mr. Luker was robbed of a receipt which he had got from his banker’s—was he not? What was the receipt for?”

“For a valuable gem which he had placed in the safe keeping of the bank.”

“That’s what the newspapers say. It may be enough for the general reader; but it is not enough for me. The banker’s receipt must have mentioned what the gem was?”

“The banker’s receipt, Rachel—as I have heard it described—mentioned nothing of the kind. A valuable gem, belonging to Mr. Luker; deposited by Mr. Luker; sealed with Mr. Luker’s seal; and only to be given up on Mr. Luker’s personal application. That was the form, and that is all I know about it.”

She paused. She looked at her mother and sighed, as if the sigh were something she could not swallow. Then she looked again at Mr. Godfrey.

“Some of our private affairs, at home,” she said, “seem to have got into the newspapers?”

“I grieve to say, it is so.”

“And some idle people, perfect strangers to us, are trying to trace a connexion between what happened at our house in Yorkshire and what has happened since, here in London?”

“The public curiosity, in certain quarters, is, I fear, taking that turn.”

“The people who say that the three unknown men who ill-used you and Mr. Luker are the three Indians, also say that the valuable gem—”

She stopped. While she had been speaking she had been turning whiter and whiter, until the strong black of her hair made her face look ghastly by contrast, like flour on a dark board. We thought she would faint.

Mr. Godfrey again tried to rise. My aunt begged her to say no more. I followed my aunt’s appeal with a modest medicinal offering—a bottle of salts, ready to hand. None of it reached her. She held herself like a creature cornered.

“Godfrey, stay where you are. Mamma, there is not the least reason to be alarmed about me. Clack, you’re dying to hear the end of it—I won’t faint, expressly to oblige you.”

Those were her words—set down in my diary the moment I returned home. But do not judge.

She turned to him again, and forced the question through.

“Tell me plainly, Godfrey—do any of them say that Mr. Luker’s valuable gem is—the Moonstone?”

At the very name of the Indian Diamond, I saw a change pass over Mr. Godfrey. His complexion deepened; the easy suavity dropped away; and an indignation—noble, as I must call it—took him by the voice.

“They do say it,” he answered. “There are people who don’t hesitate to accuse Mr. Luker of telling a falsehood to serve some private interests of his own. He has over and over again solemnly declared that, until this scandal assailed him, he had never even heard of the Moonstone. And these vile people reply, without a shadow of proof to justify them: He has his reasons for concealment; we decline to believe him on his oath. Shameful! shameful!”

Rachel looked at him in a manner I cannot rightly express—strange, searching, as if she listened not only to his words but to the sound behind them. When he had done, she said:

“Considering that Mr. Luker is only a chance acquaintance of yours, you take up his cause, Godfrey, rather warmly.”

“I hope, Rachel,” he replied, “I take up the cause of all oppressed people rather warmly.”

It was spoken with such melting sincerity that it might have softened stone. Yet what is stone to the unregenerate heart? She sneered—she sneered at him to his face.

“Keep your noble sentiments for your Ladies’ Committees, Godfrey. I am certain that the scandal which has assailed Mr. Luker, has not spared you.”

Even my aunt’s torpor stirred at that.

“My dear Rachel,” she remonstrated, “you have really no right to say that!”

“I mean no harm, mamma—I mean good. Have a moment’s patience with me, and you will see.”

She turned back to Mr. Godfrey with something like pity, and—unladylike again—took him by the hand.

“I am certain,” she said, “that I have found out the true reason of your unwillingness to speak of this matter before my mother and before me. An unlucky accident has associated you in people’s minds with Mr. Luker. You have told me what scandal says of him. What does scandal say of you?”

“Don’t ask me!” he pleaded. “It’s better forgotten, Rachel—it is, indeed.”

“I will hear it!” she cried, fiercely, at the top of her voice.

“Tell her, Godfrey!” my aunt urged. “Nothing can do her such harm as your silence is doing now!”

His eyes filled with tears. He looked at Rachel as if asking leave to spare her—and then he said the words that fell like a dropped plate in a quiet room.

“If you will have it, Rachel—scandal says that the Moonstone is in pledge to Mr. Luker, and that I am the man who has pawned it.”

She sprang up with a scream, and stared from him to my aunt and from my aunt back to him, in such a frantic manner that I thought her reason gone.

“Don’t speak to me! Don’t touch me!” she exclaimed, shrinking from us all—like some hunted animal—into a corner. “This is my fault! I must set it right. I have sacrificed myself—I had a right to do that, if I liked. But to let an innocent man be ruined; to keep a secret which destroys his character for life—Oh, good God, it’s too horrible! I can’t bear it!”

My aunt half rose, then sat down again abruptly. She beckoned to me with a faintness that frightened me, and pointed into her work-box at a little phial.

“Quick!” she whispered. “Six drops, in water. Don’t let Rachel see.”

In any other moment I might have been struck by the secrecy; now there was no time for thought. I obeyed. Mr. Godfrey, unconsciously helping my concealment by drawing Rachel’s attention to him, spoke to her at the other end of the room in tones meant to soothe.

“Indeed, indeed, you exaggerate,” I heard him say. “My reputation stands too high to be destroyed by a miserable passing scandal like this. It will be all forgotten in another week. Let us never speak of it again.”

She was beyond him—beyond generosity itself.

“I must, and will, stop it,” she answered. “Mamma! hear what I say. Miss Clack! hear what I say. I know the hand that took the Moonstone. I know—” she stamped as she spoke, laying violent emphasis on the words—“I know that Godfrey Ablewhite is innocent! Take me to the magistrate, Godfrey! Take me to the magistrate, and I will swear it!”

My aunt caught my hand.

“Stand between us for a minute or two,” she whispered. “Don’t let Rachel see me.”

A bluish tinge had come over her face. I was startled. She read it and tried to quiet me.

“The drops will put me right in a minute or two,” she said; closed her eyes; waited.

While I stood as she bade me, I heard Mr. Godfrey remonstrating still.

“You must not appear publicly in such a thing as this,” he said. “Your reputation, dearest Rachel, is something too pure and too sacred to be trifled with.”

“My reputation!” She burst out laughing—a hard laugh, like a spoon struck against a glass. “Why, I am accused, Godfrey, as well as you. The best detective officer in England declares that I have stolen my own Diamond. Ask him what he thinks—and he will tell you that I have pledged the Moonstone to pay my private debts!”

She broke off, ran to her mother, and dropped on her knees.

“Oh mamma! mamma! mamma! I must be mad—mustn’t I?—not to own the truth now!”

Too vehement to notice my aunt’s condition, she was up again at once and back with Mr. Godfrey.

“I won’t let you—I won’t let any innocent man—be accused and disgraced through my fault. If you won’t take me before the magistrate, draw out a declaration of your innocence on paper, and I will sign it. Do as I tell you, Godfrey, or I’ll write it to the newspapers—I’ll go out, and cry it in the streets!”

We will call it hysterics, and not remorse. Mr. Godfrey pacified her by taking a sheet of paper and writing the declaration. She signed it with a feverish speed, as if her hand were being chased.

“Show it everywhere—don’t think of me,” she said, thrusting it to him. “I am afraid, Godfrey, I have not done you justice, hitherto, in my thoughts. You are more unselfish—you are a better man than I believed you to be. Come here when you can, and I will try and repair the wrong I have done you.”

She gave him her hand. Alas for our fallen nature; alas for Mr. Godfrey. He kissed it, and answered her in a tone so tender it was, in such circumstances, little better than a compromise with sin.

“I will come, dearest,” he said, “on condition that we don’t speak of this hateful subject again.”

Before anyone could speak further, a thundering knock at the street door startled us all. I looked through the window and saw the World, the Flesh, and the Devil waiting below—as typified in carriage and horses, a powdered footman, and three women dressed with such audacity that their silks and ornaments seemed to clatter even at a distance, as if fashion had become a kind of machinery.

Rachel steadied herself at once, as people do who have been crying in private and must go out into public brightness.

“They have come to take me to the flower-show,” she said, crossing to her mother. “One word, mamma, before I go. I have not distressed you, have I?”

Is such bluntness of moral feeling to be pitied or condemned? I incline to mercy; let us pity it.

The drops had done their work. My aunt’s complexion had returned to itself.

“No, no, my dear,” she said. “Go with our friends, and enjoy yourself.”

Rachel stooped and kissed her. As she came to the door, another change passed over her—she was in tears. I was moved by the momentary softening and, thinking to speak earnestly, allowed my compassion to show.

“What do you mean by pitying me?” she whispered bitterly as she passed. “Don’t you see how happy I am? I’m going to the flower-show, Clack; and I’ve got the prettiest bonnet in London.”

She blew me a kiss—hollow mockery—and was gone.

I cannot properly express the pity I felt for her; words, like money, are not plentiful with me. My heart bled for this misguided girl.

When I turned back, Mr. Godfrey was searching, softly and carefully, in different parts of the room. Before I could offer assistance, he had found what he wanted. He came to us with the declaration in one hand and a box of matches in the other.

“Dear aunt, a little conspiracy!” he said. “Dear Miss Clack, a pious fraud which even your high moral rectitude will excuse! Will you leave Rachel to suppose that I accept the generous self-sacrifice which has signed this paper? And will you kindly bear witness that I destroy it in your presence, before I leave the house?”

He struck a match; the sulphur scent rose sharp for an instant; and he set the paper alight, laying it to burn in a plate upon the table. The thin flame ate greedily through the inked lines, curling them into blackened lace.

“Any trifling inconvenience that I may suffer is as nothing,” he said, “compared with the importance of preserving that pure name from the contaminating contact of the world. There! We have reduced it to a little harmless heap of ashes; and our dear impulsive Rachel will never know what we have done! How do you feel? My precious friends, how do you feel? For my poor part, I am as light-hearted as a boy!”

He beamed on us, holding out a hand to my aunt and a hand to me. I was too affected to speak. In a kind of spiritual self-forgetfulness I lifted his hand to my lips. He murmured a soft remonstrance. For a moment I was lost—lifted, as it were, out of the room and out of myself.

When I opened my eyes again, it was like coming down from a high place to the ordinary floor. There was nobody but my aunt. Mr. Godfrey had gone.

I should like to stop here; I should like to let my narrative end at the ashes in the plate. But the unrelenting pressure of Mr. Franklin Blake’s cheque forces me onward; there was more to be disclosed in that Tuesday visit to Montagu Square.

Left alone with Lady Verinder, I turned naturally to the subject of her health—touching, as delicately as I could, on the strange anxiety she had shown to hide her indisposition and the remedy from her daughter’s observation.

Her reply surprised me.

“Drusilla,” she said—and if I have not mentioned that my Christian name is Drusilla, I mention it now—“you are touching—quite innocently, I know—on a very distressing subject.”

I rose at once; delicacy offered me only one course: apologies, and departure. She stopped me and insisted I sit again.

“You have surprised a secret,” she said, “which I had confided to my sister, Mrs. Ablewhite, and to my lawyer, Mr. Bruff, and to no one else. I can trust in their discretion; and I am sure, when I tell you the circumstances, I can trust in yours. Have you any pressing engagement, Drusilla? or is your time your own this afternoon?”

My time, of course, was wholly at my aunt’s disposal.

“Keep me company then,” she said, “for another hour. I have something to tell you which I believe you will be sorry to hear. And I shall have a service to ask of you afterwards, if you don’t object to assist me.”

So far from objecting, I was eager.

“You can wait here,” she went on, “till Mr. Bruff comes at five. And you can be one of the witnesses, Drusilla, when I sign my Will.”

Her Will. I thought of the little phial in the work-box; of the bluish tinge on her face; of the haste, the concealment, the drops measured out like a recipe whose bitterness must not be tasted by the young. A light—cold, steady, and not of this world—seemed to fall across my mind from an unmade grave.

My aunt’s secret was a secret no longer.
