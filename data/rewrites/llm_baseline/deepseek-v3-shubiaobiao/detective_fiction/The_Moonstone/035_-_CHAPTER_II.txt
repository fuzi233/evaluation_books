The next matter I must relate concerns the Moonstone—or, more precisely, the continued machinations of those who sought to steal it. Though the details I possess are few, they are of considerable significance, for they cast a shadow over events yet to unfold.

Some days after Miss Verinder’s departure, my clerk interrupted my work with a visitor’s card. The name upon it was foreign—now lost to memory—but beneath it, in clear English, was written:

The audacity of a man like Luker daring to recommend anyone to *me* was so staggering that, for a moment, I could only stare in silence. My clerk, noting my bewilderment, offered his own observations on the stranger below.

"An unusual sort of gentleman, sir. So dark he might be taken for an Indian."

The mention of an Indian, coupled with Luker’s name, immediately stirred suspicion. Could this be connected to the Moonstone? Setting aside professional caution, I instructed my clerk to admit him at once.

Let it be understood—no man in England has been more entangled in the Diamond’s history than I. I knew Herncastle’s fear of assassination, received his letters confirming his survival, drafted his will bequeathing the cursed stone, persuaded his executor to act, and even overcame Franklin Blake’s reluctance to deliver it to Lady Verinder. If any man had a right to curiosity about the Moonstone, it was I.

The moment my visitor entered, I knew him. Not by name, but by bearing. Though dressed impeccably as an Englishman, his complexion, his fluid grace, and the quiet precision of his movements betrayed his true origin. One of the three Indians—likely their leader.

He offered a courteous apology for intruding, his English flawless, then produced a small parcel wrapped in cloth of gold. Unfolding it, he revealed a second layer of silk before placing upon my desk an exquisite casket—ebony inlaid with jewels.

"I seek a loan," he said, "and leave this as surety of repayment."

I indicated his card. "You come at Mr. Luker’s suggestion?"

A bow confirmed it.

"And why does Luker not lend the money himself?"

"Mr. Luker informed me he had none to spare."

"So he sent you to me?"

The Indian merely touched the card. "It is written there."

Succinct. Efficient. Had I possessed the Moonstone, I’ve no doubt this man would have slit my throat without hesitation. Yet for all that, he was the model of an ideal client. He valued my time—a courtesy no Englishman had ever shown me.

I declined his request. Strangers and unusual collateral fell outside my professional practice.

To my surprise, he did not press further. No protest, no persuasion. Only another bow before carefully rewrapping his casket. Then, as he turned to leave, he paused.

"Might I ask one final question?"

One question! Most men would have plagued me with fifty.

"Had you lent me the money," he said, "what period would custom allow for repayment?"

"One year is the usual term."

His final bow was the deepest of all. Then, like a shadow, he was gone—swift, silent, unnervingly feline.

Alone again, I turned the encounter over in my mind. His control had been absolute—face, voice, manner all impenetrable. Yet one crack had appeared. Only when I spoke of the repayment period had his gaze locked onto mine. That question, then, had been his true purpose. The casket, the loan—mere formalities.

Before I could unravel the meaning, a letter arrived—from Luker himself. Groveling, he begged an audience, promising explanations. Against my better judgment, I agreed.

The Indian had come to him first. Though disguised, Luker recognized him instantly as the chief of the three who had once loitered near his home, forcing him to seek a magistrate’s protection. Terror convinced Luker he faced one of the men who had once blindfolded, gagged, and robbed him.

The Indian, playing the stranger, made the same request—a loan, with the casket as surety. Luker, desperate to be rid of him, claimed poverty. When asked whom he might approach instead, Luker, in his panic, named the first solicitor that came to mind: me.

"I was sweating like a drowned rat, sir," he moaned. "Hardly knew what I was saying!"

I dismissed him, but not before one final question: Had the Indian said anything notable upon leaving?

Indeed he had. The same question he had put to me—*how long before repayment?*

What it meant, I could not yet fathom. Luker’s account offered no clarity, and my own thoughts circled uselessly. That evening, as I prepared for a dinner engagement, I little suspected that the path to my dressing-room would also lead me toward revelation.
