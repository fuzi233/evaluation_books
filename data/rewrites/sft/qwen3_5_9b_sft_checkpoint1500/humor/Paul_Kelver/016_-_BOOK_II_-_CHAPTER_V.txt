How on a sweet grey morning the future came to Paul.

Over our supper, Dan and I swapped stories. They revealed a strange kinship. He had left school long before I had, gone to Cambridge, but fate intervened. Two years later, his father died, and a wound from the Indian Mutiny, never fully healed, forced him to abandon his college career.

"You might not expect that to grieve me," Dan said with a smile, "but it was a terrible blow. At Cambridge, I realized I was a scholar by temperament. At school, learning was deliberately made boring. Like tourists rushed through a gallery, we were hurried past education just so we could claim we'd seen it. At college, I chose my own subjects, studied them my own way. I fed on knowledge, not stuffed like a Strasbourg goose."

Back in London, he took a job in a bank, where the chairman was an old friend of his father. The perk was earning a small income while studying, but the deadly monotony horrified him. After his mother died, he shook the cloying dust of the City from his brain and joined a small theatrical troupe. On stage, he lasted another eighteen months, playing everything from Romeo to Paul Pry, helping paint sets and posting bills. He found the latter particularly tricky; the paste-laden posters had an innate habit of recoiling onto the amateur's head. Tired of the stage, he joined a circus, posing as "Signor Ricardo" and performing trapeze acts with the Brothers Roscius. Seeking respectability again, he worked as a waiter in Ostend, then a footman.

"One never knows," Dan remarked. "I might become a society novelist; inside knowledge of the aristocracy would give me an edge."

He had tried many other paths: tramping Ireland with a fiddle, lecturing on Palestine in Scotland with dissolving views, marking billiards, teaching school. For the last three months, he had been a journalist, a dramatic and musical critic for a Sunday paper. I often dreamed of such a life.

"How did you get it?" I asked.

"The idea hit me late one afternoon," Dan replied. "Sauntering down the Strand, wondering what to do next, I was penniless but lucky. I walked into the first office I saw, climbed the stairs, and opened the first door without knocking. I passed through a small, empty room into a larger one, littered with books and papers. It was growing dark. A very young gentleman was running around, cursing himself because he'd spilled ink, couldn't find matches, and had broken the bell-pull. In the gloom, I assumed he was the office boy. It seemed fun to mistake him for the editor. As it turned out, he *was* the editor—a slim young man with the voice and manner of a schoolboy, likely not more than twenty-six, owing his position to an aunt who owned the paper. He seemed laboring under a general sense of injury.

"'People come into this office,'" he complained, "'looking for shelter from the rain—folks I don't know from Adam. That damn fool downstairs lets them march right up—anyone, men writing about safety valves, folks just there to cause trouble. Half my work is on the stairs.'"

I suggested he insist strangers write their business on a slip of paper. He liked the idea.

"For the last three-quarters of an hour," he said, "I've been trying to finish this column, and I've been interrupted four times."

Just then, another knock came.

"I won't see him!" he cried. "I don't care who he is; I won't see him. Send him away!"

I went to the door. An elderly gentleman tried to sweep past, but I barred his way, closing the editorial door behind him. Surprised, he seemed ready to leave, but I told him the editor was unavailable and handed him a sheet of paper to write his business on. I stayed in the ante-room for half an hour, sending away about a dozen people. Their business hardly seemed important, or I would have heard of it later. The last visitor was a tired-looking gentleman smoking a cigarette. When I asked his name, he looked at me in surprise and answered, "Idiot!"

I remained firm, refusing to let him pass.

"It's a bit awkward," he retorted. "Don't you think you could make an exception for the sub-editor on press night?"

"No," I replied. "Contrary to my instructions."

"Oh, all right," he sighed. "I'd just like to know who's going to the Royalty tonight. It's seven o'clock already."

An idea sparked in me. If the sub-editor didn't know who to send to a theater, the dramatic critic's post must be vacant.

"Oh, that's all right," I told him. "I'll be in time."

He seemed neither pleased nor displeased. "Have you arranged with the Guv'nor?" he asked.

"Just waiting to see him again for a few minutes," I returned. "It'll be all right. Got the ticket?"

"Haven't seen it," he replied.

"About a column?" I suggested.

"Three-quarters," he preferred, and departed.

The moment he was gone, I slipped downstairs and met a printer's boy coming up.

"What's the name of your sub?" I asked. "Tall man, black moustache, looks tired."

"Oh, you mean Penton," the boy explained.

"That's the name," I answered. "Couldn't think of it."

I walked straight back into the editor, who was still irritable. "What is it? What is it now?" he snapped.

"I only want the ticket for the Royalty Theatre," I answered. "Penton says you've got it."

"I don't know where it is," he growled.

After a brief search, I found it on his desk.

"Who's going?" he asked.

"I am," I said. And I went.

They never discovered I had appointed myself. Penton thinks I am some relation of the proprietress, so everyone treats me with marked respect. Mrs. Wallace, the owner, believes I am Penton's discovery, in whose judgment she places great trust, and with her, I get along admirably. The paper isn't doing terribly well, and the salary is small, but sufficient. Journalism suits my temperament, and I daresay I'll stay with it."

"You've been somewhat of a rolling stone hitherto," I commented.

He laughed. "From the stone's point of view," he answered, "I never could see the advantage of being smothered in moss. I'd always prefer remaining the stone, unhidden, able to move and see about me."

"Now, to speak of other matters," he continued, "what are your plans for the immediate future? Your opera, thanks to the gentlemen the gods have dubbed 'Goggles,' will, I fancy, run through the winter. Are you getting any salary?"

"Thirty shillings a week," I explained, "with full salary for matinees."

"Say two pounds," he replied. "With my three, we could set up an establishment of our own. I have an idea that is original. Shall we work it out together?"

I assured him with fervor that nothing would please me better.

"There are four delightful rooms in Queen's Square," he continued. "Charming furnishings: a fine sitting-room in the front, with two bedrooms and a kitchen behind. Their last tenant was a Polish Revolutionary, who, three months ago, foolishly ventured back to Russia and is now living rent-free. The landlord is an original old fellow, Deleglise the engraver. He occupies the rest of the house himself. He's told me I can have the rooms for anything I like to offer, and I'd suggest thirty shillings a week, though under normal circumstances, they'd be worth three or four pounds. But he'll only let us have them on the understanding that we 'do for' ourselves. He's quite an oddity. He hates petticoats, especially elderly ones. He has one servant, an old Frenchwoman, who, I believe, was housekeeper to his mother, and he and she do the housework together, most of their time quarreling over it. Nothing else of the domestic female genus will he allow inside the door—not even an occasional charwoman. On the other hand, it's a beautiful old Georgian house, with Adams mantelpieces, a stone staircase, and oak-paneled rooms; and our portion would be the entire second floor: no pianos and no landlady. He's a widower with one child, a girl of about fourteen or maybe a little older. Now, what do you say? I'm a very fair cook; will you be house-and-parlour-maid?"

I needed no pressing. A week later, we were installed there, and for nearly two years, we lived there. At the risk of offending an adorable but somewhat touchy sex, convinced that man, left to himself, is capable of little more than putting himself to bed in a rough-and-ready fashion, truth compels me to record that without female assistance or supervision of any kind, we passed through those two years and still exist to tell the tale. Dan had not idly boasted. Better plain cooking I never want to taste; so good a cup of coffee, omelette, or devilled kidney I rarely have tasted.
