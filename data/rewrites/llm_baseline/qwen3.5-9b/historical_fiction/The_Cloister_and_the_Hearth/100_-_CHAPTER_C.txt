In obedience to a custom I detest, yet lack the courage to defy, I pause here to gather the scattered fragments of humanity I have introduced along the way. Let it be understood that these minor figures hold little personal claim upon me; they serve merely to convince you that Gerard traversed the distance from Holland to Rome among real people, rather than walking through a parade of lifeless dolls.

Eli and Catherine lived to a remarkable age. They survived so long that both Gerard and Margaret eventually faded into dim memories. Giles too was long-lived; he traveled to the court of Bavaria and remained alive there at ninety, though he had somehow shrunk into nothing more than leather and bone, his voice reduced to a hollow trumpet tone. Cornelis, unchallenged by rivals and long forgiven by his mother—who clung to him with increasing intensity as her children dispersed—waited endlessly for his parents' deaths. But Catherine's sharp prediction proved true: before she and her partner wore themselves out, this worthy man rusted away. At sixty-five, lying dying of old age in his mother's embrace—a hale woman of eighty-six—he regained consciousness in his final moments. Seeing her beside him, he spoke of transforming the shop and its premises once they were his own. "Yes, my darling," the poor old woman soothed gently. Within another minute, he was clay, and that clay was followed to the grave by every footstep he had ever waited for.

Denys, shattered by his comrade's death, returned to Burgundy. There, a modest pension granted by the court sustained him until, unexpectedly, he inherited a substantial sum from a distant relative. For many years, he was known in his homeland as a crusty old soldier, capable of recounting tales of war when he pleased, yet always a bitter critic of women. Jerome, disgusted by the laxity of the north, retired to Italy. Through high connections, he became a mitred abbot at the age of seventy. He imposed strict discipline upon his monks, whom he simultaneously revered and loathed. He ruled with an iron rod for ten years. Then, one night, he died alone, for he had never learned the path to a single human heart. The Vulgate lay upon his pillow, the crucifix in his hand, and upon his lips rested something resembling a smile—not seen during his entire life. Thus, I believe, in that terrible hour, he was not entirely alone. *Requiescat in pace.* The Master He serves has many servants, each with many minds; occasionally, a faithful one proves to be a surly one, just as it often happens within our mortal dwellings.

The golden-haired lad, Gerard Gerardson, does not belong to fiction but to history. She has recorded his birth in terms far different from mine. Above the tailor's house in the Brede Kirk Street, she has inscribed: *"Haec est parva domus natus qua magnus Erasmus,"* and she has penned half a dozen biographies of him. Yet, there remains much for her still to accomplish. She understands the great Erasmus no better than any other dwarf comprehends a giant, or a partisan judges a magistrate. As the first scholar and divine of his era, he was also the heaven-born dramatist of his century. Many of the finest scenes in this new volume originate from his medieval pen, illuminating the pages where they appear. For the words of such a towering genius are not born to die. Once their immediate impact upon mankind is fulfilled, they may seem to lie dormant; yet, with every fresh shower of intelligence that Time pours upon their students, they demonstrate their immortal lineage. They revive, rising from the dust of great libraries; they bud, flower, bear fruit, and sow seeds, passing from generation to generation, from age to age.
