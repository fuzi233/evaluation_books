Where is the woman who cannot feign a role? Where is she who refuses to play the part, yet plays it so well that she saves the man she adores? In moments of supreme crisis, nature itself seems to intervene on behalf of the humblest souls, teaching them to cast dust into the eyes of Solomon. The moment the men had withdrawn, Margaret rose from her bed. Clad in her nightgown, skirt, petticoats, and stockings, she dragged the heavy chest—upon which she had been reclining—to the side of the room and propped its lid firmly against the wall. With silent, gliding steps, she moved to the threshold and pressed her ear to the wood. Through the silence of the house, the fading echoes of footsteps traveled past her father's chamber and descended the stairs until nothing remained but a hushed void.

Within that chest lay a secret, a peculiarity so subtle that even a stranger might easily miss it. Some years prior, when the floorboards of the room had been damaged, Gerard, lacking proper timber, had devised an ingenious solution. He had cut away a precise section of the flooring, just large enough to accommodate what appeared to be a small, shallow box, and used those planks to repair the surrounding area. To the untrained eye, the room looked sound; the "bed" seemed merely a low, flat container resting directly upon the floor. Yet, in truth, the chest sat suspended beneath the original joists, two feet deep, concealed by the illusion of a mere foot of depth.

Absolute silence reigned. Margaret knelt briefly, offering a prayer of gratitude to Heaven for the men's departure. Rising again, she approached the chest and leaned over the lid, whispering softly, "Gerard!"

There was no response.

Encouraged by the lack of movement, she increased her volume slightly. "Gerard, all is safe, thank God! You may rise now—but beware!"

Silence answered her.

Desperate, she placed her hand upon his shoulder. "Gerard!"

Still, no sound.

"Oh, what is wrong?" she cried, panic seizing her throat. Her hands flew wildly across his face and bosom, searching for any sign of life. She gripped him by the shoulders, shaking him violently, lifting him up. But he slipped from her trembling grasp, falling back not with the weight of a living man, but with the terrible, final heaviness of a corpse. A cold dread, sharper than ice, froze her blood. The lid had been lowered. She had lain upon him. The intruders must have been in the room for some time already. With the frenzied strength of madness, she tore him from the chest, bearing him in her arms to the window. She flung the casement wide, and the cool, sweet night air rushed in, mingling with the silver glow of the moon. She laid him upon the sill, bathed in pale light. His face was the color of ashes, his limbs utterly limp and motionless. Reaching for his heart, she found it still as stone. Horror! A horror beyond measure! She had suffocated him with her own body.

Human minds cannot instantly accept such a sudden, catastrophic, and absurd tragedy. Gerard, who had barely crawled into that chest five minutes prior, how could he be dead?

She called upon every endearing name her heart could conjure, every phrase her tongue could form. She kissed him, caressed him, coaxed him, imploring him to speak.

Yet, no answer returned to the words of love she had never dared utter before, nor believed herself capable of speaking. Trembling from head to toe, the poor girl began to mutter foolish, terrible, and pitiable things over his ashen countenance.

"Oh, Gerard! I am so sorry you are dead. I am so sorry I have killed you. Forgive me for not letting the men take you; it would have been kinder than this. Oh, Gerard! I am so, so sorry for what I have done."

Suddenly, her voice broke into a ravaging frenzy.

"No! No! This cannot be! If this is true, there is no God! It is monstrous! How can my Gerard be dead? How could I have killed my Gerard? I love him! Oh, God, you know how I love him! He does not know. I never told him. If he truly knew the depths of my heart, he would speak to me, he would not remain so deaf to his poor Margaret. Is it all a trick to make me scream and betray him? But no! I love him too deeply for that deception. I will strangle myself rather than yield." And she clutched her own throat, stifling the wild urge to shriek in her terror and agony.

"If he would but say one word... Oh, Gerard! Do not die without a single word. Have mercy on me and scold me, but speak to me! If you are angry, curse me! I deserve it! I am the fool who slew the man she loved more than her own life. Ah, I am a murderer. The worst criminal in all the world. Help! Help! I have murdered him. Ah! Ah! Ah! Ah! Ah!"

She tore at her hair, emitting shrieks so wild and piercing that they rang like a funeral knell through the house. They reached the ears of Dierich Brower and his men, who were lingering nearby. Startled from their vigil, they leaped to their feet, eyes wide with alarm, gazing at one another as the sounds of wailing and despair echoed into the night.
