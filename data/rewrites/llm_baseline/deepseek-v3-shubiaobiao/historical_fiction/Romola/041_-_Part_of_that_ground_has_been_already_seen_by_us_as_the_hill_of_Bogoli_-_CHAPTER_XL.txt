An Arresting Voice**

Romola sat beneath the cypress, the weight of her flight pressing upon her. The morning air was still, the mountains somber in the distance, and for the first time, she felt utterly alone—no walls, no streets, no law but the vast indifference of earth and sky. There was freedom in it, and terror.

Then the voice came, quiet and certain.

"You are Romola de’ Bardi, the wife of Tito Melema."

She knew it at once—that voice which had stirred her before, like a summons she could not ignore. She did not turn. A monk’s interference, nothing more. Yet her hands trembled.

"You flee Florence in disguise. God commands me to stop you. You are not permitted to leave."

Anger flared. "By what right do you speak to me? What right to hinder me?"

"The right of a messenger. You wear the garb of devotion without its purpose. It was revealed to me who you are—that you seek to escape the lot appointed to you. You would hide your name, your place, and choose anew, as if life were yours to reshape at will. But you are called back. Return to your duty."

Her defiance hardened. "I will not. Priests have no power over me."

"It is not the monk who commands you," he said, "but truth itself. And truth is a chain you cannot break. Obey, and it will guide you. Refuse, and it will drag behind you forever."

She stood then, facing him at last—Fra Girolamo, his features austere, his gaze unwavering. She had meant to challenge him, but the words died on her lips. There was no anger in his eyes, only a quiet intensity, as if he saw not just her, but the struggle within her.

"You claim freedom," he said, "but who is more a slave than the debtor who denies his debt?"

A flicker of shame crossed her face.

"You turn from your vows—as a Florentine, as a wife. You abandon your city in its need, as if you were a bird with no ties to the earth. But duties are not chosen, Romola. They are given, like the blood in your veins. You owe more than your own grief."

She clutched the crucifix beneath her mantle, its edges sharp against her palm.

"Conform your life to this," he said, pointing to it. "Make your sorrow an offering. The world groans under injustice, and you say, *I will go where none demand of me*? But every bond is a debt, and the right lies in its payment."

Her resistance wavered. "If I stay—what then? My love is gone. Must I live a lie?"

"Marriage is not for pleasure alone," he answered. "It is a vow, sacred even when the heart grows cold. If your husband were a criminal, your place would be beside him in his cell. But he is not. Then your place is where your vows have bound you."

The conflict tore at her. To return was to embrace pain, to stay was to deny the very fiber of duty. Yet his words rang with a conviction deeper than her doubt.

"Florence needs you," he said, his voice low now, fervent. "Not as a wanderer, but as a daughter. The work of God begins here, in the streets you would abandon. Will you leave your people to their sorrow while you seek your own escape?"

She closed her eyes. The image rose before her—not of Tito, not of her own anguish, but of the city, alive with suffering and hope.

At last, she whispered, "Father... I will go back."

Her knees bent of their own will. Savonarola lifted his hands over her, and in the silence, something within her—some fierce, rebellious self—bowed as well.
