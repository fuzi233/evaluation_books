It nettled Oleron a little that Miss Bengough should dismiss, with one broad glance and no more, the rooms he had found so oddly, so dangerously engaging. She scarcely lifted her eyes to the pale walls, the high mouldings, the way the light lay—like sifted flour—along the boards. Yet she had always been like that: indifferent to the small courtesies of beauty, careless of appearances, more honestly herself with a paper bag of biscuits on her knee than at any table laid with ceremony.

Elsie Bengough was thirty-four, an unattached journalist, large and showy, fair as churned butter, pink as a hedgerow rose, as if she had been cut fresh from a florist’s display and set—too boldly—in the world. She moved in sudden, ample sweeps, leaving little whirlwinds behind her, and she spoke with a moist explosiveness that made the very air seem to slap and ring. She “pulled a better living out of the pool” than Oleron did; and by certain cunningly disguised puffs of drapers and haberdashers she “pulled” as well the better part of her various wardrobe. When she came into a room she brought the outside in with her: a gust of street-air in the folds of her veil, the stale tang of omnibus smoke clinging to cloth, the metallic click of her hatpins like small knives.

He had been a month in the new flat when he heard her on the staircase—the flurry of skirts rising and falling, the quickened rhythm of her steps, and then a single loud knock that made the panels answer.

When he opened the door she was already half inside, a bundle of ladies’ journals under one arm as if she meant to barricade herself, and a paper parcel in the other hand.

“Don’t knock off for me,” she said across a mouthful of those large-headed hatpins as she set to work at her hat and veil. “I didn’t know whether you were straight yet, so I’ve brought some sandwiches for lunch. You’ve got coffee, I suppose?—No, don’t get up—I’ll find the kitchen—”

“Oh, that’s all right, I’ll clear these things away. To tell the truth, I’m rather glad to be interrupted,” Oleron said, and he gathered his papers as though the very act of stacking them were a relief, as if he could pack away with them a certain pressure in his skull.

She was already in the kitchen. He heard the tap run, the kettle filled—sound as intimate as breath in a house so empty it seemed to listen to itself. He joined her and watched her brisk competence: a hand at the matchbox, the rasp and flare, the steadying of the kettle on the flame. She moved as if she had always owned every room she entered, regardless of how little she seemed to notice it.

Ten minutes later he followed her back into the sitting-room with coffee and sandwiches on a tray. The coffee was ordinary enough, ground too long ago and kept too close to other smells; yet as the hot water struck it the room took on that familiar dark bitterness that promises comfort even when it cannot deliver it. The sandwiches, unwrapped, had that slightly compressed look of things made in haste: pale bread, a conservative ration of filling, corners softened by their journey in the paper.

They sat with the tray between them on a small table.

“Well,” Oleron asked, as she poured out the coffee in a generous stream that hissed faintly against the cup, “what do you think of the new place?”

“Hm!… Anybody’d think you were going to get married, Paul.”

He laughed, though the sound came out thin. “Oh no. But it’s an improvement on some of them, isn’t it?”

“Is it? I suppose it is; I don’t know. I liked the last place, in spite of the black ceiling and no watertap. How’s Romilly?”

Oleron thumbed his chin, dry-shaving with two fingers the way he did when he was thinking too hard to keep still. “Hm! I’m rather ashamed to tell you. The fact is, I’ve not got on very well with it. But it will be all right on the night, as you used to say.”

“Stuck?”

“Rather stuck.”

“Got any of it you care to read to me?…”

It had long been his habit to read bits of his work to Elsie now and then. Her comments were quick, practical, sometimes directly useful, sometimes suggestive in a way he could not have explained to her. She, in return for his confidence, kept all mention of her own work carefully from him. His, she said, was “real work”; hers merely filled space, not always even grammatically.

“I’m afraid there isn’t,” Oleron replied, still at his chin, still listening to the soft scrape of his own fingertips on skin, the little domestic sound made loud by the quiet of the rooms.

Then, with a suddenness that surprised even him, he added: “The fact is, Elsie, I’ve not written—not actually written—very much more of it—any more of it, in fact. But, of course, that doesn’t mean I haven’t progressed. I’ve progressed, in one sense, rather alarmingly. I’m now thinking of reconstructing the whole thing.”

Miss Bengough gave a gasp as if he had proposed to throw the coffee on the floor. “Reconstructing!”

“Making Romilly herself a different type of woman. Somehow, I’ve begun to feel that I’m not getting the most out of her. As she stands, I’ve certainly lost interest in her to some extent.”

“But—but—” Miss Bengough protested, “you had her so real, so living, Paul!”

Oleron smiled faintly. He had been prepared for her disapproval. He wasn’t surprised she liked Romilly as she at present existed; she would. Whether she admitted it to herself or not, there was much of Elsie in his fictitious creation. Naturally Romilly would seem “real,” “living,” to her—like a familiar voice overheard in another room.

“But are you really serious, Paul?” she asked at last, staring at him with a round-eyed steadiness over the rim of her cup.

“Quite serious.”

“You’re really going to scrap those fifteen chapters?”

“I didn’t exactly say that.”

“That fine, rich love-scene?”

“I should only do it reluctantly, and for the sake of something I thought better.”

“And that beautiful, beau-tiful description of Romilly on the shore?”

“It wouldn’t necessarily be wasted,” he said, a little uneasily, as if the words had a weight he didn’t want to feel.

Miss Bengough made one of her large windy gestures—an arm cutting the air, bracelets giving a small bright clatter—and then she let him have it.

“Really, you are too trying!” she broke out. “I do wish sometimes you’d remember you’re human, and live in a world! You know I’d be the last to wish you to lower your standard one inch, but it wouldn’t be lowering it to bring it within human comprehension. Oh, you’re sometimes altogether too godlike!… Why, it would be a wicked, criminal waste of your powers to destroy those fifteen chapters! Look at it reasonably, now. You’ve been working for nearly twenty years; you’ve now got what you’ve been working for almost within your grasp; your affairs are at a most critical stage (oh, don’t tell me; I know you’re about at the end of your money); and here you are, deliberately proposing to withdraw a thing that will probably make your name, and to substitute for it something that ten to one nobody on earth will ever want to read—and small blame to them! Really, you try my patience!”

As she talked, Oleron shook his head slowly. It was an old story between them. Elsie—noisy, able, practical—was an admirable friend up to a certain point; beyond that… well, each of us knows that point beyond which we stand alone. She sometimes said that if she had one-tenth part of Oleron’s genius there were few things she could not have done—as if genius were a measurable ingredient, a pinch of spice one could add to a dish to make it palatable. That it was not an additive but a nature, essential and indivisible, informing everything it touched, passed her comprehension. Their spirits parted company there. Oleron knew it. She did not appear to know it.

“Yes, yes, yes,” he said at last, a little wearily, “practically you’re quite right, entirely right, and I haven’t a word to say. If I could only turn Romilly over to you you’d make an enormous success of her. But that can’t be, and I, for my part, am seriously doubting whether she’s worth my while. You know what that means.”

“What does it mean?” she demanded bluntly.

“Well,” he said, smiling wanly, “what does it mean when you’re convinced a thing isn’t worth doing? You simply don’t do it.”

Miss Bengough’s eyes swept the ceiling as if seeking help from the plaster against this impossible man. “What utter rubbish!” she broke out at last. “Why, when I saw you last you were simply oozing Romilly; you were turning her off at the rate of four chapters a week; if you hadn’t moved you’d have had her three-parts done by now. What on earth possessed you to move right in the middle of your most important work?”

Oleron tried to put her off with a recital of inconveniences—the last place too cramped, too grimy, too hungry for daylight—but she wouldn’t have it. Perhaps in her heart she suspected the reason, and feared it. He was mortally weary of narrow circumstances: twenty years of garrets and roof-chambers and dingy flats and shabby lodgings. He was tired of the way rooms could smell of other people’s boiled cabbage and paraffin, tired of ceilings that pressed down as if they had weight, tired of hearing, night after night, the same thin noises through walls that made a man feel he had no skin.

The reward was as far off as ever—or if it was not, he no longer cared as he once would have cared to put out his hand and take it. It is all very well to tell a man at the point of exhaustion that only another effort is required; if he cannot make it, he is as far off as ever.

“Anyway,” Oleron said, summing it up with a forced briskness, “I’m happier here than I’ve been for a long time. That’s some sort of a justification.”

“And doing no work,” Miss Bengough returned pointedly.

At that, a trifling petulance that had been gathering in him came to a head, sharp as grounds between the teeth.

“And why should I do nothing but work?” he demanded. “How much happier am I for it? I don’t say I don’t love my work—when it’s done; but I hate doing it. Sometimes it’s an intolerable burden that I simply long to be rid of. Once in many weeks it has a moment, one moment, of glow and thrill for me; I remember the days when it was all glow and thrill; and now I’m forty-four, and it’s becoming drudgery. Nobody wants it; I’m ceasing to want it myself; and if any ordinary sensible man were to ask me whether I didn’t think I was a fool to go on, I think I should agree that I was.”

Miss Bengough’s comely pink face grew serious, the colour deepening as if the words had struck blood nearer the surface. “But you knew all that, many, many years ago, Paul—and still you chose it,” she said in a low voice.

“Well, and how should I have known?” he demanded. “I didn’t know. I was told so. My heart, if you like, told me so, and I thought I knew. Youth always thinks it knows; then one day it discovers that it is nearly fifty—”

“Forty-four, Paul—”

“—forty-four, then—and it finds that the glamour isn’t in front, but behind. Yes, I knew and chose, if that’s knowing and choosing… but it’s a costly choice we’re called on to make when we’re young!”

Miss Bengough’s eyes dropped to the floor. Without moving them she said, “You’re not regretting it, Paul?”

“Am I not?” he said, catching at her words as if they were something he could shake. “Upon my word, I’ve lately thought I am! What do I get in return for it all?”

“You know what you get,” she replied.

He might have known, from her tone, what else he could have had for the holding up of a finger—herself. She knew, and could not tell him, that he could have done no better thing for himself. Had he, any time these ten years, asked her to marry him, she would have replied quietly, “Very well; when?” He had never thought of it; or if he had, the thought had passed like a sound in the street, unheard because another sound filled his head.

“Yours is the real work,” she continued quietly. “Without you we jackals couldn’t exist. You and a few like you hold everything upon your shoulders.”

For a minute there was silence. In it, the house seemed to settle and breathe. There were small noises that in other places would be swallowed—some distant creak in the building’s bones, the faint tick of a cooling kettle, the soft shifting of fabric as Elsie adjusted in her chair. Oleron became aware of how different these sounds were from the old districts where carts rattled, pipes hammered, and cheap music ground its way through walls. Here the quiet was not empty; it was attentive. It put weight on words, and made even a pause feel like something spoken in a language you only half understood.

Then it occurred to Oleron that this had been common vulgar grumbling, and it was not his habit. Abruptly he rose and began to stack cups and plates on the tray as if he could tidy the mood away.

“Sorry you catch me like this, Elsie,” he said, with a little laugh that tried to be light. “No, I’ll take them out; then we’ll go for a walk, if you like….”

He carried out the tray and came back with his manner smoothed. To turn her thoughts—and his—he began to show her round the flat.

She made few comments. She moved through the rooms with those sudden ample motions, scarves fluttering, veil-tails stirring. The place, which had seemed to Oleron to have a singular grace, took on, under her indifference, a kind of stern plainness. Only the silence clung to them both, and the way their footsteps made different notes in different corners, as if the floorboards kept their own memory of pressure and release.

In the kitchen she stopped short and pointed.

“What’s that?” she asked, nodding at an old faded square of reddish frieze that Mrs. Barrett used as a cushion for her wooden chair.

“That? I should be glad if you could tell me what it is,” Oleron answered. He unfolded it from the bag he had kept it in and told her again how he had found it crammed into the window-seat, as if someone had shoved it away in haste and forgotten it.

Miss Bengough took it between finger and thumb, rubbing the worn fabric as though testing its history by touch. The stuff had a smell of dust long shut from air, and of something faintly sweet and stale beneath that—like old varnish, or dried flowers pressed in a book.

“I think I know what it is,” she said. “It’s been used to wrap up a harp before putting it into its case.”

“By Jove, that’s probably just what it was,” Oleron said, honestly relieved. “I could make neither head nor tail of it….”

They finished the tour and returned to the sitting-room.

“And who lives in the rest of the house?” Miss Bengough asked, glancing—not up at the cornice or the pictures, but sideways, as if listening for the answer in the building itself.

“I dare say a tramp sleeps in the cellar occasionally. Nobody else.”

“Hm!…” She set her mouth a little, thinking. Then: “Well, I’ll tell you what I think about it, if you like.”

“I should like.”

“You’ll never work here.”

“Oh?” Oleron said quickly. “Why not?”

“You’ll never finish Romilly here. Why, I don’t know, but you won’t. I know it. You’ll have to leave before you get on with that book.”

He mused for a moment, his gaze going to the quiet corner where the light lay, and then back to her. “Isn’t that a little—prejudiced, Elsie?”

“Perfectly ridiculous. As an argument it hasn’t a leg to stand on. But there it is,” she replied, her mouth once more full of the large-headed hatpins, which clicked softly as she gathered them like weapons.

Oleron reached down his hat and coat. He laughed, but the sound didn’t quite break the stillness; it merely made it sharper.

“I can only hope you’re entirely wrong,” he said, “for I shall be in a serious mess if Romilly isn’t out in the autumn.”
