"If anyone should press me to give a reason why I loved him, I feel it could be expressed in no other way than by answering, 'Because it was he, because it was I.' There exists, beyond what I can articulate, some inexplicable power that drew us together." — *Montaigne, On Friendship*

The moment had arrived to prepare Mordecai for the revelation of his restored sister and the impending change of residence, a necessity before Mirah's reunion with her brother. Mrs. Meyrick, to whom Daniel Deronda had entrusted every detail save Mordecai's unique bond with himself, had energetically assisted him in securing a suitable lodging in Brompton. It was located mere minutes' walk from her own home, ensuring that the siblings would remain within easy reach of her maternal care. Her delightful blend of Scottish fervor and Gaelic vivacity had successfully guarded the secret from the younger girls and from Hans; any accidental breach to them risked reaching Mirah prematurely, thereby spoiling the delicate opening of the endeavor designed to secure her independence—a form of dependence, however arduous and dignified, that society often labels "independence."

Both Mrs. Meyrick and Deronda possessed deeper, unspoken reasons for insisting that Mirah prove capable of maintaining herself. Perhaps the "little mother" was aided in her secrecy by a certain hesitation regarding the remarkable brother described to her. Certainly, if she felt any joy or anticipatory admiration, it stemmed solely from her faith in Deronda's judgment. The reality of consumption was a sorrowful fact that appealed to her tenderness, yet how could she truly celebrate an enthusiasm she could only view as Jewish pertinacity? Furthermore, she feared the introduction of a man whose conversation lacked the modern encouragement she valued, resembling instead the rigid discourse of Scott's Covenanters. Her mind was far from purely prosaic; she retained a share of Mab's delight in the romance of Mirah's story and her new domestic arrangement. Yet, the romantic or unusual elements of real life require adaptation. We stay awake at night to read of Sakya-Mouni, St. Francis, or Oliver Cromwell; but whether we would welcome someone exactly like them calling on us the next morning, or revealing themselves as a new relative, is a vastly different matter.

Moreover, Mrs. Meyrick had secretly hoped that the intensity of Mirah's feelings regarding Judaism would gradually subside, merging into the deepening current of loving exchange with her new friends. In truth, her favorite continuation of the romance had not involved the discovery of Jewish relations, but rather something more favorable to the hopes she harbored for Hans. Now, however, here stood a brother who would plunge Mirah's mind once again into the deepest dye of Jewish sentiment. She could not help telling Deronda,

"I am as glad as you are that the pawnbroker is not her brother. There are Ezras and Ezras in the world; indeed, it is comforting to think that not all Jews are like those shopkeepers who simply won't let you leave their premises. Besides, what he said to you about his mother and sister makes me bless him. I am sure he is good. But I have never liked anything fanatical. I suppose I heard too much preaching in my youth and lost my palate for it."

"I do not believe you will find that Mordecai imposes any preaching," Deronda replied. "He is not what I would call fanatical. I reserve the term 'fanatic' for a man whose enthusiasm is narrow and blinded, lacking a sense of proportion, becoming unjust and unsympathetic to those outside his own circle. Mordecai is an enthusiast; I use that word for the highest order of minds—those who care supremely for grand and universal benefits to humanity. He is not a strictly orthodox Jew and is full of allowances for others; his conformity in many matters is itself an allowance for the condition of other Jews. The people he lives with adore him, yet they cannot comprehend his ideas."

"Oh, well, I can live up to the level of the pawnbroker's mother and like him for what I perceive as good in him; for what I do not see, I will take your word. By your definition, one might argue that worshipping common-sense is fanatical; my poor husband used to say the world would be a poor place if it contained nothing but common-sense. However, Mirah's brother will have excellent bedding—I have seen to that—and I shall paste up this extra window to prevent drafts." (The conversation took place in the destined lodging.) "It is comforting to know that the householders are no strangers to me—no hypocritical harpies. Once the children know, we shall be able to make the rooms much prettier."

"The next stage of the affair is to tell everything to Mordecai and arrange his move, which may prove more difficult," Deronda noted.

"And will you tell Mirah before I say anything to the children?" Mrs. Meyrick asked. Deronda hesitated, and she continued in a tone of persuasive deliberation, "No, I think not. Let me tell Hans and the girls the evening before, and they will be gone the next morning?"

"Yes, that is best. But please do justice to my account of Mordecai—or Ezra, as I suppose Mirah will wish to call him. Do not assist their imaginations by referring to Habakkuk Mucklewrath," Deronda smiled, noting that Mrs. Meyrick herself had previously compared the Covenanters to the situation.

"Trust me, trust me," said the little mother. "I shall have to persuade them so hard to be glad that I shall convert myself. When I am frightened, I find it beneficial to have someone to be angry with for not being brave; it warms the blood."

Deronda might have been more argumentative regarding the view to be taken of Mirah's brother had he not been anxiously preoccupied with a more immediate and important task, one he wished to accomplish without wounding the Cohens. Mordecai, through a memorable response, had made it clear that he would be acutely sensitive to any inadvertence concerning their feelings. In the interim, he had met Mordecai at the *Hand and Banner*, but after careful reflection, he wrote to invite him to his own home the following evening. He requested to sit with Mordecai in his workroom for an hour, provided the Cohens did not consider it an intrusion. He phrased the invitation so that if there were any objection, Mordecai would accompany him elsewhere. Deronda hoped this approach would create a modest expectation, serving as a preparatory measure.

He was received with customary friendliness, accompanied by additional costumes worn by the women and children, and by a slight air of wonder among the elders—even Cohen himself could not allow it to pass the bounds of silence. The guest's dealings with Mordecai remained a mystery, one that Cohen was rather proud to believe lay outside the sphere of light enclosing his own understanding. However, when Deronda remarked, "I suppose Mordecai is at home and expecting me," Jacob, who had absorbed the family remarks, climbed onto his knee and asked, "What do you want to talk to Mordecai about?"

"Something very interesting to him," Deronda replied, pinching the boy's ear, "but that you cannot understand."

"Can you say this?" Jacob demanded, immediately reciting a string of rote-learned Hebrew verses with a wonderful mixture of throaty and nasal tones, nodding his small head at his listener with a sense of offering formidable evidence that might alter their mutual standing.

"No, really," Deronda answered gravely, "I cannot say anything like that."

"I thought not," Jacob declared, performing a dance of triumph with his small scarlet legs. He produced various objects from the deep pockets of his knickerbockers and returned them, signaling his resources, before running to the door of the workroom. He opened it wide, leaned his back against it, and announced, "Mordecai, here's the young swell"—a copy of his father's phrase, which he deemed fitting to cap his recitation of Hebrew.

He was recalled with hushed warnings by his mother and grandmother. Deronda entered, closing the door behind him, to find that a piece of carpet had been laid down, a chair placed, and the fire and lights tended to—all signs of the Cohens' respect. As Mordecai rose to greet him, Deronda was struck by the air of solemn expectation on his face, a demeanor that would have seemed entirely natural if his letter had declared a revelation regarding the lost sister. Neither spoke until Deronda, with his usual tenderness, drew the vacant chair from the opposite side of the hearth and seated himself near Mordecai.

"You are coming to tell me something that my soul longs for," Mordecai said in a tone of fervid certainty.

"It is true I have something very weighty to tell you—something I trust you will rejoice in," Deronda replied, guarding against the possibility that Mordecai had prepared himself for something entirely different.

"It is all revealed—it is made clear to you," Mordecai said more eagerly, leaning forward with clasped hands. "You are even as my brother that sucked the breasts of my mother—the heritage is yours—there is no doubt to divide us."

"I have learned nothing new about myself," Deronda stated. The disappointment was inevitable; it was better not to strain the feeling in a mistaken hope.

Mordecai sank back in his chair, momentarily unable to care what was actually coming. All day his mind had been in a state of tension toward one fulfillment. The reaction was sickening, and he closed his eyes.
