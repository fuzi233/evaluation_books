I arose the following morning before dawn, completing my embroidery guard while kneeling on the dormitory floor beside the central stand, illuminated only by the dying flicker of the night-lamp. Every bead and spool of silk I owned had been exhausted before the chain achieved the length and opulence I desired. Knowing that to appeal to a specific taste required an undeniable visual impact, I had worked the piece double-thick. To finish it, I needed a gold clasp; luckily, I salvaged one from the fastener of my only necklace. I detached it, secured it to the guard, and then coiled the finished masterpiece into a small box purchased specifically for its brilliance. Crafted from a tropical shell of "nacre" hue and adorned with a tiny circlet of sparkling blue stones, the box served as its own casing. Inside the lid, I carefully incised certain initials with the tip of my scissors.

You may recall the description of Madame Beck's annual festival, where a generous gift was traditionally subscribed and presented by the school. This observance was a privilege reserved exclusively for Madame, and in a diminished capacity, for her relative and advisor, Mr. Emanuel. In his case, the honor was bestowed spontaneously, not premeditated, serving as further testament to the esteem in which the professor of literature was held by his students—despite his known partialities, prejudices, and irritabilities. Unlike others, no valuable item was offered him; he had explicitly stated he would accept neither silver nor jewelry. Yet, he appreciated a modest tribute. The monetary value mattered little to him; a diamond ring or gold snuffbox presented with pomp would have pleased him less than a simple flower or a sketch offered with genuine sentiment. Such was his nature. Though not a man of profound wisdom in his era, he possessed a filial sympathy toward "the dayspring on high."

Mr. Paul's festival fell on the first of March, a Thursday. It was a fine, sunny day, coinciding with the morning Mass service and the half-holiday that allowed for afternoon walks, shopping, or visits. These factors combined to induce a general crispness and freshness in dress. Clean collars were fashionable, and the usual drab woolen *classe* dresses were swapped for lighter, brighter fabrics. Even Mademoiselle Zélie St. Pierre, on this particular Thursday, donned a "silk robe"—considered in the frugal household of Labassecour an article of hazardous splendor. Indeed, it was noted that she summoned a hairdresser that morning; astute pupils discovered she had perfumed her handkerchiefs and hands with a new, trendy scent. Poor Zélie! She frequently declared around this time that she was weary of her secluded, laborious existence, longing for the means and leisure to relax, to have someone work for her—a husband who would settle her crushing debts, provide her wardrobe, and grant her the freedom to "taste a little of pleasure." Rumors had long circulated that her eye was fixed on Mr. Emanuel. And certainly, Mr. Emanuel's eye was often fixed upon her. He would sit and watch her persistently for minutes at a time. I have witnessed him staring at her for a quarter-hour while the class composed silently, seated regally on his platform, seemingly unoccupied. Always conscious of this basilisk-like attention, she would writhe beneath it, half-flattered, half-confused. Mr. Paul would follow her reactions, sometimes appearing terrifyingly acute. For in some instances, he possessed a terrible, unerring instinctual penetration, piercing through the hiding places of the heart to discern the bare, barren landscapes of the spirit beneath florid veils. Yes, he perceived perverted tendencies and hidden false curves—all that people would not admit: the twisted spine, the malformed limb born with them, and far worse, the stains or disfigurements they inflicted upon themselves. No calamity so cursed could escape Mr. Emanuel's pity and forgiveness if acknowledged candidly; but where his questioning eyes met dishonest denial, where his ruthless investigations uncovered deceitful concealment—then, oh, he could be cruel. I thought him wicked! He would triumphantly snatch the screen from poor, shrinking souls, passionately hurrying them to the summit of exposure, displaying them all naked, all false—poor living lies, the spawn of that horrible Truth which cannot be viewed unveiled. He believed he was dispensing justice; for my part, I doubt if man has the right to mete out such justice to another. More than once during these visitations, I felt compelled to shed tears for his victims and direct sharp reproach toward himself. He deserved it, yet it was difficult to shake his firm conviction that his work was righteous and necessary.

After breakfast and Mass, the school bell rang and the rooms filled. A very pretty spectacle unfolded in the classroom. Pupils and teachers sat neatly attired, orderly and expectant, each holding a bouquet of felicitations—the finest spring flowers, fresh and fragrant. I alone had no bouquet. While I enjoy seeing flowers grow, once gathered, they cease to please me. They seem rootless and perishable; their resemblance to life saddens me. I never offer flowers to those I love, nor do I wish to receive them from hands dear to mine. Mademoiselle St. Pierre noticed my empty hands; she could not believe I had been so negligent. Her eye roved over and around me with avidity: surely I must possess some solitary symbolic flower somewhere, some small cluster of violets to win praise for taste and commendation for ingenuity. The unimaginative "Englishwoman" proved superior to the Parisian's fears: I sat literally unprovided, as bare of bloom or leaf as a winter tree. Once this was confirmed, Zélie smiled, thoroughly pleased.

"How wisely you have acted to keep your money, Miss Lucie," she said proudly. "How silly I have been to throw away two francs on a bouquet of hot-house flowers!"

She displayed her magnificent nosegay with evident satisfaction.

But hush! A step: *the* step. It arrived promptly, as usual, yet with a promptitude that made us feel inclined to flatter ourselves, inspired by feelings deeper than mere nervous excitability or vehemence of intent. We thought our Professor's "footfall" (speaking romantically) carried a friendly promise this morning; and so it did.

He entered in a mood that made him akin to a new sunbeam in the already well-lit first class. Morning light dancing among our plants and laughing on our walls caught an added luster from Mr. Paul's all-benign greeting. Like a true Frenchman (though I do not know why I should say so, for he was of neither French nor Labassecourian strain), he had dressed for the occasion. Not obscured by the vague, sinister folds of his soot-dark *paletot*, his figure (such as it was, I do not boast of it) was well highlighted by a civilized coat and a silken vest quite pleasant to behold. The defiant, pagan *bonnet grec* had vanished; bare-headed, he approached us, carrying a Christian hat in his gloved hand. The little man looked excellent, very well. There was a clarity of friendship in his blue eyes and a glow of good feeling on his dark complexion that perfectly substituted for conventional beauty. One did not mind observing that his nose, though not small, lacked a particular shape, his cheek was thin, his brow marked and square, his mouth not a rosebud; one accepted him as he was and felt his presence was anything but dampening or insignificant.

He moved to his desk, placing his hat and gloves upon it. "Bonjour, mes amies," he said in a tone that somehow compensated for many previous sharp snaps and savage snarls. It was not a jocund, good-natured tone, nor an unctuous priestly accent, but a voice belonging solely to himself—a voice used when his heart conveyed the words to his lips. That same heart did speak occasionally; though irritable, it was not an ossified organ. In its core lay a place tender beyond a man's tenderness, a place that humbled him before little children and bound him to girls and women to whom, rebellious as he might be, he could not disown his affinity, nor entirely deny that, on the whole, he was better with them than with his own sex.

"We all wish Monsieur a good day and present to him our congratulations on the anniversary of his fête," announced Mademoiselle Zélie, acting as spokesperson for the assembly. Advancing with no more twists of affectation than were indispensable for her movements, she laid her costly bouquet before him. He bowed over it.

The long procession of offerings followed. All the pupils, moving with the gliding step characteristic of foreigners, left their tributes as they passed. Each girl so skillfully adjusted her separate gift that when the final bouquet was placed on the desk, it formed the apex of a blooming pyramid—a structure spreading, towering, and overflowing with such exuberance that it eventually eclipsed the hero standing behind it. Once this ceremony concluded, seats were resumed, and we sat in dead silence, awaiting a speech.

I suppose five minutes might have elapsed, and the hush remained unbroken; ten minutes passed, and there was still no sound.

Many present undoubtedly began to wonder what Mr. Paul was waiting for; as well they might. Voiceless, invisible, still, and wordless, he maintained his position behind the mountain of flowers.

Finally, a voice issued forth, rather deep, as if speaking from a hollow:—"Est-ce là tout?" ("Is that all?")
