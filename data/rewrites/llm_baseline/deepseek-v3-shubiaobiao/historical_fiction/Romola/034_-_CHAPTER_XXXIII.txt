Baldassarre wandered through Florence, searching for a place to rest his weary bones—some out-of-the-way corner where he might lay his head without cost. His steps led him to the one uneven stretch within the city walls, a slope where the ground rose above the rooftops, offering a rare glimpse beyond the crowded streets to the distant hills and the valley cradling the Arno.

This was the hill of San Giorgio, a quiet place of gardens and rough, untamed patches where landslides had long ago left scars of tumbled stone. Among the scattered dwellings, he spotted a peculiar little building, half tower, half shed, its roof tiled in fluted curves. Nearby stood a small outhouse, wedged against a crumbling wall, its door gaping open to reveal straw and rusted tools.

An old woman, wiry and sun-browned, was untethering a goat beneath a half-dead mulberry tree. Baldassarre approached, raising his voice above the wind.

"I seek shelter," he said. "A place to sleep. I can pay."

The woman squinted at him, her ears slow to catch his words. "No lodgings," she muttered, shaking her head.

But Baldassarre was persistent. He spoke of distant lands, of his poverty, of the little he could offer—a quattrino or two for the straw in her outhouse. At last, a cunning gleam lit her eyes. She fetched a hatchet and pointed to a stubborn log half-buried in the corner.

"Chop that," she said, "and you may stay the night."

He agreed, and as the blade bit into the wood, Monna Lisa watched, lips curled in satisfaction.

*Cheaper than hiring a man,* she thought. *And if he’s quiet, who’s to know?*

Above, unseen, a pair of bright blue eyes peered through a shutter’s knothole. Tessa, restless and curious, had been drawn by the stranger’s voice. Now she watched as the old man worked, his strong arms splitting the wood with ease. A plan formed in her mind—a small rebellion, a secret kindness.

When dusk settled, she slipped from the house, a bowl of steaming macaroni in one hand, a lantern in the other. She nudged the outhouse door with her foot.

Baldassarre looked up, startled, as light spilled across the straw.

"I brought you supper," Tessa announced, her voice loud as if he, too, were deaf like Monna Lisa. She plopped down beside him, grinning. "Eat. I’ll stay awhile."

He ate in silence, and she studied him—his sunken eyes, his rough hands.

"You’re not from here," she observed. "I live with Monna Lisa. She’s old, but kind. And I have a baby—a beautiful baby. You’ll see tomorrow."

Baldassarre said little, but her chatter was a warmth against the cold of his thoughts. When she left, promising to return, he sat in the dark, the ghost of a forgotten smile touching his lips.

The next morning, Tessa was true to her word. She brought bread, broth, and—most proudly—her swaddled infant.

"Look," she said, placing the bundle in his arms. "Isn’t he perfect?"

Baldassarre gazed at the child, something stirring in his chest. Not joy, not sorrow, but a quiet, protective ache.

"You mustn’t be sad," Tessa told him, reclaiming her baby. "We’ll take care of you."

He did not answer, but as she skipped away, he whispered to the empty air:

"Poor thing."

And whether he meant the child, the girl, or himself, even he could not say.
