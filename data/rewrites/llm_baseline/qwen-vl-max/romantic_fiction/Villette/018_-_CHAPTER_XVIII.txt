In the early days of my stay at the Terrace, Dr. John Graham never sat near me, nor did he come close when pacing the room. He moved with a quiet gravity, his expression thoughtful, but I felt certain he was thinking of Miss Fanshawe—of her name, her face, her presence. I waited, listening, ready to respond with tenderness and understanding whenever he might speak of her. My patience was like a soldier on guard, ever alert; my sympathy, a well-stocked fountain, prepared to flow freely at the slightest sign of need.

Then, after a brief inner hesitation—one I could see in his eyes, and which I respected—he finally brought up the subject. It came gently, almost anonymously. “I hear your friend is traveling for her vacation?” he said.

“Friend, indeed!” I thought, though I kept it to myself. To contradict him would have been unkind. He had to believe what he wished; so I nodded and let the word stand. But still, curious, I asked, “Whom do you mean?”

He had taken a seat beside my worktable. Now, he picked up a spool of thread and began unwinding it carelessly, as if distracted. “Ginevra—Miss Fanshawe,” he said. “She’s gone with the Cholmondeleys on a tour through southern France.”

“She has,” I replied.

“Do you two correspond?”

It surprised me that he should assume such intimacy between us. “You’ll be astonished,” I said, “to learn that I’ve never once thought to ask for that privilege.”

“You’ve seen letters from her?”

“Yes—several addressed to her uncle.”

“They must be full of wit and charm,” he said. “She has such sparkle, so little pretense in her soul.”

“She writes quite plainly enough when she writes to M. de Bassompierre,” I replied. “Anyone can read them.” (Indeed, Ginevra’s letters to her wealthy cousin were often nothing more than formal requests for money.)

“And her handwriting?” he asked. “Must be delicate, elegant, feminine?”

It was—so I told him.

“I truly believe everything she does is done well,” he said.

I hesitated. He pressed further: “And you, who know her—can you name any flaw in her?”

“She does many things very well,” I answered.

(Flirtation among them, I added silently.)

“When do you think she’ll return to town?” he asked soon after.

“Pardon me, Dr. John,” I said, “but I must clarify. You honor me by assuming a closeness with Miss Fanshawe that I don’t possess. I’ve never known her plans or secrets. Her true friends are elsewhere—among the Cholmondeleys, for example.”

He looked startled—as if I’d been wounded by jealousy, just as he was. “Please forgive her,” he said. “Judge her kindly. The dazzle of society may mislead her, but she’ll soon see these people are empty. She’ll return to you, stronger in trust, deeper in affection. I know the Cholmondeleys—they’re shallow, showy, selfish. But Ginevra values you more than a hundred such.”

“You are kind,” I said simply.

A sharp retort burned on my lips—I wanted to deny that I was her confidante, that I was hurt by her absence—but I held back. I allowed myself to be seen as the discarded, pining companion of the beloved Miss Fanshawe. But reader, it was not easy. It was painful.

Yet he went on: “Still, while I comfort you, I cannot comfort myself. I cannot hope she will do me justice. De Hamal is worthless, yet I fear he pleases her. A terrible illusion!”

That was too much. My patience snapped—without warning. Illness and fatigue had weakened me, made me brittle. “Dr. Bretton,” I burst out, “there is no delusion like your own. In every way but one, you are honest, clear-headed, sound-minded. But on this one point—you are enslaved. I say plainly: where Miss Fanshawe is concerned, you deserve no respect—and I give you none.”

I rose and left the room, my heart pounding.

The scene had happened in the morning. By evening, I had to face him again. And then I saw I had done harm.

Dr. John was not made of ordinary flesh. His nature was broad and strong, yet delicately wrought—finer, far finer, than most men’s. His sensitivity was hidden, unless something sharp touched it and revealed its depth. He didn’t wear his feelings openly; he wasn’t quick to perceive others’ emotions, but he *felt* deeply. He was generous, kind—always ready to help, always willing to listen. But expect subtlety, intuition, and you’d be disappointed.

That night, when he entered the room and the lamp lit his face, I saw him clearly—his whole being laid bare. To be called “slave” and stripped of respect—how must he feel? He didn’t argue against it. Instead, he quietly considered whether it might be true. He searched for answers in that accusation, wondering if it explained his failure, his unrest. He seemed distant, perhaps cold, to both me and his mother. Yet there was no bitterness, no malice. Only sorrow—a man’s quiet dignity even in pain.

When I hurried to place his chair and hand him his tea with trembling hands, he said, “Thank you, Lucy,” in a tone as warm and pleasant as ever.

For my part, I knew only one course: I must make amends—or I could not sleep. I couldn’t bear the tension. Convent silence, solitary study—anything seemed better than living in conflict with him.

As for Ginevra—she could fly on silver wings to the highest stars, to wherever her lover’s imagination placed her. I would no longer dispute the sky she occupied.

I tried long and hard to catch his gaze. Once, twice, our eyes met—then he looked away. There was nothing to say, so I remained silent.

After tea, he sat quietly, reading. I wished I could go sit beside him—but feared he’d react with anger. I longed to speak, but dared not whisper.

His mother left the room. Then, overwhelmed by regret, I murmured, “Dr. Bretton.”

He looked up. His eyes were not cold. His mouth was not cruel. He was open, willing to hear. His spirit was too gentle to sour in one moment.

“Dr. Bretton,” I said, “forgive my hasty words. Please, please forgive me.”

He smiled.

“Perhaps I deserved them, Lucy. If you don’t respect me, it’s because I’m not worthy. I’m an awkward fool—perhaps I fail in some way. Where I wish to please, I seem not to.”

“You can’t be sure of that,” I said. “Even if you do, is it your fault—or another’s blindness? But now, let me take back what I said in anger. In every way, I deeply respect you. If you think too little of yourself and too much of others—that is not weakness. That is nobility.”

“Can I think too much of Ginevra?”

“I believe you may,” I said. “You believe you cannot. Let us agree to differ. Just forgive me—that is all I ask.”

“Do you think I hold ill-will for one warm word?”

“I see you don’t—and can’t. But say it: ‘Lucy, I forgive you.’ Say it—so I can lay down my pain.”

“Put aside your pain,” he said, “as I put mine. You wounded me a little, Lucy. But now the pain is gone. I don’t just forgive—I’m grateful. As if you were a true friend.”

“I *am* your friend,” I said. “You are right.”

So ended our quarrel.

Reader, if later in this story you find my view of Dr. John changing, please forgive the shift. I write as I felt then—my perceptions as they appeared at the time. And he showed his depth by being kinder to me afterward than before.

Yes, the misunderstanding altered our relationship—though not as I feared. Something invisible, thin as frost, had always separated us—a fragile barrier of reserve. Those few angry words, however, melted it. From that day forward, he no longer stood on ceremony with me. He spoke freely, honestly, about himself, about what mattered most. And I found myself eager to listen.

Of course, I heard much about Ginevra.

“Ginevra!” he would say, his voice soft with admiration. “So fair, so good—her sweetness, her innocence!” And though I knew the truth—the plain, practical facts—I could not help feeling a glow around her image, reflected from his devotion.

Still, I confess—he often spoke nonsense. But I learned to be patient. I had been taught a lesson: crossing him, grieving him, displeasing him—each caused me deep pain.

In a strange new way, I became selfish. I gave myself entirely to indulging his moods, bending to his will.

And yet—when he stubbornly doubted his chances with Ginevra, I found myself believing more strongly than ever that she was only teasing him, playing games to keep him longing. At heart, I believed she cherished every glance, every word he offered.

Sometimes, despite my resolve, he tested me. In the midst of my quiet endurance, he struck a spark from my remaining firmness—and fire flashed.

One day, trying to calm him, I said, “In my mind, I’m certain Miss Fanshawe *must* eventually accept you.”

“Certain?” he asked. “Easy to say—but what grounds do you have?”

“The best.”

“Now, Lucy, tell me—what?”

“You know them as well as I. And knowing them, Dr. John, I’m amazed you don’t trust her fidelity. To doubt now is almost to insult.”

“Now you’re speaking fast,” he said, “and breathing short. Go on—faster, shorter—until you explain fully. I must have it.”

“You shall,” I said. “You’re generous—lavish, even. You’d give Père Silas alms, light his tapers, enrich his shrine. Ginevra, Dr. John—”

“Hush,” he said.

“No,” I insisted. “I will not. I *will* go on. Ginevra has received gifts from your hands more times than I can count. You’ve sought the rarest flowers, devised the most delicate presents—gifts so fine, only a woman could imagine them. And she owns a set of ornaments—purchased with generosity verging on extravagance.”

His face flushed—modesty, though he’d never shown it himself.

“Nonsense,” he said, cutting a silk thread with my scissors. “I gave them to please myself. I felt honored she accepted.”

“She did more than honor you,” I said. “She pledged her word—to repay you. And if she cannot pay in love, she owes you gold.”

“But you don’t understand her,” he said. “She’s too disinterested to care for gifts. Too simple to know their worth.”

I laughed. I knew how she valued each jewel. Money, schemes, scarcity—these had shaped her thoughts for years.

He continued. “Whenever I gave her something—no excitement, no joy. Just quiet acceptance. She’d leave a bouquet beside her, forget it. Or if I fastened a bracelet on her arm—however lovely it was—she wouldn’t look at it. Never dazzled.”

“So she returned it?”

“No. Too kind for that. She’d pretend to forget, keep it quietly, without thought.”

“In that case,” I said, “how can you build hope on such acceptance?”

“If I gave her everything,” he said, “and she took it—still, I’d hesitate. Her nature is beyond material sway.”

“Love is blind,” I began.

But then—his eye flickered. A blue, subtle ray—like a memory, like a secret—passed sideways. It reminded me of old days, of his portrait. For a moment, I wondered: was his belief in her naïveté genuine—or was it a mask?

Was he, perhaps, seeing her more clearly than he admitted?

It was only a glance—maybe imagined. But it silenced me. And so, the conversation ended.
