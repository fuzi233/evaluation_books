To truly grasp the architecture of Margaret's recovery, one must first understand the absolute fragility of her universe at that precise moment. She lay prostrate, her head resting upon Catherine's arm, having been invaded by the honest, rough-hewn members of her family who stood around her like a phalanx of warriors, shouting words of crude encouragement. Among them roared Giles, a man of immense physical presence, declaring with booming certainty that Gerard was alive and well, citing the letter itself as proof—a document he proclaimed to be the largest mankind had ever seen, and furthermore, the most beautiful, moving, and smallest written thing in history.

"Ay, good Master Giles," Margaret sighed, her voice a feeble thread against the roar of the room. "He was alive. But how can I know what hath since befallen him? Oh, why did he leave Holland to wander among strangers fierce as lions? And why did I not drive him from me sooner, rather than part him from his own flesh and blood? Forgive me, you who are his mother!"

With trembling hands, she gently dislodged Catherine's arm and made a valiant, if futile, attempt to slide from the chair to her knees. Superior forces, however, conspired against her gravity-defying ambition; after a brief struggle, she found herself once again ensconced upon Catherine's bosom, a sanctuary of softness amidst the chaos. There, she extended the letter toward Eli, speaking faintly yet with a sweetness that cut through the tension. "I will trust it from my hand now. In truth, I am little fit to read any more—and loath to leave my comfort;" and she wound her other arm tightly around Catherine's neck, seeking anchor in the living.

"Read thou, Richart," commanded Eli, his voice cutting through the domestic drama. "Thine eyes be younger than mine."

Richart accepted the parchment. "Well," he remarked, scanning the script with a critical eye. "Such writing I have never seen. It is penned with a needle's point; and clear to boot. Why is he not in my counting-house at Amsterdam instead of vagabonding it out yonder!"

"When I came to myself," Richart began, his voice dropping into the cadence of the traveler, "I was seated in the litter, and my good merchant holding fast to my hand. I babbled nonsense, and then shuddered in silence for a while. He put a horn of wine to my lips."

"Bless him! Bless him!" cried Catherine, her relief palpable.

"Whisht!" Eli hushed her sharply.

"And I told him what had befallen. He examined my leg. It was sprained sore, swollen at the ankle; and all my joints were broken, as I could scarce keep up my hose. I said, 'Sir, I shall be but a burden to you, I doubt, and can make you no harmony now; my poor psaltery it is broken;' and I grieved over my broken music, the companion of so many weary leagues. But he patted me on the cheek, bidding me not fret; also, he laid my leg upon a pillow and tended me like a kind father."

"January 19," Richart continued, reading aloud. "I sit all day in the litter, for we push forward with haste, and at night the good, kind merchant sends me to bed, refusing to let me work. Strange! Whenever I fall in with men like fiends, the next moment God still sends me some good man or woman, lest I should turn away from humankind. Oh, Margaret! How strangely mixed they are, and how old I feel compared to what I was three months ago. And lo! If good Master Fugger has not already bought me a psaltery."

"Eli, my man," Catherine interrupted, her eyes gleaming with commercial acumen, "if that merchant comes our way, let us buy a hundred ells of cloth from him, and not haggle."

"That will I, take your oath on't!" Eli declared immediately.

As Richart prepared to continue, Kate glanced at her mother, a faint blush coloring her cheeks as she drew a piece of work from under her apron. With her head bowed slightly lower than necessity demanded, she resumed sewing. Her mother, observing this silent communion, produced a similar specimen from her pocket, and both women stitched in tandem while Richart read. These surreptitious creations, newly exposed to observation, were babies' caps, more than half finished, telling a silent tale of devotion. Horror! They resembled little monks' cowls in their shape and delicacy.

"January 20," Richart intoned. "Laid up in the litter, and as good as blind, but halting to bait, the Lombardy plains burst upon me. Oh, Margaret! A land flowing with milk and honey; all sloping plains, goodly rivers, jocund meadows, delectable orchards, and blooming gardens; and though winter, it looks warmer than poor beloved Holland at midsummer, making the wanderer's face shine and his heart leap for joy to see earth so kind and smiling. Here be vines, cedars, olives, and cattle plenty, but three goats to a sheep. The draft oxen wear white linen on their necks, and standing by dark green olive-trees, each one is a picture; and the folk, especially women, wear delicate straw hats adorned with flowers and leaves fairly imitated in silk, mixed with silver. This day we crossed a river prettily in a chained ferry-boat. On either bank was a windlass, and a single man, by turning it, drew our whole company to his shore, whereat I did admire, being a stranger. Passed over with us some country folk. And an old woman, looking at a young wench, hid her face with her hand and held her crucifix out like a knight holds his sword in tournament, dreading the evil eye."

"January 25," Richart paused, shifting the tone. "Safe at Venice. A place whose strange and passing beauty is well known to thee by report of our mariners. Dost mind too how Peter would oft fill our ears withal, we handed beneath the table, and he still discoursing of this sea-enthroned and peerless city, in shape a bow, and its great canal and palaces on piles, and its watery ways plied by scores of gilded boats; and that market-place of nations, *orbis*, non *urbis*, Forum, St. Mark, his place? And his statue with the peerless jewels in his eyes, and the lion at his gate? But I, lying at my window in pain, may see none of these beauties as yet, but only a street, fairly paved, which is dull, and houses with oiled paper and linen, in lieu of glass, which is rude; and the passers-by, their habits and their gestures, wherein they are superfluous. Therefore, not to miss my daily comfort of whispering to thee, I will e'en turn mine eyes inward, and bind my sheaves of wisdom reaped by travel. For I love thee so, that no treasure pleases me not shared with thee; and what treasure so good and enduring as knowledge? This then have I, Sir Footsore, learned, that each nation hath its proper wisdom, and its proper folly; and methinks, could a great king, or duke, tramp like me, and see with his own eyes, he might pick the flowers, and eschew the weeds of nations, and go home and set his own folk on Wisdom's hill. The Germans in the north were churlish, but frank and honest; in the south, kindly and honest too. Their general blot is drunkenness, the which they carry even to mislike and contempt of sober men. They say commonly, 'Kanstu niecht sauffen und fressen so kanstu kienem hern wol dienen.' In England, the vulgar sort drink as deep, but the worshipful hold excess in this a reproach, and drink a health or two for courtesy, not gluttony, and still sugar the wine. In their cups the Germans use little mirth or discourse, but ply the business sadly, crying 'Seyte frolich!' The best of their drunken sport is 'Kurlemurlehuff,' a way of drinking with touching deftly of the glass, the beard, the table, in due turn, intermixed with whistlings and snappings of the finger so curiously ordered as 'tis a labour of Hercules, but to the beholder right pleasant and mirthful. Their topers, by advice of German leeches, sleep with pebbles in their mouths. For, as of a boiling pot the lid must be set ajar, so with these fleshy wine-pots, to vent the heat of their inward parts: spite of which many die suddenly from drink; but 'tis a matter of religion to slur it, and gloze it, and charge some innocent disease therewith. Yet 'tis more a custom than very nature, for their women come among the tipplers, and do but stand a moment, and as it were, kiss the wine-cup; and are indeed most temperate in eating and drinking, and of all women, modest and virtuous, and true spouses and friends to their mates; far before our Holland lasses, that being maids, put the question to the men, and being wived, do lord it over them.
