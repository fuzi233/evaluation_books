Old Whateley moved with a terrifying, grinding persistence, buying cattle not to expand his herd, but perhaps to feed something else, and cutting timber with the same relentless rhythm. He turned his hand to repairing the vast, peaked-roofed ruin of his home, where the rear wall vanished into the jagged rock of the hillside. For years, the three remaining ground-floor rooms had sufficed for him and his daughter, Lavinia, but now he sought to reclaim the dead weight of the upper stories. It required a prodigious, unnatural reserve of strength to haul lumber and mortar across the decaying floors, yet his movements, though occasionally interrupted by moments of babbling madness, possessed a cold, mathematical precision. The restoration had truly begun the moment Wilbur drew breath, evidenced by the sudden, frantic organization of a toolshed—clapboards nailed tight, locks forged strong. Now, applying the same meticulous care to the forgotten attic, he transformed it into a fortress.

His obsession manifested most clearly in the windows of the reclaimed space, which he boarded over with iron-clad determination until not a sliver of light could enter, despite the local gossip claiming such labor was pointless folly. Downstairs, however, he prepared a sanctuary for his grandson, a chamber visible to the curious eyes of callers but barred to entry by heavy doors. Inside, Whateley erected towering shelves of rough-hewn wood, lining them with the rotting, ancient volumes and fragments of books that had cluttered his mind and walls for decades. With a trembling, reverent hand, he sorted these forbidden tomes, arranging them in an order that suggested a terrible cataloging system.

"I made some use of 'em," the old man would murmur, attempting to glue together a tattered page of black-letter script using paste boiled on a scorching stove, "but the boy... the boy will know how to wield them. He ought to keep them close, safe as he can, for they hold all his knowledge."

By September of 1914, when Wilbur was just a year and seven months old, his development had crossed the boundary from natural to monstrous. He stood as tall as a four-year-old, his intellect flowing with a fluency that unnerved the adults around him. While he roamed freely across the fields, shadowing his mother, he spent hours inside, poring over the cryptic charts and diagrams in his grandfather's library. In those long, silent afternoons, Old Whateley would lecture the child, his voice dropping to a hush that seemed to dampen the air itself. As the house renovation neared completion, observers noted a peculiar alteration: one of the upper windows had been replaced by a solid oak door, reinforced with cleats. A narrow, rickety wooden ramp wound up from the earth to this inaccessible portal, perched high against the eastern gable where the hill pressed close. Simultaneously, the toolhouse, previously secured with iron and glass-sealed since Wilbur's birth, fell into abandonment. When Earl Sawyer ventured inside after selling cattle, he recoiled from the air—a stench so profound and alien it felt less like decay and more like the presence of some non-earthly entity, a气味 reminiscent of the pagan circles whispered about on the hills. Yet, Dunwich was accustomed to the foul, the homes of its folk rarely smelling of cleanliness.

For months, nothing happened visibly, save for a slow, creeping crescendo of sounds emanating from the hill. By May Eve of 1915, the ground shook beneath the feet of the Aylesbury townsfolk. Then, on Halloween, the earth below Sentinel Hill groaned, a subterranean rumble perfectly timed with flashes of unnatural fire bursting from the summit—"the witch Whateleys' work," the locals whispered. Wilbur grew at a pace that defied biology; entering his fourth year, he resembled a boy of ten. Yet, the boy who once chattered endlessly became a creature of silence. A deep, settling taciturnity consumed him, replacing his curiosity with a brooding malice. The local children spoke of the devil's seedling taking root in his goatish features. He began to mutter words that sounded like no tongue on earth, chanting in jagged, bizarre rhythms that froze the blood and filled listeners with a primal, unexplainable dread. Even the dogs, loyal guardians of the valley, turned away from him with snarls of hatred, forcing Wilbur to carry a pistol if he wished to walk the roads without being torn apart.

Inside the house, Lavinia often sat alone on the ground floor, listening to the chaotic symphony of steps and wild cries echoing through the sealed-off second story. She never revealed the nature of the activities upstairs, though once, her face drained of color, she fled when a joking fishmonger attempted to pry open the locked stairwell door. To the idlers in Dunwich Village, the fishmonger claimed he heard horses stampeding on that floor. The villagers, piecing together the clues—the sealed ramp, the missing livestock, the old tales of sacrifices to heathen gods—shivered with a mixture of awe and terror. Soon, the entire Whateley property became a zone of aversion for every dog in the county, hated as fiercely as the boy himself.

Then, in 1917, the Great War arrived. As chairman of the local draft board, Squire Sawyer Whateley found himself paralyzed by a lack of eligible men; the youths of Dunwich seemed physically and mentally degraded. Alarmed by reports of wholesale regional decadence, federal officers and medical experts descended upon the town, conducting an investigation that would later be etched into the annals of New England journalism. It was the scandalous nature of this inquiry that put the spotlight squarely on the Whateleys. Reporters from the *Boston Globe* and the *Arkham Advertiser* swarmed the farm, printing lurid Sunday specials about Wilbur's terrifying precocity, Old Whateley's dealings with black magic, the shelves of arcane texts, the sealed second story, and the eerie, singing hills. At the time, Wilbur was four and a half, looking like a fifteen-year-old. His lips and cheeks were fuzzed with coarse, dark hair, and his voice cracked with a harsh, adult timbre.

Earl Sawyer stood before the throng of cameras and journalists, gesturing toward the house where a faint, sickening odor now drifted down from the sealed upper chambers. "It smells," he declared, his voice grim, "just like I found in that toolshed when we first fixed the house, and like the faint, wrong smells I catch near the stone circles on the mountain." The papers printed their sensational accounts, and the townsfolk of Dunwich read them with a cynical grin, dismissing the obvious errors as mere hysteria. Still, they marveled at the reporters' fixation on one detail: that Old Whateley invariably paid for his cattle in gold coins of extreme antiquity, minted centuries ago. The Whateleys received the press with a cold, distasteful politeness, neither inviting further scrutiny nor daring to offer a violent resistance that might draw unwanted attention from the authorities.
