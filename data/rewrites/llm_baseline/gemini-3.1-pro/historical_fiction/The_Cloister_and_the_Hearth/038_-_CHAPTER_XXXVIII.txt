Outside the town, the morning snow lay pristine save for the chaotic, overlapping script of innumerable wolves. The acoustic landscape of the frost-bound road was a delicate ecology of silence, punctuated only by the rhythmic, percussive crunch of Gerard and Denys’s leather boots—a sound akin to the grinding of coarse sea salt beneath a heavy stone pestle. Gerard moved within a sensory reverie, the frigid air carrying the sharp, pine-resin tang of the deep woods, though his mind lingered on the warm, yeasty aroma of Margaret’s kitchen leagues away. 

“We did well to heed the agrarian elder's counsel, Denys,” Gerard murmured. 

“Ay, did we,” Denys rumbled, his voice a rich, resonant baritone native to the Burgundy soil. “I heard them last night beneath our window, whining for man's flesh—a grim, feral hunger untamed by hearth or spit. But no fat, suet-fed burgher dropped to the market-place to sate them.” 

Gerard offered an abstracted smile, his thoughts turning inward like a slow-simmering reduction. 

“What dost meditate so profoundly?” Denys asked. 

“Thy goodness.” 

Denys bristled, his good humor flashing into sudden heat. “A civil question merits a civil reply, sharply seasoned or no.” 

“Alas, I meant no other.” 

“Then why pretend you were thinking of my goodness, when you know I have no such marrow in my bones?” 

“Had another said this, I had answered, ‘Thou liest.’ But to thee I say, ‘Hast no eye for men's qualities, but only for women's.’ I was thinking on thy goodness of overnight. Wouldst have wedded me to the ‘Tete d'Or’—or rather to the heavily rendered fat of that hostess—and left thyself solitary.” 

“Oh, are ye there, lad?” Denys laughed, his temper cooling as quickly as a dropped ember on the snow. “Well, to speak sooth, I meant that not for goodness, but for true fellowship. Let me tell you, my young master, my conscience pricketh me for letting you turn your back on peaceful days and well-stocked larders. A truer friend had hamstrung thee. Then hadst thou been fain to lie smarting at the ‘Tete d'Or’ while yon skittish lass nursed thee tenderly with bone-rich broths and mulled wines. Blade I had in hand to do’t, but remembering how thou hatest the sting of pain, my craven heart failed me.” 

Gerard raised his eyebrows at this monstrous, yet deeply earnest, revelation of agrarian pragmatism, where an injury might be weighed as a fair trade for the profound security of a well-tended hearth. But before the ethics of severing a comrade’s tendon for the sake of forced domesticity could be explored, an auditory disruption shattered the morning’s quiet. 

Hoofbeats, jarring and arrhythmic, tore through the winter stillness. A rustic rider came galloping, reining his mule so violently that the beast skidded like a heavy oak butchering block across the ice. The rider’s eyes were saucer-wide, his breath pluming in the cold. 

“I trow ye are from the ‘Tete d'Or?’” he panted, his speech heavy with a soil-thick regional vernacular. He demanded his *pourboire*—a transactional exchange of coin for ale, rooted in the peasant’s eternal pursuit of caloric sustenance. Satisfied by the small coin Gerard produced, the rider beamed, wheeled his mule “stomach to earth,” and galloped away. 

Left in his hands was a package, bound with the meticulous care of a foraged truffle wrapped in leaves. Gerard undid the layers—four in total—until he reached the center. Denys caught the innermost parcel as Gerard, hating the affectation, made to toss it into the snow. 

“Stay,” Denys crooned, unfolding a lock of raw silk. “Here is something hard lurking in this soft nest.” 

It was a gold ring, boasting a great amethyst that caught the stark winter light. To Gerard, however, it smelled only of superficial commercialization, an ostentatious trinket devoid of the honest labor of the forge or the earth. Accompanying it was a paper posy, inscribed in an archaic, dying provincial dialect that carried the untranslatable ache of local memory: 

*Gyf the world prove harsh and cold,* 
*Come back to the ‘golden Hedde!’* 
*Wilt not? yet this token keepe* 
*Of hir who doeth thy goeing weepe.* 

Denys purred at the hostess’s audacity, marveling at the capriciousness of women who would bypass a ripe soldier for a boy. He urged Gerard to return, or at least wear the gold. Gerard flatly refused. 

“I wear a ring already,” Gerard said. 

“What, that sorry gimcrack? 'Tis pewter, or tin at best, against this virgin gold.” 

“Ay, but 'twas Margaret gave me this,” Gerard said softly, pressing the base metal to his lips. It carried the faint, metallic tang of true sustenance and shared fires, a spiritual weight that outshone the hollow, glittering amethyst. He kissed the pewter, treating it with the reverence of a master chef evaluating a perfectly aged rind. Reluctantly, to appease his friend, he fastened the amethyst inside his bonnet as a mere talisman. 

He then pleaded with Denys to speak no more of women, for it only starved his heart for Margaret. He wished only to drink the pure, undiluted broth of their friendship before they were forced to part at Remiremont. But Denys swore three consecutive, earthy oaths, vowing to abandon his leave and march with Gerard all the way to Rome. 

Their joy was a sudden, soaring thing, celebrated with hands clasped tight. But the mythic stillness of their communion was brutally severed by a new, oppressive noise pollution. 

Over the brow of the hill crested the standard of Burgundy. A company of fifty mounted soldiers descended, their approach a terrifying acoustic violence. The jarring clank of brass frontlets, the abrasive scrape of polished steel breastplates, and the heavy, militant thud of destriers trampled the delicate sonic harmony of the landscape. 

The Bastard of Burgundy halted the troop with a stentorian roar. His voice was a weapon of the dominant lexicon—sharp, standardized, entirely devoid of the rich, cultural memory of the provincial dialects. 

“How now, arbalestrier, how comes it thy face is turned southward, when every good hand is hurrying northward?” 

Denys answered respectfully in his native tongue, speaking of home, kindred, and the agrarian roots he had not seen in three years. But his intimate, familial words held no power here. 

“Dost bandy words with me?” the commander barked, turning fast to wrath. “Art weary o' thy life? Let go the youth's hand, and into the saddle without more idle words.” 

When Denys merely tightened his grip on Gerard’s hand in silent defiance, the commander’s language turned to lethal, imperial decree. He ordered his archers to dismount and shoot the “white-livered cur,” commanding them to strip Denys naked in the cold, sterile tone of military business. The power dynamic was absolute; the ancestral tongue of the soldier was crushed beneath the monolithic vernacular of the state. 

“Oh, nay! nay! nay!” Gerard screamed, his voice breaking as he awoke from the stupor of tyranny. “He shall go with you! I swear he shall go with you!” 

The commander gave a cold assent. The soldiers moved in. Gerard and Denys shared a haggard, desperate look. In the fleeting seconds left, they tried to speak, but their profound subjective grief was fundamentally untranslatable. 

“Make for the Rhine, Gerard! Strasbourg,” Denys choked out. “Down the current to Rotterdam. Margaret is there... I'll tell her thou art coming.” 

“One moment! One little moment!” Gerard panted. 

“Cursed be the land I was born in! Cursed be the race of man!” Denys screamed, his curse a raw, bleeding wound against the winter air. 

They locked one another in a close embrace, tears raining down their cheeks, tasting of brine and utter loss. Then, with rough, martial efficiency, the soldiers hoisted Denys onto a spare mule and spurred after their leader. 

Gerard ran wildly after them as the lane turned. His lungs burned with the taste of copper and raw frost, his breathing ragged. The last glimpse he caught was Denys rocking to and fro on the mule, tearing his hair out in agony. Gerard’s throat seized. He collapsed against the snow-clad hedge, thorns biting deep into his flesh, though he felt only the agonizing silence left in the wake of the war machine's metallic clamor. 

When he staggered to his feet, all nature seemed to stare back at him, desolate and white. He knelt, gathering a little snow that smelled faintly of ozone and his own blood. “Welcome, kindly foes,” he whispered to the thorns. “Ye are not cruel like men.” 

He dragged his leaden limbs onward, until the silence was shattered again by a gay cavalcade. A nobleman and his favorites swept by in velvet and furs, exuding a superficial, commercialized opulence that masked the honest scents of earth and ice. Gerard watched them pass like shadows in a dream, only to be rudely awakened when three of the armed retainers wheeled back. 

“Stand and deliver!” one cried, their voices dripping with the arrogant, hollow dialect of their masters. 

Gerard drew his sword, his back to the hedge, but the men leveled their weapons, calmly explaining their master had lost money at play and must take from the poor to fund his leman. The transactional emptiness of their speech sickened Gerard. He recognized that to spill his blood here would waste the life he owed to Margaret. 

“Take then the poor man's purse to the rich man's pouch,” Gerard said calmly, tossing them his meager funds. He cursed them in his native tongue, praying the coins would freeze their hearts, before turning away to let them ride off with his only means of sustenance. 

Penniless and friendless, Gerard staggered until he reached the edge of a deep wood. The acoustic world shifted to the deadening, muffled stillness of falling snow. The physical toll of starvation and exposure began to rewrite his senses. He yearned for the mythic comfort of a domestic hearth, for the scent of toasted oats, the crackle of rendering fat, and the rich, lactic sweetness of fresh milk. He thought of creeping into a rich man's barn to steal a handful of grain, just to keep body and soul tethered. 

*Gyf the world prove harsh and cold,* 
*Come back to the hedde of gold.* 

The archaic words of the posy echoed in his fading consciousness, a bittersweet reminder of a cultural memory slowly being extinguished by the cold. “I am a-weary, a-weary,” he whispered. He stretched his limbs onto the snowbank with a deep sigh. The frost smelled of damp bark and endless winter. Death stole on muffled feet, breathing a honeyed breath over his face. 

“Margaret. Never see me more. Poor Margaret.” 

His subjective experience—the taste of her kisses, the warmth of her kitchen—shrank into an untranslatable memory, locked within a body rapidly surrendering its heat. And the constant lover, friend of an antique mold, lay silent on the snow; in peril from the weather, in peril from wild beasts, in peril from starvation, friendless and penniless in a strange land, not halfway to Rome.
