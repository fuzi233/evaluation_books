Full Term had just wrapped at St James’s College, the high table feast thick with port fumes and post-exam chatter, when a man beside the young Ontography Professor leaned over, napkin still tucked at his starched collar.
“I suppose you’ll be escaping off pretty soon now, eh, Professor?”
Parkins was neat as a newly bound lecture manuscript, his speech sharp and unhurried, no wasted syllables. “Yes,” he said. “My friends badgered me into taking up golf this term, you see. I’m heading for the East Coast—Burnstow, to be precise—for a week or ten days, to fix my swing. Plan on leaving tomorrow.”
The man on his other side, a bearded antiquary whose name no one at the table would remember long past the dessert course, perked right up. “Burnstow? You’ll do me a favour, then, won’t you? Go take a look at the Templars’ preceptory site up the coast there, and tell me if it’s worth running a dig there come summer.”
“Of course,” Parkins said at once. “Map out the location for me, and I’ll give you a full lay of the land when I’m back, or drop you a letter if you’d rather.”
“Not necessary, thanks. Only thing is, I’m bringing the family down that way in Long Vacation, and hardly a single English Templar preceptory’s ever been properly surveyed. Figured I could do some useful work on my afternoons off.”
Parkins almost sniffed. Useful? The man had never so much as catalogued a pottery shard properly, far as he knew, but he kept his polite little smile fixed in place.
“Hardly anything shows above ground these days,” the antiquary went on. “The sea’s eaten miles of that stretch of coast, you know. From the maps, it’s three-quarters of a mile north of the Globe Inn, far as I can tell. Where are you staying?”
“As a matter of fact, the Globe itself,” Parkins said. “Couldn’t get a spot anywhere else; all the winter lodging houses are shut up. Trouble is, the only large room they’ve got free is a double-bedded one, and they haven’t a spare corner to stow the extra bed. I need space to spread out my books and do a bit of work while I’m down, so I suppose I’ll just rough it for the week, even if it does feel odd having a whole second empty bed in what’ll double as my study.”
“Roughing it?” boomed bluff Mr Rogers across the table, nudging his neighbour so hard the man’s wine sloshed over the rim. “I’ll come down and occupy that bed for you! Be good company, keep you from getting bored all alone.”
Parkins froze, then forced a polite little laugh. “By all means, Rogers, nothing I’d like better. But I’m afraid you’d find it terribly dull. You don’t play golf, do you?”
“Thank the good Lord, no,” Rogers said.
“See, then? When I’m not writing I’ll be out on the links. You’d be stuck with nothing to do.”
“Nah, there’s bound to be someone I know hanging around. But hey—if you don’t want me there, just say the word. You’re always telling us truth never offends, right?”
Poor Parkins was scrupulously polite, pathologically honest, and Rogers knew it all too well. For a long moment he sat there, chest tight, trying to dig out an answer that wouldn’t come off as rude. “Well, if you insist on exact truth,” he said at last, “I was wondering if the room’s big enough for both of us to be comfortable, and… well, you might prove a bit of a distraction from my work.”
Rogers laughed loud enough to make the candelabra rattle. “Fair play, Parkins! I won’t come bothering you, don’t worry. Only thing is, I thought I’d be doing you a favour, keeping the ghosts off.” He winked so hard his eye almost stayed shut. “Sorry—forgot you hate all that silly ghost talk. No levity on the topic, got it.”
“Since you’ve brought it up,” Parkins said, voice rising a notch, “I will say I do not care for careless chatter about so-called ghosts. A man in my position cannot be seen to sanction that kind of popular superstition, you know full well. I have never hidden my views: even the faintest hint that such things might exist is a betrayal of every principle I hold dear.”
“Right you are, old man,” Rogers muttered under his breath, already half listening to someone else.

Next day, Parkins was on the first train out of Cambridge, and by dusk he was settled into that large double room at the Globe Inn. The space sat at the very edge of the building, three windows facing straight out to the sea—central pane dead on the water, left and right looking north and south down the shingle shore. Sixty yards, no more, separated the inn’s back wall from the tide line. Between them was a strip of rough grass dotted with rusted old anchors and weathered capstans, then a gravel path, then the wide stretch of grey beach. The rest of the inn’s guests were all golfers, save for Colonel Wilson, the retired military man whose voice was loud enough to rattle the windows on a stormy night, and who made no secret of his staunch Protestant disdain for the local vicar’s rather high church rituals.
Parkins spent the whole next day out on the links with the Colonel, and by late afternoon the man’s face was so purple and bristling with unspoken rage over a lost golf ball that Parkins decided the only sensible thing to do was skip walking back with him, let tea and tobacco mellow him out before dinner.
“I’ll take the shore path back,” he thought. “There’s still enough light to hunt down that Templar preceptory site. I can’t miss it, surely.”
He very nearly literally stumbled over it, his foot catching on a gorse root and a half-buried lump of stone, sending him tumbling straight into a patch of uneven ground pocked with low mounds and shallow hollows. When he brushed the turf off his trousers and looked around, every mound was crammed with flint and mortar, grown over with grass. This was the preceptory, no doubt about it. He paced out the clear circular arrangement of the humps, all that was left of the Templars’ round church, jotted dimensions down in his notebook, then wandered over to an oblong rise of turf he figured was the old altar base. At its northern edge, a patch of turf had been ripped away, leaving bare earth. He pulled out his pocket knife to scrape at the soil, to check for masonry underneath—and a whole chunk of earth caved in under the blade, revealing a small, rectangular artificial cavity carved into the old stonework.
He struck match after match, but the north wind snuffed every one out. Tapping around with the knife, he could tell the sides, top, and bottom of the little niche were smoothed almost perfectly flat. He thought it was empty, until he pulled the knife out and heard a sharp metallic clink against the stone. Reaching his hand into the dark cavity, his fingers closed around a small, heavy bronze cylinder. By the time he’d fished it out, the light was almost gone. It was four inches long, pitted with age, clearly very old.
The view as he turned to walk back to the inn was bleak and vast: the pale yellow sunset streaked the west over the links, the squat martello tower, the distant lights of Aldsey village, the pale ribbon of sand cut through at regular intervals by dark wooden groynes, the sea murmuring low as it dragged shingle back and forth. The bitter north wind was at his back, pushing him along. Halfway home, he glanced over his shoulder—and saw a figure, half-obscured by the dusk, running hard behind him, limbs flying, but never seeming to close the gap, not even by a single yard.
Parkins told himself he didn’t know the man, there was no reason to wait for him, but the hair on the back of his neck prickled anyway. He found his mind drifting to that old childhood story he’d read once, of Christian meeting the foul fiend on the desolate path. What would he do, he wondered, if he turned right now and saw a black shape silhouetted against the sunset, horns and wings and all? The figure behind never drew any closer, though, and by the time he’d clomped up the inn steps he’d almost forgotten about it.
Dinner went smooth, the Colonel had mellowed, Parkins won most of their bridge hands, and by midnight he was heading back up to his room, well pleased with the day. The inn’s boots stopped him in the passage.
“Beg pardon, sir,” the man said. “I was brushing your coat earlier, and a little bronze thing fell out your pocket. Sat it on your chest of drawers, sir—bit of an old pipe, I thought. Good night, sir.”
Parkins lit his candles and picked the object up off the chest. It was no pipe. It was a whistle, almost exactly the shape of a modern dog whistle, caked full of fine old sand. He carefully scraped the dirt out onto a scrap of paper, and walked over to the sea-facing window to tip the dust out into the dark night. The sky was bright with stars, the water glinting a faint silver, and on the beach right outside the inn, a lone figure was standing, stock still, staring up at the windows. He shut the casement, frowning at how late some people stayed out walking, then turned back to the whistle.
Scrubbing a little more grime off its surface, he found two lines of deeply carved Latin text. One read FLA FUR BIS FLE. The other was QUI EST ISTE QUI VENIT—Who is this who is coming?
“Bit rusty on my mediaeval Latin,” Parkins muttered to himself. “Well—best way to find out who’s coming, isn’t it? Just whistle him up.”
He blew, soft at first. The note that came out was thin, high, strange, and carried a weight of infinite distance, like it was echoing over miles of empty moor and shore. For a split second he could see it, clear as day: a wide dark expanse of night, a cold fresh wind blowing, and a single lonely figure standing right in the middle of it, doing something he couldn’t quite make out. Then a huge gust of wind slammed hard against the casement, so hard it shook the glass, and the vision shattered. He caught a flash of white seabird wing vanishing into the dark.
He couldn’t help himself, he blew the whistle again, harder this time. No vision came. Instead, a gale roared up out of nowhere, so strong the window fastenings creaked and snapped, and both his candles blew out, plunging the room into total darkness.
Parkins fought the casement shut for almost a full minute, pushing against it like he was straining to hold back a grown man, until the wind slackened all at once, and the window banged hard shut and latched itself. He fumbled for his matches, relit the candles, and saw no damage at all—no broken glass, nothing out of place. Upstairs, he could hear the Colonel thumping around in his socks, growling at the wind.
The gale howled on, rushing past the inn walls, crying so loud and desolate Parkins thought anyone of a fanciful turn would have been absolutely terrified. He couldn’t fall asleep for hours. He lay there, listening to someone in the next room tossing and turning restlessly in their bed, and when he finally shut his eyes to try to drift off, a terrible persistent vision played behind his eyelids, over and over again.
It was that same stretch of shore, dark, spitting thin cold rain. First there was nothing, then a man came running over the shingle, scrambling over groynes, glancing back over his shoulder every two seconds, face white with terror, so exhausted he could barely put one foot in front of the other. He half-fell, half-climbed over the last groyne, and huddled down behind it, shaking, staring back up the beach. Then, far in the distance, a pale fluttering shape came darting over the sand, arms outstretched, weaving back and forth, moving faster than any human could run. It stopped, bowed low to the sand, then darted forward again, straight for the huddled man behind the groyne.
Parkins couldn’t stand it. He sat bolt upright, fumbled for a match to light his candle, determined to stay up reading all night rather than watch that scene play out again. The match scraped, flared, and went out—but the second one caught. He pulled a book from his bag and read until he fell asleep, candle still burning on the table beside him.
When the maid came in at half past eight the next morning, Parkins was already up, lacing his golf shoes, getting ready to head out on the links with the Colonel for the day. She knocked on the door, and stepped inside.
