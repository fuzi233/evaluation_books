The die was cast. Sir Philip Nunnely knew it; Shirley knew it; Mr. Sympson knew it. That evening, when the entire Fieldhead family gathered for dinner at Nunnely Priory, sealed their fate.

Three factors converged to push the baronet toward a decision. First, he noted that Miss Keeldar appeared pensive and delicate, a new phase that struck him on his sentimental side. A spontaneous sonnet brewed in his mind. Before it could fully form, one of his sisters persuaded his lady-love to sit at the piano and sing a ballad—one of Sir Philip's own compositions. It was his least elaborate, least affected work, yet by far the finest of his many efforts.

Just prior, Shirley had gazed from a window down at the park. She had witnessed the stormy moonlight that "Le Professeur Louis" might have contemplated from his own oak-parlour lattice; she had seen the isolated trees of the domain—broad, strong oaks and high-towering beeches—wrestling with the gale. Her ears caught the forest's roar; the swift-rushing clouds and the moon, seeming to hasten even faster, crossed her vision. Turning from sight and sound, she was touched, if not rapt; wakened, if not inspired.

She sang as requested. The ballad spoke of faithful love that refused to abandon its object, love that disaster could not shake, love that grew fonder in calamity and clung closer in poverty. The words were set to a fine old air; simple and sweet in themselves, perhaps lacking force on the page, but requiring nothing when sung well. Shirley sang them beautifully. She breathed softness into the feeling and poured force around the passion. Her voice was fine that evening, dramatically expressive. She impressed everyone and charmed one person in particular.

Upon leaving the instrument, she moved to the fire and sat on a semi-stool, semi-cushion. The ladies surrounded her, saying nothing. The Misses Sympson and the Misses Nunnely regarded her with the same detached curiosity that poultry might show an egret or an ibis. Why did she sing so? They never sang so. Was it proper to sing with such expression, such originality, so unlike a schoolgirl? Decidedly not. Shirley was judged.

Moreover, old Lady Nunnely eyed her stonily from her great chair by the fireside. Her gaze declared, "This woman is not of my kind, nor my daughters'. I object to her as my son's wife."

Her son caught the look and understood its meaning. He grew alarmed. What he wished to win, he feared he might lose. He must act quickly.

The room they occupied had once been a picture gallery. Sir Philip's father, Sir Monckton, had converted it into a saloon, yet it retained a shadowy, withdrawing quality. A deep recess with a window, holding one couch, one table, and a fairy cabinet, formed a room within a room. Two persons standing there could exchange a dialogue, and if it were neither long nor loud, no one would be the wiser.

Sir Philip induced two of his sisters to perform a duet, occupied the Misses Sympson, and let the elder ladies converse. He was pleased to observe that meanwhile, Shirley rose to look at the pictures. He had a tale to tell about one ancestress, whose dark beauty seemed akin to a southern flower. He joined her and began to recount it.

Mementoes of that lady adorned the cabinet in the recess. While Shirley stooped to examine a missal and rosary on the inlaid shelf, and the Misses Nunnely indulged in a prolonged, guiltless screech—pure of originality, perfectly conventional, and absolutely meaningless—Sir Philip stooped too and whispered a few hurried sentences. At first, Miss Keeldar was so still one might have fancied the whisper a charm turning her to stone; but she presently looked up and answered. They parted. Miss Keeldar returned to the fire and resumed her seat. The baronet gazed after her, then went to stand behind his sisters. Only Mr. Sympson had marked the pantomime.

That gentleman drew his own conclusions. Had he been as acute as he was meddling, as profound as he was prying, he might have found correction in Sir Philip's face. But ever shallow, hasty, and positive, he went home quite cock-a-hoop.

He was not a man who kept secrets well. When elated, he could not avoid talking. The next morning, having occasion to employ his son's tutor as secretary, he announced in mouthing accents and with flimsy pomp that he must prepare for a return to the south at an early day. The important business detaining him in Yorkshire was on the eve of fortunate completion. His anxious efforts were likely to be crowned with success. A truly eligible addition was about to be made to the family connections.

"In Sir Philip Nunnely?" Louis Moore conjectured.

Whereupon Mr. Sympson treated himself to a pinch of snuff and a chuckling laugh, checked only by a sudden choke of dignity and an order to the tutor to proceed with business.

For a day or two, Mr. Sympson remained as bland as oil, yet he seemed to sit on pins. His gait emulated a hen treading a hot girdle. He was forever looking out the window and listening for chariot wheels. Bluebeard's wife or Sisera's mother were nothing to him. He waited for the matter to be opened in form, for himself to be consulted, for lawyers to be summoned, for settlement discussions to pompously begin.

At last, a letter arrived. He handed it to Miss Keeldar himself. He knew the handwriting and the crest on the seal. He did not see it opened, for Shirley took it to her own room; nor did he see it answered, for she wrote her reply shut up and took nearly a day. He questioned her if it was answered; she responded, "Yes."

Again he waited, in silence, absolutely daring not to speak, kept mute by something in Shirley's face—a very awful, inscrutable thing, like writing on the wall to Belshazzar. He was moved to call Daniel, in the person of Louis Moore, for interpretation, but his dignity forbade the familiarity. Daniel himself, perhaps, had his own difficulties with that baffling translation; he looked like a student for whom grammars are blank and dictionaries dumb.

Mr. Sympson had gone out to while away an anxious hour at De Walden Hall. He returned sooner than expected. His family and Miss Keeldar were assembled in the oak parlour. Addressing the latter, he requested she step with him into another room for a "strictly private interview."

She rose, asking no questions and professing no surprise.

"Very well, sir," she said, in the tone of a determined person informed that the dentist has come to extract a troublesome tooth. She left her sewing and thimble in the window-seat and followed him.

Shut into the drawing-room, the pair took seats in arm-chairs placed opposite, a few yards between them.

"I have been to De Walden Hall," said Mr. Sympson. He paused. Miss Keeldar's eyes were on the pretty white-and-green carpet. That information required no response. She gave none.

"I have learned," he went on slowly, "a circumstance which surprises me."

Resting her cheek on her forefinger, she waited to be told what.

"It seems that Nunnely Priory is shut up—that the family has gone back to their place in ----shire. It seems that the baronet, Sir Philip himself, has accompanied his mother and sisters."

"Indeed!" said Shirley.

"May I ask if you share the amazement with which I received this news?"

"No, sir."

"Is it news to you?"

"Yes, sir."

"I mean—I mean," pursued Mr. Sympson, now fidgeting and returning to his customary wordy, confused style—"I mean to have a thorough explanation. I will not be put off. I shall insist on being heard. My questions must be answered. I will have clear, satisfactory replies. I am not to be trifled with."

"It is a strange and extraordinary thing! I thought all was right, knew no other; and there—the family are gone!"

"I suppose, sir, they had a right to go."

"Sir Philip is gone!" (with emphasis).

Shirley raised her brows. "Bon voyage!" she said.

"This will not do; this must be altered, ma'am."

He drew his chair forward, pushed it back, looking perfectly incensed and helpless.

"Come, come now, uncle," expostulated Shirley, "do not begin to fret and fume, or we shall make no sense. Ask me what you want to know. I am as willing to come to an explanation as you. I promise truthful replies."

"I want—I demand to know, Miss Keeldar, whether Sir Philip has made you an offer?"

"He has."

"You avow it?"

"I avow it. But now, go on. Consider that point settled."

"He made you an offer that night we dined at the priory?"

"It is enough to say that he made it. Go on."

"He proposed in the recess—the room that used to be a picture-gallery?"

No answer.

"You were both examining a cabinet. I saw it all. Subsequently you received a letter from him. On what subject?"

"No matter."

"Ma'am, is that the way you speak to me?"

Shirley's foot tapped quickly on the carpet.

"There you sit, silent and sullen—you who promised truthful replies."

"Sir, I have answered you thus far. Proceed."

"I should like to see that letter."

"You cannot see it."

"I must and shall, ma'am; I am your guardian."

"Having ceased to be a ward, I have no guardian."

"Ungrateful being! Reared by me as my own daughter—"

"Once more, uncle, have the kindness to keep to the point. Let us both remain cool. For my part, I do not wish to get into a passion; but you know, once you drive me beyond certain bounds, I care little what I say. Listen! You have asked me whether Sir Philip made me an offer. That question is answered. What do you wish to know next?"

"I desire to know whether you accepted or refused him, and know it I will."

"Certainly, you ought to know it. I refused him."

"Refused him! You—you, Shirley Keeldar, refused Sir Philip Nunnely?"

"I did."

The poor gentleman bounced from his chair, rushing and trotting through the room.

"There it is! There it is!"

"Sincerely speaking, I am sorry, uncle, you are so disappointed."

Concession and contrition never do any good with some people. Instead of softening, they embolden and harden them. Mr. Sympson was of that number.

"I disappointed? What is it to me? Have I an interest in it? You would insinuate, perhaps, that I have motives?"

"Most people have motives of some sort for their actions."

"She accuses me to my face! I, that have been a parent to her, she charges with bad motives!"

"Bad motives I did not say."

"And now you prevaricate; you have no principles!"

"Uncle, you tire me. I want to go away."

"Go you shall not! I will be answered. What are your intentions, Miss Keeldar?"

"In what respect?"

"In respect of matrimony?"

"To be quiet, and to do just as I please."

"Just as you please! The words are to the last degree indecorous."

"Mr. Sympson, I advise you not to become insulting. You know I will not bear that."

"You read French. Your mind is poisoned with French novels. You have imbibed French principles."

"The ground you are treading now returns a mighty hollow sound under your feet. Beware!"

"It will end in infamy, sooner or later. I have foreseen it all along."

"Do you assert, sir, that something in which I am concerned will end in infamy?"

"That it will—that it will. You said just now you would act as you please. You acknowledge no rules—no limitations."

"Silly stuff, and vulgar as silly!"

"Regardless of decorum, you are prepared to fly in the face of propriety."

"You tire me, uncle."

"What, madam—what could be your reasons for refusing Sir Philip?"

"At last there is another sensible question; I shall be glad to reply. Sir Philip is too young for me. I regard him as a boy. All his relations—his mother especially—would be annoyed if he married me. Such a step would embroil him with them. I am not his equal in the world's estimation."

"Is that all?"

"Our dispositions are not compatible."

"Why, a more amiable gentleman never breathed."

"He is very amiable—very excellent—truly estimable; but not my master—not in one point. I could not trust myself with his happiness. I would not undertake the keeping of it for thousands. I will accept no hand which cannot hold me in check."

"I thought you liked to do as you please. You are vastly inconsistent."

"When I promise to obey, it shall be under the conviction that I can keep that promise. I could not obey a youth like Sir Philip. Besides, he would never command me. He would expect me always to rule—to guide—and I have no taste whatever for the office."

"You no taste for swaggering, and subduing, and ordering, and ruling?"

"Not my husband; only my uncle."

"Where is the difference?"

"There is a slight difference—that is certain. And I know full well any man who wishes to live in decent comfort with me as a husband must be able to control me."

"I wish you had a real tyrant."

"A tyrant would not hold me for a day, not for an hour. I would rebel—break from him—defy him."
