The winter following the first strange journey outside Dunwich brought a trial of equal strangeness to Wilbur Whateley. Every letter sent to the Widener Library at Harvard, the Bibliothèque Nationale in Paris, the British Museum, the University of Buenos Aires, and the Library of Miskatotic at Arkham had returned empty-handed; the volume he desperately sought refused to leave its sanctuary. Thus, shabby, filthy, beard-bristling, and speaking with a jarring accent, Wilbur traveled personally to Arkham to consult the forbidden copy held within the university's locked vaults. Standing nearly eight feet tall and dragging a cheap, new valise from Osborn's general store, this dark, goatish gargoyle arrived one day seeking the dreaded tome kept under guard—the hideous *Necronomicon* of the mad Arab Alhazred, as rendered in Olaus Wormius's Latin translation and printed in Spain during the Seventeenth Century. Having never witnessed a city, his mind knew no path other than to reach the university grounds, where he carelessly stepped past the great white-fanged hound that barked with unnatural fury and tugged frantically at its heavy chains.

In his possession lay the priceless, though flawed, English edition of Dr. Dee's work, bequeathed to him by his grandfather. Upon gaining access to the Latin original, Wilbur immediately began comparing the texts, hunting for a specific passage located on the 751st page of his own defective volume. He could not help but mention this mission to the librarian—Erudite Henry Armitage, A.M., Miskatonic, Ph.D., Princeton—who had once visited the farm and now questioned him politely. Wilbur admitted he sought a formula or incantation containing the frightful name *Yog-Sothoth*. To him, the text offered discrepancies, duplications, and ambiguities that made locating the passage a labyrinthine struggle. As he copied down the chosen formula, Dr. Armitage glanced involuntarily over his shoulder at the open pages. The left-hand page, in the Latin version, brimmed with monstrous threats against the peace and sanity of the world.

*"Nor is it to be thought,"* the text read, as Armitage mentally translated the chilling words, *"that man is either the oldest or the last of earth's masters, nor does the common bulk of life and substance walk alone. The Old Ones were, the Old Ones are, and the Old Ones shall be. Not in the spaces we know, but BETWEEN them. They walk serene and primal, undimensioned and to us unseen. Yog-Sothoth knows the gate. Yog-Sothoth IS the gate. Yog-Sothoth is the key and guardian of the gate. Past, present, future—all are ONE in Yog-Sothoth. He knows where the Old Ones broke through of old, and where THEY shall break through again. He knows where THEY have trod earth's fields, and where THEY still tread them, and why no one can behold THEM as THEY tread. By THEIR smell can men sometimes know THEM near, but of THEIR semblance can no man know, saving ONLY in the features of those THEY have begotten on mankind; and of those are there many sorts, differing in likeness from man's truest eidolon to that shape WITHOUT sight or substance which is THEY. They walk unseen and foul in lonely places where the WORDS have been spoken and the RITES howled through at their SEASONS. The wind GIBBERS with THEIR voices, and the earth MUTTERS with THEIR consciousness. They bend the forest and crush the city, yet may not forest or city behold the HAND that smites. Kadath in the cold waste hath known THEM, and what man knows Kadath? The ice desert of the South and the sunken isles of Ocean hold stones whereon THEIR seal is engraved, but WHO hath seen the deep frozen city or the sealed tower long garlanded with seaweed and barnacles? Great Cthulhu is THEIR cousin, yet can HE spy THEM only dimly. Iä Shub-Niggurath! As a Foulness SHALL ye know THEM. Their hand is at your throats, yet ye SEE THEM not; and THEIR habitation is even one with your GUARDED THRESHOLD. Yog-Sothoth is the KEY to the gate, whereby the SPHERES meet. Man rules NOW where THEY ruled once; THEY shall soon rule WHERE man rules now. After summer is winter, and after winter summer. They wait patient and potent, for HERE shall THEY reign AGAIN."*

Dr. Armitage, connecting the text with what he had heard of Dunwich's brooding presences and of Wilbur Whateley's dim, hideous aura stretching from a dubious birth to a cloud of probable matricide, felt a wave of terror as tangible as a draft from a tomb's cold dampness. The bent, goatish giant before him seemed the spawn of another planet or dimension; something only partially human, linked to black gulfs of essence and entity that stretched like titanic fantasies beyond all spheres of force, matter, space, and time.

Soon, Wilbur raised his head and began to speak in a strange, resonant fashion that hinted at sound-producing organs unlike any found in ordinary mankind.

"Mr. Armitage," he said, "I calc'late I've got to take that book home. They's things in it I've got to try under sarten conditions that I can't git here, an' it 'ud be a mortal sin to let a red-tape rule hold me up. Let me take it along, sir, an' I'll swar they wun't nobody know the difference. I dun't need to tell ye I'll take good keer of it. It wa'n't me that put this Dee copy in the shape it is...."

He paused, seeing firm denial hardening the librarian's face, and his own goatish features twisted into cunning. Armitage, half-ready to suggest copying just the necessary portions, suddenly recoiled at the potential consequences. There was too much terrible responsibility in handing such a being the key to such blasphemous outer spheres. Whateley sensed the refusal and attempted to answer lightly.

"Well, all right, if ye feel that way 'bout it. Maybe Harvard won't be so fussy as yew be." With that, he rose and strode out of the building, stooping low at every doorway as if avoiding invisible eyes.

From behind the glass, Armitage heard the savage yelping of the great watchdog and studied Whateley's gorilla-like lope across the visible patch of campus. He thought of the wild tales he'd heard, recalling the old Sunday stories in the *Advertiser*, the lore gathered from Dunwich rustics and villagers during his single visit. Unseen entities—not of earth, or at least not of tri-dimensional earth—rushed fetid and horrible through New England's glens and brooked obscenely atop the mountain peaks. Of this, he had long been certain. Now, however, he sensed the close presence of some terrible fragment of the intruding horror and glimpsed a hellish advance within the black dominion of the ancient, once-passive nightmare. With a shudder of disgust, he locked away the *Necronomicon*, yet the room still reeked with an unholy, unidentifiable stench. *"As a foulness shall ye know them,"* he whispered, quoting the text. Yes—the odor was identical to that which had sickened him at the Whateley farmhouse less than three years prior. Thinking of Wilbur, goatish and ominous, he laughed mockingly at the village rumors regarding his parentage.

"Inbreeding?" Armitage muttered, half-aloud to himself. "Great God, what simpletons! Show them Arthur Machen's *Great God Pan* and they'll think it a common Dunwich scandal! But what thing—what cursed, shapeless influence on or off this three-dimensional earth—was Wilbur Whateley's father? Born on Candlemas, nine months after May Eve of 1912, when the talk of queer earth noises reached clear to Arkham... what walked on those mountains that May Night? What Roodmas horror fastened itself on the world in half-human flesh and blood?"

During the ensuing weeks, Dr. Armitage devoted himself to collecting every scrap of data concerning Wilbur Whateley and the formless presences surrounding Dunwich. He contacted Dr. Houghton of Aylesbury, who had attended Old Whateley in his final illness, finding much to ponder in the grandfather's last words as recounted by the physician. A visit to Dunwich Village yielded little new information, but a close scrutiny of the *Necronomicon*, specifically in the sections Wilbur had sought so avidly, revealed terrifying new clues to the nature, methods, and desires of the strange evil vaguely threatening the planet. Conversations with various students of archaic lore in Boston and letters to others elsewhere fed a growing amazement that slowly transmuted from varied degrees of alarm into a state of truly acute spiritual fear. As summer drew on, he felt a dim, pressing certainty that something must be done about the lurking terrors of the upper Miskatonic valley and about the monstrous being known to the human world as Wilbur Whateley.
