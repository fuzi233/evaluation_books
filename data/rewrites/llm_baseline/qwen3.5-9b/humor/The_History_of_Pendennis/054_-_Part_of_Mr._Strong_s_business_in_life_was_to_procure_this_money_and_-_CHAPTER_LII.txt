Doctor Portman's letter was dispatched to London, and the good clergyman busied himself soothing Mrs. Pendennis into a semblance of composure until a reply arrived—a response he optimistically believed (or at least stubbornly maintained) would vindicate Mr. Pen's morality. For the moment, however, Helen's plan to travel to London and confront her son personally was impossible. The apothecary strictly forbade her leaving her bed, let alone venturing to Fairoaks, for the first twenty-four hours. By the following morning, she was finally permitted to return to her sofa at home, attended by the faithful, albeit silent, Laura.

Unfortunately for everyone involved, Mr. Pen never read Doctor Portman's earnest homily until weeks after it had been written. Day after day, the widow awaited her son's reply to the accusations laid against him, her own health deteriorating with every passing hour. It was a harrowing trial for Laura: to witness her dearest friend's suffering, to endure Helen's estrangement, and to absorb the sting of that withheld affection. Yet, this young lady possessed a remarkable capacity for endurance. Through the "gracious assistance" of Heaven, poured out via her pure and constant prayers, she fulfilled her duty. Since this duty was performed in absolute silence—her supplications taking place in her private chamber, invisible to mortal eyes—we, too, must remain silent regarding these virtues. They are as unsuited for public display as a flower blooming in a ballroom. We need only say that a good woman is the loveliest flower under heaven; we gaze with love and wonder at her silent grace, her pure fragrance, and her delicate beauty. Alas, it is a tragedy to see such flowers bowed down or consumed by inexorable grief, wasting in disease, pining under long pain, or snatched away by sudden fate in their prime. We may deserve sorrow, but why should *these* be unhappy? Only because Heaven chastises those it loves most, refining pure spirits through repeated trials.

So, the letter never reached Pen. Despite being duly posted and faithfully delivered by the postman into his letterbox in Lamb Court, it was subsequently carried by the laundress to his writing table amidst the rest of his correspondence. Recall, if you will, the portrait of him entering from his small bedroom just as Mrs. Flanagan, his laundress, was in the act of drinking his gin.

For those readers who have tracked Mr. Arthur's career thus far, the prevailing flaw in his character and his greatest adversary should now be obvious. Many among us, my beloved public, struggle with the very same rascal. This scoundrel seizes every opportunity to drag us into mischief, plunge us into quarrels, and lead us toward idleness and bad company. In short, Pen's greatest enemy was himself. Having pampered, coaxed, and indulged this internal rogue all his life, the fellow had grown insolent, much like a spoiled servant. At the slightest attempt at coercion or the demand to perform an unpleasant task, he became frantically rude and unruly. While someone accustomed to sacrifice, like Laura, who regularly gives up her pleasures for others, handles such demands with ease, Pen—who was unused to any self-denial—suffered grievously when called upon to pay his share, grumbling savagely at the prospect of forgoing anything he desired.

Consequently, he resolved never to see Fanny again. He vowed he wouldn't. To banish the image of that fascinating little person from his mind, he threw himself into constant occupation, vigorous exercise, and dissipation. He worked excessively, walked and rode too much, and consumed food, drink, and tobacco in prodigious quantities. Yet, despite the endless stream of cigars and punch, little Fanny's image remained burned into his inflamed brain. After a week of this rigorous discipline and self-denial, our young gentleman ended up in bed with a fever. Let any reader who has never suffered a fever in a garret pity the wretch condemned to endure such a calamity.

A committee of marriageable ladies, or any Christian group dedicated to propagating domestic virtues, should commission a Cruikshank or a Leech to illustrate the horrors of bachelor life in chambers. Consider the bachelor's lonely breakfast: the black kettle simmering in a dreary fire in midsummer, or worse, the fire extinguished at Christmas, half an hour after the laundress has departed the sitting-room. The owner enters shivering, forced to hunt for coal and wood before beginning his studies, effectively performing the duties of a housemaid in the absence of Mrs. Flanagan. Or consider the bachelor's shirt—the garment needed just at dinner-time, found lacking buttons. Picture, furthermore, the bachelor's return after a merry Christmas spent in a cozy country house filled with pretty faces and kind welcomes. He leaves his portmanteau at the barber's, lights his dismal candle at the sputtering stair-lamp, and enters his blank, familiar room. The only tokens greeting him are Christmas bills, amiably spread across his reading table. Add to this an appalling depiction of bachelor illness, and the rents in the Temple would surely plummet. To be well in chambers is melancholy and selfish enough; but to be *ill* in chambers—to spend long nights in pain and watchfulness, longing for the morning and the laundress, serving oneself medicine while watching one's own fevered thoughts, with no kind hand to offer water or smooth the hot pillow—is a fate so dismal and tragic that we shall not enlarge upon its horrors. We can only heartily pity those bachelors in the Temple who brave it daily.

This lot befell Arthur Pendennis after the excesses described above. One night he went to bed ill; the next, he awoke worse. His only visitors that day, aside from the laundress, were the Printer's Devil from the *Pall Mall Gazette*, whom the narrator tried desperately to appease. These efforts to finish his work exacerbated his fever. He could produce only a fraction of his usual copy. With Shandon absent and Warrington not in London, the political and editorial columns of the *Gazette* looked dreadingly blank, leaving the sub-editor at a loss.

Mr. Finucane rushed to Pen's chambers and found the gentleman so exceedingly unwell that the good-natured Irishman immediately stepped in to fill his place. He produced a series of political and critical compositions that undoubtedly greatly edified the readers. Magnificent allusions to the greatness of Ireland and the genius of its inhabitants flowed freely from Finucane's pen. Shandon, the Chief Editor, who was relaxing placidly at Boulogne-sur-Mer, received the journal and instantly recognized the handwriting of the great Sub-editor. Laughing, he tossed the paper to his wife, "Mary, look here! Here is Jack at work again!" Indeed, Jack was a warm friend and a gallant partisan. When holding the pen, he rarely missed an opportunity to inform the world that Rafferty was the greatest painter in Europe, lamenting the petty jealousy of the Academy; to report that Mr. Rooney, M.P., was appointed Governor of Barataria; or to insert compliments to the Round Towers or the Giant's Causeway into any subject. Beyond doing Pen's work, his kind-hearted comrade offered to forego his Saturday and Sunday holidays to act as nurse-tender to Arthur. However, Arthur insisted that Finucane not sacrifice his pleasure, assuring him he could bear his malady alone.

After finishing the paper's work and eating supper at the Back Kitchen on Friday night, Finucane informed Captain Costigan of their young friend's illness in the Temple. Remembering the matter two days later, the Captain visited the invalid on Sunday afternoon.

He found Mrs. Flanagan, the laundress, weeping in the sitting-room. She had received a terrible report of the poor young gentleman inside. Pen's condition had so terrified her that she resorted to brandy to sustain herself against the grief his illness caused. Hanging about his bed and attempting to minister to him, her attentions became intolerable to the invalid, who begged her peevishly to stay away. This drove the laundress to fresh tears and renewed applications to the bottle, which she used as an anodyne. The Captain reprimanded the woman soundly for her intemperance, pointing out the fatal consequences of persisting in such imprudent behavior.

Pen, though in a highly fevered state, was delighted by Costigan's arrival. Hearing the familiar voice in the sitting room while lying in the bedroom, he called the Captain eagerly, thanking him profusely and begging him to sit and talk. The Captain checked the young man's pulse with gravity—his own trembling, clammy hand steadying momentarily as his fingers pressed Arthur's throbbing vein. The pulse beat fiercely; Pen's face was haggard and hot; his eyes were bloodshot and gloomy. His "bird," as the Captain termed it, hadn't been shaved in nearly a week. Pen gestured for his visitor to sit, tossing and turning in his comfortless bed, before attempting to chat lively about the Back Kitchen, Vauxhall, future outings, and Fanny. "How is little Fanny?" he asked.

Indeed, how was she? We know she returned home very sadly the previous Sunday evening after seeing Arthur light his lamp in his chambers during his interview with Bows. Bows returned to his own rooms shortly after, passing the lodge door and glancing into Mrs. Bolton's as per his custom, but with a deeply melancholy countenance. She endured another weary night. Her restlessness woke her little bedfellows repeatedly.
