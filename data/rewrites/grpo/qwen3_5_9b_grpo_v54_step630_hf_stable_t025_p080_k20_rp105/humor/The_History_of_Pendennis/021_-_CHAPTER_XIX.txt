Our friend Pen breathed a sigh of relief when his Mentor finally departed on the second day of their arrival in Oxford, and we can safely assume the Major felt a profound sense of liberation at having discharged his duty. More than three months of his life had been sacrificed to his nephew—a martyrdom rarely matched by any man, let alone a Major. While a man will readily risk his life for honor, asking him to surrender his ease or his heart's desire is a trial few can bear. Yet, the Major had done it. He deserved his holiday. Foker and Pen saw him off in the carriage, with Foker giving specific instructions to the driver to ensure the Major's comfort. The elder Pendennis was pleased to entrust his nephew to a young man who would introduce him to the university's elite. The Major, meanwhile, rushed to London and then to Cheltenham, descending upon neighboring estates where the families hadn't fled abroad, seeking good shooting and fine company.

A quarter of the serial space has been dedicated to this single passage of Pen's career, a testament to the fact that his entire academic journey cannot be detailed with such granularity unless a future chronicler picks up the pen after his death. We shall not recount his university life with such minute detail, for the lives of such boys are not meant to be told in full. As long as one's honor is clear, the mind remains at ease. Women are often portrayed as pure and unselfish, but men? Men are complicated. I would not dare claim Arthur Pendennis was worse than his peers, only that his peers were, for the most part, flawed. Can you name ten spotless men in your acquaintance? My list is large, yet I cannot find ten saints among them.

During his first term, Pen attended classical and mathematical lectures with tolerable diligence. However, quickly discovering he lacked the genius for exact sciences, and annoyed by being beaten in the lecture hall by vulgar youths who wore shoes and stockings so thick they required straps to hold them up, he abandoned mathematics. He announced to his indulgent parent that he would now devote himself exclusively to Greek and Roman literature. Mrs. Pendennis was delighted, provided he didn't ruin his health. She had heard tragic tales of students dying of brain fever from overwork, and she insisted his delicate constitution took precedence over vain honors. Pen, though unaware of any lurking disease, promised not to stay up late reading. He kept this promise with surprising tenacity, unlike his behavior in other areas where he was often remiss.

Soon, Pen realized he learned little in the classical lectures. His fellow students were dull, and the tutor, Mr. Buck, was no better scholar than a fifth-form boy at Grey Friars. Mr. Buck might have some humdrum notions about Aeschylus or Aristophanes, but possessed no true poetic sense, much like Mrs. Binge, his bed-maker. Pen grew weary of watching dull minds blunder through lines he could read in a tenth of the time they spent. He concluded that private reading was the only truly profitable study and announced he would read far more privately and far less in public. His mother, knowing nothing of Homer or Algebra herself, was content with his arrangements, confident he would secure the place he merited.

Pen did not return home until after Christmas, disappointing his mother and Laura, who had been longing for him to build a fine snow fortification as he had three winters prior. Instead, he was invited to Logwood, Lady Agnes Foker's estate, for private theatricals and a gay Christmas party of fine folk whom the Major would never want his nephew to neglect. However, he stayed home for the final three weeks of the vacation. Laura noted the quantity of fine new clothes he brought, and his mother admired his improved appearance and manly tone.

He did not return at Easter, but when he arrived for the long vacation, he brought even smarter attire. He appeared in morning wearing wonderful shooting jackets with remarkable buttons, and in the evening in gorgeous velvet waistcoats, richly embroidered cravats, and curious linen. Peering into his room, his mother discovered a beautiful dressing-case with silver mountings, lovely rings, and jewelry. He had a new French watch and gold chain, replacing the big old chronometer with its bunch of jingling seals that had once hung from John Pendennis's fob—the very watch the late doctor had used to feel patients' pulses. Just months prior, Pen had longed for this watch, considering it the most splendid timepiece in the world. Before leaving for college, Helen had taken it from her trinket-box, where it had sat unwound since her husband's death, and given it to Pen with a solemn speech about his father's virtues and the proper use of time. Now, Pen pronounced the portly chronometer "out of date," comparing it disrespectfully to a warming-pan. Laura thought this rude, but he left the watch in a drawer alongside soiled primrose gloves and cravats that had gone out of fashion, joining another school watch previously mentioned in this history. His old horse, Rebecca, was deemed too light for his weight; he swapped her for a more powerful steed, paying a heavy price for it. Mr. Pendennis provided the funds, and Laura wept when Rebecca was fetched away.

Pen also brought a large box of cigars branded with exotic names like Colorados, Afrancesados, and Telescopios. He began consuming them not only around the stables and greenhouses—where they were beneficial for Helen's plants—but in his own study. His mother initially disapproved, but he claimed he was working on a prize poem and could not compose without a cigar, quoting the late Lord Byron in favor of the habit. As he smoked with such apparent purpose, his mother eventually relented. One day, finding him on the sofa consulting a recent novel to cultivate light literature, Helen simply went to his bedroom, retrieved a light-box and his cigar-case, placed the cigar in his mouth, and lit the match. Pen laughed and kissed her hand as it rested fondly over the back of the sofa. "Dear old mother," he said, "if I were to tell you to burn the house down, I think you would do it." It is likely he was right; the foolish woman would have done almost anything for him.

Besides English light literature, Pen imported boxes of French light literature. When Helen dipped into these pages, she read things that opened her eyes in wonder. Pen assured her he did not make the books, but it was his duty to keep up his French by reading celebrated contemporary writers, including the eminent Paul de Kock, just as much as Swift or Molière. Mrs. Pendennis sighed with perplexity but yielded. Miss Laura, however, was strictly warned off the books by both her anxious mother and the rigid moralist Arthur Pendennis himself, who believed such reading was unsuitable for a young lady whose business in life differed from his.

During this long vacation, Pen drank through the bin of claret his father had stored, a vintage the son had remarked contained not a single headache in a hogshead. Once exhausted, he wrote to his wine merchants, Messrs. Binney and Latham of Mark Lane, London, for a further supply. Old Doctor Portman had recommended them, advising against Oxbridge tradesmen whose liquor was deleterious and exorbitantly priced. "You will have to entertain your young friends at Boniface with wine-parties," the honest rector had noted. The obedient young gentleman followed this advice. When he hinted that the university account for wine should arrive with the Fairoaks bill, the poor widow was frightened by the amount. Pen laughed at her old-fashioned views, claiming the bill was moderate and that everyone now drank claret and champagne. Finally, the widow paid, feeling dimly that her household expenses were swelling and her narrow income struggling to meet them. Yet, these were only occasional costs. Pen merely visited for a few weeks at a time; Laura and his mother could pinch when he was gone. In the brief time he was with them, ought they not to make him happy?

Arthur's allowances were liberal, far exceeding those of sons of far wealthier men. Years ago, the thrifty and affectionate John Pendennis, determined to give his son a university education denied to him by his own father's extravagance, had begun laying aside a store of money called Arthur's Education Fund. Year after year, his executors found entries of sums vested as A.E.F., and during the widow's lifetime before Pen's entry, she added sundry sums. Thus, when Arthur went up to Oxford, the fund reached a considerable amount. "Let him be liberally allowanced," was the Major's maxim. "Let him make his first entrée into the world as a gentleman, take his place with men of good rank and station; after giving it to him, it will be his own duty to hold it." There is no worse policy than stinting a boy or putting him on a lower allowance than his fellows. Arthur must face the world and fight for himself presently; meanwhile, they would procure him good friends, gentlemanly habits, and back him well against the time the real struggle came. These liberal opinions the Major probably advanced both because they were just and because he was not dealing with his own money.

Thus, young Pen, the only son of an estated country gentleman, with a good allowance, a gentlemanlike bearing, and a handsome person, looked to be a lad of much more consequence than he truly was. He was held by Oxbridge authorities, tradesmen, and undergraduates as quite a young buck and a member of the aristocracy. His manner was frank, brave, and perhaps a little impertinent, fitting a high-spirited youth. He was perfectly generous and free-handed with his seemingly plentiful money. He loved joviality and had a good voice for a song. Boat-racing had not yet reached the frenzy it would attain later; riding and tandem-driving were the fashions of the ingenuous youth. Pen rode well to hounds, appeared in pink as became a young buck, and, not particularly extravagant in equestrian pursuits, managed to run up a fine bill at Nile's, the livery-stable keeper, and in other quarters. In fact, this lucky young gentleman had almost every taste to a considerable degree. He was very fond of books of all sorts; Doctor Portman had taught him to like rare editions, and his own taste led him to beautiful bindings. It was marvelous what tall copies, gilding, marbling, and blind-tooling the booksellers and binders put upon Pen's shelves. He had a fair taste in art and a keen relish for prints of a high school—Stranges, Rembrandt etchings, and Wilkies before the letter—with which his apartments were furnished in perfect good taste, earning him no small reputation in the university. He exhibited a partiality for rings, jewelry, and fine raiment. During his time at the university, he was rather a dressy man, loving to array himself in splendour. He and his polite friends would dress with as much care to dine at each other's rooms as others would to enslave a mistress. They said he wore rings over his kid gloves, which he always denied, but what follies will not youth perpetrate with its own admirable gravity? That he took perfumed baths was a truth; he claimed to take them after meeting men of a very low set in hall.

In Pen's second year, when Miss Fotheringay made her chief hit in London and scores of prints were published of her, Pen had one hung in his bedroom. He confided to his set how awfully, wildly, and passionately he had loved that woman. He showed them the verses he had written, his brow darkening, eyes rolling, and chest heaving with emotion as he recalled that fatal period, describing the woes and agonies he had suffered. The verses were copied, handed about, sneered at, admired, and passed from coterie to coterie. Few things elevate a lad in the estimation of his brothers more than a character for great and romantic passion. Perhaps there is something noble in it among very young men, considered heroic. Pen was pronounced a tremendous fellow. They said he had almost committed suicide; that he had fought a duel with a baronet about her. Freshmen pointed him out to each other. As he swaggered out of college at promenade time, surrounded by cronies, he was famous to behold. Elaborately attired, he would ogle the ladies who came to lionize the university, passing before him on the arms of happy gownsmen, and give his opinion on their charms or toilettes with the gravity of a critic. Men said they had been walking with Pendennis and were as pleased to be seen in his company as some would be walking with a duke down Pall Mall. He and the Proctor capped each other as they met, as if they were rival powers, and the men hardly knew which was the greater.

In fact, during his second year, Arthur Pendennis became one of the men of fashion in the university. It is curious to watch that facile admiration and simple fidelity of youth. They hang round a leader, wonder at him, love him, and imitate him. No generous boy ever lived without some wonderment for another; Monsieur Pen at Oxbridge had his school, his faithful band of friends, and his rivals. When the young men heard at haberdashers' shops that Mr. Pendennis of Boniface had just ordered a crimson satin cravat, you would see a couple of dozen crimson satin cravats in Main Street within the week. Simon the Jeweller sold no less than two gross of Pendennis pins from a pattern the young gentleman had selected.

Now, if any person with an arithmetical turn of mind calculates the cost of indulging freely in all the propensities we have attributed to Pen, it will be seen that a young fellow with such tastes must spend or owe a very handsome sum in two or three years. We said Pen had not a calculating turn. No single propensity was outrageously extravagant, yet Paddington's tailor's account, Guttlebury's cook's bill for dinners, Dillon Tandy's bill with Finn the print seller for Raphael-Morgheus and Landseer proofs, Wormall's dealings with Parkton the great bookseller for Aldine editions and illuminated Missals of the XVI. Century, and Snaffle's or Foker's score with Nile the horsedealer, were each incomparably greater than any little bills Mr. Pen might run up with the above tradesmen.
