Ah, the noble Henry Foker—our gallant, melancholy, and altogether too romantic gentleman—has been busy these past few pages doing what any man of his temperament would do when love is in the air: chasing it like a hare with a broken leg, and failing to catch it every time.

I’d advise my younger readers—especially those who’ve recently discovered the joy of sighing at dinner tables or writing poetry on napkins—to pause for a moment and consider how much of their lives they’re spending on this grand passion. It’s not just time lost, you know—it’s *everything* lost. Sleep? Gone. Breakfast? Served cold while you stare into space, wondering if she’ll wear blue tonight. Work? Forgotten, like a forgotten hat left on a park bench. Friends? Left behind, as you rush off to see if her carriage has passed by the Serpentine. Even your own family becomes a nuisance—your mother’s stories about the weather seem unbearable; your father’s jokes fall flat. You barely taste the soup, and yet you’re still hungry—for her.

And so poor Mr. H. Foker, bred to indulge every whim (which he did with the enthusiasm of a man who believes that self-indulgence is a virtue), found himself utterly captivated by Miss Blanche Amory. He had once given Arthur Pendennis some sound advice—about life, love, and the dangers of marrying a woman with a bad temper. But now, dear reader, even the wisest man can be undone by a pretty face and a well-timed glance. Who among us hasn’t preached against passion—and then fallen headfirst into it? I say again: we all have. We preach to others, but practice on ourselves. And if you’re one of those rare souls who never felt anything stronger than mild curiosity, then I say: good for you! But also—boring. We don’t need angels in this story. We need people who stumble, who blunder, who cry over lost gloves and laugh at the wrong moments. We want the real ones—the ones who get drunk, fall in love, and occasionally forget their own names.

So let’s not pretend that only perfect beings are worthy of admiration. The world is full of odd couples: the transported pickpocket who fell madly in love with a female convict—both ancient, unattractive, and fond of gin—but whose romance was no less passionate than Antony and Cleopatra’s. Or the Polish dwarf, Count Borulawski, who made the most beautiful baroness at Dresden swoon with devotion. And then there’s young Cornet Tozer, who ran off with Mrs. Battersby—old enough to be his grandmother, mind you—and declared it the happiest day of his life.

So yes, Henry Foker was smitten. Deeply, desperately, absurdly smitten. When Lord Gravesend’s family decamped to the country (leaving behind a proxy for the venerable Lord Bagwig), Harry stayed behind in London—not because anyone missed him, especially Lady Ann, whom he was supposed to marry. She didn’t miss him. Not even a little. But Harry? Oh, he was following Miss Clavering like a dog after a squirrel. And since everyone knew he was engaged to Lady Ann, he couldn’t exactly announce his new affection. So he kept it locked up inside—like a volcano waiting to erupt. One day, he might just burst open from the pressure of his own feelings and die a noble death of emotional overload.

But let’s skip ahead to a certain June evening, when Gaunt House hosted a ball so grand it could have been mistaken for a royal coronation. The next day’s newspapers printed two whole columns of guests—names so numerous and aristocratic they could have filled a telephone directory. Among them were Sir Francis and Lady Clavering, Miss Amory (courtesy of Major Pendennis’s tireless efforts), and our two heroes: Arthur and Harry.

They danced. A lot. Especially with Miss Blanche. The Major, meanwhile, took charge of Lady Clavering—his duty being to escort her to the refreshment room, where the feast was more magnificent than a king’s banquet. There, amid portraits of Titian and Giorgione, gold salvers, wax candles shaped like stars, and mountains of food, Lady Clavering ate with the determination of a bear preparing for winter. The Major followed her like a loyal shadow, fetching plates, adjusting chairs, calling servants, and enduring the entire ordeal with a smile that said, “I am suffering, but I am dignified.”

Mr. Wagg, ever the statistician, counted the dishes she consumed—though, having imbibed a generous amount of champagne, his tally may have included a few phantom peaches and invisible soups. He advised Lady Steyne’s doctor, Mr. Honeyman, to check on the Begum the next morning—just in case.

Sir Francis, however, didn’t last long. After a couple of glasses of wine, he slipped away to Jermyn Street, where his friends—Loder, Punter, Moss Abrams, and Captain Skewball—were gathered around the green table, playing cards and drinking themselves into a state of cheerful idiocy. Sir Francis rejoined them, spirits lifted by the familiar rattle of dice and the sound of men laughing at nothing.

Now, during the dancing, Mr. Pynsent—a man whose name sounds like a tax form—asked Miss Amory to dance. But when he saw Arthur Pendennis nearby, he scowled. Arthur, sensing danger, sprang up and claimed Miss Amory as his partner. Pynsent bit his lip, bowed deeply, and retreated, muttering something about surrendering his claim.

“Who *is* that man?” thought Arthur. “A provincial fool with a novel under his arm. His soul is in Downing Street. His neckcloth is foolscap. His hair is sand. His legs are rulers. His insides are tape and sealing-wax. He was a prig in his cradle and has never laughed since birth—except three times, at the same joke told by his boss. I hate him. I hate him as much as I hate cold boiled veal.”

Blanche, naturally, responded with, “Oh, Mr. Pendennis, you’re wicked, mechant, perfectly abominable!”—but secretly thrilled at the attention.

“I say,” Arthur replied, “you have the most beautiful figure and the slimmest waist in the world—Miss Amory, I mean. I beg your pardon. Another turn? This music would make an alderman dance.”

“And you’ve stopped tumbling when you waltz now?” Blanche asked, looking up at him with a mischievous glint.

“One falls and gets up again in life, Blanche,” Arthur said. “You know I used to call you that. It’s the prettiest name in the world. Besides, I’ve practiced.”

“And with many partners, I’m afraid,” she said, shrugging dramatically.

Well, yes. Arthur had practiced. He’d become quite the dancer.

Meanwhile, poor Harry Foker—usually so chatty and cheerful—was utterly silent when dancing with Blanche. Holding her waist was ecstasy. Whirling around the room with her was divine. But speaking? What could he possibly say? What clever remark could match her wit? What pearl of wisdom could be worthy of such a queen?

It was Blanche who did all the talking. She asked about his little pony—how it was doing, whether it was still sweet. She thanked him tenderly, then sighed delicately when he offered to give it to her. “I have nobody to ride with in London,” she said. “Mamma is timid, and her figure isn’t pretty on horseback. Sir Francis never goes out with me. He loves me like—like a stepdaughter. Oh, how delightful it must be to have a father—a father, Mr. Foker!”

“Oh, uncommon,” Harry said, completely missing the sentimental mood.

At which point, Blanche’s eyes sparkled with laughter, and Harry—now relaxed—began telling her silly stories about horses, himself, and various people in the room. He spoke with the charm of a man who thinks he’s funny, though most of his jokes were probably about stable boys and bad weather.

When the conversation lagged, Blanche revived it by asking about Logwood. Was it pretty? Did he hunt? Did he like women who hunted? (She was ready to declare herself a hunting enthusiast.)

Harry, however, disapproved of sporting ladies. He pointed out Lady Bullfinch, passing by with a cigar in her mouth, as proof. Blanche agreed—she hated the idea of foxes being killed. “It would make me shudder,” she said.

Harry laughed and waltzed with renewed vigor.

At the end of the night, as they danced their final waltz, Blanche asked about Drummington. Was it a fine house? Her cousins were very accomplished—had she met Lord Erith? Which cousin did he favor? Wasn’t it Lady Ann? Yes, she was sure it was. She could tell by his blushes.

“I’m tired,” she said. “It’s late. I must go to mamma.” And without another word, she slipped from Harry’s arm and grabbed Arthur’s. “Mamma, mamma! Take me to mamma, dear Mr. Pendennis!” she called, shooting Harry a parting glance that could have melted steel.

My Lord Steyne arrived, resplendent in garter and ribbon, bald head gleaming, whiskers red as fire. He made a grand impression on Lady Clavering. With his own hand, he handed her a glass of wine, complimented her daughter, and asked to be introduced to her. At that moment, Arthur appeared with Blanche.

The peer bowed low. Blanche curtsied deeper than any courtier had ever done. He shook Arthur’s hand, praised his book—“very wicked and clever”—and asked Blanche if she’d read it. Arthur winced. Because Blanche was one of the heroines—Neaera in *Walter Lorraine*, slightly altered, with black ringlets instead of golden ones.

She had read it. Her eyes said everything: admiration, delight, recognition.

Lord Steyne bowed twice more and moved on.

As soon as he was gone, mother and daughter whispered praises. “He said they make a very nice couple,” murmured the Major to Lady Clavering. “Did he now? Really?” Mamma was thrilled. She tapped Arthur on the arm, tapped Blanche, tapped the Major—her joy was boundless.

Down the great staircase they went. Dawn was breaking—pink skies, clear light. And the faces of the party? Ah, how pale they looked! Especially the Major. Poor man—hours spent feeding Lady Clavering, flattering her, keeping her happy. His eyes were dark rings of bistre. His face was lined like a map of old wounds. Silver stubble glittered on his chin, like morning dew on a grave.

He stood there, silent, patient, exhausted—longing for bed, but staying because it was his duty. If martyrdom is admirable, then surely the gods smiled upon the Major’s endurance. Like the priests of Baal who cut themselves with knives, or the slaves of Mumbo Jumbo who tattooed themselves with hot skewers—he suffered for love, for duty, for friendship.

And when Lady Clavering insisted on leaving him at his door, he climbed into the back seat, polite to the last, resolute in his service.

As the carriage rolled away, Blanche looked out, wondering if she looked pale under her rose-colored hood. Arthur saw it clearly—she was yellow, drained. But Harry? For him, she was radiant. As bright as the sun rising over London.

Later, walking home, Arthur and Harry indulged in their usual habits: smoking cigars and mocking the nobility.

Arthur was sarcastic, dandyish, full of wit. He sneered at Lady John Turnbull’s French, Mrs. Slack Roper’s fake jewels, the old dandies, the young ones—everyone.

“You fire at everybody, Pen,” said Harry. “You’re awful.”

“Why don’t you take a shot at a brown wig?” Arthur asked. “You know whose I mean. It got into Lady Clavering’s carriage.”

“Under your uncle’s hat?” Harry asked, alarmed.

“My uncle is a martyr,” Arthur said. “He’s been working all night. He hates supper. He gets gout. He’s going home to pain and sleep. Shall I mock him? No. Not for Venice.”

“But why did he do it for your sake?” Harry asked.

“Boy,” Arthur said, striking a dramatic pose, “can you keep a secret? Swear you won’t tell?”

“What the deuce are you driving at?” Harry said, nervous.

Arthur ignored him. “Henry, friend of my youth—witness of my early follies—you may be dull at books, but you’re not without sense. You’ve courage, kindness—blush not, Henrico, you’ve plenty. Were I poor, I’d come to your purse. Were I sad, I’d pour my grief onto your shoulder—”

“Gammon, Pen,” Harry said. “Go on.”

“I would, Henrico, upon thy studs, and thy cambric worked by beauty! Know this: Arthur Pendennis, student-at-law, feels lonely. Care is furrowing his temples. Baldness is creeping in. I want money. I want to settle down. I’m thinking of marriage. A steady port and sherry character. Two maids, a man, a brougham, a house near the Parks—ha! What sayest thou?”

“But you haven’t got any money, Pen,” Harry said, still alarmed.

“Exactly,” Arthur said. “And that’s why I need help.”
