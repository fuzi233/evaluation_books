The crowd surged forward, their voices fading into the distance as Romola stepped down into the street. She moved swiftly toward the steps of San Stefano, where Baldassarre stood alone, his back pressed against the church door, the horn-cup clutched in his hand. His eyes, once clouded with confusion, now burned with a fierce intensity—a caged animal’s glare, fixed on some unseen prey.

Romola hesitated. That look, so sudden and venomous, struck her like a blade. Had he seen Tito? The thought coiled tight in her chest. If this man was the wronged one, if Tito had done him some terrible harm, then perhaps—perhaps her husband was not the man she believed. A desperate hope flickered. If she could bring them face to face, the truth might tear free at last.

“Come with me,” she said, keeping her voice steady. “I can give you shelter, food, until you’ve regained your strength.”

His lips moved, a low mutter more to himself than to her. “Yes. I need my strength. I must have it.”

She led him toward the Ponte Rubaconte, choosing the quieter path along the Arno. The city’s clamor receded, leaving only the rush of water beneath them.

“You’re not Florentine,” she ventured.

He turned his head sharply, studying her with guarded suspicion. But her gaze, open and searching, seemed to loosen something in him.

“No,” he admitted at last. “I am a man alone.”

She let the silence stretch, fearing any question might drive him away. Yet each step deepened her unease. If he had been the offender, why this dread? Why this secrecy?

They reached the Via de’ Bardi. At the sight of the half-open courtyard door, Baldassarre stiffened, his breath catching. Romola turned—and saw recognition flash in his eyes.

“Ah,” he rasped. “You are *his* wife.”

Her pulse stuttered. “Whose wife?”

But no name came. His jaw clenched, his stare unrelenting, as though the very image of Tito choked the words in his throat.

She pushed the door wider, revealing the straw-covered courtyard, the sick lying listless, the hollow-eyed children playing with idle fingers. “If you come inside,” she said, “I’ll find you a place to rest. And food.”

His refusal was swift. “No.” Yet he lingered, torn between impulse and caution.

“Then let me give you this.” She pressed coins into his palm—more than she would have given any other stranger.

He curled his fingers around them. “I’ll take them.”

She wanted to ask *where will you go? what will you do?* But he was already turning away, his figure shrinking into the distance as he vanished toward the bridge.

---

The bells of Florence rang in triumph, their joyous peals shaking the air. But to Baldassarre, they were just another taunt from a world that had crushed him.

He moved like a shadow through the streets, Niccolò Caparra’s shop drawing him like a lodestone. The apprentice, indifferent, sold him a knife for three *grossi*—the traitor’s own money buying the blade that might slit his throat. The irony was bitter, satisfying.

He tucked the weapon into his tunic, its weight a promise. Armor could be removed. Men grew careless. And when the moment came—

He bought bread, meat, devoured them beneath a loggia as the bells roared on.

---

Romola watched him go, relief and guilt twisting together. She should have pressed harder, demanded the truth. But what if that truth was a serpent coiled around her marriage? A wife had the right to ignorance, didn’t she? To look away from a husband’s sins, since she alone could not denounce him?

The thought was too heavy. She turned instead to the courtyard, to the faces lifted in hope as she told them of the coming grain. Their voices, weak but fervent, blessed her like a prayer.

This was her anchor. Not the wrangling of doctrine, not the dizzying prophecies of scourge and rebirth, but the simple act of easing pain. Savonarola had given her purpose—not in his visions, but in his fire, his unyielding will to mend a broken world.

She knelt beside a child, its tiny fingers tugging at her skirts. Here, in the dirt and the need, she was needed. Here, she could forget the gulf between herself and Tito, if only for a moment.

No soul is truly desolate so long as it can still revere another. And so long as she believed in Savonarola’s greatness, she could keep walking forward—even if the ground trembled beneath her.
