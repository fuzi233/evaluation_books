“No man,” a Rabbi had said, setting down his truth as if it were a law of the body as well as of the soul, “may turn the bones of his father and mother into spoons.” The saying had the clean, sudden hardness of a knife laid flat on a board: it stopped a hand in mid-reach. There were economies which would never become common because the only barrier against them was not statute but shudder—an inherited recoil, a taste, a sacredness that could not be melted down and recast for profit. And yet the world, being forever hungry for use, forever inventing new markets, was always tempting some man to ask, “Why not?” as if progress were only a more ingenious grinding of old substance.

Deronda, since Mirah had come like a dropped spark into his guarded life, had taken to a less fashionable exercise than parading a horse among glossy riders. He walked. Not the West End promenades, but the parts of London where the air thickened with human closeness, and shop-signs spoke in strange syllables, and the faces in doorways—women with heavy earrings, men with anxious eyes—carried the stamp of a race that history had battered into a sort of visible endurance. He went when he could to synagogues at the hour of service, standing apart, listening, watching lips move in prayer as if prayer were a language older than breath. He looked into shops, at counters where scales and measures clicked softly like insect wings, at windows where old silver flashed a cold, patient light.

He told himself, more than once, that if he were wise he would go directly to some Rabbi, some trusted man among the Jews, and state simply what he knew: a mother once named Cohen; a son named Ezra; a child, Mirah; a vanishing. He thought of doing so—after Christmas. It was always after something: after the next duty at home, after the next claim from Sir Hugo, after the next day’s fatigue. It was not that he lacked sympathy; it was that reality, when it pressed close and warm, could make even a man who loved common things feel as if his nerves were being rubbed against a coarse cloth. There is an easy enthusiasm that floats above facts—can imagine a beautiful maiden’s kindred in Cordova, can let garlic be medieval perfume, can turn dingy streets into Rhine-border legend, and hear in the modern shout the far-off bay of crusading bloodhounds. But to stand at noon among unbeautiful faces, with the beloved idea made flesh and liable to disappointment—that asks for a different imagination, the kind that can pierce what is solid without fleeing from it.

Now Mirah was not an idea. She was a living, trembling certainty with a voice that had learned fear and a silence that carried the weight of exile. And because she was so delicate in his mind—because she had the sort of inward music that made him listen for the least discord—he began, in spite of himself, to see every common Jew and Jewess under the harsh light of comparison. He could not help forecasting the collision between what she hoped—some mother transfigured by love, some brother purified by remembrance—and what might be found: a life dulled by trade, thickened by years, forgetful, even indifferent. The forecast was sharpened by a faint, suppressed consciousness that somewhere in his own destiny there might lie a similar collision between expectation and discovered fact.

He rambled, telling himself that he looked only to prepare his own mind. He did not want—he almost dared not want—to find anybody in particular. And yet he could not keep from glancing up at shop-fronts as he passed, scanning names as one tastes an unfamiliar dish with caution, hoping it will not be too strange upon the tongue. When his eye met “Cohen,” his heart gave a small, involuntary contraction—then eased if the next word was not “Ezra.” He had even the unreasonable wish that if he ever met an Ezra Cohen, he should not be a shopkeeper. One has these superstitions of fastidious hope, as if fate were a perverse cook who will always add what you most dislike the moment you confess your dislike.

One morning, turning into a little side street away from the grind and clatter of Holborn, he felt tired enough to hail a hansom and let the city roll past without his feet bearing him. He had paused, the cab coming toward him, when something in a window at his right caught his attention: clasps of chased silver, old and finely wrought, lying with a restrained gleam amid other objects whose histories were mute and dubious. The metal looked as if hands long dead had polished it with habit, and the engraving held the delicate sharpness of work done slowly, as if time itself were an artisan.

His first thought—domestic, almost tender in its ordinariness—was of Lady Mallinger, whose strictly Protestant taste had a weakness for Catholic spoils, and how these missal-clasps might be made into a bracelet to clasp her wrist. His eyes moved over the window. It was one of those pawnbrokers’ shops where jewelry led the show and lace and odd relics of gentility lay about like shed skins. In one corner a placard announced: Watches and Jewelry exchanged and repaired.

His looking had been noticed. A figure appeared at the door and, with the cordial push of trade, said, “Good day, sir.”

Deronda needed only that instant to see the face was unmistakably Jewish: a young man, about thirty, with the sleek confidence of a seller who believes in the persuasive power of his own smile. Deronda, shrinking from the coming pressure of attentions, returned the “good day” and crossed quickly to the other side of the street. There, as if he had meant only to avoid the man’s approach, he beckoned the cabman to draw up.

From his safer station he saw the name painted over the window.

EZRA COHEN.

The letters were black and plain, yet they struck him like a sound—like a name called unexpectedly in a crowded room. There might be a hundred Ezra Cohens in London, he reasoned, and he had happened upon one. But he had not seen a hundred; he had seen this. And the man at the door had been about the age one would expect for Mirah’s brother, grown while she was still small.

As the cab carried him away, he worked at his own mind with the obstinacy of one trying to knead a lump of dough into the shape his hands require. There was no warrant, he said; no solid presumption. And even if it should prove that this Ezra was Mirah’s brother, and her mother were dead—then it was not necessarily his duty to tell her. Why should he fling her into sorrow, perhaps into disillusion? He could keep her safe from that shock.

But the thought would not stay quiet under his hand. He had learned, through Mirah’s timid religious earnestness, that she would have a sacred desire to know even of her mother’s death—to mourn in truth, to pray with knowledge. And was he justified in ordering another life by his own notions of tenderness? Was not his secret complaint against the ordering of his own life that he had been denied open daylight—left without the clear claims of primary duty?

The immediate relief was the notion that he had discovered nothing—only a name on glass. Facts would surely dismiss this uneasy fantasy. He resolved to return as soon as he could and buy the clasps for Lady Mallinger; the purchase would be his pretext and his test.

But several days were wasted by Sir Hugo, who, being about to make an after-dinner speech on a burning topic, required Deronda to forage for him on the legal part of the question, and to fence with him daily in arguments that always ended in a draw. They differed, as they often did; Sir Hugo enjoyed opposition as he enjoyed any lively sport, but he also had his impatiences.

“Confound it, Dan!” he said one day, when Deronda had put his point with quiet firmness. “Why don’t you make an opportunity of saying these things in public? You’re wrong, you know. You won’t succeed. You’ve got the massive sentiment—the heavy artillery of the country—against you. But it’s all the better ground for a young man to display himself on. When I was your age, I should have taken it. And it would be quite as well for you to be in opposition to me here and there. It would throw you more into relief. If you would seize an occasion of this sort to make an impression, you might be in Parliament in no time. And you know that would gratify me.”

“I am sorry not to do what would gratify you, sir,” Deronda answered. “But I cannot persuade myself to look at politics as a profession.”

“Why not? If a man is not born into public life by his position in the country, there’s no way for him but to embrace it by his own efforts. The business of the country must be done—her Majesty’s Government carried on, as the old Duke said. And it never could be, my boy, if everybody looked at politics as if they were prophecy, and demanded an inspired vocation. If you are to get into Parliament, it won’t do to sit still and wait for a call either from heaven or constituents.”

“I don’t want to make a living out of opinions,” Deronda said; “especially out of borrowed opinions. Not that I mean to blame other men. I dare say many better fellows than I don’t mind getting on to a platform to praise themselves, and giving their word of honor for a party.”

“I’ll tell you what, Dan,” Sir Hugo returned, “a man who sets his face against every sort of humbug is simply a three-cornered, impracticable fellow. There’s a bad style of humbug, but there is also a good style—one that oils the wheels and makes progress possible. If you are to rule men, you must rule them through their own ideas; and I agree with the Archbishop at Naples who had a St. Januarius procession against the plague. It’s no use having an Order in Council against popular shallowness. There is no action possible without a little acting.”

“One may be obliged to give way to an occasional necessity,” said Deronda. “But it is one thing to say, ‘In this particular case I am forced to put on this foolscap and grin,’ and another to buy a pocket foolscap and practice myself in grinning. I can’t see any real public expediency that does not keep an ideal before it which makes a limit of deviation from the direct path. But if I were to set up for a public man I might mistake my success for public expediency.”

The talk jarred on him. It left a taste, as if a heavy, over-sauced dish had been pressed on his palate when he was craving something plain. It was after this that he set out again toward Ezra Cohen’s, with his pretext of the silver clasps and his deeper reluctance, slower and stickier than any fatigue in his limbs.

He entered the street from the end farthest from Holborn. The roar behind him fell away; here, the sound changed—voices nearer, more distinct, a thinner rattle of carts, the clink of small objects handled, the occasional sharp cry of a child like a bird’s complaint. The sonic air of the place—the way noise nested here in narrow channels rather than spreading—pressed on him with a kind of intimacy. Modern London had its industrial brute sounds, the endless iron grind; but in this street the soundscape had older textures: bargaining voices, coins dropped, doors opened and shut, and under it all the hum of lives kept close to their own necessities.

He found himself turning Sir Hugo’s words inward, translating “public expediency” into the private difficulty that drew him back. Was it wise—was it merciful—to conceal possible kindred? Such decisions, in most lives, are made rough-and-ready; men keep secrets as they keep money, without much scruple, calling it prudence. But Deronda had come to feel concealment as a kind of moral mildew—spreading, spoiling, making even good intentions smell faintly stale. To act under the necessity of concealment was, to him, often the sign of a wrong path.

Still, he hesitated. He paused before shop-windows with the air of a gentleman merely looking, as if he could delay the next step by admiring a tie-pin or a ring. A bare possibility, when it threatens pain, can weigh more than the best-clad likelihood.

One of the windows he paused at belonged to a second-hand bookshop. Outside, on a narrow table, the ages lay in compromise: Homer and railway novels, sermons and scandal, battered histories and bright lies. Deronda’s eye, trained by hunger for meaning rather than for fashion, lit on something he wanted at once: Salomon Maimon’s autobiography. He had heard of it, and the very title had the taste of strong bread—something made of difficult grain, nourishing and austere.

He slipped the book into his hand and entered to pay, expecting to see the usual grimy nonchalance of second-hand trade—the seller who never presses you, as if even he does not believe in the good of what he sells. But behind the counter was a figure that startled him into stillness.

A man in threadbare clothing sat on a stool against shelves that projected beyond the counter. His age was hard to guess; the flesh had the dead, yellowish flatness of old ivory. He was reading yesterday’s Times. When he let the paper rest on his lap and looked up, Deronda had the sudden, absurd sense that he was being looked at by a face that belonged, not to this dim shop, but to some older world—a prophet of exile, or a poet who had made a language burn in the middle ages. It was a fine, typical Jewish face, wrought into intensity by long striving where satisfactions were indirect and far away, and perhaps by bodily suffering that permitted no ease in the present. The features were clear-cut, not large; the brow not high but broad, edged sharply by crisp black hair. It might never have been handsome; it must always have been forcible. The dark gaze, far off and yet piercing, made the shop’s gloom seem like a prison cell.

For a moment they merely looked. The air held the faint odor of old paper and dust—dry, almost edible in its way, like the scent that rises when one breaks a crust of stale bread. Outside, a cart wheel squealed; inside, the silence tightened around their breathing.

Deronda asked at last, “What is the price of this book?”

The man took it and examined the fly-leaves without rising. “There is no mark, and Mr. Ram is not in now. I am keeping the shop while he is gone to dinner. What are you disposed to give for it?” He held the book close on his lap, his hand upon it, and looked examiningly at Deronda.

A disagreeable thought crossed Deronda—was this an attempt to extract more from a stranger? Yet the man’s look seemed less mercantile than hungry, as if he were tasting Deronda’s words for some flavor.

Without weighing further, Deronda said, “Don’t you know how much it is worth?”

“Not its market-price.” The man’s voice was low, dry. “May I ask—have you read it?”

“No. I have read an account of it, which makes me want to buy it.”

“You are a man of learning—you are interested in Jewish history?” The tone deepened, sharpened with eager inquiry, as if something in him had leaped awake at the mention of that book.

“I am certainly interested in Jewish history,” said Deronda, quietly, curiosity overcoming his distaste at being inspected.

The man rose abruptly. A thin hand pressed Deronda’s arm with a tightness that made him flinch, and a hoarse, excited voice—scarce above a loud whisper—said, “You are perhaps of our race?”

Deronda colored deeply. He disliked the grasp, the sudden claim implied by it, the way the question sounded like a gate creaking open—or shutting. He shook his head slightly. “No.”

The hand fell away. The eagerness collapsed into a melancholy indifference, as if a spirit that had rushed up to the eyes and fingers sank back again into some dark inward chamber. The man moved a little off, held out the book, and said with distant civility, “I believe Mr. Ram will be satisfied with half-a-crown, sir.”

Deronda, afterwards, could smile at the odd humiliation of it—as if he had been measured for a robe and found unworthy, dismissed with a curt permission to go. There was nothing to say. He paid his half-crown, took Salomon Maimon’s Lebensgeschichte, and left with a bare “good-morning,” carrying the book like a warm stone that had been in another man’s hand.

He felt vexed at the abrupt closing of the encounter, at the sense that he had been forbidden to know more of a man who was clearly uncommon. The contrast grew sharper as, a little later, he stood before the door of Ezra Cohen’s shop and went in.

Here the air was different—more metallic, more perfumed with cheap sweetness: the faint scent of worn velvet and old lace, of human skin warmed in close rooms, and underneath it the cold, thin smell of silver and plated ware. On the counter lay two plated stoppers and three teaspoons, spread like specimens. Behind the partition Ezra Cohen’s flourishing face hung in negotiation, glistening on its way to fatness.

Seeing Deronda enter, he called out, “Mother! Mother!” and then, with a familiar nod and smile, “Coming, sir—coming directly.”

Deronda’s eyes went anxiously toward the back door. His uneasiness was not soothed when a vigorous woman beyond fifty came in and approached. There was nothing ghastly about her; the worst one could say was that she looked as if she had made her toilet with little water and by twilight—one of those faces on which comfort has left a film and hardship a stain. Her large earrings had the look of having been slept in; rings and necklace sat on her with a sort of permanent assertion, as if she would not be stripped of ornaments even by night.

What caused Deronda’s sinking was not that she was too coarse and ugly to be Mirah’s mother, but that she was not. He did what all men do when they hunt kinship in a stranger’s face: he tried to think away the fat laid on by years, to press back time with his mind until he could glimpse the earlier outlines. The eyebrows vexed him with a similarity of line; and who can decide how far youth may be masked by uncherishing years, thrust onward in the procession where every face is altered and yet remains itself?

The good-humor of her glance shone out, motherly, as she said in a mild guttural tone, “How can I serve you, sir?”

“I should like to look at the silver clasps in the window,” said Deronda. “The larger ones, please, in the corner there.”

They were not quite easy to get at from where she stood. Ezra called out, “I’ll reach ’em, mother; I’ll reach ’em,” and ran forward with alacrity. He handed the clasps to Deronda with a smiling remark that seemed half apology, half performance.

“Mother’s too proud: she wants to do everything herself. That’s why I called her to wait on you, sir. When there’s a particular gentleman customer, sir, I daren’t do any other than call her. But I can’t let her do herself mischief with stretching.”

He made way again for his parent, and she gave a little guttural, amiable laugh, looking at Deronda as if to say, This boy will be at his jokes, but you see he’s the best son in the world. The son plainly enjoyed pleasing her, and yet wanted Deronda to understand that he himself could have served with equal readiness, if only proper ceremony allowed it.

Deronda examined the clasps as if they required long judgment. The silver had a cool, old sheen; the chased work caught the light in tiny ridges like ripples frozen. He thought, absurdly, of hands that had once closed a book with them, of prayers that might have risen while the metal rested against leather, of a language murmured that might now be dead on living tongues.

“They are only three guineas, sir,” said the mother, encouragingly.

“First-rate workmanship, sir—worth twice the money; only I get ’em a bargain from Cologne,” said Ezra, from a little distance, tossing the word “Cologne” into the air like a spice meant to improve the flavor of the sale.

Two new customers came in. Ezra’s repeated call—“Addy! Addy!”—brought from the back a small group that Deronda turned frankly to stare at, sure the stare would be taken as compliment. The group was noisy in its own way: not loud, but full of quick little sounds—the soft thump of a child set down, the rustle of skirts, the brisk inhalations and exclamations of people entering a familiar place.

A black-eyed young woman carried a black-eyed little one, its head already covered with black curls, and set it on the counter, where it looked round with more than the usual intelligence of babies, as if it listened to everything. With her came a robust boy of six and a younger girl—both black-eyed, black-ringed hair close about their faces—looking more Semitic than their parents, like young lion-cubs showing spots from far-off progenitors.

“Addy”—a bright, complacent creature in a vivid blue dress, coral necklace and earrings, her hair built up into a huge bush—looked as lively and unrefined as her husband. Her presence, by difference from the elder woman, deepened Deronda’s unwelcome impression that the mother was not so utterly common as to exclude her being Mirah’s mother.

While that thought passed like a cold draught through him, the boy stamped energetically forward into the shop and set himself about four feet from Deronda, hands deep in the pockets of his miniature knickerbockers, to look at him with a precocious air of survey—as if the child had learned already the commercial habit of measuring a stranger’s worth.

Perhaps to linger, perhaps from a sudden need to touch something innocent amid his own uneasy calculations, Deronda patted the boy’s head and said, “What is your name, sirrah?”

“Jacob Alexander Cohen,” the small man answered, with ease and distinctness.

“You are not named after your father, then?”

“No, after my grandfather; he sells knives and razors and scissors—my grandfather does,” said Jacob, anxious to impress Deronda with the grandeur of that connection. “He gave me this knife.” He drew out a pocket-knife; the small fingers, naturally and artificially dark, opened two blades and a cork-screw with quick, practiced deftness. The metal made a small, keen click that seemed too sharp for such a young hand.

“Is not that a dangerous plaything?” Deronda said, turning to the grandmother.

“_He_’ll never hurt himself, bless you!” she answered, contemplating her grandson with placid rapture.

“Have _you_ got a knife?” said Jacob, edging closer, his small voice hoarse in its glibness, as if it belonged to an aged bargaining soul that had been talking prices through generations.

“Yes. Do you want to see it?” Deronda said, taking a small penknife from his waistcoat-pocket.
