Harry Warrington told his kinswoman his plain tale of Castlewood—of rivers and rides and raw mornings in the tobacco-fields, of a house that was half manor and half village, of a mother who governed as if she had a crown tucked into her cap-strings. Madam Bernstein, bred where streets answered to bells and carriages, could scarce keep her city soul from shrinking at the notion of such country rule: days measured not by assemblies and visits, but by bread ovens, spinning-wheels, and the sickbed in a slave’s cabin. Yet Rachel Esmond—Madam Warrington to all Virginia—knew no other greatness, and wore her dominion as naturally as she wore her keys.

Nothing was done at Castlewood without the click of those keys and the brisk patter of her little shoes. If a pie was to be baked, her finger—small, quick, authoritative—must press the crimp into the edge; she would taste the custard for nutmeg and judge the fire by the smell of hickory on the air. She set the girls to their flax and wool, saw the kitchen-wenches scalding the great pans, peered into the dairy where cream thickened with a clean, sweet breath, and went herself to the still-room, that warm, herb-scented closet where pennyroyal and balm hung drying, where roses surrendered into waters, and where she compounded cordials and bitter draughts from a book of receipts as carefully kept as any prayer-book. Her medicine-chest was a legend: neighbours, hearing she had crossed the yard towards their door, would rather have borne a fever in peace than be seized upon by her resolute little hands and dosed into obedience.

In those days Virginia had few towns; each gentleman’s seat was a small polity, a cluster of cabins and barns and smokehouses about a great house, with the fields spreading like an empire. Rachel ruled Castlewood like a queen, and the neighbouring planters—some grand, many needy, most improvident—kept open gates and ragged liveries, turned away no traveller, hunted and drank and boasted of lineage, while their cellars emptied faster than their accounts could be made up. She was as hospitable as any and twice as thrifty; her table might be plentiful without being wasteful, and even her indulgences had order. Many a widower or bachelor would gladly have stepped into the place of father to her boys and master to her acres; but Rachel, who had been taught to think nobly of the Esmond blood, found no candidate equal to her idea of herself. The gossips swore she waited for a prince; and she, with grave composure, would hint that alliances had been offered to the Esmond family by persons as high as any duke.

Her chief lieutenant in this little kingdom was an Englishwoman, Fanny Parker—Mrs. Mountain now—who had stood by Rachel long ago, when they were girls at the Kensington Academy and the English pupils baited the American stranger for her proud little airs and her odd accent of the plantations. Fanny defended her then; they wrote to each other as “my Rachel” and “my Fanny,” married ensigns in the same regiment, and were knitted by that quick intimacy which youth makes and sorrow seals. Fanny’s husband died leaving debts and an infant, and Captain Franks, in one of his yearly passages, brought the widow over in the ship named the Young Rachel, as if Providence itself would not let the old friendship be stranded.

Castlewood had room enough to take in a whole new life, and Mrs. Mountain filled it with bustle and laughter. She played cards with the mistress, kept count of linen and candles, saw that strangers’ chambers were aired, and knew enough music to guide the elder boy’s fingers over a tune. She was brisk, rosy, and jolly-looking; more than one planter asked her to change Mountain for his own name. But she hugged that unlucky name close. One marriage was enough, she said: her poor Mountain had spent her little fortune and his own, and her last trinkets had gone to pay for his burial. While Rachel would keep her, she preferred a home without a husband to any that Virginia offered.

They quarrelled as regularly as they took chocolate in the morning, and made up with equal certainty. Their disputes were as domestic as bread-making: a heat, a rising, a knock-down, and then a softening. When either boy was ill, each vied in watching, brewing possets, warming bricks for cold feet, and sending a servant running for herbs. Mrs. Mountain’s cheerfulness in the Colonel’s last sickness had been dear to him; Rachel, who kept her dead husband’s memory like a relic, kept his friend’s friend too. Every year Captain Franks would ask, with a wink, whether Mrs. Mountain meant to go back to England in his ship; and every year she would say, No—one year more.

Suitors came, as suitors will to a youngish widow with broad acres. Rachel received their compliments as a queen receives embassies, and would sometimes ask, with an air of innocence that was not always innocent, whether it was she or her dependant they sought. If the gentleman persisted it was she, she would drop him her gravest curtsey and remind him the daughter of the Marquis of Esmond lived for her people and her sons, and did not propose to alter her state. Like that great Queen Elizabeth of whom she had heard tales, she liked to inspire awe and affection together. She accepted praise as her due, was kind or cruel as whim dictated, and her little foible was so well understood that wags practised upon it: Rattling Jack Firebrace lived for months at Castlewood on free quarters because he brought her verses—stolen, it was said, from other men’s pocket-books. Tom Humbold of Spotsylvania won a wager of hogsheads by persuading her to institute an order of knighthood, with Castlewood ribbons and ridiculous ceremonies, and she, delighted, played her part as if she had been born to dubbing.

George, the elder twin, watched these antics with a hot, silent indignation. Even as a child he revolted at the incense offered to his mother and tried, with sharp young wit, to prick the flatterers’ bubbles. Rachel, offended yet secretly pleased, would say gravely that the Esmonds were a jealous race and her boy took after his grandsire. George hated Jack Firebrace and Humbold and their crew; Harry, on the contrary, went fowling and fishing and cock-fighting with them, and took all the rough mirth of the country as naturally as he took corn-bread with bacon.

After the first tutor was dismissed, Rachel carried the boys to Williamsburg for such schooling as the place could give. There they heard the famous Mr. Whitfield, whose trumpet voice shook the drowsy colony where the established clergy—paid by the State, living on glebes, imported from England as one might import tools—were too often neither saints nor orators. Men fled from bailiffs and creditors and brought their stained cassocks across the sea in hope of a safe living. No wonder Whitfield’s thunder woke people whom mild Mr. Broadbent never stirred. Rachel and the boys were kindled alike. They sang hymns, they waited for the sacred “call” as if it might come like a sudden gust through the pines at night. Had Whitfield lingered, both lads might have worn black coats instead of epaulettes one day.

But Whitfield’s mission was to sweep the continent, not to nest in one little congregation. He went, leaving Rachel letters that she hoarded, and promising to send her a tutor who should teach not only “profane learning” but confirm them in a precious science.

In time such a “chosen vessel” arrived from England—young Mr. Ward, a handsome fellow with a voice like a market bell and a tongue that could run as readily as a mill-race. Morning and evening the hall rang with his exhortations. The very soundscape of Castlewood changed under him: where once you heard only the scrape of chairs, the clink of trenchers, the low talk of servants, and the outdoor chorus of birds beyond the windows, now there rose a ceaseless religious declamation, and beneath it the hum and shuffle of listeners. The house-servants—black faces and crisp heads—crept to doorways and crowded the porch windows; and when Ward gave out a hymn there burst such a rolling negro chorus as might have been heard across the Potomac—deep, antiphonal, swelling and falling like wind in a pinewood. It was strange music, at once homely and ominous: a new power taking possession of familiar rooms. In the Colonel’s time no such thing had been endured; he distrusted cassocks and said he would never have controversy with a clergyman except over backgammon. Yet the Colonel had been charitable where money was needed, and the easy parson who loved dice and boards would say charity covered other shortcomings.

Rachel drank Ward’s sermons as she drank her cordials—eagerly, credulously, sure they cured whatever ailed her. Women, a century before, had fewer defences against admiration; they were trained to believe, and belief, once kindled, could blaze. For weeks and months she never tired of Ward’s great glib voice and his commonplaces, and would summon neighbours to listen as she summoned them to dinner, ordering their conversions as she ordered their dishes. She was especially anxious to influence her young favourite, Mr. Washington; but that tall, handsome gentleman found, with a soldier’s punctuality, that particular business always called him away when the hour of Ward’s exercises drew near, and he would order his horse.

The twins grew quickly weary and then rebellious. Ward, for all his volume, was a bad scholar. George knew more Latin and Greek than his master and caught him in blunders and false quantities. Harry—bolder, permitted liberties denied to his elder—mimicked Ward’s way of chewing and talking until Mrs. Mountain laughed outright and even Rachel’s stern mouth twitched; little Fanny Mountain would crow as if it were a game. Rachel might have discovered Ward for a vulgar quack but for the very opposition that should have opened her eyes. Her sons’ contempt only hardened her will. What mattered profane learning, she cried, when he taught the one precious book? Rough manners proved Heaven did not choose its elect from the great and wealthy. It was wicked pride—the pride of all the Esmonds—that made them resist. They must pray to be corrected.

Ward, that evening, discoursed on Naaman the Syrian and the pride of Abana and Pharpar against Jordan—making himself, without shame, the keeper of the healing waters and the boys the conceited sufferers who must bathe in his stream or perish. George listened with a face too still, and something began in him—an inherited gift perhaps, a quiet, skilful sarcasm which, once used, can make a whole household miserable. He took up Ward’s pompous sayings and turned them into jokes; Ward chafed, and his great meals sat ill on him. Rachel grew angrier the more George’s small smiles set Harry laughing. Her authority was mocked, her officer scorned, her younger son “perverted,” she said, by the obstinate elder. She resolved on a desperate attempt to restore command.

The boys were fourteen. Harry, taller and forward, already had the look of a young man; George was delicate, childlike beside him, with a fine, pale face that could look proud and hurt at once. In those days the cane was a common argument: sergeants, schoolmasters, overseers used it as readily as speech. The boys had been “horsed” often enough by Mr. Dempster, their old Scotch tutor, in their grandfather’s time. But since the Colonel’s death the rod had been laid aside; the young gentlemen of Castlewood had tasted the sweetness of licence. Now Rachel, feeling her command slipping, took counsel with Ward, who could find chapter and verse for any severity he desired. He had begun with flattery—snug quarters are agreeable to a man with no estate—but the boys laughed at his compliments, despised his bad breeding, yawned at his sermons; and the more Rachel favoured him, the more they hated him.

Mrs. Mountain, who loved the lads—especially George, whom she thought unjustly treated—warned them in the pantry one day as she oversaw the slicing of ham and the setting out of a cold pie whose crust gave off a nutty, buttery smell. The kitchen noises—the thump of a cleaver, the hiss of a pot-lid—made a safer cover for speech than the echoing hall where Ward’s voice prowled like a watchdog.

“Ward is more obsequious than ever to your mamma,” she whispered, her nostrils flaring as if his flattery had an odour. “It turns my stomach, it does, to see him gobble. Be on your guard, my poor boys. Learn your lessons. Don’t anger him. Mischief will come—I know it will. Your mamma was talking about you to Mr. Washington t’other day when I stepped in.”

“I don’t like that Major Washington, you know I don’t,” she added, then checked herself as Harry began to protest. “Don’t say, ‘O Mounty!’ You always stand up for your friends. He is handsome and tall, and may be very good, but he is much too old a young man for me. Bless you, the wild oats your father sowed and my poor Mountain when they were ensigns in Kingsley’s would fill sacks full. Show me Mr. Washington’s wild oats—not a grain! I came in, and I’m sure they were speaking of you, for he said: ‘Discipline is discipline, and must be preserved. There can be but one command in a house, ma’am, and you must be mistress of yours.’”

“The very words he used to me,” Harry cried. “He told me he did not like to meddle, but mother was dangerously angry, he said, and begged me to obey Mr. Ward, and to press George to do so.”

“Let him manage his own house, not mine,” George said, haughtily; and the warning, instead of softening him, made him more stiff.

Next day the storm broke. In morning study words passed between George and Ward; the boy was insubordinate, and even Harry cried out and owned his brother wrong. Ward “kept his temper”—that is, he corked it down and promised it should burst later—and said he would speak to Madam Esmond. At dinner, when the meats had been carved and the room still held the savour of peppered gravy and roasted fowl, Ward requested her ladyship to remain. Temperately, he laid his complaint before her.

He called on Harry to confirm him, and poor Harry, trapped between truth and loyalty, admitted the dominie’s statements. George, standing under his grandfather’s portrait by the chimney, said stiffly that what Mr. Ward had reported was correct.

“To be tutor to such a pupil is absurd,” Ward began, and launched into one of his long speeches, thick with Scripture phrases. At each solemn citation George smiled—small, scornful—and made a faint pish of contempt. Ward ended by asking leave to retire.

“Not before you have punished this wicked and disobedient child,” Rachel said. Her anger had been gathering through Ward’s harangue, and it flared hottest at George’s demeanour.

“Punish?” George repeated, as if the word were in a foreign tongue.

“Yes, sir—punish! If means of love and entreaty fail, as they have with your proud heart, other means must bring you to obedience. I punish you now to guard you from greater punishment hereafter. The discipline of this family must be maintained. There can be but one command in a house, and I must be the mistress of mine. You will punish this refractory boy, Mr. Ward, as we have agreed; and if there is the least resistance, my overseer and servants shall lend you aid.”

She spoke with vehemence, seasoning her private wrath with sacred texts as if Heaven were her witness and ally in a domestic quarrel. This habit—dragging the thunder of Scripture into the narrow room of family—was a new language Rachel had learned under her impetuous guide; and if it gave her power, it also thinned something in her, like a sauce reduced too far until it turns bitter and catches at the throat.

In the midst of it George felt his own fault. He grew very white. “There can be but one command in the house,” he said slowly, “and you must be mistress—I know who said those words before you.” He swallowed. “And I know, mother, that I have acted wrongly to Mr. Ward.”

“He owns it! He asks pardon!” Harry cried, catching at hope as at a knife’s edge. “That’s right, George! That’s enough—isn’t it?”

“No, it is not enough!” Rachel answered, sharp as vinegar. “The disobedient boy must pay the penalty. When I was headstrong before my spirit was humbled, my mamma punished me, and I submitted. So must George. Do your duty, Mr. Ward.”

“Stop, mother—you don’t quite know what you are doing,” George said, his voice shaken.

“I know that he who spares the rod spoils the child,” Rachel replied, and George heard her as if from far off, pale and desperate.

On the mantelpiece beneath the Colonel’s portrait stood a china cup Rachel prized above any in her cupboard because her father had always drunk from it. It was part of the house’s memory—thin, bright porcelain that had crossed seas, held lips long dead, and still carried, if you lifted it close, a faint ghost of tea and smoke and old cedar. George’s hand went to it. A strange smile crossed his white face.

“Stay one minute. Don’t go away yet,” he cried as Rachel turned. “You—you are very fond of this cup, mother?” Harry stared, not understanding. “If I broke it, it could never be mended, could it? All the tinkers’ rivets would not make it whole again. My dear old grandpapa’s cup! I have been wrong. Mr. Ward, I ask pardon. I will try and amend.”

Rachel looked at him with indignation that was almost scorn. “I thought,” she said, “I thought an Esmond had been more of a man than to be afraid, and—”

She gave a little scream. Harry cried out and sprang forward, hands flung wide.

George lifted the cup, opened his fingers, and let it fall. It struck the marble slab below with a clean, cruel sound—porcelain shattering into bright fragments. The room seemed to hold its breath. Harry’s hands closed on nothing.

“It is too late, Hal,” George said quietly. “You will never mend that again—never. Now, mother, I am ready, as it is your wish. Will you come and see whether I am afraid? Mr. Ward, I am your servant. Your servant? Your slave! And the next time I meet Mr. Washington, madam, I will thank him for the advice he gave you.”

“I say, do your duty, sir!” Rachel cried, stamping her little foot. George bowed low to Ward and begged him to go first to the study.

“Stop! For God’s sake, mother, stop!” Harry pleaded. But passion boiled in Rachel like a pot left too long on a fierce fire; it had burned past listening. “You only abet him, sir!” she cried. “If I had to do it myself, it should be done!” And Harry, with wrath and sorrow set in his face, followed them out.

Rachel sank into a great chair near the door and stared at the cup’s broken pieces as if they were bones. The house had gone oddly silent: no hymn, no lesson, only the distant creak of a board, the far-off chirr of insects outside, the faint clatter from the kitchen where life, indifferent, went on with plates and knives. She inclined her head toward the carved mahogany door—one of the Colonel’s European purchases—listening as if her ear could catch and translate what her will had set in motion.

For a moment there was nothing. Then a loud outcry—raw, human—made her start as if struck.

In another instant Mr. Ward burst out, blood streaming from a great wound on his head; behind him came Harry with eyes aflame, brandishing a little couteau-de-chasse of his grandfather’s that had hung among the Colonel’s weapons on the library wall.

“I don’t care. I did it,” Harry said. “I couldn’t see this fellow strike my brother; and, as he lifted his hand, I flung the great ruler at him. I couldn’t help it. I won’t bear it; and if one lifts a hand to me or my brother, I’ll have his life,” he shouted, shaking the blade.
