Denys, positioned squarely between his companions to prevent any desperate flight, was borne away in a torment of grief and self-reproach. For his sake, Gerard had forsaken the German road to Rome; and what recompense awaited him? To be cast adrift, utterly alone in the heart of Burgundy. This thought drove Denys to madness, causing him to rage against the heavens and the earth, or to lapse into a silence so dark and menacing that his captors deemed it wise to subdue him. They seized their leader just beyond the town walls, and the entire cavalcade halted at the "Tête d'Or" to rest.

The young landlady, though preoccupied with the Count and even more so with the Bastard, spotted Denys and queried him anxiously regarding the fate of his youthful companion. With a sudden outburst of sorrow, Denys recounted everything, imploring her to dispatch someone in search of Gerard. "Now that he is severed from me, he may yet heed my counsel," he declared. "Poor wretch, he cannot bear solitude."

The landlady shook her head dismissively. "I believe I have already shown you too much kindness," she replied, flushing slightly, before turning her back.

"You will not?" Denys pressed.

"Not I."

"Then," he erupted, unleashing a torrent of curses and invectives upon her.

She wheeled away, retreating while whimpering that she was unused to such treatment, and immediately ordered her servant to saddle two mules. Denys rode north with his troop, head bowed and spirit crushed, unaware that the truthful young woman had donned her riding gear in a mere forty minutes, driven by urgency, and spurred off with her servant in the exact opposite direction.

By nightfall, after a grueling march, the Bastard and his men arrived at "The White Hart." Their entrance stirred a tremendous commotion, and it took some time before Manon discerned her old friend amidst the crowd. When she finally recognized him, her reaction was marked only by a deepening flush; she made no move to acknowledge the acquaintance. The poor soul had already begun to despise her.

"The base steps by which she climbed."

Denys observed her but could not muster a smile. The inn brought memories of Gerard too sharply to the forefront.

Before night fully fell, the wind shifted. Manon entered the room and beckoned him with a finger. He rose sullenly, followed by his guards.

"Nay, I wish to speak a word to thee in private."

She led him to a corner of the chamber and whispered, asking if he would grant her a favor.

He answered aloud, "No, I would not; I am not in the mood to bestow kindnesses on man or woman. If I were to show kindness, it should be to a dog; and even then, only if I could help it."

"Alas, good archer, I once did you a kindness, you and your pretty comrade," Manon said humbly.

"You did, dame, you did; well then, for his sake—what is required?"

"You know my tale. I was unfortunate. Now I am honored. But a woman cast him in my teeth this day. And so he shall hang there forever. I would have him taken down; alas!"

"With all my heart."

"And none dare I ask but thee. Wilt thou do it?"

"Not I, even were I not a prisoner."

Upon this stern refusal, the tender Manon sighed and clasped her palms together in despair. Denys assured her she need not fret. There were soldiers of a lower caliber who would not hesitate to take two bites out of such a cherry. It was merely a matter of coin; if she could provide two angels, he would find two soldiers to perform the dirty work at "The White Hart."

This proposition was not entirely palatable. Yet, reflecting that soldiers were transient creatures, drinking here tonight and knocked on the head tomorrow, she whispered softly, "Send them out to me. But pray, tell them it is for one who is my friend; let them not think it is for me; I should sink into the earth; times have changed."

Denys found warriors eager to win an angel apiece so easily. He dispatched them and instantly dismissed the subject with contempt, sitting instead to brood over his lost friend.

Manon and the warriors soon reached a general understanding. But what were they to do with the body once removed? She murmured, "The river is near—the place."

"Fling him in, eh?"

"Nay, nay; be not so cruel! Could ye not place him—gently—and—with somewhat weighty?"

She must have been contemplating the matter in detail, for she was not one to whom ideas came swiftly.

All was quickly agreed upon, save for the timing of payment. The mail-clad warriors itched for it and sought it in advance. Manon hesitated at that.

"What? Did she doubt their word?" Then let her come along with them, or watch them from a distance.

"Me?" Manon exclaimed with horror. "I would sooner die than witness it done."

"Which yet you would have done."

"Ay, for sore is my need. Times have changed."

She had already forgotten her precept to Denys.

An hour later, the disagreeable relic of caterpillar existence ceased to fester the worshipful matron's public life, and the grim eyes of the past ceased to cast malignant glances down into the white hind's clover field.

In total, she made the landlord an average wife, a prime house-dog, and outlived everyone.

Her troops, upon returning from executing with medieval naivety the precept "Off wi' the auld love," received a shock. They found the market-place black with groups; it had been empty an hour prior. Conscience struck them. This was the result of meddling with the dead. However, the bolder of the two, encouraged by the darkness, stole forward alone and slyly mingled with a group. He soon returned to his companion, saying in a tone of reproach that was not strictly reasonable,

"You born fool, it is only a miracle."
