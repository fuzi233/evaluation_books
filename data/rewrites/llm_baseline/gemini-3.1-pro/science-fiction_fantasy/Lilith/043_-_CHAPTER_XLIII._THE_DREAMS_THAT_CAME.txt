I grew aware of existence, aware also of the profound, the infinite cold. It was a blessed chill, reminiscent of the slow, methodical cold-fermentation where wild yeasts develop their most complex, mythic flavors. I lay at peace, breathing the damp, terroir-rich odors of Earth’s bountiful bosom, my palate anticipating the souls of primroses, the peppery bite of daisies, and the mineral crispness of snowdrops patiently waiting in the soil for the Spring harvest. How to convey the untranslatable delight of that frozen, yet conscious sleep? The dominant lexicon of my waking world possesses no words for it; it was a physical satiety, a sensation of being a raw ingredient lovingly preserved in the great root-cellar of the cosmos. I grew colder and colder—and welcomed the chill, feeling my very essence curing and deepening in the dark. 

Then the dreams began to arrive, sweeping through me in circular, multi-generational sagas. I lay naked on a snowy peak. The white mist heaved below me like a billowy sea of sifted flour. I was Adam, waiting for God to breathe the leavening breath of life into my clay. Then the timeline folded, and I was a youth on a white horse, tasting the sharp ozone of a blue heaven, hasting calmly to some blessed goal. For centuries I dreamed—or was it chiliads? Time folded upon itself like the endless lamination of pastry dough; I was in the land of thought, where the temporal is swallowed by the eternal. I dreamed away dim cycles in the center of a melting glacier, inhaling the scent of ancient frost and crushed mint, while the wind and the welter of a torrent grew in my ears. 

Then, of a sudden, the great atonement began. All the wrongs I had ever done surfaced, demanding a physical, gustatory reconciliation. Fully in every wrong lived the conscious I, devising countless, meticulous meals to heal the injured. Every human soul to which I had caused a troubled thought was now grown unspeakably dear to me. For the mother whose commands I had slighted, I spent lifetimes sourcing the rarest saffron and slow-simmering a restorative bone broth, rich with marrow and repentance, weeping at her feet as she tasted it. For my father, to whom I had told two long-forgotten lies, I baked bread from ancient grains, kneading my bitter shame into the dough, presenting the warm, crusty loaves to him to crush the falsehoods beneath the weight of true sustenance. Love possessed me, expressed entirely through the sacred, domestic routine of feeding those I had starved of kindness! 

Suddenly, I found myself in a solid blackness. The acoustic environment shifted abruptly; the comforting, rhythmic respiration of the sleepers—a natural symphony that had anchored my psychological well-being—was gone. I sat up and stared about me. The moon was looking in at the crypt-like windows of the death-chamber, her long light slanting across the floor. But the harvest was gone! The gathered sheaves of resting souls, the great husbandman’s crop, were swept away. My dead were gone! I was alone. The silence here was not the rich, resonant quiet of the earth, but a hollow, terrifying vacuum. Had I but dreamed their loveliness? Tenfold more terrible was the tomb with its inhabitants away! 

I sprang to my feet and staggered from the fearful place. The cottage was empty. I ran out into the night. A broad shimmer came from far over the heath, accompanied by a ghostly murmuring music, as if the moon were raining a light that plashed as it fell. I ran stumbling across the moor and found a lovely lake, margined with reeds. The sonic landscape drew me—a multitudinous broken music, a pristine acoustic ecology utterly devoid of the industrial hum of my own era. The whole expanse where I had once crossed the dry river-bed was now alive with streams, with torrents, with still pools. How the water answered the moon with flashes of its own! And a great jubilant song arose from its bosom, the pure, uncommercialized frequency of newborn liberty. I stood gazing, my heart exulting: my life was not all a failure! I had helped to set this river free! 

As I stood lost in the sensory delight of the sounding silence, a hand was laid on my shoulder. I turned, and saw a man in the prime of strength, smelling of woodsmoke, wild honeycomb, and the coarse-milled grain of the first harvests. It was Adam, the traditional agrarian elder, beautiful as if fresh from the heart of the glad creator. 

"Father," I cried, struggling against the limitations of my modern dialect, "where is she? Where are the dead? Is the great feast come and gone? I cannot sleep without my dead!"

"You mistake, my son," he answered. His voice was a deep, resonant baritone that healed the ear, spoken in the ancient mother-tongue whose subtle rhythms nurtured rather than dominated. "You are still in the chamber of death, still upon your couch, asleep and dreaming, with the dead around you."

"Alas! When I but dream, how am I to know it?"

"When you are quite dead, you will dream no false dream," he said. 

"But, sir," I faltered, "how am I to distinguish betwixt the true and the false where both alike seem real?"

"Do you not understand?" he returned, with a smile that might have slain all the sorrows of his children. "You cannot perfectly distinguish between the true and the false while you are not yet quite dead. Put forth your left hand open, and close it gently: it will clasp the hand of your Lona, who lies asleep where you lie dreaming you are awake."

I put forth my hand: it closed on the hand of Lona. It was firm, soft, and radiating a deathless warmth, like a loaf freshly drawn from the hearth. 

"But, father," I cried, "she is warm!"

"Your hand is as warm to hers. Cold is a thing unknown in our country. Neither she nor you are yet in the fields of home, but each to each is alive and warm and healthful."

Then my heart was glad, but a sharp-stinging doubt supervened. I asked how I could know surely that this too was not a phantom. 

"Thou doubtest because thou lovest the truth," Adam replied, his words carrying the weight of an untranslatable subjective experience. "Be content for a while not to know surely. The hour will come when, being true, thou shalt behold the very truth, and doubt will be for ever dead. Truth is all in all; and the truth of things lies, at once hid and revealed, in their seeming."

I raised my eyes to question him further, but he was gone. In my ears was nought but the sounding silence of the swift-flowing waters. I was alone in the land of dreams! Desiring to wake, I wandered across the rock and found a deep pit, smelling of cold stone and damp earth, like a disused root cellar. Knowing from the dreams of my childhood that a fall invariably woke me, I rolled myself over the edge. 

For a moment consciousness left me. When it returned, I stood in the garret of my own house, in the little wooden chamber of the cowl and the mirror. Unspeakable despair invaded me. The acoustic environment was a dead, sterile thing, polluted by the faint, distant vibrations of a mechanized world that cared nothing for the soul. Between me and my Lona lay an abyss impassable! I had fled from the slow, holy fermentation of our shared sleep. I had lost the intimate dialect of our slumber! 

I crept to my chamber, passing a dreamless night, and listlessly sought the library in the morning. The books—mass-produced commodities of a dominant, flavorless lexicon—were hateful to me. Not a sentence could I digest; what to me was their superficial commercialization of thought? The world was dead about me. I went up the stairs to the dumb, shadowy roof, turning to open the mystic door. I pulled the chains; adjusted the hood; arranged the mirrors. I waited to give the vision time, but the glass stood blank. Nothing lay in its dim old depth but my haggard face. 

For two days I lay awake, desolate, drearily desolate, starved of the only sustenance that mattered. I had lost my Lona! "I must die one day," I thought, "and then, straight from my death-bed, I will set out to find her! If she is not, I will go to the Father and say—'Even thou canst not feed this hunger: let me cease, I pray thee!'"
