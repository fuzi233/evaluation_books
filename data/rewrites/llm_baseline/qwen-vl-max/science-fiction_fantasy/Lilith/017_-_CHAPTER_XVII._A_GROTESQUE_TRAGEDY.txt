I had not taken more than ten steps when something odd caught my eye. I drew closer, curious, and found myself before an ancient carriage—rotted and crumbling, yet still standing upright on its massive wheels. On either side of the pole, where horses once pulled it forward, lay two skeletal steeds, their heads raised like grim sentinels. From those hollow skulls, shriveled reins stretched upward to the bony hand of a coachman seated stiffly upon his tattered seat. The doors were gone; inside, two skeletons sat slumped in opposite corners.

As I watched, they stirred. With a dry rattle of brittle bones, each lurched from the door beside them. One collapsed instantly, lying motionless. The other staggered upright, joints grinding as if rusted shut, and slowly crept along the side of the carriage, gripping the frame for support. Its thin leg bones trembled under its weight. At last, it knelt beside the fallen one and tried to lift it—nearly falling itself in the effort.

After a long struggle, the prostrate skeleton rose into a sitting position. It turned its yellow skull this way and that, then, ignoring its companion, grasped the spokes of the rear wheel and pulled itself half to its feet. There it stood, back turned, hands clutching one knee joint as if it might fall apart at any moment.

The kneeling figure rose next, with great difficulty, and addressed the other.

“Have you hurt yourself, my lord?” it asked, voice distant and muffled, as though carried by a wind that did not belong to this world.

“Yes,” came the reply, rougher and more strained. “You would do nothing to help me—and now this cursed knee is out!”

“I did my best, my lord.”

“No doubt, my lady—but it was bad! I thought I’d never stand again. Bless my soul—madam—are you out in your bones?”

She glanced down at herself.

“I have nothing else to be out in,” she replied. “And you, at least, cannot complain. But what does it mean? Am I dreaming?”

“You may be dreaming,” he said, “but this knee forbids me the illusion. Ha! I see—I too have nothing to walk in but bones. Not so unbecoming for a man, however. I trust these aren’t *my* bones—every one aches worse than the last, and this loose knee worst of all. The bed must’ve been damp—and I far too drunk to know it.”

“Probably, my lord of Cokayne.”

“What? What?!” He stared at her. “You make me think I’m dreaming—aches and all! How do you know the name my bullies used to call me? I don’t remember you!”

“Anyhow,” she said, “you have no right to take liberties. My name is—well, I am lord—tut, tut! What do you call me when I’m… when you’re sober? I can’t recall. Why—what *is* my name? I must’ve been very drunk when I went to bed. Often am.”

“You come so seldom to mine,” she said, “that I don’t know. But I’ll take your word for that.”

“I hope so.”

“If for nothing else.”

“Hoity toity! I never told you a lie in my life.”

“You never told me anything but lies.”

“Upon my honour—why, I never saw the woman before!”

“You knew me well enough to lie to, my lord.”

“I do seem to dream I’ve met you before—but upon my oath, there’s nothing to recognize you by! Out of your clothes—who knows who you might be? One thing I *can* swear—never saw you so undressed before! By heaven, I have no recollection of you!”

“I’m glad to hear it. My recollections of you are less distasteful. Good morning, my lord.”

She turned away, hobbled a few paces, clacking softly, then stopped.

“You’re just as heartless as—any other woman, madam!” he called after her. “Where in this hell shall I find my valet? What was that cursed fool’s name?”

He twisted his bare skull on its creaking neck, still clutching his knee.

“I’ll be your valet for once,” she said, turning back. “What can I do for you? It’s not easy to tell.”

“Tie my leg on, of course, you fool! Can’t you see it’s nearly off? Heigho, my dancing days!”

She searched the ground with her empty eyes and found a piece of tough grass. She wound it tightly around the shattered joint.

When she finished, he tested it with cautious stamps.

“You used to stamp rather differently, my lord,” she remarked, rising.

“Eh? What?” He looked at her again. “Now I think of it—it seems I used to hate you!”

“Naturally, my lord. You hated many people—your wife, of course, among them.”

“Oh, I begin to remember… I must have been somewhere a long time. Really forget—there! Your damned bit of grass is breaking!”

“We used to get on pretty well together, eh?”

“Not that I recall, my lord. The only happy moments I had with you were scattered over the first week of our marriage.”

“Was that the way of it? Ha, ha! Well, it’s over now, thank goodness.”

“I wish I could believe it. Why were we sitting in that carriage? It wakes fear.”

“I think we were divorced, my lady.”

“Hardly enough. We’re still together.”

“A sad truth—but capable of remedy. The forest seems extensive.”

“I doubt it. I doubt.”

“I’m sorry I can’t think of a compliment to pay you—without lying. Judging by your figure and complexion, you’ve lived hard since I last saw you. I can’t surely be quite so naked as your ladyship! I beg your pardon, madam—I trust you’ll take it as jesting in a dream. It matters little, though. Dreaming or waking, all’s appearance. You can’t be certain of anything—and that’s as good as knowing there’s nothing. Life teaches even fools that.”

“It has taught me the fool I was to love you.”

“You weren’t the only one. Women fell for me—had a trick of it. I’d forgotten you were one.”

“I did love you, my lord—a little—once.”

“Ah, there was your mistake, my lady. You should have loved me much—devotedly, savagely—eternally. Then I’d have tired of you sooner—and not hated you so bitterly afterward. But let bygones be bygones. Where are we? Locality is the question. To be or not to be is NOT the question.”

“We are in the other world, I presume.”

“Granted. But which other world? This can’t be hell.”

“It must be. There’s marriage here. You and I are damned in each other.”

“Then I’m not like Othello—damned in a fair wife. Oh, I remember my Shakespeare, madam.”

She picked up a broken branch from a bush, balanced it in her hand, and walked away, tossing her tiny skull.

“Give that stick to me!” he cried. “I need it more than you.”

She gave no answer.

“You mean to make me beg?”

“Not at all, my lord. I mean to keep it.”

She continued her slow retreat.

“Give it me at once! I want it! I require it!”

“Unfortunately, I think I require it myself,” she replied, walking faster now, her joints cracking sharper, bones clinking.

He lunged after her—but stumbled. His knee-grass had burst. He swore and grabbed his leg.

“Come and tie it properly!” he shouted—but only a weak whistle escaped his mouth.

She turned.

“Come and tie it up instantly!” he repeated.

She took another step.

“I swear I will not touch you!” he roared.

“Swear on, my lord. No one’s here to believe you. But pray—don’t lose your temper. Or you’ll shake yourself to pieces. And where to find string enough to bind all your crazy joints? I can’t say.”

She returned, knelt again—first placing the disputed stick beyond his reach.

The moment she re-tied his knee, he lunged for her, fingers clawing at her hair. They slipped off the smooth bone.

“Disgusting,” he muttered, seizing her upper arm-bone instead.

“You’ll break it!” she warned, looking up.

“I will, then!” he snapped, wrenching.

“I won’t tie your leg again next time it comes loose!” she threatened.

He twisted her arm violently—but her bones held better than his.

She reached toward the broken branch.

“That’s right—hand me the stick!” he grinned.

She swung it with such force that a bone in her sound leg snapped.

He fell, choking with curses.

She laughed.

“Now you’ll have to wear splints always,” she said. “Such dry bones never mend.”

“You devil!” he spat.

“At your service, my lord. Shall I fetch you a couple of wheel-spokes? Neat—but heavy, I fear.”

He turned his bony face away. He didn’t answer. Only groaned.

I marveled he hadn’t shattered on impact.

She rose and walked away—not without grace, I thought.

“What can come of this?” I murmured. “They’re too wretched for any world—and this can’t be hell. The Little Ones are here. The sleepers too. What does it mean? Can anything ever be right for skeletons?”

“There are words too big for you and me,” a voice whispered near me. “ALL is one. EVER is another.”

I looked around—but saw no one.

“You are not in hell,” the voice continued.

“Neither am I,” I replied. “But those skeletons are.”

As I spoke, I saw the raven perched on a beech branch above. In the same breath, it dropped—landed on the ground—and became the thin old man from the library, long-nosed, long-coated.

“The male was never a gentleman,” he said. “In this bony stage of decay, with his skeleton showing through his skin, and his character outside his manners—he doesn’t look like one. The female is less vulgar. Has a little heart. But with society’s restraints gone—here they are: just as they were, and always were.”

“Tell me, Mr. Raven,” I said, “what will become of them?”

“We shall see. In their day, they were the handsomest couple at court. Even now, in their dry bones, they cling to that old reputation. To see their faces again—may yet change them. They felt rich while they had pockets—but already feel pinched. My lord once saw my lady as a worthless burden—tired of her beauty, spent her money. Now he needs her to mend his joints. These changes hold roots of hope. Besides—they can’t go far from each other. No others of their kind nearby. They’ll grow weary of hatred—and begin to love. For love, not hate, is deepest in what Love ‘loved into being.’”

“I saw many more like them an hour ago—in the hall nearby.”

“Of their kind—but not of their sort,” he replied. “For years, these two will see none such as you saw last night. Those are centuries ahead. You saw them dressing themselves—barely. They can’t keep clothes long—only part of the night. But they’re growing steadily stronger. Soon they’ll develop faces. Every grain of truth adds a thread to their humanity. Nothing but truth can appear. Whatever is—must seem.”

“Are they upheld by hope?”

“They are upheld by hope—but they don’t know it. To understand it is still immeasurably beyond them.”

His sudden presence surprised me not at all. I was like a child—always wondering, never astonished.

“Did you come to find me, sir?” I asked.

“Not at all,” he said. “I have no anxiety about you. Such as you always return to us.”

“Then please—tell me—who am I, such as?”

“I cannot make my friend the subject of conversation,” he smiled.

“But when that friend is present?” I urged.

“I decline more strongly.”

“But when that friend asks?”

“Then most positively I refuse.”

“Why?”

“Because he and I would be talking of two persons—as if they were one. Your consciousness of yourself—and my knowledge of you—are worlds apart.”

His coat flared open—lapels flying, lappets lifting. I thought I saw the transformation of HOMO to CORVUS unfolding before me.

But the coat closed again.

And he added, almost casually:

“In this world—never trust a person who has once deceived you. Above all—never do anything such a one may ask you to do.”

“I’ll try to remember,” I said. “But I may forget.”

“Then some evil that is good for you will follow.”

“And if I remember?”

“Some evil that is not good for you—will not follow.”

The old man seemed to sink into the earth.

And in an instant, I saw the raven—several yards away—flying low and fast.
