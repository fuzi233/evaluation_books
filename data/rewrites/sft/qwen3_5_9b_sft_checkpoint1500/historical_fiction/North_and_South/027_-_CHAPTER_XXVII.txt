Mr. Thornton approached the business of the following day with unerring clarity. A slight demand for finished goods presented itself, and he seized upon it, driving hard bargains with the precision of a clockmaker. His performance at the meeting of the magistrates was equally sharp; he offered his strongest sense and his unique ability to perceive consequences instantly, leading to rapid decisions. While older men, those of greater wealth and established standing, looked to him for wisdom, he remained indifferent to their deference, regarding it no more than the soft west wind that barely disturbed the smoke rising from Milton's great chimneys. Unaware of the silent respect paid to him, he focused solely on the swift accomplishment of his duties. To him, the whispers circulating among the womenfolk of the town—that if he had not been present, matters would have gone much worse—were merely background noise. He swept aside his business with relentless efficiency. It appeared that the deep mortification of the previous day, followed by hours of stunned inaction, had finally burned away the mists from his intellect. He felt his power and reveled in it, capable of defying his own heart. Had he known it, he could have sung the song of the miller living by the river Dee: "I care for nobody—Nobody cares for me."

Before him sat the evidence against Boucher and other ringleaders of the riot; the charges against three others, accused of conspiracy, failed. Yet, he sternly charged the police to remain vigilant, insisting that the swift arm of the law stand ready to strike the moment proof of guilt emerged. Leaving the hot, reeking room of the borough court, he stepped out into the fresher, yet still sultry, streets. Suddenly, he seemed to give way; a profound languor overtook him, and his thoughts wandered inevitably to *her*. They recalled not the scene of his recent repulsion, but the looks and actions of the day before. He walked mechanically through the crowded thoroughfares, weaving in and out among the populace without truly seeing them, nearly sick with a longing for that brief, halcyon hour when she had clung to him and her heart beat against his.

"Why, Mr. Thornton! you're cutting me very coolly, I must say. And how is Mrs. Thornton? Brave weather this! We doctors don't like it, I can tell you!"

"I beg your pardon, Dr. Donaldson. I really didn't see you. My mother's quite well, thank you. It is a fine day, and good for the harvest, I hope. If the wheat is well got in, we shall have a brisk trade next year, whatever you doctors have."

"Ay, ay. Each man for himself. Your bad weather, and your bad times, are my good ones. When trade is bad, there's more undermining of health, and preparation for death, going on among you Milton men than you're aware of."

"Not with me, Doctor. I'm made of iron. The news of the very worst bad debt I ever had, never made my pulse vary. This strike, which affects me more than any one else in Milton,—more than Hamper,—never comes near my appetite. You must go elsewhere for a patient, Doctor."

"By the way, you've recommended me a good patient, poor lady! Not to go on talking in this heartless way, I seriously believe that Mrs. Hale—that lady in Crampton, you know—hasn't many weeks to live. I never had any hope of cure, as I think I told you; but I've been seeing her to-day, and I think very badly of her."

Mr. Thornton fell silent. For an instant, the vaunted steadiness of his pulse faltered.

"Can I do anything, Doctor?" he asked, his voice altered. "You know—you would see, that money is not very plentiful, are there any comforts or dainties she ought to have?"

"No," replied the Doctor, shaking his head. "She craves for fruit,—she has a constant fever on her; but jargonelle pears will do as well as anything, and there are quantities of them in the market."

"You will tell me if there is anything I can do, I'm sure," replied Mr. Thornton. "I rely upon you."

"Oh! never fear! I'll not spare your purse,—I know it's deep enough. I wish you'd give me a carte-blanche for all my patients, and all their wants."

Yet Mr. Thornton possessed no general benevolence, nor universal philanthropy; few would have credited him with strong affections. However, he proceeded directly to the first fruit-shop in Milton. With meticulous care, he selected a bunch of purple grapes bearing the most delicate bloom, the richest-colored peaches, and vines with the freshest leaves. These treasures were packed into a basket, and the shopman awaited the answer to his inquiry, "Where shall we send them to, sir?"

There was no reply. "To Marlborough Mills, I suppose, sir?"

"No!" Mr. Thornton said abruptly. "Give the basket to me,—I'll take it."

The burden required both his hands to carry, forcing him to navigate the busiest section of the town where ladies shopped. Many a young acquaintance turned to stare, finding it strange to see him occupied like a porter or an errand-boy.

*I will not be daunted from doing as I choose by the thought of her,* he thought. *I like to take this fruit to the poor mother, and it is simply right that I should. She shall never scorn me out of doing what I please. A pretty joke, indeed, if, for fear of a haughty girl, I failed in doing a kindness to a man I liked! I do it for Mr. Hale; I do it in defiance of her.*

Moving at an unusual pace, he reached Crampton quickly. Ascending two steps at a time, he entered the drawing-room before Dixon could announce him. His face was flushed, his eyes shining with a kindly earnestness. Mrs. Hale lay on the sofa, heated with fever. Mr. Hale was reading aloud, and Margaret worked on a low stool beside her mother. Her heart fluttered violently, though whether it was due to his presence or the gravity of the situation, he took no notice of her—not even of Mr. Hale himself. He advanced straight to Mrs. Hale with the basket, speaking in a subdued, gentle tone that was profoundly touching when uttered by a robust man addressing a feeble invalid.

"I met Dr. Donaldson, ma'am, and as he said fruit would be good for you, I have taken the liberty—the great liberty—of bringing you some that seemed to me fine."

Mrs. Hale was excessively pleased, trembling with eager delight. Mr. Hale, with fewer words, expressed a deeper gratitude.

"Fetch a plate, Margaret—a basket—anything." Margaret rose by the table, half afraid to move or make a sound that might alert Mr. Thornton to her presence. She feared an awkward collision of consciousness, assuming that from her position on the low seat, now standing behind her father, he had overlooked her in his haste. Yet, as if he did not feel the weight of her awareness, his eyes never rested upon her.

"I must go," he said. "I cannot stay. If you will forgive this liberty,—my rough ways,—too abrupt, I fear—but I will be more gentle next time. You will allow me the pleasure of bringing you some fruit again, if I should see any that is tempting. Good afternoon, Mr. Hale. Good-bye, ma'am."

And he was gone. Not one word, not one look directed toward Margaret. She was convinced he had not seen her. She moved silently to fetch a plate, lifting the fruit out tenderly with the tips of her delicate fingers. It was kind of him to bring it; and after everything that had happened yesterday!

"Oh! it is so delicious!" said Mrs. Hale, her voice weak. "How kind of him to think of me! Margaret love, only taste these grapes! Was it not good of him?"

"Yes!" said Margaret, quietly.

"Margaret!" Mrs. Hale said, rather querulously, "you won't like anything Mr. Thornton does. I never saw anybody so prejudiced."

Mr. Hale had been peeling a peach for his wife; cutting off a small piece for himself, he remarked, "If I had any prejudices, the gift of such delicious fruit as this would melt them away. I have not tasted such fruit—no! not even in Hampshire—since I was a boy; and, to boys, I fancy, all fruit is good. I remember eating sloes and crabs with a relish. Do you remember the matted-up currant bushes, Margaret, at the corner of the west-wall at the garden at home?"

Did she not? Did she not remember every weather-stain on the old stone wall; the gray and yellow lichens marking it like a map; the little crane's-bill growing in the crevices? Shaken by the tumultuous events of the last two days, her entire existence currently strained upon her fortitude. Somehow, these careless words of her father's, touching on the remembrance of sunny times past, caused her to start up. Dropping her sewing on the floor, she hurried out of the room into her own little chamber. Hardly had she yielded to the first choking sob when she became aware of Dixon standing at her drawers, evidently searching for something.
