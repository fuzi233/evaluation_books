The moment we were left alone on the Shivering Sand, I made another attempt to rise. Mr. Franklin stopped me with a hand on my shoulder.

“This cursed place has one advantage,” he said. “Privacy. Stay where you are, Betteredge. I’ve something to discuss.”

I studied him as he spoke, searching for the boy I remembered in the man before me. The years had reshaped him entirely. Gone were the rosy cheeks, the trim jacket—replaced by a pale complexion and a thick brown beard that unsettled me. His manner was quick, engaging, but lacked the careless charm of his youth. Worse still, he’d fallen short of the height he’d once promised. Neat, slight, well-built—yet undeniably ordinary in stature. Only his eyes remained unchanged: bright, direct, unmistakably *him*.

“Welcome home, Mr. Franklin,” I said. “Earlier than expected, sir.”

“There’s a reason for that,” he replied. “I believe I’ve been followed in London these past few days. I took the morning train to lose a certain dark stranger.”

The words struck like a spark. The three jugglers—Penelope’s suspicions—flashed through my mind.

“Who’s watching you, sir? And why?”

Instead of answering, he said, “Tell me about those Indians who came to the house today. It’s possible my pursuer and your jugglers are part of the same puzzle.”

“How do you know of them?” I asked—a breach of manners, but shock loosens the tongue.

“Penelope told me,” he said, amused. “She’s grown into a fine young woman. Small ear, small foot—did her mother share those *inestimable* advantages?”

“The late Mrs. Betteredge had many faults,” I said dryly. “Not least her habit of flitting from topic to topic like a fly.”

“She’d have suited me,” Mr. Franklin laughed. “I’ve never settled on anything either. Now, the jugglers—what did they do?”

Reluctantly, I relayed Penelope’s account. His amusement faded as I spoke. By the end, his brow was knotted, fingers twisting his beard. He repeated the jugglers’ questions with eerie precision:

Then, to my astonishment, he drew a sealed packet from his pocket.

“I suspect ‘It’ means *this*,” he said. “And *this*, Betteredge, is my uncle Herncastle’s cursed Diamond.”

“God alive!” I burst out. “How did *you* come by that villain’s gem?”

“The Colonel left it to Rachel as a birthday gift,” he said. “My father, as executor, entrusted me to deliver it.”

Had the sea before us turned to stone, I couldn’t have been more stunned.

“The Diamond—to Miss Rachel? And your father agreed to handle the Colonel’s affairs? I’d have wagered my life he’d sooner touch a viper!”

Mr. Franklin’s gaze sharpened. “You called him ‘the wicked Colonel.’ Why?”

So I told him.

The late Lord Herncastle had five children: two sons, three daughters. My mistress, Lady Verinder, was the youngest and finest. The eldest son inherited title and estate. The second, John—the Honourable John—took a military path.

And here, I must tread carefully. A servant doesn’t blacken his master’s family lightly. But the truth is this: John Herncastle was a devil in human skin.

After being dismissed from the Guards (reasons best left unsaid), he fled to India, seeking laxer discipline. Brave? Undoubtedly—a mix of bulldog and gamecock, with a streak of savagery. He fought at Seringapatam, hopped regiments, and finally returned to England as Lieutenant-Colonel Herncastle—sunstruck, disgraced, and shunned.

The whispers clung to him like rot. The worst? The Moonstone. He’d acquired it through means even *he* dared not name. Never sold it. Never showed it. Some said he feared military retribution; others, assassination. Both held grains of truth. His life had been threatened twice in India—both times, the Diamond lurked in the shadows.

England offered no refuge. Clubs barred him. Women refused him. Family crossed streets to avoid him. Most men would’ve groveled for redemption. Not the Colonel. He kept the Diamond, defying death abroad and scorn at home. A man who laughed at hell itself.

We heard sporadic rumors: opium, obscure books, chemical experiments, debauchery in London’s filthiest corners. A recluse, rotten to the core.

I saw him once—two years before his death. Miss Rachel’s twenty-first birthday. A ball was underway when a footman summoned me. There stood the Colonel: gaunt, ragged, eyes burning with undiminished malice.

“Tell my sister,” he said, “I’ve come to wish my niece a happy birthday.”

I delivered the message. My lady’s reply was ice: *“Miss Verinder is engaged. I decline to see him.”*

I softened the blow, but the Colonel only laughed—a sound like poison dripping into glass.

“Thank you, Betteredge. I’ll remember my niece’s birthday.”

He left. The next year, he was dead. A clergyman’s letter claimed he’d repented, forgiven his sister, died edified. I don’t believe it. The devil collected his own.

Mr. Franklin listened like a man deciphering a cipher. When I finished, his face was taut.

“You’ve set the stage,” he said. “Now let me clarify my suspicions.” He counted off on his fingers:

“One: Was the Diamond hunted in India?

“Two: Have its hunters followed it to England?

“Three: Did the Colonel *know* this—and deliberately plant a trap for his sister through Rachel?”

The implications coiled around me like smoke. Our peaceful home, targeted over a jewel? In *England*? In this age of reason? Preposterous—yet his logic was sound.

My stomach churned. Mr. Franklin noticed.

“What’s wrong?” he snapped.

I didn’t answer. But I’ll confess to you: I craved the comfort of my pipe and *Robinson Crusoe*. Some storms require steadying.
