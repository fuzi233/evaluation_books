In the guest-chamber of a Dominican convent there lay a single stranger, spent as a man is spent who has wrestled all night, not with an enemy, but with his own body. The violent retchings that had shaken him like a sail in a squall were over at last; they had left him emptied and trembling, as weak as a child new-born. A great log-fire laboured on the hearth, resinous and sweet, cracking as the sap hissed out; its warmth licked the stone and made a small summer in that cold, high room. By the blaze his garments hung to dry, giving off the sour river-smell of drowned cloth—mud and rank weed and the sharp tang of wet leather—until the fire, little by little, conquered it with smoke and pine.

Beside the bed sat a gigantic friar, solid as an oaken settle. He read aloud from his breviary, the Latin rolling slow and even, like a mill-wheel that will not be hurried. Sometimes the sick man—Gerard—watched him with a dull, questioning gaze, and seemed to catch at the words as at driftwood. At other times he closed his eyes and only moaned, his throat raw, his lips salted with dried vomit and river-water.

The monk at length knelt, his broad brow touching the floor, and prayed for him with a whole body’s devotion; then rose and drew his cloak about him.

“Day breaks,” said he. “I must prepare for matins.”

“Good Father Jerome,” whispered Gerard hoarsely, “before you go—how came I hither?”

“By the hand of Heaven. You flung away God’s gift. He bestowed it on you again. Think on it! Hast tried the world and found its gall. Now try the Church! The Church is peace. Pax vobiscum.”

He went; and Gerard lay back, staring at the dark rafters where the firelight wavered, wondering at the strange turn of events, until weakness folded him down into a doze.

When he woke again the room had altered in those small ways a sick man notes before he knows why: the fire was newly fed; the air had a faint clean scent of warmed water and herbs, as if someone had rinsed a cloth and steeped something bitter in it. And by the bedside sat not the tranquil giant, but a layman—slight, alert—his eye small and restless, darting like a rat’s in lamplight.

He leaned in. “How feel you now, signor?”

“Very—very weak.” Gerard’s tongue felt too big for his mouth. “Where have I seen you before, messer?”

“None the worse for my gauntlet?” The man’s voice held anxious pride. “I was fain to strike you with it, or both you and I should be at the bottom of Tiber.”

Gerard stared, trying to fit memory to this face as one fits a lid to a pot. “What—’twas you saved me? How?”

“Well, signor, I was by the banks of Tiber on—on an errand, no matter what.” He licked his lip; even the motion was quick and wary. “You came to me and begged hard for a dagger stroke. But ere I could oblige you—ay, even as you spoke—I knew you for the signor that saved my wife and child upon the sea.”

A coldness ran through Gerard that was not fever. “It is Teresa’s husband. And an assassin?!!?”

“At your service.” The fellow’s mouth twitched, half grimace, half salute. “Well, Ser Gerard, the next thing was you flung yourself into Tiber, and bade me hold aloof.”

“I remember that,” Gerard muttered; and with the words came a flash of black water, the choking, and the mad sweetness of letting go.

“Had it been any but you, believe me I had obeyed you, and not wagged a finger. Men are my foes. They may all hang on one rope, or drown in one river for me.” He spoke with a brutal simplicity, as if stating the price of fish in a market. “But when thou, sinking in Tiber, didst cry ‘Margaret!’—”

“Ah!” Gerard’s breath caught on the name like a bone in the throat.

“My heart it cried ‘Teresa!’ How could I go home and look her in the face, did I let thee die, and by the very death thou savedst her from? So in I went; and luckily for us both I swim like a duck. You, seeing me near, and being bent on destruction, tried to grip me, and so end us both. But I swam round thee, and”—he lifted his gloved hand, awkwardly apologetic—“receive my excuses—so buffeted thee on the nape of the neck with my steel glove, that thou lost sense; and I with much ado, the stream being strong, did draw thy body to land, but insensible and full of water.

“Then I took thee on my back and made for my own home. ‘Teresa will nurse him, and be pleased with me,’ thought I. But hard by this monastery, a holy friar—the biggest e’er I saw—met us and asked the matter. So I told him. He looked hard at thee. ‘I know the face,’ quoth he. ‘’Tis one Gerard, a fair youth from Holland.’ ‘The same,’ quo’ I.

“Then said his reverence, ‘He hath friends among our brethren. Leave him with us! Charity, it is our office.’ Also he told me they of the convent had better means to tend thee than I had. And that was true enow. So I just bargained to be let in to see thee once a day—and here thou art.”

As he finished, the man let his gaze rest on Gerard with a strange, dog-like affection—pride of possession mingled with genuine concern. But Gerard’s spirit recoiled as if from a snake warmed on the hearthstone. He turned his face away and shut his eyes.

“Ah, thou wouldst sleep,” said the miscreant eagerly, and rose at once, light on his feet. “I go.” He backed out on tiptoe, promising to come every day.

Left alone, Gerard lay with eyes closed—not sleeping, but sinking through thought after thought as through cold water. Saved from death by an assassin. Was not this the finger of Heaven? Of that Heaven he had insulted, cursed, defied. The memory of his blasphemies burned him worse than the river-water in his lungs.

He tried to pray. Words he had learned as a boy came readily to his lips; his mouth could shape them. But his heart would not lift with them. It was as if the true meaning—the very savour and warmth of them—had been taken away, leaving only husk and sound.

“I am doomed eternally,” he whispered, and then louder in his despair, “doomed, doomed.”

At that instant the convent church—close by, yet in another world—sent its great organ-voice through the stone. It did not come like speech, but like weather: a solemn wind filling the room with harmony. Then rose the choir, a full service, the Latin syllables placed like measured steps. Among the voices one soared, a sweet boy’s voice, pure and high, seeming to hover above the rest and yet to spring from them, as a lark’s song springs from the same earth that bears wheat.

Gerard lay still and listened. The sound was not merely heard; it was felt, as the fire’s warmth was felt—entering the ribs, quieting the pulse, settling the mind. In those cadences his own boyhood came back, not as a thought, but as an atmosphere: cloistered mornings, clean rushes on stone, the smell of wax and old wood, and the untroubled certainty of a world ordered by bells.

No earthly dross there, no foul, fierce passions rending and corrupting the soul. Only peace—peace—sweet, balmy peace.

“Ay,” he sighed, the tears coming without his leave, “the Church is peace of mind. Till I left her bosom I ne’er knew sorrow, nor sin.”

And even as he wept, there beamed on him the sweet and reverend face of one he had never thought to see again. It was Father Anselm.

The good priest had come to the convent only the night before last. He was worn with travel, but his eyes were the same as ever—tender, steady, as if they had listened long to the world’s harshness and yet refused to become harsh.

Gerard knew him in a moment and stretched out a shaking hand. “Oh, Father Anselm! You cured my wounded body in Juliers: now cure my hurt soul in Rome! Alas, you cannot.”

Anselm sat by the bedside and laid his hand upon Gerard’s head, and first spoke a few soothing words that were like cool water to a scorched mouth. Then—having learned how Gerard had come there—he grew kind but solemn, and made him feel his crime, and urged him to repentance and to gratitude toward the Divine Power that had thwarted his will in order to save his soul.

“Come, my son,” said he, “first purge thy bosom of its load.”

“Ah, father,” Gerard groaned, “in Juliers I could; then I was innocent. But now, impious monster that I am, I dare not confess to you.”

“Why not, my son? Thinkest thou I have not sinned against Heaven in my time, and deeply? oh, how deeply! Come, poor laden soul; pour forth thy grief, pour forth thy faults; hold back nought! Lie not oppressed and crushed by hidden sins.”

The words—simple, homely, spoken in a tone that carried no curiosity, only pity—broke the last stubbornness in Gerard. He slid from his pillow as far as weakness allowed and, with a sob, was at Father Anselm’s knees, pouring out everything: his sins, his angers, his despair; each confession dragged up like something foul from a well, stinking in the air the moment it saw light, yet lighter once spoken. He sighed and groaned with penitence until his throat ached and his body shook as if the river’s current still ran through him.

When all was told, Anselm’s face did not harden; it saddened.

“Thy sins are great,” said he. “Thy temptation also was great—terribly great. I must consult our good prior.”

He kissed Gerard’s brow and left him to seek the superior, to learn what penance should be laid upon this torn, worn soul.

And lo—Gerard could pray now. Not with the tongue alone, but with the whole being. The phase through which his remarkable mind now passed might be summed in a word: Penitence.

He turned with terror and aversion from the world, and begged passionately to remain in the convent. To one nurtured among cloisters it was like a wounded bird, weary of storms, finding again the gentle nest. The days that followed were made of prayer, mortification, pious reading, and long meditations broken only by the measured music of the house: the bells that divided darkness and dawn; the soft scuff of sandals on stone; the breath and rise of chanting that entered his room like bread enters a hungry body, sustaining him more truly than broth.

Meanwhile, beyond those quiet walls, the world went on with its loud bargains and its sudden cruelties.

The Princess Claelia’s spy returned and told her, with the certainty of a man reporting coin counted, that Gerard was certainly dead, though the manner of his death was not yet known.

For a moment she seemed struck dumb. When at last she found breath, it was not to accuse herself, but to bemoan her lot as one ill-served by tools.

“So soon,” she sighed. “See how swift these monsters are to do ill deeds. They come to us in our hot blood, and first tempt us with their venal daggers, then enact the mortal deeds we ne’er had thought on but for them.”

And yet, ere many hours had passed, her pity for Gerard and her hatred of his murderer rose to fever heat—blood heat; for with such as she, feeling and appetite ran the same course.

“Poor soul!” she cried. “I cannot call thee back to life. But he shall never live that traitorously slew thee.”

She sent and set armed men in ambush, keeping them on guard all day, ready that when Lodovico should come for his money they should fall upon him in a certain antechamber and hack him to pieces.

“Strike at his head,” she commanded, “for he weareth a privy coat of mail; and if he goes hence alive your own heads shall answer it.”

So she sat weeping her victim, and all the while pulling the strings of her machines to shed the blood of a second—for having been her machine to kill the first.
