There was no delay in the city of Atlantis when Phorenice willed it. Her belief was absolute: the land and all within it existed solely for her sake, and if she desired something, no price was too high to pay.

She had issued her decree—the burial alive of Nais—and though the command named me as builder of the stone throne, it was understood that others would do the labor. Heralds carried the proclamation through every district, and masons, laborers, sculptors, engineers, and architects abandoned their tasks and rushed to the appointed site. The chief architect was chosen on the spot, his orders relayed by subordinates, and the work began at once. Any material within the city walls marked by their seal was seized without compensation. The stones they selected were colossal—so massive that entire buildings had to be torn down to clear a path for them.

I have mentioned before the modern obsession with constructing new palaces and pyramids. Even now, while rebel armies battered the city’s defenses with siege engines, the building guilds labored tirelessly. Their skill, enhanced by Phorenice’s ingenious inventions, grew ever sharper. They could not move the titanic blocks once placed by the ancient gods for the sacred circle of our Lord the Sun—but they had rams, cranes, and wheeled platforms capable of lifting astonishing weights.

The throne stood in the open square before the royal pyramid. Seven tiers of stone formed its base, each tier knee-high and fronted with three steps. At the top, a hollow cavity awaited the body of Nais. Above it, suspended by lifting rams, hovered the immense block that would serve as the seat of the throne.

Through the night, under torchlight, relays of stonemasons, laborers, and engineers worked without rest—cutting, shaping, hauling, and placing each stone with precision. Machines were built to lift the slabs, and architects ensured every joint was perfect. Whips cracked; men collapsed from exhaustion, only to be replaced instantly. No delay was tolerated when the Empress had spoken.

By dawn, the square was filling with spectators drawn by the spectacle of the day. The chippings and scaffolding were cleared away—along with the bodies of about half a dozen workers who had died during construction. The throne stood complete, carved with intricate designs, ready for its final act. Its lower portion rose more than two man-heights above the ground, each stone weighing at least twenty men. The upper slab, twice as heavy, bore the image of the royal serpent coiled around the chair, its great hooded head looming overhead. For now, it remained suspended—held aloft by hydraulic rams, its purpose known to all.

It was to this scene that I stepped out of the royal pyramid at the summons of the chamberlains, just as the morning air turned cool. Each noble arrived with banner-bearers and trumpeters announcing their presence. The middle class wore their finest garments; even the poorest, gaunt and sunken-cheeked, adorned themselves with colored cloths and turbans to mark the occasion.

Trumpets blared as I approached. The crowd roared in welcome. With the chamberlains walking backward ahead of me, I crossed to a scarlet awning and took my seat upon cushions beneath it.

Then came Phorenice—my bride-to-be—fresh from sleep, radiant in beauty. She emerged from the pyramid in an open litter of gold and ivory, carried by fierce savages from Europe. The contrast between her refined elegance and their brutish appearance heightened her grace. A collective breath seemed to catch in the crowd as they beheld her. It was easy to believe that no man among them failed to envy me her choice. And none could imagine—except perhaps Ylga, hidden somewhere in shadow—that I felt anything but pride in winning such a wife.

For myself, I summoned every ounce of discipline I had learned. This was ceremony—grim, yes, but still ritual. I intended to show Phorenice a composed dignity, nothing to reproach. After everything we had endured, I refused to let grief or pain betray me in a trembling hand or twitching jaw. When the moment came, I told myself, I would place Nais’s living body into the stone cavity as calmly as a priest might lay a corpse to rest. And she, I knew, would not reveal our secret. For both of us, it was “Before all Atlantis.”

To my relief, there was no mockery, no jest in what followed. The proceedings were solemn, grand, and deeply moving. Though the vulgar crowd missed the deeper meaning, the outward majesty of the double sacrifice would linger in their memories long after. When it suited her whim, Phorenice could be fiercely strict about ritual—and today, she had ordered every detail observed with the precision of ancient times.

She was borne forward by her European bearers to the awning, and I helped her to the ground. She sat beside me, entwining her fingers in mine, as custom demanded between rulers and consorts of Atlantis.

Then, soldiers marched forward, forming a line and revealing Nais in their midst. A metal collar encircled her neck, chains hanging from it, held taut by two guards so she could not dash herself against the spears—thus avoiding a swift death, which is often the fate of those condemned to slow suffering.

Yet she still wore her clothes. Clothing has value, and tradition dictates that the garments of the condemned go to the guards. So her attire remained intact. I realized then that someone must have paid the guards—a gesture I suspected came from Ylga. A quiet gratitude stirred within me.

The soldiers brought her to the edge of the awning’s shade. She was commanded to kneel before the Empress—and wisely obeyed, avoiding violence. Her face was pale, but calm. No fear, no defiance. Her eyes were steady. She remembered her duty to Atlantis, and I watched her with love and pride.

I remained impassive as stone. I knew Phorenice watched me closely, yet I showed nothing—not a flicker of emotion. I had trained myself well.

“Nais,” the Empress said, “you have eaten from my table, drunk from my cup. What was yours, I gave you. You should have known gratitude—and the reach of the Empress’s power. Yet you did not. You attempted to take what belongs to me alone. Pardon was offered, with terms. You refused. You were foolish. But today, I am merciful. Soon, seated upon that granite throne he has built for me, I shall take Deucalion as my husband. Speak plainly: repent, and name a man you would wed, and I will spare you. You may be married before we rise from these cushions.”

“I will not wed,” she said quietly.

“Think again, Nais. Your other choice is this: you will be taken—alive, warm, beautiful as you stand now—and laid in the hollow beneath the throne-stone. Deucalion, my future husband, will place you there, as a symbol of how all enemies of Phorenice shall perish. Then he will release the rams, and the upper stone will descend. You will vanish from sight. Look at the sky. Breathe the air. Think of what that end means. Believe me, girl—I do not wish to make an example unless you force me.”

“I will not wed,” she repeated.

Phorenice released my arm and leaned back. “If she thinks I jest—or clings to old familiarity—show her, Deucalion, that I do not.”

“The Empress does not jest,” I said. “I will do this because it is her wish, and because it must be made a lasting symbol. Lead your prisoner to the place.”

The soldiers turned. The guards gripped the chains around Nais’s neck, prepared to drag her up the steps. But she walked willingly, her color unbroken. I rose, bowed to the Empress, and followed.

Before ten thousand eyes, neither of us displayed emotion—not for Atlantis, nor for the pride we both carried. We were not like Phorenice, who flaunted affection openly.

Yet when I signaled the guards to unfasten the collar, and drew Nais into my arms—pretending the roughness of one seizing a captive—a surge of emotion threatened to overwhelm me. I had never embraced a woman with tenderness before. Now I held the one I loved, and the intensity of that union nearly broke me. Only sheer will kept me standing.

I dragged her across the platform, forcing her down into the hollow beneath the throne. She resisted not at all.

To prevent her from struggling free and escaping the crushing weight, brazen clamps were fitted into grooves above the cavity. One by one, I secured them, holding her down with one hand while working with the other. Sweat poured from me, falling onto the stone. I could not stop it.

When the last bar was locked, I released my grip.

Pale furrows marked her throat where my fingers had pressed. I could not help glancing at her face. But she looked neither at me nor at the stone. Her gaze remained fixed upward, unyielding. I left her, knowing it was best not to linger.

I stepped from beneath the stone and signaled the engineers. Fires were removed from the rams. Metal contracted. Slowly, the vast throne-stone began its descent.

Oh, how slowly! My soul tore apart as I watched.

Yet I kept my face still, like an officer overseeing a task under his command. I waited until the stone settled fully, then joined the master architect, who tested it with plumbline and level. He declared it perfect.

It was a formality—calculated results already confirmed—but the guilds insisted on such rituals, especially in ceremonial moments. They wished to appear mysterious, powerful, beyond the common folk. Perhaps I was harsh in judging them. Yet who could blame a man forced to measure the tomb of his beloved?

I had entered the steps a grieving man, outwardly cold. As I descended, I felt a hollow inside me—as if half my being had been left behind with Nais. A void ached within me. Still, I kept my expression blank. Before all else, the High Council’s command loomed: the Ark of the Mysteries awaited.

I returned to Phorenice and spoke the prescribed words.

“Now,” she said, “I give myself to you as wife. You and I are not like others. There is no law or form for us. No priest, no scribe. I am the law here. Soon, you will be part of me. We need no outsiders. We will make our own ceremony. Witnesses enough stand ready. Later, the record shall be carved into the throne you built—letters filled with gold, enduring forever.”

“The Empress can do no wrong,” I replied formally, taking her hand and helping her rise.

We stepped from the awning into the sunlight. She leaned on me, flushed, radiant—so lovely that the crowd erupted in cries: “A Goddess! Our Goddess Phorenice!” But for me, no cheers came. Their eyes met my grim face, and they recoiled.

We climbed the steps to the throne. She sat beneath the serpent’s head. I joined her.

She raised her jeweled hand. Silence fell over the throng.

“Hear me, O my people,” she proclaimed, “and hear me, O High Gods, from whom I come. I take Deucalion as my husband—to share the prosperity of Atlantis, to guard our great possession. May all our enemies perish as she perishes beneath us.”

Then she wrapped her arms around my neck and kissed me fiercely.

In turn, I spoke: “Hear me, O most High Gods, whose servant I am, and hear me, O ye people. I take Phorenice, the Empress, as my wife—to aid in the prosperity of Atlantis, to join with her in protecting our realm. May all enemies fall as they have fallen in the past.”

And then, I—whose lips had never touched the woman I loved—placed my first kiss upon Phorenice, now my wife.

But we were not yet fully joined.

“A woman is one, a man is one,” she declared, invoking the ancient words. “But in marriage, they become one. In token of this, we will now unite, so all may see and remember.” She drew her dagger, pricked my forearm until blood welled, and pressed her lips to it, drinking the drop.

“Oh,” she whispered, eyes bright, “now you are truly part of me, Deucalion. I feel your strength already.” She pulled down her robe. “Let me return the gift.”

I pricked her shoulder. Gods—when I thought of the girl buried beneath us, I could have driven the blade deep. But I pressed my lips to her skin and tasted her blood.

My tongue was dry. My throat burned. I thought I might choke.

But Phorenice, usually so sharp, was deceived. “It thrills you?” she laughed. “It burns like fire? I felt it too. By my face! If I’d known this joy of becoming a wife, I wouldn’t have waited so long. And only with you—no other man could stir this in me. They say a woman feels this but once. I wouldn’t trade it for all my conquests or all my power.” She leaned close, her red curls brushing my cheek. “Have you ever tasted sweetness so pure as knowing we are one now, Deucalion—forever?”

I could not lie. I had tried to play the role for Atlantis’s sake. But human endurance has limits. I was not wholly angry with her. I pitied her passion—unbidden, unchecked. Her boldness was merely the garb of rulers who flaunt what others hide. Yet always before me stood the image of Nais, sacrificed by her jealousy. To feign love now was impossible.

“Words are weak,” I said. “I am not skilled in speech, especially with women. A week ago, I knew nothing of love. Now I know it—suddenly, deeply—but the language still lags behind. My heart speaks, full of things I cannot express.”

At that moment, the High Gods, perhaps, spared me. A wounded man burst into the square, shouting. The crowd echoed his cry. People on rooftops, pyramids, and pavements reached for weapons.

“The rebels are in!” “They’ve tunneled under the wall!” “They killed the cave-tigers and breached a gate!” “They’re storming the city!” “They’ll leave no one alive!”

Our wedding ended abruptly. With rebels only scratching at the walls, confidence was easy. But now they were inside—hordes likely behind them. The mood shifted instantly. Phorenice was the first to grasp it. The fire in her eyes changed to battle-lust.

I stood, drawing breath. “Here,” I said, “is work I understand. Let me drive this rabble back to their holes, Phorenice.”

“But not alone,” she said. “This is my city. And we are newly wed. We cannot be parted.”

“Then as you wish,” I said. Together, we descended the throne steps. Under my breath, I whispered farewell to Nais.

Our armor-bearers met us, handing weapons. We stepped into litters, and slaves bore us swiftly through the streets. The wounded messenger had fainted—no more news. But the sounds of battle guided us. Soon, we passed fleeing citizens, carrying their belongings, screaming in panic.

We let them pass. They could not fight. To stay would mean death—and they’d only hinder our troops. We pushed through them, ignoring their terror.

From a soldier’s eye, the rebels’ advantage was slight. They had dug a tunnel from a mound beneath the wall, breaching a middle-class house. They gathered there, hoping the Empress’s wedding would weaken the guards. But they were discovered early. A strong troop nearby meant their destruction was only a matter of time—even without us.

Phorenice laughed, reclining in her litter. “We grow nervous, my Deucalion. Afraid our happiness might be snatched too soon.”

But I would not lose my moment. “Grant me a wedding gift,” I said.

“It is yours before you name it.”

“Then give me troops—and open a city gate a mile from here.”

“You can gather five hundred on the way—two hundred from here, more from barracks along the wall. But what is your plan?”

I did not answer. The war was beginning. And I had no time to explain.
