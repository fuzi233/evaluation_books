In the rarefied world of late-Victorian journalism, the Pall Mall Gazette emerged as a formidable force, its influence felt across the corridors of power. Rumors abounded about its alleged connections to an influential political party, with several notable contributors cited among its ranks. While the veracity of these claims remains unclear, it is evident that the Gazette's editorial board cultivated a sophisticated network of alliances, leveraging information from exclusive sources to shape public opinion. A case in point lies in the enigmatic articles penned by Captain Shandon, whose prose, often attributed to a noble Lord with ties to the Foreign Office, actually originated in the unassuming parlor of the Bear and Staff public house near Whitehall Stairs. Similarly, a series of trenchant essays on finance, widely believed to be the work of a prominent statesman in the House of Commons, were, in fact, the product of Mr. George Warrington's intellect, resident in the Upper Temple.

The Gazette's relationship with influential parties, including the family of Percy Popjoy, whose father, Lord Falconet, was a key figure in the party, suggests a complex web of alliances and negotiations. The publication's penchant for publishing cryptic poems signed "P. P." and effusive praise for the novel of an anonymous author further underscores its capacity to cultivate and amplify voices within the upper echelons of society.

In the midst of this intricate dance of power and influence, Mr. Pen, a rising literary star, found himself drawn to the Gazette's offices in Catherine Street, the Strand. There, he would submit his manuscripts with great fanfare, still basking in the thrill of seeing his words in print and imagining their impact on the world. As he navigated the labyrinthine corridors of high society, Pen was often accompanied by his friend Jack Finucane, the Gazette's sub-editor, whose role in compiling the paper's Journal was both a testament to his industry and a source of fascination for those who knew him. With an eagle eye for detail, Finucane scoured the newspapers for tidbits on high-society events, from aristocratic funerals to lavish dinner parties, and recontextualized them within the Gazette's pages. The juxtaposition of his humble demeanor and refined writing style proved a constant source of amusement for Pen, who marveled at the incongruity between Finucane's rustic origins and his mastery of the high-brow language that flowed from his pen.
