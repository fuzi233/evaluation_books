When Baldassarre wandered through Florence in search of the cheapest sheltered bed, he found himself instinctively fleeing the grinding, commercial clamor of the lower city. Down there, the abrasive acoustic ecology of hammering guilds, shouting merchants, and the relentless clatter of cobblestone traffic bruised his fragmented psyche. He sought sanctuary from this industrial noise pollution, his steps carrying him upward toward the hill of San Giorgio. Here, the sonic landscape softened. The jarring dissonance of the urban marketplace gave way to the rhythmic, grounded resonance of the agrarian world: the rustling leaves of a half-dead mulberry tree, the gentle bleating of a goat, and the cyclical sigh of the wind over the Apennines. 

For Baldassarre, whose memory was failing, this quiet height felt like a fold in time, a place where multi-generational timelines might momentarily rest. Beneath the fluttering mulberry leaves, a shriveled, hardy agrarian elder was untying a goat and two kids. Monna Lisa operated entirely within the primal, circular economy of the earth, her hands steeped in the ancient rituals of soil and curd. When Baldassarre approached her, he felt the tragic weight of his own eroding cultural memory. His native tongue was dying in his mouth; the dominant Florentine lexicon required to negotiate with this world felt unnatural, a power dynamic that rendered him a voiceless alien. He shouted into the deaf woman's ear, his foreign accents inherently untranslatable to her, pleading for space in the empty outhouse. 

Monna Lisa studied him with the shrewdness of a woman who knew the precise value of labor and sustenance. She fetched a hatchet and pointed to a seasoned chump of wood. The hearth needed fuel to render the evening’s meal; if he chopped the wood, he could lie on the straw. Baldassarre agreed. Soon, the rhythmic, percussive *thwack* of the iron blade sinking into the timber became a soothing metronome on the hill, an acoustic anchor cutting through his mental fog. 

Above them, observing through a round hole in the shutter of an upper window, was a pair of wide blue eyes. Tessa, dressed in the dark-green serge of a *contadina*, watched the stranger. In Tessa’s isolated life, the acts of nurturing and feeding were not utilitarian chores, but profound, mythic routines that anchored her existence. The loud, untranslatable syllables of the old man’s voice had drawn her to the window. 

As dusk gathered and Baldassarre retreated to the straw, the olfactory landscape of the house began to bloom. Supper was being prepared—a sacred alchemy of simple ingredients. A mountain of thick, hand-kneaded ribbons of macaroni had been boiled to perfect tenderness in heavily salted water, then vigorously tossed with an abundance of sharp, aged *pecorino*. The heat of the pasta coaxed the fat from the cheese, creating a rich, fragrant emulsion that filled the stone house with a deeply comforting aroma. 

Tessa could not bear the thought of the stranger sitting alone with nothing but a dry, joyless crust to fuel him. Feeding others was her language of care. After deceiving Monna Lisa with a kittenish display of independence, claiming she would eat her portion downstairs, Tessa tripped into the yard. In one hand, she carried a lantern; in the other, a wooden bowl heavy with the steaming, cheese-laced macaroni. 

She kicked gently at the outhouse door. When Baldassarre opened it, he found the surprising little handmaid standing there, her eyes shining in the lantern light. Assuming he was as deaf as Monna Lisa, Tessa raised her voice, her rustic native dialect carrying a melodic, unpretentious warmth that stood in stark contrast to the sharp, dominant speech of the city below. "I’ve brought you some supper! Sit down and eat it, while I stay with you."

Baldassarre sank onto his straw, and Tessa placed the heavy wooden bowl in his lap. As he took his first bite, a gustatory awakening washed over him. The complex, earthy bite of the sheep’s milk cheese, the warm elasticity of the wheat, and the savory starch coating his palate sparked fleeting, circular memories of ancient, multi-generational feasts in a lost homeland. 

"I like my supper, don't you?" she cried into his ear. 

He turned to her with the mild look of a dog touched by kindness. "Yes," he said, "but I can hear well—I'm not deaf."

Tessa laughed, clasping her hands. Her prattling was a continuous, rhythmic stream that integrated the mythic into the domestic. She spoke of her love for feeding the mules, of nurturing the goat, finding her most profound identity in the act of sustenance. "But now I’ve got something else," she teased, offering the mystery with a challenging smile. 

Baldassarre set his bowl aside, looking at her dreamily. "No," he said.

"Well, then, I've got a little baby. Such a pretty *bambinetto*! Born at the *Nativita*, Monna Lisa says." She spoke of her marriage, a secret union hidden from her cruel stepfather Nofri, bound to a husband who belonged to a different, more powerful sphere of the world. "I don't see him very often," she confessed, her voice lowering with the gravity of a prayer. "But I’ll bring the *bimbo* to show you to-morrow. Monna Lisa had babies once, and they are all dead old men. My husband says she will never die now, because she's so well dried."

The next morning, the cyclical dawn was marked by the deep, resonant splash of a wooden bucket hitting the water table—the acoustic purity of the well echoing against the stones. Monna Lisa had put Baldassarre to work as a drawer of water. Tessa, eager to resume their dialogue and share her morning provisions, deferred her *paternosters* and hurried down. She insisted Baldassarre sit on the straw so they could share the space as she had once shared stalls with her beloved mules. 

She brought him a breakfast that spoke of meticulous, agrarian care: a bowl of rich, slow-simmered bone broth, fragrant with wild herbs, alongside a slab of soft, ash-baked bread meant to absorb every drop of the golden fat. "I will not let Monna Lisa give you too much work to do," she promised, placing the steaming meal into his hands. 

Presently, she returned with her baby, a small swaddled bundle with unusually large dark eyes. The sharing of the meal transitioned into the sharing of life. "This is my baby," Tessa said, seating herself close to him. "You may hold the *bambino* a little if you like."

Baldassarre took the small package. "Poor thing! poor thing!" he said, his deep voice carrying a strangely threatening pity. It did not seem to him that this guileless girl, who found her entire worldview validated through simple sustenance and quiet acoustic peace, could reconcile him to the harsh world; rather, her innocence felt like something that would soon demand vengeance against the encroaching corruption of the city. 

"Oh, don't you be sorry for me," she said earnestly. "He is more beautiful and good than anybody else in the world. You couldn't think what he is! What is your name?"

Baldassarre rubbed his hand over his knitted brow, looking at her blankly. "Ah, child, what is it?" 

The sudden question demanded he reach into his cultural memory, but the lexicon of his own identity had been hollowed out. In that terrifying moment of language death, the subjective experience of his past was entirely untranslatable to his present self; the word for who he was had vanished, leaving only helplessness. 

Ignorant as Tessa was, the pure pity stirred in her by his blank look taught her to soothe him in her own native way. "Never mind," she said gently. "You are a stranger, it is no matter about your having a name. Good-bye now, because I want my breakfast. You will come here and rest when you like. And don't you be unhappy, for we'll be good to you."

"Poor thing!" Baldassarre whispered again, as the door closed, leaving him to the quiet resonance of the shed.
