"Far, far from hence
The Adriatic breaks in a warm bay
Among the green Illyrian hills..."

Thus sang Matthew Arnold, and truly, the glens cutting the high table-land where Devon meets Cornwall possess a splendor akin to that distant vision. Yet, theirs is a solitude unlike any other in England. Here, the landscape rises abruptly from rich oak woodlands to steep slopes of furze, smooth turf, and finally, into the jaws of the sea—a jagged array of black ironstone cliffs, spires, and towers that plunge directly into the deep. Between these towering walls lies a narrow ribbon of fertile earth, threaded by crystal streams teeming with salmon-trout returning from the Atlantic, and bordered by fields of blown sand glowing with wildflowers. But it is the "mouth" itself—the cove—that commands awe. It is paved with shark's-tooth rocks, streaked with shell sand and laced with the white foam of the eternal surge, stretching outward in chaotic strata set upright by ancient tremors. To the east, the land offers softness and peace; to the west, a howling wilderness of rock and breaker, barren to the fisherman and a graveyard for the shipwrecked.

Of all these mouths, only one permits a landing: Marsland, protected by a formidable sea-wall of rock. It is here, the dwelling of the White Witch, Lucy Passmore, that Sir Richard Grenville correctly surmised the Jesuits had fled. Before their arrival, however, two figures stood upon this lonely beach under a brilliant October moon: Rose Salterne and the Witch herself. Driven by feverish curiosity and superstition, allured by the perilous enchantment of the place, Rose had kept her appointment. Just before midnight, she stood on the gray shingle with her counselor.

"You be safe enough here tonight, miss," Lucy assured her, her voice sharp with local dialect. "My old man is snoring soundly, and no soul else sets foot here save perhaps the mermaids now and then."

Rose pointed to a strip of sand some forty yards nearer the water where a small boat lay stranded. "Oh, the lazy old villain!" she exclaimed, bustling toward the vessel. "He's been fishing for pollock and neglected to haul the boat ashore. I'll trounce him when I return. I only pray he has secured her fast!"

"And indeed, 'tis fast, sure enough," Lucy replied, watching Rose approach. "The oars are aboard. Sit in the boat, dear, and watch me while you descend. You must be entirely alone to witness anything. Take the looking-glass; dip your head three times, and beware—do not look toward land or sea until you have spoken the words and gazed into the glass. Haste, it is nearly midnight."

Coiling herself into the boat, Lucy watched as Rose faltered down the sandy strip. Reaching the water's edge, the girl slipped off her clothes, shivering violently in the crisp air, and stood hesitating before entering the surf.

She found herself hemmed in by two walls of rock. To her left, a sheer cliff rose twenty feet, casting her in deepest shadow; to her right, a lower slope basked in the blazing light of the moon, adorned with heavy festoons of live and purple sea-weed hanging from dark crevices—the likely haunt of goblins. Above her left, the peaks frowned ghastly black; above her right, the downs slept bright and cold. The breeze had died away completely; not a single wave broke the perfect stillness of the cove. The gulls were silent on the ledges. An autumn silence reigned, so profound it could almost be heard.

Rose stood awestruck, listening for a sign of life beside her. Suddenly, a faint bleat, like a newborn lamb, echoed high above. She started, looking upward. A wail followed from the cliffs, answering with another cry from the opposite rocks. They were but the passing snipe and an otter calling to her young, yet to Rose, they sounded like supernatural spirits summoned to aid her. Nevertheless, they only quickened her dread. As instructed, she stepped into the cold water, clutching the mirror, and waded swiftly as far as she dared before stopping, aghast.

A ring of flame encircled her waist; every limb bathed in lambent light, the myriad life of the autumn sea, stirred by her presence, flashed suddenly into glory. Around her swam the lamps of sea-nymphs—myriad fiery globes—and rainbows of crimson, azure, and emerald shattered into star-showers, illuminating the wine-dark depths of the crystal garden where coral fans and tangled weeds beckoned her down to a grave amidst their chilly bowers. Hardly had the words ceased when her eyes strained into the mirror, seeing only the sparkle of droplets from her tresses, when the sound of rattling footsteps and hooves echoed down the pebbles. Men and horses were approaching.

Rose darted into a cavern within the high rock, hastily dressing. Peering out, half-paralyzed with terror, she saw four men. Two had dismounted and released their horses, helping the others launch the boat. From the stern emerged the portly, angry figure of Lucy Passmore, shrieking in a shrill treble:

"Eh! ye villains, ye rogues, what do ye want stealing poor folks' boats by night?"

The party recoiled in fright. One shouted, "It's a marmaiden! A marmaiden asleep in Willy Passmore's boat!"

"I wish it were any such good luck," Rose overheard Will mutter, shrinking back. "'Tis my wife, oh dear!" He cowered, bracing himself for the blow, as the White Witch, leaping from the boat, challenged any man to touch her and thundered orders to her husband to retire.

The cunning dame, as Rose suspected, used this diversion to buy time for her pupil. Yet, she fought harder than necessary, for she had already spotted the ungainly figure of one of the men—the suspicious Welsh gentleman whose arrival she had foreseen. She held her husband's interference with these "Popish skulkers" in extreme horror, unless bribed with a handsome sum. In vain did Parsons thunder, Campian entreat, and Mr. Leigh's groom swear; the husband danced in an agony of fear and greed.

"No!" cried Lucy, her voice ringing with false piety. "As I am an honest woman and loyal! Is this why you left the boat down to the shore, you old traitor? To smuggle out such noxious trade from Her Majesty's realm? Stand back, cowards! Dare ye strike a woman?"

Her words were merely prelude to violence. Seizing an oar, she swung it wildly, striking Father Parsons across the shins until he retreated with a howl.

"Lucy, Lucy!" shrieked her husband in a falsetto scream. "Are you mazed? They promised me two gold nobles before I'd lend the boot!"

"Two nobles?" shrieked the matron, with tones of infinite scorn. "Do you call yourself a man?"

"Two nobles! Give her ten, give her ten, brother Pars—" Campian interjected, stepping forward. "And take care of your shins, Offa Cerbero... Oh, virago! What can a woman do?"

"Take that, for your Lamias and Gorgons to an honest woman!" cried Lucy. In a flash, Campian fell, his legs knocked from under him. Mounting astride him, like the famous beast in *Hudibras*, she declared, "Ten nobles, or I'll keep ye here till morning!" The ten nobles were paid into her hand.

Now pacified, the boat was pushed into the sea, passing close to the nook where Rose was hiding in the shadows among wet sea-weed and rough barnacles, holding her breath. As the boat cleared the water, Lucy followed closely, spying a dark cavern near the edge. Cunningly guessing it harbored Rose, she blocked the entrance with her ample form, grumbling at her husband and the strangers, and specifically threatening Mr. Leigh's groom with Launceston Gaol and the gallows. The wretched groom, who would sooner leap from Welcombe Cliff than return her railing, vainly begged for mercy and dodged frantically to keep between himself and the Witch's dreaded eye.

Yet the night's adventures were not concluded. Just as the boat launched, a faint halloo rang out, and a minute later, a horseman plunged down the pebbles, racing along the sand. He pulled his horse to a halt beside the terrified group and slid from the saddle.
