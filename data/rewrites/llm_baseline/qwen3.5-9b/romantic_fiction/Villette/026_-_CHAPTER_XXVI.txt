Since that day, my existence had ceased to seek variety. I ventured out frequently, with the full approval of Madame Beck, who delighted in the caliber of my acquaintances. From the outset, this capable director had treated me with genuine respect; but when she discovered I was invited regularly to a château and a grand hotel, her respect evolved into a calculated distinction.

Madame Beck was not effusive in her praise. In all worldly matters, she possessed immense strength; there was precision and logic even in her most aggressive pursuit of self-interest, and a cool consideration within her tightest grasp of profit. Without exposing herself to my contempt as a sycophant, she displayed remarkable tact in noting that those associated with her establishment should mingle with peers who cultivated elevation rather than those who fostered decline. She rarely praised me or my friends directly. Only once, while seated comfortably in the garden sun with a cup of coffee and the *Gazette* in hand, did she offer a gracious concession when I requested leave for the evening:

"Oui, oui, ma bonne amie: je vous donne la permission de cœur et de gré. Your work in my house has always been admirable, filled with zeal and discretion; you have every right to enjoy yourself. Go out as much as you wish. As for your choice of company, I am pleased; it is wise, dignified, and praiseworthy."

She closed her lips and returned to her newspaper.

The reader need not take too seriously the minor incident wherein the triple-wrapped packet of five letters vanished from my desk. My initial reaction was blank dismay, but quickly I rallied.

"Patience!" I whispered to myself. "Say nothing, wait peacefully; they will return."

They did return. They had merely paid a brief visit to Madame's chambers; having passed inspection, they came back duly and truthfully. I found them all intact the next day.

I wondered what she thought of my correspondence. What estimate did she form of Dr. John Bretton's epistolary powers? How did the often pithy thoughts, the generally sound and occasionally original opinions, presented without pretension in an easy, spirited style, appear to her? How did she react to that genial, half-humorous vein that brought me such delight? What did she make of the few kind words scattered sparingly throughout, not thickly like diamonds in Sindbad's valley, but sparsely like gems in unfabled beds? Oh, Madame Beck! How did these things seem to you?

I suspect the five letters found favor in her eyes. One day, after she had "borrowed" them (one must use suave terms for such a smooth little woman), I caught her examining me with a steady, contemplative gaze—a look puzzled, yet entirely non-malevolent. It was during the brief interval between lessons when the pupils played in the courtyard; she and I remained alone in the first classroom. When our eyes met, her thoughts nearly spilled from her lips.

"There is," she said, "something remarkably notable in the English character."

"How, Madame?"

She laughed softly, repeating the word "how" in English.

"I cannot tell you 'how'; but, finally, the English possess ideas of their own regarding friendship, love, and everything else. But at least, it is not necessary to monitor them," she added, rising and trotting away like the compact little pony she was.

"Then I hope," I murmured to myself, "you will graciously leave my letters alone in the future."

Alas, tears rushed into my eyes, blinding me completely, erasing the schoolroom, the garden, and the bright winter sun from my sight. I recalled that no more letters, such as she had read, would ever reach me. I had witnessed the end of them. That noble river along whose banks I had sojourned, from whose waves a few reviving drops had trickled to my lips, was turning toward another course. It was abandoning my small hut and field, leaving them forlorn and sand-dry, carrying its wealth of waters far away. The change was right, just, and natural; no word could alter it. Yet I loved my Rhine, my Nile; I had almost worshipped my Ganges, and I grieved that the grand tide should roll estranged, vanishing like a false mirage. Though stoical, I was not quite a stoic; tears streamed rapidly down my hands and onto my desk. I wept one sultry, heavy, and brief shower.

Soon, however, I told myself, "The hope I mourn caused me suffering and made me suffer greatly; it did not die until it was time. Following such a lingering agony, death ought to be welcome."

I endeavored to welcome it. Indeed, long pain had made patience a habit. In the end, I closed the eyes of my dead, covered its face, and arranged its limbs with great calm.

Nevertheless, the letters had to be put away, out of sight. Those who have endured bereavement always jealously gather and lock away mementos; it is unbearable to be stabbed to the heart at every moment by a sharp resurgence of regret.

One empty holiday afternoon (a Thursday), going to my treasure to consider its final disposition, I perceived—with a strong impulse of displeasure—that it had been tampered with again. The packet was there, but the ribbon securing it had been untied and retied; other signs indicated my drawer had been visited.

This was too much. Madame Beck herself was the soul of discretion, possessing a brain as sound and judgment as robust as ever adorned a human head. That she knew the contents of my casket was unpleasant, but bearable. However, the idea that she had ventured to communicate this information to others—that she might have amused herself with a companion over documents I considered most sacred—shocked me cruelly. Yet, I now saw reason to fear this was exactly the case; I even suspected her confidante. Her kinsman, M. Paul Emanuel, had spent the previous evening with her. She was accustomed to consulting him and discussing with him matters she shared with no one else. That very morning, in class, that gentleman had bestowed upon me a glance seemingly borrowed from the actress Vashti; I had not immediately comprehended that blue, yet lurid flash in his angry eye, but I understood its meaning now. *He*, I believed, was not inclined to view what concerned me fairly, nor to judge me with tolerance or candor. I had always found him severe and suspicious. The thought that these letters, mere friendly missives, had fallen into his hands once and might fall again, jarred my very soul.

What should I do to prevent this? In what corner of this strange house could I find security or secrecy? Where could a key serve as a safeguard, or a padlock a barrier?

In the attic (*grenier*)? No, I disliked the attic. Moreover, most boxes and drawers there were rotting and unlocked. Rats gnawed through the decayed wood, and mice nested among the debris of its contents. My dear letters (still most dear, despite "Ichabod" written on their covers) might be consumed by vermin; certainly, the ink would soon blur from damp. No; the attic would not do—but where then?

While pondering this dilemma, I sat on the dormitory window-seat. It was a fine frosty afternoon; the winter sun, already setting, gleamed pale on the tops of the garden shrubs in the "defended alley." One great old pear-tree—the nun's pear-tree—stood up like a tall, dryad skeleton, grey, gaunt, and stripped. A thought struck me—one of those queer, fantastic notions that sometimes assail solitary minds. I donned my bonnet, cloak, and furs, and walked out into the city.

Turning my steps toward the old historical quarter of the town, whose hoary and overshadowed precincts I always sought by instinct during melancholy moods, I wandered from street to street. Crossing a half-deserted square, I arrived before a sort of broker's shop—an ancient place full of ancient things. What I needed was a metal box to be soldered, or a thick glass jar or bottle that could be stoppered or sealed hermetically. Among miscellaneous heaps, I found and purchased the latter.

I then rolled my letters tightly, wrapped them in oiled silk, bound them with twine, placed them inside the bottle, and instructed the old Jewish broker to stopper, seal, and make it air-tight. While obeying my directions, he glanced at me now and then suspiciously beneath his frost-white eyelashes. I believe he thought some evil deed was underway. In all this, I felt a dreary something—not pleasure, but a sad, lonely satisfaction. The impulse driving me, the mood controlling me, were similar to those that had led me to the confessional. With quick strides, I returned to the pensionnat just as darkness fell, in time for dinner.

At seven o'clock, the moon rose. At half-past seven, when the pupils and teachers were studying, and Madame Beck was with her mother and children in the dining hall, when the half-boarders had gone home, and Rosine had left the vestibule, and all was still—I shawled myself, took the sealed jar, and slipped out through the first-class door, into the nursery, and thence into the "defended alley."
