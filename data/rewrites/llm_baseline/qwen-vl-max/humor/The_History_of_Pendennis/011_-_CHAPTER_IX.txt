CHAPTER IX. In Which the Major Launches His Campaign Against Love, Romance, and Common Sense

Let us pause for a moment to honor the true hero of this tale—Major Pendennis, a man whose devotion to duty, family, and social propriety knew no bounds, except perhaps the bounds of his own sanity. For what greater sacrifice could a gentleman make than to abandon London in May? Not just the newspapers and morning walks (though those were treasures), but the entire glittering apparatus of high society: the club-hopping afternoons, the whispered confidences with ladies of title, the jaunty rides through Rotten Row, the opera stalls where he sat like a king, and even the Saturday excursions to Fulham or Richmond—where one might see a nobleman’s hat bobbing over a hedge, or catch a glimpse of a duke’s carriage passing by.

And yet—this was not all. He gave up the very *idea* of London. The Morning Post no longer bore his name in its social pages; the Court Guide lost its charm. He resigned himself to a quiet country house, surrounded by a widow (his sister-in-law), a boy who had never seen a proper dinner party, a curate who spoke of God as if he were a distant cousin, and a ten-year-old girl who probably thought her uncle was a bit odd.

But here is the rub: the Major didn’t do it for virtue. He did it for *duty*. And because he believed, with the solemnity of a man who’d once been at the head of a regiment, that such sacrifices were necessary for the good of the nation—and especially for the salvation of young Pen.

Now, you may think this sounds noble. You may even weep at the image of him refusing invitations with a sigh, as though each refusal were a martyrdom. But let us be honest: few people noticed. Helen, Pen’s mother, merely smiled when he lamented the loss of “the Court Guide.” She didn’t understand the weight of his sorrow—how could she? She wasn’t a man of the world. But young Pen? Ah, Pen listened. He listened with wide eyes, like a child who has just discovered that there are whole continents beyond his village.

The Major, ever the mentor, filled his nephew’s ears with tales of royalty, dukes, and fashionable scandals. He regaled him with stories of Admiral Bowser, who had “rat-ted” to Ministers (a serious offense, according to the Major), only to have his reputation shredded by a young man named Mr. Suckling—whose joke about the Admiral’s conduct was interrupted by none other than Lady Grampound Bowser’s daughter, sitting right beside him, and Lord Grampound Bowser’s son-in-law across the table.

“Imagine,” said the Major, “the horror! The man was doomed. Never again will he dine in South Street. I promise you.”

Pen absorbed these lessons like a sponge. He learned that genealogy was more important than theology, and that reading Debrett every day was essential. “Not so much the history,” the Major explained, “as the alliances. A man’s career can be ruined by ignorance on this point.” He then paused, as if waiting for Pen to nod wisely. Pen nodded.

He also advised Pen on church attendance. “It don’t matter so much in town,” the Major said, “but when a gentleman is sur ses terres, he must set an example.” He mentioned the Duke of Saint David’s, who sang in church—“a doosed fine effect from the family pew”—and added that Pen, being the first man in the parish (since the Claverings were away), should consider himself “as good as any.”

“Not if you marry a lady whom the country folk won’t meet,” he warned. “That would be unfortunate.”

Ah, yes—the marriage question. This was the Major’s favorite topic. He returned to it constantly, like a clockwork bird returning to its perch. Pen, poor fellow, began to feel that every conversation was leading to the same conclusion: *You’re throwing yourself away.*

And why not? It’s easy to convince a boy that he’s destined for greatness. Pen, eager for approval, took his uncle’s advice seriously. He found Captain Costigan—Emily’s father—increasingly unbearable. The man reeked of punch, wore clothes that looked as if they’d been dug out of a cellar, and had a habit of staring at the ceiling during conversations as if trying to remember where he left his dignity.

Pen couldn’t bring this man near his mother. He couldn’t imagine introducing him to anyone. Even thinking about it made him shudder. And yet—here was the cruel irony—he had already written to Costigan, enclosing a sovereign and signing off as “your affectionate son, Arthur Pendennis.”

Yes. That’s right. He’d called himself someone else’s son.

When the Major questioned him about Emily, Pen faltered. Was she accomplished? No. Clever? Well, average. Could he show some letters? Only three—invitations and replies, nothing passionate, nothing poetic. “She is cautious enough,” the Major said, dry as a bone. “Older than you, my poor boy.”

Then came the apology: a humble, heartfelt plea from a fond uncle who only wanted to protect the family’s honor. But Pen was furious. He swore, before Emily herself, before Miss Rouncy (the confidante), before the Captain himself, that he would never betray Emily. The Captain, moved to tears, embraced him, wiped his eye with a hand that smelled faintly of gin, and declared that no man should meddle with his daughter’s heart.

Pen shuddered. He thought of his uncle. He thought of the life he was supposed to lead.

And so he avoided Chatteris. Not because he didn’t love Emily—but because he feared his uncle’s judgment. Every time he rode over, the Major seemed to know exactly why. There was no hiding it. One day, Pen walked half a mile beyond the Lodge gates to wait for the Competitor coach. On the roof of the Fairoaks Inn, a man waved. It was Mr. Morgan—the Major’s valet. Who else?

Mr. Morgan had been sent to investigate. He’d gone to Chatteris multiple times. He’d questioned waiters, ostlers, barflies. He learned that Captain Costigan was broke, drunk, and banned from the messroom. He’d asked the actors at the theatre—“What do you think of Miss Fotheringay?”—and received answers like, “She’s a splendid woman. Very careful. Her father watches her like a hawk.”

Mrs. Creed, the pew-opener, confirmed it. Emily never entertained alone. Always a chaperone—a child, a teacher, a theater girl. Once, when Pen visited, she’d come downstairs only after ensuring Mrs. Creed was present.

So the Major sighed. “If it were but a temporary liaison,” he said, “one could bear it. But a virtuous attachment? That’s the deuce. It comes from being brought up by women.”

Doctor Portman disagreed. “Nothing could be better for Pen than a virtuous attachment,” he said. “But this one—I deplore it.”

“And I say,” replied the Major, “that the best way to stop him from marrying her is to make him marry *someone else* tomorrow.”

They discussed it. Then Doctor Portman suggested something bold: “Suppose we go and see her?”

The Major laughed. “I hadn’t thought of that. After all, she’s not my niece. She’s Miss Fotheringay the actress. We’ve as good a right as any other man to see her—if we pay our money.”

So they went. They dined at the George Inn, where two other guests sat quietly—officer and student, both unremarkable. Then they hurried to the theatre. It was *Hamlet*, of course. Shakespeare was Article XL of Doctor Portman’s creed. He always saw it once a year, just to prove he was still a man of culture.

Miss Fotheringay played Ophelia. As usual, she delivered the same performance night after night—precise, rehearsed, flawless. But the Major and the Doctor watched with new eyes. They saw the beauty, the grace, the intelligence. “Gad,” muttered the Major, “the young rascal hasn’t made a bad choice.”

The Doctor clapped loudly. “She’s a clever actress. And remarkably attractive.”

And then the Major pointed to the stage-box. “Look,” he said. “That young dragoon is clearly smitten.”

Indeed, the young officer was applauding wildly. And Miss Fotheringay—well, she glanced at him with a look that could only be described as… encouraging.

The Major closed his opera-glass with a snap. “No more tonight,” he said.

They left. The Doctor returned to his wife at the Deanery. The Major walked back to the George, where he had reserved a bed.

And as he passed under the gaslight, he thought not of romance, nor of duty, nor even of Pen.

He thought of the next move.

Because in the game of love, as in war, the best strategy is often to watch your enemy closely—and then strike when they least expect it.
