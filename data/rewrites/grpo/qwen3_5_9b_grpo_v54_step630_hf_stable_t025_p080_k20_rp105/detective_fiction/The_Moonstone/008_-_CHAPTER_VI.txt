I kept my private thoughts to myself and respectfully urged Mr. Franklin to proceed. "Don't fidget, Betteredge," he replied, and continued.

My young master's opening remarks revealed that his discoveries regarding the wicked Colonel and the Diamond had originated with a visit he paid, prior to arriving at our house, to the family lawyer in Hampstead. A casual remark dropped by Mr. Franklin one afternoon, after dinner, disclosed that his father had entrusted him with a birthday present for Miss Rachel. One thing led to another, culminating in the lawyer revealing the true nature of the gift and the origins of the friendly connection between the late Colonel and Mr. Blake, senior. The facts were so extraordinary that I doubted my own ability to render them justice; thus, I preferred to report Mr. Franklin's findings as nearly as possible in his own words.

"You remember the time, Betteredge," he said, "when my father was striving to prove his claim to that unfortunate Dukedom? Well, that coincided with the period when my uncle Herncastle returned from India. My father discovered that his brother-in-law held certain papers likely to aid his lawsuit. He called upon the Colonel under the pretext of welcoming him home. The Colonel was not easily deceived. 'You want something,' he said, 'or you would never compromise your reputation by calling on _me_.' My father realized his only chance was to show his hand; he immediately admitted he desired the papers. The Colonel asked for a day to consider, and his reply arrived in the form of a most extraordinary letter, which my friend the lawyer showed me. The Colonel began by stating he wanted something from my father and proposed an exchange of friendly services. The fortunes of war had placed him in possession of one of the largest diamonds in the world, and he believed neither he nor his precious jewel was safe in any house or quarter of the globe where they coexisted. Under these alarming circumstances, he resolved to entrust his Diamond to another. This person was not expected to run any risk; they might deposit the stone in any specially guarded location, like a banker's or jeweller's strongroom, for the safe custody of high-value items. His main responsibility was passive: he was to receive, either personally or through a trusted representative, at a prearranged address on specific days each year, a note from the Colonel certifying he was still alive. If the date passed without the note, the Colonel's silence would be deemed a sure token of murder. In that sole event, certain sealed instructions regarding the Diamond's disposal, deposited with it, were to be opened and followed implicitly. If my father accepted this strange charge, the Colonel's papers were at his disposal in return. That was the letter."

"What did your father do, sir?" I asked.

"Do?" Mr. Franklin said. "He applied the invaluable faculty of common sense to the Colonel's letter. He declared the whole affair simply absurd. Somewhere in his Indian wanderings, the Colonel had picked up some wretched crystal he mistook for a diamond. As for the danger of murder and the precautions devised to preserve his life and his piece of glass, this was the nineteenth century; any man in his senses would apply to the police. The Colonel had been a notorious opium-eater for years; if the only way to obtain the valuable papers was to accept opium as a matter of fact, my father was quite willing to take the ridiculous responsibility, especially since it involved no trouble to himself. The Diamond and the sealed instructions went into his banker's strongroom, and the Colonel's periodic letters reporting him alive were received and opened by our family lawyer, Mr. Bruff, as my father's representative. No sensible person in a similar position could have viewed the matter differently. Nothing in this world is probable unless it appeals to our own trumpery experience; we only believe in a romance when we see it in a newspaper."

It was plain that Mr. Franklin considered his father's notion of the Colonel hasty and incorrect.

"What is your own private opinion, sir?" I asked.

"Let's finish the story of the Colonel first," Mr. Franklin said. "There is a curious lack of system in the English mind; your question, my old friend, is an instance of it. When we are not occupied in making machinery, we are mentally the most slovenly people in the universe."

"So much," I thought to myself, "for a foreign education! He has learned that way of girding at us in France, I suppose."

Mr. Franklin resumed the thread. "My father obtained the papers he wanted and never saw his brother-in-law again. Year after year, on the prearranged days, the prearranged letter came from the Colonel and was opened by Mr. Bruff. I have seen the letters, piled together, all written in the same brief, business-like form: 'Sir,—This is to certify that I am still a living man. Let the Diamond be. John Herncastle.' That was all he ever wrote, regularly to the day, until some six or eight months ago, when the form varied for the first time. It now read: 'Sir,—They tell me I am dying. Come to me, and help me to make my will.' Mr. Bruff went and found him in the little suburban villa, surrounded by its own grounds, where he had lived alone since leaving India. He had dogs, cats, and birds for company, but no human being except the daily housekeeper and the doctor at his bedside. The will was simple. The Colonel had dissipated most of his fortune in chemical investigations. His will consisted of three clauses dictated from his bed in perfect possession of his faculties. The first provided for the care of his animals. The second founded a professorship of experimental chemistry at a northern university. The third bequeathed the Moonstone as a birthday present to his niece, on condition that my father act as executor. My father initially refused, but on second thoughts, he agreed, partly because he was assured the executorship involved no trouble, and partly because Mr. Bruff suggested, in Rachel's interest, that the Diamond might be worth something after all."

"Did the Colonel give any reason, sir," I inquired, "why he left the Diamond to Miss Rachel?"

"He not only gave the reason—he had it written in his will," Mr. Franklin said. "I have an extract you shall see presently. Don't be slovenly-minded, Betteredge! One thing at a time. You have heard of the Colonel's Will; now you must hear what happened after his death. It was formally necessary to value the Diamond before the Will could be proved. All consulted jewellers confirmed the Colonel's assertion that he possessed one of the largest diamonds in the world. Valuing it accurately presented serious difficulties. Its size made it a phenomenon in the market; its colour placed it in a category of its own; and, adding to these uncertainties, there was a defect, a flaw, in the very heart of the stone. Even with this serious drawback, the lowest estimate was twenty thousand pounds. Conceive my father's astonishment! He had been within a hair's-breadth of refusing to act as executor and allowing this magnificent jewel to be lost to the family. His renewed interest induced him to open the sealed instructions deposited with the Diamond. Mr. Bruff showed this document to me, and it suggests, to my mind, a clue to the nature of the conspiracy threatening the Colonel's life."

"Then you do believe, sir," I said, "that there was a conspiracy?"

"Not possessing my father's excellent common sense," answered Mr. Franklin, "I believe the Colonel's life was threatened exactly as he said. The sealed instructions explain how he died quietly in his bed. In the event of his death by violence—that is, the absence of the regular letter at the appointed date—my father was directed to send the Moonstone secretly to Amsterdam. It was to be deposited with a famous diamond-cutter and cut into four to six separate stones. These were then to be sold, and the proceeds applied to founding the professorship of experimental chemistry which the Colonel has since endowed by his Will. Now, Betteredge, exert those sharp wits of yours and observe the conclusion to which the Colonel's instructions point!"

I instantly exerted my wits. They were of the slovenly English sort and muddled the matter until Mr. Franklin took them in hand.

"Remark," says Mr. Franklin, "that the integrity of the Diamond as a whole stone is here artfully made dependent on the preservation of the Colonel's life from violence. He is not satisfied with saying to his enemies, 'Kill me—and you will be no nearer to the Diamond than you are now; it is where you can't get at it—in the guarded strongroom of a bank.' He says instead, 'Kill me—and the Diamond will be the Diamond no longer; its identity will be destroyed.' What does that mean?"

Here I thought I had a flash of wonderful foreign brightness.

"I know," I said. "It means lowering the value of the stone and cheating the rogues in that way!"

"Nothing of the sort," says Mr. Franklin. "I have inquired about that. The flawed Diamond, cut up, would actually fetch more than the Diamond as it now is; for this plain reason—that from four to six perfect brilliants might be cut from it, which would collectively be worth more money than the large but imperfect single stone. If robbery for gain were the motive, the Colonel's instructions absolutely made the Diamond better worth stealing. More money could have been got for it, and its disposal in the diamond market would have been infinitely easier if it had passed through the hands of the workmen of Amsterdam."

"Lord bless us, sir!" I burst out. "What was the plot, then?"

"A plot organized among the Indians who originally owned the jewel," says Mr. Franklin—"a plot with some old Hindoo superstition at the bottom of it. That is my opinion, confirmed by a family paper I have about me."

I saw now why the appearance of the three Indian jugglers at our house had struck Mr. Franklin as noteworthy.

"I don't want to force my opinion on you," Mr. Franklin went on. "The idea of certain chosen servants of an old Hindoo superstition devoting themselves, through all difficulties and dangers, to watching the opportunity of recovering their sacred gem, appears to _me_ perfectly consistent with everything we know of the patience of Oriental races and the influence of Oriental religions. But then I am an imaginative man; and the butcher, the baker, and the tax-gatherer are not the only credible realities in existence to _my_ mind. Let my guess go for what it is worth, and let us get on to the only practical question. Does the conspiracy against the Moonstone survive the Colonel's death? And did the Colonel know it when he left the birthday gift to his niece?"

I began to see my lady and Miss Rachel at the end of it all. Not a word he said escaped me.

"I was not very willing, when I discovered the story of the Moonstone," said Mr. Franklin, "to be the means of bringing it here. But Mr. Bruff reminded me that somebody must put my cousin's legacy into my cousin's hands—and that I might as well do it as anybody else. After taking the Diamond out of the bank, I fancied I was followed by a shabby, dark-complexioned man. I went to my father's house to pick up my luggage and found a letter there that detained me in London. I returned to the bank with the Diamond and thought I saw the shabby man again. Taking the Diamond out of the bank this morning, I saw the man for the third time, gave him the slip, and started by the morning train instead of the afternoon one. Here I am, with the Diamond safe and sound—and what is the first news that meets me? I find that three strolling Indians have been at the house, and that my arrival from London, and something I am expected to have about me, are special objects of their investigation when they believe themselves alone. I don't waste time on their pouring ink into the boy's hand and telling him to look for a man at a distance. The thing is 'hocus-pocus' in my opinion. The present question is: am I wrongly attaching meaning to a mere accident, or do we have evidence that the Indians are on the track of the Moonstone the moment it leaves the bank's safe keeping?"

Neither he nor I fancied dealing with this part of the inquiry. We looked at each other, then at the tide oozing smoothly higher over the Shivering Sand.

"What are you thinking of?" Mr. Franklin asked suddenly.

"I was thinking, sir," I answered, "that I should like to shy the Diamond into the quicksand and settle the question in _that_ way."

"If you have got the value of the stone in your pocket," answered Mr. Franklin, "say so, Betteredge, and in it goes!"

It is curious how a very small joke goes far toward relief when the mind is anxious. We found a fund of merriment in the notion of making away with Miss Rachel's lawful property and getting Mr. Blake, as executor, into dreadful trouble—though where the merriment lay, I am quite at a loss to discover now.

Mr. Franklin was the first to bring the talk back to its proper purpose. He took an envelope from his pocket, opened it, and handed me the paper inside.

"Betteredge," he said, "we must face the question of the Colonel's motive in leaving this legacy to his niece, for my aunt's sake. Bear in mind how Lady Verinder treated her brother from the time he returned to England until he told you he should remember his niece's birthday. And read that."

He gave me the extract from the Colonel's Will. I have it by me while writing these words and copy it for your benefit:
