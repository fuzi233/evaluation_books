The carriage waited at the steps of Guessens, its roof laden with Durrance's luggage, his servant poised at the door. Within the drawing-room, Durrance bid Ethne farewell with a hurried efficiency, arranging their parting to be brief. Yet, as they stepped onto the terrace, Mrs. Adair stood at the top of the stone flight, intercepting the moment.

"I must speak to Colonel Durrance before he departs," she said.

Ethne nodded, turning to her lover. "Then we shall say goodbye here," she told Durrance, her voice tight with the weight of recent months. "Write from Wiesbaden? Soon, please!"

"The instant I arrive," Durrance promised.

He descended the steps with Mrs. Adair, leaving Ethne alone on the terrace. The theater of pretense had finally closed; the long vigil of surveillance was over, and both men and women breathed freely. Durrance moved across the lawn with brisk energy, eager to leave the house behind. Mrs. Adair, however, trailed in his wake, her step heavy.

"So you are going," she murmured, her voice dripping with desolation. "In two days, you will be in Wiesbaden, and Ethne at Glenalla. We shall be scattered. This place will feel terribly empty."

She had achieved her goal: separation. The sight of them together, the sound of their voices, was no longer a torture. Yet, strangely, her victory tasted like ash. "The house will feel very hollow after you are all gone," she added, turning to walk beside him into the garden.

"We shall return, no doubt," Durrance offered, trying to soothe her.

Mrs. Adair scanned the grounds. The summer blooms were gone, the sunlight dimmed by gathering clouds. The grass beneath their feet was a dull green, the stream running grey through the trees, and autumn leaves—russet and yellow—drifted across the lawns.

"How long will you stay in Wiesbaden?" she asked.

"I cannot say. As long as it is advisable," he replied.

"That tells me nothing," she snapped. "I suppose that was the point."

Durrance said nothing, and her silence bred resentment. She knew nothing of his plans, unaware if he intended to honor his engagement to Ethne or sever it entirely. Curvature gnawed at her. The prospect of a long separation filled her with dread.

"Do you distrust me?" she demanded, her voice rising with anger.

Durrance answered gently. "Have I no cause to distrust you? Why did you reveal Captain Willoughby's arrival? Why did you interfere?"

"I thought you ought to know."

"But Ethne wished that secret kept. I am glad to know, very glad. Yet, after all, you told me, and you were Ethne's friend."

"Yours, too, I hope," Mrs. Adair retorted, her composure cracking. "How could I maintain silence? Don't you understand?"

"No."

Durrance might have understood, but he had never truly considered her, and she felt the sting of that indifference. His simple denial rankled.

"I spoke brutally, didn't I?" she said. "I told you the truth as harshly as I could. Does that not help you understand?"

Again, Durrance said only "No." The monosyllable shattered her restraint. Suddenly, she began to speak incoherently, pouring out thoughts she had long suppressed. Standing outside herself, she watched her own madness unfold, yet she could not stop.

"I told you the truth brutally on purpose," she confessed, her voice trembling. "I was stung because you refused to see what was plain to anyone else. I wanted to hurt you. I am a bad, bad woman, I suppose. There you and she were in the room, talking in the dark; there I was alone on the terrace. It was the same today. You and Ethne inside, I outside. I wonder if it will always be so. But you will not say—you will not say." She struck her hands together in despair, but Durrance walked silently toward the stile, quickening his pace until she had to hurry to keep up. It was a silent rebuke, but it failed to deter her.

"I would not have minded so much," she continued, her scorn sharpening, "if Ethne had truly cared for you. But she never did more than a friend cares. And what is friendship worth?"

"Something, surely," Durrance said.

"It does not prevent Ethne from shrinking from her friend!" Mrs. Adair cried. "She shrinks from you. Shall I tell you why? Because you are blind. She is afraid. While I... while I will tell you the truth—I am glad. When the news came from Wadi Halfa that you were blind, I was glad; when I saw you in Hill Street, I was glad; ever since, I have been glad—quite glad. Because I saw her shrink. From the beginning, she shrank, fearing her life would be hampered and fettered," her voice dropping to a whisper, thick with venom. "I am not afraid," she repeated passionately. "I am not afraid. I am not afraid."

To Durrance, this outburst from Ethne's friend seemed more horrific than anything he had ever witnessed.

"Ethne wrote to you at Wadi Halfa out of pity," Mrs. Adair insisted. "That was all. She wrote out of pity; then, terrified of what she had done, she lacked the courage to admit her fear. You would not have blamed her if she had been frank; you would have remained her friend. But she had no courage."

Durrance knew there was another explanation, the true one, for Ethne's timidity. He also knew that arguing against Mrs. Adair's slanders now would be futile. Tomorrow he would leave the Salcombe estuary, and Ethne would head for the Irish Channel. He merely said quietly, "You are not just to Ethne."

At those words, Mrs. Adair's madness evaporated. She recognized the futility of her boasts and her detractions. Her words might be true, but they achieved nothing. Durrance was always in the room with Ethne, never on the terrace with her. Realizing her degradation, she fell back into excuses.

"I am a bad woman, I suppose," she whispered, sounding pitiful even to herself. "But after all, I have not had the happiest of lives. Perhaps there is something to be said for me."

They reached the stile, and Durrance turned to face her. He saw the sternness fade from his own expression, replaced by a quiet readiness to listen. He recalled the dignity and reticence she once possessed, and the violence of the contrast made him willing to hear her side.

"Will you tell me?" he asked gently.

"I was married almost straight from school," she began, her voice simple and unadorned. "I was a mere girl, knowing nothing, married to a man I knew nothing of. It was my mother's doing. She thought she was acting for the best, securing me a position, comfort, and safety from poverty. I accepted blindly, ignorantly. My mother was imperious, and I was accustomed to obedience. I married dutifully the man she chose. The case is common, no doubt, but its frequency makes it no easier to endure."

"But Mr. Adair?" Durrance interjected. "After all, I knew him. He was older, certainly, but kind. I think he cared for you."

"Yes. He was kindness itself, and he cared for me. Both are true. That knowledge was the only link. At first, I was content. I had a house in town and another here. But it was dull," she gestured with her arms, spreading them wide. "Oh, how dull it was! Do you know the little back streets of a manufacturing town? Rows of identical houses, ugly and regular, with the same windows, the same doors, the same steps. Overhead, a drift of smoke; below, every green thing black and dirty. It is the sort of street where any charlatan promising color to grey lives can gather followers. When I thought of my life, one of those streets always came to mind. There are women, countless women, for whom managing a large house, the London season, and the round of visits are enough. I, alas, was not one of them. Dull! You, with your hundred thousand duties, cannot conceive how oppressively dull my life was. And that was not all!" She hesitated, unable to stop midway.

"I married, knowing nothing of the important things. I believed at first that this was the allotted life of all women. But soon doubts arose. I learned that there was something more to be won from existence than mere dullness; at least, something more for others, though not for me. One cannot help learning this. One passes a man and woman riding together, glances into the woman's face, or speaks briefly to her alone, and from the happiness in her eyes and voice, one knows with absolute certainty that there is ever so much more. Only the chance of that 'ever so much more' my mother had denied to me."

All sternness had vanished from Durrance's face. Mrs. Adair spoke with great simplicity, the violence of her earlier outburst gone. She did not appeal for pity; she simply told her story.

"And then you came," she continued. "I met you, and met you again. You went away on your duties and returned; and I learned now, not that there was 'ever so much more,' but exactly what that meant. But it was still denied to me. Yet, in spite of that, I felt happier. I thought I would be content to have you as a friend, to watch your progress, and to feel pride in it. But you see—Ethne came, too, and you turned to her. At once—oh, at once! If you had only been a little slower to turn to her! In a very short while, I was sad and sorry that you had ever entered my life."

"I knew nothing of this," Durrance said. "I never suspected. I am sorry."

"I took care you should not suspect," Mrs. Adair replied. "But I tried to keep you; with all my wits I tried. No matchmaker in the world ever worked harder to bring two people together than I did to unite Ethne and Mr. Feversham, and I succeeded."

The statement struck Durrance like a blow. He leaned back against the stile, almost laughing. Here was the origin of the entire tragedy. From such small beginnings it had grown! It was a trite reflection, but personally applied, it stole the breath. Durrance thought backward to those days when he walked his own path, heedless of the people he touched, never dreaming they were influencing his life right up to his dying day. Feversham's disgrace, Ethne's years of unhappiness, the wearying pretenses of the last few months—all stemmed from years ago when Mrs. Adair, to keep Durrance for herself, had thrown Feversham and Ethne into each other's company.

"I succeeded," Mrs. Adair continued. "You told me one morning in the Row that I had succeeded. How glad I was! You did not notice, I am sure. The next moment, you took all my joy by telling me you were starting for the Soudan. You were away three years. They were not happy years for me. You came back. My husband was dead, but Ethne was free. Ethne refused you, but you went blind, and she claimed you. You can see what ups and downs have fallen to me. But these months here have been the worst."

"I am very sorry," Durrance said. Mrs. Adair was right; there was indeed something to be said for her. The world had been cruel to her. He realized her suffering, for he suffered in much the same way himself. It was intelligible why she had betrayed Ethne's secret that night on the terrace, and he could not help but be gentle.

"I am very sorry, Mrs. Adair," he repeated lamely. There was nothing more to say, and he held out his hand.

"Good-bye," she said. Durrance climbed over the stile and crossed the fields to his house.

Mrs. Adair stood by the stile for a long time after he had gone. She had fired her bolt, hitting no one but herself and the man she cared for. She realized this distinctly. She looked forward slightly and understood that if Durrance did not keep Ethne to her promise and marry her, he would return to Guessens. That reflection revealed the folly of her outcry even more clearly. If she had kept silent, she would have had a true and constant friend for her neighbor, and that would have been something. It would have been a great deal. But since she had spoken, they could never meet without embarrassment. No matter how cordially they practiced, there would always remain in their minds the recollection of what she had said and what he had listened to on the afternoon he left for Wiesbaden.
