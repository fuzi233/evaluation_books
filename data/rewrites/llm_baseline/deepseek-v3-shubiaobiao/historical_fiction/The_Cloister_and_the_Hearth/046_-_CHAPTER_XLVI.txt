Catherine was a practical housewife who rarely left home, for whenever she did, something always went amiss. She was acutely aware of this, so she waited for a lull in household duties before making her trip to Sevenbergen—only to find Peter Buyskens’ mule unavailable on the one afternoon she could have gone.

At last, Eli confronted her in front of the family. "Is it true you meant to visit Margaret Brandt?"

"Aye, husband."

"Then I forbid it."

"Oh, do you?"

"I do."

"Well, then I suppose that settles it," she said, flushing.

"Not another word," Eli replied sternly.

Alone with Kate later, Catherine turned her frustration inward.

"I should’ve gone like a cat after a robin—quick and quiet. But no, I must cluck about it like a foolish hen and bring the whole house down on my plans. Next time we mean to do anything the least bit questionable, let’s do it then and there, without a word. Then, if they scold us, we can plead ignorance—‘Oh, we never thought! Who would’ve guessed?’ For two pins, I’d march straight there now, defiance in my teeth!"

Kate was taken aback by such boldness. "Nay, mother, patience. Father will come around."

"Aye, and so will Michaelmas—but when? I wanted you to see the girl for yourself. Then we could have judged her fairly. No matter what they say, you must lay eyes on a creature to know its worth. If I had fifty sons, I’d never thwart one’s fancy without seeing the lass first. But there—life’s a school, and the lesson’s never done. We mend one fault only to take up another, blundering on till we blunder into our graves."

Kate hesitated. "Mother..."

"Well? Out with it. That look bodes no good news."

"I have a confession," she murmured.

Catherine’s face lit with pride. "A fine word! Our Gerard taught you that, I’ll warrant. Speak, then."

"The truth is... I’ve seen her."

"And?"

"Spoken with her, too."

"And never told me? Wonders never cease!"

"You were so set against her. I waited for a time when it wouldn’t anger you further."

Catherine sighed, half bitter, half sad. "Like mother, like daughter. Cowardice is our curse. The others I scold, else the house would fall apart—but you, Kate? Have I ever been harsh with you?" She softened. "No tears, child. Likely you had your reasons. Now tell me everything."

Kate obliged. She explained how she and Giles had gone to the Stadthouse tower at night to comfort Gerard—only to find a stranger already there.

"At first we frightened each other half to death—what with the tower’s ill name and our own superstitions. But then I said, ‘You’re Margaret,’ and she answered, ‘And you’re Kate.’ Imagine!"

Catherine clapped her hands. "Of course! Gerard must have spoken of you to her, and her to you."

Kate responded with a kiss—a single gesture that conveyed understanding, admiration, and reconciliation all at once.

"Now speak plainly," Catherine urged. "Is she comely?"

"Very, and little different from her portrait."

"Pah! Looks are nothing. Does she love my boy as he deserves?"

"Sevenbergen is farther from the Stadthouse than we are, yet she got there before me. More than that—she had already freed him by the time I arrived."

Catherine scoffed. "The highest tower for miles? Impossible."

"But true. She and an old man sent up a rope. When I first saw it dangling, I thought it witchcraft. But when she embraced me—her heart pounding, her cheeks wet—I knew it was no spell. It was love. She’s strong, not frail like me, but even I feel sometimes as if I could move the world for those I love. And she loves Gerard."

"God bless her for it!"

"But—"

"But what, child?"

"Is her love truly honest? Strange as it sounds, the very thing that warms your heart chills mine. She’s no wife, when all’s said and done."

"They stood at the altar."

"Aye, but left as they came—maid and bachelor."

"The priest—does he say so?"

"I don’t know."

"Then I’ll take no man’s word but his in such a tangle. Still, you’re right. I’ll go to Sevenbergen alone. I’m a wife, not a slave. We’re in the dark here, and she holds the key. I must question her without ears about—least of all yours. I’ll bring no lily with a blight into this house, not even for a palace of gold."
