Fra Colonna had free rein of the Pope’s library, and often, when his labors ended at the same hour as Gerard’s, the two would walk the city together. To Gerard, Rome was a vision of grandeur—its churches, palaces, and ruins bathed in golden light. But the friar, ever the skeptic, tempered his enthusiasm with cold reason.

“Rome?” he scoffed. “This is but the tomb of mighty Rome.” He showed Gerard how twenty or thirty feet of ancient triumphal arches lay buried beneath the streets, how modern paths ran over the bones of forgotten columns and palaces. With a sardonic smile, he likened the city to a swallow’s nest perched on the crumbling eaves of an abbey.

Gerard marveled. “Then old Rome must have been glorious indeed.”

“Judge for yourself,” said the friar. “You see the great sewer, a feat of Rome’s infancy, yet sturdier than Vesuvius. You glimpse fragments of the Temple of Peace. But imagine the Capitol crowned with five-and-twenty temples! Even this Monte Savello—what is it but the carcass of Marcellus’s theater? And Testacio, that towering hill? A refuse heap, where Roman women flung their broken pots. Now it stands a monument to time’s mockery. *Ex pede Herculem; ex ungue leonem.*”

Gerard listened, respectful but uneasy when the friar’s admiration for pagan virtues edged toward heresy.

“Has the world lost by Christ’s coming?” he ventured, then flushed, fearing he had insulted his benefactor.

“Saints forbid!” Fra Colonna waved a hand. “’Twere rank heresy to say so.” Yet his next words spiraled into subtle evasion, praising heathen wisdom while paying hollow tribute to the Church.

Their walk led them to a church where a knot of onlookers gathered. A demon was being exorcised within. The friar let out a small, derisive moan—his customary protest against superstition.

“Come away,” he muttered.

“Nay, father, I beg you! I’ve never seen a devil cast out.”

With a shrug, the friar relented. Inside, the demoniac knelt before the altar, a scarf knotted around his neck like a leash. The priest, breviary in hand, began his incantations, calling forth fiends by name—Ashtaroth, Belial, Nebul—until at last the victim shuddered at *Nebul*.

“Ah! There you are, reptile!” The priest launched into prayer, commanding the demon’s expulsion from every limb and orifice. Then, turning to the crowd, he sighed. “A stubborn devil, this.”

What followed was a grotesque pantomime—cuffs, kicks, spittle, and candle flames pressed to flesh, all met with howls from the possessed. The priest concluded by displaying the Host, and the demon, in a final shriek, confessed defeat—not by holy rites, but by a stone on the windowsill, once touched by the angel Gabriel.

The friar moaned again. “You *believed* him?”

“Of course! Who but an infidel would doubt such a revelation?”

“A liar’s tale, bolstered by a relic. Fiends, if they exist, are not so simple.” He hustled Gerard away, muttering, “Some rogue split his wife’s skull on that stone—mark my words.”

Gerard winced. “You shamed the good priest.”

The friar chuckled. “A touch of Socratic wit blisters them every time.”

---

Days later, Gerard was summoned to the Palazzo Cesarini. Ushered into a grand chamber, he found a haughty young princess surrounded by maids. She demanded a love letter for one Ercole Orsini—then promptly declared she despised him.

“What lie shall I tell him?” she mused, chin resting on her hand, a vision of regal deceit.

Seizing the moment, Gerard sketched her profile. When she noticed, she was at first indignant, then enchanted. “You *must* color it!” she cried. Thus began a profitable arrangement: portraits, full-length studies, and a steady stream of gold.

Margaret’s dowry grew nearer.

---

Holy Thursday brought processions, piety, and spectacle. The Pope, resplendent in crimson and white, blessed the crowds from horseback. Cardinals flanked him—all bareheaded save de’ Medici, who chatted with him as an equal.

At St. John Lateran’s, the heads of St. Peter and St. Paul were unveiled—waxen effigies, the friar insisted, not relics. Gerard, awestruck, disagreed.

“They did not exist in the year 800,” the friar grumbled. “This is mere theater.”

But Gerard saw only holy wonder.

Later, they witnessed Flagellants, their backs striped with blood, laughing as they lashed themselves. “For *your* sins!” one boy jeered at a pitying woman.

“Vanity,” the friar muttered. “No whip can scourge *that* out.”

---

In Gerard’s quarters, a debate raged between him and Jacques Bonaventura over the Pope’s blessing of beasts—ridiculous, Jacques sneered. Gerard defended it.

The argument was cut short by a gentle voice.

“Happy youth, to burn so for trifles.”

They turned—and knelt. The Pope stood there, frail yet serene, his white beard like silk. He examined Gerard’s work on Plutarch and praised it.

“A master of character, this Greek,” he mused. “Boccaccio? A skillful storyteller, but narrow. His world is all monks and nuns and stolen pleasures. Plutarch shows us war, policy, love—life as it is.”

Jacques dared to mention the Pope’s own novel.

His Holiness sighed. “A folly of my youth. When I wrote tales, I little thought to wear this ring.”

And with that, the Vicar of Christ departed, leaving Gerard torn between reverence and doubt—between the grandeur of Rome and the whispers of a skeptic friar.
