The journey began under a heavy cloud of melancholy, the road stretching out with a tedious monotony that matched their spirits. Yet, as the landscape shifted and the familiar contours of Barton Valley came into view, a fresh light seemed to break through their gloom. It was a fertile, verdant expanse, rich with pasture and sheltered by woods, promising a sanctuary against their recent losses. As they wound along the lane for over a mile, the cottage finally appeared, nestled within a small, green courtyard guarded by a neat wicket gate.

Barton Cottage itself was a study in contrasts. Though compact and comfortable, it lacked the romantic charm of their memories; its roof was tiled rather thatched, the shutters unpainted, and the walls bare of climbing vines. A narrow passage cut straight through the heart of the house to the garden behind, flanked by two sitting rooms of merely sixteen feet square. Beyond lay the offices and stairs, leading up to four bedrooms and two garrets. Compared to the grandeur of Norland, it was meager, yet as they stepped inside, the tears of recollection were quickly wiped away. The servants' eager joy and the shared resolve to find happiness in one another's company lifted their hearts. The fine weather of early September sealed their approval, casting a golden glow over the new home that made its deficiencies seem forgivable.

Mrs. Dashwood surveyed the property with a practical optimism. While the house was too small for her family's accustomed scale, she found delight in the prospect of improvement. "We shall make ourselves tolerably comfortable for the present," she declared, noting that the season was too far advanced for major changes. She dreamed of expanding the parlors, converting the passage, and adding a new drawing room and chambers above, perhaps in the spring when her finances might allow. For now, however, they were wise enough to content themselves. With an income of five hundred pounds a year—a sum she had never known how to save—they busied themselves making the space feel like home. Marianne unpacked her pianoforte, filling the air with music, while Elinor pinned her sketches to the walls, transforming the empty rooms into a sanctuary of art and sound.

Their quiet preparations were interrupted the following morning by the arrival of their landlord, Sir John Middleton. He was a man of about forty, good-looking and radiating a thoroughly good-humored nature. His manners were as warm and friendly as his letter had promised, and his genuine solicitude for their comfort was evident. He pressed them so cordially to dine daily at Barton Park until they were settled that, though his persistence bordered on impertinence, they could not refuse without offense. His kindness was immediate and tangible; within an hour of his departure, a basket brimming with garden produce and fruit arrived, followed shortly by a gift of game. He even insisted on managing their correspondence and delivering his newspaper daily, ensuring they felt cared for in every detail.

Lady Middleton followed, arriving the next day with a message of polite intent. She was a striking figure, tall and elegant, with a handsome face and graceful bearing. Yet, beneath her polished exterior lay a reserve that chilled the Dashwoods. Where Sir John possessed frank warmth, she offered only the cool, measured politeness of perfect breeding, lacking the spontaneous engagement that makes conversation alive. Her visit was long enough to temper their initial admiration, revealing a woman who had little to say beyond the most conventional inquiries.

Fortunately, conversation was not entirely absent, for Sir John proved inexhaustibly chatty. To facilitate this, Lady Middleton had wisely brought her eldest son, a fine little boy of about six. This addition became the sole topic of discourse, a lifeline for the ladies in moments of silence. They admired his beauty, asked endless questions about his name and age, and listened as his mother answered for him, while the child hung shyly by her side, holding his head down to the astonishment of his mother, who marveled at his reticence despite his boisterous nature at home. On every formal visit, a child serves as a provision for talk, and in this instance, ten minutes were spent debating whether the boy resembled his father or mother, with everyone differing in their opinion and all agreeing that the resemblance was a delightful mystery. Before the afternoon ended, Sir John secured their promise to dine at the park the following day, leaving the Dashwoods with a sense of obligation and a lingering curiosity about the future they had just begun.
