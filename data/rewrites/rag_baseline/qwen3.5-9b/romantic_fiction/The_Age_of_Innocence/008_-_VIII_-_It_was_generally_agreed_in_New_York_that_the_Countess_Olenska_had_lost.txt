New York had reached a consensus: the Countess Olenska had lost her looks. The memory of her arrival persisted, however—a brilliant, painted doll of nine or ten, a child of wandering parents who had vanished, leaving her to the care of Aunt Medora Manson. Medora, a perpetual refugee herself, returned to the city only to "settle down," usually in a cheaper apartment, bringing a new husband or an adopted child before inevitably discarding them both. Yet, amidst this cycle of dispossession, New York tolerated the eccentricities of the Chivers-Manson line. But when Medora returned with her orphaned niece, dressed in crimson merino and amber beads while wearing a crape veil too short for propriety, the city's indulgence wavered. Only a few old ladies shook their heads at the gaudy display; the rest were charmed by the child's high color and high spirits. Little Ellen Mingott was fearless, asking disconcerting questions, performing Spanish shawl dances, and singing Neapolitan songs to a guitar.

This chaotic upbringing continued until Chivers died in a madhouse, and Medora, draped in strange weeds, departed once more with Ellen. Years later, news arrived of Ellen's marriage to a Polish nobleman of legendary wealth, a union consummated in a sulphurous apotheosis at the Tuileries. When Medora eventually returned, impoverished and mourning a third husband, the city wondered why her rich niece had not sent word. Then came the report of the marriage's collapse and Ellen's return to seek rest among her kinsfolk.

These facts drifted through Newland Archer's mind a week later as he watched the Countess Olenska enter the van der Luyden drawing-room. The occasion was solemn, a dinner attended by New York's most chosen company, now augmented by the presence of a Duke. Archer noted her lateness, her ungloved hand, the hurried fastening of a bracelet, yet she entered without haste or embarrassment. In the center of the room, she paused, surveying the assembly with a grave mouth and smiling eyes. In that instant, Archer rejected the general verdict. True, her early radiance was gone; the red cheeks had paled, she was thin and worn, nearly thirty. But there existed a mysterious authority of beauty, a sureness in the carriage of the head, a movement of the eyes that was not theatrical but highly trained, full of conscious power. She was simpler in manner than the others, quieter in voice and motion. New York expected resonance from a woman with such a history, but she offered silence instead.

The dinner proceeded with formidable gravity. The crystal and porcelain were displayed; Mrs. van der Luyden resembled a Cabanel painting, Mrs. Archer an Isabey miniature. Old Miss Lanning wore her mother's cameos. Among the smooth, plump elderly faces adorned with diamonds and ostrich feathers, the Countess appeared curiously mature. The Duke of St. Austrey, seated at the hostess's right, was the nominal chief figure, yet he was almost invisible. His evening clothes were shabby and baggy, worn with an air of homespun indifference, his vast beard spreading over his shirt-front. He spoke rarely, in low tones lost to everyone except his neighbors.

After dinner, the Duke approached the Countess immediately, ignoring protocol to pay respects to the elder ladies. They retreated to a corner, plunging into animated talk for twenty minutes, seemingly unaware of the social breach. Then the Countess rose and walked alone across the wide drawing-room to sit beside Newland Archer. It was not customary for a lady to leave one gentleman to seek another; etiquette demanded she remain immovable as an idol while suitors rotated. The Countess sat with perfect ease, looking at him with the kindest eyes.

"I want you to talk to me about May," she said.

Instead of answering, Archer asked, "You knew the Duke before?"

"Oh, yes—we used to see him every winter at Nice. He's very fond of gambling—he used to come to the house a great deal." She said it simply, as if noting a preference for wildflowers. After a pause, she added candidly, "I think he's the dullest man I ever met."

Archer felt a surge of excitement. To meet a lady who found the van der Luydens' Duke dull and dared to speak it was thrilling. He longed to question her further, to probe the life hinted at in her careless words, but fear of distressing memories held him back. Before he could formulate a query, she shifted topics.

"May is a darling; I've seen no young girl in New York so handsome and so intelligent. Are you very much in love with her?"

Archer reddened and laughed. "As much as a man can be."

She considered him thoughtfully, missing no shade of meaning. "Do you think, then, there is a limit?"

"To being in love? If there is, I haven't found it!"

She glowed with sympathy. "Ah—it's really and truly a romance?"

"The most romantic of romances!"

"How delightful! And you found it all out for yourselves—it was not in the least arranged for you?"

Archer looked at her incredulously. "Have you forgotten," he asked with a smile, "that in our country we don't allow our marriages to be arranged for us?"

A dusky blush rose to her cheek, and he instantly regretted his words. "Yes," she answered, "I'd forgotten. You must forgive me if I sometimes make these mistakes. I don't always remember that everything here is good that was—that was bad where I've come from."

She looked down at her Viennese fan of eagle feathers, and he saw her lips tremble. "I'm so sorry," he said impulsively; "but you _are_ among friends here, you know."

"Yes—I know. Wherever I go I have that feeling. That's why I came home. I want to forget everything else, to become a complete American again, like the Mingotts and Wellands, and you and your delightful mother, and all the other good people here tonight."

Her eyes turned from the door, where May Welland was arriving in a dress of white and silver, looking like a Diana fresh from the chase, to rest on Archer's face. "Ah, here's May arriving, and you will want to hurry away to her," she added, remaining still.

Drawing-rooms filled with after-dinner guests. Following Madame Olenska's glance, Archer saw May enter, surrounded. "Oh," said Archer, "I have so many rivals; you see she's already surrounded. There's the Duke being introduced."

"Then stay with me a little longer," Madame Olenska said in a low tone, just touching his knee with her plumed fan. It was the lightest touch, but it thrilled him like a caress.

"Yes, let me stay," he answered in the same tone, hardly knowing what he said.

Just then, Mr. van der Luyden approached, followed by old Mr. Urban Dagonet. The Countess greeted them with her grave smile. Feeling his host's admonitory glance, Archer rose and surrendered his seat. Madame Olenska held out her hand as if to bid him goodbye.

"Tomorrow, then, after five—I shall expect you," she said, turning back to make room for Mr. Dagonet.

"Tomorrow—" Archer heard himself repeating, though there had been no engagement, and during their talk she had given him no hint that she wished to see him again.

As he moved away, he saw Lawrence Lefferts leading his wife forward. Gertrude Lefferts beamed on the Countess with her large, unperceiving smile. "But I think we used to go to dancing-school together when we were children—"

Behind her, waiting their turn to introduce themselves, Archer noticed a number of the recalcitrant couples who had declined to meet her at Mrs. Lovell Mingott's. As Mrs. Archer remarked: when the van der Luydens chose, they knew how to give a lesson. The wonder was that they chose so seldom.

A touch on his arm drew his attention upward. Mrs. van der Luyden looked down on him from the pure eminence of black velvet and the family diamonds. "It was good of you, dear Newland, to devote yourself so unselfishly to Madame Olenska. I told your cousin Henry he must really come to the rescue."

He smiled vaguely at her. She added, as if condescending to his natural shyness, "I've never seen May looking lovelier. The Duke thinks her the handsomest girl in the room."
