The letter from the Major arrived with the speed of a thunderclap, sending Doctor Portman sprinting toward Fairoaks with the frantic energy of a dentist rushing to extract a molar before it rots the jaw. He treated the news as a necessary surgery: the tooth must be pulled, and quickly. While he might hesitate if it were his own tooth, he knew his duty was to apply the forceps with vigor. After reading the epistle aloud to Myra and Mrs. Portman, showering the young scapegrace with damning commentary on his descent into perdition, the Doctor departed. The ladies, armed with their usual efficiency, spread the tidings through Clavering society before striding over to deliver the blow to the widow.

To their surprise, Helen had already received the news. Having read Pen's letter herself, a heavy cloud of dread that had hung over her for months had suddenly lifted. She knew the worst, yet her darling boy had returned, repentant and tender-hearted. Was there more to fear? The Rector's sensible, ancient warnings failed to ignite indignation in her; she felt only sorrow that her son should be so unhappy. Why did they insist on sending him to a place teeming with temptation and devoid of virtue? Why not keep him at home? As for the debts, she reasoned they were merely his father's money, and surely he had a right to spend it. Thus, the widow met the virtuous Doctor's arrows with a gentle bosom that refused to bruise.

Meanwhile, the dynamic between Pen and his sister Laura had shifted dramatically. The childish ritual of greeting each other with a kiss, once a frequent delight, had ceased. Upon returning from college, Pen found not the simple girl he remembered, but a tall, slim, handsome young woman of sixteen. She received him with a gracious curtsey and a blush that climbed to her cheeks—the very spot he had once saluted.

Pen, a man who claimed to value virtue over fleeting charms, offered no detailed description of Miss Laura Bell. Yet, at five feet four inches, she was tall enough to be called a "Maypole" by those who preferred smaller women, though she wore beautiful roses around her head that attracted many suitors. She was pale with a faint rose tint, flushing vividly when emotions struck, leaving a lingering bloom. Her eyes, large since childhood, shone with an intensity that defied control, framed by lashes so long and dark they seemed designed to soften their brilliance. Her complexion rivaled Lady Mantrap's without the powder, her smile revealed pearly teeth, and her voice was a low, sweet music. While some mocked her long dresses and implied her feet were large, she was, in essence, a sweet young lady of goodness and modesty.

However, Laura's admiration had curdled into disappointment. For two years, her mind and person had matured alongside her resentment of Pen's conduct. His letters to their mother had grown sparse and brief. "It is better he loses a prize than forget his mother," Laura argued, urging Helen to stop accepting Pen's excuses about college engagements and fine houses. She insisted he should stay home, where he would be loved more than anywhere else. Helen, clinging to maternal infatuation, tried to believe Pen's excuses about cultivating useful acquaintances and the complexities of male life, though she suspected Laura shared her skepticism.

Laura was further shocked by Pen's newfound freedom and audacity. He spoke lightly of women, neglected the little offices of courtesy, and smoked his horrid pipes indoors. He refused to accompany his mother to church or walks, often found yawning over novels in his dressing-gown while she returned from her duties. The hero of her infancy, the brave boy she had praised to Helen for years, had transformed into a bold, sarcastic young man who scorned the simple devotions of the women around him.

The Fotheringay affair sealed her outrage. Hearing of a Pendennis throwing himself at an actress, drinking with her father, and bringing such a woman into their home, Laura was furious. "I would have run away, mamma, if I had to walk barefoot through the snow!" she declared. Helen's response—that Laura would have left her too—prompted an immediate embrace, dissolving the anger into a warm, feminine solidarity. Perhaps Laura's indignation stemmed from jealousy, or perhaps she was misled by the bitter gossip of the Clavering set and the Doctor, who had been insulted by Pen's cigar-smoking in church. Regardless, her wrath against Pen did not extend to his mother. Instead, she devoted herself to Helen with a passion bordering on folly, showering her with caresses and endearments.

Yet, the moment Laura learned of Pen's true misery, her anger evaporated, replaced by unreasonable compassion. He was the old Pen again: frank, generous, and tender-hearted. She sided immediately with Helen against the Doctor's outrage. Debts? A trifle! He had been forced into expensive society by his uncle. Disgraced for failing his degree? He was ill, distracted by debt, perhaps even victimized by jealous tutors. With flushed cheeks and eyes bright with righteous anger, Laura reasoned that others were cruel and unfair. She seized Helen's hand, kissed her in the Doctor's presence, and dared him to speak against her darling mother's son.

Discomfited, the Doctor departed. Laura then redoubled her embraces and arguments with Helen, convincing her that Pen must have offended examiners who took mean revenge. The announcement of the misfortune vexed the ladies very little; indeed, calamity is often welcome to women if it promises the return of truant affection. If a mistress is reduced to a crust, she will not repine, provided her lover eats the remainder in her company.

Directly the Doctor left, Laura ordered fires lit in Pen's rooms and his bedding aired. By the time Helen finished a tender letter to Pen, the preparations were complete. Laura added a postscript calling him her "dearest Pen," urging him to come home instantly to happiness with his mother and affectionate sister.

That night, after reading their Bibles and glancing into Pen's room, the sisters lay awake. Laura, who often occupied the pillow Pen had once pressed, suddenly whispered, "Mamma, are you awake?" Helen stirred, though she had been staring at the night-lamp, thinking of Pen for hours. Laura, equally awake and plotting, proposed a notable plan. She had lived with them for ten years without spending a penny, effectively owing Helen fifty times ten pounds. "Tomorrow we will go to Chatteris," she suggested, "ask Mr. Rowdy for the five hundred pounds, and he will likely send two more. We will save and repay them, send the money to Pen to clear his debts, and live happily ever after."

Helen responded with incoherent ejaculations and embraces. They slept well. When the sun rose over the purple hills and birds sang among the leafless trees, Helen woke to find Laura sleeping beside her. The sight of the girl's sweet face, the gentle rise and fall of her bosom, filled Helen with gratitude beyond words.

In London, Pen's remorse was so profound that he refused an inside seat on the coach, sitting behind with the Guard, who lent him great-coats against the January chill. His knees trembled as he descended at the lodge gate, whether from the cold or the agitation of approaching the woman he had wronged. Old John, now a garner and stable man in a fustian jacket rather than livery, grinned with welcome. But as Pen turned the corner of the shrubbery, Helen appeared, her face beaming with love and forgiveness—a quality some women cherish above all else.

Helen had already written to Pen detailing Laura's noble, magnanimous offer, filling the letter with benedictions. This knowledge caused Pen to blush deeply when he finally saw Laura in the hall. For this one occasion, she broke their usual arrangement of non-physical greetings, though the narrator notes that too much has been said about kissing in this chapter.

Thus, the prodigal returned, the fatted calf was killed, and he was made as happy as two simple women could make him. No mention was made of the Oxbridge mishap or his future plans for some time. But Pen debated anxiously in his own mind. A few days later, he rode to Chatteris, returned on the coach, and handed his mother a cheque for the sale of his horse—an act of virtue he and Laura deemed strict justice. Though he rarely mentioned the loan, accepting it with hesitation, he was wild to repay the orphan.

He quit wine for whisky-and-water, gave up cigars for pipes, and fell into a moody melancholy. He watched coaches, read papers, dined with anyone who would ask him, and played cribbage with Captain Glanders. He avoided the Doctor, who glared severely from under his shovel-hat. He attended church regularly and read prayers at home, though the household had shrunk to a couple of maids, the silver dish-covers gathering dust, and the kitchen fire burning low. John donned his livery only for Sunday's formality. The gloom deepened.

For a while, Pen refused to return to Oxford, but Laura, blushing, urged him to get his degree as reparation for his idleness. Reluctantly, he went back. A "plucked" man is a dismal creature in a university, belonging to no set and owned by no one. Pen felt stripped of his fine feathers, rarely leaving his rooms except for morning chapel. The duns were gone, the cards stopped coming, and his yearmates had graduated. He passed his second examination with ease and felt slightly more comfortable in his bachelor's gown.

On his way back, he visited his uncle in London, only to be met with cold looks and a refusal of a handshake. Returning to Fairoaks, he threw himself into books, idleness, loneliness, and despair. He wrote gloomy tragedies and verses, formed plans to enlist or change professions, and only broke them. He chafed against his captivity, cursing the idleness that caused it. Helen saw him breaking his heart, predicting that soon they would afford to send him abroad or to London to escape their dull society. The widow's habitual melancholy deepened into a sadder gloom, and Laura watched with alarm as her dear friend grew languid, weary, and wan.
