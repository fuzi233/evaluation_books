The pale, milky glow of Milton’s smoky afternoon was just fading to grey when Dr. Donaldson arrived for his first formal visit to Mrs. Hale. The fragile intimacy Margaret had hoped had softened the household’s old barriers snapped back into place at once: she was turned away from the sick room, while Dixon was ushered inside at once. Margaret was not quick to show soft affection, but when she loved, she loved fiercely, and a sharp, burning jealousy pricked at her that the two people closest to her mother should shut her out.

She slipped into her mother’s bedroom, separated from the drawing room only by a thin wooden partition, and paced the narrow floorboards back and forth, pausing every few heartbeats to press her ear to the wall. At last she was sure she heard a half-stifled moan, and she clenched her fists so tight her nails dug half-moons into her palms, holding her breath until her chest ached. For several long minutes after that, there was only silence, then the scrape of chair legs, the low murmur of farewells, the turn of the doorknob.

She stepped out into the hall the moment the door opened. “My father is out, Dr. Donaldson—he is attending a pupil at this hour. Will you come down to his study for a moment, if you please?” She cut straight past every petty obstruction Dixon tried to put in her path, stepping into her rightful place as mistress of the house with a quiet, unyielding dignity that quelled the old servant’s officiousness at once. For half a second, even through the thrum of her anxiety, Margaret nearly laughed at the shocked, impressed look on Dixon’s face—she must look absurdly grand, she thought, and the flicker of absurdity carried her all the way down the stairs before the full weight of the moment crashed back, stealing her breath.

She spoke at once, steady and sharp as a command. “What is wrong with my mother? Tell me the plain truth, if you will.” When the doctor hesitated, she leaned forward a little. “I am her only child here. My father is far too unalarmed for his own good, and if the news is grave, it must be broken to him gently. I can do that. I can nurse her. Say it plainly, sir—watching you hold back is a far worse dread than any words you could speak.”

“My dear young lady, your mother has a most attentive, devoted servant who is more like an old friend to her—”
“I am her daughter, sir.”
“But she expressly asked that you not be told—”
“I am not gentle enough to submit to that prohibition. And I am sure you are too wise to have promised to keep the secret.”

Dr. Donaldson half-smiled, his face heavy with sadness. “You are quite right. I made no promise. In fact, I fear the secret will make itself known soon enough, no matter what I do.”

He paused. Margaret’s face blanched to a deathly white, her lips pressing thin, no other feature moving. With the swift insight that had made him the most respected physician in the county, he saw she would not accept half-truths, that any small omission would torture her far worse than the full knowledge. He spoke two quiet, short sentences, never taking his eyes off her, and watched the pupils of her eyes blow wide with black horror, her fair skin turning a livid ash colour. He stopped speaking, waiting for her gasping breath to return.

At last she found her voice. “I thank you for your honesty, sir. This dread has haunted me for weeks. Poor, poor mother.” Her lips began to quiver, and he let her weep for a few minutes, certain her iron self-control would stop the tears before they overwhelmed her. She wiped her face quickly, the questions already tumbling out. “Will she suffer much?”

“No one can say for certain. It depends on her constitution, on a thousand small variables. But modern medical discoveries have given us great power to ease pain, you may be sure.”

Margaret trembled all over. “My father—”
“I do not know Mr. Hale well, so I can only offer gentle advice. Let the knowledge rest with you for a little while, until it no longer feels like a sudden, brutal shock. Then you can break it to him softly, when the time is right. Between now and then, my repeated visits, small changes in the household routine, will wake his alarm little by little, and he will be far more prepared to bear it. I saw Mr. Thornton earlier, by the by— and I honour your father for the sacrifice he made to leave his old parish, even if I think it was ill-advised. And do not think of me as only a doctor now. We meet at times like these, we know each other far better than we ever could over a dozen morning calls.”

Margaret could not speak for crying, but she wrung his hand so hard he noticed the pressure long after he was settled in his carriage. “What a remarkable girl,” he thought to himself, rubbing the faint indent on his ringed hand. “Who would have thought that small soft hand could squeeze so hard? She is queenly, every inch of her—demanding the truth head on, then leaning forward to listen so eagerly. Any other girl, gone as white as that, would have fainted or fallen into hysterics. But not her. She is game to the very backbone. If I were thirty years younger, I would fall in love with her, no doubt of it. Ah, well—we are at the Archers’ now.” He hopped out, his mind already fully turned to the next family waiting for his care, as if no other grief existed in the world.

Back in the study, Margaret sank down by her father’s desk for a moment, to collect herself before going up to her mother. “Oh God, this is terrible,” she whispered to herself. “How shall I bear it? No hope at all. Oh mother, mother, I wish I had never spent all those precious years away from you at Aunt Shaw’s! Poor mamma, how much you have borne alone. Let her pain never be too sharp, too dreadful. How will I stand to watch it? How will I tell papa? It would break him at once, if I told him today. I will not waste a single more moment away from you, my dear mother.”

She ran upstairs. Dixon was not in the room. Mrs. Hale lay propped in her easy chair, wrapped in a soft white shawl, wearing a neat, pretty cap in expectation of the doctor’s visit. A faint flush coloured her cheeks, and the exhaustion of the examination had left her looking peaceful, almost rested. Margaret was surprised to see her so calm.

“Margaret, you look terribly strange. What is the matter?” Mrs. Hale’s face tightened with a faint displeasure as the thought dawned on her. “You didn’t corner Dr. Donaldson and ask him questions, did you, child?”

Margaret did not reply, only looked at her mother with a yearning, helpless expression. Mrs. Hale grew more upset. “He would not break his word to me, surely—”

“Oh no mamma, he did. I made him do it. Blame me.” She knelt at her mother’s side, caught her hand and would not let go, even when Mrs. Hale tried to pull it away, kissing it over and over until her hot tears wet the skin.

“Margaret, that was very wrong. I did not want you to know.” But she was tired, and after a moment she let her hand rest in Margaret’s, and faintly returned the pressure.

Encouraged, Margaret spoke again. “Let me be your nurse. I will learn every trick Dixon can teach me. I am your child—surely I have a right to care for you, to do everything for you. It will be such a comfort to me, mamma.”

“You have no idea what you are asking for,” Mrs. Hale shuddered.

“I do. I know far more than you think. No one in the world will ever try half as hard to take care of you as I will. Please.”

“My poor child. Very well, you may try. Do you know, Dixon and I both thought you would shrink away from me the moment you knew—”

“Dixon thought that?” Margaret’s lip curled. “Dixon did not give me credit for half as much love as she has for you! She thought I was one of those silly soft creatures who want to lie on rose leaves and be fanned all day. Don’t let Dixon’s fancies come between us any more, mamma, please.”

“Do not be cross with Dixon,” Mrs. Hale said anxiously.

Margaret softened at once. “I won’t. I will be humble, and learn all her ways, if only you let me care for you. I used to lie awake at night when I was at Aunt Shaw’s, crying because I was afraid you would forget me.”

“And I used to lie awake at night worrying how you would bear our shabby little makeshift life at Helstone, after all the luxury of Harley Street. I was more ashamed of you seeing our rickety little contrivances there than I would have been of any stranger finding them.”

“Oh mamma, I loved all of that! It was far more fun than the dull routine of Harley Street. The wardrobe shelf with the handles that we used as a supper tray on feast days! The old tea chests we stuffed and covered to make ottomans! Those were the very best parts of Helstone life.”

“I shall never see Helstone again, Margaret,” Mrs. Hale said, and tears welled up in her eyes. Margaret could not find words to reply. “I was always wanting to leave it when we lived there, thinking every other place was better. And now I will die far away from it. It feels like a just penance.”

“You must not talk like that,” Margaret said, half-impatient with grief. “The doctor said you might live for years. We will get you back to Helstone yet, I promise.”

“No, never. But Margaret—Frederick!”

The name burst out of her in a wild, sharp cry, all her calm shattering in an instant. “Frederick! Come to me! I am dying! My first little boy, come see me once more!” She fell into violent, raving hysterics. Margaret ran in terror to call Dixon. Dixon came in in a huff, snapping at Margaret for over-exciting her mother, but Margaret bore every word of it meekly, obeying every direction Dixon gave her to the letter, never making a single excuse for herself. Her quiet submission mollified the old servant. They got Mrs. Hale into bed, and Margaret sat by her until she fell asleep, until Dixon finally beckoned her out of the room, and led her to the drawing room, pressing a cup of hot coffee into her hands with a grumpy, commanding air, making her drink every drop.

“You shouldn’t have been so curious, miss, and you wouldn’t have had to fret ahead of your time. It would have all come soon enough. And I suppose now you will run off and tell master, and we will have the whole household in tears for weeks.”

“No Dixon,” Margaret said sorrowfully. “I will not tell papa. He could not bear it the way I can.” And to prove how well she was bearing it, she burst into tears.

“Ah, I knew it would be so. You will wake your mamma up, just after she fell asleep so quiet. Miss Margaret my dear, I have carried this secret for weeks now. I don’t pretend I love her the way you do, but I have loved her better than any soul alive—no one but Master Frederick ever came close. Ever since Lady Beresford’s maid first took me in to see her all dressed up for a ball, in white crape with corn ears and scarlet poppies in her hair, and I ran a needle right down into my finger and broke it off. She tore up her own fine embroidered handkerchief after the maids had cut my bandage open, and came back from the ball to bathe my finger with lotion herself, when she was the prettiest girl in the whole room. I never loved anyone half as much after that. I little thought I would live to see her brought so low. I don’t mean no blame to no one. Everyone says you are a pretty girl, miss, and they are right, even in this smoky Milton that blinds you so the owls can see clear. But you will never be half as beautiful as your mother was—never, not if you live to be a hundred.”

“Mamma is still beautiful now. Poor mamma.”

“Don’t you start crying again, or I will start blubbering too,” Dixon whimpered. “You will never be able to face master when he comes home and starts asking questions, at this rate. Go take a walk, get some air, come back looking half-way normal. I’ve walked the streets for hours many a time, trying to wear off the weight of this secret, not knowing how it would all end.”

“Oh Dixon,” Margaret said, “how many times I was cross with you, never guessing what a terrible weight you were carrying all this time.”

“Bless you, child! I like to see you show a little spirit. That’s the old Beresford blood. The second last Sir John shot his steward dead on his own doorstep once, just because the man told him he was racking his tenants so hard he couldn’t squeeze any more money out of them than he could get skin off a flint.”

“Well Dixon, I will not shoot you, and I will try not to be cross ever again.”

“You never were. When I said you were, it was only to myself, for the sake of a bit of conversation, since there’s no one else in this smoky hole worth talking to. And when you fire up, you look exactly like Master Frederick. I could half provoke you into a temper every day just to see that same stormy, dark look cloud over your face. But go on out now, miss. I will watch over your mother. Master is so wrapped up in his books, he won’t notice a thing if he comes home before you are back.”

“I will go,” Margaret said. She hovered by Dixon for a moment, half-afraid, half-fond, then suddenly leaned in and kissed her cheek, before hurrying out of the room.

“Bless her,” Dixon whispered to herself, grinning as she wiped at her eye. “She is as sweet as a nut. There are only three people in the whole world I love: missus, Master Frederick, and her. The rest can go hang, for I don’t know what they are even here for. Master was born, I suppose, to marry missus. If I thought he loved her half as much as he ought, I might get to love him in time. But he never paid her half enough attention, always reading, reading, thinking, thinking. Look where it got him. Plenty of men who never open a book in their lives end up Rector, and Dean, and all that. Master could have done it too, if he’d just minded missus, and left all that weary thinking and reading alone.” She leaned to the window as she heard the front door click shut. “There she goes. Poor young lady. Her clothes are all worn thin now, compared to the day we moved from Helstone. Back then she didn’t have a darned stocking or a mended pair of gloves in all her wardrobe. And now—!”
