"I remember, I remember—"
Ah, but what *do* I remember? The first scrap of poetry I ever learned, that’s what—or at least the respectable part of it. None of your frivolous "Hey diddle diddle" for me; I was a child of artistic solemnity. I recited those lines with such gravity that I earned fourpence—a fortune! Though, true to form, I have no earthly recollection of what I spent it on. Memory, that fickle librarian, hands me torn pages instead of whole books.

Take the time I tumbled into a dust-hole as a boy. I recall the plunge vividly—the sudden descent, the cloud of filth, the existential dread of a child who has mistaken life for a rubbish bin. But do I remember crawling out? Not a whit. For all my memory cares, I might still be there, buried beneath the debris of my own past, philosophizing about the futility of cleanliness.

Or consider the tenderest love scene of my youth. What lingers? Not the whispered sweet nothings, not the trembling hands, not even the moonlight (if there was any). No, what Memory has preserved is the dramatic interruption: "Emily, you’re wanted," delivered in a tone so sepulchral it suggested Emily was wanted by the hangman. The rest? Lost, like a sock in the laundry of time.

And isn’t that just the way of it? The past is a ruin—a crumbled castle where only the prettiest vines remain. The broken shards of Latin verbs? Gone. The toothaches, the humiliations? Dust. What lingers are the golden hours: the scent of gingerbread, the thrill of stolen apples, the heroic delusion that we were happy. Even heartbreak softens with age. That girl who told us she could "only be a sister"? Why, we chuckle now! (Though at the time, we’d have preferred a more romantic rejection—say, joining a nunnery or emigrating to Patagonia.)

Old folk, of course, are the worst offenders. Their youth was a paradise of perpetual sunshine, winters that froze rivers solid, summers that ripened fruit with a single glance. Bulls were wrestled for sport, schoolmasters were giants, and the moon, they swear, never took a night off. It’s the same tune sung by every generation—a chorus of "Things were better before you lot ruined it." The ancient Greeks moaned about it; the Victorians wrote ballads about it; and today’s youth will, no doubt, bore their grandchildren with tales of how *their* smartphones had *character*.

But let’s not wallow. The past is a foundation, not a dwelling. Regret is a poor walking stick, and backward glances won’t steer us clear of tomorrow’s potholes. I once heard of a knight who, daunted by the road ahead, turned to see the path behind him vanish into a bottomless chasm. His only choice? Forward. A metaphor for life, if ever there was one—no refunds, no rewinds, and no use weeping over spilt milk, especially if you’ve forgotten where you left the cow.

A friend once tried to sell me a system for remembering *everything*. I declined. Some things *ought* to be forgotten—the follies, the stumbles, the mortifying moments when we mistook clubs for trumps. Memory is a gardener: let it tend the roses, but for heaven’s sake, don’t let it cultivate the weeds. Dickens’ haunted man begged for forgetfulness, then begged to remember. We need our ghosts—just not the ones that rattle chains and moan about unpaid debts.

Ah, the ghosts! They multiply as we age. Not the specters of gothic novels, but the whispers of old friends, old loves, old selves. The schoolyard echoes with laughter; the lane where we once lingered holds a shadow in a white frock; the firelight flickers with the face of the boy we were—solemn, hopeful, appallingly earnest. He stares at us, that boy, as if to say, *Is this what you meant to become?*

We shrug. We meant to be great. We meant to be good. Instead, we’re… *us*. And perhaps that’s enough.

So here’s to Memory—the keeper of lost smiles, the thief of sorrows, the architect who shows us only the prettiest parts of the ruin. Let the past be a lantern, not a shackle. And if you must look back, do it with a wink. After all, the child who fell into the dust-hole *did* climb out.

Probably.
