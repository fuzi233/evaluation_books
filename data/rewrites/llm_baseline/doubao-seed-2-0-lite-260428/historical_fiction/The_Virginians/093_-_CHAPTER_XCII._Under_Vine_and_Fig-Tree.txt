Young readers, need I paint the delight of that homecoming, the soft glow of our mother’s joy when every last one of her brood was gathered safe under her roof again? It was written plain across her smiling face, and poured out in tearful, grateful prayers on her knees. Our great old country house held more than enough room for all of us, but Aunt Hetty would not stay. She spoke plainly, and no one could blame her: for nearly three years, she had stood in a mother’s place to George’s children, and to step aside and yield her place to their true mother, after all that love, was a pang she could not yet bear. So she and her father took a small house not far off, in Bury St. Edmunds, where they lived quiet lives, and where Hetty spoiled her eldest nephew and niece in secret, unchallenged.

The very year after we came home, that Jamaica planter Mr. B. died, leaving her half his entire fortune. That was the first time I learned how the good gentleman had been deeply in love with Hetty during her time in the West Indies, and even when she refused his suit, he had left her that fortune to show his unchanging regard. Heaven only knows how much of Aunt Hetty’s fortune that scapegrace Miles has already devoured! The cost of his army commission and outfit, his gold-laced uniforms, his gambling debts, his wild little sprees in the Minories—do you think I do not know human nature? I know full well the cost of Pall Mall tavern suppers, the small private dinners, even moderate play at the Cocoa-Tree Club, and that a gentleman cannot keep up such appearances on the five hundred a year I allow him. Hetty swears she will die an old maid. “I vowed I would never marry until I found a man as good as my dear father,” she says, patting the old General’s arm, “and I never did, Sir George. No, my sweet Theo, not half so good. And Sir George can put that in his pipe and smoke it.”

When that good old General finally passed, calm, full of years, and glad to depart for a better world, I think it was my wife Theo who shed the most tears. “I weep because I fear I never loved him half enough,” the tender creature said. Hetty, by contrast, kept a steady, outward calm before all of us, and even now speaks of him constantly as if he were still in the next room, laughing over his old jokes, telling of his gentle kindness to the children, until her face brightens and she looks like the young girl she was forty years prior. She sits cheerfully in church, looking up at the marble slab carved with his name and his virtues, a slab that for once tells no lies.

I once suspected that my brother Harry, for whom Hetty nursed a girlish passion long ago, still held a soft spot in her heart. Ten years back, when Harry came to stay with us, I told him of that old childish romance, half-hoping he might ask Hetty to take the place of his late wife Fanny, who had been laid to rest. Theo, in her usual habit of berating herself as a miserable sinner, never stopped reproaching herself for not mourning Fanny deeply enough. Harry arrived plunged in grief for his wife, vowing no woman in the whole world could ever equal her. Our dear old General, still hale then, took Harry in and housed him exactly as he had done in the happy old days at Oakhurst. The women of the house played all the old familiar tunes Harry had loved as a boy, every heart was soft with old memories, and Harry never tired of pouring out his grief and his endless tales of his wife’s perfections to Hetty, before switching to gush about his dear Aunt Lambert, whom he loved with all his heart, whose praises were, you may be sure, the sweetest music to my own ears.

Later, Harry went to Paris as an American general, wearing that blue and yellow uniform Mr. Fox and his set had made fashionable back in England too. He was fêted at Versailles, presented by the Marquis de Lafayette to the Most Christian King and Queen of France—who, for all the favour they showed the young nobleman, did not care for him at all. A certain Marquise there took such a fancy to our Virginian general that she would have married him on the spot had he not resisted, and fled straight back to England, to Warrington and Bury, where the neighbours would listen for hours with endless patience to his stories of his perfect late wife. The rest of us, who had known Fanny well enough, were polite but never quite as eager to deify her, and often found ourselves flummoxed trying to answer our children’s endless questions about “Uncle Hal’s angel wife.”

Many a night after dinner, the two Generals, myself, Captain Miles, and Parson Blake (who got wounded at the Battle of Monmouth the year after I left America, came home, hung up his old soldier’s coat, and took up my local parish living) would sit over our wine and re-fight the battles of the Revolution all over again. The parson, who had traded his old military oaths for “By Jupiter!” once he entered the church, would cry out, “By Jupiter, General! You are the Tory, and Sir George here is the Whig! He is forever picking fault with our old revolutionary leaders, and you are forever defending them. Last Sunday when I prayed for the King, I heard you muttering the words right out loud behind me, plain as day!”

“And so I did, Blake, with all my heart,” Hal would answer. “I can never forget I once wore that King’s coat.”

“Ah, if Wolfe had lived twenty years longer!” old General Lambert would sigh.

“Ah sir,” Hal would cry, “you should hear my General talk about him!”

“Which general?” I would tease him, just to ruffle his feathers.

“My General,” Hal would say, standing up and filling his glass brim full. “His Excellency General George Washington!”

“With all my heart!” I would toast, though the parson would make a face like he did not care for the toast, or the claret we drank it in.

Hal never tired of speaking of his adored chief, and one night during just such a friendly fireside chat, he told us the story of the time he actually fell out of favour with the great man, over that very same Marquis de Lafayette. The young French nobleman had won rank and fame for his role in the war, before vanishing suddenly from public view later. Hal’s own long service, and his proven gallantry on a dozen battlefields, ought to have earned him easy promotion to general in the Continental Army—yet a beardless boy like Lafayette had barely stepped off the boat before Congress handed him a Major General’s commission, no questions asked. Hal, never a man to chase rank for its own sake, spent all his officer’s pay on extra blankets and food for his men, and declared he cared no more for the title than he did for a feather in his hat, and would just as happily serve as colonel as any higher grade. But he could not hold his tongue when he saw unworthy men promoted over veterans, and he grew especially angry over Lafayette’s sudden elevation, the young French boy who happened to be the great Washington’s particular favourite. Hardly three men in the whole Continental Army spoke fluent French, which let Hal get to know the new Major General far more casually than the Commander-in-Chief ever could, and Washington good-naturedly chided Hal for being jealous of the “beardless commander of Auvergne.” The great chief had every political reason to treat Lafayette with honour and affection: the young man’s favour was the key to unlocking massive French aid for the struggling revolution.

All the while, Washington was pushing for Hal’s promotion through Congress, even as he faced almost impossible odds. That terrible winter at Valley Forge found him entrenched with barely five or six thousand ragged men, almost no firewood, no blankets, no food, no ammunition, facing down Sir William Howe’s perfectly supplied army three times his size. As if the British were not enough, he had to bear the cowardly distrust of Congress, and the open conspiracy of disloyal officers in his own camp. In the brutal winter of 1777, one sharp attack from the sluggish British general could have ended the entire revolution, and despair and reigned everywhere in the American camp—save in Washington’s single indomitable breast. He had won no glorious triumphs like Gates had earned at Saratoga, victories that had turned public favour against him. He had met defeat after defeat at the hands of a far better supplied enemy. Congress did not trust him. Half his officers hated him. Men disappointed of promotion, men caught stealing from army supplies, men whose selfishness and incompetence his honest eyes had seen through—all of them banded together against him, turning to Gates as their chosen hero, the only man fit to lead the war. Gates, in his vanity, did nothing to turn away their adoration.

To show how unbearable Washington’s burdens were at the time, we all know the story of the Conway Cabal. A certain Irish adventurer, a Chevalier of St. Louis and an officer of the French army, arrived in America in early 1777 looking for employment, was immediately made a Brigadier General, and immediately demanded to be promoted straight to Major General. He had friends in Congress who promised him the promotion, and when Washington remonstrated that elevating the youngest brigadier in the whole service over every veteran colonel was rank injustice, he came into possession of a letter Conway had written to Gates, sneering that “Heaven had determined to save America, or a weak general and bad councillors would have ruined it.” Washington sent the note straight to Conway without a single word of comment, Conway offered his resignation, which Congress refused, and instead named him Inspector General of the entire army, with the rank of Major General.

“And it was then,” Hal cried, his voice thick with rage at his own foolishness and awe for his chief, “when that glorious man was crushed under troubles enough to break ten thousand men, that I had to go spouting my petty jealousies about that French boy! I never said much, just some silly nonsense to Greene and Cadwalader about stocking up on frogs for dinner when the French marquis came to call, and how Congress was importing whole sacks of marquises from Paris to hand out general’s commissions to, as if we did not have enough of our own. I should have known how heavy his burdens were, that he had a wiser head than mine, and held my foolish tongue.

“For a while the General said nothing, but I could see the cold distance in his manner, and knew something had rifted us apart. Mrs. Washington, who had come to camp for the winter, noticed it too. Women have a way of soothing cross men and digging out their secrets. I was proud too, you know, and never would have tried to ask him why he was cold to me—besides, I tell you, you did not want to approach the Chief when he was angry. But Mrs. Washington cornered me. ‘Meet me after dinner, Colonel Hal,’ she said. ‘Come out to the parade ground before the dining hall, I will tell you all.’ I left a dozen general officers drinking around the General’s table, and found her waiting for me. She told me my silly joke about the box of marquises was what had offended him. ‘I would not have heeded it from any other man,’ he told her, ‘but I never thought Harry Warrington would join my enemies against me.’

“That night I went to his quarters, and found him alone at his desk. ‘Can your Excellency spare me five minutes?’ I said, my heart in my mouth. ‘Yes, surely, sir,’ he said, pointing to the other chair. ‘Pray be seated.’

“‘It was not always “Sir” and “Colonel Warrington” between us, your Excellency,’ I said.

“He said calmly, ‘The times are altered.’

“‘Et nos mutamur in illis,’ I said. Times and people both change.

“‘You had some business with me?’ he asked.

“‘Am I speaking to the Commander-in-Chief, or to my old friend?’ I asked.

“He looked at me gravely. ‘Well—both, sir. Pray sit, Harry.’

“‘If I speak to General Washington, I tell his Excellency that I, and many officers of this army, are furious to see a boy of twenty made a Major General over us, just because he is a marquis and barely speaks a word of our language. If I speak to my old friend, I have to say that you have shown me no trust or friendship these past weeks, and I have no wish to sit at your table and hear other men make impertinent jokes about the way your Excellency turns your back on me.’

“‘Which charge shall I take first, Harry?’ he said, turning his chair away from the desk and crossing his legs, settled for a long talk. ‘You are jealous, I gather, of the Marquis?’

“‘Jealous, sir!’ I cried. ‘An aide-de-camp of General Wolfe is not jealous of a Jack-a-dandy who was getting whipped at school five years ago!’

“‘You yourself turned down a higher rank than the one you hold now,’ the Chief said, flushing a little.

“‘But I never bargained to have a macaroni marquis giving me orders!’ I shouted. ‘I will not carry that young gentleman’s commands, for one. Since Congress and your Excellency insist on pulling generals out of the nursery, I shall humbly ask leave to resign, and go back to my plantation.’

“‘Do, Harry,’ he said, and his gentleness took all the fire right out of me. ‘When your old friend is drowning in troubles, that is surely the very best time to desert him.’

“‘Sir!’ I said.

“‘Do as so many of the rest are doing, Mr. Warrington,’ he said, with a sad little smile. ‘Et tu, Brute, as the play says. Well, well, Harry! I never thought it of you. But you are in the fashion, at least.’

“‘You asked which charge to take first,’ I said.

“‘Ah, the promotion of the Marquis? I recommended that appointment to Congress, no doubt. You and other gentlemen disapprove.’

“‘I speak only for myself, sir,’ I said.

“‘If you take that tone with me, Colonel Warrington, I have nothing to answer!’ he said, standing up very sharply. ‘Do you presume I need your approval before I recommend men for promotion?’

“‘Since we are on that tone, sir,’ I said, ‘I respectfully offer my resignation to your Excellency. My reason for resigning is that Congress, on your recommendation, gives our highest commands to boys of twenty, who barely know our language.’ I stood and bowed to him.

“‘Great heavens, Harry!’ he cried, he was stumped on that point, you know, he had no good retort. ‘Can you not believe that in this critical hour for our cause, there are reasons why we must show special favour to the first man of rank and distinction from France who ever comes to our aid?’

“‘No doubt, sir. If your Excellency is saying Monsieur de Lafayette’s merits have nothing to do with the favour.’

“‘I acknowledge nothing, I deny nothing, sir!’ he said, stamping his foot, looking as terrible angry as I ever saw him. ‘Am I here to be cross-examined by you? Stay. Hark, Harry. I speak to you not as Commander-in-Chief, but as a man of the world, and an old friend. This appointment humiliated you and the other veteran officers, you say? Be it so! Must we not bear that humiliation, along with every other burden and grief, for the sake of our country? It is no more just that the Marquis should be set over you veterans than that your Prince Ferdinand or the Prince of Wales should command men who fought for thirty years in the army. But if honoring this young nobleman pleases an entire nation, and wins us twenty millions of allies, will you and the rest of you sulk over a petty slight? It is easy to sneer at him—and mind you, the Marquis has far more merit than any of you give him credit for. It would be far more generous, far more manly, for Harry Warrington to welcome this stranger for the sake of the great good he can do our country, not laugh at his peculiarities, but teach him, guide his ignorance with your experience as an old soldier. That is what I expected of you. But you chose to side with my enemies. When I am in the deepest trouble, you tell me you will leave me. That is why I was hurt. That is why I was cold. I thought I could count on your friendship as I would a brother’s. And you come and tell me you will resign. Be it so. I have embarked on this contest, and by God’s grace I will see it to the end. You are not the first man who has deserted me along the way.’

“He spoke with such tender unhappiness on his face that I was seized with such remorse I could barely speak. I babbled about old times, old adventures before Braddock’s field, and swore if he said the word I would never leave his side. ‘You never loved him, George,’ Hal turned to me, eyes bright, ‘but I loved him beyond any other man on earth. I am no clever man like you, but my instinct is right. He has a greatness no other man alive can touch.’

“I do not deny that, brother,” I said. “Not anymore.”

“Greatness, pooh!” grumbled the parson, stirring the remains of his wine.

“We walked into Mrs. Washington’s tea room arm in arm,” Hal went on. “She looked up, smiled, and saw we had made it up. ‘Is it all over, Colonel Harry?’ she whispered. ‘Do you know he has petitioned Congress for your promotion over and over?’

“‘I will never take that promotion,’ I said. And that was how I did my penance, the very next winter, serving right under Lafayette’s command.” Hal laughed, and mimicked the marquis’s high, lisping French accent. “‘I will go weez heem,’ I said. ‘I know the road to Quebec, and when we are not fighting Sir Guy Carleton, I can sit and hear his Excellency the Major General recite his lesson.’ There was no fight, of course. We could not raise enough men to invade Canada, and we trundled back to headquarters. Do you know what upset that young Frenchman most? The thought that people would laugh at him, because his grand Canadian expedition had come to nothing. And laugh at him they did, almost to his face. Everyone knew the Chief’s one soft spot was that Marquis.
