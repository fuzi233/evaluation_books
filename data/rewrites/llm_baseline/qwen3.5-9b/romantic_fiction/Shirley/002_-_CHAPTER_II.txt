The night was absolute, a heavy blanket of pitch-black silence where stars and moon had been smothered by gray rain-clouds—clouds that looked merely gray in daylight but transformed into somber sable against the night sky. Curate Malone was not a man inclined toward close observation of nature; the shifting moods of the heavens usually passed him entirely unnoticed. He could walk miles on a brilliant April morning without seeing the delicate interplay between earth and sky, failing to notice when a sunbeam kissed a hilltop to make it gleam in green light, or when a shower wept over the landscape, hiding the peaks beneath the disheveled tresses of low-hanging clouds. Consequently, he cared little to contrast the current sky—a muffled, streaming vault of black, broken only by a tremulous, lurid shimmer cast by the furnaces of Stilbro' ironworks on the eastern horizon—with a clear, frosty night. He did not trouble himself to locate the missing constellations or lament the loss of the "black-blue" serenity of the air-ocean, dotted with its white islets, now replaced by a heavier, denser ocean rolling below and concealing it all.

Instead, he doggedly pressed onward, leaning slightly forward in his stride and wearing his hat pushed back over his ears, a habit of his Irish heritage. *Tramp, tramp* went his footsteps along the causeway, where the road allowed for such an accompaniment; *splash, splash* echoed through the mire-filled cart ruts, where flagstones yielded to soft mud. His gaze sought only specific landmarks: the spire of Briarfield Church ahead, and further on, the lights of Redhouse. This was an inn, and as he approached, the glow of a fire visible through a half-curtained window, accompanied by visions of glasses on a round table and revelers on an oaken settle, nearly diverted him from his path. He longed deeply for a tumbler of whisky-and-water. In a strange place, he would have instantly embraced the dream, but the company gathered in that kitchen were Mr. Helstone's own parishioners; they all knew him. Sighing, he passed on.

Now, the main highway was to be abandoned, as the remaining distance to Hollow's Mill could be significantly shortened by cutting across the fields. These fields were level and monotonous. Malone took a direct line through them, leaping over hedges and walls. He passed only one building here, which appeared large and hall-like despite its irregular shape. A high gable loomed, followed by a long front, then a low gable, and finally a thick, lofty stack of chimneys. Trees stood behind it. It was dark; not a single candle shone from any window. Absolute stillness reigned, broken only by the rain trickling from the eaves and the rather wild, yet very low, whistle of the wind circling the chimneys and threading through the branches.

Having passed this building, the fields, previously flat, began a rapid descent. Clearly, a valley lay below, through which the sound of running water drifted. A single light glimmered in the depths. Malone steered directly for that beacon.

He arrived at a small white house—visible as white even through the dense darkness—and knocked. A fresh-faced servant opened the door. By the candle she held, a narrow passage was revealed, leading to a narrow staircase. Two doors covered in crimson baize and a strip of crimson carpet running down the steps contrasted sharply with the light-colored walls and white floor, making the tiny interior appear clean and fresh.

"Mr. Moore is at home, I suppose?" Malone asked.

"Yes, sir, but he is not in."

"Not in! Where is he then?"

"At the mill—in the counting-house."

As she spoke, one of the crimson doors swung open.

"Are the wagons here, Sarah?" a female voice asked, and simultaneously, a female head appeared. It might not have been the head of a goddess—indeed, a roll of curl-paper on either side of her temples quite forbade such a supposition—but neither was it the head of a Gorgon. Yet, Malone seemed to perceive it in the latter light. Despite his considerable size, he shrank bashfully backward into the rain at the sight of it, muttering, "I'll go to him," and hurrying in with apparent trepidation down a short lane, across an obscure yard, toward a huge black mill.

The working hours had ended; the laborers were gone. The machinery rested, the mill shut up. Malone walked around its perimeter. Somewhere in its great, sooty flank, he found another sliver of light. He knocked at another door, using the thick end of his shillelagh to beat out a rousing tattoo. A key turned; the door unlatched.

"Is it Joe Scott? Any news of the wagons, Joe?"

"No; it's myself. Mr. Helstone sent me."

"Oh! Mr. Malone." The voice carrying this name carried the slightest possible tone of disappointment. After a brief pause, it continued, politely but somewhat formally, "I beg you will come in, Mr. Malone. I regret extremely that Mr. Helstone deemed it necessary to trouble you so far. There was no necessity—I told him so—and certainly not on such a night; but please, walk forward."

Through a dark apartment of indistinguishable character, Malone followed the speaker into a light and bright room within. Very light and bright indeed it seemed to eyes that, for the past hour, had struggled to penetrate the double darkness of night and fog. Except for an excellent fire and a lamp of elegant design casting a vivid luster on a table, it was a very plain place. The boarded floor was bare; the three or four stiff-backed, green-painted chairs seemed once to have furnished the kitchen of some farmhouse. A desk of strong, solid construction, the aforementioned table, and several framed sheets on the stone-colored walls displaying plans for buildings, gardens, machinery designs, and similar projects, completed the furnishings.

Plain as it was, it satisfied Malone. Having removed and hung up his wet coat and hat, he pulled one of the rheumatism-inducing chairs to the hearth, setting his knees almost within the bars of the red grate.

"Comfortable quarters you have here, Mr. Moore; and all snug to yourself."

"Yes, but my sister would be glad to see you, if you prefer stepping into the house."

"Oh, no! Ladies are best alone; I have never been a lady's man. You don't mistake me for my friend Sweeting, do you, Mr. Moore?"

"Sweeting! Which of them is that? The gentleman in the chocolate overcoat, or the little gentleman?"

"The little one—he of Nunnely; the cavalier of the Misses Sykes, with the whole six of whom he is in love, ha! ha!"

"Better to be generally in love with all than specially with one, I should think, in that quarter."

"But he is specially in love with one besides, for when I and Donne urged him to make a choice among the fair bevy, he named—which do you think?"

With a queer, quiet smile, Mr. Moore replied, "Dora, of course, or Harriet."

"Ha! ha! You have an excellent guess. But what made you hit on those two?"

"Because they are the tallest, the handsomest, and Dora, at least, is the stoutest; and as your friend Mr. Sweeting is but a little slight figure, I concluded that, according to a frequent rule in such cases, he prefers his contrast."

"You are right; it is Dora. But he has no chance, has he, Moore?"

"What else does Mr. Sweeting possess besides his curacy?"

This question tickled Malone immensely. He laughed for nearly three minutes before answering.

"What has Sweeting? Well, David has his harp or flute, which amounts to the same thing. He has a sort of pinchbeck watch; ditto, ring; ditto, eyeglass. That is what he has."

"How would he propose to keep Miss Sykes in gowns only?"

"Ha! ha! Excellent! I'll ask him that next time I see him. I'll roast him for his presumption. But no doubt he expects old Christopher Sykes to do something handsome. He is rich, isn't he? They live in a large house."

"Sykes carries on an extensive concern."

"Therefore he must be wealthy, eh?"

"Therefore he must have plenty to occupy his wealth, and in these times, he would be about as likely to think of drawing money from the business to provide dowries for his daughters as I would be to dream of pulling down the cottage there and constructing on its ruins a house as large as Fieldhead."

"Do you know what I heard, Moore, the other day?"

"No. Perhaps I was about to effect some such change. Your Briarfield gossips are capable of saying that or sillier things."
