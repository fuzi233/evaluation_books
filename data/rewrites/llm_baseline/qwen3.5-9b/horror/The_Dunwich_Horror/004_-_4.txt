For ten years, the history of the Whateleys dissolved into the grim, stagnant routine of a town accustomed to their peculiarities and desensitized to their macabre rituals. Twice annually, they ignited pyres atop Sentinel Hill, triggering earth-shaking tremors that grew progressively violent; meanwhile, ominous activities persisted within the isolated farmhouse throughout every season. Over time, visitors claimed to hear noises emanating from the sealed upper floors even when the entire family remained below, speculating on the speed or duration required to slaughter a cow or ox. Rumors circulated regarding a potential report to the Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals, yet such actions never materialized, for the people of Dunwich possessed no desire to draw external scrutiny upon themselves.

Around 1923, when Wilbur was merely ten years old—though possessing the mental acuity, vocal depth, physical stature, and full beard of a grown man—a second major construction project commenced within the ancient house. This work took place entirely behind the locked upper walls. From scraps of discarded timber scattered nearby, neighbors deduced that the boy and his grandfather had demolished every internal partition and stripped away the attic flooring, creating a single, cavernous void spanning from the ground floor to the peak of the roof. They had also razed the massive central chimney, replacing the rusted iron range with a precarious exterior tin stovepipe.

In the spring following these renovations, Old Whateley observed an increasing influx of whippoorwills emerging from Cold Spring Glen to chirp beneath his window at night. He regarded this phenomenon with profound gravity, informing the loafers at Osborn's that his departure was imminent. "They whistle exactly in tune with my breathing now," he rasped. "I reckon they're preparing to catch my soul. They know it's coming and don't intend to miss it. You'll see, boys, once I'm gone, if they succeeded or failed. If they did, they'll keep singing and laughing until dawn. If they didn't, they'll settle down somewhat. I expect those birds and the souls they hunt often engage in rather fierce struggles."

On Lammas Night, 1924, Dr. Houghton of Aylesbury was urgently summoned by Wilbur Whateley, who had tethered his sole remaining horse against the dark and dialed Osborn's in the village. He discovered Old Whateley in a critical condition, his heart racing and breathing stertorous, signaling an end approaching rapidly. Beside the bed stood the formless albino daughter and the strangely bearded grandson, while from the empty abyss above descended a disturbing sensation of rhythmic surging, akin to waves lapping against a shore. However, the physician was most unsettled by the chatter of the night birds outside—a seemingly infinite host of whippoorwills crying an endless, repetitive message perfectly synchronized with the dying man's wheezing gasps. It was uncanny, unnatural—an excess that made Dr. Houghton feel as though he had stumbled deeper into a nightmare than he wished to admit.

Toward one o'clock, Old Whateley regained consciousness, interrupting his labored breathing to choke out instructions for his grandson. "Make room, Willy! More space, soon! You grow—and *that* thing grows faster. It will be ready to serve you shortly, boy. Recite the long chant found on page 751 of the complete edition to open the gates to Yog-Sothoth, and *then* set fire to the prison. No earthly flame can burn it!" He appeared utterly deranged. After a pause, during which the flock of whippoorwills outside shifted their cries to match the new tempo and distant rumblings echoed from the hills, he added a final warning. "Feed it regularly, Willy, and watch the quantity carefully. Do not allow it to expand too quickly for the location, for if it bursts the quarters or escapes before you open to Yog-Sothoth, everything is lost and useless. Only those from beyond can cause it to multiply and function... Only them, the old ones seeking to return..."

Speech gave way once more to ragged gasps, prompting Lavinia to scream as the whippoorwills outside seemed to follow the change in rhythm. This cycle repeated for over an hour until the final, throaty rattling ceased. Dr. Houghton gently covered the glazed, gray eyes with shrunken lids as the chaotic bird chorus faded imperceptibly into silence. Lavinia wept, but Wilbur merely chuckled, his heavy bass voice cutting through the quiet as the hill noises rumbled faintly in the distance. "They didn't get him," he muttered.

By this time, Wilbur had become a scholar of staggering, albeit singular, erudition, corresponding quietly with librarians across distant lands who guarded rare and forbidden texts of antiquity. He was increasingly hated and feared in Dunwich due to vague suspicions surrounding certain youthful disappearances attributed to him, yet he invariably silenced inquiries through terror or by deploying his substantial reserve of ancestral gold, which continued to flow outward regularly and in growing amounts for cattle purchases. Now immensely mature in appearance, his height, having reached the standard adult limit, seemed poised to exceed it further.

In 1925, when a scholarly correspondent from Miskatonic University visited and left pale and bewildered, Wilbur stood fully six and three-quarters feet tall. Throughout the intervening years, he had treated his half-deformed albino mother with escalating contempt, eventually forbidding her from accompanying him to the hills on May Eve and Hallowmass. By 1926, the poor creature confessed to Mamie Bishop that she feared him. "There's more about him than I can tell you, Mamie," she whispered. "And nowadays, there's more than I even know myself. I vow by God, I don't know what he wants or what he's trying to do."

That Halloween, the hill noises roared louder than ever, and fires burned on Sentinel Hill as tradition dictated, but villagers paid heightened attention to the rhythmic shrieking of vast flocks of unnaturally late whippoorwills gathered near the unlit Whateley farmhouse. After midnight, their shrill notes erupted into a pandemonium of laughter that filled the countryside, subsiding only at dawn. Then, they vanished, hastening southward despite being nearly a month overdue. The meaning of this event remained uncertain until later. No countryfolk appeared to have perished—but poor Lavinia Whateley, the twisted albino, was never seen again.

In the summer of 1927, Wilbur repaired two sheds in the farmyard and began relocating his books and belongings to them. Shortly thereafter, Earl Sawyer informed the regulars at Osborn's that additional carpentry was underway in the Whateley farmhouse. Wilbur was sealing all doors and windows on the ground floor while removing interior partitions, mirroring the work done upstairs four years prior. Living in one of the sheds, he appeared unusually agitated and trembling. Most suspected he knew something concerning his mother's disappearance, and very few dared approach his vicinity. His height had surpassed seven feet, showing no sign of stopping its relentless growth.
