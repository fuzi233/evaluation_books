It was a disconcerting surprise for Deronda when he returned from the Abbey to discover that Mirah’s father had taken up residence in their lodgings at Brompton. Mirah had felt it necessary to speak of Deronda to her father—though not without hesitation. She had carefully explained how her friendship with Ezra began, and how a deep mutual sympathy had grown between them. But she spoke lightly of what Deronda had done for her, omitting entirely the dramatic rescue from drowning. Instead, she framed her stay with Mrs. Meyrick’s family as something arranged through those connections, allowing her father to believe that Deronda had come into her life indirectly, rather than through any direct involvement.

She could not bring herself to reveal more. To let her father’s breath touch the truth of her bond with Deronda felt like an unbearable violation. And Lapidoth, for his part, did not press her on the circumstances of her escape or arrival in England. Yet he took keen interest in the idea that his children had found a benefactor—someone of standing in society, seemingly devoted to their well-being. It was Ezra who broke the news to Deronda: “I have become calm in seeing him now,” he said. “And I try to believe that my sister’s kindness, and the peace of daily life, may yet turn him away from temptation. I have commanded her not to give him money—and she has promised. I’ve made her understand that accepting such gifts would be to buy his own ruin.”

Deronda first visited three days after Lapidoth’s arrival. His new clothes were not ready, and wanting to make a good impression, he chose not to appear in his old garments. From the window, Lapido5th watched Deronda leave, surprised by his youthfulness—something Mirah had never mentioned. He had imagined Deronda as someone older, perhaps grave and scholarly, like Ezra himself. Now, seeing this young man, Lapidoth began to suspect that love might be the true motive behind his attentions. And if so, all the better—because a connection to Mirah offered more hope than one to Ezra. Lapidoth saw a chance to win favor with Deronda, to soften any prejudice he might carry.

He behaved with quiet charm, making efforts to settle into domestic life—joining Mirah at the piano, showing willingness to abstain from smoking in Mrs. Adam’s parlor, even walking in the square with his German pipe and tobacco supplied by Mirah. He refrained from challenging her refusal to give him money, knowing it was a solemn promise made to Ezra. He would wait. Patience, he thought, was the key.

When Deronda came again, Lapidoth was dressed in his new clothes, satisfied with his appearance, and present in the room with Ezra, who endured his father’s presence as part of a painful duty. Deronda arrived cold and distant—the mere sight of this man, who had ruined his wife and children, stirred in him a deep repulsion, almost physical. But Lapidoth remained undeterred. He asked permission to stay, listened to Ezra read documents from the old chest, and even proved useful by helping decipher a difficult German manuscript.

This led him to suggest transcribing the papers—a task he offered to take on, along with copying any documents written in Latin or Greek characters. Though Ezra’s eyes were growing weak, Lapidoth’s still held strength. Deronda accepted the offer, noting a glimmer of grace in Lapidoth’s willingness to contribute meaningfully. And he saw a quiet satisfaction in Ezra’s face—though it faded quickly. “Let all writing be done here,” Ezra said firmly. “I cannot trust these papers out of my sight. There must be no risk of fire—or worse.”

Poor Ezra felt as though he were guarding a prisoner on parole. Unless he saw his father working, he couldn’t believe the work was being done in good faith. This arrangement bound Ezra to endure his father’s presence—not just because of long-standing pain, but because Lapidoth’s restless nature grew more pronounced as he settled in. Once freed from awe of his son, he became increasingly uncontrolled: moving about, talking too much, gesturing wildly, even falling back into old habits when Mirah was near—gossiping about past lives, retelling jokes, quoting lines from plays he once adapted, convinced he could summon the spark of his younger self at will.

To Ezra, it was torture. When Mirah was home, she tried to ease his burden by taking her father down to the parlor, where she could keep watch over him.

What does duty mean when it rests on a single, hard-won resolve? The real challenge lies not in the decision itself, but in enduring its consequences each day—waking to the prospect of irritation to suppress, shame to bear. These were the burdens they carried, like many others born to unworthy fathers—burdens that stretched, for Mirah at least, into the core of their lives.

Meanwhile, Lapidoth’s presence had created an invisible wall between Deronda and Mirah. Each feared what the other might think, misreading the other’s silence as coldness, their reserve as rejection. But soon, light began to break through.

As soon as he returned from the Abbey, Deronda called on Hans Meyrick, feeling it was only right that his friend know the reasons for his journey and the changes it had brought. Hans wasn’t there—he’d gone to the country for a few days. Deronda left a note and waited a week, expecting a reply. When none came, worried about Hans’s volatile temperament—especially since his planned retreat to the Abbey had been delayed—he went again.

He was admitted into the painting room, where Hans stood before his easel, palette in hand, wearing a light coat and no waistcoat, his long hair damp from a bath. His face looked haggard, far from refreshed. He had picked up his brushes only moments before Deronda entered.

They shook hands. “You don’t look much like you’ve been in the country,” Deronda said. “Cambridge?”
“No,” Hans replied curtly, dropping his palette as if caught in a lie. Then he pulled out a chair for Deronda and sank into another, leaning back with his hands behind his head. “I’ve been to I-don’t-know-where—No Man’s Land. A terribly unpleasant place.”

“You don’t mean you’ve been drinking?” Deronda asked, concerned.
“Nothing so good,” Hans said. “I’ve been smoking opium. Always meant to try it—see how much bliss one could get. Found myself low on other kinds lately, so I thought it wise to seize the moment. But I swear I’ll never touch it again. It doesn’t agree with me.”

“What happened?” Deronda asked gently. “You seemed cheerful when you wrote.”
“Oh, nothing special,” Hans said, forcing a smile. “The world started looking seedy—a cabbage garden with all the cabbages cut. A malady of genius, no doubt. And honestly, I was tired of being virtuous without reward—especially in this sweltering London heat.”

“Nothing else? No real trouble?”
Hans shook his head. “I came to tell you about my own affairs—but I can’t do it well if you’re hiding yours.”
“I haven’t any,” Hans said flippantly. “Just a quarrel with a bric-a-brac dealer. Besides, this is the first time you’ve ever spoken to me about your life. You’re only beginning to repay a long debt.”

Deronda sensed Hans was acting—performing—but he believed honest talk would restore their old closeness. “You laughed at the mystery of my trip to Italy,” he began. “But it was about something that touched my happiness at its roots. I knew nothing of my parents. I went to Genoa to meet my mother. My father died when I was a baby. My mother was the daughter of a prominent Jew; my father was her cousin. I’d suspected this all along. So when I found out, I was glad—glad to discover I’m Jewish.”

“You needn’t look surprised,” Hans said, shifting his posture and examining the heel of his slipper. “You knew?”
“My mother told me. She came to the house the morning after you were there—both brother and sister told her. You can imagine we don’t rejoice as they do. But whatever makes you happy, I’ll come around to it eventually—when exactly, I can’t say.” He spoke softly, as he often did when discontented, but unwilling to make a fuss.

“I understand you can’t share my joy,” Deronda said. “But I couldn’t let this stand between us. It changes everything for me. I’ve embraced some of Mordecai’s ideas—and I mean to live them, as far as one man can. I may go east someday and be away for years.”

Hans said nothing. He rose, grabbed his palette, and began brushing it idly, his back turned to Deronda, who felt suddenly awkward, as if the ground beneath him had shifted.

After a pause, Hans spoke again, voice low and without turning. “Excuse the question—but does Mrs. Grandcourt know all this?”

“No,” Deronda said, stiffening. “And I beg you, Hans, stop joking about that. Your assumptions are completely wrong—entirely the opposite of the truth.”

“I’m not joking,” Hans said flatly. “But I’m not sure you know what my thoughts really are.”

“Perhaps not,” Deronda said. “But let me say this once: in relation to Mrs. Grandcourt, I’ve never been—and never will be—her lover. If you’ve ever interpreted anything I’ve done that way, you’re profoundly mistaken.”

Silence followed—thick, uncomfortable.

“Perhaps I’ve been wrong in another interpretation too,” Hans said finally.

“What?”
“That you had no desire to love another woman—one who is neither married nor widowed.”

Deronda understood immediately. “You mean Mirah.”

“I can’t pretend I don’t understand,” he said. “It’s painful that our desires clash. But if you truly believe I have reason to envy you, then you’re mistaken.”

“Not the slightest,” Hans said bitterly. “You’ve measured my pride and know it towers above all your advantages.”

“I’m a nuisance to you, Meyrick,” Deronda said, rising. “I’m sorry. But I can’t help it. After what passed between us, I needed this talk. And I don’t see how my feelings have changed things for you. They won’t change things for me either, under these circumstances. Now—did you know her father is there?”

“Yes,” Hans said. “If he weren’t a Jew, I’d damn him—with faint praise, I mean.” He didn’t smile.

“She and I meet under greater constraint than ever,” Deronda continued. “We could go two years without understanding her heart. That’s the situation, Hans. Neither of us has hurt the other. We must accept this rivalry—hopeful, but likely to end in nothing. Our friendship can bear this strain, surely.”

“No,” Hans said sharply, throwing down his tools, hands in pockets, turning to face him. “Our friendship—my friendship—can’t bear the strain of pretending I’m not a thankless coward, begrudging you your happiness. Because you *are* the happiest man alive. If Mirah loves anyone more than her brother—it’s you.”

He turned abruptly and sat, staring up at Deronda with an expression utterly devoid of tenderness. A shock ran through Deronda. After a moment, he said, “That’s just a kind fiction of yours, Hans.”

“I’m not in a kind mood,” Hans replied. “I found it disagreeable when I realized it—especially because I thought you were pledged to the duchess. But now—confound you!—you’re in love where it matters. A Jew. Everything perfect.”

“Tell me what convinced you,” Deronda said, half doubting the joy he felt.

“Don’t ask,” Hans said. “Mother saw it. The result is—Mirah is jealous of the duchess. The sooner you confess, the better. There! I’ve paid off a score or two. Now I may curse you for getting what you deserve—the best luck I know.”

“God bless you, Hans,” Deronda said, reaching out his hand. Hans took it, and they gripped each other silently—two friends, bound by honesty, pain, and a love that had just begun to shine through the shadows.
