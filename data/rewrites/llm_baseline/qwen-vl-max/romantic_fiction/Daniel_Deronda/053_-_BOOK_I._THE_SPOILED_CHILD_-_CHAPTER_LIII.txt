CHAPTER LIII. “My desolation does begin to make A better life.” —SHAKESPEARE: _Antony and Cleopatra._

Before Daniel Deronda was summoned for a second meeting with his mother, a day had passed in silence—only a message sent, saying she was not yet well enough to receive him. But on the third morning, a note arrived: “I leave today. Come at once.”

He was led into the same room as before, though now it was draped in shadows—blinds drawn, curtains pulled tight, dimming the light like the veil of memory. The Princess was not there when he entered, but soon she appeared, wrapped in a loose silk robe the color of sunset fire, her head adorned with black lace that floated around her like smoke. Her arms emerged bare from beneath wide sleeves, and her face, illuminated by the low light, seemed more striking than ever—the eyes larger, the lines sharper, as if carved by time and defiance alike. One might have thought her a woman who had once ruled over youth and beauty, but now chose to stand apart from them, having tasted too much.

She stepped forward at once, placing her arms gently on his shoulders and kissing his cheeks—one after the other—then sat down among her cushions with an air of quiet authority, far removed from the restless energy of their first encounter. She gestured for him to sit beside her. He obeyed, murmuring, “You are feeling better now, I hope?”

“Yes,” she replied, calm and composed. “I am at ease again. Is there anything else you wish to ask me?”

“Can I find the house in Genoa where you lived with my grandfather?” Deronda asked.

“No,” she said, lifting her hand slightly in dismissal. “It’s gone—demolished. Not to be found. But everything about our family—where your father lived, what he did—you’ll find it all in the chest, written down. Better than I could tell you. My father was a physician. My mother, a Morteira. I heard those things often—but never truly listened. You’ll find the truth there. I was born into them without choice. I left them behind as soon as I could.”

Deronda felt a pang, but held back. “Then,” he said softly, “is there nothing else you would want to share—something that might bring peace to both of us?”

“I think I’ve told you all that needed to be said,” she answered, her voice cool, almost distant. It was as if she had already given up the struggle. In truth, she had decided: *I’ve confessed. I’ve done all I can. I will not reopen this wound.* And so she stood firm in her resolve.

But for Deronda, the moment was unbearable. His longing—for connection, for belonging—had brought him here, only to meet silence. The shrine of his mother’s identity had been stripped bare; no relics remained. And now, as he looked at her, he saw not just a woman, but someone who had chosen exile even from love itself.

“Then,” he whispered, his voice trembling, “are we to part forever? Will I never mean anything to you?”

“It is better this way,” she said, softer now, almost tender. “There could be no true bond between us—not even if you were truly mine. You would not love me. Don’t deny it,” she added sharply, raising her hand. “I know what lies within you. You disapprove of what I did. You’re angry. You believe I stole something from you. You side with your grandfather—and you’ll always carry judgment in your heart.”

Deronda felt himself silenced. He rose from his seat, choosing to stand rather than sit under her command, as if standing meant refusing to surrender to emotion. Yet she looked up at him then, her gaze softening with something new—admiration.

“You’re wrong to be angry,” she said. “You are stronger because of what I did.”

After a pause, she asked, “And now—what will you do?”

“Do you mean right now?” he asked. “Or about your future?”

“The future,” she said, more sharply. “What difference does it make that I’ve told you of your birth?”

“A very great difference,” he replied firmly. “Perhaps the greatest thing that could happen to me.”

“And what will you do?” she pressed.

“Become like my grandfather? Be what he wished? Turn myself into a Jew as he wanted?”

“That is impossible,” Deronda said, his voice growing stronger. “The education I’ve received cannot be undone. The Christian world shaped me. But I feel it is my duty—my deepest impulse—to connect with my people. If I can find a purpose for them, one I can give my soul and hands to—I will choose it.”

She stared at him, her eyes searching his face as if reading a language only she could understand. He met her gaze with steady courage, holding fast to who he was.

Then she leaned toward him, speaking with sudden certainty: “You are in love with a Jewess.”

Deronda flushed. “My reasons are not dependent on that.”

“I know better,” she said, decisive. “I’ve seen how men are. Tell me the truth. She is a Jewess who refuses anyone but a Jew. There are a few such,” she added, with a flicker of disdain.

He hesitated. He didn’t wish to lie, nor to confirm what might hurt her further. So he stayed silent.

She waited, then said, “You love her as your father loved me. She draws you as I drew him.”

The words struck him deeply. A tenderness flickered in his eyes—and she mistook it for agreement.

Her voice rose, charged with passion: “But I was pulling him in another direction. And now your grandfather is avenging himself.”

“Mother,” Deronda said gently, “don’t see it that way. I admit your choices may have brought benefit. I prefer gratitude to resentment. I believe I should have known my heritage from the start. But you gave me something else—broadness, depth, understanding. And now—events have restored more than you could have imagined. You’ve saved my people from losing me, and me from losing my duty. Can’t you let your heart open to this?”

He paused, sensing her attention. She listened, her expression unreadable, yet slowly shook her head.

He tried again, more urgently: “You said you sought what you believed best for me. Now, open your heart to your grandfather—he sought what he believed best for you.”

“No,” she said, shaking her head firmly, folding her arms tightly. “Not for me. He never thought of me as a daughter—only as a tool. Because I had desires outside his plan, I was to be confined, twisted. If that is the law of the world, I refuse to accept it. If God demands I surrender what I kept—punish me for deceiving my father, for not warning him I would betray his trust—well, I’ve told everything. I’ve done all I could. And your soul agrees. That is enough. I was, after all, the instrument he wanted.”

As she spoke the last lines, she narrowed her eyes, nodded slowly, and lowered her voice, speaking as if quoting something sacred—yet forced:
*“I desire a grandson who shall have a true Jewish heart. Every Jew should rear his family as if he hoped a Deliverer might spring from it.”*

“Were those your grandfather’s words?” Deronda asked.

“Yes,” she said, almost fiercely. “Yes. And you’ll find them written. I wanted to thwart him.” Then, quieter: “You would have me love what I’ve hated since I was this high”—she held her hand a foot above the floor—“That can never be. But what does it matter? His yoke was upon me—whether I liked it or not. You are the grandson he wanted. You speak like wise men—like you know the meaning of it all. What does it mean?”

Her tone was sharp, dismissive.

Deronda, caught between sorrow and reverence, clung to the memory of their bond, afraid his words might wound.

“Mother,” he said, voice deep and pleading, “don’t say I feel myself wise. We are caught in hard truths. I see no way to clarity except through honesty—through speaking what must be said. No wonder such truths reveal themselves, despite silence. Generations prepare consequences that outlive any single will. Your will was strong—but your father’s trust, which you accepted and failed to fulfill—what you call his yoke—is rooted deeper, bound into the sacred foundations of humanity. You renounced me—you still reject me—as a son.” His voice faltered. “But that greater force has determined I shall be exactly the grandson you wished to destroy.”

She watched him, still. Then admiration returned to her face.

After a long silence, she said, softly, “Sit down again.”

He obeyed, settling beside her. She placed her hand on his shoulder.

“You rebuke me,” she said. “Well—then I am the loser. And you are angry because I banish you. What could you do for me but wear yourself out? I am broken. My life is little more than memory—except when pain comes. You accuse me of letting go of you. But I had joy without you. Now you return—and I cannot give you joy. Do you carry the curse of the Jew in you? Can you not forgive me? Will you be glad knowing I am punished for not being a Jewish mother to you?”

“How can you ask that?” Deronda replied, distressed. “I’ve begged to be your son—even now. My sorrow is that you say I cannot comfort you. I’d give up much—everything dear—to ease your suffering.”

“You shall give nothing,” she said, agitated. “You shall be happy. Let me believe you are happy. I’ve done you no harm. You have no reason to curse me. Think of me as they think of the dead—pray for me. Long that I be freed from pain, from punishment. And I shall see you—instead of always seeing your grandfather. Will any harm come to me for breaking his trust after he was gone? I don’t know. But if you believe _Kaddish_ helps—say it. Say it. You will come between me and the dead. When I am in your thoughts, you will look at me—as you do now—like a tender son. As if I had been a tender mother.”

She fought to keep control, but her hand trembled on his shoulder.

A wave of compassion overwhelmed Deronda. With a face full of sorrow, he put his arm around her and gently pressed her head against his chest.

They sat like that for moments—silent, heavy with unspoken grief.

Then she lifted her head, sighed deeply, as if releasing a lifetime of burden.

Deronda sensed the farewell drawing near.

But suddenly, her mood shifted. “Is she beautiful?” she asked abruptly.

“Who?” he said, turning red.

“The woman you love.”

He couldn’t delay. “Yes.”

“Not ambitious?”

“No. I don’t think so.”

“Doesn’t demand her own path?”

“I think her nature is not made for claiming greatness.”

“She is not like that?” she said, pulling a miniature from her purse—a portrait of herself in youth, radiant with fire and life. She held it up to him.

Deronda looked at it with quiet sadness.

“Did I not have a right to be more than just a daughter, a mother?” she asked. “My voice, my spirit matched my face. Whatever else was wrong—acknowledge that I had a right to be an artist, even if my father forbade it. My nature gave me a charter.”

“I do acknowledge it,” Deronda said, looking from the portrait to her face—still powerful, still alive, even in weariness.

“Will you take it?” she asked gently. “If she is kind, teach her to remember me kindly.”

“I’ll be grateful,” he said, “but—I must confess, I have no assurance she loves me. I’ve said nothing.”

“Who is she?” she demanded.

It was not a question—it was a command.

“She was raised to be a singer,” Deronda said, reluctant. “Her father took her from her mother early. Her life has been unhappy. She’s young—only twenty. Her father wanted her to reject her Jewish roots, but she clings to her mother’s memory and her people’s fellowship.”

“Ah, like you,” the Princess said, cutting in. “She’s attached to Judaism she knows nothing of. Poetry—fit for an opera night. Does she enjoy her art? Is her singing good?”

“Exquisite,” he said. “But her voice isn’t suited for the stage. I think the artist’s life repels her.”

“Then she is made for you,” she said. “Sir Hugo said you despised being a singer. And I see—you would never have allowed yourself to be lost in a wife, as your father was.”

“I repeat,” Deronda said firmly, “I have no promise of her love. No certainty we can be together. Painful choices may lie ahead. I’ve always prepared myself to renounce such hopes. Perhaps I feel that way about happiness in general. Whether it comes or not, one must learn to live without it.”

“Do you feel that way?” she asked, placing her hands on his shoulders, studying his face, speaking slowly, pausing between words.

“Poor boy,” she murmured. “I wonder—what if I had kept you? Would you have turned to the old ways against me? Would we have quarreled? Would your grandfather have lived in you? Would you have weighed down my life with your growth from the old root?”

“I think my affection could have endured,” Deronda said, growing sadder. “And it wouldn’t have hindered you—surely it would have enriched your life.”

“Not then,” she said, voice thick with regret. “Not then. I didn’t want it. But now—perhaps I might have welcomed it—if I could welcome anything.”

“But you love your other children,” he asked, anxious. “And they love you?”

“Oh, yes,” she said, as if it were obvious. Then, lower: “But I am not a loving woman. That’s the truth. Love is a gift I lack. Others have loved me—and I’ve played their love. I know what love does—it makes one subject to another, absorbing the self into another’s.” She touched her chest. “I was never willing to be subject. Men were subject to me.”

“Perhaps the man who was subject was the happier,” Deronda said, not smiling, but with a quiet sorrow for her loneliness.

“Perhaps,” she said. “But I was happy—for a time. If I hadn’t feared failure, I might have continued. I miscalculated. And now—nothing remains. Another life! People talk of ‘another life’ as if it begins beyond death. But I’ve already entered it.”

With those words, she raised her arms until they were bare to the elbow, her brow furrowed, her eyes closed, her voice stifled. In her flame-colored dress, she seemed a vision—something from beyond the world of the living.

Deronda felt himself unraveling. A sob escaped him.

She opened her eyes, let her hands rest once more on his shoulders, and said, “Good-bye, my son. Good-bye. We shall hear no more of each other. Kiss me.”

He embraced her, kissed her, and then stepped back.

He did not remember how he left the room.

When he reached the hall, he felt older—no longer a boy, but a man who had walked through fire. All his childhood dreams of his mother were gone. He had faced a truth that would shape the rest of his life. And in its shadow, he understood—love, duty, and destiny were not gifts to be claimed, but burdens to be carried with grace.
