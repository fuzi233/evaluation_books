The Three Tools of Death

Father Brown, both by calling and conviction, understood better than most that every man is dignified in death. Yet even he felt a flicker of unease when, at dawn, he was roused from sleep with news that Sir Aaron Armstrong had been murdered. There was something jarringly absurd about such violence befalling a man so universally beloved and cheerfully eccentric. Sir Aaron Armstrong was not merely popular—he was a national figure, a comic force in the world of philanthropy. To hear of his murder was like learning that Mr. Pickwick had died in an asylum or that Sunny Jim had taken his own life.

Though he championed temperance—his favorite cause—Sir Aaron approached it with such relentless gaiety that it seemed almost a performance. His speeches were floods of anecdote and laughter; his health was robust to excess; his ethics radiated optimism. He spoke of drink as if it were a villain in a farce, which only made him more endearing to those who’d never touched a drop. His conversion story was well known: once a boy drawn to Scotch whisky rather than Scotch theology, he had risen above both and become—modestly enough—the man he now was. But his white beard, cherubic face, and sparkling spectacles made it hard to believe he’d ever been anything so somber as a Calvinist or a drunkard. He was, one felt, the most earnestly joyful man alive.

He lived on the edge of Hampstead, in a tall, narrow house perched high above the railway line. The house was modern, plain, and slightly ominous—a tower of glass and stone. Its narrow side overhung the steep grassy bank beside the tracks, trembling each time a train passed. Sir Aaron claimed he had no nerves. But this morning, the tables had turned: it was the house that gave the shock—not the train.

The engine slowed just beyond the point where the house’s corner jutted onto the slope. Most mechanical stops are gradual, but this one was sudden. A man dressed entirely in black—black coat, black trousers, even black gloves—appeared atop the bank, waving his arms like a windmill of mourning. Alone, this would not have halted a train. But then came a cry—something so unnatural, so piercing, that even the driver said he would have stopped regardless of what words were shouted. The word was “Murder!”

Once the train halted, the scene unfolded swiftly. The man in black was Magnus, Sir Aaron’s manservant. The baronet had often laughed at Magnus’s gloomy attire, but no one was laughing now.

A few moments later, the first onlookers crossed the smoky hedge and saw the body of an old man in a yellow dressing gown with a vivid red lining, rolled down nearly to the bottom of the bank. A scrap of rope was caught around one leg, likely from a struggle. Blood was present but minimal. The body was bent in a way no living thing could endure. It was Sir Aaron Armstrong.

Then came Patrick Royce, the secretary—a large, fair-bearded man once famous in Bohemian circles. He emerged in a daze, echoing Magnus’s horror. By the time Alice Armstrong, the dead man’s daughter, appeared, tottering into the garden, the train had resumed its journey, bound for help.

Father Brown had been summoned at the request of Royce, an Irishman and a Catholic by birth, though one who rarely remembered his faith until trouble struck. The summons might not have been obeyed so promptly had it not been for the young detective Merton, whose friendliness with Flambeau had led him to hear many stories of the priest. As they walked across the fields toward the house, their conversation grew more personal.

“As far as I can tell,” Merton said, “there’s no sense to any of this. No one we can suspect. Magnus is a solemn fool—too much of a fool to be a killer. Royce has been with the baronet for years. And Alice clearly adored him. Besides, it’s all too absurd. Who would kill such a cheerful old man? It’s like murdering Father Christmas.”

“Cheerful, yes,” Father Brown replied quietly. “But did he make others cheerful? Was anyone else in the house truly happy?”

Merton paused. He had visited the Armstrong home often on minor police matters. Now, recalling it, he realized how bleak it really was. The rooms were high and cold, poorly decorated, lit by harsh electric bulbs that cast a ghostly glow. Though Sir Aaron’s presence blazed like a fire, it left no warmth behind. His vitality seemed to drain the air. The others in the house were shadows: Magnus, morose and gloved; Royce, big and solid, but with a face worn by sorrow; and Alice, pale and delicate, her frame trembling like a leaf in the wind. She seemed to flinch at the thunder of passing trains.

“You see,” said Father Brown, blinking modestly, “I’m not sure Armstrong’s cheerfulness was so cheerful for others. You say no one would kill such a happy man. But I wonder… *ne nos inducas in tentationem*. If I ever killed someone, it might be an optimist.”

“Why?” Merton asked, amused.

“People like laughter,” Father Brown said, “but not a permanent smile. Cheerfulness without humor is exhausting.”

They walked in silence along the grassy bank until Father Brown suddenly said, “Of course, drink itself isn’t good or bad. But sometimes I think men like Armstrong need a glass of wine now and then—to soften them.”

At the house, Gilder, a grizzled senior detective, stood waiting with Royce, whose broad shoulders and bristling hair made him seem larger than life—even though he walked with a heavy stoop, like a buffalo pulling a cart.

Royce greeted Father Brown warmly and stepped aside to speak privately. Merton addressed Gilder respectfully, though with a hint of impatience.

“Well, sir, have you made any progress?”

“There’s no mystery,” Gilder replied, staring at the rooks. “Three minutes after you left to fetch the priest, everything came out. You know the servant in black gloves who stopped the train?”

“I’d recognize him anywhere. He gave me the creeps.”

“After the train left,” Gilder drawled, “he vanished. Rather cool of a criminal to escape on the very train sent for the police.”

“You’re certain he killed the baronet?” Merton asked.

“Quite certain,” Gilder said dryly. “He took twenty thousand pounds from the master’s desk. The only puzzle is how he killed him. The skull appears broken by a heavy blow, but there’s no weapon. And if he carried it away, it must have been small—or perhaps too big to notice.”

“Perhaps the weapon was too big to notice,” Father Brown said, giggling faintly.

Gilder turned sharply. “What do you mean?”

“Silly phrasing, I know,” Brown apologized. “Sounds like a fairy tale. But poor Armstrong was killed by a giant’s club—a great green club, too large to see. We call it the earth. He was thrown against this very bank.”

Gilder frowned, then looked up. Following Brown’s gaze, they saw an attic window open at the top of the house’s blind rear wall.

“Don’t you see?” Brown pointed awkwardly. “He was thrown from there.”

Gilder examined the window. “Possible. But why are you so sure?”

Brown blinked. “Look at the rope around the corpse’s leg. See the other piece caught at the window’s corner?”

From below, it looked like a speck. But Gilder nodded. “You’re right, sir. That’s one to you.”

Just then, a special train arrived, disgorging more police—and Magnus, the missing servant.

“By Jove!” Gilder exclaimed. “They’ve got him.” He stepped forward. “Have you recovered the money?”

“No,” the policeman replied. “Not here.”

“Which is the inspector?” Magnus asked.

His voice—clear, sharp, unnaturally precise—made everyone jump. He was a dull man, with flat black hair, a colorless face, and eyes that slanted slightly, suggesting Eastern ancestry. His past was murky—rescued by Sir Aaron from a London restaurant, some said from worse things. But his voice was alive where his face was dead.

“I always knew this would happen,” he said calmly. “My master mocked my black clothes, but I always said I’d be ready for his funeral.”

He raised his gloved hands.

“Sergeant,” Gilder snapped, “put the bracelets on him—he looks dangerous.”

“Actually, sir,” the sergeant said, “we can’t.”

“What do you mean?”

“We arrested him,” the sergeant said gravely, “just as he was leaving the Highgate police station—where he’d deposited the money in Inspector Robinson’s care.”

Gilder stared. “Why would he do that?”

“To keep it safe from the criminal,” Magnus replied placidly.

“But surely,” Gilder began, “it could have stayed with the family.”

The train roared past, drowning the rest—but through the noise, Magnus’s voice rang clear: “I have no reason to trust Sir Aaron’s family.”

Everyone froze. Then Alice Armstrong appeared behind Father Brown—pale, beautiful, yet with hair so faded it seemed gray in shadow.

“Be careful what you say,” Royce muttered. “You’ll frighten Miss Armstrong.”

“I hope so,” Magnus said.

She winced. He continued: “I’ve seen her tremble for years. Some thought it was fear or cold. I know it was hatred—wicked anger. She would have fled with her lover and the money, but for me. Ever since her father stopped her marriage to that drunken scoundrel—”

“Stop!” Gilder commanded. “We don’t care about your family quarrels. Unless you have evidence—”

“Oh, I’ll give you evidence,” Magnus cut in. “You’ll have to subpoena me. And I’ll tell the truth. After the old man was thrown from the window, I ran into the attic and found her unconscious—fainting—on the floor, with a red dagger still in her hand. Allow me to hand it over.” He pulled a horn-handled knife from his pocket, smeared with blood, and handed it to the sergeant.

He stepped back, his eyes narrowing into a sneer.

Merton felt sick. “Surely you’d take her word over his?”

Father Brown lifted his face—so fresh, so innocent, it seemed he’d just washed it. “Yes,” he said gently. “But is Miss Armstrong’s word against his?”

Alice gasped—a small, startled sound. Her body stiffened. Only her face moved—alive with horror.

“This man claims,” Gilder said slowly, “that you were found with a knife, unconscious, after the murder.”

“He speaks the truth,” she whispered.

Then Royce strode forward, his massive head bowed. “Well, if I’ve got to go,” he said, “I’ll have a bit of pleasure first.”

He swung a fist—crashing into Magnus’s face. The servant fell like a sack of stones. Police rushed forward.

“None of that, Mr. Royce,” Gilder said firmly. “I arrest you for assault.”

“No,” Royce replied, voice like iron. “You’ll arrest me for murder.”

Gilder glanced at Magnus, who was already sitting up, wiping blood from his unharmed face.

“What do you mean?”

“It’s true,” Royce said. “Miss Armstrong fainted with a knife in her hand. But she didn’t use it to attack her father. She used it to defend him.”

“Against whom?” Gilder asked.

“Against me.”

Alice looked at him—her expression tangled with grief and something else. Then softly: “After all, I am still glad you are brave.”

“Come upstairs,” Royce said heavily. “I’ll show you the whole cursed thing.”

The attic was Royce’s private room—small, cramped, filled with signs of violence. A revolver lay near the center, as if tossed aside. A whisky bottle, half-empty, sat nearby. The tablecloth was torn, a length of rope sprawled across the windowsill. Two vases were shattered on the mantel, one on the floor.

“I was drunk,” Royce said simply. “And this is the beginning of the end.”

He continued, voice thick with shame: “You know my story. I was once called clever. Armstrong saved what was left of me from the taverns. He was kind. But he wouldn’t let me marry Alice. And maybe he was right. But you don’t need me to explain. That’s my whisky. That’s my gun—empty. That’s my rope. That’s my window. I confess—I killed him. I give myself to the gallows. And by God—that’s enough.”

The police moved to escort him. But Father Brown was kneeling on the carpet, examining the floor as if praying.

“I say,” he said cheerfully, “this won’t do at all. At first, you said there was no weapon. Now we’ve found three: a knife, a rope, a pistol—and yet he broke his neck falling! It’s not economical.”

He shook his head like a grazing horse.

“And now three impossible things. First, these bullet holes in the carpet. Why shoot at the floor? A drunk man shoots at the enemy’s head—what’s grinning at him. Not at the feet. Then the rope—why would a drunk man try to strangle someone, then wrap it around their leg? Royce wasn’t that drunk. And finally—the whisky bottle. You say a dipsomaniac fought for it, then rolled it away, spilling half? That’s the last thing a drunk would do.”

He rose awkwardly. “I’m terribly sorry, sir, but your story is rubbish.”

Alice approached Brown. “Can I speak to you alone?”

They stepped into the next room.

“You’re trying to save Patrick,” she said. “But it’s no use. The truth is black. The more you learn, the worse it gets for the man I love.”

“Why?” Brown asked.

“Because,” she said steadily, “I saw him commit the crime.”

“Ah,” he said, unmoved. “And what did he do?”

“I was in the next room. Both doors were closed. Then I heard a voice—like nothing on earth—roaring ‘Hell, hell, hell!’ Again and again. Then the doors shook with the first gunshot. Three more times it fired before I could open them. Smoke filled the room. The pistol was smoking in Patrick’s hand. I saw him fire the final shots. Then he lunged at my father, who clung to the window-sill. He threw the rope over his head, but it slipped to his shoulders, then his legs. It tightened around one leg, and Patrick dragged him down like a madman. I grabbed a knife from the mat and cut the rope—before I fainted.”

“I see,” Father Brown said, equally calm. “Thank you.”

And he returned to the attic—quietly, as if nothing had changed.
