As Carthoris, Xodar, Tars Tarkas, and I stood in awe before the magnificent vessel that loomed above us, a second ship crested the hilltop, followed swiftly by a third. The air shimmered with motion as a dozen one-man fliers launched from the upper decks of the nearest warship, diving like silver birds toward the ground. In moments, armed sailors surrounded us, their weapons drawn. An officer stepped forward, his eyes scanning our group—then they locked onto Carthoris.

With a gasp of recognition, he rushed forward, placing his hands on the boy’s shoulders. “Carthoris, my Prince!” he exclaimed, his voice thick with emotion. “Kaor! Kaor! Hor Vastus greets the son of Dejah Thoris, Princess of Helium, and her husband, John Carter. Where have you been? All Helium has mourned your absence. Since the day you vanished, calamity has fallen upon our great nation.”

“Grieve not, Hor Vastus,” Carthoris replied, his voice steady. “I return not alone—but with one whom all Barsoom once loved best: John Carter, Prince of Helium, savior of our world!”

The officer turned, his gaze sweeping over me—and then froze. His breath caught. “John Carter…” he whispered, his face pale. A flicker of pain crossed his eyes, but he did not press further. He knew the unspoken truth: that I had returned from beyond the River Iss, from the shores of the Lost Sea of Korus, from the Valley Dor itself. That I had walked through death and come back alive was a secret too sacred to force from my lips.

Yet still, he knelt. With a gesture that spoke volumes, he unbuckled his sword and laid it at my feet. On Mars, such an act meant more than loyalty—it meant surrender. It was the oath of fealty: body, soul, life, honor—all pledged to the one who now held the blade. No man had ever offered me such devotion. There was only one answer.

I bent, picked up the sword, kissed the hilt, and fastened it back upon him. “Hor Vastus,” I said, laying a hand on his shoulder, “you know your heart better than any. I may need your sword, but swear this: I will never ask you to draw it save for truth, justice, and righteousness.”

“That I knew, my Prince,” he answered, eyes bright. “Even before I cast it down.”

As we spoke, more fliers darted between the ships and the ground. Soon, a larger craft descended—a boat capable of carrying a dozen. It landed gently beside us. From its deck stepped an officer who saluted Hor Vastus. “Kantos Kan commands that you be brought immediately to the _Xavarian_.”

We boarded the vessel, but as I looked around, I realized Thuvia was missing. None had seen her since Carthoris sent her thoat racing into the hills—hoping to spare her from danger. Without hesitation, Hor Vastus dispatched a dozen scouts in every direction, desperate to find her.

On the _Xavarian_, Kantos Kan awaited us. My old comrade had risen to command Helium’s entire navy, yet he remained the same brave warrior who once shared a dungeon with me. He greeted Carthoris and Tars Tarkas with joy, but said nothing of where we had been. Like Hor Vastus, he dared not ask.

Then, quietly, he drew me aside. “For a year, Carthoris has been lost,” he said. “Dejah Thoris has grieved—her heart broken. When you disappeared, she suffered terribly. But when her son vanished… she began to fade. Her father and grandfather led expeditions to find him, but no word has come back. Rumors say they perished.”

He paused. “Zat Arras has renewed his demands for her hand. She despised him, feared him. But now, with both her father and grandfather gone, he holds power. Six days ago, he had a private audience with her. The next day, she vanished—along with Sola, Tars Tarkas’ daughter, and a dozen of her guards.”

He looked at me grimly. “They left no word. We believe she has taken the final pilgrimage—to the icy bosom of Iss. And her servants went with her. Zat Arras commands this fleet. He searches for her, but I fear it is already too late.”

My heart sank. Then came the news of Thuvia’s disappearance—another burden. I had vowed to return her to her people. Now, both women were lost.

Before I could speak, a flier arrived from Zat Arras’ flagship. The officer bore a message: “Bring the prisoners before me.”

Kantos Kan read it, then turned to me. “He demands we bring you to him. He is supreme in Helium—but it would be more fitting if he came to greet the savior of Barsoom.”

I smiled. “You know well, my friend, that Zat Arras hates me. Nothing would please him more than to humiliate and kill me. Let us go—see if he has the courage.”

We boarded a small flier. As we approached Zat Arras’ ship, his face showed no warmth—not even for Carthoris. Cold. Proud. Unyielding.

“Kaor, Zat Arras,” I said.

No reply.

“Why were these men not disarmed?” he asked Kantos Kan.

“They are not prisoners,” Kantos Kan replied. “Tars Tarkas is Jeddak of Thark, ally of Tardos Mors. The other is a friend of the Prince of Helium.”

“It is not enough,” Zat Arras snapped. “Where have you been, John Carter?”

“I have just returned from the Valley Dor,” I said. “From the Land of the First Born.”

He exhaled sharply. “Ah! So you admit it—you’ve returned from Iss?”

“I have returned from a land of lies and torment,” I said. “From a valley of death. We escaped the clutches of monstrous deceivers. I am back to save Barsoom—this time, from true annihilation.”

“Cease, blasphemer!” he roared. “Do not expect to save yourself with horror-filled lies—”

But he did not finish. One does not call John Carter a coward or a liar lightly.

Before anyone could react, I was on him—hand gripping his throat. “Come I from heaven or hell, Zat Arras, I remain the same John Carter. And no man calls me such names and lives—unless he apologizes.”

I bent him backward across my knee. His face turned purple. “Seize him!” he gasped.

A dozen officers lunged forward. Kantos Kan stepped close. “Desist,” he whispered. “If you fight, we all fall into chaos. My men will stand with you. A mutiny could spark revolution. For Tardos Mors, for Helium—stop.”

I released Zat Arras. Turned my back. “Come, Kantos Kan. The Prince of Helium returns to the _Xavarian_.”

No one stopped us. Zat Arras stood trembling, white-faced. Some officers looked at him with scorn. One, a veteran of Tardos Mors’ court, murmured as I passed: “Count me among your soldiers, John Carter.”

I nodded. We returned to the _Xavarian_ in silence.

Fifteen minutes later, orders came: proceed to Helium.

Our journey was quiet. Carthoris and I were lost in thought. Kantos Kan worried about what might happen if Zat Arras enforced the ancient law—death for those who returned from Dor. Tars Tarkas grieved for Sola. Xodar, ever the outlaw, shrugged. “Let us hope we die with red blood on our blades,” he said.

It was a simple wish—one likely to come true.

As we neared Helium, I saw division among the crew. Some gathered near us. Others kept their distance—afraid, perhaps, of the sin we carried. To return from Dor was sacrilege. To speak of it was heresy. We were blasphemers in their eyes—even if they still honored us personally.

Kantos Kan refused to talk of the therns or the First Born. “I risk enough by standing with you,” he said. “Do not ask me to hear what I’ve always believed was the greatest lie.”

I understood. Soon, the choice would be made. In Helium, we would face judgment. If Tardos Mors had not returned, Zat Arras would rule. To oppose him was treason. Yet many would follow me—despite god, man, or devil.

But my mind was consumed by Dejah Thoris. I saw her in the darkness—chained, tormented, facing horrors beyond imagining. The plant men. The white apes. I covered my face, trying to shut out the nightmare.

At last, we reached Helium—the city marked by its towering scarlet spire. As we circled downward, I saw the streets below filled with a vast, silent crowd. They had been warned by radio-aerogram.

From the _Xavarian_, we transferred to a smaller flier—bound for the Temple of Reward. This was where justice was given: heroes crowned, criminals condemned. We were taken directly to a chamber overlooking the Avenue of Ancestors, five miles long, stretching to the Gate of Jeddaks.

Below, the people stood packed tight—silent, solemn. Many wept when they saw us at the window.

Late in the afternoon, a messenger arrived. “Zat Arras commands,” he said, “that you be tried by a council of nobles tomorrow at the first zode—about 8:40 Earth time.”

And so we waited.

In the silence of the temple room, the weight of the coming trial pressed upon us. The fate of Barsoom hung in the balance. And somewhere, in the frozen wastes of Iss, my beloved princess may already be gone.
