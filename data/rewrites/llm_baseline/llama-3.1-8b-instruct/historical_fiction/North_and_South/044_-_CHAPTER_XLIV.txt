As the quiet of the Harley Street house enveloped her like a soothing balm, Margaret found solace in the stillness that reigned during Edith's convalescence. The gentle rhythms of daily life, lubricated by the efficient machinery of the household, rolled along with a smoothness that belied the turmoil that lurked beneath the surface. Mrs. Shaw and Edith fawned over Margaret, lavishing attention upon her as if she were a cherished treasure, a welcome addition to their opulent domain. They insisted on calling her home, though Margaret's mind wandered back to the humble vicarage at Helstone, where her father's anxious face and her mother's fragile health had once been her constant companions. Even the modest house at Milton, with its simple pleasures and quiet struggles, held a deeper allure for her than the lavish surroundings that now enveloped her.

As Edith gradually regained her strength, she longed to reclaim her place at the center of the household, and Margaret, happy to oblige, resumed her role as her cousin's devoted companion. Together, they navigated the intricacies of high society, with Edith's notes and engagements a reminder of the whirlwind of events that lay ahead. But as the days passed, Margaret found herself increasingly disconnected from the world outside the Harley Street mansion. The servants moved unseen, their lives a mystery to her, and the only sound that broke the stillness was the soft rustle of fabrics, the murmur of polite conversation, and the occasional clinking of fine china.

In the evenings, as Edith rested, Margaret would sit by her side, lost in thought, her mind drifting back to the simplicity of her former life. She felt a growing unease, a sense of disconnection from the world around her, as if she were sleepwalking through a dream. Her words to Edith, barely whispered, had been met with a gentle stroke of her cheek, a soft dismissal of her concerns. "Poor child," Edith had cooed, "it's only natural to feel a bit moped when the world is so full of life." But Margaret wasn't convinced that the dinner parties that lay ahead would be the panacea Edith promised, a solution to the emptiness that gnawed at her soul.
