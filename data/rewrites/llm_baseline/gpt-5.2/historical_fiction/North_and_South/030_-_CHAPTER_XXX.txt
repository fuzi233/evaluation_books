Mrs. Thornton came the next morning with the rigid punctuality of a woman who believed time itself should obey her. She had come because her son had asked it as a personal favour, and because she had yielded—tight-lipped, as if yielding were a kind of hunger she would not admit to feeling.

All the way, her mind had been marching through its old grievances. She told herself—again—that the Hales brought only disorder into a house that had stood straight without them; that Mr. Hale’s learning was a useless weight, and the dead languages dead for good reason. She had said as much to her son the night before, with that harsh relish she had when she thought she was telling a truth no one else dared to speak. He had listened as he always did when his mother flung her judgments like stones: silently, letting them fall where they might. But when she had finished, and imagined she had moved him, he had only repeated, in his curt way, the time he wished her to go.

So she went—proud, offended, and secretly a little pleased with him for insisting. She exaggerated, as she walked, his perseverance into some extraordinary goodness, and her compliance into a sacrifice.

And then she entered the sick-room.

Whatever pride she had carried in like armour seemed suddenly absurd before that bed. Mrs. Hale lay there as if the night had drained her. In one short space of hours suffering had taken a great visible stride; the face—once soft with timid graces—had become gray and hollow, and the skin, drawn tight, seemed too thin for the bones beneath. Even the air of the room had changed: not merely warm and close, but steeped in that faint, sick sweetness of exhausted breath and cooling tea, of linen that has been turned often and hands that have been washed often. There were the small sounds—so few and so sharp they seemed to cut the silence: the faint click of a cup set down, the brush of a dress, the delicate hush with which Dixon moved like a creature trained to respect pain.

Mrs. Thornton, strong with health, with her firm step and full pulse, felt the room take the strength out of her as a wind takes heat from a coal. Mrs. Hale did not open her eyes at once. Yet her face, so still, showed consciousness; and when she at last looked up, heavy tears lay on her eyelashes like dew.

Her hand groped weakly over the bedclothes, searching—blindly—for something steady. Mrs. Thornton, almost against her will, put her own large, warm fingers where they were sought. Mrs. Hale’s touch was light, like a bird resting.

“Margaret—you have a daughter—my sister is in Italy,” she whispered, words broken and thin as threads. “My child will be without a mother;—in a strange place,—if I die—will you—”

She could not finish. But her eyes fixed themselves on Mrs. Thornton’s face with such desperate entreaty that even that stern countenance could not evade it.

For a minute Mrs. Thornton’s features remained hard. A cloud crossed them—swift, dark. Not for her son, not for her living daughter, did her heart loosen; but for a ghost. Something in the room—the careful ordering, the mother’s helplessness—brought back, as sudden as a smell carried on heat, the memory of her own child long dead in infancy, that had once lain so small in her arms. The recollection melted the ice in her, though no softness appeared in her voice.

“You wish me to be a friend to Miss Hale,” she said, measured and distinct.

Mrs. Hale pressed her hand, unable to speak.

“I will be a true friend, if circumstances require it,” Mrs. Thornton continued, as if weighing each word on a scale. “Not a tender friend. That I cannot be. It is not my nature to show affection even where I feel it; nor do I volunteer advice in general. Still—at your request—if it will be any comfort to you, I will promise you.”

It was as if she were signing a bond.

“I promise,” she said again, with grave severity, “that in any difficulty in which Miss Hale—”

“Call her Margaret!” gasped Mrs. Hale, a small passionate insistence, like the last assertion of a mother’s right.

“In which she comes to me for help,” Mrs. Thornton amended, “I will help her with every power I have, as if she were my own daughter. I also promise that if ever I see her doing what I think is wrong—”

“But Margaret never does wrong—not wilfully wrong,” pleaded Mrs. Hale, her breath fluttering with the effort.

Mrs. Thornton went on as if she had not heard; there was something merciless in her honesty, something that made it feel more secure than kindness.

“If ever I see her doing what I believe to be wrong—such wrong not touching me or mine, in which case I might be supposed to have an interested motive—I will tell her of it faithfully and plainly, as I should wish my own daughter to be told.”

The pause that followed was long and full. Mrs. Hale’s mind, weakened, caught at the promise and yet felt the spaces within it—the reservations she could not name. Still it steadied her, as a hard chair steadies a fainting person better than a soft pillow.

“I thank you,” she murmured at last. “I pray God to bless you. I shall never see you again in this world. But my last words are, I thank you for your promise of kindness to my child.”

“Not kindness,” Mrs. Thornton replied—truthful even there, even then.

Perhaps the words were not heard; perhaps mercy lay in the dying woman’s failure to take them in. Mrs. Thornton pressed the languid hand once more and left without seeing anyone, as if she feared that one other human face might demand some softness she had not the habit of paying out.

While this took place upstairs, Margaret and Dixon were below, heads bent together like conspirators over a kitchen table that smelled faintly of yesterday’s bread and the clean sharpness of soap.

Frederick might come any day. A letter might arrive—might already be on its way—but he would follow quickly, letter or no letter; Margaret’s heart had known that with the surety of hunger. Yet his coming must be hidden as if it were a crime.

Martha must be sent away at once on her holiday; Dixon must keep stern guard at the front door, and admit only the few visitors who ever came—and those to Mr. Hale’s room downstairs, for Mrs. Hale’s worsening gave excuse enough. If Mary Higgins was called in to help in the kitchen, she must hear and see as little as possible; Frederick, if named at all, should be “Mr. Dickinson.” But Mary’s own slow incuriosity was their best lock and key.

They resolved Martha should go that afternoon. Margaret wished, with a pang, that it had been done the day before; it might seem strange to grant a holiday when her mistress lay so ill. Yet necessity presses its own logic upon the scruples of the tender-hearted.

All that afternoon Margaret had to be strong on borrowed strength. Mr. Hale, broken by fear, swung between hope and despair as a pendulum swings between two truths. In the spaces between pain, he would persuade himself the worst was over—that the respite meant recovery. Then, when the paroxysms returned, each more cruel than the last, they were fresh agonies, sharpened by disappointment.

He could not bear his study; he sat in the drawing-room, head buried in his folded arms on the table, as if he could hide from grief by not looking at it. Margaret sat by the window watching the lamps bloom one by one in the street, though she saw nothing but darkness thickened by her own thoughts. The house seemed to have drawn in its breath and held it. Dixon was with Mrs. Hale. Martha had gone. No one moved to fetch candles; dusk gathered like soot.

Margaret did not like to leave her father even to light the house, fearing that if her presence were withdrawn he might break into some violence of sorrow with no one to steady him. Yet she was thinking she must at least see to the kitchen fire—there was no one else—when the bell rang.

It was not loud, but it was pulled hard; the wires jingled through the house with a shaken, nervous music. That sound—so small, and yet so startling in the hush—seemed to strike into her bones. She rose, passed her father—who did not stir—and came back to kiss him, tenderly, on the temple. He neither moved nor seemed aware. Then she went down through the dark, her hand on the banister, feeling her way like a blind person.

At the door, the street’s light made a pale rectangle on the floor. A tall figure stood between her and that light. Dixon would have put the chain on before opening; but Margaret’s mind, crowded with grief and plans, had no room left for fear.

The man turned at the sound of the latch.

“Is this Mr. Hale’s?” he asked. The voice was clear and full and delicate, and something in it—some remembered cadence—made Margaret’s body shake.

She could not answer at once. Her breath caught; then the name broke out of her as if it had been waiting behind her teeth all these years.

“Frederick!”

She stretched out both hands to seize his; to draw him in; to make him real.

“Oh, Margaret!” he said, and after they kissed, he held her by the shoulders, keeping her a little away as if even in that dim hall he could read the truth in her face faster than she could speak it.

“My mother! Is she alive?”

“Yes,” Margaret whispered, and the word seemed to taste like prayer. “She is alive, dear, dear brother! She—as ill as she can be she is; but alive! She is alive!”

“Thank God,” he said, with a breath that trembled.

“Papa is utterly prostrate with this great grief.”

“You expect me, don’t you?”

“No. We have had no letter.”

“Then I have come before it. But my mother knows I am coming.”

“Oh! we all knew you would come.” She took his hand again, as if she must keep hold of him. “But wait a little. Step in here. Give me your hand. What is this?—your carpet-bag. Dixon has shut the shutters; but this is papa’s study. I can take you to a chair to rest for a few minutes while I go and tell him.”

She groped for the taper and the lucifer matches. When the small flame sprang up, the first thin light made them suddenly visible to each other; and she felt shy, foolishly, as if they were strangers. She saw his face was darker than she remembered, the complexion swarthy as if the sun of other seas had baked it into him; and his eyes—long-cut, blue, and oddly bright—lifted to meet hers with a quick, amused consciousness that they were both, in that instant, taking stock. Their glance held a moment of kinship that needed no words.

Margaret’s heart felt lighter even as she ran upstairs; the sorrow remained, but it was no longer solitary. She could face her father now with a new tool in her hands—the spell of good news.

He was still as she had left him, bowed over the table like a broken man.

“Papa,” she said, and put her arms round his neck, lifting his weary head with a gentle insistence until it rested against her, and she could make him look into her eyes. “Papa! Guess who is here!”

He stared at her. The idea flickered across his face—hope and disbelief in the same breath—and then was rejected as a madness.

He threw himself forward again, hiding his face. Margaret bent close and heard the whisper, raw with fear.

“I don’t know. Don’t tell me it is Frederick—not Frederick. I cannot bear it—I am too weak. And his mother is dying!”

And he began to cry aloud, like a child—wailing, helpless. It was so unlike what Margaret had imagined—so far from the joy she had wanted to give him—that a sickness of disappointment rose in her throat. For a moment she could not speak. Then she changed her tone, made it careful, as if handling glass.

“Papa, it is Frederick. Think of mamma—how glad she will be! And for her sake, how glad we ought to be. For his sake, too—our poor, poor boy!”

Her father did not lift his head, but she felt him trying to understand the fact, as one feels someone in the dark reaching for a wall.

“Where is he?” he asked at last, face still hidden.

“In your study. Quite alone. I lighted the taper and ran up to tell you. He is quite alone, and will be wondering why—”

“I will go to him,” Mr. Hale broke in, and rose with a sudden desperate purpose. He leaned on Margaret’s arm, not as on a daughter’s, but as on a guide’s.

She led him to the study door; but her own spirits, so strained, so shaken by relief, could not bear the sight of the meeting. She turned away and ran upstairs, and cried, sobbing as if her very body had been waiting for permission to break. For days she had not dared to yield; now the tears were like water let out of a sluice. Frederick was here—her one precious brother—safe among them again. She stopped suddenly, almost frightened by the ease of believing it. Had she dreamt?

She opened her door and listened. No voices reached her. She went down, softly, and stood by the study door. Then she heard the low buzz of two voices together, and that was enough. She fled to the kitchen with the same restless need to do something with her hands.

She stirred up the fire; she set coals; she coaxed a flame as carefully as if it were life itself. She lit the house, room by room, candle by candle, and the smell of warm tallow and fresh struck match-heads threaded through the earlier scent of grief. She began preparing something for Frederick: not merely sustenance, but welcome—bread and butter cut thick, tea strong enough to taste through travel-weariness, whatever the cupboard could offer. Even in that mourning house, she could not help noticing how hunger made its own clean demand, how the body insists. The very act of spreading butter—firm, pale, and cool—over bread that yielded softly under the knife gave her a steadiness no reasoning could.

How fortunate her mother slept. Margaret knew it from the thin line of candlelight thrust under Mrs. Hale’s door, and from Dixon’s hushed movements beyond. Let Frederick be warmed and fed; let the first sharpness of meeting his father pass; let their hearts settle before the greater tremor of a dying mother’s joy.

When all was ready, Margaret opened the study door and entered like a serving-maid, arms extended with a heavy tray. She was proud—fiercely, tenderly proud—of serving him.

But Frederick sprang up at once and took the tray from her hands, as if the gesture were a promise: you shall not carry this alone now.

They arranged the little meal together. They spoke little; their fingers brushed; their eyes did the talking in that swift, intimate language of blood that needs no translation. The fire in the study had gone out, leaving a cold, dead smell of ash. Margaret knelt to lay kindling, trying to do it quietly, because every noise seemed a potential cruelty to the sick room above.

“Dixon says it is a gift to light a fire,” she said softly, “not an art to be acquired.”

“Poeta nascitur, non fit,” murmured Mr. Hale; languid, yes, but the old habit of quotation was a sound from former life, and Margaret felt a strange comfort in it—the house remembering its own voice for an instant.

“Dear old Dixon!” Frederick said, with a sudden brightness. “How we shall kiss each other! She used to kiss me, and then look in my face to be sure I was the right person, and then set to again. But, Margaret—what a bungler you are! I never saw such a little awkward good-for-nothing pair of hands. Run away and wash them, ready to cut bread-and-butter for me, and leave the fire. I’ll manage it. Lighting fires is one of my natural accomplishments.”

So Margaret went, and washed her hands, and came back; and passed in and out of the room in a glad restlessness that could not be satisfied with sitting still. The more he wanted—tea, bread, more coal—the more pleased she was. He understood without asking. The joy was stolen in the house of mourning, and therefore tasted sharper, like a mouthful of something sweet when one has been living on bitterness. Beneath it lay the knowledge of what was coming, like a heavy pan set on the back of the stove, warming slowly.

In the middle of this, they heard Dixon’s foot on the stairs.

Mr. Hale started from his dreamy watching, and a strange anxiety seized him—an instinctive need to conceal Frederick from any entering eyes, even Dixon’s faithful ones. He stood and faced the door as if someone hostile might burst in. Margaret’s heart shivered. A new fear had entered their lives, and it had a sound: it made every tread on the stairs ring like threat.

She caught Frederick’s arm and held it tight, her jaw set with a sternness that surprised even herself. Yet they knew the step: measured, heavy, familiar. They heard Dixon walk the length of the passage—then into the kitchen.

Margaret rose.

“I will go to her, and tell her,” she said. “And I shall hear how mamma is.”

Mrs. Hale was awake. She rambled at first, the mind wandering like a hand searching in darkness. They gave her tea—carefully, with the spoon against the cup kept from clinking, as if even that tiny metallic note might hurt. The warmth revived her a little, though she did not care to speak. It seemed best that the night should pass before she was told Frederick had come. Dr. Donaldson’s visit later would bring excitement enough; perhaps he would advise how to prepare her for seeing her son. Frederick was in the house; that knowledge was a kind of medicine, ready to be administered at the right moment.

Margaret could not be still. It relieved her to help Dixon with every preparation for “Master Frederick.” In the kitchen the fire made its own living noise: coal shifting, a soft roar when the draught caught, the kettle beginning its low prelude. Against the remembered clatter of Milton streets and mill-bells, these domestic sounds were a refuge—an older music, one that held the mind together instead of tearing it into nervous fragments. Margaret thought, with sudden bitterness, how the town’s industrial din had trained them all to start at every sharp sound, as if danger came always with noise; and how in this sick-house they were fighting for quiet as for breath.

Each glimpse she caught into the room where Frederick sat with her father—talking, she knew not what, nor cared—gave her strength. Her time for listening and speaking would come; she was certain of that. Meanwhile she took in her brother’s appearance and liked it. The delicacy of his features was redeemed from any softness by the swarthiness of his skin, by the quick intensity that crossed his mouth and eyes. Most often he looked merry; but at moments, in a flash, his expression hardened into something fierce—latent passion surfacing like flame when the lid is lifted from a pot. It was not dogged, not vindictive; rather that instantaneous wildness one sees in children of southern climates, a heat that vanishes as quickly as it appears. It made her a little afraid of the violence of his impulses, but it did not make her recoil. She trusted him at once; and their intercourse, from the beginning, was charming.

He understood their father and mother with an ease Margaret had forgotten was possible—he knew their weaknesses and their habits of pain, and yet moved among them with a careless freedom that never bruised. He seemed to know, without being told, when his natural brilliancy might jar, and when it might soothe. When gaiety would be cruelty, he became patient and watchful, an admirable nurse. He knew how to hold a cup, how to move a chair without scraping it, how to quiet his very breath. That kind of care is its own skill, learned not from books but from love.

And he spoke often of their childish days in the New Forest—of Helstone as if he had carried its green hush in his pocket all these years, taking it out at need, like a charm. He had not forgotten her; he had not forgotten the place. Margaret could talk to him of it without fear of wearying him, and the old names tasted in her mouth like something homely and good—words from a dialect of the heart that no one else in Milton spoke.

She had been afraid of him before he came. Seven or eight years had changed her so much; she had imagined his wandering life must have substituted another Frederick for the tall stripling in his middy’s uniform. But absence had oddly made them nearer. They met now not as child and grown brother, but almost as equals—two people bearing the same sorrow, the same secret, the same longing.

So the weight lifted a little from her. Other light she had none.

For a few hours after Mrs. Hale was told, she rallied on seeing her son. She sat with his hand in hers and would not let it go even when sleep came. When he tried to move, she tightened her faint fingers with surprising strength. Margaret had to feed him like a baby—lifting bread to his mouth—rather than he should disturb their mother by withdrawing even a finger. Mrs. Hale woke while they were thus engaged; she slowly turned her head on the pillow and smiled, understanding at once, as mothers do.

“I am very selfish,” she said. “But it will not be for long.”

Frederick bent and kissed the feeble hand that held him prisoner, as if he were grateful for the bondage.

This tranquillity could not last. Dr. Donaldson told Margaret so plainly. Not many days—perhaps not many hours. His voice, kind as it was, carried the hard discipline of truth.

After he had gone, Margaret stole down to Frederick. During the visit he had been commanded to remain concealed in the back parlour—Dixon’s room usually, now given up to him like a hiding-place in a house that had become a fortress.

Margaret repeated what the doctor had said.

“I don’t believe it,” Frederick cried. “She is very ill; she may be dangerously ill, in immediate danger too; but I can’t imagine she could be as she is if she were on the point of death. Margaret! She should have other advice—some London doctor. Have you never thought of that?”

“Yes,” Margaret answered. “More than once. But I don’t believe it would do any good. And we have not the money to bring any great London surgeon down. Besides, I am sure Dr. Donaldson is only second in skill to the very best—if, indeed, he is second to them.”

Frederick began to pace, impatience in every step, like a man walking the quarter-deck while storms gather.

“I have credit in Cadiz,” he said, “but none here, owing to this wretched change of name. Why did my father leave Helstone? That was the blunder.”

“It was no blunder,” Margaret said gloomily. “And above all possible chances, avoid letting papa hear anything like what you have just been saying. I can see he is tormenting himself already with the idea that mamma would never have been ill if we had stayed at Helstone—and you don’t know papa’s agonising power of self-reproach.”

Frederick walked away, then stopped sharply and faced her. He looked at her drooping posture, the wetness on her cheeks she had not known she was showing.

“My little Margaret,” he said, and drew her to him with a tenderness that made her feel, for the first time in days, not solely responsible. “Let us hope as long as we can. Poor little woman! What—is this face all wet with tears? I will hope. I will, in spite of a thousand doctors. Bear up, Margaret, and be brave enough to hope!”

She tried to answer, but her voice choked. When words came they were low.

“I must try to be meek enough to trust. Oh, Frederick! mamma was getting to love me so. And I was getting to understand her. And now comes death to snap us asunder!”

“Come, come, come!” Frederick said, as if briskness could beat back fate for an hour. “Let us go upstairs and do something, rather than waste time that may be so precious. Thinking has, many a time, made me sad, darling; but doing never did in all my life. My theory is a sort of parody on the maxim of ‘Get money, my son, honestly if you can; but get money.’ My precept is: ‘Do something, my sister; do good if you can; but, at any rate, do something.’”

“Not excluding mischief,” Margaret said, and a faint smile—thin as a slice of bread in famine, yet real—trembled through her tears.
