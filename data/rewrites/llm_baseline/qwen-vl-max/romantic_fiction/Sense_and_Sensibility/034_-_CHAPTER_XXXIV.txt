Mrs. John Dashwood, trusting fully in her husband’s discernment, called upon Mrs. Jennings and her daughter the very next day. Her confidence was soon rewarded: she found Mrs. Jennings, though not particularly refined, quite tolerable—and Lady Middleton, she declared, was one of the most delightful women she had ever met. There was a quiet, unfeeling sort of self-absorption that bound them together, a shared preference for polished manners without depth, and a mutual appreciation for the absence of real intellect. Their mutual admiration was sincere, though born more of similarity than warmth.

But while Lady Middleton welcomed Mrs. Dashwood with open arms, Mrs. Jennings saw only a stiff, aloof woman—proud in bearing, cold in greeting—whose visit to Berkeley Street had been marked by nearly eight minutes of silence. She gave no sign of affection toward her sisters-in-law, and seemed entirely at ease in her own reserve.

Elinor longed to know whether Edward was in town, though she hesitated to ask. Fanny Dashwood, however, would never have spoken his name freely—she waited until she could announce his engagement to Miss Morton or until her husband’s hopes regarding Colonel Brandon were fulfilled. She feared their lingering attachment, and so kept them apart, word and deed alike, with careful discretion.

Yet the news came from another source. Lucy Steele arrived shortly afterward, pleading Elinor’s sympathy: Edward had reached London, but dared not come to Bartlett’s Buildings for fear of discovery. Though their longing to see each other was intense, they could do little more than write. And within days, Edward himself confirmed his presence—twice he called on the Dashwoods, leaving his card behind when they returned from their morning walks. Elinor was glad he had come; yet even gladder that she had missed him.

The Dashwoods, enchanted by the Middletons, decided to host a dinner party—a rare gesture for such reserved people. They invited the Middletons to dine at their new home in Harley Street, a comfortable house rented for three months. Mrs. Jennings and the Miss Dashwoods were included, and John Dashwood took care to invite Colonel Brandon as well. The colonel, always drawn to the Miss Dashwoods, accepted with quiet pleasure, though surprised by the warmth of his welcome.

They were to meet Mrs. Ferrars—the mother of Edward’s betrothed. Elinor did not know if her sons would attend, but the mere thought of seeing Mrs. Ferrars stirred her curiosity. Once, she might have trembled at such an encounter, fearing judgment, disapproval, or rejection. But now, she faced it with calm resolve. Her feelings for Edward were unchanged, yet her anxiety about others’ opinions had faded. Still, she longed to see the woman who had shaped his life—to understand her character, her expectations.

That longing grew sharper when she learned the Miss Steeles would also be there. Lucy, especially, had made herself agreeable to Lady Middleton, whose favor she now enjoyed. Though neither sister was elegant—Lucy lacking grace, her sister barely genteel—they had won a place at the table through their tireless attentions. It suited the Miss Steeles perfectly; they could begin their visit just before the dinner, and Lucy, who had long wished to observe the Dashwoods up close, was overjoyed at receiving Mrs. Dashwood’s invitation.

For Elinor, the news was distressing. If Edward were to attend, as his mother surely would, then she would see him again—after everything—surrounded by Lucy. The thought filled her with dread. Yet her fears were eased not by reason, but by Lucy’s kindness. When Lucy confessed Edward would not be at the dinner, and even suggested he stayed away because of his deep devotion to her, Elinor felt a strange relief. She almost believed it.

The day arrived—Tuesday, the day of reckoning. As they ascended the stairs together, Lucy clutched Elinor’s arm. “Pity me, dear Miss Dashwood!” she whispered. “There is no one here but you who truly understands how I feel. I can hardly stand. In a moment, I shall see the woman whose approval will make or break my happiness—my future mother!”

Elinor could have offered comfort by pointing out that the woman might just as easily be Miss Morton’s mother. But instead, she said sincerely, “I do pity you.” Lucy stared, astonished. She had expected envy, not sympathy—though she herself was trembling with nerves.

Mrs. Ferrars was small and thin, standing upright with rigid formality. Her face was serious, even sour, her complexion pale and sallow. Her features were plain, devoid of beauty or expression—yet a constant frown had carved lines of pride and ill temper into her brow. She spoke little, her words few and measured, as if carefully rationed. And when she did speak, none of them were directed to Elinor, whom she regarded with unmistakable disdain.

But Elinor was no longer hurt by this. A few months ago, such treatment would have wounded her deeply. Now, it meant nothing. She watched with quiet amusement as Mrs. Ferrars and Lady Middleton lavished attention on Lucy—on *her*, the girl whose claim to Edward was known only to Elinor. The contrast was painful, yet revealing. The older women smiled and praised Lucy as if she were a jewel, while Elinor sat unnoticed, ignored, yet untouched by sorrow.

And as she observed the scene, she despised them all—the insincerity, the manipulation, the petty vanity. Lucy exulted in her favor; Miss Steele, content in her own way, only wanted someone to tease about Dr. Davies. The dinner itself was grand—lavish servants, fine china, and every detail designed to show off wealth and status. Despite the Dashwoods’ recent financial strains, no trace of poverty showed, except perhaps in conversation. John Dashwood had little to say worth hearing, and Fanny even less. But then, most of the guests suffered from similar shortcomings—lacking wit, elegance, spirit, or good temper.

After dinner, the ladies retired to the drawing room. The men had carried the conversation with talk of politics, land enclosures, and horse training—but once they left, the topic narrowed sharply: the height of Harry Dashwood versus Lady Middleton’s younger son, William. Both boys were roughly the same age, but only Harry was present. So the debate became pure speculation, repeated endlessly by all.

The mothers politely claimed the other’s child was taller. The grandmothers, more openly biased, defended their own descendants with fierce conviction. Lucy, eager to please both sides, insisted both boys were remarkably tall and couldn’t imagine any difference at all. Miss Steele, with even greater finesse, praised each in turn, depending on who was listening.

Elinor, having once sided with William, offended both Mrs. Ferrars and Fanny. She did not repeat her opinion. Marianne, when asked, refused to give one—“I’ve never thought about it,” she said plainly—offending everyone further.

Earlier, Elinor had painted a pair of screens for her sister-in-law, which were now mounted and displayed in the drawing room. As the gentlemen entered, John Dashwood noticed them and, with exaggerated politeness, handed them to Colonel Brandon. “These are done by my eldest sister,” he said. “You, as a man of taste, will surely appreciate them. I don’t know if you’ve seen her work before, but she’s generally considered an excellent painter.”

Colonel Brandon, though modest about his own judgment, admired them warmly—as he would anything created by Elinor. The others, naturally curious, passed the screens around. Mrs. Ferrars, unaware of their origin, requested to see them. After Lady Middleton praised them, Fanny presented them to her mother, tactfully noting, “These were painted by Miss Dashwood.”

“Hum,” Mrs. Ferrars replied. “Very pretty.” And without looking at them again, she handed them back.

Fanny flushed slightly, perhaps thinking her mother had been too dismissive. “They are very pretty, ma’am—are they not?” she added quickly.

Then, perhaps fearing she’d been too kind, she continued, “Do you not think they resemble Miss Morton’s style? She paints most delightfully! Her last landscape—how beautifully it was done!”

“Beautifully indeed,” Mrs. Ferrars agreed. “But *she* does everything well.”

Marianne could not bear it. Already annoyed by Mrs. Ferrars’s coldness, she was enraged by this comparison—this false praise, offered at Elinor’s expense. Without hesitation, she stepped forward. “This is admiration of a very particular kind!” she exclaimed. “What is Miss Morton to us? Who knows her? Who cares? It is Elinor we think of—Elinor we speak of!”

She took the screens from Fanny’s hands and held them close, admiring them as they deserved to be admired.

Mrs. Ferrars turned sharp and angry. “Miss Morton is Lord Morton’s daughter,” she snapped.

Fanny looked furious. John Dashwood paled, horrified by his sister’s boldness.

Elinor was more distressed by Marianne’s outburst than by the insult that provoked it. But Colonel Brandon’s eyes, fixed on Marianne, revealed only tenderness—admiration for the love and loyalty that could not endure seeing her sister slighted.

Marianne did not stop. The cold arrogance of Mrs. Ferrars’s behavior—her indifference to Elinor—spoke of future pain, of struggles Elinor might face. Overcome by emotion, Marianne moved to her sister’s side, wrapped an arm around her neck, pressed her cheek to hers, and whispered fiercely, “Dear, dear Elinor—don’t mind them. Don’t let them make you unhappy.”

She could say no more. Her voice broke, and hiding her face on Elinor’s shoulder, she wept.

Everyone turned. Colonel Brandon rose instinctively. Mrs. Jennings offered her salts with a knowing “Ah! Poor dear!” Sir John, incensed, moved beside Lucy Steele and whispered a full account of the emotional scene.

In minutes, Marianne recovered enough to sit quietly among the others. But the evening’s mood remained heavy—her spirits still shaken by what had passed.

Later, John Dashwood leaned toward Colonel Brandon, speaking softly. “Poor Marianne,” he murmured. “She doesn’t have Elinor’s strength. She’s very sensitive, very nervous. You wouldn’t think it, but she was once remarkably beautiful—just as lovely as Elinor. Now… well, you can see it’s all gone.”
