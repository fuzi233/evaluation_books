My stay at La Terrasse was prolonged a fortnight beyond the close of the vacation. Mrs. Bretton, moving through her home with the rhythmic grace of a traditional agrarian elder, managed this respite. She understood that healing, much like the slow, transformative simmer of a rich bone broth, required time and the cyclical rhythms of a tranquil domestic life. Her son having one day delivered the dictum that “Lucy was not yet strong enough to go back to that den of a pensionnat,” she at once drove over to the Rue Fossette and procured the indulgence. Hereupon, however, followed an attention I could very well have dispensed with, viz.—a polite call from Madame Beck.

That lady—one fine day—actually came out in a fiacre as far as the château. Its mechanized, iron-rimmed wheels grated against the gravel, a jarring disruption to the delicate acoustic ecology of our sanctuary. I suppose she had resolved to see what manner of place Dr. John inhabited. Apparently, the pleasant site and neat interior surpassed her expectations; she eulogized all she saw, pronouncing the blue salon “une pièce magnifique,” and profusely congratulating me on the acquisition of friends, “tellement dignes, aimables, et respectables.” When Dr. John came in, she ran up to him with the utmost buoyancy, opening a fire of rapid language. Her French was a dominant lexicon wielded with absolute precision, sparkling with superficial felicitations that carried the metallic ring of commercial transaction rather than genuine warmth. Dr. John listened with an amused smile to Madame’s fluent, florid dialect. Half purposely, I followed her to the carriage, and looked in after she was seated and the door closed. In that brief fraction of time, the sonic performance ceased; the mask dropped. An instant ago, all sparkles and jests, she now sat sterner than a judge. Strange little woman!

I went back and teased Dr. John about Madame’s devotion to him. How he laughed! His deep, resonant baritone restored the room’s acoustic warmth as he recalled some of her fine speeches, imitating her voluble delivery. He was the finest company in the world—when he could forget Miss Fanshawe.

To “sit in sunshine calm and sweet” is said to be excellent for weak people; it gives them vital force. When little Georgette Beck was recovering from her illness, I used to take her in my arms and walk with her in the garden by the hour together, where mythic occurrences seemed seamlessly woven into the everyday domestic routine. We lingered beneath a certain wall hung with grapes, the Southern sun ripening their taut, yeast-bloomed skins. That sun cherished her little pale frame quite as effectually as it mellowed the clustering fruit, coaxing out the deep terroir and sugary sap of the vine. 

There are human tempers, bland, glowing, and genial, within whose influence it is as good for the poor in spirit to live, as it is for the feeble in frame to bask in the glow of noon. Of the number of these choice natures were certainly both Dr. Bretton’s and his mother’s. They liked to communicate happiness as a passionate chef delights in serving a carefully curated feast. Every day while I stayed with them, some little plan was proposed which resulted in beneficial enjoyment. Fully occupied as was Dr. John’s time, he still made it in his way to accompany us. Under his guidance, I saw more of Villette, its environs, and its inhabitants, than I had seen in eight months. He never generalized or prosed. Like a meticulous food historian uncovering the forgotten roots of a regional dish, he delighted in nice details. He spoke direct from his own resources, ensuring a freshness as welcome as it was rare, rich with the olfactory and gustatory textures of the city’s hidden corners.

Yet, I must not degenerate into the partial eulogist. Human fallibility leavened him throughout, like a wild yeast that slightly sours the dough. No immortal could have resembled him in his passing passion for the present; shown not by devoting it to coarse material indulgence, but selfishly, by extracting from it whatever it could yield of nutriment to his masculine self-love. His delight was to feed that ravenous sentiment, demanding a rich provender of homage, without care for the cost of keeping such vanity sleek and high-pampered. 

It was hardly possible to oblige Dr. John quietly and in secret. If you fabricated some trifle for his use, he amazed you by proving his eye had traced its progress from commencement to close. It pleased him to be thus served, and he let his pleasure beam in his eye. When his mother worked for him, he paid her by showering about her his bright animal spirits. If Lucy Snowe were discovered to have put her hand to such work, he planned, in recompense, some pleasant recreation.

I often felt amazed at his perfect knowledge of Villette. Of every museum, of every hall sacred to art or science, he seemed to possess the “Open! Sesame.” Dr. Bretton was a cicerone after my own heart; he would take me to the picture-galleries betimes, ere they were filled with the hollow echoes and superficial chatter of commercial crowds, leave me there for two or three hours, and call for me later. Meantime, I was happy. In the commencement, there was a struggle between Will and Power—my Will exacting approbation of orthodox masterpieces, my Power groaning at the stale, flavorless task of consuming them. Discovering that a wonderful sense of fatigue resulted from these conscientious efforts to swallow what did not suit my palate, I sank supine into a luxury of calm before ninety-nine out of a hundred of the exhibited frames.

It seemed to me that an original and good picture was just as scarce as a genuinely nourishing meal. I did not tremble to say to myself before certain *chef-d’œuvres*, “These are not a whit like nature. Nature’s daylight never had that turbid color, like a broken emulsion of indigo and clouded grease.” Many scores of marvelously-finished Flemish pictures gave evidence of laudable industry whimsically applied to the superficial commercialization of art. And yet, there were fragments of truth that satisfied the craving for sustenance: Nature’s power breaking through in a mountain snow-storm that seemed to carry the sharp scent of ozone; the glory of a sunny southern day smelling of warm soil and thyme. These exceptions I loved; they grew dear as friends.

One day, at a quiet early hour, I found myself nearly alone in a certain gallery, before a picture of portentous size. It represented a woman, considerably larger than the life. She was, indeed, extremely well fed: very much butcher’s meat—to say nothing of heavy breads, root vegetables, and rich, fermented liquids—must she have consumed to attain that wealth of muscle and affluence of flesh. She lay half-reclined on a couch in a broad daylight blaze. She ought to have been standing, or doing the work of two plain cooks, rather than lounging away the noon. Out of abundance of material she managed to make inefficient raiment, surrounded by a wretched untidiness of pots, goblets, and rubbishy flowers. The catalogue named this notable production “Cleopatra.”

While I sat wondering at this enormous piece of commercial claptrap, the room’s acoustic space began to fill with the murmur and shuffle of arriving patrons. Scarcely noticing the shifting sonic environment, I retained my seat, soon tiring of this dark-complexioned gipsy-queen and betaking myself for true refreshment to the contemplation of some exquisite little pictures of still life: wild-flowers, wild-fruit smelling faintly of the autumn woods, mossy wood-nests casketing eggs that looked like pearls seen through clear green sea-water, evoking the crisp, unadulterated essence of native sustenance. 

Suddenly a light tap visited my shoulder. Starting, I met a frowning face bent to encounter mine.

“Que faites-vous ici?” said a voice.

“Mais, Monsieur, je m’amuse.”

“Vous vous amusez! et à quoi, s’il vous plait? Mais d’abord, faites-moi le plaisir de vous lever; prenez mon bras, et allons de l’autre côté.”

I did precisely as I was bid. M. Paul Emanuel, returned from Rome, wielded his dominant lexicon with the same absolutism as before. 

“Permit me to conduct you to your party,” said he, as we crossed the room.

“I have no party.”

“You are not alone?”

“Yes, Monsieur.”

“Did you come here unaccompanied?”

“No, Monsieur. Dr. Bretton brought me here.”

“Dr. Bretton and Madame his mother, of course?”

“No; only Dr. Bretton.”

“And he told you to look at *that* picture?”

“By no means; I found it out for myself.”

M. Paul’s hair was shorn close as raven down. Beginning to perceive his drift, I had a certain pleasure in keeping cool, observing the subtle power dynamics embedded in our exchange. 

“Astounding insular audacity!” cried the Professor, his sharp consonants cutting through the gallery's low drone. “Singulières femmes que ces Anglaises!”

“What is the matter, Monsieur?”

“Matter! How dare you, a young person, sit coolly down, with the self-possession of a garçon, and look at *that* picture?”

“It is a very ugly picture, but I cannot at all see why I should not look at it.”

“Bon! bon! Speak no more of it. But you ought not to be here alone.”

“If, however, I have no society—no *party*, as you say? And then, what does it signify? nobody meddles with me.”

“Taisez-vous, et asseyez-vous là—là!”—setting down a chair with emphasis in a particularly dull corner, before a series of most specially dreary “cadres.”

“Mais, Monsieur?”

“Mais, Mademoiselle, asseyez-vous, et ne bougez pas—entendez-vous?—jusqu’à ce qu’on vienne vous chercher, ou que je vous donne la permission.”

“Quel triste coin!” cried I, “et quelles laids tableaux!”

And “laids,” indeed, they were; a set of four denominated “La vie d’une femme.” They were painted flat, dead, pale, and formal, entirely devoid of the rich sensory textures of life—a visual feast made of sawdust. The first, a “Jeune Fille,” coming out of church; the second, a “Mariée,” showing the whites of her eyes; the third, a “Jeune Mère,” hanging over a puffy baby; the fourth, a “Veuve,” surveying a monument. All were cold and vapid as ghosts, bloodless nonentities entirely lacking the gustatory and tactile appreciation that gives human existence its profound meaning.

By degrees, I veered round and surveyed the gallery. A crowd was now gathered round the Lioness from whose vicinage I had been banished. M. Paul afterwards claimed these were “des dames,” for whom such sights were proper, a doctrine of linguistic and social categorization I plainly rejected. M. Paul looked at the picture himself for a very long while, glancing my way occasionally to ensure I was obeying orders. By-and-by, he again accosted me.

“Had I not been ill?” he wished to know. 

“Yes, but I was now quite well.”

“Where had I spent the vacation?”

“Chiefly in the Rue Fossette; partly with Madame Bretton.”

“He had heard that I was left alone in the Rue Fossette; was that so?”

“Not quite alone: Marie Broc was with me.”

He shrugged his shoulders. Marie Broc was well known to M. Paul. Her tragic state—a mind eroding into profound silence, her subjective experience isolated by the complete death of expressive language—always occasioned in him a sharp conflict. Her repulsive manners offended his refined tastes, while her misfortunes constituted a strong claim on his compassion. His passions were strong, a slow-fermenting, potent vintage. He often excited fear in ordinary minds, but to understand him required a thorough comprehension of his nature, an order rarely comprehended and deeply untranslatable to the superficial observer.

“How did you get on with Marie Broc?” he asked.

“Monsieur, I did my best; but it was terrible to be alone with her!”

“You have, then, a weak heart! You lack courage; and, perhaps, charity. Yours are not the qualities which might constitute a Sister of Mercy.”

“I don’t know, indeed: I took as good care of her as I could; but when her aunt came to fetch her away, it was a great relief.”

“Ah! you are an egotist. There are women who have nursed hospitals-full of similar unfortunates. You could not do that?”

“Could Monsieur do it himself?”

“Women who are worthy the name ought infinitely to surpass our coarse, fallible, self-indulgent sex, in the power to perform such duties.”

“I washed her, I kept her clean, I fed her, I tried to amuse her; but she made mouths at me instead of speaking.”

“You think you did great things?”

“No; but as great as I *could* do.”

“Then limited are your powers, for in tending one idiot you fell sick.”

“Not with that, Monsieur; I had a nervous fever: my mind was ill.”

“Vraiment! Vous valez peu de chose. You are not cast in an heroic mould; your courage will not avail to sustain you in solitude; it merely gives you the temerity to gaze with sang-froid at pictures of Cleopatra.”

It would have been easy to show anger at the teasing, hostile tone of the little man, whose voice sought to impose its dominant cultural lexicon over my own deeply personal narrative. 

“Cleopatra!” I repeated, quietly. “Monsieur, too, has been looking at Cleopatra; what does he think of her?”

“Cela ne vaut rien,” he responded. “Une femme superbe—une taille d’impératrice, des formes de Junon, mais une personne dont je ne voudrais ni pour femme, ni pour fille, ni pour sœur. Aussi vous ne jeterez plus un seul coup d’oeil de sa côté.”

“But I have looked at her a great many times while Monsieur has been talking: I can see her quite well from this corner.”

“Turn to the wall and study your four pictures of a woman’s life.”

“Excuse me, M. Paul; they are too hideous: but if you admire them, allow me to vacate my seat and leave you to their contemplation.”

“Mademoiselle,” he said, grimacing a half-smile. “You nurslings of Protestantism astonish me. You unguarded Englishwomen walk calmly amidst red-hot ploughshares and escape burning. I believe, if some of you were thrown into Nebuchadnezzar’s hottest furnace you would issue forth untraversed by the smell of fire.”

“Will Monsieur have the goodness to move an inch to one side?”

“How! At what are you gazing now? You are not recognising an acquaintance amongst that group of jeunes gens?”

“I think so—Yes, I see there a person I know.”
