Margaret had scarcely been gone five minutes when Mr. Thornton came in, his whole face lit as if he had been running against a wind that stung and brightened the blood. The dining-room smelt faintly of bruised lavender and the sharp, clean ghost of eau de Cologne, as if the air itself had been wiped and polished after violence.

“I could not come sooner: the superintendent would—” His words broke off. He looked round, first at the place where she had sat, then—almost with a kind of fierceness—at his mother, who was setting chairs straight again, turning a displaced table-napkin as if the room were a dish to be re-laid after an accident. “Where is she?”

Mrs. Thornton did not answer at once. She folded the napkin with measured exactness, as one might fold a cloth over cooling bread to keep it from taint.

“Where is Miss Hale?” he asked again.

“Gone home,” said she, curtly.

“Gone home!”

“Yes. She was a great deal better. Indeed, I don’t believe it was so very much of a hurt; only some people faint at the least thing.”

“I am sorry she has gone home,” he said, and began to pace—an uneasy, caged movement, as if the walls kept time with his strides. “She could not have been fit for it.”

“She said she was; and Mr. Lowe said she was. I went for him myself.”

“Thank you, mother.” He stopped and half held out his hand, the impulse frank and grateful; but she did not see it—or would not.

“What have you done with your Irish people?” he asked, taking refuge in what could be spoken without that name making his throat close.

“Sent to the Dragon, for a good meal for them, poor wretches. And then, luckily, I caught Father Grady, and I’ve asked him in to speak to them, and dissuade them from going off in a body.”

Even in that brief statement there was the whole sound of a town: the clatter of plates at an inn, the murmur of a priest’s brogue, the damp shuffle of hungry feet made suddenly docile by broth and bread. Beyond it, like a constant mill-wheel in the mind, the day’s din returned—iron gates groaning, stones striking, a thousand throats made hoarse by anger. He forced his attention back.

“How did Miss Hale go home? I’m sure she could not walk.”

“She had a cab. Everything was done properly, even to the paying. Let us talk of something else. She has caused disturbance enough.”

“I don’t know where I should have been but for her.”

“Are you become so helpless as to have to be defended by a girl?” Mrs. Thornton’s contempt fell clipped and cold.

He reddened, not from shame but from a sudden heat of remembered touch, the shock of her arms round his neck—soft, clinging, and yet strong with purpose. “Not many girls would have taken the blows on herself which were meant for me;—meant with right down goodwill, too.”

“A girl in love will do a good deal,” replied Mrs. Thornton, briefly, as if she had tasted something too sweet and would not own it pleased her.

“Mother!” He stepped forward, then held himself still. The effort to master what rose in him made his breath thicken. His eyes shone; his whole frame seemed enlarged by conflicting forces—pride, anger, glad astonishment, a trembling doubt he hated. She could not read it; she only felt the danger of strong feeling unshared.

She went to the side-board, pulled out a drawer, and took up a duster kept there for chance blemishes. A drop of cologne had freckled the polished arm of the sofa; she rubbed at it as if she could wipe away the day’s disorder with the same small, brisk motions. She kept her back to him longer than needful; and when she spoke, her voice had a constraint that did not belong to her.

“You have taken some steps about the rioters, I suppose? You don’t apprehend any more violence, do you? Where were the police? Never at hand when they’re wanted!”

“On the contrary,” he answered, forcing his mind into the hard grooves of business, “I saw three or four of them, when the gates gave way, struggling and beating about in fine fashion; and more came running up just when the yard was clearing. I might have given some of the fellows in charge then, if I had had my wits about me. But there will be no difficulty; plenty of people can identify them.”

“But won’t they come back to-night?”

“I’m going to see about a sufficient guard for the premises. I have appointed to meet Captain Hanbury in half an hour at the station.”

“You must have some tea first.”

“Tea! Yes, I suppose I must.” He glanced at the clock as if time were a debtor. “It’s half-past six, and I may be out for some time. Don’t sit up for me, mother.”

“You expect me to go to bed before I have seen you safe, do you?”

“Well, perhaps not.” He hesitated; and then, as if the word had been waiting behind his teeth, it came. “But if I’ve time, I shall go round by Crampton, after I’ve arranged with the police and seen Hamper and Clarkson.”

Their eyes met—held. In that pause the room seemed to sharpen: the tick of the clock, the faint crackle of the coal, the far-off roar of Milton’s looms like a sea that never ceased. It was the town’s ceaseless sound that had driven men to fury in the streets; it was the same sound that now pressed on a private room, insisting that no heart should be left quiet.

“Why are you going round by Crampton?” Mrs. Thornton asked at last.

“I want to ask after Miss Hale.”

“I will send. Williams must take the water-bed she came to ask for. He shall inquire how she is.”

“I must go myself.”

“Not merely to ask how Miss Hale is?”

“No, not merely for that. I want to thank her for the way in which she stood between me and the mob.”

“What made you go down at all? It was putting your head into the lion’s mouth!”

He looked keenly at her, and saw she did not know what had passed between him and Margaret earlier—whatever might have been spoken upstairs, here it had not been told. He answered her question with another, not cowardly, but urgent with the press of duty.

“Shall you be afraid to be left without me, until I can get some of the police; or had we better send Williams for them now, and they could be here by the time we have done tea? There’s no time to be lost. I must be off in a quarter of an hour.”

Mrs. Thornton left the room. The servants, accustomed to her decisions falling as clean and sure as a knife through pastry, were surprised by the indistinctness of her orders now—directing, retracting, directing again.

Mr. Thornton stayed in the dining-room, trying to think of his business at the police-office—and thinking, instead, of Margaret. The world beyond her seemed blurred: gates, mob, superintendent, Captain Hanbury, all dim; and only one thing cruelly clear—the feel of her arms, the sudden shelter and surrender of that clasp. The colour rose and fell in his cheek as if it obeyed her touch still.

Tea came. It should have been a comfort—hot liquid, bread, the small domestic rites that promise stability—but it tasted of interruption. The cups clinked too loudly in the hush between them, and the coal’s whisper seemed an echo of the mill’s breath. Fanny filled the silence with her own recital, repeated and ornamented, like a cook who cannot stop describing the heat of a kitchen fire she has escaped: how she had been alarmed—and then thought they were gone—and then felt sick and faint and trembling in every limb.

“There, that’s enough,” said her brother at last, rising. “The reality was enough for me.”

He moved to leave, but his mother laid her hand upon his arm—light, yet arresting him as surely as a bar across a door.

“You will come back here before you go to the Hales’,” she said in a low, anxious voice.

“I know what I know,” thought Fanny to herself, with a mean triumph.

“Why? Will it be too late to disturb them?”

“John, come back to me for this one evening. It will be late for Mrs. Hale. But that is not it. To-morrow, you will—” Her voice faltered on what she could not bear to name. “Come back to-night, John!”

She seldom pleaded; pride had made supplication an unfamiliar language on her lips. But she had never pleaded in vain.

“I will return straight here after I have done my business.” He paused, as if the words pulled at him like a cord. “You will be sure to enquire after them?—after her?”

Mrs. Thornton did not answer directly; but her eyes hardened into the look that meant she would do what she chose, and that would be enough.

While he was away, she was neither talkative to Fanny nor a good listener; the girl’s chatter fell like crumbs on a table no one intends to clear. Yet when John returned, her attention fastened—eager, exact—on every detail of locks, guards, and men to be set in place, as if the night were a joint to be trussed and tied so that no knife could slip.

He spoke plainly. Punishment and suffering, to him, were the natural consequence of riot. Property must be protected; order must cut clean, like a sharpened blade. In the way he described it there was the cold certainty of the master of a house who will not let thieves or vermin into his pantry.

Then, in a pause—when she had, at least, forgotten Margaret for a moment—he turned suddenly, driven back to what he had been holding in all day.

“Mother! You know what I have got to say to Miss Hale, to-morrow?”

The question struck her like a bell in the stillness. She looked up.

“Yes! I do. You can hardly do otherwise.”

“Do otherwise! I don’t understand you.”

“I mean that, after allowing her feelings so to overcome her, I consider you bound in honour—”

“Bound in honour!” His scorn was sharp. “I’m afraid honour has nothing to do with it. ‘Her feelings overcome her!’ What feelings do you mean?”

“Nay, John, there is no need to be angry. Did she not rush down and cling to you to save you from danger?”

“She did!” he said; and then he stopped short in front of her, as if the ground under him had changed. His voice altered—lower, shaken. “But, mother—” He could scarcely bring it out. “I dare not hope. I never was faint-hearted before; but I cannot believe such a creature cares for me.”

“Don’t be foolish, John. Such a creature! Why, she might be a duke’s daughter, to hear you speak. And what proof more would you have, I wonder, of her caring for you? I can believe she has had a struggle with her aristocratic way of viewing things; but I like her the better for seeing clearly at last.” She smiled slowly; tears stood in her eyes, the unaccustomed moisture of a pride that had been long dry. “It is a good deal for me to say,” she went on, the truth breaking through her stiffness, “for after to-night, I stand second. It was to have you to myself, all to myself, a few hours longer, that I begged you not to go till to-morrow.”

“Dearest mother!” he cried; and kissed her with real feeling. Yet love, even when true, can be selfish; almost at once he was pulled back to his own fear and desire, and the small, cold shadow of that neglect crept over her heart.

“But I know she does not care for me,” he said, with the obstinacy of a man who cannot bear hope because it makes him helpless. “I shall put myself at her feet—I must. If it were but one chance in a thousand—or a million—I should do it.”

“Don’t fear!” Mrs. Thornton said, crushing down the pang of jealousy that had revealed how fierce her maternal love had been, and how little it was noticed. Her voice grew colder as she mastered herself. “Don’t be afraid. As far as love may go she may be worthy of you. It must have taken a good deal to overcome her pride. Don’t be afraid, John.” She kissed him as she wished him good-night.

She went out slowly and with her usual majesty; but when she reached her own room she locked the door and sat down to cry tears she had not wept for years—quiet, unwitnessed, bitter as strong spirits taken alone.

* * * * *

Margaret entered the sitting-room where her father and mother still sat, speaking low together in the manner illness teaches—half whisper, half sigh. The room was dim with lamplight; it smelt of tea-leaves and the faint medicinal sweetness that clings to invalid rooms like a second curtain. Margaret looked very pale; she came close before she could trust her voice.

“Mrs. Thornton will send the water-bed, mamma.”

“My dear, how tired you look! Is it very hot, Margaret?”

“Very hot,” said Margaret, and the remembered heat of bodies pressed together in the street seemed to rise at the words, “and the streets are rather rough with the strike.”

A vivid colour sprang up in her cheeks as if the blood itself protested at the memory; then it ebbed away, leaving her face whiter than before.

“Here has been a message from Bessy Higgins, asking you to go to her,” said Mrs. Hale. “But I’m sure you look too tired.”

“Yes,” said Margaret. “I am tired, I cannot go.”

She made the tea with hands that trembled despite her will—measuring, pouring, setting down cups with a care that felt like acting. The familiar domestic sounds—the spoon against china, the soft rush of water—should have steadied her; instead, they seemed too thin to cover the day’s harsher music. She was thankful her father was so taken up with her mother—adjusting her shawl, speaking to her, watching her breath—as not to notice Margaret’s looks.

Even after Mrs. Hale went to bed, Mr. Hale was not content to leave her; he undertook to read her to sleep, his gentle voice and slow cadence like a lullaby meant to keep pain at bay. When at last he went, Margaret found herself alone.

“Now I will think of it—now I will remember it all,” she said aloud, as if speech could force order on what had happened. “I could not before—I dared not.”

She sat quite still, hands clasped on her knees, lips pressed, eyes fixed with the gaze of one who sees a thing that is not in the room. The air seemed to thicken with remembered sounds: the mill’s hoarse roar, the sudden shout when the gates gave way, the stones skittering, the fierce chorus of men’s voices swelling and breaking like waves. Underneath it all was the sharper noise inside her head—her own heart, beating too loudly.

She drew a deep breath.

“I, who hate scenes—I, who have despised people for showing emotion—who have thought them wanting in self-control—I went down and must needs throw myself into the mêlée, like a romantic fool! Did I do any good? They would have gone away without me, I dare say.”

Even as she said it, her steadier judgment rose and checked her. “No—perhaps they would not. I did some good. But what possessed me to defend that man as if he were a helpless child! Ah!” She clenched her hands. “It is no wonder those people thought I was in love with him, after disgracing myself in that way. I in love—and with him too!”

Her cheeks flamed; she covered her face. When she drew her hands away, her palms were wet with tears that felt scalding, as if shame itself had a heat.

“Oh, how low I am fallen that they should say that of me!” she whispered. “I could not have been so brave for any one else, just because he was so utterly indifferent to me—if, indeed, I do not positively dislike him.”

She forced herself on. If she let the thought drift, it would return to the wrong place—to his neck beneath her hands, to his astonished eyes, to the sensation that she had claimed him for an instant in front of all those watching faces.

“It made me the more anxious that there should be fair play on each side,” she said, more firmly, as if argument were a shield. “And I could see what fair play was. It was not fair,” she continued, vehement now, “that he should stand there—sheltered, awaiting the soldiers, who might catch those poor maddened creatures as in a trap—without an effort on his part, to bring them to reason. And it was worse than unfair for them to set on him as they threatened. I would do it again, let who will say what they like of me. If I saved one blow, one cruel, angry action that might otherwise have been committed, I did a woman’s work. Let them insult my maiden pride as they will—I walk pure before God!”

She looked up. The tightness in her face loosened; something like a noble calm descended, and for a moment she was very still—stiller than carved stone.

Dixon came in.

“If you please, Miss Margaret, here’s the water-bed from Mrs. Thornton’s. It’s too late for to-night, I’m afraid, for missus is nearly asleep; but it will do nicely for to-morrow.”

“Very,” said Margaret. “You must send our best thanks.”

Dixon went out and returned almost at once, her curiosity and duty both pulling her back.

“If you please, Miss Margaret, he says he’s to ask particular how you are. I think he must mean missus; but he says his last words were, to ask how Miss Hale was.”

“Me!” Margaret drew herself up, as if the very idea were an impertinence that demanded posture. “I am quite well. Tell him I am perfectly well.”

But as she spoke, her face went as white as the handkerchief in her hand, and a heavy pain began to pulse behind her eyes.

Mr. Hale came in then, leaving his sleeping wife because his own gentle restlessness needed companionship. Margaret saw at once that he wanted to be amused, to be drawn away from the sick-room’s hush. With patient sweetness—without one complaint—she bore the ache in her head and rummaged up small subjects for talk: bits of London memory, a book she had seen, a trivial incident in the street. She never once named the riot. The thought of it made her feel physically sick, as if the smoke and sweat and clamour had lodged in her throat.

At last he kissed her good-night.

“Good-night, Margaret. I have every chance of a good night myself, and you are looking very pale with your watching. I shall call Dixon if your mother needs anything. Do you go to bed and sleep like a top; for I’m sure you need it, poor child!”

“Good-night, papa!”

When he was gone, the false smile slipped away. Her eyes dulled with pain; the muscles of her face relaxed as if she had been holding up a weight. She gave up the hard work of seeming well.

She lay down and scarcely moved. To lift a hand, to turn a finger, seemed beyond either will or strength. She was so tired—so stunned—that she thought she never slept at all. Her feverish thoughts slid back and forth across the boundary between waking and sleeping, keeping their miserable sameness. She could not be alone. A cloud of faces looked up at her—faces without distinct anger or personal menace now, but full of attention; and in that attention she felt again the day’s worst wound.

Not fear—shame.

An unwinking glare of many eyes seemed to hold her pinned, as if she were displayed, named, judged in a language she could not answer. The sound of their shouting haunted her—words turned into noise, meaning swallowed by the town’s roar—until she longed, with an aching intensity, to burrow into the earth and hide; and yet she could not escape the gaze that followed her even into the dark.
