Once the final carriage had rolled away, I retreated to the inner hall, where Samuel was busy pouring brandy and soda. Lady Verinder and Miss Rachel emerged from the drawing-room, followed closely by the two gentlemen. Mr. Godfrey accepted a glass, but Mr. Franklin declined, appearing utterly exhausted; the festivities of the birthday celebration had clearly taken their toll.

Lady Verinder paused, turning to bid everyone goodnight. Her gaze lingered intensely on the "wicked Colonel's" legacy, now gleaming in her daughter's dress.

"Rachel," she asked sharply, "where do you intend to place the Diamond tonight?"

Miss Rachel was in a giddy mood, prone to speaking nonsense with the conviction of absolute logic—a trait often seen in excited young women at the close of an exhilarating day. First, she claimed ignorance of where to put it. Then, she suggested her dressing-table, alongside her other jewelry. Suddenly, remembering the stone's eerie, moon-like luminescence in the dark, she recoiled at the terror it might inspire. Next, she recalled an Indian cabinet in her sitting-room and resolved to deposit the gem there, allowing two magnificent Eastern treasures to admire one another.

Her rambling musing was cut short by her mother.

"My dear!" Lady Verinder interrupted. "Your Indian cabinet has no lock."

"Good heavens, Mamma!" cried Miss Rachel, feigning horror. "Is this an inn? Are there thieves in the house?"

Ignoring this theatrical display, Lady Verinder dismissed the gentlemen. Turning to her daughter, she kissed her forehead. "Why not let *me* keep the Diamond for you tonight?" she proposed.

Miss Rachel reacted as if someone had offered to confiscate her favorite doll. Seeing that reason was useless, her mother sighed. "Come into my room, Rachel, first thing tomorrow morning," she instructed. "I have something to say to you." With that, she departed slowly, her expression suggesting deep unease regarding the path her daughter's behavior had taken.

Next, Miss Rachel said her farewells. She shook hands with Mr. Godfrey, who stood gazing at a picture, before turning to Mr. Franklin, who remained seated, weary and silent in a corner.

Though I cannot recount their exact words, standing near the large mirror framed in old oak, I witnessed a private exchange. Miss Rachel's reflection showed her subtly removing the locket Mr. Franklin had gifted her from her bodice, holding it up for his inspection with a smile laden with meaning before darting off to bed. This sight unsettled me, casting doubt on my earlier confidence in my own judgment. Perhaps Penelope was correct about the state of Miss Rachel's affections.

Immediately after Miss Rachel vanished, Mr. Franklin spotted me. His mood, as ever, was fluid.

"Betteredge," he remarked, "I'm beginning to suspect I took Mr. Murthwaite too seriously during our chat in the shrubbery. Does he truly intend to unleash the dogs?"

"I shall remove their collars, sir," I replied, "and allow them to roam freely if they detect a scent worth pursuing."

"Very well," Mr. Franklin agreed. "We shall decide tomorrow. I am not inclined to alarm my aunt without compelling cause. Good-night."

He looked so pale and drained that I offered him a nightcap of brandy-and-water. Mr. Godfrey, approaching from the opposite end of the hall, seconded the suggestion warmly. These minor details mattered to me; despite the verbal sparring heard earlier, the two gentlemen seemed reconciled. Their rivalry for Miss Rachel's favor had not bred genuine animosity. Both were men of the world, accustomed to maintaining civility regardless of circumstance.

Mr. Franklin initially refused the drink but, on the landing, seemingly swayed by his cousin's persuasion—or perhaps merely changing his mind as he often did—he called down, "Perhaps I shall need it later. Send up some brandy-and-water."

I dispatched Samuel with the drink and then released the dogs. They leaped onto me with puppy-like enthusiasm until the cool rain dampened their ardor. They drank a sip of water and retired to their kennels. As I entered the house, I noted the clouds breaking, promising clearer skies ahead, though the rain still poured heavily and the grounds were saturated.

Samuel and I conducted our nightly rounds, securing every latch personally. By the time I retired between midnight and one, feeling the weight of the day's anxieties, the house was silent save for the rhythmic drumming of rain and the wind sighing through the trees.

I slept fitfully, plagued by insomnia similar to Mr. Franklin's. Sunrise finally claimed me. Waking at half-past seven to find a brilliant sun, I stepped out to secure the dogs when a frantic rustling of skirts echoed on the stairs behind me.

I spun around to find Penelope rushing toward me. "Father!" she shrieked. "Come upstairs immediately! The Diamond is gone!"

"Are you out of your mind?" I demanded.

"Gone!" Penelope insisted. "Gone, nobody knows how! Come and see!"

She dragged me into Miss Rachel's sitting-room, which opened directly into her bedroom. There, standing frozen on the threshold, was Miss Rachel. Her face was as pale as her white dressing gown. Beside her stood the double doors of the Indian cabinet, flung wide open. Inside, one drawer was pulled out to its limit.

"Look!" Penelope pointed. "I saw Miss Rachel place the Diamond in that drawer last night."

I approached the cabinet. The drawer was empty.

"Is this true, miss?" I asked gently.

With a gaze and tone entirely alien to her usual self, Miss Rachel repeated Penelope's words: "The Diamond is gone!"

She then retreated into her bedroom and slammed the door, locking it securely.

Moments later, Lady Verinder entered, alerted by my commotion. The news of the theft seemed to paralyze her. She rushed to her daughter's room and demanded entry. Miss Rachel admitted her.

Panic spread through the household like wildfire, reaching the two gentlemen. Mr. Godfrey emerged first, throwing his hands up in bewildered despair. Mr. Franklin, whom I had relied upon for clarity, appeared equally stunned. Sleep, it seemed, had temporarily stupefied him. However, after consuming his customary cup of coffee, his faculties sharpened. He assumed command with remarkable resolve.

First, he ordered the servants to leave all external doors and windows undisturbed, noting the exception of the front door I had unlocked. He then proposed a thorough search of the immediate vicinity—the space behind the cabinet and beneath the table—to rule out accidental displacement. Finding nothing and receiving no new information from Penelope, he directed attention inward.

Penelope knocked on Miss Rachel's door. Lady Verinder answered, closing the door behind her. Instantly, the lock clicked from the inside.

"The loss of the Diamond has completely overwhelmed Rachel," Lady Verinda reported, looking deeply troubled. "She refuses to speak of it, even to me. You cannot see her at present."

Recovering her composure, Lady Verinder faced the reality. "There is no help for it," she stated calmly. "Must we summon the police?"

"And the first duty of the police," Mr. Franklin interjected eagerly, "is to arrest the Indian jugglers who performed last night."

Both Lady Verinder and Mr. Godfrey stared in surprise, unaware of the deduction Mr. Franklin and I shared.

"I cannot elaborate now," Mr. Franklin continued. "The Indians stole the Diamond. Obtain a letter of introduction to a magistrate in Frizinghall, stating that I act on your behalf. I must depart immediately. Our success depends on speed."

Reluctantly, Lady Verinder penned the letter. To her, losing a jewel valued at twenty thousand pounds was a tragedy, yet privately, given her distrust of her late brother and his gift, she might have preferred the thieves escape unpunished.

Accompanied by Mr. Franklin, I rode to the stables to inquire how the intruders could have entered.

"One likely slipped into the hall amidst the confusion of the departing guests," Mr. Franklin theorized. "He may have waited beneath the sofa while we discussed the Diamond's placement. Once the house settled, the cabinet was open game."

Calling the groom to open the gate, Mr. Franklin spurred his horse forward. This seemed the only logical explanation, yet a nagging doubt persisted: how had the thief escaped? The front door was locked and bolted as I had left it. The other exits remained secure. The dogs? Had they been drugged? As these questions raced through my mind, the dogs themselves bounded past, full of life and spirit, easily tamed and chained once more. Mr. Franklin's theory felt increasingly insufficient.

Breakfast proceeded as usual, regardless of the robbery or potential murder. Afterwards, Lady Verinder summoned me. I disclosed everything I had withheld concerning the Indians' plot. Her courage quickly steadied her; her concern focused less on the conspirators and more on her daughter's bizarre behavior.

"You know how erratic Rachel can be," she observed. "Yet I have never seen her so strange or withdrawn. The Diamond seems to have seized her mind. Who would have imagined such a terrible influence could strike so swiftly?"

Indeed, Miss Rachel's obsession with trinkets was unusual, yet she remained locked in her room, inconsolable. The entire household was thrown into disarray.
