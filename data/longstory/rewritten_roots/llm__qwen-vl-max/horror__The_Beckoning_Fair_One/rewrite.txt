I. The three or four "To Let" signs had stood within the low paling as long as anyone in the little triangular Square could remember. If they’d ever been upright, it was ages ago. Now they leaned at crooked angles, like a row of wooden axes poised to fall—always on the verge of striking, yet never quite cutting through the flow of passersby. Not that there was much flow to speak of; the real stream of life passed a furlong or more away, beyond the tangled maze of tenements, alleys, and backways that had grown up since the old house was built—walls closing in, sealing it off from the world. It was likely only kept standing until a lease expired, when the whole area would be cleared, razed, and forgotten.

The house itself was made of aged red brick, its surface worn smooth by time and weather. Carved into its walls were the faded emblems of defunct insurance companies—crowns, clasped hands, insignias now meaningless, their purpose lost to memory. Children from the quiet square had swung on the low gate at the end of the entrance alley until little remained but the solid top bar. The alley ran past basement windows boarded shut, where tramps had once scrawled cryptic symbols in chalk. The path was uneven, gouged by rainwater spilling from the eaves of the neighboring house, and claimed by cats and dogs who moved through it like ghosts of the neglected.

No one bothered to keep the "To Let" boards legible. Why should they? No tenant had come in years. For six months, Oleron had walked past the place twice daily—on his way from lodgings to his workroom, ten minutes’ walk away—and not once had a sign toppled across his path. Perhaps because he usually took the other side of the square. But one morning, he found himself on the opposite side, passing the broken gate and the rain-stained alley. He paused before one of the tilted boards.

It bore the name of an agent, and beneath it, in fading ink, a note: *Key available at Number Six.* The writing looked as if it had been scrawled during Oleron’s own childhood.

He was already paying more than a man without private means ought to for a separate bedroom and workroom. On top of that, he rented storage space for most of his grandmother’s furniture. Books he wanted in bed were always at his workroom half a mile away. Letters he needed during the day were tucked into coats hanging behind his door. The split between home and office was a constant irritation.

So when he stopped before the board, something clicked. He glanced down through thin privet bushes at the boarded basement windows, then up—past the grimy first-floor panes, to the second floor, to the flat stone coping of the lead roof. He stood still, fingers brushing his lean jaw. Then, with a final look at the sign, he crossed the square and went to Number Six.

He knocked. Waited two or three minutes. The door stood open, but no one answered. He knocked again when a gaunt man in shirt sleeves appeared, wiping his mouth.

“I was asking a blessing on our food,” he said, by way of explanation.

Oleron asked for the key to the old house.

The man retreated. Five minutes later, he returned, chewing slowly. “Key’s lost,” he announced. “But you won’t need it. The front door isn’t closed. A push’ll open any of the others. I’m the agent, if you’re thinking of taking it.”

Oleron crossed back to the house. Descended the two steps at the broken gate. Walked down the alley. Pushed open the wide, ancient doorway.

To the right, steps led down into spacious cellars. Before him, a grand staircase rose—carved railings, broad treads, elegant proportions—yet filthy, layered with dust and neglect. He climbed carefully, avoiding contact with the walls and rail. At the first landing, a door faced him, boarded over. He pushed the one beside it. A rusted bolt gave way with a groan. He stepped inside.

The first floor was empty. Hollow. Silent. He spent fifteen minutes walking through it—touching the cold wood, testing the doors, listening to the echo of his footsteps. Then he left.

Back at Number Six, he asked how much rent it would cost.

The man named a figure—low enough, given the neighborhood, the decay, the absence of light, the silence.

“Could I rent just one floor?”

The man hesitated. “Might be possible. I’ll see.”

“Who handles it?”

“A firm of lawyers in Lincoln’s Inn.”

“Tell them Barrett sent me.”

Work kept Oleron from visiting Lincoln’s Inn that day. But the next morning, he went. He was met instantly with an offer: the entire house, for fifty pounds down, the rest on mortgage.

He spent thirty minutes explaining he didn’t want to buy—it was a rental, just one floor.

That changed things. The lawyer hemmed and hawed. He wasn’t sure he could make such an arrangement. But eventually, he admitted: *If* the "To Let" boards stayed up, *and* if the lease ended automatically if the whole house was taken, perhaps something could be done.

Oleron saw no risk. The house had stood vacant for decades. Why would it suddenly let over his head?

He promised a decision within a week.

The next day, he returned. He walked every room—from cellar to attic. Then went home to bathe.

He was enchanted. The space he’d chosen—his floor—had potential. Cleaned, painted, filled with his grandmother’s furniture, it would be beautiful. He visited the warehouse to recall what he owned, took measurements, then went to a decorator.

His regular work pressed hard, but he decided to suspend it entirely until after the move.

Two weeks later, his floor was painted—a soft, elder-flower white. The paint dried quickly. Oleron began moving in.

He was thrilled. He rubbed his hands together as he arranged his grandmother’s things—the tall lattice-paned china cupboard, full of Derby, Mason, Spode; the Sheraton folding table; the long, low bookshelves (two of which he’d had copied); the chairs, the Sheffield candlesticks, the riveted rose-bowls. He placed them against the newly painted walls—wood paneling in perfect proportion, coffered and molded around the low window recesses, carved with a sense of joy and repose unknown in modern design.

The ceilings soared. Faintly painted stars dotted the plaster. Even the iron fireplace’s tapering moldings were delicately shaped, like fine jewelry.

He wandered from room to room, smiling, stopping often just to admire the way the light fell from one white space to another.

“Charming,” he whispered. “Charming. I wonder what Elsie Bengough will think of this?”

He bought a bolt and a Yale lock, secured his door, and sealed off his quarters from the rest of the house.

Now, if he wanted a book in bed, he simply stepped into the next room.

All the while, he thought how lucky he was—how fortunate—to have found this place.

He hung a hat-rack in the small square hall, draped his coats and hats upon it.

And late at night, those who passed through the quiet Square might catch sight of light glowing behind Oleron’s red blinds—or watch as one blind darkened and another lit up, as he moved from room to room with a candlestick in hand, arranging furniture, preparing to return to the work his move had interrupted.

But the house, though silent, was not empty. And the boards outside still leaned forward, like axes waiting to fall.

As far as the chief business of his life—his writing—was concerned, Paul Oleron gave the world more than it ever gave him. Yet he rarely paused to calculate how far behind he was, at forty-four, on the cruel ledger of time and talent. To do so would have changed nothing—and might have broken him. He had chosen this path long ago, and now it was too late to turn back. Perhaps in youth, when idealism still burned bright and noble impulses stirred him, he’d followed a dream that whispered of generosity, honor, and selflessness. And even now, if he dared question himself, he’d still say: no life without such things was worth living. But lately, faintly, almost against his will, he’d begun to suspect there was something deeper beneath it all—something darker, older, perhaps more dangerous. Still, he refused to face the day when he might stand at the peak of his powers, knowing he could never climb higher, and ask himself whether he’d been foolish to live by such lofty ideals.

For now, the move into the old house—with its brickwork scarred by insurance marks—had merely interrupted *Romilly Bishop* at Chapter Fifteen. As he wandered through his new rooms, arranging furniture, adjusting the light, still not quite settled into his rhythm, he seemed almost ascetic in his precision. For twenty years, across garrets and flats, furnished and bare, he had done for himself what others took for granted. He’d learned early that order saved both time and temper. With the wife of the long-nosed Barrett—a stout Welshwoman with a voice like a cracked bell and an accent untouched by London—he’d arranged for breakfast each morning and a weekly cleaning. Otherwise, he welcomed the small tasks of housekeeping as a reprieve from the strain of creation.

His kitchen, joined to a narrow space where a modern bath had been crammed in, looked out onto a narrow alley. At one end stood a large closet with a door and a square sliding hatch near the top. It had once been a powder closet, where a man’s powdered wig was lifted through the hatch to receive the puff of a pistol. Oleron studied it, puzzled. Then, understanding dawning, he smiled—a quiet, distant smile, stirred by something he couldn’t name. It wouldn­’t serve its original purpose now. It would have to be his larder.

It was in that closet he made his first discovery. The back wall was lined with shelves. On an upper shelf, deep within the wall, he found two wooden wig-stands, mushroom-shaped, ancient, and forgotten. He didn’t know how they’d ended up there—likely cast off by painters during renovations. But his five rooms were short on storage. Every inch mattered. He’d need ingenuity to fit in his linen, boxes, and the growing pile of papers he could not bring himself to discard.

He moved in early spring, eager to finish *Romilly* before autumn. But he wouldn’t rush it. If it needed longer, so be it. He knew its importance—its weight in his artistic journey. It must unfold in its own time. In the last room, he’d been making progress. *Romilly* had begun, as they say, to speak and act on her own. He believed she would continue once the chaos of moving passed.

And it was passing. He told himself it was time to return to work. On a March morning, he stepped out, returned with two great bunches of yellow daffodils, placed one on the mantelpiece beside the Sheffield sticks, the other on the table before him, then pulled out the half-written manuscript.

But before he began, he went to a small rosewood cabinet, opened a drawer, and took out his cheque-book and pass-book. He added them up. His face grew thoughtful, drawn. The move had cost more than expected. His balance was barely fifty pounds, with no income in sight.

“Hm,” he murmured. “I forgot rugs and chintz curtains add up so.” He paused. “But it would have been a shame to spoil the place for ten pounds… Well, *Romilly* simply *must* be out by autumn. That’s all.”

He pushed the papers toward himself. But he couldn’t write. Not properly. The square outside had its own rhythms—new, intrusive. First came hawkers, shouting from carts. At noon, children streamed in from school, swinging on his gate. When they left, an itinerant musician appeared beneath his window, strumming a mandolin. Oleron raised the window, tossed him a penny, and returned to his desk.

Still, nothing came. He drifted in and out of awareness, staring around the room, imagining its past—whether a settee of buttercup or petunia satin had sat under the far window, whether a crystal chandelier once hung from the high ceiling, where now only dust gathered. Where had the tambour-frame stood? The picquet-table?

No. This wasn’t working. Better to admit defeat than waste energy. He decided to walk. But instead, he sat down—and fell asleep.

When he woke at half-past four, he yawned. “This won’t do,” he said aloud. “I must do better tomorrow.” He felt deliciously lazy. For a moment, he considered skipping his evening appointment.

The next morning, he sat down without answering any of the three letters waiting—two bills, one from Miss Bengough, forwarded from his old address. It was a bright, noisy day—white clouds rolling northeast, wind dancing through the trees, the world tinged with new green. Light flickered across his room, shifting with the clouds, reflected in the polished table, even in the worn floorboards. The morning sounds began again.

Oleron drew a pattern of dots on the paper. Then stopped. Moved the jar of daffodils exactly opposite a creamy panel. Wrote a sentence that ran two lines—then broke into notes and fragments. For a while, he convinced himself he was working. Then he rose and began pacing.

Suddenly, an idea struck him. The room—too pale, too soft. Like a kind old face, sweet but drained of life. Yes, it needed stronger color—richer flowers, brighter fabrics for the window seats.

“Of course, I can’t afford it,” he muttered, grabbing a measuring tape. He knelt to measure the recesses beneath the windows.

Then—his posture shifted. He froze. Something caught his eye. He rose slowly, rubbing his hands together with quiet excitement.

“Oho, oho!” he said. “These look like window-boxes, nailed shut. We must look into this! Yes, those are boxes—or I’m mistaken. Oho, this is an adventure!”

On the wall of his sitting room were two windows (a third in another corner). Beyond the open bedroom door, on the same wall, was a fourth. All the window seats had been painted over—again and again. Oleron’s finger barely touched the old nailheads beneath the layers. Under the ledge, a keyhole had been filled with putty.

He took out his penknife. Worked carefully for five minutes. Then fetched a hammer and chisel. Gently, he drove the chisel under the seat. The lid lifted slightly. He used the knife to cut along the hinge and edges. Then brought a wedge and a mallet.

“Now for our little mystery—” he whispered.

The sound of the mallet—sharp, sudden—in that delicate, pale room felt somehow violent. Even shocking. The panelling rang, rattled, vibrated like a sounding board. The whole house seemed to echo. From cellar to attic, shadows stirred. The noise grated on Oleron’s nerves.

He paused. Took a duster. Muffled the mallet.

When the edge was loose, he slipped his fingers under and lifted. Paint flaked. Rusty nails groaned. The lid came up, revealing the box beneath.

Empty—except for a few inches of grime, cobwebs, and decay.

“No treasure,” he said, amused at himself. “Well, *Romilly* still has to be out by autumn. Let’s check the others.”

He turned to the second window. By late afternoon, he’d opened all three seats. The bedroom’s was empty. But from the second seat in the sitting room, he pulled something soft, folded, thick with dust. He carried it to the kitchen, swept it over a bucket, then cleaned it with a duster.

It was a bag—large, made of some ancient frieze-like cloth. Unfolded, it covered most of the kitchen floor. Irregularly triangular, with wide flaps and remnants of straps and buckles. One side was faded yellow-brown; the rest, crimson, though the shade varied with exposure.

“What in heaven’s name could this have been?” Oleron murmured, staring at it.

He shrugged. “I give up. Whatever it is, it’s ruined my work for today.”

He folded it carelessly and shoved it into a corner. Then, armed with pans, brushes, and an old knife, he returned to the sitting room. He scraped, washed, and lined the newly discovered boxes with paper. Once done, he stored boots, books, papers inside. Closed the lids.

Smiling faintly, he walked away—amused by his little discovery, yet already anxious for the moment when he could truly begin again.

It irritated Oleron slightly that Miss Bengough should dismiss his new flat with a single glance—especially since he himself had found it so unexpectedly agreeable. She scarcely raised her eyes to the room at all, as if it were merely another piece of scenery to be endured rather than appreciated. But then, she had always been like that—unmoved by beauty, indifferent to comfort, more herself when tearing open a paper bag of biscuits than when seated at a properly laid table. At thirty-four, she was an unattached journalist, large and striking, fair as butter, pink as a dog-rose, resembling nothing so much as a florist’s prize bloom—plump, vivid, and bursting with life. Her movements were sudden and expansive; her speech moist and emphatic. She “pulled a better living out of the pool,” as she liked to say, than Oleron ever did—and through cleverly concealed advertisements from drapers and haberdashers, she also pulled together most of her remarkably varied wardrobe. When she moved, she left small whirlwinds in her wake, veils and scarves flapping wildly behind her.

Oleron heard the rustle of her skirt on the stairs and the sharp, loud knock at his door—just one month after he’d moved into this new place. She carried the outside air in with her garments and dropped a bundle of women’s journals onto a chair. “Don’t knock off for me,” she said, mouth full of large-headed hatpins as she removed her hat and veil. “I didn’t know whether you were straight yet, so I brought sandwiches for lunch. You’ve got coffee, I suppose?—No, don’t get up—I’ll find the kitchen.”

“Oh, that’s all right,” Oleron said, gathering his papers. “To tell the truth, I’m rather glad to be interrupted.” He tucked his work away. She was already in the kitchen; he heard water gurgle into the kettle. He joined her, and ten minutes later followed her back to the sitting-room with coffee and sandwiches on a tray. They sat down, the tray between them on a small table.

“Well,” Oleron asked as she poured, “what do you think of the new place?”

“Hm!” she grunted. “Anybody’d think you were about to get married, Paul.”

He laughed. “Oh no. But it’s an improvement over some of them, isn’t it?”

“Is it? I suppose it is. I don’t know. I liked the last one, even with the black ceiling and no tap. How’s _Romilly_?”

Oleron rubbed his chin thoughtfully. “Hm. I’m ashamed to admit it. The truth is, I haven’t made much progress. But it’ll be all right on the night, as you used to say.”

“Stuck?”

“Rather stuck.”

“Got anything you’d care to read to me?”

Oleron had long been in the habit of reading passages to Miss Bengough. Her feedback was quick, practical—sometimes directly useful, sometimes subtly suggestive. In return, she never spoke of her own writing. His work, she insisted, was “real”; hers was merely filler, often not even grammatically sound.

“I’m afraid not,” he replied, still dry-shaving his chin. Then, with a sudden burst of honesty: “The fact is, Elsie, I haven’t actually written any more of it—none at all. But that doesn’t mean I haven’t progressed. In a way, I’ve gone further than I expected. I’m thinking of reconstructing the whole thing.”

Miss Bengough gasped. “Reconstructing?”

“Making Romilly a different kind of woman. Somehow, I’ve begun to feel I’m not getting the most out of her. As she stands now, I’ve lost interest in her—partially.”

“But—*but*—” she protested, “you had her so real, so *alive*, Paul!”

Oleron smiled faintly. He’d expected this. He wasn’t surprised she liked Romilly as she was—after all, there was much of Miss Bengough in the character. Of course she would find her “living,” “real.”

“But are you really serious?” she asked, staring at him with wide eyes.

“Quite serious.”

“You’re going to scrap fifteen chapters?”

“I didn’t say exactly that.”

“That fine, rich love-scene?”

“I’d only do it reluctantly—if something better came of it.”

“And that beautiful, *beautiful* description of Romilly on the shore?”

“It wouldn’t necessarily be wasted,” he said uneasily.

But Miss Bengough waved her arms dramatically and launched into her rebuke. “Really, you are *too* trying! Sometimes I wish you’d remember you’re human, and live in a world! I wouldn’t dream of asking you to lower your standard—but you could bring it within human comprehension. Oh, you’re sometimes altogether too godlike! It would be a wicked, criminal waste of your powers to destroy those fifteen chapters! Look at it reasonably. You’ve worked nearly twenty years. You’re so close to what you’ve been striving for—your affairs are at a critical point (don’t pretend I don’t know you’re nearly out of money)—and here you are, deliberately proposing to discard something that might make your name, and replace it with something no one will want to read—and no blame to them! Really, you try my patience!”

Oleron shook his head slowly. This was familiar ground. Miss Bengough was a wonderful friend—up to a point. Beyond that… well, we all know where that line lies. She once said that if she’d had just one-tenth of Oleron’s genius, she could have done almost anything—treating genius as though it were a measurable ingredient, something to be added or subtracted. But it wasn’t. It was qualitative, essential, indivisible. That idea passed her understanding. Their spirits diverged at that point. Oleron knew it. She didn’t seem to.

“Yes, yes, yes,” he said wearily. “You’re absolutely right—practically. And I have no argument. If I could hand _Romilly_ over to you, you’d make a huge success of her. But I can’t. And I’m beginning to wonder if she’s worth the effort. You know what that means.”

“What does it mean?” she demanded bluntly.

“Well,” he said, smiling weakly, “what does it mean when you’re convinced a thing isn’t worth doing? You simply don’t do it.”

Miss Bengough looked toward the ceiling as if seeking divine intervention against such madness. “What utter rubbish!” she finally exploded. “When I saw you last, you were oozing _Romilly_—turning out four chapters a week! If you hadn’t moved, you’d have had three-quarters of it done by now. What on earth possessed you to move in the middle of your most important work?”

Oleron tried to explain the inconveniences, but she wouldn’t listen. Perhaps she suspected the real reason. He was simply exhausted—exhausted by twenty years of garrets, roof-chambers, dingy flats, shabby lodgings. He was tired of squalor. The reward was still distant—or if it wasn’t, he no longer cared to reach for it. It’s easy to tell a man at breaking point that one more effort will save him—but if he can’t make that effort, he remains where he was.

“Anyway,” he concluded, “I’m happier here than I’ve been in years. That’s some justification.”

“And doing no work,” she pointed out sharply.

At that, a flicker of irritation flared in Oleron. “And why should I do nothing but work?” he retorted. “How much happier am I for it? I don’t deny I love my work—when it’s finished. But I hate doing it. Sometimes it feels like a crushing weight I long to cast off. Once every few weeks, maybe, it gives me one moment of joy—remembering when it was all joy. Now I’m forty-four, and it’s become drudgery. Nobody wants it. I’m ceasing to want it myself. If any sensible man asked me if I were a fool to keep going, I think I’d have to agree.”

Miss Bengough’s face softened, but remained grave. “But you knew all this years ago, Paul—and you chose it anyway.”

“Well,” he said, “how could I have known? I was told. My heart, if you like, told me. I thought I knew. Youth always thinks it knows. Then one day, you discover you’re nearly fifty—”

“Forty-four, Paul—”

“Forty-four, then—and you realize the glamour isn’t ahead—it’s behind. Yes, I knew and chose. But is that truly knowing? Is that truly choosing? It’s a terrible choice we’re asked to make when we’re young.”

She stared at the floor. Without lifting her eyes, she whispered, “You’re not regretting it, Paul?”

“Am I not?” he countered. “I’ve lately wondered if I am. What *do* I get in return for all this?”

“You know what you get,” she said quietly.

He might have known from her tone what else she could have offered—herself. She knew, but couldn’t say it. Had he asked her to marry him at any time in the past ten years, she would have replied calmly, “Very well. When?” But he had never considered it.

“Yours is the real work,” she continued softly. “Without you, we jackals couldn’t survive. You and a few like you hold everything up on your shoulders.”

For a moment, silence settled between them. Then Oleron realized—he was being childish. It wasn’t like him. Suddenly he rose and began stacking cups and plates on the tray.

“Sorry you catch me like this, Elsie,” he said, laughing lightly. “No, I’ll take them out. Then, if you like, we can go for a walk.”

He carried the tray out and began showing her around the flat. She said little. In the kitchen, she paused before an old, faded square of reddish frieze Mrs. Barrett used as a cushion. “What is that?” she asked.

“That?” Oleron replied, unfolding the cloth. “I’d be glad if you could tell me. I found it in the window-seat. It was wrapped around something—something I couldn’t identify.”

“I think I know,” Miss Bengough said. “It was used to wrap a harp before putting it in its case.”

“By Jove,” Oleron murmured. “That’s probably just what it was. I couldn’t make sense of it.”

They completed the tour and returned to the sitting-room.

“And who lives in the rest of the house?” she asked.

“I suppose a tramp sleeps in the cellar now and then. No one else.”

“Hm.” She paused. “Well, if you like, I’ll tell you what I think.”

“I should like.”

“You’ll never finish _Romilly_ here.”

“Oh?” he said quickly. “Why not?”

“You won’t. I don’t know why—but I know it. You’ll have to leave before you get anywhere with that book.”

He fell silent, then said: “Isn’t that a little—prejudiced, Elsie?”

“Perfectly ridiculous,” she answered, her mouth again full of hatpins. “As an argument, it has no legs. But there it is.”

Oleron reached for his hat and coat. He laughed. “I can only hope you’re entirely wrong,” he said. “Because if _Romilly_ isn’t out by autumn, I shall be in a serious mess.”

As Oleron sat by the fire that evening, the flames casting long shadows across the room, he found himself brooding over Miss Bengough’s prediction—that difficulties lay ahead in his work. He clenched his jaw. It would have been better if she had kept her thoughts to herself. No man performs well when his confidence is undermined before he even begins. To speak of obstacles is, in a way, to summon them. Words become spells, and soon doubt gathers like fog, thickening until the very thing warned against seems inevitable. He cursed her silently. A force opposed to the completion of *Romilly* had taken root—something born not of circumstance, but of her words. And in that peculiar, unreasoning way women sometimes possess, she had pinned this ill omen to his new home. Was there ever such nonsense?

“You’ll never finish *Romilly* here,” she had said.

Why not? Was this her notion of luxury—the kind that drains the will, saps ambition, and drags a man into indolence? The house was pleasant enough—indeed, it was charming—but hardly so corrupting. No. Elsie had missed the mark entirely.

He shifted his chair, turning to take in the room bathed in firelight. It seemed to smile back at him, warm and inviting. Even the slight pallor he’d noticed earlier vanished in the soft glow. The chintz curtains, patterned with flowers, trellises, baskets, and oaten pipes, hung in quiet folds over the window seats. The spines of books in the old cases caught the light richly. The last trace of sallowness had faded with daylight. And now, if truth be told, it was Elsie who had seemed out of place—out of harmony with the space.

The thought struck him sharply. He returned to it. Yes, the room had, quite by accident, done her a disservice that afternoon. In some subtle, unmistakable way, it had set her apart—highlighted a contrast between her and the atmosphere around her. Suppose, for argument’s sake, that this room lacked vigor, that it was spare and understated—then so much the worse for Miss Bengough. She overflowed with excess. She was all *too much*: too large, too bright, too pink, too lush. She radiated the sort of vitality one might expect from a prize bloom—exuberant, almost vulgar in its fullness.

And if one must compare abstract qualities, Oleron preferred austerity. He liked clarity, restraint. Now, for the first time, he saw clearly: Elsie Bengough was not merely different—she was fundamentally *opposite* to what he valued. He pictured her again as she had stood that afternoon—large, moistly flushed, alive with an almost unnatural warmth. And instantly, in his mind, she diminished. He realized now that something had felt off then, though he hadn’t known it. Unconsciously, even while she was there, he had been judging her.

Her charm was mechanical. Her warmth was chemical—produced by processes he could dissect. Behind her, lurking just beyond perception, was the shadow of mortality. He had never, in ten years of closeness, dreamed of asking her to marry him. Yet now, for the first time, he felt a strange relief that he hadn’t.

Then, suddenly, his face burned with shame. How could he think such things of Elsie? Of Elsie Bengough—his friend, the companion of countless afternoons, the woman whose loyalty he had always trusted, whose support he would have turned to if the world had failed him? To dissect her thus—mentally, critically—was monstrous. He was an ingrate, a cad.

If she had been there, he would have knelt before her. For ten minutes, he sat motionless, staring into the fire, the humiliation slowly fading from his cheeks. Silence filled the house—except for a faint, musical sound drifting from the kitchen: water dripping from a tap that hadn’t been fully shut off. It fell into the vessel below with tiny, rhythmic taps.

Mechanically, he began tapping his finger in time with the drops. The small movement seemed to draw the heat from his face. He cooled. When he resumed his thoughts, he did so without realizing he had picked up exactly where he left off.

It wasn’t only her physical excess that he now judged; it was her mind. The differences between their intellects were vast. He no longer felt gratitude that they had once walked side by side—he felt questions. Their minds diverged. Had there ever truly been alignment? He doubted it now. True, he had read his writings to her, and she had responded with seeming understanding, with apt comments. But what does a man do when he assumes another sees the world as he does—only to be jolted by something that shatters every assumption?

He doubted everything now.

For a moment, he considered: perhaps the man who demands more from a friend than can be given risks losing that friend. But he pushed the idea aside. Again, he stopped thinking. Again, his finger tapped to the distant rhythm of the drip.

And then—(he resumed later)—if these truths applied to Elsie, they applied also to the character she inspired: Romilly Bishop.

And since he could say of Romilly what he dared not say of Elsie, he let his thoughts run free.

In that firelit room, to the faint echo of the tap, he admitted it: he hated the central figure of his novel.

She overwhelmed the senses—coarse-fibred, garish, rank. As he described her, she became unbearable. Gulliver had written of the Brobdingnagian maids-of-honour in much the same terms—and mentally, spiritually, Romilly matched them: unsensitive, limited, common. The model—Elsie—had bled through fifteen chapters of vulgar, blaring prose so insistently that he had simply been unable to begin the sixteenth. He marveled that it had only just dawned on him.

And *this* was to have been his Beatrice—his vision? She was to have entered the furnace of his art, purified, reborn as the ideal woman—all men’s desire. Her thoughts were to have drawn from his deepest convictions, her form from his most cherished dreams, her setting from any place worthy of her. He had meditated long before beginning. Then one day, he had felt her stir within him—as a mother feels a child quicken—and he had begun to write. Chapter after chapter followed.

And this was the result—fifteen sodden, lifeless pages.

He sat again, finger tapping softly.

Then he stirred. She must go. Every word of her. All fifteen chapters. That was settled. What would replace her, he did not know. But one thing at a time. A man isn’t excused from taking the wrong path just because the right one isn’t revealed. Better things might come—if they were meant to. In the meantime—

He rose, fetched the manuscript, and read it over before casting it into the fire.

But instead of burning it, he dropped it from his hand.

He became aware again of the tap—the tiny, tinkling melody of four or five notes, shifting irregularly, sweet and delicate as a dulcimer. In his mind, he saw each drop forming, trembling at the edge of the faucet, falling with a “plink—plunk” barely audible. After the lowest note, a brief phrase repeated itself—slightly off-key, yet oddly beautiful. Soon, he found himself waiting for it to return.

It was lovely.

But it didn’t awaken him. He drifted into sleep, lulled by the rhythm of the drops.

When he woke, the fire had died low, the candle flames licking the rims of the Sheffield sticks. Slowly, he rose, yawned, checked the locks and windows, and went to bed.

He slept deeply.

But the next morning brought a curious turn.

Mrs. Barrett usually tapped not on his door, but on the wooden wall beside his bed. Oleron rose, threw on his dressing-gown, and opened it.

He wasn’t aware that as he did so, he hummed a tune.

But Mrs. Barrett lingered, hand on the knob, face turned slightly away, smiling.

“De-ar me!” her soft falsetto rose. “But that will be a very o-ald tune, Mr. Oleron! I will not have heard it this for-ty years!”

“What tune?” Oleron asked.

“The tune you was humming, sir.”

He had his thumb in the flap of a letter. It stayed there.

“*I* was humming?”

“Sing it, Mr. Oleron.”

Mrs. Barrett prut-prutted. “I have no voice for singing, Mr.7 Oleron; it was Ann Pugh was the singer of our family; but the tune will be very o-ald, and it is called ‘The Beckoning Fair One.’”

“Try to sing it,” Oleron said, still holding the envelope.

With much dimpling and embarrassment, she hummed the melody.

“They do say it was sung to a harp, Mr. Oleron,” she concluded, “and it will be very o-ald.”

“And *I* was singing that?”

“Indeed you wass. I would not be likely to tell you lies.”

With a curt “Very well—let me have breakfast,” Oleron opened his letter.

But the incident troubled him more than he would admit.

The phrase he had hummed—the one he had associated with the falling water the night before—was the same.

More unsettling than the steady, ordinary drip of a water tap—so eerily in tune with the breath of the air—was the way it awakened in Oleron a heightened awareness of every whispering movement within the house. It had long been said that silence gains its deepest resonance when pierced by a single, faint sound; and here, silence was never complete. The spring air, soft and insistent, seemed to stir the old timbers as if they were alive. Perhaps the heat from Oleron’s fires made the structure groan and shift, or perhaps the countless insects hidden in the beams and floorboards were gnawing their way through the very bones of the building. Whatever the cause, all he needed was stillness—just a moment of quiet—and the world around him would dissolve into a symphony of unseen life.

He sat in his chair, listening. At first, only the drip. Then, beneath it, a thousand other sounds emerged: the slow sigh of a beam settling into its mortise, the tick of some unseen creature in the wall, the creak of a joint, the moan of a board under pressure. A windowpane shifted in its frame with a soft knock, though no wind stirred. And yet, no one moved. No breath was drawn. Yet the house *spoke*—not in words, but in language only the sensitive could perceive.

Oleron smiled, not with humor, but with a strange awe. He thought of how humans so neatly divide the living from the inert. But here, in this ancient dwelling, life wasn’t confined to blood and breath. It lived in the wood, in the stone, in the dust that settled like memory. The place breathed. It sighed. It remembered. And slowly, without warning, Oleron began to feel a bond forming—not just between himself and the house, but *through* it. As if his soul were mirrored in its walls. He toyed with the absurd notion that one day, another tenant might walk in and say, “This place is haunted.” And what would he say? That he, Paul Oleron, a man of letters, a harmless dreamer, had unknowingly planted the seed of a ghost?

But as his connection to the house deepened, Elsie Bengough grew more distant—more uneasy. Her dislike of the place became palpable, almost physical. She spoke plainly, her voice sharp with disapproval.

“It doesn’t belong to today,” she declared. “And for you especially, it’s dangerous. You’re already slipping away—losing touch with reality. You need concrete floors, gas meters, a real job. Something that forces you to interact with people. If I could get you work—just two or three days a week—would you take it?”

Oleron bristled slightly. He thanked her, but coldly. “Thank you, but no. We each have our own path.”

“Your own life to live?” she echoed, her tone laced with irony. “How long has it been since you’ve stepped outside?”

“About two hours.”

“No—since you last walked anywhere meaningful. Since you last stretched your legs?”

“I don’t know. Some time.”

“Since I was last here?”

“I haven’t been out much.”

“And has *Romilly* improved because of it?”

“I think so. I’m laying the foundations. I’ll begin writing soon.”

She frowned, turned away, then snapped back. “Ah! So you still cling to that ridiculous idea?”

“If you mean,” Oleron replied slowly, “that I’ve abandoned the old *Romilly* and am starting anew… yes. That’s exactly what I’m doing.”

Her eyes narrowed. “Where is the old manuscript?”

“Why?”

“I want to see it. I want to show you parts of it. I want to bring you back to your senses.”

He turned his back.

When he faced her again, his voice softened. “It’s no use, Elsie. I must follow my own instincts—even if you think them wrong. I’m thinking about it. The manuscript? I nearly burned it. But I didn’t. It’s in the window-seat—if you must see it.”

She rushed to the seat, lifted the lid. Then stopped.

A small cry escaped her. She pressed her hand to her mouth.

“You ought to knock those nails in, Paul,” she said, her voice trembling.

He lunged forward. “What? What happened?”

“I did knock them in—or rather, pulled them out.”

She held up her hand. From the wrist to the tip of her little finger, a fresh wound bled steadily, red and raw.

“Good God!” Oleron gasped.

He led her to the bathroom, turned on warm water, cleaned the cut gently, then held it under cold water. His hands shook. “I can’t understand—how could this happen? I checked the boxes myself. I opened them a dozen times. There shouldn’t have been anything there…”

She murmured, eyes closed, “One moment longer—I can bear it.”

Afterward, he bound her hand with a handkerchief. But his face remained clouded. He had spent half a day preparing the window-boxes—opening them, cleaning them, making them usable. He had seen no nail. Not once. And yet, there it had been—an inch and a half of rusted iron, protruding like a tooth from the wood.

“It shall come out now,” he muttered, fetching pincers.

He extracted it carefully.

Elsie sat in a chair, pale, but in her hand—still clutched tightly—was the manuscript of *Romilly*. She hadn’t let go of it.

Then she spoke again, her voice low but urgent. “Paul, if you don’t publish this, it will be the biggest mistake of your life.”

He hung his head. The incident with the nail replayed in his mind. *Romilly* was secondary now. But she persisted.

“What can I say?” he whispered. “Only that I hope, when you read the new version, you’ll understand why I must do this. And if you still don’t like it… well…” He gestured helplessly. “I must follow my own light.”

Silence.

“Come, Elsie,” he said softly. “We’ve worked well together. Don’t let this tear us apart.”

The moment the words left his lips, he regretted them.

She had been resting her injured hand, eyes shut. Now, her lips trembled. Her eyelids fluttered.

“I can’t help saying it,” she whispered. “You’ve changed. You’re not yourself anymore. I wish you’d never seen this house. It’s stopped your work. It’s turning you into someone I don’t recognize. And it’s making me afraid for you…”

“Oh, my hand—it’s beginning to throb,” she added, wincing.

“Poor child,” he murmured. “Let me take you to a doctor.”

“No,” she said. “I’ll be fine. Just keep it raised.”

She rested her elbow on the back of the chair. The bandaged hand touched his shoulder.

That touch sent a jolt through him—one unlike any he’d felt before. Hundreds of times, she had slipped her hand into his arm during walks, always casually, affectionately, like a sister. He had accepted it as such. But now—now it was different. Her eyes were closed. Her head tilted back. A fragile, sorrowful smile played on her lips.

And suddenly, it hit him.

She was in love with him.

Not just fondness. Not friendship. Love.

And he had never known.

And worse—he didn’t feel pity. Not guilt. Not tenderness.

Instead, something dark and unfamiliar surged within him: irritation. Resentment. A sharp, selfish impulse.

Why was she here? Why wasn’t she working? Why was she meddling in his life? Who gave her the right to judge him? To claim guardianship over him?

*Changed?* He looked at her, and realized: it wasn’t he who had changed. It was *her*.

By the time she opened her eyes, he had mastered himself.

“I wish you’d let me take you to a doctor,” he said, quietly.

She rose. “No, thank you, Paul. I’ll go now. If I need care, I’ll get it. Take my other hand, please. Goodbye.”

He didn’t stop her.

He walked her to the stairs. Halfway down the narrow alley, she paused.

“It would be a long way to come,” she said, “if you weren’t home. I’ll send you a postcard next time.”

At the gate, she turned once more.

“Leave here, Paul,” she said, her voice heavy with sorrow. “Everything’s wrong with this house.”

Then she was gone.

Oleron stood alone.

He returned to the room. Went straight to the window-box. Opened the lid.

Stared.

Then closed it.

He turned away, his breath shallow.

“That’s frightening,” he whispered. “It’s impossible. I should have seen that nail. I *did* see it. I removed it. I swear I did…”

But the box was empty. The nail was gone.

And the house, silent again, seemed to watch.

Oleron understood, with a cold clarity that settled deep in his bones, what Elsie had meant when she said her next visit would come with a postcard. She hadn’t been hinting at convenience—she’d been warning him. And now, at last, he knew. He knew—and it was too late. The knowledge sat like a stone in his chest, heavy and unyielding. He didn’t want her. Not truly. Not the way she wanted to be wanted.

So when she appeared again within days—unannounced, knocking sharply on the door—he felt only a sickening pang of pity. From the landing, she called down, voice clipped and brittle: “I won’t stay long. Just came to tell you about an inquiry for short stories. You might want to apply.” Her words were polite, rehearsed. But there was no warmth in them. No trace of the woman who once laughed beside him in the garden, or whose hand he once held without thought.

He thanked her. He pressed her gently, but she refused even to step inside. Only after he insisted did she allow herself into the hallway, her face pale, her eyes darting around as if afraid the walls might swallow her whole.

Then, just as quickly, she turned to leave.

“Wait,” he said, though he didn’t mean to. “Let me see you out.”

She nodded, not looking at him. They descended the stairs together, silence thick between them. Halfway down, a sharp crack echoed through the house—a sound like wood splitting under pressure. Elsie gasped, then choked back a cry.

Oleron froze.

He knew the staircase was old. He knew it creaked. But he’d walked it countless times without incident. Yet now—now, beneath Elsie’s foot—the tread gave way. A jagged hole opened in the floorboards, and her boot tore through, sending splinters flying.

“Oh God—my poor girl!” he breathed, rushing forward.

She laughed—not a real laugh, but something broken, high-pitched, edged with hysteria. “It’s my weight… I know I’m getting fat…”

“Don’t move,” he snapped, hands trembling as he knelt. “Let me get this clear.”

But she wouldn’t stop laughing. Sobbing through it. “I’m too much… too heavy for everything… I’m not wanted…”

The words struck him like a blow. He looked up, heart pounding. “Elsie—”

“No,” she whispered, shaking her head violently. “Not that. Not *you*. Don’t say it. Not now. Please—just let me go.”

Her voice cracked. “I didn’t want to come. I tried—I really tried. But I couldn’t keep away. I *had* to see you one more time…”

Oleron felt the world tilt. He reached for her, but she pulled back, arms crossed tight over her chest.

“I’m not wanted,” she repeated, voice thin and raw. “Let me take what’s left of me and go.”

“Elsie—” he began, voice breaking. “You’re dear to me. You always have been.”

She flinched as if struck. “No. Not that. Not less than that. Don’t offer me half. Don’t make me feel like I’m begging. Let me keep my pride.”

He stared at her, helpless. He could offer nothing. Because he didn’t love her. Not in the way she needed. Not in the way that mattered.

“Let me get my coat,” he muttered. “Let me take you to a doctor.”

“No,” she said firmly. “And don’t touch me. I’ll be fine.”

She refused even his arm. As they reached the bottom, she smiled faintly, painfully. “Sorry I broke your stairs, Paul. You’ll look into those stories, won’t you?”

He groaned. “If you won’t go to a doctor, at least let Mrs. Barrett look at you. Look—there she is now.”

Barrett, tall and thin-faced, stood at the edge of the alley, staring up. But as Oleron opened his mouth to call, the man turned and vanished without a word.

Elsie didn’t wait. She stepped out into the street, shoulders hunched, and walked quickly away.

Oleron watched until she passed the “To Let” boards—those grim, hatchet-shaped signs that seemed to hang like tombstones over the doorway. He feared even they might fall upon her, crush her, end her.

That night, he did not eat.

He wandered from room to room, bare feet silent on the worn floorboards. His mind replayed her words: *"I'm not wanted... don't offer me anything less... let me take away what's left of me..."*

Each phrase echoed like a curse.

Poor Elsie. So fragile. So broken.

He thought of her face—pale, swollen with tears, drawn by pain. If tears were weapons, hers were self-inflicted. Weapons turned inward. And the thought came, unwanted: suicide.

Women had done such things before. Drowned themselves in dark rivers. Hung themselves from hooks on doors. Left behind only whispers and unanswered questions.

And then—what would happen? An inquest. Questions. “How did she get this wound on her hand? This abrasion on her ankle?” Barrett would testify: “I saw her leaving the house.” And Oleron—Oleron would be called to identify her body.

He shuddered.

He forced himself to stop. These thoughts were madness. Morbid. Unfair.

Yet still, the fear clung to him.

He sat in the dimming light, fire low, shadows stretching like fingers across the walls. He listened—to the creaks, the tickings, the soft raps from the panelling. The house was alive. Always had been.

And then—something else.

A sound.

Soft. Silent almost. Like silk brushing against silk. A slow, sweeping rustle, with a tiny, almost imperceptible crackle beneath it.

He frowned.

He’d heard it before. Earlier. When he’d first sat down.

But now—it came again.

Closer.

More insistent.

He stiffened.

His breath caught.

And then—like a switch flipping—he sprang to his feet, chair overturning, iron poker clattering to the hearth.

He turned slowly, hand reaching behind him, searching the air.

There was nothing.

But the sound—oh, the sound—was unmistakable.

Only one thing made that noise.

A woman brushing her hair.

He stood frozen, heart hammering against his ribs.

“God in Heaven,” he whispered.

The sound stopped.

For a moment—silence.

Then—scuttling. A mouse. He swallowed hard. It wasn’t that. That wasn’t what had made his blood freeze.

But the other sound—gone.

Still—his skin crawled.

He called out. “Who’s there? What is it?”

No answer.

Then—another scuttle. A mouse. He sank to his knees, trembling. He couldn’t breathe.

He couldn’t speak.

He moved toward the mantelpiece, keeping close to the wall, hands outstretched. Matches. There must be matches.

He found the box. It fell to the hearth.

He groped, fingers numb, until he cornered it in the fender.

One match. Struck. Flame flickered. Then died.

Second try. Lighter. The candle on the table—yes. He lit it.

The flame steadied. Grew bright.

He looked around.

Nothing.

The room was empty.

Just as it had always been.

But it wasn’t.

Because he had *heard* it.

He had *felt* it.

That soft, whispering brush of hair—long, wet, slow—moving through the air where no one stood.

And now—now he knew.

The house was not alone.

It had never been.

Something lived here.

Something that remembered.

Something that brushed its hair in the dark.

And it had been waiting.

Waiting for him.

Waiting for her.

Waiting—for someone to finally listen.

Oleron didn’t know how he’d gotten there. One moment he was in his room, the next he was stepping over the loose board laid across the broken stair—Miss Bengough’s step, the one that had given way under her weight. He was hatless, moving down the stairs as if pulled by some unseen hand. Only later, when memory returned like a fog, did he recall leaving the candle burning on the table, slipping through the door just wide enough for his body, and closing it softly behind him.

At the bottom of the stairs, something startled him—a sudden flurry from the disused cellar, darting out through the back door. It was only a cat. But Oleron let out a whimper, childlike and helpless. He stepped outside, stood beneath the “To Let” signs, tugging at his lip with nervous fingers, staring up at the faint glow behind one of his red blinds. Then, still glancing over his shoulder, he stumbled forward into the square.

There was a small pub around the corner—one he’d never entered before—but now he walked in without hesitation. He dropped a shilling that missed the counter by inches. “B—b—bran—brandy,” he stammered, then bent to retrieve the coin.

The bar was quiet. The sawdust floor muffled his steps. He sat on a hard wooden settee with a perforated seat, drank half the brandy, and then finished it—because why waste it? What else could he do?

He thought about who might move his things tomorrow. He wouldn’t go back—not yet. Not to that room with the flickering candle, not to the broken step, not to the pith-white, unnerving bedroom. No. He’d return to his old arrangement—workroom separate from sleeping quarters. He’d find his former landlady, ask for a night’s lodging. Soon. After this drink.

His glass was empty. He rose, ordered another.

And if anyone asked why he was moving again? He had reasons—plenty of them. Nails that reinserted themselves into wood, slicing hands open. Stairs that collapsed underfoot. Women appearing in the dark, brushing their hair like ghosts. That was reason enough. He was furious. He hadn’t taken the place for invisible women to haunt; he’d taken it for himself. And that lawyer in Lincoln’s Inn would hear about it soon. This was outrageous—letting people sign agreements like that, knowing full well what lurked within the walls.

A cut-glass partition separated his side of the bar from where the white-haired landlady moved among bottles and taps. It stopped short, leaving a gap above the counter. There was no partition beyond the far end of the bar. Oleron raised his eyes—and saw faces watching him through the opening. When he looked, they vanished.

He shifted to a corner where he couldn’t be seen from the other side—but now he faced the landlady directly. She recognized him, perhaps from seeing him pass by. She remarked on the weather. He replied—though he didn’t remember what. She added that winter had been brutal for influenza, but spring seemed finally arriving.

Even that simple exchange steadied him. A fleeting, absurd thought crossed his mind: did she brush her hair every night? Did it crackle with static like Elsie’s had? He dismissed it instantly. He felt better.

With his next drink, he decided he would go back. Not go back? Of course he would. They’d see whether he’d be driven out so easily. He began to wonder why he was doing something so unusual—sitting hatless, drinking brandy in a public house. Suppose he told the landlady everything—the nail that scratched her hand, the rotten step, the whispers in the walls, the tiny sound that made him flee in terror? What would she think? That he was mad. Of course.

Pshaw. The truth was simpler. He hadn’t been working enough. He’d been dreaming all day, wasting time on fantasies about rewriting *Romilly*—as if the original wasn’t good enough. Now he was surprised the devil had found an empty head. Yes, he would go back. He’d take a walk first—he hadn’t walked enough lately—and then get his life back in order. Finish that cursed sixteenth chapter. Destroy fifteen chapters? How foolish he’d been! He had duties. To others. To himself. That was the whole matter.

He finished his brandy and left.

For a while, the fresh air heightened the warmth of the alcohol. But gradually, clarity returned—clearer than it had been since morning. And the clearer he became, the less convincing his earlier bravado felt. The fear wasn’t gone—it had changed shape. It wasn’t about him anymore.

It was about Elsie.

Something in that house was dangerous to her. Her accidents alone might not have convinced him—but she had said it. “I’m not wanted here.” And she’d sensed it before he had. She’d seen what he hadn’t.

So now he knew: she must stay away. Not because of his own cowardice, but because of hers. He’d make sure of it. And now he realized his earlier decision—to never return—was absurd. People didn’t run from shadows. Not real men. Not men with obligations.

With Elsie safe, he could face whatever waited in that house. He owed it to himself. He turned back.

As he walked, he considered whether he’d need another drink if fear returned. But by the time he reached the street leading to the square, it was too late. The pub was closed, lit but shuttered. A few men stood on the kerb, talking. Silence fell when he passed. And Barrett—long-nosed, sour-faced Barrett—didn’t nod or say goodnight.

Oleron stepped through the broken gate, paused briefly in the alley, then climbed the stairs.

Only an inch of wax remained in the Sheffield stick. He didn’t light another candle. Deliberately, he forced himself to walk through each of his five rooms before retiring.

It was when he crossed the hall from the kitchen that he noticed it: a letter lying on the floor.

He carried it into the sitting room, glanced at the envelope. Unstamped. Hand-delivered. The handwriting was clumsy, unbroken by commas or periods. He read the first line, then the signature—and finished quickly.

It was from Barrett. He wrote that he would appreciate if Mr. Oleron made other arrangements for breakfast and cleaning. The postscript held the real blow: a scripture passage. One that could only refer to Elsie.

A deep frown carved itself into Oleron’s brow. So. That was it. Very well. He’d deal with it tomorrow.

But the letter served another purpose. It ignited a rage that burned away the lingering chill of fear. He crumpled the paper, held it to the flame, and ground the ashes under his heel.

Yet one last thing unsettled him. As he undressed, he glanced at the bed. The coverlets bore an impression—as if someone had lain there.

He couldn’t remember lying down during the day. Offhand, he was certain he hadn’t. But he couldn’t be sure.

Anger for Elsie surged—perhaps mixed with the last traces of brandy. Other thoughts faded. He blew out the candle, lay down, and slipped into a deep, dreamless sleep. It lasted nearly twenty-four hours—unbroken, uninterrupted—until the morning sun crept through the blinds.

VIII — To the man who listens to that inner voice warning him that twilight and peril are creeping over his soul, terror becomes an absolute force—something not to be trifled with, something that must be walled off at once or else he risks a complete unraveling of his very nature. Fortunately, such a man need not look far for defenses. From the smallest customs, the most ordinary routines, the quietest habits of daily life, he constructs barriers against the encroaching dark. He even accepts—yes, embraces—the idea that joy, like fear, can be treated as absolute, something sacred and unyielding. The one crime he will never commit is the dismantling of boundaries, not just for himself, but for the good of all souls. Yet Oleron was beginning to do exactly that.

He began by allowing the inexplicable, the monstrous, to grow familiar. Not deliberately, not at first—only through neglect, through a slow abandonment of the rules Elsie Bengough had once insisted upon as essential. Two months ago, the phrase “a haunted house” would have sent ice through his veins. Now, his nerves dulled, he could ask, “Haunted by what?” without realizing how much horror loses its power when it becomes merely relative. He was eroding the borders. Mist and confusion were closing in. And yet—he felt nothing so strongly as hunger. A ravenous curiosity. He *had* to know. Nothing less than knowledge would satisfy him. He schemed, quietly, carefully, searching for ways to reach it.

But he didn’t need to scheme. The path was already laid out. Just as in the past, when writing had come to him in moments of effortless inspiration—words rising from nowhere, perfect and unalterable—so now, the questions he asked seemed answered before they were fully formed. There was a thrilling ease to it. He hadn’t felt this kind of exhilaration in years—not since his writing had been fresh, alive, a delight. It was almost as if the way forward was being dictated to him. And the first step? Defining the problem.

He framed it mathematically. Let’s suppose: he did not have the house to himself. Let’s suppose: the old house had seized his spirit, bound it. Let’s suppose: by some shared denominator, this unseen tenant was linked to him. Then—what next? Clearly, the nature of the other numerator must be discovered. How?

Ordinarily, this might seem impossible. But to Oleron, it was suddenly clear. The key—of course—was in his half-written novel. Or rather, in both *Romillys*: the original, abandoned version, and the new one he was trying to birth. A few weeks earlier, he would have laughed at such a notion. Now, he accepted it without hesitation, without tremor.

He began to examine both versions of *Romilly*. From the moment he did, progress accelerated. He reviewed the first *Romilly*, the fifteen chapters he had written. He remembered now—vividly—that on the very first morning in the new house, he had found her lacking. Other moments confirmed it: the night he nearly burned the entire manuscript; the next day, when he began sketching the new *Romilly*. That same morning, Mrs. Barrett had heard him humming a tune—a melody suggested by the dripping of a tap—and told him he was singing “The Beckoning Fair One,” a song he’d never heard before.

The name struck him like lightning. Without pause, he thought: the first *Romilly* discarded, the second instantly took hold, demanding space in his mind, clamoring for existence. He even imagined now—looking back—that there had been rage in the replacement, a near-hatred. More than once, a stray thought about the old creation had—(the idea was absurd, yet somehow plausible)—offended the new one. But that a malice, almost murderous, should extend to the real woman who inspired the fiction…?

A choked cry escaped him: “Good God!”

This sudden intrusion of Elsie into his thoughts stopped his investigation cold. The mere image of her destroyed any abstract reasoning. He couldn’t think of Barrett’s letter either, nor the scene that followed it—without a flush, without a tightening of the brow.

For he had confronted Barrett immediately. Striding across the square the next morning, he had challenged him at his own doorstep. Returning minutes later, he knew he had made things worse. The man was vague, evasive. He muttered phrases—“Certain things... Mrs. Barrett... respectable house... if the cap fits... proceedings that shall be nameless”—over and over, refusing to clarify. “Not that I make any charge—” he ended.

“Charge!” Oleron had shouted. “I’ve my ideas of things, as I don’t doubt you have yours—”

“Ideas—mine!” Oleron snapped, lowering his voice as heads appeared at windows. “Look here, my man—you’ve an unwholesome mind, which you probably can’t help, but a tongue you *can* help—and shall! If a word of this is repeated…”

“I’ll not be talked to on my own doorstep like this by anybody…” Barrett sputtered.

“You shall, and I’m doing it.”

“Don’t you forget there’s a Gawd above all, Who has said…”

“You’re a low scandalmonger!”

And so it went—worse than it had begun. Oleron returned furious, and ever since, whenever he looked out his window, he saw Barrett’s face—peering from behind blinds, glancing around corners—as if gathering evidence for some invisible trial.

The incident altered small things in Oleron’s life. Barrett’s words had spread. Neighbors eyed him warily. He decided to shop farther away, until he could find another helper. Housekeeping was no novelty to him—he’d revert to his old bachelor ways. Besides, he was deep in investigations too delicate for interruption.

One midday, tired and unwell, he stood at his window, glad he wouldn’t need to go out. Then he saw Elsie Bengough crossing the square toward his house. The weather had turned—cold, gusty. Her wide skirt billowed around her, her veil flapped wildly behind her. A panic seized Oleron. She must not enter the house. He grabbed his hat, ran down the stairs, and sprinted along the alley. As he ran, his eyes flickered upward, drawn to the eaves—though he didn’t know why. Was a slate loose? He didn’t stop to wonder.

He met her at the gate and spoke quickly, fluently. “This is really too bad, Elsie! Just as I’m urgently called away! I’m afraid it can’t be helped though, and you’ll have to think me an inhospitable beast.”

It poured out—whatever came to mind. She asked if he was going to town.

“Yes, yes—to town,” he replied. “I’ve got to call on Chambers. You know Chambers, don’t you? No, I remember you don’t—a big man you once saw me with… I ought to have gone yesterday, and—” (he paused, proud of this lie) “—and he’s going out of town this afternoon. To Brighton. I had a letter from him this morning.”

He took her arm and led her across the square. She reminded him that his way to town was the opposite direction.

“Oh—how stupid of me!” he laughed loudly. “I’m so used to going the other way with you—of course, it’s the other way to the bus. Will you come along with me? I’m so awfully sorry it’s happened like this…”

They walked to the bus stop. This time, Elsie showed no sign of inner conflict. If she sensed anything odd in his manner, she said nothing. He, seeing her calm, began to speak more cautiously.

By the time they reached the bus, no one would have guessed that one of them was trembling with relief—relief that, as he believed, he had saved the other from unthinkable danger.

They climbed to the top deck. Oleron protested that he’d miss his coat, claiming the day was oppressively hot. They sat together on the front seat.

Now that they were together, he had something else to say—something difficult, something he’d long hesitated over. He recalled the success of his lie about the urgent errand and cut through the tension with another falsehood.

“I’m thinking of going away for a little while, Elsie,” he said.

She only replied, “Oh?”

“Somewhere for a change. I need a change. I think I’ll go tomorrow—or the day after. Yes, tomorrow, I think.”

“Yes,” she said.

“I don’t quite know how long I’ll be,” he continued. “I’ll have to let you know when I return.”

“Yes, let me know,” she said evenly.

The tone—unusually even for her—made him uneasy.

“You don:’t ask where I’m going,” he said, trying to lighten the mood.

She stared straight ahead, past the driver.

“I know,” she said.

He froze. “How do you know?”

“You’re not going anywhere,” she replied.

He could not speak.

A minute passed before she continued, in the same controlled voice.

“You’re not going anywhere. You weren’t leaving this morning. You only came out because I appeared. Don’t pretend we’re strangers, Paul.”

A blush rose to his cheeks. He noticed the wind had given her skin the pale pink of early rhubarb. Still, he said nothing.

“Of course, you should go away,” she said. “I don’t know if you often look in the mirror, but you’re rather noticeable. Several people turned to look at you today. So, naturally, you should leave. But you won’t. And I know why.”

He shivered, coughed, then broke the silence.

“If you know, then there’s no point in continuing,” he said curtly.

“Not for me, perhaps—but for you,” she replied.

“Shall I tell you what I know?”

“No,” he said, voice rising slightly.

“No?” she asked, her round eyes fixed on him.

“No.”

Again, irritation stirred in him. Again, he felt the strain. Her love, her devotion, her loyalty—it was unbearable. She humiliated herself—and him. It would have been bad enough if he’d ever given her reason to cling to him… but there it was—the worst part of such lives for women. Women like her—working women, in and out of offices, living freely, taken at face value—then, inevitably, they fell in love. No wonder there was gossip in shops, squares, pubs. In a way, the gossips were right.

Independent, yet not truly self-sufficient; possessing some of womanhood’s charm, yet missing others; half-worldly, yet not wise—Oleron was tired of it all.

It was time he told her.

“I suppose,” he said, trembling, looking down between his knees, “the real trouble lies in the life women who earn their own living are forced to lead.”

He couldn’t tell how she took it. She simply said, “I suppose so.”

“It can’t be helped,” he continued, “but you do sacrifice a great deal.”

She agreed—yes, a great deal. Then, after a moment: “What, for instance?”

“You may be slowly gaining a new status,” he pressed, “but you’re in a false position now.”

“Very likely,” she said. “I hadn’t thought of it that way.”

“And,” he went on desperately, “you’re bound to suffer. Your simplest actions are misunderstood. Motives you never dreamed of are attributed to you. And in the end, it comes to—” He hesitated—then plunged in. “To the sidelong glance. To the leer.”

She understood perfectly. She shivered slightly as she said the name.

“Barrett?”

His silence told her the rest.

Anything further had to come from her. It did, as the bus stopped and new passengers boarded.

“You’d better get off here and go back, Paul,” she said. “I understand perfectly. It isn’t Barrett. You could handle Barrett. It’s convenient to say it’s Barrett. I know what it is—but you said I wasn’t to tell you. Very well. But before you go, let me tell you why I came this morning.”

He asked, dully, why.

She looked straight ahead again.

“I came to force your hand. Things couldn’t go on as they have. And now, it’s all over.”

“All over,” he repeated, blankly.

“All over. I want you to consider yourself, as far as I’m concerned, completely free. I make only one reservation.”

He barely had the strength to ask what it was.

“If *I* merely need *you*,” she said, “please don’t worry. That means nothing to me. I won’t come near for that. But”—she lowered her voice—“if *you’re* in need of *me*, Paul—I’ll know if you are, *and you will be*—then I shall come, no matter the cost. Do you understand?”

He could only groan.

“So that’s understood,” she concluded. “And I think that’s all. Now go back. I suggest you walk—your hands are shaking. Good-bye.”

She gave him a cold hand. He stepped down.

He turned on the curb as the bus pulled away. For the first time in all the years he had known her, she left without a smile, without a wave.

He stood on the curb, lost in sorrow, watching her vanish into the distance—until she was gone. Then, as if a weight had been lifted from his chest, a strange lightness surged through him. She had set him free. Not that he had ever truly been bound; but now, at last, he could move without restraint. The chains of obligation, of expectation, of love—all dissolved. He was unshackled. And with each step toward home, the joy grew. By the time he reached halfway, he already knew what must come next.

The vicar lived within ten minutes’ walk of the square. Oleron turned toward his house, determined to learn everything he could about the old dwelling—the one with the faded insurance marks and the crooked "To Let" signs. The vicar, he believed, would be the best source of knowledge. After all, such matters were not merely architectural or legal—they were rooted in memory, in rumor, in silence.

But the vicar offered little more than whispers. The house was ancient, yes—though that needed no confirmation. It was said to be haunted. Oleron’s ears pricked up. But the vicar shrugged, as though dismissing superstition. “There are few old houses,” he said, “that don’t carry some tale of ghosts among the ignorant.” He added, with a sigh, that modern disbelief only made such stories linger longer, like smoke refusing to fade.

Oleron smiled. “You may leave my nerves out of it,” he said. “How long has the place been empty?”

“A dozen years, I should say.”

“And the last tenant—did you know him? Or her?”

The question seemed to startle the vicar slightly. A flicker passed across his face—something between hesitation and pity. “Him,” he said. “A man. Madley. An artist. A recluse. Never left the house much. And…” He paused, then leaned forward, lowering his voice. “Since you’re clearly seeking this truth—and since it’s better to speak plainly than let falsehoods spread—I’ll tell you: Madley died there. Under unusual circumstances.”

Oleron listened, still.

At the post-mortem, they found no food in his stomach. Not a scrap. Yet he had money. His body was hollowed out—worn thin as parchment. Suicide was suggested, but deliberate starvation? That was rare, even monstrous. No verdict was given. Only an open one.

“Ah,” Oleron murmured.

“And do you have any history of the parish?” he asked.

“No comprehensive record,” the vicar replied. “Only fragments. I’ve compiled notes myself—ecclesiastical records, registers—but it’s a large parish, and I have only one curate. My time is limited.”

They spoke of the parish boundaries, the lack of manpower, the burdens of ministry. Oleron thanked him and took his leave, walking slowly back—not because he was tired, but because he was preparing himself.

Twice he approached his gate, then turned away. Twice he walked another twenty minutes before returning. This was delicate work. He had to clear his mind—not just of thoughts, but of *self*. He needed to return to the moment when he first entered the house after moving in, when he sat down to write the sixteenth chapter of *Romilly*—when the place had spoken to him, faintly, almost imperceptibly.

Then, as now, it had been a slow saturation. But then, he’d been weighed down—by duty, by a woman, by the dead weight of a novel he no longer loved. Now, he was clean. Free. Light. The past was gone. The woman was gone. *Romilly* was dead. And so, perhaps, was the thing that dwelled in silence.

He wanted more than inspiration now. He wanted to *see*.

At half-past two, he unlocked the Yale lock, stepped inside, and closed the door behind him.

And instantly—he felt it.

It wasn’t a presence. Not yet. But a *release*. As if the air itself had changed. Thicker. Brighter. Full of oxygen, or something else—something older, deeper. He laughed aloud. “Oh, you’re pretty, pretty!” he whispered, almost mocking, almost adoring.

He lay down on the couch, letting the quiet fill him. The flowers in their bowls seemed to glow. The meadowsweet-colored panels shimmered. The polished floor reflected the ceiling’s faint starlight. Everything smiled. He smiled back.

He remained there until darkness fell—until the house began to breathe again. The creaks. The groans. The distant tap of unseen feet.

But nothing came.

Not that night. Not the next. He tried to remain receptive—like a plate exposed to light, waiting for an image to form. But the house remained silent.

On the third day, he understood.

A sly smile touched his lips. “Oho, oho,” he chuckled. “Coquettish, are we? Well, we shall see what a little neglect will do.”

He left. Took a train. Spent the day in the countryside, pretending not to care.

He returned after eleven.

“Now, my Fair Beckoner,” he murmured, unlocking the door.

Inside, he moved with perfect control. One candle lit. He yawned, deliberately. Walked through the kitchen, where moonlight painted a blue lozenge on the floor. Closed the powder-closet door. Sat to remove his boots.

Then, into the bathroom. Moonlight cut across the pipes. Into the study. Gazed at the silver roofs across the square. Through the sitting room—silent, barefoot—into the bedroom.

He placed the candle on the chest of drawers. His face wore only fatigue. But beneath it, his mind was sharp, alert, *hungry*.

He drew the blind. Took off his coat. Bent to retrieve his slippers from under the bed.

That was when it happened.

His heart leapt. Then stopped. A terrible pause. His hand found the slippers. But he stayed on his knees. He had not fallen. He did not want to.

The bed was low. His head tilted sideways as he searched. He kept that position—until he regained composure.

When he rose, blood glistened on his lower lip. He had bitten down hard. His watch had slipped from his waistcoat pocket, dangling from its leather guard.

But he was calm again by the time it stopped swinging.

He turned to the mantelpiece. There stood a portrait—his grandmother. He positioned himself so he could see the candle flame behind him, reflected in the glass. He saw the glints from the mirror’s facets, the tiny sparkles dancing in the dark.

And then—something else.

A faint gleam. Moving. Up and down. Not fixed. Not part of the reflection. It was the candle’s light on his black vulcanite comb—*but the comb was not in his hand*.

It was being combed.

He didn’t move. Didn’t blink. He reached slowly for his watch, wound it up, then emptied his pockets—pennies, halfpennies—placing them neatly in a row on the mantel.

The sound filled the room—a soft, electric rustling. Like silk dragged over stone. Like breath caught in a throat.

If he had shifted his gaze, he might have seen the dim gleam trace the outline of his grandmother’s head. But it wasn’t hers.

It was following something invisible.

He finished his task. Then, under cover of a yawn, he spun around.

The combing continued. The comb had shifted angle, moved slightly left. Sweeping up and down—between five feet and the level of the chest of drawers.

Oleron kept acting. He walked to the washstand, poured water, washed his hands. Removed his waistcoat. All while the comb moved.

Then he smiled. “Hm… I think I’ll read for a quarter of an hour,” he said aloud.

He left the room.

Two minutes later, he returned.

Silence.

The comb lay still—between his collar and a pair of gloves.

Without hesitation, he picked it up.

It was ordinary. Eighteen pence. From a chemist’s shop. Made of vulcanite. Of definite weight. Bound by physics. Impossible to defy nature.

Yet it had moved.

He set it down again. Glanced at the papers in his hand—the fifteen chapters of *Romilly*.

He threw them into a chair.

“Hm,” he muttered. “As I thought… She’s just blindly, ragingly, murderously jealous.”

And so, night after night, day after day—how many, he no longer counted—Oleron pursued the unseen. He courted it. Provoked it. Ignored it. Threatened it. Begged it. His life became a single, burning desire—to know what waited in the shadows, what watched from the walls, what combed the air with a voiceless hand.

And still, it did not show itself.

As time passed, fewer and fewer people ascended Oleron’s stairs—save for the postman, whose tread had grown so rare it was heard no more than once or twice a week. Even he brought little news: men who do not write letters receive few in return. A letter arrived from his publishers, inquiring when they might expect the manuscript of his new book. Oleron delayed replying; then forgot entirely. A second letter followed. He ignored that too. No third came.

The weather turned warm and bright. Privet bushes bloomed among the chopper-like notice-boards, and flower-women lined the kerbs with baskets brimming with color in the streets where Oleron shopped. He bought flowers every day—his room demanded them, clamored for them, fresh and renewed without end. Yet even this necessity began to wear on him. The act of stepping outside grew heavier with each passing day. And the relief of returning home swelled into something almost unbearable—a quiet, trembling joy at being shut away again.

He noticed, slowly, that his senses had shifted. Not returned to what they once were, but deepened, stretched—now embracing terror in a form he had never known before. _Lux orco, tenebrae Jovi._ The name of this dread was agoraphobia. Open air, empty space—the thought of exposure to the unseen horror that might spring from behind an unguarded back—filled him with a cold, coiling fear. Soon, he arranged for food and flowers to be delivered daily to his door. He rubbed his hands together when he devised this plan. *That* was better! Now he could choose whether to go out—or not.

And he chose not to.

It became his pleasure to remain immured. But he was not happy. Or perhaps he was—only in a way that twisted like a shadow beneath a moonlit floor. He fretted, restless and weak, sometimes close to tears for no reason at all. Yet he knew, dimly, that he would not trade this sorrow for all the noise and laughter of the world beyond his windows.

Noise itself had become unbearable. The sharp clatter of boots, the distant shout, the shriek of a siren—each struck him like a blow. Worse still was the Sunday ritual beneath his window: the sect Barrett belonged to gathered each morning in the square. They brought drums and brass instruments, their voices raised in anguished cries toward God. Barrett himself, face lifted, eyes closed, brow furrowed, prayed aloud—not for mercy, but for conquest. His voice pierced through Oleron’s walls like a blade.

One day, mid-rhapsody, Oleron yanked down the blind and froze—because in the midst of their fervent cry, his own name had been invoked. A fresh torrent of exorcism, aimed directly at him.

Sometimes, without expecting an answer, Oleron stood still and whispered. Once or twice, he called “Romilly!”—but mostly, his murmurs remained shapeless, wordless, drifting like smoke.

There was one place in his apartment he visited more often than any other: the threshold of his bedroom door. One day, he realized that by opening every door in the house—except the outer one, which he only opened when forced—he could see into all five rooms without moving. From that spot, he saw the full length of the sitting-room, nearly all of the bedroom, glimpses of kitchen, bathroom, and study. He lingered there often, breath held, finger pressed to his lips.

One afternoon, standing there, he suddenly wondered: Had Madley ever discovered this vantage point?

Light, too, had become an enemy. Sunlight, as it traced its path across the rooms, felt like fire in his skull. Diffused light was a dull ache, a numbness. So he began, hour by hour, to lower the crimson blinds—one after another—until each room glowed in a blood-red half-light, like a photographer’s darkroom. These small excursions to darken the windows were daring, but he always left the bedroom door open—just in case.

Soon, lowering the blinds became a ritual. And when he finished his rounds, his apartment pulsed with a deep, silent red.

One day, as he pulled down the blind in his tiny study and backed carefully out, he let out a soft laugh. “_That_ bilks Mr. Barrett,” he said. For a while, the triumph of silencing Barrett’s sermons filled him with quiet glee.

But soon after, he had a fright that left him trembling for an hour. He reached for the cord to darken the window over the seat where he’d found the harp-bag. Standing with his back protected in the embrasure, he thought he saw the tail of a black-and-white check skirt vanish around the corner of the house. He couldn’t be certain—had he run to the opposite window, already blinded, the skirt would have been gone—but he was *almost* sure it was Elsie.

He listened. In agony. Waiting for her footsteps on the stairs.

They never came.

After several minutes, he exhaled slowly. “By Jove, but that would have compromised me horribly,” he muttered. And he repeated it, again and again: “Horribly compromising... no woman would stand that... not *any* kind of woman... oh, absolutely compromising!”

Yet he was not happy. He could not explain the sudden waves of quiet weeping that seized him—unbidden, unexplained, like clouds passing over the square. Perhaps he wasn’t unhappy either. To be truly miserable, something must have been taken from him. But nothing had been taken—because nothing had ever been given. He waited. Waited in his flower-strewn, terrifyingly inviting room, where pith-white walls bled into crimson gloom under the lowered blinds.

He paid no mind to the dwindling banknotes, nor to the fact that he had stopped writing. Stopped? No. He had not ceased to work. Those who thought so knew nothing of Oleron’s true labor. He was only now beginning.

He was creating something vast—something impossible. A Masterpiece. A Mistress. Born not in haste, but in the slow, agonizing gestation of Art. Let him endure this probation, this waiting—let him suffer—then the world would see.

How could they know her, this Fair One of his, until *he* knew her? Radiant creations are not flung off like casual greetings. The artist must weep, as Oleron did. Must swell with false hopes, as Oleron did. Must chase the elusive Spirit—capricious, mocking, slipping just beyond reach—knowing that she delights in the pursuit, that she ensures the chase never ends.

Let Oleron hunt this Huntress a little longer… He would have her sparkling, panting, in his arms yet.

No—those who believed Oleron had stopped working were very far from the truth.

And if all else slipped away from him, he welcomed it. So do we all when the Fair One beckons. At first, we pretend to resist—we promise to keep our distance, to ignore her, to flirt with others. We may even harbor a cruel sprite within us, immune to her charm. But in the end, everything falls away. She calls—and we obey.

So Oleron stayed at his strategic post, within the frame of his bedroom door, watching, waiting, smiling, finger pressed to his lips. It was his duty. His worship. His vow. All he had ever known of love.

And when, now and then, he found himself hating the dead man Madley—wishing he had never lived—he felt that too was a service offered willingly.

But as he prepared himself for a marriage of the soul, growing more restless with each passing day that the Bride made no sign, he made a discovery he should have made weeks earlier.

It came to him through a thought of Madley.

Since that night when, in youthful arrogance, he’d believed a little neglect would bring the Beckoning One to her knees—using her own jealousy to banish her—he had not looked at the fifteen discarded chapters of _Romilly_. He’d thrown them back into the window-seat and forgotten them.

But now, his own jealousy of Madley stirred memories of hers—of her fury toward the flesh-and-blood rival she had tried, twice, to destroy. Fool that he had been! Had he expected his Desire to manifest while proof of his divided heart still existed?

Fool!

If that was all she required—this sacrifice—then he would give it. Quickly.

He retrieved the manuscript from the window-seat and carried it to the fire. He kept the fire burning constantly now—the warmth drew out the last scent of the flowers that crowded his room. He didn’t know the time. Long ago, he’d let his clock wind down. Time meant nothing in the face of what was unfolding.

He knelt before the flames.

But before he could untie the sheets, he jerked his head around, listening.

A sound—soft, barely there—tap, tap, tap. Repeated. Again.

It sent ice through his veins. He stared into the darkness.

Then—outside on the landing—a voice.

“Paul!... Paul!…”

Elsie.

Let her sob.

With his jaw tight, he dropped the first page of _Romilly_ into the fire.

Then the rest—sheet by sheet.

Then—silence.

Nothing.

She was spared. Her rival had allowed her to crawl away, broken and humiliated.

Who showed forbearance? What was forborne?

Ah, Oleron knew.

Contempt, perhaps. But that mattered little. The pestering creature had been allowed to go—unharmed.

Now—now—now for his reward.

Oleron crossed the room. All doors were open. His eyes gleamed as he positioned himself at the bedroom threshold.

Fool that he had been, not to destroy the manuscript sooner.

XI. One knows not whether there can be human compassion for anemia of the soul.

When life’s pitch drops, when the spirit is turned inside out so that what once was sweet and bright becomes grotesque and alien, the bonds of humanity dissolve. The sane mind recoils—not merely to protect itself, but to preserve sanity itself. We are not gods. We cannot exorcise demons. We must only guard against their entry into our own minds. Even love, when it swells within us like a divine tide, cannot override this necessity. To speak of honor or duty then is mere vanity. The letter left on the floor before the darkened door will go unread. If it is seen at all, it will be glimpsed between two abyssal lapses—then forgotten. The telegram remains undelivered. The whistling messenger, guided by some unconscious instinct, walks away unaware that a blind has been drawn aside by a trembling finger, only to be pushed back with horror. Let the wretched wrestle with their own shadows. Let him, if he is mad enough, clutch and bind and embrace the succubus—but do so in a house where no breath of heaven enters, where no ray of sun pierces the thick, foul twilight. The lost must remain lost. Humanity has other duties.

But they meant nothing to him—no more than echoes from a dream long faded. When the telegraph boy pounded on the door, barely feet from Oleron’s bed, he clenched his teeth and塞住 his ears. His strength began to fade. The Abomination fed upon him. The slow draining left him hours at a time, staring blankly at the ceiling, letting idle fancies drift through his mind unresisted. Even his strongest memories held only a fragile grip on his attention. Sometimes a half-formed thought would flicker—of a novel he must write, one vital to his purpose—only to vanish. Other times, entire novels would rise fully formed, radiant and eternal, as if conjured from nowhere. He had found a place he did not intend to leave until they dragged him out—a place others might call sickeningly green, too steeped in decayed things for any living man to dwell in. Yet it was irresistible. It had its own authority, its own presence. Once you stopped resisting, it offered delights beyond imagining. A novel? Someone should write about such a place! There must be endless stories hidden within its walls—if only one could reach the truth beneath the surface. Perhaps it had already been painted by a man named Madley… someone who lived there once. But Oleron had never met Madley. He had a strong sense that he wouldn’t have liked him. In fact, he loathed him—hated him, truly. (Aha! That was a joke.) He seriously doubted Madley had lived the life he ought. Oleron often considered telling the prying moralist across the way about him—but probably the man already knew. Probably he’d made his little prayers and sermons for Madley already. That was his trade. Why, Oleron himself had clashed with him once—over something, a girl, perhaps. Elsie Bengough. Her name came to him now…

Oleron felt uneasy about Elsie Bengough. Not so much because of her, but because of how she persisted in intruding into his thoughts. Whenever she appeared, he drove her away. She was not just a bore—she always had been. Now, her very presence in his imagination threatened the fullness of certain experiences. She had no tact. She didn’t understand that people aren’t always accessible to every thought. She should have known better—that men have private hours, moments when their veins run hot with fire and power, when they may act as Oleron once did with that meddlesome Barrett—shutting others out entirely.

But no. She returned. Always. And when she did, everything collapsed. Bright towers of creation, more magnificent than even the perfect novels he dreamed of, vanished at her intrusion. It was like fog swallowing a star, like a pit opening at the threshold of gold, like a bat-shadow turning dawn to night.

So Oleron fought to silence even the birth of her image. Yet one night—though he could not say exactly when—he felt her come without warning. She was there. No effort could shake her from his mind. She haunted him. At that moment? Of all moments? It was unbearable. How could she endure it? To watch—watch—the triumph of a rival?

Good God. It was monstrous. Tact? Reticence? He had never believed she possessed much of either. But this—this was beyond words. Monstrous. Did she mean to stand there forever? To *look*? Good God. To look on!

Oleron felt blood surge to his scalp. Rage flared. “Damnation take her!” he choked.

Then, instantly, the rage cooled into icy terror. Panic seized him. What had he done? He didn’t know what, but he knew it was irreversible. Fatal. He had felt anger—but not *this*. This blaze of fury that flooded his consciousness with a white, hellish light—this was not his. That rift of searing fire, that terrible hand drawn back to strike—was not his. His was the feeble blow of a child. But *that*—what was *that*? Had *he* set it in motion? Had *he* sparked the dormant power of the place? He didn’t know. Only that his own spark had been snuffed out. And—oh, impossible—what had begun as a clinging kiss on his lips had twisted into a gnashing, a tearing. For pity’s sake, he had to warn her. The woman he had raged against—*guard yourself!*

“**Look out!**” he screamed aloud.

* * * * *

The shock was immediate. As if a cold wave broke over him, he woke. He was in bed. The mist and horror had lifted. He was Paul Oleron. Sick. Naked. Helpless. Utterly abandoned.

His faculties, though weak, responded. He realized it had been a nightmare—some monstrous dream that left him drenched in sweat, shaking.

Yes. He was Paul Oleron. A novelist past his prime. Already descending from the peak of his work, empty-handed. He had failed. The word came whole, complete—no need for thought. He had missed.

Not one happiness, but two. He had missed the comfort of this world—what most men crave—and also the greater prize: the fierce, triumphant grasp of something rare and brilliant, the justification of the reckless adventurer. And there was no second chance. Fate offers no tomorrow.

Oleron’s tomorrow would be to sit again at his desk, writing profitless, poorly done, unwanted work—again and again, as many tomorrows as remained.

He lay there, weak but clear-minded, accepting it.

And since the whole venture had failed, why bother considering salvage? The half-finished novel? Useless. He had promised it for autumn. Contracted for it. But it was better to pay the forfeit than waste what days were left. He was spent. Age loomed. The path ahead was one of wisdom and sorrow—fitting for the remainder of the journey.

If only he had chosen the wife, the child, the faithful friend by the fireside. Let them chase after their *ignis fatuus*—let them follow the false light. He would have stayed.

Yet now, something puzzled him. Why was he so weak? Why did the room reek of rotting plants? And why—when his hand brushed his face in the dark—did it meet a beard?

“Most extraordinary,” he muttered. “Have I been ill? Am I ill now? And if so, why have they left me alone?... Extraordinary…”

He thought he heard a sound—from the kitchen or bathroom. He raised himself slightly on the pillow and listened.

Ah. He was not alone. That would have been strange indeed—left ill and unattended. Alone? No. He would be cared for. If everyone else had abandoned him, Elsie Bengough—his dearest friend—would not. Bless her faithful heart.

Then—a sharp, stifled cry: “**Paul!**”

From the kitchen.

And suddenly, it struck him: minutes earlier, a sound—unnoticed at the time—had tried to reach him. A faint clink of metal on metal. Just like the sound he made when inserting his key into the lock.

“Hallo!” he called sharply from his bed. “Who’s that?”

No answer.

He called again. “Hallo! Who’s there? Who is it?”

Now he was certain—he heard soft, heavy movements in the kitchen.

“This is queer,” he muttered. “By Jove, I’m as weak as a kitten. Hallo, there! Somebody called, didn’t they? Elsie? Is that you?”

He began knocking on the wall beside his bed. “Elsie! Elsie! You called, didn’t you? Please come here—whichever you are!”

A sound—like a door closing. Then silence.

Oleron grew alarmed.

“It may be a nurse,” he whispered. “Elsie’d get me one, of course. She’d sit with me as long as she could spare the time. Brave lass. And she’d bring a nurse for the rest. But it sounded awfully like her voice…”

“Elsie—or whoever it is! I can’t make this out at all. I must go and see what’s wrong…”

He swung one leg over the side of the bed. Feeling its weakness, he reached for the wall.

* * * * *

But before putting out the other leg, he paused. He touched his beard again. Suddenly, he wondered—could he dare go into the kitchen?

It was such a long way. Who knew what horror might leap upon him if he ventured that far? When a man feels the urge to return to bed, he should heed it. Besides—why go? What was there to see? If it was that Bengough creature again, let her fend for herself. Oleron wouldn’t burden himself defending a spoilsport like her. If she was in, let her let herself out—quickly. He simply couldn’t be bothered. He had work to do.

On the morrow, he must begin writing a novel—with a heroine so winsome, capricious, adorable, jealous, wicked, beautiful, inflaming, and altogether evil, that men would stand amazed.

She was coming now. He could feel it—the air changed when she drew near. That soft thrill of bliss stirring within him—only she could summon it. She was beckoning. Beckoning.

He let go of the wall and fell back into bed—as the other half of that interrupted kiss was placed upon his lips—(how else describe it?)—robbing him of breath, of thought, of self.

The June sun beat down on the square, turning the red bricks of the old house into a furnace of light. A crowd had gathered, drawn by something unseen but felt—like a breath of cold air in the heat. They stood close together, faces turned upward toward the windows of the house, its walls scarred with faded insurance marks and its paling lined with wooden "To Let" signs that hung like gallows warnings.

Two constables stood at the broken gate of the narrow alley leading to the house, arms crossed, blocking the way. The women lingered at the edges of the throng, whispering as they shifted their positions, trying to catch sight of the red blinds drawn tight across the upper floor. The children were inside, behind locked doors, safe from whatever this was.

A long-nosed man with a face like dried leather had gathered a small group around him. He repeated his story again and again, each time with more conviction: “I was standing there—where Constable Saunders is now—or rather, I was passing about my business—when they came out. There was no deceiving me. Oh, no deceiving *me*! I saw her face…”

“What was it like, Mr. Barrett?” someone asked.

“Like hers whom our Lord said, ‘Woman, doth any man accuse thee?’—white as paper, and no mistake! Don’t tell *me*!” He paused, letting the silence stretch. “So I walked straight across to Mrs. Barrett and said, ‘Jane, this must stop, and stop at once. We are commanded to avoid evil.’ And she said—‘John,’ she said, ‘it’s four-and-sixpence a week’—that was her exact words.”

“Then I said, ‘Jane, if it was forty-six thousand pounds, it should stop!’” He clenched his jaw. “And from that day to this, she hasn’t set foot inside that gate.”

A hush fell over the listeners. Then, from the back, a voice whispered: “Did Mrs. Barrett ever… *see* anything?”

Barrett turned sharply. “What Mrs. Barrett saw—and what she did not—shall not pass these lips. Even as it is written: keep thy tongue from speaking evil.”

Another man stepped forward. “He was pretty near canned up in the *Waggon and Horses* that night, wasn’t he, Jim?”

“Yes,” replied the second man, “he hadn’t half copped it. Not standing treat much, neither. He was in the bar, all on his own.”

“So he was,” nodded the first. “We talked about it.”

But the fat man with wide, frightened eyes tried again. “She got the key off me—she had the number—came into my shop Tuesday evening…”

No one listened. A heavy labourer grunted, “Shut your heads. She hasn’t been found yet. Here come the inspectors. We’ll know more soon.”

Two inspectors arrived, their boots crunching on gravel as they approached the gate. The fat man rushed forward, breathless. “She bought the key from me! I remember the number—three ones and three threes—11133!”

An inspector waved him aside. “Nobody’s been in?” he asked the constable.

“No, sir.”

“Then you, Brackley—come with us. Smith, keep the gate. A squad’s on its way.”

They entered the alley, passed through the broken gate, and climbed the wide, carved staircase. One inspector kicked aside a pile of dead leaves and crumpled paper outside a door marked with no name. “This don’t look like he’s been out much lately,” he muttered.

“I don’t think we need knock,” said the other. “Break a pane, Brackley.”

The door had two glazed panels. Glass shattered. Brackley shoved his elbow through the hole, reached for the latch, and pulled.

“Faugh!” choked one of the inspectors as they stepped inside. “Let some light and air in, quick—it stinks like a hearse.”

Outside, the crowd watched as the red blinds were raised and the windows flung open. Sunlight poured in, blinding the figure on the bed.

“This is better,” said the inspector, leaning out. “That seems to be the bedroom—Simms, go in while I check the rest.”

The blind had been drawn up. On the bed lay a man—waxy-white, hollow-eyed, emaciated. He held his hands up like shutters against the glare. His mind reeled. Two policemen bent over him, asking where “she” was.

“She?” he croaked. “Who?”

“This woman Bengough—goes by the name of Miss Elsie Bengough. Where is she?”

He shook his head. “I don’t know… I don’t know…”

“No good, Brackley,” said the inspector. “Get him up. Careful with him. I’ll just stick my head out.”

The other inspector searched the study—empty. Then the kitchen. Dead flowers covered the floor, ankle-deep. He kicked them aside, and noticed tracks—shuffling, deliberate—leading to a cupboard in the corner.

The cupboard door had a small square panel, sliding on runners. The inspector reached for the knob and slid it open.

He froze.

Framed in the aperture, slightly tilted forward before jamming, was something wrapped in faded brownish-red frieze—a lumpy mass like a pudding, but heavier, darker. It pulsed faintly.

“Ah,” he breathed.

He didn’t want to touch it. To close the hatch, he’d have to push it back with his hand. Instead, he turned the handle of the cupboard itself.

It resisted. He pulled. It opened only a few inches. He peered inside—then pushed with his shoulder to close it.

As he did, a triangle of black-and-white check skirt protruded from beneath the door, sticking out just above the floor.

He went into the hall. “All right!” he called.

Oleron had been dressed. He still shielded his eyes, confused. The mess—the dead flowers everywhere—the police—the stretcher being fetched—he couldn’t make sense of it. Why was there so much decay? Why was he being moved?

“What’s the matter?” he murmured.

Then he heard the murmur from the square. A four-wheeler stopped outside. An officer leaned in. Oleron whispered something, and the man snapped back a string of words—something about evidence.

They lifted him to his feet. He stumbled, dazed. Down the stairs. Through the alley. The crowd roared—angry voices, shouts, demands for justice. Someone wanted to lynch someone.

Oleron’s attention caught on the fat man again—talking, trembling, telling his story into a notebook.

“I’d seen her with him… they were often together… she came into my shop… said it was for him… I thought it was all right… 111333…”

The people were furious. Police formed a line. But one inspector’s voice cut through—calm, almost gentle. He told someone to get another person into the cab before something else was brought out.

Oleron saw the cab. He saw the hooded stretcher behind it.

They lifted him in. The inspector stood between him and the stretcher—too late. Oleron saw it.

The door remained open. He glimpsed the “To Let” boards among the privet trees. One read: *Key at Number Six.*

Then—silence.

Shuffling footsteps echoed down the alley. The second inspector appeared at the cab door.

“Right away,” he said to the driver.

He climbed in, closed the door, and blocked the window with his back.

Between the two inspectors, Oleron slept.

The cab rolled out of the square. The other vehicle climbed the hill.

The mortuary lay that way.
