Clement stood in the dimly lit oratory, the scent of old parchment and wood smoke hanging heavy in the air. Before him, the hermit of Gouda lay on his deathbed, a steel corselet worn next to his skin, a testament to his tumultuous past. As he prepared to shrive the dying man, Clement couldn't help but feel a sense of unease. The hermit's words, spoken just hours before, echoed in his mind: "A hard fight against the world, the flesh, and the devil, and I had never thoroughly baffled them till I retired into the citadel of Solitude." 

As the hermit's labored breathing slowed, Clement's thoughts turned to his own struggles. His passion for Margaret had left him reeling, and the weight of his own weakness threatened to consume him. He felt like a house built on shifting sands, vulnerable to the whims of the wind. "I love her then better than God," he whispered, the words a bitter taste on his lips. "Better than the Church. From such a love, what can spring to me, or to her?" The thought sent a shiver down his spine. He had always prided himself on his piety, but now he saw it for what it was – a fragile construct, easily shattered by the slightest provocation.

The hermit's words, however, gave him pause. "Let the strong battle temptation; 'tis for the weak to flee." Clement knew he was weak, and that knowledge filled him with a sense of despair. He thought of Margaret, of the life they might have built together, and the pain he would bring upon her by abandoning her. Yet, he also knew that he must flee, for his own sake as well as hers. "What is my penitence, my religion? A pack of cards built by degrees into a fair-seeming structure; and lo! one breath of earthly love, and it lies in the dust."

In the silence that followed, Clement made a decision. He would leave Holland, seeking solitude and a chance to rebuild himself. He would spend years in a distant convent, away from the temptations of the world, and return a wiser, stronger man. By then, Margaret would have moved on, married another, and found happiness.
