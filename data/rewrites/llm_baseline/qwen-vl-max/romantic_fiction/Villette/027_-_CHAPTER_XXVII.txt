The next day unfolded with more activity and liveliness than I had anticipated—though perhaps only my own expectations had been so modest. It was the birthday of one of the young princes of Labassecour, the eldest, I believe, the Duc de Dindonneau, and in his honor, a holiday had been declared at the schools, especially at the prestigious Athénée—the town’s leading college. The students there had prepared a loyal address to present, and for this occasion, they were gathered in the grand public hall where annual examinations and prize-givings took place. After the presentation, a professor would deliver an oration—a *discours*—to mark the event.

Several of M. de Bassompierre’s scholarly friends, many of whom were connected with the Athénée, were expected to attend, along with the respected municipal officials of Villette, including M. le Chevalier Staas, the burgomaster, as well as parents and relatives of the students. M. de Bassompierre had been invited by his peers to join them, and naturally, his daughter Paulina would be included. She sent a brief note to Ginevra and me, inviting us to come early so we could meet her there.

As Miss Fanshawe and I dressed in our dormitory on the Rue Fossette, she suddenly burst into laughter, pausing mid-gesture as she adjusted her clothes and staring at me.

“What is it now?” I asked, curious.

“It just seems so strange,” she replied, her usual blend of honesty and insolence flashing in her eyes, “that you and I should now be on such equal footing—visiting the same circles, sharing the same connections.”

“Indeed,” I said, “I never held much regard for the sort of company you used to frequent. Mrs. Cholmondeley and her set would never have suited me at all.”

“And who *are* you, Miss Snowe?” she asked, her tone open and unguarded, so candid that I couldn’t help but smile.

“You once called yourself a nursery governess,” I reminded her. “When you first arrived here, you actually looked after the children in this house—I’ve seen you carry little Georgette like a nursemaid. Few governesses would stoop so low. And now Madame Beck treats you with more respect than she does the Parisienne St. Pierre—and your proud cousin, no less, calls you her closest friend!”

“Amazing,” I agreed, amused by her playful confusion. “Who am I, then? Perhaps some hidden figure in disguise. Too bad I don’t look the part.”

“I’m surprised you’re not flattered,” she pressed on. “You take it all so calmly. If you truly are the nobody I once thought you were, you must be remarkably composed.”

“The nobody you once thought me?” I repeated, feeling a flush rise to my cheeks—but I refused to let anger take hold. What did it matter how a schoolgirl casually labeled someone as *nobody* or *somebody*? I simply said, “I’ve merely been treated with courtesy. And what is so remarkable about that? Why should kindness throw a person into disarray?”

“One can’t help being struck by certain things,” she insisted.

“By wonders of your own making,” I countered. “Are you ready now?”

“Yes. Let me take your arm.”

“I’d prefer not. We’ll walk side by side.”

She always leaned heavily on me when she took my arm, and since I was neither a gentleman nor her lover, I found it uncomfortable.

“There again!” she exclaimed, as we crossed a large square and entered the quiet, pleasant park on our way to the Rue Crécy. “I meant it as a compliment—to show approval of your dress, your appearance. I wanted to say I’m not ashamed to be seen walking with you.”

“Ah,” I said, “so you meant to declare that if Mrs. Cholmondeley were gazing from her window with her lapdog, or Colonel de Hamal brushing his teeth on a balcony, and happened to catch sight of us, you wouldn’t blush for your companion?”

“Yes,” she answered, her frankness—her greatest strength—giving even her falsehoods a kind of honest clarity. It was, in short, the only thing keeping her character from fading into nothingness.

I left any reply to my expression—my lower lip twitching before words could form. My face spoke scorn, not reverence; the look I gave her carried no trace of deference.

“Scornful, sneering creature!” she snapped as we walked. “No one has ever been such a Turk to me as you!”

“You bring it upon yourself,” I said. “Leave me alone. Be sensible. I will.”

“As if one could leave you alone when you’re so odd, so mysterious!”

“The mystery is entirely in your imagination—nothing more than idle thoughts. Please keep them out of my sight.”

“But are you really *anyone*?” she persisted, pushing her hand under my arm despite my resistance. Her arm pressed tightly against mine, as if trying to force itself into place.

“Yes,” I said. “I’m a rising woman: once a companion to an elderly lady, then a nursery governess, now a teacher.”

“Do tell me who you are,” she urged, clinging to the idea of a secret identity with absurd determination. She squeezed my arm, coaxed, pleaded—until I had to stop in the park and laugh. Throughout the walk, she spun endless variations on this theme, proving by her stubborn belief—or disbelief—that she couldn’t imagine how anyone without wealth, title, or name could maintain dignity. As for me, I found peace in knowing I was known where it mattered. The rest weighed lightly on me. Lineage, social rank, intellectual depth—all occupied the same small corner in my mind: third-class lodgers, given only a tiny sitting room and back bedroom. Even if the grand rooms stood empty, I never admitted it to them, believing modest accommodations better suited their station. The world judged differently—and I suppose it’s right to do so. Yet I still believe I’m not wholly wrong in my view.

There are those whose moral standing crumbles with lowered status, whose self-respect vanishes with lost connections. Are they not justified in valuing position and association as shields against degradation? If a man fears he would despise himself if it were known his ancestors were humble, poor, laborers—not aristocrats, not rich men—would it be fair to condemn him for hiding such truths, trembling at the risk of exposure? The longer we live, the wider our understanding grows. We grow less eager to judge others’ choices, less quick to doubt the wisdom of the world. Wherever defenses are layered—whether guarding virtue or reputation—know that they are needed.

We reached the Hôtel Crécy. Paulina was waiting, and Mrs. Bretton was with her. Under their escort and that of M. de Bassompierre, we were soon led into the assembly hall and seated in good seats, near enough to see the Tribune clearly. Before us, the students of the Athénée were arranged in order. The town’s officials and burgomaster occupied places of honor. The young princes and their tutors sat prominently. The hall was filled with the city’s nobility and leading citizens.

At first, I hadn’t cared who would deliver the speech. I expected some learned man to rise and offer a formal address—half lecture to the students, half flattery to the royals.

The Tribune was empty when we entered. But within ten minutes, it filled abruptly. In a single instant, a head, chest, and arms emerged above the crimson desk. I recognized that head instantly—its shape, color, bearing, expression. The dark, tight skull, the broad pale brow, the blue fire in the eyes—details so familiar, so woven into memory with countless whimsical associations, that their sudden reappearance stirred a flicker of laughter in me. I confess, I laughed until I felt warm—then I lowered my head, pulled my veil down, and let my handkerchief conceal my mirth.

I think I was glad to see M. Paul. It was pleasant, even, to see him there—fierce and frank, dark and candid, testy and fearless—as though he still ruled his classroom. His presence was a surprise. Though I knew he taught Belles-Lettres at the college, I hadn’t expected to see him. With *him* in the Tribune, I knew we wouldn’t suffer through empty formalities or insincere flattery. But what followed—what poured forth swiftly, passionately, continuously—I was utterly unprepared for.

He addressed the princes, nobles, magistrates, and townspeople with the same ease, the same sharp, fiery earnestness, as he used to speak to the three divisions of the Rue Fossette. He spoke to the students not as boys, but as future citizens, as budding patriots. The political upheavals that would later shake Europe had not yet been foreseen, and M. Emanuel’s spirit seemed fresh and new to me. Who would have thought the flat, dull soil of Labassecour could produce such strong convictions, such deep national feeling?

I need not detail his opinions here. But I may say I believed him not only sincere but right. Despite his passion, he was grounded and rational. He crushed Utopian dreams beneath his heel, dismissed wild fantasies with contempt. But when he faced tyranny—ah, then! A light sparked in his eye, one worth seeing. When he spoke of injustice, his voice rang clear and bold, like a trumpet echoing through twilight from the park.

His audience didn’t fully grasp his fire, but some of the students caught it. As he finished, they rose and gave him a long, loud, thunderous cheer. For all his temper, he was their favorite professor.

As we left the hall, he stood at the entrance. He saw me, recognized me, and raised his hat. He offered his hand as we passed and asked, “Qu’en dites-vous?”—a question perfectly characteristic, reminding me even in his moment of triumph of his restless curiosity, his lack of what I considered necessary self-control—among his flaws. He shouldn’t have cared what I thought, or what anyone thought. But he did care. And he was too true to nature to hide it, too impulsive to suppress it. Well—if I faulted his eagerness, I admired his honesty. I wanted to praise him. I had plenty of praise in my heart. But alas—no words came. Who finds words at the perfect moment? I stammered something awkward, and was relieved when others rushed up with enthusiastic congratulations, filling the silence I could not.

A gentleman introduced him to M. de Bassompierre. The Count, equally impressed, invited him to dine with his friends—many of whom were also acquainted with M. Emanuel. He declined dinner, for he was always somewhat reserved around the wealthy. There was a quiet strength in him—a sturdy independence—not flashy, but evident as one came to know him. He promised, however, to visit later with his friend, M. A——, a French Academician.

At dinner, both Ginevra and Paulina looked beautiful—each in her own way. Ginevra had the advantage in physical charm, perhaps, but Paulina shone in subtler, deeper ways: in the brightness and eloquence of her eyes, in her graceful demeanor, in the gentle variety of her expressions. Ginevra’s deep crimson dress complemented her light curls and rosy complexion. Paulina’s attire—simple yet flawless, white and sheer—highlighted the delicate warmth of her skin, the soft animation of her face, the tender depth of her eyes, the rich flow of her dark hair—darker than her Saxon cousin’s, as were her brows, lashes, irises, and mobile pupils. Nature had sketched these features lightly and carelessly in Miss Fanshawe, but in Miss de Bassompierre, they had been refined into exquisite detail.

Paulina was awed by the scholars, yet not silenced. She spoke softly, modestly, with effort—but with such sweetness, such fine intelligence, that her father often paused mid-sentence to listen, his gaze fixed on her with proud delight. It was M. Z——, a courteous and learned Frenchman, who drew her into conversation. I was enchanted by her French—it was flawless: correct structure, true idioms, pure accent. Ginevra, who had lived half her life abroad, could never match it—not because she lacked words, but because she lacked precision and purity. On this point, M. de Bassompierre was pleased; he was a strict critic of language.

Another observer was present—Dr. Bretton, who had arrived late due to professional duties. At the moment he sat down, both ladies were quietly examined by him. That watchful glance returned several times. His arrival stirred Miss Fanshawe, who had seemed listless. She became bright, cheerful, chatted—though rarely on topic, or rather, on topics below the occasion’s dignity. Her light, disconnected chatter might have pleased Graham once—perhaps it still did. Or perhaps it was only fancy that suggested while his eyes were satisfied and his ears fed, his taste, his keen intellect, remained unfulfilled. Certainly, though his attention was taxed, he responded courteously. He showed no irritation, no coldness. Ginevra sat beside him, and during dinner, he focused almost entirely on her. She seemed content and left the table in high spirits.

Yet no sooner had we entered the drawing-room than she sank into silence. She threw herself onto a couch and declared both the speech and the dinner dull affairs, asking her cousin how she could endure such a group of prosaic “gros-bonnets” as her father gathered around him. The moment the men began to move, she stopped speaking, sprang up, and rushed to the piano, playing with spirited energy.

Dr. Bretton, one of the first to enter, took his place beside her. I thought he would not stay long—he had a seat near the hearth that seemed to call to him. But he only glanced at it. While he watched, others moved toward Paulina. Her grace and mind captivated the thoughtful Frenchmen. Her delicate beauty, gentle manner, and natural tact pleased their sensibilities. They gathered around her—not to discuss science, which would have silenced her—but to talk literature, art, life—subjects on which it soon became clear she had read and reflected deeply.

I listened. And I knew Graham heard too—his hearing as keen as his vision. He absorbed every word. I felt the conversation suited him perfectly—pleased him nearly to pain.

In Paulina, there was more strength—of feeling, of character—than most suspected—even Graham himself. More than she would ever reveal to those who didn’t seek to see it. To speak plainly, reader, no true beauty, no real grace, no genuine refinement exists without strength as firm, as complete, as reliable. As you might expect good fruit from a rootless tree, so you might expect lasting charm from a weak soul. For a time, beauty may bloom around weakness—but it cannot withstand a storm. It fades, even under sunlit skies.

Graham would have recoiled if anyone hinted at the strength beneath Paulina’s delicacy. But I, who had known her as a child, sensed the solid roots anchoring her graces to the earth of reality.

While Dr. Bretton listened and waited for a break in the circle, his gaze swept the room. By chance, it landed on me—seated quietly near my godmother and M. de Bassompierre, who were engaged in what Mr. Home called “a two-handed crack,” or what the Count would call a tête-à-tête. Graham smiled, crossed the room, asked how I was, remarked that I looked pale.

I smiled in return—my own thought. It had been nearly three months since Dr. John had spoken to me—three months he didn’t even notice had passed. He sat down, fell silent. He preferred to observe rather than speak. Ginevra and Paulina were opposite him—now he could study both forms, both faces.

New guests had arrived since dinner—ladies and gentlemen alike. Among the men, I had already glimpsed, in passing, a stern, dark, professorial figure hovering in an inner salon, visible only in silhouette. M. Emanuel knew many of the men present, but I believe he was unfamiliar with most of the women—except me. As he looked toward the hearth, he saw me and instinctively moved forward. But seeing Dr. Bretton nearby, he hesitated, then stepped back. If that had been all, no quarrel would have arisen. But he didn’t just stop—he frowned, pouted his lip, and made such an ugly face that I turned away.

M. Joseph Emanuel had arrived, along with his austere brother. At that very moment, he took over from Ginevra at the piano. What a masterful touch replaced her childish jingle! How grand and grateful the instrument sounded under the hands of a true artist!

“Lucy,” Dr. Bretton began, breaking the silence with a smile as Ginevra passed him, glancing sideways, “Miss Fanshawe is certainly a fine girl.”

Of course I agreed.

“Is there,” he continued, “another in the room as lovely?”

“I think there is none as handsome.”

“I agree, Lucy. You and I often agree in opinion, in taste, I think—at least in judgment.”

“Do we?” I said, uncertain.

“I believe if you had been a boy, Lucy—my mother’s godson instead of her goddaughter—we might have been close friends. Our opinions would have blended.”

He wore a teasing air—a light, half-caressing, half-ironic gleam in his eye. Ah, Graham! I’ve spent many solitary moments wondering how you truly saw Lucy Snowe. Was your estimation always kind? Had I possessed wealth and station, would your regard for me have been the same? And yet—I do not mean to accuse. No—you sometimes saddened or troubled me. But my temperament is easily disturbed—like a shadow under a cloud. Perhaps before the scales of justice, I stand more at fault than you.

Still, I struggled to suppress the ache in my heart as I realized that while Graham could give others his deepest, most serious attention, he offered me only light mockery—only jests. So I asked calmly, “On what points do we agree so closely?”

“We both possess a sharp observant faculty. You may not credit me with it, but I do.”

“But you spoke of taste. We may see the same things and judge them differently.”

“Let us test it. Of course, you admire Miss Fanshawe’s merits. Now, what do you think of others? Your mother, for instance—or the gentlemen yonder, Messieurs A—— and Z———or, say, that pale little lady, Miss de Bassompierre?”

“You know what I think of your mother. I haven’t thought of Messieurs A—— and Z——.”

“And the other?”

“I think she is, as you say, a pale little lady—pale, certainly, now, from fatigue and excitement.”

“You don’t remember her as a child?”

“I wonder sometimes whether you do.”

“I had forgotten her. But it’s notable that memories—people, events, even looks or words—can reappear under certain conditions, certain shifts in mind.”

“That’s possible.”

“Yet,” he went on, “the return is incomplete—dream-like, airy. It needs confirmation. A witness is required. Were you not a guest at Bretton ten years ago, when Mr. Home brought his little daughter—whom we called ‘little Polly’—to stay with my mother?”

“I was there the night she arrived and the morning she left.”

“A peculiar child, wasn’t she? I wonder how I treated her. Was I fond of children then? Was I kind—great, reckless, schoolboy that I was? But you don’t remember me, of course?”

“You’ve seen your portrait at La Terrasse. It resembles you. In manner, you were almost the same yesterday as today.”

“But, Lucy, how is that? Such an oracle makes me curious. What am I today? What was I ten years ago?”

“Gracious to whatever pleased you—unkind or cruel to nothing.”

“There you are wrong. I think I was almost a brute to *you*, for example.”

“A brute? No, Graham. I would never have endured brutality.”

“This,” he said, “I do remember: quiet Lucy Snowe tasted nothing of my grace.”

“As little of my cruelty.”

“Oh, had I been Nero himself, I could not have tormented a being as harmless as a shadow.”

I smiled—but I also stifled a groan. Oh, I wished he would leave me alone—stop referring to me. These labels—these attributes—I pushed them away. His “quiet Lucy Snowe,” his “inoffensive shadow”—I returned them, not with scorn, but with deep weariness. Their weight was leaden; let him not burden me with it. Happily, he soon changed the subject.
