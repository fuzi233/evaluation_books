His Excellency the Commander-in-Chief set out to pay his visit to Madam Esmond with a bravery of show that became, as his attendants would have it, the foremost personage in all his Majesty’s colonies in North America. The dragoons clattered first—steel on leather, bits ringing, hooves drumming a hard measure that ran ahead of the coach like a proclamation. Williamsburg answered with its own noise: a loyal shouting that rose and fell in gusts, carried on the warm air, and in it the sharper cries of a chiefly negro crowd whose mirth and awe were both pressed into service for the day.

The General rode in his own heavy, emblazoned coach, which groaned as it took the rutted road, its springs complaining at every plunge. Captain Talmadge, Master of the Horse, kept at the door, and on horseback beside it, as though the vehicle itself were a great creature to be managed. In the front seat sat Major Danvers, and with him the little postmaster from Philadelphia, Mr. Franklin; a man who, though he had begun as a printer’s boy, had a bright, gathering mind, and a store of odd, useful knowledge that made even great gentlemen tolerate being taught.

“’Tis extraordinary,” the Commander-in-Chief observed, touching his hat with a grandeur that cost him nothing, “how a person of such humble origin should have got such a variety of learning, and such politeness of breeding too, Mr. Franklin!”

Mr. Franklin bowed as if he had been bred to bow before he could walk. He said it had been his occasional good fortune to fall among gentlemen like his Excellency, and he had taken advantage to study their honours’ manners and to fit himself, as far as a man might, to the company where Providence placed him. As for education, he could not boast it: his father had been in straitened circumstances, and in New England the advantages were small; yet he had gathered what he could, though it was nothing like what they had in England.

Mr. Braddock burst into laughter, the sound of it filling the coach more completely than his scarlet bulk did. “Education! There are gentlemen in the army, by George, who don’t know whether to spell bull with one b or two. I’ve heard the Duke of Marlborough was no penman. I didn’t serve under him—his Grace was before my time—but he thrashed the French soundly, scholar or no scholar.”

Mr. Franklin said mildly that he was aware of both those facts.

“Nor is my Duke a scholar,” Braddock went on, with that blunt affection for his own opinions which made them feel like blows; “aha, Mr. Postmaster, you have heard that too—I see by the wink in your eye!”

Mr. Franklin withdrew the offending wink with such speed that his face became instantly infant-innocent, his eyes wide and clear upon the General’s jolly, swearing countenance. “He is no scholar, sir,” he said, “but he is a match for any French general that ever swallowed the English for fricassee de crapaud. He saved the crown for the best of kings, the royal father of his Royal Highness—his Most Gracious Majesty King George.”

Off flew Mr. Franklin’s hat, and from the great buckled wig rose a halo of powder, white as flour sifted fine. He spoke of the Duke as the soldier’s best friend, the enemy of beggarly Scotch rebels and Romish Jesuits; and Braddock drank the praise as readily as he drank anything poured, his patriotism as hot as his oaths.

“You shall drink his health to-day, Postmaster,” said the General. “He is the best of masters, the best of friends, the best of sons to his royal old father—the best of gentlemen that ever wore an epaulet.”

“Epaulets are quite out of my way, sir,” Franklin said, laughing; “you know I live in a Quaker city.”

“Of course they are out of your way,” Braddock answered, satisfied to have every man filed into his place like a regiment on parade. “Every man to his business. You, and gentlemen of your class, to your books—welcome. We encourage you. We—to fight the enemy and govern the country. Hey, gentlemen? Lord! what roads you have in this colony, and how this confounded coach plunges! Who have we here, with the two negro boys in livery? He rides a good gelding.”

“It is Mr. Washington,” said the aide-de-camp.

“I would like him for a corporal of the Horse Grenadiers,” the General said, peering. “He has a good figure on a horse. He knows the country too, Mr. Franklin.”

“Yes, indeed.”

“And he is a monstrous genteel young man, considering the opportunities he has had. I should have thought he had the polish of Europe, by George I should.”

“He does his best,” Mr. Franklin said, looking up with that same mildness which, if it mocked, mocked without heat. Outside, the tall Virginian rode up, grave as a church door, followed by two neat negro grooms in smart livery-coats and velvet hunting-caps. The very sound of their horses—light, measured, well-shod—seemed cleaner than the coach’s struggling clank.

“Good day, Mr. Washington,” Braddock called out. “A pretty nag, sir. That was your first affair, last year?”

“That at Fort Necessity? Yes, sir,” Mr. Washington answered, saluting with composed exactness. “I began ill, sir, never having been in action until that unlucky day.”

“You were all raw levies, my good fellow. You should have seen our militia run from the Scotch and be cursed to them. You should have had some troops with you.”

“Your Excellency knows ’tis my passionate desire to see and serve with them,” said Mr. Washington; and though his voice stayed even, there was in it the hunger of a young man who had measured himself against misfortune and wished to be tried again by something better than chance.

“By George, we shall try and gratify you, sir,” Braddock swore, and the coach rolled on toward Castlewood, the dragoons’ hooves and the coach-wheels making a rough music over the country’s bones.

The progress of so great a personage could not be swift; and the road, like a stream gathering tributaries, drew in lesser guests bound for Madam Esmond’s table. None dared pass the General’s carriage; so behind it formed a procession—comical and dutiful in equal parts—raising a single long dust-cloud that hung in the warm air and muffled the sounds of the countryside.

First came Mr. Dinwiddie, the Lieutenant-Governor, with negro servants riding and running near; and with him Parson Broadbent, the jolly Williamsburg chaplain. After them, little Mr. Dempster, the young gentlemen’s schoolmaster, in a great Ramillies wig that made him look like a man dressed for a ceremony rather than a ride. Then Mr. Laws, the judge of the court, with Madam Laws perched behind him on a pillion, and their negro man riding a mule and carrying a box that held her ladyship’s cap, as carefully as if it were a crown.

Major Danvers and Mr. Franklin, spying this tail of dignities, laughed outright—though not so loud as to wake his Excellency, who, rocked by the coach’s violent lurching, had dropped asleep with the easy confidence of a man accustomed to be obeyed. Danvers bade the whole queer rearguard move on and leave the Commander-in-Chief and his dragoons to follow at their leisure.

At Castlewood there was room for all. There was meat and drink and the best tobacco for his Majesty’s soldiers; there was laughing for the negroes; and for their masters a welcome so full that it seemed to stand in the doorway before them, like a lit hearth in winter.

The house itself—broad-porched, familiar to the Virginia sun—did not glitter with any courtly magnificence; yet it had that older splendour which comes of plenty honestly ordered. From the kitchens drifted the steady breathing of labour: the low thump of a cleaver on a block; the hiss where fat met iron; the murmuring stir of spoons against pot-sides; the occasional sharp call in the negro women’s own speech, a cadence different from the English above-stairs, bearing meanings that did not all translate cleanly but that carried the work forward as surely as hands did. Outside, insects played their small incessant instruments in the dusk; within, the clink of glasses and the scrape of chairs began to gather like a second chorus. In such a place, sound itself made a kind of comfort—so long as it was living sound, and not the harsh, inhuman racket of some future world of wheels and engines that no one here could imagine yet.

The honest General required to be helped to most dishes at table, and more than once. He was forever holding out his glass for drink as if it were a duty for others to keep it from emptiness. Nathan’s sangaree he pronounced excellent, and had drunk largely of it before dinner. There was cider and ale, brandy, and plenty of good Bordeaux—some which Colonel Esmond himself had brought home to the colony, and which little Mr. Dempster declared, with learned satisfaction, was fit for pontifices’ feasts. He winked at Mr. Broadbent, and Broadbent returned the wink and nodded, drinking the wine without troubling himself over the Latin. Why should he? He had never made much of scholarship, but he could make a great deal of sport.

Parson Broadbent had the air of a man transplanted by accident into religion from some livelier soil: a guzzling, cock-fighting divine, with a history in the Fleet Prison and at Newmarket and Hockley-in-the-Hole, and sent at last to a Virginia living. He and young George had fought cocks together, taken roe, hauled in shad and salmon, slain wild geese and swans, pigeons and plovers, and destroyed myriads of canvas-backed ducks. Envious tongues said Broadbent was the midnight poacher on whom Mr. Washington once set his dogs, and whom he caned by the river-side at Mount Vernon; that the fellow escaped into a boat in the dark, but went about two Sundays after with the remains of a black eye and a new collar to his coat. Between Broadbent, the schoolmaster, and the wild abundance of Virginia, the Castlewood boys had professors enough. When they rode to get their reading with Mr. Dempster, it was easy to suspect that Harry sometimes stayed behind to take his European “accomplishments” from the other tutor—George going his own way, reading his own books, and telling no tales.

All the birds of the Virginia air and all the fish of the sea in season seemed gathered upon Madam Esmond’s board, and the ordering of it was no mere brute show of wealth. The gumbo was declared perfection—thickened and dark with patient simmering, the okra’s subtle silk binding the broth, the spices blooming in heat until they rose in steam that struck the nose first sweet, then sharp, then deep. Young Mr. George’s black servant had been found once behind a door with his head in a bowl of this delicious hotch-potch, and the late Colonel, grimly amused, christened the boy “Gumbo” on the spot; and thus a dish had become a name, and a name a yoke, in the careless custom of power. The shad were rich and fresh, laid in a dish with their fat shining and their smell of river and salt still living in them. The terrapins came stewed in a grave, savoury liquor, their flesh tender from slow coaxing rather than violence, worthy, as more than one swore, of London aldermen—worthy even, his Excellency deigned to say, of a Duke.

The negro-women had a genius for cookery, and in Castlewood kitchens there were adepts brought up under the keen eye of the late and present Madam Esmond. Certain dishes—especially the sweets and a flan—Madam Esmond prepared herself with neatness and dexterity. She carved several principal pieces too, the kindly cumbrous fashion of the day requiring the mistress to labour for her guests: pressing them with decent eagerness, watching who might be tempted to further enjoyment, knowing the hidden joints and yielding places of fowl and fish as if she had studied anatomy for charity. When she pushed up the laced lappets of her sleeves, she showed the prettiest round arms, small hands and wrists, as she wielded the knife with a practised confidence.

“I have kept for your Excellency the jowl of this salmon,” she whispered to Mr. Braddock. “I will take no denial. Mr. Franklin, you drink only water, sir, though our cellar has wholesome wine which gives no headaches. Mr. Justice, you love woodcock pie?”

“Because I know who makes the pastry,” said Mr. Laws, with a bow so low it might have swept crumbs from the table. “I often say to my wife, ‘My dear, I wish you had Madam Esmond’s hand.’”

“It is a very pretty hand; I am sure others would like it too,” said Mr. Franklin, and Mr. Esmond looked but half-pleased at the little gentleman.

“Such a hand for a light pie-crust,” the Judge continued, hastening back into safer admiration, “and my service to you, madam.”

She answered simply that she had lessons at home in England for her education, and certain dishes her mother had taught her to make, which her father and sons both liked. She was glad if they pleased her company. Then came more dishes, more urging, ten times more meat than the company could need, the old law of hospitality insisting on excess as a proof of heart.

Mr. Washington did not embark much in the general conversation. He sat with Mr. Talmadge and Major Danvers and the Postmaster, deep in talk of roads, rivers, conveyances, sumpter-horses, and an artillery train. On the table before him, bits of bread were laid at intervals—stations and distances—where he set his finger and spoke low and steady, while the others bent in. Yet even this small mapping had its fragility: a negro servant, changing the courses, brushed off the Potomac with a napkin and swept up the Ohio in a spoon, and the whole little campaign vanished as easily as crumbs.

At the end of dinner Parson Broadbent left his seat, went behind the Lieutenant-Governor’s chair, and said grace; then returned and resumed his knife and fork as if devotion were only another course. The sweets and puddings came—Madam Esmond’s careful triumphs—set out with such order and generosity that the eye could taste before the mouth. And then the table being cleared, Nathan, chief manager, laid a glass to every person and filled his mistress’s. Bowing, she said she drank but one toast, but knew how heartily all present would join her.

“His Majesty,” she called, bowing toward Mr. Braddock. The name was echoed loyally by aides-de-camp and colonial gentlemen alike. She drank, saluted them all with a composure that had cost her dear, and then withdrew, between a row of negro servants, making one of her handsomest curtsies at the door.

She had borne her part with admirable spirit, so gay and handsome, speaking with such cheerfulness and courage, that the few ladies present could not but congratulate her upon the elegance of the feast and her manner of presiding. But scarcely were they in the drawing-room when her artificial courage failed. She sank upon the sofa by Mrs. Laws’ side and burst into tears in the midst of a compliment.

“Ah, madam,” she said, “it may be an honour, as you say, to have the King’s representative in my house; and our family has received greater personages than Mr. Braddock. But he comes to take one of my sons away from me. Who knows whether my boy will return, or how? I dreamed of him last night—as wounded, and quite white, with blood streaming from his side. I would not be so ill-mannered as to let my grief be visible before the gentlemen; but you, my good Mrs. Justice—who has parted with children, and has a mother’s heart—would like me none the better if mine were very easy this evening.”

The ladies administered such consolations as seemed proper. Madam Esmond tried not to give way further, remembering she had still duties to perform before sinking into her own sadness.

“It will be time enough,” she said, wiping her eyes and forcing her voice back into steadiness, “to be sorry when they are gone. My boy must not see me following him with a wistful face, and have our parting made more dismal by my weakness. It is good that gentlemen of his rank and station should show themselves where their country calls them. That has always been the way of the Esmonds; and the same Power which graciously preserved my dear father through twenty great battles in the Queen’s time, I trust and pray, will watch over my son now his turn is come to do his duty.”

And now, instead of lamenting, she sat down with her female friends to a pool of cards and a dish of coffee—coffee dark and fragrant, its bitterness softened with sugar, the steam rising in little ghosts that carried comfort to the nose before it reached the tongue. In the neighbouring parlour the gentlemen still called their toasts and drank their wine, the laughter and loud talk swelling and sinking like surf against the walls. When one lady objected they had sat rather long, Madam Esmond answered that it would improve and amuse the boys to be with English gentlemen; such society was rare in a distant province; and though their conversation sometimes was free, she was sure men of fashion would have regard to youth and say nothing before her sons which young people should not hear.

It was evident the English gentlemen relished the good cheer. Whilst the ladies were yet at their cards, Nathan came in and whispered Mrs. Mountain. Mrs. Mountain at first cried out that she would give no more—the common Bordeaux they might have, and welcome, if they still wanted more—but she would not give any more of the Colonel’s.

It appeared the dozen bottles of particular claret had already been drunk up by the gentlemen, “besides ale, cider, Burgundy, Lisbon, and Madeira,” Mrs. Mountain said, counting the depleted riches with the dismay of a housekeeper who hears the pantry emptying itself.

But Madam Esmond would have no stint in the hospitality of the night. Mrs. Mountain was forced to bustle away with her keys to the sacred vault where the Colonel’s particular Bordeaux lay—surviving its master, who had long since gone underground. As they went, she asked whether any of the gentlemen had had too much. Nathan thought Mister Broadbent was tipsy—he always tipsy. Then he thought the General gentleman was tipsy. And he thought Master George was a lilly drunk.

“Master George!” cried Mrs. Mountain. “Why, he will sit for days without touching a drop.”

Nevertheless Nathan persisted: Master George was a lilly drunk. He was always filling his glass; he had talked; he had sung; he had cut jokes—especially against Mr. Washington—which made Mr. Washington quite red and angry, Nathan said.

“Well, well!” Mrs. Mountain cried, eagerness overcoming prudence. “It is right a gentleman should make himself merry in good company, and pass the bottle along with his friends.” And she trotted all the faster to the particular Bordeaux.

The tone of freedom—of something almost like impertinence—which young George Esmond had of late adopted toward Mr. Washington had deeply vexed and annoyed that gentleman. There was scarcely half a dozen years’ difference between him and the Castlewood twins; yet Washington had always been remarked for discretion and sobriety beyond his time, while the boys, long under their mother’s anxious tutelage, still carried about them the heedlessness of sheltered youth. They had looked up to their neighbour of Mount Vernon as guide, director, friend; indeed almost everyone did who came in contact with the simple, upright young man. Scrupulously grave and well-bred, he seemed in speaking with other folks to exact—if not by design, then by the mere weight of his nature—the same behaviour. Levity and jokes fell dead in his presence, as if embarrassed. He was slow to comprehend them; and when they did reach him, they had no place to rest.

Years afterward, Harry Warrington would write of him with a kind of awe: how Washington seemed always great; how to see him ride to hounds was like watching him charge an army; how if he fired a shot the bird must fall, and if he flung a net the largest fish must be in it; how his words were few but wise, grave, sober, strong, never idly spilled. Even George, for all his antipathy, respected and admired the man more than any other mortal.

Mr. Washington was the first to leave the jovial party doing so much honour to Castlewood’s wine. Young George, taking his mother’s place when she rose, had been free with glass and tongue. A score of little sayings—sharp-edged, half-laughing, half-stinging—had been aimed at their guest, and each one, because it came without cause, left Washington without reply. At length, angry beyond endurance, he rose and walked away through the open windows into the broad verandah that belonged to Castlewood as to all Virginian houses.

There the air met him—warm, smelling of crushed grass and distant river, and underneath it the kitchen’s faint savour of spice and smoke. Crickets kept their insistent tiny fiddling. The gentlemen’s laughter behind him was louder, coarser, threaded with the clink of glasses and the scrape of chairs. It was not merely loud; it was the wrong kind of loud—sound that trampled rather than accompanied. Washington strode up and down before the windows, as if his long body might walk off the heat in his blood.

Madam Esmond caught sight of him. The evening was warm, and perhaps her game was over; at any rate she gave up her cards to another lady and went out to her neighbour. He tried to compose his countenance; it was impossible he should explain to his hostess why and with whom he was angry.

“The gentlemen are long over their wine,” she said. “Gentlemen of the army are always fond of it.”

“If drinking makes good soldiers,” Mr. Washington answered, “some yonder are distinguishing themselves greatly, madam.”

“And I dare say the General is at the head of his troops?”

“No doubt, no doubt.” He received her remarks—playful or serious—with a peculiar softness and kindness that belonged to him with women, and with this woman in particular. “But the General is the General, and it is not for me to make remarks on his Excellency’s doings at table or elsewhere. I think very likely military gentlemen born and bred at home are different from us of the colonies. We have such a hot sun, that we need not wine to fire our blood as they do. And drinking toasts seems a point of honour with them. Talmadge—hiccupped to me—I should say, whispered to me just now, that an officer could no more refuse a toast than a challenge. He said it was after the greatest difficulty and dislike at first that he learned to drink. He has certainly overcome his difficulty with uncommon resolution.”

“What, I wonder, can you talk of for so many hours?” asked the lady.

“I don’t think I can tell you all we talk of, madam; I must not tell tales out of school. We talked of the war—of the force Mr. Contrecoeur has, and how we are to get at him. The General is for making the campaign in his coach, and makes light of it and the enemy. That we shall beat them, if we meet them, I trust there is no doubt.”

“How can there be?” she said, her father’s old Marlborough blood speaking through her confidence.

“Mr. Franklin—though he is only from New England—spoke great good sense, and would have spoken more if the English gentlemen would let him. But they reply invariably that we are only raw provincials, and don’t know what disciplined British troops can do. Had they not best hasten forward and make turnpike roads and have comfortable inns ready for his Excellency at the end of the day’s march? ‘There’s some sort of inns, I suppose,’ says Mr. Danvers, ‘not so comfortable as we have in England: we can’t expect that.’ ‘No, you can’t expect that,’ says Mr. Franklin, who seems a very shrewd and facetious person. He drinks his water, and seems to laugh at the Englishmen; though I doubt whether it is fair for a water-drinker to sit by and spy out the weaknesses of gentlemen over their wine.”

“And my boys? I hope they are prudent?” Madam Esmond asked, laying her hand on his arm. “Harry promised me; and when he gives his word, I can trust him for anything. George is always moderate. Why do you look so grave?”
