“The old oddity’s vanished, left for Arezzo the very next morning—couldn’t stomach the stench of the French, I reckon, after being their captive. I stopped by the hospital to ask after him, wanted to see if those broth-dispensing monks had figured out if he was mad or merely wandering. They said no signs of frenzy at all, only that he ignored every question, like his mind was miles off tending a vineyard twenty miles outside the city. A proper mysterious old tiger, that one. I’d give a pretty penny to know more of his story.”

It was Piero di Cosimo, the sharp, idiosyncratic painter, speaking in Nello’s barber shop on the 24th of November, exactly one week after King Charles VIII of France had marched his troops through Florence’s gates. Six or seven regulars lingered in the cluttered, soap-scented space at the unheard-of hour of three in the afternoon, for the whole city hummed with electric tension, all waiting word of a fateful final decision on the treaty with the French king. Every public lounging spot was packed, every shopkeeper without a wife or trusted deputy to mind his counter stood propped in his doorway, thumbs hooked in his belt. Artisans drifted through the streets like loose splinters bobbing on a current, pausing mid-step to peer towards the piazza, ready to surge forward in a rush the second any exciting news broke. Nello had been half-perched on a wooden sill against the shop window, strumming his lute and keeping a constant eye fixed on the square outside.

“Ah!” he exclaimed, setting the lute down with a sharp thud of emphasis. “I’d not trade a gold florin for having missed the sight of those French soldiers waddling in their absurd broad-soled boots after their runaway prisoner! That’s what I get for stepping away to shave the chins of grand men. It never fails: the second I leave this little navel of the world, something remarkable happens right outside in my piazza.”

“Aye, you should have been there,” Piero retorted in his usual biting dry tone, “just to watch your favourite Greek scholar look so terrified you’d think old Satan himself had a grip on his coat. I love seeing those smooth, ever-smiling gentlemen of yours caught off guard by a sudden gust of trouble, forced to show their true colours no matter how hard they try to hide them. What colour do you think a man’s liver turns, that goes as pale as a bleached deer the moment a stranger grabs him unawares?”

“Piero, save that vinegar of yours as sauce for your own dinner!” Nello laughed. “What fault is it of my *bel erudito*’s that he looked startled when a pair of rough claws clamped onto him and he saw an unchained madman at his elbow? Your scholar is nothing like those brutish Swiss and German mercenaries, whose heads are only good for ramming down walls, whose appetites are so huge they’d think nothing of swallowing a cannonball for breakfast. We Florentines value more in a man than that vulgar stuff they call bravery, the kind you can buy by the dozen for a few coins a head. I tell you: the second men realized they had more wits than oxen, they set the oxen to pull their carts. When we Florentines realized we had more wits than other men, we set other men to do our fighting for us.”

“Treason, Nello!” a voice called out from the back parlour. “That’s not the state’s doctrine! Florence is sharpening her weapons, and the Frate’s most recent verified vision showed Mars standing atop Palazzo Vecchio, arm slung over the shoulder of Saint John the Baptist, who was offering him a piece of honeycomb.”

“Fair enough, Francesco,” Nello waved a hand dismissively. “Florence has a few thick-skulled lads who’ll do just fine bombarding Pisa. The finer wits can stay home to do the thinking and the shaving. And if Piero here is so very set on valour, let him grab his biggest paintbrush for a weapon and his palette for a shield, and challenge the loudest-mouthed Swiss brute he can find in the Prato to single combat.”

“Pah, Nello,” Piero growled. “Your tongue runs nonstop, like a mill when the Arno’s in flood—grist or no grist, it keeps spinning.”

“Plenty of good grist, I promise you! It’s every bit as ridiculous to expect a grizzled old painter like you to run willingly at a javelin as it is to expect a man who’s honed his wits on the classics to enjoy having his handsome face clawed by a wild beast.”

“There you go again, pretending people will slip their legs into a sack just because you call it a fine pair of hose,” Piero shot back. “Who said anything about wild beasts, or unarmed men rushing headlong into battle? Fighting is a trade, and it’s not my trade. I’m no fool to go chasing danger, but I’d face it straight on if it came for me.”

“Then how is it you hide under the table with the rats the second thunder starts?” Nello teased, determined to catch the painter out. “You, of all people, ought to understand why a man might shake at something that seems trivial to others—you, who scurry for cover the moment a storm rumbles.”

“That’s a peculiar sensitivity to loud noise, nothing to do with my courage or my conscience,” Piero huffed, stuffing his ears with tow from his leather pouch in indignant contempt.

“Well then! Tito Melema may have a peculiar sensitivity to being grabbed unawares by prisoners fresh from running away from French soldiers,” Nello went on triumphantly, drawing a round of laughter from the others. It was clear Piero’s grudge against Tito was not shared by the group. “Men are born with all kinds of silly antipathies: I myself can’t stand the smell of mint. Tito was born with an antipathy to old stumbling prisoners who reach out and clutch people. There! Makes perfect sense.”

“By the by,” Francesco Cei spoke up, “did you hear Camilla Rucellai has outdone the Frate at his own prophecy game? She predicted two years ago that Pico della Mirandola would die in the time of lilies. He died in November, so the scoffers mocked her. ‘Nonsense,’ Camilla said, ‘the lilies I meant are the lilies of France, and they’re sitting right under your noses now.’ Euge, Camilla! If the Frate can show a single one of his visions came true that neatly, I’ll turn Piagnone tomorrow.”

“You’re too flippant about the Frate, Francesco,” the scholarly Pietro Cennini put in gently. “We all owe him a great debt these weeks, for preaching peace and calm, for telling the people to set aside their old party feuds. Only fools would be glad to see the people slip the leash of his guidance right now. And if the Most Christian King digs in his heels today and refuses to sign a treaty fair and honourable to Florence, Fra Girolamo is the only man we can trust to bring him round.”

“You speak true, Messer Pietro,” Nello nodded. “The Frate is one of the sturdiest nails Florence has to hang her hopes on—at least, that’s the opinion of every most respectable chin I’ve had the honour of shaving. But young Messer Niccolò was saying here the other morning, and I suspect Francesco means much the same: visions stretch as easily as Dido’s oxhide. A dream can mean whatever happens to come after it. As our Franco Sacchetti wrote: a woman dreams a serpent bit her overnight, breaks a drinking cup the next day, and cries ‘See! I knew something bad would happen—now it’s obvious the serpent meant the cup!’”

“The Frate’s visions are nothing of the sort,” Cronaca cut in. “He doesn’t just say vague things—he says the Church will be scourged, renewed, and all heathens converted, and that it will happen soon. He’s no slippery pretender hiding behind loopholes, he’s—”

“What’s that? What’s all that noise?” Nello yelped, leaping off the sill and sticking his head out the shop door. “People are flooding into the piazza, shouting. Something must have happened in Via Larga! Aha!” He burst out laughing, waving his cap above his head in delighted surprise.

The rest of the group scrambled to the door to see. News from Via Larga was exactly what they’d all been waiting for, but no one expected it to arrive the way it did. Hoisted high above the crowd, perched on a wrenched-up street bench, sat Tito Melema himself, grinning in amused embarrassment at being manhandled by the cheering people. His cap had slipped off his head, hanging loose by the soft silk becchetto wrapped around his neck, and when he spotted the group gathered at Nello’s door, he lifted his fingers in a playful beckoning wave. A moment later he had leaped from the bench onto a cart stacked high with wool bales parked in the wide space between the Baptistery and the Duomo steps, the crowd swarming around him as noisy and eager as chickens gathered for feed. The second he opened his mouth in his clear, rich, melodic voice, the noise died completely.

“Citizens of Florence! I have no official right to share this news, save for the fact that you all want to hear it. But it is good news, and no harm will come of speaking it aloud. The Most Christian King is signing a treaty honourable to Florence. And you owe that victory to one of your own citizens, a man who spoke a word worthy of the ancient Romans: you owe it to Piero Capponi!”

A roar of voices exploded at once: “Capponi! Capponi! What did our Piero say? We knew he wouldn’t stand to be passed back and forth like a letter between Herod and Pilate!”

When the shouts died down just enough, Tito spoke again: “The Most Christian King made demands far too unfair, dug in his heels, and finally declared ‘I will order my trumpets to sound for attack.’ Then, citizens of Florence, your Piero Capponi spoke with the full voice of a free city: ‘If you sound your trumpets, we will ring our bells!’ He snatched the copy of those dishonourable terms right out of the secretary’s hands, tore it to shreds, and turned to walk straight out of the royal presence.”

Loud shouts burst out again, the crowd clamouring for more details. “Then, Florentines, the great majesty of France felt, perhaps for the very first time, all the weight of the majesty of a free people. The Most Christian King himself rushed from his throne to call Piero Capponi back. The great spirit of your city did its work with a single brave word, no need for the armed force all of you had waiting behind your walls. The King has agreed to sign a treaty that defends Florence’s honour and her safety both. The banner of France will fly above every Florentine galley as a sign of friendship and shared privilege—but written in letters above that French banner will be one word: *Liberty*!”

As the shouts rose again, Tito glanced over the crowd with quiet, amused contempt. Every last man there was swelling with the conviction that Piero Capponi had spoken for him personally, that he was the very mind behind Capponi’s bold words. He delighted in the absurdity of the moment: he, a Greek foreigner, a former associate of the exiled Medici, had been transformed in the space of ten minutes into a popular orator feeding the crowd’s hunger for a hero of liberty. He was glad the crowd had grabbed him and swept him off the street as he left the palace in Via Larga with a message for the Signoria. It was all so easy, so pleasant, to speak and leave every party convinced you were on their side. A man who knew how to persuade never need fear trouble. The grinning, excited faces of the weavers, dyers, and wool-carders below him were wonderfully entertaining to watch from his perch atop the wool cart.

Tito was just settling comfortably into that easy confidence, feeling his old unflappable self, one hand resting on his recovered cap and the other hooked at his belt, his long dark eyes glinting with a complacent smile, ready to bow to the crowd before leaping down—when his gaze locked onto a face at the far edge of the throng that was nothing like the jubilant artisans surrounding it. The man was clean-shaven, his hair cropped short, wearing a plain, decent felt hat. Any other person would have barely recognised him as the escaped prisoner who had grabbed him on the palace steps, but Tito knew him at once. It was Baldassarre. The sight of that face, long thought lost to him, shot through him like a searing fiery arrow, every memory of his old betrayal flashing across his mind in a single heartbeat. He leaped down from the cart immediately, fast enough that no one noticed the flicker of terror cross his face. He told himself he had moved fast anyway, that he was in a hurry to deliver his message to Palazzo Vecchio, that he had given no sign of panic. He would be ready for this face now, he told himself, ready for it to pop up like a recurring blot in diseased vision, over and over. But seeing Baldassarre shaven, neatly dressed, looking sane and steady, stripped away all his excuses that the old man was mad. If Baldassarre was in full possession of all his wits, all his old knowledge, why was he lingering? It had to be to make his revenge perfect. Flight, Tito thought suddenly, flight is the only way out. He turned his back on the crowd, his smug new role as a popular hero completely forgotten, his whole mind caught up in the dark, narrow private terror of the man hunting him.

All the while, the crowd Tito had stoked so casually had no idea of the secret fear he carried away with him. The people were just turning to head toward Via Larga, when four mace-bearers stepped into the piazza from Via de’ Martelli, announcing the approach of the city’s highest dignitaries. They were the syndics, the commissioners who had negotiated the treaty. Piero Capponi himself would be with them. The crowd fell completely still, voices softening, until the silence was so total the crisp tread of their feet on the stone pavement and the rustle of their heavy black silk robes sounded like soft rain falling in the dark of night. The crowd did not wait for the learned lawyers Vespucci and Bonsi, nor for Francesco Valori, popular as he had become in recent weeks. This moment belonged to Piero Capponi, the stolid, resolute merchant-soldier who cared nothing for flattering the crowd, only for preserving order and Florentine freedom. Only when he stepped fully into the piazza did the silence break, and a single thunderous roar rolled over the square: “Capponi! Capponi! Well done, Capponi!”

The plain, unpretentious warrior looked around at the cheering faces, grave quiet joy softening his stern features. Two years later, he would die in battle, and the city would give him a grand state funeral, torches carried by every magistrate, rows of banners fluttering over his bier. No record says he felt any joy at the orations spoken in his honour that day. We may be glad he got to feel the warm, living praise of his fellow citizens while he still walked among them, in that bright moment of triumph in the Piazza del Duomo.
