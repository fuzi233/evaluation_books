Clement heaved a quiet sigh, the sort a man draws when he feels the ground shift beneath rules he has trusted. He began to doubt whether he had chosen the wisest course with a creature so vehement, so swift in feeling that her very body seemed to leap before her judgment. Yet young as he was, he had already learned one craft of the cloistered mind: to pause—there are storms in which the safest pilot neither rows nor speaks until the water shows its own channel.

So he held his peace and prayed for wisdom. He did nothing but gently draw back his foot.

But his penitent, with a cry that went through the chamber like a knife on stone, flung both arms about it and clung as if it were a plank in deep water. Her tears ran hot over his instep; her whole frame shook. Shame, not less than penitence, broke over her like a sudden flush of wine: the red rose up past her cheeks and crept down even to her throat as she lay quivering at his feet. The small sounds—the hitching breath, the wet fall of tears on leather, the rustle of her gown against the floor—filled the room more than any sermon could have done. Even the house noises beyond, muffled as they were, seemed to hush, as if the air itself had learned reverence.

“My daughter,” said Clement at last, very gently, “take courage. Torment thyself no more about this Gerard, who is not. As for me, I am Brother Clement, whom Heaven hath sent to thee this day to comfort thee, and help thee save thy soul. Thou last made me thy confessor; I claim, then, thine obedience.”

“Oh, yes,” sobbed she, her voice breaking upon the word like bread pulled too fast from the loaf.

“Leave this pilgrimage, and instant return to Rome. Penitence abroad is little worth. There where we live lie the temptations we must defeat, or perish; not fly in search of others more showy, but less lethal. Easy to wash the feet of strangers, masked ourselves; hard to be merely meek and charitable with those about us.”

“I’ll never—never lay finger on her again,” she gasped, as if that one promise were the last rung of a ladder.

“Nay,” said he, “I speak not of servants only, but of dependents, kinsmen, friends. This be thy penance: the last thing at night, and the first thing after matins, call to mind thy sin, and God His goodness; and so be humble and gentle to the faults of those around thee. The world it courts the rich; but seek thou the poor: not beggars; these for the most are neither honest nor truly poor. But rather find out those who blush to seek thee, yet need thee sore. Giving to them shalt lend to Heaven. Marry a good son of the Church.”

“Me? I will never marry.”

“Thou wilt marry within the year. I do entreat and command thee to marry one that feareth God. For thou art very clay. Mated ill thou shalt be naught. But wedding a worthy husband thou mayest, Dei gratia, live a pious princess; ay, and die a saint.”

“I?” She said it as if the syllable were a strange tongue in her mouth—untranslatable to the life she had imagined for herself.

“Thou.”

He bade her rise and go about the good work he had set her. She lifted herself to her knees; then, with a motion at once timid and resolute, she took off her mask. Her face, freed from that dumb covering, spoke more than any courtly rhetoric: she cast on him one long, eloquent look—such a look as women give when they would pour out a whole life and are forbidden speech—then lowered her eyes, obedient, meek.

“I will obey you as I would an angel. How happy I am, yet unhappy; for oh, my heart tells me I shall never look on you again. I will not go till I have dried your feet.”

“It needs not,” said Clement. “I have excused thee this bootless penance.”

“’Tis no penance to me.” She leaned nearer, and her voice, still wet with tears, softened to a pleading that had almost the warmth of a hearth. “Ah! you do not forgive me, if you will not let me dry your poor feet.”

“So be it then,” said Clement, resigned; and in his heart, with that half-humorous weariness monks allow themselves in secret, he thought, Levius quid foemina.

Yet these weak creatures, that gravitate towards the small as heavenly bodies towards the great, have their own sudden illuminations—swift as the flash of a knife in lamplight. She fetched the linen and did her office with a care that was almost ceremonial. The cloth, clean and faintly scented, drew the damp from his skin and the road-dust from the seams; her hands, though trembling, moved with the steadiness of one who has watched kitchens and chambermaids and knows how stubborn grime sits in the creases. The little rasp of fabric on leather, the gentle tap as she set his boot aside, were homely sounds—and in that homeliness, strangely, the act grew more human than any pageant of penitence abroad. It was as if she were being taught, by touch and smell and sound, that holiness must live in rooms where people eat and sleep and weep.

When she had done, she looked up. Tears stood in her beautiful eyes, and she murmured—so tenderly, so simply, that the words seemed to come not from pride abased but from pity awakened:

“I will have masses said for her soul. May I?” she added, timid as a child asking leave to give bread away.

A faint blush rose in the monk’s cheek; the cold blue of his eye moistened. It came so suddenly from one he had been ready to rate so low, and the thought carried with it the weight of names—how a soul may be remembered or forgotten, how prayer keeps a person’s story from being swallowed by silence.

“It is a gracious thought,” said he. “Do as thou wilt: often such acts fall back on the doer like blessed dew. I am thy confessor, not hers; thine is the soul I must now do my all to save, or woe be to my own. My daughter, my dear daughter, I see good and ill angels fighting for thy soul this day—ay, this moment; oh, fight thou on thine own side. Dost thou remember all I bade thee?”

“Remember!” said she, and the word came out with a fervour that was almost fierce. “Sweet saint, each syllable of thine is graved in my heart.”

“But one word more, then. Pray much to Christ, and little to his saints.”

“I will.”

“And that is the best word I have light to say to thee. So part we on it. Thou to the place becomes thee best, thy father’s house; I to my holy mother’s work.”

“Adieu,” faltered the princess. “Adieu, thou that I have loved too well, hated too ill, known and revered too late; forgiving angel, adieu—for ever.”

He caught her words, though they were breathed rather than spoken, like a sigh lost in a chapel.

“For ever?” he cried aloud, and the ardour in him sprang up like fire taking dry flax. “Christians live ‘for ever,’ and love ‘for ever,’ but they never part ‘for ever.’ They part, as part the earth and sun, to meet more brightly in a little while. You and I part here for life. And what is our life? One line in the great story of the Church, whose son and daughter we are; one handful in the sand of time, one drop in the ocean of ‘For ever.’ Adieu—for the little moment called ‘a life!’ We part in trouble, we shall meet in peace: we part creatures of clay, we shall meet immortal spirits: we part in a world of sin and sorrow, we shall meet where all is purity and love divine; where no ill passions are, but Christ is, and His saints around Him clad in white. There, in the turning of an hour-glass, in the breaking of a bubble, in the passing of a cloud, she, and thou, and I, shall meet again; and sit at the feet of angels and archangels, apostles and saints, and beam like them with joy unspeakable, in the light of the shadow of God upon His throne, FOR EVER—AND EVER—AND EVER.”

The words rang through the room; even after his voice ceased, it seemed the air kept the echo, as a cloister keeps the last note of a chant. Outside, some distant sound—a footfall, a door settling, a muted call—came back into hearing, and with it the world’s ordinary life resumed, as if the great and the daily were stitched together without seam.

And so they parted: the monk erect, his eyes turned heavenwards and glowing with the sacred fire of zeal; the princess slowly retiring, turning more than once to cast a lingering glance of awe and tender regret upon that inspired figure.

She went home subdued, and purified.

Clement, in due course, reached Basle and entered on his duties—teaching in the University, and preaching in the town and neighbourhood. He led a life that can be comprised in two words: deep study, and mortification. My reader has already a peep into his soul. At Basle he advanced in holy zeal and knowledge, and the brethren of his order began to see in him a descendant of the saints and martyrs.
