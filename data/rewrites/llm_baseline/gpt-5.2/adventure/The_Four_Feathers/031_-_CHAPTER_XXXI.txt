On an August morning of that same year Harry Feversham rode across the bridge over the Lennon and into Ramelton.

The Soudan had baked him to a darker shade than the folk of Donegal remembered, and the long probation of his years had thinned his cheeks and hollowed his eyes. He went up the narrow street unrecognised, the iron-shod hooves ringing a hard, clean note on the stones—one sound among many: the far lace of the river under the bridge, a cartwheel grumbling somewhere behind a wall, the soft, tireless hiss of wind in the hedges. The town lay quiet as a kitchen before the first pan is set to the fire.

At the top of the hill he turned into the broad highway that runs, in one straight line, down through valleys and up over the shoulders of hills towards Letterkenny. He rode quickly, and yet it was as though he travelled in company. Not men beside him—shadows. The years between had been falling away from his mind all through his road across Egypt and the Continent, flaking off like old soot from a pot scoured too often. They were no more than visions now. He was not occupied with dreams of what might have been had he acted otherwise; the might-have-beens had grown thin and ghostly.

What had been here in this small Irish town was not thin at all. It had weight and taste. Here he had been most happy; here he had known the uttermost of misery. Here his presence had brought pleasure; here too he had done his worst harm.

Once he checked his horse opposite the church set high above the road on his right, and wondered whether Ethne was still at Ramelton—whether old Dermod Eustace lived, and what manner of welcome might await him. But the thought broke against the plain fact of where he was: sitting astride a sweating horse in an empty road on a clear August morning.

Sound came to him as it always does to a man who has been too long in the hush and thunder of alien deserts. Larks stitched their bright song into the pale blue overhead; a landrail rasped from the meadow on the left; and from the valley below the crow of a cock rose sharp and domestic, as if someone had just opened a back door and thrown down grain. Those familiar notes steadied him more than any argument. He looked about him and rode briskly down the incline and up the ascent beyond.

Again the road filled with his ghosts—phantoms of people with whom upon this very stretch he had walked and ridden and laughed; ghosts of old thoughts, and remembered words, and the particular tones in which they had been spoken. He came to a thick grove of trees, a broken fence, and a gateway with no gate. Without attending to these small signs of abandonment, he turned in and rode along a drive grown weedy and neglected, where nettles leaned over the ruts and the branches had begun to meet above like hands clasped too late.

At the end of it he came out upon an open space before a ruined house.

The sight of the tumbling walls and the rooms open to the sky struck him as a slap of cold water. He swung down, tied his horse to the branch of a tree, and ran into the house calling aloud.

No voice answered.

He ran from deserted room to deserted room. The air inside had that dead smell which comes when a place is no longer lived in: damp plaster, char, dust, and the faint green reek of plants that have begun to take possession. He descended into the garden. No one came to meet him; and from the uncut grass, the tangled disorder of flower-beds gone to seed, he understood that no one would come.

He mounted again and rode back at a sharp trot, as though speed could outstrip what he had just seen.

In Ramelton he stopped at the inn, gave his horse to the ostler, and ordered lunch. The room smelt of bread and tea and the sharpness of pickles; a good smell, homely and honest, though he scarcely tasted what he was given. The landlady waited on him with a napkin over her arm and curiosity ready as a ladle.

“So Lennon House has been burned down?” he asked. “When was that?”

“Five years ago,” she replied. “Just five years ago this summer.”

And, without needing further invitation, she poured out her story as if it were stew long kept warm: the night of the conflagration and the cause of it; the ruin of the Eustaces; the inebriety of Bastable; the death of Dermod Eustace at Glenalla. There were names and judgements and the relish with which small towns season any calamity.

“But we hope to see the house rebuilt,” she concluded, drawing breath. “It’s likely to be, we hear, when Miss Eustace is married.” Her voice carried that particular note which suggests there is more salt in the pot if only one will ask.

He did not.

“And where does Miss Eustace live now?” he said.

“At Glenalla.” She lifted her chin as though pointing with it. “Halfway on the road to Rathmullen there’s a track leads up to your left. It’s a poor mountain village is Glenalla, and no place for Miss Eustace, at all, at all. Perhaps you will be wanting to see her?”

“Yes. I shall be glad if you will order my horse to be brought round to the door,” he answered, rising to end the interview.

But she was not to be put off so neatly. She lingered in the doorway and said, as if sharing a morsel too good to keep to herself: “Well, that’s curious—that’s most curious. For only a fortnight ago a gentleman burnt just as black as yourself stayed a night here on the same errand. He asked for Miss Eustace’s address and drove up to Glenalla. Perhaps you have business with her?”

“Yes, I have business with Miss Eustace,” he replied, with a patience he had learned in harsher places. “Will you be good enough to give orders about my horse?”

While he waited he turned the leaves of the hotel book, and there, under a date towards the end of July, he saw the name of Colonel Trench. The sight of it was like the sudden taste of a spice one had not expected—bitter at first, then bringing back a whole dish of memory with it.

“You will come back, sir, to-night?” the landlady asked as he mounted.

“No,” he said. “I do not think I shall come again to Ramelton.”

And he rode down the hill and crossed the Lennon bridge once more, the river’s running talk under him taking no notice of his going, as rivers do.

Four miles on he came to the track opposite a little bay of the Lough. He turned in and rode past a few white cottages, their doors shut against the late afternoon, their chimneys quiet. The road climbed into the purple hollow of the hills. It was about five o’clock when he came to the long, straggling village of Glenalla, built without plan, as though each cottage had set itself down where it pleased and left the next to find room.

A few cottages stood together; then a gap of fields; then a small plantation of larches and a house standing by itself. Beyond the house was another gap, through which he could see straight down to the water of the Lough shining in the afternoon sun, and the white gulls poised and swooping above it. Their cries came thinly up, and with them the faint lap of water on stones—a clean, soothing sound after so much in the world that was made to clang and shriek for profit. There were no machines here, no iron engines tearing at the air; only wind, birds, and the minute, persistent noises of living things.

After passing that gap he came to a small grey church, bare to the winds upon its tiny plateau. A pathway of white shell-dust led from the door to the little wooden gate. As he drew level with it a collie barked at him from behind the gate.

Feversham looked at the dog. It was very grey about the muzzle. He noticed the markings and stopped his horse altogether. He glanced towards the church and saw the door standing open.

At once he dismounted, fastened his horse to the fence, and entered the churchyard. The collie thrust its muzzle into the back of his knee, sniffed once or twice doubtfully, and then, as if some remembered scent had unlocked a whole buried language in it, broke into an exuberant welcome.

The dog had a better memory than the landlady at the inn. It barked and wagged its tail, crouched and sprang up at the stranger’s shoulders, whirled round in front of him, burst into sharp, excited screams of pleasure, ran to the church door and barked furiously there, then ran back and jumped again upon its friend.

Feversham caught it as it stood up with its forepaws upon his chest, patted it, and laughed—once, briefly, like a man who has almost forgotten the sound of his own ease. Then he stopped laughing and stood stock-still, his eyes fixed upon the open door.

In the doorway Ethne Eustace stood.

He put the dog down and walked slowly up the shell-white path towards her. She waited on the threshold without moving, without speaking, watching him until he was close enough that he could see every alteration time had made, and every thing it had not.

Then she said simply, “Harry.”

After that there was silence; nor did he speak. At the utterance of his name the company of ghosts in which he had travelled all day vanished. Words in the mouth of someone who knows you are never merely words; they are a dialect that contains what cannot be translated. Six years had passed since his feet had crushed the gravel on the dawn of a June morning beneath her window. Now the sound of gravel under his step, and the thin cries of gulls, and the dog’s panting joy, made a kind of choir about them that had nothing to do with any distant battlefield.

They looked at one another and saw, with the clear cruelty of first sight, the changes those six years had brought. Changes unnoticed by those who had lived daily with them sprang distinct to these two.

Feversham was thin; his face wasted. The strain of life in the House of Stone had left its marks about his sunken eyes and in the look of age beyond his years. But Ethne saw at once what mattered more than fatigue and tanning. He came back without anger towards her for that fourth feather snapped from her fan. He stood quietly, self-possessed—not arrogant, yet no longer diffident. That timidity of old, the continual expectation of other men’s contempt, had gone. He had tested himself long and hard and knew he had not failed.

She saw it all, and her face lightened.

“It is not all harm which has come of these years,” she said. “They were not wasted.”

He thought then of her years in this lonely village—of her youth set down among these scattered cottages and bare hills—and with a man’s thought assumed it for exile, not knowing that nowhere else would she have chosen to live. He looked into her face and saw time’s marks there too. She had not aged greatly; her big grey eyes shone as clearly as before, and the colour was still bright on her cheeks. But there was more of character. Suffering had done what it always does: it had altered the flavour of her, deepened it. She had eaten of the tree of knowledge.

“I am sorry,” he said. “I did you a great wrong six years ago, and I need not.”

She held out her hand.

“Will you give it me, please?”

For a moment he did not understand.

“That fourth feather,” she said.

He drew his letter-case from his coat and shook two feathers out into the palm of his hand. The larger one, the ostrich feather, he held out to her. But she said, “Both.”

There was no reason why he should keep Castleton’s feather any longer. He handed them both to her. She clasped them, and with a smile pressed them against her breast as though they were not mere plumes but tokens with warmth still in them.

“I have the four feathers now,” she said.

“Yes,” Feversham answered. “All four. What will you do with them?”

Her smile broke into a laugh, quick and bright as the sudden rattle of shells underfoot.

“Do with them!” she cried, with scorn for the idea. “I shall do nothing with them. I shall keep them. I am very proud to have them to keep.”

She kept them as she had once kept his portrait. Perhaps Durrance had been right when he said that women, more than men, gather up their experiences and live upon them, looking backwards, keeping the past like a book of recipes written in a hand no one else can read. Feversham, for his part, would have dropped the feathers then and there and crushed them into the shell-dust with his heel. They had done their work; they could no longer reproach, were no longer needed to encourage; dead things.

Ethne held them tight. To her they were not dead.

“Colonel Trench was here a fortnight ago,” she said. “He told me you were bringing it back to me.”

“But he did not know of the fourth feather,” Feversham replied. “I never told any man that I had it.”

“Yes. You told Colonel Trench on your first night in the House of Stone at Omdurman. He told me.” She added, with gravity and no smile, as if stating a truth that must be precisely spoken: “I no longer hate him.”

“I am glad of that,” Feversham said. “He is a great friend of mine.”

Ethne was silent for a moment. The collie sat down, its tail thumping now and again on the hard ground, listening with that canine attentiveness that seems to take meaning from tone as much as from words, as if it too belonged to the place’s old speech.

Then she said, “I wonder whether you have forgotten our drive from Ramelton to our house when I came to fetch you from the quay? We were alone in the dog-cart, and we spoke—”

“Of the friends whom one knows for friends the first moment,” Feversham interrupted, “and whom one seems to recognise even though one has never seen them before. Indeed I remember.”

“And whom one never loses whether absent or dead,” Ethne continued. “I said that one could always be sure of such friends, and you answered—”

“I answered that one could make mistakes,” he said, interrupting again.

“Yes, and I disagreed. I said that one might seem to make mistakes, and perhaps think so for a long while, but that in the end one would be proved not to have made them.” Her voice shook a little and pleaded as she went on. “I have often thought of those words. I remembered them very clearly when Captain Willoughby brought to me the first feather, and with a great deal of remorse. I remember them again very clearly to-day, although I have no room in my thoughts for remorse. I was right, you see, and I should have clung firmly to my faith. But I did not.”

She paused, opened her hand, and gazed for a while at the white feathers lying there—mute, unmistakable, a little bundle of history in a language only the two of them fully understood.

“I was young,” she said softly. “I knew very little. I was unaware how little. I judged hastily; but to-day I understand.”

Then she turned and went inside the church.

Feversham followed her.
