“I’ve lost a beautiful woman, a prestigious social standing, and a comfortable income,” Mr. Godfrey began, “and yet I’ve accepted it all without resistance. What possible reason could there be for such behavior? My dear friend, there is no reason.”

“No reason?” I echoed.

“Let me appeal to your experience with children,” he continued. “A child acts in a certain way—something striking enough to make you wonder why. You ask for the motive, but the child can’t explain. It’s like asking grass why it grows or birds why they sing. Well, that’s exactly my situation—I’m like that child, like the grass, like the birds. I don’t know why I proposed to Miss Verinder. I don’t know why I neglected my dear Ladies. I don’t know why I abandoned the Mothers’ Small-Clothes. You ask a child, ‘Why were you naughty?’ And the little angel puts its finger in its mouth and says nothing. That’s me, Miss Clack! I couldn’t confess this to anyone else—but I feel compelled to tell it to *you*!”

I began to regain my composure. A psychological puzzle was unfolding before me—one I found deeply compelling. I’ve always had an interest in human minds, and I believe I possess some skill in understanding them.

“Dear friend,” he pressed on, “help me solve this. Why does it suddenly feel as if my marriage plans were made in a dream? Why do I now realize that true happiness lies not in wealth or status, but in serving my Ladies, doing modest work, speaking earnestly when called upon by my Chairman? What do I need with position? I already have one. What do I need with income? I can afford bread, cheese, a small room, two coats a year. What do I need with Miss Verinder? She herself told me—between us only—that she loves another man, and that her only reason for marrying me was to forget him. What a dreadful union! Oh, what a dreadful union! These are my thoughts as I travel to Brighton. I approach Rachel like a criminal awaiting judgment. When I learn she has changed her mind too—when she suggests ending the engagement—I feel (without doubt) overwhelming relief. A month ago, I was embracing her passionately. An hour ago, the joy of knowing I’ll never embrace her again intoxicates me like strong wine. It seems impossible—it can’t be. Yet here are the facts, as I stated when we first sat down. I’ve lost a woman, a position, an income—and I’ve surrendered quietly. Can *you* explain this, dear friend? It baffles me entirely.”

His noble head sank forward, defeated by his own confusion.

I was profoundly moved. The truth, from my perspective as someone who studies the soul, was now clear. It’s not uncommon for even the most gifted individuals to be humbled to the level of the least capable among us. Perhaps this is part of a divine design—to remind greatness that it is mortal, and that power can be withdrawn as easily as granted. Now, I could see such a lesson in Mr. Godfrey’s recent actions, which I had witnessed unnoticed. Equally evident was the return of his nobler self—the horror he felt at the thought of marrying Rachel, and the eager desire to return to his charitable work and his community.

I shared these reflections with him in gentle, sincere words. His reaction was radiant. As I spoke, he compared himself to a lost soul emerging from darkness into light. When I assured him of a warm welcome back to the Mothers’ Small-Clothes, his gratitude overflowed. He pressed my hands to his lips. Overcome by the joy of reclaiming him, I allowed him to hold my hands. I closed my eyes, feeling my head sink onto his shoulder in a moment of spiritual surrender. I would have fainted then and there—had not the outside world intervened. A clatter of cutlery sounded beyond the door, and the footman entered to set the table for luncheon.

Mr. Godfrey sprang up, glancing at the clock.

“How quickly time passes with *you*!” he exclaimed. “I’ll barely make the train.”

I asked why he was so eager to return to London. His reply hinted at unresolved family tensions and future conflicts.

“I’ve heard from my father,” he said. “Business requires him to leave Frizinghall for London today. He plans to come here—either tonight or tomorrow. I must tell him about Rachel and me. He’s determined we should marry. I fear great difficulty in convincing him otherwise. I must stop him from coming until he’s reconciled. Best and dearest of friends—we shall meet again!”

With those words, he rushed out. I hurried upstairs to compose myself before joining Aunt Ablewhite and Rachel for lunch.

I am aware—though I pause briefly on the subject—that the public has accused Mr. Godfrey of having selfish motives for releasing Rachel at the first opportunity. Some whisper that his eagerness to restore his standing in my regard stems from a mercenary desire to reconcile with a wealthy committee member at the Mothers’ Small-Clothes—a woman close to me and blessed with riches. I mention these vile rumors only to state that they never influenced my judgment. I have recorded every shift in my opinion of Mr. Godfrey exactly as written in my diary. Once restored to my esteem, he never fell from it again. I write with tears burning behind my eyes, longing to say more—but I must remain faithful to my experience. Within a month of these events, financial upheavals (which reduced even my meager income) forced me into exile abroad, leaving me only with a cherished memory of Mr. Godfrey—one that the world’s slander has attacked, but failed to destroy.

Let me dry my eyes and continue.

I descended to luncheon, curious to observe how Rachel was handling her freedom.

It seemed to me—though I admit I lack expertise in such matters—that regaining her liberty had reawakened her feelings for the man she truly loved, and that she was furious with herself for being unable to suppress emotions she secretly despised. Who was he? I suspected—but speculation was pointless. When I succeeded in guiding her toward spiritual awakening, she would surely reveal everything to me. I would hear about the man. I would hear about the Moonstone. Even if my purpose weren’t higher, the desire to free her from guilt would have been enough to justify my efforts.

That afternoon, Aunt Ablewhite took her walk in a wheelchair. Rachel accompanied her. “I wish I could pull the chair,” she said abruptly. “I wish I could exhaust myself till I collapse.”

She remained in this mood through the evening. In one of my favorite books—*The Life, Letters, and Labours of Miss Jane Ann Stamper*, forty-fourth edition—I found passages eerily suited to Rachel’s condition. When I suggested reading them aloud, she went to the piano. How little she must have known about serious people, to think I’d grow tired waiting! I kept the book near, ready for any moment.

Old Mr. Ablewhite did not appear that night. But I knew how much he valued his son’s marriage to Miss Verinder—and I was certain (despite Mr. Godfrey’s efforts) he would arrive the next day. With his arrival, the storm I anticipated would break, and Rachel’s resistance would inevitably weaken. I understand that old Mr. Ablewhite is widely regarded—as many inferiors claim—as a remarkably kind man. From my observations, he deserves that reputation only as long as things go his way. No longer.

The next day, as expected, Aunt Ablewhite was nearly stunned by her husband’s sudden appearance. He had scarcely entered when Mr. Bruff followed—unexpectedly, and to my dismay. I had never felt his presence so unwelcome.

“This is a pleasant surprise, sir,” Mr. Ablewhite said, feigning warmth. “When I left your office yesterday, I didn’t expect to see you in Brighton today.”

“I reconsidered our talk after you left,” Mr. Bruff replied. “I thought I might be useful. I caught the train just in time and couldn’t find your carriage.”

He seated himself beside Rachel. I retreated to a corner, book in hand—ready for anything. My aunt sat by the window, fanning herself calmly. Mr. Ablewhite stood in the center, his bald head redder than ever, addressing his niece with false affection.

“Rachel, my dear,” he said, “I’ve heard extraordinary news from Godfrey. I’m here to inquire. You have a private room. Will you show me the way?”

Rachel did not move. Whether she intended to force a confrontation or acted on a signal from Mr. Bruff, I couldn’t tell. She refused to guide him.

“Whatever you wish to say,” she replied, “can be said here—in front of my relatives, and in front” (she glanced at Mr. Bruff) “of my mother’s trusted friend.”

“Just as you please, my dear,” Mr. Ablewhite said, taking a seat. The others watched his face, expecting honesty after seventy years of worldly craft. I watched the top of his head—where, I’d noticed before, true emotion often surfaced.

“Some weeks ago,” he continued, “my son told me Miss Verinder had honored him by engaging to marry him. Is it possible, Rachel, that he misunderstood—or assumed—what you actually said?”

“Certainly not,” she answered. “I did promise to marry him.”

“Very honest!” Mr. Ablewhite said. “And reassuring, my dear. So far, Godfrey hasn’t erred. The mistake must lie in what he told me yesterday. I now see it. You two had a quarrel—and my foolish son took it seriously. Ah! I should have known better at his age.”

Rachel stiffened. The inner conflict stirred.

“Let’s be clear, Mr. Ablewhite,” she said. “No quarrel occurred between your son and me. If he told you I proposed ending our engagement—and he agreed—he spoke the truth.”

The color deepened on his scalp. His face remained pleasant, but the pink intensified.

“Come now, my dear,” he said soothingly. “Don’t be angry. Don’t punish poor Godfrey! He likely said something unfortunate. He’s always been clumsy—but he means well, Rachel, he means well!”

“Mr. Ablewhite,” she replied firmly, “either I’ve expressed myself poorly, or you’re deliberately misreading me. Once and for all: it is settled between your son and me that we will remain cousins—and nothing more. Is that clear?”

Her tone left no room for doubt. Even Mr. Ablewhite could no longer pretend. The red deepened further.

“So,” he said, “your engagement is broken off?”

“It is,” she confirmed.

“And you were the one who first proposed withdrawing?”

“I was. And he consented.”

The crimson turned scarlet.

“My son is a cowardly cur!” he roared. “In justice to myself as his father—not to him—I demand to know: what complaint do you have against Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite?”

Mr. Bruff stepped in.

“You are under no obligation to answer,” he said to Rachel.

Mr. Ablewhite snapped at him.

“Don’t forget, sir,” he said, “you’re a guest here. Your interference would have been more welcome if you’d waited to be asked.”

Mr. Bruff ignored him. His expression remained smooth, unbroken.

Rachel thanked him, then turned to Mr. Ablewhite—calm, composed, terrifying in her poise.

“Your son asked me the same question,” she said. “I gave him one answer—and I give you the same. I proposed ending our engagement because reflection convinced me it was best for both of us to retract a hasty promise and allow him to choose freely.”

“What has my son done?” Mr. Ablewhite insisted. “I have a right to know.”

She held firm.

“You’ve received the only explanation I deem necessary,” she said.

“In plain terms,” he spat, “you’re choosing to jilt my son?”

Rachel paused. Behind her, I heard her sigh. Mr. Bruff gently squeezed her hand. She regained her composure.

“I’ve faced worse accusations,” she said, “and borne them silently. The time has passed when you could shame me by calling me a jilt.”

Her voice carried bitterness—I sensed the Moonstone scandal had resurfaced. “I have nothing more to say,” she added, weary, looking out the nearest window.

Mr. Ablewhite rose, shoved his chair violently—it toppled over.

“I have more to say,” he declared, slamming his palm on the table. “If my son doesn’t feel this insult, I do!”

Rachel started, startled.

“Insult?” she repeated. “What do you mean?”

“Insult!” he thundered. “I know your motive, Miss Verinder! I know it as clearly as if you confessed it. Your cursed pride insults Godfrey—just as it insulted *me* when I married your aunt. Her family—her beggarly family—shunned her for marrying a man who built his own fortune. I had no ancestors. I wasn’t born into a line of thieves and murderers. I couldn’t trace a time when the Ablewhites had a shirt or could sign their names. Ha! ha! I wasn’t good enough for the Herncastles when I married. And now, when it matters, my son isn’t good enough for *you*. I suspected it all along. You carry the Herncastle blood, young lady! I suspected it!”

“A shameful suspicion,” Mr. Bruff remarked. “I’m astonished you admit it.”

Before Mr. Ablewhite could respond, Rachel spoke, coldly contemptuous.

“Surely,” she said to the lawyer, “this is beneath notice. If he chooses to think that way, let him.”

Mr. Ablewhite turned purple. He gasped, staring wildly between Rachel and Mr. Bruff, unsure whom to attack. His wife, previously calm, finally showed alarm, trying—futilely—to soothe him. Throughout, I felt urged to intervene, but feared the consequences. Yet now, I rose above mere caution. I might have hesitated to speak on my own—but the crisis was miraculously resolved by Miss Jane Ann Stamper’s correspondence—Letter 1001, “Peace in Families.” I stood in my corner and opened my book.

“Dear Mr. Ablewhite,” I said, “one word!”

At first, he seemed poised to snap at me. My gentle address stopped him. He stared, bewildered.

“As a devoted friend,” I continued, “and one accustomed to awaken, convince, prepare, enlighten, and strengthen others, I take the humblest liberty—the liberty to calm your mind.”

He began to recover, but bristled. With anyone else, he would have erupted. But my voice—usually soft—has a sharp edge in emergencies. This time, I needed it.

I held up the book, tapped the page sharply. “Not my words!” I cried. “Don’t imagine I seek attention for my humble thoughts! Manna in the wilderness, Mr. Ablewhite! Dew on parched earth! Words of comfort, wisdom, love—the blessed, blessed words of Miss Jane Ann Stamper!”

I was interrupted by a sudden catch in my breath. Before I could continue, he bellowed:

“Miss Jane Ann Stamper be——!”

I cannot write the word. It was obscene. I shrieked, snatched my bag, pulled out tracts, grabbed “Hush, for Heaven’s Sake!” and handed it to him, pleading. He tore it in half and flung it across the table. The others rose in alarm. I sat back down. Once before, under similar circumstances, Miss Jane Ann Stamper had been thrown from a room. I waited—inspired by her spirit—for martyrdom.

But it didn’t happen. He turned to his wife.

“Who—who—who,” he stammered, “who invited this insolent fanatic into my house? Did you?”

Before Aunt Ablewhite could speak, Rachel answered.

“Miss Clack is here,” she said, “as my guest.”

The effect was immediate. Mr. Ablewhite transformed—from blazing fury to icy disdain. Everyone saw Rachel had struck a blow—brief, decisive—giving him the upper hand.

“Oh?” he said. “Miss Clack is your guest—in *my* house?”

Rachel lost her temper. Her face flushed, her eyes flashed. She turned to Mr. Bruff.

“What does he mean?” she demanded.

Mr. Bruff stepped in again.

“You seem to forget,” he said, “that you took this house as Miss Verinder’s guardian, for her use.”

“Not quite so fast,” Mr. Ablewhite interjected. “I have one last word—which I should have spoken earlier, had this”—he looked at me, searching for a name—“if this Rampant Spinster hadn’t interrupted. I inform you, sir, that if my son is not good enough for Miss Verinder, I cannot assume his father is good enough to be her guardian. Understand: I refuse the role offered by Lady Verinder’s will. In legal terms, I decline to act. This house was hired in my name. I accept full responsibility. It is *my* house. I may keep it or vacate it as I please. I have no wish to hurry Miss Verinder. On the contrary, I request she remove her guest and belongings at her convenience.” He bowed low and left.

That was Mr. Ablewhite’s revenge—on Rachel, for refusing his son.

The moment the door closed, Aunt Ablewhite performed a miracle: she crossed the room.

We all fell silent.
