The Ark had neither rudder nor oar nor any obedient engine of man’s devising. It went, as a loaf of bread on a flood goes, where the High Gods and the set of the waters chose to carry it. Within was dark—dark as the inside of a sealed granary—close with an old, breathed-on smell; and the air was crowded with the groaning of strained timbers, the slap and suction of water on the outer skin, and the harsh, unceasing clatter of loose things shifting in their places. The very sound was a torment: not the clean, intelligible noises of a hearth and a farmyard—knife on board, pestle in mortar, the low talk of kin—but a blind industrial din without pattern, as though the sea itself had become a monstrous mill with no grain worth grinding.

I groped for the fire-box to relight the fallen lamp and could not find it; and so we were forced to endure what was dealt us without even the small comfort of seeing our own hands. I held to the side of Nais, keeping her braced against the bed as the Ark was thrown about in merciless sport. We spoke little. There was no need. Our grief had its own language, and in that silence was companionship enough—like two folk sitting with empty bowls after a feast has been stolen from the table.

Atlantis, when she sank to make new ocean-bed, left behind her a final bequest to the Gods of the Sea: great whirlpools, and spoutings from drowned fires that must have hissed like quenched iron. And then—so I judge, for in the black belly of the Ark we could see nothing—there came a vast hurricane to leave no piece of desolation incomplete. For seven nights and seven days the dreadful turmoil continued, as we counted later by the hour-reckoner hanging within the Ark. The soundscape of it was a punishment in itself: the screaming wind above, the boom and jar as seas struck, the incessant rasp of spray on wood. There was no lull in which the mind might set itself straight, no familiar cadence like a hand-mill turning or a kettle beginning to sing. Only the wild, commercial roar of a world smashed and remade.

At last the howling departed. A long swell remained—regular, oily, and deliberate. I knew it by the change of motion: the Ark no longer snapped and flung; she rose and fell as a breast rises in slow sleep. Then for the first time I dared climb the stair and push open the door set in the roof.

Sweet air came gushing down and swept the foulness clean as a sudden breeze in a smoke-filled kitchen. I filled my lungs greedily, tasting salt and sun and the sharp, pure edge of sky. The Ark rode dryly over the seas. I went below, lifted Nais up with me, and brought her into the light so that the curing rays of our Lord the Sun could fall on her face and hands like warmth laid gently on bread.

Duly we adored Him and gave thanks that He had chosen to light another day. Then we stretched ourselves where we were, intending to doze and take that easy rest our bodies demanded. Yet though I was tired beyond words, sleep would not visit me quickly. Wearily I stared across the sunlit waters.

No blur of land met the eye. The ring of ocean was unbroken on every side; overhead the vault of heaven remained unchanged, a hard bright bowl turned over us. The bosom of the deep was littered with the poor wreckage of Atlantis—proof enough, if proof were needed, that what had come about was fact and not some horrid dream. Trees went by, some still leafy, some stripped; squared timbers, as though torn from a house-frame; a smashed and upturned boat of hides. Here and there the rounded corpse of a man or beast shouldered over the swell and kept convoy with our Ark in solemn silence. Even the sea had its inventory—its floating pantry of loss—and each object as it passed seemed to carry a smell that memory could supply: resin from snapped wood, wet hide, the rank sweetness of decay. There were times when I thought I heard, in the hush between one lift and the next, the ghosts of old sounds—market shouts, the clink of bowls, the chant of prayers in tongues already drowning—yet it was only my mind trying to translate ruin into something it could bear.

Sleep came at last. I dropped into unconsciousness holding Nais’s hand, and when I woke she was open-eyed too, watching me with a tenderness that made the bright air feel less empty. We were finely rested, both of us; and rest and strength bring a kind of complacency. We were readier now to accept the station the High Gods had made for us without repining. So we went below again into the belly of the Ark to eat and drink, and to maintain strength for the new life that lay before us.

A wonderful vessel this Ark proved, once we could see her at leisure and intimately. Though for the first time in all her centuries of life she swam upon the waters, she showed no leak, no suncrack. Even the floor was bone-dry. She was built of wood—one could see the grainings where the hand touched—but nowhere could one find suture or joint. The living timbers had been set in place and then grown together by an art we have lost to-day, but which the Ancients knew to perfection; and afterwards some treatment, also a secret of those forgotten builders, had made the wood hard as metal and proof against all weather.

In the gloomy cave of her belly were stored many matters, ordered with the calm forethought of old agrarian elders who measure seasons longer than a single life. At one end, in great tanks on either side of a central alley, lay a prodigious store of grain. I plunged my hand in and let it run through my fingers. It was sound—hard kernels that clicked together like clean pebbles, smelling faintly of sun and husk and the patient dryness of granaries. In other tanks at the far end was sweet water, cool as if it had been drawn from a deep well at dawn. Elsewhere were drugs and samples and essences of the life of beasts, sealed up for use while the Ark roamed under the guidance of the Gods upon the deep.

We ate simply, yet with reverence, as though each mouthful were a stitch holding our souls to our bodies. Grain, when it has been long stored, needs care to make it friendly to the stomach; we soaked what we could in water to soften it, and chewed slowly, letting the sweetness come out—thin, but present, like a remembered song in a language half-forgotten. In that dark chamber, every small sound became precious: the splash of water ladled, the crunch of kernels, the sigh with which Nais swallowed. It was a domestic music, and it defended the mind against the sea’s unreason.

On all the walls of the Ark, and on the partitions of the tanks and other woodwork, were carvings in the rude art of bygone time—representations of all the beasts that had lived in Atlantis. I studied them with a hunter’s interest. Some were strange to me, and I understood with a chill that they had died out with the men who had perpetuated them in these sculptures. The very pictures were a last vocabulary: names once spoken in village dialects and mothers’ lullabies now made mute, turned into mere shape on wood. There is a kind of power in that—how the dominant silence of the sea can swallow not only bodies and buildings but the words by which a people knew their own world. One cannot translate a vanished animal; no foreign lexicon can replace the exact sound and taste of its true name.

There was a good store of weapons too, and tools for handicrafts. But the Ark gave us no tool to govern her course. For many weeks our life endured as the Gods drove her here and there over the face of the waters. We could not choose direction; we could not, by so much as a hair’s breadth a day, increase her speed. The High Gods who had chosen the two of us to be saved out of all Atlantis held sole control of our fate, and into Their hands we resigned our future cheerfully, for there was nothing else to do.

Of the land we reached in due time—where we made our abiding place, and where our children were born—I shall tell in its place. But since this chronicle has proceeded so far in exact order of events as they came to pass, it is necessary first to narrate how we came by the sheets on which it is written.

In a great coffer set in the centre of the Ark’s floor, the whole of the Mysteries learned during the study of ages were set down in accurate writing. When first I opened it, a smell rose up—wax, old wood, and something sharp like dried herbs—such as clings to storerooms where knowledge is kept as carefully as seed. I read through some during the days that passed, and the awfulness of the Powers over which they gave control appalled me. I had seen some of those Powers set loose in Atlantis and had been witness to her destruction; but here were powers higher than those. Here was the great Secret of Life and Death which Phorenice also had found, and for which she had been destroyed. There were other things too—things I cannot bring my stylo to scribe. The words themselves seemed to carry a weight, and I grew afraid not only of what they commanded, but of how easily a man’s tongue and mind can be bent by a dominant lexicon that calls itself wisdom.

To be custodian of those writings became more than I could endure. The thought sat on me as heavily as a soaked cloak. Day by day, as the Ark rose and fell and the sea’s vast breathing sounded through the hull, the burden grew more intolerable. I imagined those sheets surviving us, carried to some new shore, interpreted by mouths that did not know the old cadence, sold and traded and mangled into slogans—mysteries made into merchandise, and the world made noisy again with the clamour of power.

At last I took hot irons, and with them seared the wax on the sheets until every letter of the old writings was obliterated. The smell of it was bitter—burnt beeswax and scorched fibre—and the faint hiss each time the iron touched seemed like a small, private storm. Nais watched me without speaking. In her silence I heard both grief and assent, as though she too understood that some things, once named, become hungry.

If I did wrong, the High Gods in Their infinite justice will punish me. If it is well these great secrets should endure on earth, They in Their infinite power will dictate them afresh to some fitting scribes. But I destroyed them there as the Ark swayed with us over the waves. Later, when we came to land, I rewrote upon the sheets the matters which led to great Atlantis being dragged to her death-throes.
