The country-houses of Eastern England possess a peculiar, damp charm that has always drawn me in. They are typically modest structures, often in the Italian style, nestled within parks of eighty to a hundred acres. One imagines the grey palings of split oak, the noble trees, the meres choked with reeds, and the distant lines of wood. There is the pillared portico, perhaps clumsily attached to a red-brick Queen Anne house, disguised with stucco to mimic the "Grecian" fashion of the late eighteenth century. Inside, the hall stretches to the roof, ideally accompanied by a gallery and a small organ. The library holds treasures ranging from thirteenth-century Psalters to Shakespeare quartos. Above all, I delight in imagining the life within such a house during its founding days, amidst the landlords' prosperity, and even now, when money may be scarce but taste is varied and life remains intriguing. I dream of owning one, possessing enough wealth to maintain it and host friends modestly.

This is a digression. I must recount a series of curious events that unfolded in a house fitting this description: Castringham Hall in Suffolk. While the building has undergone significant changes since the era of my tale, its essential features remain—the Italian portico, the white square block, the older interior, the park fringed with woods, and the mere. However, one defining feature has vanished. To the observer standing in the park, a great old ash-tree once grew within a dozen yards of the wall, its branches almost touching the building. It likely stood there since Castringham ceased to be a fortified place, surviving the filling of the moat and the construction of the Elizabethan dwelling. By 1690, it had nearly reached its full, terrifying dimensions.

That year, the district became the scene of numerous witch trials. It will take centuries to form a just estimate of the solid reason, if any existed, behind the universal fear of witches in those times. Did the accused truly believe they possessed supernatural power? Did they harbor the will, if not the power, to harm their neighbors? Or were the countless confessions merely extorted by the cruelty of witch-finders? These questions remain unsolved, and the present narrative gives me pause. I cannot dismiss it as mere invention; the reader must judge for themselves.

Castringham contributed a victim to the *auto-da-fé*. Her name was Mrs. Mothersole. She differed from the typical village witch only in her relative wealth and influence. Reputable farmers of the parish attempted to save her, testifying to her character and showing considerable anxiety regarding the jury's verdict.

Yet, what proved fatal to the woman was the evidence of the then proprietor of Castringham Hall, Sir Matthew Fell. He deposed that on three separate occasions, under the full moon, he had watched her from his window gathering sprigs "from the ash-tree near my house." She had climbed into the branches, clad only in her shift, cutting small twigs with a peculiarly curved knife while appearing to converse with herself. Each time, Sir Matthew attempted to capture her, but she took alarm at accidental noises he made. When he descended to the garden, all he could see was a hare running across the path toward the village.

On the third night, he followed at his best speed, going straight to Mrs. Mothersole's house. He waited fifteen minutes, battering at her door, before she emerged cross and sleepy, as if just awakened, offering no explanation for his visit.

Based largely on this testimony, supplemented by lesser evidence from other parishioners, Mrs. Mothersole was found guilty and condemned to die. She was hanged a week after the trial, alongside five or six other unfortunate souls, at Bury St Edmunds.

Sir Matthew Fell, then Deputy-Sheriff, attended the execution. It was a damp, drizzly March morning as the cart ascended the rough grass hill outside Northgate, where the gallows stood. The other victims were apathetic or broken by misery; Mrs. Mothersole, however, remained distinctively herself. Her "poisonous Rage," as a contemporary reporter noted, worked so violently upon the bystanders—even the hangman—that she was universally regarded as presenting the living aspect of a mad devil. Yet, she offered no resistance to the officers of the law. Instead, she looked upon those who laid hands upon her with such direful and venomous intensity that one officer later assured Sir Matthew that the mere thought of her gaze preyed inwardly upon his mind for six months afterward.

All that she reportedly said were the seemingly meaningless words: "There will be guests at the Hall." She repeated them more than once in an undertone.

Sir Matthew Fell was not unimpressed by the woman's bearing. After the assizes, he discussed the matter with the Vicar of his parish, Mr. Crome, with whom he traveled home. His evidence at the trial had been given reluctantly; he was not particularly infected with the witch-finding mania, yet he declared he could offer no other account than the one he had given and could not possibly have been mistaken. The whole transaction repugned him, for he was a man who preferred pleasant terms with his neighbors, but he felt a duty to be done. The Vicar applauded this sentiment, as any reasonable man must.

A few weeks later, under the full moon of May, the Vicar and the Squire met again in the park and walked to the Hall. Lady Fell was with her dangerously ill mother, leaving Sir Matthew alone at home; thus, the Vicar was easily persuaded to take a late supper at the Hall.

Sir Matthew was not good company that evening. Conversation centered on family and parish matters. By chance, Sir Matthew made a written memorandum of certain wishes regarding his estates, a document that would later prove exceedingly useful.

When Mr. Crome considered departing around half-past nine, the two men took a preliminary walk on the gravelled path at the rear of the house. The only incident that struck the Vicar occurred as they approached the ash-tree near the windows. Sir Matthew stopped and asked, "What is that running up and down the stem of the ash? Is it never a squirrel? They should all be in their nests by now."

The Vicar looked and saw the moving creature, but the moonlight obscured its color. The sharp outline, visible for an instant, was imprinted on his brain. He swore, though it sounded foolish, that the creature, whether squirrel or not, possessed more than four legs.

Still, little could be made of this momentary vision, and the two men parted. They may have met since, but it was not for a score of years.

The next day, Sir Matthew Fell was not downstairs at six, nor seven, nor eight. The servants knocked at his chamber door, listening anxiously and battering the panels. Finally, the door opened from the outside. They found their master dead and black. There were no immediate marks of violence, but the window was open.

One servant fetched the parson, who then rode to notify the coroner. Mr. Crome hastened to the Hall and was shown the room where the dead man lay. His notes reveal the genuine respect and sorrow felt for Sir Matthew. He transcribed a crucial passage regarding the circumstances of the death:

"There was not the least trace of a forced entrance to the chamber; the casement stood open, as my poor friend would always have it in this season. He had his evening drink of small ale in a silver vessel of about a pint measure, and tonight he had not drunk it out. This drink was examined by the physician from Bury, Mr. Hodgkins, who could not, upon oath before the coroner's quest, discover any venomous matter within it. Naturally, given the great swelling and blackness of the corpse, neighbors spoke of poison. The body was much disorder'd, twisted in an extreme manner, suggesting my worthy friend expired in great pain and agony. What remains unexplained, and to myself the argument of some horrid and artful design by the perpetrators of this barbarous murder, is this: the women entrusted with laying out and washing the corpse, both sad and respected in their mournful profession, came to me in great distress of mind and body. They confirmed, upon the first view, that no sooner did they touch the breast of the corpse with their naked hands than they felt a violent smart and aching in their palms, which spread to their forearms. The pain continued, swelling them immoderately, forcing them to lay aside their calling for many weeks, yet no mark was seen on the skin.

"Upon hearing this, I summoned the physician still in the house. With the aid of a small crystal magnifying lens, we carefully examined the skin but detected nothing of importance beyond a couple of small punctures or pricks. We concluded these were the spots by which the poison might have been introduced, recalling the ring of Pope Borgia and other specimens of the horrid art of Italian poisoners of the last age.

"As to what I must add, it is merely my own experiment, left to posterity to judge its value. On the table by the bedside lay a small Bible, in which my friend, punctual as in matters of less moment, used nightly and upon rising to read a set portion. Taking it up—with a tear duly paid to him, as from the study of this poorer adumbration we pass to the contemplation of its great original—it occurred to me, in moments of helplessness, to try that old and superstitious practice of drawing the *Sortes*. A principal instance, in the case of his late sacred majesty King Charles and my Lord Falkland, was much talked of. I must admit my trial afforded little assistance; yet, as the cause and origin of these dreadful events may hereafter be searched out, I set down the results, in case they point the true quarter of the mischief to a quicker intelligence than my own.

"I made three trials, opening the book and placing my finger upon certain words. The first yielded Luke xiii. 7: 'Cut it down.' The second, Isaiah xiii. 20: 'It shall never be inhabited.' And upon the third experiment, Job xxxix. 30: 'Her young ones also suck up blood.'"

This concludes the quotation from Mr. Crome's papers. Sir Matthew Fell was duly coffined and buried. His funeral sermon, preached by Mr. Crome the following Sunday, was printed under the title "The Unsearchable Way; or, England's Danger and the Malicious Dealings of Antichrist." It was the Vicar's view, shared by the neighborhood, that the Squire was a victim of a resurgence of the Popish Plot.

His son, Sir Matthew the Second, succeeded to the title and estates. It is unsurprising that the new Baronet did not occupy the room in which his father had died, nor did anyone else sleep there during his entire occupation. He died in 1735, leaving no particular mark on his reign save for a curiously constant mortality among his cattle and livestock, which tended to increase over time. Those interested in details will find a statistical account in a letter to the *Gentleman's Magazine* of 1772, drawn from the Baronet's own papers. He finally ended the affliction by a simple expedient: shutting up all his beasts in sheds at night and keeping no sheep in his park. He observed that nothing was ever attacked that spent the night indoors. Afterward, the disorder confined itself to wild birds and beasts of chase. Lacking a good account of the symptoms and finding all-night watching unproductive, I do not dwell on what the Suffolk farmers called the "Castringham sickness."

The second Sir Matthew died in 1735 and was succeeded by his son, Sir Richard. It was in his time that the great family pew was built on the north side of the parish church. So large were the Squire's ideas that several graves on that unhallowed side had to be disturbed to satisfy his requirements. Among them was that of Mrs. Mothersole, whose position was accurately known thanks to a note on a plan of the church and yard made by Mr. Crome.

Interest was excited in the village when it was known that the famous witch, still remembered by a few, was to be exhumed. Surprise and disquiet were strong when it was found that, though her coffin was sound and unbroken, there was no trace inside of body, bones, or dust. It is a curious phenomenon, for at the time of her burial, resurrection-men were not dreamed of, and it is difficult to conceive any rational motive for stealing a body other than for the dissecting-room.

The incident revived stories of witch trials and witch exploits, dormant for forty years. Sir Richard's orders that the coffin be burnt were thought foolhardy by many, though they were duly carried out.

Sir Richard was a pestilent innovator. Before his time, the Hall had been a fine block of mellow red brick. Having traveled in Italy and become infected with the Italian taste, and possessing more money than his predecessors, he determined to leave an English house for an Italian palace. Stucco and ashlar masked the brick; indifferent Roman marbles were planted in the entrance hall and gardens; a reproduction of the Sibyl's temple at Tivoli was erected on the opposite bank of the mere. Castringham took on an entirely new, and I must say, less engaging, aspect. Yet it was much admired and served as a model for neighboring gentry in after-years.

One morning in 1754, Sir Richard woke after a night of discomfort. The wind had blown persistently, causing his chimney to smoke, yet it was so cold he needed a fire. Something had rattled about the window, denying him peace. Furthermore, guests of position were expected that day, and the inroads of the distemper among his game had been serious, threatening his reputation as a game-preserver. What touched him most, however, was the matter of his sleepless night. He could certainly not sleep in that room again.

This was the chief subject of his meditations at breakfast. Afterward, he began a systematic examination of the rooms to find one suitable to his notions. Long before he found one: this had an eastern window, that a northern; this door servants would always pass, and he disliked the bedstead there.
