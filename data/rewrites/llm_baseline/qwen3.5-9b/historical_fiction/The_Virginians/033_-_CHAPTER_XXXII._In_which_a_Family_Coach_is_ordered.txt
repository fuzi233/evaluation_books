Our pleasant task now is to reveal the secret Mr. Lambert whispered into his wife's ear at the close of the previous chapter—a revelation that brought such delight to the entire Oakhurst household. With the hay harvested and the corn still green, the farm horses were free. "Why," asked Colonel Lambert, "should we not harness them to the coach? Let us all pay a visit to Tunbridge Wells, stopping at Westerham to join our friend Wolfe on the way?"

Mamma embraced the proposal with evident enthusiasm, and one dares say the honest gentleman who devised it felt equally pleased. The children leaped for joy. Instantly, the girls scattered to gather their finest attire: calamancoes, paduasoys, falbalas, furbelows, capes, cardinals, sacks, negligees, solitaires, caps, ribbons, mantuas, clocked stockings, and high-heeled shoes, along with countless other articles of toilet. Mamma's best robes were summoned from the presses, garments reserved solely for rare, solemn occasions, retreating afterward to lavender and silence. The brave Colonel produced his laced hat, waistcoat, and silver-hilted hanger. Charley rejoiced in a *rasee* holiday suit belonging to his father—the very coat in which the Colonel had been married—which Mrs. Lambert cut up, not without a pang of regret. Even the dogs were dressed; Ball and Dumpling had their tails and manes tied with ribbon. Chump, the old white cart-horse, was appointed unicorn leader to assist the carriage-horses up the first five hilly miles from Oakhurst to Westerham.

The carriage itself was an ancient vehicle, rumored to have served in the procession bringing George I. from Greenwich to London upon his first assumption of sovereignty. It had belonged to Mr. Lambert's father, and the family had regarded it, ever since memory served, as one of the most splendid coaches in the three kingdoms. Brian, coachman—and, must it be confessed?—ploughman of the Oakhurst family, took his place on the box beside Mr. Charley. Their precious clothes were packed in imperials atop the roof. The Colonel's pistols were secured in the carriage pockets, while the blunderbuss hung behind the box within easy reach of Brian, an old soldier.

Fortunately, no highwayman molested the convoy, nor did any innkeeper levy contributions on Colonel Lambert, whose slender purse and large family rendered him immune to such depredators on the King's Highway. A reasonably cheap, modest lodging had been arranged by young Colonel Wolfe at his usual haunt, whither he rode ahead to meet them. These lodgings lay directly opposite Madame Bernstein's establishment. As the Oakhurst family arrived on a Saturday evening, they witnessed chair after chair discharging powdered beaux and patched, brocaded beauties at the Baroness's door, where she hosted one of her numerous card parties. The sun had not yet set—for our ancestors began their dissipation at early hours, indulging in meat, drink, or cards anytime after three in the afternoon until late at night or early morning. From their window, the young country ladies and their mother observed the various personages entering the Bernstein rout.

Colonel Wolfe identified the guests for the ladies. It was almost as delightful as attending the party themselves, Hetty and Theo decided, for they could not only see the arrivals but also gaze through the Baroness's open casements to watch them inside. Among the few figures they recognized, the passing of the Duchess of Queensberry prompted Martin Lambert to recite a score of lines from his favorite Mat Prior regarding "Kitty, beautiful and young."

"Think that old lady was once like you, girls!" cried the Colonel.

"Like us, Papa? Well, certainly, we never set ourselves up for being beauties!" replied Miss Hetty, tossing her little head.

"Yes, like you, you little baggage! Like you at this very moment, wishing to go to that drum yonder:—'Inflamed with rage at sad restraint / Which wise mamma ordained, / And sorely vexed to play the saint / Whilst wit and beauty reigned.'"

"We were never invited, Papa," said the satirical daughter. "And I am sure if there is no beauty more worth seeing than that, the wit cannot be much worth the hearing."

"Oh, but he is a rare poet, Mat Prior!" continued the Colonel. "Though, mind you, girls, you will skip over all the poems I have marked with a cross. A rare poet! To think you should see one of his heroines! 'Fondness prevailed, mamma gave way' (she always will, Mrs. Lambert!)—'Fondness prevailed, mamma gave way, Kitty at heart's desire / Obtained the chariot for a day, / And set the world on fire!'"

"I am sure it must have been very inflammable," remarked Mamma.

"So it was, my dear, twenty years ago; much more inflammable than it is now," noted the Colonel.

"Nonsense, Mr. Lambert," answered Mamma.

"Look, look!" cried Hetty, running forward to point to the little square and covered gallery where the door to Madame Bernstein's apartments stood, surrounded by a crowd of street urchins, idlers, and yokels watching the company.

"It's Harry Warrington!" exclaimed Theo, waving a handkerchief toward the young Virginian. However, Warrington did not see Miss Lambert. He walked arm-in-arm with a portly clergyman in a crisp, rustling silk gown, and the two entered Madame de Bernstein's door.

"I heard him preach a most admirable sermon here last Sunday," said Mr. Wolfe. "A little theatrical, but most striking and eloquent."

"You seem to be here most Sundays, James," said Mrs. Lambert.

"And Monday, and soon till Saturday," added the Colonel.

"See, Harry has beautified himself already; his hair is in a buckle, and I have no doubt he is going to the drum too."

"I had rather sit quietly generally on a Saturday evening," said the sober Mr. Wolfe, "at any rate, away from card-playing and scandal; but I own, dear Mrs. Lambert, I am under orders. Shall I go across and send Mr. Warrington to you?"

"No, let him have his sport. We shall see him tomorrow. He won't care to be disturbed amidst his fine folks by us country-people," said meek Mrs. Lambert.

"I am glad he is with a clergyman who preaches so well," said Theo softly, her eyes suggesting, *You see, good people, he is not so bad as you thought him, and as I, for my part, never believed him to be.*

"The clergyman has a very kind, handsome face."

"Here comes a greater clergyman," cried Mr. Wolfe. "It is my Lord of Salisbury, with his blue ribbon, and a chaplain behind him."

"And whom, a mercy's name, have we here?" broke in Mrs. Lambert as a sedan-chair, covered in gilding and topped with no fewer than five earl's coronets, approached the door. Carried by bearers in richly laced clothes and preceded by three footmen in matching splendid liveries, it halted before the Baroness's entrance. The Bishop, who had been about to enter, stopped and ran back with the most respectful bows and curtsies to the sedan-chair, offering his hand to the lady who stepped forth.

"Who on earth is this?" asked Mrs. Lambert.

"Sprechen Sie Deutsch? Ja, mein Herr. Nichts verstanden," said the waggish Colonel.

"Pooh, Martin."

"Well, if you cannot understand High Dutch, my love, how can I help it? Your education was neglected at school. Can you understand heraldry?—I know you can."

"I make," cried Charley, reciting the shield, "three merions on a field or, with an earl's coronet."

"A countess's coronet, my son. The Countess of Yarmouth, my son."

"And pray, who is she?"

"It hath ever been the custom of our sovereigns to advance persons of distinction to honor," continued the Colonel, gravely. "And this eminent lady hath been so promoted by our gracious monarch to the rank of Countess of this kingdom."

"But why, Papa?" asked the daughters together.

"Never mind, girls!" said Mamma.

But that incorrigible Colonel would go on. "Y, my children, is one of the last and most awkward letters of the whole alphabet. When I tell you stories, you are always saying 'Why.' Why should my Lord Bishop be cringing to that lady? Look at him rubbing his fat hands together and smiling into her face! It is not a handsome face anymore. It is all painted red and white like Scaramouch's in the pantomime. See, there comes another blue-ribbon, as I live. My Lord Bamborough. The descendant of the Hotspurs. The proudest man in England. He stops, he bows, he smiles; he is hat-in-hand, too. See, she taps him with her fan. Get away, you crowd of little blackguard boys, and don't tread on the robe of the lady whom the King delights to honor."

"But why does the King honor her?" asked the girls once more.
