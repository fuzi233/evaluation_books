What passed through that keen, scrupulous spirit in those few hours—after a night spent beside a dying hermit, trading prayers like bread broken in the dark—cannot be carried whole into another man’s mouth. Some hungers are not spoken; they are heard: the rasp of a breath, the soft click of beads, the thin water-sound in a stone basin when dawn comes and the world pretends it is ordinary again.

At daybreak he set out—yet not toward Rotterdam. He turned his feet toward Tergou.

There was a reason as plain as salt: to face Ghysbrecht the burgomaster, the man who had sat warm in another household’s inheritance as if it were his own hearth-stool; and to make him, by the force of conscience and of Holy Church, bring up again what he had swallowed. Clement carried with him a parchment whose own story would have filled a winter’s evening by a peat-fire; but he did not finger it, nor take comfort from its crackle. He let it lie hidden, as one keeps a knife sheathed until it must be used, and prayed rather to win with words.

He walked hard. Dust clung to his sandals and rose at each step in little puffs that tasted of dry road and horse-dung and last year’s chaff. The country lay about him in its plain, domestic labor: ditches shining, alder-tops murmuring, a cart somewhere groaning on its axle, and far off a bell that did not ring for him and yet seemed to touch his ribs as if it knew his business.

By the time he reached the town his throat was parched. He stopped at the fountain, where the water ran with that cool, clean voice that can quiet a man’s inward tumult for the length of a swallow. He drew from his wallet a piece of black bread—coarse, sour with rye, the crust gritty where flour and bran had baked into one—and began to eat, letting the fountain-water wash it down. It was a poor meal; yet even poor bread has its comfort when a body is weary, and the mouth remembers the simple honesty of grain.

He had taken but a few bites when a woman came running—servant by her cap and apron, fear by her breath. Her words tumbled over each other like beans spilled on a board.

“Father—come—my master is dying. Shrieve him. Quick, for the love of God!”

Clement looked once at the bread in his hand as if it belonged to another life, then put it back into his wallet. He rose without a question and followed her.

Her haste led him not to a merchant’s house, nor to a craftsman’s low door smelling of pitch and onions, but straight to the Stadthouse.

At the threshold something went cold along his skin. He drew back—a small shudder, quick as a startled bird—seeing where she was taking him. The very boards seemed to remember: a stair creaking like accusation, voices once lifted in judgment, the weight of official words spoken in a tongue that could make theft sound like law. Yet he mastered himself almost at once; he would not be governed by the body’s recoil. He followed her in, and up, and up, while the building’s hush pressed on his ears, broken only by the servant’s sobbing and the faint, relentless tick of a clock that counted out the burgomaster’s remaining breaths.

In a chamber where the air was close with old linen and bitter herbs, propped high on pillows, lay Ghysbrecht. The man’s face was already half withdrawn from life—yellowed, slack, the lips thinned as if the mouth had at last learned what it would not say. Clement paused at the door and looked at him; and in that one look there rose like a tower in his mind the memory of wrongs—the tower, the wood, the letter—each a stone laid on another’s heart.

He spoke, low, and his voice trembled in spite of him.

“Pax vobiscum.”

Ghysbrecht’s eyes opened wider, eager in their weakness; even fear can be hungry.

“Thank Heaven,” he whispered, “thou art come in time. Absolve me, father, and pray for my soul—thou and thy brethren.”

“My son,” said Clement, and the old formal words came to him like a well-known knife-handle finding the hand, “before absolution cometh confession. In which act there must be no reservation, as thou valuest thy soul’s weal. Bethink thee, therefore, wherein thou hast most offended God and the Church, while I offer up a prayer for wisdom to direct thee.”

He knelt. The floor was hard, and he felt through his knees the solid, indifferent timbers; but the sound in the room changed as he prayed—the servant’s weeping softened, the sick man’s breath caught and let go, and beyond the shutter a sparrow called, careless of human accounts. When Clement rose, his face was composed, as if he had put his own anger—like a pot set too near the flame—further from the heat.

“My son,” he said, “confess thy sins.”

“Ah, father,” whispered Ghysbrecht, “they are many and great.”

“Great, then, be thy penitence, my son; so shalt thou find God’s mercy great.”

Ghysbrecht joined his hands. He began, with the exactness of a man reciting market-weights: he had eaten meat in mid-Lent; he had been absent from mass on the Lord’s day and on saints’ days; he had trifled with fasts and prayers and other observances, naming each omission as if each were a coin laid down on a table.

When he had ended, Clement said very quietly, “’Tis well, my son. These be faults. Now to thy crimes. Thou hadst done better to begin with them.”

The sick man’s eyes blinked, baffled.

“Why, father—what crimes lie to my account if these be none?”

“Am I confessing to thee,” said Clement, and there was steel under the softness, “or thou to me?”

Ghysbrecht swallowed, his throat moving like a bird’s. “Forgive me, father! Surely I to you. But I know not what you call crimes.”

“The seven deadly sins—art thou clear of them?”

“Heaven forefend I should be guilty of them. I know them not by name.”

“Many do them all that cannot name them,” said Clement. “Begin with that one which leads to lying, theft, and murder.”

“I am quit of that one, any way. How call you it?”

“Avarice,” said Clement. “Avarice, my son.”

“Avarice?” Ghysbrecht’s voice rose, thin and plaintive. “Oh, as to that—I have been a saving man all my day; but I have kept a good table, and not altogether forgotten the poor. Alas, I am a great sinner. Mayhap the next will catch me. What is the next?”

“We have not yet done with this one,” said Clement, and the air seemed to tighten, as if the room itself listened. “Bethink thee. The Church is not to be trifled with.”

“Alas! am I in a condition to trifle with her now?” The dying man’s fingers worried the coverlet. “Avarice? Avarice?”

He looked, for a moment, almost like a child who cannot understand why a grown man is angry.

“Hast thou ever robbed the fatherless?” Clement asked.

“Me? robbed the fatherless?” Ghysbrecht gasped. “Not that I mind.”

Clement’s patience had depth, but it had a bottom. “Once more, my son, I am forced to tell thee thou art trifling with the Church. Miserable man! another evasion, and I leave thee; and fiends will straightway gather round thy bed and tear thee down to the bottomless pit.”

At that the dying man’s courage broke like a crust under a knife.

“Oh, leave me not! leave me not!” he cried, clutching at Clement’s sleeve with a strength that startled in so wasted a body. “The Church knows all. I must have robbed the fatherless. I will confess. Who shall I begin with? My memory for names is shaken.”

It was a cunning shelter—fog thrown on a narrow road—yet Clement cut through it with a word, and that word was a name spoken as if it had weight and history and blood.

“Hast thou forgotten Floris Brandt?”

Ghysbrecht reared up among his pillows, eyes bulging, the skin about his mouth drawn tight with terror.

“How knew you that?” he panted.

“The Church knows many things,” said Clement coldly, “and by many ways that are dark to thee. Miserable impenitent, thou calledst her to thy side hoping to deceive her. Thou saidst, ‘I will not confess to the curé, but to some friar who knows not my misdeeds. So will I cheat the Church on my deathbed, and die as I have lived.’ But God, kinder to thee than thou art to thyself, sent to thee one whom thou couldst not deceive.

“He has tried thee. He was patient with thee, and warned thee not to trifle with Holy Church; but all is in vain: thou canst not confess, for thou art impenitent as a stone. Die, then, as thou hast lived. Methinks I see the fiends crowding round the bed for their prey. They wait but for me to go. And I go.”

He turned his back.

There is a sound a man makes when the world is dropping away: not speech, not sobbing, but a raw, animal crack in the breath. Ghysbrecht made it. In extremity he caught Clement by the frock, clutching as if cloth were salvation.

“Oh, holy man—mercy! stay. I will confess all, all.” His voice shook and scraped. “I robbed my friend Floris. Alas! would it had ended there; for he lost little by me; but I kept the land from Peter his son, and from Margaret, Peter’s daughter. Yet I was always going to give it back—but I couldn’t, I couldn’t.”

“Avarice, my son,” said Clement, and the severity in him was not hatred now, but the surgeon’s steadiness. “Avarice. Happy for thee ’tis not too late.”

“No—no; I will leave it her by will.” Ghysbrecht’s eyes darted as if seeking an escape even from repentance. “She will not have long to wait for it now—not above a month or two at farthest.”

“For which month’s possession,” said Clement, and his voice cut clean, “thou wouldst damn thy soul for ever. Thou fool!”

Ghysbrecht groaned, and pleaded weakly for reason, for time, for the small comfort of dying with his fingers still closed on what was not his. Clement held firm; but he did not rage. He spoke with a gentleness that did not yield, persuading and pressing as one kneads stubborn dough—patient, rhythmic, unwilling to be cheated by softness on the surface while the lump remains hard within.

More than once his own blood leaped, and his hand was tempted toward his bosom where the parchment lay: the swift, simple ending; the proof produced; the victory taken. Yet he remembered the previous day’s storm within him, and feared the taste of his own anger as a man fears meat too rich for a fasting stomach. He would not let choler season the work of God.

So he endured. His flesh crept with repugnance to the man, with the stale smell of fear and sweat in the sickroom, with the thin, complaining whine of self-pity; but he mastered that too. Step by step he loosened the dying miser’s grip upon another’s property—upon another’s history, another’s hearth—and at last, without once naming the deed he carried, he won Ghysbrecht to full and prompt restitution.

How that restitution was made shall be told elsewhere; and likewise certain curious effects it wrought in Ghysbrecht, and when and on what terms the burgomaster and the friar parted.

This was Clement’s first overt act, and it showed the temper of his mind after the first fierce tempest had fallen.

The second is told in two words.

As soon as he was quite sure Margaret had her own, and was a rich woman—he disappeared.
