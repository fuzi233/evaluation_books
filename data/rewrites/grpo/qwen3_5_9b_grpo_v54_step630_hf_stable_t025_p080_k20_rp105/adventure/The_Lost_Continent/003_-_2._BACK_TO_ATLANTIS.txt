The words spoken by Tatho did not induce sleep in me that night. Instead, they ignited a cold realization within my chest: I had erred in wrapping myself so completely in the administration of Yucatan, severing my ties to the currents of events rippling through Atlantis. For years, the silence imposed by the Empress had been a thick wall, and I, consumed by my duties in the colony, had possessed neither the curiosity nor the moral imperative to tear down a veil placed by my superiors. It is against my nature to torture men into breaking orders of silence merely to satisfy my private curiosity.

Yet, the iron discipline of the Priestly Clan offered me no alternative path. My removal from office was sudden and absolute. From that moment, all authority and records passed to my successor. While courtesy might allow me to remain in the pyramid I had once commanded to watch another sunrise, the decree was clear: I must leave the territory immediately and hasten back to Atlantis.

Tatho, however, proved eager to facilitate my departure. Before dawn, he stood by my side, offering all his resources. My request was simple: "A ship to take me home, and I shall be your debtor."

His reaction was one of surprise. "You shall certainly have it if you wish," he replied. "But my fleet is foul from the long passage and requires a thorough careen. To take them would mean a slow, tedious voyage to Atlantis. Why not take your own navy? The ships are in harbor; I saw them when we arrived. They are brave vessels."

"But they are not mine," I countered. "That navy belongs to Yucatan."

"Very well, Deucalion," Tatho said, shrugging. "You are Yucatan, or rather, you were yesterday, and have been for twenty years."

I saw the implication and it displeased me. I answered stiffly that the ships belonged to private merchants or the State, and I could not claim even a single ten-slave galley.

Tatho sighed. "I suppose you know your own policies best, though to me it seems risky for a man of your stature not to maintain a personal navy. One never knows when a recall order arrives; without such precautions, a lifetime's earnings can vanish in a dozen hours."

"I have no fear for mine," I said coldly.

"Of course not, because you know me to be your friend. But had another man been appointed to this vice-royalty, you might have been sadly shorn, Deucalion. It is rare for a fellow to resist a snug hoard waiting in the very coffers they guard."

"My Lord Tatho," I replied, "it is clear we have grown apart in taste. The wealth I have amassed in this colony is coveted by few. I wear the poor clothes you see, and possess only a box of stomachic drugs and three slaves: two scribes and a sturdy European savage who cooks and bathes me. For my maintenance, I have drained the State of only a soldier's ration. If any man has extorted a single ounce of bronze from a Yucatan citizen in my name, I request you, as a final service, to hang him as a liar and a thief."

Tatho studied me with a mixture of admiration and pity. "I do not know whether to be astonished or despised. We heard tales of your uprightness in Atlantis, of your stern justice, but no soul guessed you carried your austerity this far. Money is power, Deucalion. With it, nothing can stop you; without it, you are easily trodden down."

"The Gods will choose my fate."

"Possibly. But I prefer to nourish my own. I have not come here to follow your pattern of ascetic vice-royalty. I shall govern Yucatan wisely, but I shall also govern it for the good of Tatho, the viceroy. I brought my navy of eight ships, a personal bodyguard, and my wife with her retinue. These must be provided for. If a people is to be governed, it is their privilege to pay handsomely for their prince."

"We shall not agree on this," I said. "If I thought it useful, I would supplicate you to deal with lenience when taxing these people. They have grown dear to me."

"I have disgusted you, and I am grieved. Yet, even to retain your good opinion—which I value above all—I cannot act as you did. It would be impossible, even if I wished it. Do not judge all men by your strong standard: a Tatho is no colossus like a Deucalion. Besides, I have a wife and children who must be provided for, even if I neglect myself."

"Ah," I said, "there seems to be my advantage. I have no wife to clog me."

He seized on my words quickly. "It seems you have nothing that makes life worth living. No wife, children, riches, cooks, retinue, or finery. You are plaguey ignorant of certain matters. For instance, you do not know how to dine. During our weary voyage, I promised myself that your future feasts would repay the meager sea rations. And when we arrived at the reality? A country farmer sits to more delicate fare than you offer. Your savage cook is lusty, perhaps faithful, but he is a devil-possessed chef. Gods! I have lived better on campaign."

"I eat when hunger bids me," I said coldly, "and I wear this robe until it wears out. The grossness of excessive banqueting and the effeminacy of many clothes never met my fancy. But I think we have talked long enough. There is little chance of agreement. You have changed, Tatho, with the years, and perhaps I have too. Let us part, forgetting these differences, and remembering only our friendship of twenty years ago. That, at least, always had a pleasant savour."

Tatho bowed his head. "So be it."

"And I would still charge myself upon your bounty for that ship. Dawn cannot be far off, and it is indecent for a dismissed ruler to walk the streets in daylight."

"So be it," Tatho agreed. "You shall have my poor navy. I could have wished you asked for something greater."

"Not the navy, Tatho; one small ship. Believe me, more is wasted."

"Now," said Tatho, "I shall act the tyrant. I am viceroy here, and I will have my way. You may depart naked of possessions—that I cannot help—but you shall not depart unattended."

Thus, as fate decreed, I made my transit aboard the "Bear," Tatho's private ship, escorted by the rest of his navy.

The departure was not immediate. The vessels lay moored against the stone quays, gutted of stores, their crews exhausted. To force them out instantly would have been suicide. First, the courtesies were fulfilled: the ships hauled out into the entrance basin, anchored in the swells of the fairway, and then took on wood, water, cured meat, and fish. They refitted with all possible speed.

For myself, there came, for the first time in twenty busy years, a breathing space from work. I had no further official connection with the land of my labors. By rule, I was forbidden to inspect or interfere with the working of the ship, as sea matters were the exclusive, jealously guarded property of the Mariners' Guild.

My days were spent gazing upon the quays, the harbors, the palaces, and the pyramids of the splendid city I had watched rise from its foundations. I roamed my eye over the pastures and grain lands beyond the walls, looking longingly at the dense forests behind, from which we had so tediously carved our territory.

Would Tatho continue the healthy work begun? I trusted so, despite his selfish words. At all hours, under the sun or the stars, I was free to pursue the study of higher mysteries, on which we of the Priestly Clan are trained, without aid of books or temples.

The refitting was completed with unprecedented speed. Never had ships been reprovisioned and caulked so rapidly for an over-ocean voyage. Barely a month after arriving, the fleet put out beyond the walls and began their eastward journey.

Rowing-slaves from Europe were no longer taken for such long passages, as modern humanity forbids the practice of letting them eat one another. Sails alone were an indifferent standby; instead, modern science had shown how to extract force from the Sun. This secret method drew seawater at the forepart of the vessel and ejected it with immense force at the stern, driving the ship forward even against adverse winds.

Navigation had also vastly improved. It was no longer necessary to trust wholly to a starry night. A small image, balanced in the forepart of every vessel with an arm outstretched, pointed constantly to the Southern Cross. By setting an angle, a true course could be steered. Other instruments existed for finding a true position on the ocean wastes; the newer mariner put little trust in the Gods and confided mightily in his own strength and wit.

Still, it was amusing to see these tarry fellows take their last farewell of the harbor town. Once the ship was stowed and ready, they washed, donned their finest attire, and went ashore to seek obscure temples where their gods held little sway among shore folk. There, they made sacrifices with clamor and lavish outlay, followed by a feast. They returned to the ship drunk, heavy, and evil-humored with gluttony.

Our voyage was vastly different from my previous sea-goings. We did not creep timidly along the coasts. We stood straight across the open gulf toward home, passing the band of the Carib Islands with confidence, as though they were signposts marking the sea highway. We stopped only twice to replenish wood, water, and fruit. The savages brought these commodities freely, their subjection so complete that neither stop involved even the semblance of a fight. It was a certificate of the growing power of Atlantis and her finest overseas colony.

Then boldly on we went across the vast ocean beyond, with no sacrifices to implore the Gods for direction. One might censure these rugged mariners for their impiety, yet one could not help admiring their lusty skill and confidence.

The dangers of the desolate sea are dealt out as the Gods will. We encountered storms, fought with stubborn endurance, and twice saw blazing stones from Heaven hiss into the sea beside us without injury. As unavoidable, the great beasts of the sea hunted us with their accustomed savagery. Only once did we suffer material loss, when three great sea lizards attacked the "Bear" simultaneously.

The onset occurred during the blazing midday heat, when our solar machines were drawing full force. The vessel traveled faster than a man could walk on dry land, but against the beasts, it might as well have been standing still. Three of them rose over the horizon, beating the sea into foam and waving great necks like masts. Our navy was spread in a long line; in olden days, each beast would have selected separate prey, but these creatures had learned the necessities of warfare. They hunted in packs.

They made for our ship. Tob, the captain, urged me to retreat to the after-castle for safety, citing his responsibility to Lord Tatho for my safe conduct. He warned that the beasts would seize some of the crew before being satiated, and if harm came to Lord Deucalion, Tob would have to surrender himself voluntarily to the beasts to avoid a painful death at Tatho's hands later.

However, my mind was set. A man can never have too much experience in fighting enemies, whether human or bestial. This attack was new to me, and I was eager to learn its method. I gave Tob a letter to Tatho explaining my resolve (for which the rude fellow seemed little grateful) and stayed in my chair under the awning.

The beasts surged up with champing jaws, and all the shipmen stood armed for defense. Two females flanked the ship, while the giant male approached alone. Their great heads swooped high as the yards, and the reek from them induced physical sickness.

The shipmen faced the monsters with sturdy courage. Arrows were useless against the smooth, bull-like hides. Throwing fire could not even singe them; only twenty axe blows delivered together on an attacking head could beat it back, and even then, only through sheer weight of metal, causing no real wound.

Since time immemorial, beasts have disputed with man the mastery of the earth. Only in Atlantis, Egypt, and Yucatan has man dared to hold his own, fighting with a mind strengthened by previous victories. In Europe and mid-Africa, the greater beasts hold full dominion, forcing man into crannies and tree-tops as a fugitive. Upon the great oceans, the beasts are lords, unchecked.

Still, upon this desolate sea, though the giant lizards were new to me, it was a pleasure to pit my knowledge of war against their brute strength. Since the first men did business upon the waters, they fulfilled their instincts in fighting these beasts with desperation. Hiding cowardly in the hold was useless; if the enemy could not find men above decks to glut them, they would break the ship with their paddles, slaughtering all. Thus, it was recognized that the fight must go forward as desperately as possible, ending only when the beasts had their fill and departed.

In this one-sided conflict, I found myself feeling the joy of having my thews in action. After delivering a dozen lusty blows with my axe—blows that might have been delivered against a city wall or the ark of the Mysteries—I sought a lance. With that, I made very different play.

The lizards' eyes are small and set deep in bony sockets, but I judged them vulnerable. I attacked the eyes. The decks were slippery with horrid slime, and the crew surged about, constantly offering themselves as a rampart before me due to Tob's threats. I gave a few shrewd proges with the lance to show I would not have my will overridden, and presently was given room for maneuver.

Deliberately, I placed myself in the sight of one of the lizards and offered my body to its attack.
