If my dear friend from Flatland still possessed the sharp mental vigor he held when he first penned these memoirs, I would not need to speak for him here. Yet, he asks me to address three matters on his behalf: first, to thank the readers and critics of Spaceland whose enthusiastic reception has necessitated this second printing; second, to offer apologies for various errors and misprints (though he claims partial blame for none of them); and third, to clarify a few misunderstandings regarding his arguments.

However, the man who wrote the original text is not the same as the one seeking this revision. Seven years of solitary confinement, compounded by the crushing weight of universal skepticism and ridicule, have worn down his spirit. Coupled with the inevitable decline of old age, these hardships have eroded many of the specific concepts and technical terms he learned during his brief exile in Spaceland. Consequently, he has asked me to respond to two primary objections raised against his work: one intellectual, the other moral.

The first objection is this: A Flatlander, observing a Line, sees something that must possess *thickness* to be visible to the eye, not merely length. Therefore, it is argued, Flatlanders must admit that their world possesses a third dimension—however slight—that makes beings "high" as well as long and broad. To Spacelanders, this argument is nearly irrefutable, and I must confess that upon hearing it initially, I found myself at a loss for a rebuttal.

Yet, my aged friend's response strikes me as definitive.

"I concede," he told me, "the factual basis of your critic's claim, but I reject his conclusion. It is undeniable that within Flatland exists a Third Dimension, unrecognized by us, which we might term 'Height,' just as it is true that within Spaceland exists a Fourth Dimension, currently unnamed by you, which I shall call 'Extra-Height.' However, just as you cannot perceive or measure your 'Extra-Height,' we cannot perceive or measure our 'Height.' Even I—who spent twenty-four hours in Spaceland and grasped the concept of Height—cannot now fully comprehend it through sight or pure logic; I can only grasp it through faith.

"The reason is self-evident. A dimension implies direction, measurement, and the distinction between 'more' and 'less.' All lines in Flatland are equally and infinitesimally thick (or high). Because there is no variation in this attribute, our minds cannot conceive of it as a distinct dimension. No 'delicate micrometer,' as one overly eager Spaceland critic suggested, would help us; for we would not know *what* to measure, nor *in which direction*. When we see a Line, we see something Long and Bright. Brightness is essential to a Line's existence; without it, the line vanishes. Thus, whenever I attempt to explain this unrecognized dimension to my fellow Flatlanders, they simply ask, 'Do you mean Brightness?' When I insist, 'No, I mean a true spatial dimension,' they immediately counter, 'Then measure it, or tell us its direction.' And I am silenced, for I can do neither.

"Only yesterday, the Chief Circle—our High Priest—visited the State Prison for his seventh annual inspection. He looked at me and asked, 'Are you any better?' I attempted to demonstrate that he, too, possessed 'Height,' alongside his known Length and Breadth, though he remained unaware of it. His reply was chilling: 'You claim I am "high"? Measure my height, and I will believe you.' Stumped, unable to meet his demand, I was crushed. He departed victorious.

"Do this scenario still seem absurd? Imagine instead a visitor from the Fourth Dimension descending to visit you. They would say: 'Every time you open your eyes, you see a Plane (Two Dimensions) and infer a Solid (Three), but in reality, you also witness a Fourth Dimension—not color, not brightness, but a true dimension. Yet, I cannot show you its direction, nor can you measure it.' What would you think of such a guest? Would you not imprison them? That is my fate. Just as it is natural for Flatlanders to jail a Square for preaching the Third Dimension, it is equally natural for Spacelanders to jail a Cube for preaching the Fourth. Alas, how deeply rooted is the similarity among blind and persecuting humanity across all dimensions! Whether Point, Line, Square, Cube, or Hypercube, we are all prone to the same errors, all enslaved by the prejudices of our respective dimensional prisons, as one of your Spaceland poets wisely noted: 'One touch of Nature makes all worlds akin.'"

Regarding this intellectual defense, I find it impregnable. However, I fear his answer to the second objection—the moral charge—is less clear. Critics have vehemently accused him of misogyny. Since this accusation comes largely from the larger half of the Spaceland population, I wish to address it as best I can.

My friend is unfamiliar with the nuanced moral vocabulary of Spaceland, so a literal transcription of his defense would be unfair to him. Instead, acting as his interpreter, I summarize his evolution. During his seven-year imprisonment, his personal views on Women and the Isosceles (Lower Classes) shifted significantly. Privately, he now aligns with the Sphere's belief that Straight Lines hold superiority in many crucial aspects compared to Circles.

Yet, writing as a historian, he feels compelled to identify with the prevailing views of Flatland—and indeed, traditional historians of Spaceland—as well. Until very recently, historical records in both realms rarely deemed the fates of women and the masses worthy of serious consideration. Furthermore, he seeks to distance himself from the circular, aristocratic leanings often attributed to him. While acknowledging the immense wisdom and generational dominance of the Circles over the multitude, he argues that the raw facts of Flatland speak for themselves: revolutions cannot always be quelled by bloodshed. Moreover, Nature itself has sentenced the Circles to infertility, condemning them to eventual extinction. As he puts it, "Herein I see the fulfillment of the Great Law of all worlds: while human wisdom believes it is forging one outcome, the wisdom of Nature compels it to produce another, and a vastly superior one."

Finally, he begs his readers not to expect every trivial detail of Flatland to map perfectly onto Spaceland. Nevertheless, he hopes that, viewed as a whole, his work offers both amusement and profound suggestion to those Spacelanders of moderate and humble minds. These are the individuals who, when contemplating truths of supreme importance lying beyond direct experience, refuse to dismiss them with "This can never be," nor accept dogmatically that "It must be exactly thus, and we know everything about it."

"Patience, little friend. The world is vast, and its dimensions are often hidden until we learn to look beyond the surface."

In this realm of Flatland, existence is defined strictly by two coordinates: length and breadth. There is no height, no depth, no third dimension to offer refuge or perspective. We live on an infinite plane where every line segment stretches forever unless bounded by others. Our climate is uniform, governed solely by the angle at which lines intersect; our houses are simple polygons, their stability determined entirely by the number of sides they possess. A triangle is humble but sharp; a square is sturdy and common; a pentagon holds a place of minor nobility.

The inhabitants of Flatland move with purpose along these flat axes. To recognize one another, we rely first on touch—a gentle tap against the side reveals the shape of our neighbor. But sight, too, has become a refined art. By observing how light reflects off our edges, we can deduce our status and identity without contact. Yet, irregular figures exist among us—those who lack symmetry or proper angles—and they are viewed with suspicion, often feared as chaotic anomalies that threaten the order of the plane.

For centuries, we have practiced painting, attempting to capture the essence of shapes through color. However, this led to a dangerous sedition when some dared to claim that color was a fundamental property of reality rather than a mere decoration. The Universal Colour Bill was enacted to suppress this heresy, stripping away hue and enforcing a monochrome society where geometry alone defines truth.

Our priests, clad in circles—the perfect figure with infinite sides—guard the Doctrine of Two Dimensions. They preach that any suggestion of a third dimension is blasphemy, a delusion born of ignorance. Their authority is absolute, rooted in the belief that perfection lies within the flatness of our world.

But recently, strange visions have begun to plague my mind. I dreamt of a world called Lineland, where beings exist as mere points moving back and forth on a single line, unaware of anything beyond their forward and backward paths. I tried to explain the nature of Flatland to them, describing our width and length, but my words fell on deaf ears; to a point, all directions are indistinguishable except for the axis of motion.

Then came a stranger from Spaceland—a being of such profound complexity that he appeared as a circle to my eyes, yet moved with a fluidity impossible for a flatlander. He spoke of a third dimension, a direction perpendicular to our own, into which we could step if only we knew how. At first, his words were met with ridicule and fear. When language failed him, he resorted to action, projecting shadows upon our plane that danced and shifted in ways no polygon could replicate.

Through these demonstrations, I was transported briefly into Spaceland itself. There, I beheld wonders beyond comprehension: spheres rolling freely, cubes floating in voids, and creatures whose forms defied the rigid constraints of our two-dimensional logic. Though the Sphere showed me many mysteries, my desire for knowledge remained insatiable. Encouraged by these revelations, I returned to share what I had seen, even as the authorities sought to silence me.

Now, I attempt to teach the Theory of Three Dimensions to my grandson, hoping to awaken his mind to possibilities beyond the flat horizon. So far, his understanding remains limited, much like mine once was. Undeterred, I seek other means to spread this liberating idea, knowing full well that in a world built on the illusion of completeness, the truth of higher dimensions may be the most radical revolution of all.

I designate our realm *Flatland* not merely as a name we use, but as a descriptor essential for clarifying its true essence to you, my fortunate readers dwelling in the boundless expanse of *Space*. Envision a vast, infinite plane—a sheet of paper where straight lines, triangles, squares, pentagons, hexagons, and myriad other geometric forms drift freely across the surface. Crucially, these shapes possess no ability to rise above or sink below this plane; they exist strictly within it, resembling shadows cast upon a wall, yet possessing substance, hardness, and luminous edges. This mental image offers a remarkably accurate conception of my nation and its citizens. Once, I might have termed this entire existence "my universe," but recent revelations have expanded my perspective to comprehend dimensions beyond our own.

In such a realm, it is logically impossible for anything of a "solid" nature to exist. One might reasonably assume, however, that despite this limitation, we could visually distinguish between the various figures—triangles, squares, and others—as they moved about. This assumption is fundamentally incorrect. We cannot see distinct shapes at all; rather, the only visual reality available to us consists entirely of straight lines. I shall demonstrate this necessity immediately.

Place a coin upon the center of a table in Space and lean over it to gaze downward. To your eyes, suspended above the surface, the coin appears as a perfect circle. Now, draw back to the table's edge and gradually lower your viewpoint, aligning your eye closer and closer to the tabletop. As you descend, mimicking the condition of a Flatlander, the coin transforms from a circle into an increasingly elongated oval. Finally, position your eye precisely flush against the edge of the table. At this exact moment, the coin ceases to appear oval entirely; it resolves into a simple, straight line.

The same phenomenon occurs with any shape cut from pasteboard, whether a triangle, square, or complex polygon. When viewed with the eye perfectly level with the surface, the object loses all definition of form and presents itself solely as a linear segment. Consider, for instance, an equilateral triangle, which among us signifies a tradesman of respectable standing. From a high vantage point, one perceives the full triangular geometry. As the observer lowers their gaze, the shape distorts. But when the eye rests completely on the plane—the sole mode of vision for Flatlanders—the triangle vanishes as a figure, leaving behind nothing but a straight line.

When I resided in Spaceland, I observed that your sailors encounter a striking parallel during their voyages. When gazing upon a distant island or coastline stretching along the horizon, the intricate details of bays, headlands, and indentations remain invisible. Unless illuminated sharply by your sun to create contrast through light and shadow, the landmass appears merely as a continuous, grey line upon the water. This is precisely our experience in Flatland. There is no sun here, nor any lighting mechanism capable of casting shadows to reveal depth. Consequently, when a companion approaches, regardless of whether they are a triangle, square, pentagon, hexagon, or circle, they appear to us only as a straight line growing larger. As they recede, the line shrinks, yet it remains a line, devoid of angularity or curvature.

You may naturally inquire how we manage to distinguish friends from strangers under such severe visual constraints. The answer to this profound question requires a deeper understanding of our society, which I shall address when describing the inhabitants themselves. For now, permit me to briefly touch upon the climate and architecture of our unique world.

Just as with you, so too with us, there exist four cardinal points: North, South, East, and West. However, since we possess neither a sun nor any other celestial body, determining the direction of North is impossible through conventional observation. Instead, we rely upon a unique natural law governing our realm. There exists a constant gravitational pull toward the South. While this attraction is negligible in temperate zones—allowing even a woman of sound health to travel several furlongs northward without significant strain—it remains sufficiently potent to function as a reliable compass across most of our land. Furthermore, the rain, which descends at regular intervals exclusively from the North, serves as an additional navigational aid. Within our cities, the architecture itself provides orientation; our dwellings are constructed with side-walls aligned primarily North-South, ensuring that their roofs effectively shield inhabitants from northern precipitation. In the countryside, lacking built structures, the trunks of trees offer a similar, albeit rougher, guide. Consequently, we encounter far less disorientation than one might anticipate.

Yet, in our milder regions where the southern pull is barely perceptible, I have found myself stranded in utterly desolate plains, devoid of both habitation and vegetation. On such occasions, I have been forced to stand motionless for hours, awaiting the arrival of the rain before resuming my journey. This force affects the frail, the elderly, and particularly delicate females with far greater severity than the robust males. Thus, it is considered a mark of refined breeding to yield the North side of the road to any lady encountered in the street—a courtesy that proves difficult to execute promptly when one is in vigorous health and struggling to distinguish between North and South amidst the confusion.

Our houses contain no windows. For light permeates our existence uniformly, whether indoors or outdoors, by day or by night; its source remains entirely unknown to us. In ancient times, our scholars devoted considerable effort to investigating the origin of light, attempting various solutions that ultimately resulted only in overcrowding our institutions for the mentally disturbed. Following failed attempts to curb such inquiries via taxation, the Legislature recently enacted an absolute prohibition on the subject. Alas, I alone among the citizens of Flatland now grasp the true nature of this enigma. My understanding—that light originates from a third dimension—is unintelligible to my compatriots. They mock me, the solitary keeper of the secrets of Space and the theory of dimensional intrusion, treating me as the most deranged of the insane.

Setting aside these painful diversions, let us return to our dwellings. The predominant architectural form is the pentagon, as illustrated below. The two northern sides, labeled RO and OF, constitute the roof and typically lack doors. The eastern wall features a small entrance reserved for women, while the western wall boasts a significantly larger doorway for men. The southern base, serving as the floor, is generally left without a door.

Square and triangular residences are strictly prohibited. The acute angles inherent in squares—and even more so in equilateral triangles—pose a severe hazard. Since the outlines of inanimate objects like buildings appear dimmer than the distinct lines of living beings, there is a grave risk that the sharp corners of such structures could inflict serious injury upon an unsuspecting traveler who happens to collide with them. Recognizing this danger, laws banning triangular housing were established as early as the eleventh century of our era. Exceptions were granted solely for military necessities: fortifications, powder magazines, barracks, and other state facilities deemed unsuitable for casual public access.

At that time, square houses remained legal throughout the land, though they were discouraged by a specific levy. Approximately three centuries later, legislation decreed that in any town exceeding a population of ten thousand, the pentagonal angle must be the minimum allowable for residential construction to ensure public safety. The wisdom of the populace has largely supported these legislative efforts; consequently, even in rural areas, the pentagonal design has completely supplanted all others. Today, one finds a square house only rarely, hidden away in some distant, backward agricultural district, preserved merely by an antiquarian's curiosity.

The average adult citizen of Flatland measures roughly eleven inches in length or width, with twelve inches serving as the absolute limit. Among us, women exist solely as straight lines. Our soldiers and the lowest tier of laborers take the form of triangles possessing two equal sides, approximately eleven inches each, joined by a third side so minuscule—sometimes barely half an inch—that they create a vertex of terrifying acuteness. When these bases degrade further, shrinking to no more than one-eighth of an inch, these figures become nearly indistinguishable from the straight-line women; their points are so sharply defined that they appear merely as lines. We classify these specific triangles as *Isosceles*, a designation I shall retain throughout this account.

Our middle class comprises equilateral triangles, those with three perfectly equal sides. Above them stand the professional men and gentlemen, represented by squares and pentagons—a category to which I, the narrator, belong. Rising further are the nobility, graded by increasing complexity: starting with hexagons (six-sided figures) and ascending through polygons with ever-greater numbers of sides until they achieve the prestigious title of "Polygonal." At the apex of society lies the Circular or Priestly order. These beings possess so many sides, each so infinitesimally small, that they appear indistinguishable from circles. This is the supreme caste.

A fundamental law of nature governs our reproduction: a male child must possess exactly one more side than his father. Consequently, humanity naturally ascends the ladder of nobility with every generation. A square fathers a pentagon; a pentagon, a hexagon; and so forth. However, this progressive law holds true only for the tradesmen, rarely for soldiers, and almost never for the workmen. Lacking equal sides, these lower castes are scarcely deserving of the term "human figures." For them, the natural law is suspended; the son of an Isosceles triangle remains an Isosceles triangle.

Yet, despair is not total for the Isosceles. Through a prolonged series of military victories or diligent, skilled labor, the more intellectually gifted artisans and soldiers often exhibit a subtle transformation: their narrow base widens slightly while their two longer sides contract. When priests arrange marriages between the children of these enlightened lower-class individuals, the resulting offspring increasingly resemble the ideal equilateral triangle. Though exceedingly rare given the vast population of Isosceles births, the production of a genuine, certified equilateral triangle from Isosceles parents occurs occasionally.

*Note:* One might ask, "Why require certification? Does not the birth of a square son prove the father's regularity?" My answer is that no woman of standing will wed an uncertified triangle. While irregular triangles have occasionally produced square offspring, the defect usually resurfaces in the third generation, preventing advancement to the pentagonal rank or causing a regression to triangularity. Achieving a true equilateral birth demands not only strategic intermarriage but also decades of frugal living, rigorous self-discipline, and the steady cultivation of intellect across multiple generations. The birth of such a "True Equilateral" from humble roots triggers celebrations spanning dozens of miles. Upon passing a stringent inspection by the Sanitary and Social Board, the infant is ceremoniously elevated to the class of Equilaterals. Immediately thereafter, he is removed from his proud yet grieving biological family and adopted by a childless equilateral noble. An oath binds the adopter to sever all ties with the child's origins forever, fearing that mere exposure to his former environment might cause the newly formed organism to regress to its hereditary state through unconscious imitation.

This phenomenon serves a dual purpose. It offers a glimmer of hope to the impoverished serfs, illuminating the bleak monotony of their lives, while simultaneously reassuring the aristocracy. The higher classes understand that these rare exceptions act as a vital buffer against revolution. If the masses were entirely devoid of ambition, they might find charismatic leaders capable of challenging the wisdom of the Circles. Instead, nature has ordained a Law of Compensation: as the working classes grow in intelligence, virtue, and knowledge, their defining acute angle diminishes, rendering them physically less threatening and closer to the harmless geometry of the equilateral triangle. Even within the most brutal soldier class—beings otherwise comparable to women in their lack of intellect—it is observed that as they develop the mental capacity to wield their formidable penetrating power effectively, their physical sharpness wanes. What a magnificent balance! What irrefutable evidence of the divine design behind our aristocratic hierarchy!

Through the astute application of this natural law, Polygons and Circles routinely extinguish sedition in its infancy, capitalizing on the boundless optimism inherent in the human spirit. Furthermore, art aids the machinery of law and order. State physicians can artificially compress or expand the bodies of rebellious leaders to render them geometrically regular, instantly granting them entry into the privileged classes. Those deemed insufficiently advanced but hopeful are lured into State Hospitals for honorable lifelong confinement. Only the most obstinate, foolish, and hopelessly irregular face execution. Ultimately, the wretched, leaderless mob of Isosceles is either neutralized by the small contingent of loyalists maintained by the Chief Circle for such emergencies, or, more frequently, driven into internecine conflict by jealousies and suspicions skillfully incited by the ruling Cyclic faction, perishing under each other's sharp angles. Our historical records document no fewer than one hundred and twenty major rebellions, alongside two hundred and thirty-five minor uprisings—all concluded in this manner.

If our sharply pointed Triangles of the Soldier class are formidable, it requires little inference to deduce that our Women are far more dangerous. While a Soldier is merely a wedge, a Woman is a needle—essentially pure point at both extremities. Combine this lethal geometry with the ability to render oneself practically invisible at will, and one realizes that a Female in Flatland is a force never to be trifled with.

Perhaps my younger readers in Spaceland might wonder *how* a two-dimensional being achieves invisibility. The answer lies in perspective, requiring no complex machinery. Consider a needle placed on a table. Viewed from the side, its full length is visible; viewed from the tip, it appears as nothing but a tiny dot, effectively vanishing. So too with our Women. When their broad side faces us, we perceive a straight line—a distinct threat. When the end bearing their eye or mouth (which, in our dimension, serve as a single organ) turns toward us, we see only a brilliant, lustrous point, rendering them nearly invisible. Conversely, when their back is presented, appearing dull and dimmer than an inanimate object, their rear extremity functions as a natural "Invisible Cap."

The peril posed by such entities should be obvious to anyone of average intellect in Spaceland. If the obtuse angle of a respectable middle-class Triangle can cause injury; if colliding with a Working Man results in a deep gash; if striking an Officer inflicts a grievous wound; and if a mere brush with the vertex of a Private Soldier risks death—then running into a Woman guarantees absolute and immediate destruction. Furthermore, given their tendency to vanish into visibility, avoiding collision becomes a near-impossible feat for even the most cautious citizen.

Consequently, various states across Flatland have enacted strict laws to mitigate this existential hazard. These statutes are particularly rigorous in southern regions where gravity is stronger, causing inhabitants to move more erratically. The core of our Female Code generally includes three pillars:

1.  **Restricted Entry:** Every dwelling must feature a dedicated eastern entrance used exclusively by Females. They must approach "in a becoming and respectful manner," strictly forbidden from using the western door reserved for Males.
2.  **The Peace-Cry:** No Female may traverse any public space without continuously emitting a specific auditory signal—the "Peace-cry"—under penalty of death.
3.  **Elimination of Volatility:** Any Female certified to suffer from conditions causing involuntary motion—such as St. Vitus's Dance, epileptic fits, or severe allergic reactions—is to be instantly destroyed. Some states go further, mandating that women constantly sway their backs right-to-left to ensure visibility, or require them to be escorted by a son, servant, or husband. Others confine women entirely within their homes except during religious festivals.

However, the wisest legislators have recognized that excessive restriction breeds disaster. Over-confinement exasperates the female temperament, leading to domestic violence that often decimates entire villages in moments of collective outrage. Thus, the three laws above remain sufficient for stable societies. Ultimately, our greatest safeguard is not legislation, but self-preservation. A Woman can kill instantly with a backward thrust, but unless she can withdraw her stinging tip before the struggle ends, her own fragile form shatters.

Moreover, the power of fashion aids us. In civilized states, rhythmic back-swaying is considered a mark of breeding, a natural instinct rather than a legal requirement. Ladies of high rank perform a modulated undulation of the spine, envied and emulated by wives of lesser geometric shapes—from the simple pendulum-like swing of the Equilateral to the aspirational movements of the Isosceles. Consequently, in households of status, this "back motion" is ubiquitous, granting husbands and sons immunity from invisible attacks.

Do not mistake this for a lack of affection. Unfortunately, in the Frail Sex, the passion of the moment overrides reason. Deprived of angles, they possess no brain-power, lacking reflection, judgment, foresight, or memory. In a fit of fury, they recognize no distinctions or past grievances. I have witnessed a woman exterminate her entire household, only to ask thirty minutes later, after her rage had subsided, where her husband and children had gone. Therefore, a Woman must never be provoked while able to turn around. Once confined to apartments designed to deny her rotational freedom, she becomes harmless; unable to strike, she forgets threats and broken promises within minutes.

Domestic life proceeds relatively smoothly, save for the lower military classes. Here, husbands' reliance on their sharp angles rather than diplomacy leads to catastrophe. Lacking the tact to retreat or the skill to offer lavish, soothing promises, they provoke their wives with bluntness outside the home and fail to retract their words immediately. The result is massacre—an outcome some Circles view providentially as a method of culling redundant populations and stifling revolution.

Even in our most harmonious, circular families, life lacks the depth found in Spaceland. Safety comes at the cost of comfort. Harmony of taste is rare. An ancient instinct dictates that mothers and daughters must constantly face their husbands and male guests; for a lady of distinction to turn her back is deemed a portent of lost status. While working-class wives enjoy brief periods of silence while facing inward toward their chores, the homes of the elite are perpetually noisy. With eyes and mouths fixed eternally on the master, the stream of feminine discourse is relentless. The skills required to dodge a physical sting are useless against a verbal barrage. Since a Woman has nothing meaningful to say and no moral constraint to stop her, many cynical men prefer the silent danger of the invisible sting to the deafening noise of her open end.

To the citizens of Spaceland, the plight of our Women seems tragic, and rightly so. While a lowly Isosceles male might hope to improve his angle and elevate his caste, no Woman harbors such aspirations. "Once a Woman, always a Woman" is a decree of nature, seemingly suspending the laws of evolution against her. Yet, we find solace in this prearranged design: having no hopes for the future, they possess no memory to recall past humiliations and no foresight to anticipate new ones. Their misery is total, but so is their oblivion.

To you, inhabitants of the Third Dimension, who enjoy the luxury of shadow as well as light, possess the dual vision required to grasp perspective, and delight in a spectrum of colors, how can I possibly convey the sheer impossibility we face in trying to recognize one another? Remember what I have told you: whether animate or inanimate, regardless of our true shape, everything in Flatland presents itself to our gaze merely as a straight line. If everyone looks exactly the same, how do we tell one from another?

The solution lies in three distinct methods.

First, we rely upon **Hearing**. This sense is far more refined in us than yours, allowing us not only to identify friends by their voices but also to distinguish between the three lowest castes: the Equilateral Triangle, the Square, and the Pentagon. I make no such claim regarding the Isosceles, whose voices are too variable. However, as we climb the social ladder, auditory identification becomes increasingly unreliable. Voices tend to blur together, and the ability to discern subtle tonal differences is considered a trait of the common folk, rarely cultivated among the aristocracy. Consequently, whenever deception is suspected, we abandon sound entirely. Furthermore, among the lower orders, vocal cords are often overdeveloped relative to our hearing capabilities. An Isosceles can easily mimic the tone of a Polygon, and with sufficient training, even feign the voice of a Circle. Thus, hearing alone is insufficient for security.

Therefore, we turn to the second, and most common, method: **Touch** or *Feeling*. For women and the lower classes, this is the primary test of identity, especially when determining someone's rank rather than their specific individuality. Just as introductions in your world involve presenting oneself, here the standard greeting—particularly among older gentry in rural districts—is, *"Permit me to ask you to feel and be felt by my friend Mr. So-and-so."* In the bustling cities and among merchants, the phrase "be felt by" is dropped, leaving simply, *"Let me ask you to feel Mr. So-and-so,"* with the mutual nature of the action implied. Even the most modern, hurried young gentlemen have shortened this further, using "to feel" as a technical term meaning "to recommend for the purpose of feeling and being felt." Indeed, current slang in high society permits the barbarism, *"Mr. Smith, permit me to feel Mr. Jones."*

Do not imagine, however, that this process is as laborious for us as it would be for you, nor that we must trace every side of a stranger to classify them. Through rigorous schooling and lifelong practice, we can instantly differentiate the sharp corners of an equilateral triangle, square, or pentagon by touching just a single angle. The obtuse, dull vertex of an Isosceles is equally obvious to the slightest touch. Therefore, feeling one corner usually suffices to reveal a person's class, except when dealing with the higher nobility.

There, the challenge intensifies. Even a Master of Arts at our University of Wentbridge may struggle to distinguish a ten-sided figure from a twelve-sided one. Few Doctors of Science can confidently and immediately tell the difference between a twenty-sided and a twenty-four-sided member of the aristocracy.

Moreover, the procedure demands caution. As you recall from the laws governing women, improper handling during introduction can be fatal. The person being touched (*the Felt*) must remain absolutely motionless. A sudden jerk, a restless shift, or even a violent sneeze has proven deadly to the unsuspecting toucher (*the Feeler*), ending countless budding friendships prematurely. This risk is particularly acute among the lower-class Triangles. Their eyes are positioned so far from their apex that they cannot monitor the interaction occurring at their tip. Combined with their rough, insensitive natures, they lack the delicacy required for the highly organized Polygons. It is no surprise, then, that an involuntary head toss has previously cost the state a valuable life.

My grandfather, a remarkably upright Isosceles who recently earned four of seven votes to advance to the status of an Equilateral, often recounted a tragedy involving his great-great-grandfather. That ancestor, a working man with an angle of precisely 59 degrees and 30 minutes, was suffering from rheumatism while being examined by a Polygon. Startled, he involuntarily thrust forward, piercing the nobleman diagonally. The resulting scandal, coupled with the shame of imprisonment, caused the family's genetic potential to regress. For five generations, their angles remained stuck at 58 degrees. Only after a long period did they recover the lost ground, reaching the full 60 degrees required to escape the Isosceles caste forever. One accidental movement during a simple introduction thus doomed a lineage for centuries.

At this juncture, I anticipate the protest of my more enlightened readers: *"How can you Flatlanders know angles, degrees, or minutes? You see only a line; we see the inclination of two lines forming a corner. How can you discern what you cannot see?"*

My answer is that while we cannot *see* angles, we can *infer* them with remarkable precision. Our sense of touch, honed by necessity and discipline, allows us to detect angular differences more accurately than your unaided sight. Furthermore, nature provides us with a built-in scale. By law, the brain of an Isosceles begins at half a degree and increases by half a degree per generation until it reaches the threshold of 60 degrees, granting freedom. Consequently, elementary schools across the land display specimens representing every half-degree increment from 0.5° up to 60°.

Due to societal stagnation and the prolific birth rates of criminal and vagrant classes, there is a surplus of individuals with small angles. Most of these, lacking the intellect for war, are conscripted by the state as living teaching tools. Immobilized to prevent harm, they serve as static models in infant classrooms, allowing children of the middle class to learn the tactile nuances of geometry—a skill these specimens themselves lack. While some states feed these specimens for years, more progressive regions find it economically superior to provide no sustenance, replacing them monthly. This cycle lasts roughly the average lifespan of a criminal, balancing educational costs against the slight reduction in angular accuracy that occurs over time. Additionally, the constant rotation of these specimens contributes marginally to reducing the surplus population of low-angle Isosceles, a goal every statesman cherishes.

While some school boards favor the cheaper, longer-lived specimens, I believe that in matters of education, expense is often the truest economy. Yet, I must not let political debates distract from my main point.

It is evident that recognition by touch is far more reliable than recognition by sound. Nevertheless, despite its utility, the inherent dangers of physical contact mean that many in the middle and lower classes—and all members of the Polygonal and Circular orders—prefer a third method. The description of this final technique will be reserved for the following section.

I stand ready to admit a glaring inconsistency. Earlier, I asserted that all figures in Flatland appear merely as straight lines, implying that visual distinction between social classes is impossible. Yet, now I must explain to my Spaceland critics how we *do* recognize one another by sight.

If the Reader recalls the section on "Recognition by Feeling," they will note a crucial qualifier: that ability is universal only "among the lower classes." Visual recognition is the privilege of the higher castes and thrives exclusively in our temperate climates. This unique faculty exists solely because of the **Fog**. While you in Spaceland view fog as a blight—obscuring landscapes and sapping vitality—we regard it as a blessing nearly equal to air itself, the very nurse of our arts and parent of our sciences. Without this atmospheric veil, all lines would appear with identical clarity, rendering discrimination impossible. Indeed, in those desolate regions where the air is perfectly dry and transparent, such confusion reigns.

However, where Fog abounds, objects at varying distances lose intensity differently. An object three feet away appears noticeably dimmer than one twenty-nine inches distant. Through rigorous training and constant observation of these gradients of light and shadow, we deduce the precise configuration of an approaching figure.

Consider this practical example. Suppose I observe two figures approaching: a Merchant (an Equilateral Triangle) and a Physician (a Pentagon). How do I tell them apart?

Imagine aligning my gaze to bisect the angle of the approaching stranger. My view sits squarely between the two sides closest to me. In the case of the Merchant, I perceive a single line where the center is brilliant, but the edges fade into darkness with alarming speed. His sides recede sharply into the mist, making his extremities vanish quickly. Conversely, the Physician presents a line where the center shines brightly, but the fading toward the edges is gradual. His sides penetrate deeper into the Fog, keeping his extremities significantly brighter than the Merchant's.

Through this nuanced reading of light decay, the educated elite can distinguish between the middle and lowest orders with surprising accuracy. However, do not mistake this for simplicity. Life rarely offers static angles. If my father, the Triangle, approaches presenting a flat side rather than an angle, I am momentarily confused, unsure if he is a Triangle or a Woman (a Straight Line). Similarly, observing a Hexagon face-on reveals a complex interplay of bright central segments and dim peripheral lines that requires instant mental calculation.

To navigate a crowded ballroom filled with rotating polygons of various ranks is a task that taxes even the sharpest intellect. It justifies the existence of our prestigious universities, where Static and Kinetic Geometry are taught to the elite. Only the wealthy and noble can afford the decades of study required to master this art. Even I, a respected mathematician and grandfather of two Hexagons, often feel bewildered amidst the swirling chaos of high-society gatherings. To a commoner or serf, such a scene is incomprehensible—a chaotic blur of shifting lights. Even a student versed in theory would struggle to move through such a crowd without accidentally colliding with superiors, a breach of etiquette punishable by shame, as these refined individuals possess an intuitive awareness of movement that the untrained lack.

Ultimately, to behave with propriety in Flatland, one must essentially *be* a polygon.

The mastery of Sight Recognition is honed by habit and strictly discouraged alongside the older method of "Feeling." Just as the deaf-mute in Spaceland fails to achieve true speech if reliant on gestures, the Flatlander who relies on touch never masters vision. Consequently, "Feeling" is banned among the upper classes. Children are sent directly to exclusive seminaries; to touch another person is a grave offense leading to expulsion. Among the poor, however, visual acumen remains an unattainable luxury. Their sons work from youth, gaining a natural, rapid adaptability that initially seems superior to the slow, deliberate progress of the scholar. Yet, once the scholar completes their university education, they undergo a transformation, overtaking the tradesman in every pursuit.

There is a tragic underclass: those who fail the final university examination. Despised by the nobility for lacking their discipline, and looked down upon by the commoners for lacking their innate agility, they are trapped in limbo. Denied professional careers and struggling to form families due to fears of producing "irregular" offspring, they become the seedbed of rebellion. Some progressive statesmen argue that true mercy lies in their total erasure, proposing laws to imprison or silently eliminate those who fail the Final Test.

But I fear I am drifting into the dangerous topic of Irregularities, a subject too vast and vital for this section.

Throughout the preceding sections, I have operated on a premise—perhaps one that ought to have been established as a foundational axiom from the outset—that every inhabitant of Flatland possesses a Regular Figure, meaning a shape of consistent, symmetrical construction. In our world, a Woman is not merely a line; she must be a perfectly straight line. An Artisan or Soldier must possess two sides of equal length. A Tradesman requires three equal sides. Lawyers, to which I humbly belong, must have four equal sides. Generally speaking, within every Polygon, all sides must be identical in length. Naturally, the magnitude of these sides correlates with the individual's age: a female newborn measures approximately one inch, whereas a mature woman may stretch to a full foot. Regarding males across all classes, the sum of an adult's side lengths typically totals around two feet, though this dimension is secondary to the critical issue of equality.

It requires little contemplation to realize that the entire edifice of Flatlandian social life rests upon this singular truth: that Nature decrees all figures must maintain equal sides. Were our sides unequal, our angles would inevitably follow suit. Under such conditions, the current method of identification would collapse. Instead of relying on a single visual estimation of an angle to discern a person's form, we would be compelled to physically trace every angle through the act of Feeling. Life, however, is too brief for such exhaustive verification. Consequently, the sciences and arts of Sight Recognition would vanish instantly; the skill of Feeling would perish shortly thereafter; social interaction would become fraught with danger or rendered impossible entirely; trust, foresight, and security would evaporate. No citizen could safely arrange the simplest of social engagements. Civilization would regress immediately into barbarism.

Am I rushing my readers toward these inevitable conclusions? I believe a moment's reflection, supported by a single analogy drawn from daily existence, suffices to convince anyone residing in Spaceland that our entire societal structure is predicated on Regularity, or the Equality of Angles. Consider encountering two or three Tradesmen on the street. At a glance, you identify them by their characteristic angles and slightly dimmed sides, inviting them into your home for lunch. Today, you proceed with absolute confidence because everyone knows, to within an inch or two, the exact footprint of an adult Triangle. Now, imagine that your Tradesman drags behind his regular, respectable vertex a distorted parallelogram measuring twelve or thirteen inches diagonally. What becomes of such a monstrosity wedged firmly against your doorway?

I risk insulting the intelligence of my audience by elaborating on details that should be self-evident to those accustomed to the laws of geometry. Clearly, measuring a single angle would no longer suffice under such dire circumstances; one's entire life would be consumed by the tactile surveying of acquaintances' perimeters. While the challenges of navigating a crowded street already test the wit of even a well-educated Square, imagine a scenario where no individual can calculate the Regularity of a single figure among them. Chaos and confusion would ensue. The slightest panic would result in severe injuries—or, given the presence of Women or Soldiers, potentially significant loss of life.

Thus, expediency aligns with Nature in endorsing the seal of approval upon Regularity of form, and the Law has eagerly joined this consensus. In Flatland, "Irregularity of Figure" carries the same weight, or indeed greater significance, than a combination of moral corruption and criminality does in Spaceland, and is treated accordingly.

There are, admittedly, proponents of paradoxical views who argue that no intrinsic link exists between geometric irregularity and moral failing. They claim that the "Irregular" is shunned by parents from birth, mocked by siblings, neglected by servants, and viewed with suspicion by society. Excluded from positions of responsibility, their movements are monitored by the police until adulthood, at which point they face a grim choice: destruction if their deviation exceeds a fixed margin, or imprisonment in a government office as a seventh-class clerk. Denied marriage, forced into menial labor for a pittance, living and working under constant surveillance, and even taking vacations under strict watch—it is no wonder that human nature, even in its purest forms, becomes embittered and twisted by such an environment.

While this reasoning appears plausible, it fails to persuade me, nor has it swayed the wisest statesmen, that our ancestors erred in establishing the axiom that tolerating Irregularity compromises the safety of the State. Undoubtedly, the life of an Irregular is harsh; yet the interests of the majority demand that it remain so. If individuals with triangular fronts and polygonal backs were permitted to exist and propagate an increasingly irregular lineage, what fate awaits the arts of civilized life? Must our houses, doors, and churches be reconstructed to accommodate such anomalies? Must ticket collectors measure every patron's perimeter before granting entry to theaters or lecture halls? Should an Irregular be exempted from military service? If not, how can we prevent them from bringing devastation to their comrades-in-arms? Furthermore, what irresistible temptation to fraud would such a creature face? How easily could one enter a shop with a polygonal front forward, deceiving a trusting merchant into supplying unlimited goods!

Let advocates of so-called philanthropy plead as they wish for the repeal of these penal laws regarding Irregularity. For my part, I have never encountered an Irregular who did not embody exactly what Nature seemingly intended: a hypocrite, a misanthrope, and, within the limits of their capability, a perpetrator of every conceivable mischief.

This does not imply that I endorse the extreme measures taken by certain nations, where infants deviating by half a degree from perfect angularity are summarily destroyed at birth. Many of our greatest geniuses suffered deviations as severe as, or exceeding, forty-five minutes in their earliest years; their premature deaths would have constituted an irreplaceable loss to the State. Moreover, the medical arts have achieved glorious victories through compressions, extensions, trepanning, ligatures, and various dietary regimens that have partially or wholly corrected Irregularity.

Therefore, advocating for a *Via Media*, I propose no rigid or absolute boundary. However, at the stage when the body begins to set and the Medical Board deems recovery unlikely, I suggest that the Irregular offspring be painlessly and mercifully extinguished.

If my readers have kept pace with my narrative thus far, they will find little surprise in learning that existence in Flatland is, by many measures, profoundly monotonous. I am not referring to the absence of conflict—battles, conspiracies, political upheavals, and factional strife abound, providing a dramatic texture that history books elsewhere might envy. Nor should I dismiss the unique intellectual thrill derived from the constant interplay between the mysteries of daily life and the rigid laws of geometry; this fusion offers a sharp, verifiable zest for living that your inhabitants of Spaceland struggle to imagine. However, when viewed through the lens of aesthetics and art, our reality is starkly barren. How can it be otherwise? Our entire visual universe consists merely of lines. Our landscapes, historical scenes, portraiture, flora, and still lifes offer no depth, no volume, and no variety save for subtle shifts in shade and darkness.

It was not always this way. According to tradition, during a fleeting epoch spanning perhaps half a dozen centuries, color once bathed the lives of our ancestors in a transient, radiant glory. Legend attributes the discovery of primitive pigments and the rudiments of painting to a singular visionary—a figure variously identified as a Pentagon—who began by adorning his own dwelling, then his servants, eventually turning his hand to his father, sons, and grandsons, finally applying the vibrant touch to himself. The dual benefits of utility and beauty quickly captivated the populace. Wherever Chromatistes—the name by which historians universally recognize this pioneer—displayed his variegated form, he commanded instant attention and profound respect. In a world where identity is often ambiguous, no one needed to guess whether he faced forward or backward; his neighbors could discern his orientation instantly, eliminating the need for awkward calculations or physical jostling. Furthermore, his voice was spared the exhausting labor required by us colorless Squares and Pentagons to announce our presence amidst crowds of oblivious Isosceles.

The trend ignited like wildfire. Within a week, every Square and Triangle in the region had emulated Chromatistes, leaving only a handful of conservative Pentagons behind. Within months, even the Dodecagons had succumbed to the innovation. Less than a year passed before the practice permeated the realm, sparing only the highest echelons of the nobility. Naturally, the custom radiated outward from Chromatistes' district until, within two generations, the only beings left untouched by pigment were the Women and the Priests.

Here, nature seemed to raise an impassable barrier, resisting the extension of this revolution to these two distinct classes. For the Innovators, multiplicity of sides served as the perfect justification: *"Nature intends distinction of sides to imply distinction of colors"* became the mantra that swept through towns, converting populations en masse. Yet, this logic fell flat for the Priests and the Women. The latter possess but a single side—or, strictly speaking, *no* sides at all. The former, claiming the status of true Circles rather than polygons with infinite microscopic edges, boasted of possessing no sides either, existing instead as a continuous circumference. Consequently, neither group felt bound by the axiom linking sides to hue. While the rest of society surrendered to the allure of bodily decoration, the Priests and Women remained the sole guardians of the unpainted state.

Though labeled immoral, licentious, anarchic, and unscientific, those ancient days of the Color Revolt represented the glorious infancy of art in Flatland—an age that, tragically, never matured into adulthood nor even bloomed into youth. Life itself was then a delight, for to exist was to perceive. Social gatherings were spectacles of joy; the rich tapestry of hues filling a church or theater occasionally overwhelmed our greatest scholars and performers. Most magnificent of all was the legendary splendor of a military parade. Imagine a line of twenty thousand Isosceles pivoting in unison, swapping the somber black of their bases for blazing oranges and purples along their flanks; the militia of Equilateral Triangles shimmering in red, white, and blue; the Square artillerymen swirling in mauve, ultramarine, gamboge, and burnt umber around their vermilion cannons; and the dazzling flash of five-colored and six-colored Pentagons and Hexagons darting across the field as surgeons, geometers, and aides-de-camp. These sights were so overwhelming that legend holds an illustrious Circle, blinded by the artistic majesty of his troops, discarded his marshal's baton and royal crown to take up an artist's pencil forever.

The sensory richness of that era is further evidenced by the language itself. The speech of ordinary citizens during the Color Revolt appears to have been infused with a deeper vibrancy of thought and expression. We owe our finest poetry and the rhythmic cadences remaining in our modern scientific discourse directly to that luminous, chromatic dawn.

While the intellectual arts were rapidly crumbling, the specific skill of Sight Recognition became obsolete and ceased to be practiced. Consequently, the rigorous studies of Geometry, Statics, Kinetics, and related disciplines, once revered at our universities, quickly fell into disrepute and neglect. Similarly, the lesser art of Feeling suffered the same fate within our elementary schools.

The Isosceles classes, claiming that specimens were no longer required, grew increasingly insolent. Refusing to pay the traditional tribute owed by criminal elements to support education, they leveraged their newfound immunity from this burden—which had previously served to tame their brutality and reduce their numbers—to expand their ranks and arrogance annually. Meanwhile, soldiers and artisans began to loudly proclaim, with growing justification, that there was little distinction between them and the highest Polygon classes. Having been elevated to equality with the latter, they argued they could master life's complexities—whether static or kinetic—through the simple mechanism of Colour Recognition.

Dissatisfied with the natural decline of Sight Recognition, revolutionaries boldly demanded the legal prohibition of all "monopolistic and aristocratic arts." They called for the abolition of endowments supporting Sight Recognition, Mathematics, and Feeling. Their argument escalated: since Colour, a force akin to a second nature, had erased the necessity for aristocratic distinctions, the law must follow suit. They insisted that all individuals and classes were now absolute equals, entitled to identical rights.

Finding the higher orders hesitant, the revolutionary leaders pushed further. They eventually demanded that every class, including Priests and Women, submit to Colour by undergoing painting. When objections arose regarding the lack of sides in Priests and Women, the agitators countered that both Nature and expediency dictated that the front half of every human—the portion containing the eyes and mouth—must be distinguished from the rear half.

Thus, a bill was presented to a special assembly of all Flatland states. It proposed that in every Woman, the anterior semicircle (containing the face) be painted red, and the posterior semicicle green. Priests were to receive the same treatment: red on the forward-facing arc where the eyes and mouth resided, and green on the hindward arc.

This proposal displayed considerable cunning. Though it did not originate from an Isosceles—who lacked the angularity to conceive such sophisticated statecraft—it was devised by an Irregular Circle spared from death in childhood solely to unleash desolation upon his nation and countless followers.

On one level, the plan sought to win over the female population across all classes. By granting Women the exact same color scheme as Priests, the revolutionaries ensured that, depending on the viewing angle, a Woman would appear indistinguishable from a Priest. This prospect promised respect and deference, a lure impossible for the female sex to resist en masse.

For those readers who might miss the visual trickery inherent in this legislation, consider the geometry:

Imagine a Woman adorned per the new code: her front half red, her back half green. Viewed from the side, she presents a straight line, half red, half green.

Now envision a Priest, whose mouth sits at point M, with his front semicircle (AMB) colored red and his hind semicircle green. The diameter AB separates the red from the green. If you observe this Great Man along the same straight line as his dividing diameter (AB), you perceive a straight line (CBD). One segment (CB) is red; the other (BD) is green.

Though the Priest's line may be slightly shorter and fade more sharply toward its ends than a full-sized Woman's, the identical coloring creates an immediate impression of shared class. Amidst the societal decay threatening Sight Recognition—and considering that Women would quickly learn to soften their edges to mimic Circles—it becomes evident that the Colour Bill endangered the conflation of Priests with young Women.

How enticing this confusion must have seemed to the Frail Sex! They delighted in the chaos it promised. At home, they could overhear political and ecclesiastical secrets meant for husbands and brothers, or even issue commands in the name of a priestly circle. Outdoors, the stark red-and-green combination, devoid of other hues, guaranteed endless errors among the populace, allowing Women to seize whatever deference the Circles lost. As for the scandal that might implicate the Circular Class in the frivolous conduct of Women, or the subsequent subversion of the constitution, the Female Sex showed no concern. Indeed, even within the households of the Circles, the Women championed the Universal Colour Bill.

The bill's second objective was the gradual demoralization of the Circles themselves. Despite the general intellectual rot, the Nobility retained their pristine clarity and mental strength. Familiar from childhood with the total absence of Colour, they alone preserved the Sacred Art of Sight Recognition, gaining immense advantage from this rigorous intellectual training. Until the Colour Bill's introduction, the Circles had not only maintained their standing but widened their lead by abstaining from popular fashions.

However, the crafty Irregular architect of this diabolical scheme aimed to strike simultaneously: lowering the hierarchy's status by forcing submission to the pollution of Colour, and destroying their domestic environment for Sight Recognition training. This would enfeeble their intellects by stripping away their pure, colorless homes. Once tainted by chromatic influence, every parental and childish Circle would corrupt one another. An infant Circle would struggle merely to distinguish father from mother—a problem easily corrupted by maternal deception, thereby shaking the child's faith in logic itself.

Gradually, the intellectual luster of the Priestly Order would dim, paving the way for the total destruction of the Aristocratic Legislature and the subversion of our privileged classes.

The campaign for the Universal Colour Bill raged for three years, and until its final hour, it appeared inevitable that chaos would reign supreme. A massive levy of Polygons, conscripted to fight as individual soldiers, was obliterated by a superior force of Isosceles Triangles, while the Squares and Pentagons stood aloof in neutrality. Yet, the tragedy deepened within the nobility itself. Some of the most brilliant Circles became victims of domestic fury. Blinded by political hatred, the wives of many distinguished households exhausted their husbands with pleas to abandon their resistance to the Colour Bill. Where these entreaties failed, despair drove mothers to slaughter their own children and spouses before taking their lives in the ensuing carnage. History records that during this turbulent triennium, no fewer than twenty-three Circles perished in the fires of marital discord. The peril was absolute; it seemed the Priesthood faced only two choices: total submission or mutual extermination.

Then, abruptly, the tide of history shifted due to a singular, picturesque incident—one that statesmen ought always to anticipate, for such events possess an absurdly disproportionate power to sway public sentiment. An Isosceles of the lowest caste, possessing a intellect barely exceeding four degrees, accidentally stained himself with the pigments of a tradesman whose shop he had looted. Whether he applied the paint himself or hired someone else to do so remains a matter of debate, but the result was identical: he adorned himself with the twelve distinct hues of a Dodecagon. Venturing into the Marketplace, he approached a young maiden, the orphaned daughter of a noble Polygon whom he had unsuccessfully courted in the past. Adopting a falsetto voice, he deceived her through a series of cunning stratagems—aided by a string of fortunate accidents too tedious to recount, and compounded by the near-inconceivable negligence of the bride's family—who ultimately led to the consummation of the marriage. Upon discovering the fraud, the unfortunate girl took her own life.

When news of this catastrophe rippled from state to state, the women of Flatland were thrown into violent agitation. Empathy for the tragic victim, coupled with the terrifying anticipation that similar deceptions awaited their sisters and daughters, transformed their perspective on the Colour Bill entirely. Many openly declared their conversion to the anti-colour cause; the remainder required merely a spark to follow suit. Seizing this opportune moment, the Circles hastily convened an extraordinary Assembly of the States. Alongside the customary guard of convicts, they ensured the presence of a large contingent of reactionary women. Amidst an unprecedented crowd, Pantocyclus, the Chief Circle of that era, rose to find himself hissed and booed by a hundred and twenty thousand Isosceles. However, he quelled the uproar by announcing a policy of concession. Yielding to the will of the majority, he proclaimed that the Hierarchy would accept the Colour Bill.

Instantly, the jeers turned to applause. Pantocyclus then invited Chromatistes, the leader of the sedition, to the center of the hall to receive the submission of the hierarchy on behalf of his followers. What ensued was a speech of rhetorical mastery, delivered over nearly a full day, defying any attempt at summarization. With a grave air of impartiality, Pantocyclus argued that since they were now irrevocably committing to reform, they must first survey the entire perimeter of the issue, weighing both its flaws and benefits. He gradually introduced warnings regarding the dangers posed to traders, professionals, and gentlemen, silencing the rising murmurs of the Isosceles by reminding them that despite these drawbacks, he was prepared to accept the bill if ratified by the majority. It was evident that everyone present, save the Isosceles, was swayed by his words, leaning toward neutrality or outright opposition.

Turning his gaze to the workmen, he asserted that their interests could not be ignored. If they wished to adopt the Colour Bill, they must do so with eyes wide open to the consequences. He warned that many were on the verge of ascending to the rank of Regular Triangles, while others looked forward to distinctions their children might never achieve. Such honorable ambitions would now be sacrificed. With the universal adoption of color, all social distinctions would vanish; regularity would be confused with irregularity, and progress would yield to regression. The workman would, within a few generations, be degraded to the level of the military or even the convict class. Political power would fall into the hands of the greatest numbers—the criminal classes, who were already more numerous than the workers and would soon outnumber all other castes combined once the compensative laws of nature were violated.

A subdued murmur of agreement swept through the ranks of the artisans. Alarmed, Chromatistes attempted to step forward and address them directly. But he found himself surrounded by guards and forced into silence while the Chief Circle, in a few impassioned words, made a final appeal to the women. He cried out that if the Colour Bill passed, no marriage would ever be safe, no woman's honor secure; fraud, deceit, and hypocrisy would permeate every home, and domestic bliss would share the fate of the constitution, rushing swiftly to ruin.

"Sooner than this," he thundered, "come death!"

These words served as the prearranged signal for action. The Isosceles convicts surged forward, piercing the wretched Chromatistes with their angles. Simultaneously, the regular classes parted their ranks to allow a band of women, directed by the Circles, to move backward-first, invisibly and infallibly, against the unconscious soldiers. The artisans, emulating their superiors, also opened their lines. Meanwhile, bands of convicts seized every entrance, forming an impenetrable phalanx. The conflict, or rather the massacre, lasted but a brief moment. Under the skilled command of the Circles, almost every charge by the women proved fatal, and many stung their attackers without injury, poised for a second strike. Yet, no second blow was necessary; the rabble of the Isosceles finished the job among themselves. Surprised, leaderless, attacked in front by invisible foes, and trapped behind by the convicts cutting off escape, they instantly lost all composure, raising the cry of "treachery." This sealed their doom. Suddenly, every Isosceles perceived a foe in every other. Within half an hour, not one of that vast multitude remained alive; the scattered fragments of seven hundred thousand members of the criminal class, killed by their own kind's angles, testified to the triumph of order.

The Circles did not delay in pushing their victory to its logical conclusion. They spared the working men but decimated them. The militia of the equilateral triangles was immediately mobilized, and every triangle suspected of irregularity on reasonable grounds was executed by court-martial, bypassing the formalities of precise measurement by the Social Board. Over the course of a year, the homes of the military and artisan classes were systematically inspected. During this period, every town, village, and hamlet was purged of the surplus of the lower orders—a condition exacerbated by the failure to collect the tribute of criminals for schools and universities, and by violations of other constitutional natural laws. Thus, the balance of classes was restored.

Needless to say, the use of color was thereafter abolished, and its possession strictly prohibited. Even the utterance of any word denoting color, save by the Circles or qualified scientific instructors, incurred severe penalties. Only within our University, in certain highly esoteric courses—which I myself have never been privileged to attend—is the sparing use of color still sanctioned to illustrate profound mathematical problems. But of this, I can speak only from hearsay. Elsewhere in Flatland, color is nonexistent. The art of creating it is known to only one living soul: the Chief Circle for the time being. By him, it is transmitted solely to his successor on his deathbed. There exists but one manufactory where it is produced; and lest the secret be betrayed, the workmen are annually consumed, replaced by fresh recruits. So great is the terror with which our aristocracy now regards the distant days of the agitation for the Universal Colour Bill.

It is finally time to leave behind these preliminary sketches of Flatland's geography and physics to address the true purpose of this journey: my initiation into the fourth dimension. Everything written thus far serves only as a prologue to this revelation. Consequently, I must omit countless fascinating details regarding our mode of locomotion without limbs, our methods of construction without hands, the meteorological patterns of our zones, the biology of our flora, and the mechanics of our script. These omissions are not due to oversight, but rather a respect for your limited attention span. However, before unveiling the secrets of Space, there is one final pillar of our society that demands discussion: the Controllers of Destiny, the Objects of Universal Adoration—our Circles, or Priests.

Do not mistake the title "Priest" for its common usage in your world. In Flatland, a Circle is the supreme administrator of every conceivable domain: commerce, engineering, education, statecraft, morality, and theology. They are the invisible causes behind every visible effect; they direct all action while performing none themselves. While the uneducated masses assume a Circle is a perfect geometric curve, the enlightened know the truth: a Circle is merely a polygon with an immense number of infinitesimal sides. As the count of sides grows, the shape approaches perfection. A figure with three hundred sides is nearly indistinguishable from a true circle to the human eye.

Yet, there is a profound taboo associated with this form. Among the elite, tactile recognition is forbidden. To physically trace the perimeter of a superior being is considered the height of insolence. This cultural aversion to touch allows the upper classes to maintain the mystique of their status. By avoiding contact, they preserve the illusion of their infinite smoothness. Mathematically, if a standard Circle averages three feet in circumference, a figure with three hundred sides possesses edges barely one-tenth of an inch long. Those with six or seven hundred sides have edges smaller than the head of a pin from Spaceland. Tradition dictates that the High Priest currently reigning possesses ten thousand sides—a number so vast it effectively renders him a true sphere in the eyes of the law.

Unlike the rigid caste system governing triangles and squares, where social mobility is strictly dictated by generational inheritance (gaining exactly one side per birth), the path to becoming a Circle is fluid and perilous. Nature imposes two conflicting laws on this evolution: as a lineage ascends socially, its rate of development accelerates, but its fertility plummets. In the homes of polygons nearing circularity, sons are rarely born; twins are unheard of. Conversely, the offspring of a near-perfect polygon may inherit hundreds of additional sides instantly.

To bridge the gap between a complex polygon and a Circle, art intervenes through a brutal medical practice. Physicians have discovered that the fragile edges of a high-class infant can be surgically fractured and reset with surgical precision. This procedure, known as Neo-Therapy, carries a staggering mortality rate. Roughly nine out of ten children perish during the operation. Nevertheless, driven by an obsessive desire for nobility, parents of borderline status rush their infants to the Neo-Therapeutic Gymnasium within their first month of life. The entire ordeal lasts just one year. Most often, the child returns to the family crypt, another nameless entry on the crowded cemetery stones. But occasionally, against all odds, a procession emerges carrying a survivor—not a polygon, but a Circle. This singular miracle validates the sacrifice, prompting countless other families to gamble their children's lives on the slim chance of achieving divine geometry.

The entire theological and philosophical framework of the Circles can be distilled into a single, absolute maxim: *"Attend to your Configuration."* Whether applied to politics, religion, or morality, every teaching serves one ultimate goal: the refinement of individual and collective shape. Specifically, this applies to the Circles themselves, whose perfection renders all other pursuits secondary.

It is the supreme achievement of the Circles that they have eradicated the ancient heresy suggesting that character is shaped by willpower, effort, training, or encouragement. They have convinced humanity that *Configuration makes the man*. If you are born an Isosceles with unequal sides, you are destined for error unless corrected at the Isosceles Hospital. Similarly, Triangles, Squares, and Polygons born with irregularities must seek cure at the Regular Hospitals; failure to do so results in imprisonment or execution by the Angle of the State Executioner. Under the philosophy of Pantocyclus—the great Circle who quelled the Colour Revolt—no fault, from minor misconduct to heinous crime, exists outside of physical deviation from perfect Regularity. These deviations may stem from birth, collisions in crowds, improper exercise, or sudden temperature changes causing the body to expand or contract. Consequently, Pantocyclus argued that praising or blaming conduct is illogical. Why praise a Square's integrity when one should merely admire the geometric precision of his angles? Why condemn a thiefly Isosceles when the true tragedy is the incurable inequality of his sides?

While theoretically flawless, this doctrine creates severe practical dilemmas. When an Isosceles pleads poverty of spirit due to his unevenness, the Magistrate replies that such a nuisance deserves consumption. However, in domestic disputes where death is not an option, the theory falters. I often find myself torn when a Hexagonal grandson excuses his disobedience by citing a recent chill that distorted his perimeter. Logically, I cannot accept that his shape dictates his behavior; yet, I lack the courage to reject his plea outright. She naturally demanded to know why the Circle had summoned me for such a mysterious audience and how I had managed to enter the house undetected. I provided her with an elaborate explanation—one I feared was less consistent with the truth than my readers in Spaceland might wish—but I successfully persuaded her to retreat quietly to her domestic duties, ensuring she extracted no further questions regarding the World of Three Dimensions.

With the coast clear, I summoned my grandson. To confess the truth, I felt the vivid images of my vision beginning to slip away, dissolving like the faint outline of a half-remembered dream. I longed to solidify these concepts and create my first disciple.

When the boy entered, I swiftly barred the door. Sitting beside him with our mathematical tablets—or, as you would term them, Lines—I announced that we would resume yesterday's lesson. I reviewed the fundamentals: how a point moving along a single dimension generates a line, and how a straight line traversing two dimensions creates a square.

Then, feigning a mischievous grin, I turned to him. "Now, you impertinent scamp," I said, "you attempted to convince me that a square could, through motion 'Upward, not Northward,' generate a new figure—an extra square existing within a third dimension. Repeat that, you young rascal."

At that precise moment, the Herald's cry echoed again from the street: *"O yes! O yes!"* announcing the new law. Despite his youth, my grandson possessed an intelligence far exceeding his years, having been raised in total reverence for the Circle's authority. He grasped the peril instantly. He held his breath until the final syllables of the proclamation faded into silence.

Then, bursting into tears, he cried out, "Oh, dear Grandpapa! That was merely a joke! I meant absolutely nothing by it! We didn't know about the new Law back then, and I'm certain I never mentioned the Third Dimension. And I am positive I didn't speak of 'Upward, not Northward'; that sounds like utter nonsense! How can something move upward without moving northward? Even a baby wouldn't be so absurd! How ridiculous! Ha! ha! ha!"

"Not at all ridiculous!" I snapped, my patience fraying. "Here, for instance, take this square." Seizing a movable model lying nearby, I demonstrated. "I move it, you see—not Northward, but... yes, I move it Upward—that is to say, not Northward, but somewhere else—not quite like this, but somehow..."

I trailed off, shaking the square aimlessly, unable to articulate the spatial shift required to break the plane. The gesture was futile, bordering on inane. My grandson dissolved into laughter, louder than before, declaring that I wasn't teaching him mathematics but merely playing a prank. With a triumphant unlock of the door, he fled the room.

Thus concluded my first, and ultimately disastrous, attempt to convert a pupil to the Gospel of Three Dimensions.

My initial attempt to convert my grandson using the simple maxim—"Upward, not Northward"—proved a resounding failure. This setback did not drive me into despair, nor did it silence me entirely. Instead, it taught me a crucial lesson: slogans are insufficient weapons against the tyranny of limited perception. To truly liberate the mind, I needed proof, not just poetry. I realized that I had to construct a logical edifice, a comprehensive treatise that would lay bare the architecture of the Third Dimension.

Thus, I retreated into solitude for several months, dedicating every waking hour to the composition of a work I titled *Through Flatland to Thoughtland*. However, my task was fraught with peril. To avoid immediate detection by the Axioms and the Law, I could not speak plainly of a physical dimension. Instead, I framed my argument around the concept of "Thoughtland"—a metaphysical realm where a figure could gaze downward upon Flatland, perceiving the interior of all objects simultaneously. In this theoretical space, I described figures surrounded by six squares and terminating in eight points. Yet, even within this veil of abstraction, I faced a catastrophic obstacle: the inability to draw. In Flatland, reality consists solely of lines. There are no surfaces, no volumes, only variations in length and luminosity. Without the ability to render diagrams that show depth, shading, or perspective, my written words risked remaining as opaque and confusing to my readers as the concepts I sought to explain. I feared that despite my eloquence, the average citizen would remain blind to my meaning.

As I labored over this manuscript, my personal life crumbled under the weight of suppressed revelation. Every pleasure became a torture; every sight in our two-dimensional world served as a taunting reminder of its true three-dimensional form. The sun, the houses, the streets—all were merely cross-sections of magnificent solids, stripped of their essence. I found myself unable to contain the comparison between the flat illusion and the solid truth. I neglected my clients and abandoned my trade, consumed by a feverish desire to visualize the mysteries I had witnessed but could not share. Even attempting to recall the Cube with my eyes closed proved agonizingly difficult. On one occasion, nearly a year after my journey through Spaceland, I managed to conjure the image, yet I remained haunted by the doubt that my mental reconstruction was imperfect. Was it truly a cube, or merely a distorted memory? This uncertainty deepened my melancholy and fueled a desperate resolve to act.

I felt a willingness to sacrifice my very life for the Cause, believing that conviction was worth any price. But how could I persuade the highest Circles of the land if I could not move my own grandson? Despite this fear, my spirit often rebelled against caution. I found myself slipping into dangerous speech, quoting ancient prophecies about those who claim to see the inside of things, referring casually to "the all-seeing land," and occasionally dropping the forbidden names of the "Third" and "Fourth Dimensions."

The breaking point came at a gathering of the Local Speculative Society, hosted by the Prefect himself. An absurd speaker had just delivered a paper arguing that Providence deliberately limited us to two dimensions and reserved omniscience solely for the Supreme Being. Overcome by a surge of righteous indignation, I lost control. I recounted my entire voyage—the descent into Space, the encounter with the Sphere, the tour of the Metropolitan Assembly Hall, and my return. Initially, I feigned that I was describing the fictional adventures of another, but my passion tore through the disguise. In a final, fervent appeal, I urged the assembly to shed their prejudices and embrace the reality of the Third Dimension.

The silence that followed was absolute, broken only by the sound of guards rushing forward. Before I could finish a sentence, I was seized and dragged before the Council.

The next morning, I stood in the very chamber where the Sphere had once appeared to me. For a brief moment, I believed I might be granted the same audience. The President listened to my defense, allowing me to speak without interruption. But the atmosphere was thick with foreboding. As I spoke, the President noted the presence of the elite police—Circles of high angularity, barely below 55 degrees—and ordered them replaced by a squad of lesser officers, their angles significantly smaller. I understood instantly what this substitution signified: I was to be silenced permanently. Either I would be executed, or I would be imprisoned, and the witnesses to my story would be destroyed to ensure the secret died with me. The President preferred cheap victims to expensive ones.

When my defense concluded, the President, seemingly moved by my sincerity, posed two final questions: Could I demonstrate the direction of "Upward"? Could I describe a Cube without relying on imaginary sides or angles? I answered that I could offer no further proof than the Truth itself, which would eventually prevail. The President nodded in agreement. "Then you shall be sentenced to perpetual imprisonment," he declared. "But if the Truth intends for you to emerge and spread your message, we trust the Truth will facilitate that outcome. You shall face no unnecessary hardship, and unless you attempt to escape, you will be permitted to visit your brother periodically."

Seven years have now passed. I remain a prisoner, isolated from all human contact save for my jailers and the occasional visit from my brother. He is a Square of noble character—just, sensible, and kind. Yet, our weekly meetings are the source of my deepest sorrow. He was present when the Sphere revealed itself; he saw the shifting cross-sections and heard the explanations given to the Council. Despite my relentless efforts to recount the phenomenon and argue for the existence of Solids through analogy, he remains unconvinced. Not a single week goes by without me repeating my tale, detailing the wonders of Spaceland, yet he continues to deny the existence of a Sphere.

I am utterly devoid of converts. It seems the millennium-long revelation promised to humanity has been rendered void by my confinement. Like Prometheus chained to a rock, I sought to bring fire to my fellow citizens, but instead, I lie in darkness, offering nothing but shadows. My only hope lies in these memoirs—if they can somehow reach the minds of beings in another dimension, stirring up a rebellion against the chains of limited perception. This is the comfort of my brightest hours.

Alas, the shadow returns. Heavy is the burden of knowing that I cannot be certain of the exact shape of the Cube I once saw. In my sleep-deprived nights, the cryptic command "Upward, not Northward" haunts me like a riddle carved by a demon. There are moments of profound mental weakness when the images of Cubes and Spheres dissolve into mere fantasies, when the Third Dimension feels as illusory as the Void or the Point. In these dark seasons, even the walls of my cell, the paper beneath my pen, and the solid ground of Flatland seem less than real—merely the delirious hallucinations of a broken mind, a baseless fabric woven from the threads of a nightmare.
