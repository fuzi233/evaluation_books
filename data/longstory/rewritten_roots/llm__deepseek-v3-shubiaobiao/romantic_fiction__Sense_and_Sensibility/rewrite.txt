The Dashwoods had been a family of consequence in Sussex for generations, their fortunes anchored to the sprawling grounds of Norland Park. The estate, grand and well-tended, stood as a testament to their name, earning the respect of neighbors and acquaintances alike. The last master of Norland was an elderly bachelor whose quiet existence had long been softened by the presence of his devoted sister. When she passed, leaving him bereft of companionship, he turned to his nephew, Mr. Henry Dashwood—the rightful heir—and welcomed him into the house along with his wife and daughters.

For the old gentleman, their company was a balm. Mr. and Mrs. Dashwood attended to him with genuine kindness, not merely out of duty, but from warmth of heart. Their children—three bright, lively girls—brought joy to his fading years. Henry Dashwood had a son from his first marriage, already settled with a comfortable inheritance from his late mother, and thus less dependent on Norland’s fortune. But for the three Dashwood sisters—Elinor, Marianne, and Margaret—their prospects were far less certain. Their mother brought no fortune to the marriage, and their father’s own means were modest, leaving them vulnerable should misfortune strike.

When the old man died, his will brought both relief and bitterness. He had not forgotten his nephew—the estate would indeed pass to Henry—but it was entailed so strictly upon Henry’s son, young John, that Henry himself could do little to secure his wife and daughters. A boy of four, with nothing more than childish charm and the heedless exuberance of youth, had somehow eclipsed years of devotion from Henry’s own family. Still, the old gentleman was not entirely unfeeling; he left the girls a thousand pounds each, a gesture, though small, meant to show some regard.

Henry Dashwood, though disappointed, was not a man to despair. He resolved to make the best of what he had, to economize, and to improve the estate where he could, ensuring that his beloved wife and daughters would never want. But fate was unkind. Within a year, he too was gone, leaving his widow and children with only ten thousand pounds to their name—a pittance compared to what they had expected.

On his deathbed, Henry implored his son, John, to look after his stepmother and sisters. John, though not a man of deep sentiment, was moved enough by the plea to promise assistance. He was not cruel by nature, only selfish in the way of men who have never known hardship. His wife, however, was worse—shrewd, grasping, and entirely devoid of generosity. Where John might have been swayed by kindness, she hardened his resolve, twisting his initial impulse of goodwill into something far meaner.

No sooner was the funeral over than Mrs. John Dashwood arrived at Norland, unannounced and unwelcome, to claim her new domain. The intrusion was callous, and Mrs. Dashwood—proud and sensitive—felt the insult keenly. Had it not been for her eldest daughter, Elinor, she might have left Norland at once, cutting all ties with her stepson. But Elinor, wise beyond her nineteen years, tempered her mother’s indignation with reason, urging prudence for the sake of her sisters.

Elinor was the steady hand in the family—clear-eyed, composed, and capable of restraint even in grief. Marianne, her younger sister, was her opposite in temperament: passionate, unrestrained, and given to extremes of emotion. Their mother, much like Marianne, felt everything deeply, and in their shared sorrow, they indulged in it, weaving their grief into something almost sacred. Margaret, the youngest, was still a girl of thirteen, more inclined to daydreams than sense, but good-natured nonetheless.

For now, they remained at Norland, bound by necessity and the fragile threads of family duty. But the winds of change were stirring, and the Dashwood women stood on the precipice of a life far different from the one they had known.

Mrs. John Dashwood had scarcely taken possession of Norland before she settled into her new role as its mistress with effortless ease, while her mother-in-law and sisters-in-law found themselves reduced to mere guests in what had once been their home. Still, they were met with cool politeness from Fanny and the occasional, half-hearted kindness from John—whenever his thoughts could stray beyond himself, his wife, and their son.

John, with a show of earnestness, urged his stepmother and half-sisters to remain at Norland as long as they pleased. For Mrs. Dashwood, still raw with grief, the familiarity of the place was a comfort, every room whispering memories of happier days. She was a woman of passionate temperament—boundless in joy, unrestrained in sorrow. Where another might find solace in moderation, she felt everything too keenly, her happiness untainted by doubt, her grief inconsolable.

Fanny, however, had no patience for her husband’s generosity. The idea of parting with three thousand pounds from their son’s inheritance struck her as nothing short of ruinous. "How can you justify robbing Harry of so much?" she pressed. "Those girls are only your half-sisters—scarcely family at all! No one expects affection between children of different marriages. Why should you beggar yourself and our boy for their sake?"

John hesitated. "It was my father’s last request—that I look after his widow and daughters."

Fanny scoffed. "He was hardly in his right mind at the end. Had he been thinking clearly, he would never have asked you to sacrifice half your fortune."

"He didn’t specify an amount," John countered weakly. "Only that I assist them in some way."

"Then let that 'some way' be something sensible," Fanny insisted. "Five hundred pounds would be more than generous! Once given, the money is gone forever—what if Harry has need of it later?"

John’s resolve wavered further. "True… if the sum were smaller, it might be better for all. Five hundred apiece, perhaps?"

Fanny brightened. "An excellent notion! No brother alive would do half so much, especially for half-sisters. You’ve such a noble heart!"

"I wouldn’t wish to appear stingy," John mused.

"Of course not," Fanny agreed smoothly. "But consider—they’ll each have three thousand pounds when their mother passes. With ten thousand between them, they’ll want for nothing, married or not."

John nodded, warming to the idea. "Perhaps, then, it’s better to assist their mother directly—an annuity, perhaps? A hundred a year?"

Fanny’s smile tightened. "Annuities are troublesome. They drag on endlessly, and people cling to life when there’s money to be had. My mother suffered with three such payments—endless disputes, false reports of deaths. No, better to give occasional gifts. Fifty pounds now and then would serve just as well, without the burden."

John, ever pliant, conceded. "You’re right. Occasional help will keep them comfortable without encouraging extravagance."

Fanny pressed her advantage. "I daresay your father never intended you to give them money at all. A little practical aid—finding them a house, sending game in season—that’s all he meant."

"Very likely," John agreed. "Seven thousand pounds among them is ample. What could four women need more than five hundred a year?"

"Precisely!" Fanny exclaimed. "They’ll live simply—no carriages, no grand expenses. Why, they might even spare something for *you*!"

John laughed. "You’ve convinced me. I’ll honor my promise with kindness, not coin."

Fanny’s eyes gleamed. "And remember—your mother keeps the china and plate from Stanhill. A fine legacy indeed."

"Too fine for their means," John muttered.

"Just so," Fanny said sweetly. "Your father thought only of them. You owe him no particular loyalty."

The argument settled the matter. John resolved then to do nothing more than what strict propriety demanded—and not a penny more.

Mrs. Dashwood lingered at Norland longer than she had anticipated, though not for lack of desire to leave. The memories that once brought her such sharp grief had softened with time, and as her spirits began to mend, she grew eager to depart—yet not too far. To abandon Norland entirely was unthinkable. Still, finding a suitable home nearby proved difficult; every house either strained their modest income or failed to meet Elinor’s prudent standards, leaving Mrs. Dashwood caught between her own longing for comfort and her daughter’s practicality.

She took solace in the promise her husband had extracted from his son—a vow meant to secure their future. It had comforted him in his final hours, and she, too, trusted in its sincerity. For her daughters’ sake, she was grateful, though she herself believed she could live quite well on far less than seven thousand pounds. More than that, she felt a quiet gladness for her stepson’s sake, chastising herself for ever doubting his generosity. His kindness toward her and his sisters in those early days reassured her, and she clung to the belief that his intentions were truly benevolent.

Her daughter-in-law, however, was another matter. The more Mrs. Dashwood came to know Fanny, the less she liked her. Only politeness and a mother’s reluctance to uproot her daughters had kept the peace between them—that, and one other, unexpected development: the growing affection between Elinor and Edward Ferrars, Fanny’s brother.

Edward was a quiet, amiable young man who had become a frequent visitor at Norland. Some might have encouraged the match for his prospects—he was, after all, the eldest son of a wealthy family—or discouraged it for the same reason, given that his fortune lay entirely at his mother’s discretion. But Mrs. Dashwood cared for none of that. She saw only that he was good, that he loved Elinor, and that Elinor loved him in return. To her, it was inconceivable that fortune should stand in the way of two people so well-suited in temperament.

Edward was not dashing, nor particularly polished at first glance. Shyness often masked his true nature, but when he warmed to those around him, his kindness and intelligence shone through. He had no taste for the grand ambitions his mother and sister held for him—no desire for politics or society’s glittering circles. His heart belonged to quiet domesticity, a preference that endeared him all the more to Mrs. Dashwood.

She had scarcely noticed him in those early weeks, too consumed by her own sorrow. But when Elinor remarked upon the striking contrast between Edward and his sister, Mrs. Dashwood’s interest was piqued.

"To say he is unlike Fanny is recommendation enough," she declared. "It speaks volumes in his favor. I already love him."

Elinor smiled. "I think you will like him even more once you know him better."

"Like him?" Mrs. Dashwood laughed. "My feelings go far beyond mere liking. I esteem him deeply."

"And is that not the same as love?"

"To me, they have always been inseparable."

With that, she made it her mission to draw Edward out, and soon his reserve melted under her warmth. She recognized his virtues—his steady affection, his quiet devotion to Elinor—and even his unassuming manners, so unlike her expectations for a young man, became endearing once she understood the sincerity behind them.

Before long, she was certain of his attachment to Elinor and began to imagine their future with delighted anticipation.

"In a few months," she told Marianne one day, "Elinor may very well be settled for life. We shall miss her dearly, but she will be happy."

Marianne’s face clouded. "How shall we bear it, Mama? To be without her?"

"My dear, it will hardly be a separation. We shall live but a few miles apart and see one another every day. And you will gain a brother—a true, affectionate one. I have the highest regard for Edward’s heart."

Marianne hesitated. "I confess, I find it all rather surprising. Edward is amiable, and I do love him, but… there is something lacking. His manner is so subdued. He has none of the passion, the fire, one might expect in the man who wins Elinor’s heart. And I fear he has no real taste—music scarcely moves him, and though he admires her drawings, it is with a lover’s eye, not a true connoisseur’s. To be content, I should need a man who shares my every enthusiasm—who feels the poetry I feel, who is stirred by the same music, who loses himself in the same books. Last night, when Edward read to us—oh, Mama, it was so lifeless! How could Elinor endure it with such patience? It would have broken my heart had I loved him."

Mrs. Dashwood smiled gently. "You are not yet seventeen, my dear. There is time yet for you to find a man who meets your every expectation. Why should you not be as fortunate as I was? Only in one thing, perhaps, should your destiny differ from mine."

Marianne said nothing, but her gaze was distant, already lost in dreams of a love more thrilling than the quiet devotion Edward offered—a love that burned as fiercely as her own heart.

Marianne sighed as she set down her sketchbook. “It’s such a shame Edward doesn’t care for drawing.”

Elinor glanced up from her needlework, a faint smile touching her lips. “No taste for drawing? Why would you say that? He may not sketch himself, but he takes great pleasure in the work of others. And though he hasn’t had the opportunity to refine his eye, his natural judgment is sound—he simply lacks the confidence to voice it.”

Marianne bit her tongue, unwilling to argue, though privately she doubted whether mere *appreciation* could ever match the fervent admiration true art deserved. Still, she softened at Elinor’s quiet defense of him. There was something endearing in her sister’s loyalty.

“I hope,” Elinor continued carefully, “you don’t think him lacking in taste altogether. You’re always so warm toward him—I should hate to think you merely tolerate him.”

Marianne hesitated. She would never wish to hurt Elinor, yet neither could she feign enthusiasm where she felt none. At last, she said, “I would never speak against him, Elinor. I may not know his finer inclinations as you do, but I think him everything good and sensible.”

Elinor’s smile deepened. “I doubt even his dearest friends could ask for higher praise.”

Relieved, Marianne pressed on. “His goodness is beyond question, and his understanding is remarkable—when he chooses to share it. But you’ve had the advantage of studying him far more closely than I.”

“We *have* spoken often,” Elinor admitted. “And I can assure you, his mind is keen, his love of books sincere, and his taste refined. Even his manner improves upon acquaintance. At first glance, one might overlook him—but there’s a kindness in his expression, a warmth in his eyes…” She caught herself, flushing slightly. “You’ll think me absurd.”

Marianne laughed. “Not at all! If you tell me to love him as a brother, I shall soon find him as handsome as you do.”

Elinor fell silent, uneasy at the implication. She *did* hold Edward in the highest regard—perhaps too high. But matters of the heart were never so simple. “I esteem him greatly,” she said at last. “I… *like* him very much.”

Marianne recoiled. “*Like* him? Elinor, how can you be so cold? If you feel more, why not say it?”

“Because I cannot presume his feelings,” Elinor said firmly. “And even if his regard *were* certain, there are obstacles. His mother’s expectations, his dependence—Fanny has made it plain enough that fortune and rank will weigh heavily in any match he makes.”

The truth struck Marianne like a dash of cold water. “Then you’re not engaged?”

“No. And I advise you not to leap to conclusions where none exist.”

Marianne sighed, though her optimism soon resurged. “Well, the delay has its advantages! I shan’t lose you so soon, and Edward may yet cultivate a true passion for your interests. Imagine if he took up drawing himself!”

Elinor smiled but said nothing. The truth was far less romantic. Edward’s spirits were often subdued—not from indifference, she hoped, but from the weight of his circumstances. His mother’s ambitions loomed over him, and until he was free of them, any hope of happiness remained uncertain.

Fanny, of course, wasted no time in making her disapproval known. A few sly remarks about “fortune-hunters” and “imprudent attachments” were all it took to send Mrs. Dashwood into a fury. By the time the post arrived with an offer of a cottage in Devonshire—a modest but welcoming retreat—her mind was already made up.

Norland’s shadow had grown too heavy. Distance, she decided, would be a blessing.

Elinor, ever practical, raised no objections. The rent was fair, the location sensible. And if her heart ached at the thought of leaving Edward behind—well, that was a pain she had long since learned to bear in silence.

No sooner had her letter been sent than Mrs. Dashwood allowed herself the satisfaction of informing her son-in-law and his wife that she had secured a house and would trouble them no longer than was necessary to prepare for her departure. The announcement was met with astonishment. Mrs. John Dashwood remained silent, but her husband, with polite indifference, expressed a hope that she would not settle too far from Norland.

With quiet triumph, she replied that she was bound for Devonshire.

At this, Edward turned sharply toward her, his voice laced with surprise—and something deeper. "Devonshire!" he repeated. "You are truly going so far? And to what part?"

She explained: a cottage, four miles north of Exeter. "It is modest," she admitted, "but I trust it will welcome many friends. Rooms may be added, and if those dear to me do not shrink from the journey, I shall not hesitate to receive them." Then, with deliberate warmth, she extended an invitation—first to John and Fanny, then, with greater tenderness, to Edward himself.

Her recent exchange with Fanny had only hardened her resolve to leave Norland, yet it had done nothing to dissuade her from her greater purpose: to keep Edward and Elinor together. This invitation was her defiance, a silent rebuke to Fanny’s disapproval.

John, for his part, feigned regret, lamenting that the distance would prevent him from assisting with the move. His conscience pricked him—not for any real concern for his mother’s comfort, but because this arrangement stripped him of the bare minimum effort required to fulfill his promise to his late father. The furniture was dispatched by water—linens, china, books, and Marianne’s fine pianoforte. Fanny watched it go with a pang of resentment; it galled her that a woman of such limited means should possess anything of elegance.

The cottage was taken for a year, ready furnished, and Mrs. Dashwood lost no time in preparing for departure. Elinor’s practicality guided their decisions: the carriage was sold, their servants reduced to three—two maids and a manservant, all retained from Norland. The man and one maid were sent ahead to Devon, for Mrs. Dashwood had no wish to impose as a guest at Barton Park before taking possession of her new home. Sir John’s enthusiastic description sufficed; she would judge the cottage for herself when it was hers.

Fanny’s poorly concealed relief at their impending departure only sharpened Mrs. Dashwood’s eagerness to be gone. Had there been any lingering hope that John might fulfill his promise now, as they quit his house, it was soon dashed. His endless complaints of mounting expenses and the burdens of his station made it plain: he had no intention of parting with a penny.

Within weeks of Sir John’s first letter, all was settled. The final farewells to Norland were tearful, particularly for Marianne, who wandered the grounds on their last evening, her heart aching.

"Dear, dear Norland!" she murmured, gazing at the house. "When shall I stop missing you? When shall I call another place home?" The trees stood unchanged, indifferent to her grief. "You will go on without us," she whispered, "as if we had never loved you. But who will love you as we did?"

The question hung in the air, unanswered.

The journey began in sorrow, the weight of their recent loss pressing heavily upon them, rendering the miles long and wearisome. Yet as the carriage drew nearer to their new home, a shift occurred—the landscape, fresh and unfamiliar, roused them from their melancholy. The sight of Barton Valley, with its lush pastures and thick woodlands, breathed life into their spirits.

Their first glimpse of Barton Cottage was modest but promising. A small green court lay before it, enclosed by a neat wicket gate. The house itself, though far humbler than Norland, was sturdy and well-kept, its tiled roof and plain shutters lacking the rustic charm of a true cottage but offering comfort nonetheless. Inside, the rooms were snug—two parlors flanking the entry, a narrow passage leading to the garden, and upstairs, four bed-chambers and two garrets. It was a far cry from the grandeur they had known, yet the sight of it, so clean and new, eased some of the sting of memory.

Mrs. Dashwood, ever the optimist, surveyed their new dwelling with a practical eye. "It is small, yes," she admitted, "but with a few alterations—perhaps merging these parlors, adding a drawing room—we might make it quite charming by spring." Her daughters, though quieter in their hopes, set about making the space their own. Marianne arranged her beloved pianoforte, while Elinor hung her drawings upon the walls, each gesture a quiet defiance against despair.

The next morning brought an unexpected visitor—Sir John Middleton, their landlord and a relation by marriage. He was a man of hearty demeanor, his good humor evident in his booming voice and warm smile. No sooner had he welcomed them than he insisted they dine at Barton Park daily until they were settled, his generosity extending to baskets of fresh produce and game delivered to their door.

His wife, Lady Middleton, arrived the following day—a vision of elegance, tall and striking, though her reserved manner offered little beyond polite pleasantries. Fortunately, their young son provided ample distraction, the ladies marveling over his shyness and debating whom he favored more, his father or mother. By the time Sir John departed, he had secured their promise to dine at the Park the next day, ensuring further acquaintance—and, no doubt, further discussion of the children.

For now, however, the Dashwoods were content. The cottage was not Norland, but it was theirs, and in that, there was hope.

Barton Park stood a mere half-mile from the cottage, nestled just beyond the curve of a hill that shielded it from the Dashwoods’ view. The house was grand and finely kept, a testament to the Middletons’ dual pursuits of hospitality and refinement—Sir John favoring the former, his wife the latter. Their home was seldom without guests, for they entertained more lavishly than any family in the neighborhood. Though dissimilar in demeanor—Sir John boisterous, Lady Middleton reserved—they shared a mutual deficiency in intellectual pursuits, leaving their interests narrowly confined. He was a sportsman; she, a doting mother. While she indulged her children year-round, Sir John’s outdoor diversions were seasonal, yet both found ample occupation in the constant whirl of social engagements that masked their lack of deeper occupations.

Lady Middleton prided herself on the elegance of her household, her greatest satisfaction lying in the admiration it provoked. Sir John, however, reveled in society with genuine warmth, particularly when surrounded by lively young people—the more, the merrier. To the youth of the neighborhood, he was a benefactor: summer picnics, winter balls, and an open-handed hospitality that left no one wanting for amusement. The arrival of the Dashwoods at Barton Cottage had delighted him immensely, for they were young, pretty, and refreshingly unpretentious—qualities he admired above all in ladies.

Upon their arrival at Barton Park, Sir John greeted them with hearty sincerity, ushering them into the drawing room with apologies for the scarcity of guests. Aside from himself, there was but one other gentleman in attendance—Colonel Brandon, a friend of steady temperament, neither young nor particularly spirited. Sir John lamented the absence of more lively company, though he took solace in the unexpected arrival of Lady Middleton’s mother, Mrs. Jennings, whose boisterous good humor might, he hoped, compensate for the small gathering.

Mrs. Jennings was a jovial, talkative woman, her laughter as abundant as her figure. She delighted in teasing the young ladies about love and matrimony, her remarks so pointed that Marianne grew indignant on her sister’s behalf, casting anxious glances at Elinor to gauge her composure. Colonel Brandon, in contrast, remained quiet and observant, his demeanor as restrained as Sir John’s was effusive. Though neither young nor handsome, there was a quiet dignity in his bearing that commanded respect—especially from Marianne, who found his subdued appreciation of her music far more gratifying than the others’ exaggerated praise.

Lady Middleton, though generally indifferent, brightened only when her children burst into the room, their clamorous presence eclipsing all conversation. Later, when Marianne’s musical talents were revealed, she was pressed to perform. The piano, long untouched since Lady Middleton’s marriage, was opened with ceremony, and Marianne’s voice and playing drew enthusiastic applause—from all but Colonel Brandon, who listened with quiet attentiveness. His tempered admiration, so unlike the others’ raptures, struck Marianne as the mark of true discernment. She conceded that a man of his years might no longer feel the fervor of youth, yet his quiet appreciation spoke of a depth the others lacked.

In that assembly, where Lady Middleton’s coldness repelled and Sir John’s exuberance overwhelmed, even the colonel’s reserve seemed a virtue. And Marianne, though she might have wished for more spirited company, could not help but respect the one man who listened without pretense.

Mrs. Jennings, a widow of considerable fortune, had but one occupation left in life: matchmaking. Having successfully married off her own two daughters, she now turned her attentions to the romantic prospects of every young person in her acquaintance. Few escaped her notice, and fewer still avoided her meddling. She possessed an uncanny ability to detect affection where others saw none, and she delighted in teasing young ladies with hints of their secret admirers.

Upon arriving at Barton, she was quick to observe Colonel Brandon’s attentions to Marianne Dashwood. The colonel, usually so reserved, listened with uncommon interest whenever Marianne played the pianoforte or sang—a fact Mrs. Jennings noted with satisfaction. By the time the Middletons dined at the cottage, she had settled the matter in her own mind: Colonel Brandon was unmistakably in love.

The match pleased her exceedingly. He was wealthy; Marianne was beautiful. What could be more natural? Mrs. Jennings had long wished to see the colonel married, and she took great pleasure in imagining the union. Her amusement, however, extended beyond mere goodwill—she could not resist teasing them both mercilessly.

Marianne, for her part, was baffled by the suggestion. "Colonel Brandon in love with me?" she exclaimed to her mother. "The notion is absurd! He is old enough to be my father!"

Mrs. Dashwood, ever patient, attempted reason. "He is scarcely five years older than myself, my dear. You judge him too harshly."

"But he complains of rheumatism!" Marianne protested. "He speaks of flannel waistcoats—such things belong to infirmity, not romance!"

Elinor, overhearing, could not suppress a smile. "If a slight chill condemns a man to eternal bachelorhood, then half the gentlemen in England must remain unwed. You would have him bedridden before his time."

Marianne sighed. "I do not deny his merits, but must love always be so practical? Must a woman of seven-and-twenty resign herself to a marriage of convenience simply because her suitor is respectable and kind?"

"Not all matches are born of wildfire passion," Elinor replied gently. "Affection may grow quietly, without fanfare."

Marianne was unconvinced. "Then let others settle for such tepid bonds. I shall not."

The conversation soon turned to another matter—Edward Ferrars’ prolonged absence. Marianne fretted over his delay, certain some illness detained him.

"Perhaps he never intended to come," Mrs. Dashwood mused. "He seemed reluctant when first invited."

"But Elinor expects him," Marianne insisted. "Why else would she be so composed? She does not pine as I would—as I do."

Elinor, returning, offered no explanation. Her calm demeanor revealed nothing, leaving Marianne to wonder at the mysteries of her sister’s heart.

The Dashwoods had settled into Barton Cottage with a quiet contentment that slowly mended the ache left by Norland. The house, the garden, the rolling hills beyond—all had become familiar, a gentle rhythm of life that soothed rather than stirred. Though Sir John Middleton marveled at their industriousness—visiting daily in those first weeks, astonished to find them always occupied—they had little company beyond the occasional call from Barton Park. Mrs. Dashwood, ever independent, resisted his eager encouragements to mingle more with the neighborhood, preferring the solace of their own society.

Yet curiosity could not be entirely subdued. On one of their earliest walks, the sisters had discovered an elegant old house nestled in the valley of Allenham, its dignified air reminding them faintly of Norland. They longed to know its inhabitants, but learned with disappointment that its owner, an elderly lady of excellent reputation, was too infirm to receive visitors.

The countryside, however, offered endless diversion. The high downs beckoned with their sweeping views, and it was on one such morning—after days of rain had confined them indoors—that Marianne and Margaret resolved to venture out. The sky was still uncertain, patches of blue wrestling with looming clouds, but Marianne, ever ardent, declared it would clear entirely. Elinor and their mother, less persuaded, remained behind with their books, while the younger sisters set off, laughing as the wind rushed against them, invigorating in its boldness.

For a blissful half-hour, they reveled in the open air, until without warning the clouds darkened and the rain descended in a sudden torrent. Forced to retreat, they turned back—only for Marianne to lose her footing on the slick hillside, twisting her ankle painfully. As she struggled to rise, a figure emerged from the mist: a gentleman with a gun, two pointers circling at his heels. Without hesitation, he set his weapon aside and strode forward.

Marianne, pride warring with necessity, would have refused his aid, but he acted before she could protest, lifting her effortlessly and carrying her down the slope. Through the garden gate (left open by a breathless Margaret) and into the cottage parlor he went, depositing her gently in a chair before stepping back with an apology for the intrusion.

It was then that the family truly saw him—his striking features, the easy grace of his manner, the warmth in his voice as he introduced himself. *Willoughby.* Of Allenham. And before he left—despite the rain still lashing outside—he secured permission to call again the next day.

The moment the door closed behind him, the room seemed to brighten with the afterglow of his presence. Even Elinor, not prone to fancy, could not deny his charm. But Marianne—flushed, disheveled, her ankle throbbing—was utterly spellbound. Here was a man who matched, even surpassed, the heroes of her most cherished novels. His gallantry, his looks, the very way he had swept her up without ceremony—all of it thrilled her. Pain was forgotten; her thoughts raced ahead, weaving possibilities.

When Sir John Middleton arrived later, full of jovial concern over Marianne’s mishap, their eager questions about Willoughby sent him into delighted laughter.

"Willoughby! So he’s in the country at last? Capital! I’ll have him to dine with us this week—you’ll like him immensely."

"And what sort of man is he?" pressed Marianne, impatient with vague praise.

"The best sort! Excellent shot, fearless rider—danced till dawn last Christmas without a pause!"

Marianne’s eyes lit at this. "With spirit? With grace?"

Sir John chuckled. "I see where this is headed. Poor Brandon won’t stand a chance."

Marianne bristled. "I detest such vulgar phrases. If admiration is a crime, then I am guilty—but not of scheming."

Mrs. Dashwood, amused but firm, interjected. "My daughters do not *catch* men, Sir John. But if Mr. Willoughby is half as agreeable as you say, his acquaintance will be welcome."

And with that, the matter was settled—though Marianne’s heart had already decided far more than she would admit.

The following morning brought Willoughby—whom Margaret had rather poetically dubbed *Marianne’s preserver*—to the cottage, eager to inquire after her health in person. Mrs. Dashwood received him with warmth that went beyond mere courtesy, her kindness fueled by Sir John’s glowing accounts and her own gratitude. Every exchange during his visit only deepened his admiration for the Dashwoods—their intelligence, refinement, mutual affection, and the quiet harmony of their home.

As for their beauty, he had needed no second glance to confirm it. Elinor possessed a delicate complexion, fine features, and a graceful figure. But Marianne—Marianne was something more striking still. Though not as classically proportioned as her sister, her height lent her an arresting presence, and her face was so radiant that even the most overused compliments could not exaggerate it. Her sun-kissed skin glowed with vitality, her features were exquisite, and her smile held an irresistible charm. But it was her eyes—dark, lively, brimming with spirit—that truly captivated. At first, she had been restrained by the memory of his gallantry, but as her shyness melted away, replaced by the ease of his manner and the discovery of shared passions, her gaze lit with such approval that Willoughby found himself speaking to her more than anyone else.

Mention any subject dear to her, and she would seize upon it with unguarded enthusiasm. Dancing, music, literature—their tastes aligned so perfectly it was as though they had been shaped by the same hand. When they spoke of books, her fervor was so infectious that any man of five-and-twenty would have been compelled to share her ardor, even if he had never before appreciated the authors she adored. They idolized the same passages, and where they differed, her spirited arguments and luminous eyes swiftly dissolved any resistance. Willoughby yielded to her opinions with delight, absorbing her zeal as if it were his own. By the time he departed, their conversation flowed with the ease of old friends.

"Well, Marianne," Elinor remarked once he was gone, "for a single morning, you have managed rather thoroughly. You’ve extracted Mr. Willoughby’s views on nearly every matter of consequence—Cowper, Scott, Pope—all properly appraised. But at this pace, how shall you sustain the acquaintance? Another meeting or two, and you’ll have exhausted every topic. What then? Will you quiz him on landscape aesthetics or the merits of second marriages?"

"Elinor!" Marianne cried, half-laughing, half-indignant. "Is this fair? Must my thoughts be deemed so meager? But I see your meaning—I have been too unguarded, too happy, too sincere. I have violated every rule of decorum by speaking openly when I ought to have been dull and reserved. Had I confined myself to the weather or the state of the roads, and uttered but one remark in ten minutes, you would have spared me this reproach."

"My dear," interposed Mrs. Dashwood gently, "do not take offense. Elinor spoke in jest. Were she truly inclined to stifle your joy in our new friend’s company, I should be the first to scold her."

Marianne’s irritation dissolved at once.

As for Willoughby, his delight in their acquaintance was evident in his eagerness to nurture it. He called daily, at first under the pretext of inquiring after Marianne’s health—but her swift recovery soon rendered even that excuse unnecessary. Confinement had never been so pleasant, for his visits transformed it into a time of laughter and shared passions. He was everything to engage Marianne’s heart: quick-witted, spirited, and open in his affections, with a charm that went beyond mere appearance. His enthusiasm mirrored her own, stoked by her fervor, and it was this quality above all that endeared him to her.

Together, they read, debated, and sang—his musical talent a welcome contrast to Edward’s unfortunate deficiency in that regard. To Mrs. Dashwood, he was faultless; to Elinor, nearly so, save for one tendency he shared with Marianne: an unchecked habit of speaking his mind, heedless of propriety or company. His readiness to judge, his disregard for polished manners when his heart was engaged, and his casual dismissal of societal niceties struck Elinor as imprudent—though neither Willoughby nor Marianne saw any fault in it.

Marianne now realized how foolish she had been at sixteen to despair of ever meeting a man who matched her ideals. Willoughby was the embodiment of all she had imagined in her most despondent and hopeful moments alike. His attentions left no doubt of his sincerity, and his abilities matched his devotion. Even Mrs. Dashwood, who had not once considered the prospect of his wealth in connection to marriage, found herself within a week quietly hopeful—and inwardly pleased at the thought of gaining two such admirable sons-in-law as Edward and Willoughby.

Colonel Brandon’s regard for Marianne, once noted only by his friends, now became apparent to Elinor precisely when the others ceased to remark upon it. Their attention had shifted to Willoughby, the more fortunate rival, and the teasing Brandon had once endured faded as his feelings grew earnest enough to warrant genuine sympathy. Elinor could not help but acknowledge that Mrs. Jennings’ earlier conjectures had been correct—his admiration was now unmistakably fixed upon Marianne.

She observed it with unease. What chance had a reserved man of five-and-thirty against one of five-and-twenty, brimming with vivacity? She could not wish Brandon success, yet she could not wish him indifference either. Despite his gravity, she found him interesting—his manners gentle, his reserve seeming born of melancholy rather than coldness. Sir John’s hints of past sorrows only deepened her respect and pity. That Willoughby and Marianne dismissed him so readily, prejudiced by his lack of youth and levity, struck her as unjust.

"Brandon," Willoughby remarked one day, "is precisely the sort of man whom everyone praises and no one remembers—delighted to see him, but never troubled to speak to."

"Exactly my thought!" Marianne agreed.

"Do not pride yourselves on it," Elinor said sharply. "It does you no credit. He is esteemed by all at Barton Park, and I, for one, always make a point of engaging him in conversation."

"That *you* patronize him speaks in his favor," Willoughby countered, "but the approval of the others is condemnation enough. Who would value the regard of Lady Middleton and Mrs. Jennings if they could command anyone else’s notice?"

"Perhaps the disdain of people like you and Marianne balances their esteem. If their praise is meaningless, your censure may be praise in itself—for they are no more blind than you are prejudiced."

"You defend your *protégé* with spirit!"

"My *protégé*, as you call him, is a sensible man—and sense will always recommend someone to me. Yes, Marianne, even in a man past thirty. He has traveled, he has read, he thinks deeply. He has answered my inquiries with intelligence and kindness."

"That is to say," Marianne scoffed, "he informed you that the East Indies are hot and plagued by mosquitoes."

"Had I asked, no doubt he would have. But those were not my questions."

"Perhaps," Willoughby added, "his observations extend to nabobs, gold mohrs, and palanquins."

"I suspect his knowledge surpasses your own. But why must you dislike him?"

"I don’t dislike him. I consider him perfectly respectable—a man with universal approval and no real notice, ample fortune and nothing to spend it on, endless time and no way to fill it, and two new coats a year."

"And," Marianne added, "no genius, no taste, no spirit. His mind lacks brilliance, his heart lacks fire, and his voice lacks expression."

"You condemn him so thoroughly," Elinor said dryly, "that my praise must seem tepid by comparison. I can only call him sensible, well-bred, well-informed, and amiable at heart."

"Miss Dashwood," Willoughby protested, "you are unfair. You argue so reasonably that you nearly disarm me—but I shall not relent. I have three irrefutable reasons to dislike Colonel Brandon: he predicted rain when I wished for sun, criticized the hang of my curricle, and refused to buy my brown mare. Still, if it pleases you, I’ll concede that in all other respects, his character is unimpeachable. But that admission pains me—so you cannot deny me the satisfaction of disliking him as much as ever."

Little had Mrs. Dashwood or her daughters imagined, upon their arrival in Devonshire, that their days would soon be so occupied with engagements, invitations, and visitors that leisure for quiet reflection would become a rarity. Yet such was the case. Once Marianne had recovered her health, Sir John’s plans for amusement—both at home and abroad—were swiftly put into motion. Private balls were held at the park, and fair weather permitted frequent excursions upon the water.

At every gathering, Willoughby was present, his ease and familiarity deepening his acquaintance with the Dashwoods. His admiration for Marianne was evident, and she, in turn, made no secret of her affection. Elinor observed their attachment without surprise, though she occasionally wished for a little more restraint. Marianne, however, saw no shame in open devotion where no disgrace could follow. To her, concealing true feeling was not only unnecessary but a surrender to dull convention. Willoughby shared her sentiments, and their behavior reflected their shared conviction.

When together, they were insensible to all else. Every word he spoke was clever; every action, right. If the evening’s entertainment was cards, he contrived to ensure she held the winning hand. If dancing was the amusement, they partnered often, and when forced to separate, they stood near, speaking little to others. Such conduct invited laughter, but neither ridicule nor reproach could sway them. Mrs. Dashwood, warm-hearted and indulgent, saw only the natural ardor of youthful love and made no effort to check it.

For Marianne, this was a season of joy. Her heart, once tethered to Norland, now found solace in Willoughby’s presence, making her new home dearer than she had imagined possible. Elinor, however, was less content. No companion could replace those she had left behind, nor ease the ache of remembrance. Lady Middleton’s conversation was dull, and though Mrs. Jennings was ceaselessly talkative, her discourse offered little substance—only repeated tales of her late husband’s final hours.

Colonel Brandon alone stood apart from the rest. In him, Elinor found intelligence, kindness, and quiet understanding. Willoughby, devoted entirely to Marianne, could offer no such companionship. Brandon, however, bore the marks of past sorrow, and Elinor suspected he too had known the sting of disappointed affection. One evening, as they sat together while others danced, he spoke with unexpected candor.

"Your sister, I understand, does not approve of second attachments," he remarked.

"No," Elinor replied. "Her views are decidedly romantic."

"Or rather," he said with a faint smile, "she believes them impossible."

"Perhaps. Though how she reconciles this with our father’s own second marriage, I cannot say. Time may temper her opinions with experience."

He sighed. "There is something touching in the fervor of youthful conviction—yet its loss is inevitable."

Elinor shook her head. "Marianne’s ideals, however noble, often disregard propriety. A broader understanding of the world would serve her well."

Brandon hesitated before speaking again. "Does she make no exception for those whose first love was ill-fated? Must they resign themselves to solitude?"

"I cannot say. She has never allowed for such a case."

His expression darkened, as if recalling some private grief. "A change of heart is not always voluntary. Sometimes circumstance forces it upon us." He broke off abruptly, leaving Elinor to wonder at the pain behind his words.

Had Marianne been in her place, she might have woven a tale of tragic romance from his silence. Elinor, however, let the matter rest—though the weight of unspoken sorrow lingered between them.

The following morning, as Elinor and Marianne strolled together along the garden path, Marianne shared news that—despite Elinor’s prior awareness of her sister’s impulsiveness—struck her as astonishing in its recklessness. With sparkling eyes, Marianne revealed that Willoughby had gifted her a horse, a fine creature bred on his own estate in Somersetshire, perfectly suited for a lady’s riding.

Elinor’s practical mind raced through the complications: their mother had no intention of keeping horses; accepting such a gift would necessitate hiring a groom, acquiring a second horse for the servant, and even building a stable. Yet Marianne had accepted without hesitation, already lost in fantasies of galloping across the open downs.

*"You shall ride with me, Elinor!"* she exclaimed. *"Imagine the wind in our hair, the freedom of it!"*

Elinor hesitated before voicing her concerns—first the impracticality, then the greater impropriety of accepting so lavish a gift from a man they had known so briefly. Marianne’s reply was swift, fervent.

*"You mistake me, Elinor. Time does not dictate intimacy—disposition does. I know Willoughby’s heart as well as I know yours!"*

Seeing the stubborn set of Marianne’s jaw, Elinor shifted tactics, appealing instead to their mother’s comfort. The thought of burdening her softened Marianne’s resolve, and at last she agreed to refuse the gift.

That very afternoon, Willoughby called at the cottage. Elinor, unnoticed, overheard the exchange—Marianne’s quiet regret, his earnest reassurances.

*"The horse is still yours,"* he murmured. *"When you leave Barton for a home of your own, Queen Mab shall be waiting."*

The familiarity in his tone, the use of her Christian name—it confirmed what Elinor had suspected. They were engaged.

Later, young Margaret, ever observant, added further proof.

*"Elinor!"* she whispered gleefully. *"Marianne will marry Mr. Willoughby—I saw him cut a lock of her hair!"*

Elinor could not dismiss the claim, not when it aligned with all she had witnessed. But Margaret’s candor soon caused mischief. At a gathering, pressed to reveal the name of Elinor’s supposed admirer, Margaret teased, *"I must not tell!"*—igniting laughter at Elinor’s expense.

Marianne, flushing with indignation, scolded her. *"You have no right to spread conjecture!"*

*"It’s not conjecture,"* Margaret retorted. *"You told me yourself!"*

The room erupted in amusement, leaving Elinor mortified. Only Lady Middleton’s abrupt remark about the rain spared her further embarrassment.

The evening concluded with plans for an excursion the next day—a visit to a grand estate belonging to Colonel Brandon’s relation. Though the promise of boating and picnics delighted the party, Elinor, noting the incessant rains and her mother’s fragile health, persuaded Mrs. Dashwood to remain home.

As the others buzzed with anticipation, Elinor’s thoughts lingered on Marianne and Willoughby—their whispered words, the lock of hair, the horse that would one day carry her sister into a new life. The pieces of the puzzle fit together, yet she wondered why they had chosen secrecy.

And what, in the end, would come of it?

The planned outing to Whitwell unfolded in a manner entirely unforeseen by Elinor. She had braced herself for rain, exhaustion, and perhaps even a touch of alarm—but fate had a far crueler twist in store, for the excursion did not take place at all.

By ten o'clock, the party had gathered at the park, where breakfast was to be served. The morning, though damp from the night’s rain, showed promise; clouds drifted apart, and the sun broke through intermittently. Spirits were high, each member determined to embrace the day’s pleasures, no matter the inconveniences they might endure.

As they breakfasted, the post arrived. Among the letters was one addressed to Colonel Brandon. He took it, glanced at the direction, paled, and abruptly left the room.

"What ails Brandon?" Sir John demanded.

No one had an answer.

"I hope it is not ill news," murmured Lady Middleton. "It must be something grave indeed to draw him from my table so suddenly."

He returned within minutes.

"Nothing amiss, I trust, Colonel?" Mrs. Jennings inquired the moment he reappeared.

"Not at all, madam. Only a matter of business."

"From Avignon? Not word of your sister’s worsening condition, I pray?"

"No, it comes from London—merely a trifling affair."

"Then why did the handwriting unsettle you so? Come now, Colonel, no evasions! Out with it!"

"My dear Mrs. Jennings," Lady Middleton interjected, "mind your words."

"Perhaps it announces your cousin Fanny’s marriage?" Mrs. Jennings persisted, heedless of her daughter’s reproof.

"Indeed not."

"Ah, then I know who it is from. And I hope the lady is well."

"To whom do you refer?" he asked, his color rising slightly.

"Oh, you know very well whom I mean!"

With visible effort, Colonel Brandon addressed Lady Middleton. "I must beg your pardon, but this letter demands my immediate presence in town."

"To London!" Mrs. Jennings exclaimed. "What business could possibly call you there at this season?"

"I deeply regret disrupting our plans," he continued, "but my absence may also hinder your admission to Whitwell."

This declaration struck the party like a thunderbolt.

"Could you not write a note to the housekeeper?" Marianne pressed eagerly.

He shook his head.

"We must go," Sir John declared. "We are too close to abandon it now. At least delay your journey until tomorrow, Brandon."

"Were it so simple, I would. But even a single day’s delay is impossible."

"If you disclosed the nature of this business," Mrs. Jennings suggested, "we might judge whether postponement is feasible."

"You would scarcely lose six hours," Willoughby added, "if you waited until our return."

"I cannot spare even one."

Elinor overheard Willoughby murmur to Marianne, "Some men cannot abide amusement. Brandon is of that breed. Fear of a chill, no doubt—this is merely an excuse. I’d wager fifty guineas he penned that letter himself."

"Undoubtedly," Marianne agreed.

Sir John sighed. "You’ve always been immovable once resolved, Brandon. But I urge you to reconsider. Think of the Miss Careys, come all the way from Newton, the Dashwood sisters walking from the cottage, and Willoughby rousing himself two hours early—all for Whitwell!"

Colonel Brandon repeated his regrets but stood firm.

"Very well. When do you return?"

"We shall expect you at Barton," Lady Middleton said, "as soon as your affairs permit. The Whitwell party must wait for you."

"You are too kind. But my return is uncertain—I dare not make promises."

"Nonsense! If you are not back by week’s end, I shall fetch you myself," Sir John declared.

"Do, Sir John," Mrs. Jennings urged. "Then you might uncover this mysterious business!"

"I’ve no wish to pry. No doubt it’s something he’d rather keep hidden."

The Colonel’s horses were announced.

"You do not mean to ride all the way to town?"

"Only to Honiton. From there, I take the post."

"Then I can only wish you a safe journey—though I still say you ought to stay."

"That is beyond my power."

With that, he took his leave of the company.

Turning to Elinor, he asked, "Is there no hope of seeing you and your sisters in town this winter?"

"I fear not."

"Then I must bid you farewell for longer than I would wish."

To Marianne, he offered only a silent bow.

As he departed, Mrs. Jennings called after him, "Colonel! At least tell us what you’re about!"

But he was gone.

The moment politeness no longer restrained them, the group erupted in disappointment.

"I know his business," Mrs. Jennings announced triumphantly.

"Oh?" came the eager chorus.

"It concerns Miss Williams, mark my words."

"Who is Miss Williams?" Marianne asked.

"You’ve never heard of her? She is the Colonel’s relation—a very near one. I shan’t say more, lest I shock the young ladies." Then, sotto voce to Elinor, "His natural daughter."

"Indeed!"

"Oh yes, the very image of him. And likely to inherit his fortune."

Sir John, upon rejoining them, lamented the thwarted plans—though he swiftly proposed an alternative: a drive through the countryside to salvage the day.

Carriages were summoned. Willoughby’s was first, and Marianne’s radiant expression as she stepped into it spoke volumes. They set off at speed and were lost to view until their return, long after the others had come back. Both seemed exhilarated, though they spoke only in vague terms of keeping to the lanes while the rest traversed the downs.

The evening was to feature dancing, and high spirits prevailed. More of the Carey family joined for dinner, swelling their numbers to nearly twenty—a sight that filled Sir John with satisfaction.

Willoughby sat, as usual, between Elinor and Marianne. Mrs. Jennings, beside Elinor, soon leaned past her to address Marianne in a carrying whisper:

"I’ve discovered your secret, despite your cleverness. I know where you spent the morning."

Marianne flushed. "Where, pray?"

Willoughby laughed. "You know we were out in my curricle."

"Yes, yes, Mr. Impudence, but I mean *where*? I hope you admired your future home, Miss Marianne—though it sorely needs refurbishing. I remarked as much six years ago."

Marianne turned away, mortified. Mrs. Jennings roared with laughter, confessing to Elinor that she had interrogated Willoughby’s groom and learned of their visit to Allenham—where they had wandered the gardens and explored the house at length.

Elinor was aghast. How could Marianne enter the home of Mrs. Smith, a stranger, under such circumstances? At the first opportunity, she confronted her sister—only to find every detail true.

Marianne was indignant at her doubt. "Why should we *not* go? You’ve often wished to see it yourself!"

"Not without proper company, and certainly not while Mrs. Smith resides there."

"Willoughby has every right to show it. And it was a perfectly delightful morning."

"Pleasure does not always signify propriety."

"Nonsense! Had I acted wrongly, I would have felt it. Instead, I recall only happiness."

"But Marianne—must Mrs. Jennings’s vulgar remarks not give you pause?"

"If her impertinence defines impropriety, we are all guilty daily. I care nothing for her opinion. There was no harm in walking those grounds. They will one day be Willoughby’s, and—"

"Even were they yours, it would not justify the visit."

At this, Marianne colored—yet the hint seemed to please her. After a pensive silence, she admitted,

"Perhaps it was unwise, Elinor. But the house is enchanting! There is a corner sitting room upstairs—just the right size, with windows on two sides. One overlooks the bowling-green and a hanging wood, the other the village church and the hills beyond. The furnishings are wretched, but with two hundred pounds, Willoughby says it could be the loveliest summer-room in England."

Had the others not interrupted, Marianne would have described every chamber with equal fervor.

Colonel Brandon’s abrupt departure from the park, coupled with his steadfast refusal to disclose the reason for it, became the chief occupation of Mrs. Jennings’ thoughts for days. She was a woman who thrived on speculation, particularly when it concerned the affairs of those in her circle, and the mystery of the colonel’s sudden exit proved irresistible.

"Something dreadful must have happened—I’m certain of it," she mused aloud, her imagination running wild with possibilities. "Did you see how grave he looked? Poor man! Perhaps his finances are in disarray—Delaford was never a grand estate, and his brother left things in such disorder. Or worse, what if it’s Miss Williams? He did seem uneasy when I mentioned her. She’s always been delicate—perhaps she’s taken ill in London. Or maybe his sister in Avignon has summoned him? The haste does suggest a family emergency. Oh, I do hope he finds relief from whatever troubles him—and a good wife to ease his burdens!"

Her theories shifted with each passing hour, each new conjecture as plausible as the last. Elinor, though sincerely concerned for Colonel Brandon, could not match Mrs. Jennings’ fervor for speculation. Her own wonder was reserved for the marked silence maintained by Marianne and Willoughby—a silence so profound it bordered on secrecy.

Their attachment was obvious in every glance, every word exchanged between them. Why, then, did they not openly declare their engagement? Elinor could understand caution if their circumstances were uncertain—Willoughby, though independent, was not wealthy, and marriage might not yet be within their means. But the secrecy itself was baffling, so contrary to Marianne’s passionate nature and Willoughby’s usual openness that it planted a seed of doubt in Elinor’s mind. Could they truly be engaged at all?

Yet Willoughby’s actions spoke of devotion. To Marianne, he was all tenderness; to the rest of the family, he behaved as a fond brother might. Barton Cottage had become a second home to him, with more of his hours spent there than at Allenham. If no prior engagement called him away, his mornings often ended at the cottage, where he would remain at Marianne’s side, his loyal pointer curled at her feet.

One evening, nearly a week after Colonel Brandon’s departure, Willoughby’s affection for the place seemed especially pronounced. When Mrs. Dashwood mentioned her tentative plans to make improvements to the cottage in the spring, he reacted with surprising vehemence.

"Improve this cottage? No—I beg you, not a single stone!" he exclaimed. "It is perfection as it is. If my feelings matter at all, you will leave it untouched."

Elinor laughed. "You need not fear—my mother’s funds would never stretch so far."

"Thank heaven for that," he said fervently. "May she always lack the means if this is how she would use them."

Mrs. Dashwood smiled. "You need not worry, Willoughby. I would never disregard your attachment—or anyone’s—for the sake of mere improvements. But surely you cannot think this place without fault?"

"To me, it is flawless," he insisted. "Indeed, I believe it the only design in which true happiness can reside. Were I wealthy enough, I would tear down Combe Magna and rebuild it exactly like this cottage."

"Dark stairs and all?" Elinor teased.

"Every last detail," he declared. "Only then could I hope to be as happy there as I have been here."

Elinor arched a brow. "I suspect that in time, even grander rooms and a wider staircase will not diminish your affection for your own home."

"Circumstances may endear it to me," he conceded, "but this place will always hold a claim on my heart that no other can rival."

His gaze lingered on Marianne, whose answering look spoke volumes. Mrs. Dashwood observed them with quiet satisfaction.

"Do you know," Willoughby continued, "this time last year, whenever I rode past this cottage, I wished it inhabited. I admired its situation and lamented its emptiness. And then—what joy to return and learn it was taken! I felt an immediate thrill, as if I somehow knew the happiness it would bring me. Was it not so, Marianne?"

His voice dropped to a murmur as he addressed her, then rose again as he turned back to Mrs. Dashwood. "And yet you would alter it? Strip it of its charm? This dear parlor, where we first met, where so many happy hours have been spent—you would reduce it to a mere passage? No room, however grand, could offer the comfort we’ve found here."

Mrs. Dashwood assured him once more that no changes would be made.

"You are too good," he said warmly. "Your promise eases my mind. Extend it further—promise that not only the cottage will remain unchanged, but that you and your family will too. Let me always find in you the kindness that has made your home so dear to me."

She agreed readily, and the rest of the evening passed in contentment, Willoughby’s every look and gesture radiating happiness.

As he took his leave, Mrs. Dashwood asked, "Shall we see you tomorrow for dinner? We must call on Lady Middleton in the morning, but we shall expect you by four."

He smiled. "Without fail."

Mrs. Dashwood and her two eldest daughters paid their visit to Lady Middleton the following day, but Marianne excused herself, claiming some small task demanded her attention. Her mother, suspecting a private understanding with Willoughby—that he would call upon her in their absence—was content to leave her behind.

Upon their return, the sight of Willoughby’s carriage at the cottage seemed to confirm Mrs. Dashwood’s expectations. Yet the scene that greeted them inside was far from what she had imagined.

No sooner had they crossed the threshold than Marianne rushed past them, her face hidden behind a handkerchief, her distress unmistakable. Without a word, she fled upstairs, leaving her mother and sisters frozen in surprise.

Willoughby stood by the hearth, his back turned, but at their entrance, he faced them. His expression mirrored Marianne’s anguish.

"What has happened?" Mrs. Dashwood cried. "Is she ill?"

"I hope not," he replied, forcing a smile that did not reach his eyes. "It is I who should claim illness—I am suffering a bitter disappointment."

"Disappointment?"

"Yes. I must break my engagement with you. Mrs. Smith, in the manner of the wealthy toward their dependents, has dispatched me to London on urgent business. My orders came this morning, and I have already bid farewell to Allenham. Now, I come to take my leave of you."

"To London? So soon?"

"Within the hour."

"How unfortunate! But surely Mrs. Smith’s affairs will not keep you long?"

He colored. "You are kind, but I do not expect to return to Devonshire soon. My visits to her are seldom repeated within the year."

"And is hers the only house open to you? Must you wait for an invitation here?"

His flush deepened, his gaze fixed on the floor. "You are too generous."

Mrs. Dashwood exchanged a glance with Elinor, both bewildered. Silence settled heavily until Willoughby broke it with a strained smile.

"It is folly to prolong this. I can no longer bear the pain of remaining where I am no longer at liberty to stay."

With that, he bid them a hurried farewell and was gone. They watched his carriage vanish down the lane, the air thick with unspoken questions.

Mrs. Dashwood, too overcome for speech, withdrew to her room, leaving Elinor to grapple with her own unease. Willoughby’s abrupt departure, his forced cheer, his refusal of her mother’s invitation—none of it aligned with the man they knew. Had his affection for Marianne wavered? Or had some quarrel driven them apart? Marianne’s grief suggested a rupture, yet Elinor could scarcely believe it.

When Mrs. Dashwood returned, her eyes bore traces of tears, but her manner was composed.

"Our dear Willoughby is miles away by now," she said, resuming her needlework. "And with what sorrow he must travel!"

"It is all so sudden," Elinor murmured. "Last night he was all warmth and affection. Now he leaves without promise of return. There must be more to it than he confessed."

"His reluctance was not from want of inclination, Elinor—but from impossibility. I have turned it over in my mind, and the answer is clear. Mrs. Smith suspects his attachment to Marianne and disapproves. She has contrived to separate them, sending him away under false pretenses. He, dependent on her favor, dares not defy her—and so he must go."

Elinor frowned. "It is plausible, yet why keep such a secret from us?"

"Secrecy? Have they not been open in their affection? His looks, his attentions—have they not spoken plainly enough?"

"But words, Mother. Why no declaration?"

Mrs. Dashwood sighed. "Must you doubt where there is no cause? Willoughby is honorable. If circumstances force discretion, must we fault him? Consider his position—bound to please Mrs. Smith, yet devoted to Marianne. His distress today speaks volumes."

Elinor fell silent, weighing her mother’s words. Perhaps she was too quick to distrust. Yet the absence of a clear understanding gnawed at her.

Their discussion was cut short by Margaret’s arrival, leaving Elinor to reflect. She wished to believe the best—for Marianne’s sake, and for Willoughby’s.

Marianne did not appear until dinner, her eyes swollen, her manner brittle. She ate nothing, spoke not at all, and when her mother pressed her hand in silent sympathy, she dissolved into tears and fled once more.

The evening passed in heavy quiet. Any mention of Willoughby undid her entirely. Her family treaded carefully, yet every topic seemed to circle back to him, deepening her sorrow.

Love, it seemed, was as much a trial as a joy—and Willoughby’s absence had left a wound too fresh to bear.

Marianne’s first night without Willoughby was sleepless, as she had known it would be. To have rested would have felt like betrayal. Dawn found her hollow-eyed, her pillow damp with tears, her heart too full for speech or food. Every glance from her mother and sisters pricked her conscience, yet she turned away their comfort, wrapped in the bitter luxury of her sorrow.

She wandered alone through Allenham that morning, retracing steps once walked beside him, each memory a fresh wound. The hours bled into evening, and still she sought the echoes of his presence—playing their songs, tracing the notes he had penned for her, until the weight of grief left her numb. Books they had shared only deepened the ache, the words now hollow without his voice to give them life.

Days softened the edge of her anguish, but solitude remained her companion. Her walks were solitary, her musings silent, though now and then a sudden surge of feeling would break over her like a storm.

No letter came from Willoughby. Marianne, strangely, seemed to expect none. Mrs. Dashwood invented excuses—Sir John’s interference with the post, the need for secrecy—but Elinor’s unease grew.

"Ask her plainly," Elinor urged her mother one evening. "Surely she would not deny you the truth."

Mrs. Dashwood recoiled. "To force her confidence would be cruel. If there is an understanding, she will tell me in time."

Elinor bit back her frustration. Romance, it seemed, outweighed sense.

Willoughby’s name was carefully avoided until Mrs. Dashwood, lifting a volume of Shakespeare, sighed, "We never finished *Hamlet* with our dear Willoughby. We must save it for his return—though it may be months yet."

"Months?" Marianne’s sharp reply was almost a rebuke. "No—not even weeks." The certainty in her voice sent a flicker of hope through Elinor.

A week after his departure, Elinor coaxed Marianne into joining their walk—a small victory. They strolled in silence through the valley, the autumn air crisp underfoot, until the road widened into open country. There, a horseman appeared in the distance.

Marianne’s breath caught. "It is him!" She quickened her steps, radiant.

Elinor squinted. "No, Marianne—that is not Willoughby."

But Marianne was already rushing forward, only to halt abruptly, her joy crumbling. The rider was Edward Ferrars.

Disappointment warred with courtesy. Marianne forced a smile, though her eyes still shimmered with unshed tears. Edward, dismounting, greeted them all with polite reserve—his manner so unlike a lover’s that even Marianne noticed. Where was the warmth? The stolen glances?

As they walked back to Barton, conversation limped along. Edward spoke of Devonshire, of Plymouth, but his words were stiff, his smiles fleeting. Marianne, bewildered, found herself comparing him to Willoughby—his ease, his passion—and the contrast stung.

When he remarked on the muddiness of winter lanes, Marianne nearly scoffed. "How can you think of dirt with such beauty before you?"

Elinor steered the talk to safer ground—the Middletons, the cottage—but Edward’s replies were perfunctory. His coldness gnawed at her, yet she masked her hurt. If he chose indifference, she would match it with grace.

For now, at least, the charade would hold.

Mrs. Dashwood’s surprise at his arrival lasted only a moment—for his coming to Barton seemed, to her, the most natural thing in the world. Her warmth and kindness far outshone any lingering astonishment, and he was met with such genuine affection that any trace of hesitation in him could not withstand it. Indeed, before he had even crossed the threshold, his reserve had begun to soften, and under Mrs. Dashwood’s gracious welcome, it melted entirely.

It was impossible, after all, for a man to love either of her daughters without extending some measure of that devotion to their mother as well. Elinor, observing him closely, was pleased to see him gradually ease into his usual manner, his attentions reawakening, his concern for their well-being becoming evident once more. Yet despite this, his spirits were undeniably low. He complimented their home, admired the view, and was as courteous as ever—but the shadow over him was unmistakable.

The entire family noticed, and Mrs. Dashwood, quick to lay the blame upon his mother’s supposed miserliness, took her seat at dinner with fresh indignation toward all parents who put their own interests before their children’s happiness.

Once they had gathered by the fire after the meal, she turned to him with directness.

“And what plans has Mrs. Ferrars laid out for you now, Edward? Are you still to be thrust into greatness against your will?”

“No,” he replied. “I believe my mother is at last convinced that I possess neither the talent nor the inclination for public life.”

“But how, then, will you achieve the renown your family expects? For famous you must be, and yet you shun expense, avoid strangers, have no profession, and lack the boldness to impose yourself upon the world. It strikes me as a difficult path.”

“I do not intend to attempt it. I have no desire for distinction—and every reason to hope I shall never attain it. Heaven be thanked, I cannot be forced into eloquence or genius.”

“Ambition was never your failing,” Mrs. Dashwood remarked. “Your wishes have always been modest.”

“As modest as any man’s, I suppose. Like everyone else, I wish to be happy—but, like everyone else, it must be on my own terms. Greatness would not secure it for me.”

“How strange that it should!” Marianne exclaimed. “What has wealth or grandeur to do with happiness?”

“Grandeur, very little,” Elinor said mildly. “But wealth has rather more to do with it.”

Marianne turned to her sister with playful reproach. “Elinor, for shame! Money can only bring happiness where nothing else does. Beyond a comfortable living, it offers no true satisfaction—not in matters of the heart.”

Elinor smiled. “Perhaps we are not so far apart as you think. *Your* idea of comfort and *mine* may well be the same. Without it, as the world stands, we must both agree that life would lack ease. You only phrase it more nobly. Come, then—what is this competence you speak of?”

“Perhaps eighteen hundred or two thousand a year. No more than that.”

Elinor laughed. “Two thousand! And *one* is my idea of wealth! I see how this will end.”

“And yet two thousand a year is hardly excessive,” Marianne countered. “A family cannot live decently on less. I assure you, I am not unreasonable—a proper household staff, a carriage, perhaps two, and hunters cannot be kept on a smaller income.”

Elinor’s smile deepened at her sister’s precise accounting of their imagined expenses at Combe Magna.

“Hunters?” Edward repeated. “But why must you have hunters? Not everyone hunts.”

Marianne flushed slightly. “Most people do.”

Margaret, struck by a sudden thought, interjected, “I wish someone would give each of us a vast fortune!”

“Oh, if only they would!” Marianne cried, her eyes alight at the fantasy.

“I suppose we are all in agreement on that,” Elinor said dryly, “despite our differing views on the necessity of wealth.”

Margaret sighed dreamily. “How happy I should be! I wonder what I would do with it all!”

Marianne’s expression suggested she had no such uncertainty.

Mrs. Dashwood chuckled. “I confess, I should be at a loss myself—if my children were all rich without my help.”

“You might begin by improving this house,” Elinor suggested. “That would solve the dilemma quickly enough.”

Edward smiled faintly. “What a flurry of orders would go up to London from this household! The booksellers, the music shops—they would rejoice. You, Miss Dashwood, would demand every new print of merit, and Marianne—” He glanced at her. “Well, I know her soul. There would not be enough music in all of London to satisfy her. And books! Thomson, Cowper, Scott—she would buy them all, again and again, if only to keep them from lesser hands. She would own every volume that teaches one to admire a gnarled old tree. Am I wrong, Marianne? Forgive me if I presume too much—but I wished to prove I have not forgotten our old debates.”

Marianne’s face softened. “I love to remember the past, Edward—whether sweet or sorrowful. You could never offend me by speaking of it. And you are quite right in guessing how I would spend my fortune—or at least part of it. My ready money would certainly go to music and books.”

“And the rest?” he teased. “Annuities for the authors, perhaps?”

“No,” she said with a quiet smile. “I should find other uses for it.”

“Ah, then perhaps a reward for whoever best defends your favorite belief—that one can only truly love once in a lifetime? Unless your opinion has changed?”

“It has not. At my age, opinions are rather fixed. I doubt anything could alter them now.”

“You see, Edward,” Elinor said lightly, “Marianne is as steadfast as ever. She has not changed at all.”

“Only grown a little more serious,” he observed.

Marianne met his gaze. “You need not reproach me, Edward. You are hardly in spirits yourself.”

He sighed. “Gaiety was never part of my nature.”

“Nor hers,” Elinor added. “I would not call Marianne a *lively* girl—she is earnest, eager in all she does, often speaks with passion—but she is not often truly merry.”

Edward nodded slowly. “I suppose you are right—though I always thought of her as lively.”

“I have often made such mistakes,” Elinor mused. “Misjudging someone as more cheerful or solemn than they truly are. Sometimes we are swayed by what people say of themselves—or more often, by what others say of them—without pausing to form our own judgment.”

Marianne raised a brow. “But I thought it was proper to be guided by others’ opinions. I believed our own judgments were meant only to serve those of our neighbors. Surely that has always been your doctrine?”

Elinor laughed. “No, indeed! I have never argued for the surrender of one’s own understanding. I only wished to guide *behavior*—not thought. You must not mistake me. I may have urged you to show more courtesy in company, but when have I ever advised you to adopt their sentiments on matters of importance?”

Edward turned to Elinor with amusement. “You have not succeeded in converting your sister to your philosophy of general politeness, then? No progress?”

“Quite the opposite,” Elinor said, casting a meaningful glance at Marianne.

“My judgment is entirely on your side,” Edward admitted. “But my actions, I fear, align more with Marianne’s. I never mean to give offense, yet my wretched shyness often makes me seem indifferent when I am merely awkward. Sometimes I think nature meant me for humbler company—I am so ill at ease among those of refinement.”

“Marianne cannot claim shyness as an excuse,” Elinor remarked.

“No,” Edward agreed. “She knows her own worth too well for false modesty.”

“Shyness,” he added after a moment, “is only the mark of feeling oneself inferior in some way. If I could believe my manners truly graceful, I should not be shy.”

“But you would still be reserved,” Marianne said pointedly. “And that is worse.”

Edward stiffened. “Reserved? Am I reserved, Marianne?”

“Yes. Very.”

He colored slightly. “I do not take your meaning. How so? What would you have me say?”

Elinor, startled by his reaction, sought to lighten the mood. “Surely you know my sister well enough to understand her? She calls everyone reserved who does not speak as quickly or admire things as rapturously as she does.”

Edward fell silent. The weight of his earlier melancholy settled over him once more, and for a long while, he said nothing at all.

Elinor observed with quiet concern the melancholy that clung to Edward like a shadow. His visit, though a pleasure, was marred by his own restless discontent. She longed for some sign—some assurance—that his heart still held its old tenderness for her, but his manner was a shifting thing: one moment warm, the next withdrawn, leaving her to doubt where she had once been certain.

The next morning, he joined her and Marianne in the breakfast room before the others had stirred. Marianne, ever eager to encourage their intimacy, soon excused herself, only to pause halfway up the stairs when the parlor door opened abruptly. Edward emerged, his hat in hand.

“I’m off to the village to see to my horses,” he said briskly. “Since breakfast isn’t ready, I’ll return shortly.”

When he did, it was with fresh praise for the countryside. His walk had granted him new views of the valley, and the village, perched higher than the cottage, had offered a sweeping prospect that pleased him greatly. Marianne, ever ardent in matters of scenery, seized the topic at once, but Edward held up a hand.

“You mustn’t press me too far, Marianne. I’ve no eye for the picturesque, and if we descend into particulars, my ignorance will offend you. I’ll call a steep hill *steep*, not *bold*; a rough surface *strange*, not *rugged*. Distant objects? Merely *out of sight*, not artfully obscured by haze. You must accept my admiration as it is—plain and honest. I find this a fine country: steep hills, sturdy woods, snug valleys with rich meadows and tidy farmhouses. It suits me precisely—beauty and usefulness combined. And since *you* admire it, I’ve no doubt it’s picturesque as well—all rocks and moss and wild brush—but those charms are lost on me.”

Marianne sighed. “I fear you’re right—but must you boast of it?”

Elinor smiled. “I suspect Edward, to avoid one affectation, dons another. Disgusted by false raptures over nature, he feigns indifference rather than admit his own discernment. His fastidiousness breeds its own pretension.”

“True enough,” Marianne conceded. “Admiration of landscapes has become mere jargon. Everyone mimics the first man who defined the picturesque, parroting phrases until they’re drained of meaning. I’ve often held my tongue for lack of fresh words.”

Edward nodded. “I believe *you* feel the delight you claim. But your sister must permit me to feel no more than I profess. I like a fine view—but not by picturesque rules. Twisted, blighted trees? No. Tall, straight ones? Admirable. Ruined cottages? No charm in decay. Give me a snug farmhouse over a watchtower, happy villagers over romantic bandits.”

Marianne stared at him, torn between astonishment and pity. Elinor only laughed, and the topic drifted away, leaving Marianne pensive—until a glint at Edward’s hand caught her eye. As he accepted tea, a ring on his finger flashed, its center holding a lock of hair.

“You’ve never worn a ring before, Edward!” she exclaimed. “Is that Fanny’s hair? I recall her promising you some—though I thought it darker.”

The words escaped her thoughtlessly. At once, she regretted them, for Edward’s face flushed deeply. He glanced at Elinor, then muttered, “Yes—my sister’s. The setting alters the shade.”

Elinor met his gaze, her own cheeks warming. She knew, as surely as Marianne, whose hair it was—only Marianne assumed it a gift, while Elinor suspected some secretive snip. Yet she took no offense, deftly steering the conversation elsewhere, though inwardly resolved to study that ring at every chance.

Edward’s discomfort lingered, his distraction deepening as the morning wore on. Marianne chastised herself bitterly—though she might have spared herself had she known how little her sister minded.

By midday, Sir John and Mrs. Jennings arrived, eager to inspect the cottage’s new guest. With Mrs. Jennings’s help, Sir John soon deduced the initial of Edward’s surname, arming himself with future jests at Elinor’s expense—though propriety held his tongue for now. Still, his pointed looks betrayed his suspicions, sharpened by Margaret’s hints.

True to form, Sir John pressed them to dine at the Park the next day and take tea that very evening. “You *must*,” he insisted. “Tonight we’ll be quiet—tomorrow, a merry party!”

Mrs. Jennings added, “And who knows? Perhaps a dance!”

“A dance?” Marianne cried. “Impossible! Who would dance?”

“Who? You, the Careys, the Whitakers! Did you think no one could dance now that a certain someone is gone?”

Sir John sighed dramatically. “I wish Willoughby were back among us.”

At this, Marianne colored—and Edward, seated beside Elinor, murmured, “Who is Willoughby?”

Elinor answered briefly. Marianne’s face spoke volumes. By the time their visitors left, Edward had pieced together what had once puzzled him. He approached Marianne, lowering his voice.

“I’ve been guessing. Shall I tell you?”

“What do you mean?”

“May I?”

“If you like.”

“Then I guess… Mr. Willoughby hunts.”

Marianne started, then—despite herself—smiled at his quiet mischief. After a pause, she said, “Oh, Edward! How can you? But in time, I hope… I’m sure you’ll like him.”

“I don’t doubt it,” he replied, surprised by her fervor. Had he not thought it a jest—a mere nod to some trifling flirtation—he’d never have dared mention it.

Edward spent a week at the cottage, and though Mrs. Dashwood urged him to stay longer, he seemed determined to deny himself the pleasure, insisting on departure just as his happiness among them had reached its peak. His spirits, though still uneven, had brightened considerably in those final days. He spoke fondly of the house and its surroundings, sighed at the thought of leaving, and claimed to have no pressing engagements—yet still, he would not be swayed.

The week had flown by, and he marveled at how quickly time had slipped away. He confessed as much repeatedly, along with other remarks that betrayed the true state of his heart—words that contradicted his stubborn resolve to go. Norland held no joy for him; London was detestable. Yet to one or the other, he must return. Their kindness meant everything to him, and his greatest contentment lay in their company. Still, after only seven days, he would leave, despite their wishes and his own, bound by some invisible constraint.

Elinor, though disappointed and at times vexed by his uneven attentions, was inclined to make allowances. She blamed his mother—a convenient explanation, given how little she truly knew of Mrs. Ferrars. His melancholy, his reticence, his wavering resolve—all seemed the natural consequences of a man fettered by dependence, forced to navigate his mother’s expectations. His brief visit, his steadfast insistence on leaving, all pointed to the same struggle: duty warring against desire, a son bound to a parent’s will.

She often wondered when this oppressive dynamic might end—when Mrs. Ferrars might relent, freeing Edward to seek his own happiness. But such musings were futile. Instead, she found solace in the certainty of his affection, in the warmth of his glances, his words, and most of all, in the token he wore upon his finger.

On his final morning, as they sat at breakfast, Mrs. Dashwood ventured a gentle reproach.

"You would be far happier, Edward, if you had some profession—something to occupy your time and give purpose to your days. True, it might mean less leisure for your friends, but at least you would know where to direct yourself once you left us."

He sighed. "I have thought the same myself, often enough. It is a lasting regret that I have no true occupation, no means of independence. But between my own fastidiousness and the opinions of my family, I remain idle and adrift. We could never agree on a suitable path. I favored the church—I still do—but it was deemed too humble. The army was suggested, but far too bold for my tastes. The law was considered respectable, but I had no inclination for it. As for the navy, I was already too old when the notion arose. And so, with no pressing need to choose, idleness was declared the most advantageous course. At eighteen, one does not easily resist such persuasion."

Mrs. Dashwood smiled. "Then I suppose your sons shall be raised to every profession under the sun, lest they suffer the same discontent."

His expression darkened. "They will be raised to be everything I am not—in temperament, in circumstance, in every possible way."

She shook her head. "You speak out of melancholy, Edward. All men must endure partings and uncertainties, no matter their station. What you lack is patience—or if you prefer a sweeter name, call it hope. Your mother will, in time, grant you the independence you long for. Surely she cannot wish to see your youth wasted in unhappiness."

But Edward only replied, "I doubt even months will bring me any relief."

His despondency cast a shadow over their farewell, leaving Elinor with a quiet ache that lingered long after he had gone. Yet where Marianne might have indulged in solitude and sorrow, Elinor turned to her drawing-table, filling her days with purposeful work. She spoke of Edward without avoidance, engaged in the household’s affairs, and if her heart remained heavy, at least she spared her family the added worry of her grief.

Marianne, observing her sister’s composure, could not comprehend it. To her, such restraint was neither admirable nor natural—how could affection exist without passion? Yet she loved Elinor too well to disdain her for it, even as she privately questioned the depth of her feelings.

Elinor, in truth, thought of Edward often. In quiet moments, when conversation lapsed and solitude settled around her, her mind wandered freely—turning over his words, his looks, his contradictions with tenderness, doubt, and sometimes censure.

One such morning, as she sat sketching, the sound of the garden gate drew her attention. A party approached—Sir John and Lady Middleton, Mrs. Jennings, and two strangers, a man and woman she did not know.

Sir John, ever boisterous, bypassed the formality of knocking and called up to her through the window.

"Well! We’ve brought you visitors! What do you think of them?"

She hushed him, laughing. "They’ll hear you!"

"Let them! It’s only the Palmers. Charlotte’s very pretty—look, there she is!"

Elinor, who would meet her properly in moments, declined the invitation to stare.

Mrs. Jennings, never one for patience, added her voice to the clamor. "My dear! How do you do? And your sisters—where are they? All alone? Well, we’ve brought company for you!"

Introductions were swiftly made. Mrs. Palmer, all smiles and exuberance, was a striking contrast to the reserved Lady Middleton. She admired the cottage with unchecked enthusiasm, exclaiming over every detail, while her husband, Mr. Palmer, scarcely looked up from his newspaper.

"Mr. Palmer doesn’t hear me," she laughed, as if this were the most amusing thing in the world.

Mrs. Dashwood, unaccustomed to finding humor in rudeness, could only stare in bemusement.

The conversation turned to the Palmers’ unexpected arrival, with Mrs. Jennings delighting in the retelling.

"They came all the way round by London!" she confided to Elinor in a stage whisper. "Not the wisest choice, given her condition—but she insisted on coming!"

Mrs. Palmer waved off the concern. "It does me no harm!"

Lady Middleton, visibly uncomfortable, seized the first opportunity to redirect the conversation. "Mr. Palmer, is there any news in the paper?"

"None," he replied, without glancing up.

When Marianne returned from her walk, she was greeted with teasing inquiries about Allenham—a jest that sent Mrs. Palmer into fresh laughter. Mr. Palmer, at least, looked up long enough to stare at Marianne before retreating behind his newspaper once more.

As the visit concluded, Sir John pressed them all to dine at the park the following day. Mrs. Dashwood declined, but the girls, despite their reluctance, found themselves overruled by the collective insistence of the party.

"Why must we go?" Marianne grumbled once they had gone. "Is our rent so cheap that we must pay in forced socializing?"

Elinor shook her head. "They mean to be kind. If their parties have grown dull, the fault is not in their intentions—but perhaps in our own changing expectations."

The drawing-room at Barton Park was alive with chatter when the Dashwood sisters arrived. No sooner had they stepped through one door than Mrs. Palmer burst in through the other, her cheerful demeanor unchanged from their last meeting. She clasped their hands warmly, her laughter bright as ever.

*"I’m so delighted you’ve come!"* she exclaimed, settling herself between Elinor and Marianne. *"I was dreadfully afraid the weather might keep you away, and that would have been too terrible—especially since we leave again tomorrow! The Westons expect us, you see, and it was all so sudden—Mr. Palmer only thought to mention it as the carriage rolled up!"* She sighed dramatically. *"He’s such a riddle, never telling me a thing! But you must promise we’ll meet again in London—I’ve already imagined you in the loveliest house, right beside ours in Hanover Square!"*

Elinor and Marianne exchanged glances before politely declining.

*"Not go to town?"* Mrs. Palmer cried, laughing. *"Oh, but you must! I’d be the most devoted chaperone—at least until my confinement!"*

Just then, Mr. Palmer entered, offering the ladies a curt nod before scowling at the rain-streaked windows. *"This weather is insufferable,"* he grumbled. *"It turns every acquaintance into a bore. If Sir John had any sense, he’d have a billiard room—anything to make his guests less tedious."*

Marianne stiffened, but Mrs. Palmer only giggled. *"Isn’t he absurd? He contradicts everyone!"*

As the party gathered for dinner, Sir John lamented their small numbers. *"Why didn’t you invite the Gilberts?"* he asked his wife.

*"Because they dined with us just yesterday,"* she replied patiently.

Mrs. Jennings chimed in, *"Oh, we needn’t stand on ceremony!"*

*"Then you’d be shockingly ill-bred,"* Mr. Palmer retorted.

His wife laughed as if it were the finest joke. *"You see? There’s no pleasing him!"*

Elinor observed the pair with quiet amusement. Mr. Palmer’s sourness seemed more performance than genuine malice—perhaps a reaction to being wedded to such a frivolous woman. His rudeness, she suspected, was less about true displeasure and more about asserting superiority.

Later, Mrs. Palmer cornered Elinor with another plea. *"You simply must visit us at Christmas! Cleveland is delightful, and the Westons will be there—oh, say you’ll come!"*

*"Mr. Palmer is quite eager for it,"* she added with a wink, though her husband’s answering sneer suggested otherwise.

Elinor demurred again, steering the conversation toward safer waters. *"Do you know Mr. Willoughby well?"* she ventured.

*"Oh, yes!"* Mrs. Palmer exclaimed. *"Not that we’ve spoken much, but he’s often in town. Everyone adores him—your sister is *very* fortunate!"*

Elinor’s pulse quickened, though she kept her tone light. *"You seem quite certain of their attachment."*

Elinor’s composure faltered. *"Colonel Brandon?"*

*"Yes! He mentioned it just before we left London. Though,"* Mrs. Palmer added conspiratorially, *"I’ve always thought *he* fancied your sister. But no matter—Mr. Palmer is far more to my taste!"*

As the evening wore on, Elinor turned the revelations over in her mind. Mrs. Palmer’s gossip was hardly reliable, yet even the faintest praise of Willoughby soothed her fears—for Marianne’s sake, if not her own.

When parting, Mrs. Palmer clasped her hands again. *"Now we’re friends, you *must* visit Cleveland! Promise me!"*

Elinor smiled, offering no promises—but as the carriage rolled away, she wondered what truths, or fictions, the next encounter might bring.

The Palmers had scarcely departed from Cleveland when the quiet rhythm of life at Barton was once again interrupted—this time by the eager hospitality of Sir John Middleton and Mrs. Jennings. No sooner had Elinor ceased puzzling over Charlotte’s inexplicable cheerfulness or Mr. Palmer’s baffling blend of intelligence and simplicity than a fresh diversion arrived.

During an excursion to Exeter, Sir John and Mrs. Jennings had encountered two young ladies—Miss Anne and Lucy Steele—whom Mrs. Jennings proudly claimed as distant relations. This tenuous connection was enough for Sir John to insist they visit Barton Park as soon as their engagements permitted. The Miss Steeles, flattered by the invitation, promptly abandoned their plans in Exeter, leaving Lady Middleton in a state of uneasy anticipation. How could she possibly entertain two strangers of whose manners and breeding she knew nothing?

When the young ladies arrived, however, their polished appearance and effusive admiration for the house and its furnishings quickly soothed Lady Middleton’s fears. They were fashionably dressed, impeccably civil, and—most importantly—utterly enchanted by her children. Within an hour, the young Middletons were clinging to them like long-lost playmates, and Lady Middleton, rarely one for excessive praise, declared them *most* agreeable.

Sir John, triumphant, hastened to the cottage to persuade the Dashwood sisters to meet these delightful additions to their society.

"You *must* come," he insisted, his enthusiasm boundless. "Lucy is all prettiness and charm, and the children adore her already! They’ve brought toys enough to fill a carriage—how can you resist?"

Elinor and Marianne, however, were not so easily swayed. They promised to call at the Park soon but declined an immediate visit, leaving Sir John to marvel at their indifference.

When they did finally meet the Miss Steeles, Elinor was struck by the contrast between the sisters. Anne, the elder, was plain and dull-witted, her conversation marked by an artless lack of refinement. Lucy, however, possessed a lively prettiness—sharp-eyed and quick, if not truly elegant. Both were exceedingly attentive to Lady Middleton, lavishing praise on her children and her taste in decor. The little Middletons, in turn, treated them as indulgent playthings, tugging at their sashes, rummaging through their workbags, and shrieking with delight at every stolen trinket.

Lady Middleton watched these antics with maternal pride, interpreting even the most unruly behavior as proof of her children’s high spirits. When young William pinched Lucy’s fingers, she cooed, "How playful he is!" And when little Annamaria—usually so "gentle and quiet"—let out an ear-piercing wail after being scratched by a stray pin, the Miss Steeles rushed to comfort her with lavender water and sugar plums.

Elinor observed it all with quiet detachment, while Marianne barely concealed her disdain.

"Poor little creatures," sighed Anne Steele once the children had been carried off. "What a frightful accident!"

Marianne arched a brow. "It would only have been frightful had the circumstances been different. As it stands, the fuss seems rather excessive."

Lucy, eager to smooth things over, turned the conversation. "Lady Middleton is *such* a sweet woman, is she not?"

Marianne said nothing. Elinor, ever the diplomat, murmured polite agreement, though her praise was measured compared to Lucy’s effusions.

"And Sir John!" Anne added. "What a charming man!"

Again, Elinor offered a restrained acknowledgment of his good humor—far less glowing than the Steele sisters’ declarations.

"I declare, I *adore* children," Lucy exclaimed. "These little ones are the finest I’ve ever seen!"

Elinor smiled faintly. "I gathered as much from this morning’s display."

"You think them too indulged, perhaps?" Lucy ventured.

"At Barton Park," Elinor replied smoothly, "I find I do not mind spirited children at all."

The conversation might have lapsed then, had Anne not abruptly inquired, "How do you like Devonshire, Miss Dashwood? You must miss Sussex dreadfully!"

Elinor, startled by the familiarity, admitted she did.

"Norland is terribly fine, is it not?" Anne pressed.

Lucy, sensing her sister’s lack of tact, interjected, "Sir John speaks of it often."

Elinor nodded. "It is beautiful—though no one can appreciate it quite as we do."

Anne, undeterred, plunged ahead. "And were there many beaux in Sussex? I daresay Devonshire has fewer. Though Exeter has its share—Mr. Rose, for instance, is quite the smart young man, though he’s a fright in the mornings. Your brother must have been a beau, Miss Dashwood, before he married?"

Elinor’s reply was measured. "I cannot say. If he was, marriage has not altered him."

Anne laughed. "Oh, married men don’t count as beaux—they’ve other things to occupy them!"

Lucy, mortified, attempted to steer the conversation toward admiration of the house, but the damage was done. Elinor had seen enough. The elder Miss Steele’s vulgarity was insufferable, and Lucy’s calculated charm did little to disguise her lack of true refinement.

Yet the Miss Steeles were determined to cultivate the acquaintance. They showered the Dashwoods with compliments, declaring them the most accomplished and beautiful women they had ever met—flattery that Sir John heartily endorsed. Elinor knew escape was impossible; Sir John would ensure their paths crossed daily.

And so, within mere days, Anne Steele was already hinting at secrets.

"I hear your sister has caught a very smart beau!" she said to Elinor with a knowing look. "You may have one waiting in the wings yourself!"

Elinor said nothing, but Sir John—never one for subtlety—leaned in.

"His name is Ferrars," he stage-whispered. "But not a word—it’s a great secret!"

"Ferrars!" Anne gasped. "Why, I know him! A most agreeable man!"

Lucy quickly corrected her. "We’ve only met him once or twice at our uncle’s—hardly enough to claim acquaintance."

Elinor listened, startled. Who was this uncle? How did they know Edward? Her curiosity burned, but no further clues were offered. And Miss Steele’s tone when speaking of him left an uneasy suspicion—had she heard some ill report?

But the moment passed. The subject was dropped, and Elinor was left to wonder in silence.

Marianne, whose patience was easily exhausted by anything she deemed vulgar or beneath her tastes, found the Miss Steeles particularly grating to her sensibilities. Her current melancholy only sharpened her disdain, and her coldness toward them was so unwavering that any attempt at familiarity was swiftly rebuffed. It was no surprise, then, that the sisters—Lucy in particular—turned their attentions to Elinor, whose quiet courtesy offered a far more welcoming reception.

Lucy, though lacking refinement, possessed a quick wit that made her tolerable company in small doses. Yet her ignorance was impossible to overlook, and Elinor, though pitying the waste of such natural cleverness, could not ignore the calculating nature beneath Lucy’s flattery. There was an artfulness in her manner, a deliberate charm wielded like a blade, and Elinor found little comfort in the company of one so adept at feigned sincerity.

One afternoon, as they walked from the park toward the cottage, Lucy ventured a question with affected nonchalance.

“You will think my inquiry odd, I’m sure,” she began, “but tell me—are you acquainted with Mrs. Ferrars, your sister-in-law’s mother?”

Elinor did indeed find the question peculiar. “I have never met the lady,” she replied, cautious.

Lucy sighed. “I only wondered what she was like. You see, I have reason to be curious.”

Elinor’s brows lifted slightly. “Reason?”

Lucy hesitated, then lowered her voice conspiratorially. “Mrs. Ferrars is nothing to me now, but in time… well, we may be very closely connected indeed.”

Elinor’s pulse quickened, though her expression remained composed. “You cannot mean you are acquainted with Mr. Robert Ferrars?”

“Oh no,” Lucy said, her gaze fixed on Elinor with unsettling intensity. “His elder brother.”

For a moment, the world seemed to tilt. Elinor forced herself to breathe, to think. It was impossible. A mistake. A lie.

“You are surprised,” Lucy continued, feigning bashfulness. “And no wonder—he would never have spoken of me. It has been a secret these four years.”

*Four years.* The words struck like a blow. Elinor’s mind raced, grasping for reason. “You expect me to believe,” she said carefully, “that you have been engaged to Mr. Edward Ferrars all this time?”

“Indeed!” Lucy’s smile was triumphant. She drew a miniature from her pocket. “See for yourself.”

Elinor’s fingers trembled as she took it. The face gazing back was undeniably Edward’s. Her stomach twisted, but she handed it back with forced calm.

“I have longed to give him my portrait in return,” Lucy sighed. “But we have had to be so very discreet. His mother would never approve—I’ve no fortune, you see.”

Elinor fought to keep her voice steady. “Why confide in me?”

“Because I trust you,” Lucy said sweetly. “And after my questions about Mrs. Ferrars, I owed you some explanation.”

Elinor said nothing. The pieces fell into place with cruel clarity—Edward’s melancholy, his evasiveness. Had it all been guilt?

Lucy prattled on, lamenting the trials of their secret love, but Elinor scarcely heard. When they reached the cottage at last, she bid Lucy a stiff farewell and retreated to the sanctuary of solitude.

Alone, the weight of it crashed upon her. The letters, the ring—proofs too damning to dismiss. Edward, whom she had believed so honorable, bound to another.

She pressed a hand to her mouth, stifling the cry that threatened to escape. Then, with a slow, steadying breath, she straightened.

There would be no tears. Not yet.

For now, she would endure.

Elinor could no longer doubt the truth of Lucy’s claims. The weight of evidence—Edward’s visit to Plymouth, his melancholy, his inconsistent behavior, the Steeles’ intimate knowledge of Norland, the picture, the letter, the ring—was too substantial to dismiss as mere deception. Though every fiber of her being resisted it, reason forced her to accept the painful reality: Edward was engaged to Lucy Steele.

Her first reaction was a storm of wounded pride and indignation. Had he played her false? Had his affection been nothing but pretense? No—she could not believe that. His love for her had been genuine; she had felt it, and others had seen it. That conviction softened the edges of her anger. If he had wronged her, he had wronged himself far more. His thoughtless attachment to Lucy, formed in the rashness of youth, now bound him to a woman whose shallow nature could never satisfy a man of his integrity and intellect. Where Elinor might, in time, find peace, Edward faced a lifetime of regret.

The injustice of it ached in her chest, but she refused to burden her family with her sorrow. Marianne, lost in her own romantic dreams, noticed nothing amiss, and their mother remained blissfully unaware. To reveal the truth would only cause them pain—and worse, it might provoke harsh words against Edward, which Elinor could not bear to hear. She would endure this heartbreak alone, shielding them with a composed face and steady voice.

Yet she needed to speak with Lucy again—not out of morbid curiosity, but to prove her own detachment. If Lucy suspected her lingering attachment, jealousy might twist the situation further. Elinor would not give her that satisfaction. She would converse calmly, even kindly, to demonstrate that her heart was untouched.

Opportunity, however, was scarce. Their gatherings at Barton Park were loud, chaotic affairs—filled with cards, laughter, and Lady Middleton’s relentless hospitality—leaving no room for private conversation. Then, one morning, Sir John arrived with an invitation: Lady Middleton was dining alone, save for her mother and the Steele sisters, and begged their company.

Elinor accepted at once. A quieter evening might provide the chance she needed.

The dinner passed as insipidly as expected, the conversation as dull as the gray sky outside. Even when they withdrew to the drawing room, the presence of the children and the clatter of tea-things made private speech impossible. It was only when the card table was set that Elinor saw her opening.

As Lady Middleton urged Lucy to abandon her needlework for a game, Elinor intervened. "If I might assist Miss Steele instead," she offered smoothly, "I should be glad to help with her filigree basket. There seems much left to do."

Lucy, ever eager to please, agreed at once. With a gracious nod from Lady Middleton, the two settled side by side, their hands busy with the delicate task.

Nearby, Marianne had retreated to the pianoforte, her music filling the room with a sonorous shield. Under its cover, Elinor drew a quiet breath.

Now was the moment.

Elinor spoke with measured firmness. “I would be unworthy of your confidence if I did not wish to understand more. Forgive me for revisiting the subject, but my curiosity remains.”

Lucy’s face brightened. “Oh, thank you for speaking first! I’ve been uneasy, fearing I’d displeased you the other day.”

“Displeased me?” Elinor’s voice softened with sincerity. “How could you think such a thing? Your trust honors me. Was there ever a reason for it that was not kind?”

Lucy’s sharp eyes gleamed. “Yet your manner was so cool—I thought you angry. I’ve reproached myself since for burdening you. But now I see it was only my own foolish worry. You don’t blame me, and that is such a relief! If you knew how my heart aches with this secret, how speaking of it eases me, you’d forgive any clumsiness in my words.”

“I can well imagine the weight it carried,” Elinor replied gently. “And I assure you, you’ve no cause for regret. Your situation is indeed difficult. The obstacles are many, and only the strongest affection could sustain you through them. Edward, I gather, depends entirely on his mother?”

“He has but two thousand pounds of his own—hardly enough to marry on, though I’d gladly live on nothing for his sake. But I love him too well to rob him of what his mother might grant if he obeyed her. We must wait, even if it takes years. With any other man, such uncertainty would terrify me, but Edward’s love is steadfast.”

“That certainty must comfort you greatly,” Elinor observed. “And no doubt he clings to the same hope. Had your attachment faltered over these years—as so many do—your position would be truly pitiable.”

Lucy glanced up, but Elinor kept her expression carefully neutral.

“Edward’s devotion has been tested,” Lucy insisted. “Years apart, yet never once has he given me reason to doubt him. If his manner changed, if he seemed distracted or spoke too often of another, I would know at once—for I am not blind in such matters.”

*How neatly she defends him,* Elinor thought, *as if rehearsed.*

After a pause, she asked, “What, then, are your hopes? Must you simply wait for Mrs. Ferrars’s death? A grim prospect. Is Edward resigned to years of delay rather than risk her anger by confessing?”

“If her displeasure were temporary, we might dare it. But she is proud and willful. If enraged, she might disinherit him entirely in favor of Robert. I cannot let him sacrifice so much for me.”

“Nor should you sacrifice yourself beyond reason,” Elinor murmured.

Lucy fell silent, her gaze searching.

“Do you know Robert Ferrars?” Elinor asked.

“Not at all. By all accounts, he’s foolish and vain—nothing like Edward.”

“Vain!” Miss Steele interjected, drawn by the lull in Marianne’s music. “Ah, discussing their admirers, no doubt!”

Lucy flushed. “No, sister, you mistake. Our favorites are *not* vain.”

Mrs. Jennings chuckled. “Miss Dashwood’s certainly isn’t—he’s the most modest young man! But Lucy’s too sly to reveal hers.”

Miss Steele smirked. “I daresay Lucy’s beau is just as modest as Miss Dashwood’s.”

Elinor’s cheeks warmed despite herself. Lucy shot her sister a venomous look. The room lapsed into silence until Lucy leaned closer, her voice barely audible over the piano.

“I’ve a notion to hasten matters. Since Edward prefers the church, if he took orders, your influence might secure him Norland’s living. It’s a fine one, and the current holder is elderly. That income would suffice for marriage, and we could trust time for the rest.”

Elinor hesitated. “I would gladly assist Mr. Ferrars, but my influence is unnecessary. As Mrs. John Dashwood’s brother, his claim is already strong.”

“Yet she would oppose his entering the clergy.”

“Then my efforts would do little.”

Another silence stretched between them before Lucy sighed. “Perhaps ending the engagement would be wisest. We are trapped on all sides. It would pain us now but spare greater misery later. Yet you will not advise me?”

Elinor forced a smile. “On such matters, my opinion is irrelevant unless it aligns with your wishes.”

“You wrong me,” Lucy protested. “I value your judgment above all. If you urged me to release him, I would.”

*How false,* Elinor thought, *and how cruel to Edward.* Aloud, she said, “Such faith in my impartiality overwhelms me. The power to part two devoted hearts is too grave for an outsider.”

“Precisely because you *are* indifferent,” Lucy pressed, “your counsel would weigh heavily. Were you partial, your view would be worthless.”

Elinor chose not to reply, wary of provoking greater frankness. The conversation lapsed until Lucy, with sudden brightness, asked, “Will you winter in town?”

“No.”

“A pity! I’d hoped to meet there. My sister and I visit relations in January—though I go chiefly to see Edward. He joins us in February. Without him, London holds no charm.”

Elinor was summoned then to cards, ending their exchange—a relief, for nothing had reconciled them. As she took her seat, her heart ached for Edward: bound to a woman he did not love, his chance of happiness forfeit to Lucy’s selfish resolve.

The subject arose no more between them, save when Lucy, with pointed cheer, mentioned letters from Edward. Elinor responded with polite detachment, ending such talks swiftly. To indulge Lucy was neither deserved nor safe.

The Steeles’ stay at Barton Park stretched far beyond initial plans. Favored by all, they lingered nearly two months, their scheduled return to Exeter deferred again and again—until the season’s festivities demanded their presence, and even Sir John could delay them no longer.

Though Mrs. Jennings was a woman who divided her time generously among the households of her children and friends, she was not without a home of her own. Since the passing of her husband—a man who had prospered in trade, though not among the fashionable circles of London—she had taken up residence each winter in a modest but comfortable house near Portman Square. As January approached, her thoughts turned toward the city, and one day, with characteristic spontaneity, she extended an invitation to the elder Dashwood sisters to accompany her.

Elinor, ever observant yet unaware of the eager flush that had risen to Marianne’s cheeks or the brightness in her eyes that betrayed her longing, declined at once on behalf of them both, citing their unwavering resolution not to leave their mother during the winter months.

Mrs. Jennings, undeterred, pressed the matter. "Good heavens, child! Your mother will manage perfectly well without you, and I *insist* you come. I’ve quite set my heart on it. You shan’t be any trouble—I’ll send Betty ahead by coach, and we three shall travel comfortably in my chaise. Once in town, if my company grows tiresome, you may take refuge with one of my daughters. And mark my words, I’ll see at least one of you well married before the season is out!"

Sir John, ever eager to encourage amusement, added his voice to the cause. "I daresay Miss Marianne would not refuse such an opportunity, if only her sister would consent. Must she be denied every pleasure on Miss Dashwood’s account? Take my advice—when you tire of Barton, slip away to London without another word."

Mrs. Jennings laughed. "Lord bless me, I’ll be glad of Miss Marianne’s company whether her sister joins us or not—though it would be far pleasanter for them both to be together. They might whisper and laugh at my oddities when my back is turned! But one of them, at least, I *must* have. How am I to endure the winter alone, when I’ve always had Charlotte with me before? Come, Miss Marianne, give me your hand—we’ll call it settled. And if Miss Dashwood relents, so much the better!"

Marianne’s voice trembled with feeling as she replied, "I thank you, ma’am, from the bottom of my heart. Your kindness overwhelms me, and nothing would bring me greater joy than to accept. But my mother—my dear, devoted mother—Elinor is right. If our absence were to diminish her comfort in the slightest, I could not bear it. No temptation in the world could sway me."

Yet Mrs. Jennings, undaunted, assured them that Mrs. Dashwood would spare them without a second thought. Elinor, watching her sister closely, recognized the truth: Marianne’s eagerness to go to London stemmed from one desire alone—to see Willoughby again. The realization pained her, but she held her peace, deferring instead to their mother’s judgment, though she had little hope of dissuasion.

When the invitation was laid before Mrs. Dashwood, she embraced it with enthusiasm. "It is the very thing I would have wished for you both! Margaret and I shall be perfectly content in your absence—we shall read, play music, and I may even rearrange your rooms without disturbance. You *must* go. Every young woman of your standing ought to experience London. Mrs. Jennings is a kind soul, and you may even see your brother. Whatever his faults, he is still my son—I cannot bear this estrangement."

Elinor, ever cautious, ventured a final objection. "Mrs. Jennings is warm-hearted, but her society does not offer much pleasure, nor does her patronage lend us consequence."

Marianne, impatient, interjected, "If Elinor refuses on such grounds, *I* need not be hindered. I can endure any inconvenience if it means going to London."

Elinor nearly smiled at this sudden indifference to manners she had so often urged her sister to tolerate. Resigned, she decided that if Marianne went, she must go as well—if only to temper her sister’s impulsiveness and spare Mrs. Jennings the full force of Marianne’s disdain. Besides, she recalled Lucy Steele’s mention that Edward Ferrars would not arrive in town before February. Their visit might conclude before any awkward encounter could occur.

Mrs. Dashwood dismissed all objections. "You shall *both* go. Elinor, you will find pleasure if you allow yourself to anticipate it—perhaps even in better acquaintance with your sister-in-law’s family."

Elinor seized the moment to gently prepare her mother for future disappointment. "I esteem Edward Ferrars greatly, but as for the rest of his family, I care not if I ever meet them again."

Mrs. Dashwood only smiled, while Marianne stared in astonishment. Elinor sighed, knowing she had spoken in vain.

The matter was settled. Mrs. Jennings rejoiced, Sir John exulted at the prospect of livelier company, and even Lady Middleton exerted herself to appear pleased. As for the Miss Steeles—Lucy in particular—their delight was boundless.

Elinor yielded with less reluctance than she had anticipated. Seeing her mother’s satisfaction and Marianne’s radiant happiness—her spirits restored, her voice alive with excitement—she could not regret the decision. Marianne’s joy bordered on delirium, her only sorrow the parting from their mother, whose own grief was scarcely less acute. Only Elinor viewed the separation as temporary.

Their departure was fixed for the first week of January. The Middletons would follow a week later, while the Steeles remained at Barton Park until the family’s return to London.

Elinor settled into the carriage beside Mrs. Jennings, the wheels rolling toward London, and marveled at how swiftly circumstances had changed. Only days ago, she would have balked at the idea of traveling under this woman’s chaperonage—their acquaintance so brief, their temperaments so mismatched. Yet Marianne’s fervent enthusiasm had swept aside all reservations, her bright-eyed anticipation leaving no room for protest.

Watching her sister now, Elinor felt the weight of her own quiet resignation. Marianne’s joy was palpable, her every thought fixed on the promise of reunion with Willoughby. For Elinor, there was no such certainty—only the hollow space where hope might have been. Still, she would not dampen Marianne’s spirits. If Willoughby proved true, she would school her heart to rejoice without envy. If not, she would be the steady hand to guide her sister through the disappointment.

The journey passed in marked contrast between the sisters. Marianne drifted in silent reverie, stirred only by glimpses of scenic beauty, her exclamations reserved solely for Elinor. In turn, Elinor played the gracious companion to Mrs. Jennings, filling the silence with amiable chatter, though her mind was elsewhere—already tracing the steps she would take in London to discern Willoughby’s intentions.

Upon their arrival, the house welcomed them with warmth, Charlotte’s former room now theirs, its silk-adorned landscape a silent testament to polished education. Yet Marianne had little patience for admiration. No sooner had they settled than she seized paper and pen, her hurried movements betraying the recipient of her note before Elinor even glimpsed the telltale *W* upon the folded letter. The footman dispatched it swiftly, and Marianne’s restless energy filled the room, her anticipation too sharp to be soothed by mere distractions.

Dinner was a trial. Marianne barely touched her plate, her ears straining for the sound of carriage wheels. When at last a knock came, she flew to the door—only to freeze as Colonel Brandon stepped across the threshold. The devastation on her face was unmistakable. She fled, leaving Elinor to smooth over the awkwardness, her own disappointment tempered by sympathy for the Colonel, whose quiet regard for Marianne had clearly noted the wound his presence inflicted.

Mrs. Jennings’s return spared them prolonged discomfort, her effusive chatter filling the space, though Brandon’s replies were as measured as ever. He did not stay long. That night, as they retired early, Elinor’s thoughts churned. Marianne’s faith in Willoughby was absolute—but where was he?

Morning restored Marianne’s optimism, her spirits rebounding with the new day’s possibilities. Mrs. Palmer’s visit brought laughter and aimless chatter, though Marianne’s attention remained fractured, her gaze forever wandering in search of a familiar figure. Even the bustle of Bond Street could not anchor her; every shop, every passing stranger was scrutinized and dismissed. By the time they returned home, her impatience had sharpened into agitation. No letter awaited her. No message.

*How strange,* Elinor thought, watching Marianne pace by the window. If Willoughby cared, why this silence? The question gnawed at her. Soon, if this uncertainty continued, she would have no choice but to press their mother for answers.

The evening brought no relief. Marianne’s attempts to read faltered, the book abandoned as she resumed her vigil by the window, ears straining for the knock that did not come. Elinor, forced into the role of hostess for Mrs. Jennings’s whist party, watched her sister with growing unease.

Hope, it seemed, was a relentless tormentor.

The following morning at breakfast, Mrs. Jennings remarked with cheerful practicality, “If this fine weather continues, Sir John will be sorely tempted to delay his departure from Barton. A lost day’s sport is a tragedy for such devoted gentlemen—though I confess, I’ve never understood their passion myself.”

Marianne, whose spirits had been uneven of late, brightened at once. “How right you are!” she exclaimed, crossing to the window as if the mere mention of outdoor pursuits had renewed her vigor. “This sunshine will keep many in the countryside. They must be reveling in it.” Her voice held a lightness Elinor had not heard in days.

“Indeed,” Marianne continued, seating herself with restored animation, “it is perfect for them. Though—” here her expression flickered, “—such mildness cannot last. Frost may come any night now.”

Elinor, observing both her sister’s fragile optimism and Mrs. Jennings’ perceptive gaze, interposed smoothly, “I imagine Sir John and Lady Middleton will join us in town by week’s end.”

“Oh, no doubt,” agreed Mrs. Jennings. “Mary has a way of getting what she wants.”

Elinor privately suspected that a letter to Combe Magna would soon be dispatched, though Marianne gave no outward sign. Still, if her sister’s mood had lifted, even temporarily, Elinor could not begrudge her the small comfort.

Marianne remained preoccupied—not with the morning’s errands, which consisted of leaving cards at Mrs. Jennings’ acquaintances, but with the shifting skies. “Don’t you feel the air growing colder, Elinor?” she pressed. “The clouds are thinning. I daresay we’ll have frost by nightfall.”

Elinor wavered between amusement and unease. Yet Marianne persisted, finding in every evening’s fire and every dawn’s haze fresh portents of winter’s arrival.

Their lodgings, at least, offered no cause for complaint. Mrs. Jennings’ hospitality was generous, and her circle—save a few steadfast city friends—posed no discomfort to the Dashwoods. Elinor, though indifferent to the nightly card parties, appreciated the colonel’s frequent visits. His quiet conversations were a solace, though his silent attentiveness to Marianne troubled her. His spirits, she noted, were lower than in Devonshire.

A week later, confirmation came: Willoughby was in London. His card lay upon the table when they returned from their outing.

“He was here!” Marianne cried, her composure shattered.

Elinor, relieved by proof of his presence, ventured, “He will call again tomorrow, surely.” But Marianne scarcely heard her, seizing the card before escaping upstairs.

From that moment, Marianne’s restlessness returned tenfold. She could think of nothing but his promised visit, refusing even to join the others the next morning. When they returned, Elinor needed only one glance to confirm he had not come.

A letter arrived—for Mrs. Jennings, to Marianne’s visible dismay.

“You were expecting one?” Elinor asked gently.

“A little,” Marianne admitted after a pause. Then, with sudden heat: “But you cannot reproach me for secrecy when you keep your own counsel so closely!”

Elinor, stung, could not deny it. Yet how could she explain what she herself did not fully understand?

Mrs. Jennings broke the tension, reading aloud Lady Middleton’s invitation for the following evening. Though obliged to attend, Marianne was torn between dread of missing Willoughby and her own disinterest in society.

The evening proved as tedious as expected. Sir John, ever the enthusiast, had assembled a makeshift ball—much to Lady Middleton’s chagrin, for town decorum frowned upon such spontaneity. Mr. Palmer ignored them entirely, while Marianne, after one despairing survey of the room, sat in silent misery.

“I thought you were in Devonshire,” Mr. Palmer remarked idly when they chanced to speak.

“Did you?” Elinor replied.

“When do you return?”

“I don’t know.”

Marianne danced with such reluctance that even Mrs. Jennings noticed. “If a certain gentleman were here,” she hinted, “you’d not be so weary. Though I must say, it was unkind of him to decline.”

“Decline?” Marianne paled.

“Sir John invited him just this morning!”

Marianne said nothing, but her face spoke volumes.

Determined to act, Elinor resolved to write to their mother, hoping to prompt long-overdue questions about Marianne’s well-being. The next morning, as Marianne penned yet another letter (undoubtedly to Willoughby), Elinor set to her task.

Midway through, Colonel Brandon was announced. Marianne fled at once.

He seemed burdened, though relieved to find Elinor alone. After a prolonged silence, he asked abruptly, “When may I offer congratulations on your sister’s engagement?”

Elinor startled. “Engagement?”

“Is it not widely known? Their correspondence is open, and talk of marriage is common.”

“By whom?” Elinor demanded.

“Mrs. Jennings, the Palmers—half of London, it seems. Yet I would not have credited it had I not seen a letter in your sister’s hand, addressed to Willoughby, today.” His voice grew strained. “Is it decided, then? Or is there still—? Forgive me. I have no right to ask.”

Elinor, shaken by his raw emotion, chose her words carefully. Though uncertain of Willoughby’s true intentions, she could not offer false hope. “I have never been explicitly told of an engagement,” she admitted, “but their affection seems certain.”

He absorbed this in silence, then rose. “Then I wish her every happiness,” he said thickly. “And him—may he strive to be worthy of her.” With that, he departed.

Elinor was left more unsettled than ever. The colonel’s heartache was plain, yet she could not wish it otherwise without betraying her sister. The weight of so many unspoken truths pressed upon her, leaving no room for comfort.

The days that followed Elinor’s letter to their mother brought no word from Willoughby—no visit, no letter to explain his silence. Marianne moved through them as if in a dream, her spirit dulled, her usual care for her appearance abandoned. When the evening arrived for Lady Middleton’s party—an engagement they could not refuse—Marianne prepared with listless indifference, her face pale, her eyes absent. She sat motionless by the drawing-room fire until the moment of departure, lost in thought, scarcely aware of Elinor beside her.

The party itself was a stifling affair, the rooms overcrowded, the air thick with heat and perfume. Marianne, in no state for conversation or amusement, allowed herself to be led to a chair near the card tables, where she sat in silent wretchedness. Elinor, ever watchful, soon caught sight of Willoughby standing not far off, deep in conversation with a strikingly fashionable young woman. Her breath caught—would he acknowledge them?

He did, but only with a distant bow, his gaze flickering over them before returning to his companion. Elinor’s heart sank. Marianne, however, had seen him. A flush of wild hope lit her face, and she half-rose from her seat.

"He is here!" she whispered urgently, her voice trembling. "Why does he not come to me?"

Elinor gripped her sister’s arm. "Be calm," she urged. "Do not draw attention."

But Marianne was beyond restraint. When Willoughby at last turned fully toward them, she called his name, her hand outstretched. He approached—yet his manner was all wrong. His words were hurried, his attention fixed on Elinor as if avoiding Marianne’s gaze.

"Have you not received my letters?" Marianne demanded, her voice breaking. "Will you not speak to me?"

His discomfort was plain. He took her hand briefly, then withdrew as if burned. "I called in Berkeley Street," he said stiffly. "I regret I missed you."

Marianne’s distress was unbearable. "There must be some mistake—tell me what has happened!"

But Willoughby only grew paler, his eyes darting toward the young woman he had been speaking with earlier. With a muttered excuse, he bowed again and retreated.

Marianne collapsed into her chair, her face bloodless. Elinor, fearing she might faint, shielded her from the room’s prying eyes, pressing lavender water to her temples.

"Make him come back," Marianne pleaded, her voice a ragged whisper. "I must know the truth—I cannot bear this!"

Elinor, heartsick, could only urge patience. "Not here," she murmured. "Wait until tomorrow."

But Marianne was beyond reason. Her anguish was too great for tears, her body rigid with shock. When Elinor saw Willoughby slip from the room entirely, she gently broke the news, urging resignation. Marianne, desperate, begged to leave at once.

Lady Middleton, though mid-game, acquiesced with polite concern. The journey home was silent, Marianne’s agony too deep for words. Once in their room, she sank into bed, her face turned away.

Left alone, Elinor grappled with the truth she could no longer deny: Willoughby’s regard had faded. His manner spoke of guilt, not misunderstanding. Whatever had passed between him and Marianne, his heart was no longer in it.

And Marianne? The pain of this encounter was only the beginning. The future, Elinor feared, held greater sorrow still.

The dawn had scarcely broken over London, its pale light struggling against the chill of a January morning, when Marianne, still half-dressed, knelt by the window, her face illuminated only by the faint glow seeping through the glass. Tears streamed down her cheeks as she wrote with feverish haste, her quill trembling between her fingers.

Elinor stirred from sleep, roused by the muffled sounds of her sister’s grief. For a moment, she watched in silent anguish before softly asking, *"Marianne, may I ask—?"*

*"No, Elinor,"* Marianne interrupted, her voice brittle with forced composure. *"You will soon know all."* But the fragile calm shattered as quickly as it had come, and she dissolved into fresh sobs, her letter momentarily forgotten.

Elinor moved quietly to her side, offering what comfort she could without intrusion. Yet Marianne, raw with emotion, begged to be left alone. Understanding the necessity of solitude, Elinor withdrew, though her heart ached with helplessness.

Breakfast was a torment. Marianne sat motionless, her untouched plate a silent testament to her suffering. Elinor, desperate to shield her from prying eyes, engaged Mrs. Jennings in lively conversation, steering attention away from her sister’s distress. The older woman, ever cheerful, remarked with a laugh, *"Upon my word, I never saw a young woman so desperately in love! When is the wedding to be?"*

Elinor forced a smile, though her voice was firm. *"You are mistaken, ma’am. There is no engagement."*

Mrs. Jennings scoffed, waving away the denial. *"Nonsense! The whole town knows of their attachment!"*

But Elinor had no strength to argue further. The arrival of a letter—delivered to Marianne with trembling hands—sent her sister fleeing from the room, her face deathly pale. Elinor knew at once it was from Willoughby.

She found Marianne collapsed across the bed, her body wracked with sobs, the damning letter clutched in her grasp. Without a word, Elinor gathered her close, letting her own tears fall as she stroked her sister’s hair. When Marianne finally pressed the letter into her hands, Elinor read it with mounting horror.

Willoughby’s words were cold, formal, devoid of remorse. He spoke of *"esteem"* rather than love, of an *"engagement elsewhere,"* and returned Marianne’s letters and lock of hair as though they were mere trifles. The cruelty of it stole Elinor’s breath.

*"He has broken no faith,"* Marianne whispered hoarsely. *"He never spoke of love—not plainly. But I believed— Oh, Elinor, I was certain!"*

Elinor could offer no comfort, only the bitter truth: *"He is not worthy of you."*

Marianne’s anguish was relentless. She spoke of returning home, of escaping the pitying eyes of London society. But Elinor, ever practical, reminded her of their obligations to Mrs. Jennings.

*"Then let it be soon,"* Marianne pleaded, her voice breaking. *"I cannot bear this."*

Exhaustion finally claimed her, and she lay still, though her fingers trembled around Willoughby’s letter. Elinor remained at her side, watching as the lavender drops she had persuaded her sister to take brought a fragile calm.

But the storm was far from over.

Mrs. Jennings swept into their room the moment she returned, her usual buoyancy subdued by genuine concern. Without waiting for an answer, she pushed open the door, her eyes softening as they fell upon Marianne, who turned her face away, unwilling—or unable—to speak.

“My poor dear,” Mrs. Jennings murmured, her voice thick with sympathy. “How are you bearing it?” She glanced at Elinor, who stood nearby, her own expression carefully composed. “Heavens, she looks dreadful—no wonder, after such news! That wretched man! Mrs. Taylor told me not half an hour ago, and I declare, I nearly collapsed on the spot. If this is true—and I’ve no doubt it is—then he has treated you abominably, my dear, and I hope his new wife torments him endlessly.” She shook her head, righteous indignation flaring. “Mark my words, he’ll regret it. But there, there—you mustn’t despair. A pretty girl like you won’t lack for admirers. And the Parrys and Sandersons are coming tonight—that will divert you, won’t it?”

With a final pat on Marianne’s shoulder, she tiptoed out, as though noise might deepen the girl’s misery.

To Elinor’s surprise, Marianne insisted on joining them for dinner, despite her sister’s gentle protests. “No, I will go down,” she said, her voice hollow. “The bustle will be better than sitting alone.”

Elinor did not argue further, though she doubted Marianne could endure an entire meal in company. Still, she helped her sister prepare, smoothing her dress and guiding her to the dining room when summoned.

Marianne, pale and silent, ate more than Elinor expected. Had she spoken—or truly registered Mrs. Jennings’s well-meant but clumsy attempts at comfort—her composure might have shattered. But she remained lost in thought, deaf to the conversation around her.

Elinor, ever gracious, stepped in to acknowledge Mrs. Jennings’s kindness, though the older woman’s effusions often bordered on absurdity. Their hostess, determined to lift Marianne’s spirits, showered her with attention—urging the best seat by the fire, pressing delicacies upon her, chattering about the day’s trivialities. Had Elinor not seen the anguish in Marianne’s face, she might have been amused by the notion that sweets and gossip could mend a broken heart.

But Marianne could bear no more. With a choked exclamation, she rose abruptly and fled the room.

“Poor lamb!” Mrs. Jennings sighed as the door closed behind her. “How it pains me to see her so! And she didn’t even finish her wine—or the cherries! If only I knew what might comfort her.” She shook her head. “But what can one expect? When a man must choose between love and money, love seldom wins.”

“Miss Grey is wealthy, then?” Elinor asked quietly.

“Fifty thousand pounds, my dear! A stylish girl, they say, though not pretty. And the family’s fortune is secure—unlike Willoughby’s, by all accounts. Men will always chase riches over affection.”

Elinor said nothing, though the injustice stung.

Mrs. Jennings, ever hopeful, soon brightened. “Well! This may yet turn out for the best. Colonel Brandon will have his chance now—mark my words, they’ll be married by midsummer. Two thousand a year, and Delaford is a charming estate. Why, the gardens alone—”

Elinor cut her off gently. “I think, ma’am, what Marianne needs most is peace.”

Upstairs, she found her sister slumped by the dying fire, her face streaked with silent tears. “Leave me,” Marianne whispered.

“I will,” Elinor replied, “if you promise to rest.”

After some resistance, Marianne yielded, allowing Elinor to guide her to bed. She pressed a kiss to her sister’s forehead before slipping out, hoping sleep might bring some respite.

Below, Mrs. Jennings intercepted her with a glass of wine. “The finest Constantia—my late husband swore by it for his gout. Perhaps it might soothe poor Marianne?”

Elinor smiled faintly. “I fear her ailment is beyond even this remedy. But I thank you.” She drank it herself, wryly acknowledging that a disappointed heart might need such comforts as much as any physical pain.

When Colonel Brandon arrived for tea, his searching glance told Elinor he already knew of Willoughby’s betrayal. Mrs. Jennings, oblivious, nudged her to share the news.

Brandon soon drew near, his voice low. “Marianne—is she unwell?”

“She is,” Elinor admitted. “The shock has been severe.”

He hesitated. “What I heard this morning—I scarcely believed it at first.”

“His engagement to Miss Grey? Yes, we know.” Her voice was steady, though bitterness threaded through it. “He has proven himself unworthy.”

Brandon’s jaw tightened. “And Marianne—does she see it as you do?”

Elinor sighed. “You know her heart. She would defend him still, if she could.”

He said nothing, and the subject was soon buried beneath the clatter of teacups and the bustle of card tables. Mrs. Jennings watched in vain for some sign of elation in Brandon—some hopeful spark at the thought of Marianne’s newfound freedom. But his manner remained grave, his thoughts unreadable.

The evening passed, heavy with unspoken sorrow.

Marianne awoke the next morning to the same heavy weight of sorrow that had burdened her when she closed her eyes. Elinor, ever patient, encouraged her to speak freely, and before breakfast was served, they had revisited the subject again and again—Elinor steady in her counsel, Marianne fluctuating between desperate hope and bitter despair.

At times, she convinced herself Willoughby was as unfortunate as she, as blameless. Then, just as swiftly, the illusion shattered, leaving her raw and inconsolable. She vacillated between indifference to the world’s scrutiny and a longing to retreat from it entirely. Yet one resolve held firm: she avoided Mrs. Jennings whenever possible, enduring her presence in resentful silence.

*She cannot understand,* Marianne thought bitterly. *Her kindness is not compassion—it is curiosity.*

Elinor, though pained by her sister’s harsh judgment, recognized it as the distortion of grief. Marianne’s tender heart, so attuned to feeling, often mistook others’ intentions.

A disturbance came mid-morning when Mrs. Jennings swept into their room, beaming, a letter clutched in her hand.

“Here, my dear,” she said brightly, “something to lift your spirits!”

For one delirious moment, Marianne’s pulse raced—*Willoughby. It must be Willoughby.* She envisioned a page brimming with remorse, an explanation, a plea for forgiveness. Then reality crashed upon her: her mother’s handwriting, not his. The disappointment was a physical blow. Tears, hot and furious, spilled down her cheeks.

Mrs. Jennings, oblivious to the pain she had unwittingly inflicted, patted Marianne’s shoulder and withdrew, urging her to take comfort in the letter. But when Marianne read it, her mother’s affectionate words—so full of faith in Willoughby’s constancy—only deepened the wound.

Elinor, watching helplessly, resolved to write to their mother at once, though the task filled her with dread. She had barely begun when a sharp rap at the door startled them both.

“Who could that be?” Elinor murmured.

Marianne glanced out the window. “Colonel Brandon,” she said with a frown. “Must he intrude?”

Elinor assured her he would not enter without Mrs. Jennings present, but moments later, his voice echoed in the hall. Marianne retreated to her room, unwilling to endure another well-meaning visitor.

Colonel Brandon’s face was grave as he entered. “Forgive the intrusion,” he said to Elinor. “I had hoped to speak with you alone.”

His manner was so earnest that Elinor’s apprehension softened. “You have news?”

He hesitated. “It concerns Willoughby.”

Her breath caught. “Tell me.”

What followed was a tale of betrayal and sorrow—a story of a young woman named Eliza, deceived and abandoned, left to ruin while Willoughby moved unscathed through the world. The parallels to Marianne’s own heartbreak were undeniable.

“I thought,” Brandon finished quietly, “that knowing the truth might spare your sister greater pain.”

Elinor’s hands trembled. “You were right to tell me.”

His gaze was weary. “I only pray it helps her.”

As he took his leave, Elinor sat motionless, the weight of his words settling upon her. She would share them with Marianne—not to wound her further, but to arm her against false hope.

For sometimes, the cruelest kindness was the truth.

The weight of Willoughby’s betrayal settled upon Marianne with a quiet, suffocating heaviness. When Elinor relayed the damning details of their conversation—his callous admission, his dishonorable past—Marianne did not protest. She did not weep with the same violent abandon as before, nor did she attempt to defend him. Instead, she listened in silence, her tears falling steadily, as if she had already known the truth in her heart and only now allowed it to break her completely.

Elinor watched her sister closely, torn between relief and sorrow. There was a grim satisfaction in seeing Marianne no longer shrink from Colonel Brandon’s presence, in the softening of her tone when she addressed him—not with warmth, but with a solemn respect. Yet the change was not one of healing, but of resignation. Marianne’s grief had hardened into something quieter, deeper, as if she mourned not just the loss of Willoughby’s love, but the shattering of the man she had believed him to be. The knowledge of his cruelty to Miss Williams, the suspicion of what his intentions toward herself might once have been, festered in her mind, too painful to voice even to Elinor.

Mrs. Dashwood’s letters arrived in rapid succession, each one a testament to her own anguish. Her words mirrored her daughters’—indignation on Marianne’s behalf, despair at the ruin of such bright hopes. Yet she urged fortitude, a word so foreign to Marianne’s nature that it underscored the depth of her devastation. More than anything, Mrs. Dashwood feared the memories that awaited Marianne at Barton, where every path and parlor would whisper of Willoughby. She insisted they prolong their stay in London, despite Marianne’s silent longing for home, for the solace of her mother’s embrace.

London, at least, offered distractions—crowds to lose oneself in, engagements to occupy the hours. And there was safety in its anonymity; Willoughby’s circle would no longer intersect with theirs. His marriage, when it came, was announced with cruel efficiency. Elinor, dreading the moment, took pains to shield Marianne from the gossip, intercepting the news before it could strike from the society pages. Marianne bore it with a brittle composure, but the tears came later, in private, a storm of sorrow that left her exhausted.

The world, however, moved on. Sir John and Mrs. Jennings clucked over the scandal with indignant vigor, while Lady Middleton offered her usual detached sympathy—just enough to satisfy propriety without ever truly engaging. Only Colonel Brandon’s quiet concern provided any comfort. His discretion, his unspoken understanding, earned him Marianne’s reluctant gratitude, though she seldom had the strength to show it beyond a fleeting glance or a murmured word.

Then came the Steeles—Lucy, all false sweetness and probing curiosity, eager to pry into Elinor’s affairs under the guise of sisterly concern.

*“I should have been quite disappointed if I had not found you here still,”* Lucy simpered, her eyes sharp with calculation.

Elinor forced a polite smile, refusing to rise to the bait. But when Nancy Steele suggested, with grating presumption, that they might intrude upon Marianne’s solitude, it was Lucy herself who cut her short—not out of kindness, but to maintain the appearance of propriety she so prized.

And so the days passed, each one a trial of patience and endurance. Marianne, adrift in her sorrow, seemed a shadow of herself, while Elinor guarded her fiercely—from prying eyes, from careless words, from the world that had wounded her so deeply.

But the heart, no matter how broken, could not remain in stasis forever. Change, however imperceptible, was coming.

Marianne had resisted at first, but after gentle persuasion from Elinor and Mrs. Jennings, she agreed to accompany them on a brief outing—though with strict conditions. She would pay no visits, she insisted; she would only go as far as Gray’s in Sackville Street, where Elinor intended to exchange some of their mother’s old-fashioned jewels.

Upon their arrival, Mrs. Jennings recalled a social obligation farther down the street and, having no business at Gray’s herself, proposed that she pay her call while the sisters conducted theirs, returning for them afterward. The Dashwoods ascended the stairs only to find the shop crowded, forcing them to wait. They settled near the counter where service seemed most likely, beside a gentleman engrossed in the meticulous selection of a toothpick-case.

Elinor, ever practical, hoped his attention might turn to civility, but his fastidiousness proved greater than his manners. For a quarter of an hour, he deliberated over size, shape, and ornamentation, subjecting every case in the shop to scrutiny before settling upon his own design. His only acknowledgment of the ladies came in the form of several bold stares—enough to impress upon Elinor the image of a man whose fashionable exterior could not disguise his innate insignificance.

Marianne, lost in her own thoughts, noticed none of it. She might as well have been in her own room for all the awareness she had of Mr. Gray’s shop or its occupants.

At last, the gentleman concluded his purchase—ivory, gold, and pearls all appointed to his satisfaction—and with a final glance at the sisters, one that demanded admiration rather than offered it, he departed in an air of satisfied vanity.

Elinor promptly attended to her own business, but just as she finished, she was startled to find her brother at her side.

Their reunion was cordial, if not effusive, their greetings measured enough to appear proper in such a public setting. John Dashwood expressed no regret at seeing them—indeed, he seemed rather pleased—and inquired after their mother with dutiful politeness. He explained that he and Fanny had been in London for two days.

"I meant to call on you yesterday," he said, "but we were obliged to take Harry to see the wild beasts at Exeter Exchange, and afterward, we were engaged with Mrs. Ferrars. Harry was delighted. This morning, I fully intended to visit, but one finds so many demands upon one’s time upon arriving in town. I am here to order a seal for Fanny. But tomorrow, I shall certainly call in Berkeley Street and be introduced to your friend Mrs. Jennings. She is a woman of good fortune, I understand? And the Middletons—you must introduce me to them as well. As my mother-in-law’s relations, I should pay them every respect. They are excellent neighbors to you in the country, are they not?"

"Indeed," Elinor replied, "their kindness has been more than we could have hoped for."

"I am very glad to hear it," he said warmly. "And so you are comfortably settled at Barton Cottage? Edward gave us a charming account of it—the most delightful little place, he said. It is a great satisfaction to know you are content."

Elinor felt a flicker of discomfort at his words but was spared further response by the arrival of Mrs. Jennings’s servant, summoning them to the carriage. John escorted them downstairs, exchanged polite introductions with Mrs. Jennings, and took his leave with promises of visiting the next day.

True to his word, he called—though without Fanny, who, he claimed, was too occupied with her mother to join him. Mrs. Jennings, ever sociable, assured him she would soon call upon Fanny herself and bring the Dashwood sisters along. His manner to them was calm but kind; to Mrs. Jennings, he was all civility. When Colonel Brandon arrived, John studied him with keen interest, as if measuring his worth.

After half an hour, he asked Elinor to walk with him to Conduit Street to introduce him to Sir John and Lady Middleton. The day was fine, and she agreed.

Once they were alone, his questions began in earnest.

"Who is Colonel Brandon? A man of fortune?"

"Yes, he has a very good estate in Dorsetshire."

"Excellent. He seems a gentleman of taste. Elinor, I believe I may congratulate you on the prospect of a most respectable establishment."

She stared at him. "What can you mean?"

"He admires you. I observed it plainly. What is his income?"

"About two thousand a year."

"Two thousand! Well, I heartily wish it were twice as much for your sake."

Elinor shook her head. "You are mistaken. Colonel Brandon has no intention of marrying me."

"Nonsense. A little encouragement on your part would secure him. Your fortune may give him pause, but with proper attention, he could be persuaded. And there can be no prior attachment to hinder it—you are too sensible for that. This is a match that would please everyone." He lowered his voice. "Fanny and Mrs. Ferrars would be most gratified."

Elinor refused to dignify his hints with a reply.

He pressed on. "Would it not be something if Fanny’s brother and my sister were to settle at the same time? Is there any news of Edward Ferrars’s marriage?"

Elinor steadied herself. "Is he engaged?"

"Not yet settled, but it is in motion. Mrs. Ferrars has promised to settle a thousand a year on him if he marries Miss Morton—Lord Morton’s daughter, with thirty thousand pounds. A most desirable match. And Mrs. Ferrars has been exceedingly generous to us as well—just the other day, she gave Fanny two hundred pounds."

Elinor forced a polite response. "Your expenses must be considerable, but your income is substantial."

"Not as large as some think. The enclosure of Norland Common has been costly, and I recently purchased East Kingham Farm—you remember it, near our property. I could not let it pass into other hands. But between that and refurnishing Norland after your mother removed your father’s effects, we have had heavy expenses. Mrs. Ferrars’s kindness has been most welcome."

Elinor said nothing.

"And then there is Fanny’s greenhouse—not yet begun—and the flower garden only planned. But in time, all will be well."

She suppressed her disapproval, grateful Marianne was not present to hear such talk.

Having sufficiently impressed upon her his financial burdens (and thus excusing himself from any future generosity), his mood brightened, and he turned to praising Mrs. Jennings.

"She is a woman of excellent income, and her favor could be of great advantage to you. When she passes, she may well remember you in her will."

Elinor disagreed. "She has daughters of her own."

"But they are well married. You, however, have been singled out. Such attention creates expectations."

"You imagine far more than exists."

He sighed. "Well, people have little power over such things. But tell me—Marianne looks unwell. Is she ill?"

"She has been unsteady for some weeks."

"A pity. Illness at her age steals one’s bloom forever. She was so handsome last autumn—just the sort of beauty to attract a man. Fanny always said she would marry before you, and better. But now? I doubt she will secure more than five or six hundred a year. You, however—Dorsetshire! I know little of it, but I shall be glad to learn more. Fanny and I will be among your first visitors, I assure you."

Elinor attempted once more to dissuade him from his notions about Colonel Brandon, but he clung to the idea with stubborn delight, already envisioning the advantages of the connection.

They found Lady Middleton at home, and Sir John joined them shortly after. Pleasantries were exchanged; Sir John, ever affable, approved of Mr. Dashwood at once, and Lady Middleton, struck by his appearance, deemed him a worthy acquaintance.

As they walked back, John was triumphant. "What a charming account I shall give Fanny! Lady Middleton is elegance itself—exactly the sort of woman Fanny will admire. And Mrs. Jennings, though not so refined, is perfectly respectable. Marianne need no longer hesitate to associate with them. I shall reassure Fanny that they are precisely the sort of people we would wish to know."

Elinor said nothing, her thoughts her own.

Mrs. John Dashwood, ever deferential to her husband’s discernment, paid her respects to Mrs. Jennings and Lady Middleton the very next day. To her satisfaction, she found even the former—despite being hostess to her sisters—not entirely beneath her notice. As for Lady Middleton, she was nothing short of enchanting. The admiration was mutual; both women shared a polished, if somewhat frigid, propriety that drew them together, united by a refined indifference to anything beyond their own concerns.

Yet while Lady Middleton found Mrs. John Dashwood perfectly agreeable, Mrs. Jennings was less impressed. To her, Fanny Dashwood seemed a proud, stiff creature, offering little warmth to her husband’s sisters—scarcely speaking to them during her brief visit. Elinor, though burning to inquire after Edward, would not lower herself to ask. Fanny, for her part, had no intention of mentioning his name unless she could announce his engagement to Miss Morton—or at least until Colonel Brandon’s fortune was secured for her husband. She still believed Edward and Elinor dangerously attached and resolved to keep them as far apart as possible.

But news, withheld by Fanny, soon arrived from another quarter. Lucy Steele appeared at Bartlett’s Buildings, lamenting that Edward, though in London with the Dashwoods, dared not visit her for fear of discovery. Their only solace was correspondence—agonizing for two so eager to meet. Edward, however, had not forgotten Elinor. Twice he called in Berkeley Street, leaving his card behind—a small but telling gesture. Elinor was glad he had come, and gladder still to have missed him.

Meanwhile, the Dashwoods, charmed by the Middletons, extended an invitation to dine in Harley Street—an unusual gesture for a couple not known for hospitality. The Miss Dashwoods and Mrs. Jennings were included, as was Colonel Brandon, whose quiet devotion to Elinor made him an easy conquest for John Dashwood’s eager civilities. The prospect of meeting Mrs. Ferrars piqued Elinor’s curiosity, though whether Edward would attend remained uncertain. Her earlier dread of facing his mother had faded, but her interest in the woman herself had not.

That interest sharpened—unpleasantly—when she learned the Miss Steeles were also invited. Their relentless flattery had won Lady Middleton’s favor, and though Lucy lacked elegance and her sister any semblance of refinement, they were now to be guests in Conduit Street. For Lucy, this was a triumph; she had long desired closer acquaintance with the Dashwoods, the better to assess her rivals and secure her position. Elinor, however, felt only dread. To see Edward—living under his mother’s roof—forced into company with Lucy, after all that had passed? The thought was unbearable.

Her fears, though acute, proved unfounded. Lucy, with malicious satisfaction, informed her that Edward would not attend—claiming his absence as proof of his devotion to *her*. Elinor, though relieved, could not suppress a pang at the girl’s gloating.

The fateful evening arrived. As they ascended the stairs, Lucy clutched Elinor’s arm. *"Pity me, dear Miss Dashwood! I can scarcely stand—soon I shall meet the woman who holds my future in her hands!"*

Elinor might have pointed out that Mrs. Ferrars was equally likely to be Miss Morton’s mother, but she merely offered a polite sympathy that left Lucy baffled.

Mrs. Ferrars was a small, rigid woman, her sallow face set in lines of perpetual displeasure. She spoke little, and not a word was wasted on Elinor, whom she surveyed with open disdain. The contrast with her effusive attention to Lucy—whom she would have despised had she known the truth—was almost comical. Elinor, no longer wounded by such slights, watched with detached amusement as the Steeles fawned over their benefactress.

Dinner was a lavish affair, though conversation lagged. The men spoke of politics and horses; the ladies, once retired to the drawing-room, could muster no topic beyond the comparative heights of Lady Middleton’s son and young Harry Dashwood—a debate sustained entirely by maternal pride and sheer repetition.

Elinor’s painted screens, newly displayed, became an unexpected focal point. John Dashwood, eager to impress Colonel Brandon, presented them as proof of his sister’s talent. The Colonel admired them warmly, prompting their circulation among the guests. Mrs. Ferrars, unaware of their creator, inspected them indifferently before dismissing them with a curt *"Very pretty."*

Fanny, torn between appeasing her mother and defending her own civility, ventured, *"They are lovely, ma’am—are they not? Almost like Miss Morton’s work!"*

This provoked Marianne beyond endurance. *"What is Miss Morton to us?"* she cried, snatching the screens from Fanny’s hands. *"It is Elinor we admire!"*

Mrs. Ferrars stiffened. *"Miss Morton is Lord Morton’s daughter."*

The room froze. Fanny flushed; John Dashwood paled. Elinor, though pained by Marianne’s outburst, was more wounded by the cruelty that had provoked it. Only Colonel Brandon, his gaze fixed on Marianne, seemed to understand the love behind her defiance.

Marianne, overwhelmed, flung her arms around Elinor. *"Do not let them make you unhappy!"* she whispered, before tears overcame her.

The scene drew immediate concern—Brandon half-rose, Mrs. Jennings produced her salts, and Sir John, indignantly whispering to Lucy, relayed the whole affair. Marianne soon recovered, but the evening’s tension lingered.

Later, John Dashwood murmured to the Colonel, *"Poor Marianne! Her nerves are not so strong as Elinor’s. And you would not believe it, but she was quite the beauty a few months ago—every bit as handsome as her sister. Now, alas, it is all gone."*

The Colonel said nothing. But his eyes, resting on Marianne, held a different opinion entirely.

Elinor’s curiosity about Mrs. Ferrars had been satisfied—and with it, all hope of a cordial connection between their families. The woman’s pride, her pettiness, and her pointed disdain for Elinor herself had laid bare the impossibility of any understanding. Had Edward been free, these very qualities would have poisoned their engagement. Instead, Elinor found herself perversely grateful for the greater obstacle that spared her further entanglement with such a woman—one whose whims she need neither fear nor court.

Lucy, however, saw none of this. Flushed with triumph, she could speak of nothing but Mrs. Ferrars’s condescension, her warmth, her *particular* attentions. That very morning, she had sought Elinor out—eyes bright, voice trembling with excitement—to relive every moment of the encounter.

*“You saw it, did you not?”* Lucy pressed. *“How she singled me out! Oh, I knew she would approve—but such kindness! And your sister, too—so gracious!”*

Elinor, struggling for composure, murmured something polite. But Lucy would not be checked.

*“You think it mere civility? No, no—it was more! She *likes* me. And why should she not?”*

Elinor could not bring herself to point out the obvious: that Mrs. Ferrars’s favor was built on ignorance. Lucy, blind to the danger, mistook indifference for approval, and a rival’s absence for her own triumph.

Then Edward arrived.

The air thickened at once. He halted in the doorway, as startled as they were. Lucy’s smile turned brittle; Elinor’s greeting, though measured, carried a tremor. Only Marianne, entering moments later, was unrestrained—clasping Edward’s hands, her joy unchecked.

His discomfort was palpable. Lucy watched, sharp-eyed, as Elinor steered the conversation—asking after his health, his plans—anything to fill the silence. Marianne, oblivious, spoke of returning to Barton and insisted he accompany them. Edward’s reply was a stammer.

Lucy’s voice cut through. *“Men are so careless with their engagements, are they not?”*

Marianne dismissed the jab with a wave. *“Not Edward. His conscience binds him tighter than chains.”* The words, meant as praise, struck like a blow. Edward flinched.

He left soon after—too soon for Marianne, who frowned at his retreating back. *“Why must he go? And why does *she* linger?”* she muttered, casting a dark glance at Lucy.

Elinor said nothing. The truth, heavy and unspoken, hung between them all.

The news spread quickly through the society papers—Mrs. Charlotte Palmer, wife of Thomas Palmer, Esq., had been safely delivered of a son and heir. For those already acquainted with the happy event, it was a matter of satisfaction; for Mrs. Jennings, it was nothing short of triumph. Her days now revolved around her daughter, and she bustled to Charlotte’s side each morning, not returning until evening.

This left the Miss Dashwoods to the mercy of the Middletons, who insisted they spend their days in Conduit Street. Elinor and Marianne would have preferred the quiet of Mrs. Jennings’s home, but refusing would have been ungracious. Thus, they resigned themselves to the company of Lady Middleton and the Miss Steeles—neither of whom truly desired their presence.

Lady Middleton, though outwardly civil, found the Dashwood sisters tiresome. They did not indulge her vanity nor flatter her children, and their fondness for reading struck her as dangerously intellectual. Lucy Steele, meanwhile, regarded them with thinly veiled resentment, fearing they might steal what little affection she had worked so hard to secure. Only Miss Steele, the younger and more foolish of the two, might have been won over—had either sister satisfied her insatiable curiosity about Marianne’s ill-fated romance with Willoughby. But Elinor offered only polite indifference, and Marianne, sharp disgust.

Mrs. Jennings, blissfully unaware of these undercurrents, rejoiced in the arrangement, often congratulating the girls on escaping her “dull company.” She flitted between Sir John’s home and her own, always in high spirits, regaling anyone who would listen—usually Miss Steele—with exhaustive details of Charlotte’s maternal progress. Her only vexation was Mr. Palmer’s refusal to acknowledge his son’s exceptional qualities, insisting the child was no different from any other.

——

A misfortune soon befell Fanny Dashwood—one born of idle gossip and hasty assumptions. During a visit from her sisters and Mrs. Jennings, another lady called and, upon hearing the Miss Dashwoods introduced, mistakenly assumed they were residing in Harley Street. The error led to an invitation—extended not only to John and Fanny but to Elinor and Marianne as well.

Fanny was appalled. To host her sisters-in-law was bad enough, but to do so under the illusion of hospitality was insufferable. She had no choice, however, but to send her carriage for them, enduring the indignity of appearing attentive. Worse still, they might expect further invitations!

Marianne, now resigned to the tedium of society, dressed mechanically for the evening’s musical party, indifferent to her appearance. Miss Steele, by contrast, scrutinized every detail of her attire—calculating its cost, admiring its make, and concluding, as always, with a condescending compliment on how very *smart* Marianne looked.

The party itself was unremarkable—filled with those who adored music and those who pretended to. Elinor, uninterested in the performances, let her gaze wander until it settled on a familiar figure: the same man who had once lectured her on toothpick cases at Gray’s. He soon caught her eye, and with a flourish, her brother introduced him—Mr. Robert Ferrars.

His manner was all easy confidence, his bow a masterpiece of self-satisfaction. Had Elinor’s regard for Edward depended on his family, Robert’s absurdity might have shattered it. But she could not blame the elder brother for the younger’s vanity—especially when Robert himself so obligingly explained the difference between them.

“It was my mother’s error,” he declared, lamenting Edward’s lack of polish. “Had he been sent to Westminster, as I was, he would have been fit for society. But private education left him hopelessly awkward.”

Elinor did not argue, though she privately doubted any school could have improved Robert’s self-importance. He prattled on, extolling the virtues of country cottages (despite never having lived in one) and advising imaginary builders on their merits. She indulged him with polite agreement, sparing herself the trouble of contradiction.

——

Later that evening, John Dashwood was struck by an idea—one he proposed to Fanny upon their return. Since Mrs. Jennings was often absent, why not invite his sisters to stay with them properly? It would ease his conscience (and fulfill his long-neglected promise to his father).

Fanny was aghast. “Lady Middleton expects them daily! And besides,” she added hastily, “I had already resolved to invite the Miss Steeles. Their uncle was so kind to Edward—it would be ungrateful not to show them attention.”

John, ever swayed, acquiesced. The Miss Steeles would come; his sisters could wait. Fanny, relieved, wrote to Lucy the next morning.

For Lucy, the invitation was a triumph. Here was Mrs. Dashwood herself advancing her cause! She accepted at once, her visit to Lady Middleton abruptly shortened.

When Elinor saw the note, a cold understanding settled over her. Lucy’s foothold in the family was firmer than she had thought. Lady Middleton was already won; Fanny Dashwood’s defenses were crumbling. And if Lucy’s influence grew—what then?

The Miss Steeles took up residence in Harley Street, and every report that reached Elinor only confirmed her fears. Sir John praised their charm; Fanny doted on them. Lucy was called by her Christian name, gifted trinkets, treated as a favorite.

The path to Edward seemed clearer than ever—for Lucy.

Mrs. Palmer’s recovery was swift, and within a fortnight, her mother no longer felt obliged to devote every moment to her care. Satisfied with visiting once or twice daily, Mrs. Jennings resumed her usual routines, returning to her own home—and her own habits—much to the relief of the Miss Dashwoods, who were eager to reclaim their former independence.

One morning, shortly after their return to Berkeley Street, Mrs. Jennings burst into the drawing-room where Elinor sat alone. Her hurried, animated manner suggested news of great importance.

“Good heavens, my dear Miss Dashwood!” she exclaimed. “Have you heard?”

“No, ma’am. What is it?”

“The most extraordinary news! But let me tell you properly.” She settled herself with dramatic flair. “When I arrived at the Palmers’, Charlotte was in a state over the child—convinced it was gravely ill. The poor thing was covered in spots! Well, I took one look and said, ‘My dear, it’s nothing but the red gum,’ and the nurse agreed. But Charlotte wouldn’t be satisfied until Mr. Donavan was summoned. Fortunately, he was just returning from Harley Street and came at once. He confirmed it was only the red gum, and Charlotte finally relaxed. But just as he was leaving—I can’t imagine what possessed me—I asked if there was any news. And then, my dear, he smirked, hesitated, and finally whispered, ‘To spare the young ladies under your care any alarm regarding their sister’s indisposition, I assure you there is no cause for concern. Mrs. Dashwood will recover.’”

Elinor’s composure wavered. “Fanny is ill?”

“That was precisely my reaction! ‘Lord!’ I cried, ‘is Mrs. Dashwood unwell?’ And then the whole story came out. The long and short of it is this: Mr. Edward Ferrars—yes, the very man I once teased you about (though thank heaven nothing came of it!)—has been secretly engaged to my cousin Lucy for over a year! Can you credit it? Not a soul suspected, not even Nancy! Oh, they’re well-matched enough, but to carry on so privately—it’s shocking! I’ve never seen them together, or I should have guessed at once.

“Well, the secret might have held if not for poor Nancy, who hasn’t a deceitful bone in her body. She thought, ‘Surely the family will rejoice in Lucy’s happiness!’ and promptly confided in Fanny—who was, at that very moment, prattling to your brother about securing Edward a match with some lord’s daughter. You can imagine the scene. Fanny flew into hysterics, shrieking loud enough to bring your brother running. Lucy arrived soon after, entirely unprepared for the storm. Poor girl! She bore the brunt of Fanny’s fury, fainted clean away, and Nancy dissolved into tears. Your brother was at a loss—pleading one moment, kneeling the next—while Fanny declared the pair must quit the house at once. Mr. Donavan found them in chaos, the carriage already waiting. Lucy could scarcely walk for distress, and Nancy was little better.

“I’ve no patience with Fanny. If Edward and Lucy marry despite her, I shall rejoice! And as for Edward—he’ll be beside himself when he learns how his beloved has been treated. Mr. Donavan and I agreed: the whole affair has been dreadfully mismanaged. Mrs. Ferrars was summoned at once, no doubt for another theatrical display. But I’ve no sympathy for her either. What does fortune matter when hearts are at stake? Edward and Lucy could live comfortably on five hundred a year—why, they’d manage as well as anyone!”

Elinor listened with practiced calm, though her thoughts churned. She was grateful that Mrs. Jennings no longer suspected her attachment to Edward—and relieved Marianne was absent, sparing her the immediate distress of the revelation.

Yet the task of informing her sister could not be delayed. The news would shatter Marianne’s romantic ideals, forcing her to confront the frailty of love and loyalty. Elinor steeled herself. She would speak plainly, sparing no detail, though it pained her to dismantle Marianne’s illusions.

When the moment came, Marianne’s reaction was as fervent as expected. She wept, raged, and—most painfully—accused Elinor of indifference.

“Four months!” Marianne cried. “You knew for four months and said nothing?”

“I was bound by promise,” Elinor replied gently. “And I would not burden you while you grieved your own disappointments.”

“But you loved him!”

“I did. Yet love does not exempt us from duty or compassion. Edward is not wicked—only imprudent. Lucy is clever enough to make him happy in time.”

Marianne recoiled. “How can you speak so coolly of such a betrayal?”

“Because I have had time to reconcile myself to the truth. My pain was real, Marianne, but so is my acceptance. Edward will marry Lucy. Resentment will not change it.”

Her sister’s anguish softened into remorse. “And I called you heartless! When all this time, you bore your sorrow alone—”

Elinor embraced her. “We must both look forward now.”

Marianne vowed silence, though her indignation simmered. When Mrs. Jennings later expounded on Edward’s folly, she clenched her fists but held her tongue. Even John Dashwood’s pompous recounting of the Ferrars family’s outrage—how Edward had been disowned, his inheritance stripped—could not provoke her.

“Two thousand pounds is a pittance!” John lamented. “Had he obeyed his mother, he might have had twenty times that. A tragic waste.”

Mrs. Jennings snorted. “If he comes to me, he shan’t want for a meal or a roof!”

Elinor’s gratitude for her kindness was tinged with melancholy. Edward’s future was bleak, yet his honor remained intact. That, at least, was worth preserving.

As the room buzzed with speculation, Elinor sat quietly, her face a mask of composure. Inside, her heart ached—not for the loss of Edward, but for the cruel twists of fate that dictated happiness. Love, it seemed, was not always enough.

Mrs. Jennings was effusive in her admiration of Edward’s honorable behavior, though only Elinor and Marianne truly grasped the depth of his sacrifice. To them alone was it clear how little temptation had been placed before him to defy his family, and how meager the consolation he could claim beyond the quiet satisfaction of having done what was right—losing both fortune and favor for the sake of principle. Elinor’s pride in his integrity was fierce, while Marianne, softened by pity, forgave every past offense.

Yet though their mutual confidence had been restored by Edward’s public vindication, neither sister found it easy to dwell on the subject when alone. Elinor avoided it deliberately, wary of Marianne’s impassioned declarations that Edward’s heart remained hers—a belief she would rather dispel than encourage. For her part, Marianne’s courage faltered whenever the topic arose, for it only sharpened the painful contrast between her own impulsiveness and Elinor’s steadfastness. The comparison weighed heavily upon her, but not as Elinor had hoped—not as a spur to action, but as a source of unrelenting regret. She lamented her past idleness bitterly, yet the thought of exertion now seemed beyond her, leaving her spirit more defeated than ever.

Days passed without further news from Harley Street or Bartlett’s Buildings. Though Mrs. Jennings had ample material for gossip already, she was determined to pay her cousins a visit of sympathy and inquiry. Only the press of other engagements delayed her.

On the third day, a Sunday so unseasonably fine that it drew crowds to Kensington Gardens despite the early March chill, Mrs. Jennings and Elinor joined the promenade. Marianne, however, remained at home, unwilling to risk an encounter with the Willoughbys, whom she knew to be in London.

Soon after their arrival, an acquaintance of Mrs. Jennings’s joined them, monopolizing her attention and leaving Elinor to her thoughts. She observed neither Willoughby nor Edward among the visitors, nor anyone else of particular interest—until, to her surprise, Miss Steele approached with a timid air, expressing great pleasure at the meeting. Encouraged by Mrs. Jennings’s warmth, she detached herself briefly from her own party to converse.

"Ask her everything, my dear," Mrs. Jennings murmured to Elinor before turning back to her companion. "She’ll tell you all if you press her."

Yet Miss Steele required no prompting. "I am so glad to see you!" she exclaimed, seizing Elinor’s arm. "I’ve been longing to speak with you. Tell me—has Mrs. Jennings heard? Is she very angry?"

"Not with you, I believe," Elinor replied.

"Oh, thank heaven! And Lady Middleton?"

"I doubt she gives it much thought."

Miss Steele sighed in relief. "What a fright I’ve been in! Lucy was in such a temper—vowed she’d never lift a finger for me again. But see!" She gestured to her bonnet. "She trimmed this for me last night. You mustn’t laugh—I know pink ribbons are the Doctor’s favorite, but how was I to know unless he’d said so? My cousins tease me mercilessly."

Elinor, with little interest in ribbons, steered the conversation back. "You mentioned Mr. Ferrars—"

"Oh! That’s the worst of it," Miss Steele cut in. "People are saying he refused Lucy, but it’s all lies. Why, only this morning he came to us straight after church and swore he’d have no one but her! He’d been tormented, you see—his mother summoned him, railed at him, but he stood firm. Then he rode off to clear his head, hid at some inn for days, thinking it over. At last he told Lucy that with no fortune left, he couldn’t bear to bind her to poverty. ‘Release me if you wish,’ he said, ‘for I’ve nothing to offer.’ But she wouldn’t hear of it—declared she’d live on nothing if she must. So they’re to wait, and he’ll take orders, and pray for a living." She giggled. "My cousins will say I ought to write the Doctor to give him one!"

Elinor’s disapproval sharpened. "You listened to this?"

"Oh, one must listen at doors sometimes! Lucy’s done the same by me."

Elinor changed the subject, but Miss Steele soon circled back. "Edward means to go to Oxford soon—lodging in Pall Mall now. His mother’s a hateful woman! And your brother and sister—but I shan’t speak ill of them to you. They did send us home in their carriage, after all." She prattled on until her party reclaimed her, leaving Elinor with little new insight. Edward’s course was set, his marriage to Lucy inevitable—but the means to achieve it remained as uncertain as ever.

Mrs. Jennings, upon their return to the carriage, was ravenous for details. Elinor relayed only the barest facts, omitting what had been improperly gleaned.

"Wait for a living!" Mrs. Jennings scoffed. "They’ll wait a year, then settle for a curacy and starve on fifty pounds a year. I’ll have to help furnish their house—two maids indeed! They’ll need a sturdy girl to manage everything."

The next morning brought a letter from Lucy herself, effusive with gratitude and hope, begging Elinor’s and Mrs. Jennings’s influence in securing Edward’s advancement.

Elinor handed it at once to Mrs. Jennings, who read it aloud with delight. "How prettily she writes! Just like her to release him if he wished—but of course she wouldn’t. Oh, I’d give anything to find him a living! She’s a dear girl—so thoughtful. Yes, I’ll call on her soon."

The letter, Elinor reflected, was artful in its flattery—but it changed nothing. The path ahead was as narrow and uncertain as it had ever been.

The Miss Dashwoods had now resided in London for over two months, and Marianne’s restlessness grew with each passing day. She longed for the open skies, the freedom, and the tranquility of the countryside, convinced that only Barton could soothe her spirits. Elinor, though equally eager to depart, tempered her impatience with practicality, mindful of the challenges of such a journey—concerns Marianne dismissed entirely.

Yet Elinor had already begun making arrangements, their intentions delicately conveyed to their gracious hostess, who protested their departure with warm insistence. Then came an unexpected proposal, one that promised both delay and greater comfort: the Palmers were to remove to Cleveland at the end of March for the Easter holidays, and Mrs. Jennings, along with her young companions, received an earnest invitation from Charlotte to join them.

Though Elinor hesitated at first—such an imposition would ordinarily have been unthinkable—Mr. Palmer’s sincere courtesy, coupled with his markedly improved demeanor since Marianne’s distress had become known, persuaded her to accept.

When she relayed the plan to Marianne, however, the reaction was far from favorable.

"Cleveland!" Marianne cried, visibly agitated. "No, I cannot go there."

"You forget," Elinor said gently, "its location is not—it is not near—"

"But it is in Somersetshire!" Marianne interrupted. "I cannot return to Somersetshire. Not there, where I had once hoped to—" She broke off, shaking her head. "No, Elinor, you must not ask it of me."

Elinor did not press the matter, instead appealing to Marianne’s deeper affections. She framed the visit as a practical step toward their eventual return to Barton—a way to shorten the journey, as Cleveland lay but a day’s ride from Bristol. Their mother’s servant could meet them there, and within a fortnight, they might be home.

Marianne’s love for her mother was steadfast, and this reasoning, at last, softened her resistance.

Mrs. Jennings, far from wearying of their company, entreated them to return with her from Cleveland—a kindness Elinor appreciated, though it did not alter their plans. With their mother’s approval secured, arrangements were soon in place, and Marianne found solace in counting the hours until their departure.

Mrs. Jennings, ever observant, could not help but note the Colonel’s attentions to Elinor during their final days. When he called upon them after their plans were settled, she lamented, "Colonel, what shall we do without the Miss Dashwoods? They insist on leaving us for the Palmers, and we shall be dreadfully dull without them!"

Her theatrics were perhaps designed to provoke a declaration, for she watched with keen interest as the Colonel later drew Elinor aside near the window. Though Marianne’s playing prevented her from overhearing, she could not mistake Elinor’s flushed cheeks and distracted manner.

When fragments of their conversation reached her—something of houses and inconveniences—she nearly exclaimed aloud at the Colonel’s unromantic pragmatism. Yet Elinor’s parting words, spoken with unmistakable warmth, reassured her: "I shall always think myself very much obliged to you."

What Mrs. Jennings did not hear was the true nature of their exchange. The Colonel, with quiet compassion, had spoken of Edward Ferrars’s misfortunes—his family’s harsh rejection for his steadfast commitment to Lucy Steele.

"I understand he intends to take orders," the Colonel said. "If he wishes it, the living of Delaford is his. It is modest, but it may offer him some independence."

Elinor was stunned. This act of generosity, so unexpected, moved her deeply. She thanked him with genuine emotion, though she hesitated to be the one to deliver such news to Edward. Yet the Colonel, deferring to propriety, insisted—and so she agreed.

When he expressed regret that the living was too small to support a family, Elinor, ever practical, replied, "Its size is no disadvantage if it suits his means."

The Colonel, surprised, realized she assumed marriage was Edward’s immediate prospect. "I fear it cannot provide for a wife," he admitted. "His happiness in that regard must wait."

These words, overheard but misconstrued, had led Mrs. Jennings to assume the worst of his intentions. Yet the truth was far nobler—a kindness extended not for show, but from genuine benevolence.

And so, with gratitude and quiet understanding, the matter was settled.

Mrs. Jennings turned to Elinor the moment Colonel Brandon had taken his leave, her eyes bright with knowing delight. "Well, Miss Dashwood, I shan’t pretend ignorance—though I made a valiant effort not to eavesdrop, one can’t help catching a word or two. And let me say, I’ve seldom been more pleased in all my days! You have my sincerest congratulations, my dear."

Elinor’s cheeks warmed, though she kept her composure. "You are very kind, ma’am. It is indeed a most generous offer, and I am deeply sensible of Colonel Brandon’s goodness. Such compassion is rare—I confess I was quite astonished."

"Modesty becomes you, but really, my dear, I’ve thought it likely for some time now. A man of his disposition doesn’t act without purpose."

"You anticipated his kindness, perhaps, but surely not the immediacy of the opportunity?"

"Opportunity!" Mrs. Jennings laughed. "When a man sets his mind to something, opportunity follows. But enough—I shan’t keep you any longer. Only think, soon there’ll be no happier pair in all of England!"

Elinor smiled faintly. "I suppose you mean to visit them at Delaford?"

"Indeed I shall! And as for the house being unfit, I cannot fathom his meaning—it’s as fine a parsonage as any I’ve seen."

"He mentioned repairs."

"Well, whose place is it to see to them, if not his own?"

Their conversation was cut short by the arrival of the carriage. Mrs. Jennings rose briskly. "I’ve scarcely said half I wished, but no matter—we’ll finish our talk this evening. You’ll want to share the news with Marianne, no doubt. And I daresay you’ve little desire for company just now."

Marianne had already withdrawn, leaving Elinor to her thoughts. "I will tell her, of course," Elinor said, "but for the present, I would rather no one else knew."

Mrs. Jennings looked slightly put out. "Not even Lucy? I had thought to call on her today."

"Not yet, if you please. I must write to Mr. Ferrars first—there are matters he must attend to without delay."

For a moment, Mrs. Jennings looked puzzled. Then her face cleared. "Ah! Mr. Ferrars is to be the man, is he? Well, that is good news indeed! Though I must say, oughtn’t the Colonel to write himself?"

Elinor, unsure of her meaning but unwilling to inquire further, replied only to the latter remark. "Colonel Brandon preferred that another should convey his intentions. He is of a delicate mind in such matters."

"How very odd! But there—I shan’t keep you from your letter." She turned to go, then paused. "Oh! I’ve just thought of Betty’s sister—she might make a fine lady’s maid, if she suits. But you’ll consider that in good time."

Elinor, barely attending, murmured assent. Once alone, she took up her pen, her thoughts racing. How to phrase her letter to Edward? The situation demanded both delicacy and clarity, and she hesitated over each word—until Edward himself entered, startling her utterly.

Mrs. Jennings, meeting him at the door, had insisted he come up, claiming Elinor had pressing business with him. Now here he stood, and Elinor’s carefully composed letter was rendered unnecessary.

His appearance flustered her. She had not seen him since his engagement had become public knowledge—nor since he had learned she knew of it. The weight of her news and the memory of their last parting made the moment painfully awkward.

Edward, too, seemed ill at ease. He apologized stiffly for intruding. "Mrs. Jennings led me to believe you wished to speak with me—otherwise I should not have presumed. Though I could not leave London without seeing you and your sister."

"You need not have gone without our good wishes," Elinor said, rallying. "But since you are here, I may deliver them in person. Colonel Brandon was with me not half an hour ago. He has asked me to inform you that—knowing you intend to take orders—he wishes to offer you the living of Delaford. It is not as valuable as he might wish, but it is a mark of his esteem."

Edward’s astonishment was plain. "Colonel Brandon offers *me* a living?"

"Yes. He acts out of concern for your present difficulties—and from respect for your character."

Edward was silent a long moment. "I cannot account for such kindness—except that it must come through you."

Elinor shook her head. "You mistake. The offer is entirely his own. I had no hand in it."

Still, Edward seemed unconvinced. At last, he spoke quietly. "He is a man of excellent reputation. Your brother thinks highly of him."

"He is all that is honorable," Elinor agreed. "And as you will be neighbors, it is all the better."

Edward’s expression darkened briefly, but he said nothing. Rising, he asked for Colonel Brandon’s direction. "I must thank him—since you will not allow me to thank *you*."

Elinor did not detain him. They parted with mutual wishes for happiness—hers earnest, his halting.

As the door closed, Elinor sighed. The next time she saw Edward, he would be Lucy’s husband. The thought was bittersweet.

When Mrs. Jennings returned, her curiosity was undiminished. "Well? Did he accept?"

"Without hesitation."

"And when shall he take orders?"

"In a few months, I imagine."

"Months!" Mrs. Jennings exclaimed. "The Colonel won’t wait so long! Surely another candidate could be found."

Elinor laughed. "The Colonel’s sole aim is to assist Mr. Ferrars."

Mrs. Jennings stared. "You don’t mean he’s marrying you just to give Edward a living?"

The misunderstanding was swiftly corrected, much to Mrs. Jennings’ amusement. "Well! The parsonage may be small, but it’s a fine house. We must urge the Colonel to make it fit for Lucy before she settles there."

Elinor agreed—privately certain that Lucy would not wait for improvements.

Edward’s gratitude to Colonel Brandon was boundless, and his joy overflowed as he hurried to Bartlett’s Buildings to share the news with Lucy. When Mrs. Jennings paid another visit the following day, Lucy declared—with sparkling eyes—that she had never seen Edward in such high spirits. Her own delight was equally undeniable, and she eagerly joined Mrs. Jennings in anticipating their cozy future at Delaford Parsonage by Michaelmas.

Far from begrudging Elinor any credit, Lucy spoke of her with warm gratitude, insisting that no kindness from Miss Dashwood could ever surprise her. As for Colonel Brandon, Lucy was ready to revere him as a saint—and to ensure he was treated as one in all practical matters. She resolved, privately, to make liberal use of his household comforts at Delaford—his servants, his carriage, even his cows and poultry.

Meanwhile, more than a week had passed since John Dashwood’s last visit to Berkeley Street. With only a cursory inquiry after his wife’s health, Elinor felt obliged to call on Fanny, though the prospect was disagreeable. Neither Marianne nor Mrs. Jennings would accompany her—Marianne vehemently opposed the visit, and Mrs. Jennings, though her carriage was at Elinor’s disposal, could not stomach another encounter with Fanny.

Thus, Elinor went alone, bracing herself for an awkward tête-à-tête. Fanny was not receiving visitors, but as Elinor’s carriage prepared to depart, John himself emerged. He greeted her with unexpected warmth, insisting she come inside, as Fanny would be delighted to see her.

The drawing room was empty. “Fanny must be in her chamber,” John remarked. “But I’ve much to discuss with you. This business of Colonel Brandon’s living—can it be true? Has he really given it to Edward?”

“It is true,” Elinor confirmed.

John was astounded. “No relation between them! And with livings so valuable—what is it worth?”

“Two hundred a year.”

“Extraordinary! Why, the next presentation alone could have fetched fourteen hundred pounds! How could Brandon be so careless? Unless—” He brightened. “Ah! Edward must merely hold it until the true purchaser comes of age.”

Elinor firmly corrected him, recounting her own role in conveying the offer. John was forced to concede, though he marveled at the colonel’s motives.

“To be of use to Edward,” Elinor said simply.

“Well, Edward is a fortunate man. But pray, do not mention it to Fanny. She bears it tolerably, but it would vex her to hear it discussed.”

Elinor suppressed a sharp retort—Fanny’s composure, she thought, should hardly falter at her brother’s good fortune, which cost her nothing.

John lowered his voice. “Mrs. Ferrars knows nothing yet. Best to keep it from her as long as possible. When the marriage occurs, she must hear of it—though it will distress her terribly.”

“Distress her?” Elinor could not hide her skepticism. “After casting Edward off so utterly, can she still claim any motherly feeling?”

“Ah, Elinor,” John sighed, “you underestimate a mother’s heart. She will grieve as if she had never disowned him. Any haste toward this marriage must be concealed.”

Elinor was silent, unconvinced.

After a pause, John shifted topics. “We now consider Robert marrying Miss Morton.”

Elinor arched a brow. “Does the lady have any choice in the matter?”

“Choice? Why, Robert is effectively the eldest son now. And both brothers are agreeable—neither has an advantage.”

Elinor said no more, and John soon resumed, lowering his voice conspiratorially. “I have it on the best authority—Fanny herself—that Mrs. Ferrars would have preferred even your connection to Edward’s current entanglement. ‘The lesser evil,’ she called it. But of course, that is all past.”

Elinor’s composure wavered, but she was spared further conversation by Robert Ferrars’ arrival. His easy arrogance, basking in his undeserved favor, only deepened her disdain.

Robert, too, had heard of the living and questioned Elinor eagerly. When she repeated the details, he burst into laughter. The image of Edward as a clergyman, he declared, was absurd beyond measure.

Elinor fixed him with a cool stare until his mirth subsided.

“A joke, of course,” Robert said, recovering. “But in truth, poor Edward is ruined. A good-hearted fellow, but so pitiable in company! When I heard of his folly, I told my mother at once—if he married that girl, I would never see him again.”

“Have you met her?” Elinor asked.

“Once. A plain, awkward creature—just the sort to ensnare Edward. Had I known sooner, I might have dissuaded him. But now? He must starve.”

His callous verdict was interrupted by Fanny’s entrance. Though she avoided the subject, her strained smile and forced cordiality betrayed her discomfort. She even lamented Elinor’s impending departure, hoping to see more of her—a performance her husband watched with doting admiration.

And so the visit ended, leaving Elinor with much to ponder—and little satisfaction.

A final, fleeting visit to Harley Street marked the end of Elinor and Marianne’s time in London. There, John Dashwood congratulated his sisters on the economy of their journey to Cleveland—thanks to Colonel Brandon’s generosity—and assured Elinor, with more warmth than propriety, that he would not hesitate to visit her at Delaford. Fanny, ever dutiful, extended a hollow invitation to Norland, should they ever find themselves passing by—an event as improbable as it was unwelcome.

Elinor could not help but smile at the persistence with which everyone seemed determined to send her to Delaford—a place she had no desire to see, much less call home. Even Lucy, in their parting exchange, had pressed her with an insincere eagerness to visit. It was as though the world had conspired to fix her future in a spot she wished only to forget.

April arrived, crisp and bright, and the party from Berkeley Street set out at an early hour, meeting the Middletons’ carriage by prearrangement on the road. For Charlotte’s comfort—and that of her infant—the journey stretched leisurely over two days. Mr. Palmer and Colonel Brandon, traveling with greater haste, would join them at Cleveland shortly after their arrival.

For Marianne, leaving London was a bittersweet ordeal. Though she had found little solace there, the city held the echoes of her shattered hopes. The house from which she now departed had been the last place where Willoughby’s promises had seemed real, where she had believed in his constancy. Now, knowing he remained behind, absorbed in new plans and new affections in which she had no part, her heart ached with every mile that carried her farther from him.

Elinor, by contrast, felt only relief. She left no tender attachments behind, no regrets to haunt her. Free from Lucy’s suffocating pretense of friendship, spared the humiliation of encountering Willoughby after his marriage, she looked ahead with quiet hope. A few months of peace at Barton might soothe Marianne’s wounded spirit—and perhaps restore her own.

Their journey passed without incident. By the second day, they entered Somerset—a county that existed in Marianne’s mind as both cherished and forbidden. On the morning of the third, they arrived at Cleveland.

The house was a handsome, modern structure, set upon a sloping lawn. Though lacking a park, its grounds were ample, with winding shrubberies and shaded walks. A gravel drive curved toward the entrance, flanked by firs and poplars that shielded the service quarters from view.

Marianne stepped inside with a heart too full for words. Eighty miles from Barton. Thirty from Combe Magna. Before the others had finished admiring Charlotte’s child, she slipped away, drawn outside to the gardens, just stirring with the first blush of spring. She wandered until she reached a Grecian temple perched on a distant rise. From there, her gaze swept the horizon, lingering on the farthest line of hills—where, in her imagination, Combe Magna lay hidden.

In these moments of exquisite sorrow, she found a bitter comfort. Cleveland, at least, offered the freedom to grieve unseen. She resolved to spend every possible hour in such solitary walks.

Her return coincided with the others’ preparations to explore the nearer grounds. The morning passed easily—inspecting the kitchen garden, pitying the frost-nipped plants in the greenhouse, laughing at the misfortunes of Charlotte’s poultry. But by afternoon, the skies darkened, and a steady rain confined them indoors. Marianne, who had counted on an evening walk to the temple, chafed at the confinement.

Their small party made the best of it. Mrs. Palmer doted on her child; Mrs. Jennings busied herself with needlework. They spoke of London acquaintances, speculated on Lady Middleton’s engagements, and wondered whether Mr. Palmer and Colonel Brandon would reach Reading by nightfall. Elinor, though indifferent to the subject, joined the conversation. Marianne, ever resourceful, discovered the library and lost herself in a book.

Mrs. Palmer’s hospitality was warm, if not polished. Her good humor compensated for her occasional lapses in decorum, and her pretty face made her kindness all the more endearing. Even her silliness was inoffensive—though Elinor could have done without her laugh.

The gentlemen arrived late the following evening, enlivening the party after a day dulled by unrelenting rain. Elinor had scarcely known Mr. Palmer well enough to form an opinion, but in his own home, he proved every inch the gentleman—save for his occasional brusqueness toward his wife and mother-in-law. He was, when he chose to be, an agreeable companion, though his sense of superiority often intruded.

Elinor found him more tolerable than expected—but no more than that. His epicurean tastes, his vanity, only sharpened her appreciation of Edward’s simpler virtues. It was Colonel Brandon, however, who brought news of Edward—or at least of his circumstances. Having recently visited Dorsetshire, the colonel spoke freely of the Delaford parsonage, its shortcomings, and his own plans for its improvement.

His manner toward Elinor—his eagerness to see her, his readiness to confide—might have convinced a less discerning observer of his attachment. But Elinor, though flattered, remained certain that Marianne held his heart. Only once did she waver: when she noticed his anxious glances as Marianne succumbed to the first signs of a heavy cold. Mrs. Jennings saw nothing amiss, but Elinor recognized the silent vigilance of a man in love.

Two reckless evening walks—through dew-laden grass and beneath ancient trees—had left Marianne chilled. Worse still, she had lingered in damp stockings, dismissing all concern until her symptoms could no longer be ignored. Feverish, aching, her throat raw, she insisted a night’s rest would cure her. Elinor, with some effort, persuaded her to accept the mildest of remedies before bed.

But the illness, trifled with too long, would not be so easily dismissed.

Marianne rose at her usual hour the next morning, insisting she was better as she attempted to resume her ordinary routines. Yet the day betrayed her claims—she spent it shivering by the fire, unable to focus on her book, or lying listlessly upon the sofa, her weariness speaking louder than her assurances. When she retired early, her condition worsening, Colonel Brandon could scarcely conceal his surprise at Elinor’s composure. Though Elinor had tended to her sister all day, pressing remedies upon her despite Marianne’s protests, she trusted—perhaps too readily—in the restorative power of sleep.

But the night brought no relief. Marianne tossed in feverish unrest, and by morning, her strength had so failed her that she could not rise without assistance. Elinor, her own calm now fraying, at last heeded Mrs. Jennings’s advice and summoned the apothecary, Mr. Harris.

The man’s examination was brisk, his tone reassuring—Marianne would recover in a matter of days, he declared. Yet his mention of a *putrid tendency* and the dreaded word *infection* sent Mrs. Palmer into immediate alarm for her child. Mrs. Jennings, who had long suspected Marianne’s illness more serious than Elinor allowed, grew grave. She urged Charlotte to depart at once with her infant, and though Mr. Palmer scoffed at their fears, his wife’s distress wore him down. Within the hour, she was gone, retreating to a relative’s home beyond Bath, her husband promising to follow shortly.

Mrs. Jennings, however, refused to abandon Marianne. With a kindness that endeared her deeply to Elinor, she resolved to stay at Cleveland, offering not just companionship but practical aid—her experience in nursing proving invaluable.

Marianne, meanwhile, sank deeper into melancholy. The journey home, meant to begin that very day, now seemed impossibly distant. Every ache was sharpened by the thought of their mother’s disappointment when they did not arrive as promised. Elinor soothed her as best she could, though her own confidence wavered.

The days that followed were a blur of uneasy waiting. Mr. Palmer, though reluctant to appear driven by his wife’s fears, was persuaded by Colonel Brandon to depart. The Colonel himself nearly followed, until Mrs. Jennings—with a tact born of genuine concern—insisted he remain, if only to play piquet with her in the evenings. The truth, unspoken but understood, was that his presence was a comfort to them all.

Marianne knew nothing of these shifts. She noticed Mrs. Palmer’s absence only in its lack of disturbance. But her condition did not improve. Mr. Harris, ever optimistic, spoke of recovery, yet Elinor could not ignore the dread settling in Mrs. Jennings’s eyes—or the quiet despair in Colonel Brandon’s.

Then, on the third morning, hope flickered. Mr. Harris pronounced Marianne stronger, her symptoms abating. Elinor, heart lifting, dared to imagine their departure. But by evening, the fever returned, fiercer than before. Marianne’s murmurs grew incoherent, her mind fixed on their mother. *“Is Mama coming?”* she cried out in restless sleep. *“She must not go by London—I shall never see her!”*

Elinor, her own pulse racing, checked Marianne’s wrist—the beat was rapid, weak. Panic struck. She sent for Mr. Harris at once, then resolved to summon their mother.

Colonel Brandon, when she found him, needed no explanation. Before she could voice the difficulty of arranging a messenger, he offered himself. Elinor’s protest died before it began. His steadiness, his quiet competence, was a balm. Within moments, preparations were made: a servant dispatched for fresh horses, a note hastily penned to Mrs. Dashwood.

As the Colonel departed with solemn urgency, Elinor returned to her vigil. The night was long, Marianne’s delirium unrelenting. Mr. Harris, when he came, offered tempered reassurance—new treatments, he insisted, would bring relief. But Elinor, though she clung to his words, could not quiet the fear gnawing at her.

Dawn brought no change. Mrs. Jennings, learning of the night’s trials, shared none of Elinor’s fragile hope. Her certainty of impending loss hung heavy in the room. Even Colonel Brandon, usually a bulwark against despair, seemed shaken.

Then—near noon—a shift. Elinor, scarcely daring to believe it, detected steadiness in Marianne’s pulse. The flush of fever eased; her breathing softened. By the time Mr. Harris returned, his delighted confirmation—*Marianne was out of danger*—unleashed in Elinor a flood of silent, trembling gratitude.

Mrs. Jennings, her grim predictions overturned, rejoiced openly. But Elinor’s relief ran too deep for celebration. She kept her place beside Marianne, watching each steady breath, each quiet blink of awareness, as proof of life reclaimed.

Evening fell. The wind howled, rain lashing the windows, but Elinor, her heart alight, barely noticed. Marianne slept peacefully now, and somewhere on the road, their mother—and the Colonel—hurried toward them.

At eight o’clock, Elinor started at the sound of carriage wheels. It was too soon—surely too soon—but she rushed to the window nonetheless. There, in the flickering lamplight, a coach and four horses dashed up the drive.

Her composure shattered. *Her mother’s terror, her desperate haste—* The thought alone was agony. She flew downstairs, past the murmuring servants, into the drawing-room—

And found Willoughby.

Elinor recoiled at the sight of him, her first instinct urging her to flee the room. Her fingers had already closed around the door handle when his voice—sharp, almost commanding—halted her.

"Miss Dashwood, I beg you—stay. Only for half an hour. Ten minutes."

"No," she said firmly. "Whatever business you have cannot concern me. The servants must have neglected to inform you that Mr. Palmer is not at home."

He let out a bitter laugh. "Had they told me the entire household was in hell, it would not have kept me from this door. My business is with you, and no one else."

"With me?" Her astonishment sharpened her tone. "Then be swift, sir—and if possible, less theatrical."

"Sit down, and I will be both."

She hesitated. The thought of Colonel Brandon arriving to find her in such company unsettled her, but curiosity and a stubborn sense of obligation prevailed. After a moment, she moved to the table and sat, her posture rigid. He took the chair opposite, and for a long moment, silence stretched between them.

"Please, Mr. Willoughby," she said at last, "do not waste my time."

He had been lost in thought, his gaze distant, but now he looked up abruptly. "Your sister—she is out of danger. The servant told me. Is it true?"

Elinor did not answer.

"Tell me," he pressed, desperation edging his voice. "For God's sake, is she safe?"

"We hope so."

He stood abruptly, pacing the length of the room before returning to his seat with forced composure. "Had I known sooner—but no matter. For once, Miss Dashwood, let us speak plainly. This may be the last time. Tell me honestly—" His voice roughened. "Do you think me more a fool or a villain?"

She studied him, suspicion hardening her gaze. His manner was so erratic she wondered if drink had addled his senses. "I advise you to return to Combe. If you have anything to say to me, let it wait for a clearer head."

A wry smile touched his lips. "You think me drunk? I confess, a pint at Marlborough may have gone to my head, but I assure you, my mind is intact."

"Marlborough?" Her confusion deepened.

"I left London at dawn. Ten minutes at an inn was all the respite I allowed myself."

The steadiness of his eyes convinced her he spoke the truth. She exhaled sharply. "Mr. Willoughby, after all that has passed, your presence here demands explanation. What do you want?"

He leaned forward, intensity burning in his expression. "To make you despise me a little less. To offer—some semblance of apology. To prove that though I have been a fool, I was not always a scoundrel. And perhaps... to beg forgiveness from your sister."

"Is that truly why you’ve come?"

"Upon my soul, it is." The raw sincerity in his voice reminded her painfully of the man she once thought him to be.

"Then you may rest easy. Marianne has long since forgiven you."

His breath caught. "Then she forgave too soon. But she will hear me again, and this time with reason." He searched her face. "Will you listen?"

Reluctantly, she nodded.

He exhaled, gathering his thoughts. "I do not know what motives you’ve ascribed to my actions—what cruelty you believe drove me. But let me lay bare the truth. When I first knew your family, I sought only amusement. Your sister’s beauty and warmth drew me in, but I never intended to return her affection. I played the devoted suitor while guarding my own interests."

Elinor’s eyes flashed. "Then spare us both the sordid details. No justification can follow such an admission."

"You will hear it all," he insisted. "I was drowning in debt, and marriage to a woman of fortune was my only escape. Attaching myself to Marianne was unthinkable—yet I led her on, careless of the pain I would cause." His voice dropped. "But then... I began to care. Truly. The hours I spent with her were the happiest I’ve known. Even so, I delayed, fearing the shackles of engagement while my finances were in ruin." He gave a hollow laugh. "A cunning fool, indeed."

Elinor’s voice was icy. "And when you abandoned her?"

"Mrs. Smith discovered my past indiscretions—my entanglement with Eliza. She demanded I marry the girl or lose her favor. I could not. And so I was cast out, left to scramble for survival." His jaw tightened. "The night before I left Devonshire, I saw Marianne one last time. The look in her eyes..." He trailed off, pain flickering across his face.

"You should have written. Why torment her with a visit?"

"Pride," he admitted bitterly. "I could not bear to slink away like a coward. But seeing her—her grief, her trust—it shattered me."

A silence fell between them.

"And in London?" Elinor pressed. "The letter?"

His expression darkened. "My wife intercepted Marianne’s note. In a fit of jealousy, she dictated my reply—cruel words I shamefully transcribed. I kept no tokens, no letters. Even the lock of hair was torn from me." His voice broke. "Every memento, gone."

Elinor’s anger wavered, pity threading through her disdain. "And now? What do you expect from this confession?"

"Nothing." He stood, weariness weighing on him. "Only to unburden myself before your sister... departs." His voice faltered. "Is she truly recovering?"

"Yes."

"Then I will go." He moved toward the door, then paused. "Tell her... tell her I am sorry. Not for my sake, but for hers."

And with that, he was gone.

Elinor remained, her heart a tumult of indignation and reluctant sympathy. The man was a ruin of his former self—a living testament to the consequences of selfishness. And yet, in his misery, she could not help but see the faintest shadow of remorse.

It changed nothing. But it was, at last, the truth.

Elinor stood motionless long after Willoughby’s departure, the echo of his carriage wheels fading into silence. Her mind was a tumult of conflicting thoughts, each more sorrowful than the last. How strange it was—that Willoughby, whom she had so recently despised as the worst of men, could now stir in her such aching pity. His suffering, born of his own faults, softened her heart toward him, and she found herself thinking of him with a tenderness she had not expected. It was not his merits that moved her, she knew, but the lingering traces of what he had once been: his charm, his warmth, his unfeigned love for Marianne—a love that should have been innocent but was now tainted beyond repair.

Yet even as she acknowledged these feelings, she fought them. It was long before she could steady herself enough to return to Marianne’s bedside.

Her sister, at least, was peaceful—revived by deep, healing sleep, her face no longer shadowed by pain. Elinor watched her, her own heart too full for words. The past, the present, the future—Willoughby’s visit, Marianne’s fragile recovery, their mother’s imminent arrival—all pressed upon her at once, leaving no room for exhaustion. She could only pray that her composure would hold.

But there was no time for lingering unease. Within half an hour, another carriage arrived. Elinor flew to the door, desperate to spare her mother even a moment of dread. Mrs. Dashwood, pale with terror, stepped inside, her fear so overwhelming that she could not speak. Elinor needed no words; she clasped her mother’s hands and at once assured her—Marianne was safe.

The relief was instant. Mrs. Dashwood, trembling, embraced her daughter again and again, then turned to Colonel Brandon with tearful gratitude, pressing his hand as if words could not capture what she owed him. The Colonel, ever restrained, accepted her thanks in silence.

As soon as she had gathered herself, Mrs. Dashwood hurried to Marianne’s side. There, she found her beloved child weakened but alive—dearer now than ever after such peril. Elinor, watching them, felt a swell of joy, though she feared the excitement might disturb Marianne’s rest. But Mrs. Dashwood, for all her usual exuberance, was gentle, careful—prudent where her daughter’s life was concerned. Marianne, content merely to have her mother near, did not stir.

That night, Mrs. Dashwood insisted on keeping vigil. Elinor, though weary, found sleep impossible. Her thoughts circled back to Willoughby—*poor Willoughby*—as she now permitted herself to call him. She could not dismiss his anguished defense, nor quiet her own guilt for having judged him so harshly. Yet the thought of recounting it all to Marianne filled her with dread. What would it do to her sister’s heart? Would she ever recover? For a fleeting, shameful moment, Elinor even wished Willoughby free of his marriage—then recoiled at herself, remembering Colonel Brandon’s quiet devotion.

The Colonel’s arrival at Barton had been softened for Mrs. Dashwood by her own mounting fear; she had already resolved to leave for Cleveland that very day, unwilling to wait for further news. Marianne’s improvement brought radiant joy to her mother, who declared herself the happiest of women. Elinor, though glad, could not help wondering—did their mother ever think of Edward? But Mrs. Dashwood, buoyed by Elinor’s own measured account of her disappointment, allowed herself only thoughts that amplified her happiness.

Then came the revelation.

"At last we are alone," Mrs. Dashwood whispered eagerly when they had a private moment. "My Elinor, you do not yet know all my happiness. Colonel Brandon loves Marianne. He has told me so himself."

Elinor was silent—surprised, yet not entirely so.

"You are never like me, dear Elinor," her mother continued, "or I should wonder at your composure. If I had prayed for any blessing upon this family, it would have been for Colonel Brandon to marry one of you—and I believe Marianne will be happier with him than you would have been."

Elinor nearly asked why—for she could think of no impartial reason—but held her tongue. Her mother’s imagination, once seized upon an idea, would not be checked.

"He confessed it all to me as we traveled," Mrs. Dashwood went on, her voice warm with conviction. "His love has been steadfast from the very beginning, enduring even Marianne’s ill-fated attachment to Willoughby. And never once did he act selfishly! He would have stood aside if she had been happy with another. Such nobility! Such sincerity!"

Elinor smiled faintly. "Colonel Brandon’s character is beyond reproach."

"Indeed it is," her mother agreed. "Had it been otherwise, I should never have encouraged such feelings. But his kindness—his devotion—proves his worth beyond doubt."

Elinor nodded. "I esteem him highly. If Marianne can find happiness with him, I will rejoice as much as you."

Her mother clasped her hands. "I told him—if she lives, as I pray she will—nothing would make me happier than their union. And now that she is safe, I have repeated it. A little time is all that is needed. Marianne’s heart will not remain bound to Willoughby forever."

Yet Elinor noted the Colonel’s restraint. "He does not share your confidence?"

"No," Mrs. Dashwood admitted. "He fears her attachment is too deep, that even if she recovers from it, he himself—with his age and temperament—could never win her. But he is wrong. His maturity is an advantage, his disposition precisely what she needs. And his manners—so sincere, so gentle—are far better suited to her than Willoughby’s showy charm ever was."

Elinor said nothing, though she did not entirely agree.

"Besides," her mother added brightly, "Delaford is but a short distance from Barton! And surely there is some cottage nearby where we might settle."

Elinor nearly laughed at this fresh scheme to bring her to Delaford, though her resolve remained firm.

"And his fortune!" Mrs. Dashwood continued. "Not that I care for such things, but one must be practical—"

Their conversation was interrupted then, and Elinor withdrew, turning it all over in her mind. She wished fervently for her friend’s happiness—even as the thought of Willoughby’s sorrow pierced her heart anew.

Marianne’s illness, though severe, had not lingered long enough to slow her recovery. With youth, resilience, and her mother’s comforting presence, she improved swiftly—so swiftly that within days of Mrs. Dashwood’s arrival, she was well enough to move into Mrs. Palmer’s dressing room. There, eager to express her gratitude, she requested Colonel Brandon’s visit.

When he entered, his face betrayed an emotion deeper than mere concern. His eyes lingered on her pallor, her weakened frame, and the thin hand she extended to him. Elinor, watching closely, sensed something more than brotherly affection—perhaps the stirring of old sorrows, revived by Marianne’s resemblance to another lost love.

Mrs. Dashwood, however, saw only the Colonel’s gentle devotion and fancied a tender attachment growing between them. Marianne, for her part, spoke to him with warmth, her gratitude sincere but free of lingering distress.

Within days, Marianne’s strength returned enough for plans to be made. Mrs. Dashwood resolved to return to Barton, and Colonel Brandon, at the urging of both Mrs. Jennings and herself, offered his carriage for the journey. He promised to visit them soon after—a prospect met with quiet pleasure.

The morning of departure arrived. Marianne bid Mrs. Jennings a heartfelt farewell, her manner gracious and earnest, as though making amends for past indifference. Colonel Brandon handed her into the carriage with careful attention, ensuring her comfort before stepping back.

The journey passed peacefully. Marianne bore it well, her spirits calm, her fatigue manageable. Elinor watched her closely, relieved to see not resignation but a quiet resolve.

At Barton, familiar sights awaited—each field, each tree a reminder of joys and sorrows past. Marianne grew silent, her gaze distant, but she did not turn away. Instead, she faced them, steady.

That evening, she attempted the piano, only to find sheet music Willoughby had once given her. She set it aside without bitterness, declaring she would practice anew when stronger.

The next morning, she spoke of plans—walks to the farm, visits to the ruins, hours spent in study. “I mean to rise early,” she said, “to fill my days with purpose.” Elinor smiled, admiring her sister’s determination even as she feared its fragility.

Days later, on a soft morning, Marianne ventured outside, leaning on Elinor’s arm. They paused at a familiar hill. “There,” Marianne said softly, “I fell. And there I first saw him.” Her voice wavered but did not break. “I can speak of it now without pain. That must mean something.”

Elinor hesitated, then carefully shared Willoughby’s confession—his regrets, his remorse. Marianne listened, trembling but composed. When the tale was done, she pressed Elinor’s hand. “Thank you,” she whispered. Then, with quiet dignity, she withdrew to reflect alone.

Elinor let her go, hopeful. The storm had passed. What remained was the steady work of healing—and the promise of a future built not on fleeting passion, but on enduring strength.

Mrs. Dashwood listened to the defense of her once-beloved Willoughby with a heart not untouched. Though she was glad to see some of the accusations against him disproven, and though pity softened her toward his plight, the past could not be undone. No words could mend the shattered trust between him and Marianne, nor erase the pain he had caused—both to her daughter and to poor Eliza. His actions had left marks too deep to ignore, and no amount of regret could restore him to the place he once held in their hearts. Colonel Brandon’s steadiness, by contrast, shone all the brighter.

Had Mrs. Dashwood heard Willoughby’s confession firsthand, seen the torment in his eyes, she might have softened further. But Elinor, careful and measured, relayed the truth without embellishment, unwilling to stir misplaced sympathy. Time had cooled her own initial distress, and she wished only to present the facts plainly, leaving no room for romantic illusions.

That evening, as the three sat together, Marianne broke the silence. Her voice wavered, her cheeks flushed—every sign of struggle was there—but she pressed on. *"I wish you both to know that I see everything clearly now, just as you would have me see it."*

Mrs. Dashwood nearly interrupted with soothing words, but Elinor, eager to hear Marianne’s unguided thoughts, stayed her with a glance.

*"Elinor’s words this morning have given me peace,"* Marianne continued, steadying herself. *"I know now what I needed to know. I am satisfied. I wish for no change. I could never have been happy with him—not truly. Confidence, esteem… they would have been impossible."*

*"Of course, my dear,"* Mrs. Dashwood exclaimed. *"Happiness with a man of such reckless character? A man who wounded our dearest friend, the best of men? No, your heart could never have found contentment with him. Your conscience would have tormented you for his sins!"*

Marianne sighed. *"I wish for no change."*

Elinor, ever practical, added, *"You judge rightly. Marriage to him would have brought inevitable hardship. His extravagance, his lack of restraint—even he admits it. On a small income, with your inexperience, you would have faced struggles neither of you were prepared for. Your own sense of honor would have driven you to economize, but how much could you truly have done? And had you tried to curb his pleasures—would he have thanked you for it? Or resented you?"*

Marianne’s lips trembled. *"Selfish?"* she echoed, as if questioning the word itself.

*"Every part of his conduct has been selfish,"* Elinor replied firmly. *"First, he toyed with your affections. Then, when his own heart was engaged elsewhere, he delayed confessing it. And finally, he fled Barton rather than face the consequences. His own comfort was always his first concern."*

*"You are right,"* Marianne murmured. *"My happiness was never his aim."*

*"Now he regrets—but why?"* Elinor continued. *"Because his choices have not brought him joy. His circumstances are easy now, yet he finds himself bound to a woman of lesser temper than yours. But had he married you, his misery would only have taken another shape. Poverty would have chafed him, and he would have soon valued wealth over affection."*

*"I doubt nothing of it,"* Marianne said quietly. *"I regret only my own folly."*

*"Call it my imprudence, child,"* Mrs. Dashwood insisted, but Marianne would not hear it.

Elinor, content that each acknowledged their mistakes, turned the subject. *"One truth stands clear in all this: every trouble Willoughby now faces began with his betrayal of Eliza Williams. That first sin led to all the rest."*

Marianne agreed fervently, while Mrs. Dashwood seized the moment to praise Colonel Brandon’s virtues—though Marianne seemed scarcely to listen.

In the days that followed, Marianne’s strength did not return as swiftly as before, yet she bore herself with quiet resolve. Elinor trusted time to heal her. Margaret’s return reunited the family at Barton Cottage, and though their studies had slowed, they planned to resume them with vigor.

Yet Elinor’s thoughts lingered on Edward. Since leaving London, she had heard nothing—no word of his plans, not even his whereabouts. A brief mention in her brother’s letter offered no comfort: *"We know nothing of Edward, nor can we inquire. He is likely still at Oxford."* His name did not appear again.

But fate was not done with revelations. One morning, their servant returned from Exeter with news. *"I suppose you know, ma’am, that Mr. Ferrars is married?"*

Marianne started violently, her eyes flying to Elinor, whose face went pale. A gasp, a collapse—Marianne fell back in hysterics. Mrs. Dashwood, torn between her daughters, barely managed to steady herself as the servant, alarmed, summoned help.

Once Marianne was calmer, resting in another room, Elinor—though shaken—gathered herself enough to question the man.

*"I saw Mr. Ferrars myself, ma’am, in Exeter—him and his lady, Miss Steele that was. They were at the New London Inn. I recognized her, and she called to me, sent her compliments, and said they were sorry they couldn’t visit, but they were in a hurry to travel further."*

Elinor understood his silence too well.

Elinor exchanged a glance with her mother. She knew Lucy’s words for what they were—empty politeness. Edward would not come.

Once alone, Mrs. Dashwood hesitated, fearing to speak. She saw now how she had misjudged Elinor’s feelings, believing them milder than they were. In her focus on Marianne’s visible grief, she had neglected the quieter sorrow in her elder daughter’s heart. The realization weighed heavily upon her.

Silence stretched between them, full of unspoken regret.

Elinor had always believed herself prepared for the inevitable—yet the stark reality of Edward’s marriage struck her with a force she had not anticipated. Though she had told herself to expect it, some stubborn corner of her heart had clung to hope: that he might yet break free of Lucy Steele, that some intervention of family or fortune might spare him a loveless match. But now it was done. He was wed. And the pain of it, sharp and unexpected, left her breathless.

That they had married so hastily—before Edward had taken orders, before the living at Delaford was secured—spoke volumes of Lucy’s relentless ambition. No delay could be risked; no advantage left unclaimed. They had been married in London, and now, no doubt, Lucy would drag him to her uncle’s estate, triumphant. The thought of Edward so near Barton—passing her mother’s servant, receiving Lucy’s gloating messages—twisted like a knife. Soon they would settle at Delaford, that place which held such bittersweet significance for Elinor. She could too easily picture it: Lucy, ever the calculating mistress of the parsonage, flaunting frugality as virtue while angling for favor with every wealthy connection. And Edward—what of Edward? She dared not dwell on him. Whether he was content or wretched, the image pained her equally.

Days passed without word. No letter arrived from London to confirm the marriage or offer details. Elinor chafed at the silence, blaming absent friends for their indifference. “When do you write to Colonel Brandon, Mama?” she asked, desperate for some thread of news to grasp.

“I wrote last week,” Mrs. Dashwood replied. “I expect him to call any day now.”

The thought was a small comfort. Surely the colonel would know more.

No sooner had the hope formed than hoofbeats sounded outside. A rider approached—a gentleman. Elinor’s pulse quickened. But as the figure dismounted, her breath caught. It was not Colonel Brandon.

It was Edward.

Her hands trembled. She forced herself to sit, to steady her voice. *He has come from Mr. Pratt’s. I will be composed.* Around her, the room tensed. Marianne paled; their mother exchanged a glance with her. Elinor longed to speak, to beg them not to slight him—but words failed her. Silence stretched, brittle, until his footsteps echoed in the hall.

When he entered, his face was ashen. He looked like a man braced for condemnation. Mrs. Dashwood, ever gracious, extended a stiff congratulations. Edward flushed, stammering incoherently. Elinor’s lips had moved with her mother’s, but now she wished she had offered her hand. Too late. She turned instead to the weather, voice too bright.

Marianne had retreated to the shadows, misery etched in every line. Margaret, half-comprehending, assumed an air of solemn distance. The conversation faltered.

At last, Mrs. Dashwood ventured, “I hope Mrs. Ferrars is well?”

“Yes,” Edward answered hastily.

Another pause. Elinor gripped her needlework. “Is Mrs. Ferrars at Longstaple?”

Edward blinked. “No, my mother is in town.”

She dared not raise her eyes. “I meant—Mrs. *Edward* Ferrars.”

A beat of silence. Then, haltingly, he said, “You mean… my brother’s wife. Mrs. *Robert* Ferrars.”

Marianne gasped. Their mother’s eyes widened. Elinor’s gaze snapped up.

Edward stood abruptly, moving to the window. He fumbled with a pair of scissors, ruining them as he spoke. “You may not have heard. My brother—he has married Lucy Steele.”

The room erupted in shock—save for Elinor, who bent over her work, trembling.

“Last week,” Edward added tightly. “They are at Dawlish now.”

She could bear no more. Excusing herself, she fled—and once the door closed, tears of relief spilled over. Behind her, Edward stood frozen, as if stunned by his own words. Then, without another syllable, he strode out into the fading light, leaving the others in bewildered silence.

The truth, at last, had set them free.

Edward’s sudden freedom was met with astonishment by all, yet none doubted what he would do with it. His heart had long belonged to Elinor, and now, unshackled, he hastened to Barton with a single purpose—to ask for her hand.

Though no stranger to declarations, Edward found himself uncharacteristically nervous as he approached the cottage. The weight of past mistakes, the relief of release, and the trembling hope of a future with Elinor left him restless. Yet love steadied him. The words came, earnest and true, and Elinor’s answer—her tearful, joyful acceptance—brought a peace he had scarcely dared imagine.

By evening, the household brimmed with happiness. Mrs. Dashwood, torn between laughter and tears, could scarcely decide whether to embrace Edward or her daughter first. Marianne, overcome, spoke little but radiated quiet joy. And Elinor—Elinor, who had borne uncertainty with such grace—now glowed with a serenity that seemed to light the room.

Edward, once burdened by duty and regret, stood among them as a man transformed. The shadow of his ill-fated engagement had lifted, leaving only gratitude—for Lucy’s mercenary betrayal had unwittingly granted him his heart’s desire.

Theirs was a love tempered by trial, all the stronger for it. And as they walked together in the twilight, speaking of Delaford and the life they would build, Edward marveled at the turn of fate that had led him here—to Elinor, to happiness, and to a future bright with promise.

Yet when reason failed to sway him, Mrs. Ferrars, chastened by past losses, yielded—though not without a show of reluctance befitting her dignity. Her consent, once given, came with a stingy allowance: while Robert enjoyed a thousand a year, Edward was left to make do with a clergyman’s modest income. Still, it was more than he and Elinor had dared hope for, and with Colonel Brandon’s eager patronage, their future was assured.

The wedding took place at Barton in early autumn, once the parsonage was made ready. Mrs. Jennings, ever the matchmaker, rejoiced to see them settled—and happier, she declared, than any couple in England. They lacked nothing, save for Marianne’s marriage to the Colonel and better grazing for their cows.

Visitors came in steady succession. Mrs. Ferrars inspected their domestic bliss with grudging approval, while even the Dashwoods journeyed from Sussex to pay their respects. John, though careful not to seem disappointed, could not resist hinting that Marianne might yet secure Colonel Brandon—if only she were thrown often enough in his path.

Yet true warmth from Mrs. Ferrars remained reserved for Robert and Lucy, whose cunning had won her over entirely. Lucy’s relentless flattery and false humility had smoothed their reconciliation, and they now thrived in London, basking in Mrs. Ferrars’ favor while Edward remained an afterthought.

As for Marianne—her fate was one she could never have foreseen. The girl who once scorned second attachments, who had deemed Colonel Brandon too old and too dull, now found herself his devoted wife. Love had come not in wildfire passion, but in steady, quiet devotion. And Willoughby, though he bore the sting of her loss, did not perish of it. He lived, if not happily, then at least comfortably—though no woman ever measured up to the memory of Marianne Brandon.

And so the sisters, bound by affection and proximity, lived in harmony—Elinor at Delaford, Marianne at the manor, and Margaret on the cusp of her own adventures. The Dashwood legacy, once so uncertain, had found its happy ending.
