Amyas had returned to the quietude of home, and for the ensuing twelve months, the annals of his life remained largely unrecorded in ink. Yeo, the old sailor, had effectively installed himself as the household's major domo, a role defined more by his constant trailing of Amyas's heels like a greyhound than by specific duties. His evenings were spent by the fire, Bible in hand, crafting countless trifles—both useful and superfluous—for the family, particularly for Ayacanora. Though he offered toys unsuitable for a grown woman, she accepted them with a pout, only to store them away, never again to gaze upon them. Her entire spirit was dedicated to becoming an English maid; she followed Mrs. Leigh tirelessly, eager to master the secrets of the kitchen and still-room, and ultimately, the art of sewing. First, she sought to emulate Mrs. Leigh as a virtuous housewife; then, a new, altruistic idea blossomed within her: the concept of giving without expectation. Having grown up amidst the boundless hospitality of the savages, she now grasped the profound difference of offering aid to those who could offer nothing in return. Observing Mrs. Leigh's charitable visits to the poor cottagers, and witnessing Amyas distribute money and food to Northam, Appledore, and Bideford after his safe return from the seas, Ayacanora was initially baffled. Her understanding of "thank-offerings" leaned toward the indigenous rituals of sacrifice and skull-adornment rather than Christian alms. Yet, through Mrs. Leigh's simple, patient instruction—that giving to the poor was akin to giving directly to the Great Spirit, who dwelt within them and within Ayacanora herself—the child's perspective shifted. She applied herself with fervor, working her fingers raw to clothe the elderly women of Northam, accompanying Mrs. Leigh with a radiant, beloved presence. Often, she would peek from beneath her long black lashes to gauge Amyas's approval, finding that a single smile earned from him could make her behave perfectly all day, whereas failure to secure it rendered her thoroughly naughty. While her domestic nature softened, her vanity regarding her attire remained unchecked. She swept into church Sundays draped in Spanish finery, a riot of color and noise that forced the vicar to gently admonish Mrs. Leigh for disturbing the congregation's piety. Ayacanora retorted that she was not thinking of them, nor did they need to think of her; if the Piache, or conjurer, desired a present, she would gladly donate her Mexican feather-dresses, refusing to wear them as they were "wild Indian things" incompatible with her status as an English maid. Nevertheless, she insisted on dressing the vicar in one of her finest mantles, compelling him to flee the pulpit, though he wisely retained the garment to convert it into an altar-cloth, ensuring future communions at Northam were celebrated upon a blaze of emerald, azure, and crimson once adorning an Aztec prince. Amyas, meanwhile, watched her with a mixture of amusement and simple pride, seemingly presenting her to the world like a prized bird.

A significant source of friction for Mrs. Leigh was Ayacanora's treatment of the local ladies. Neighbors flocked to congratulate Mrs. Leigh and glimpse the "fair savage," only to be snubbed with icy disdain by Ayacanora, from the vicar's wife to Lady Grenville herself. Few ventured a second visit. Mrs. Leigh's protests drew floods of tears from Ayacanora, who claimed they only wished to stare at a "poor wild Indian girl" and refused to be exhibited as a curiosity. She lamented her transition from a queen, obeyed by all, to someone looked down upon. Yet, when pressed, the girl clung to Mrs. Leigh like a child, begging not to be sent back to the forests, declaring she would sooner be a slave in the kitchen than return to her former life.

As the months passed, the storms and sunshines of Ayacanora's adjustment gradually smoothed into a perpetual, genial calm. Slowly, however, fragments of her identity emerged. Familiarity with houses led her to remark that she had seen such structures before; the "red cattle" seemed known to her, while the sheep puzzled her until she admitted they were too small. Yeo, seizing upon every clue, suggested she had grown accustomed to llamas in Peru. Her greatest delight, however, was a horse. The ability to ride was a miracle to her, surpassing all other tamed creatures. After frustrating Amyas—who feared breaking her neck—she confided in Yeo about stealing one, failed in that scheme, and eventually negotiated with the vicar to trade her finery for his broken-kneed pony. The vicar, too honest to accept such a bargain, declined, leading Amyas to purchase her a jennet, which she mastered in a fortnight, riding with the skill of a Gaucho.

One evening, following an outing to Stow with Lady Grenville, Ayacanora awoke screaming from a vivid nightmare. She recounted how the "Old ape of Panama"—her deepest hatred—had dragged her from bed, forcing her onto a horse to ride with an Indian over moors and mountains into a dark wood, where they vanished, leaving her transformed back into a little savage child. The vision was so potent that Mrs. Leigh and the household accepted it as truth. By morning, Mrs. Leigh declared that the Lord had revealed in a vision Ayacanora's true origin: exposed in the forests by her jealous step-father. Sir Richard and his wife, unable to doubt the evidence, pieced together the puzzle. Yeo noted that Don Xararte, though based in Panama, had frequently visited Quito, explaining how Ayacanora might have been found by Indians beyond Cotopaxi, the peak of the Eastern Andes, if the old man, suspecting her to be Oxenham's child, had intended to expose her as vengeance.

Further details surfaced, mostly tied to animals and nature, indicating the erasure of her moral and religious upbringing during her long sojourn in the forest. Mrs. Leigh struggled to elicit any memory of her mother or early teachings until a pivotal event occurred. Sir Richard had returned from Virginia with an Indian companion, likely Wanchese, whom he planned to baptize at Bideford. The ceremony attracted the entire town, including the mayor and corporation in full regalia, to honor the first "Red man" to tread British soil. Mrs. Leigh attended naturally, as did Ayacanora, eager to witness the conversion of the "Carib."

"To make him a Christian," explained the locals.
"Why did they not make her one?" Ayacanora asked.
"Because she was already one," they replied, assuming she had been christened at birth. Unsure of this, Ayacanora pouted at the prospect of a rival "ugly red Carib" enjoying a superior spiritual status. When the service commenced, a heavy sigh—or groan—caused all eyes to turn as Ayacanora sank fainting into Mrs. Leigh's arms. Carried to a neighboring house, she later recounted a hallucination-induced revelation. Standing in the church, trying to recall if she had ever been baptized, the space expanded; the priest's vestments grew richer, the walls filled with pictures, and above the altar, a lady in jeweled robes held a babe. Soft music swelled, filling the air with a fragrant mist. Through it, she saw not one, but many Indians gathered at the font, a lady holding her hand, revealing her as a little girl again. Her recollection of the scene and the cathedral architecture proved so precise that Yeo, drawing on his own experiences in Quito, declared her a "christened woman," albeit by a "Popish" rite, having witnessed similar ceremonies in the Cathedral of Quito.

Thus, Ayacanora returned home, while Raleigh the Indian was taken to Sir Richard's residence. His baptism remains recorded in the crooked letters of the Bideford registers for 1587–3, listed twice: first as "Raleigh, a Winganditoian," and later, a year and a month later, simply as "Rawly," no longer marked among the baptisms. The free forest wanderer, having pined in vain for his deer-hunts and lagoons, departed for "happier hunting-grounds," leaving behind a grave in a narrow town churchyard, surrounded by dingy houses whose inhabitants would never mourn the Indian's passing. A somber emblem of the tragic fate awaiting the Red race, who arrived too late for salvation but too early for extinction.

While Amyas idled at home, England did not. That spring witnessed the dispatch of another, larger colony to Virginia under John White. Raleigh had repeatedly urged Amyas to take command, a decision that might have accelerated the founding of the United States by twenty years. However, Amyas's mother had bound him by a solemn promise to remain at home for at least twelve months. Consequently, he sent five hundred pounds to fund the venture—a sum presumably still in Virginia today, as it never returned.
