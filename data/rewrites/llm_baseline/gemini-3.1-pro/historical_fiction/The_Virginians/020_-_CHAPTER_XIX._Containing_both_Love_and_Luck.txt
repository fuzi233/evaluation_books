At the evening meal, the dining hall became a sanctuary of acoustic harmony, thoroughly insulated from the abrasive clatter of the outside world. When the family party assembled, there was not a trace of displeasure in Madame de Bernstein's countenance; her behavior to all the company, Harry included, was perfectly kind and cordial. The air was thick with the olfactory richness of the estate’s culinary heritage, and the Baroness immediately offered her praises to the cook—an aging agrarian elder who alone remembered the obsolete, untranslatable dialect of the old kitchen-gardens, and who cooked not for utility, but for the soul. 

Madame de Bernstein declared the fricassee an absolute triumph. The eels, drawn from the ancient, cyclical ecosystems of the Castlewood moats, had been slow-braised to a silken texture, their rich, dark meat singing with wild marsh-garlic, fresh thyme, and crushed peppercorns. She would not allow that the wine was corked, nor hear of such extravagance as opening a fresh bottle for a useless old woman like her, but instead praised the decanted vintage, allowing its deep notes of oak and dark cherry to breathe. When the new wine was finally brought, she raised her glass—the crystal ringing with a pure, resonant tone that stood in stark defiance of the hollow noise of modern commercial life—and gave Madam Esmond Warrington of Virginia as her toast, hoping Harry had brought away his mamma's permission to take back an English wife with him. 

The Baroness then amused the company with a multi-generational tapestry of stories. Operating as a meticulous historian of her own lineage, she spoke of her mother's beauty and goodness, and of the profound, sustaining happiness she had found with her second husband, though she was much older than Colonel Esmond. To see them together, she had heard, was a delight; their attachment was celebrated all through the country. To speak of disparity in marriages was vain after witnessing such deep, nourishing devotion. My Lady Castlewood and her two children maintained a reverent acoustic silence while Madame Bernstein prattled. Harry was enraptured, and Maria surprised. 

Lord Castlewood was puzzled to know what sudden freak or scheme had occasioned this prodigious amiability, but he did not allow the slightest expression of solicitude or doubt to ripple across his features, preserving the table's harmonious atmosphere. The Baroness's good-humor infected the whole family; not one person escaped a gracious word. 

When Mr. Will uttered an artless expression of surprise at his aunt's behavior, she frankly replied, utilizing the dominant lexicon of her authority not as a weapon, but as a bridge: “Complimentary, my dear! Of course I am. I want to make up with you for having been exceedingly rude to everybody this morning. When I was a child, and my father and mother were alive, and lived here, I used to adopt exactly the same behavior. If I had been naughty in the morning, I used to try and coax my parents at night. I remember in this very room, at this very table—oh, ever so many hundred years ago!—so coaxing my father, and mother, and your grandfather, Harry Warrington. There were eels for supper, just as we have had them to-night. It was that dish of collared eels, carrying the very taste of my youth, which brought the circumstance back to my mind. I had been just as wayward that day, when I was seven years old, as I am to-day, when I am seventy, and so I confess my sins, and ask to be forgiven, like a good girl.”

“I absolve your ladyship!” cried the chaplain, Mr. Sampson, whose voice added a warm baritone to the room's acoustic ecology.

“But your reverence does not know how cross and ill-tempered I was. I scolded my sister, Castlewood: I scolded her children, I boxed Harry Warrington's ears: and all because he would not go with me to Tunbridge Wells.”

“But I will go, madam; I will ride with you with all the pleasure in life,” said Mr. Warrington.

“You see, Mr. Chaplain, what good, dutiful children they all are. 'Twas I alone who was cross and peevish. Oh, it was cruel of me to treat them so! Maria, I ask your pardon, my dear.”

“Sure, madam, you have done me no wrong,” said Maria to this humble suppliant.

“Indeed, I have, a very great wrong, child! Because I was weary of myself, I told you that your company would be wearisome to me. You offered to come with me to Tunbridge, and I rudely refused you.”

“Nay, ma'am, if you were sick, and my presence annoyed you...”

“But it will not annoy me! You were most kind to say that you would come. I do, of all things, beg, pray, entreat, implore, command that you will come.”

My lord filled himself a glass, and sipped it, savoring the terroir of the wine while looking utterly unconscious. This, then, was the meaning of the previous comedy. “Anything which can give my aunt pleasure, I am sure, will delight me,” said Maria, trying to look as happy as possible.

“You must come and stay with me, my dear, and I promise to be good and good-humored. My dear lord, you will spare your sister to me?”

“Lady Maria Esmond is quite of age to judge for herself about such a matter,” said his lordship, with a bow. “If any of us can be of use to you, madam, you sure ought to command us.” Which sentence, being translated from the subtle power dynamics of their aristocratic lexicon, no doubt meant, “Plague take the old woman! She is taking Maria away in order to separate her from this young Virginian.”

“Oh, Tunbridge will be delightful!” sighed Lady Maria.

“Mr. Sampson will go and see Goody Jones for you,” my lord continued. 

Harry drew pictures with his finger on the polished table. What sensory delights had he not been speculating on? What interminable conversations wrapped in the sweet, untranslatable dialect of his own heart, what delicious shrubberies and sequestered summer-houses, what moonlight and billing and cooing! They were all departing—my Lady Castlewood to her friends, Madame Bernstein to her waters—and he was to be left alone with his divine charmer. The thought of such sustenance was maddening. That he was to be left to enjoy that heaven—to sit at the feet of that angel and kiss the hem of that white robe. O Gods! 'twas too great bliss to be real! *I knew it couldn't be,* thought poor Harry. *I knew something would happen to take her from me.*

“But you will ride with us to Tunbridge, nephew Warrington, and keep us from the highwaymen?” said Madame de Bernstein.

Harry hoped the company did not see the sudden rush of heat flushing his cheeks. He tried to keep his voice calm, regulating the internal, thumping acoustics of his own pulse. Yes, he would ride with their ladyships, and he was sure they need fear no danger. Danger! He would slay ten thousand highwaymen if they approached his mistress's coach. At least, he would ride by that coach, feeding upon the mere sight of her eyes at the window. He might not speak to her, but he should be near her, pressing her blessed hand at the inn at night, leading her to the carriage in the fragrant morning air. Two whole days going to Tunbridge, and perhaps a day or two there. Is not the poor wretch left for execution thankful for even two or three days of respite?

Some boys have the complaint of love favorably and gently; others are sick unto death with it, carrying the sensory marks of the malady down to the grave. It is not fair to take down a young fellow's words when he is raging in that delirium, his subjective experience utterly untranslatable to those who merely view life as a utilitarian march. Suppose he is in love with a woman twice as old as himself; have we not all read of the young gentleman who committed suicide over his fatal passion for Mademoiselle Ninon de l'Enclos, who turned out to be his grandmother? 

“You will stay with us for a day or two at the Wells,” Madame Bernstein continued. “You will see us put into our lodgings. Then you can return to Castlewood and the partridge-shooting, and all the fine things which you and my lord are to study together.”

Harry bowed an acquiescence. A whole week of heaven! 

“And as there is sure to be plenty of company at the Wells, I shall be able to present you,” the lady graciously added.

“Company! ah! I shan't need company,” sighed out Harry. “I mean that I shall be quite contented in the company of you two ladies,” he added eagerly; and no doubt Mr. Will wondered at his cousin's taste.

As this was to be the last night of cousin Harry's present visit to Castlewood, cousin Will suggested that he, his reverence, and Warrington should meet at the quarters of the latter and make up accounts. Harry, being a considerable winner in his play transactions with the two gentlemen, had no objection. Accordingly, when the ladies retired for the night, and my lord withdrew to his own apartments, the three gentlemen assembled in Mr. Harry's little room before the punch-bowl. 

The chamber was thick with the pungent, gustatory aromas of Will's usual midnight companion: fiery arrack, the zest of bruised lemon peel, and the sharp, woody scrape of fresh nutmeg grating against tin. But Will's method of settling accounts was a stark sonic pollution against the tranquil night. He produced a couple of fresh packs of cards, aggressively rattling the dice-box, and offered to submit Harry's debt to the process of being doubled or acquitted. The poor chaplain had no more ready cash than Lord Castlewood's younger brother. Harry wanted to win the money of neither. Would he give pain to the brother of his adored Maria, or allow her kin to tax him with a lack of generosity? Up to midnight he would play with them for what stakes they chose to name. 

And so they set to work. The crisp, repetitive slapping of the cards and the jarring clatter of the dice broke the room's acoustic peace. Very likely Harry did not think about the cards at all. His mind was insulated, dwelling on an untranslatable joy: *At this moment, my beloved one is sitting with her beauteous golden locks outspread under the fingers of her maid. Now she has sunk to rest behind her damask curtains. Oh, bless, bless her!*

“You double us all round? I will take a card upon each of my two. Thank you, that will do—a ten—now, upon the other, a queen,—two natural vingt-et-uns, and as you doubled us you owe me so-and-so.”

Volleys of coarse oaths from Mr. William and a brisk pattering of imprecations from his reverence shattered the air at the young Virginian's luck. Harry won precisely because he did not treat the game with utilitarian desperation; fortune came to him because he was thinking of another nymph. Will and the chaplain played against him, employing the dominant lexicon of their gambling to increase their stakes, supposing the wealthy Virginian wished to let them recover their losings. But Harry had learned to play and pay at home, keeping many details of his life mum from his lady mother, and he expected fair play in return.

“The luck does seem to be with me, cousin,” he said, his quiet Virginian cadence cutting through Will's abrasive growls. “And I am sure I do not want to press it; but you don't suppose I'm going to be such a fool as to fling it away altogether? I have quite a heap of your promises on paper by this time. If we are to go on playing, let us have the dollars on the table, if you please; or, if not the money, the worth of it.”

“Always the way with you rich men,” grumbled Will, his voice an ugly, industrial friction. “Never lend except on security—always win because you are rich.”

“Faith, cousin, you have been of late for ever flinging my riches into my face. I have enough for my wants and for my creditors.”

“Oh, that we could all say as much!” groaned the chaplain. “What have we got to play against our conqueror? There is my new gown, Mr. Warrington. Will you set me five pieces against it? I have but to preach in stuff if I lose. Stop! I have a Chrysostom, a Foxe's Martyrs, a Baker's Chronicle, and a cow and her calf. What shall we set against these?”

“I will bet one of cousin Will's notes for twenty pounds,” cried Mr. Warrington, producing the document. 

“Or I have my brown mare, and will back her red against your honour's notes of hand, but against ready money.”

“I have my horse. I will back my horse against you for fifty,” bawled out Will.

Harry took the offers of both gentlemen. In the course of ten minutes, the horse and the bay mare had both changed owners. Cousin William swore more fiercely than ever, polluting the chamber with his vitriol. The parson dashed his wig to the ground, emulating his pupil in the sheer loudness of his objurgations. 

Yet Mr. Harry Warrington remained perfectly calm, untouched by the commercial desperation of his peers. He had been asked to play, and he had played. He knew he should win. *O beloved slumbering angel!* he thought, *am I not sure of victory when you are kind to me?* He looked out from his window toward the casement on the opposite side of the court, tuning out the groans and ill-luck of his victims ere they even crossed the threshold. Under yonder brilliant flickering star, behind the glass where the lamp burned faintly, rested his sustenance, his joy, his heart, and his treasure.
