Two nights following this event, Pietro Vanucci and Andrea sat in the dim chamber, awaiting supper for Gerard. The atmosphere thickened with tension until Pietro, growing peevish under the weight of uncertainty, glanced at the clock. It was well past nine o'clock. In a final, desperate gambit, he dispatched Andrea to Gerard's room, hoping against hope that the scholar had entered unnoticed. Andrea shrugged, accepting the mission with a mixture of duty and apprehension, and departed.

When he returned, Gerard was absent, but he carried something far heavier than a meal ticket: a folded slip of paper. Illiterate in the formal arts, as scholars of his era and charity boys of ours understand such distinctions, Andrea possessed a sharp visual acuity and had memorized the specific cadence of Pietro Vanucci's handwriting. "That is for you, I trow," he declared, swelling with a pride born of his limited but keen intelligence.

Pietro snatched the note, his eyes scanning the lines with a grim intensity before reading aloud, punctuated by biting satire intended to mask his dread. "'Dear Pietro, dear Andrea, life is too great a burden.'"

"So 'tis, my lad," Pietro muttered, though his voice lacked conviction, "but that is no justification for wandering abroad at supper-time. Supper is not a burden."

"'Wear my habits!'"

"Said the poplar to the juniper bush," Pietro retorted, forcing a laugh that died quickly in his throat. "'And thou, Andrea, mine amethyst ring; and me in both your hearts a month or two.'"

"Why, Andrea?" Pietro pressed, the irony failing to reach his eyes.

"'For my body, ere this ye read, it will lie in Tiber,'" Pietro continued, his tone shifting from mockery to a hollow recitation of doom. "'Trouble not to look for it. 'Tis not worth the pains. Oh unhappy day that it was born—oh happy night that rids me of it.'"

"'Adieu! adieu!'"

"'The broken-hearted Gerard.'"

Pietro set the paper down, dismissing it as "a sorry jest of the peevish rogue." Yet, the pallor of his cheeks and the violent trembling of his teeth betrayed the truth he tried so desperately to conceal.

Andrea, however, could not contain the storm within him. He filled the house with his anguished cries, his voice rising to a shrill crescendo. "O, miserable day! O, calamity of calamities! Gerard, my friend, my sweet patron! Help! help! He is killing himself! Oh, good people, help me save him!"

His alarm rippled through the household like wildfire. Soon, a crowd gathered, drawn by the sheer volume of his distress. But poor Andrea found himself powerless to animate their sluggish compassion. "Go down to the river?" someone suggested skeptically. "No. It is not their business." "What part of the river?" another countered. "It is a wild goose chase." The prevailing sentiment was rooted in superstition; it was ill-fortune to venture near the river after sunset, for too many ghosts haunted those banks throughout the long, watchful night.

Yet, a lackey who had been lingering opposite the house stepped forward, claiming he would accompany Andrea. This single offer sparked a chain reaction, turning three or four of the younger servants into a small, frantic band. Together, they moved toward the river.

As the lackey questioned Andrea, the boy, sobbing uncontrollably, recounted the letter and Gerard's increasingly erratic, moody behavior. The lackey, revealed to be a spy of Princess Claelia, understood the gravity of the situation immediately. Their Italian tongues flew rapidly, exchanging urgent details as they neared the Tiber. But the moment they felt the cool, damp air of the riverbank and caught the faint, metallic scent of the stream in the calm spring night, their conversation ceased abruptly. Silence descended upon them, heavy and ominous.

The moon hung high and clear in a cloudless sky, casting silver light that seemed to mock their desperation. Their footsteps echoed loudly on the stones, sounding like gunshots in the quiet. Their tongues were hushed, replaced by the ragged sound of breathing.

Suddenly, hurrying around a shadowed corner, they collided with a man. He froze, irresolute, at the sight of them. He was bareheaded, his dripping hair glistening unnaturally in the moonlight. With the next step, they saw his clothes were soaked through, clinging to his frame.

"Here he is!" cried one of the young men, unfamiliar with Gerard's face and figure.

The stranger turned instantly and fled. They chased him might and main, Andrea leading with reckless speed, the princess's lackey close behind. Andrea gained on him briefly, but in a heartbeat, the fugitive twisted into a narrow alleyway. Andrea shot past, unable to check his momentum, and the pursuers found themselves trapped in a labyrinthine maze. It was futile to chase a quick-footed fugitive who knew every inch of the city's underbelly; now, they could follow him only by the echo of his retreat.

Returning to their companions, they found them standing exactly where the man had paused, utterly confounded. "Are ye verily sure?" they demanded. "He had been in the river. Why, in the saints' names, did he flee at our approach?"

Then spoke Vanucci, his voice weary. "Friends, methinks this has nought to do with him we seek. What shall we do, Andrea?"

Here, the lackey interjected. "Let us track him to the water's side, to make sure. See, he hath come dripping all the way."

This advice was approved, and with little difficulty, they retraced the man's path. But soon, they encountered a new enigma. Having traveled scarcely fifty yards, the trail of water droplets veered sharply away from the river, directing them instead toward the gate of a large, gloomy building—a monastery. They stood irresolute before the dark pile, gazing at its imposing silhouette, sensing that it hid some terrible mystery.

Presently, Andrea let out a shout of realization. "Here be the drops again!" he cried. "And this road leadeth to the river."

They resumed the chase, and soon it became clear that the moisture was guiding them homeward. The track grew wetter and wetter, leading inexorably to the Tiber's edge. There, on the bank, a bucketful of water appeared to have been discharged directly from the stream.

At first, they shouted in triumph, believing they had made a discovery. But reflection quickly sobered them. It amounted to nothing. Certainly, a man had been in the water and emerged safely, but that man was not Gerard.

One of them recalled knowing a fisherman nearby who owned nets and drags. They located the fisherman and paid him liberally to sink nets in the river below the scene and drag them upstream and downstream, promising gold if he found the body.

Then, they ran vainly up and down the river, which flowed so calm and voiceless, guarding its strange secrets. Suddenly, Andrea, with a cry of renewed hope, sprinted back toward the house. He returned in less than half an hour.

"No," he groaned, wringing his hands in despair. "There is no sign."

"What is the hour?" asked the lackey, his face grave.

"Four hours past midnight," Andrea replied, his voice breaking.

"My pretty lad," said the lackey solemnly, placing a comforting hand on the boy's shoulder, "say a mass for thy friend's soul: for he is not among living men."

Morning broke, gray and indifferent. Worn out by fatigue and grief, Andrea and Pietro trudged home, their hearts sick with the certainty of loss. The days rolled on, mute as the Tiber itself, offering no answer regarding Gerard's fate.
