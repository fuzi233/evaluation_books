The next day, as the Miss Dashwoods entered the drawing-room of the park through one door, Mrs. Palmer burst in at the other, her usual cheerfulness undimmed by the weather or any other circumstance. She greeted them all with warm affection, clasping their hands and exclaiming with genuine delight, “I am so glad to see you again! I was afraid you might not come—such a dreadful day! But it would have been a shame if you hadn’t, since we’re leaving tomorrow. The Westons are coming to us next week, you know.”

She paused only briefly before adding, “It was quite sudden that we came at all—I didn’t even know until the carriage was at the door. Then Mr. Palmer asked me if I’d go with him to Barton. He’s so droll—he never tells me anything!” She laughed, then added with regret, “I’m so sorry we can’t stay longer. But we’ll meet again in town soon, I hope.”

Elinor and Marianne exchanged glances. They were compelled to gently disappoint her. “Not go to town?” Mrs. Palmer exclaimed, laughing. “Oh, I should be terribly disappointed if you didn’t. I could find the most lovely house in Hanover Square—right next door to ours! You must come. And I’d be delighted to chaperone you, if Mrs. Dashwood doesn’t feel like attending public events. I’ll be happy to do it until I’m confined.”

They thanked her warmly but politely declined, explaining their plans. Mrs. Palmer turned to her husband, who had just entered the room. “Oh, my love,” she said, “you must help me persuade the Miss Dashwoods to come to town this winter!”

Mr. Palmer offered no reply. After a brief bow to the ladies, he began grumbling about the weather. “How horrid all this is!” he declared. “Rain makes everything and everyone unbearable. Dullness creeps in just as much indoors as out. It makes one loathe every acquaintance. What on earth does Sir John mean by not having a billiard room? How few people truly understand comfort! Sir John is as dull as the weather itself.”

As the rest of the company arrived, Sir John remarked with mild dismay, “I’m afraid, Miss Marianne, you’ve missed your usual walk to Allenham today.”

Marianne looked grave and said nothing.

“Oh, don’t be so coy,” Mrs. Palmer teased. “We know all about it, I assure you. And I admire your taste—he’s extremely handsome, isn’t he? We don’t live far from him in the country—only ten miles, I’d say.”

“More like thirty,” Mr. Palmer corrected dryly.

“Oh, well—there’s not much difference,” she replied, unfazed. “I’ve never been to his house, but they say it’s a sweet little place.”

“Vile,” Mr. Palmer snapped. “The worst spot I’ve ever seen.”

Marianne remained silent, though her expression betrayed her interest.

“Is it really so ugly?” Mrs. Palmer pressed. “Then perhaps it’s some other place they’re referring to.”

At dinner, Sir John observed with regret that only eight were present. “My dear,” he said to his wife, “it’s very frustrating that we’re so few. Why didn’t you invite the Gilberts?”

“I did tell you earlier,” she replied, “that it wouldn’t work—they dined with us last time.”

“Ah, but you and I, Sir John,” Mrs. Jennings interjected, “shouldn’t be so formal.”

“That would make you very ill-bred,” Mr. Palmer retorted sharply.

“My love, you contradict everyone,” Mrs. Palmer said, laughing as always. “Do you know you’re being quite rude?”

“I didn’t realize I was calling your mother ill-bred,” he muttered.

“Ay, you may abuse me all you like,” Mrs. Jennings chuckled, “but you took Charlotte off my hands and can’t give her back. So there—now I have the upper hand.”

Charlotte laughed heartily, clearly pleased that her husband couldn’t rid himself of her. “I don’t care how cross he gets,” she declared, “we’re stuck together.”

Mrs. Palmer’s good humor was unshakable. Her husband’s studied indifference, insolence, and constant complaints seemed to amuse her rather than offend. When he scolded or mocked her, she found it endlessly entertaining.

“Mr. Palmer is so droll!” she whispered to Elinor. “He’s always in such a bad mood.”

Elinor, after watching him closely, did not believe he was genuinely ill-tempered or uncivil as he pretended to be. Perhaps his sourness stemmed from the common male frustration of marrying a woman whose charm outweighed her sense—but she knew such misjudgments were too frequent for any sensible man to remain wounded by them. Instead, Elinor suspected his behavior arose from a desire to appear superior—a wish to stand apart from others by belittling everything around him. It was a familiar motive, though the method—by cultivating an air of rudeness—was unlikely to win him many friends beyond his wife.

Soon, Mrs. Palmer leaned toward Elinor and said, “Oh, my dear Miss Dashwood, I have a favor to ask—will you and your sister come spend Christmas with us at Cleveland? Please do—come while the Westons are here. I cannot imagine how happy I’d be! It will be delightful.”

She turned to her husband. “My love, don’t you long to have the Miss Dashwoods come to Cleveland?”

“Certainly,” he replied, sneering. “I came into Devonshire with no other purpose.”

“There now!” Mrs. Palmer triumphantly declared. “You see, Mr. Palmer wants you—so you can’t refuse.”

But both sisters firmly declined.

“But you *must* come,” Mrs. Palmer insisted. “I’m sure you’ll love it. The Westons will be here, and it will be charming. Cleveland is such a lovely place—and we’re so lively now, because Mr. Palmer is constantly traveling around the countryside canvassing for the election. So many new people have come to dine with us—I’ve never seen such a crowd! But poor fellow—it’s exhausting for him. He has to make everyone like him.”

Elinor struggled to keep a straight face as she sympathized with the burden.

“How wonderful it will be when he’s in Parliament!” Charlotte exclaimed. “Won’t it be ridiculous? I shall laugh so hard to see all his letters addressed with ‘M.P.’ But do you know—he says he won’t frank for me. He refuses! Don’t you, Mr. Palmer?”

Mr. Palmer ignored her.

“He hates writing,” Charlotte continued. “He says it’s simply shocking.”

“No,” he replied coldly, “I never said anything so absurd. Don’t attribute your own misuse of language to me.”

“There now!” Mrs. Palmer giggled. “See how droll he is. That’s always the way with him. Sometimes he won’t speak to me for hours, and then suddenly blurts out something hilarious—about anything at all.”

Later, as they returned to the drawing-room, Mrs. Palmer surprised Elinor by asking, “Do you like Mr. Palmer very much?”

“Certainly,” Elinor replied. “He seems quite agreeable.”

“Well, I’m so glad you do. I knew you would—he’s so pleasant. And Mr. Palmer adores you and your sister, I can tell you. He’ll be terribly disappointed if you don’t come to Cleveland. I can’t understand why you’d object.”

Elinor once again declined, changing the subject to end the conversation.

Yet she had hoped that, living in the same county, Mrs. Palmer might offer more insight into Mr. Willoughby’s character than the Middletons’ limited acquaintance could provide. Any confirmation of his worth would ease Marianne’s fears. So Elinor cautiously asked, “Do you see much of Mr. Willoughby at Cleveland? Are you well acquainted with him?”

“Oh, dear yes,” Mrs. Palmer answered brightly. “I know him extremely well—though I’ve never actually spoken to him. I’ve seen him countless times in town. Somehow, I never stayed at Barton while he was at Allenham. Mama saw him once here, but I was with my uncle in Weymouth. Still, if we’d been in Somersetshire together, we’d have seen a great deal of him. He’s rarely at Combe, I believe—but even if he were, Mr. Palmer wouldn’t visit him. He’s in the opposition, you know, and Combe is so far away.”

She paused, then added, “I know exactly why you’re asking. Your sister is going to marry him, isn’t she? I’m absolutely thrilled—for then she’ll be my neighbor.”

Elinor blinked. “Upon my word, Mrs. Palmer, you seem to know more about it than I do. Do you really expect such a match?”

“Don’t pretend to deny it,” Mrs. Palmer insisted. “Everyone’s talking about it. I heard it myself on the way through town.”

“My dear Mrs. Palmer,” Elinor said, astonished.

“Upon my honor, I did,” she continued. “I met Colonel Brandon Monday morning in Bond Street, just before we left London, and he told me right away.”

Elinor was stunned. “Colonel Brandon told you? Surely you’re mistaken. To reveal such news to someone unconnected—especially if it’s true—is not what I’d expect from him.”

“But I swear it’s true,” Mrs. Palmer insisted. “Let me explain. When we met, he turned back and walked with us. We talked about my brother and sister, and various things. Then I said, ‘So, Colonel, there’s a new family at Barton cottage, I hear—and mama says they’re very pretty. One of them is supposed to be marrying Mr. Willoughby of Combe Magna. Is it true? You must know, since you’ve been in Devonshire recently.’”

“And what did he say?” Elinor asked.

“Oh, he didn’t say much. But he looked—well, as if he knew it was true. From that moment, I decided it was certain. It will be quite delightful, I declare! When is it to happen?”

“Was Mr. Brandon well?” Elinor asked.

“Oh, perfectly. And he spoke so highly of you—kept praising you nonstop.”

Elinor smiled. “I’m flattered by his regard. He seems an excellent man—uncommonly pleasing.”

“So do I,” Mrs. Palmer agreed. “He’s such a charming gentleman—it’s a pity he’s so serious and dull. Mama says *he* was in love with your sister too. Imagine—what a compliment! He hardly ever falls in love with anyone.”

Elinor hesitated. “Is Mr. Willoughby well known in your part of Somersetshire?”

“Oh, yes—extremely,” Mrs. Palmer replied. “Though not many people actually know him, since Combe Magna is so remote. But everyone thinks him extremely agreeable. Nobody dislikes him wherever he goes. Tell your sister that—she’s a lucky girl to get him, truly. Though he’s luckier still to have her, because she’s so beautiful and delightful—nothing could be good enough for her. Of course, I don’t think she’s any handsomer than you. I think you’re both exquisite—and so does Mr. Palmer, though he wouldn’t admit it last night.”

Mrs. Palmer’s words offered little substance, but any praise of Willoughby was welcome to Elinor.

“I’m so glad we’re finally acquainted,” Charlotte said, turning to Elinor. “Now I hope we’ll be great friends. I’ve longed to meet you! It’s such a joy that you’re living at the cottage—nothing could be better! And I’m so happy your sister is marrying well. I hope you’ll visit Combe Magna often. It’s said to be a lovely place.”

“You’ve known Colonel Brandon for a long time, haven’t you?” Elinor asked.

“Yes, a long while,” Charlotte replied. “Ever since my sister married. He was a close friend of Sir John’s. In fact,” she added softly, “I believe he would have liked to marry me—if he could. Sir John and Lady Middleton wanted it very much. But mama thought the match wasn’t good enough for me. Otherwise, Sir John would have mentioned it to the Colonel, and we’d have been married immediately.”

“Did Colonel Brandon know about Sir John’s proposal before it was made? Had he ever confessed his feelings to you?”

“Oh, no,” Charlotte said. “But if mama hadn’t objected, I’m sure he would have been overjoyed. He hadn’t seen me more than twice—before I left school. Still, I’m much happier as I am. Mr. Palmer is the kind of man I like.”
