"It is virtue, truly virtue, gentlemen, that makes a gentleman; that turns the poor rich, the base-born noble, the subject a sovereign, the deformed beautiful, the sick whole, the weak strong, and the most miserable most happy. There are two principal gifts in human nature: knowledge and reason. One commands, the other obeys; and neither the turning wheel of Fortune, nor the deceitful chatter of worldly men, nor sickness, nor age, can alter or abolish these." — *Lilly's Euphues*, 1586.

Now comes my turn to recount the founding of that most chivalrous brotherhood of the Rose. Within a few short years, it became renowned not only in its native Devon but grew formidable across Ireland, the Netherlands, the Spanish Main, and the heart of South America. Should this chapter seem to any modern eye quixotic or fantastical, let them remember that the generation who spoke and acted thus regarding love and honor were, nonetheless, practiced and valiant soldiers, prudent politicians, and shrewd diplomats. He who penned the *Arcadia* was simultaneously one of Europe's subtlest statesmen; the poet of the *Faerie Queene* also authored serious treatises on the state of Ireland. Even if critics sneer at quoting Lilly's *Euphues*, I ask: Have they ever read it? If they have, I pity them if they fail to find within it, despite occasional tedium, a book as brave, righteous, and pious as any man could seek. No better proof exists of the nobility of the Elizabethan age than the fact that *Euphues* and the *Arcadia* were the era's two dominant romances. While Sir Walter Scott may have ridiculed the Euphuists in his *Sir Piercie Shafton*, mocking their affected politeness, we must note the anachronisms therein—such as an Euphuist knight discussing Sidney's quarrel with Lord Oxford a decade before it occurred. We deny that Lilly's work could produce such a coxcomb; his spiritual ancestors were surely Gabriel Harvey or Lord Oxford himself.

Indeed, there is a double anachronism in Scott's character, for he belongs not to Sidney's day, but to the worse times following Elizabeth's death, when folly flourished under James. Those days, perhaps, for another time. Meanwhile, let those unread of *Euphues* believe that training a son after the model of Ephoebus would save fathers money and their sons' virtue. Let us instead open our eyes to see in these old Elizabethan gallants our own ancestors, displaying with youthful wildness all the virtues that make a true Englishman. Their commercial daring, military courage, reverence for law, and solemn sense of national calling serve as examples for us today. We must confess that their chivalry is merely another garment for that beautiful tenderness and mercy which remains the twin sister of English valor. Even their fondness for Continental manners reveals that old Anglo-Norman teachability and wide-heartedness, allowing us to profit from global wisdom without losing our distinct character.

Thus, I proceed to my story. If anyone dislikes it, they need only turn the page until they find pasture to their liking.

Amyas could not sail the next day, nor the day after, for a southerly wind freshened into a gale, blowing directly into the bay. Having lowered the *Mary Grenville* downriver to Appledore Pool, ready to depart with the first favorable breeze, he rode quietly home. When his mother mounted the pillion behind an old servant to ride to Clovelly, where Frank lay wounded, Amyas accompanied her as far as Bideford. There, meeting a procession descending the High Street, he saw Will Cary leading a group of horsemen. Clad cap-a-pie in shining armor, sword at thigh, and helmet resting on the saddle-bow, Cary looked as gallant a young gentleman as any Bideford damsel had ever admired from her window. Behind him rode four or five stout serving-men carrying lances, baggage, bows, swords, and bucklers. Bringing up the rear, in a horse-litter to Mrs. Leigh's delight, was Master Frank himself.

Frank reported that his wounds were mere flesh injuries, the dagger having glanced off his ribs. He insisted on seeing his brother one last time and declared that, with permission, he would not return to Burrough but take up residence with Cary at the Ship Tavern near the Bridge-foot. He did so immediately, settling onto a couch where he held court in state, surrounded by the town's gossipers, who spun white tales about how he had fallen into such sorry plight.

Meanwhile, Amyas and Frank devised a scheme executed the following day, which happened to be market-day. First, the innkeeper, acting on Amyas's orders, created a bustle of roasting, boiling, and frying unprecedented in the history of the Ship Tavern. Next, Amyas ventured into the market and invited, one by one, as many of his old schoolfellows as Frank had identified, to a merry supper and subsequent revelry. By this crafty plan, each of Rose Salterne's gentle admirers arrived to find himself, to his considerable disgust, seated at the same table with six rivals, none of whom he had spoken to in half a year. Yet, all were too well-bred to allow the Leighs to notice; knowing the situation fully, they arranged their guests so that Frank lay on his couch at the head of the table and Amyas took the bottom seat. They managed to fill everyone's mouths with good food, sparing them the pain of conversation until the wine loosened tongues and warmed hearts.

In the meantime, Amyas and Frank ignored their guests' silence with provoking good humor, chatting, joking, and telling stories until they became such delightful company that Will Cary, finding merriment contagious, melted into jest after jest. Finding good humor far more pleasant than bad temper, he attempted to make Mr. Coffin laugh, eliciting only a bow; he tried to make Mr. Fortescue laugh, receiving only a frown. Unabashed, he began firing his light artillery at the waiters until they burst with laughter and fled the room.

When the cloth was removed and sack and sugar became the order of the day, and "Queen and Bible" had been duly toasted with all honors, Frank made his final move.

"Gentlemen," he announced, "I propose a toast: 'To the gentlemen of the Irish wars! May Ireland never lack a St. Leger to stand by a Fortescue, a Fortescue to stand by a St. Leger, and a Chichester to stand by both.'"

Of course, this required toasting the three representatives of those families, followed by mutual compliments. The ice cracked slightly further. Young Fortescue then proposed the health of "Amyas Leigh and all bold mariners." Amyas replied bluntly and kindly, expressing his wish to know no greater fortune than sailing around the world again with the present company as fellow-adventurers, thereby giving the Spaniards another taste of the men of Devon.

By this time, the sweet wine had caused sleepers to speak. The ice broke completely, and every man began conversing rationally with his neighbor.

"Now, gentlemen," said Frank, sensing the perfect moment for his grand assault, "let me offer a health none of you will refuse to drink with heart and soul as well as lips. It is for one whom beauty and virtue have so ennobled that in their light, the shadow of lowly birth vanishes. I would proclaim him peerless in loveliness, were it not that every gentleman here has sisters who might well challenge him for the girdle of Venus. Yet, what else can I say, while those same lovely ladies—who, if they used their own mirrors, must be far better judges of beauty than I—have repeatedly awarded the palm to her in my presence? Surely, if the goddesses decided among themselves the question of the golden apple, Paris himself must vacate the judgment seat. Gentlemen, your hearts have undoubtedly already bid you, as my unworthy lips do now, to drink 'The Rose of Torridge.'"

Had the Rose of Torridge herself entered the room, she could scarcely have caused more blank astonishment than Frank's bold declaration. Every guest flushed red, then pale, then red again, casting looks that said, "What right has anyone but me to drink her health? Lift your glass, and I will dash it from your hand!" But Frank, with sweet effrontery, drank "The health of the Rose of Torridge, and a double health to that worthy gentleman, whosoever he may be, whom she is fated to honor with her love!"

"Well done, cunning Frank Leigh!" cried blunt Will Cary. "None of us dare quarrel with you now, however much we may sulk at each other. For there isn't one of us, I warrant, who doesn't think she likes him best of all; and so we are bound to believe you've drunk our healths all round."

"And so I have," Frank replied. "And what better thing can you do, gentlemen, than drink each other's healths all round likewise, proving yourselves true gentlemen, true Christians, and true lovers? For what is love, let me speak freely, but the very inspiration of that Deity whose name is Love?
