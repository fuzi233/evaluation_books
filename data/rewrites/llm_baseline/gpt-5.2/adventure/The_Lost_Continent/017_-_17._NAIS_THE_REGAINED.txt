From where we stood together just under the Sacred Mountain’s crest, the city lay open below us as though some careful steward had unrolled a chart upon a table. The harbour’s dark water held a faint sheen where the estuary broadened; beyond, the rim of hills gave off their habitual threads of smoke and sour vapour. In the city’s heart the Lord Sun’s stone circle rose bare and grim, and nearer still the royal pyramid shouldered up—its gilded faces catching what little light remained and throwing it back, new gold answering old. In the square before it, dwarfed by distance to a mere knob upon the paving, waited the granite throne. Nine years it had sat there with its secret—nine years my Nais had slept beneath that cold weight.

Old Zaemon watched the direction of my gaze as a man reads a kettle’s murmur before it boils. “Ay,” he said, and the sigh that left him was dry as husk. “I know where your chief interest lies. Deucalion, when he landed here new from Yucatan, was a strong man. The King we have chosen—and he is the best we have to choose—has his weakness.”

“It can be turned,” I said. “Give me Nais here, living and warm to fight for, and I am stronger than the cold viceroy and soldier you speak about.”

“I have passed my word to that already, and you shall have her,” he answered. “But at the cost of bruising this new kingdom of yours. Maybe too we may rid you of Phorenice and her brood. But I do not think it likely. She is too wily. Once we begin our play she will guess whence it comes and how it ends, and she will slip away before harm can reach her. The High Gods—who send trials for our refinement—have also seen fit to give her some knowledge of how these earth tremors may be set a-moving.”

“I have seen her juggle with them,” I said, tasting bile at the memory. “But may I hear your scheme?”

“It will be shown you in good time,” Zaemon said. “For the present, sleep. To-night it is your part to go into the city and take your woman—my daughter—when she is set free, and bring her here as best you can. For that you will need all a strong man’s strength.”

He stepped back and looked me up and down, weighing me like a butcher judging a carcass, not with cruelty but with the calm exactness of one who knows what the body can bear. “There are not many folk that would take you for the tidy, clean-chinned Deucalion now, my brother. Your appearance will be fine armour down yonder to-night when we wake the city with earth-shaking and terror. As you stand, you are hairy enough, shaggy enough, naked enough, and dirty enough for some wild savage newly landed out of Europe. Have a care no fine citizen takes a fancy to your thews and seizes on you as his servant.”

“I pity him in his household if he does.”

Zaemon’s laugh came quick and rare as a spark struck from flint. Then the gravity returned at once, as though laughter were a foreign tongue he could not comfortably speak. “Get you to bed, my King. Leave me to go into the Ark of Mysteries and prepare there with another of the Three the things that must be done. It is no light business to handle the tremendous powers we must put into movement this night. There is danger for us as for you. So if by chance we do not meet again till we stand behind the stars, giving account to the Gods—fare you well, Deucalion.”

I slept as a soldier sleeps: the body taking its ration of rest without bargaining, the mind refusing to pick at fear like a scab. Only the weak permit their sleep to be broken on such occasions. When the dark was well set I roused and fetched those who should attend the rope. Our Lady the Moon did not shine at that turn of the month; the air was thick with blackness, a palpable thing that seemed to press against the eyes. So I was out of sight all the while they lowered me.

At the deep foot of the cliff the rocks lay tumbled like bones, and I began to move with a caution as fine as a cook’s knife work, for Phorenice’s soldiers squatted uneasily round their camp-fires as though they already heard, in the smallest creak of stone, the prelude to disaster. Their voices were low; their armour made the wrong little clinks; even their coughing sounded afraid. I had no mind to stir that wakefulness. I slipped in the darkest seams of shadow, and at last came to the spot where the passage ended—the secret way I had used before to get beneath the city walls.

The lamp was in place. I made my way along the windings swiftly. The air down there was more noxious than I remembered: hot breath laced with mineral reek, the throat catching on it as on rancid smoke. It was as though the earth had begun already to simmer, brewing its poisons in unseen pots. Zaemon, I judged, had started to stir the internal activities that would shortly convulse the city.

But again I had trouble finding an exit—not because there were people moving above, but because the very set of the masonry had changed. In older times the Priests’ Clan oversaw all plans and ruled out anything that would clash with their passages and hidden chambers. In this modern day the Priests were of small account and had no say. The builders, with their proud new measures and their careless dominant speech, had blundered and sealed up outlets as a thoughtless hand spoils a family recipe by changing the old words it cannot understand.

So I got out of the net of tunnels where I could, not where I would, and in the event I found myself at the farther side of the city, near where the outer wall ran down toward the harbour. I came out unseen, and—even in that extremity—closed all traces behind me. Ordinances are a kind of memory; break them once, and the next breaking comes easier.

The earth under my feet had lost its dead solidity. It sprang like a ship’s deck in smooth water, alive with a subtle, sickening give. Yet the people slept on in their houses and shelters without alarm. They did not listen well. They had for years lived amid the ceaseless clang of Phorenice’s city—metal on stone, shouts in narrow streets, the coarse blare of spectacle—and their ears had grown dulled, as tongues grow dulled when a mother’s dialect is mocked into silence. The ground was speaking. They could not translate it.

Gods—should I be too late? Would Nais be laid bare to public gaze when fear drove people into open spaces and their rude hands seized whatever lay near? I saw in fancy their grasping fingers, the careless curiosity, the cruelty that comes when terror has made men forget reverence. My inwards dried at the thought.

I ran down and down the narrow winding streets with the single purpose of reaching the square before the royal pyramid before that could happen. With my own hands, nine years ago, I had been forced to place her alive in her burying-place beneath the granite throne. If thews and speed could do it, I would not miss the reward of taking her forth again with those same strong hands.

Few disturbed my hurry. At first, here and there, some gutter-wretch cried, “A thief! Throw a share or I pursue!” If any followed, I do not know. My speed would have outdistanced them. Soon, as the earth’s swing grew keener and the stonework of buildings began to grate and groan—dust sifting down in quick little showers—people started to run with scared cries from their doors. But none tried to stop the ragged, shaggy savage who ran so swiftly and flung the mud from his naked feet.

At last I came to the great square, none too soon. It was filling with people who fled the narrow streets, and it was full of darkness, noise, dust, and sickness. Beneath us the ground rippled in slow undulations like a sea whose swell is gathering. The terror in it was its patience: each wave more sure than the last.

Ever and again a house crashed down unseen in the gloom and added to the tumult. But the great pyramid had been planned for rude shocks. Its stones were dovetailed with marvellous cunning and clamped by tongues of metal. It was a boast that half its foundations could be dug away and still it would stand, square to heaven, more enduring than hills.

Flickering torches showed its great stone doors stood open, and now and again some frightened inmate scurried out and vanished into the dark. For the pyramid’s fate I had little concern then. I did not even care whether Phorenice was trapped or escaped to do further mischief. I crouched by the granite throne, and listened to its stones grate together like broken bone as it rocked upon the earth-waves.

In that dust-thick night it was hard to see the outline of one’s own hand. Yet I think the Gods, in some requital for love that had ached so long, gave me an edge of sight. As I watched, the great carved rock that formed the capstone moved—slightly, then again, then again. A tiny jerk for each pulse of earth; but there was shifting, and always to one side.

There was method in Zaemon’s desperate work, and in my blind panic I had overlooked it. I went up the throne’s steps on the side toward which the capstone crept, and clung there, all my blood afire with expectation.

The earth-swing grew more violent, though its increase came in such graduations one could not measure them. The din of falling houses and the shrieks of hurt and frightened people rose louder into the night. Dust thickened till breathing was choking, till coughs tore at the chest; the rare stars were blotted away. I crouched on the upper steps, one below the capstone, and could scarcely keep my place against the tremors.

Still the huge stone—carved with the snake and the outstretched hand—held my love locked in her living tomb. I could have bitten the granite in impotent rage. The square thronged fuller every moment, and their numbers made my case more desperate. “Phorenice, Goddess, aid us now!” some cried. When no instant relief came, they began to yammer out old confessions learned in childhood, turning back in their dread to Gods they had spurned through years of dishonour. Their faith, like their language, returned only when the dominant voice failed them.

The crash of masonry grew. From the pyramid—though its outline was lost in blackness—came grinding of stones and the crack of metal bars under strain. Zaemon’s old threat in the painted, perfumed banqueting-hall came back to me, ringing in memory like a gong: “You shall see this royal pyramid which you have polluted torn tier from tier, stone from stone, scattered as feathers before a wind!”

Heavier grew the surging of earth. The square’s paving gaped and heaved; people screamed as their feet were crushed by grinding blocks. Now even the pyramid began to split and topple. Roofs gave way; ponderous masonry shuttered down and filled splendid chambers. And then the sky itself joined the uproar: thunders roared above, and vivid lightning splashes lit the flying dust-clouds, turning the ruin into brief, stark pictures.

In one of those harsh illuminations I saw Phorenice safe in the square, standing not far from me.

It was perhaps natural she should be there, but it struck like a blow. She stood on the middle of a great flagstone, swaying her supple body to the shocks as a sailor sways on a deck. Her face was calm; its loveliness untouched by years. From time to time she brushed dust from the short red hair curled at her neck. There was no fear on her—only weariness, contempt, and, I thought, a tinge of amusement. It took more than the crumbling of her royal pyramid to impress Phorenice with powers arrayed against her.

The sight maddened me. I had it in me to strangle her if she came within reach. But she stood where she was, and beside her, crouched on the same flagstone and overcome with nausea, was Ylga—again made her fan-girl. In the lightning’s white bite of light, I remembered Ylga was twin sister to Nais, and that I owed her for an ancient kindness; but there was leisure for nothing. With each shock the capstone jarred farther from its bed. My love was coming nearer. She claimed all my service.

Once, in blind panic, a knot of people thought the granite throne too solid to be overturned and saw in it an oasis of safety. They flocked toward it, some dragging themselves up the steep steps on hands and knees because their feet had been injured by the billowing flagstones.

I was in no mood to have the place profaned by their trembling and staring. I beat at them with my hands, tore them away, hurled them back down the steps. They cried to know my title above theirs. I answered with blows and gnashing teeth. I did not care what they thought me. Only I wished them gone. And they went, wailing that I was a devil of the night; their spirit was too broken to defend themselves.

Farther and farther the great capstone slid, and its slowness maddened me. A life’s training in patience left me in that moment. I thrust at the block with shoulder and head, clawed at it with my hands until muscles stood out in ropes and knots. The High Gods must have laughed at my helplessness. All was ordered by the Three in Their good time. The work of the Gods may be slow, but it is sure.

At last—when the people were numbed with terror and incapable of more emotion, save Phorenice who still showed no concern—the threatened thing came to pass. The capstone slid until it reached balance, and the next shock threw it with a roar and clatter to the ground.

A strange tremor seized me then. What I had prayed for had come about. Yet my inwards sank at the thought of mounting again where I had mounted before, of reaching down into the hollow where my own hands had laid her. I knew Phorenice’s vengefulness; I valued her cleverness. Had she left Nais to lie in peace? Had she stolen her away to suffer indignities elsewhere? Had she ended her sleep with death and left the corpse for my finding as a grisly jest? I could not tell. I dared not guess. Never in a hard-fighting life had my emotions been so wrenched. Love for Nais had sapped my hardihood in a matter that was privately hers.

It came to me, too, that the infernal uproar was beginning to slacken, as though Zaemon knew he had done his promised work and meant to give the wretched city a breathing-space. I took my fortitude in hand and clambered up onto the flat of the fallen stone. The lightning had ceased; all was darkness again and stifling dust. At any moment the sky might flare once more, and if I were seen there—shaggy, changed though I was—Phorenice would not be slow to guess my name and errand.

So changed was I that I confess the idea of a fight was loathsome. I wanted my business done and to be gone.

With hands that shook, I fumbled over the stone’s face and found the clamps and bars of metal still where I had clenched them nine years before. I let my fingers pass between, and felt the curves of my love’s body at rest beneath. Exultation whirled in me. I did not know if she had been touched since I left her. I did not know if the drug would still do its office and bring her back to warmth and sight. But dead or alive, I had her there, and she was mine—mine—and I could have yelled aloud in joy at the mere fact of possession.

The earth still shook; masonry still roared and crashed. I had to cling with one hand while I unhasped the clamps with the other. At last I swung the upper half clear and left those that pinned her feet. I stooped, drew her soft body up onto the stone beside me, and pressed my lips again and again to the face I could not see.

Some mad thought took me that the heat and fierceness of kisses might bring her back to wakefulness. I will own it plainly: I did sorry credit to my training in calmness. She lay in my arms cold and nerveless as a corpse. By degrees sober wits returned.

This was no place for either of us. Let the tremors cease—as they threatened. Let daylight come. Let the panicked recover enough to think of names and gains. Then all the great cost would be wasted. We would be seen. Someone would put a name to Nais, and then it would be easy to guess Deucalion beneath beard and shaggy hair and browned nakedness. By the Gods, I was scared as the veriest trembler when that thought struck.

With ruin spread everywhere, it was hopeless to rely on any secret galleries under the ground; they would be broken, choked, or betrayed by collapse. The old means of passing under the walls was useless. But shouts from the frightened mob, carried through din and darkness, gave me another idea. “The city is accursed,” they cried. “If we stay here it will fall on us. Let us get outside the walls where there are no buildings to bury us.”

If they went, I could not see. But one gate lay nearest the pyramid, and in panic men do not go farther than needful. I slung Nais over my shoulder to leave my right arm free and blundered off through the stifling dark.

It was hard to keep direction. It was hard to walk over ground tossed like a frozen sea. As the earth still quaked, it was hard to keep footing. If I fell a score of times, my dear burden got no bruise. At last I reached the skirts of the square and found a street I knew. The worst of the shaking was done; no more buildings fell, but enough lay sprawled across the roadway to turn walking into climbing. Sweat rolled from me as I laboured.

There was no difficulty at the gate. There was no gate. There was no wall. The Gods had driven Their plough through it, and it lay flat. Proud Atlantis stood as defenceless as open country. Though I knew the cause, though I had in some measure incited it, I was almost sad at the ruthlessness. The pyramid might go; houses and palaces might be levelled—these I could spare. But when I saw the stately ramparts also filched away, the soldier in me woke and grieved at the humbling of the mighty city that once had been my only mistress.

It was a passing regret. I had a different love now, wrapped round me deeper and tighter. My duty was toward her first. Night would soon be past, and then danger would increase. None had interfered with us so far, though many had jostled me as I clambered over ruins. That forbearance could not last. The tremors were dying, and after panic and storm comes the time for spoiling.

The poor seize what lies nearest. Those of higher station—and any soldiers still true to command—strip and stop any man they see making off with plunder. I had no mind to clash with such guardians of law and property. I fled swiftly through the night with my burden, taking unfrequented ways, crying to the few who met me that the woman had the plague and would they lend shelter, since ours had fallen. The word “plague” was understood in any tongue; fear translates quickly.

So in time we came to the place where the rope dangled from the precipice. Nais was drawn up to the Sacred Mountain’s safety, and then I put my leg into the loop and followed.

Now came the keenest anxiety. We carried the girl and laid her upon a bed in one of the houses, and there, in a room lit cleanly for the first time, I saw her clearly.

Her beauty was drawn and pale. Her eyes were closed, but so thin and transparent had the lids grown that one could almost see the brown of the pupil beneath. Her hair had grown to inordinate thickness and length and lay as a cushion behind and beside her head, a dark, heavy abundance like stored grain left too long in a bin. There was no flicker of breath, no pulsing of life; and yet she did not look like ordinary death. The Nais I had placed nine years before beneath the stone had been a fine grown woman, full-bosomed and well boned. The Nais that remained was half her weight. The old Nais would have puzzled me to carry an hour; this was no burden to impede a man.

In other ways she had altered. The nails of her fingers had grown to great length and twisted in spirals, like vine tendrils seeking a trellis that was not there. Her fingers and hands were waxy and transparent so that the bony core showed beneath the flesh in dull outline. Her lips were clay-cold and so white that I sighed to remember their carmine. Her shoulders and neck had lost their comely curves; bony hollows had formed where dust of entombment lodged black and thick.

Reverently I set about preparing those things which, if all went well, should restore her. I heated water and filled a bath, and tinctured it heavily with those essences of the life of beasts which the Priests extract and store against urgent need and sickness—sharp, animal-sweet spirits that rose with the steam and struck the nostrils like the memory of fresh blood and warm hide, like broth made from marrow bones simmered until it gives up its strength. I laid her chin-deep in this bath and sat beside it to watch, keeping the water at a constant blood heat.
