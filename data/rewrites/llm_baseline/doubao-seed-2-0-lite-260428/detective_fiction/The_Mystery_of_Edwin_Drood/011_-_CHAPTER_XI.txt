Tucked behind the oldest, gabled stretch of Holborn—where 300-year-old houses still press their weathered faces to the street, as if lingering to grieve the long-dried Old Bourne stream they once overlooked—lies Staple Inn: two lopsided, quiet quadrangles sealed off from the city’s roar. Step off the clattering pavement, and you feel instantly as if you’ve stuffed cotton in your ears and slipped velvet soles over your boots. A handful of smoky sparrows hop in equally smoky little trees, twittering to each other as if playing at country, their patch of garden mould and gravel a tiny, make-believe rural escape. It is one of London’s old legal nooks, home to a tiny hall with a lantern slung from its roof, whose exact obstructive purpose no one in this story will ever bother to explain.

Back when the nearby town of Cloisterham was still fuming at the very existence of a distant railroad, treating it like an insult to the sensitive, contradictory British national character that panics over progress, brags about it, and complains about it in equal measure no matter what happens anywhere in the world—Staple Inn sat unshadowed by tall new buildings. The westering sun spilled over its roofs, and the south-west wind blew through its courtyards unobstructed.

Not so on a foggy December afternoon, close to six o’clock. The whole of Staple Inn was swallowed in thick yellow smog, every occupied chamber glowing with dim, blurred candlelight through frosted panes. The murk clung thickest in a corner house in the inner quadrangle, its ugly black-and-white portal carved with the mysterious inscription:

Sitting by his fire in the chambers behind that door, Mr. Grewgious paid the carving no mind save the occasional passing, idle thought that it might stand for Perhaps John Thomas, or Perhaps Joe Tyler. No one looking at him could have guessed if he had ever known ambition or heartbreak. Trained for the Bar, he had set out to build a conveyancing practice, to draw deeds and transfer property, as Shakespeare’s Pistol once put it. But Conveyancing had never taken to him: coy as a shy suitor, she never turned up, and the two had parted amicably before they ever really met. Then an unexpected arbitration case landed in his lap, and he won widespread acclaim for his relentless, fair pursuit of the truth, until a plump, well-paid receivership for a wealthy estate fell right into his hands. By pure chance, he had found his niche. Now he acted as agent and receiver for two large fortunes, passed all the actual legal work to a solicitor’s firm on the floor below, snuffed out whatever faint ambition he might once have lit, and settled down for the rest of his days under the dry, unassuming shadow of whoever P.J.T. had been, back in 1747.

His office was lined with neatly stacked account books, correspondence files, and heavy iron strongboxes, arranged so precisely they never cluttered the space. The mere thought of dropping dead mid-calculation, leaving a single fact or figure incomplete or unclear, would have struck Mr. Grewgious stone dead on the spot. Absolute fidelity to every trust he held was the very lifeblood in his veins—fancier, faster, more sparkling kinds of lifeblood existed, but none ran truer. There was no luxury in the room, only the bare comforts of dry air, a warm hearth, and a faded but snug fireside corner. After business hours, a round mahogany occasional table, usually kept propped in a corner like a polished shield, would be dragged out onto the rug, and a closet behind it almost always held a bottle of good drink. His clerk worked in the outer room, he slept across the common staircase, and his cellar down the stairs was almost never empty. At least 300 days a year, he trundled over to the hotel in Furnival’s Inn for dinner, then trundled back to his quiet, simple rooms until the next workday dawned.

That foggy afternoon, Mr. Grewgious wrote by his fire, and his clerk Bazzard wrote by his own fire next door. Pale, puffy-faced, dark-haired, and somewhere in his thirties, Bazzard had big dull dark eyes and a doughy, unsatisfied complexion that looked like it was begging to be sent off to a baker’s oven. He was a strange, inexplicable presence, with some unspoken power over his employer: like a magical familiar summoned by a spell that had fizzled out before anyone could cast the dismissal, he clung to his stool despite the fact that getting rid of him would have made Grewgious’s life far more comfortable. Gloomy, with tangled dark locks and an air of having been raised under the shade of the toxic upas tree of Java, said to shelter more lies than the entire rest of the plant kingdom, Bazzard was treated by Grewgious with a baffling level of gentle consideration.

“Bazzard,” Grewgious said, looking up from his papers as his clerk stepped through the door, “what’s blowing in besides this fog?”

“Mr. Drood,” Bazzard said.

“What about him?”

“He called,” Bazzard said.

“You could have shown him in at once.”

“I am doing it now,” Bazzard replied, and stepped aside to let the visitor enter.

“Dear me!” Grewgious said, peering around the pair of tallow candles on his desk. “I thought you’d called, left your name, and headed off again. How do you do, Mr. Edwin? Good grief, you look like you’re choking!”

“It’s this fog,” Edwin gasped, wiping his stinging eyes. “Makes them burn like someone stuffed cayenne pepper right in them.”

“Terrible stuff. Take off your wrappers, sit close to the fire. Lucky I’ve got a good blaze going here—though Bazzard’s supposed to tend it for me.”

“No I haven’t,” Bazzard called flatly from the doorway.

“Ah, then that means I’ve been taking care of it myself without noticing,” Grewgious laughed. “Take the easy chair by the corner, won’t you? You came straight out of that murk, you need the warmth.”

Edwin collapsed into the soft chair, and the fog clinging to his greatcoat and neck-shawl was swiftly devoured by the hungry fire.

“I look like I’m planning to move in,” Edwin said, grinning.

“Speaking of which,” Grewgious cut in, “do stay. The fog might clear in an hour or two, but until then, we can send out for dinner from the hotel across Holborn. Far better you eat here choking on good soup than out there choking on coal smoke. Stay, please.”

“You’re far too kind,” Edwin said, glancing around, the impromptu, cozy little party clearly appealing to him.

“Not at all, you’re doing me a favor joining a grumpy old bachelor for potluck,” Grewgious said, his eyes crinkling with a sudden bright thought, and he dropped his voice. “I’ll ask Bazzard to join us too. He might take it the wrong way if I don’t. Bazzard!”

The clerk reappeared.

“Come dine with Mr. Drood and me in a few minutes.”

“If I’m ordered to, sir, of course I will,” Bazzard replied gloomily.

“Good heavens, man, you’re invited, not commanded!” Grewgious protested.

“Thank you, sir,” Bazzard said. “In that case, I don’t mind if I do.”

“Perfect. Would you mind popping over to the Furnival’s Inn hotel, then, and telling them to send over everything we need for a dinner setup? Get the hottest, strongest soup they have on hand, their best fancy side dish, a haunch of mutton, and a goose or turkey or whatever stuffed roast they’ve got in the larder—just send every hearty thing they can spare.”

He gave these orders in the same flat, rote tone he used to read through inventory lists or recite business checklists. Bazzard dragged the round table out onto the rug, then left to run the errand.

“I was careful not to send him on this food errand without framing it right,” Grewgious whispered to Edwin once Bazzard was out of earshot. “He might have taken it as a slight, otherwise.”

“He seems to get his own way around here, sir,” Edwin remarked.

“His own way? Goodness no, poor fellow, you’ve got him all wrong,” Grewgious said. “If he had his own way, he wouldn’t be working here at all.”

Edwin wondered where on earth Bazzard would be, but kept the thought to himself as Grewgious stepped over to the fire, leaned his shoulders against the mantel, and settled in for a casual chat.

“I don’t have the gift of prophecy, but I’m guessing you stopped by to tell me you’re heading back down to Cloisterham soon—where I can tell you for a fact everyone’s expecting you—and offer to run any little errand for me to my ward Rosa, maybe nudge me along on any paperwork that needs sorting? Eh, Mr. Edwin?”

“I stopped by as a courtesy, sir, before I travel down,” Edwin said.

“Courtesy, of course,” Grewgious said. “Not impatience, though?”

“Impatience, sir?”

Grewgious had been trying to make a playful little jab, and had leaned so close to the fire trying to sell the joke that he almost singed his coat, as if trying to burn the wittiness into the fabric. But the cool, unreadable look on Edwin’s face made his little playful mood vanish instantly, and he yelped and rubbed his singed sleeve.

“I was down in Cloisterham not long ago,” Grewgious said, rearranging his coat tails to cover the burnt spot, “that’s what I mean when I say they’re all waiting for you there.”

“Indeed? I knew Pussy was looking out for me,” Edwin said casually.

“Pussy? Do you keep a cat down there?” Grewgious asked, confused.

Edwin flushed. “No, that’s my nickname for Rosa.”

“Oh, that’s very… friendly of you,” Grewgious said, smoothing down the hair on his head, his face as impassive as a clock face.

“It’s just a pet name, sir,” Edwin explained, uncomfortable.

“Mmph,” Grewgious nodded, in that ambiguous way that said he neither approved nor disapproved, leaving Edwin thoroughly disconcerted.

“Did Rosa—” Edwin stumbled, trying to recover.

“Rosa?” Grewgious repeated.

“I was going to say Pussy and changed my mind,” Edwin said, flustered. “Did she tell you anything about the Landless siblings?”

“No,” Grewgious said. “What are the Landlesses? An estate? A villa? A farm?”

“A brother and sister. The sister’s staying at the Nuns’ House, she’s become great friends with Rosa—”

“Rosa’s,” Grewgious cut in, his face perfectly still.

“She’s strikingly handsome, sir, I thought you might have heard Rosa talk about her, or even met her by now.”

“Neither,” Grewgious said. “Ah, here’s Bazzard.”

Bazzard stepped back inside with two waiters in tow: one completely immovable, glum and grand, and the other a harried, running fellow who seemed to never stop flying across Holborn. The three of them brought in so much fog that the fire roared higher, starved for air. The flying waiter laid the tablecloth in seconds, polishing every glass until it shone, while the immovable waiter who had brought nothing nitpicked every single thing he did. The flying waiter darted back across Holborn for the soup, then darted back for the side dish, then again for the roast, making extra detours every time the immovable waiter remembered he had forgotten some last little necessary item. No matter how fast the poor man ran, the immovable waiter always snapped at him for bringing fog in and being out of breath. By the end of the meal, the flying waiter was gasping for air, and the immovable waiter swept the tablecloth under his arm with a grand, indignant air, staring at Grewgious like he was clearly demanding the entire tip for himself, not a penny for the overworked slave he’d been lording over. Then he herded the flying waiter out the door ahead of him.

The whole little performance was like a perfect miniature oil painting of the British Circumlocution Office, of government commanderships, of every useless, overpaid bureaucrat who ever took credit for a subordinate’s work. It was good enough to hang on the line in the National Gallery.

The fog itself was the best sauce for the meal: the sound of clerks outside sneezing, wheezing, and stamping their feet on the gravel tasted spicier than any famous chef’s recipe. Grewgious trundled down to his cellar and came back up with bottles of ruby red wine, pale straw white, and sparkling golden champagne, all of which had ripened decades ago in sunlit, fog-free countries, and slumbered in his dark cellar ever since. The wines pushed at their corks when he popped them, as eager to escape as prisoners helping rioters break down their jail gates, and spilled out bright and dancing. If whoever P.J.T. was had drunk wine this good back in 1747, he was definitely Pretty Jolly Too.

Grewgious’s wooden, expressionless face didn’t shift an inch even after a few glasses, you’d never guess he’d had a drop. But his eyes were sharp, watching Edwin all the while, and when dinner wrapped up, he gestured Edwin back to his easy chair, watching the young man between his fingers as he smoothed down his hair.

“Bazzard!” Grewgious said, suddenly turning to his clerk.

“I’m right with you, sir,” Bazzard mumbled, who had plowed through his entire dinner in near total silence.

“I drink to you, Bazzard. Mr. Edwin, success to Mr. Bazzard!”

“Success to Mr. Bazzard!” Edwin echoed, with forced enthusiasm, silently wondering success at what, exactly.

“May—” Grewgious continued, fumbling for words, “I can’t be specific here, my conversational skills are terrible, no imagination at all—but may… the thorn of anxiety I’ve been carrying… finally work its way out.”

Bazzard frowned at the fire, ran a hand through his tangled hair like the thorn was stuck there, then patted his waistcoat, then his pockets, as if searching for it. Edwin stared at him the whole time, half expecting the man to yank a literal thorn out of his clothes, but nothing came of it, and Bazzard only said, “I follow you, sir, and I thank you.”

“I’m about to drink a toast to my ward,” Grewgious whispered to Edwin, leaning in. “I put Bazzard first, you see. He might take it the wrong way if I didn’t.” He winked, or tried to, as much of a wink as his stiff, slow-moving face could manage, and Edwin winked back, not having the faintest clue what the man meant.

“Now then, a bumper toast to the fair and fascinating Miss Rosa!” Grewgious announced. “Bazzard, the fair and fascinating Miss Rosa!”

“I follow you, sir,” Bazzard said. “I pledge you.”

“And so do I,” Edwin said.

“Good Lord,” Grewgious said, breaking the awkward silence that followed the toast, “I’m a terribly angular, stiff old man, but I think—even without a scrap of imagination—that I could paint you a picture of what a true lover’s state of mind ought to be, tonight.”

“Let’s hear it, sir,” Bazzard said. “Show us the picture.”

“Mr. Edwin here can correct me where I get it wrong, add in the real life touches I’m bound to miss,” Grewgious went on. “I was born a block of uncarved chip, no soft sympathies, no soft romantic experiences. Well, my first guess is that a true lover’s whole mind is saturated with the person he loves. Their name is precious to him, he can’t hear it or say it without feeling something, he keeps it sacred. Any private little pet name he has for her? That’s for her, and her alone. Something it would be a privilege to call her, alone, in private? Using it casually out in the open, to show off, would be rude, cold, almost a breach of faith.”

He sat bolt upright, hands on his knees, chopping out every sentence like a charity schoolboy reciting his catechism by rote, no emotion visible on his face save the occasional tiny twitch at the tip of his nose.

“My picture goes on,” he continued, “to show the true lover as impatient to be near the person he loves, bored and listless in any other company, always seeking her out. I’d say he seeks her the way a bird seeks its nest, but that’s poetry, and I’ve never gotten within ten thousand miles of poetry in my life. Besides, I don’t know the first thing about bird habits, save the sparrows in Staple Inn that nest in gutters and chimneys, not natural nests at all. So no bird analogies. But my picture says the true lover can’t imagine a life separate from the woman he loves—he lives a doubled life, and a halved life at the same time. If I’m not making sense, it’s not that I don’t mean what I say, I’m just bad at saying it.”

Edwin flushed bright red, then pale, as Grewgious’s words sank in, and he sat staring hard at the fire, biting his lip.

“An old stiff man’s guesses are probably all wrong about something as soft and round as love,” Grewgious said. “But I picture there’s no room for coolness, or laziness, or doubt, or indifference, no half-fire half-smoke half-heartedness, in a real lover. Am I anywhere close to right in this picture?”

He snapped the question at Edwin abruptly, as if he’d jerked a hidden trap shut, and went completely quiet.

“Sir, since you’re asking me—” Edwin stammered.

“Yes, I’m asking you as an authority on the subject,” Grewgious said.
