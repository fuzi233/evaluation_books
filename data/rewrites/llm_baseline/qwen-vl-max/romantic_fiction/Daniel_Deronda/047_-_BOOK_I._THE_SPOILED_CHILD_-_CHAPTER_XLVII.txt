CHAPTER XLVII. “And you must love him ere to you He will seem worthy of your love.” —WORDSWORTH.

There was something almost tender in the way Daniel Deronda imagined Mordecai dressed in his new clothes—gray flannel shirts, a dressing-gown like a Franciscan’s brown robe, with only his head and neck visible above it. It was as if he were painting a portrait in his mind, not just of the man, but of the moment Mirah would see him. Half of Deronda’s delight came from seeing Mordecai through her eyes—knowing how deeply she would rejoice, untouched by any sense of discomfort or judgment. But after all was arranged, doubt crept in. Was he perhaps mistaken? Was he replacing the deeper truth with mere appearances? Could Mirah not feel the same reverence for her brother precisely because of the poverty that marked him—the quiet dignity of a life lived apart, shaped by hardship and memory?

Yet there were others to consider. The Meyricks, kind-hearted though they were, might find Mordecai too foreign, too unlike their own world. And Deronda found himself quietly shielding Mordecai from anything that might provoke their prejudice—anything that could stir up that instinctive aversion felt by those who lack the gift of seeing beyond surface, who cannot bathe even the humblest thing in the golden light of compassion and awe. That ability, born of love and reverence, was rare—and it was exactly what Deronda saw in Mirah.

That evening, long after Mirah had gone to bed, the household gathered around Mrs. Meyrick’s fire. Hans, now settled in his Chelsea rooms, lingered on. With the flames dancing, Mrs. Meyrick said, “Now, Kate, put out your candle, and come close. Hans, dear, stop laughing at those poems—just once, please—and join us. I have something wonderful to tell.”

“Oh, I know,” Kate replied, smiling. “I’ve seen it in your eyes for days. You’ve been pretending to run errands when you really wanted to wait until we were all together.”

The girls drew near, feet warming on the fender. Hans pushed his chair closer, sitting astride it, chin resting on his fists. “Well then,” said Mrs. Meyrick, speaking clearly, “if you’re so clever, maybe you already know that Mirah’s brother has been found.”

“Confound it!” Hans burst out, nearly jumping up.

“Hans, that’s cruel,” Mab scolded. “Suppose we’d lost you?”

“I can’t help feeling sorry,” Kate admitted softly. “But where is her mother?”

“She’s dead,” Mrs. Meyrick said gently.

“And the brother?” Amy asked. “Is he a nice man?”

“Not some flashy fellow covered in jewelry—a Crystal Palace Assyrian with a hat,” Hans muttered, annoyed.

“You unfeeling children!” Mrs. Meyrick sighed. “You don’t think of Mirah’s joy at this news.”

“But Mirah hardly remembers him,” Kate reminded her.

“People lost for twelve years should never come back,” Hans declared. “They always get in the way.”

“Hans!” she reproached him. “If I’d been missing twenty years, you’d feel differently.”

“I said twelve,” Hans interrupted sharply. “Twelve years—that’s the time when lost relatives ought to stay away.”

“Well, finding people is still nice,” Mab said, clasping her knees. “It means there’s a story to tell.”

“Did Prince Camaralzaman find him?” she asked.

Then Mrs. Meyrick told them everything she knew—how Mr. Deronda had helped bring Mordecai into their lives, how he admired him deeply, and how he believed Mirah was the sister who could truly understand him.

“Deronda is getting completely absurd about Jews,” Hans declared, rising abruptly and slamming his chair back. “He wants to encourage Mirah in every one of her strange beliefs.”

“Oh, shame on you, Hans!” Mab protested. “To speak like that of Mr. Deronda!”

Mrs. Meyrick’s face flickered with an emotion she quickly hid—something between pride and sorrow.

“And now,” Hans continued, pacing with hands in his pockets, “we’ll never be all together again. We’ll have to invite this prophet Elijah for tea, and Mirah will spend all her time dreaming of Jerusalem’s ruins. She’ll become narrow-minded—like a nun. Everything will change—our home, our peace. I’ll start drinking.”

“Oh, Hans,” Kate said, exasperated. “Men are the most selfish creatures alive. They must have everything their way, or they become unbearable.”

“Oh, oh, oh—it’s dreadful!” Mab cried. “I feel as if ancient Nineveh has returned.”

“If you went to university and learned so much,” Amy said, “you’d think you could be more mature. You should learn to accept the people Providence sends us. _We_ will have to accept him.”

“I hope you all enjoy the new Lamentations of Jeremiah—‘to be continued in our next,’” Hans said, grabbing his wide-awake hat. “It’s no use being educated if you have to endure men who stare at you with fixed ideas, demanding every word you say be a footnote to their sermon. If you’re under a petrifying wall, better be an old boot. I don’t feel like an old boot.”

Then, suddenly, “Good night, little mother,” he whispered, bending to kiss her brow quickly—almost desperately. On his way out, he added, “Good night, girls.”

“Suppose Mirah knew how you’re behaving,” Kate called after him.

His reply was the slam of the door.

“I wish I could see Mirah when Mr. Deronda tells her,” Kate said, turning to her mother. “She’ll look so beautiful.”

But Deronda, reconsidering, had written a letter the previous night—asking Mrs. Meyrick to break the news herself, rather than waiting for him. He didn’t reveal the real reason: that he feared repeating a tale in which he sounded too grand, too self-sacrificing. Instead, he said he wished to stay with Mordecai while Mrs. Meyrick brought Mirah, so they could meet privately before any sudden changes in their living arrangements.

Still, Deronda wondered—how would Mordecai react? After years of isolation, consumed by thought and weakened by illness, would he allow himself to feel warmth for his sister beyond duty? Deronda had noticed Mordecai’s affection for the Cohens, especially little Jacob—proof that his heart still held room for love. But these were familiar faces, part of his daily life. Now, Mirah was different. Mordecai asked no questions about her. He remained unusually silent, accepting the changes without comment. He wore the new clothes obediently, but later smiled faintly and said, “I’ll keep my old garments—just for memory.”

When they sat waiting, Mordecai kept his eyes closed, yet his face twitched with tension. His hands moved restlessly. He was experiencing a deep inner disturbance—one known only to minds long fixed in a single current, suddenly forced into a new channel. For those whose strength lies in long-held habits, such a revival of the past can feel like a threat—like an illness. Joy may come, but joy too can be terrifying.

Deronda felt the tremor of excitement. When the doorbell rang, he stepped out—not knowing why—so he could greet Mirah first.

He was startled to see her wearing the very hat and cloak he had first seen her in—the one that had once been soaked and used as a shroud. She had come down in that dress deliberately. When Mrs. Meyrick asked, “You like to go in that dress, dear?” Mirah answered quietly, “My brother is poor. I want to look like him. Otherwise, he might feel distant.”

Deronda could not respond. He felt a quiet shame for his own careful preparations.

They shook hands silently. Mirah looked pale, overwhelmed. When Deronda opened the door, Mordecai rose slowly, his eyes fixed on the entrance.

Mirah took only a few steps—then stopped.

They stood motionless, not quite seeing each other, but meeting in the echo of memory. Their presence was less important than the past they carried.

Mirah spoke first, in the same gentle tone she had used when telling of her mother’s call: “Ezra.”

Mordecai moved forward swiftly, placing his hand on her shoulders. He was taller, looking down at her with tenderness. “That was our mother’s voice,” he said softly. “Do you remember her calling me?”

“Yes,” Mirah whispered. “And how you answered—‘Mother!’—and I knew you loved her.”

She threw her arms around his neck, clasped her hands behind his head, and pulled him down, kissing his face with innocent, joyful abandon. Her hat fell backward, revealing her curls.

“Ah, the dear head, the dear head,” Mordecai murmured, touching her hair gently.

“You’re very ill, Ezra,” Mirah said, her eyes full of concern.

“Yes, dear child,” he replied calmly. “I shall not be long with you in the body.”

“Oh, I’ll love you,” she said, her voice sweet and steady. “We’ll talk. I’ll tell you everything. And you’ll teach me—how to be a good Jewess. What Mother would have wanted me to be. I’ll be with you whenever I’m not working. I work now. I’ll earn money to keep us. Oh, I’ve had such good friends.”

Until then, Mirah had forgotten they were not alone. Now she turned, keeping one hand on her brother’s arm, and looked toward Mrs. Meyrick and Deronda. The little mother’s heart swelled with happiness at the sight—this reunion, this fragile bond restored. To her, Mordeci seemed more dignified, more refined than she had dared imagine, based only on Deronda’s descriptions.

“See this dear lady,” Mirah said. “I was a stranger, a poor wanderer. She believed in me, treated me like a daughter. Please give my brother your hand,” she added, taking Mrs. Meyrick’s hand and placing it in Mordecai’s, then pressing both hands to her lips.

“The Eternal Goodness has been with you,” Mordecai said softly. “You have helped fulfill our mother’s prayer.”

“Let’s go now,” Deronda said gently, laying a hand on Mrs. Meyrick’s arm. “We’ll return later.”

She nodded at once. Deronda did not want to risk mentioning anything about himself that he had withheld from Mordecai. And now, he felt no unease at leaving them alone—together at last.
