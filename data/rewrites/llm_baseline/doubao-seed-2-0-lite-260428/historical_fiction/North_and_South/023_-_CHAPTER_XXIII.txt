Margaret Hale had not been gone five minutes when Mr. Thornton burst through his own front door, face glowing hot from exertion and the raw, lingering thrum of the riot. “I could not come sooner — the superintendent would not release me— Where is she?” He scanned the empty dining room in two quick strides, then fixed his mother with a near-fierce gaze, as she slowly straightened the furniture the mob had knocked askew, and did not answer at once. “Where is Miss Hale?” he repeated, sharper this time.

“Gone home,” she said shortly.

“Gone home?” The shock sent him pacing back and forth across the rug, his boots thudding hard against the floorboards. “She could not possibly have been fit to walk that far.”

“She had a cab,” Mrs. Thornton said. “Mr. Lowe signed off that she was well enough to travel. I fetched him myself, you know. Her injury was never half so bad as it looked — some people faint dead away at the smallest knock, that is all.”

“Thank you for that, mother.” He paused, half extending his hand to shake hers in gratitude, but she did not notice the gesture, her eyes fixed straight ahead.

“What have you done for the Irish labourers caught up in the crowd?” she asked.

“Sent them all to the Dragon Inn for a hot square meal, poor wretches. By some luck I ran into Father Grady on the street right after, and persuaded him to come speak to them, to talk them out of clearing out of Milton as a group. Margaret could not have walked home, surely?”

“Everything was paid for and seen to properly, never you fear,” she said, dismissive. “Let us talk of something else. That girl has caused more than enough disturbance this afternoon.”

“I do not know what would have become of me but for her,” Thornton said quietly.

“Are you so helpless now that you must be defended by a slip of a girl?” his mother scoffed, her voice sharp with scorn.

He flushed bright red. “Not one girl in a hundred would have thrown herself between the blows meant for me, blows that were struck with full, vicious intent.”

“A girl in love will do a great many foolish things,” Mrs. Thornton replied, brief and unyielding.

“Mother!” He stepped forward, then froze, his whole frame heaving with suppressed passion. She started a little at the obvious, enormous effort he was expending just to hold his temper in check. She could not parse the tangle of emotion on his face — joy, anger, pride, wild surprise, breathless doubt all warring beneath his glowing eyes, his chest rising and falling fast — only its raw, unnameable intensity. It made her uneasy, as any strong feeling whose root you do not fully understand always does. She crossed to the sideboard, pulled open a drawer, and took out a duster stowed there for odd jobs, pretending to wipe a stray drop of eau de Cologne off the polished sofa arm. She kept her back turned to him far longer than the task required, and when she spoke her voice was tight, constrained, nothing like her usual crisp, decisive tone.

“I suppose you have taken steps about the rioters? You do not expect more violence tonight, surely? Where were the police, anyway? Never around when you actually need them!”

“On the contrary, I saw three or four of them struggling to hold the crowd back the second the yard gates gave way, and a dozen more came running just as the mob scattered. I could have arrested half a dozen of the ringleaders right then if I had been thinking straight. There will be no trouble identifying them later, plenty of witnesses saw their faces.”

“Will they not come back after dark?”

“I am off to arrange for a full, permanent guard on the mill premises. I am meeting Captain Hanbury at the station in half an hour.”

“Sit and have some tea first,” she said. “You cannot run about on an empty stomach.”

“Tea — yes, I suppose I must. It is half past six now, and I may be out for hours. Do not sit up waiting for me, mother.”

“You would have me go to bed before I know you are safe?” she retorted.

He hesitated. “Well, perhaps not. But if I have time after sorting the police arrangements and meeting Hamper and Clarkson, I was going to swing round by Crampton.” Their eyes locked, and they stared at each other intently for a long, quiet minute.

“Why would you go to Crampton?” she asked.

“To ask after Miss Hale.”

“I will send Williams. He can take that water-bed Margaret came to beg for, and ask after her state while he is there.”

“I must go myself.”

“Not merely to ask how she fares?”

“No. Not merely that. I have to thank her for the way she stood between me and that mob.”

“What on earth possessed you to go down to the yard in the first place? You might as well have stuck your head straight in the lion’s mouth!”

He glanced at her sharply, and saw that she had no idea of the quiet, charged exchange he and Margaret had shared in his drawing room before the riot turned violent. He deflected the question smoothly. “Will you be afraid to stay here alone without me until the police patrol arrives? Or ought I send Williams for them now, so they can be here by the time we finish tea? There is no time to waste, I have to be out the door in fifteen minutes.”

Mrs. Thornton left the room, her directions to the servants muddled and uncertain — a stark contrast to her usual sharp, unwavering orders. Left alone, Thornton stared at the dining table, trying to force his mind to focus on the business he had to conduct at the station, but every other thought slipped away back to Margaret: the weight of her arms around his neck, the soft, desperate pressure of her clinging to him, the way the colour rose and fell in his own cheeks every time he replayed the moment. The tea meal would have been utterly silent if not for Fanny chattering nonstop about her own terror that afternoon, how she had been certain they would all be murdered, then thought the mob was gone, then grew so faint and sick her whole body shook.

“That is quite enough,” her brother said, pushing back his chair to stand. “I saw the real thing with my own eyes, I have no need to hear your dramatic retelling of it.” He was half way to the door when his mother laid a hand firm on his arm to stop him.

“Come back here to this house first before you go to the Hales’,” she said, low and anxious. Fanny, hovering in the doorway, smiled to herself, certain she had guessed every secret.

“Why? Will it be too late to disturb them?”

“John, come back to me this one evening. It will be late for Mrs. Hale, that is true, but that is not why I ask. Tomorrow you will — come home first tonight, John!” She almost never pleaded with her son, too proud to beg for anything, but she had never once asked him for something he refused.

“I will come straight back here as soon as I finish my business. You will make sure to ask after them — after her, won’t you?”

When he returned later that night, Mrs. Thornton was unusually attentive, her eyes and ears fixed on every detail he gave of the steps he had taken to secure the mill, to punish the rioters who had dared threaten his property and his life. To him it was simple: punishment was the natural, just consequence for anyone who had joined the riot, and property must be protected, the will of the factory owner cutting clean and sharp as a sword to its end.

“Mother,” he said suddenly, mid-step as he paced the room, “you know what I am going to say to Miss Hale tomorrow?”

The question caught her off guard; for a moment she had forgotten Margaret entirely, lost in her own thoughts. She looked up at him. “Yes. I do. You can hardly do anything else.”

“Do anything else? I do not follow you.”

“I mean that, after letting your feelings for her get so far, after the way she rushed to your defense, you are bound in honour to —”

“Bound in honour?” he laughed, scornful. “I am afraid honour has little to do with it. ‘Her feelings overcame her?’ What feelings are you talking about?”

“Now John, do not be angry. Did she not fling herself at you, clinging to your neck to save you from harm?”

“She did,” he said, his voice softening. “But mother — I dare not let myself hope. I was never faint-hearted about anything before, but I cannot bring myself to believe a creature like that could care for me.”

“Do not be a fool, John. To hear you talk, she might as well be a duke’s daughter! What more proof could you possibly need that she cares for you? I imagine she fought that aristocratic, distant pride of hers tooth and nail before she acted. I respect her all the more for seeing past those silly scruples at last.” She smiled slowly, tears glistening in her eyes. “It is no easy thing for me to say this. After tonight, I know I stand second in your heart. I begged you not to go to her straight away because I wanted you all to myself for just a few more hours.”

“Dearest mother!” But his love was selfish, and in the next breath he was back to his own swirling hopes and fears, casting a cold, quiet shadow over Mrs. Thornton’s heart. “But I know she does not care for me. I will lay myself at her feet, I have to. If there was only one chance in a thousand — in a million — that she might return my feelings, I still have to try.”

“Do not be afraid,” his mother said, crushing down her own sharp, stung mortification at how little notice he had taken of her rare, vulnerable display of maternal love, the bitter pang of jealousy that betrayed how fiercely she hated to lose him. “Do not be afraid. As far as love goes, she is worthy of you. It must have taken an enormous force to break through that stiff pride of hers. Goodnight, John.” She kissed his forehead, then turned and left the room slowly, with her usual stately grace. But once she had locked the door to her own bedchamber, she sank down onto her bed and wept tears she had not let fall in decades.

Margaret slipped into the Hales’ parlour, where her father and mother sat talking quietly together, her face as pale as fresh linen. She walked all the way up to them before she could trust her voice to speak. “Mrs. Thornton will send the water-bed for you, mamma.”

“Dear child, you look so worn out!” Mrs. Hale fussed. “Is the street air that hot today?”

“Very hot. And the roads are rough, what with the strike going on.” A bright, vivid flush rose in Margaret’s cheeks, then faded away just as fast, leaving her paler than before.

“Bessy Higgins sent round a message not long ago, asking if you could come visit her,” her mother said. “But you look far too tired to go out again tonight.”

“Yes,” Margaret said. “I am tired. I cannot go.” She was quiet, trembling faintly as she made tea, and she was grateful to see her father so occupied tending to her mother that he did not notice how unsteady she was. Even after Mrs. Hale went up to bed, Mr. Hale would not leave Margaret alone, insisting on sitting by her to read aloud until she fell asleep.

At last he too retired, and Margaret was left by herself.

“Now I will think about it — now I will remember all of it,” she told herself. She sat perfectly still in her armchair, hands clasped tight on her knees, lips pressed together, eyes fixed on the wall as if she was staring at a vision only she could see. She drew a long, shaky breath. “I, who hate all public scenes — I, who have always despised people for showing unseemly emotion, who thought anyone who lost their self-control was weak — ran down and threw myself into the middle of that crowd like a silly, romantic fool. Did I even do any good? They would have left anyway, I dare say.” But her sharp, well-honed judgment corrected her instantly. No, they might not have. She had done some good. “But what possessed me to defend that man as if he was a helpless child! No wonder all those rioters thought I was madly in love with him! Me, in love — with him, of all people!” Her pale cheeks burst into flame, and she covered her face with her hands. When she pulled them away, her palms were wet with scalding tears.

“Oh how shameful, that they would all think that of me! I never would have been that brave for anyone else — except that he was so utterly indifferent to the suffering of the men on strike, so blind to how desperate they were, that I could not stand by and let unfairness happen on either side. It was not fair that he should stand there sheltered, waiting for the soldiers to trap those poor, maddened rioters like animals, without even trying to reason with them. And it was worse than unfair for the mob to turn on him and beat him as they threatened. I would do it all again, no matter what anyone says of me. If I stopped even one cruel blow, one needless act of violence, I did a woman’s work. Let them insult my maiden pride all they like. I walk pure before God.” A noble, quiet peace settled over her face, stiller and calmer than chiselled marble.

Dixon knocked and stepped in. “If you please, Miss Margaret, the water-bed has arrived from Mrs. Thornton’s. It is too late to set it up for your mother tonight, I am afraid, she is nearly fast asleep, but it will do her a world of good tomorrow.”

“Thank you. Send our best thanks back to them,” Margaret said. Dixon stepped out for a moment, then returned. “The man who brought it says he is under orders to ask particularly how you are, Miss Margaret. I think he must have meant Mrs. Thornton, but he kept repeating he had to ask after you, specifically.”

“Me?” Margaret drew herself up straight, her voice cold and distant. “I am quite well. Tell him I am perfectly well.” But her face was as white as the linen handkerchief tucked in her pocket, and her head throbbed so fiercely she could barely see straight.

Mr. Hale came in then, fresh from checking on his sleeping wife, eager for Margaret to tell him some light, pleasant story to pass the evening. She bore her pain with gentle patience, not a single word of complaint, digging up dozens of tiny, harmless little topics to chat about — never once mentioning the riot, just thinking of it made her feel sick to her stomach.

“Goodnight, Margaret,” her father said at last, rising to head to his own room. “I shall sleep very well tonight, and you look so pale and worn out from all your watching over us. I will call Dixon if your mother needs anything in the night. Go to bed now and sleep like a top, you poor child, you have earned the rest.”

“Goodnight, papa,” she whispered. She let the false, pleasant smile slip off her face, let her eyes go dull with heavy, throbbing exhaustion. She released the iron grip of her will, which had held her together all evening. Until morning, she did not have to fight the weariness. She lay down on her bed and never stirred. To lift a hand, or a foot, or even a single finger was beyond her power, of will or of muscle. She was so tired, so stunned by the day’s events, that she thought she never slept at all. Her feverish thoughts drifted back and forth across the line between waking and sleeping, replaying the same miserable scenes over and over. She could not escape the feeling that hundreds of eyes were fixed on her, every person from the mob staring at her, judging her, and the shame of it was so acute she half wanted to burrow straight down into the earth to hide — but she could not move, could not slip out from under that unblinking, universal glare.
