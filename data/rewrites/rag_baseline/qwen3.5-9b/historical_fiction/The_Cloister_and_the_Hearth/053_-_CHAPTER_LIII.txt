"Nay, Richard," Catherine finally spoke, her voice cutting through the tension like a knife through silk, "for Heaven's sake, let not this single wretched woman drag us all into the mud. Has she not already poisoned the well with enough malice?"

"In truth, she has," Richard replied, his tone steady despite the storm brewing in the room. "Do not fear, good mother. Let her enter and read the letter of the poor boy she has bewitched with diabolical arts, and then let her depart. Grant me only your word that you will afford her no more than the barest, most constrained civility. We cannot avoid her presence, for she is in our own house; and I promise, I shall say no further word."

With this fragile truce established, they awaited the intruder. Catherine, for her part, armored herself in a spirit barely less hostile than war. When Denys arrived to announce that the household would not visit her but would instead receive her, her lips curled in disdain. She commanded him to note how every petty emotion within them swelled larger than their love for Gerard. "Very well," she muttered, her voice dripping with venom. "I possess no such excuse. Why then should I mimic the petty pride of that bourgeois merchant—the arrogance common to all the unlettered? I shall go to them solely for Gerard's sake. Oh, how I loathe them!"

Thus, poor, good-natured Denys carried the volatile ingredients of an explosion into a single dwelling.

Margaret prepared herself with the solemn gravity of a knight donning armor for battle—he to deflect physical blows, she to parry the invisible arrows of contempt aimed at her poverty or the ironies directed at her perceived extravagance. Her kirtle was woven from English cloth, a deep, somber blue, matched by her farthingale and hose, though the latter were of a glossy roan, a claret hue that spoke of hidden richness. There was not an inch of pretentious fur upon her frame, only plain, snowy linen wristbands and intricately plaited linen framing her neck from the bosom of her gown upward, forming a square collar rather than a circle. Two waves of her front hair escaped, styled in a manner Mary Queen of Scots would revive a century hence; yet, eschewing the silver nets that would have clashed with her current plight, the remainder of her head was veiled in a tiny, tight-fitting hood of dark blue cloth, hemmed with silver thread. Her shoes were red, but the roan petticoat and hose prepared the observer for a visual shock, accentuating the arch of her instep and the elegance of her foot.

Beauty, even in adversity, knows its own business.

Despite this striking appearance, she kept her enemies waiting until the clock struck three.

Finally, she moved forward, accompanied by her male comrade. Halfway across the threshold, she halted, her expression thoughtful. "Denys!"

"Well, she-general?"

"I must return home," she whispered, the plea etched into her voice.

"What? Have you left something behind?"

"What?"

"My courage. Oh! Oh! Oh!"

"Nay, nay, be brave, she-general. I shall walk beside you."

"Aye, but will you stay close when I arrive?"

Denys promised, and she resumed her march, though with cautious, tentative steps.

Meanwhile, inside, the household gathered, a tableau of conflicting emotions: mortification mingled with curiosity, panting affection twisted by aversion to the woman who came to satisfy those very feelings, yet driven by another curiosity to see what magic could bewitch Gerard and unleash such chaos.

At last, Denys entered alone, whispering, "The female comrade waits without."

"Bring her in," Eli commanded gravely. "Now, silence. No one speaks to her save me."

All eyes turned to the door in a deathly hush. A faint murmur drifted from the hallway—a clash between Denys's rough baritone and a woman's soft, mellifluous voice. Soon, the sounds ceased. Slowly, the door swung open, and Margaret Brandt stepped onto the threshold. Dressed as described, she appeared somewhat pale, yet radiating a profound calm and loveliness. She looked straight ahead, meeting no gaze.

They all rose except Kate, who remained mute and staring.

"Be seated, mistress," Eli said solemnly, gesturing to a chair reserved specifically for her.

She bowed her head and crossed the room. In doing so, her condition became undeniable—not merely in her form, but in the palpable languor that weighed upon her. Cornelis and Sybrandt despised her for it; Richard felt it marred her beauty. Yet, strangely, it softened the women.

She withdrew her letter from her bosom, kissing it with a tenderness reserved for solitude, before settling to read it with the air of one fulfilling a singular, sacred duty.

As she began, she noted they had isolated her, seating her alone like a leper. She glanced at Denys, lowered her hand to her side, and made a swift, furtive gesture inviting him closer.

He responded with an obedient start, as if she had shouted "March!", and stood at her shoulder like a sentinel. However, this zealous display revealed to the company that he had been ordered there, and at that revelation, she flushed crimson. Now, she began to read of Gerard, *their* Gerard, to their rapturous ears. Her voice was mellow, clear, so soft and earnest that her very soul seemed to cling to every precious syllable. It was the voice of a woman's bosom given speech by Heaven itself.

"I do not doubt, my Margaret, that long before this meets thy beloved eyes, Denys, my dearest friend, will have sought thee out and recounted the manner of our unexpected and most tearful parting. Therefore, I shall begin at that most sorrowful day. What befell him afterward, poor faithful soul, I fain would hear, but may not. Yet I pray for him day and night, next after thee, dearest. A friend more steadfast and loving had David never known than I in him. Be good to him, for poor Gerard's sake."

At these words, which struck Denys with unexpected force, he leaned his head against Margaret's high chair and groaned aloud.

She turned quickly in her seat, found his hand, and pressed it tightly.

So the sweetheart and the friend held hands while the sweetheart read.

"I proceeded forward, dizzy and disoriented, like one trapped in a nightmare. Presently, a gentleman approached with his servants, all mounted on horseback, and nearly rode over me. He drew rein at the brow of the hill and sent his armed men back to strip me. They robbed me civilly enough, taking my purse and the last copper coin, then rode gaily away. I wandered on, a stuporous, friendless pauper."

A collective sigh rippled through the room, followed by a sharp oath from Denys.

"Shortly thereafter, a strange dimness enveloped me. I lay down to sleep upon the snow. It was ill-advised, with packs of wolves lurking nearby. If I had loved thee as thou deservest, I would have shown more manhood. But oh, sweet love, the drowsiness that crawled over my desolate, benumbed senses was greater than nature intended. Thus I slept; and but for God's mercy, superior to mine own or thyself, from that sleep I never woke, so all say. I had slept an hour or two, I suppose, when a hand shook me rudely. I awoke to my troubles. Standing there was a serving maid in her holiday attire. 'Are ye mad,' she said, seemingly in anger, 'to sleep in the snow beneath wolves' noses? Are ye weary of life, and not long weaned? Come, now,' she said, more kindly, 'rise like a good lad;' so I did rise. 'Are ye rich, or are ye poor?' But I stared at her as one amazed. 'Why, 'tis easy of reply,' she said. 'Are ye rich, or are ye poor?' Then I gave a great, loud cry, causing her to start back. 'Am I rich, or am I poor? Had ye asked me an hour ago, I would have said I am rich. But now I am so poor that surely no earth bears upon her bosom one poorer. An hour ago I was rich in a friend, rich in money, rich in hope and the spirits of youth; but now the Bastard of Burgundy has taken my friend, and another gentleman my purse; and I can neither proceed to Rome nor return to the one I left in Holland. I am poorest of the poor.' 'Alack!' said the wench. 'Nevertheless, if ye had been rich, ye might have lain down again in the snow for any use I had for ye; and then I trow ye would soon fare out of this world as bare as ye came into it. But, being poor, you are our man: so come with me.' Then I went because she bade me, and because I cared no longer whither I went. She took me to a fine house nearby, into a noble dining hall hung with black tapestry. There was set a table with many dishes, but only one plate and one chair. 'Fall to!' she whispered. 'What, alone?' I asked. 'Alone? And which of us, think ye, would eat out of the same dish with ye? Are we robbers of the dead?' Then she queried where I was born.
