Buck never learned the news the way men did—by ink and paper laid beside a breakfast plate—yet the trouble that came for him began in just that ordinary fashion: words printed far away, read aloud in barber shops and saloons, passing from mouth to mouth until they hardened into hunger. Men, feeling their way through Arctic dark, had found yellow metal, and the companies that lived on freight and fares shouted the find like a tune too loudly played. The sound of it—steam winches, whistles, stamped boots, promises—rolled down the coast. And with it came a craving for dogs: big-bodied, deep-coated dogs that could haul and endure.

In the Santa Clara Valley, where the air held the warm sweetness of sun on grapes and the evenings carried the soft hush of irrigated earth, Buck knew a different music. Judge Miller’s place lay back from the road, wrapped in trees that filtered light into green, steady shade. Gravel under wheels made a polite crunch along the winding drives; poplars clapped their leaves together in breezes; and from the rear of the house there rose the daily chorus of a prosperous estate—stable doors, boys calling, water sluicing, the faint metallic tick of the pumping plant working the artesian well. Even the big cement tank where the Judge’s boys plunged each morning had its own sound: the slap of bodies on water, the gasp, the laughter that echoed off hard walls and then softened into the garden.

Over all of it, Buck moved like the master of a kingdom that never questioned him. He had been born there; four years of good living had rounded his strength and given him the composed pride of one who expects doors to open. Other dogs existed, of course—there were always dogs on a place that large—but they were satellites. Toots, the Japanese pug, and Ysabel, the Mexican hairless, were curiosities of indoor life, peering out windows, guarded by housemaids who hissed and swatted at the fox terriers below. The terriers themselves were a sharp, yelping fringe of noise at the edges of Buck’s authority. He did not waste attention on them. He stalked through them as through dry grass.

He was not a house-dog and not a kennel-dog. He swam when he pleased, shoulder-deep and powerful, sending rings across the tank that spread to the coping stones. He hunted with the Judge’s sons and trotted in the lavender hours beside Mollie and Alice, the Judge’s daughters, when the valley cooled and the scents of crushed grass, damp earth, and ripe fruit thickened in the air. In winter he lay before the library fire and listened to the steady crackle of oak and the low voices of men, a domestic sound that meant safety as surely as the Judge’s hand meant approval. He carried the Judge’s grandsons on his back, endured their squeals and clumsy embraces, and guarded their small, reckless expeditions toward fountain and paddock and berry patch.

His father, Elmo, had been a huge St. Bernard and the Judge’s shadow; Buck, though smaller, took after him in bearing. His mother had been a Scotch shepherd, and her quickness lived in him. One hundred and forty pounds of muscle, wrapped in a warm coat, and the confident dignity that comes from being treated as important—these made him king. He had the fine egotism of the well-fed and well-loved, like a country gentleman who rarely meets a will he cannot overrule. Yet he was saved from becoming soft. He hunted. He ran. He loved water the way some creatures love a tonic—plunging in until the cold tightened his skin and left his blood singing.

So it was in the fall of 1897, when the world beyond the valley began to change its tune, that Buck remained what he had always been: certain, secure, and ignorant of how quickly certainty can be taken.

He did not know Manuel.

Manuel was one of the gardener’s helpers, a man with a wife, many children, and a single, gnawing weakness that kept his mind counting when it should have been resting. He played the Chinese lottery. Worse than the gamble was his faith in a system—the belief that luck could be forced into obedience if only one followed the right pattern and paid enough to keep playing. Wages that must stretch to rice and rent and small hands reaching for food could not stretch that far. Desire made the missing money seem already his, just waiting to be collected.

On the night Manuel chose, the Judge was away at a meeting of the Raisin Growers’ Association, and the boys were busy organizing an athletic club. The house ran on familiar routine—the clink of dishes, the murmur of servants, the creak of veranda boards—but no one watched the orchard paths closely. Manuel called, and Buck, trusting the voices of his domain, went with him as if for a stroll. The orchard at night smelled of damp leaves and fallen fruit beginning to sweeten toward rot. Crickets stitched their thin sound through the dark. Buck’s pads made no noise on the earth.

At the little flag station called College Park, a solitary man waited. The air there tasted of coal dust and oiled wood. Manuel spoke with the stranger; money chinked, bright and hard, a sound that can sharpen a man’s breath. The stranger’s voice was rough.

“You might wrap up the goods before you deliver ’m.”

Manuel slipped a stout rope around Buck’s neck beneath his collar. Buck tolerated it with quiet dignity. Men did odd things sometimes. He had learned to trust those he knew and to assume their wisdom reached beyond his own.

“Twist it, an’ you’ll choke ’m plentee,” Manuel said.

The stranger grunted. Then he took the rope’s ends.

Buck’s lip lifted. A low growl vibrated in his chest—an aristocrat’s warning, not yet an attack. He believed that to show displeasure was enough to correct the world.

The rope tightened.

It shut the breath off as cleanly as a door shut. Fury flashed, hot and immediate. Buck sprang for the man’s throat. The man met him halfway, seized him close, and with a practiced twist flung him onto his back. The rope drew tighter, merciless. Buck thrashed, tongue lolling, chest heaving uselessly. Rage burned, but air would not come. The world narrowed to pain and the harsh scrape of rope fibers against skin. Then the train was flagged; the baggage car door yawned; and Buck, half-conscious, was heaved into the darkness of it.

When he came to, his tongue hurt as if it had been rasped raw. His body jolted in a moving conveyance. Somewhere close, a locomotive shrieked at a crossing, its call sawing through him. He had traveled with the Judge; he knew the particular sway and rattle of a baggage car. Memory rushed in after the sound, and with it an anger so pure it felt like being flung awake.

He opened his eyes. The man was there. Buck lunged, jaws snapping shut on the hand that reached toward his throat. He held on until the world blackened again—choked out, not by shame now but by the hard, practiced cruelty of men who knew how to steal a dog.

“Yep, has fits,” the man said later, hiding his mangled hand when a baggageman’s curiosity was stirred by the struggle. “I’m takin’ ’m up for the boss to ’Frisco. A crack dog-doctor there thinks that he can cure ’m.”

That night ended in a shed behind a saloon on San Francisco’s water front, where the air stank of sour beer, old fish, sweat, and tallow smoke. The saloon-keeper, his face bulging and shiny in candlelight, listened to the kidnapper’s complaints.

“All I get is fifty for it,” the man grumbled, “an’ I wouldn’t do it over for a thousand, cold cash.”

His hand was wrapped in a bloody handkerchief; his trousers were torn down the leg.

“How much did the other mug get?” the saloon-keeper asked.

“A hundred.”

“A hundred and fifty,” the saloon-keeper counted, “and he’s worth it, or I’m a squarehead.”

The kidnapper unwrapped his hand and stared at the lacerations as if he could bargain with pain.

“If I don’t get the hydrophoby—”

“It’ll be because you was born to hang,” the saloon-keeper laughed. “Here, lend me a hand before you pull your freight.”

Buck, dazed and throat-scorched, tried to rise with the pride of a king calling his court to order. They threw him down. They choked him again and again until they had filed the heavy brass collar from his neck. The scrape of file on metal was a grinding, intimate noise, and Buck, who had known the pleasant clink of chain at the stables and the gentle buckle of leather before a hunt, learned a new kind of sound: the sound of property being stripped of its mark.

They shoved him into a crate like a cage. He lay through the weary night with his wrath swelling and pressing against his ribs. Several times the shed door rattled open; hope leapt—ridiculous, stubborn hope that the Judge’s step or a familiar boy’s whistle would fill the doorway. Each time, instead, the saloon-keeper’s face peered in by candlelight. The bark that rose in Buck’s throat twisted itself into a growl.

Morning brought four men, ragged and unkempt, smelling of stale tobacco and unwashed wool. They lifted the crate into a wagon. Their laughter was thin and mean; they poked sticks through the slats, and Buck snapped until he understood their game. He lay down then, not defeated but measuring.

From that yard his passage became a chain of hands and wheels: clerks at an express office; another wagon; a truck piled with boxes and parcels; a ferry steamer where water slapped the hull and gulls screamed overhead; a great depot full of clanging iron and echoing footsteps; and at last an express car that swallowed him into its dim, jolting belly.

For two days and nights the car was dragged behind shrieking locomotives. For two days and nights Buck did not eat or drink. At first he met the express messengers with growls. They retaliated with mockery. They barked like bad dogs, mewed, crowed, flapped their arms—silly performances that made the insult worse because of their childishness. Buck hurled himself against the bars until saliva frothed, until his eyes reddened, until the thirst turned his throat into a raw fire. Hunger he could bear; he had hunted, he had fasted between kills. But the lack of water—his tongue swollen, his mouth dry as dust—drove him toward fever and made his anger into a steady blaze.

One thought steadied him: the rope was gone. That had been their unfair advantage. No rope would ever again tighten around his neck if he could help it. He stored that resolve the way a creature stores fat against winter.

By the time the train reached Seattle, Buck was so altered by rage and thirst that Judge Miller himself would not have recognized him. The messengers were relieved to hand him off.

Four men carried the crate into a small, high-walled back yard. A stout man in a red sweater that sagged at the neck stepped out, signed a book, and looked at Buck with a grim, patient amusement.

Buck flung himself against the bars.

The man did not hurry. He brought a hatchet and a club. The driver hesitated.

“You ain’t going to take him out now?”

“Sure,” the man said, and drove the hatchet into the crate to pry.

The four carriers scattered at once, scrambling onto the wall’s top to watch from safety. Buck attacked the splintering wood from the inside, teeth sinking into boards, body surging as if he could burst the world by sheer force. Wherever the hatchet bit from without, his snarling muzzle was there within.

When the opening was big enough, the man dropped the hatchet and shifted the club to his right hand.

“Now, you red-eyed devil,” he said quietly.

Buck gathered himself and sprang—one hundred and forty pounds of pent fury launched like a weapon. In midair, just as his jaws were about to close, shock slammed through him. Teeth snapped together with an agony that rang down his skull. He flipped and hit the ground on his back and side.

He had never been struck by a club. The experience did not fit any law he knew. Confusion flared for an instant—then rage swallowed it. He sprang again, screaming more than barking.

Again the club checked him. Again he crashed down.

After the second blow he understood: it was the club. Yet madness left him no caution. He charged a dozen times. Each time the club broke him and threw him down. Blood began to flow—hot from his nose, thick from his mouth, slick from his ears—spraying his beautiful coat and mixing with slaver. The yard smelled of splintered wood, sweat, and the iron tang of blood.

Buck staggered, dazed, too shaken for another rush. The man advanced and struck him deliberately on the nose. The pain was exquisite, a lightning bolt that erased everything else. Buck’s roar rose almost lionlike, and he launched himself again.

This time the man shifted the club to his left and caught Buck by the underjaw, wrenching down and back with practiced strength. Buck’s body described a full circle in the air and half another, then crashed to the ground on head and chest.

He rose for one last rush. The man struck the blow he had been saving, and Buck folded and went down into darkness.

From atop the wall, a voice cried with enthusiasm, “He’s no slouch at dog-breakin’, that’s wot I say.”

“Druther break cayuses any day, and twice on Sundays,” the driver replied, climbing onto the wagon.

When Buck returned to himself, he found strength still missing, as if the club had knocked it into another world. He lay where he had fallen and watched the man in the red sweater read from a letter.

“‘Answers to the name of Buck,’” the man murmured. Then, in a genial tone that seemed almost obscene after the violence: “Well, Buck, my boy, we’ve had our little ruction, and the best thing we can do is to let it go at that. You’ve learned your place, and I know mine. Be a good dog and all ’ll go well and the goose hang high. Be a bad dog, and I’ll whale the stuffin’ outa you. Understand?”

He patted Buck’s head—the very head he had battered. Buck’s hair bristled at the touch, but he did not protest. He had learned something that could not be unlearned: against that club, his teeth and pride meant nothing.

When water was brought, Buck drank as if he could pour it into the cracked fire of his throat and put himself out. The cold wetness was almost sweet. Later, raw meat was offered from the man’s hand. Buck bolted it chunk by chunk, the flesh sliding down, feeding not only his body but a deeper instinct that now began to awaken—an older, harder understanding that survival sometimes begins with eating what is given, even by the hand that hurts you.

He was beaten, but he was not broken. The difference mattered. He did not fawn. He did not lick the hand that held the club. But he accepted the law it represented.

In the days that followed, more dogs arrived: some quiet, some raging, all crated or roped. Buck watched them face the red-sweatered man. Again and again, the club spoke its blunt language. Some dogs learned quickly; some fought longer; one refused both obedience and conciliation and was killed in the struggle. The yard rang with snarls, yelps, the dull thud of wood on flesh, the sharp whistle of a lash now and then. It was a brutal acoustic schooling, and Buck, whose ear had once been tuned to the pleasant domestic rhythms of Judge Miller’s estate, began to understand what constant violence does to the spirit: it narrows it, quickens it, makes every silence suspicious.

Now and then strangers came, talking excitedly, wheedlingly, their voices rising and falling like bargaining in a market. Each time money passed hands. One or more dogs were taken away. None returned. Buck did not know where they went, but the future pressed on him like weather. Fear did not make him small; it made him watchful. Each time he was not chosen, he felt relief—an emotion he did not like admitting to himself.

Then came his turn.

A little weazened man appeared, spitting broken English and strange exclamations that bucked and clattered on the tongue—sounds with no home in Buck’s understanding, as if language itself could be made foreign enough to keep power in the speaker’s hands.

“Sacredam!” the man cried when he saw Buck. “Dat one dam bully dog! Eh? How moch?”

“Three hundred, and a present at that,” the man in the red sweater replied promptly. “And seem’ it’s government money, you ain’t got no kick coming, eh, Perrault?”

Perrault grinned. He knew dogs. He looked at Buck the way a careful cook looks at a prime cut—judging weight, muscle, coat, the kind of strength that promises long labor without wasting. In the boom of northern demand, three hundred dollars was steep, but not unfair for such an animal. Government dispatches would not travel slower for lack of good dogs.

Buck watched the money change hands. He was not surprised when he and Curly—a good-natured Newfoundland—were led away. That was the last he saw of the man in the red sweater. From the deck of the Narwhal, as Seattle receded, it was also the last he saw of the warm Southland, the orchards and lawns and the quiet, civil sounds of home.

Below decks, Perrault handed them to a black-faced giant named François. Perrault was French-Canadian, swarthy; François was half-breed French-Canadian, darker still, with a controlled way of moving that suggested strength kept under discipline. They were a new kind of men to Buck. He did not love them, but he felt, almost at once, the steadiness in them—an impartiality that did not waste cruelty for amusement. They carried authority the way an elder carries a family recipe: not as a performance, but as something earned and practiced, a knowledge that keeps a household alive through hard seasons.

In the ’tween-decks Buck and Curly met two other dogs. One was a big snow-white fellow from Spitzbergen, brought south by a whaling captain and later hardened in the Barrens with a Geological Survey. He greeted them with a friendly manner that did not reach his eyes; it was the smile of a thief who believes charm is a kind of camouflage. At the first meal he stole from Buck’s food. Buck sprang to punish him, but François’s whip sang—its sound slicing air cleanly, landing on the culprit first. Buck recovered the bone. That was fair. The half-breed rose in Buck’s estimation with that single, precise act of justice.

The other dog, called Dave, was gloomy and morose. He wanted only to be left alone. He ate, slept, yawned between times, and took interest in nothing—not even when the Narwhal crossed Queen Charlotte Sound and rolled and pitched and bucked like a thing possessed. When Buck and Curly grew excited, half wild with fear at the ship’s violence, Dave lifted his head as if annoyed, gave them an incurious glance, yawned, and went back to sleep. His indifference was its own kind of strength.

Day and night the ship throbbed to the tireless pulse of its propeller. The sound was constant, a mechanical heartbeat that filled the wooden belly of the vessel and invaded sleep. One day was like another, yet Buck felt the air shifting. The cold grew, inch by inch, as if the world ahead were exhaling.

At last, one morning, the propeller stopped. Silence—real silence, not the hush of a library but the sudden absence of a machine—fell across the Narwhal and made every small sound sharp: chains clinking, men’s voices on deck, gulls crying somewhere above. Excitement moved through the ship like an odor.

François leashed the dogs and brought them up.

Buck’s first step onto the deck landed on a cold surface that yielded under his pads. He sank into a white, mushy substance like mud. He jerked back with a snort. More of the white stuff drifted down through the air in soft, soundless pieces. It clung to his fur. He shook himself; it fell and more replaced it.

He sniffed. It smelled of nothing he could name—clean and blank as a new dish before seasoning. He licked it.

It bit like fire, a cold so sharp it stung, and the next instant it vanished on his tongue. He blinked, puzzled, and tried again. The same sting, the same disappearance. Men laughed loudly at his confusion.

Buck felt ashamed without knowing why, as if he had mispronounced some word everyone else took for granted. But it was his first snow, and he stood in it while it fell on his coat, and the world’s familiar language of warmth and orchard-scent began to be replaced by another dialect entirely—one made of silence, frost, and the thin, clean bite of cold.

Buck’s first day on Dyea beach did not unfold like a day at all, but like a whole bad season crammed into a handful of hours—each moment startling, each breath edged with a cold so clean it seemed to scrape the inside of his nose. He had been lifted from warm lawns and the soft, human clatter of comfort and dropped into a camp that lived by different sounds: the flat crack of clubs on hide, the thin whine of a lash, the iron click of teeth meeting, and the constant undertone of men shouting in tongues that did not bother to translate themselves for a newcomer.

Here, there was no gentleness in the air. Even the domestic things—tents, fires, sacks of provisions—were wrapped in urgency. Smoke smelled of green wood half-wet with snow. Fish, split and dried hard as boards, gave off a sharp, briny tang that clung to fur and mitten alike. Life moved fast, and if you failed to read it you were simply erased. Buck learned that law before he had a chance to pretend he didn’t.

It was Curly who paid the first price in front of his eyes.

They were camped near the log store. Curly, friendly as she had always been, went up to a big husky—wolf-sized in manner, if not in bulk—with the same open-hearted confidence a house dog might bring to a neighbor’s porch. There was no growl to warn her, no ritual, no polite testing. The attack arrived like a thrown blade: a leap in, a metallic clip, and a leap out again so swift it seemed the air itself had bitten her. Her face opened from eye to jaw.

Curly lunged back, brave and outraged, trying to fight as she knew how—front-on, honest, trusting that effort and courage would be answered in kind. But the other dog fought in the wolf way: strike and vanish, strike and vanish, letting her spend herself against emptiness and cold. Then he met her with his chest at an angle—an ugly, practiced trick—and toppled her. The moment her feet lost the ground, the camp’s sound changed.

Thirty or forty huskies rushed in, but not with the noisy chaos Buck expected. They formed a ring that was intent and terribly quiet, a hush full of licking mouths and waiting throats. Buck did not yet understand that silence—how it held hunger the way a covered pot holds steam—but he saw their tongues slide over their chops, saw their bodies quiver for the instant when cruelty would be permitted.

When Curly went down, they poured over her as if a trapdoor had opened beneath the snow and released them. The circle collapsed into a bristling heap of bodies—snarling, yelping, tearing. Curly screamed once, twice, and the screams were swallowed under fur and fang. The sound of it struck Buck harder than the sight: agony beaten into a muffled, wet ruin.

Spitz stood close enough to see. He did not help. He merely ran out his scarlet tongue in a way that looked like laughter.

François, the swart half-breed, burst in swinging an axe, and three men came with clubs. The blows landed with dull, meaty thuds; the pack scattered in grudging fragments. It was over quickly—two minutes from Curly’s fall to the last assailant being clubbed away—but Curly lay trampled and torn, limp in bloodied snow, almost literally in pieces. François stood over her, cursing so fiercely the words became more sound than sense, a rough music of rage that belonged to the North as surely as the wind.

That night and many nights after, Buck would wake with the memory of that circle—its quiet, its sudden closing—staring at him from the dark.

So that was the rule: no fairness. No pause for pity. Once down, you were finished. Buck decided without ceremony that he would never go down.

And when Spitz laughed again, Buck’s hatred began—bitter and clean, like a knife laid on ice.

Before the shock had cooled inside him, another lesson arrived in leather and buckles.

François fitted straps around Buck’s chest and shoulders. The harness felt wrong—too intimate, too demanding—as if it claimed him. It was like what Buck had seen grooms put on horses at home, except there was no stable smell here, no sweet hay, no familiar creak of stalls. Only snowlight and the sharp scent of men’s mittens, tar, and fish.

He was put to work at once, hauling François on a sled to the dark fringe of forest and back with a load of firewood. The logs carried their own perfume—resin and sap, crushed green life locked under frost. Buck’s pride flared at being made a draught animal, but wisdom, newly learned by pain, kept him from rebellion. He bent to it, pulled hard, and tried to read the language being spoken to him: not a language of words, but of whip, teeth, and weight in the traces.

François demanded instant obedience, and the whip made it sensible. Dave, an experienced wheeler, corrected Buck with quick nips whenever he blundered. Spitz, leader and veteran, couldn’t always reach him, but when he could, he growled sharp reproof, or slyly leaned his body in the harness to jerk Buck into line. Their corrections came like a stern apprenticeship in some craft where mistakes cost blood.

Buck learned fast. “Ho” meant stop. “Mush” meant go. On bends he must swing wide. On descents he must keep clear of the sled that came shooting downhill behind them like a pursued thing. The work began to make a strange kind of sense—rhythm in the strain, a grim satisfaction in doing it correctly.

“T’ree vair’ good dogs,” François told Perrault. “Dat Buck, heem pool lak hell. I tich heem queek as anyt’ing.”

By afternoon, Perrault returned in a hurry, and with him came two more dogs, brothers.

“Billee” and “Joe,” he called them—true huskies, sons of the same mother, yet as different as day and night. Billee overflowed with good nature; his tail seemed always ready to negotiate peace. Joe was sour, inward, with a perpetual snarl that looked less like anger than a settled distrust of the world.

Buck greeted them as comrades. Dave ignored them. Spitz, on the other hand, set about asserting himself, thrashing first one and then the other. Billee tried appeasement—tail wagging, whines of submission—and when that failed he turned to run, still crying softly as Spitz’s teeth scored his flank. Joe would not run. He spun on his heels to face Spitz, mane up, ears flat, lips writhing back from snapping jaws, eyes gleaming with a terrible promise—the belligerence of fear turned into a weapon. Joe’s display was so formidable Spitz chose to save face by turning again on Billee, driving the yelping, inoffensive brother to the edge of camp.

By evening, Perrault added another: an old husky, long and lean and gaunt, with a scarred face and a single eye that flashed warning. He was called Sol-leks—the Angry One. Like Dave, he asked nothing and offered nothing; he moved into their midst with deliberate slowness, as if the world owed him space. Even Spitz left him alone.

Sol-leks had one peculiarity Buck discovered with a sudden sting. Approaching him on his blind side, Buck meant no offense—only curiosity—but Sol-leks whirled and slashed Buck’s shoulder to the bone, three inches up and down. The pain was bright and instructive. After that, Buck kept clear of the blind side, and never again gave Sol-leks cause.

That night brought Buck his next great problem: where to sleep.

The tent glowed warmly, candlelit, a domestic little pocket in the white waste. Buck, following the habit of his former life, stepped in as naturally as he might have entered Judge Miller’s house. Perrault and François answered him not with welcome but with curses and flying cooking utensils. A battered pot clanged off the ground near his paws; the sound alone told him he was trespassing in a language he did not speak.

He fled back into the cold, his wounded shoulder bitten by wind that slid under fur like a thin blade. He lay on the snow, tried to sleep, and the frost drove him up shivering. He wandered among tents where other dogs rushed him savagely. Buck bristled and snarled—learning quickly the posture that kept teeth at a distance—and they let him pass.

At last, desperate and puzzled, he returned to his own camp to see how his team-mates managed. They had vanished. He circled the tent, tail low, body trembling, feeling more forlorn than he had ever been on Judge Miller’s land. Then the snow gave way under his forelegs and he sank.

Something wriggled beneath him.

Buck sprang back, hackles up, snarling at what he could not see. A friendly yelp answered him, and a puff of warm air rose to his nostrils—air that smelled of dog and damp heat, of life conserving itself. In the hollow under the snow lay Billee, curled in a snug ball. He whined, wiggled, and, as a bribe for peace, licked Buck’s face with a warm wet tongue.

Another lesson. So that was how they did it.

Buck chose a spot and began to dig, clumsy at first, wasting effort with too much motion and too little knowledge. But soon his body heat began to fill the small space, and the snow became walls that held warmth the way an oven holds it once the door is shut. He slept at once, deeply—growling and barking in dreams, wrestling phantoms that smelled of blood and heard like Curly’s scream.

He did not open his eyes until the waking camp’s noises reached him. At first he did not know where he was. Snow had fallen in the night, burying him completely. Pressed in on every side, he felt a sudden, primitive terror—the fear of the wild thing caught in a trap. It rose from somewhere older than his own memories, a knowledge belonging to ancestors who had never seen a house or heard a civilized street. His muscles contracted; his hair stood. With a ferocious snarl he bounded straight up into blinding daylight, snow exploding around him in a flashing cloud.

Before he landed, he saw the camp spread white and busy. The memory of everything—from his stroll with Manuel to the hole he had dug—returned in a rush.

François shouted in triumph. “Wot I say? Dat Buck for sure learn queek as anyt’ing.”

Perrault nodded gravely. As courier for the Canadian Government, carrying important despatches, he wanted the best dogs, and Buck pleased him.

Within an hour three more huskies were added, making nine in all. A quarter of an hour later they were in harness and swinging up the trail toward Dyea Cañon.

Buck was glad to leave the beach behind. The work was hard, but he did not despise it as he had expected. The eagerness of the team possessed him—an energy that ran along the traces like a current. More surprising still was the change in Dave and Sol-leks. Out of harness they had been passive, uninterested; in harness they became new creatures—alert, intent, fiercely irritated by any delay. The toil of the traces was their pleasure and their meaning, as if they were built for this single function and became fully alive only when straining against it.

Dave ran as wheeler at the sled. Buck pulled just ahead of him, then Sol-leks, and the rest strung out single file up to Spitz at lead. Buck had been placed between Dave and Sol-leks to be taught. They were good teachers, enforcing their lessons with teeth. Dave was fair and wise; he never nipped without cause, and he never failed to nip when correction was due. With François’s whip behind them, Buck learned it was cheaper to improve than to fight back.

Once, during a brief halt, Buck tangled the traces and delayed the start. Dave and Sol-leks flew at him and administered a trouncing. Their attack worsened the tangle, but the pain went into Buck’s memory like a nail. After that he kept the traces clear. By day’s end he had improved so much his mates almost ceased nagging him. François’s whip snapped less. Perrault even honored Buck by lifting each foot and examining it carefully.

They ran hard—up the Cañon, through Sheep Camp, past the Scales and the timber line, across glaciers and snowdrifts hundreds of feet deep, and over the great Chilcoot Divide that stands between salt water and fresh, guarding the sad North with a forbidding shoulder of ice. They made good time down the chain of lakes filling the craters of extinct volcanoes, and late that night pulled into the huge camp at the head of Lake Bennett, where thousands of goldseekers were building boats for the spring break-up.

Buck dug his hole and slept the sleep of the exhausted just. But before dawn fully owned the sky he was routed out into the cold darkness and harnessed again.

That day they made forty miles on a packed trail. Then came many days of breaking their own path—harder work, poorer time. Perrault often travelled ahead on webbed shoes, packing snow so the dogs could pull. François guided at the gee-pole, sometimes exchanging places, though not often. Perrault was in a hurry, proud of his knowledge of ice—a knowledge that mattered, because fall ice was thin and swift water held none at all.

Day after day, miles unending, Buck toiled. They broke camp in darkness; the first gray of dawn found them already moving. They pitched camp after dark, ate their ration, and crawled into the snow to sleep. The camp’s nightly music became familiar: the rasp of knives on frozen fish, the crackle of fire, the groan of sled runners cooling, the low, satisfied grumbles of dogs curling into holes.

Buck was ravenous. His ration—pound and a half of sun-dried salmon—seemed to vanish without reaching his belly, as if his body were a furnace that ate and ate yet never held heat. The fish was tough and leathery, salted by the sea and dried until it resisted the teeth. When it warmed briefly in the mouth it released a rich, oily smell—life preserved against winter—but it was never enough. The other dogs, smaller and born to this, received a pound and kept good condition. Buck’s hunger was a constant gnaw, a voice louder than fatigue.

In his old life he had been fastidious. Here fastidiousness was a way to be robbed. If he ate slowly, his mates—finishing first—would steal what remained. There was no defending it. While he fought off one thief, another would gulp down his portion. So he learned to eat fast, ripping and swallowing without pause, letting hunger drive him past delicacy. And hunger drove him further: he was not above taking what did not belong to him.

He watched and learned.

Pike, one of the new dogs, was a clever malingerer and thief. Buck saw him steal a slice of bacon when Perrault’s back was turned—bacon that smelled impossibly rich, smoky and fatty, the kind of food that haunted the air with its promise. The next day Buck duplicated the performance, but better: he got away with the whole chunk. The camp erupted in accusation and noise, men’s voices harsh, dogs’ snarls answering. Buck, careful and silent, was unsuspected. Dub, awkward and always caught, was punished for Buck’s theft.

That act marked Buck. Not because theft was noble, but because it was necessary. In this Northland, where the law was club and fang, moral niceties did not keep a body alive. Buck did not reason it out; he simply adapted. What had been called “good” in the Southland—respecting property, honoring feelings—was here a handicap, a soft language that could not be spoken without being bitten.

He did not steal for joy. He stole because his stomach clamored like an empty pot left on the fire too long. He did not steal openly; he did it slyly, with respect for club and fang. It was easier to do than not to do, and ease here meant survival.

His development—his stripping-down—was rapid. His muscles hardened like iron. Ordinary pain became something he could carry without complaint. He grew economical inside and out: he could eat anything, however loathsome, and his stomach would take it apart and salvage the last particle of nourishment. His blood delivered that salvaged strength to every reach of him, building tough tissue and enduring sinew.

His senses sharpened. Smell became a map: smoke, fish, fur, old blood, the faint mineral scent of ice. Sight took in movement at the edge of snowlight. Hearing became so keen that in sleep he registered the smallest sound and knew whether it promised peace or peril. He learned to bite ice from between his toes, to rear and strike through a scum of frozen water when thirst demanded. Most of all, he learned the wind—how to scent it a night ahead, how to choose a place to dig so that when the gale finally arrived it would find him sheltered on the lee, snug and warm.

And with each day, something older stirred.

The domesticated generations fell away. In vague, unbidden ways he remembered back—not as a thought he could name, but as a bodily certainty—to the youth of the breed, when wild dogs ran in packs through primeval forest and killed meat as they ran it down. Fighting came to him in the cut-and-slash and quick wolf snap, not as a lesson learned but as a skill waking from sleep. The tricks stamped into heredity arrived without effort, as if he had always known them.

On still, cold nights, when camp finally quieted and the noises of men dulled into distance, Buck would lift his nose to the hard stars and howl long and wolflike. The sound did not feel like something he invented; it felt like something passing through him—an ancient cadence carried down the centuries, a voice that belonged to forgotten ancestors who had howled under different skies yet the same indifferent cold. In that cry lived the meaning of stiffness and darkness, of hunger and endurance—things no human word could fully translate.

And thus the old song surged through him, domestic comfort loosening its hold, while the world hurried him north because men had found a yellow metal in the ground, and because Manuel, a gardener’s helper, needed money for the needs of his wife and the small copies of himself.

Buck carried within him something older than the warm lawns of Judge Miller’s place—something that had slept under kitchen floors, under hearthstones, under the very language of home, until the North’s hard weather woke it and fed it day by day. Trail life was a lean cook: it gave no garnish, no second helpings, no mercy. Yet what it did give—cold, hunger, labor, danger—was the kind of fare that seasons bone and spirit. The change in Buck was not loud. It rose like a slow boil in a covered pot. Newborn cunning kept him steady. He learned to waste no motion, no breath, no anger. Even with Spitz, the white dog who carried his dominance like a knife between his teeth, Buck did not rush. He kept his appetite for the final bite hidden.

Spitz, for his part, could not leave Buck alone. Perhaps he smelled the threat the way a baker smells yeast waking in dough—silent, inevitable, swelling. Spitz bared teeth whenever he could, shouldered him at harnessing, stole inches of space, stared too long. He wanted the fight that ended everything.

The chance nearly came on a night so bitter the air seemed to crack when you inhaled it.

They made camp on the shore of Lake Le Barge, and everything about it was wrong. Snow drove sideways. The wind cut like a blade heated white, not by flame but by cruelty. Darkness pressed in so close the men groped like blind cooks at a cold stove, searching for anything that would light. Behind them stood a wall of rock—sheer and black—and so Perrault and François were forced to build their fire on the ice itself. They had thrown away the tent back at Dyea to travel light. Now the lightness cost them. A handful of driftwood sticks, stingy and damp, gave a fire that gnawed down through the ice and left them crouching to eat supper by a hole of thin warmth, their food swallowed more than tasted.

Buck found shelter where the rock broke the wind. He dug and turned and pressed snow into a nest the way his body remembered without being taught, packing it until it held heat like a covered dish. It was snug—so snug that when François thawed the fish over the fire and began handing out rations, Buck did not want to leave his hollow. The fish smelled sharp and oily, sea-cold turned to smoke-cold, and he came because hunger called louder than comfort. He ate, then returned quickly to the nest that was his.

It was not his anymore.

A low, possessive snarl met him in the dark. Spitz lay in Buck’s warmth, in Buck’s place, his pale coat a smear of arrogance against the snow.

Buck had avoided trouble. He had turned his shoulder to insults. He had swallowed anger the way a starving dog swallows bones—whole, quick, no chewing. But this was theft of the most intimate kind: the stealing of heat, of shelter, of the small domestic center a creature makes in a wilderness. Something in him—older than training, older than the club—rose up with a roar.

He sprang.

They burst from the ruined nest in a knot of fur and teeth, rolling and snapping, their breath puffing hard, their bodies striking the ice-packed snow with dull thuds. Spitz, who had read Buck as heavy and timid, was stunned by the sudden ferocity. François, seeing them shoot out, understood at once.

“A-a-ah!” he shouted, voice cracking like kindling. “Gif it to heem, by Gar! Gif it to heem, the dirty t’eef!”

Spitz gave nothing away. He circled, crying in rage, quick-footed, seeking an opening. Buck circled too—equally eager, but measuring, saving himself, watching for the advantage like a hunter watching wind and cover.

And then the night split open.

Perrault swore—a harsh sound that struck the air like iron. A club landed with a flat, resounding blow on ribs that were too close to the skin. A sharp yelp followed, and in that instant the camp was no longer camp. It was a pantry discovered by famine.

Shapes flowed out of the dark: skulking huskies—four or five score—skeleton dogs from some Indian village, drawn by the smell of food the way smoke draws wolves. They had crept in while Buck and Spitz fought. Now they rushed the fire, the packs, the men. When Perrault and François sprang among them with clubs, the invaders fought back, crazed by scent: bread, bacon, fish—anything. One had its head buried in the grub-box. Perrault’s club crashed on its gaunt ribs; the box toppled, spilled its precious insides onto the snow like a careless hand dumping flour into ash. In a blink, a score of starving mouths were on it. The clubs rained down, but hunger was armor. They yelped and howled under blows, jaws working as if the pain itself were something to chew. They ate with the frenzy of a people whose language for “tomorrow” has died.

The team dogs erupted from their nests, only to be met by the invaders’ teeth. Buck had never seen dogs like these—so thin it looked as though their bones might punch through their hides, eyes blazing in loose, draggled skins. They were terrible in their hunger, and their hunger made them irresistible. The team was driven back toward the cliff at the first onset. Buck was jumped by three at once; in moments his head and shoulders were ripped open, skin scored, blood warming then freezing. The noise was a maddened orchestra—howls, snarls, clubs thudding, men shouting, harness bells clinking as bodies slammed together.

Billee cried as he always cried, a high, pleading sound. Dave and Sol-leks, blood streaking their coats, fought shoulder to shoulder, grim as oxen. Joe snapped like a demon; once he caught a husky’s foreleg and crunched through bone, the sound like breaking dry sticks. Pike, the shameless malingerer, leaped onto the crippled animal and finished it with a quick twist, neck snapping. Buck got one by the throat; his teeth found the jugular, and blood surged hot into his mouth—warmth with a metallic tang that made him fiercer, made him forget pain. He sprang on another—

—and felt teeth sink into his own throat.

Spitz. Not as enemy in open battle, but as thief of advantage, slicing in from the side while Buck was occupied. It was a treacherous bite, meant not simply to wound but to topple. Under the crush of those starving bodies, one fall could mean death.

Perrault and François, having beaten the worst of the raiders away from the grub, rushed to the dogs. The wave of famished beasts rolled back before their clubs; Buck shook free. But only for a heartbeat. The men were forced again to protect what food remained, and the huskies surged back toward the team.

Panic became strategy. Billee, frightened into a sudden courage, broke through the savage circle and fled over the lake ice. Pike and Dub followed. The others streamed after, the whole team spilling out into the open, bells jangling like frantic cutlery.

Buck gathered himself to follow—and saw Spitz rush him, aiming to knock him down into the mob. Buck braced for the impact, held his ground just long enough to keep his feet, then turned and fled with the rest, the ice beneath them groaning with cold.

Later the nine gathered in the forest, huddled under trees that offered little but the illusion of shelter. No one pursued them. The invaders had come for food, not sport. Still, the team was wrecked. Each dog wore four or five wounds, some deep and ugly. Dub’s hind leg hung wrong. Dolly—newest to the team—had a throat torn ragged. Joe’s eye was gone, leaving a dark, wet hollow. Billee’s ear was shredded, and he whimpered through the night, a thin, broken sound that made sleep hard.

At daybreak they limped back to camp. The marauders were gone. The two men were not in the forgiving mood of people who have eaten well. Half the grub was gone. The huskies had chewed through lashings and canvas coverings. Anything remotely eatable had been tested by teeth: Perrault’s moose-hide moccasins eaten down, chunks gnawed from leather traces, even two feet of lash bitten clean from François’s whip, as if hunger had decided to sample the very means of discipline. François stared at the frayed end, mourning it the way a cook mourns a lost knife.

Then he turned to his dogs and spoke softly, voice worn thin. “Ah, my frien’s… mebbe it mek you mad dog, dose many bites. Mebbe all mad dog, sacredam! Wot you t’ink, eh, Perrault?”

Perrault shook his head, uneasy. Four hundred miles still lay between them and Dawson; madness in the team would be a disaster they could not afford.

Two hours of cursing and raw work got the harnesses into some shape. The team, stiff with wounds, was put back on the trail. Ahead waited the hardest stretch yet—the Thirty Mile River.

It ran wide open, wild water shouldering aside the frost. Ice held only in eddies and quiet corners, thin and treacherous as sugar crust. Six days of exhausting toil they spent on those thirty miles, and every foot was paid for in risk—dog and man.

A dozen times Perrault, probing ahead with his pole, broke through ice bridges. Each time, he saved himself by the way he held the pole so it fell across the hole, giving him purchase like a plank. But a cold snap had tightened the world; the thermometer fell to fifty below. Whenever he went in, he had to build a fire and dry his clothes, for wet at that cold meant death in minutes. The fire was their only hearth, their only kitchen; they fed it as carefully as they fed themselves, coaxing flame from mean wood, breathing smoke that smelled of wet bark and desperation.

Nothing daunted Perrault. It was why he had been chosen. He pushed his small, weazened face into the frost and drove on from dim dawn to dark, skirting frowning shores on rim ice that bent and crackled beneath them. They dared not halt on it. Once, the sled broke through with Dave and Buck. They were hauled out half-frozen, water clinging to them in sheets that turned instantly to ice. The men built the usual fire and ran them close around it, forcing them to move, to sweat, to thaw. The flames licked so near their fur they were singed, the smell of burnt hair mixing with fish grease and smoke.

Another time Spitz went through and dragged the whole team after him as if he meant to drown them all in his pride. Buck, at the rear, braced backward with every muscle, forepaws on the slippery edge, ice quivering and snapping around him. Behind him Dave strained too, grim and silent, and behind the sled François pulled until tendons stood out like rope.

Once the rim ice broke away both in front and behind, leaving them trapped between cliff and water. There was no escape except up. Perrault scaled the cliff by what felt like a miracle. François prayed for the same miracle, and then made it with work: every thong, every lashing, every bit of harness was made into a long rope. The dogs were hoisted one by one, swinging above the river like sacks of grain. François came up last, after sled and load. Then began the search for a way down. By rope they descended, inch by inch, and night found them back on the river with only a quarter mile gained.

By the time they reached the Hootalinqua and good ice, Buck was played out. The others were scarcely better. But Perrault, driven by time and pride, pushed them late and early as if he could knead strength into them by sheer will. One day they ran thirty-five miles to the Big Salmon; the next, thirty-five to the Little Salmon; the third, forty, bringing them toward the Five Fingers.

Buck’s feet were the weak point. The huskies’ pads were tight and hard as cured hide, shaped by generations of ice and stone. Buck’s had softened in the many generations since his last wild ancestor had been tamed to fire and roof. Now the packed trail cut him like a rasp. All day he limped in agony. When camp was made he dropped like a dead dog, ribs heaving. Hunger gnawed him, but he would not rise even for fish. François brought the ration to his mouth.

Each night after supper François sat with Buck’s paws between his hands, rubbing them as carefully as if he were working dough, trying to drive warmth back into the flesh. Then he sacrificed the tops of his own moccasins, cutting and shaping them into four little coverings for Buck’s feet. The leather smelled of smoke and sweat and old trail. Those moccasins were relief—softening the cruelty of the snow the way a good broth softens a hard crust.

One morning François forgot them, and Perrault’s weazened face cracked into a grin, because Buck lay on his back, four feet waving helplessly in the air, refusing to move without his foot-gear, as though he had learned not only pain but the language of protest.

Later his feet hardened. The moccasins wore through, and were thrown away.

At the Pelly one morning, as they harnessed up, Dolly—never notable before—went suddenly mad.

It began with a long wolf howl, so full of heartbreak it made every dog bristle. The sound sliced through the morning’s hush, through the little domestic noises of camp—snapping straps, muttered oaths, the soft thump of fish being handled—and turned them all into one raw, frightened silence. Then Dolly sprang straight for Buck, frothing, eyes gone wrong.

Buck had never seen madness, but his body understood it instantly as horror. He fled in a blind panic. Dolly followed one leap behind, panting and snarling, foam flying. He could not lose her; terror drove him, and her madness drove her. He plunged through the wooded breast of an island, crashed down to the lower end, crossed a back channel filled with rough ice to another island, then a third. He curved back toward the main river, desperate, and started to cross it.

All the while he did not look back, yet he heard her—always one leap behind, her breathing a harsh rasp, her snarl like a saw.

François called from far off, a quarter mile away. Buck doubled back toward that voice the way a starving creature runs toward the smell of food. He put all his faith in François. The dog-driver stood with his axe poised, a clean tool for a terrible job. Buck shot past him, and the axe came down on Dolly’s head. The sound was final.

Buck staggered against the sled, exhausted, sobbing for air, legs shaking. He was helpless.

Spitz saw it as opportunity.

He sprang upon Buck and, twice, his teeth sank in and ripped to the bone, taking payment while Buck could not answer. Then François’s lash whistled. It fell on Spitz with a fury that tasted of justice. Buck, gasping, had the grim satisfaction of watching Spitz receive the worst whipping yet given.

“One devil, dat Spitz,” Perrault said. “Some dam day heem keel dat Buck.”

“Dat Buck two devils,” François replied, eyes narrowed. “All de tam I watch dat Buck I know for sure. Lissen: some dam fine day heem get mad lak hell an’ den heem chew dat Spitz all up an’ spit heem out on de snow. Sure. I know.”

From then on, it was war.

Spitz, as lead-dog and acknowledged master, felt his supremacy threatened by this Southland dog who did not die, did not soften, did not fade the way the others from warm places had. Buck endured and even prospered, growing stronger in that harsh way meat toughens and sweetens under smoke. What made him more dangerous was not just strength and savagery—it was the club that had beaten blind rashness out of him. He was cunning. He could wait. He could hide his hunger for power until the moment it could be eaten cleanly.

And Buck wanted leadership. He wanted it with a pride as old as the traces. It was a pride that made dogs labor beyond sense, that lured them to die in harness rather than be cut loose. It was in Dave at the wheel, in Sol-leks pulling with all he had, in the way the whole team would change at break of camp—sour brutes becoming straining, eager creatures once the work began. It held Spitz too, making him thrash shirkers and blunderers. It made him fear Buck.

Buck began to threaten his rule openly. He stepped between Spitz and the dogs Spitz meant to punish. He did it deliberately, not from fairness—fair play had frozen out of him long ago—but from strategy.

One night a heavy snowfall buried the camp. In the morning Pike, the malingerer, did not appear. He was hidden in his nest beneath a foot of snow like a thief under a table after supper. François called. He searched. Nothing.

Spitz raged, sniffing, digging, snarling so horribly that Pike trembled in his hiding place, hearing the sound as if it were teeth already in his flesh.

When Pike was finally unearthed and Spitz flew at him to punish him, Buck launched himself between them with equal rage. The move was sudden and shrewd. Spitz was hurled backward, knocked off his feet. Pike, heartened by this mutiny, sprang on his fallen leader. Buck joined in—no code, no honor, only advantage.

François, chuckling even as he kept justice, brought his lash down on Buck with all his might. Buck did not yield. François struck again, then used the butt of the whip. The blow half-stunned Buck, knocked him back. The lash fell again and again until Buck was driven off, while Spitz rose and punished Pike soundly for his many offenses.

After that, Buck continued his interference—but craftily, when François was not near. Under Buck’s covert rebellion the team’s solidarity began to crumble. Dave and Sol-leks stayed what they were, but the others slid into insubordination. Bickering grew constant. Camp became a bedlam of snarls and quarrels that tore at the night’s quiet. François lived in dread of waking to the final fight between Buck and Spitz, and more than once he burst from his sleeping robe at sounds of strife, lash in hand, only to find the others tangled in their own feuds.

Still the great fight did not come. They pulled into Dawson one dreary afternoon with it hanging ahead like a storm that refuses to break.

Dawson was noise and work. Men everywhere, dogs everywhere, and every creature moving as if labor were the only law. Teams swung up and down the main street all day. At night the bells still passed, jingling through darkness like thin music. Dogs hauled cabin logs and firewood and freight to the mines, doing the work that horses did back in the Santa Clara Valley. Buck saw a few Southland dogs, out of place and soft-eyed; mostly he saw huskies, wolf-blooded and sharp.

And every night, regularly—at nine, at twelve, at three—the huskies sang.

It was not a song meant for the market stalls or the painted stage. It was older than commerce and too wild for applause. It rose from throats shaped by cold into a weird chant, pitched in a minor key with long-drawn wails and half-sobs. In that sound lived the acoustic world of the North: the far crack of trees in frost, the whisper of snow, the deep silence that made every howl a kind of truth. Buck delighted to join, his voice finding a place in the chorus as if he were remembering words his mouth had never learned.

Under the cold flame of the aurora borealis, under stars that seemed to leap and dance in the brittle air, the song carried the woe of unnumbered generations. It was the pleading of life in a land that answered with hunger. When Buck moaned with them, it was not philosophy; it was sensation and memory intertwined—pain of living, fear of dark, mystery of cold. It was as if some part of him spoke a dialect older than Judge Miller’s commands, a tongue that could not be translated into any warm-house vocabulary. He did not have words for it, only sound.

Seven days after reaching Dawson, they dropped down the steep bank by the Barracks to the Yukon Trail and turned back toward Dyea and salt water. Perrault carried urgent despatches—and more than that, he carried the fierce pride of travel. He meant to make the record trip of the year. Luck favored him: the week’s rest had put the dogs in trim; the trail was packed hard by later travelers; the police had laid grub deposits in places; and he traveled light.

They made Sixty Mile—a fifty-mile run—in the first day. The second day they boomed up the Yukon, well on their way to Pelly.

But fine running did not mean peace.

Buck’s quiet revolt had ruined the team’s unity. No longer did they leap in the traces as one creature. The encouragement Buck gave the rebels turned into petty crimes. Spitz was still lead-dog, but he was no longer feared as before. Pike stole half a fish one night and gulped it down under Buck’s protection. Another night Dub and Joe stood up to Spitz and forced him to forego the punishment they deserved. Even Billee whined less placatingly. Buck never came near Spitz without bristling and snarling. At times he swaggered openly before Spitz’s nose, the way a young cook might walk too close to an old master’s station just to claim the space.

The breakdown spread. The dogs fought among themselves more than ever until camp sounded like a kitchen where every pot boils over at once. Dave and Sol-leks remained unchanged, but the constant squabbling made them irritable. François swore strange barbarous oaths and stamped the snow and tore his hair, lash singing uselessly. When his back turned, the fighting started again. He backed up Spitz with his whip. Buck backed up the rest. François knew Buck was at the root of it, and Buck knew François knew—but Buck was too clever to be caught again in the act. In harness he worked faithfully; toil had become delight. Yet it was a greater delight to tangle traces and precipitate fights with a sly nudge here, a calculated shoulder there.

At the mouth of the Tahkeena one night after supper, Dub turned up a snowshoe rabbit. He blundered and missed. In an instant the whole team was in full cry, the sudden burst of sound ripping through the night’s hush like a knife through skin. A hundred yards away lay a camp of the Northwest Police with fifty huskies. They heard the chase and joined, the pack swelling until it was sixty dogs, a moving storm of paws and breath and bell-jingle.

The rabbit fled downriver, then turned into a small creek, running up its frozen bed. It skimmed lightly over the snow’s crust while the dogs plowed through, chest-deep at times, driving forward by brute force. Buck led the pack, his body flashing in the moonlight, low to the run, whining with eagerness. Bend after bend he drove, but he could not gain. Ahead the rabbit flitted like a pale frost wraith, quick and unreal.

In Buck, the old instincts surged—blood lust, joy of the kill—not as borrowed sport, not as distant entertainment bought and sold, but as something intimate and necessary. He ran not to shoot, not to pose, but to take living meat with his own teeth and feel warm blood wash his muzzle.

He was alive with every muscle, every joint, every sinew singing. He forgot himself in the pure rush of motion, flying under the stars over the dead matter of snow and ice that did not move.

But Spitz, cold and calculating even in that ecstasy, cut away from the pack. He crossed a narrow neck of land where the creek looped in a long bend, taking a shortcut as sharp as a thief’s hand.

Buck did not see him go. He rounded the bend, rabbit still ahead—and then another larger frost wraith dropped from the overhanging bank into the rabbit’s path.

Spitz.

The rabbit could not turn. Spitz’s white teeth caught it midair and broke its back. The rabbit shrieked—a humanlike, terrible sound—and at that cry, the whole pack raised a chorus of delight, a savage music that rolled through the cold night and made the wilderness itself seem to listen.

“Eh? Wot I say? I spik true w’en I say dat Buck two devils.”

François said it into the cold the way a cook might taste a stew and declare, with a shake of his head, that there were two sharp peppers where only one had ever been used before. Morning had hardly unstitched the night from the Yukon sky when he found Spitz gone and Buck standing in the trampled snow, his coat torn open in red seams. François hauled him close to the fire, turning him so the flames could read the story written on his hide—gaping rips, ragged cuts, the slick dark of blood already stiffening where the air bit it.

“Dat Spitz fight lak hell,” Perrault said, bending to look as if he were judging meat at market, not wounds.

“An’ dat Buck fight lak two hells,” François answered, and the pride in his voice was the same pride men take when a hard loaf comes out of the ashes light and sound, proof that hands and patience can master hunger. “An’ now we make good time. No more Spitz, no more trouble, sure.”

Perrault went to packing the camp outfit, stowing and lashing as neatly as any pantry is set in order before a long winter. François began to harness the team, leather straps and frozen buckles snapping and creaking. The dogs watched the ritual with the solemn attention of those for whom supper is the true clock of the world.

Buck trotted up without hesitation to the place where Spitz had stood—at the head, where the trail first spoke into the darkness and the leader answered. He planted himself there as if the snow had been waiting for his feet.

But François, not looking, brought Sol-leks forward instead. In his mind Sol-leks was the best lead-dog left: an old hand, steady as a well-seasoned pot, blind on one side but canny on the other.

Buck sprang at him like a sudden boil-over. He drove Sol-leks back, shouldered him out, and stood where he had meant to stand all along.

“Eh? eh?” François cried, slapping his thighs with delight. “Look at dat Buck. Heem keel dat Spitz, heem t’ink to take de job.”

Then his voice hardened. “Go ’way, Chook!”

Buck did not move. He had the stubbornness of a root crop in frozen ground—if you wanted it, you’d have to dig, and digging would cost you.

François took him by the scruff. Buck growled, low and thick, a sound that carried heat in it though the air was fifty below. François dragged him aside and put Sol-leks in front again. Sol-leks showed plainly he wanted no part of it; his body said fear even when his face tried to say nothing.

François turned to another buckle. When his back was half a heartbeat away, Buck was back in the lead, displacing Sol-leks again. Sol-leks did not resist; he slid away like a dog thankful to avoid the knife.

François returned with a heavy club. The club’s dark grain caught the firelight, and Buck, seeing it, remembered another club, another man—red sweater, red anger—and the lesson that had burnt itself into his bones: there were laws beyond teeth.

He retreated slowly, not in defeat but in calculation, circling just outside the reach of the club, his lips lifted, his eyes fixed not on François but on the arc the wood could travel. He had learned the geometry of punishment.

The driver called him when the harnessing reached the point where Buck belonged in his old place in front of Dave. Buck backed away two steps. François advanced; Buck backed away again. The dance went on until François, thinking the fear had done its work, threw the club down.

It was then the truth showed itself. Buck was not asking for mercy. He was refusing it. He wanted the lead. He had bought it in blood, and he would not accept change as if it were charity.

Perrault stepped in. Between them they chased him around camp for the better part of an hour. They flung clubs; Buck slipped aside. They cursed him—his fathers, his mothers, his seed to the remotest generation, every hair and drop of blood—and Buck answered with snarls and the hard grin of a dog who will not bargain his hunger down.

He did not run away. He kept close, orbiting the fire and the sled and the line of harnessed dogs, making it plain: meet my demand, and I will come in. Leave it unmet, and I will stay out forever.

At last François sat down and scratched his head as if trying to find the missing sense under his fur cap. Perrault looked at his watch and swore. Dawn was a faint promise behind the trees, and already they were late.

François scratched again, then shook his head and grinned sheepishly at Perrault. Perrault shrugged, beaten.

François went to Sol-leks, unfastened him, and led him back to his old place. The team stood in one long line, harnessed and waiting, the sled ready. There was nowhere left for Buck but the front.

François called. Buck, at a distance, laughed—dogs do laugh, with a loose mouth and bright eyes—and kept away.

“Throw down de club,” Perrault ordered.

François obeyed. The club fell into the snow with a dull thud, like a lid set down.

Buck came in at once, trotting lightly, laughing triumphantly, and swung into position at the head. François fastened his traces. The sled was broken out. Both men ran, and the team dashed onto the river trail, bells chiming thinly in the wide white silence.

Highly as François had valued Buck—his “two devils”—he found before the day was old that he had still undervalued him. Buck took up leadership as if it were a craft learned long ago in another life and only lately remembered. Where quick judgment mattered—choosing the firmest line over glare ice, sensing the weak snow crust before a runner broke through—he proved superior even to Spitz, whom François had once believed unmatched.

But it was in laying down the law that Buck shone. Dave and Sol-leks did not care. Their pride was the pull itself, the deep satisfaction of harness biting shoulders and the sled moving because they willed it. So long as the traces stayed taut and the day’s work was honest, leadership was no affair of theirs. Let even Billee, the soft-hearted, lead, if only the team kept order.

The others had grown unruly in Spitz’s last days, like a kitchen left without a head cook—each pot boiling its own way, each hand stealing taste when it pleased. Buck set them straight.

Pike, sly and lazy, who loved to nip at heels and yet hold back his own weight until forced, learned quickly what loafing cost. Buck shook him again and again, swift as a hand slapping a thieving boy away from the cooling rack. Before the first day ended, Pike was pulling more than he ever had.

That night, Joe—the sour one—tested the new rule as he had tested every rule. Buck did what Spitz had never managed: he smothered Joe with sheer weight, pinned him, and cut him up until snapping turned to whining, and the bitterness in him found no purchase.

The tone of the team lifted at once. Old solidarity returned. They leaped as one dog in the traces, and the runners sang their thin song across packed snow.

At Rink Rapids, two native huskies were added—Teek and Koona—raw as newly split kindling. Buck broke them in so quickly François stared as if he’d watched a man knead frozen flour into bread without fire.

“Nevaire such a dog as dat Buck!” he cried. “No, nevaire! Heem worth one t’ousan’ dollair, by Gar! Eh? Wot you say, Perrault?”

Perrault nodded. The record run was on, and they were ahead of it, gaining day by day.

The trail was good—hard packed, clean, with no new-fallen snow to drink the strength from shoulders and pads. It was not too cold, though the thermometer dropped to fifty below and stayed there, a steady iron taste in the air. The men ran and rode by turns. The dogs were kept on the jump, stops few and short.

The Thirty Mile River lay comparatively coated with ice, and they covered in one day going out what had taken them ten days coming in. From the foot of Lake Le Barge to the White Horse Rapids they made a sixty-mile dash. Across Marsh, Tagish, and Bennett—seventy miles of lakes—they flew so fast the man whose turn it was to run was towed behind the sled on the end of a rope, skimming like a dragged branch.

On the last night of the second week they topped White Pass and dropped down the sea slope with the lights of Skaguay and the shipping at their feet. Fourteen days, forty miles a day: a record run.

For three days Perrault and François threw chests up and down Skaguay’s main street, the work clattering and booming between buildings, and were deluged with invitations to drink. The team was the center of a worshipful crowd of dog-busters and mushers, men whose talk was all lash, lead-dog, and luck. Then three or four western bad men tried to clean out the town and were riddled like pepper-boxes for their pains, gunfire cracking the air and scattering attention as quickly as wind scatters ash. Public interest turned to other idols.

Then came official orders, crisp as paper that has crossed too many hands. François called Buck to him, threw his arms around him, and wept over him, his tears freezing on the fur as if sorrow itself could be preserved in this country.

And that was the last of François and Perrault. Like other men, they passed out of Buck’s life for good.

A Scotch half-breed took charge of Buck and his mates. In company with a dozen other dog-teams they started back over the weary trail to Dawson. It was no light running now, no glory of record time. This was the mail train, and the load behind was heavy with letters—thin sheets of the outside world, tasting of ink and old rooms, carrying names and words across the white emptiness to men who sought gold under the shadow of the Pole.

Buck did not like it. The work was different—steady, grinding, without the bright edge of racing—but he bore up, and soon took pride in it in the same manner as Dave and Sol-leks. He kept discipline and saw that each dog, whether he cared for pride or not, did his share.

Life became monotonous, machine-like in its regularity. One day was very like another.

At a certain time each morning the cooks turned out. Fires were built. The smoke rose and flattened under the cold, and the sound of axes and the quick pop of sap in burning wood made a small music in the vast quiet. Breakfast was eaten. While some broke camp, others harnessed dogs, and they were under way an hour or so before the darkness thinned into the warning of dawn.

At night, camp was made again. Some pitched the flies; others cut firewood and pine boughs for beds. Still others carried water or ice for the cooks. And always, first for the dogs, came food—fish, frozen hard, then thawed and softened by heat until it gave off its heavy river smell. To them, that smell was home, history, and promise. Afterward it was good to loaf for an hour with the other dogs—five-score and odd—listening to the camp settle, to the iron clink of pots, to men’s voices roughened by cold, to the far-off hush of snow-laden timber. Here sound mattered. A sudden shout, a snapping whip, the harsh ring of a pan dropped carelessly could set nerves on edge like a knife on glass. In the clean hush, every noise was a choice; and Buck learned, as all creatures learn in the North, that peace is partly the absence of useless clamor.

There were fierce fighters among the dogs, but three battles with the fiercest brought Buck to mastery. After that, when he bristled and showed his teeth, they got out of his way.

Best of all, perhaps, he loved to lie near the fire, hind legs crouched under him, fore legs stretched out, head raised, eyes blinking dreamily at the flames. Sometimes he thought of Judge Miller’s big house in the sun-warmed Santa Clara Valley, of the cement swimming-tank, of Ysabel the Mexican hairless and Toots the Japanese pug. But oftener he remembered the man in the red sweater, the death of Curly, the great fight with Spitz—and, with a clarity that made his mouth wet, the good things he had eaten or would like to eat: warm meat steaming in the air, grease that coated tongue and throat, marrow-scent rising when bone cracked, the salt tang of blood in snow, fish softened at the edge of a fire until its skin split and its smell thickened into comfort.

He was not homesick. The Sunland was dim and distant, and such memories had no power over him. Far more potent were the memories of his heredity—instincts, the old habits of blood—giving to things he had never seen a strange familiarity. And sometimes, as he blinked into the flames, it seemed they were not these flames at all.

The fire became another fire.

He crouched beside it and saw another man—not the half-breed cook, but a man shorter of leg and longer of arm, with muscles stringy and knotted rather than rounded. The hair of this man was long and matted; his head slanted back under it from the eyes. He uttered strange sounds—no words Buck could translate, only the raw shapes of feeling pushed out into the cold—and he peered continually into the darkness, afraid of it, clutching in a hand that hung midway between knee and foot a stick with a heavy stone fastened to the end.

The man was all but naked, a ragged fire-scorched skin hanging partway down his back. Yet on his body there was much hair, matted into almost a fur across chest and shoulders and down the outside of arms and thighs. He did not stand erect but leaned forward from the hips on legs bent at the knees. There was in him a peculiar springiness, almost catlike, and the quick alertness of one who lived in perpetual fear of things seen and unseen.

At other times that hairy man slept by the fire, squatting with head between his legs, elbows on knees, hands clasped above his head as though to shed rain by hairy arms.

And beyond that fire, in the circling darkness, Buck could see many gleaming coals, two by two—always two by two—which he knew for the eyes of great beasts of prey. He could hear them crash through undergrowth, could hear the night noises they made, and those sounds—so old they were newer than any word—raised the hair along his back until it stood in a ridge over shoulders and up neck. He would whimper low or growl softly, and the half-breed cook would shout, “Hey, you Buck, wake up!”

Then the other world vanished. The real camp returned: the smoke, the men, the iron pots, the lean smell of fish. Buck would get up, yawn, stretch as if he had slept.

It was a hard trip with the mail and the heavy work wore them down. They were short of weight and in poor condition when they made Dawson, and should have had a week’s rest, ten days at least. But in two days’ time they dropped down the Yukon bank from the Barracks, loaded with letters for the outside.

The dogs were tired. The drivers grumbled. To make matters worse, it snowed every day, softening the trail, increasing friction on runners, and making every pull heavier. Yet the drivers were fair through it all, doing their best for the animals. Each night the dogs were attended to first. They ate before the drivers ate, and no man sought his sleeping-robe till he had seen to the feet of the dogs he drove—pads checked, ice cut away, cracks tended as carefully as a cook tends blistered hands.

Still, their strength went down. Since the beginning of winter they had traveled eighteen hundred miles, dragging sleds the whole weary distance. Eighteen hundred miles will tell upon the toughest life.

Buck stood it, keeping the team up to their work and maintaining discipline, though he, too, was very tired. Billee cried and whimpered regularly in his sleep each night. Joe grew sourer. Sol-leks became more unapproachable on either side, blind or seeing.

But it was Dave who suffered most.

Something had gone wrong with him, some inward hurt that showed itself only in the way his patience curdled. He became morose and irritable. When camp was pitched he made his nest at once and let his driver feed him there. Once out of harness and down, he did not rise again till harness-up in the morning.

Sometimes in the traces, when the sled jerked at a sudden stop or strained to start, Dave cried out with pain—a sharp sound in the clean air, a note that did not belong among bells and runners. The driver examined him, but could find nothing. All the drivers grew interested. They talked over it at meal-time and over their last pipes before sleep, voices low, the fire hissing softly as new wood caught.

One night they held a consultation. They brought Dave from his nest to the fire and pressed and prodded until he cried out many times. Something was wrong inside, but they could locate no broken bone and could not make it out.

By the time they reached Cassiar Bar he was so weak he fell repeatedly in the traces. The Scotch half-breed called a halt and took him out, making Sol-leks fast to the sled in his place. His intention was to rest Dave and let him run free behind, where the trail was easier.

Sick as he was, Dave resented it. He grunted and growled as the traces were unfastened, and when he saw Sol-leks standing in the position he had held so long, a broken-hearted whimper tore out of him. The pride of trace and trail was his. Sick unto death, he could not bear that another dog should do his work.

When the sled started he floundered in the soft snow alongside the beaten path, choosing the hardest going as if hardship itself were proof of worth. He attacked Sol-leks with his teeth, rushed against him, tried to thrust him off into the deep drift. He strove to leap inside the traces, to wedge himself between dog and sled, whining and yelping, crying with grief and pain.

The half-breed tried to drive him away with the whip. Dave paid no heed to the stinging lash, and the man had not the heart to strike harder. Dave refused the easy running behind. He continued to flounder beside the trail until he was exhausted. Then he fell and lay where he fell, howling lugubriously while the long train of sleds churned by, the runners whispering and grating, the bells tinkling with a cheerfulness that suddenly sounded shallow in the great white.

With the last remnant of his strength he staggered after them until the train made another stop. Then he floundered past the sleds to his own and stood alongside Sol-leks.

His driver lingered a moment to get a light for his pipe from the man behind. When he returned and started his dogs, they swung out on the trail with remarkable lack of exertion, then turned their heads uneasily and stopped in surprise.

The driver stopped too. The sled had not moved.

The men gathered, and there was the sight: Dave had bitten through both of Sol-leks’s traces. He stood directly in front of the sled, in his proper place, body shaking, eyes pleading with a desperate clarity. Let me stay. Let me do it. Let me be what I have been.

The driver was perplexed. The others talked of how a dog could break its heart when denied the work that killed it. They recalled instances—old dogs, injured dogs—who had died because they were cut out of the traces. Since Dave was to die anyway, they said, it was a mercy that he should die in harness, heart-easy and content.

So Dave was harnessed in again. Proudly he pulled as of old, though more than once he cried out involuntarily from the bite of his inward hurt. Several times he fell and was dragged in the traces. Once the sled ran upon him so that afterward he limped on a hind leg. Still he held out until camp was reached, and his driver made a place for him by the fire.

Morning found him too weak to travel. At harness-up he tried to crawl to his driver. By convulsive efforts he got on his feet, staggered, and fell. Then he wormed his way forward toward where the harnesses were being put on his mates. He advanced his fore legs, dragged his body up in a hitch, then advanced again—inch by inch, as if will alone could haul him into the traces.

His strength left him. The last his mates saw of him he lay gasping in the snow, yearning toward them. But they could hear him mournfully howling as they moved on, until the sound was swallowed behind a belt of river timber.

There the train was halted. The Scotch half-breed slowly retraced his steps to the camp they had left.

The men ceased talking. In the pause, the world’s small sounds grew large—the faint creak of leather, the soft impatient snort of a dog, the distant hush of snow falling as it had fallen for ages before mail or gold ever carved tracks into this country.

A revolver-shot rang out.

The man came back hurriedly. The whips snapped. The bells tinkled merrily again. The sleds churned along the trail.

But Buck knew—and every dog knew—what had taken place behind the belt of river trees.

Thirty days out of Dawson the Salt Water Mail dragged itself into Skaguay with Buck and his mates still in the lead, and the town met them the way a kitchen meets the end of a long shift: with the sour tang of sweat, wet wool, and old fish clinging to everything, and with eyes that could tell at a glance what the body could not admit.

They were used up. Buck, who had once carried one hundred and forty pounds as if it were a proud coat, had boiled down to one hundred and fifteen, his frame pared as clean as a stock after a hard simmer—no fat left to soften the edges. The others, lighter to begin with, had been reduced even more cruelly. Pike, who had made a life’s art of limping to avoid a pull, limped now because the joint truly burned. Sol-leks favored a leg in the dumb, loyal way he did all things. Dub’s shoulder-blade was wrenched and never set right, a torn hinge in a door that still must open.

Worst of all were their feet. There was no spring in them, no rebound—only the heavy, jarring fall of pads bruised into tenderness, each step a dull thud through bone and sinew. It wasn’t the clean exhaustion of a sprint that sleep can cure like a hot broth. It was the slow emptying-out of months, the sort of fatigue that drains not only muscle but the very wish to lift muscle again. In less than five months they had eaten up twenty-five hundred miles of trail, and in the last eighteen hundred they had been granted only five days’ rest. They came down Skaguay’s main street as if the traces were made of lead and their bodies of ash. On down grades they did not so much run as simply try not to be ground under the sled.

“Mush on, poor sore feets,” their driver coaxed, the old phrase tasting in the air like something said over a pot to keep it from sticking. “Dis is de las’. Den we get one long res’. Eh? For sure. One bully long res’.”

The men who drove them believed it. They had earned it. Any fair reckoning would have allowed loafing after twelve hundred miles with two days’ rest. But the mail had swollen like dough left too long near the stove—too many stamped envelopes, too many bundles for sweethearts and wives and kin who had stayed behind while men poured north. Orders came down with the same chill authority as winter itself: fresh Hudson Bay dogs were to replace the trail-worn; the worn were to be disposed of. Dogs counted for little against dollars. Even the language of it—worthless—was a knife: a dominant word that could cut life down to a ledger-mark.

Three days passed, and in those days Buck and the team learned how close to the end they truly were. On the morning of the fourth day two men from the States bought them harness and all, cheaply enough that the deal smelled of bargain more than justice. Buck watched the chaffering, saw money flash from hand to hand, and understood—without any word being spoken to him—that the Scotch half-breed and the mail-train drivers were slipping out of his life the way Perrault and François had done before.

The men called each other Hal and Charles. Charles was middle-aged, pale, with watery eyes and a mustache twisted up fiercely as if it could lend backbone to the weak lip beneath. Hal was nineteen or twenty and made noise without meaning: a big Colt’s revolver on his hip, a hunting-knife, a belt bristling with cartridges like a rack of shiny utensils in a kitchen owned by someone who couldn’t yet cook. The belt announced him—callow, loud, convinced the trappings were the craft.

At their camp Buck saw disorder the way a seasoned animal sees it the way a seasoned cook does: instantly, in the placement of things, in the waste, in the sourness beginning. The tent was only half stretched. Dishes lay unwashed. Bundles were scattered like ingredients left out to spoil. And there was a woman—Mercedes—Charles’s wife and Hal’s sister. The three of them made a family party, and for all their talk they moved like people who had never learned the humble grammar of work.

They set about taking down the tent and loading the sled with great effort and no method. The canvas was rolled into an awkward bundle three times the size it should have been, like dough overhandled until it toughens. Tin dishes were packed away still crusted, old grease and cold porridge smells clinging to them. Mercedes fluttered in and out of their way, chattering advice as incessantly as boiling water. When a clothes-sack was put at the front she insisted it belonged on the back; when it was moved to the back and covered over, she remembered something that “could abide nowhere else” but in that very sack, and the whole load was undone and done again.

Three men from a neighboring tent stepped out to watch. They grinned and winked at one another the way old hands do when they see a beginner about to burn the stew.

“You’ve got a right smart load as it is,” one said. “An’ it’s not me should tell you your business, but I wouldn’t tote that tent along if I was you.”

“Undreamed of!” Mercedes cried, hands up in dainty dismay. “However in the world could I manage without a tent?”

“It’s springtime,” the man answered. “You won’t get any more cold weather.”

She shook her head, and Charles and Hal piled on the last odds and ends until the sled looked like a pantry overturned and lashed down with hope.

“Think it’ll ride?” another asked.

“Why shouldn’t it?” Charles snapped.

“Oh, that’s all right,” the man soothed, backing away from offense. “Just a-wonderin’. Seems a mite top-heavy.”

Charles turned his back and drew the lashings down as best he could, which was to say poorly, the knots like bad stitching that will not hold when strain comes.

“And of course the dogs can hike along all day with that contraption behind them,” a second onlooker said, voice dry as smoke-cured fish.

“Certainly,” Hal replied with a politeness so cold it rang. He took the gee-pole and swung his whip. “Mush!” he shouted. “Mush on there!”

The dogs sprang against their breast-bands, strained until their lungs sawed at the air, then relaxed. The sled did not move. They could not shift it an inch.

“The lazy brutes, I’ll show them,” Hal cried, raising the whip.

Mercedes snatched it, a quick, pretty gesture like brushing flour from a sleeve. “Oh, Hal, you mustn’t. The poor dears! Promise you won’t be harsh with them for the rest of the trip, or I won’t go a step.”

“Precious lot you know about dogs,” Hal sneered. “They’re lazy. You’ve got to whip them. That’s their way. Ask any one.”

Mercedes turned her face to the men, imploring, her repugnance to pain plain as a white cloth. Their answer came blunt as an axe.

“They’re weak as water,” one said. “Plum tuckered out. They need rest.”

“Rest be blanked,” Hal snapped, and Mercedes flinched as if the oath itself were a blow.

But she was clannish. She whirled instantly to defend her brother against the stranger’s voice. “Never mind that man. You’re driving our dogs. Do what you think best.”

Hal’s whip fell. The team heaved themselves low, claws biting packed snow, shoulders shaking with the effort. The sled held fast as if rooted. After two tries they stood panting, and Hal’s whip whistled again—until Mercedes dropped to her knees before Buck and put her arms around his neck, tears in her eyes.

“You poor, poor dears,” she sobbed. “Why don’t you pull hard? Then you wouldn’t be whipped.”

Buck did not like her. Her hands smelled of perfumed soap and worry, not of trail-smoke and competent labor. But misery makes even dislike a thin thing; he endured the embrace as one endures a badly fitted harness.

One of the onlookers, jaw clenched, finally spoke, voice sharp with the effort not to curse.

“It’s not that I care a whoop what becomes of you,” he said, “but for the dogs’ sakes—break out that sled. The runners are froze fast. Throw your weight against the gee-pole, right and left. Break it out.”

A third attempt was made. Hal worked the pole as directed, and the runners cracked free from the snow with a sound like a crust giving way. The overloaded sled lurched forward. Buck and the others threw themselves into the pull, frantic under the rain of blows. A hundred yards ahead the trail turned and pitched steeply into the main street. It would have taken an experienced man to keep that top-heavy load upright, and Hal was not such a man.

On the turn the sled went over. Loose lashings spilled half the load. The dogs did not stop. Lightened, angered by the unjust burden and the club and whip, they surged forward. Buck, rage burning through weakness, broke into a run, the team following his lead. Hal shouted “Whoa! Whoa!” but he was hauled off his feet. The capsized sled ground over him as it skated on its side, and the dogs dashed up the street, scattering pots and blankets and sacks along Skaguay’s chief thoroughfare as if the town itself were to be seeded with their owners’ foolishness.

Kind-hearted citizens caught the dogs and gathered the scattered belongings. They offered advice the way elders offer it at a table where a young cook has ruined a pot: without much tenderness, because tenderness doesn’t fix hunger.

“Half the load and twice the dogs,” they said, “if you ever expect to reach Dawson.”

Hal and Charles and Mercedes listened as if the words were an insult. They pitched the tent again and began to overhaul the outfit. Canned goods were turned out that made men laugh—canned goods on the Long Trail, as absurd as carrying a piano into a blizzard. One man shook his head and said, “Blankets for a hotel. Half as many’s too much. Throw away that tent, and all those dishes—who’s going to wash them anyway? Good Lord, do you think you’re travelling on a Pullman?”

So began the ruthless cutting down—the inexorable elimination of the superfluous. Mercedes cried when her clothes-bags were dumped onto the ground. She cried over each discarded thing as if every object held a word of her past that could not be spoken again once it was thrown away. She rocked with her hands around her knees and vowed she would not go an inch, not for a dozen Charleses. She appealed to everyone and everything, then wiped her eyes and began, with sudden fierce zeal, to throw away even what a wiser woman would have called necessity. When she finished with her own things she attacked her husband’s and brother’s belongings and stripped them like a tornado.

Even halved, the outfit remained bulky. That evening Charles and Hal went out and bought six Outside dogs. Added to the original six and Teek and Koona—the huskies gained at the Rink Rapids—this made fourteen. Hal and Charles glowed with pride at the number; it was showy, like laying out a feast of tins and wrappers instead of real food. They had watched other sleds come and go, but never had they seen one harnessed to fourteen dogs. There was a reason for that, a simple arithmetic that any food historian would have smiled at bitterly: one sled cannot carry food for fourteen mouths for the length of the trail. But Hal and Charles did not know the old northern wisdom that lives not in books but in bodies. They had “worked the trip out” with a pencil—so much to a dog, so many dogs, so many days, Q.E.D.—and Mercedes nodded over their shoulders as if it were a recipe any fool could follow.

The next morning Buck led the long team up the street. There was no snap in him now, no eagerness, no proud lift. They were starting dead weary. Buck had traveled between Salt Water and Dawson four times already. The knowledge that he faced that same trail again, jaded and thin, made him bitter. His heart was not in the work, and no dog’s heart was. The Outside dogs were timid and frightened, their bodies untrained to the hard economy of the North; the Inside dogs had no confidence in their new masters.

Buck felt, in the dim, practical way he felt most truths now, that these two men and the woman could not be depended upon. They did not know how to do anything. As days passed it became clear they could not learn. They were slack in all things, without order or discipline. Half the night went to pitching a slovenly camp. Half the morning went to breaking it and loading the sled so badly that all day they were stopping to rearrange bundles. Some days they did not make ten miles. Other days they could not get started at all. And on no day did they make more than half the distance their pencil reckoning required.

It was inevitable they would go short on dog-food, but they hastened it with their ignorance. They overfed at first, bringing the famine sooner. The Outside dogs, not trained by months of hunger to make a mouthful last, ate with frantic appetite. When the worn huskies pulled weakly, Hal decided the orthodox ration was too small and doubled it. And when Mercedes, tears shining, could not cajole him into giving even more, she stole from the fish-sacks and fed the dogs slyly, as if kindness could be measured in handfuls and not in the far rarer currency of rest. But it was rest Buck and the huskies needed, not extra mouthfuls. The heavy load kept draining them, no matter what went down their throats.

Then the underfeeding came, as it always does when people mistake a calculation for a living world. Hal woke one day and saw the truth: half the dog-food was gone and only a quarter of the trail covered; and no more dog-food could be bought for love or money. So he cut even the orthodox ration and tried to increase the day’s travel. Charles and Mercedes seconded him with anxious voices and useless hands. It was easy to give the dogs less. It was impossible to make them go faster under a load still too heavy, with masters who could not rise early or work cleanly. Not only did they not know how to work dogs, they did not know how to work themselves.

Dub went first. Poor, blundering thief who was always getting caught and punished, he had been faithful in the traces. His wrenched shoulder, never treated, never given rest, went from bad to worse until Hal shot him with the big Colt’s revolver. The sound cracked across the spring air—an ugly, blunt noise that did not belong among the trickle of thaw-water and the returning birds. It was the sort of violent note that industrial life makes—sudden, careless, and deaf to the living soundscape around it.

They said in the country that an Outside dog starves on the ration of a husky. The six Outside dogs under Buck did no less than die on half the ration of a husky. The Newfoundland went first, then the three short-haired pointers. The two mongrels, with no spirit to break, clung longer, but they too went in the end.

By then the Southland gentlenesses had fallen away from the three people like frosting melting off a cake left in the sun. Arctic travel, stripped of its romance, became a reality too harsh for their manhood and womanhood. Mercedes stopped weeping over the dogs; she was too busy weeping over herself and quarrelling with her husband and brother. Quarrelling was the one thing they were never too weary to do. Their irritability rose out of misery, doubled with it, outdistanced it. They did not learn the wonderful patience the trail can teach to those who toil and suffer and yet keep sweet speech. They had no inkling of such a dialect of endurance. Their muscles ached, their bones ached, their hearts ached, and sharp words came first in the morning and last at night.

Charles and Hal wrangled whenever Mercedes gave them the smallest opening. Each believed he did more than his share, and neither hesitated to announce it. Mercedes, shifting alliances between husband and brother, made a beautiful and unending family quarrel. A dispute over who should chop a few sticks for fire would swell grotesquely—fathers and mothers dragged in, uncles, cousins, dead relatives, people thousands of miles away, as if old grievances were canned and opened now to spill their brine into the present. Hal’s opinions on art, or the society plays his mother’s brother wrote, somehow became relevant to the chopping of a Yukon fire. Charles’s political prejudices became proof of his laziness. Mercedes found Charles’s sister’s “tale-bearing tongue” relevant to building a camp. While they fought, the fire stayed unbuilt, the tent half pitched, the dogs unfed.

Mercedes nursed a special grievance—the grievance of sex. She was pretty and soft and had been treated chivalrously all her days. Here her husband and brother were everything but chivalrous, and because helplessness had been her accepted role, their complaints struck at what she believed her right. She made their lives unendurable. She stopped considering the dogs at all and, because she was sore and tired, insisted on riding the sled. Pretty and soft, she still weighed one hundred and twenty pounds—an added sack of grain on a starving team. She rode for days until the dogs fell in the traces and the sled stood still. Charles and Hal begged her to walk, pleaded, entreated, and she answered with tears and prayers that told Heaven of their brutality.

Once they took her off the sled by main strength. She went limp like a spoiled child and sat down in the trail. They traveled three miles without her. She did not move. They unloaded, came back, and by main strength put her on again. They never tried it a second time.

In the excess of their own misery they became callous to the suffering of their animals. Hal’s theory was that one must get hardened. He had preached it at first to his sister and brother-in-law; failing there, he hammered it into the dogs with a club. At the Five Fingers the dog-food gave out entirely. A toothless old squaw offered to trade a few pounds of frozen horse-hide for Hal’s Colt’s revolver—the same revolver that had taken Dub. Hal gave it up. The hide was a poor substitute, stripped from starved horses months before. Frozen, it was like galvanized iron. When a dog worried it into his stomach it thawed into thin, innutritious leather strings and a mass of short hair—irritating, indigestible, a mockery of sustenance. It filled the mouth but did not feed the body, like a word from a dominant tongue that names hunger without knowing its taste.

Through it all Buck staggered on at the head of the team as in a nightmare. He pulled when he could; when he could not, he fell and lay until whip or club drove him up again. The stiffness and gloss had gone from his coat. The hair hung limp and draggled, or clotted with dried blood where the club had bruised him. His muscles had wasted to knotty strings; the flesh pads were gone so each rib and bone stood out clean beneath loose hide wrinkled in folds of emptiness. It was heartbreaking—only Buck’s heart was unbreakable. The man in the red sweater had proved that long ago.

As it was with Buck, so it was with his mates. They were perambulating skeletons. In their misery they had grown dull to pain. The lash and the club were distant sensations, like sounds heard through thick walls. Their eyes saw dimly; their ears heard dimly. They were not half living. They were bags of bones with faint sparks fluttering. When a halt came they dropped in the traces like dead dogs and the spark seemed to gutter out. When the club fell, it flickered up just enough to make them totter to their feet and stagger on.

A day came when Billee, good-natured to the last, fell and could not rise. Hal had traded away the revolver, so he took the axe and knocked Billee on the head where he lay in the traces, then cut the carcass out of the harness and dragged it aside. Buck saw it. The others saw it. They knew the circle was closing.

The next day Koona went. Then only five remained besides Buck: Joe, too far gone to be malignant; Pike, crippled and limping, no longer conscious enough to malinger; Sol-leks, one-eyed, still faithful, mournful because he had so little strength left to give; Teek, who had not traveled so far that winter and so was beaten more because he was fresher; and Buck, still in lead, but no longer enforcing discipline, blind with weakness half the time, keeping the trail by its loom and by the dim feel of his feet.

It was beautiful spring weather, but neither dogs nor humans had room in themselves to notice it. Each day the sun rose earlier and set later; dawn came by three in the morning and twilight lingered until nine. The long day blazed. The ghostly winter silence had given way to the great spring murmur of awakening life, and that murmur came up from all the land like music returning to a house after a long illness. Sap rose in the pines. Willows and aspens burst into young buds. Shrubs and vines dressed themselves in fresh green. Crickets sang at night; in the day creeping, crawling things rustled out into the sun. Partridges boomed and woodpeckers knocked in the forest. Squirrels chattered. Birds sang. Overhead, wild-fowl honked north in cunning wedges that split the air.

From every hill slope trickled running water, the music of unseen fountains. All things thawed—bending, snapping, letting go. The Yukon strained to break loose the ice that bound it; water gnawed from beneath while sun ate from above. Air-holes formed. Fissures sprang and spread. Thin sections fell bodily into the river. And amid that bursting, rending, throbbing of life returning—under blazing sun and through soft-sighing breezes—staggered, like wayfarers to death, the two men, the woman, and the huskies.

When John Thornton had frozen his feet the December before, his two partners had done the decent, human thing: they’d built him up a snug place, fed him, kept the worst of the wind off, and then gone on up-river after a raft of saw-logs bound for Dawson. By the time the river ran free again and the days unhooked themselves from winter’s dark, he was still a touch stiff, a man who limped as if the cold had left teeth in him. But spring is a slow cure that works like broth—steady, warming, impossible to argue with. And lying there by the riverbank through long, bright hours, Thornton watching the running water, Buck listening to it, both of them letting the world’s own sound do its mending, Buck’s strength came back in layers.

Rest, after three thousand miles of whip-crack and harness-rub, comes like a thick blanket. It must be confessed: as his wounds knit, Buck grew lazy. The raw places closed; the swell of muscle returned; the bones stopped showing their sharp edges beneath the hide. He ate and slept and stretched in the sun the way a creature does when it has learned, for the moment, that no club is coming down. They were all loafing—Thornton, Buck, and the two other dogs—waiting for the raft that would carry them down to Dawson.

Skeet was a little Irish setter with bright, competent eyes and a way of getting her will without ever seeming to demand it. She had the doctor-trait that makes some animals tender and methodical; she tended Buck as if tending were a daily craft, like kneading dough until it turns smooth beneath the heel of the hand. Each morning, after Buck finished his breakfast—fish or bacon grease scraped from a pan, a crust softened with hot water, whatever the camp had—Skeet went to work. Her tongue was warm, rasping, clean as river-stone, and she licked away crusted blood and the sour edge of infection. She worried the edges of wounds the way an old cook worries a simmering pot—never letting it boil over, never letting it go cold. Day after day she did it, until Buck began to look for her ministrations as surely as he looked for Thornton’s voice.

Nig was different: huge, black, half bloodhound and half deerhound, with a laugh in his eyes and a body built for steady work rather than show. He was friendly without fuss, content to be near, content to share warmth and space and a place by the fire when the night wind came down.

To Buck’s surprise, neither dog begrudged him Thornton. No jealousy soured their companionship. They seemed to have taken on something of the man’s own largeness, as if kindness were a stew that had flavored them all. As Buck grew stronger, Skeet and Nig drew him into ridiculous games—feints and chases, ambushes behind spruce stumps, mock-fights that ended in rolling heaps of fur and panting laughter. Thornton himself would join, clapping his hands, calling out, letting his deep voice tumble into the air like a drumbeat. In that fashion Buck romped through convalescence and into a new existence.

Love—genuine, passionate love—came to him then for the first time. Down in Judge Miller’s Santa Clara Valley there had been comfort, yes: a roof, a yard, clean straw, the taste of well-fed life. There had been a working partnership with the Judge’s sons and their guns and horses; there had been a pompous, half-amused guardianship with the grandsons; there had been stately friendship with the Judge himself. But love that burned and rushed and made the blood run hot—adoration, madness—had waited for John Thornton to wake it.

Thornton had saved his life; that alone could have bound a dog. Yet it was more than that. He was the sort of master other men could not pretend to be. Many men looked after dogs because dogs were property, because a dog was muscle on four legs and teeth at the end of a chain—good business, good sense. Thornton looked after them the way a man looks after his own—because he could not help it. He remembered a kindly greeting. He spent words the way a good camp-cook spends salt: not much, but enough to make the whole thing worth eating. He liked to sit and “gas” with his dogs as if their silence were only another language, one he could half understand if he listened long enough.

He had a way of taking Buck’s head roughly between his hands, pressing his own forehead down against Buck’s, shaking him back and forth while he called him names that sounded like curses to any stranger but were love-words to Buck. Buck knew no greater joy than that harsh embrace and the rumble of Thornton’s voice close to his ear, the man’s breath smelling of smoke and coffee and the greasy sweetness of bacon. With each jerk it seemed Buck’s heart would be shaken loose from his chest, so fierce was the ecstasy. Then Thornton would let go, and Buck would spring up, mouth open in a soundless laugh, eyes bright, throat vibrating with something he could not turn into speech. And Thornton, half in awe, would say, “God! you can all but speak!”

Buck’s own way of showing love bordered on hurt. Often he would take Thornton’s hand in his mouth and close down until the flesh held the print of his teeth. It was his caress, and Thornton understood it as Buck understood the man’s oaths—intimate, rough, and true.

Yet for the most part Buck loved at a distance. He went wild when Thornton touched him or spoke to him, but he did not pester for it. Skeet would push her nose under Thornton’s hand, nudging until petted; Nig would stalk up and lay his great head on Thornton’s knee as if to claim his share of contact. Buck was content to worship. He could lie for hours at Thornton’s feet, alert as a sentinel, studying the man’s face—each small change of expression, each twitch of cheek or narrowing of eye, the lift of a shoulder, the settling of hands. Or he would lie a little aside or behind, watching the outline of Thornton’s body against firelight or snowlight. Often, in the quiet communion of their days, the force of Buck’s gaze would pull Thornton’s head around. The man would look back, wordless, and in the shining of his eyes Buck saw his own devotion answered.

For a long time after his rescue Buck could not bear Thornton to leave his sight. From the moment Thornton stepped from the tent to the moment he returned, Buck followed. Transient masters since the South had taught him that men vanished—Perrault and François, the Scotch half-breed, all gone out of his life like smoke blown from a chimney. Even at night that fear rose in him. He would shake himself awake, creep through the cold to the tent-flap, and stand listening to the sound of his master breathing. That sound—steady, human, alive—was as comforting as the quiet simmer of a pot on coals when the storm’s outside.

And yet, for all this love that might have seemed to civilize him, the primitive strain the Northland had awakened did not sleep. Faithfulness and devotion—things born of fire and roof—were his; but wildness and wiliness were his too. He was not a soft Southland dog stamped with generations of easy living. He was a thing out of the wild come in to sit by John Thornton’s fire. Because of his love, he could not steal from Thornton. But from any other man, in any other camp, he would steal without a blink. He stole with such cunning that he escaped discovery, and he ate his stolen food with a quick, secret savor, as if the very act sharpened the taste.

His face and body were scored by old fights, and he fought more fiercely than ever, and more shrewdly. Skeet and Nig were too good-natured to quarrel—and besides, they were Thornton’s. But strange dogs, of whatever breed or valor, either acknowledged Buck’s supremacy quickly or found themselves struggling for life. Buck was merciless. He had learned the law of club and fang and had learned it well: never surrender an advantage, never draw back from a foe you have started on the road to death. He had learned from Spitz and the hard-bitten leaders of police and mail. There was no middle course. Master or be mastered. Mercy was not a virtue in that primeval life; it was misunderstood for fear, and misunderstandings killed. Kill or be killed, eat or be eaten—that old mandate out of the depths of time beat in him like a drum.

He was older than the days he had lived. He linked past and present, and the eternity behind him throbbed through him in a mighty rhythm. He sat by Thornton’s fire broad-breasted, white-fanged, long-furred, but behind him moved the shades of other dogs—half-wolves and wild wolves—urgent as hunger. They tasted the savor of the meat he ate, thirsted for the water he drank, lifted their invisible muzzles with him to scent the wind. They listened with him and told him what the forest said in its own tongue: the dry tick of a squirrel in spruce boughs, the soft pad of something cautious in moss, the far cry that braided itself through the trees at dusk. It was a language without words, untranslatable into any human talk, and yet it worked on his blood more surely than any command. It dictated moods, directed actions, lay down with him when he slept, and dreamed through him and beyond him until the dreams became theirs.

So strongly did those shades beckon that each day the claims of mankind slipped farther away. Deep in the forest a call sounded, mysteriously thrilling and luring. When Buck heard it he felt compelled to turn his back on the fire and trampled ground and plunge into the green dimness, on and on, not knowing where or why. He did not wonder; the call did not allow wonder. It was imperative as the smell of fresh-killed meat. Yet as often as he gained the soft unbroken earth and the shadowed hush, the love for John Thornton drew him back again, as if a stronger hand held the end of his spirit’s leash.

Thornton alone held him. The rest of mankind was as nothing. Chance travelers might praise him or pat his head; Buck endured it coldly, and if a man grew too demonstrative he would get up and walk away. When Thornton’s partners, Hans and Pete, arrived at last on the long-expected raft, Buck refused to notice them until he understood they were close to Thornton. After that he tolerated them, accepting favors as though he bestowed a favor by accepting. They were of Thornton’s large type—men who lived close to earth and fire, thinking simply and seeing clearly. Before they swung the raft into the big eddy by the saw-mill at Dawson, they understood Buck and did not press for intimacy the way Skeet and Nig did.

For Thornton, Buck’s love kept growing. Thornton alone could pack him in summer travel—strap a load on his back and have Buck take it without resentment. Nothing was too great for Buck when Thornton commanded. One day, after they had grub-staked themselves with the raft’s proceeds and left Dawson for the headwaters of the Tanana, the three men and the dogs sat on the crest of a cliff that dropped sheer to naked bedrock three hundred feet below. Thornton sat near the edge, Buck at his shoulder. A thoughtless whim seized the man. He gestured out over the gulf as if it were only a ditch in a yard.

“Jump, Buck!” he commanded.

In the next instant he was grappling with Buck on the very lip, for Buck had begun the movement with blind obedience, and Hans and Pete were dragging both dog and man back into safety. Buck’s claws scrabbled for purchase, and the wind that rose from the chasm smelled cold and empty as a kettle scraped clean.

“It’s uncanny,” Pete said when they had their breath back.

Thornton shook his head slowly. “No. It is splendid—and it is terrible, too. Do you know, it sometimes makes me afraid.”

“I’m not hankering to be the man that lays hands on you while he’s around,” Pete said, nodding toward Buck.

“Py Jingo!” Hans added. “Not mineself either.”

It was at Circle City, before the year was out, that Pete’s feeling proved itself. “Black” Burton—evil-tempered, malicious, with a mean streak that wanted to prove itself in public—had been picking a quarrel with a tenderfoot at the bar when Thornton stepped between them good-naturedly. Buck lay in a corner with his head on his paws, watching his master’s every movement the way a careful listener watches a musician’s hands. Burton struck without warning, straight from the shoulder, and Thornton spun, saving himself from falling only by clutching the rail.

Those watching heard a sound that was neither bark nor yelp—something closer to a roar—and they saw Buck leave the floor as if the air itself had thrown him. He went for Burton’s throat. The man saved his life by instinctively flinging out his arm, but he was hurled backward and Buck landed on him. Buck tore into the arm, then drove again for the throat. Burton blocked only partly. The throat opened. The room broke into shouting, boots scraping, chairs shoved back; in that sudden ugly noise Buck’s growl cut through like a saw.

Men were on him then, clubs and fists driving him back. A surgeon checked the bleeding while Buck prowled and snarled, trying to rush in, forced off again and again by hostile sticks. A miners’ meeting was called on the spot, quick as a jury in a rough land, and it was decided Buck had had sufficient provocation. He was discharged. But his reputation was made. From that day his name traveled through every camp in Alaska, passed from mouth to mouth over sour whiskey and strong coffee, spreading the way a good recipe spreads—remembered, repeated, altered in telling, but always returning to the same core truth: Thornton’s dog would kill for him.

Later, in the fall, Buck saved Thornton’s life in another fashion. The three partners were lining a long, narrow poling-boat down a bad stretch of rapids on Forty-Mile Creek. Hans and Pete moved along the bank, snubbing the boat with a thin Manila rope from tree to tree. Thornton stayed in the boat, poling, shouting directions to shore. Buck kept abreast on the bank, worried and anxious, ears full of the river’s roar—the kind of sound that drowns out thought and makes a creature’s heart hammer.

At a particularly bad spot, where a ledge of barely submerged rocks jutted into the current, Hans cast off the rope. Thornton poled the boat out into the stream while Hans ran downbank with the end in his hand, ready to snub it when it cleared the ledge. It cleared—and flew downstream in a current swift as a mill-race. Hans checked it too suddenly. The boat flirted over and snubbed into the bank bottom up. Thornton was flung out and carried down toward the worst of the rapids, wild water where no swimmer could live.

Buck sprang in at once. Three hundred yards down, in a mad swirl, he overhauled Thornton. When he felt Thornton’s hand grasp his tail, Buck headed for shore and swam with all his splendid strength. But the river’s pull was brutal; their progress toward land was slow, while their progress downstream was terrifyingly fast. Below came the fatal roaring where the current went wilder and was shredded into spray by rocks that thrust through like the teeth of an enormous comb. The suck of water as it took the last steep pitch was frightful. Thornton knew the bank was impossible. He scraped over one rock, bruised across a second, struck a third with crushing force, and caught its slippery top with both hands. He released Buck and shouted over the roar, “Go, Buck! Go!”

Buck fought, but the current owned him. He swept downstream, struggling, unable to win back. Thornton repeated the command, and Buck obeyed. He half reared from the water, throwing his head high for a last look, then turned toward the bank. Pete and Hans dragged him out at the very point where swimming ceased to be possible and destruction began.

They knew a man could cling to such a rock only minutes. They ran up the bank to a point far above Thornton. They fastened the snub-line around Buck’s neck and shoulders, careful not to choke him or foul his stroke, and launched him back into the river. Buck struck out boldly, but not straight enough. He discovered the error too late—Thornton was abreast of him, only a half-dozen strokes away, while Buck was being carried helplessly past.

Hans snubbed hard, as if Buck were a boat. The rope tightened in the sweep of current and jerked Buck under. He stayed under until his body struck the bank and they hauled him out. Half drowned, he lay limp while Hans and Pete pounded breath into him and water out, the way men work a bellows to bring fire back to coals. Buck staggered up and fell again. Then, faint through the thunder, Thornton’s voice reached them—unintelligible words, but unmistakably his. In that sound was everything Buck could not translate and yet understood perfectly: need, command, life slipping away.

It hit Buck like electricity. He sprang up and ran along the bank to the launch point ahead of the men.

Again the rope was attached. Again he was thrown in. This time he drove straight into the stream. He had miscalculated once; he would not be guilty twice. Hans paid out the rope with no slack while Pete kept it clear of coils. Buck held on until he was directly above Thornton’s rock; then he turned, and with the speed of an express train headed down upon him. Thornton saw him coming. As Buck struck like a battering ram with the whole force of the current behind him, Thornton reached up and closed both arms around the shaggy neck. Hans snubbed the rope around a tree. Buck and Thornton were jerked under.

They came up, went under again—strangling, suffocating—sometimes one uppermost, sometimes the other. They were dragged over jagged bottom, smashed against rocks and snags, and then veered in toward the bank. Hands seized them. The world became blows and cold and roaring sound, then the hard certainty of earth.

Thornton came to belly-down across a drift log while Hans and Pete worked him back and forth until life returned. His first glance was for Buck. Buck lay limp, apparently lifeless. Nig set up a howl—long, raw, full of the river’s grief—while Skeet licked Buck’s wet face and closed eyes with desperate thoroughness, as if her tongue could restore what the water had stolen. Thornton, bruised and battered himself, went carefully over Buck’s body when he could, hands pressing, listening with fingertips. He found three broken ribs.

“That settles it,” he said. “We camp right here.”

And camp they did, staying until Buck’s ribs knit and he could travel again.

That winter at Dawson, Buck performed another exploit—perhaps not so heroic, but one that raised his name many notches higher on Alaska’s totem-pole of fame. It pleased the three men mightily, for the exploit furnished them with the outfit they needed to make a long-desired trip into the virgin East, where miners had not yet appeared. It began with talk in the Eldorado Saloon, where men grew boastful over their favorite dogs, and the room rang with laughter, clinking glasses, the slap of cards—an indoor din that tried to outshout the clean, wide silence outside. In that commercial clamor, where everything had a price and every marvel was bait for a wager, Buck’s record made him a target. Thornton was driven to defend him.

After half an hour a man claimed his dog could start a sled with five hundred pounds and walk off with it. Another bragged six hundred. A third boasted seven hundred.

“Pooh! pooh!” said John Thornton. “Buck can start a thousand pounds.”

“And break it out? and walk off with it for a hundred yards?” demanded Matthewson, a Bonanza King and the man of the seven-hundred vaunt.

“And break it out, and walk off with it for a hundred yards,” Thornton said, cool as if he were ordering coffee.

“Well,” Matthewson said slowly, so all could hear, “I’ve got a thousand dollars says he can’t. And there it is.” He slammed a sack of gold dust down on the bar, big as a bologna sausage.

Silence fell. Thornton felt blood creep up his face. His tongue had tricked him. A thousand pounds—half a ton—suddenly stood in his mind not as a phrase but as a thing with weight and stubbornness. He had faith in Buck’s strength and had often thought him capable of such a start, but he had never faced it like this, with men’s eyes fixed and waiting. Worse: he didn’t have a thousand dollars. Nor Hans. Nor Pete.

“I’ve got a sled standing outside now,” Matthewson went on, “with twenty fifty-pound sacks of flour on it. So don’t let that hinder you.”

Thornton stared from face to face like a man hunting for a match in the dark. His gaze caught Jim O’Brien’s—Mastodon King, old-time comrade—and something in that look roused him into action he’d never have dreamed of.

“Can you lend me a thousand?” Thornton asked, almost a whisper.

“Sure,” O’Brien answered, thumping down a fat sack beside Matthewson’s. “Though it’s little faith I’m having, John, that the beast can do the trick.”

The Eldorado emptied into the street. Tables and cards were abandoned; dealers and gamekeepers came out; gamblers followed with their voices already shaping odds. Several hundred men, furred and mittened, banked around Matthewson’s sled. It had been standing for hours, and in the intense cold—sixty below—the runners had frozen fast to the hard-packed snow as if welded there. Men offered odds two to one that Buck could not budge it.

A quibble rose over the phrase “break out.” O’Brien argued it was Thornton’s privilege to knock the runners loose and leave Buck to start from a dead standstill. Matthewson insisted breaking out included breaking the runners from the frozen grip. The majority who had heard the bet decided for Matthewson, and the odds climbed to three to one against Buck.

There were no takers. Not a man believed. Thornton, hurried into the wager and now heavy with doubt, looked at the load—twenty sacks of flour, squat and pale, stacked like blocks—and the task seemed monstrous. The regular team of ten dogs lay curled in the snow before it, as if even they wanted no part of such folly. Matthewson grew jubilant.

“Three to one!” he proclaimed. “I’ll lay you another thousand at that figure, Thornton. What d’ye say?”

Thornton’s doubt showed, but his fighting spirit rose—blind to odds, deaf to the word impossible. He called Hans and Pete. Their sacks were slim, and with Thornton’s own they could scrape together only two hundred dollars. In the ebb of their fortunes it was all they had. They laid it down without hesitation against Matthewson’s six hundred.

The team of ten was unhitched. Buck, wearing his own harness, was put into the traces. Excitement had infected him; he felt, without knowing how, that he must do a great thing for John Thornton. Men murmured admiration at his splendid appearance. He was in perfect condition, without an ounce of waste. The one hundred and fifty pounds he weighed were all grit and virility. His coat shone like silk. Along his neck and shoulders the mane lifted with each movement as though excess vigor made each hair alive. His breast was great, his forelegs heavy, and the muscles along his body showed in tight rolls under the skin.

Men ran mittened hands over him and swore the flesh was hard as iron. The odds drifted down to two to one.

“Gad, sir! Gad, sir!” stuttered a king of the Skookum Benches. “I offer you eight hundred for him, sir, before the test, sir—eight hundred just as he stands.”

Thornton shook his head and stepped to Buck’s side.

“You must stand off from him,” Matthewson protested. “Free play and plenty of room.”

The crowd fell silent; only the gamblers’ voices rose, offering two to one without finding takers. Buck was magnificent, but the weight of the flour sat in every man’s imagination like a stone, and nobody wanted to loosen his pouch-strings.

Thornton knelt by Buck. He took Buck’s head in his hands and rested cheek against cheek. He did not shake him playfully or speak the rough love-curses Buck knew so well. He whispered into Buck’s ear—low, intimate, a private dialect meant for one listener.

“As you love me, Buck. As you love me.”

Buck whined, eager and contained.

Men watched, puzzled. The moment felt like conjuration—like calling power into a body with nothing but breath and words. When Thornton rose, Buck seized his mittened hand between his jaws, pressed his teeth in, and released slowly, half reluctant. It was his answer—speechless, but exact.

Thornton stepped well back.

“Now, Buck,” he said.

Buck tightened the traces, then slacked them for several inches—his learned way of taking the jerk. The harness creaked, leather stiff in the cold, the sound sharp in the hush.

“Gee!” Thornton’s voice rang out.

Buck swung to the right, ending in a plunge that took up the slack. The sudden jerk arrested his one hundred and fifty pounds. The load quivered. From beneath the runners rose a crisp crackling like ice breaking.

“Haw!” Thornton commanded.

Buck’s five minutes of fury and muscle, when he had dragged a weight no dog should have moved, had dropped sixteen hundred dollars into John Thornton’s hands as cleanly as if it were a haunch laid on a block. It unknotted debts, loosened the men from the grip of winter wages, and set a new hunger simmering in the partners—a hunger older than any of them, spoken of in cabin corners and river-mouth talk the way elders speak of vanished seed-grain: the Lost Cabin and the gold that went with it.

The tale had the sour tang of too many mouths repeating it. No one had the first name right; no living tongue could reach back far enough to say it whole. The oldest tradition stopped short and shrugged, and that shrug was part of the legend. But men had died swearing they had seen a rickety cabin sunk in spruce shadow, and they had clinched the swear with nuggets that were not like other nuggets in the North—odd in color and grain, as if the earth there had its own dialect of gold.

So John Thornton, Pete, and Hans turned their faces eastward with Buck and half a dozen dogs, and they went after a rumor as if it were meat.

They sledded up the Yukon for seventy miles, swung left into the Stewart, passed the Mayo and the McQuestion, and kept on until the Stewart thinned down to a thread that stitched between upstanding peaks—the continent’s backbone, cold and hard under snow, but in thaw smelling of stone and old water. Behind them the river towns and their clatter fell away. Ahead lay a quieter world, a world in which the loudest thing might be the sigh of runners on crust, or the soft complaint of a spruce bough easing snow from its shoulders.

John Thornton asked little of man or country. He carried salt as a cook carries his one sure seasoning, and his rifle as calmly as another man carries a ladle. If there was dinner, he took it as they traveled; if there wasn’t, he traveled on, sure as a farmer who trusts that the next field will have potatoes under the soil even if the first did not. Their bill of fare was straight meat, the kind that steams in the cold air when you cut it and smells of iron and clean fat. The sleds bore ammunition and tools; time was something they spent as freely as pine knots in a fire.

To Buck it was a feast without a table. It was fish pulled from black pools and flung down, flanks silvering in the light; it was fresh blood on snow, warm as broth; it was the brief, rich reek of marrow when Thornton cracked a bone and the dogs came in, each taking his share by right and rank. Weeks they traveled, steady as a heart-beat—day after day of moving, then weeks where they camped and the men burned holes through frozen muck, melting and washing dirt by firelight until their hands were raw and their wrists ached. Sometimes they ate poorly and the smell of their own hunger followed them like smoke. Sometimes game was abundant and they lay back gorged, dozing while the last grease crackled in a pan and the lodge filled with that homely, holy scent that means the body will live another day.

Summer came, and the world’s pantry changed. Dogs and men packed their loads on their backs, rafted across blue mountain lakes, and went down or up unknown rivers in slender boats whipsawed out of standing forest. Sap and fresh-cut wood perfumed their hands. Smoke from their cooking fires drifted through spruce and alder, and in its thin sweetness Buck heard the land’s breathing—the hush of water sliding under root and stone, the small dry clicks of beetles, the far hollow calls of birds that did not waste sound.

Months went and came. They twisted through uncharted vastness where no men were and yet where men might once have been, if the Lost Cabin were true. They crossed divides in summer blizzards, shivered under the midnight sun on naked mountains between timber line and eternal snows, dropped into valleys swarming with gnats that made a living whine around ears and eyes. In the shadows of glaciers they picked strawberries—small, fierce, red things that burst sharp and sweet on the tongue—and flowers as ripe and fair as any the Southland boasted. When fall arrived they penetrated a weird lake country, sad and silent. Wildfowl had been there, the elders’ chatter said so, but now there was no life, no sign—only chill winds combing the water, ice forming in sheltered places, and the melancholy rippling of waves on lonely beaches, a sound like a language dying out, syllable by syllable, where no one remains to answer.

Through another winter they wandered the obliterated trails of men who had gone before. Once they came upon a path blazed through the forest—ancient, its cuts half-healed, the sapwood greyed with time. It seemed to point straight at the Lost Cabin, as a spoon points to a mouth, but the path began nowhere and ended nowhere, and it stayed a riddle like the man who made it. Another time they found the wreckage of a hunting lodge worn down by years, the blankets rotted to brown shreds that smelled of mouse and mould. John Thornton, lifting the tatters, found a long-barrelled flint-lock and knew it for a Hudson Bay Company gun from the young days in the Northwest, when such a weapon was worth its height in flat-packed beaver skins. That was all. No note, no name, no word to translate the relic into a story the living could use.

Then spring came again. At the end of their wandering they did not find the Lost Cabin, but they found a shallow placer in a broad valley where gold showed in the washing-pan like yellow butter smeared across the bottom. The color of it made the eyes ache. They sought no farther. Each day they worked earned them thousands in clean dust and nuggets, and they worked every day. They sacked the gold in moose-hide bags—fifty pounds to a bag—tied and piled outside the spruce-bough lodge like so much firewood. They toiled like giants, and the days flashed by as if a man had blinked through them, and the treasure grew in a heap that looked too big to belong to any one season of a man’s life.

There was little for the dogs to do now beyond hauling in meat when Thornton killed it. So Buck spent long hours by the fire, his great head on his paws, the heat loosening his muscles until he was half asleep and half awake. In the crackle and pop of sap in the spruce logs he heard a second music underneath—a deeper, older cadence. The vision of the short-legged hairy man came more frequently now that there was less work to fill his days. Buck would blink into the coals and be there again, padding behind that man in another world he remembered with his body rather than his mind.

The salient flavor of that other world was fear. The hairy man slept by the fire with his head between his knees, hands clasped above, and he slept as men sleep when they have never truly trusted the dark. He started awake often, peering into shadow and flinging more wood on the fire, feeding the flame the way a cook feeds a stove when the pot must not go cold. By the sea-beach he gathered shellfish and ate them as he gathered—brine on his fingers, raw flesh swallowed quick—eyes roving for danger, legs ready to run at the first wrong sound. Through forest they crept noiselessly, Buck at his heels, alert and vigilant. The man heard and smelled as keenly as Buck, and Buck felt that shared listening in his bones: a life tuned to the smallest twig-snap, the faintest wing-brush, the distant cough of something large moving through timber.

The man could spring into trees and travel ahead as fast as on the ground, swinging from limb to limb, letting go and catching again without falter, as if he belonged to the branches. Buck carried memories of nights spent beneath trees where the man roosted, holding tight even in sleep. Fear lived there too, not shameful but necessary, like salt in meat: what preserved them.

Closely woven with these visions was the call that sounded in the deep forest. It filled Buck with a great unrest and strange desires. It tasted sweet in his blood, and it made his heart leap toward something he could not name. Sometimes he pursued it as if it were tangible, barking softly or defiantly according to his mood. He thrust his nose into cool wood moss and snorted at the fat earth smell—rich as turned loam, damp as a cellar where potatoes are stored. He pressed his muzzle into black soil where long grasses grew, reading the ground the way Thornton read a pan. Or he crouched for hours behind fungus-covered trunks, wide-eyed, wide-eared to all that moved and sounded. He did not know why he did these things. He was driven to them, and there was no reasoning in it, only appetite.

The impulses came like sudden cravings. He might be dozing in the heat of the day in camp, belly full, the air steeped with the faint lingering savor of boiled meat, when his head would lift and his ears would cock, as if some distant note had struck him like the ring of a spoon on an iron pot. He would spring up and dash away, on and on for hours, through forest aisles and across open spaces where the niggerheads bunched. He loved to run down dry watercourses where old water had left its map, and to creep and spy on bird life. He would lie for a day in underbrush where he could watch partridges drum and strut, their hollow thumps rolling through the leaves like muffled hands on a skin. Most of all he loved to run in the dim twilight of summer midnights, listening to the subdued and sleepy murmurs of the forest—wind sifting needles, water gurgling under stones, insects rasping their small instruments—reading the land’s signs and sounds as man reads a book, and seeking the mysterious something that called him waking or sleeping.

One night he sprang from sleep with a start, nostrils quivering, mane bristling in recurrent waves. From the forest came the call—or one note of it, for the call had many notes—distinct and definite as never before: a long-drawn howl, like yet unlike any noise made by husky dog. Buck knew it, in the old familiar way, as a sound heard before—heard, perhaps, by fires that were not made of split spruce, by tongues that had no words for “mine” or “money.”

He sprang through the sleeping camp and, in swift silence, dashed into the woods. As he drew nearer he went more slowly, caution in every movement, until he came to an open place among the trees. There, erect on haunches, nose pointed to the sky, was a long, lean timber wolf.

Buck made no noise, yet the wolf stopped howling and tried to sense him. Buck stalked into the open half crouching, tail straight and stiff, body compact and ready. Every movement showed threat braided with friendliness—the menacing truce of beasts that prey. But at the sight of him the wolf fled.

Buck followed with wild leapings, a frenzy to overtake. He ran the wolf into a blind channel in the bed of a creek where a timber jam barred the way. The wolf whirled about, pivoting on hind legs after the fashion of cornered huskies, snarling and bristling, teeth clipping together in rapid snaps.

Buck did not attack. He circled, hedged him in with friendly advances, stepping in and out of reach as if to say, I am not here to take your life. The wolf was suspicious and afraid—Buck was three times his weight and the wolf’s head barely reached Buck’s shoulder. Watching a chance, the wolf darted away and the chase resumed. Time and again he was cornered and the same dance repeated, though the wolf was in poor condition, or Buck would not have taken him so easily. He would run until Buck’s head was even with his flank, then whirl at bay, only to dash off again at the first opening.

At last Buck’s persistence wore down fear. The wolf, finding no harm in Buck’s closeness, finally sniffed noses with him. A breath passed between them—hot meat-breath and wild-cold breath, each tasting the other’s history. Then they became friendly and played about in that nervous, half-coy way fierce beasts have when they pretend they are not fierce.

After some time the wolf started off at an easy lope that plainly showed he was going somewhere. He made it clear Buck was to come. Side by side they ran through sombre twilight, straight up the creek bed into the gorge from which it issued and across the bleak divide where it took its rise.

On the opposite slope they came down into level country—great stretches of forest, many streams. Hour after hour they ran, the sun rising higher, the day growing warmer. Buck was wildly glad. He knew he was at last answering the call, running beside his wood brother toward the place from which the call surely came. Old memories came upon him fast. He stirred to them as he had once stirred to the realities they were shadows of. He had done this thing before somewhere in that dim remembered world, and he was doing it again: free, the unpacked earth underfoot, wide sky overhead, the forest’s vast hush broken only by their breathing and the rhythmic drumming of their feet.

They stopped by a running stream to drink. The water was sharp as new-cut ice, and when Buck lifted his head the world seemed to ring with its own clean sound. In that pause, John Thornton came back to him—Thornton’s hand, Thornton’s voice, the domestic certainty of the camp-fire, the smell of meat cooking slow and steady. Buck sat down.

The wolf started on, then returned, sniffing noses and making little urging motions. But Buck turned and began to retrace his steps. For the better part of an hour the wild brother ran beside him, whining softly. Then the wolf sat, pointed his nose upward, and howled—a mournful sound that thinned with distance as Buck held on, until it was lost.

John Thornton was eating dinner when Buck dashed into camp and sprang on him in a storm of affection, overturning him, scrambling over him, licking his face, biting his hand in play—“playing the general tom-fool,” as Thornton called it, while he shook Buck back and forth and cursed him lovingly. The lodge smelled of cooked meat and smoke, and the sound of Thornton’s laughter was a hearth in Buck’s chest.

For two days and nights Buck never left camp. He did not let Thornton out of his sight. He followed him at his work, watched him while he ate, saw him into his blankets at night and out of them in the morning, as if the simple rituals—fire made, meat shared, bodies sleeping close—could hold the world in place.

But after two days the call began to sound more imperiously than ever, threading through the forest’s own speech. Restlessness came back on him. Recollections haunted him: the lean wolf, the smiling land beyond the divide, the long run through wide timber stretches. Once again he took to wandering, but the wild brother came no more, and though Buck listened through long vigils, the mournful howl was never raised.

He began to sleep out at night, staying away from camp for days at a time. Once he crossed the divide at the head of the creek and went down into the land of timber and streams. For a week he wandered, seeking fresh sign of the wild brother, killing his meat as he traveled and traveling with the long, easy lope that seems never to tire. He fished for salmon in a broad stream that emptied somewhere into the sea, and the fish were living silver, their bodies cold and strong between his jaws, their taste rich as oil and river stones. By that stream he killed a large black bear that had been blinded by mosquitoes while fishing too, raging helpless through brush—terrible and stupid with torment. Even so it was a hard fight, and it woke the last latent remnants of Buck’s ferocity. Two days later he returned to the kill and found a dozen wolverenes quarrelling over the spoil. He scattered them like chaff; those that fled left two behind who would quarrel no more.

The blood-longing grew stronger. He was a killer, a thing that preyed, living on living things—unaided, alone—by virtue of strength and craft, surviving where only the strong survived. Pride rose in him and flowed outward into his very carriage. It spoke in the way he placed his feet, in the lift of his head, in the calm of his eyes. His coat, already glorious, seemed more glorious still, each hair alive to air and touch. Save for the stray brown on his muzzle and above his eyes, and the splash of white that ran down the middle of his chest, he might have been mistaken for a gigantic wolf—larger than the largest of the breed. The size and weight were his St. Bernard father’s gift, but the shape of that size—muzzle long as a wolf’s, head broader but still wolf-like—came from his shepherd mother.

His cunning was wolf cunning and wild cunning; his intelligence was shepherd and St. Bernard intelligence; and the schooling he had endured made him formidable as any creature that roamed those ranges. On straight meat he was in full flower, at the high tide of life, overspilling with vigor. When Thornton passed a hand along his back, a snapping and crackling followed—each hair discharging its pent magnetism at the contact. Brain and body, nerve and fiber, were keyed to exquisite pitch, balanced and adjusted so perfectly that to sight, sound, and event he responded with lightning swiftness. Another dog would need time to see and then act; Buck saw, decided, and answered in what seemed one instant. His muscles snapped into play like steel springs, and glad life streamed through him until it seemed it would burst him and spill over the world.

“Never was there such a dog,” John Thornton said one day as the partners watched Buck march out of camp.

“When he was made, the mould was broke,” Pete added.

“Py jingo! I t’ink so mineself,” Hans affirmed.

They saw him go, but they did not see what happened the instant he slipped into the secrecy of the forest. He no longer marched. He became a thing of the wild—cat-footed, a passing shadow among shadows. He took cover as naturally as breath, crawled on his belly like a snake, and like a snake could leap and strike. He could take a ptarmigan from its nest, kill a rabbit as it slept, snap in midair the chipmunks that fled a second too late. Fish in open pools were not too quick for him; beaver mending dams were not too wary. He killed to eat, not for wantonness, but he preferred what he killed himself—the way a hunter prefers his own smokehouse meat to a stranger’s flour. A lurking humor ran through his deeds: he delighted in stealing upon squirrels and, when he all but had them, letting them go chattering in mortal fear to the treetops, the forest ringing briefly with their scolding and then settling again into its older, wiser quiet.

As fall came on, moose appeared in greater abundance, moving slowly down to meet winter in lower valleys. Buck had dragged down a stray part-grown calf, but he hungered for larger, more formidable quarry. He came upon it one day on the divide at the head of the creek: a band of twenty moose, and chief among them a great bull in savage temper. He stood over six feet at the shoulder, a tower of muscle and resentment. Back and forth he tossed palmated antlers branching to fourteen points, spanning seven feet within the tips. His small eyes burned with bitter light, and he roared with fury at Buck’s appearance.

From the bull’s side, just forward of the flank, protruded a feathered arrow-end, and pain sharpened his rage. Guided by old hunting instinct—older than Thornton’s maps and pans—Buck set about cutting the bull from the herd. It was no slight task. He barked and danced in front of the bull, always just out of reach of antlers and terrible splay hoofs that could stamp life out in one blow. The bull could not turn his back on that fanged danger and go on, and his helplessness drove him into paroxysms. He charged; Buck retreated craftily, luring him on by pretending he could barely escape. But as the wounded bull began to separate, two or three younger bulls would rush back at Buck, giving their leader a chance to rejoin the herd.

There is a patience of the wild—dogged, tireless, persistent as hunger—that holds a spider motionless, a snake coiled, a panther waiting. It belonged to Buck now. For half a day he clung to the herd’s flank, retarding its march, irritating young bulls, worrying cows with half-grown calves, driving the wounded bull mad with rage. He seemed to multiply, attacking from all sides, a whirlwind of menace that cut his victim out as fast as it could regain the others. The prey’s patience is less than the hunter’s; Buck knew it without knowing.

As the day wore and the sun dropped toward its bed in the northwest—darkness returned, and the fall nights ran six hours long—the young bulls came back to aid their leader more and more reluctantly. Winter pressed them toward lower levels. It seemed they could never shake off this tireless creature that held them back. Besides, it was not their lives demanded—only one life, the toll the herd could afford. In the end they paid it.

As twilight fell the old bull stood with lowered head, watching his mates—the cows he had known, calves he had fathered, bulls he had mastered—shamble on through fading light. He could not follow. Before his nose leaped the merciless fanged terror that would not let him go. He weighed three hundredweight more than half a ton. He had lived long and strong, full of struggle, and now he faced death at the teeth of a creature whose head did not reach beyond his great knuckled knees.

From then on, night and day, Buck never left his prey. He gave it no rest, permitted it neither to browse willow shoots nor strip birch. He did not let it slake thirst in the slender trickling streams they crossed. Often, desperate, the bull burst into long stretches of flight. Buck did not try to stop him then; he loped easily at his heels, satisfied with the game, lying down when the moose stood, attacking fiercely when it tried to eat or drink.

The great head drooped more and more under its tree of horns. The shambling trot weakened. The bull began to stand for long periods, nose to ground, ears dropped limply. Buck found more time to drink himself, more time to rest, tongue lolling red, eyes fixed on his quarry. And in those pauses it seemed to him a change was coming over the face of things. A new stir moved through the land. As moose came in, other life came in too. Forest and stream and air felt palpitant—not with summer’s small busyness, but with larger presences. The knowledge came not by sight, smell, or sound alone, but by some subtler sense. Still, the soundscape had altered: birds spoke of it differently, squirrels chattered in sharper notes, even the wind whispered a warning as it sifted through needles. Strange things were afoot and ranging. Buck resolved to investigate once he finished what he had begun.

At last, at the end of the fourth day, he pulled the great moose down. For a day and a night he remained by the kill, eating and sleeping, turn and turn about. The meat was heavy and satisfying, warm from the body at first, then cooling and firming, the fat congealing white along the edges. He ate deep, the way a creature eats who knows the land can go lean without notice.

Then, rested and strong, he turned his face toward camp and John Thornton. He broke into the long, easy lope and went on hour after hour, never at loss in tangled way, heading straight home through strange country with a certainty of direction that shamed man’s magnetic needle.

As he held on, he grew more and more aware of the new stir. Life abroad was different from the summer life. Now he did not merely feel it in mystery. The very air carried message. Several times he stopped and drew in the fresh morning in great sniffs, reading what the wind wrote and what the forest’s muted noises underlined. Each time the message made him leap forward with greater speed.

A sense of calamity pressed him—if it were not calamity already happened. And as he crossed the last watershed and dropped down into the valley toward camp, he moved with greater caution, his feet quiet, his ears straining to catch what the land was trying, in its own untranslatable tongue, to tell him.
