I walked to the railway station, accompanied by Gabriel Betteredge. The letter was in my pocket, and the nightgown—carefully packed in a small bag—was ready for Mr. Bruff’s inspection before I slept that night. We left the house in silence. For the first time in all my dealings with him, I found Betteredge unusually quiet, as if something heavy pressed on his thoughts. I had questions to ask, so I broke the silence as soon as we passed through the lodge gates.

“Before I go to London,” I began, “I have two things I want to know. They concern me personally—and I think they’ll surprise you.”

“If they take that poor creature’s letter out of my head,” he replied, “they can do anything else to me. Please begin surprising me, sir, as soon as you like.”

My first question was this: “Betteredge, were you ever drunk on the night of Rachel’s birthday?”

“You drunk?” he exclaimed, startled. “Why, it’s your greatest flaw, Mr. Franklin—that you only drink with your dinner, and never touch a drop after!”

“But the birthday was special,” I said. “I might have broken my usual habits.”

He paused, then answered slowly. “You did step outside your routine, sir. You looked dreadfully ill. We thought it best to give you a little brandy and water to revive you.”

“I’m not used to spirits,” I said. “It’s possible——”

“Wait,” he interrupted. “I knew you weren’t used to it. So I poured you half a wineglass of our fifty-year-old Cognac—then (shame on me!) I drowned it in nearly a tumbler of cold water. A child couldn’t get drunk on that, let alone a man.”

I trusted his memory. He was too precise, too honest, to exaggerate such a detail. It was impossible—I had not been intoxicated.

Then I asked my second question. “Before I went abroad, Betteredge, you saw me often when I was a boy. Now tell me honestly—did you ever notice anything strange about me at night? Did you ever catch me walking in my sleep?”

He stopped, looked at me sharply, nodded once, and resumed walking. “Ah, now I see where you’re going, Mr. Franklin! You’re trying to explain how the paint got on your nightgown without knowing it. But no, sir—it won’t work. You’ve still got miles to go before you reach the truth. Walk in your sleep? Never. Not once in your life.”

Again, I felt compelled to believe him. My life had never been solitary—abroad or at home. If I had been a somnambulist, someone would have noticed. Hundreds of people would have warned me. Yet still, despite all reason, I clung stubbornly to one of two explanations—the only ones I could imagine—for the unbearable position I now occupied.

Seeing I wasn’t satisfied, Betteredge shifted tactics. He spoke of later events in the Moonstone’s history, and with a few well-placed words, shattered both my theories completely.

“Let’s try another way, sir,” he said. “Hold onto your own ideas. See how far they lead you toward the truth. Suppose we accept the nightgown as evidence—though I don’t, mind you. Then you not only smeared the paint off the door without knowing it, but you also took the Diamond without realizing it. Is that correct so far?”

“Quite right,” I said. “Go on.”

“Very well. Let’s say you were drunk—or asleep—when you took the jewel. That explains the night and morning after the birthday. But what about everything since? The Diamond has been taken to London. It’s been pledged to Mr. Luker. Did you do those things without knowing it too? Were you drunk when I saw you off in the pony-chaise that Saturday evening? And did you walk in your sleep all the way to Mr. Luker’s after the train brought you there?”

He paused, then added gently, “Forgive me, Mr. Franklin, but this ordeal has shaken you deeply. You’re not yet fit to judge your own case. The sooner you put your head beside Mr. Bruff’s, the sooner you’ll find your way out of this trap.”

We reached the station with just minutes to spare. I hastily gave Betteredge my address in London, promising to write if anything came to light. As I turned to leave, I glanced toward the book-and-newspaper stall—and there was Ezra Jennings again, speaking to the keeper. Our eyes met. He removed his hat; I returned the gesture, and stepped into the carriage just as the train pulled away.

It was a relief, I suppose, to focus on something trivial. At least, I found myself wondering—ridiculously enough—how I’d seen the man with the piebald hair twice in one day.

When I arrived in London, it was too late to find Mr. Bruff at his office. I drove instead to his private residence in Hampstead. There, I found the old lawyer dozing in his dining room, a pug dog curled on his lap, a bottle of wine within reach.

I shall describe the effect of my story on Mr. Bruff best by recounting his actions after I finished. He ordered lights and strong tea brought to his study, and sent word to his family that we were not to be disturbed under any circumstances.

Once settled, he examined the nightgown, then read Rosanna Spearman’s letter aloud.

When he finished, he addressed me for the first time since we’d shut ourselves in.

“Franklin Blake,” he said, “this is serious—in more ways than one. In my view, it concerns Rachel just as much as it does you. Her behavior makes sense now. She believes you stole the Diamond.”

I had avoided facing that conclusion—but it had already forced itself upon me. My determination to speak to Rachel directly rested entirely on that very point.

“The first step,” Mr. Bruff continued, “is to confront Rachel. She’s remained silent—understandably, given her nature. But we can no longer endure her silence. She must either be persuaded to speak, or made to speak. If we can break through her reserve, the whole case may collapse.”

“That’s comforting for me,” I said. “I confess I’d like to know——”

“You’d like to know how I justify that hope,” he interjected. “I can tell you in two minutes. I approach this from a legal standpoint. It’s a matter of evidence. Very well—where does the evidence fail?”

“Where?”

“At the outset. I admit the name proves the nightgown belongs to you. I admit the paint marks prove it touched the door. But what proof is there that *you* wore it on the night the Diamond vanished?”

The objection struck hard—because it mirrored one I’d already felt.

“Now,” he continued, picking up the letter, “I understand this letter is painful for you. You may hesitate to analyze it impartially. But I am not in your position. I can apply professional judgment. Without discussing her past crimes, consider this: she admits she was skilled in deception. Therefore, I suspect she hasn’t told the full truth. I won’t speculate on what she may have done. But if Rachel suspected you based solely on the nightgown, the odds are ninety-nine to one that Rosanna showed it to her. And if so—her letter reveals she was jealous of Rachel, that she switched the roses, that she saw a chance to drive a wedge between you. She didn’t intend to die then. And with that opportunity—she had the motive, the means, and the character to act. What do you say?”

“Something like that,” I admitted, “crossed my mind when I opened the letter.”

“Exactly. But you pitied her. You couldn’t bring yourself to suspect her. Does you credit, my dear sir—does you credit!”

“But suppose I *did* wear the nightgown? What then?”

“I don’t see how we could prove it,” he said. “But even if we could, clearing your name wouldn’t be easy. Let’s wait and see whether Rachel’s suspicion rests only on the nightgown.”

“Good God!” I burst out. “How calmly you talk about Rachel accusing me!”

“A fair question,” he replied. “Hotly phrased—but worth pondering. What troubles you troubles me too. Reflect—did anything happen while you were at the house that might have shaken her trust in your principles, even if not your honor?”

I jumped to my feet, overwhelmed. His question stirred a memory long buried.

In Betteredge’s account, there’s mention of a foreigner who came to visit me on business. I had foolishly accepted a loan from a Parisian restaurateur—a man I knew well. When I couldn’t repay it, I sent a bill. It bounced. His affairs had collapsed. His relative, a French lawyer, came to England to demand payment. He was furious, and I reacted poorly. We argued fiercely—my aunt and Rachel heard us. Lady Verinder intervened. The man produced papers, accused me of ruining an honest man. My aunt paid him immediately and sent him away. She knew me better than to believe the worst—but she was shocked by my recklessness. Either she told Rachel, or Rachel overheard. Whatever happened, Rachel took it to heart. I was “heartless,” “dishonorable,” “without principle.” She said things I’d never heard from a woman before. We quarreled for a day. I mended things the next—but forgot it until now.

Mr. Bruff listened, then nodded gravely. “That would affect her mind. And I wish, for your sake, it hadn’t happened. But now we know there was a predisposition against you. One uncertainty cleared. What else can we do? We must go to Rachel.”

He paced the room, deep in thought. Twice, I almost told him I intended to speak to her myself—twice, I hesitated, mindful of his age and dignity.

“The real difficulty,” he said, “is how to make her reveal everything—without reservation. Any suggestions?”

“I’ve decided,” I said, “to speak to her myself.”

“You?” He stopped abruptly, staring at me as if I’d lost my senses. “You, of all people!”

He turned away, then paused. “Wait. In cases like this, the reckless path is sometimes the right one.”

He considered, then nodded firmly. “Nothing venture, nothing have. You have an advantage I lack—and you shall try it.”

“An advantage?”

He smiled faintly. “This is how it stands. I don’t trust your discretion—or your temper. But I do believe Rachel still harbors, somewhere deep inside, a foolish weakness for you. Stir that—and trust to the consequences. But how will you see her?”

“She’s been a guest here,” I said. “May I suggest—if nothing was said beforehand—that I might meet her here?”

“Cool,” he murmured, turning slowly.

“In plain terms,” he said, “my house becomes a trap. The bait? An invitation from my wife and daughters. If you were anyone else, or if this were less serious, I’d refuse. But I believe Rachel will thank me one day for betraying her in my old age. Consider me your accomplice. She’ll be invited to lunch and stay the afternoon. You’ll be notified.”

“When?”

“Not tomorrow. Too short a notice. The day after.”

“How shall I hear?”

“Stay at home all morning. Expect me.”

I thanked him sincerely, declined his offer to stay overnight, and returned to my lodgings.

The next day was the longest of my life. Though I knew I was innocent, though I was certain the accusation would eventually be cleared, I felt a profound shame. I avoided every visitor. I stayed indoors all day, emerging only under cover of darkness.

The following morning, Mr. Bruff surprised me at breakfast. He handed me a large key and confessed he felt ashamed for the first time in his life.

“Is she coming?”

“She is. Today. To lunch and spend the afternoon.”

“Are Mrs. Bruff and your daughters in on it?”

“Inevitably. But women, as you know, have no principles. My family doesn’t share my guilt. Their only concern is bringing you and Rachel together. They’ll overlook the means, as if they were Jesuits.”

“I’m grateful. What’s this key?”

“The gate in the back garden wall. Be there at three. Let yourself in, go through the conservatory, cross the small drawing-room, and open the door into the music-room. You’ll find Rachel—alone.”

“How can I thank you?”

“Don’t blame me for what happens next.”

And with that, he left.

I waited through weary hours. To pass the time, I read my letters. Among them was one from Betteredge. I opened it eagerly—only to find it began with an apology: no news of importance. Then, once again, Ezra Jennings appeared. He’d stopped Betteredge at the station, asked who I was, and mentioned seeing me to Mr. Candy. Mr. Candy, hearing this, had driven over to express regret at our missing each other. He had something urgent to say to me—and begged I inform him when I returned near Frizinghall.

Beyond a few typical Betteredge reflections, that was all. He admitted he’d written mainly for the joy of writing to me.

I crumpled the letter and forgot it—consumed by the approaching meeting with Rachel.

At three o’clock, I inserted the key into the garden gate. As I stepped in and locked it behind me, I felt a sudden unease. I glanced around, half-expecting to see a witness hidden in the shadows. But the garden was empty—only birds and bees present.

I walked through the garden, entered the conservatory, crossed the small drawing-room, and placed my hand on the door opposite.

Inside, I heard soft piano chords—familiar notes, played idly, just as she had done when I lived at her mother’s house.

I paused, steadying myself. The past and present stood side by side. The contrast shook me.

After a minute, I summoned my courage—and opened the door.
