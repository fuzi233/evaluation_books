Mrs. Dashwood and her daughters had scarcely anticipated, upon their arrival in Devonshire, that their days would be so swiftly consumed by social engagements. Instead of finding leisure for quiet reflection, they were besieged by invitations and visitors, leaving little room for serious pursuits. Once Marianne recovered from her illness, Sir John's long-planned schemes for amusement were set in motion. Private balls at the park became frequent, and parties on the water were organized whenever the October rains permitted. In every gathering, Willoughby was present, and the casual intimacy of these occasions served only to deepen his connection with the Dashwood family. He found ample opportunity to observe Marianne's virtues, to express his fervent admiration, and to receive, in her conduct toward him, unmistakable signs of her affection.

Elinor, unsurprised by the depth of their attachment, merely wished that it were displayed with less openness. On a few occasions, she ventured to suggest that Marianne exercise some self-restraint. However, Marianne detested concealment where no genuine disgrace existed. To her, attempting to suppress sentiments that were not inherently wrong seemed not just unnecessary, but a shameful submission of reason to mundane prejudice. Willoughby shared this view, and their behavior consistently reflected their convictions.

In Willoughby's presence, Marianne saw no one else. Every action he took was deemed correct; every word he spoke was considered witty. When evenings at the park concluded with card games, he would cheat himself and the entire party to ensure she held a winning hand. If dancing was the evening's entertainment, they were partners for half the night, and when forced apart, they stood close together, speaking to no one else. Such conduct inevitably drew laughter, yet ridicule failed to shame or provoke them.

Mrs. Dashwood embraced their feelings with such warmth that she felt no inclination to curb this excessive display. To her, it was simply the natural result of a passionate heart in a young, ardent mind.

For Marianne, this was a season of profound happiness. Her heart belonged entirely to Willoughby, and the deep affection she once held for Norland was beginning to soften, perhaps more than she had dared hope, under the charm of his company in her new home.

Elinor's happiness, however, was not so complete. Her heart was not at ease, nor was her satisfaction in these amusements pure. None of her companions could console her for what she had left behind, nor could they help her forget Norland with less regret. Neither Lady Middleton nor Mrs. Jennings could provide the conversation she craved. Although Mrs. Jennings was an incessant talker who regarded Elinor with kindness, she had already recounted her own history three or four times. Even if Elinor's memory matched her aunt's capacity for improvement, she might have learned early on the details of Mr. Jennings' final illness and his last words to his wife. Lady Middleton was preferable only in her silence, which Elinor perceived as a mere calmness of manner devoid of true sense. She treated her husband and mother with the same reserve, making intimacy neither expected nor desired. Lady Middleton had nothing new to say; her spirits remained unchanged, and her insipidity was constant. Though she did not oppose the parties arranged by her husband, provided everything was conducted with style and her two eldest children were present, she derived no greater enjoyment from them than she might have found sitting at home. Her presence added so little to the pleasure of others through conversation that they were sometimes reminded only of her by her concern for her troublesome sons.

Of all her new acquaintances, Elinor found only Colonel Brandon capable of earning her respect, inspiring her friendship, or providing genuine pleasure as a companion. Willoughby was out of the question. Her admiration and regard, even her sisterly affection, were entirely his, yet he was a lover whose attentions were wholly directed toward Marianne. A far less agreeable man might have been more universally pleasing. Unfortunately for himself, Colonel Brandon received no such encouragement to focus solely on Marianne. In conversing with Elinor, he found the greatest solace for the indifference shown to him by her sister.

Elinor's compassion for him grew as she suspected that the pain of disappointed love had already weighed heavily upon him. This suspicion arose from a remark he dropped one evening at the park while sitting together by mutual consent, as the others danced. His gaze was fixed on Marianne, and after a silence lasting several minutes, he said with a faint smile, "Your sister, I understand, does not approve of second attachments."

"No," replied Elinor, "her opinions are all romantic."

"Or rather, as I believe, she considers them impossible to exist."

"I believe she does. But how she manages this without reflecting on the character of her own father, who had himself two wives, I know not. A few years, however, will settle her opinions on the reasonable basis of common sense and observation; and then they may be more easily defined and justified than they are now by anyone but herself."

"This will probably be the case," he replied. "And yet, there is something so amiable in the prejudices of a young mind that one is sorry to see them yield to the reception of more general opinions."

"I cannot agree with you there," said Elinor. "There are inconveniences attending such feelings as Marianne's that all the charms of enthusiasm and ignorance of the world cannot atone for. Her systems have the unfortunate tendency of setting propriety at naught, and a better acquaintance with the world is what I look forward to as her greatest possible advantage."

After a brief pause, he resumed the conversation. "Does your sister make no distinction in her objections against a second attachment? Or is it equally criminal in everyone? Are those who have been disappointed in their first choice, whether due to the inconstancy of its object or the perverseness of circumstances, to remain equally indifferent for the rest of their lives?"

"Upon my word, I am not acquainted with the minutiae of her principles. I only know that I have never yet heard her admit any instance of a second attachment being pardonable."

"This," he said, "cannot hold; but a change, a total change of sentiments—No, no, do not desire it; for when the romantic refinements of a young mind are obliged to give way, how frequently are they succeeded by opinions that are too common and too dangerous! I speak from experience. I once knew a lady who, in temper and mind, greatly resembled your sister, who thought and judged like her, but who, from an enforced change—from a series of unfortunate circumstances—" Here he stopped abruptly, appearing to realize he had said too much. By his countenance, he aroused conjectures that might not otherwise have occurred to Elinor. The lady would likely have passed unnoticed had he not convinced Miss Dashwood that what concerned her ought not to escape his lips. As it was, it required but a slight effort of imagination to connect his emotion with the tender recollection of past regard. Elinor attempted no further inquiry. But Marianne, in her place, would not have done so little. The whole story would have been quickly constructed under her active imagination, and everything established in the most melancholy order of disastrous love.
