In the quiet sanctuary of the prior's garden, Gerard found a companion in a monk of singular ingenuity. This brother, a master of mechanics, had crafted the great sundial, a wheel to draw water from the well, and a winnowing machine for the grain. His workshop was a testament to restless invention; he had already built several psalteries and two dulcimers, and was currently striving to construct a set of regalles, small organs intended for the choir.

Gerard played the humble psaltery with modest skill, but the monk responded with a divine touch, revealing to Gerard his own profound ignorance of music. Yet, where the monk faltered in illumination, Gerard excelled in the art of writing. In an age where true accomplishment flowed from natural bent rather than forced study, their differences dissolved. Youth and Age stood side by side like brothers, and Gerard was warmly pressed to remain for the night. He sought the counsel of Denys, who, with a rueful shrug, granted his consent.

Gerard recounted his journey to his old friend, softening the harsher edges of their recent adventures. "Alack!" sighed the good old man. "I have traveled far in my day, yet never was I molested." He advised Gerard to shun the inns, which were haunts of rogues and rioters that could harm the soul even if the body escaped, urging him instead to seek refuge in peaceful monasteries. Suddenly, his demeanor shifted; he looked at Gerard with the sharp intensity of a needle and asked how long it had been since he had confessed. Gerard flushed, answering weakly, "Better than a fortnight."

"And thou an exorcist!" the monk exclaimed. "No wonder perils have overtaken thee. Come, thou must be absolved at once."

"Yes, father," Gerard replied, sinking to his knees with hands joined. But the monk stopped him, half-fretful. "Not to me! Not to me! I am as full of the world as any man living. My soul is bound to these wooden pipes and sorry leather stops, which shall perish with those whose minds are fixed on such vanities."

"Dear father," Gerard pleaded, "they are for the use of the Church, and surely that sanctifies the labor?"

"That is precisely what the devil has been whispering in my ear," the monk retorted, shaking a finger playfully yet threateningly. "He quoted Solomon building the Lord a house with rare hangings, claiming it was gracious and sinless. Oh, he can quote Scripture rarely! But I am not so simple a monk as you think, my lad," he cried, turning his gaze not to Gerard but to the empty space beside him. "This one toy finished, vigils, fasts, and prayers for me—prayers standing, prayers lying on the chapel floor, and prayers in a right good tub of cold water." He nudged Gerard and winked knowingly. "Nothing he hates more than seeing us monks at our orisons up to our chins in cold water. For *corpus domat aqua*. So now, go confess thy little trumpery sins, pardonable in youth and secularity, and leave me to mine, sweet to me as honey, to be expiated in proportion."

Gerard bowed his head, unable to resist asking, "Where shall I find a confessor more holy and clement?"

"In each of these cells," the monk replied simply as they moved into the corridor. "Go to Brother Anselm, yonder."

Gerard hastened toward the indicated cell, but the doors stood close together, and he seemed to have misjudged his destination. Just as he raised his hand to knock, he heard his old friend crying out in an agitated whisper, "Nay! Nay! Nay!" He turned to find the monk pacing anxiously before his own door, beating the air with double hands like a sawyer. Gerard feared the cell he sought housed some wild beast, or perhaps the very personage whose presence had been so distinctively proclaimed. Looking back inquiringly, he moved to the next door. Then, his old friend nodded rapidly, his face bursting into a blissful expression before shooting back into his den. He took his hourglass, turned it, and returned to his regalles, muttering, "Well-a-day, the sands how swift they run when the man is bent over earthly toys."

Brother Anselm was a venerable monk with an ample head and a face radiating dignity and love. As Gerard confessed, responding to the gentle yet searching questions, he found himself distracted by the sheer nobility of the man. "Here is a head!" he thought. "Oh dear! Oh dear! I wonder whether you will let me draw it when I have done confessing." His own mind grew confused, and he forgot certain crimes. Yet, he did not lower his guard regarding the nature of his past, nor did he diminish the pagan character of the stories he had bolstered.

The penance assigned was brief: Gerard was to enter the convent church, prostrate himself, and kiss the lowest step of the altar three times. Then, kneeling on the floor, he was to recite three *Paternosters* and a *Credo*, after which he must return immediately.

Having performed this short mortification, Gerard returned to find Father Anselm spreading plaster. "After the soul comes the body," the monk said. "Know that I am the chirurgeon here, for want of a better. This is going on thy leg; to cool it, not to burn it; the saints forbid."

During the operation, the monastic leech, having taken interest in the Düsseldorf branch of Gerard's confession, sided with Denys on the matter of bleeding. "We Dominicans seldom let blood nowadays," he explained. "The lay leeches say it is from timidity and want of skill; but in sooth, we have long found that simples will cure most ills that can be cured at all. Besides, they never kill in capable hands, whereas other remedies slay like thunderbolts. As for the blood, the Vulgate expressly says it is the life of a man.' And in medicine or law, as in divinity, to be wiser than the All-Wise is to be a fool. Moreover, simples are mighty. The little four-footed creature that kills the poisonous snake, if bitten herself, finds an herb powerful enough to quell that poison, though stronger and of swifter operation than any mortal malady; and we, taught by her wisdom and our own traditions, still search and try the virtues of those plants the good God hath strewed this earth with, some to feed men's bodies, some to heal them. Only in desperate ills we mix heavenly with earthly virtue. We steep the hair or the bones of some dead saint in the medicine, and thus work marvellous cures."

"Think you, father, it is along of the relics? For Peter a Floris, a learned leech and no pagan, denies it stoutly."

"What knows Peter a Floris? And what know I?" the monk countered. "I take not on me to say we can command the saints, and will they nill they, can draw corporal virtue from their blest remains. But I see that the patient drinking thus in faith is often bettered as by a charm. Doubtless faith in the recipient is for much in all these cures. But so 'twas ever. A sick woman, that all the Jewish leeches failed to cure, did but touch Christ's garment and was healed in a moment. Had she not touched that sacred piece of cloth she had never been healed. Had she without faith not touched it only, but worn it to her grave, I trow she had been none the better for't. But we do ill to search these things too curiously. All we see around us calls for faith. Have then a little patience. We shall soon know all. Meantime, I, thy confessor for the nonce, do strictly forbid thee, on thy soul's health, to hearken to learned lay folk on things religious. Arrogance is their bane; with it they shut heaven's open door in their own faces. Mind, I say, learned laics. Unlearned ones have often been my masters in humility, and may be thine. Thy wound is cared for; in three days 'twill be but a scar. And now God speed thee, and the saints make thee as good and as happy as thou art thoughtful and gracious."

Gerard hoped there was no need to part, for he was to dine in the refectory. But Father Anselm, with a shade of regret just perceptible, told him he would not leave his cell that week, being himself in penitence. With this, he took Gerard's head delicately in both hands, kissed him on the brow, and almost before the cell door had closed, was back to his pious offices. Gerard went away chilled to the heart by the isolation of the monastic life, and saddened too. "Alas!" he thought. "Here is a kind face I must never look to see again on earth; a kind voice gone from mine ear and my heart forever. There is nothing but meeting and parting in this sorrowful world. Well-a-day! Well-a-day!"

This pensive mood was interrupted by a young monk who came for him and took him to the refectory. There he found several monks seated at a table, and Denys standing like a poker, being examined as to the towns he should pass through. The friars then clubbed their knowledge, marking out the route and noting all the religious houses on or near that road, which they gave to Gerard. After supper, the old monk carried Gerard to his cell, and they had an eager chat. The friar incidentally revealed the cause of his pantomime in the corridor. "Ye had well-nigh fallen into Brother Jerome's clutches. Yon was his cell."

"Is Father Jerome an ill man, then?"

"An ill man!" the friar crossed himself. "A saint, an anchorite, the very pillar of this house! He had sent ye barefoot to Loretto. Nay, I forgot, ye are bound for Italy; the spiteful old saint upon earth had sent ye to Canterbury or Compostella. But Jerome was born old and with a cowl; Anselm and I were boys once, and wicked beyond anything you can imagine." Gerard wore a somewhat incredulous look. "This keeps us humble more or less, and makes us reasonably lenient to youth and hot blood."

Then, at Gerard's earnest request, one more heavenly strain upon the psalterion, and so to bed, the troubled spirit calmed, and the sore heart soothed.

I have described in full this day, marked only by contrast, a day that came like oil on waves after so many passions and perils—because it must stand in this narrative as the representative of many such days which now succeeded to it. For our travelers on their weary way experienced that which most of my readers will find in the longer journey of life, viz., that stirring events are not evenly distributed over the whole road, but come by fits and starts, and as it were, in clusters. To some extent this may be because they draw one another by links more or less subtle. But there is more in it than that. It happens so. Life is an intermittent fever. Now all narrators, whether of history or fiction, are compelled to slur these barren portions of time or else line trunks. The practice, however, tends to give the unguarded reader a wrong arithmetical impression, which there is a particular reason for avoiding in these pages as far as possible. I invite therefore your intelligence to my aid, and ask you to try and realize that, although there were no more vivid adventures for a long while, one day's march succeeded another; one monastery after another fed and lodged them gratis with a welcome always charitable, sometimes genial; and though they met no enemy but winter and rough weather, antagonists not always contemptible, yet they trudged over a much larger tract of territory than that, their passage through which I have described so minutely. And so the pair, Gerard bronzed in the face and travel-stained from head to foot, and Denys with his shoes in tatters, stiff and footsore both of them, drew near the Burgundian frontier.
