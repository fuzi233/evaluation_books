By six o’clock the next evening Sally had arrived at the sort of conclusion that tastes like cold tea: all day she had meant to find Ginger and pour a little wholesome counsel down his ear, and all day Roville had contrived to mislay him. In another hour she would be on the seven-fifteen express for Paris, on to Cherbourg, on to the great Atlantic liner, on to New York—on to Mrs. Meecher’s boarding-house and the particular kind of breakfast Mrs. Meecher believed in, which was chiefly noise.

Sally disliked leaving any human creature unelevated, even slightly. It was a habit of hers, almost domestic—like straightening a picture on the wall before you go out. Her brother Fillmore called it “messing about with people’s lives.” Sally called it “taking an interest.” Ginger Kemp, with his candid air of having been dropped into existence without proper instructions, seemed to need a shove in the direction of competence.

So, having deposited her baggage at the station and left it in the charge of an amiable porter who looked as if he had been born leaning on things, she went, at half-past six, to the Casino Municipale for a last prowl.

There were two casinos at Roville-sur-Mer. One, on the Promenade, specialized in sea air, mild virtue, and a game called boule that gives you time to reflect on your sins between rolls. The Casino Municipale, down by the Palace Massena near the railway station, was for men who meant business and, as a rule, meant it loudly.

Sally had barely entered when she saw it: a flare of red hair in the crowd at the roulette-table, like a flame among moths. Ginger. And Ginger was not merely present; Ginger was prospering.

He was entrenched behind a rich little landscape of square counters, piled with the care of a pastrycook building a display in a shop-window—layers and stacks, the sort of structure you expected to see topped with spun sugar. A wooden-faced croupier raked a fresh instalment toward him as if shoveling coal.

“Épatant!” murmured a wistful man near Sally, pulling an elbow out of her ribs so he could gesture more freely at Ginger’s magnificence.

Sally was no scholar, but she had been around enough hungry people to recognize admiration. The whole congregation around the table had the rapt, churchy look of persons attending a miracle in progress. Spectators at roulette always do. They don’t want to win themselves so much as they want to witness the existence of luck, in the same way children like to watch somebody else’s Christmas.

The croupier twisted his moustache with one hand, the wheel with the other, and a hush dropped like a cloth. Sally edged to a place where the crowd pressed less hard and could now see Ginger’s face clearly.

And at the sight of it she laughed.

Ginger looked exactly like a dog at a rat-hole: nose forward, eyes bright, hair practically on end, the whole of him quivering with that intense hopefulness which, in the kitchen, makes a man keep lifting a pot-lid every ten seconds to see if stew has decided to become done by sheer force of attention.

In the tense silence, Sally’s laugh rang out with an atrocious clearness. Heads turned with the pained horror of worshippers interrupted during a particularly sacred hymn. If she had guffawed in a cathedral, the effect would have been gentler. The crowd looked at her as if she had tossed a tomato at the altar.

Ginger started, turned, saw her—and jumped up.

At that precise instant the ball clicked into a red compartment.

Ginger had backed black.

A groan ran through the crowd, thick and communal, like the moan at a revival meeting when the preacher announces that somebody has gone back to drink. Glances of reproach hit Sally from all directions. It was plainly felt that her laughter had poisoned the very air over the wheel.

Ginger, however, showed no desire to lynch her. He swept up his winnings, shoved them into his pocket with the speed of a man putting away hot rolls before someone else notices them, and elbowed through to her. The crowd was bereaved. It was as though the star tenor had walked off in the middle of his big note, and even the sudden quarrel between two excitable gamblers over a disputed five-franc counter could not wholly distract them.

“I say,” said Ginger, deftly plucking Sally out of the press, “this is topping, meeting you like this. I’ve been looking for you everywhere.”

“It’s funny you didn’t find me, then, because that’s exactly where I’ve been. I was looking for you.”

“No, really?” He seemed absurdly pleased. He led her into the quiet ante-room outside the hall, where the air was calmer and smelled faintly of cigar smoke and polished wood instead of human suspense. They sat in a corner; the only other presence was a gorgeously uniformed attendant by the door, watching them with the indulgent interest of a man who has seen romance performed in many keys.

“That was awfully good of you,” said Ginger.

“I felt I must have a talk with you before my train went.”

Ginger gave a violent start.

“Your train? What do you mean?”

“The puff-puff,” explained Sally. “I’m leaving to-night, you know.”

“Leaving?” He stared at her as if she had just announced that gravity had been discontinued. “You don’t mean leaving? You’re not going away from Roville?”

“I’m afraid so.”

“But why? Where are you going?”

“Back to America. My boat sails from Cherbourg tomorrow.”

“Oh, my aunt!”

“I’m sorry,” said Sally, touched. It was pleasant, being regretted. “But—”

“I say—” Ginger flushed a bright, helpless scarlet and glared at the uniformed official, who was now openly basking in the idea that love was happening near him. The man even began to hum a love-ballad under his breath, softly, as if providing accompaniment.

“I say, look here,” said Ginger, “will you marry me?”

Sally stared at his vermilion profile in frank amazement. Ginger had a gift for doing surprising things with no apparent preparation, like an amateur who suddenly juggles three knives.

“Marry you!”

“You know what I mean.”

“Well, yes. The holy state. I do understand the general direction of your remarks.”

“Then how about it?”

Sally recovered her composure by degrees. Her sense of humour, always near the surface, wriggled delightedly.

“But isn’t this—don’t think I’m trying to make difficulties—isn’t this a little sudden?”

“It’s got to be sudden,” complained Ginger. “I thought you were going to be here for weeks.”

“But, my infant, my babe, has it occurred to you that we are practically strangers?” She patted his hand with an aunt-like tolerance that made the uniformed official emit a tender sigh. “I see what’s happened. You’re mistaking me for some other girl—some girl you were properly introduced to. Take a good look at me and you’ll see.”

“If I take a good look at you,” said Ginger feverishly, “I’m dashed if I’ll answer for the consequences.”

“And this,” said Sally, “is the man I was going to lecture on Enterprise.”

“You’re the most wonderful girl I’ve ever met, dash it!” said Ginger. He still wouldn’t look at her properly; he continued to aim his passion at the official by the door, as if the sight of a witness made it easier to be brave. “I dare say it is sudden. I can’t help that. I fell in love with you the moment I saw you, and there you are!”

“But—”

“Now look here, I know I’m not much of a chap and all that, but… well, I’ve just won the deuce of a lot of money in there…”

“Would you buy me with your gold?”

“I mean to say, we’d have enough to start on, and… of course I’ve made an infernal hash of everything I’ve tried up till now, but there must be something I can do, and you can jolly well bet I’d have a goodish stab at it. I mean to say, with you to buck me up and so forth, don’t you know. Well, I mean—”

“Has it struck you that I may already be engaged to someone else?”

“Oh, golly! Are you?”

For the first time he turned and looked at her. There was something in his eyes—so boyish, so honestly stricken—that punched the laughter out of her. Absurd though it was, he meant every word.

“Well, yes,” she said quietly. “As a matter of fact I am.”

Ginger bit his lip. For a moment he didn’t speak.

“Oh, well, that’s torn it!” he said at last.

Sally felt a wave of something she couldn’t neatly name—pity and amusement and an odd softness that was almost maternal. She wanted to pick him up and kiss him and tell him it would all come right, even though she couldn’t see how it would. What she could not do was regard him, at that instant, as a fully responsible adult.

“You don’t really mean it, you know,” she said.

“Don’t I!” said Ginger hollowly. “Oh, don’t I!”

“You can’t. There isn’t such a thing in real life as love at first sight. Love’s a thing that comes when you know a person well and—” She stopped. An awkward thought had popped up like a lump in a sauce: her own engagement to Gerald Foster had been fast enough to startle a stopwatch. What did she know of Gerald except that she loved him? They had become engaged within two weeks of their first meeting.

Her eloquence collapsed.

“It’s ridiculous,” she finished tamely.

Ginger subsided into a gloomy resignation.

“I couldn’t have expected you to care for me, I suppose, anyway,” he said. “I’m not much of a chap.”

That, at least, was useful. Sally seized it as a cook seizes a wooden spoon when a pot begins to boil over.

“That’s exactly what I wanted to talk to you about,” she said briskly. “I’ve been looking for you all day to go on with what I was starting to say in the lift last night, when we were interrupted. Do you mind if I talk to you like an aunt—or a sister, suppose we say? Really, the best plan would be for you to adopt me as an honorary sister. What do you think?”

Ginger did not look wildly thrilled by the proposed relationship.

“Because I really do take a tremendous interest in you.”

He brightened a little, as if interest were a warm thing you could put in your pocket.

“That’s awfully good of you.”

“I’m going to speak words of wisdom. Ginger, why don’t you brace up?”

“Brace up?”

“Yes—stiffen your backbone and stick out your chin and square your elbows and really amount to something. Why do you flop about and do nothing and leave everything to what you call ‘the family’? Why do you have to be helped all the time? Why don’t you help yourself? Why do you have to have jobs found for you? Why don’t you rush out and get one? Why do you have to worry about what the family thinks of you? Why don’t you make yourself independent of them? I know you’ve had hard luck, suddenly finding yourself without money and all that, but good heavens, everybody who’s ever done anything has been broke at one time or another. It’s part of the fun.

“You’ll never get anywhere by letting yourself be picked up by the family like—like a floppy Newfoundland puppy and dumped down in any old place that happens to suit them. A job’s a thing you’ve got to choose for yourself and get for yourself. Think what you can do—there must be something—and then go at it with a snort and grab it and hold it down and teach it to take a joke. You’ve managed to collect some money. It’ll give you time to look round. And when you’ve had a look round, do something! Try to realize you’re alive, and try to imagine the family isn’t!”

She stopped for breath.

Ginger sat very still, as if the speech had filled him like a strong draught.

“When you talk quick,” he said slowly, in a serious, reflective voice, “your nose sort of goes all squiggly. Ripping, it looks!”

Sally emitted an indignant little cry.

“Do you mean to say you haven’t been listening to a word I’ve been saying?”

“Oh, rather! Oh, by Jove, yes.”

“Well, what did I say?”

“You… er…” He considered. “And your eyes sort of shine, too.”

“Never mind my eyes. What did I say?”

“You told me,” said Ginger, with effort, “to get a job.”

“Well, yes. I put it much better than that, but that’s what it amounted to. All right, then. I’m glad you—”

“I say,” he interrupted, and his mournful devotion returned like a dog that has wandered off and found its way home, “I wish you’d let me write to you. Letters, I mean, and all that. I’ve an idea it would kind of buck me up.”

“You won’t have time for writing letters.”

“I’ll have time to write them to you. You haven’t an address or anything of that sort in America, have you? I mean, so that I’d know where to write to.”

“I can give you an address that will always find me.”

She told him the number and street of Mrs. Meecher’s boarding-house. He wrote them down reverently on his shirt-cuff, as if it were a recipe for salvation.

“Yes,” said Sally, relenting, “do write. Of course I’ll want to know how you’ve got on. I—oh, my goodness! That clock’s not right?”

“Just about. What time does your train go?”

“Go? It’s gone! Or, at least, it goes in about two seconds.”

She shot through the swing-door, startling the uniformed official, who had not expected romance to turn into athletics. Ginger followed with the sudden efficiency he always produced when bodies had to be moved quickly.

“Good-bye, Ginger,” she called over her shoulder as they burst into the square. “Write to me, and remember what I said!”

They ran. Roville’s evening had its own soundscape: the scrape of shoes on pavement, the thin whistle of distant trains, a motor-car coughing like a bored brass instrument, and underneath it all the steady sea-hush that made even human panic feel briefly theatrical. Sally, who believed the world was kinder when it made decent noises, found herself regretting the growing industrial grumble that seemed to stalk every station platform in Europe like an ill-tempered relative.

“Stick it!” said Ginger encouragingly, loping easily, as becomes a man who had once been a snip of an international scrum-half.

Sally saved her breath.

The train was already beginning to move as they sprinted on to the platform. Ginger dived at the nearest door, wrenched it open, gathered Sally in his arms with the confident handling of a man lifting a sack of flour, and flung her in.

She landed squarely on somebody’s toes.

Then, bounding upright, she made for the window. Ginger ran alongside the train as it gathered speed.

“Ginger! My poor porter! Tip him. I forgot.”

“Right ho!”

“And don’t forget what I’ve been saying.”

“Right ho!”

“Look after yourself and ‘Death to the Family!’”

“Right ho!”

The train slid out of the station. Sally took one last look back at her red-haired friend, who had halted and was waving a handkerchief. Then she turned, flushed and panting, to apologize to the other occupant of the compartment.

“I’m so sorry,” she said. “I hope I didn’t hurt you.”

She found herself facing Bruce Carmyle.

Mr. Carmyle was not a man who readily allowed life to ruffle his dignity, but he could not help feeling slightly stunned. He recognized her: the girl from yesterday’s beach episode, whom he had assumed to be French. Now she addressed him in fluent English, as if languages were hats one changed between luncheon and dinner. Also she appeared to have become, in the space of a single day, intimate enough with his cousin Lancelot to sprint with him down platforms and shout “Ginger” out of railway windows.

Bruce Carmyle held that nickname to be vulgar. It was a little like calling a duchess “Pinky.” Yet here it was, in a pretty girl’s mouth, and the universe had not exploded.

If Sally had been less pretty, he would have frowned. Her abrupt entry, her breathlessness, her general air of having treated the compartment as a tackleable objective, all gave his rigid proprieties a nasty jar. But she was extremely pretty—prettier flushed than at rest—and he contrived a smile.

“Not at all,” he said, though his left big toe throbbed with a private, dignified agony. Even a small foot can communicate meaning to a big toe if a scrum-half has aimed well.

“If you don’t mind,” said Sally, sitting down, “I think I’ll breathe a little.”

She breathed. The train clattered on, making that stitched-together music of wheels on rails—steady, repetitive, almost comforting, like a good cook’s knife on a board. Mr. Carmyle found his irritation settling. The pain in his toe began to fade.

“Quite a close thing,” he observed affably. “You nearly missed it.”

“Yes. It was lucky Mr. Kemp was with me. He throws very straight, doesn’t he.”

“Tell me,” said Carmyle, “how do you come to know my cousin? On the beach yesterday morning—”

“Oh, we didn’t know each other then. But we were staying at the same hotel, and we spent an hour or so shut up in an elevator together. That was when we really got acquainted.”

A waiter entered and announced, in unexpected English, that dinner was served in the restaurant car.

“Would you care for dinner?” asked Mr. Carmyle.

“I’m starving,” said Sally.

As they went along the corridor, with the train’s noises changing timbre from compartment hush to dining-car clink, Sally reproved herself for having judged this grim-faced man too quickly. He could be perfectly pleasant. By the time they sat down she had decided she liked him.

At the table, unfortunately, Mr. Carmyle altered.

He became a different specimen: a man who took meals seriously and believed waiters should be managed with severity, like dangerous machinery. He shuddered at a stain on the cloth as if it were an insult to his ancestors and bent over the bill of fare with a frown of moral disapproval.

Sally, meanwhile, was establishing cosy relations with the waiter, a cheerful old fellow who instantly adopted her as a favourite daughter. He spoke no English; Sally spoke no French; but they were communicating beautifully in the universal dialect of appetite and goodwill. The man had the warm, oniony smell of kitchens—wine, soap, and lingering stock—and Sally felt suddenly homesick for the honest music of cooking: the sizzle that means butter has reached the right heat, the soft thunder of a simmer, the little whisper when a knife slices ripe fruit.

Mr. Carmyle waved aside the waiter’s friendly hovering—at the Hotel Splendide, waiters did not bend over you and breathe suggestions down your cheek—and ordered crisply in the Anglo-Gallic of the travelling Briton.

The waiter exclaimed “Boum!” with pleasure and vanished.

“Nice old man!” said Sally.

“Infernally familiar,” said Mr. Carmyle.

Sally saw they would not agree about the waiter. She changed the subject, being determined to be courteous.

“By the way,” she said, “my name is Nicholas. I always think it’s a good thing to start with names, don’t you?”

“Mine—”

“Oh, I know yours. Ginger—Mr. Kemp told me.”

Mr. Carmyle, who had been thawing, stiffened at once, as if the word had been a draught.

“Indeed?” he said coldly. “Apparently you got intimate.”

Sally disliked his tone. It sounded like a rebuke, and Sally did not accept rebukes from strangers. Her eyes widened.

“Why ‘apparently’? I told you we had got intimate, and I explained how. You can’t stay shut up in an elevator half the night with anybody without getting to know him. I found Mr. Kemp very pleasant.”

“Really?”

“And very interesting.”

Mr. Carmyle lifted his eyebrows.

“Would you call him interesting?”

“I did call him interesting.”

Sally felt the bright, reckless exhilaration of battle. Men usually made themselves agreeable to her; when one did not, she became instantly and thoroughly ready.

“He told me all about himself,” she added.

“Indeed?” Mr. Carmyle’s gaze went to the length of French bread on the table, as if wishing it were a gavel. “He has an engaging lack of reticence.”

Soup arrived with a flourish; the waiter dumped it down.

“V’là!” he announced, beaming as if he had produced it out of his hat.

He smiled at Sally confidently, expecting applause. But Sally’s face had set. Being snubbed was new, and she did not find the novelty soothing.

“I think Mr. Kemp had hard luck,” she said.

“If you will excuse me,” said Mr. Carmyle, “I would prefer not to discuss the matter.”

His manner said: Pretty girl or not, you are a stranger, and strangers do not discuss the private stew-pot of the Family.

“He was quite in the right. Mr. Scrymgeour was beating a dog—”

“I’ve heard the details.”

“Oh. I didn’t know that. Then don’t you agree with me?”

“I do not. A man who would throw away an excellent position simply because—”

“Oh, well, if that’s your view, I suppose it is useless to talk about it.”

“Quite.”

“Still,” said Sally, tasting indignation like pepper, “there’s no harm in asking what you propose to do about Gin—about Mr. Kemp.”

Mr. Carmyle grew even more glacial.

“I’m afraid I cannot discuss—”

Sally’s patience, which had been hanging by a thread like a poorly tied bundle of herbs, snapped.

“Oh, for goodness’ sake,” she said, “do try to be human, and don’t always be snubbing people. You remind me of one of those portraits of men in the eighteenth century, with wooden faces, who look out of heavy gold frames at you with fishy eyes as if you were a regrettable incident.”

“Rosbif,” said the waiter genially, appearing at their elbows with the suddenness of a kitchen timer.

Mr. Carmyle attacked his roast beef with morose precision. Sally sat in silence, the sort of silence that crackles.

“I am sorry,” said Mr. Carmyle at last, ponderously, “if my eyes are fishy. The fact has not been called to my attention before.”

“I suppose you never had any sisters,” said Sally.

“They would have told you.”

Mr. Carmyle relapsed into offended dumbness, which held until coffee arrived. The dining-car was full of clinks and murmurs and that steady undertone of rails—sound that ought to have been soothing, but now only underlined the uncomfortable rhythm between them, like bad accompaniment.

“I think,” said Sally, rising, “I’ll be going now. I don’t seem to want any coffee, and if I stay on I may say something rude. I thought I might be able to put in a good word for Mr. Kemp and save him from being massacred, but apparently it’s no use. Good-bye, Mr. Carmyle, and thank you for giving me dinner.”

She went down the car.

Bruce Carmyle watched her go with an expression that was indignant—yet, in some unexplained corner of him, fascinated. Strange emotions were stirring in Mr. Carmyle’s bosom, and he did not in the least like the noise they made.
