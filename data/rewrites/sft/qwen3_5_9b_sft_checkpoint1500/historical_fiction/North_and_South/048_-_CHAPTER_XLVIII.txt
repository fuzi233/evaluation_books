The gatherings hosted by Mrs. Lennox were characterized by a specific alchemy: her friends supplied the beauty, Captain Lennox offered an effortless command of current affairs, and young Henry Lennox, surrounded by a select circle of rising men, furnished the wit and the sharp, encyclopedic knowledge they possessed only because they knew how to wield it without appearing pedantic. These dinners were ostensibly delightful. Yet, beneath the surface, Margaret harbored a quiet, gnawing dissatisfaction. To her, every talent, every emotion, and indeed every inclination toward virtue was consumed as fuel for fireworks; the sacred, hidden flame burned down to nothing but a fleeting sparkle and a hollow crackle. They discussed art in a purely sensual manner, fixated on external effects rather than seeking wisdom within the work itself. They fanned themselves into a theatrical enthusiasm for lofty subjects while in company, only to let those thoughts vanish the moment solitude arrived. Their capacity for deep appreciation was squandered in a mere torrent of appropriate, superficial words.

One evening, after the gentlemen had ascended to the drawing-room, Mr. Lennox approached Margaret. It was nearly the first voluntary address he had made to her since her return to Harley Street.

"You did not seem pleased by what Shirley was saying at dinner."

"Did I not?" Margaret replied. "My countenance must be remarkably expressive."

"It always has been," he observed gently. "It has not lost its facility for eloquence."

"I did not like," Margaret said quickly, "his manner of championing what he knew to be wrong—so glaringly wrong, even in jest."

"But it was remarkably clever," Mr. Lennox countered. "How every word landed! Did you recall the apt epithets?"

"Yes."

"And yet, you would likely despise them if you thought to add them yourself. Please do not hesitate, though he is my friend."

"There!" She paused abruptly, realizing the implication. "That is precisely the tone you adopt."

She stammered, turning away with a flush, unable to complete her thought. For a moment, Mr. Lennox waited to see if she would continue, but silence fell save for the beating of her heart. Before she could retreat further, he spoke again, his voice low and resonant.

"If my tones, or the nature of my thoughts, displease you, I beg you to tell me so plainly. Give me the opportunity to learn how to please you."

For several weeks, there remained no word regarding Mr. Bell's departure for Milton. He had hinted at it in Helstone as a potential journey within a fortnight, yet business could surely be conducted through correspondence. Margaret reasoned that if he could avoid travel, he certainly would; she understood his aversion to the place, and she felt certain he would not wish to undertake a trip where he might fail to grasp the private, oral significance of the explanation she required. She knew he felt the necessity of the meeting, yet whether in summer, autumn, or winter mattered little. It was August now, and despite his vague allusions to a Spanish voyage mentioned to Edith, there had been no concrete indication of movement. Margaret attempted to reconcile herself to the fading of this hope.

However, one morning a letter arrived. It announced that Mr. Bell intended to come to town the following week to discuss a plan forming in his mind. Furthermore, he planned to indulge in some treatment, having conceded to her view that it was preferable to attribute irritability and crossness to failing health rather than to his character. As Margaret read, she later noted a quality of forced cheerfulness in the letter. At the time, however, her attention was captured by Edith's dramatic outbursts.

"He is coming back to town! Oh, dear! And I am utterly exhausted by this heat; I doubt I possess the strength for another dinner. Besides, everyone has departed save for our dear, dull selves, who cannot decide where to go. There will be no one to welcome him."

"I am certain he would far prefer dining with us privately, away from any strangers, however agreeable," Margaret interjected. "Moreover, if he is unwell, he will not seek invitations. I am glad he has finally admitted it. I was convinced he was ill from the tone of his letters, yet he refused to answer my inquiries, leaving me without anyone to whom I could turn for news."

"Oh! He is not seriously ill, or he would not consider Spain."

"He never mentions Spain."

"No!" Edith insisted, her eyes wide. "But the plan he intends to propose clearly concerns that region. However, would you truly venture out in weather like this?"

"Oh, it will cool with each passing day. Yes! Consider it. I am only terrified that I have allowed myself to think and wish too deeply—with that absorbing, stubborn intensity that inevitably leads to disappointment, or perhaps fulfillment that, in spirit, brings no real pleasure."

"But that is superstitious, I'm sure, Margaret."

"No, I do not believe it is. It ought to serve as a warning, checking me from surrendering to such passionate desires. It feels like the ancient prayer, 'Give me children, or else I die.' I fear my cry is, 'Let me go to Cadiz, or else I die.'"

"My dear Margaret! You will be persuaded to remain, and then what shall I do? Oh! I wish I could find someone for you to marry here, so that I could be assured of you!"

"I shall never marry."

"Nonsense! Double nonsense! Why, as Sholto remarks, you are such an attraction to the house that he knows many men who would gladly visit next year solely for your sake."

Margaret straightened her posture with a haughty dignity. "Do you know, Edith, I sometimes think your life at Corfu has imparted..."

"Well?"

"Just a shade, or two, of coarseness."

Edith immediately burst into bitter sobs, declaring vehemently that Margaret had lost all affection for her and no longer regarded her as a friend. Overwhelmed by the shock of her own harshness and wounded pride, Margaret soon capitulated, becoming Edith's devoted slave for the remainder of the day. Meanwhile, the little lady, devastated by her perceived rejection, lay like a sacrificial victim upon the sofa, heaving profound sighs until, eventually, sleep claimed her.

Mr. Bell failed to appear even on the rescheduled day. The following morning, a letter from Wallis, his servant, revealed that his master had not been in good health for some time—the true cause of his delay—and that just as he was about to depart for London, he had suffered an apoplectic stroke. Wallis added that the physicians' consensus was grim: his master could not survive the night, and it was highly probable that by the time Miss Hale received this missive, her former tutor would already be gone.

Margaret read the letter at breakfast and turned so pale that her complexion nearly vanished. Silently, she passed the note to Edith and retreated from the room.

Edith was profoundly shaken, reading the letter with a sobbing, frightened, childlike terror that distressed her husband greatly. Mrs. Shaw was eating breakfast in her own chamber, and now the responsibility fell to him to reconcile his wife to the terrifying proximity of death for the first time in her life. A man who was to dine with them today lay either dead or dying! It took considerable time before she could summon the resolve to think of Margaret. Then, she sprang up and followed her upstairs to Margaret's room.

Dixon was packing a few toiletries, and Margaret was hastily donning her bonnet, tears streaming down her face, her hands trembling so violently she could barely tie the strings.

"Oh, dear Margaret! How shocking! What are you doing? Are you going out? Sholto would telegraph or do anything you desire."

"I am going to Oxford. There is a train departing in half an hour. Dixon has offered to accompany me, but I could have traveled alone. I must see him once more. Besides, he may be recovering and require care. He has been like a father to me. Do not hinder me, Edith."

"But I must stop you! Mother will not approve at all. Come and ask her about it, Margaret. You do not know where you are going. I would not mind if he had his own residence, but his rooms as a Fellow! Come to Mother, and please ask her before you leave. It will not take a single minute."

Margaret yielded, missing her connection. In the suddenness of the tragedy, Mrs. Shaw became bewildered and hysterical, causing the precious moments to slip away. However, another train was scheduled to depart in two hours. After various debates concerning propriety and impropriety, it was determined that Captain Lennox should accompany Margaret, for the one constant in her nature was her absolute resolution to proceed, alone or accompanied, by the next available vehicle, regardless of what others might say regarding the correctness of her step. Her father's friend, her own confidant, was lying at the point of death. The vividness of this thought surprised her with the firmness with which she asserted her right to act independently, and five minutes before departure, she found herself seated in a railway carriage opposite Captain Lennox.
