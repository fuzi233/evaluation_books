On a frosty January evening in the early 1870s, the celebrated Christine Nilsson stepped onto the stage of New York’s Academy of Music to sing the lead in *Faust*, and the whole of fashionable New York turned out to see her.

Even though whispers were already swirling of a grand new opera house to be built miles north, “above the Forties,” designed to match the opulence of Paris and Vienna’s most famous venues, the city’s elite were perfectly happy to crowd into the shabby, velvet-lined old Academy every winter. Conservatives adored it for being cramped and inconvenient enough to keep out the brash “new people” New York both feared and secretly longed to rub elbows with; sentimental patrons clung to decades of happy memories woven into its red and gold drapery; and opera fanatics swore its acoustics, the trickiest possible thing to get right in a new music hall, were finer than any in Europe.

It was Nilsson’s first performance of the season, and the papers were already calling the packed crowd “exceptionally brilliant.” Audience members had glided through slush-crusted streets in private broughams, sprawling family landaus, or the far more convenient Brown coupes—rental carriages so respectable that arriving in one was almost as much of a status symbol as pulling up behind your own family’s crest. Even better, they let you escape the opera as soon as the curtain fell, no waiting for your own coachman to rouse himself from the pub and rub the cold gin fog off his spectacles. The owner of the Brown livery stables had made a fortune on that single sharp insight: Americans are far more eager to dash home after a good time than they are to arrive on time for it.

When Newland Archer pushed open the door at the back of his club box, the curtain had just risen on the famous garden scene. There was no good reason he had been late: he had dined at seven sharp with his mother and sister, and only lingered afterward to smoke a cigar in the house’s Gothic library, the only room Mrs. Archer allowed tobacco, lined with black walnut glazed bookcases and chairs topped with carved wooden finials. But first, New York high society ran on unwritten rules as sacred and unnegotiable as the ancient tribal taboos of Archer’s long-ago ancestors—and arriving early to the opera was simply *not the thing*. Second, Archer was a man who savored the build-up to a small, delicate pleasure far more than the pleasure itself. And tonight, the moment he’d been daydreaming of for weeks was about to unfold.

His timing could not have been more perfect. The prima donna was just lifting her voice to trill the daisy song, plucking the petals one by one: “M’ama… non m’ama… M’ama!” Her notes fell as clear as dew on grass, proclaiming “He loves me” triumphantly into the hush of the house. Of course, she sang the lines in Italian, not the original French libretto, not her native Swedish: it was an unbreakable law of the opera world that all work must be translated into Italian for the comfort of English-speaking New York audiences, and Archer thought that every bit as natural as his own unshakable habits: combing his hair with two silver-backed brushes engraved with his monogram in blue enamel, never stepping out into society without a fresh gardenia pinned to his buttonhole.

He leaned against the back wall of the box, and let his gaze drift away from the stage to the opposite side of the house. Directly across from him was the private box belonging to old Mrs. Manson Mingott, whose legendary obesity had long ago made it impossible for her to climb the opera steps, though she always sent a younger relative to claim the best seat on any big performance night. Tonight, the front of the box was occupied by her daughter-in-law Mrs. Lovell Mingott, and her own daughter Mrs. Welland. Slightly withdrawn behind those two brocaded, matronly figures sat a young girl in all white, her wide blue eyes fixed rapturously on the stage lovers. When Nilsson’s final triumphant “M’ama!” rang out so clear even the chattering ladies in the surrounding boxes fell silent, a warm rose pink flooded May Welland’s cheeks, creeping up to the very roots of her fair braids and spilling down the soft slope of her throat to the edge of her modest tulle collar, fastened with a single tiny gardenia. She dropped her eyes to the massive bouquet of lilies of the valley resting on her knee, and Archer watched the tips of her white-gloved fingers brush the flower petals softly. He drew a slow, satisfied breath, a warm spark of pride and possessiveness blooming in his chest.

The stage set was widely agreed to be magnificent, even by men like Archer who had seen the opera houses of Paris and Vienna. The whole foreground down to the footlights was covered in rich emerald green cloth. Farther back, symmetrical mounds of fuzzy green moss edged with croquet hoops held topiary orange trees studded with giant pink and red roses, and enormous pansies that looked almost exactly like the embroidered floral pen-wipers parish women stitched for their favorite clergymen. Even the daisies were grafted onto rose branches, blooming twice as lushly as any wild garden flower. Nilsson wandered through that enchanted fake garden in a white cashmere gown slashed with pale blue satin, her heavy yellow braids arranged neatly over her muslin chemisette, pretending to have no idea that Faust’s flowery wooing was trying to lure her toward the neat little brick villa door peeking out from the right wing of the set.

*The darling*, Archer thought, his gaze darting right back to May’s soft, glowing face. She hasn’t the faintest guess what all his pretty lines are really about. His chest ached with a tender reverence for her bright, unspoiled purity, tangled up with masculine pride that he would be the one to show her the world. He had daydreams, already, of the two of them reading *Faust* side by side on the shores of the Italian lakes on their honeymoon. It had only been that very afternoon that May had let him know she returned his feelings, the sacred, proper New York way for a maiden to admit her love, and his imagination was already leaping past the engagement ring, the betrothal kiss, the grand march from *Lohengrin* at their wedding. He did not want his future wife to be a simpleton. With his gentle guidance, he was sure, she would grow to be as witty and socially graceful as the most admired young married women of their set—able to draw playful admiration from other men, but never step a single inch over the line of what was considered correct.

The rest of the men in the club box filtered in one by one, polished, white-waistcoated, every one of them with a gardenia in his buttonhole, and exchanged greetings with Archer. Archer privately knew he was more well-read, more thoughtful, more widely traveled than all of them put together. But as a group, they represented *New York*—and the unspoken code of masculine solidarity made him accept their unwritten rules on all questions of morality, because to strike out on his own would be not just troublesome, but terribly bad form.

“Well, upon my soul!” Lawrence Lefferts exclaimed suddenly, yanking his opera glasses away from the stage. Lefferts was the undisputed highest authority on “form” in all of New York, the man every single bachelor came to consult on whether you wore black tie or patent leather Oxfords or pumps for a given event, a man so naturally elegant he could wear the finest tailored clothes like he had thrown them on half-asleep. He silently passed his glasses over to old Sillerton Jackson.

Archer followed the direction of their gaze, and realized what had caused the outburst: a new figure had stepped into the Mingott box. She was a slim young woman, a little shorter than May, with dark brown hair curled tight against her temples and held back by a thin band of diamonds, that chic “Josephine” look all the fashion magazines had been whispering of. Her gown was deep dark blue velvet, dramatically gathered up under her bust with a heavy old-fashioned clasp, the kind of bold, theatrical design no respectable young matron would dare wear in public. She seemed completely unbothered by all the eyes fixed on her, pausing in the middle of the box to chat gently with Mrs. Welland about whether she should take the front right seat, before smiling softly and settling down next to Mrs. Lovell Mingott.

Old Sillerton Jackson was New York’s other great living authority: he knew every branch of every family tree, every hidden scandal that had smoldered under the city’s polished, polite surface for fifty years. He could explain the exact tangled cousinship between the Mingotts and the South Carolina Dallases, and the line between the Philadelphia Thorleys and the Albany Chiverses (never to be confused with the Manson Chiverses of University Place), he could recite the family traits everyone preferred to hush up: the legendary stinginess of the Long Island Leffertses, the Rushworth family’s fatal habit of making terrible impulsive matches, the streak of insanity that popped up every second generation in the Albany Chiverses. He was the only man alive who could tell you who the mysterious wealthy banker Julius Beaufort really was, and what had become of old Mrs. Mingott’s father Bob Spicer, who had vanished with a huge sum of trust money the very same day a famous Spanish dancer sailed out of the Battery for Cuba.

The whole club box went quiet, waiting for his verdict. He twisted his silvery moustache thoughtfully, blinked at the new arrival through his filmy old blue eyes, and spoke in a soft, deliberate tone that every man there heard perfectly clearly.

“I didn’t think the Mingotts would have tried it on.”
