The theater in Chatteris was so empty it felt less like a performance space and more like a mausoleum for forgotten dreams. Mr. Manager Bingley, straining to project his famous role of 'Rolla' in *Pizarro*, found himself shouting into a void. The audience was a skeleton crew; Arthur Pendennis sat alone in the royal box, his eyes red-rimmed and his spirit hollow, staring blankly at the stage. To him, the Spanish armies, the Peruvian processions, and the virgins of the sun were mere background noise. He saw only one thing: Cora Fotheringay. He later confessed that in his feverish state of love and despair, he considered grabbing a pistol and ending it all right there in front of the Chatteris gaol, had it not been for his mother's silent, steady presence at home.

On stage, Miss Fotheringay was a vision in white raiment and leopard skin, her golden eyes gleaming with a predatory lust that had once nearly destroyed Pendennis's soul. Yet, tonight, those eyes weren't fixed on him. They were locked onto two gentlemen in the box beside him. Pendennis, blinded by his own misery, missed the subtle shift in the atmosphere. The play itself was dying; the actors were yawning through their lines, and even the great Bingley was listless. Then, suddenly, the mood snapped. Mrs. Bingley began to bellow like a bull, Bingley darted about like a possessed man, and the entire cast scrambled to impress the newcomers.

The intruders were Mr. Dolphin, the mighty London impresario, and his secretary, Mr. William Minns. Dolphin was a towering figure of wealth and power, dressed in velvet and gold, smelling faintly of expensive musk and bergamot. The moment he entered, the Chatteris troupe transformed. Every actor, including the usually dull-hearted Miss Fotheringay, poured their energy into winning his favor. She stood in picturesque poses, looking stunning, though she knew her part required little more than beauty. It wasn't until the final scene, when Rolla stumbled in with a monstrous child and cried out about blood, that Dolphin finally clapped his hands and shouted, "Bravo!"

"By Jove, Billy, she'll do!" Dolphin declared, slapping his secretary on the back.

Old Billy Minns, a sardonic soul, chuckled. "Who taught her that dodge?" he asked, recalling how the girl couldn't say "Bo" to a goose at the Olympic. It was little Mr. Bows in the orchestra who had coached her. As the curtain fell, the entire company swarmed around Miss Fotheringay, congratulating her with a mixture of genuine joy and professional envy.

Why had the great Dolphin descended to this tiny provincial theater? Because his empire was crumbling. Despite his flashy advertisements and past triumphs, the Museum Theatre was bleeding money. Legitimate drama, animal shows, and grand operas had all failed to draw crowds. He was dragging out his season on half-salaries, waiting for the day his name would appear in the bankruptcy gazette.

His salvation, however, lay in the patronage of the Marquis of Steyne. The Marquis, a man of high station and questionable taste, attended the theater mostly for the ballet, hiding behind a curtain while his aides-de-camp, Wenham and Wagg, managed the proceedings. One evening, amidst raucous laughter and scandalized whispers from the pit, Wenham read a letter from Major Pendennis.

"The secret is out," Wenham announced. "There's a woman in the case."

"Damn it, Wenham, he's your age," the Marquis grumbled from behind the velvet drape.

"For my part, I hope to be a victim till I die," Wenham replied gallantly, though the Marquis knew he was merely boasting about his own vitality.

"Tell us about old Pendennis," Wagg urged, eager to hear the gossip.

Wenham read the letter aloud. Pendennis, writing with the desperation of a man in love, begged the Marquis to intervene. He described Miss Fotheringay as a splendid actress, superior to anyone on the London scene, and pleaded for a London engagement for her. He promised anything in return, asking the Marquis to use his influence to get Dolphin to sign her.

"It's a clear case," Wenham concluded. "Old Pendennis is in love."

"And wants to get the woman up to London," Wagg added.

"I'd like to see Pendennis on his knees with the rheumatism," Wenham mused.

"Or accommodating the beloved object with a lock of his hair," Wagg joked.

The Marquis dismissed their cynicism with a wave of his hand. "He has relations in the country, hasn't he? A nephew who could return a member? It's the nephew's affair. The young one is in a scrape. I was mad about a market-gardener's daughter myself at Eton... But I say, she must be a fine woman from Pendennis's account. Have in Dolphin, and let us hear if he knows anything of her."

Wenham sprang from the box, navigated the backstage chaos, and found Dolphin, who was currently cursing a ballerina for missing a step. The manager's curses died instantly upon seeing the Marquis's emissary. He greeted Wenham with a smile, pretending he had never been angry in his life, and eagerly followed him to pay his respects to the great patron.

The visit to Chatteris yielded immediate results. Dolphin wrote to the Marquis, confirming he had seen the lady and was struck by both her talents and her appearance. He had already made an engagement with Miss Fotheringay, promising she would soon appear before a London audience and the Marquis himself.

Back in Chatteris, the news spread like wildfire. The local newspaper printed a glowing review of her upcoming departure, praising her beauty and prophesying her success in the metropolis. Bingley immediately began advertising "The Last Night of Miss Fotheringay's Engagement." Only Pendennis and Sir Derby Oaks remained constant attendees; Sir Derby threw bouquets from the stage box, while Pendnis sat in the shadows, haggard and lonely.

One night, Mr. Bows, the little man from the orchestra, left his seat and approached Pendennis's box. He invited the brokenhearted major to walk. They strolled down the street and sat on Chatteris Bridge under the moonlight, talking about the woman who had captured them both.

"We may sit on the same bridge," Bows said, his voice gravelly. "We've been in the same boat for a long time. You aren't the only fool who's made a mess of himself over that woman. I have less excuse than you; I'm older and know her better. She has no more heart than the stone you're leaning against. If either of us fell into the water, she wouldn't care. Yes, she would care for me, because she needs me to teach her. She can't get on without me, so she'll be forced to send for me from London. But she wouldn't if she didn't want me. She has no heart, no head, no sense, no feelings, no griefs or cares. I was going to say no pleasures, but the truth is, she likes her dinner, and she's pleased when people admire her."

"And you do?" Pendennis asked, fascinated by the crabbed honesty of the old man.

"It's a habit, like taking snuff or drinking drams," Bows replied. "I've been taking her for five years and can't do without her. If she doesn't send for me, I'll follow her. But I know she will. She wants me. Some day she'll marry and fling me over, just as I'll drop the end of this cigar."

He flicked the burning tip into the dark water below, watching it vanish. As Pendennis rode home that night, for the first time in months, his mind wandered to someone other than himself.
