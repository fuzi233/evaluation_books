Little more than a week had passed, and yet the Piazza della Signoria, which had lately smoked with the sweet, choking incense of vanities, now prepared a stranger feast for the eyes and—so men joked with their empty bellies—for the nose. It was the seventh of April. By mid-day the great square was packed as if Florence meant to press its whole weight upon one moment and force Heaven to answer.

From early morning the city had been crawling up its own stone skin. Men wedged themselves where no human body was meant to rest: perched on iron rods that bit into the thighs, clung to slender pillars with arms and legs locked like vines, sat astride the rough shoulders of carved saints above doorways, found a palm’s breadth of seat on an architrave and held by iron rings and staples hammered into old masonry. They had come to see a Miracle, and scraped flesh or numbed feet seemed a cheap price. Most lives are spent hearing of wonders and believing them in the dim way one believes in a distant storm. To-day Florence would watch a wonder blaze in the open, as plainly as a cook watches his oil take heat. At the very least—so the crowd reasoned with that crude logic that makes spectacle into proof—they would see half a miracle: if the monk did not walk whole out of the flames, they would see him step in, and infer he burned somewhere between.

There were reasons to trust that something would be done. Before their very eyes lay the long platform, eight feet broad and twenty yards long, set slantwise across the Piazza like a serving-board for a terrible banquet. On it was piled a grove of fuel: thick boughs of dry oak laid as a foundation—seasoned wood that would catch greedily—then crackling thorns, and above them tow and rags soaked with oil, the sort used for fine flaming in Florentine illuminations, but here prepared to lick living flesh. Even at a distance, one could smell the promise in it: the resinous dryness of oak, the sharp, green-bitter tang of thorn, the rancid slick of old oil on cloth. It began at the corner of the marble terrace before the Old Palace, close to Marzocco, the stone lion, whose worn face frowned down the length of that oblique pyre as if already offended by the smoke it expected.

And there were men enough to prove the city meant business. Five hundred hired soldiers of the Signoria stood before the palace; five hundred Compagnacci, under Dolfo Spini, were massed far off on the opposite side; and three hundred armed citizens of another temper, led by Marco Salviati—Savonarola’s friend—kept station in front of Orgagna’s Loggia. Under that Loggia the Franciscans and Dominicans would stand, each with his champion, like two kitchens sworn to prove whose bread had God’s blessing.

High dignities were involved, money spent, labour measured out; the platform itself declared, with its carpentered straightness and its heaped tinder, that no one intended an anticlimax. If the monks burned, that too would be “God speaking,” and many were ready to hear Him say—plainly, like a verdict shouted over a market stall—that Fra Girolamo was no prophet. Mid-day was close, and half the Loggia already had its occupants: on the side towards the Palace, grey mantles filled the space—Franciscans who had entered and taken their places in silence. The other half, boarded off, held only a small altar, bare as a clean board waiting for bread.

Then from the farther side of the Piazza rose chanting—two hundred voices swelling into one heavy wave. The sound rolled along the stone façades and up to the roofs where men crouched, and it changed the air as surely as a drum changes marching feet. Many were satisfied, if not with the chant itself, at least with what it meant: the Dominicans were coming.

“Let God arise, and let His enemies be scattered.” The prayer, shouted and repeated, struck some ears—those who were not already intoxicated by party—as something less like humble petition than like a cook’s loud boasting over a dish not yet tasted. At the head of the procession walked Fra Domenico, his simple mind lifted into a sincere exaltation, his intention honest and terrible: to enter the flames for the glory of God and of Fra Girolamo. He wore a flame-coloured velvet cope that caught every eye the way a bright pepper catches the tongue. Cross in hand, he advanced as if he had already felt the heat and found it sweet.

Behind him came Savonarola, not in splendour but in the white vestment of a priest, carrying in both hands a vessel that held the consecrated Host. He too chanted loudly. He too looked firm; and under the pressing gaze of a whole Piazza—anxiety here, curiosity there, malice everywhere like grit between teeth—there was a hard brightening in his face, an energy that answered scrutiny as flint answers steel.

For we are made so, almost all of us, that the false seeming we dread in solitude—the part we have rehearsed with inward shrinking because necessity drives us—will take hold of our muscles and our voice, and when expectant eyes and ears are upon us it feels, for that hour, like the easiest thing in the world. And the stimulus upon Savonarola that day cannot be weighed by ordinary experience. Perhaps no man has ruled the hearts of others without a need to dominate; and that need grows more imperious when life has so tangled Self with a purpose not selfish that they are hard to separate even in conscience. So on that day, the doubleness that tempts every public career—priest, orator, statesman—stood more sharply in Savonarola’s own sense as an acted part than ever before. He was not struggling against the prospect of martyrdom; he was struggling against the prospect of ruin. Therefore he looked and moved as if confidence filled him, while foreboding lay on his heart with the dead weight of lead.

That heaviness was not only for the probable issue of the trial. Another event, already past, had set a different mind into sunny satisfaction—a mind looking down at the worn prophet from a window of the Old Palace. Two evenings earlier word had come that the Florentine courier of the Ten had been arrested and robbed of his despatches. Savonarola’s letter, so rashly trusted to travelling hands, was already with the Duke of Milan, and would soon be in the Pope’s—adding fuel to rage and a new colour of justification to extreme measures.

Tito Melema felt no malignity in his satisfaction. It was the mild, cool self-gratulation of a man who has won a game requiring hypotheses and finesse, not the hot triumph that shakes the blood. This bundle of desire and contrivance called human nature, moulded into a plain-faced Frate Predicatore with his high nose and heavy under lip, was not a pathetic object to a brilliant scholar who congratulated himself on understanding everything. Still, Tito admitted to himself, Fra Girolamo was immensely clever: he mingled absurd superstition—or fabrication—with notions about government that were not babble, and he could keep secrets. Tito bore him no more spite than he bore Saint Dominic. Indeed, Fra Girolamo’s existence had been useful—one rung in a ladder from which Tito meant presently to leap to a smooth footing far more to his taste. Everything was now arranged for that leap: let one more sun rise and set, and he hoped to be gone from Florence. He had been industrious enough to feel himself at leisure to enjoy to-day’s comedy—a comedy that thick-headed Dolfo Spini could never have staged without him.

The chanting did not cease; it grew into a deafening roar as the Piagnoni throughout the Piazza took it up, lifting their little red crosses like the badge of a guild. Sound became the day’s weather: it pressed on the skull, it filled the chest, it made men shout who had not meant to open their mouths. The good Fra Domenico in flame-colour knelt at the altar where the Sacrament had been set, waiting his summons. On the Franciscan side there was no chant, no bright colour—only grey and silence, a quiet so tense it seemed like the moment in a kitchen when hands pause because the sauce is about to turn.

Yet the silence hid its own fear. Pale Franciscan faces looked uneasily over the boarding toward that flame-coloured cope. It had an evil look; it might be enchanted. A monk might walk out of fire untouched and still be the devil’s trick. The crowd’s hunger for certainty was like hunger for bread: it made them imagine smell and taste before anything was served.

Now there was passing to and fro between the Loggia and the marble terrace of the Palazzo. The roar slackened as attention sharpened. From far vantage points it was hard to see whether the pyre had been kindled; in the distance, even fire can look like sunlight on oiled cloth. But the new movement did not begin the miracle; it delayed it. Dignified Florentines appointed as moderators went in and out of the Palace; debate thickened with the Franciscans.

At last it seemed Fra Domenico was being fetched toward the Palace. His flame-coloured cope disappeared within. Then another Dominican was taken away. And still the Loggia looked as it had: Fra Girolamo in white, standing in the same place; the fuel waiting, the air chilled by clouds gathering as if Heaven itself were drawing a lid over the day.

When Fra Domenico came out again, he was changed. He had exchanged all his clothes with another brother, and two Franciscans guarded him on either flank—as if enchantment were a scent that might cling to velvet and travel—lest, coming near Savonarola, he should be bewitched anew. From far off, perched men shifted, their cramped limbs briefly forgotten.

“Ah,” the spectators reasoned, “Fra Domenico will not enter. It is Fra Girolamo who will offer himself after all. We shall see him move, and if he comes out, we shall have a fine view.”

But Savonarola did not move toward the platform. He moved only as men move when they speak: the small gestures that match syllables. His voice—bold, firm, with an edge of ironic remonstrance, like Elijah addressing the priests of Baal—demanded an end to these petty delays. Yet speech is the most irritating argument to those out of hearing, pinched by hunger and cold. They had been standing since dawn; their mouths were dry; their ears were full of other men’s breath and murmured impatience. What need for words? If the miracle did not begin, whose fault could it be but Fra Girolamo’s—he who had been forward enough with challenge when no fuel lay in sight?

Still more movement, more slow and invisible dispute. The afternoon seemed to slip faster because the light changed: clouds gathered, dulling the colours of stone and cloth, and sending a chill through bodies already weary. The crowd began to feel the platform less as a promise than as an insult—like a table set lavishly and then withheld.

Now the objection was the crucifix. Fra Domenico wished to carry it into the fire, and the Franciscans would not allow such profanation. After some resistance Savonarola yielded—another concession he could show like a coin to those who counted such things. But immediately he placed in Fra Domenico’s hands the vessel holding the consecrated Host. In the back of his mind hovered the possibility that the sacred presence might, in extremity, avert fire’s ordinary appetite; but he counted more on a different outcome than on any physical marvel. Taking up the Host, he said quietly, as if this had been understood from the first:

“Since they are not willing that you should enter with the crucifix, my brother, enter simply with the Sacrament.”

A new horror ran through the Franciscans, audible even without words—the quickened shuffling, the intake of many breaths. Savonarola’s firmness only hardened.

It was impious presumption, the Franciscans insisted, to carry the Sacrament into the fire. If it were burned, scandal would spread among the weak and ignorant like sourness through milk.

“Not at all,” came the answer: even if it were burned, only the Accidents would be consumed; the Substance would remain.

Here was a question that could be argued until sunset and still spring back elastic as before. And because it was a preliminary question, no one could settle it simply by proceeding to the trial. All that was needed to paralyse the day was that both sides remain firm: Franciscans refusing to permit the Host in the fire, and Fra Domenico refusing to enter without it.

Meanwhile the clouds lowered; the air grew colder. The chanting, tiresome as it had been, was missed when it ceased and gave way to argument that could not be heard. In its place rose a confused talk from all parts of the Piazza, the loosening sound of expectation unravelling. It suggested, more irritatingly than any single shout, that nothing decisive would come—that the whole day would spoil like a dish neglected between hearth and table.

Then, here and there, sharp cries dropped into the murmur like stones into water, and other voices answered, until scorn rose by degrees.

“Light the fire and drive them in!”

“Let us have a smell of roast—we want our dinner!”

“Come, Prophet—shall anything happen before twenty-four hours are over?”

“Yes, yes—what’s your last vision?”

“Oh, he’s got a dozen in his inside; they’re the small change for a miracle!”

“Ola, Frate! Never mind wasting the fuel!”

The same going and coming between Loggia and Palace continued, slow and unintelligible to the multitude, like insects touching antennae and retreating with no apparent result. But the crowd did not need to hear words to invent meaning. Fra Girolamo was constantly speaking: therefore he was the hindrance. Everybody was appealing to him: therefore he was hanging back.

Soon individual shouts drowned into an uproar—not only voices, but clashing metal and trampling feet. The irritated suggestions of the people stirred old impulses in Dolfo Spini and his Compagnacci. Here, they thought, was a chance to end Florentine difficulties by taking the arch-hypocrite himself. Their armed men surged toward the Loggia, thrusting the crowd aside or driving them against the hired soldiers before the Palace.

At that movement all else paused—monks and magistrates alike—in a palpitating watch. The Loggia, however, was well guarded by Salviati’s band, and the Signoria’s soldiers helped beat back the rush. The trampling and shouting rolled backward again toward the Tetto de’ Pisani.

And then the heavens darkened as if in answer—not with fire, but with water. The scattered drops that had been felt like cold fingers became a sheet of rain, thickening rapidly, drumming on helmets, on bare heads, on the wooden platform, soaking the tow and rags, running in streams off the boards. It struck stone and cloth with a loud, unrelenting hiss; it flattened banners and mantles; it turned dust to paste underfoot. The whole Piazza smelled suddenly of wet wood and damp wool, of oil being denied its flame, of human breath trapped under rain-cooled air. Men who had fancied the savour of roasting now swallowed rainwater and bile. Disgust and rage, having no outlet, were driven inward to ferment in the damp darkness.

Everybody knew then the Trial by Fire would not happen. The Signoria could be glad of the rain; it was an excuse no one could dispute, better than any pretext, for ordering both parties home. It was the issue Savonarola had expected and, in one sense, desired; yet “glad” was a poor word for what entered him as that rain fell. The demand that he enter the fire ended; but with the same irresistible certainty with which cold wet took possession of his body, he knew his enemies’ design was fulfilled and his honour not saved. He would have to make his way back to San Marco through an enraged crowd. Hearts that once would have defended him with their lives would turn away or turn upon him.

When the rain slackened and ceased, he asked the Signoria for a guard, and it was granted. Had he said he was willing to die for the work of his life? He had not lied. But to die in dishonour—held up to scorn as a hypocrite, a false prophet—that, in his inward sense, was not martyrdom.

“O God! that is not martyrdom,” his soul cried within him. “It is the blotting out of a life that has been a protest against wrong. Let me die because of the worth that is in me, not because of my weakness.”

Light broke through the clouds as he left the Loggia with the Sacrament in his hand, guarded as he went, walking as he had come. But there was no glory in that light—no smile. It was only the patient, impartial light that shines on, justifying or condemning by revealing, slowly, the ripening history of all things. No blessing met his ears, no tone of pity—only taunts and threats, voices made hoarse by wet and disappointment. He knew it for a foretaste of bitterness; yet under moral attack his courage rose. He showed no sign of dismay.

From the window of the Old Palace, Tito watched him descend the steps.

“Well parried, Frate!” Tito said, as Savonarola passed beneath. “But I fear your career at Florence is ended. What say you, my Niccolò?”

“It is a pity his falsehoods were not all of a wise sort,” said Macchiavelli, with a melancholy shrug. “With the times so much on his side as they are about Church affairs, he might have done something great.”
