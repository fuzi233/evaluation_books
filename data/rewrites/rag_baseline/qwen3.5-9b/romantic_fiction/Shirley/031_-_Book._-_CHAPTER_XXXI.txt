The die had been cast, sealed in ink and intent. Sir Philip Nunnely understood the finality of it; Shirley understood it; even Mr. Sympson, with his prying eyes and grasping mind, felt the shift in the air. That evening, as the Fieldhead family gathered at Nunnely Priory for dinner, the fate of the house was decided not by a gavel, but by the resonance of a voice and the weight of a glance.

Three currents converged to push the baronet toward this precipice. First, he had noted the transformation in Miss Keeldar. She appeared pensive, delicate, a fragile bloom in a garden of rigid convention. This new vulnerability struck a chord in his weak, poetic heart, brewing a spontaneous sonnet in the chambers of his brain before he could even articulate it. Simultaneously, one of his sisters, perhaps sensing the tension, persuaded his lady-love to sit at the piano. She played a ballad—one of Sir Philip's own compositions. It was the least elaborate, the least affected, and paradoxically, the finest of his many labors.

Just prior to this performance, Shirley had stood gazing from a window, watching the park below. She witnessed the stormy moonlight, perhaps the same luminescence Professor Louis contemplated from his oak parlour across the divide. She watched the isolated sentinels of the domain—the broad, strong oaks and the high-towering heroic beeches—as they wrestled with the gale. Her ears drank in the full roar of the forest; her eyes tracked the swift-rushing clouds and the moon itself, hastening across the vision like a fleeing spirit. Turning from sight and sound, she was touched, if not rapt; awakened, if not fully inspired.

When called upon, she sang. The ballad spoke of love faithful enough to refuse abandonment, love that disaster could not shake, love that in calamity waxed fonder and in poverty clung closer. The words were set to a fine, ancient air; simple and sweet in isolation, perhaps lacking raw force when merely read, but possessing everything when sung with soul. Shirley sang them well. She breathed softness into the feeling, pouring force around the passion. Her voice was magnificent that evening, its expression dramatically charged. She impressed all present, and charmed one in particular.

Leaving the instrument, she moved to the fire, settling onto a seat that was half-stool, half-cushion. The ladies clustered around her, silent as statues. The Misses Sympson and the Misses Nunnely regarded her with the wary curiosity of domestic poultry observing an egret or an ibis—a strange fowl among familiar ones. Why did she sing so? They never sang with such intensity. Was it proper to sing with such expression, such dangerous originality? So unlike a schoolgirl? Decidedly not. In their minds, the strange was wrong; the unusual was improper. Shirley was judged.

Old Lady Nunnely eyed her stonily from her great chair by the hearth. Her gaze was a verdict: *This woman is not of my blood, nor of my daughters' kind. I object to her as my son's wife.* Her son, catching the look, read its meaning instantly. Alarm seized him. What he so desperately wished to win, he feared he might lose. He must make haste.

The room they occupied had once been a picture gallery. Sir Philip's father, Sir Monckton, had converted it into a saloon, yet it retained a shadowy, withdrawing atmosphere. A deep recess with a window, holding one couch, one table, and a fairy cabinet, formed a room within a room. Here, two persons might exchange a dialogue, low and brief, unheard by the rest of the assembly.

Sir Philip induced two of his sisters to perform a duet, giving occupation to the Misses Sympson. The elder ladies conversed quietly. He was pleased to observe that meanwhile, Shirley rose to inspect the pictures. He had a tale to tell of one ancestress, whose dark beauty seemed akin to a southern flower. He joined her, beginning his recitation.

In the cabinet adorning the recess lay mementoes of that same lady. As Shirley stooped to examine a missal and a rosary resting on the inlaid shelf—and as the Misses Nunnely indulged in a prolonged, guiltless screech, pure of expression, conventional and utterly meaningless—Sir Philip stooped too. He whispered a few hurried sentences. At first, Miss Keeldar was struck so still one might have fancied the whisper a charm transforming her into a statue. But she presently looked up and answered. They parted. Miss Keeldar returned to the fire, resuming her seat. The baronet gazed after her, then retreated to stand behind his sisters. Only Mr. Sympson had marked the pantomime.

That gentleman drew his own conclusions. Had he been as acute as he was meddling, as profound as he was prying, he might have found correction in Sir Philip's face. Yet, ever shallow, hasty, and positive, he departed quite cock-a-hoop.

He was not a man who kept secrets well. Elated on a subject, he could not contain himself. The next morning, finding occasion to employ his son's tutor as his secretary, he announced—in mouthing accents and with much flimsy pomp—that he must prepare for a return to the south at an early date. The important business detaining him in Yorkshire was on the eve of fortunate completion. His anxious, laborious efforts were likely to be crowned with the happiest success. A truly eligible addition was about to be made to the family connections.

"In Sir Philip Nunnely?" Louis Moore conjectured.

Upon hearing this, Mr. Sympson treated himself simultaneously to a pinch of snuff and a chuckling laugh, checked only by a sudden choke of dignity and an order to the tutor to proceed with business.

For a day or two, Mr. Sympson remained as bland as oil, yet he seemed to sit on pins. His gait, when walking, emulated that of a hen treading a hot girdle. He was forever looking out the window, listening for the rumble of chariot wheels. Bluebeard's wife, Sisera's mother—they were nothing to him. He waited for the matter to be opened in formal fashion, for himself to be consulted, for lawyers to be summoned, for settlement discussions and all the delicious worldly fuss to pompously begin.

Finally, a letter arrived. He handed it to Miss Keeldar himself, knowing the handwriting, recognizing the crest on the seal. He did not witness it being opened, for Shirley took it to her own room. Nor did he see the reply written, for she composed her answer shut up, taking nearly a full day to formulate it. He questioned her whether it was answered; she responded simply, "Yes."

Again, he waited. He waited in silence, absolutely daring not to speak, kept mute by something in Shirley's face—a very awful, inscrutable thing, as unreadable as the writing on the wall to Belshazzar. He was moved more than once to summon Daniel, in the person of Louis Moore, to ask for an interpretation; but his dignity forbade such familiarity. Daniel himself, perhaps, faced his own private difficulties with that baffling bit of translation; he looked like a student for whom grammars are blank and dictionaries dumb.

Mr. Sympson had gone out to while away an anxious hour in the company of his friends at De Walden Hall. He returned sooner than expected. His family and Miss Keeldar were assembled in the oak parlour. Addressing the latter, he requested she accompany him to another room. He wished for a "strictly private interview."

She rose, asking no questions and professing no surprise.

"Very well, sir," she said, in the tone of a determined person informed that the dentist has come to extract that large, troublesome tooth from which he has suffered such a purgatory for months. She left her sewing and thimble in the window-seat and followed her uncle where he led.

Shut into the drawing-room, the pair took seats in arm-chairs placed opposite, a few yards between them.

"I have been to De Walden Hall," said Mr. Sympson. He paused. Miss Keeldar's eyes were fixed on the pretty white-and-green carpet. That information required no response; she gave none.

"I have learned," he went on slowly, "a circumstance which surprises me."

Resting her cheek on her forefinger, she waited to be told what circumstance.

"It seems that Nunnely Priory is shut up—that the family has gone back to their place in ----shire. It seems that the baronet—that Sir Philip himself—has accompanied his mother and sisters."

"Indeed!" said Shirley.

"May I ask if you share the amazement with which I received this news?"

"No, sir."

"_Is_ it news to you?"

"Yes, sir."

"I mean—I mean," pursued Mr. Sympson, now fidgeting in his chair, abandoning his brief and tolerable phrasing for his customary wordy, confused, irritable style—"I mean to have a _thorough_ explanation. I will _not_ be put off. I—_I_—shall _insist_ on being heard, and on having my own way. My questions _must_ be answered. I will have clear, satisfactory replies. I am not to be trifled with."

(Silence.)
