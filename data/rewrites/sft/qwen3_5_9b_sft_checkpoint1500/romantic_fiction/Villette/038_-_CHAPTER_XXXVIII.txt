Yet the divine decree holds sway regardless of our humility; the creative impulse drives it forward, and unseen powers ensure its fulfillment. Proof of a future life must be forged, perhaps in fire and blood if necessity demands. Such is the record traced through nature, crossing our own trials. O sufferer, do not faint at this burning evidence. Wayfarer weary of the road, gird up your loins; look upward and press onward. Join with fellow pilgrims and mourners in friendly company. Though the wilderness of this world stretches dark before us, let our tread be equal and steady, and let our cross serve as banner. Our staff is His promise—"whose word is tried, whose way perfect"; our present hope, His providence, "who gives the shield of salvation, whose gentleness makes great"; our final home, His bosom, dwelling in the height of Heaven; and our crowning prize, a glory exceeding and eternal. Run so that we may obtain; endure hardness as good soldiers; finish our course, keeping the faith, confident in an issue where we shall come off more than conquerors. Art thou not from everlasting my Holy One? WE SHALL NOT DIE!"

On a Thursday morning, the classroom was assembled, awaiting the literature lesson. The hour arrived, and we awaited the Master.

The pupils of the first class sat immovable. Their compositions, penned with clean precision since the last session, lay ready before them, neatly tied with ribbons, waiting to be collected by the Professor's swift rounds. It was July; the morning was fair, and a fresh breeze drifted through the ajar glass door, causing plants growing at the lintel to wave, bend, and peer in, seemingly whispering tidings.

Monsieur Emanuel was not invariably punctual; we scarcely marveled at his slight delay, but wonder gripped us when the door finally opened. Instead of him, swift and aflame with spirit, there quietly emerged the cautious Madame Beck.

She approached Monsieur Paul's desk, stood before it, drawing the light shawl from her shoulders tighter. Beginning to speak in low, yet firm tones, with a fixed gaze, she declared, "This morning there will be no lesson of literature."

After a pause of approximately two minutes, the second paragraph of her address followed.

"It is probable that lessons will be suspended for a week. I require at least that interval to secure an efficient substitute for Monsieur Emanuel. Meanwhile, it shall be our study to fill the blanks usefully."

"Your Professor, ladies," she continued, "intends, if possible, to take leave of you. At the present moment, he lacks leisure for that ceremony. He is preparing for a long voyage. A very sudden and urgent summons of duty calls him to a great distance. He has decided to leave Europe for an indefinite period. Perhaps he may tell you more himself. Ladies, instead of the usual lesson with Monsieur Emanuel, you will, this morning, read English with Mademoiselle Lucy."

She bowed her head courteously, drew the folds of her shawl closer, and departed the class.

A profound silence descended, followed by a murmuring ripple through the room. I believe some pupils wept. Time elapsed; the noise, whispers, and occasional sobbing increased. I became conscious of a relaxation of discipline, a growing disorder, as if vigilance had been withdrawn and surveillance left the classroom. However, habit and a sense of duty enabled me to rally quickly. I rose, spoke in my usual tone, enjoined order, and finally established quiet. I made the English reading long and rigorous, keeping them at it the whole morning. I remember feeling a sentiment of impatience toward the pupils who sobbed. Indeed, their emotion was of little value; it was merely hysterical agitation. I told them so unsparingly, half ridiculing them. I was severe. The truth was, I could not tolerate their tears or that gasping sound. A rather weak-minded, low-spirited pupil kept it up when the others had ceased; relentless necessity obliged and assisted me to accost her so sharply that she dared not continue the demonstration, forced to conquer the convulsion.

That girl would have had a right to hate me, save that when school ended and her companions departed, I ordered her to remain. When they were gone, I did what I had never done to any of them before—I pressed her to my heart and kissed her cheek. Yet, this impulse yielded quickly. I speedily put her out of the classroom, for upon that poignant strain, she wept more bitterly than ever.

I filled every minute of that day with occupation, and I should have liked to sit up all night if I could have kept a candle burning. The night, however, proved a bad time, leaving effects that ill-prepared me for the next day's ordeal of insufferable gossip. Of course, this news fell under general discussion. Some initial reserve accompanied the first surprise, but that soon wore off; every mouth opened, every tongue wagged. Teachers, pupils, and even servants mouthed the name of "Emanuel." He, whose connection with the school coincided with its commencement, was suddenly withdrawing! All found it strange.

They talked so much, so long, so often that, out of the multitude of their words and rumors, some intelligence eventually grew. By the third day, I heard it said he was to sail in a week, then that he was bound for the West Indies. I looked at Madame Beck's face, into her eyes, seeking disproof or confirmation of this report; I scrutinized her completely for information, but no part of her disclosed anything more than what was unperturbed and commonplace.

"This secession was an immense loss to her," she alleged. "She did not know how she should fill the vacancy. She was so used to her kinsman; he had become her right hand. What should she do without him? She had opposed the step, but M. Paul had convinced her it was his duty."

She said all this in public, in the classroom, at the dinner table, speaking audibly to Zélie St. Pierre.

"Why was it his duty?" I could have asked her. I had impulses to seize her suddenly as she calmly passed me in the class, to stretch out my hand and grasp her fast, saying, "Stop. Let us hear the conclusion of the whole matter. *Why* is it his duty to go into banishment?" But Madame always addressed some other teacher, never looking at me, never seeming conscious that I could harbor any care regarding the question.

The week wore on. Nothing more was said about Monsieur Emanuel coming to bid us goodbye; none seemed anxious for his return, none questioned whether he would come, and none betrayed torment lest he depart silently and unseen. Incessantly did they talk, yet never, in all their chatter, touched on this vital point. As for Madame, she could naturally see him and say to him as much as she pleased. What should *she* care whether or not he appeared in the schoolroom?

The week consumed. We were told he was going on a specific day, that his destination was "Basseterre in Guadaloupe," and that the business calling him abroad related to a friend's interests, not his own. I thought as much.

"Basseterre in Guadaloupe." I had little sleep during this time. Whenever I *did* slumber, it followed infallibly that I was quickly roused with a start, while the words "Basseterre," "Guadaloupe," seemed pronounced over my pillow or ran athwart the darkness in zigzag characters of red or violet light.

For what I felt, there was no help, and how could I help feeling? Monsieur Emanuel had been very kind to me of late, growing hourly better and kinder. It was now a month since we had settled the theological difference, and in all that time, there had been no quarrel. Nor had our peace been the cold daughter of divorce; we had not lived aloof. He had come oftener, talked with me more than before, spending hours with me, temper soothed, eye content, manner home-like and mild. Kind subjects of conversation had grown between us; he had inquired into my plans of life, and I had communicated them. The school project pleased him; he made me repeat it more than once, though he called it an Alnaschar dream. The jar was over; the mutual understanding was settling and fixing. Feelings of union and hope made themselves profoundly felt in the heart; affection, deep esteem, and dawning trust had each fastened its bond.

What quiet lessons I had about this time! No more taunts on my "intellect," no more menaces of grating public shows! How sweetly, for the jealous gibe and the more jealous, half-passionate eulogy, were substituted a mute, indulgent help, a fond guidance, and a tender forbearance which forgave but never praised. There were times when he would sit for many minutes without speaking, and when dusk or duty brought separation, he would leave with words like these, "Il est doux, le repos! Il est précieux le calme bonheur!"

One evening, not ten short days since, he joined me while walking in my alley. He took my hand. I looked up in his face, thinking he meant to arrest my attention.

"Bonne petite amie!" he said softly, "douce consolatrice!" But through his touch and with his words, a new feeling and a strange thought found a course. Could it be that he was becoming more than friend or brother? Did his look speak a kindness beyond fraternity or amity?
