Marianne awoke the following morning to a dawn that offered no respite, only a renewed and sharper consciousness of misery. Despite sleeping longer than anticipated, the shadow of her grief had deepened rather than lifted. Before breakfast was served, Elinor had exhausted every avenue of conversation regarding her sister's torment. With steadfast affection and rational counsel on one side, and impetuous emotion and fluctuating despair on the other, the cycle of sorrow repeated itself endlessly. Marianne swung violently between believing Willoughby to be a victim of circumstance, innocent and worthy of her devotion, and viewing him as irredeemably lost, a man capable of no redemption. Her mood shifted with terrifying speed: one moment she cared not for the judgment of the world; the next, she wished to vanish from it forever; the moment after, she bristled with defensive energy against any scrutiny. Yet, in one specific matter, her resolve was ironclad: she rigorously avoided Mrs. Jennings' company whenever possible, and when forced to endure her presence, she maintained a cold, determined silence. Her heart had grown calloused toward the idea that Mrs. Jennings could truly sympathize with her anguish. "No, no, no!" Marianne cried out, her voice trembling with indignation. "She cannot feel. Her kindness is merely politeness; her good-nature is not tenderness. She craves only gossip, and she values me solely because I provide it."

Elinor observed these outbursts with a mixture of pity and understanding. She recognized that Marianne, gifted with excellent abilities and a noble disposition, was plagued by an irritable refinement of spirit and an excessive valuation of emotional delicacy. Like many of the clever and virtuous, Marianne lacked candor and reason. She demanded that others possess the exact same opinions and feelings she held, judging their motives strictly by the immediate impact their actions had upon her own sensitivities.

This flaw in Marianne's reasoning was cruelly exposed shortly after breakfast. Mrs. Jennings, armed with a letter and a radiant smile, entered the room, convinced she was delivering a balm to Marianne's wounds. "Now, my dear," she announced cheerfully, "I bring you something certain to do you good."

To Marianne, the words were a thunderclap. In a single heartbeat, her imagination conjured a scene of ultimate salvation: a letter from Willoughby, overflowing with repentance and tenderness, explaining everything, satisfying every doubt, followed immediately by Willoughby himself, rushing in to plead his case with eloquent eyes. The ecstasy of this fantasy was shattered in the next breath. Instead of Willoughby, the letter lay open before her, written by her own mother. The handwriting, previously unwelcome, now struck her with the force of a physical blow. The sharp sting of disappointed hope made her feel, in that agonizing second, as though she had never suffered before.

Mrs. Jennings stood frozen, her face pale with horror. Marianne's reaction was not one of quiet sadness, but of violent, streaming tears—a torrent of reproach that Mrs. Jennings could not possibly interpret correctly. Misunderstanding the depth of Marianne's betrayal, Mrs. Jennings offered only platitudes of pity, urging Marianne to read the comforting letter again. But when Marianne finally calmed enough to examine it, the contents brought little solace. Every page was dominated by Willoughby's name. Her mother, still blissfully unaware of the truth, wrote with warm confidence in their engagement, relying on Willoughby's alleged constancy. She pleaded with Marianne to be more open with them, expressing such tender affection for Willoughby and such certainty of their future happiness that Marianne wept with agony reading her mother's ignorance.

In that moment, Marianne's longing to return home intensified. Her mother became dearer to her than ever, precisely *because* of her mother's misguided faith in Willoughby. Desperate to escape the painful reality of Barton, Marianne urged her departure with wild urgency. Elinor, torn between knowing what was best for Marianne and respecting her mother's wishes, offered no advice of her own, suggesting only patience until their mother's response arrived. Eventually, Marianne agreed to wait.

As Mrs. Jennings departed earlier than usual, unable to rest until the Middletons and Palmers shared her grief, she refused Elinor's offer to accompany her, venturing out alone for the remainder of the morning. Left behind, Elinor sat with a heavy heart, aware of the terrible news she must convey. Having failed to prepare Marianne's mind for the blow, she began to write to her mother, seeking guidance for the future. Meanwhile, Marianne entered the drawing-room and stood fixed beside Elinor, watching the movement of her sister's pen. She mourned for Elinor the burden of such a difficult task, grieving even more deeply for the pain it would inflict upon their mother.

They sat thus for about fifteen minutes when a sharp rap at the door broke the silence. Marianne, whose nerves were already frayed, jumped. "Who can this be?" cried Elinor. "So early! I thought we were safe." Marianne moved to the window. "It is Colonel Brandon!" she exclaimed with visible vexation. "We are never safe from him."

"He will not enter, as Mrs. Jennings is away," Elinor reasoned.

"I will not trust to that," Marianne snapped, retreating to her room. "A man who owns no time of his own possesses no conscience to prevent him intruding on another's."

Her prediction proved correct, though based on a profound misunderstanding. Colonel Brandon did enter. Elinor, recognizing the genuine solicitude in his troubled gaze and his anxious inquiry after Marianne, could not forgive her sister for holding such a shallow view of the man.

"I met Mrs. Jennings in Bond Street," Brandon explained after the initial greeting, "and she encouraged me to come. I was eager to find you alone, which I desired greatly. My sole wish—to be honest—is to offer not temporary comfort, but a lasting conviction to your sister's mind. My regard for her, for yourself, and for your mother... will you allow me to demonstrate it by sharing certain circumstances? Nothing but a very sincere regard and an earnest desire to be useful could justify me, though I confess I have spent many hours convincing myself I am right, leading me to fear I may be wrong."

He paused, looking uncertain.

"I understand," Elinor said gently. "You have information concerning Mr. Willoughby that will reveal his true character. For Marianne, your disclosure would be the greatest act of friendship imaginable. My gratitude is secured immediately by any truth that serves this end, and hers will surely follow in time. Please, let me hear it."

"You shall," Brandon replied. "Briefly, when I left Barton last October..." He hesitated, realizing the complexity of his tale. "I must go further back. I am an awkward narrator, Miss Dashwood; I hardly know where to begin. Perhaps a short account of myself is necessary. On such a subject, I can expect little temptation to be diffuse."

Sighing heavily, he continued. "You likely forgot a conversation between us one evening at Barton Park, during a dance. I mentioned a lady I once knew, noting a resemblance to your sister, Marianne."

"Indeed," Elinor answered, "I have not forgotten it."

Brandon smiled slightly. "If I am not misled by the partiality of memory, the resemblance is striking, both in mind and person. The same warmth of heart, the same eagerness of fancy. This lady was my nearest relative, an orphan from infancy under my father's guardianship. Our ages were nearly identical, and from childhood we were inseparable friends. I cannot recall a time when I did not love Eliza. As we matured, my affection deepened into something I might now deem impossible to feel given my current state of desolation. Hers, for me, was equally fervent, though born of different causes, and equally unfortunate.

"At seventeen, she was lost to me forever. She was married against her will to my brother. His fortune was substantial, but our family estates were encumbered. I fear this is all that can be said of the conduct of a man who was simultaneously her uncle and guardian. My brother did not deserve her, nor did he love her. I had hoped her love for me would sustain her through difficulties; for a time, it did. But eventually, the misery of her position overwhelmed her resolve. Though she had promised me nothing would separate us—not even blindness to the truth! I have never explained how this came to pass. We were mere hours away from eloping to Scotland. The treachery—or perhaps the sheer folly—of my cousin's maid betrayed us. I was banished to a distant relative's house, and she was confined, denied liberty, society, or amusement, until my father's anger subsided. I had relied too heavily on her fortitude; the blow was severe. If her marriage had been happy, perhaps a few months would have reconciled me, or at least I would not lament it today. But it was not. My brother held no regard for her; his pleasures were base, and from the start, he treated her cruelly.

She initially submitted to her misery, but happily, she did not live long enough to succumb to the regret caused by memories of me.
