With our bread supply secured, my thoughts turned back to the wreck—and the treasures still hidden within its broken hull. Above all, I longed to claim the beautiful pinnace. But extracting it would require all our strength, and my wife hesitated at the idea of us venturing out in force.

"Let me take the boys—all but Franz," I urged. "We’ll return before nightfall."

Reluctantly, she agreed, though neither of us was entirely pleased with the arrangement. The boys, however, were thrilled, stuffing their bags with cassava bread and potatoes before we set off for Safety Bay.

After a brief stop to tend the geese and ducks thriving in the marsh, we strapped on our cork belts, boarded the tub-boat, and towed the raft toward the wreck.

Once aboard, I set the boys to gathering whatever they could carry while I inspected the pinnace. My heart sank—she was wedged deep in the hold, her pieces packed so tightly that assembling her there was impossible, yet too heavy to move piece by piece.

"Fetch axes," I ordered. "We’ll clear space around her."

We labored until dusk, making little progress against the stubborn timbers. Defeated for the day, we loaded the raft and rowed back—only to find my wife and Franz waiting at Tentholm.

"I thought I’d stay here while you work," she said with a smile. "That way, I won’t lose sight of you entirely."

Her cleverness lifted my spirits. "Then we’ll work twice as hard to return you to Falconhurst sooner!"

Fritz proudly displayed our haul—barrels of flour, butter, and other treasures—before we collapsed into exhausted sleep.

The next days blurred into relentless labor: clearing space, assembling the pinnace, hauling salvage. At last, she stood complete—yet imprisoned by the ship’s unyielding ribs. Despair crept in until an idea struck.

In secret, I prepared a gunpowder charge, securing it against the hull near the pinnace. After lighting the fuse, I herded the boys ashore. The explosion came with a deafening roar, sending my family scrambling in alarm.

"Gunpowder must have caught fire," I said casually, then "hurried" back to inspect the damage.

The blast had torn open the ship’s side, revealing the unharmed pinnace. The boys gaped, then cheered. "You planned this!" they cried. With rollers and ropes, we guided her into the water, where she floated as if born to it.

Back at Tentholm, we kept our triumph secret until the pinnace was fully armed and rigged. Then, sailing into the bay, we fired her brass guns in salute. My wife rushed out, torn between scolding and pride. "You frightened me half to death!" she laughed, marveling at our creation.

But she had surprises of her own. Leading us inland, she revealed a flourishing garden—rows of cabbages, beans, and fruit trees she’d planted in our absence. "Dinners will be far finer now," she said, eyes gleaming.

The days that followed balanced hard work with joy: tending the garden, crafting tools, even testing a new lasso technique on a stubborn tree stump. Yet amid it all, we remembered to rest, to give thanks, and to marvel at how much we’d built—not just with our hands, but together.
