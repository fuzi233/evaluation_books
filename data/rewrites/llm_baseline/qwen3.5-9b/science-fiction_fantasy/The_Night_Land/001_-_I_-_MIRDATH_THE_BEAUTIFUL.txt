"And I cannot touch her face, nor her hair; I kneel to empty shadows—mere memories of her grace. Her voice sings in the winds, in the sobbing of dawn, among the night-flowers, from the brooks at sunrise and the sea at sunset. And I answer with vain calls..."

It was the joy of the setting sun that finally drew words from my lips. I had wandered far from my home, walking alone, pausing frequently to gaze upward at the battlements of evening rising before me, and to feel the strange, beloved gathering of dusk enveloping the entire world.

On my final pause, I was utterly lost in a solemn joy at the glory of the coming night. Perhaps I let out a soft laugh in my throat, standing solitary amidst the twilight of the world. Suddenly, my solitude was answered from the trees bordering the country road to my right. It was as if someone whispered, "And thou also!" in a tone of glad understanding. I laughed again softly within my chest, though only half-believing that any true human being could answer my laugh; it felt more like a sweet delusion or a spirit tuned perfectly to my mood.

Then, she spoke, calling me by my name. When I stepped to the roadside to get a clearer view and determine if I knew her, I realized with certainty that she was the lady renowned throughout the sweet county of Kent as Lady Mirdath the Beautiful—a neighbor to me, for her guardian's estates bordered my own.

Until that moment, however, we had never met. Having spent so much time abroad and devoted so much energy to my studies and exercises at home, my knowledge of her was limited to rumors. Otherwise, I was quite content; my books held me captive, and my physical training occupied my mind. I was always an athlete, believing myself quicker and stronger than any man I knew, save perhaps in the fantasies of tales or the boasts of braggarts.

Instantly, I stood with my hat in hand, answering her gentle teasing as best I could while gazing intently and wonderingly at her through the gloom. Truly, rumor had failed to describe the beauty of this strange maiden. She now stood jesting with such a sweet spirit, claiming a kinship of cousinhood with me—which proved to be true once I fully grasped the situation.

She made no fuss; she addressed me frankly by my boyhood name and granted me permission to call her Mirdath, nothing more or less—at that time. She invited me to climb through the hedge via a gap that was her special secret, a path she used occasionally when leaving her maid to attend country festivities, dressed as a village maid. Though she likely didn't intend to deceive many, I dared believe she had her reasons.

I climbed through the gap in the hedge and stood beside her. She appeared tall to me as I looked up, and indeed she was tall, though I stood a head higher. She invited me to walk with her to the house so I might meet her guardian and apologize for my long neglect of calling upon them. Her eyes sparkled with mischief and delight as she chided me for my oversight.

Suddenly, however, she grew serious. She raised a finger to silence me, indicating she had heard something in the woods stretching along our right. Indeed, I heard it too: a rustling of leaves, followed by the sharp, clear crack of a dry twig breaking in the stillness.

Immediately, three men burst from the woods toward me. I shouted sharply for them to stand back or beware of harm, placing the maid behind my left hand while gripping my oak staff ready for action.

The three men uttered not a single word. They charged at me. I glimpsed the glint of knives, and at that sight, I moved with glad, brisk determination to attack. Behind me rang out a shrill, sweet call on a silver whistle—the maid summoning her dogs, perhaps also signaling the household servants.

Yet, aid was useless; the need was immediate. I had no reluctance to use my strength before my sweet cousin. Stepping forward swiftly, I drove the butt of my staff into the body of the man on the left, sending him dropping like a dead weight. I struck sharply at the head of the second man, cracking it so effectively that he fell instantly to the earth. The third man I met with a solid punch to the jaw; he required no second blow and joined his fallen companions immediately. Thus, the fight ended before it had properly begun. Laughing with a proper sense of pride at the bewilderment I saw in Lady Mirdath's face as she regarded me through the hushed evening, I felt triumphant.

There was no time to linger, however, before three great boar-hounds bounded up, released by her whistle. She struggled briefly to keep the beasts from attacking me, while I beat them off the men lying on the ground to prevent them from mauling the wounded. Directly, shouts erupted, lanterns flared in the night, and the footmen of the house rushed forward with weapons. Unsure whether to strike me or not in that first moment, they hesitated—but upon seeing the men on the ground, learning my identity, and recognizing my stature, they maintained a respectful distance. My sweet cousin, however, showed no desire to keep her distance from me; instead, she developed a deeper, more profound sense of kinship than she had initially displayed.

The servants asked what should be done with the footpads, who were now recovering. I left the matter to them, offering some silver. Very sound justice they administered; I heard their cries ringing out long after we departed.

Upon reaching the Hall, my cousin introduced me to her guardian, Sir Alfred Jarles—an old, venerable man whom I knew slightly due to our neighboring estates. He praised me honorably with refined courtesy, making me a welcomed houseguest from that day forward.

I remained all evening, dining, and later ventured back into the grounds with Lady Mirdath. She was more friendly to me than any woman had ever been, appearing as though she had known me forever. Truly, I shared this feeling in my heart; it was as though we understood each other's nature and turns, finding constant delight in discovering shared interests without surprise, save for the pleasant truth of our connection.

One thing I noticed held Lady Mirdath particularly dear that fore-night: the ease with which I handled the three footpads. She asked plainly if I was not truly very strong. When I laughed with young, natural pride, she seized my arm suddenly to test its strength herself. Surely, she released it just as abruptly, gasping with astonishment at its sheer hardness. Afterward, she walked silently beside me, seemingly thoughtful, never straying far from my side.

If Lady Mirdath found a strange pleasure in my strength, I harbored a constant wonder and marvel regarding her beauty, which seemed even lovelier under the candlelight at dinner.

Further delights awaited me in the days that followed. I found happiness in her appreciation of the mystery of evening, the glamour of night, and the joy of dawn. One memorable evening, as we wandered the parklands, she began to say, half-unthinkingly, that it was truly an "elves' night." She stopped immediately, fearing I lacked understanding. In truth, I was on familiar ground of inner delight. I replied calmly that the Towers of Sleep would rise that night, and I felt in my bones that it was a night to find the Giant's Tomb, the Tree with the Great Painted Head, or... I halted suddenly. She gripped me tightly, her hand trembling as she held me. When I asked what troubled her, she breathedlessly urged me to continue speaking. With half-understanding, I explained I had merely meant to mention the Moon Garden, an old, happy fantasy of mine.

In verity, when I said this, Lady Mirdath cried out in a strange, low voice, bringing me to a halt so she could face me. She questioned me earnestly, and I answered with equal fervor, suddenly excited, realizing she knew these things too. Truly, she admitted she possessed knowledge but had believed herself alone in the world with her understanding of that strange land of dreams. Now, to find that I had also traveled in those dear, strange dream-lands! Truly, the marvel of it—the marvel of it! she repeated often. Again, as we walked, she remarked that there was little wonder she had been drawn to call to me that night, seeing me pause on the road. Though she had learned of our cousinship beforehand, having seen me pass on my horse often and inquiring about me, she may have been daintily irritated that I paid so little heed to Lady Mirdath the Beautiful. Indeed, I had been thinking of other matters, yet I was human enough to have sought her out had we met earlier.

Do not think I was not utterly stirred by the wonder of sharing a dream-filled knowledge of matters each believed unknown to the other. Yet, when I probed further, much of my fancy was foreign to her, and much of hers meaningless to me. Despite this slight regret, there were moments repeatedly when one revealed something the other knew and could complete the story, bringing gladness and amazement to both.

Thus, picture us wandering and conversing constantly, growing happily older in dear knowledge and sweet friendship. How exactly time passed, I know not, but eventually, a commotion arose—shouts of men, baying dogs, and the gleam of lanterns.
