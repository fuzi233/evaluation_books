I flung myself upon the bed as a man flings down a load he has carried too far, and set my mind to worrying, like fingers at a knot, the tale she had poured into me. One loose word of hers—no more than a grain dropped from the lip—had suddenly seasoned the whole with meaning. I saw again the bank of the stream, the princess stretched prone, the leap of the leopardess—how the water, running with that clean, kitchen-knife insistence of a mountain rill, had melted her self-wrought glamour as surely as warm broth loosens congealed fat. The stream had undone her. And her errand—told as if it were some necessary errand of household and realm—had shown me, plain as salt on the tongue, what peril hung over the Little Ones. I—blind meddler—had saved the one dread enemy they had.

I had scarce reached the end of that bitter mouthful when sleep took me. Perhaps the wine she had given was not all grape and sun; perhaps some old craft had been steeped in it, like herbs left too long in spirits until they turn the head and soften the will.

When I woke, night lay thick outside, but a lamp hung from the ceiling and shed a clear, gentle light, as if it were careful not to startle the shadows. A delicious languor wrapped me. I felt as though I floated far off shore, rocking on a twilight sea whose swell was made of breath. Mere existing was pleasure. No ache anywhere, no stitch, no throb. I thought, with a calm that frightened me, Surely I am dying.

No pain—ah—

A dart went through me, clean and vile, like the puncture of some hidden fang. It struck straight through the centre of me, and left behind it a sickness as if something rancid had been poured into the heart. Again—sharper, more exact, as if the point had learned its way. I could not lift my hand to my breast. Something held it down. My body would not answer me: not dead, but numbed—paralysed like a limb slept upon, only it was my whole self. My will raged and beat at the bars and could not move so much as a finger. Some hateful thing lay upon me.

I ceased striving and let myself lie, because struggle was only a mockery. Then I became aware—first by touch, then by the faintest change in the air—of a soft hand upon my face, pressing my head into the pillow, and of a heavy weight across me. My breath, that had been pinned, came freer; the weight lifted from my chest. I opened my eyes.

The princess stood above me on the bed, steady as if she had risen there like a dream made solid. She was looking out into the room, not at me, with the still, remote attention of one walking in sleep. Her great eyes were clear and calm. Her mouth had that look—not of tenderness, but of satisfied passion, as if she had drunk deep. She drew a hand across her lips and wiped away a streak of red.

She caught my gaze. In one swift motion she bent, and with the handkerchief in her hand struck across my eyes. It was like the edge of a knife drawn over them—cold, keen, blinding. For a moment the world went white and swimming.

In that blindness I heard a dull, heavy sound: the soft landing of some great, padded thing that had taken a little leap. My sight came back in a rush of pain and watering, and I saw the long swing of a tail disappearing through the half-open door.

I sprang after it.

The creature was gone as if it had never been. I shot down the stair into the hall of alabaster. The moon was high, and the place looked like the inside of a pale shell, all washed with cold light—a faint, sun-blanched moon within the moon. The princess was not there.

I must find her. In her presence I might protect myself; out of it I could not. It was as if she had made me a tame beast to be bled at leisure—set me, living, like a fountain in her garden, for a thirst that was not human. While my eyes were open I did not fear her. But my eyes would close. Sleep would come—soft as flour sifted—then she would come. Not seeing her, I felt her everywhere, as one feels a smell in the dark and cannot tell from what corner it creeps. She might be anywhere—might even now be waiting me in some secret cavern of sleep. Only with my eyes upon her could I feel safe from her.

Beyond the alabaster hall the darkness was thick as soot. I had to grope with hands and feet, feeling my way like a man in a smoke-filled kitchen. At last my fingers found a curtain. I pushed it aside and entered the black hall.

There was a great silent assembly.

How it was visible I cannot tell; the walls, the floor, the roof were smothered in a blackness deeper than any moonless night I have ever known—black as a pot left too long on the fire until the bottom is char and the air itself tastes of it. Yet my eyes could separate—vaguely, uncertainly—individuals in that mass, forms half-swallowed and half-born of dark. It was as if sight itself had been thinned, like broth stretched too far with water.

Silence and undefined motion possessed the wide space. All was a dim, confused dance, stirred with recurrent glimpses of shapes that struck some memory in me like a forgotten word on the tongue. Now a woman appeared, but with glorious eyes looking out from a skull. Now an armed figure upon a skeleton horse. Now one, now another of the hideous burrowing phantasms I had seen before. No order held; currents and eddies crossed and broke; if I thought I caught the rhythm of a dance, it shattered and confusion poured back in.

With the shifting colours of the more solid shapes mingled a multitude of shadows, as if cut free from their owners—each moving after its own will, each a counterfeit that had forgotten what it counterfeited. There was little sound—no laughter, no cries, only a hush that pressed on the ear until I became aware of my own blood moving, the small internal surf of a body trying to be alive. The silence had a kind of tyranny in it, like a dominant language imposed: it did not merely lack speech, it forbade it; it made every unuttered thought feel suspect, every private name unpronounceable.

I searched everywhere for the princess. Through the shifting, kaleidoscopic scene I could not see her, nor any sign that she was near. Where was she? What might she not be doing? No one took the least notice of me as I wandered, as if I were only another shadow strayed from its source.

At length, losing hope, I turned away. Finding the wall—though even then I could not see it, only feel its cold smoothness—I kept to it with my hand and groped along to a curtained opening into the vestibule.

There, dimly moonlighted, the cage of the leopardess was become an arena.

What I saw was a desperate struggle, and silent as the rest. Two forms—one human, one bestial—were so entangled that limb and flank and arm seemed one writhing knot. It lasted but an instant before, with the sick certainty of nightmare, the leopardess was out of the cage and walking quietly to the open door, as if nothing had happened at all.

I hurried after her. Yet even as I moved I glanced behind me, and there—impossible—was the leopardess again in the cage, couching motionless as when I had first seen her, like a beast carved of night and patience. My mind reeled as if some familiar word had been spoken in a dialect I did not know: the sound was near meaning, but meaning slipped away.

The moon, half-way up the sky, shone round and clear. The bodiless shadow I had seen the night before was walking through the trees toward the gate, and after him went the leopardess, tail swinging with that easy, domestic assurance of a creature that belongs where it goes. I followed a little way off, as silent as they. Neither once looked round.

Through the open gate we went down to the city, lying quiet under the moonshine as a covered loaf cools on a board—still, pale, waiting. The face of the moon was very still, and its stillness looked like expectation held in the breath.

The Shadow went straight to the stair at the top of which I had lain the night before. Without pause he went up, and the leopardess followed. I quickened my pace.

A cry of horror rang out—so sudden in that muffled world that it tore the silence like cloth.

Then something fell between me and the stair. It struck with a soft, heavy thud, like a sack dropped. At my feet lay a body—frightfully blackened and crushed—yet still recognisable as the woman who had led me home and shut me out. The moon showed her as it shows spilled ink: a darkness that drinks light.

I stood, petrified, while the meaning tried and failed to enter me.

Then the spotted leopardess came bounding down the stair with a baby in her mouth.

The sight turned my stomach as if I had bitten on bitterness. I darted to seize her before she could turn at the foot—but that instant, from behind me, the white leopardess shot through the moonlight like a great bar of glowing silver and took the spotted one by the neck.

The spotted leopardess dropped the child. I caught it up—warm, startlingly heavy with its small life, its skin smelling of milk and fear and the close air of human rooms. Its tiny mouth opened soundlessly; in that hush even its breathing seemed a stolen thing.

I stood and watched the battle.

Now the one was uppermost, now the other, both too intent for any loud noise—only a low growl, a whimper, a snarl of hate, and then the quicker scrambling of claws as each, worrying and pushing and dragging, fought for foothold on the pavement. The city remained moon-quiet around them; the sounds of the fight were the only breaks in that imposed silence, like a nearly forgotten dialect forcing itself back through a throat trained to other words.

The spotted leopardess was larger than the white, and anxiety tightened in me for my friend; but soon I saw that, though neither stronger nor more active, the white leopardess had the greater endurance. Not once did she lose her hold upon the other’s neck.

At length from the spotted throat issued a howl of agony—and it changed, by swift-crowded degrees, into the long-drawn crescendo of a woman’s uttermost wail, as if beast-speech had failed and human speech, too, had only that one raw syllable left.

The white leopardess relaxed her jaws.

The spotted one drew herself away and rose on her hind legs.

Erect in the moonlight stood the princess.

A confused rush of shadows careered over her whiteness—the leopard’s spots crowding, hurrying, fleeing to the refuge of her eyes, where, merging, they vanished. The last few, outsped and belated, tangled in the cloud of her streamy hair and were gone, leaving her radiant as the moon when a legion of little vapours has flown off her disc—save that down the white column of her throat a thread of blood still trickled from every wound made by her adversary’s terrible teeth.

She turned away, took a few steps with the gait of a Hecate—ancient, ruthless, at home in night—then fell, covered afresh with her spots, and fled at a long, stretching gallop.

The white leopardess turned also.

She sprang upon me, and with a force that was not savage but absolute, pulled my arms asunder. The baby slipped from my grasp. She caught it as it fell—caught it as neatly as a cook catches a falling knife by the handle—and flew with it along the street toward the gate, white as driven moonlight, swift as hunger.
