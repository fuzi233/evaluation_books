To truly appreciate the behavioral economics of Colonel Altamont, one must first contextualize his financial trajectory within the broader framework of his military career and personal risk management strategies. The narrative commences early in the forenoon following a significant social engagement at Grosvenor Place, where the Colonel chose to project a specific persona. Upon emerging from his quarters at Shepherd's Inn, he enters the sitting room of Mr. Strong, where the Chevalier is already seated, engaged in leisure activities. Strong, a man renowned for optimizing his living conditions regardless of location, had previously secured a substantial caloric intake consisting of fried eggs and broiled bacon, prepared with surgical precision by Mr. Grady. Psychologically, Strong exhibits a high tolerance for social interaction, preferring engagement over isolation. Although he harbors no genuine affection for his fellow lodger and would welcome the latter's misfortune—a sentiment shared by Sir Francis Clavering—he maintains a facade of civility. Having attended to Altamont with apparent warmth the prior evening, including extinguishing his candle to mitigate fire risks, Strong later consumed water with satisfaction after discovering an empty spirit bottle, subsequently retiring to rest. This routine underscores a physiological resilience; Strong possesses an indomitable constitution, capable of enduring combat or incarceration alike, consistently waking with vigor and appetite regardless of the day's challenges.

The initial transactional interaction involves Colonel Altamont requesting a pint of pale ale from Grady. After transferring the beverage from a pewter flagon to a tankard, the Colonel consumes it in one draught, expressing profound revitalization. He praises the quality of the brew, specifically noting the efficacy of hot copper vessels, citing past experiences consuming Bass beer in Calcutta. Strong interjects with a humorous reference to Lucknow, acknowledging the strategic intent behind providing the alcohol post-consumption. This prompts the Colonel to recount his recent exploits, admitting to a loss of cognitive control induced by intoxication. He describes a pattern of compulsive consumption, noting that once initiated, he becomes unable to cease drinking until the vessel is empty, leading to erratic verbal output. Despite intending to remain sober at the Black and Red establishment, the presence of a sample bottle of Hollands left by Strong proved irresistible. He washed the contents down with tea, suspecting further consumption occurred at a clandestine gambling venue.

Strong queries the timing of these events, noting the early hour of departure before nine o'clock. The Colonel confirms his attendance at a gathering involving Blackland, young Moss, and other associates, describing himself as foolish for participating. Had they attempted roulette, he asserts confidently that victory would have been inevitable, attributing his losses to the cunning tactics of his opponents rather than lack of skill. Specifically, Blackland manipulated the game of hazard on the dining table, causing the Colonel to lose the funds provided by Strong earlier that morning. This financial deficit triggered a desperate attempt to secure additional capital from Clavering, though details of this encounter remain obscured by the Colonel's blackout.

Seeking to understand the mechanism of Altamont's credit acquisition, Strong asks how he consistently extracts money from Clavering. The Colonel responds with a laugh, claiming Clavering owes him a debt. Strong counters that indebtedness alone is insufficient justification for payment, given Clavering's existing liabilities. The Colonel attributes Clavering's compliance to a fraternal bond, asserting that the Baronet views him as a brother. When pressed for clarification, the Colonel adopts a rhetorical silence, advising Strong to accept the premise without inquiry. However, he acknowledges a resolve to abandon the brandy bottle, recognizing it as a potential catalyst for ruin. Strong probes further, reminding the Colonel of his statements from the previous evening at Clavering's residence. The Colonel feigns ignorance regarding any controversial remarks, fearing exposure. Strong clarifies that the Colonel's silence was not due to restraint but rather excessive intoxication, rendering his speech incomprehensible to observers. Satisfied with this deflection, the Colonel exhales in relief.

Transitioning to a broader analysis of his vices, the Colonel admits to an inability to resist three specific stimuli: brandy, dice, and beautiful women. He posits that no individual of integrity has escaped the detrimental influence of these factors throughout his life, spanning from his adolescence as a cabin boy on an Indiaman to his current status near fifty years of age. He recounts a specific incident in Paris involving a woman adorned with jewelry and ermine, who dissipated a significant portion of his capital. Initially exercising fiscal discipline and avoiding private gaming, he eventually succumbed to the allure of Madame the Countess de Foljambe. Under her invitation, he attended soirées and operas, funded by a streak of luck at the tables rather than direct expenditure on entertainment.

The narrative culminates in a detailed examination of a high-stakes gambling session at the Countess's residence, attended by aristocratic figures including Bloundell-Bloundell, the Honourable Deuceace, and the Marquis de la Tour de Force. Following a meal abundant with champagne and brandy, the group engaged in card games. The Colonel, driven by compulsion, played and drank until incapacitated. Upon awakening the next day, he discovered an IOU signed by the Honourable Deuceace for thirty louis, which the Earl's son promptly repaid in gold. Subsequently, Bloundell-Bloundell and the Marquis arrived, presenting additional IOUs totaling five hundred and fifty louis, despite the Colonel's denial of owing such sums. The Marquis threatened legal and reputational consequences, labeling the Colonel a cheater. Just as tensions escalated, a mutual acquaintance intervened, revealing that the Countess had pleaded with the group to stop, warning of the dangers posed by Bloundell and the Marquis. The friend disclosed that the Countess was distraught, fearing violence, and expressed her own romantic attachment to the Colonel, referring to him affectionately as "Ferdinand."

Upon visiting the Countess, she reacted with shock and distress, nearly fainting at his appearance. She addressed him by his Christian name, lamenting his financial losses and offering her jewelry as restitution. The Colonel, adhering to his principle of honor, refused to accept any of her property, declaring his intention to repay nothing. The Countess, however, interpreted his refusal as a threat to his safety, warning that the antagonists would kill him. The Colonel retorted with defiance, stating his willingness to confront the Marquis physically rather than engage in a duel, dismissing the concept of a "field of honour" as irrelevant to his survival instincts. Strong observes the Countess's retreat from the Colonel's blunt assertion, interpreting it as a reaction to his rejection of formal dueling protocols.
