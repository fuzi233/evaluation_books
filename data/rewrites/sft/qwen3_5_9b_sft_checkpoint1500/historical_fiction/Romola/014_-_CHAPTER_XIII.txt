The afternoon of September the seventh hung lazily over Florence, a languid interval occurring more than two months since Romola and Tito had finally laid bare their hearts to one another. Tito had just stepped out of Nello's shop, finding the barber sprawled upon his bench in a deep slumber. A cap rested heavily over the barber's eyes; one leg was drawn up in relaxation, while the other dangled loosely toward the floor, dragging behind it a manuscript volume of verse whose leaves lay crushed beneath the weight. In a corner sat Sandro, utterly absorbed in a solitary game of *mora*, his left fingers responding to the arithmetic dictates of his right with a solemn, rapt intensity.

With the utmost gentleness, Tito snatched up the lute. Bending over the sleeping barber, he plucked the strings softly, singing a melody that seemed to wake the soul itself:

Nello was as easily roused as a bird startled from its nest. With instant reflex, the cap fell from his eyes, and he sprang upright. "Ah, my Apollino!" he exclaimed, rubbing his eyes. "I am somewhat tardy with my siesta on this sweltering day. This stems from failing to sleep naturally, instead indulging in a draught of potent poetry. Listen to me, as I begin to align my words with their initial letters, like a *Trovatore*. That is a symptom of my failing health: I fear the fine wine of my understanding is draining from the spigot of authorship, leaving me an empty cask smelling only of dregs, much like many a genius I know. Pray, what is it, my Orpheus?" Stretching his arms to their full length, Nello then curled them inward, seizing Tito's curls in a playful grip. "What do you seek from your well-trained Nello? I detect a coaxing strain in that soft music. Let me peer into the needle's eye of your desire, as the sublime poets dictate, so I may thread it."

"That is merely a tailor's metaphor for the sublime poet's vision," Tito replied, letting his fingers drift lightly across the strings once more. "Yet you have guessed the cause of my eager impatience to see you awake. I crave a fresh application of your art—not upon my chin, alas, but upon the *zazzera*, which is currently as knotted as Florence's politics. Your comb works with admirable skill, flattering the skin and kindling the animal spirits in that region. A touch of your delicate orange-scent would be entirely appropriate, for I am bound for the Scala Palace and must present myself amidst radiant company. The young Cardinal Giovanni de' Medici will be there, accompanied by a certain Bernardo Dovizi of Bibbiena, whose wit moves with such speed that I see no path to surpass it save by the fragrance of orange blossoms."

Nello had already seized his comb, flourishing it as he gently guided Tito backward into the chair, draping a cloth around him. "Speak not of rivalry, my dear young lord: Bernardo Dovizi is a keen youth who will never cast a net to catch the wind. However, he bears a resemblance to his brother, Ser Piero—that sharp-muzzled weasel whom Piero de' Medici employs to slip through small holes for him. Nay, you eclipse all rivals and may soon touch the heavens with your forefinger. They tell me you have carried enough honey to sweeten the sour temper of Messer Angelo; indeed, he has declared you less of an ass than might have been expected, considering your close understanding with the Secretary."

"And between ourselves, Nello mio," Tito murmured, "Messer Angelo possesses greater genius and erudition than I can find in all the Florentine scholars combined. It suits them well to praise me now, when Poliziano is bowed down by grief, sickness, or some other affliction. I might attempt a flight with a sparrow-hawk like Pietro Crinito, but regarding Poliziano, he is a large-beaked eagle who would swallow me, feathers and all, without noticing the loss."

"I shall not contradict your modesty, if you prefer it so," Nello smiled. "Yet do you not expect us clever Florentines to repeat the same truths daily, as we must if we always spoke the absolute truth? We cry down Dante and cry up Francesco Cei simply for the sake of variety. If we acclaim you as a new Poliziano, Heaven has ensured it shall not be quite such a great falsehood as it might otherwise be. And are you not a model of virtue in this wicked city? With your ears double-waxed against all siren songs that would lure you away from the Via de' Bardi and from the great work destined to astonish posterity?"

"Posterity, in truth, whom it will likely astonish as the universe is astonished, by the sheer impossibility of discerning what the plan actually was."

"Yes, something akin to that prophecy circulated recently. Cristoforo Landino remarked that the excellent Bardo belongs to that class of scholars who lie prostrate in their learning, like cavaliers trapped in heavy armor, and then grow angry because they have been overriden—a pithy remark, it seems to me, that was not harvested from his own garden. For of all men, for feeding one with an empty spoon and gagging one with vain expectations through long discourse, Messer Cristoforo is the pearl. Behold, you are now perfectly attired." Nello withdrew the cloth. "Impossible to add further grace! But is love always to be sustained by learning, eh? Soon I shall have to dress the *zazzera* for the betrothal—is it not true?"

"Perhaps," Tito said with a smile, "unless Messer Bernardo recommends that Bardo require me to yoke a lion and a wild boar to the cart of the Zecca before I may win my Alcestis. Yet I confess he is correct in judging me unworthy of Romola. She is a Pleiad who may grow dim by marrying any mortal."

"*Gnaffè*," Nello chuckled, "your modesty finds its proper place. Yet Fate appears to have measured and chiseled you for the niche left empty by the old man's son, who, by the way, Cronaca informed me, is now at San Marco. Did you know?"

A faint electric shock coursed through Tito as he rose from the chair, though it remained imperceptible to the outside world. He immediately stooped to retrieve the fallen book, busy flattening the crumpled leaves as he spoke. "No; I believed he was at Fiesole. Are you certain he has returned to San Marco?"

"Cronaca is my authority," Nello shrugged. "I do not frequent that sanctuary, but he does. Ah," he added, taking the book from Tito's hands, "my poor Nencia da Barberino! It jars your scholarly sensibilities to see the pages dog-eared. I was lulled to sleep by the well-rhymed charms of that rustic maiden—'prettier than the turnip-flower,' 'with a cheek more savory than cheese.' But to acquire such a well-scented notion of the *contadina*, one must lie upon velvet cushions in the Via Larga—not venture out to watch the Fierucoloni stumbling into the Piazza della Nunziata this evening after sunset."

"And pray, who are the Fierucoloni?" asked Tito, seemingly indifferent as he settled his cap.

"The country women who journeyed from the mountains of Pistoia and the Casentino, and heaven knows whence else, to keep vigil within the church of the Nunziata, selling their yarn and dried mushrooms at the *Fierucola* (the Little Fair), as we term it. They make a curious spectacle, brandishing paper lanterns and howling their hymns to the Virgin on this eve of her nativity—if you possessed the leisure to behold them. No? Well, I have had sufficient of it myself, for there is wild business in the Piazza tonight. One might receive a stone or two upon the ears or shins without solicitation, and I have never favored such pressing attention. *Addio*."

Tito departed carrying a subtle undercurrent of unease, his visit concluding sooner than anticipated. The boy-cardinal Giovanni de' Medici, the youngest of the red-hatted fathers who would later present his broad, dark cheek to posterity as Pope Leo X, had been detained by his favorite pastime of the hunt and failed to appear. It was nearly half an hour before sunset when Tito left the doors of the Scala Palace, intending to proceed immediately to the Via de' Bardi. He had not traveled far, however, when, to his profound astonishment, he saw Romola advancing toward him along the Borgo Pinti.

She wore a thick black veil and a black mantle, yet it was impossible to mistake her figure or her stride. Beside her walked a short, stout form, which Tito recognized as Monna Brigida, despite the unusual plainness of her attire.
