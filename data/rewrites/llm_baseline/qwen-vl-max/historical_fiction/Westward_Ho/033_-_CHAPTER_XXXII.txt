“Full fathom deep thy father lies; Of his bones are corals made; Those are pearls which were his eyes; Nothing of him that doth fade, But doth suffer a sea-change Into something rich and strange; Fairies hourly ring his knell, Hark! I hear them. Ding dong bell.”

—*The Tempest*

It is over. The great Armada has been defeated. The eternal conflict—the battle between light and darkness, between Michael and Satan, between the children of God and the servants of the devil—has reached its climax. Once every few centuries, this spiritual war becomes flesh and blood, manifesting in mortal men who must choose sides, not with prayers alone, but with cannon fire and steel. This was such a day. Judgment had come, dividing good from evil, sheep from goats, testing every man’s work in the fire. And the devil’s grand design—his empire of fear and falsehood—proved, as always, to be nothing more than a hollow boast, a balloon bursting at the slightest touch.

Byzantine empires, Spanish fleets, papal crowns, Russian thrones—all follow the same path: rise in pride, fall in shame. One bold blow, and the monster vanishes in smoke. The children of Israel, as in ancient times, see the Egyptians dead upon the shore—though they scarcely know how, save that God has done it—and they sing the song of Moses and the Lamb.

From England and the Netherlands, from Geneva and Germany, from the Vaudois shepherds whose bones lie scattered on the cold Alpine slopes—those true seeds of faith, liberty, and future civilization—arose a cry of joy such as the world had not known for generations. It was the birth cry of a new age: North America, Australia, New Zealand, the Pacific Islands, free trade, free colonies across the globe.

Van Meteran records: “By command of Her Majesty, and by order of the States of the United Provinces, a solemn day of thanksgiving was proclaimed. All were required to attend church and give thanks to God. Preachers were instructed to exhort the people accordingly. On November 29th, the day was spent in fasting, prayer, and gratitude.

“The Queen herself, following the example of ancient Rome, rode in triumph into London, attended by all the principal nobles and officers of her realm. She entered the city in a chariot of victory, clothed in robes of state, proceeding from her palace to St. Paul’s Cathedral, where the captured banners of the vanquished Spaniards hung displayed. Citizens lined the streets in their liveries, companies bearing their colors, while blue hangings adorned the thoroughfares, creating a magnificent spectacle. Upon entering the church, the Queen gave thanks to God and listened to a sermon preached before her at Paul’s Cross—whose sole theme was praise and glory to God. With her own voice, she urged the people to join in worship, and they responded with loud acclaim, wishing her long life and prosperity, and the downfall of her enemies.”

Yes—just as the medals declared: *It came, it saw, and it fled!* Where did it go? Northward, like frightened deer, past the Orkneys and Shetlands, picking up hapless fishermen as guides. Along Norway’s coast, where even the descendants of Vikings refused them food and water. Then onward toward the Faroes and the endless dawn circling the Pole.

Their water ran out. Cattle were thrown overboard. The northern seas roared with the screams of drowning horses. They must return—each man as best he could. Let them meet again at Cape Finisterre—if they ever met. Medina Sidonia, with twenty-five of the strongest ships, would lead the way, leaving the rest to fate. He vanished quickly. Forty more, the last remnants of that mighty fleet, drifted wearily southward, hoping to reach Ireland’s southwest coast, where Roman Catholic sympathizers might offer aid—or at least fresh water.

But alas—

“Make Thou their way dark and slippery, And follow them up ever with Thy storm.”

For now, gale after gale rose from the Atlantic. Few of those doomed vessels ever reached Spain.

And where was Amyas Leigh and the *Vengeance* during all this?

At fifty-seven degrees north, the English fleet, low on provisions and entirely out of powder and shot, turned southward, deciding it was wiser to let the Spaniards face the wild northern seas. A few pinnaces remained to watch the fleeing armada. The English fleet, caught in the same storms that shattered the Spaniards, struggled back to Harwich, where they resupplied—just in case the enemy returned. But there was no need. Parma, still clinging to hope, waited weeks on the dunes of Dunkirk, believing the idol at Halle would not betray him. He cast anchors, repaired rigging, and watched the horizon—like a man waiting for a ghost.

“But lang, lang may his ladies sit, With their fans intill their hand, Before they see Sir Patrick Spens Come sailing to the land.”

The Armada was already beyond Scotland. And Amyas was following.

When the Lord High Admiral decided to return, Amyas asked leave to pursue the Spaniard. He approached Sir John Hawkins, who happened to be nearby, and requested ammunition and supplies—promising to repay them, either from plunder if he lived, or from his estate if he died. He left formal bills in Hawkins’s hands, who, being a practical man, tucked them away with his tobacco and string. Then Amyas called his crew together.

He reminded them once more of the Rose of Torridge and Don Guzman de Soto.

“Men of Bideford,” he said, “will you follow me? There will be plunder for those who love it. Revenge for those who seek it. And for all of us—who love honor—the pride of never abandoning the chase so long as one Spanish flag flies in English waters.”

Every man aboard answered yes. They would follow Sir Amyas Leigh to the ends of the earth.

I need not recount each detail of that long, grueling pursuit. How they found the *Santa Catharina*, attacked her, only to be forced off when reinforcements arrived. How, when Medina’s squadron abandoned the crippled ships, the *Vengeance* nearly perished, charging into the heart of the Spanish fleet to prevent escape. How they disabled the galleon, preventing her from beating to windward, forcing her to run south—past the Orkneys, through the Minch, between Cape Wrath and Lewis. How the younger sailors threatened mutiny when Amyas passed by several noble prizes—among them a great galley and two Venetian ships, *La Ratta* and *La Belanzara*, later wrecked on Ireland’s west coast. How they secured fresh water from Skye, where fierce Highlanders first cursed them in an unknown tongue, then fought briefly, then embraced them with wild affection—clothed no better than California’s savages. How Amyas calmed his men by letting them scavenge a wrecked Venetian on Islay, gaining enough loot to cover the cost of the voyage—only to anger them again by refusing to land and plunder two Spanish wrecks on the Mull of Cantire (whose crews James attempted to smuggle into Spain, playing both sides—until the council intervened and allowed them to go, though likely to be hanged elsewhere). How they passed a strange island, half black, half white, which the natives called Raghary—but Cary dubbed “the drowned magpie.” How the *Santa Catharina* nearly foundered on the Isle of Man, where Manxmen killed a boat’s crew with arrows. How they fought her again at Castleton, then again in a full-day battle, destroying her mainyard. How the Spaniard blundered down the Welsh coast, lost and confused. How both ships nearly foundered at Holyhead and Bardsey Island. How they were driven onto a lee shore in Cardigan Bay, caught in a heavy westerly gale, and the *Santa Catharina* ran aground on Sarn David—a submerged pebble ridge said to be the ruins of Gwalior, destroyed by Prince Seithenin the drunkard, whose name still draws spits from loyal Welshmen. How she escaped at high tide and fought Amyas a fourth time. How the wind shifted, allowing her to round St. David’s Head.

These and many other dramatic events I must pass over. The end is near.

It was the sixteenth day of the chase. The previous evening, they had seen St. David’s Head and the Welsh coast around Milford Haven, silhouetted against the lightning of an inland thunderstorm. During the night, flashes lit the sky under a gentle southwest breeze.

They strained their eyes through the darkness, hoping to catch sight of the Spaniard’s masts in the flickering light. At least they knew one thing: with the wind as it was, she couldn’t have gone far westward. To try to pass them and head north would be madness. She was likely lying-to ahead of them, perhaps between them and the shore.

Then, just after midnight, the wind shifted sharply to the west and blew hard until dawn. They were certain—unless she had tried the desperate move of running past them—they had her trapped in the mouth of the Bristol Channel.

Dawn broke slowly and wearily, bringing a dull, drizzly day beneath a low, gray sky—clouds netted and barred with black, a sign the storm was only resting before breaking again. The horizon was blurred with vapor drifting in a cold breeze. As the day wore on, the wind died, the sea settled into a long, glassy roll flecked with foam, while above brooded the inky sky, and around them, mist swallowed both land and chase.

Amyas paced the wet deck, restless and furious. He knew the Spaniard could not escape. Yet every passing moment gnawed at him, delaying the one revenge that consumed his soul. The crew sat sullenly, whistling for wind. Sails flapped listlessly. The ship rolled heavily, her yardarms dipping low.

“Watch those guns,” Amyas growled. “You’ll have something loose next.”

“We’ll watch the guns,” Yeo replied, “if the Lord watches the wind.”

“We’ll have plenty before night,” Cary said. “And thunder too.”

“So much the better,” Amyas said. “Let it roar till it splits the heavens, if it lets me finish my work.”

“He’s not far off,” Cary said. “One break in the clouds, and we’ll see him.”

“To windward, likely,” Amyas snapped. “The devil fights for him. Sixteen days on his heels, and I’ve not sent this through him yet!” He shook his sword violently.

The morning passed without sign of life—not even a gull. The gloom of the sky mirrored the gloom in Amyas’s heart. Was he to lose his prey after all? The thought filled him with rage and despair. Unthinkable. Anything but that.

“No, God!” he cried. “Let me feel this in his cursed heart, and then—strike me dead, if You will!”

“The Lord have mercy on us,” murmured John Brimblecombe. “What have you said?”

“What is that to you?” Amyas snapped. “There—dinner’s piping. Go below. I won’t come.”

Jack descended, whispering in fear about Amyas’s words. Everyone believed they foretold misfortune—except old Yeo.

“Well, Sir John,” he said, “why not? What better can the Lord do for a man than take him home when his work is done? Our captain is stubborn and vengeful—he wants to kill his man himself. But for me, I don’t care how the Don dies, so long as he dies. I owe him no grudge. May the Lord grant him repentance and forgive him all. But if I could see him safely ashore—on Mortestone or Lundy—I’d say, ‘Lord, now lettest Thou Thy servant depart in peace,’ even if it were lightning that fetched me.”

“But a sudden death?”

“And why not a sudden death, Sir John? Even fools desire a short life and a merry one. Why shouldn’t the Lord’s people pray for a short death and a merry one? Let it come as it may to old Yeo. Hark—there’s the captain’s voice!”

“Here she is!” thundered Amyas from the deck. In an instant, the crew scrambled up the hatchway as fast as the rolling ship would allow.

Yes. There she was. The cloud had lifted suddenly. A ragged patch of blue sky poured sunlight onto her tall masts and proud hull, four or five miles eastward. But no land was visible.

“There she is,” said Cary. “And here we are. But where is here? Where is there? How’s the tide, master?”

“Running up the Channel by now.”

“What matters the tide?” Amyas said, staring at the ship with cold, terrible eyes. “Can’t we get to her?”

“Not unless someone jumps out and shoves behind,” Cary joked. “I’m going down to finish my mackerel—if this roll hasn’t thrown it to the cockroaches under the table.”

“Don’t jest, Will,” Amyas said, his voice trembling. “I can’t stand it.” His whole body shook like a leaf. Cary took his arm and drew him aside.

“Dear old lad,” he said, leaning over the bulwarks, “what’s wrong? You’re not yourself—haven’t been for four days.”

“No,” Amyas said. “I’m not Amyas Leigh. I’m my brother’s avenger. Don’t reason with me, Will. When it’s done, I’ll be merry old Amyas again.” He wiped his brow.

“Do you believe,” he asked after a pause, “that men can be possessed by devils?”

“The Bible says so.”

“If my cause weren’t just, I’d think I had a devil in me. My throat, my heart—burning like hell. God, let it be done. It must be done. Now go.”

Cary left, shuddering. As he descended, he looked back. Amyas had pulled out his hone and was sharpening his sword again—over and over—as if doom demanded endless grinding.

The day dragged on. The blue sky vanished, and all grew dark and oppressive, though the air grew hotter. Distant thunder rumbled to the west.

Nothing could close the gap—every boat except one had been lost, and it was too small to tow them. As the men went below for dinner, Amyas continued sharpening, glancing up every few moments at the Spaniard, as if afraid she’d vanish.

Around two o’clock, Yeo came up.

“She’s ours now, sir. The tide’s been running eastward for two hours.”

“Safe as a fox in a trap. Satan himself can’t take him from us!”

“But God may,” Brimblecombe said simply.

“Who spoke to you, sir? If I thought He—” Thunder cracked from the west, answering his words. It rolled closer, louder, until it crashed overhead, splitting a cloud-cliff far above—and then silence.

All men stared at one another. Amyas did not flinch.

“The storm’s coming,” he said. “And the wind with it. It will be Eastward-ho now, for once, my merry men all!”

“Eastward-ho never brought us luck,” Jack whispered to Cary. But all eyes turned north-west, where a black line along the horizon began to define sea and sky—now clear in the mist.

“There comes the breeze.”

“And there the storm.”

With that strange, accelerating pace some storms possess, the thunder—once distant and slow—now roared in peal after peal directly above.

“Here comes the breeze! Round with the yards, or we’ll be taken aback!”

The yards creaked. The sea crisped. Hot wind swept their faces, tightened ropes, filled sails, bent the ship. A cheer burst from the men as the helm turned and they surged forward—straight toward the Spaniard, still becalmed.

“There’s more behind, Amyas,” Cary said. “Shall we shorten sail?”

“No. Hold every stitch,” Amyas ordered. “Give me the helm. Boatswain, pipe to clear for action.”

It was done. In ten minutes, the crew stood at quarters. Thunder rolled louder. The breeze freshened.

“The dog has it now. There he goes!” Cary said.

“Right before the wind. He doesn’t want to face us.”

“He’s running into destruction,” Yeo said. “An hour more, and he’ll either race up the Channel—or smash into shore.”

“There! He’s put his helm down. Does he see land?”

“He’s like a March hare, beaten from his home,” Cary said. “He doesn’t know where to run next.”

He was right. Ten minutes later, the Spaniard fell off again, sailing straight downwind, while the *Vengeance* gained on her fast. After two hours, the distance shrunk from four miles to one. Lightning flashed closer. From a vast black cloud-arch, a fierce gust blew. Amyas reluctantly yielded to growing murmurs and ordered sail reduced.

On they rushed, barely slower. The black arch followed, veiled by a flat gray sheet of rain, before which the sea boiled in a long white line. Every moment, a blue spark leapt into the water or zigzagged through the rain.

“We’ll have it now, and with a vengeance. This’ll test your tackle, master,” Cary said.

The boatswain shrugged and pulled up his collar as the first drops stung his ears. A minute later, the squall hit—rain like hail, lashing the sea into froth, wind tearing heads off waves, turning the ocean into a seething white waste. Above, lightning danced, blinding and dazzling, while thunder cracked like splintering iron.

“Get arms and ammunition under cover—and below with you all!” Amyas shouted from the helm.

“And heat the pokers in the galley fire,” Yeo called. “In case the rain puts out our linstocks. I hope you’ll let me stay on deck, sir—in case—”

“I need someone. Who better than you? Can you see the chase?”

No. She was lost in the gray whirlwind. She might be half a mile away—or gone.

Now Amyas and Yeo stood alone. Neither spoke. Each knew the other’s thoughts—and knew they were his own. The squall grew fiercer. Rain poured harder. Where was the Spaniard?

“If he’s laid-to, we might overshoot him, sir!”

“If he tried to lay-to, he won’t have a sail left. I know the stubbornness of those Spanish tubs. Hurrah! There he is—right on our larboard bow!”

There she was—two musket shots away, staggering with torn canvas.

“He tried to hull,” Yeo said, rubbing his hands. “Got a buffet. What now?”

“Range alongside, if it blows live imps and witches. Try our luck once more. Pah! This lightning blinds!”

They swept forward, gaining fast. “Call the men up. The rain will be over in ten minutes.”

Yeo ran forward—then leapt back, face pale and wild.

“Land ahead! Port your helm, sir! For God’s sake, port your helm!”

Amyas, with the strength of a bull, slammed the helm down. Yeo shouted to the crew below.

The ship swung. Masts bent like whips. The foresail tore like a cannon blast. What mattered? Within two hundred yards lay the Spaniard. Ahead of her, rising through the hail, a huge dark bank merged with the clouds. At its base, pillars of foam leapt upward.

“What is it? Morte? Hartland?”

It could be anything within thirty miles.

“Lundy!” Yeo shouted. “The south end! I see the head of the Shutter in the breakers! Hard a-port! Get her close-hauled! The Lord may have mercy on us yet! Look at the Spaniard!”

Yes—look at the Spaniard!

On their left, as they broached-to, a granite wall sloped down from the clouds to a lone peak two hundred feet high. Then a hundred yards of roaring breakers over a sunken shelf, where the tide raced like a cataract. Then, amid salt spray, the Shutter—a monstrous black fang—rose, waiting. Between the Shutter and the land, the great galleon loomed dimly through the storm.

She had seen her doom. She tried to broach-to. But her clumsy bulk refused to obey. She struggled—half hidden in foam—fell back, and rushed toward her end.

And Amyas, standing at the helm, watching, felt the weight of destiny. He raised his sword—one final time—then hurled it into the sea.

“It is done,” he whispered. “Now let the Lord decide.”
