The deep, unbroken quiet of the Harley Street house, while Edith recovered from her confinement, was a gentle blessing to Margaret Hale. It gave her the slow, unhurried rest her overstrained nerves had craved, and space to wrap her head around the staggering shift in her circumstances that had unfolded over the past two months. She found herself lodged in a home of such unsparing luxury that the very existence of hardship or small, grinding cares seemed never to have crossed its threshold. The cogs of daily life were so perfectly oiled that every routine turned with soft, unjarring smoothness. Mrs. Shaw and Edith fussed over her endlessly, insisting that this house was her true home now, and Margaret could not shake the faint, guilty secret that it did not feel like home at all: her idea of belonging was still tied to the sun-dappled Helstone vicarage, even to the cramped, drafty little cottage in Milton, with her anxious father, her ailing mother, and all the tiny, messy household troubles of their modest means.

Edith could barely wait to regain her strength before she set about refurnishing Margaret’s bedroom with the same heap of silk cushions, laced trinkets, and tiny porcelain curios that crowded every surface of her own chambers. Mrs. Shaw and her lady’s maid spent days restyling Margaret’s wardrobe, restoring it to an elegant, cheerful variety that suited the London season. Captain Lennox, meanwhile, was easy, kind, and unfailingly gentlemanly: he sat with Edith in her dressing room for an hour or two every day, played with his infant son Sholto for another, and lounged away the rest of his hours at his club or at dinner engagements with his fellow officers.

Just before Margaret had begun to chafe at the lack of purpose in her days, Edith came downstairs to resume her place at the head of the household, and Margaret fell back into the old familiar habit of tending to her cousin. She happily took all the trivial tedious duties off Edith’s hands: answered every social note, reminded her of forgotten calls, and sat by her side to soothe her when no gala was on the calendar and she felt languid enough to fancy herself ill. But the rest of the family was swept up entirely in the whirl of the London Season, and Margaret was often left entirely alone.

Her thoughts drifted back to Milton, and she was struck by the stark, unbridgeable contrast between that life and this one. She was growing sick of this eventless, effortless ease, where no struggle, no small act of meaningful work, was ever required. She feared she would grow sleepy and dull, forgetting every part of the world beyond the soft luxury that wrapped around her. There were toilers and drudges in London, she knew, but she never saw them: the servants lived in an invisible, underground world of their own, whose hopes and fears she would never learn, and they only seemed to spring into existence when a master or mistress rang a bell to satisfy some small want or whim. A strange, unsatisfied hollow yawned in Margaret’s chest, and once, when she hinted at this vague emptiness to Edith, the latter, still languid from dancing until three the night before, stroked Margaret’s cheek idly as she perched on a footstool beside the sofa where Edith lay.

“Poor child!” Edith said. “It’s so sad for you to be left home night after night just when all the world is at its gayest. But we’ll be hosting our dinner parties soon—just as soon as Henry comes back from circuit—and then you’ll have all sorts of nice little variety. No wonder you’re moped, poor darling.”

Margaret did not believe a string of dinner parties could fix that hollow feeling. But Edith prided herself on her dinner spreads, insisting they were nothing like the old stuffy, formal dowager dinners her mother used to host, and Mrs. Shaw herself took just as much delight in the Lennoxes’ bright, lively social circle as she had once taken in her own heavy, old-fashioned entertainments. Captain Lennox was unfailingly kind and brotherly to Margaret, and she was very fond of him—save for those rare moments when he fussed over Edith’s dress and appearance, determined that her beauty should dazzle every guest in the room. Then all the fiery, independent core of Margaret’s nature rose up, and she bit her tongue to keep from speaking her mind.

Her days unfolded in a lazy, unchanging rhythm: an hour or two of quiet before a late breakfast that no one bothered to serve punctually, eaten slowly by half-asleep, groggy people, where she was expected to sit through every dragged-out minute even though none of the family’s upcoming plans concerned her, expected to offer sympathy even if she could not offer advice. After that, there were endless stacks of notes Edith left for her to write, heaping soft compliments on Margaret’s elegant, charming letter-writing. She would play with Sholto after his morning walk, mind him while the servants ate their own dinner, go for a drive or receive callers, and then her aunt and cousins would head off to some engagement, leaving her free—yet tired, listless, her already delicate health worsened by days of no real work or purpose.

She waited, unspoken but longingly, for Dixon to return from Milton, where the old servant had been busy wrapping up every last one of the Hale family’s affairs. The total lack of news from the place she had called home for two years had felt like a famine to her. Dixon’s formal, businesslike letters only dropped in the stray mention of Mr. Thornton’s advice, about what to do with the Hale’s old furniture, or how to negotiate with the landlord of their Crampton Terrace cottage. Hardly any other Milton names ever crossed the page. One evening, she sat alone in the Lennox drawing room, holding Dixon’s half-read letters in her hand, staring into space as she thought back over old days, picturing Milton’s noisy, busy streets, wondering if that whole whirl went on just the same as if her and her father had never existed at all, wondering if anyone in that whole crowd missed her (not Higgins—she was not thinking of him at all) when the servant announced Mr. Bell. Margaret stuffed the letters into her workbasket in a hurry, and stood up blushing as if she had been caught doing something guilty.

“Oh, Mr. Bell! I never thought I would see you here!”
“I hope that surprised little start is paired with a proper welcome,” he laughed. “Have you dined? How did you get here? Let me order you something to eat at once,” Margaret fussed.
“Only if you’re planning to dine too. Otherwise, you know no one cares less about eating than I do. But where are all the rest? Gone out to dinner? Left you all alone?”
“Oh yes, and it’s such a quiet, lovely rest. I was just thinking—would you chance an impromptu dinner? I’ve no idea what’s in the larder.”
“To tell you the truth, I already dined at my club. Their cooks aren’t half as good as they used to be, though, so I thought if you were going to eat I might make a second go of it. Never mind, never mind! There aren’t ten cooks in all of England you can trust with an unexpected meal. Their skills and their fires might hold, but their tempers certainly won’t. You can make me tea instead, Margaret. Now—what were you thinking about, just now? You were going to tell me. Whose letters were those you hid away so fast, my god-daughter?”
“Only Dixon’s,” Margaret said, flushing bright.
“Whew! That’s all? Do you know who rode up from Milton in the same train as me today?”
“I’ve no idea,” Margaret said, refusing to guess.
“Your what-d’ye-call-him? The brother of your cousin-in-law? Henry Lennox?”
“Mr. Henry Lennox?” Margaret asked.
“That’s him,” Mr. Bell said. “You knew him long ago, didn’t you? What do you make of him, Margaret?”
“I liked him a very long time ago,” Margaret said, glancing down for a moment, then lifting her eyes to speak in her usual steady tone. “We’ve been corresponding about Frederick for years after that, but I haven’t seen him for nearly three. He might be quite changed now. What did you think of him?”
“I’m not sure. He was so busy trying to work out who I was, first, then what sort of status I held, that he never let a single thing slip about himself—unless all that veiled curiosity about the man he was talking to is a fair marker of his character. Do you think he’s good-looking, Margaret?”
“Certainly not,” she said. “Do you?”
“Not a bit. I thought you might, though. Is he often down here in town?”
“I think he is, when he’s not out on circuit. He’s been away on circuit ever since I arrived. But—Mr. Bell—did you come straight from Oxford, or from Milton?”
“From Milton. Can’t you see I’m still smoke-dried from the mills?”
“I did wonder if it might be all that ancient Oxford dust clinging to you,” Margaret teased.
“Come now, be sensible! In Oxford I could cajole every landlord in the city to give me my way with half the trouble your Milton landlord has given me—beaten me in the end, too. He won’t release our lease on the Crampton Terrace house until next June twelvemonth. Luckily, Mr. Thornton tracked down a new tenant for it for us. Why don’t you ask after Mr. Thornton, Margaret? He’s been a wonderfully active friend to you, you know. Took more than half the whole mess of work off my shoulders.”
“How is he? How is Mrs. Thornton?” Margaret asked, breathless, voice so quiet it was almost a murmur before she forced herself to speak louder.
“They’re well enough, as far as I can tell. I was staying at their house until I was driven half mad by nonstop chatter about the Thornton girl’s wedding. It was too much for Thornton himself, even though she’s his own sister. He kept escaping to lock himself in his study, the whole time. He’s far too old to care for all that frippery nonsense, either as the guest of honor or a bystander. I was shocked to see old Mrs. Thornton get swept up in the whole fuss, all her daughter’s glee over orange blossoms and lace carrying her away. I thought she was made of far sterner stuff.”
“She would put on any show of soft sentiment to hide her daughter’s silliness,” Margaret said softly. “You’re right, she doesn’t much care for me.”
“I know that for a fact,” Mr. Bell nodded.
“Oh, thank goodness, tea is here at last!” Margaret exclaimed, grateful for the interruption.

And with the tea came Henry Lennox himself, who had walked up to Harley Street after a late dinner, evidently expecting to find his brother and Edith at home. Margaret suspected he was just as relieved as she was to have a third party in the room: this was their first meeting since the fateful afternoon on the Helstone garden terrace, when he had proposed and she had turned him down. She did not know what to say at first, and was glad for all the little tea-table tasks to keep her hands busy and her mouth still, giving him time to collect himself too. He had forced himself to walk over tonight, after all, just to get this awkward first meeting over with—awkward even if the rest of the family had been home, and twice as awkward now that Margaret was the only other person in the room, the one he would naturally have to address most of his conversation to.

Margaret was the first to regain her composure. Once the initial flush of awkward shyness faded, she spoke of the first thing that came to her mind. “Mr. Lennox, I cannot thank you enough for all the work you’ve done on Frederick’s behalf.”
“I only wish it had been more successful,” he replied, glancing quickly over at Mr. Bell, as if gauging how much he could safely say in front of a stranger. Margaret read his thought instantly, and turned to Mr. Bell, drawing him clearly into the conversation, making it plain he knew every detail of the efforts to clear Frederick’s name.
“That last witness, Horrocks—the very last one we held out hope for—proved to be no help at all. Mr. Lennox found out he sailed for Australia last August, just two months before Frederick came back to England, and gave us all the names of—”
“Frederick was in England? No one ever told me that!” Mr. Bell exclaimed, shocked.
“I thought you knew. I never doubted someone must have mentioned it. Of course it was a great secret—perhaps I ought not to have said it now,” Margaret said, a little dismayed.
“I never named it to my brother, or your cousin, for that matter,” Mr. Lennox said, with the cool, dry tone of a professional who guarded his clients’ secrets.
“Never you mind, Margaret,” Mr. Bell said. “I don’t live in a gossiping, prying world, and none of my acquaintances go digging for hidden facts. You don’t have to look so frightened spilling the secret to an old hermit like me. I’ll never tell a soul he was here—no one will even ask me. Wait a minute!” he interrupted himself, suddenly sharp. “Was he here for your mother’s funeral?”
“He was with her when she died,” Margaret said softly.
“Of course! Of course! Someone asked me not many weeks ago if he hadn’t come over then, and I denied it stoutly—who was that? Oh, I remember!” He did not say the name, and Margaret ached to know if her suspicion was right, if it had been Mr. Thornton who had asked after Frederick—but she could not bring herself to pose the question.

There was a short, quiet pause. Then Mr. Lennox turned back to Margaret. “Since Mr. Bell now knows every circumstance of your brother’s unfortunate predicament, there is no better way forward than to walk him through exactly what remains of the evidence we once hoped to produce in his favor. If he would do me the honor of breakfasting with me tomorrow, we can go over every name of those missing witnesses.”
“I want to hear every last detail too, if I may. Can’t you both come here instead? I don’t quite dare formally invite you both to breakfast, but I know you would be more than welcome. I need to know everything we still can about Frederick, even if there is no hope of clearing him right now.”
“I have an appointment at half past eleven. But I will certainly come if you wish it,” Mr. Lennox replied, with a faint, unmissable eagerness that made Margaret draw back shyly, almost wishing she had not made the natural request.

Mr. Bell got to his feet, hunting for his hat that the servant had moved to make room for the tea tray. “Well, I don’t know what Mr. Lennox intends to do, but I’m for heading home. I’ve traveled all day, and long journeys start to wear on a man of sixty odd years.”
“I think I shall stay and wait for my brother and Edith to get back,” Mr. Lennox said, making no move to leave. A sudden, shy panic seized Margaret at the thought of being left alone with him. The memory of that afternoon on the Helstone terrace was so vivid to her, she could only assume it was just as vivid for him.
“Don’t go yet, please Mr. Bell,” she said quickly, laying a light, firm hand on his arm. “I want you to meet Edith. Please stay.” He looked at her, saw the flustered confusion stirring in her face, and sat down again at once, as if that small touch had held a strength he could not resist.
“You see how she overpowers me, Mr. Lennox,” he laughed. “And did you notice how cleverly she phrased it? First she said she wanted me to come ‘see’ her cousin Edith, the great beauty everyone talks about, but then she corrected herself to say Edith should ‘know’ me. I suppose I’m not much worth looking at, eh Margaret?” He joked to give her time to settle her frayed nerves, and she caught his light tone and bantered back easily. Mr. Lennox found himself wondering how his brother the Captain could ever have claimed Margaret had lost all her good looks. Dressed in her quiet black mourning gown, she was the perfect contrast to Edith, who drifted in a little later in her white crape mourning dress with loose golden curls floating around her shoulders, all softness and glitter. Edith dimpled and blushed prettily when she was introduced to Mr. Bell, determined to live up to her reputation as a great beauty and win over even this gruff old Oxford don. Mrs. Shaw and Captain Lennox both welcomed Mr. Bell warmly, and he found himself liking them almost against his will, especially when he saw how naturally Margaret slipped into her place as a beloved sister and daughter of the house.

“It is such a shame we were not home to greet you,” Edith said. “You too, Henry! Though I don’t know if we would have skipped our dinner party for you. But for Mr. Bell! For Margaret’s Mr. Bell—”
“There is no limit to the sacrifices you would have made,” her brother-in-law cut in, dry. “You would have skipped the whole gala, missed the chance to wear this very lovely dress, no doubt.” Edith did not know whether to frown or smile, and Henry Lennox was quick to turn the joke light before he irritated her. “Would you be willing to make a small sacrifice tomorrow morning, then? First, agree to host the breakfast to meet Mr. Bell, and second, make it half past nine instead of ten o’clock? I have a stack of letters and papers I want to show Miss Hale and Mr. Bell.”
“Please make our house your own for the whole duration of your stay in London,” Captain Lennox said to Mr. Bell. “I am only sorry we cannot offer you a bedroom for the night.”
“Thank you, I am much obliged. And I would be a churl if you did, because I should have to turn you down, even for the pleasure of such agreeable company,” Mr. Bell replied, bowing politely all around. Privately he congratulated himself on the well-turned phrase, when the plain truth of it was: I could not stand the stuffy, over-polite restraints of this household. It would be like eating meat without salt. I am thankful they have no spare bed.
