Fanny's Occupation's Gone

Since Arthur's illness, Mrs. Pendennis, known to the world as "good Helen," had effectively taken command of her son's private quarters, turning his drawers and closets into a bureau of domestic surveillance. While she ostensibly managed his laundry—repairing shirts missing buttons and stockings needing mends—she inevitably discovered the papers hidden among the linens. These were letters requiring answers, a burden she assumed during Arthur's weakened state. Though Mrs. Pendennis never explicitly broached the "dreadful Fanny Bolton mystery" to her son, it haunted her mind with inexpressible anxiety. To shield him further, she had removed the brass knocker from his inner door, ensuring that the postman's jarring double rap would not disturb his convalescence. Consequently, no mail reached Arthur, not even urgent notes from bootmakers or hatters demanding payment.

Mrs. Pendennis, however, possessed a modest fortune saved through frugality and self-denial. She delighted in using these funds to settle Arthur's debts, viewing it as a moral imperative. Indeed, many a dutiful parent would welcome the chance to intercept a son's correspondence, yet few find the same joy in paying off a youthful indiscretion. For Mrs. Pendennis, the silence of the postbox was a blessing; she spared Pen the trouble of hearing the dreaded *rat-tat*.

Yet, the widow's vigilance was not absolute. When at Richmond, she sought counsel from her brother-in-law, Major Pendennis. Sitting on a terrace bench with Pen and Mr. Warrington, she poured out her terrors regarding Arthur's conduct, though naturally omitting the grosser details of her son's financial ruin. She confessed her confusion about the "odious transaction" involving Fanny Bolton, asking the Major for guidance.

The Major, a man whose morals were far less rigid than his sister's, adopted a theatrical demeanor. He puckered his face, casting sidelong glances at the carpet, and finally declared, "My dear, good creature, I don't know anything about it... Young men will be young men." When Helen, looking stately, protested against hearing such sins spoken lightly, the Major feigned offense, claiming he spoke only to spare her feelings. He suggested that Arthur might simply be "flirting with a little grisette," a notion that caused Helen to sob violently, fearing her honorable boy had fallen low.

"The most unhappy mother in the world!" she cried.

The Major, amused by her distress, gently chided her for treating her son like a hermit. He warned that suppressing letters could breed resentment, urging caution. Then, the critical moment arrived. Helen produced a single, tiny letter, sealed with wax, which she claimed was all that existed. The Major, despite his outward gravity, struggled to maintain composure as he examined the document. It appeared to be written in a timid, sprawling hand, containing nothing more than prayers for Arthur's health and a desire to see him.

"Morgan, my man, has made inquiries," the Major explained, adopting a conspiratorial tone. "It seems this Fanny was greatly smitten, paid for her own pleasures at Vauxhall, and her father is a violent drunkard. But here is the crux: the girl's mother swears to her innocence, while the girl herself claims Arthur treated her like a brute. Yet, the facts suggest otherwise. A laundress named Flanagan confirmed that Fanny arrived only when Arthur was delirious, accompanied by an elderly gentleman, Mr. Bows, who kindly transported the Major from Stillbrook."

Helen, relieved by this revelation, hurried to the terrace where Arthur sat with Warrington. She intended to prove his innocence. However, the Major, anticipating disaster, intercepted her. He reasoned that if Helen confronted Arthur immediately, the truth would shatter everything. Instead, he proposed a grand scheme: a trip abroad to the Rhine. "We must occupy him," the Major insisted, envisioning a journey where Arthur could recover and forget his troubles under the care of a nurse and Miss Bell.

The plan galvanized Helen. She rushed to her desk, counting her meager savings and eyeing her diamond cross, desperate to fund the escape. Meanwhile, the Major, now a "fatigued and happy warrior," dozed on the sofa, dreaming pleasantly of his diplomatic triumph.

Back on the terrace, Pen and Warrington discussed future literary endeavors, Pen boasting of a new tragedy. Suddenly, Miss Bell arrived, bringing game for Pen and flowers for his mother. She reported that Helen was deeply worn out, her health failing under the strain of watching over Arthur. The Major, overhearing her concern, seized the opportunity to manipulate the narrative. He hinted that Helen's agitation stemmed from the recent scandal, proposing that they travel to Europe where painful memories could be left behind.

Helen, moved by the prospect of relief, agreed instantly. She resolved to borrow money if necessary, eager to whisk Arthur away to a distant watering-place. The Major, pleased with his strategy, turned his attention to securing Warrington's participation. Though Warrington initially hesitated, citing lack of funds and personal engagements, the Major offered to act as guarantor, promising to cover expenses and inviting him to join the party.

At the tea-table, with Helen momentarily absent, the Major renewed his plea. "Isn't it unfriendly?" he coaxed, addressing Miss Bell. "Here we have been the happiest party in the world." Under the gaze of the Major and the shy glance of Laura, Warrington blushed. When Laura softly expressed her hope that he would join, Warrington yielded, murmuring, "I will go anywhere you ask me."

Just as the Major prepared to celebrate the agreement, shouting "Bravo!" and shaking hands, Helen reappeared, candle in hand, her face pale with fright. Laura quickly withdrew her hand, crimson with embarrassment.

"What is it?" Helen asked.

"It's a bargain we have been making," the Major announced smoothly, his voice dripping with false sweetness. "We have just bound over Mr. Warrington in a promise to come abroad with us."

Helen stared, stunned, realizing that the Major's machinations had inadvertently trapped her son in a web of deception before he was even well enough to walk.
