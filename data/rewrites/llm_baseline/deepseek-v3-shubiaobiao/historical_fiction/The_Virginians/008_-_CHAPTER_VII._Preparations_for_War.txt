Sympathy is seldom so deserved as when granted to youth, bravery, and misfortune. Madam Esmond, even had she borne a dozen sons, could scarcely have helped doting upon her young soldier. Mr. Washington’s chamber had become a shrine in her eyes, his virtues the daily sermon preached to her boys. Harry’s reckless love of pleasure and George’s bookish indolence were forever measured—and found wanting—against the Major’s sterling character.

Yet George’s admiration did not flourish under such comparisons. A sullen demon took root within him, whispering doubts, breeding resentment. His jests grew so sharp that his mother, bewildered, could only stare at his brooding humor, uncertain whether to scold or pity him.

Meanwhile, the quarrel between France and England swelled beyond provincial skirmishes into open war. French reinforcements crowded Canada; English troops poured into Virginia. Letters from Quebec fluttered across the sea, their ink laced with foreboding.

*"Alas, my dear friend!"* wrote Madame de Mouchy to George. *"Must you forsake a mother’s embrace for the arms of Bellona? I tremble to wish victory for our lilies when you stand beneath the Leopard’s banner. Yet enmity has no place in my heart—only sorrow for the storm that divides us."*

The Leopard’s banner, indeed, unfurled boldly. Two royal regiments mustered in America, while Commodore Keppel’s fleet, bearing General Braddock and his staff, anchored in Hampton Roads. The Duke’s chosen commander arrived with fanfare, summoning colonial governors to council, demanding men and supplies for the campaign ahead.

The stir reached even Castlewood’s quiet halls. Harry, aflame with martial zeal, galloped to Alexandria to gape at the red-coated ranks, their drums throbbing like a pulse in his ears. He dined with officers, drank to the King’s health, and dreamed of glory. Madam Esmond, ever proud, deemed these English gentlemen a tonic against Virginia’s coarse squires and sycophants. She little guessed the raucous songs that echoed past midnight, or the unseemly scenes as officers staggered to their quarters.

The troops, swaggering through the countryside, soon wore out their welcome. Farmers cursed their looting; Indians, insulted by their arrogance, melted back into the forests—allies lost when most needed. Braddock raged against the colonials’ stinginess and his own men’s indiscipline. Yet when the General’s invitation came to Castlewood, Madam Esmond saw only honor.

His father had served with hers under Marlborough. Such ties demanded courtesy. The twins, splendid in London finery, presented themselves at his levee—only to be dismissed as *"young Squire Gawkeys"* by a bored commander.

*"Who are these popinjays?"* Braddock grumbled.

*"Sons of the Princess Pocahontas,"* sneered Lieutenant-Governor Dinwiddie, who bore the widow no love.

Dinwiddie’s malicious portrait—a pompous shrew with absurd pretensions—left the General chuckling. But Mr. Franklin, shrewder, noted her wealth: cattle, horses, stores vital for the campaign. A change of tone followed. An aide-de-camp soon bowed before the twins, bearing Braddock’s belated compliments and a dinner invitation.

Flushed with triumph, the boys returned. Their mother, quill in hand, penned a gracious reply. Yet beneath the civilities, tension simmered. War loomed; one son must answer its call. Harry ached to go, polishing swords, drilling slaves, his every thought bent on battle. George, though quieter, pored over military treatises, his fencing foil gleaming anew.

When Braddock’s offer came—a place in his family for a young Virginian—Harry’s face lit like a torch. *"I would give my soul to go!"*

George’s voice cut coldly. *"One of us must stay. Our mother cannot be left alone."*

Harry’s fists clenched, but habit bowed him to his brother’s will. The decision hung unspoken between them, a sword’s point at their throats. Not until they stood before Madam Esmond did the matter break.

*"I knew this hour must come,"* she whispered, eyes darting between them.

*"The youngest should go!"* Harry burst out.

*"The heir’s duty is first,"* George countered.

Harry reddened. *"But you’re still weak from fever! And Washington favors you—"*

*"Honor decides, not favor."* George’s hand gripped his brother’s shoulder. *"Were our births reversed, you’d claim the same. Our grandfather’s shade would demand it."*

Pride and anguish warred in their mother’s face. *"My father would say his grandsons are gentlemen,"* she murmured, and fled before her tears could shame her.

Preparations began. George’s servant, Sady, boasted of battles to come; Harry’s Gumbo whimpered—though all knew the boy feared his own shadow. Only Mrs. Mountain raged against George’s choice.

*"Folly!"* she stormed. *"You rush to your ruin!"*

*"Duty calls,"* he said stiffly.

*"Duty? Ha!"* Her laugh was bitter. *"When you return, you’ll find a new master here. Mark me—Washington means to wed your mother!"*

George recoiled. *"Lies!"*

*"Proof!"* She thrust a paper at him—Washington’s handwriting, snatched from his chamber.

*"You spied on a guest?"* George’s voice was deadly.

*"Chance! Mere chance!"* she squawked, as he flung the letter into the fire.

But the words had seared his mind. At dinner, he drank recklessly, his laughter too loud, his eyes darting to the door. Washington did not come that night—but the poison had been sown.

Jealousy, honor, and dread now marched alongside the drums of war.
