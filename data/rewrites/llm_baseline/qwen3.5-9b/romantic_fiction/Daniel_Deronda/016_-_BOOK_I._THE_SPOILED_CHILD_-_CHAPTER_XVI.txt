Men possess histories as vast and complex as the solar system, comprising both a visible trajectory and an invisible core. An astronomer maps the former with rigorous logic, tracing every arc across the dark; yet a true chronicler of human life must navigate the hidden corridors of emotion and thought that precede every deed, leading inevitably to those moments of profound suffering that transform pain into action—the anguished cries of Promethean figures whose torment burns brighter than the heavens they defy.

For Daniel Deronda, destiny had carved a singular path. One specific afternoon, bathed in July sunlight and the scent of fading pink roses, had become the defining epoch of his youth. Imagine the scene: a thirteen-year-old boy lying prone on the cool grass beneath the shadow of a Gothic cloister, his curly head resting on his arms atop a book. Beside him, seated on a camp-stool, was his tutor, similarly engrossed in reading. In Daniel's hands lay Sismondi's *History of the Italian Republics*. The boy possessed a voracious appetite for history, driven by a desire to comprehend how time had unfolded since the Flood and how humanity persisted through its quieter eras.

Suddenly, lowering his arm, he gazed at his tutor with wide-eyed curiosity. "Mr. Fraser," he asked in a tone of innocent wonder, "how was it that popes and cardinals always had so many nephews?"

His tutor, an able young Scot serving as Sir Hugo Mallinger's secretary, reluctantly shifted his gaze from his treatise on political economy. With the crisp, emphatic cadence characteristic of Scottish speech, he replied, "Their own children were called nephews."

"Why?" Daniel pressed.

"It was simply a matter of propriety," the tutor explained, his patience fraying slightly at the interruption. "Since priests cannot marry, their offspring were deemed illegitimate."

Mr. Fraser thrust out his lower lip, returning his focus to his book, while Daniel, as if struck by a sudden sting, sprang upright, facing away from his mentor.

He had always addressed Sir Hugo Mallinger as his uncle. When he had once dared to inquire about his parents, the baronet had offered only a vague reassurance: "You lost your father and mother when you were quite a little one; that is why I take care of you." In the hazy twilight of that early memory, Daniel retained only a dim sensation of being enveloped in soft, scented drapery, kissed frequently, until his fingers brushed something hard and sharp, causing him to weep. Beyond that, his world consisted entirely of the domestic sphere he inhabited. At the time, he cherished Sir Hugo so deeply that the mystery of his origins mattered little; life was a delightful gift from an indulgent, cheerful guardian—a man in the prime of his life whom Daniel regarded as flawless.

Sir Hugo's residence was a picturesque architectural extension of an ancient abbey, steeped in romance and history. In contrast, Diplo, the family seat in another county, was a modest estate acquired through marriage. The Mallinger lineage, however, traced its roots to Henry VIII's reign, descending from Hugues le Malingre, a companion of William the Conqueror. The family gallery displayed two generations of ancestors: stern men in armor with pointed beards, pale ladies confined by hoops and ruffs, grave figures in black velvet, and frightened mothers clutching their children. Through the refined portraits of Kneller, Reynolds, and Romney, the line culminated in Sir Hugo and his younger brother, Henleigh. Henleigh had married Miss Grandcourt, adopting her surname and merging their ancient heraldry, creating a powerful union embodied by Henleigh Mallinger Grandcourt—a figure who would soon become more significant to the narrative than either Sir Hugo or his nephew.

Sir Thomas Lawrence had captured Sir Hugo's youthful vigor in a portrait, though he had inadvertently elongated the baronet's nose, which was actually shorter than the family standard. Fortunately, this distinctive feature reappeared in his brother Henleigh and, most strikingly, in his nephew, Mallinger Grandcourt. Yet, in Daniel Deronda, the ancestral faces reflected nowhere. Nevertheless, he possessed a beauty surpassing all his forebears. At thirteen, he could have served as the ideal model for a painter seeking to depict the essence of noble boyhood; to behold his face was to feel convinced that humanity had achieved greatness in the past and held the potential for even greater heights in the future. Such childlike countenances hold a consecrating power, making us recoil from the world's coarseness and sorrow, fearing they might stain such purity.

But amidst the rose petals on the grass, Daniel was encountering his first lesson in grief. A new idea had invaded his mind, altering the landscape of his previously carefree existence. Just as a sailor's peace is shattered by the sudden threat of a storm, Daniel sat motionless, his face betraying a rapid internal transformation. The deep blush that initially colored his cheeks faded, replaced by an indefinable intensity of concentration. Unlike other boys, his mind blended childish innocence with surprising insight. Having devoured Shakespeare and history, he understood the plight of bastards—men forced to strive heroically to match their legitimate peers. Yet, he had never applied this knowledge to his own life, which had been too smooth to warrant such questions—until now.

With the speed of lightning, a terrifying possibility seized him: the secret of his birth lay here, and the man he called uncle was, in truth, his father. Some children discover poverty early on, realizing their parents cannot afford everything; Daniel felt the arrival of a new, ominous guest carrying veiled, dreadful truths. The fervor he poured into his imaginary worlds suddenly turned inward, illuminating his own history. The beloved uncle transformed in his mind into a father hiding secrets, committing a wrong. What had become of his mother, whom he must have been separated from? These were mysteries he dared not probe. To speak of them felt like catching fireflies in his imagination. Those who recall their own passionate childhoods understand this terror of voicing shame regarding one's parents.

These surging images overwhelmed him, leaving no room for the rationalization that he might be grasping at shadows. The clash between overwhelming emotion and the fear of exposure finally broke forth in slow, heavy tears.

"Daniel, do you see that you are sitting on the bent pages of your book?" Mr. Fraser's voice cut through the silence.

Daniel shifted the book without turning around, held it briefly before him, then rose and walked into the open grounds to dry his tears unnoticed. Once the initial shock subsided, he remembered that he lacked certainty. He had been constructing narratives about his life as he once invented tales of Pericles or Columbus to fill historical gaps. Yet, stubborn fragments of reality remained, like broken pieces of a bridge, clearly indicating where the arches should lie. Simultaneously, a moral impulse arose, viewing his speculations as a form of religious doubt—an impious prying into matters he was not meant to know. Possessing a delicate sensibility, he struggled to reconcile these conflicting currents. Ultimately, a secret impression had settled upon him, granting him a new perspective on every element of his existence. The realization that others likely harbored undisclosed judgments fostered a premature reserve, intensifying his inner turmoil. Now, words that would have previously gone unheard triggered fresh clusters of feeling around every trivial event linked to his suspicions.

A month later, another incident etched itself deeply into his soul. Daniel possessed a rare, resonant voice and a natural musical talent, often accompanying his singing on the piano. After performing "Sweet Echo" for a gathering of gentlemen sheltered from the rain, Sir Hugo summoned him.

"Come here, Dan!" the baronet called.

The boy approached with unusual hesitation. Dressed in an embroidered holland blouse that highlighted the richness of his complexion, his expression carried a quiet gravity that only enhanced his beauty. Everyone admired him.

"What do you say to becoming a great singer?" Sir Hugo asked, his tone shifting from jovial to serious. "Would you like to be adored by the world and sweep the stage, like Mario or Tamberlik?"

Daniel flushed instantly. After a brief pause, he answered with angry resolve, "No; I should hate it!"

"Well, well, well!" Sir Hugo remarked, attempting to soothe him with feigned surprise. But Daniel turned sharply, retreated to his chamber, and collapsed onto the broad windowsill, his favorite refuge. From there, he watched the rain subside, revealing gleams of light through breaking clouds that illuminated the park's ancient oaks and the green glades piercing the woods. This scenery had always been his home, a backdrop of dignified ease that he had accepted as natural. His ardent nature had embraced it wholly. Though he knew much of what it meant to be a gentleman by inheritance, losing himself in the grandeur of figures like Robert Bruce, he had never imagined being excluded from such a fate or destined for a role vastly different from his uncle's.

While it is possible for some to embrace poverty as a bride, preferring humble surroundings and universal access over exclusive privilege, Daniel's tastes aligned perfectly with his upbringing. Daily routines brought him delight, not ennui. Now, however, he was stung by the notion that his uncle—or perhaps his father—envisioned a career for him utterly alien to his own desires, a path unsuitable for the sons of English gentlemen. Although he had accompanied Sir Hugo to the opera, hearing great tenors, the image of a singer commanding a hall now repelled him. He viewed it as dressing up to perform for an audience that would regard him merely as a fascinating toy. For Daniel, Sir Hugo's suggestion was undeniable proof that his birth disqualified him from the gentlemanly class.
