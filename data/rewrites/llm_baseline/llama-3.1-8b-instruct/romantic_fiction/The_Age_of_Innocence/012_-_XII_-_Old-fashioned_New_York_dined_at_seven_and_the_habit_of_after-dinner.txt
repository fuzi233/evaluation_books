As the evening drew to a close, Newland strolled up Fifth Avenue from Waverley Place, the gas lamps casting a warm glow on the deserted thoroughfare. The only signs of life came from a group of carriages gathered outside the Reggie Chivers' townhouse, where a dinner party was in full swing for the Duke. The occasional elderly gentleman, bundled up in a heavy overcoat and muffler, ascended a brownstone doorstep and disappeared into the warmth of a gas-lit hall. As Newland crossed Washington Square, he caught sight of Mr. du Lac visiting his cousins the Dagonets, and further up the avenue, he spotted Mr. Skipworth, a colleague from his law firm, heading towards the Miss Lannings' residence. Beaufort emerged onto his doorstep, a dark silhouette against the bright lights, and descended into his private brougham, disappearing into the evening with a sense of mystery.

It was not an opera night, and there were no parties to attend, so Beaufort's outing was undoubtedly shrouded in secrecy. Newland connected it in his mind with a modest house beyond Lexington Avenue, where colorful window curtains and flowerboxes had recently appeared, and the canary-colored brougham of Miss Fanny Ring was often seen waiting patiently outside. Newland wondered what secrets lay behind those discreet facades, and whether the occupants were merely trying to keep up appearances or if they genuinely sought connection.

Beyond the narrow circle of Mrs. Archer's acquaintance lay a largely uncharted territory inhabited by artists, musicians, and writers. These individuals had never shown any desire to integrate themselves into the social hierarchy, preferring instead to maintain their independence. In her more prosperous days, Medora Manson had attempted to bridge this divide with a literary salon, but it ultimately failed due to the reluctance of the artistic community to participate. Another household, the Blenkers', offered a rare opportunity to mingle with Edwin Booth, Patti, William Winter, and other notable figures from the world of theater and literature. However, Mrs. Archer and her circle viewed these individuals with a mixture of fascination and wariness, unsure of what lay beneath their unconventional exteriors.

"We knew everyone between the Battery and Canal Street when I was young," Mrs. Archer would often remark, "and only those we knew possessed carriages.
