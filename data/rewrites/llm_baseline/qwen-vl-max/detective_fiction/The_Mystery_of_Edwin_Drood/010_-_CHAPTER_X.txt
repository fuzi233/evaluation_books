It is often said that women possess an uncanny ability to judge men’s characters—something seemingly born of instinct rather than reason, a kind of inner knowing that defies logic and resists explanation. This intuition speaks with certainty, even when evidence contradicts it. But rarely do people observe how stubborn this judgment can be once formed. Once a woman has made up her mind about a man, especially if it's unkind, she seldom revises it—even in the face of clear proof to the contrary. In fact, the mere possibility of being wrong gives such judgments a fragility akin to bias: they become entangled with personal feeling, making them as unreliable as testimony from someone with a stake in the outcome.

“Now, don’t you think, Ma dear,” said the Minor Canon one afternoon as his mother knitted quietly in his small bookroom, “that you’re being rather hard on Mr. Neville?”

“No, I do _not_, Sept,” replied the old lady firmly.

“Let us discuss it, Ma.”

“I have no objection to discussing it, Sept. I trust, my dear, I am always open to discussion.” Her cap trembled slightly, as though she were silently adding: *And I should like to see the argument that could change my mind.*

“Very good, Ma,” said her gentle son. “There’s nothing like being open to discussion.”

“I hope not, my dear,” returned the old lady, knitting with unwavering focus—clearly closed to any further debate.

“Well, then,” continued Mr. Crisparkle, “Mr. Neville did act poorly on that unfortunate night—but he was provoked.”

“And under mulled wine,” added the old lady sharply.

“I’ll admit the wine. Though I believe both young men were equally prone to excess.”

“I don’t,” she said flatly.

“Why not, Ma?”

“Because I _don’t_,” she insisted. “Still, I’m quite open to discussion.”

“But, Ma,” he pressed gently, “how can we discuss if you shut down like this?”

“Blame Mr. Neville for it, Sept,” she said with dignified severity. “Not me.”

“My dear Ma—why him?”

“Because,” Mrs. Crisparkle declared, retreating to first principles, “he came home drunk, brought great shame upon this house, and showed disrespect to our family.”

“That cannot be denied, Ma. He regrets it deeply—then, and now.”

“But for Mr. Jasper’s thoughtful courtesy in approaching me the next day, after service, still wearing his gown, and asking whether I’d been frightened or disturbed by what happened—I might never have known of that disgraceful incident at all,” she said.

“To be honest, Ma,” Mr. Crisparkle replied, rubbing his ear, “I would have kept it from you if I could. I hadn’t fully decided what to do. I was following Jasper out to talk to him about hushing the whole thing up—both of us, perhaps—when I found him speaking to you. It was too late.”

“Too late indeed, Sept. He was still pale as ashes, shaken by what had occurred in his rooms.”

“If I had hidden it, Ma, it would have been for your peace, for the sake of the young men, and in fulfillment of my duty as I see it.”

At once, the old lady rose and crossed the room to kiss him. “Of course, my dear Sept, I know that.”

“Yet,” Mr. Crisparkle went on, “it became town gossip, and slipped beyond my control.”

“And I said then, Sept,” she replied, “that I thought ill of Mr. Neville. And I say now that I still think ill of him. I hope he may come to good—but I don’t believe he will.” Her cap vibrated again, emphatically.

“I’m sorry to hear you say that, Ma—”

“I’m sorry to say it, my dear,” she interrupted, knitting steadily, “but I can’t help it.”

“—For,” he continued, “it’s undeniable that Mr. Neville is diligent, attentive, improving rapidly, and—well, I hope I may say—it seems he has genuine affection for me.”

“There’s no merit in that last point, my dear,” she snapped. “And if he claims there is, I think worse of him for boasting.”

“But, Ma, he never claimed it.”

“Perhaps not,” she conceded. “Still, I don’t see that it matters much.”

Mr. Crisparkle watched her with calm amusement—not irritation, but quiet humor, as though contemplating a fine piece of porcelain too delicate for serious argument.

“Besides,” she added, “ask yourself: what would he be without his sister? You know how much influence she has over him. You know how capable she is. Whatever he studies with you, he studies with her. Give her her fair share of credit—and how much remains for him?”

At these words, the Minor Canon fell into silence, lost in thought. He recalled the many times he had seen the brother and sister together, deeply engaged in conversation over one of his old college books—on frosty mornings during his brisk walks to Cloisterham Weir, or on dark evenings when he climbed to his favorite overlook, a jagged ruin of monastery stone. Below, the two figures would pass along the riverbank, lit by the flickering reflections of town lights, the landscape growing colder and more desolate. He remembered how, gradually, he’d realized he wasn’t just teaching Neville—he was teaching both of them. His explanations had naturally adapted to two minds: one close at hand, the other reached only through the first. He thought of whispers from the Nuns’ House—how Helena, once so proud and fierce, had bowed to the fairy-bride (as he called her), learning from her what she knew. He thought of their striking bond, so different yet so perfectly matched. And most of all, he wondered—could it truly be that these moments, so recent, had already woven themselves into the fabric of his life?

Whenever the Reverend Septimus drifted into reverie, his mother took it as a sign he “needed support.” Without delay, she hurried to the dining-room closet to retrieve the necessary aid: a glass of Constantia wine and a homemade biscuit. It was a remarkable closet—worthy of Cloisterham and its famed Minor Canon Corner. Above it hung a portrait of Handel in a flowing wig, seeming to smile knowingly at the contents below, as though ready to harmonize every flavor into a single, sweet fugue. Unlike ordinary closets, this one had a lock suspended in midair, where two sliding panels met—one descending, the other rising. Pulling the upper panel down revealed deep shelves lined with jars of pickles, pots of jam, tin canisters, spice boxes, and exotic blue-and-white vessels holding preserved tamarinds and ginger. Each item bore its name proudly on its label. The pickles—dressed in rich brown double-breasted coats and yellow or drab skirts—announced themselves in bold print: Walnut, Gherkin, Onion, Cabbage, Cauliflower, Mixed. The jams, softer in nature and adorned with curlpapers, whispered in delicate script: Raspberry, Gooseberry, Apricot, Plum, Damson, Apple, Peach. When the lower panel rose, oranges appeared beside a grand japanned sugarbox, meant to temper their tartness. Home-made biscuits stood guard before these treasures, accompanied by a generous slice of plum cake and slender ladyfingers meant to be dipped in wine and kissed. At the very bottom, a leaden vault held the sweet wine and cordials—whispers of Seville Orange, Lemon, Almond, and Caraway-seed. The entire closet seemed infused with centuries of cathedral bells and organ music, as though the air itself had been distilled into honey. Anyone who reached deep into its shelves emerged mellow-faced, transformed by sweetness.

The Reverend Septimus submitted to this sacred cupboard as willingly as he did to another, less glorious one—the medicinal herb-closet, also overseen by the china shepherdess. There, he endured potent infusions of gentian, peppermint, gilliflower, sage, parsley, thyme, rue, rosemary, and dandelion. If his mother suspected a toothache, he’d wrap his face in layers of dried leaves, swathed like a contented child. If she detected a pimple, he’d cheerfully apply botanical patches to his cheek or forehead. This herbaceous penitentiary, tucked away on a narrow staircase landing, was a whitewashed cell where dried herbs hung from rusted hooks and sat on shelves beside mysterious bottles. Here, the Reverend Septimus would go, obedient as a lamb led to slaughter—though unlike that lamb, he harmed no one but himself. Even then, if his mother was busy and happy, he’d swallow whatever was given him, wash his hands and face in bowls of dried rose petals and lavender, and leave, confident in the healing powers of Cloisterham Weir and a sound mind—as certain as Lady Macbeth was hopeless of cleansing herself with all the seas.

That evening, the Minor Canon accepted his glass of Constantia with grace, satisfied his mother’s concern, and resumed his duties. As the day progressed in orderly fashion, Vesper Service arrived, followed by twilight. The Cathedral was cold, so he set off for a brisk walk after prayers, ending in a sprint toward his favorite fragment of ruin—an outpost he intended to storm without pause.

He reached it swiftly, breathless but triumphant, and paused to gaze down at the river. At Cloisterham, the river is near enough to the sea to carry in seaweed with each tide. An unusually large amount had washed ashore with the last surge, mingling with churning water, restless gulls flapping overhead, and a stormy light beyond the blackening barges. A tempest loomed.

In his thoughts, he contrasted the wild sea with the quiet sanctuary of Minor Canon Corner—when suddenly Helena and Neville Landless passed beneath him. He’d been thinking of them all day and immediately descended to speak with them. The footing was treacherous in the dim light, but the Minor Canon was a skilled climber and stood beside them before most could have halfway down.

“A wild evening, Miss Landless! Don’t you find your usual walk with your brother too exposed and cold for this time of year? Or at least, when the sun has set and the wind comes in from the sea?”

Helena shook her head. It was their favorite walk. Very private.

“It is very private,” agreed Mr. Crisparkle, seizing the moment. “A place where one can speak without interruption—just as I wish to do. Mr. Neville, I believe you tell your sister everything that passes between us?”

“Everything, sir.”

“Then,” he said, “your sister knows that I’ve repeatedly urged you to make some sort of apology for that unfortunate incident on the night of your arrival.”

As he spoke, he looked to Helena, not Neville—and it was she who answered:

“Yes.”

“I call it unfortunate,” Mr. Crisparkle continued, “because it has certainly bred prejudice against Neville. There’s a belief around town that he’s dangerously passionate, uncontrollable, furious—so much so that people avoid him.”

“I have no doubt they do, poor fellow,” Helena said, casting a look of proud pity at her brother. “I’d believe it just from you saying so—but your words are confirmed by subtle hints and glances I encounter daily.”

“Now,” Mr. Crisparkle said, firm but gentle, “isn’t this regrettable? Shouldn’t it be corrected? These are early days for Neville in Cloisterham, and I have no fear he’ll outlive such a reputation. But wouldn’t it be wiser to act now, rather than wait for uncertain time? And besides—apart from wisdom—it’s right. There’s no question: Neville was wrong.”

“He was provoked,” Helena offered.

“He was the aggressor,” Mr. Crisparkle countered.

They walked in silence until Helena raised her eyes to his face and said, almost reproachfully: “O Mr. Crisparkle, would you really have Neville throw himself at young Drood’s feet—or at Mr. Jasper’s, who slanders him every day? In your heart, you don’t mean it. You couldn’t ask it, even if it were your own case.”

“I’ve told Mr. Crisparkle, Helena,” Neville interjected, looking respectfully toward his tutor, “that if I could do it from the heart, I would. But I can’t. I recoil from pretense. To suggest otherwise would be mockery. My nature must change before I can make such a move—and it hasn’t. I feel an unbearable insult, and the insult is compounded. I am angry. The truth is, I am as angry now remembering that night as I was then.”

“Neville,” the Minor Canon said quietly, “you’ve repeated that gesture with your hands—the one I dislike so much.”

“I’m sorry, sir,” Neville replied, “but it was involuntary. I admitted I’m still angry.”

“And I confess,” said Mr. Crisparkle, “that I hoped for better things.”

“I’m sorry to disappoint you, sir,” Neville said, “but it would be worse to deceive you. I’d lie grossly if I pretended you’d softened me. The time may come when your influence changes me—but it hasn’t yet. Is this true, Helena? Despite my struggles?”

She watched his face closely, then turned to Mr. Crisparkle and said plainly: “It is true.” After a brief pause, she gave the faintest nod in response to his silent question—and he continued:

“I’ve never had the courage to tell you, sir, what I should have said openly when you first spoke to me about this. It’s difficult to say—and I’ve been held back by fear it might seem ridiculous. That fear is strong even now, and might have stopped me from being honest, if not for my sister.—I admire Miss Bud, sir, so deeply that I cannot bear her treated with indifference or arrogance. Even if I didn’t feel personally wronged by young Drood, I’d feel it on her behalf.”

Mr. Crisparkle stared, stunned, turning to Helena for confirmation. Her expression gave full support—and a plea for guidance.

“The young lady you speak of,” he said gravely, “is soon to be married, Mr. Neville. Therefore, if your admiration is of the nature you suggest, it is wildly misplaced. It’s monstrous to take it upon yourself to defend her against her chosen husband. Besides, you’ve only seen her once. She’s become your sister’s friend—and I wonder why your sister hasn’t checked this irrational, culpable fancy.”

“She has tried, sir,” Neville said, “but uselessly. Husband or no husband, that man is incapable of the feeling I have for her. I say he lacks it entirely—as he is unworthy of her. I say she is sacrificed. I say I love her—and despise and hate him!” His voice cracked, his face flushed, his gesture violent. Helena stepped forward, seized his arm, and pleaded: “Neville, Neville!”

Startled, he regained control, covering his face with his hand, ashamed and broken.

Mr. Crisparkle observed him closely, weighing his next words. They walked in silence for a while, then he spoke:

“Mr. Neville, I am deeply saddened to see in you the same sullenness, anger, and wildness as the storm closing in. These traits are too serious to treat your confession lightly. I take it seriously—and I speak accordingly. This feud between you and young Drood must end. I cannot allow it to continue, knowing what you’ve just revealed, and knowing you live under my roof. Whatever false and bitter conclusions your rage may draw about his character, I assure you—he is honest, kind-hearted. I trust him completely. Now, listen carefully. On reflection, and based on your sister’s account, I’m willing to concede that in making peace with young Drood, you have a right to expect him to meet you halfway. I will ensure he does—indeed, I will ask him to make the first move. Once that is done, I require you to pledge me, as a Christian gentleman, that the quarrel is ended forever on your part. What lies in your heart when you shake his hand, only God can know—but if there’s deceit there, it will bring you no good. As for your feelings toward Miss Bud—this infatuation you’ve confessed—do I understand correctly that it is known only to you, your sister, and me?”

Helena whispered: “Only us three.”

“And not to the young lady herself?”

“On my soul, no!”

“I require you, then, Mr. Neville, to give me a solemn and equal pledge—that this shall remain secret, and that you will take no action upon it except to strive—with all your strength—to erase it from your mind. I won’t pretend it will vanish quickly. I won’t call it a passing fancy. I won’t say such emotions rise and fall hourly among the young. I’ll let you believe it’s rare, lasting, hard to conquer. And because of that, the pledge you give me must be complete, unreserved, and absolute.”

The young man tried to speak several times, but failed.

“Let me leave you with your sister,” said Mr. Crisparkle. “It’s time you took her home. I’ll be alone in my room shortly.”

“Please don’t go yet,” Helena begged. “Just one more minute.”

“I wouldn’t have needed even that,” Neville said, pressing his hand to his face, “if you hadn’t been so patient, so understanding, so unpretentiously good, Mr. Crisparkle. Oh, if only I’d known such a guide in childhood!”

“Follow your guide now, Neville,” Helena murmured, “and follow him to Heaven.”

Her tone touched the Minor Canon so deeply that he could not reply. Instead, he placed a finger to his lips and looked toward her brother.

“To say I give both pledges from my deepest heart,” Neville said, trembling, “and to say there is no deceit in it—is to say nothing!” He bowed his head. “I beg your forgiveness for my lapse into passion.”

“Not mine, Neville,” the Minor Canon said softly. “Not mine. You know who holds forgiveness—above all else. Miss Helena, you and your brother are twin souls. You entered the world with the same dispositions, shaped by the same hardships. What you’ve overcome in yourself—can you not help him overcome it? You see the rock in his path. Who but you can keep him clear of it?”

“Who but you, sir?” Helena replied. “What is my influence, or my weak wisdom, compared to yours?”

“You have the wisdom of Love,” Mr. Crisparkle said, “and it was the highest wisdom ever known on earth. As for mine—let’s say little about that common commodity. Good night.”

She took his offered hand and kissed it gratefully, almost reverently.

“Tut!” he whispered. “I am much overpaid.” And he turned away.

Walking back toward the Cathedral Close in the dark, he tried to plan how to fulfill his promise. “I may be asked to marry them,” he mused. “And I’d wish they were married and gone. But this comes first.”

He debated whether to write to young Drood or speak to Jasper. His popularity within the Cathedral inclined him toward the latter—and seeing the gatehouse lighted, he decided: “I’ll strike while the iron is hot. I’ll see him now.”
