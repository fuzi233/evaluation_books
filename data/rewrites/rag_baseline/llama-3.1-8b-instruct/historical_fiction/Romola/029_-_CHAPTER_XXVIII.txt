The cobblestone streets of Florence were alive with tension, the air thick with the weight of unspoken animosity between the French invaders and the proud Florentines. As Romola navigated the winding alleys, her black veil and mantle a shield against prying eyes, she felt a sense of foreboding settle in the pit of her stomach. The streets, once a vibrant tapestry of commerce and artistry, now seemed a battleground, with each side vying for dominance.

She had been waiting for four days to visit Piero di Cosimo, the renowned painter who had promised to complete a copy of her father's portrait as Oedipus. The urgency to possess this likeness had grown within her, a desire to hold onto the memories of her father in the days of his blindness. Love, she knew, was not just about the beloved object, but also about the loyalty of one's heart. And Romola felt an unshakeable sense of duty to honor her father's memory.

As she approached the painter's studio, the scent of linseed oil and turpentine wafted through the air, transporting her to a world of creativity and artistry. She lifted the iron slide and called out to Piero in a melodious voice, her tone reminiscent of the little maiden who had once served Tito. The painter's response was swift, but his words, though polite, carried a hint of distraction.

"Ah, Madonna Romola, is it you? I thought my lunch had arrived; I was famished."

Romola smiled, her eyes sparkling with amusement, as she stepped into the studio. "I've brought you something far more nourishing than bread and cheese, Piero," she said, producing a wicker basket from beneath her cloak. "Maso has prepared a selection of sweet treats and confetti, just for you."

Piero's eyes lit up, and he unfolded his arms, his gaze lingering on the offerings. "Ah, you know how much I adore these little indulgences, when they come to me without effort," he said, his voice tinged with gratitude.

Romola's eyes met his, and for a moment, the turmoil outside receded, replaced by the warmth of their shared affection. She knew that Piero's whimsical nature made him prone to abandoning projects, and she felt an instinctive need to guide him, to ensure that her father's portrait was completed to perfection.
