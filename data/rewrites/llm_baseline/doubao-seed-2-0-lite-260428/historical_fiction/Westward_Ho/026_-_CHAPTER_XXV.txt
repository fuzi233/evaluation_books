“God will relent, and quit thee all thy debt, Who ever more approves, and more accepts Him who imploring mercy sues for life, Than who self-rigorous chooses death as due, Which argues over-just, and self-displeased For self-offence, more than for God offended.”

A fortnight of brutal, unrelenting mountain toil had passed, no harsher than the hardships this band of Devon adventurers had endured a dozen times before. Bidding a final, lingering farewell to the green sea of the eastern plains, they had scaled the jagged spine of the Cordillera, and paused long enough to glimpse the white-walled spires of Santa Fe, nestling amid lush irrigated gardens high on its sunlit plateau. It was far too large, far too heavily garrisoned a settlement for their tiny band to attack, but their time had not been wasted: their young Indigenous scout had learned word of a gold-train bound from Santa Fe down toward the Magdalena River, and the men now waited hidden beside the rutted, mud-choked track that passed for a royal road.

They camped in a stand of gnarled oaks that smelled so sharply of English woodland they might have fancied themselves back on Devon’s hills—save for the feathery tree ferns that choked the undergrowth, and the sheer thousand-foot gorges that yawned at their very feet. While the cool, temperate mountain breeze whipped their cheeks, they could peer down through banks of thick, steamy mist to spot the vivid plumage and broad fronds of the unbroken tropical rainforest far below.

Their camp was pitched among the ferns, just above a stretch of path that hugged a near-vertical cliff. Once, the road had been a marvel of Cundinamarca’s Inca civilization: paved, shored up with stone, linked by aqueducts and bridges to every corner of the mountain kingdom. But three centuries of Spanish misrule had left it little more than a narrow ditch of slipping mud and half-collapsed stone steps. The great llama flocks were gone, the gardens overgrown, the post-houses crumbled to dust. All the arts of Inca civilization had been abandoned in the mad scramble for gold, save for architecture: and that skill now only survived in the soaring Catholic cathedrals raised on the ruins of Sun-worshipper temples, where a faith that still countenanced brutal human sacrifice had replaced the gentle, nature-centered rites of the old Incas. Now, fast-spreading tropical foliage had reclaimed every inch of untended land, and Amyas Leigh and his crew were as utterly alone a few miles outside of a major Spanish settlement as they would have been in the deepest, uncharted reaches of the Orinoco.

Worse still: all their efforts to mine sulphur and nitre for gunpowder had come to nothing. To the red-faced fury of old Salvation Yeo, they were forced to rely entirely on their bows, arrows, and steel swords. It mattered not, the others laughed: Drake himself had stormed Nombre de Dios and seized its gold trains with no better weapons, and they could do the same.

They felled a massive oak across the path to block the road above, then settled back among the wild mountain flowers, chewing coca leaves to stave off hunger and thirst, while they puzzled over the low, unidentifiable roaring that had trailed them every night since they left the banks of the Meta. It was no jaguar’s snarl, no monkey’s howl: none of them could place the sound, or guess why it seemed to follow them for miles. But that mystery was trivial beside the gold train they waited for.

At last, a sharp, whipping crack echoed up from below, followed by a high, piteous wail. The crack was no snapping branch, no woodpecker’s tap. The cry was no parrot’s squawk, no monkey’s shriek.

“That was a lash of manatee hide,” Yeo breathed, his fingers tightening around his bowstring. “And a woman’s cry. They’re right on top of us, lads!”

“A woman’s? Do they drive women in their work gangs?” Amyas whispered, his jaw tight.

“Why wouldn’t those brutes?” Yeo snarled. “There they are, sir—see their helmets glint in the sun?”

“Men,” Amyas hissed to the party hidden in the underbrush, “not a shot, not an arrow, till I give the word. One volley of shafts, then draw your swords and charge. Pass the order down the line.”

The procession crept slowly up the path, and every man’s heart thundered in his chest as the first of the party rounded the bend. Twenty soldiers led the way, half of them on foot, the other half borne lazily in small wooden chairs slung across the backs of single Indigenous porters. The men who walked had slung all their heavy armor and their unwieldy arquebuses over the shoulders of enslaved attendants, who were prodded in the back with pike points every time they staggered under the weight.

“The fools have let their own weapons out of their hands,” one of the sailors muttered.

“An Indian would kneel and pray to an arquebus not to shoot him,” Yeo said with a dry snort. “They never fear their guns getting stolen, sir.”

“Look at those pompous villains,” another whispered. “They treat human beings like dumb pack animals.”

“Ten foot, ten litters,” Amyas counted quickly under his breath. “Will’s party up the top of the path can lock them in easy.”

Bringing up the rear of the soldier’s detachment was a swaggering junior officer, also borne in a litter. Every other second he pulled his cigar from his lips to hurl a string of oaths, invoking the Virgin, Saint James of Compostella, and every foul curse under the sun, the sort of profanity that had earned 16th century Spaniards the reputation of being both the most superstitious idolaters and the most shameless swearers in all of Europe.

“The blaspheming dog,” Yeo snarled, fumbling to nock an arrow, and would have loosed it straight through the man’s throat if Amyas had not caught his wrist. But the sight that rounded the bend a moment later drove even the officer’s cruelty from every man’s mind.

A line of Indigenous men, Black enslaved people, and mixed-race zambos toiled up the path, chained together at their left wrists, naked, emaciated, their skin crisscrossed with old scars from whips and iron fetters. They panted and sweated under the weight of heavy baskets strapped to their foreheads with leather thongs. Yeo had spoken no lie: there were old men, teenage boys, slender young girls, mothers with small children trotting at their knees. A low, universal murmur of righteous indignation rose from the hidden Englishmen, men who had been raised on Raleigh’s speeches that all men, no matter their skin, had a right to freedom, long before the 17th century’s terrible legacy of chattel slavery had brutalized English consciences too.

Amyas counted forty of the porters bearing baskets bound tight with oiled hide, packed so heavy they bulged. He knew that wrapping all too well.

“Captain—what’s in those packs?” a sailor whispered.

“Gold.” At that single magic word, every eye strained forward, and the whole party shifted with excitement, making so much rustle Amyas had to hiss at them under his breath, “Be men, fools, or you’ll spoil all!”

The last twenty porters carried lighter baskets, full of manioc, maize bread, and supplies for the Spanish party. Behind them came another twenty soldiers, and the commanding officer, lounging in his litter, twirling his elaborate mustaches, never dreaming a band of Englishmen waited in the trees above him. The ambush was perfect: the only question left was when to strike.

Amyas hated the thought of shooting down unsuspecting men in cold blood, even monsters as cruel as these—until one of the whip-wielding drivers lashed a staggering porter twice across the bare back, the crack of his manatee hide lash ringing out like a pistol shot. Their casus belli arrived of its own accord a moment later.

The second last man in the chained line was a gray-haired old Indian, followed close behind by his slender eighteen-year-old daughter. Just as they passed the ambush, the head of the procession rounded the upper bend, and a shout rang out: “Halt, sirs! There’s a tree felled across the path!”

“A tree across the path?” the commanding officer roared, unleashing a flood of curses against every saint and demon he could name. The line of terrified porters, told to halt from the front and beaten forward from the back, surged back and forth on the crumbling stone steps until the old gray Indian stumbled, and fell face-first in the mud.

The officer leaped down from his litter and hurried up to see what the delay was. He spotted the prostrate old man at once. “Spawn of Beelzebub! Is this a place to lie down and worship your fiendish gods?” he snarled, pricking the old man’s shoulder with the point of his sword.

The old man tried to rise, but the weight of the gold basket on his forehead was too much for him. He collapsed again, and went still. The driver lashed him twice across the loins with his whip, so hard the sound echoed off the cliff faces, but the old man did not stir.

“He’s spent, Captain,” the driver said with a careless shrug. “Been dying for three months now.”

“What’s the intendant thinking sending me out with worn-out useless cattle like this?” the officer shouted. “Clear that tree away, sirs—Pedrillo, hold the chain up!”

The driver lifted the chain fastened to the old man’s lifeless wrist. The officer stepped back, flourishing his exquisite Toledo sword, the blade so perfectly tempered Amyas was nearly tempted to steal it on the spot. He expected the man to make a show of severing the chain with a single stroke of his famous steel.

He was not prepared for the cold cruelty of the conquistador blood in that man’s veins. The blade flashed once, twice, in the mountain sunlight—and it fell not on the chain, but on the wrist it was fastened to. A girl’s shriek split the air, and a spray of crimson blood arced across the stone steps.

Before Amyas could nock his own arrow to put a shaft through the officer’s throat, the girl launched herself at the murderer with the ferocity of a wild cat, wrapping both arms around his waist, and hurled herself backward off the narrow path, dragging the shrieking officer with her over the thousand-foot drop.

The whole procession froze, every Spaniard leaning over the cliff edge. The girl dangled by her chained wrist over the void. The officer’s body had already vanished, crashing through the treetops far below.

“Pull her up! Tear her to pieces! Burn the witch!” the driver screamed, yanking hard on the chain to haul her over the edge back onto the path so they could kill her.

It was Amyas’s moment. Heaven had delivered them right into his hands. His arrow flew ten yards straight through the driver’s chest, and he gave a roar like a leaping lion as he burst from the underbrush, charging straight into the middle of the stunned Spanish party.

His first thought was the girl. With one heave of his broad shoulders, he hauled her to safety off the cliff edge, while the Spaniards stumbled back in terror, half convinced he was some mountain giant come to eat them. Their shouts of “English! Lutheran dogs!” rose too late. The rest of the Devon men charged behind him, a storm of arrows felling five Spaniards dead and wounding a dozen more, then Salvation Yeo came bounding down the path, his white hair flying behind him, leading twenty more good Englishmen with drawn swords.

The Spaniards fought like madmen, but they had no room on the narrow path to set up their arquebuses, no space to wield their long pikes. The English held the high ground, and five minutes of brutal, desperate fighting left no living Spaniard standing on the stone steps. Only two men in full armor at the very back of the procession escaped mortal wounds, fleeing down the hill at a dead run.

“After them! Michael Evans, Simon Heard—run them down, even if they flee a league!” Amyas shouted. The two long, lean Clovelly men, who had spent their whole youth chasing deer through the forests, covered two feet for every one the Spaniards took, and came back ten minutes later to say the job was done. The rest of Amyas’s party raced up the path to join Cary’s small detachment that had been guarding the fallen oak, where shouts and musket fire told of a brief skirmish. Their arrival ended the fight at once: all the remaining Spaniards were cut down save three who scrambled down the cliff face, only to be picked off by arrows before they could reach the shelter of the trees below.

“Cut their chains,” Amyas ordered, passing around the armorers’ tools they had looted off a dead Spanish soldier. “We are your friends. All we ask is that you help us carry this gold down to the Magdalena River, and then every last one of you is free.”

A few of the younger porters fell to their knees, kissing Amyas’s boots and hailing him as a child of the Sun. Most of the rest were too broken by decades of cruelty, overwork, and abuse to show any reaction at all: they simply sat down where they stood, staring into empty space, the horrors they had endured having burned every last spark of hope or joy out of them.

Only the young girl who had dragged the officer over the cliff moved. As soon as her own chains were cut, she ran to her father’s body, lifted it in her thin, emaciated arms, laid it across her knees, and kissed his cold lips, stroking his furrowed cheeks, making soft cooing sounds no one but her dead father could understand. A single long, heaving sob shook her frame. She stood up, still holding the old man’s corpse tight against her chest, and leaped off the cliff without a sound.

The men watched her dark, slender limbs wrapped tight around her father’s body as they turned over and over through the air, until a distant crash in the treetops told them she had reached the forest below, and the green foliage hid her from sight.

“Brave lass,” a sailor muttered, crossing himself.

“The Lord have mercy on her soul,” Yeo said. “But your worship—we’ve got all those Spaniards’ guns and powder to collect.”

“And their uniforms too, Yeo, if we want to sail down the Magdalena unchallenged, even past the gates of Santa Martha itself,” Amyas said, turning to address his whole crew. “By God’s grace, we’ve won more gold than any of us will ever need for the rest of our lives, and we haven’t lost a single man. But remember Oxenham and his crew, lads—don’t let greed or mutiny throw this gift from God away.”

“You can trust us, Captain!” the men shouted back. “We know you’ll lead us right.”

“Thank God for that,” Amyas said. “We won’t burden the women porters with heavy packs, no man of us will do that. But we need those Spanish uniforms to disguise ourselves to get past the settlements. Who’s going to volunteer to climb down the cliff and bring up all the armor, weapons, and supplies off the Spaniards who fell over the edge?”

A dozen men stepped forward at once, as they always did when Amyas asked for volunteers—for they knew he would be the first one climbing down the cliff to do the work alongside them. He left John Brimblecombe behind with the Indigenous scout to talk to the stunned freed porters and reassure them they would never be harmed again.

“Wait—who’s that coming up the road?”

Every head turned. There, trudging up the path, her feather travel clothes tattered and smudged from two weeks in the woods, blowgun in her hand and her bow slung over her back, was Ayacanora. The whole party stood utterly stunned. When she spotted Amyas, she gave a cry of joy, ran as fast as her legs could carry her, and collapsed panting and exhausted at his feet.

“I found you!” she gasped. “You ran away from me, but you could never escape me!” She fawned around Amyas like a loyal hound that had found its long-lost master, then sank down on a rock at the side of the path and burst into wild, heaving sobs.

“God help us,” Amyas groaned, clutching at his hair. “What am I going to do with her, on top of all these poor people?” There was no time to argue, though. He scrambled down the cliff with the rest of the volunteers, and Ayacanora sat on her rock, watching him work, never moving an inch.

Half an hour later, all the weapons, powder, armor, and Spanish uniforms were hauled up the cliff, bundled up to carry down the mountain. The remaining Spanish corpses were tossed over the edge, and the whole party set off for the Magdalena, Yeo snorting like a war horse at the smell of gunpowder he had thought they would never recover.

“We could go back and take Santa Fe now, sir, easy!” he cheered.

Amyas laughed and shook his head, saying enough was as good as a feast. The freed porters trundled behind them, still so stunned they half-expected a lash across their backs at every step, their surprise growing when they were given small handfuls of coca leaves to chew, no demands made on them beyond carrying the light gold packs. When the party paused for one last look at the site of their victory, they saw lines of vultures already circling down through the sky, and knew that in a few hours, the only trace of the fight would be a handful of white bones half-buried in mountain wildflowers.

At last Amyas had time to ask Ayacanora why she had followed them. She told him, through her broken, halting Spanish, how she had tracked them day and night since they left her village, making loud, deep noises every evening, hoping they would hear her and wait. Amyas suddenly realized that the mysterious roaring that had followed them for two weeks was her.

“What were you making those noises with?” he asked.

Ayacanora grinned, drew herself up with a mysterious, proud air, and pulled a two-foot long glazed earthen tube from under her feather cloak, painted with ancient animal figures and patterns, a relic of a long-forgotten civilization. “The holy trumpet!” she whispered, as if afraid it would hear her talking.
