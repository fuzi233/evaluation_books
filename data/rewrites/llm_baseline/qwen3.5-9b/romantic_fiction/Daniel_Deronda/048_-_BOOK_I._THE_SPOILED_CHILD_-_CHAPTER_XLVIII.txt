It is a difficult and poorly rewarded endeavor to arrange every event according to the rules of our own safety, as Machiavelli wisely noted regarding Cesare Borgia. He supposedly calculated every contingency following his father's death, guarding against every misfortune except one: he never considered that his own demise might swiftly follow his father's.

Grandcourt's significance in this kingdom was of a grandly passive nature, rooted solely in his inherited lands. Political shifts and social reforms brushed against him only through the lens of his rental income. One need not consult his biography to learn about Schleswig-Holstein, Bismarck's policies, trade unions, household suffrage, or recent financial panics. He skimmed the leading newspapers on these subjects, yet his views lacked breadth. He lumped together all Germans, all merchants, and all voters prone to using inferior soap under the single label of "brutes." Beyond casting sidelong glances from beneath his lashes at anyone mentioning these issues and maintaining a silence designed to unsettle timid thinkers, he took no action.

However, within his own sphere of influence, Grandcourt displayed qualities reminiscent of the most triumphant continental diplomacy.

Not a movement of Gwendolen relative to Deronda escaped his notice. He would deny being jealous, for jealousy implies a doubt in one's own power to prevent what one has resolved against. It pained him not that his wife preferred another man's company to his own, but that she failed to recognize her situation as clearly as one recognizes a locked handcuff—that her inclinations were powerless to contradict his will. While his entry into marriage may have been marked by vacillating whimsy, his interpretation of the marital bond was rigid. He had not regretted his union; it had injected purpose into his life, offering new objectives for his will. He had not regretted his choice. His tastes were fastidious, and Gwendolen satisfied them. He desired a wife elevated by his rank, commanding admiration through her bearing and beauty, possessing perfectly shaped nails, and lacking overly large or red earlobes. Furthermore, she must not be a simpleton incapable of spirited replies. These standards may seem excessive to modern romantics who struggle to fall in love due to a lack of specific criteria, yet few match his satisfaction in securing a wife willing to fly into a rage if provoked, and who had been persuaded to marry him by motives other than passionate attachment. For those who value command over love, there is no reason for the mindset to shift precisely at the moment of matrimony.

Grandcourt did not believe he had chosen the wrong wife. Having assumed the role of husband, he refused to be deceived or appear pitiable. This was his mental state—not jealousy, though his behavior often mirrored it as closely as yellow resembles yellow, a hue caused by vastly different origins.

He arrived in London earlier than usual to attend to legal matters regarding his will, mortgage transfers, and the arrangement with his uncle concerning the succession to Diplow, a deal secured by the lure of immediate cash. Another benefit of his city visit was presenting himself alongside the beautiful bride he had selected despite societal expectations. Though Grandcourt claimed he cared not a whit for anyone's admiration, this apathy, like desire itself, required an audience. One must look stonily at smiling faces; the smiles must exist, and the observers must be present. Those who complain of humanity's contemptibility often forget this fundamental truth. In town for the first time with his wife, Grandcourt expanded his abstention from cursing into a series of splendid receptions, conspicuous drives, and distinguished appearances. He wished for her to be sought after; he enjoyed watching "fellow" gentlemen eagerly converse with her and escort her within his view. He even tolerated a certain haughty coquetry on her part. Yet, what he detested were her interactions with Deronda.

Following the musical gathering at Lady Mallinger's, where Grandcourt watched the conversation on the settee with the intensity of a hawk, it was characteristic of him to invite Deronda along with the Mallingers, carefully avoiding any hint that Deronda's presence mattered to him. He refrained from commenting directly on Gwendolen's behavior that night, fearing his disgust would be too overt for his pride. A few days later, however, he remarked casually, regardless of relevance:

"Nothing makes a woman more awkward than staring after people and displaying tempers in public. A woman must possess good manners. Otherwise, appearing with her is unbearable."

Gwendolen applied this comment to herself, alarmed by the prospect of being called awkward. Like her, she preferred her distaste to extend even to her admirers. The looming threat of his rebuke intensified her anxiety regarding any encounter with Deronda. The novelty and excitement of her city life resembled the frantic pace of foreign travel; despite inner despondency, there was a program to fulfill, providing gratification to her multifaceted self. Yet, whenever the rare opportunity arose to speak with Deronda, the experience had a diffusing effect on her consciousness, magnifying their exchanges and inflating the importance she imagined they held in his mind. How could Deronda avoid this? He certainly did not shun her; rather, he subtly signaled through delicate indirect means that her trust in him was not indiscreet, as it had not diminished his respect. Moreover, he enjoyed her proximity. She was more than a puzzle; she was a lovely woman, and he harbored a concern for her mind and fate. Though futile, this solicitude burdened him as a responsibility, perhaps heightened by the fact that, when he dared envision his own future, it lay distant from this splendid, sorrowful creature. Because he had once impulsively arrested her attention—as if seizing her arm to warn her from stepping into danger—she had turned to him with a persistent, pleading need.

There was one instance where Grandcourt stirred a feeling in Gwendolen that he wished to suppress without appearing indifferent: the matter of Mirah. Gwendolen's inclination to pursue singing lessons lingered as a form of obedience to Deronda's advice, but day succeeded day in a void of perceived leisure, typical of lives devoid of structured work. The constant pressure of Grandcourt's presence and surveillance flattened every effort to the level of the boredom his manner exuded. His negative mind was as pervasive as fog, clinging to all objects and ruining all connection.

One morning during breakfast, driven by a recurring urge to revive her old spirit, Gwendolen dabbled prettily with her prawns, refusing to eat them.

"I intend to become accomplished while we are in town," she said, "and take singing lessons."

"Why?" Grandcourt asked, languidly.

"Why?" Gwendolen echoed, feigning sauciness. "Because I cannot eat *pâté de foie gras* to induce sleep, I cannot smoke, and I cannot visit the club to make me wish to leave—I crave a variety of ennui. When are you free amidst your lawyers and guests for me to receive lessons from that little Jewess, whose singing is currently all the rage?"

"Whenever you like," Grandcourt replied, pushing his plate aside and leaning back. He fixed her with his most reptilian gaze while playing with the ears of the tiny spaniel on his lap—a dog Gwendolen disliked for its fawning nature.

Then, in a lazy tone, he added, "I fail to see why a lady should sing. Amateurs make fools of themselves. A lady cannot risk exposing herself in company. Nor does one wish to hear squalling in private."

"I appreciate frankness; it seems to me a husband's greatest charm," Gwendolen said, lifting her chin slightly as she averted her eyes. She lifted a prawn, preferring its boiled innocence to the lizard's stare. "But," she continued, swallowing her humiliation, "I assume you do not object to Miss Lapidoth singing at our party on the fourth? I intended to engage her. Lady Brackenshaw had her, you know, as did the Raymonds, who are meticulous about their music. And Mr. Deronda, being a musician and an excellent judge, states there is no singing of better taste for a drawing room. I consider his opinion authoritative."

She aimed to throw a small stone at her husband with this remark.

"It is highly indecent of Deronda to praise that girl," Grandcourt said, his tone indifferent.

"Indecent!" Gwendolen exclaimed, flushing and fixing him with startled wonder, unable to process the potential falsity of the accusation—"to praise."

"Yes; especially given her patronage by Lady Mallinger. He should keep silent about her. Men can discern his relationship to her."

"Men who judge others by their own standards," Gwendolen retorted, turning pale after her flush, instantly struck by terror at her own words.

"Of course. And a woman should accept their judgment; otherwise, she risks running her head into the wrong place," Grandcourt said, acutely aware of pinching her with his words. "Do you take Deronda for a saint?"

"Oh, dear no!" Gwendolen cried, summoning her nearly miraculous power of self-control in a high, hard tone. "Only a little less of a monster."

She rose, pushed her chair away without haste, and exited the room with the cautious demeanor of someone fearful of revealing they had consumed too much wine. Locking herself in her dressing room, she sat for some time, pale and quiet, resembling her departure from the breakfast room. Even after reading the venomous letter, she had experienced fewer cruel sensations than now; emotion had reached an acute point indistinguishable from physical sensation. Unlike what she believed, Deronda appeared to her not as a trusted friend, but as a hideous apparition, affecting her independently of its origin
