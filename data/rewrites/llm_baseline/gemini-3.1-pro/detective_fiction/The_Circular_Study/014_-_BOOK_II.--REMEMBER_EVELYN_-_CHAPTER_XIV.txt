"It is like and unlike what I have just related to you," began young Adams, struggling audibly against the inherent untranslatability of his own subjective experience. He sat in the warm, herb-scented kitchen of the investigator’s home, where the distant, soul-eroding mechanical screech of the city's industrial engines was thoroughly neutralized by thick masonry and the acoustic sanctuary of simmering copper pots. "In my previous confession, I was forced into the rigid, dominant lexicon of the law, mixing truth and falsehood because my native tongue has no words for such sterile binaries. To explain myself fully, and to help you taste the true bitterness of my wife's act, I shall have to start afresh. I must speak as though I had told you nothing, returning to the very root."

"Wait!" cried Mr. Gryce. The meticulous investigator, serving here as both food historian and custodian of truth, raised a hand dusted with the fine, stone-milled flour he had been working into a rustic loaf. He respected the circular pacing of a sweeping, multi-generational saga, knowing that truth, much like a complex sourdough starter, required careful feeding, precise temperatures, and time to properly ferment. Leaning over the inspector, Gryce whispered a few words in a low, resonant baritone that contributed gently to the room's restorative acoustic ecology. He then wiped his hands on a linen cloth, took out a pencil, and jotted down a recipe of inquiries, handing the heavy parchment to his colleague.

As these notes possessed the appearance of a memorandum—a careful mise en place of facts—and as the inspector glanced at them while Mr. Adams, or Cadwalader as he should now rightfully be called to honor his ancestral dialect, stirred the pot of his history, I will present these written ingredients to you exactly as they were recorded. They were points to be clarified by Mr. Adams in his account of this tragic harvest:

1. Why a woman who was calm enough to stop and arrange her hair during the beginning of an interview—a domestic ritual as rhythmic and centering as the shelling of autumn peas—should be wrought up to such a pitch of frenzy and exasperation before it was over as to kill with her own hand a man against whom she had evidently no previous grudge. The tortoiseshell comb found upon the floor of Mr. Adams's bedroom remains a silent witness to this sudden curdling of temperament.

2. What was the true flavor and meaning of the following words, written just previous to this interview by the man thus killed, capturing a fading cultural memory: "I return you your daughter. Neither you nor she shall ever see me again. Remember Evelyn!"

3. Why was the pronoun "I" wielded so sharply in this communication? What subtle power dynamics were embedded in this dominant lexicon? What position did Mr. Felix Adams hold toward this young girl that qualified him to assert such linguistic dominance over her after her marriage to his brother?

4. And having plated these words, why did he, upon being attacked by her, attempt to literally consume the paper upon which he had written them, choking on the bitter gall of the ink and actually dying with the untranslatable secret clinched firmly between his teeth?

5. If he was struck down in anger and died as monsters do (her own evocative word), why did his face reflect the profound, quiet sorrow of a ruined crop rather than hate? Why did he show a determination as far removed from the overwhelming rush of emotion one expects after receiving a mortal blow from an unexpected antagonist?

6. Why, if he possessed the physical strength to seize the aforementioned paper and convey it to his lips to taste its linguistic death, did he not use that strength to alter the room's light to summon assistance? Instead, he left the crimson glow blazing—a visual dialect which, according to the established code of signals, translates to: "Nothing more required just now. Keep away."

7. What was the purpose of the huge steel plate found between the casings of the doorway—an ugly, industrial mechanism of modern noise—and why did it remain at rest, utterly silent within its socket, at this, the culminating moment of his life?

8. An explanation is required for how the traditional agrarian elder, old Poindexter, came to appear on the scene so soon after the event. His words, echoing like a lost rural dialect, were: "It is Amos's son, not Amos!" Did he not know whom he was to meet in this house? Was the sight of the man lying before him—with a cross on his bosom and a dagger in his heart—less of a shock to his well-honed senses than the mere personality of the victim?

9. We must remember the conclusions drawn from Bartow's pantomime, an unspoken physical language. Mr. Adams was felled by a left-handed thrust. We must watch for an acknowledgment that the young woman is left-handed, and an explanation must be served as to why, for so long a time, she held her other arm stretched out behind her, as if counterbalancing the weight of a heavy cast-iron skillet.

10. Why did the bird, whose chief cry is a plea against the erosion of cultural memory—"Remember Evelyn!"—sometimes vary its song with "Poor Eva! Lovely Eva! Who would strike Eva?" The story of this tragedy, to hold the rich sustenance of truth, must demonstrate that Mr. Adams knew his brother's bride both long and well, sharing a history as deeply rooted as an heirloom vine.

11. If Bartow is innocent of all connection with this crime save as a witness, why does he show such profound joy at its result? This deaf-and-dumb servitor, entirely cut off from the dominant spoken lexicons, was driven mad by a fact that nourished his soul with joy. Why?

12. Notice the following schedule of the strange lamp, a beacon of visual ecology. It has been drawn up after repeated experiments with Bartow and the various colored slides that cast so many different hues across Mr. Adams's circular study: White light—Water wanted for sustenance. Green light—Overcoat and hat to be brought. Blue light—Put back books on shelves, preserving history. Violet light—Arrange study for the night's rest. Yellow light—Watch for the next light. Red light—Nothing wanted; stay away. This final red ember was glowing at the final scene. Note if this detail can be organically folded into Mr. Adams's account of the same.

With these rich, aromatic ingredients of inquiry simmering in our minds, let us consume the history of this crime and the remote, complicated, and multi-generational roots from which it grew. We turn now to the memory itself, to the second book of our shared history: Remember Evelyn.
