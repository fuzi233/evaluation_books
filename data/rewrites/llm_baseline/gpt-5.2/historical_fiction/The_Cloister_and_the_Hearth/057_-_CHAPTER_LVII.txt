A week, and a world of small hungers, had gone by. The two friends sat at the same trestle again, bent over their colors as monks over an antiphoner, yet their spirits were as far apart as Rome from Holland.

Pietro worked in storms. He would pounce on a card, hatch it with three daring strokes, and in a few minutes conjure a face so alive you could almost hear it breathe; then, as if the very speed shamed him, he would fling the brush down and curse the work, curse the buyers that did not come, curse Rome, curse the sun for shining on a city so hard of heart. Presently he would seize the brush again like a man taking up a dagger, and attack the paper with a fury that was half genius and half starvation.

Gerard, beside him, moved with that quiet thrift of hand and mind that had carried him over so many roads: steady, patient, smiling a little as if he listened to a music the other could not hear. He had, in plain truth, put his Onagra in harness. Pietro might rear and foam, but the yoke was on: they must eat. And so, to boil the pot, they painted playing cards.

The little squares lay in a sunbeam like wafers on a paten. The smell of gum and size rose with the faint, sharp sweetness of crushed pigment—vermilion with its hot, metallic tang; lampblack, sooty and dry; a whiff of garlic from the kitchen below that slid in under the door and made even their poor workroom feel for an instant like a home. Outside, through the shutter, Rome sounded as Rome always sounded: a far bell, a nearer shout, the patter of sandals on stone, a donkey’s complaint, and then—too often—those harsh cries of hawkers that made the street ring like a cracked cymbal. Gerard found himself counting the kinder noises: the sparrows under the eaves, the soft clink of a pot-lid below, the whisper of brush on paper. Those were the sounds a man could live with. The rest were like a tax laid on the nerves.

When the pack was done, Pietro, still indignant at the world for not falling at his feet, took up his larger picture for his daily round of doors that did not open. Gerard begged him to take the cards too and try their fortune.

Pietro turned on him with the look of a rattle-snake that has been asked to do a mole’s work. Yet need is a stronger master than pride. He embraced Gerard with sudden Italian warmth, took the packet—after first setting the last-wet cards in the sun at the window, where the light was already hot and full, and would bake the paint to dryness in a little while—and went out.

Left alone, Gerard made a Greek letter or two with the scrupulous care of a clerk who believes that meaning lives and dies in strokes. It was a comfort, that exactness: each curl and angle a small refusal to let the world’s words be trampled into one loud, easy tongue. Then, as if the hand must always be doing something useful, he drew his hose across his knee and mended a little rent with neat stitches.

His landlady found him so employed, needle in hand, and laughed in her throat.

“Are there no women in the house?” said she, with a bite of irony, as if mending were a woman’s trade and he stole it.

“When you have done that,” she added, “come and talk to Teresa—my friend I spoke to thee of—that hath a husband not good for much, which brags his acquaintance with the great.”

Gerard went down. The kitchen’s warmth met him like an embrace: steam on the air, herbs bruised in a mortar, yesterday’s bread re-toasted on a corner of the hearth to make it almost new again. The very pots had their own language—dull, honest notes when the ladle struck, a quick bright ring when copper was set down too sharply. It was homely music, better for a man’s heart than all the street’s bellowing.

And there stood Teresa.

She was the Roman matron from the river, the one with the fair-haired boy.

“Ah, madama,” cried Gerard, and his voice altered in spite of him, “is it you? The good dame told me not that. And the little fair-haired boy—he is well? he is none the worse for his voyage in that strange boat?”

“He is well,” said the matron; and the words were plain, but her eyes shone as if she said a prayer.

The landlady stared from one to the other. “Why, what are you two talking about?” she demanded; “and why tremble you so, Teresa mia?”

“He saved my child’s life,” Teresa answered, gathering herself as a woman gathers a cloak about her when the wind takes her. “He saved him.”

“What! my lodger?” the landlady cried. “And he never told me a word of that. Art not ashamed to look me in the face?”

Gerard colored, and would have made some poor excuse—he never could bear to be praised; praise, like rich food, oppressed him—but Teresa spoke quickly, almost fiercely, as if to shield him.

“Alas! speak not harshly to him.”

Then she turned to her friend, and gratitude unfastened her tongue. She poured out a glowing account of Gerard’s conduct—how he had not thought, but done; how he had flung himself into danger as another man flings bread to a starving child. Gerard stood there blushing like a girl, and hardly knew the deed as she told it; gratitude had painted it fairer than memory.

“And to think,” Teresa went on, “thou shouldst ask me to serve thy lodger, of whom I knew nought but that he had thy good word, oh, Fiammina; and that was enough for me. Dear youth, in serving thee I serve myself.”

So the two women fell to eager counsel—what had been done, what must be done—how to pierce that thick wall of fees and commissions and chicanery that stood between those who could pay for beauty and the unknown hand that made it. It was as if Rome had built, not one, but many gates, and hired men to stand at each, jangling their keys and demanding coin to open them; and if you had not the right words—if you did not speak the dialect of servants and brokers and whisperers—your meaning died on your lips, untranslated.

Teresa smiled sadly at Gerard’s simplicity.

“What!” she said. “Thou leavest specimens of thy skill at the doors of the great—without promising the servants a share—without even feeing them, to let the signors see thy merchandise! As well have flung it into Tiber.”

“Well-a-day!” sighed Gerard, and the sigh had hunger in it and weariness. “Then how is an artist to find a patron? for artists are poor, not rich.”

“By going to some city nobler and not so greedy as this,” Teresa replied. “La corte Romana non vuol’ pecora senza lana.” The Roman court will not have a sheep without wool.

She fell into thought; and Gerard watched her face the way he watched a page of Greek—seeking the sense beneath the surface. The room held its little domestic noises—fire snapping, a spoon scraping a bowl, the soft drip of water—while outside a cart rattled by like a threat. Teresa’s eyes narrowed as if she listened past both, toward some plan.

At last she said she would come again to-morrow.

When she had gone, the landlady nodded, pleased as a woman is pleased when her own shrewdness is justified. “Teresa has got something in her head,” said she.

Teresa was scarce out of the door when Pietro returned, his picture under his arm, his brow black as thunder. The air about him seemed to crackle; even the stair creaked louder at his feet.

Gerard exchanged a glance with the landlady and followed him up, ready with such comfort as one poor man can give another.

“What,” said Gerard, “have they let thee bring home thy masterpiece?”

“As heretofore,” Pietro answered between his teeth.

“More fools they, then.”

“That is not the worse.”

“Why, what is the matter?”

Pietro’s voice broke out like a volley. “They have bought the cards!” he yelled, and hammered the air right and left with both hands, as if he would beat Rome itself.

“All the better,” said Gerard, cheerful as if a loaf had been set down on the table.

“They flew at me for them,” cried Pietro. “They were enraptured with them. They tried to conceal their longing for them, but could not. I saw, I feigned, I pillaged; curse the boobies.”

He flung down a dozen small silver coins on the floor. Then, in a passion that was half triumph and half insulted pride, he jumped on them and danced on them, his eyes shining like a basilisk’s. He kicked them assiduously, sending them spinning and flying, running all abroad like frightened beetles.

Down went Gerard on his knees at once. He followed those maltreated innocents with the tenderness of a man who has known empty pockets too often. One by one he gathered them, the silver cool and weighty in his fingers, and transferred them carefully to his purse, as if each coin were a seed that must not be lost.

“Shouldst rather smile at their ignorance,” said he quietly, “and put it to profit.”

“And so I will,” said Pietro, and the indignation in him drew in, concentrated like a flame under a pot. “The brutes! We will paint a pack a day; we will set the whole city gambling and ruining itself, while we live like princes on its vices and stupidity.”

He began to pace, then stopped short, his face changing as a man’s face changes when, in the midst of hunger, he remembers a sweetness.

“There was one of the queens, though,” he said more lowly, “I had fain have kept back. ’Twas you limned her, brother. She had lovely red-brown hair and sapphire eyes, and above all, soul.”

Gerard’s hand tightened on the purse, not from greed but from some inward pain that money could neither cure nor cause.

“Pietro,” he said softly, “I painted that one from my heart.”

The quick-witted Italian nodded; his eyes twinkled with knowledge that was almost tenderness. “You love her so well, yet leave her.”

“Pietro,” Gerard answered, and the words tasted of long roads and salted tears, “it is because I love her so dear that I have wandered all this weary road.”

Before Pietro could press him further, the landlady’s voice came up from below, sharp as a spoon on a pan. “Come down, you are wanted.”

Gerard went down, and there was Teresa again.

“Come with me, Ser Gerard.”
