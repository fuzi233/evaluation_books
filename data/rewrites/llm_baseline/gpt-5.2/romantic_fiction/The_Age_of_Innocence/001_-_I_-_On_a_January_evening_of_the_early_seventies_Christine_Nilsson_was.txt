On a January evening in the early seventies, when the snow in New York lay bruised and shiny as fish-skin under the lamps, Christine Nilsson was singing in *Faust* at the Academy of Music. There was already talk—always the talk, in drawing-rooms warmed by coal and confidence—of a new Opera House to be raised “above the Forties,” grand as any European temple. Yet fashion still returned, winter after winter, to the Academy’s worn red-and-gold boxes as to an inherited sideboard: nicked at the edges, inconvenient to open, but valued precisely because it kept the wrong hands from the silver.

Conservatives praised the place for being small and awkward; its narrow corridors and cramped tiers were a kind of social sieve, straining out the “new people” whom New York had begun to fear even as it leaned, hungry and curious, toward their glitter. The sentimental clung to its history; the musical to its sound. In an age when so much was built to impress the eye—marble, gilt, and gaslight—acoustics remained an older, rarer integrity, the one virtue you could not purchase by simply enlarging the room. Here, at least, a voice could travel cleanly, without being chewed up by echo or smothered by upholstery. The Academy held the human note the way a good kitchen holds aroma: not by trapping it, but by giving it the right walls to bloom against.

Madame Nilsson’s first appearance that winter had drawn what the papers liked to call “an exceptionally brilliant audience,” and the streets outside had become a slow, snorting procession of private broughams, family landaus, and the humbler but cleverer Brown coupés. To arrive by Brown was almost an honor in itself—one purchased by convenience rather than lineage—and leaving by Brown had the added advantage of letting you scramble into the first conveyance in line with a teasing nod to democratic principles, instead of waiting for your own coachman’s cold-and-gin congested nose to gleam under the portico. The livery-stableman who invented that system had understood something essential about Americans: they want to get away from pleasure even faster than they want to reach it.

When Newland Archer opened the door at the back of the club box, the curtain had just risen on the garden scene. He might easily have been earlier; he had dined at seven, alone with his mother and sister, and had lingered afterward with a cigar in the Gothic library—glazed black-walnut bookcases, finial-topped chairs—the only room where Mrs. Archer permitted smoke. The house, like the society it served, had its rules for air as strict as its rules for speech.

But New York was a metropolis now, and metropolises had their ritual delays. It was “not the thing” to arrive early at the opera; and what was or was not “the thing” governed Newland Archer’s world with a power as unquestioned as any ancient taboo. He told himself this was sophistication, not superstition; yet the result was the same: one moved according to signals one had been trained to hear and trained not to name.

There was, too, a more personal reason for his lateness. He had dawdled because he was, at heart, a dilettante; anticipating a pleasure often gave him a finer satisfaction than consuming it. Some men were greedy in the blunt way—grasping at women, money, applause—but Newland’s appetite was for the delicacy itself: the first taste, the first scent lifted from a covered dish, the sensation of knowing something exquisite was about to happen. And on this occasion, the moment he had been waiting for was so exact in its timing that, had he arranged his arrival with the prima donna’s stage-manager, he could not have entered more significantly than just as Nilsson was singing:

“He loves me—he loves me not—*he loves me!*”

She sang, of course, “*M’ama…*” and not “he loves me,” since an unalterable law of the musical world required that the German text of a French opera, sung by a Swedish artist, must be translated into Italian for the clearer understanding of English-speaking audiences. It seemed natural enough to Newland, as natural as the duties that shaped his own mornings: to part his hair with two silver-backed brushes bearing his monogram in blue enamel, and never to appear in society without a flower—preferably a gardenia—in his buttonhole. Convention, like a sauce repeated without question, came to be accepted as the only possible flavor.

“*M’ama… non m’ama…*” Nilsson sang, her notes falling as clean as dew shaken from a leaf, and then “*M’ama!*” with love triumphant as she pressed the ragged daisy to her lips and lifted her large eyes to the sophisticated countenance of the little brown Faust—Capoul—who, in tight purple velvet and a plumed cap, vainly tried to look as pure as his victim.

Leaning back against the wall at the rear of the box, Newland turned from the stage to scan the opposite side of the house. Directly facing him was the box of old Mrs. Manson Mingott, whose monstrous obesity had long since made it impossible for her to attend the opera in person, though she was always represented on nights that mattered by younger members of her clan, as a great estate is represented by its stewards. Tonight the front of the box was filled by her daughter-in-law, Mrs. Lovell Mingott, and her daughter, Mrs. Welland; and slightly withdrawn behind these brocaded matrons sat a young girl in white, her eyes held raptly on the stage-lovers.

As Nilsson’s “*M’ama!*” thrilled over the hushed house—because the boxes always stopped talking during the Daisy Song—a warm pink rose in the girl’s cheek, spread to her brow beneath the roots of her fair braids, and suffused the young slope of her breast down to the modest tulle tucker fastened with a single gardenia. She dropped her eyes to the immense bouquet of lilies-of-the-valley on her knee, and Newland saw her white-gloved fingertips brush the bells softly, as though testing their coolness through kid.

He drew a breath of satisfied vanity and turned his gaze back to the stage. The setting—no expense spared—was acknowledged to be beautiful even by those who spoke familiarly of Paris and Vienna. The foreground up to the footlights was covered with emerald-green cloth. In the middle distance, symmetrical mounds of woolly green moss, bounded by croquet hoops, formed the base of shrubs shaped like orange-trees but studded with large pink and red roses. Gigantic pansies, larger than the roses and resembling the floral pen-wipers made by female parishioners for fashionable clergymen, sprang from the moss beneath; and here and there a daisy grafted onto a rose-branch flowered with a luxuriance that seemed to promise Mr. Luther Burbank’s far-off prodigies.

In the center of this enchanted garden stood Nilsson in white cashmere slashed with pale blue satin, a reticule dangling from a blue girdle, and large yellow braids disposed on either side of her muslin chemisette. She listened with downcast eyes to Capoul’s impassioned wooing and affected a guileless incomprehension whenever, by word or glance, he indicated the ground-floor window of the neat brick villa projecting obliquely from the right wing.

“The darling,” Newland thought, his glance darting back to the young girl with the lilies-of-the-valley. “She doesn’t even guess what it’s all about.”

He contemplated her absorbed face with a thrill of possessorship in which pride in his own masculine initiation mingled with a tender reverence for her abysmal purity. “We’ll read *Faust* together… by the Italian lakes…” he thought, confusing—pleasantly, lazily—the future honeymoon with the masterpieces of literature it would be his privilege to unfold to his bride. Only that afternoon May Welland had let him guess that she “cared”—New York’s consecrated phrase of maiden avowal—and already his imagination had leaped ahead of the engagement ring, the betrothal kiss, and the bridal march from *Lohengrin*, placing her at his side in some scene of old European witchery.

He did not want the future Mrs. Newland Archer to be a simpleton. He meant her, thanks to his enlightening companionship, to develop a social tact and readiness of wit that would let her hold her own among the most popular married women of the “younger set,” in which it was the recognized custom to attract masculine homage while playfully discouraging it. If he had probed the bottom of his own vanity—as he sometimes nearly did—he would have found there the wish that his wife should be as worldly-wise and as eager to please as the married lady whose charms had held his fancy through two mildly agitated years, without, of course, any hint of the frailty that had so nearly marred that unhappy being’s life and had disarranged his own plans for a whole winter.

How such a miracle of fire and ice was to be created, and sustain itself in a harsh world, he had never taken the time to work out. It was enough to desire it, as one desires the perfect dish without troubling over the labor in the kitchen. Besides, he knew the wish was shared by the carefully brushed, white-waistcoated, buttonhole-flowered gentlemen who succeeded one another in the club box, exchanging greetings with him and then turning their opera-glasses on the circle of ladies produced by the system.

In matters intellectual and artistic, Newland felt himself distinctly superior to these chosen specimens of old New York gentility. He had probably read more, thought more, and even seen more of the world than any man among them. Alone, each betrayed his inferiority; together they represented “New York.” And the habit of masculine solidarity made him accept their doctrine on all issues called moral. He instinctively felt that, in this respect, it would be troublesome—and also rather bad form—to strike out for himself.

“Well—upon my soul!” exclaimed Lawrence Lefferts, turning his opera-glass abruptly away from the stage.

Lefferts was the foremost authority on “form” in New York. He had devoted more time than anyone to that intricate and fascinating subject, yet study alone could not explain his ease. From the slant of his bald forehead and the curve of his beautiful fair moustache down to the long patent-leather feet at the other end of his lean, elegant person, he wore correctness as if it were skin. A young admirer had once said: “If anybody can tell a fellow just when to wear a black tie with evening clothes and when not to, it’s Larry Lefferts.” On the question of pumps versus patent-leather Oxfords, his authority was law.

“My God!” he added, and silently handed his glass to old Sillerton Jackson.

Newland followed Lefferts’s stare and saw that the exclamation had been occasioned not by the stage but by the entry of a new figure into old Mrs. Mingott’s box. A slim young woman, a little less tall than May Welland, had appeared—brown hair growing in close curls about her temples and held by a narrow band of diamonds. The effect, giving her what was then called a “Josephine look,” was carried out in the cut of her dark blue velvet gown, theatrically caught up under the bosom by a girdle with a large old-fashioned clasp.

She seemed unconscious of the attention she drew. She stood a moment in the center of the box, speaking with Mrs. Welland about whether she ought to take the latter’s place in the front right-hand corner; then, yielding with a slight smile, she seated herself in line with Mrs. Welland’s sister-in-law, Mrs. Lovell Mingott, who occupied the opposite corner.

Mr. Sillerton Jackson returned the opera-glass to Lawrence Lefferts. The whole of the club box turned, instinctively waiting to hear what the old man would say, for Mr. Jackson was as great an authority on “family” as Lefferts was on “form.” He knew every ramification of New York cousinship; could untangle connections as dense as vines—how the Mingotts, through the Thorleys, were linked to the Dallases of South Carolina, and how the elder Philadelphia Thorleys related to the Albany Chiverses (never to be confused with the Manson Chiverses of University Place). He could also recite the hereditary traits of each tribe: the fabulous stinginess of the younger Leffertses of Long Island; the Rushworth tendency to make foolish matches; the insanity that appeared in every second generation of the Albany Chiverses, with whom their New York cousins had refused to intermarry—except, disastrously, in the case of poor Medora Manson, who, as everybody knew… but then her mother had been a Rushworth.

Beyond this forest of family trees, Mr. Jackson carried beneath his soft thatch of silver hair a register of scandals and mysteries that had smoldered for fifty years under the unruffled surface of society. So far did his information reach, and so retentive was his memory, that people believed he might be the only man who could tell who Julius Beaufort the banker really was, and what had become of handsome Bob Spicer, old Mrs. Manson Mingott’s father, who had disappeared mysteriously—with a large sum of trust money—less than a year after his marriage, on the very day a beautiful Spanish dancer, lately delighting audiences at the old Opera-house on the Battery, had taken ship for Cuba.

Yet these stories—and many others—were locked in Mr. Jackson’s breast. His sense of honor forbade him to repeat what was privately imparted, and he knew his reputation for discretion widened the channels through which secrets flowed to him. The club box waited, visibly suspended, while he considered.

For a moment he scrutinized the attentive group out of filmy blue eyes overhung by veined lids. Then he twisted his moustache thoughtfully and said, simply:

“I didn’t think the Mingotts would have tried it on.”
