The two girls returned to the rectory in silence, passing through the quiet lanes without meeting a soul. They slipped into the house without a sound, climbed the stairs unseen, and found their way to their rooms under the soft glow of dawn. Shirley went straight to her bed. Though the room was unfamiliar—she had never slept there before—and though the events of the night had been more intense than anything she’d ever known, she fell asleep almost instantly. Her breath deepened, her face softened, and within moments she was wrapped in a restful slumber that banished every trace of fear and tension.

Shirley’s health was remarkable—her spirit strong, her nerves steady. She felt deeply, yet was not weakened by emotion. The storm had shaken her, yes, but it had not broken her. Each day brought new passion, each night restored her fully. Caroline watched her sleep, drawn to the peace reflected in Shirley’s features—the stillness of a mind at ease, even after such turmoil.

For Caroline, sleep was impossible. Her nature was different—more sensitive, more prone to reflection. Even the ordinary excitement of tea and school would have kept her awake; now, the horror of what had unfolded just hours before weighed heavily on her heart. She could not lie still. Sitting beside Shirley’s bed, she counted the minutes as the June sun climbed higher in the sky.

Hours like these—watches spent in silent suffering—are among life’s harshest trials. When the mind has no joy to feed on, no memories to comfort it, no hope to sustain it, it turns inward, trying to live on longing alone. But desire brings no nourishment. Hunger grows sharper, and the soul begins to starve. Then comes philosophy, then resolution, then resignation—each called upon in turn, each met with silence. There is no answer. No help. Only emptiness.

Caroline was a Christian. In her distress, she prayed—earnestly, repeatedly—asking for strength, patience, relief. Yet, despite her faith, she sometimes felt abandoned. Was God turned away from her? At times, she believed herself doomed, cast aside by divine will. She sank into despair, wondering if she were among those marked for rejection.

Many people know this feeling—the sense of being utterly forsaken. After long waiting, after endless hope deferred, the heart grows weary. It is a terrible moment, yet often the darkest hour before dawn. Just as winter's last wind carries both mourning and promise, so too does suffering hold within it the seed of deliverance. But the birds shivering in the cold cannot see spring coming. Neither can the grieving soul recognize salvation when it stands at its door.

Still, let no one who suffers lose faith. God does not deceive. He does not abandon. “Whom He loveth, He chasteneth.” These words are true. And they must be remembered.

By morning, the household stirred. Servants moved about, shutters opened, and daylight flooded the rooms. As Caroline rose from her restless vigil, she felt a renewal of spirit—the kind that comes with light and motion. She dressed carefully, smoothing her hair, adjusting her clothes, determined not to let her inner sorrow show. When she finished, she looked as fresh as Shirley—but where Shirley’s eyes sparkled, Caroline’s were dull with weariness.

“Today I shall have much to say to Moore,” Shirley announced, already standing before the mirror, twisting her curls with deft fingers. “He’ll have to endure a full examination. I suspect he thinks he’s outsmarted me. That’s how men treat women—hiding danger, pretending to protect them. They thought we knew nothing of where they’d gone last night. We _know_ they didn’t guess where we were. Men think women are children. That’s a mistake.”

She paused only briefly, then resumed as Caroline fastened her dress.

“If men saw us as we truly are, they’d be surprised. Even the sharpest minds among them are often mistaken. They don’t understand us—neither our goodness nor our darkness. Their ideal woman is half doll, half angel. Their wicked woman is always a monster. And then they go wild over fictional heroines—worshipping them in novels and plays! Yes, they may be beautiful, but often they’re artificial—false as the roses on my finest bonnet. If I spoke all I think about some so-called great female characters… well, I’d be buried under stones before the hour passed.”

“Shirley, you talk too much. I can’t finish dressing you. Be quiet. Besides, authors’ heroines are nearly as good as authoresses’ heroes.”

“Not at all. Women read men better than men read women. I’ll prove it in a magazine article someday—if I ever get the chance. But it’ll be rejected. ‘Declined with thanks,’ they’ll say, leaving it on the publisher’s desk.”

“To be sure. You couldn’t write well enough. You don’t know enough. You’re not learned, Shirley.”

“God knows I can’t argue with you, Cary. I’m as ignorant as a stone. But here’s one comfort—you’re not much better.”

They descended to breakfast.

“I wonder how Mrs. Pryor and Hortense Moore spent the night,” Caroline said, stirring the coffee. “I’ve been selfish—I didn’t think of either until now. They must have heard the noise. Fieldhead and the cottage are so close. Hortense is timid. So is Mrs. Pryor.”

“Don’t worry,” Shirley replied. “Moore likely sent his sister home with Miss Mann. She’ll be staying there tonight. As for Mrs. Pryor—I admit I’m concerned. But in half an hour we’ll be with her.”

By now, news of the events at the Hollow had spread across the countryside. Fanny, returning from Fieldhead with the milk, burst in breathless, reporting a battle at Mr. Moore’s mill—some said twenty men had died. Eliza, meanwhile, had learned from the butcher’s boy that the mill itself had burned to the ground. Both women rushed into the parlour, delivering their account with trembling certainty: “It’s clear master was involved. He and Thomas, the clerk—surely they joined Mr. Moore and the soldiers last night. Mr. Malone hasn’t been seen since yesterday. And Joe Scott’s family is distraught—no word of him.”

No sooner had they spoken than a knock came at the kitchen door. The Fieldhead errand-boy arrived, panting, bearing a note from Mrs. Pryor. It was hastily written, urging Miss Keeldar to return immediately—the neighborhood was in chaos, orders needed to be given, and only the mistress of the hall could manage it. In a postscript, she begged that Miss Helstone not be left alone at the rectory. It would be best if she came along.

“There’s no debate about that,” Shirley said, tying on her bonnet and hurrying to fetch Caroline’s. “But what about Fanny and Eliza? And if your uncle returns?”

“He won’t come back yet,” Shirley replied. “He has other matters to attend to. He’ll be riding between Briarfield and Stilbro’ all day, rallying magistrates and military. Fanny and Eliza can invite Joe Scott’s wife and the clerk’s wife to stay with them. And really, there’s no real danger now. Weeks will pass before the rioters gather again. I doubt Moore and your uncle won’t use last night’s outbreak to end the trouble once and for all. They’ll push the authorities into action. I only hope they won’t be too harsh—too cruel to the defeated.”

“Robert won’t be cruel,” Caroline said softly. “We saw that last night.”

“But he’ll be hard,” Shirley answered. “And so will your uncle.”

As they walked along the meadow path toward Fieldhead, they saw the highway already crowded with riders and foot travelers heading toward the Hollow. When they reached the hall, the backyard gates stood open, and the courtyard teemed with agitated milk-fetchers—men, women, children—all gathered in groups, chatting loudly. Mrs. Gill, the housekeeper, tried in vain to send them away.

Seeing the disorder, Shirley stepped forward. With calm authority, she greeted them all, her voice steady and open. She asked if everyone had received their milk. When they confirmed they had, she added, “Then I wonder what you’re waiting for?”

“We’re just talking about the battle at your mill, mistress,” one man replied.

“Talking!” Shirley laughed. “That’s all you ever do. You talk when someone dies. You talk when a fire breaks out. You talk when a mill-owner fails or is murdered. What good does it do?”

The lower classes love a firm rebuke—especially one delivered with honesty and humor. Flattery bores them. Plain speech delights them. Shirley’s sharp tone earned her instant attention.

“We’re not at war, nor above it, are we?” another man asked, smiling.

“Not a bit. You who should be models of industry are just as fond of gossip as the idle. Rich folks who do nothing may be forgiven for wasting time. But you—whose bread is earned by sweat—have no excuse.”

“That’s strange, mistress. Should we never have a holiday because we work hard?”

“Never,” Shirley said firmly. “Unless,” she added with a smile that softened her sternness, “you knew how to use it wisely—instead of gathering over rum and tea, or beer and pipes, to whisper lies about your neighbors. Now, friends,” she said, turning gentle, “please take your cans and go home. I expect visitors today. I need the paths clear.”

Yorkshire folk respond to reason more than force. Within minutes, the yard was empty.

“Thank you, and farewell,” Shirley said, closing the gate behind the departing crowd.

Let any refined Londoner dare criticize Yorkshire manners. Take them fairly, and most of the men and women of West Riding are gentlemen and ladies through and through. They only rebel against pretension and false pride.

Through the back entrance, the girls entered the kitchen—what locals call the *house*—and made their way to the hall. Mrs. Pryor rushed down the oak staircase to meet them. She was pale, trembling, her usual composure shattered. She didn’t cry out or rush into story—but her eyes darted nervously, her hands shook.

“You know,” she began, voice quivering, “that rioters attacked Mr. Moore’s mill last night. We heard the gunfire clearly. None of us slept. It was a terrible night. The house has been in chaos all morning. Servants have asked for orders—but I couldn’t give them. I didn’t feel I had the right. Mr. Moore sent for supplies for the soldiers and wounded. I couldn’t decide what to do. I feared delay might hurt someone. But this isn’t my house. You were gone, Miss Keeldar. What could I do?”

“Were no refreshments sent?” Shirley asked, her expression suddenly darkening.

“I think not.”

“And nothing for the wounded—no linen, wine, bedding?”

“I don’t know. I didn’t see what Mrs. Gill did. But I couldn’t risk sending provisions to soldiers. It seemed like allowing them to plunder. I meant to do right—but I wasn’t sure.”

“It’s simple,” Shirley said, turning sharply. “These soldiers risked their lives defending my property. They have a right to my gratitude. The wounded are human beings—we must help them. Mrs. Gill!”

She called with a voice that echoed through the thick walls—clear, commanding. Mrs. Gill, caught mid-knead in the kitchen, came running, flour still dusting her apron. She hadn’t dared stop to clean her hands. Miss Keeldar had only used that tone once before—when her dog Tartar was attacked by two carrier dogs. Then, she had stormed out bareheaded, wrestled with the animals, and fought off the carriers, refusing help. She’d ignored John for a week afterward.

Now, remembering that scene, Mrs. Gill trembled.

“Mrs. Gill,” Shirley said, “how long ago did the message arrive from the mill?”

“Not an hour, ma'am,” the housekeeper replied gently.

“Not an hour! You might as well have said not a day. They’ve probably sought help elsewhere by now. Send a man at once to tell them everything in this house is at Mr. Moore’s, Mr. Helstone’s, and the soldiers’ disposal. Do that first.”

As the order was carried out, Shirley stepped to the window, silent and distant. When Mrs. Gill returned, Shirley turned. A flush warmed her cheeks, a fire sparked in her eyes.

“Bring up the larder and wine cellar contents. Load them into the hay-carts and drive them to the Hollow. If there’s not enough bread or meat, go to the butcher and baker and ask for what they have. But I’ll check myself.”

She walked away.

“She’ll recover soon,” Caroline whispered to Mrs. Pryor. “She’ll blame herself more than you by evening. Go upstairs, dear madam. Try to be calm. She’ll need you.”

With gentle words, Caroline calmed Mrs. Pryor, escorted her to her room, and promised to return. Then she went to help where she could. The staff at Fieldhead was small, and the house was in disarray. Caroline’s quiet kindness, her quick hands, lifted spirits and eased tension. A glance from her—soft, understanding—brought a smile from Shirley, who was carrying a heavy basket from the cellar.

“This is shameful!” Shirley exclaimed, rushing to take it. “You’ll strain yourself.”

She carried it outside. When she returned, the storm in her face had passed. Her warmth returned, tempered now with quiet shame for her earlier anger.

She was still overseeing the loading when a gentleman entered the yard.

“I trust Miss Keeldar is well this morning?” he asked, studying her flushed face with quiet intensity.

She glanced at him, then turned back to her task—silent. A faint smile played on her lips, but she hid it. He leaned closer.

“Well enough, if she is good enough,” she replied. “And so is Mr. Moore, I suppose. To speak plainly, I’m not worried about him. A little misfortune would be just reward. His behavior has been—let’s say *strange*, for now. We’ll find a better word later. But what brings you here?”

“Mr. Helstone and I just received your message—that everything at Fieldhead is at our service. We assumed, from your generous wording, that you were overextending yourself. And indeed, we were correct. We’re not a regiment—only six soldiers and a few civilians. Allow me to reduce your supplies.”

Shirley blushed, then laughed—embarrassed by her own excess. Moore smiled quietly, then gave orders for baskets to be removed, vessels returned to the cellar.

“The rector must hear of this,” he said. “He’ll make a fine tale. What a brilliant army contractor Miss Keeldar would be!” He chuckled. “Exactly as I expected.”

“You should be grateful,” Shirley said, “not mocking. How could I know your numbers? For all I knew, fifty men needed feeding. You told me nothing. Provisioning soldiers suggests large scale.”

“It appears so,” Moore said, giving her another keen look. “Now,” he continued, addressing the carter, “take the rest to the Hollow. Your load will be lighter than intended.”

As the cart rolled away, Shirley regained her cheerfulness.

“What happened to the wounded?” she asked.

“None of ours were hurt,” Moore replied.

“You were injured—on the temples,” said a soft voice—Caroline’s. She had been hidden in the doorway, behind Mrs. Gill, unnoticed until now. Moore’s gaze searched the shadow.

“Are you badly hurt?” she asked.

“Like scratching your finger with a needle.”

“Lift your hair. Let us see.”

He removed his hat and obeyed—revealing only a narrow strip of plaster. Caroline nodded slightly, satisfied, and stepped back inside.

“How did she know?” Moore asked.

“By rumor, perhaps. But it’s noble of her to care. For my part, I was thinking of your opponents. What damage did they suffer?”

“One man died. Six were wounded.”

“What have you done with them?”

“They’re being cared for,” Moore said simply.
