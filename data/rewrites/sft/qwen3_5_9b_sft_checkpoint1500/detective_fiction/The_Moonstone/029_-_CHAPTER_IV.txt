The execution of the Will proceeded with a velocity that struck me as undignified, far too hurried for such a sacred transaction. The footman, Samuel, was dispatched to serve as the second witness, and the quill was placed directly into my aunt's trembling hand. I felt a compelling urge to offer a few brief, edifying words appropriate to the gravity of the moment, yet Mr. Bruff's demeanor convinced me that silence was the wiser course while he remained present. Within the span of two minutes, the legalities were concluded, and Samuel, untouched by any spiritual exhortation, descended the stairs.

Mr. Bruff folded the document and glanced toward me, seemingly uncertain whether I intended to remain in his company with my aunt. I carried a clear mandate of mercy upon my heart, and upon my lap rested a satchel brimming with divine literature. It would have been as futile to expect Mr. Bruff to attempt moving St. Paul's Cathedral as it was to imagine he could displace me. Yet, one virtue, likely cultivated by his worldly upbringing, I cannot deny: he possessed a keen eye for observation. He perceived almost the exact same impression I had made upon the cabdriver previously. He muttered a coarse interjection, retreated hastily, and departed, thereby granting me sole dominion over the scene.

Immediately upon his departure, my aunt sank onto the sofa, her countenance clouded with a semblance of confusion regarding the subject of her testament. "I trust you do not feel neglected, Drusilla," she murmured. "I intend to bestow upon you your modest legacy, my dear, with my own hands."

This presented a providential opportunity! Seizing it without hesitation, I opened my satchel and extracted the premier volume. It was an early printing—the twenty-fifth edition—of the renowned anonymous treatise, attributed to the virtuous Miss Bellows, titled *The Serpent at Home*. Its purpose, perhaps unfamiliar to the secular reader, was to expose how the Adversary lurks within the most innocuous acts of daily existence. The chapters particularly suited for feminine contemplation included "Satan in the Hair Brush," "Satan Behind the Looking Glass," "Satan Under the Tea Table," and "Satan Out of the Window."

"Attend closely, dear aunt, to this invaluable volume," I instructed gently, handing it to her open at a passage of fiery eloquence concerning "Satan Among the Sofa Cushions." "And in doing so, you shall grant me everything I request."

Poor Lady Verinder, leaning idly against her own cushions, cast a fleeting glance at the book before returning it to me, her brow furrowed in greater perplexity than before. "I fear, Drusilla," she replied, "that I must wait until I am somewhat recovered before attempting to read this. The physician..."

At the mention of the doctor, the trap snapped shut. Time and again, in my service to the lost, the members of that notorious unbelieving profession of medicine had intervened, masquerading as protectors of peace while effectively silencing the gospel. They claimed the patient required solitude, shielding her from the disturbing influence of Miss Clack and her books. This same blinding materialism, operating treacherously in the background, now sought to deprive me of the sole property right my poverty afforded: my spiritual inheritance over my dying aunt.

"The doctor informs me," continued my misguided kin, "that I am not well today. He forbids me to receive visitors, and commands that if I read at all, I consume only the lightest and most frivolous works. 'Do nothing, Lady Verinder,' he said just now, 'to weary your head or quicken your pulse.'"

Yielding was unavoidable for the moment, though only temporarily. Any bold assertion of the supreme superiority of my ministry over that of the medical man would merely provoke the doctor to exploit the frailty of his patient, threatening to abandon the case. Fortunately, there are myriad methods of sowing the good seed, and few are more adept at navigating these paths than I.

"You may feel stronger within an hour or two, dear," I suggested. "Perhaps by tomorrow morning, you will awaken with a void, and even this unassuming volume might fill it. Will you permit me to leave the book, aunt? The doctor would find it difficult to object!"

I slid the volume beneath the sofa cushions, positioning it halfway in, halfway out, adjacent to her handkerchief and smelling bottle. Each time her hand reached for either item, it would inevitably brush against the book. Soon, inevitably, the book might touch *her*. Having secured this placement, I deemed it prudent to depart. "Allow me to leave you to rest, dear aunt; I shall return tomorrow." As I spoke, I glanced casually toward the window, which overflowed with floral boxes and pots. Lady Verinder held a profound affection for these ephemeral beauties, often rising to admire their fragrance. A novel inspiration struck me. "Pray, may I take a flower?" I asked, moving to the window unnoticed. Rather than removing a bloom, I introduced another—a book from my satchel—which I concealed among the geraniums and roses. Why not extend this kindness to every chamber she inhabited? Immediately, I bid farewell and crossed the hall, slipping into the library. Samuel, ascending to escort me out, assumed I had already departed and descended again. Upon the library table, I observed two of the "frivolous volumes" recommended by the infidel physician. Instantly, I veiled them from view with two of my own sacred texts. In the breakfast room, where my aunt's favorite canary chirped in its cage, I discovered weeds strewn across a table beneath the perch; I buried a book within the foliage. In the drawing room, amidst my aunt's cherished musical scores on the piano, I inserted two more volumes. Another found its home in the back drawing room, nestled beneath unfinished embroidery known to be Lady Verinder's work. A third little room, separated from the back drawing room by curtains rather than a door, yielded further opportunities. Her plain, old-fashioned fan rested on the mantelpiece; I opened my ninth book to a pivotal page and tucked the fan inside as a bookmark, ensuring the text awaited her gaze. Then arose the question: Should I ascend further, risking the indignation of the person wearing cap-ribbons who might reside in the upper chambers? Yet, what matters this? A poor Christian fears no insult. I ascended the stairs, prepared to endure whatever trials lay ahead. The house was silent and solitary, likely during the servants' tea hour. My aunt's chamber lay forward. Opposite the bed hung the miniature of my late beloved uncle, Sir John. His painted smile seemed to whisper, "Drusilla! Deposit a book." Tables flanked the bed. She was a restless sleeper, desiring—or believing she desired—many comforts at night. I placed a volume near the matches on one side and another beneath the box of chocolate drops on the other. Whether she sought illumination or confection, a sacred publication awaited her eye or hand, silently urging, "Try me!" Only one book remained in my satchel, and but one sanctuary unexplored: the bathroom, accessible from the bedroom. Peering inside, the holy inner voice, which never errs, whispered, "You have encountered her everywhere else, Drusilla; encounter her at the bath, and the labor shall be complete." I noted a dressing gown draped over a chair, featuring a pocket. Into that pocket I placed my final volume. How shall words convey the exquisite sense of fulfilled duty when I slipped out of the house, unseen by any soul, emerging into the street with my empty satchel? Oh, ye worldly friends, chasing the phantom of Pleasure through the sinful labyrinths of dissipation, how readily happiness awaits you if you but choose to be good!

That evening, as I packed my belongings, reflecting upon the *true* riches I had scattered lavishly throughout the mansion of my affluent aunt, I declared myself free from all anxiety, as buoyant as a child. My spirits were so high that I sang a stanza of the Evening Hymn; so light was my heart that I drifted into slumber before finishing the song. Like a child! Like a child again!

Thus I passed that blessed night. Rising the following morning, I felt a rejuvenation akin to youth. I might have remarked on how youthful I appeared, had I the capacity to dwell upon the concerns of my perishable frame, but I possess no such faculty.

Approaching luncheon time, driven not by physical comfort but by the desire to locate my dear aunt, I donned my bonnet to proceed to Montagu Square. Just as I was ready, the maid at my lodging peered in and announced, "Lady Verinder's servant wishes to see Miss Clack."

I resided on the parlour floor of my London dwelling. The front parlour served as my sitting room: exceedingly small, with a low ceiling and sparse furnishings, yet oh, so meticulously neat! I ventured into the passage to identify the servant. It was the young footman, Samuel—a fair-complexioned youth with a teachable visage and an obliging disposition. I had long harbored a spiritual interest in Samuel, wishing to engage him in earnest discourse. This time, I invited him into my sitting room.

He entered bearing a large parcel under his arm. When he set it down, it appeared to terrify him. "My lady's love, Miss; and I was to inform you that you will discover a letter within." Delivering this message, the fresh-faced youth startled me by appearing ready to flee.
