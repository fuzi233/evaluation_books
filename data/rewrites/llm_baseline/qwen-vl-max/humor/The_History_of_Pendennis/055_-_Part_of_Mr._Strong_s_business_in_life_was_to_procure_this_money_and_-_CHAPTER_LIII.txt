Fanny, poor little soul, stood in the doorway like a startled mouse caught between two cats—only these weren’t ordinary cats, they were the Pendennis women: one with eyes like dried-out raisins and a face that said “I’ve seen worse,” and the other, Laura, whose expression could freeze a summer’s day into an Arctic winter. Fanny knew instantly, just by the hollow look in Mrs. Pendennis’s eyes, that she was staring at Arthur’s mother. It was like seeing Arthur himself when he’d been tossing about in fever—same haunted glint, same kind of doom.

She looked from Mrs. Pendennis to Laura, who might as well have been carved from granite. No mercy. No sympathy. Not even a flicker of pity. Just cold, hard stone faces. Fanny turned to Major Pendennis behind them, hoping for a word of kindness, but old Pendennis only dropped his eyelids like a gentleman trying not to be seen peeking at a scandal—and then gave her a glance so furtive it could’ve been mistaken for a secret signal to a spy.

“I—I wrote to you yesterday, ma'am,” Fanny stammered, trembling like a leaf in a hurricane, pale as a sheet of paper dipped in milk. “If you please.”

“Did you, madam?” Mrs. Pendennis replied, as if Fanny had just committed a minor crime like stealing a handkerchief. “Well, I suppose I may now relieve you from nursing my son. I am his mother, you understand.”

“Yes, ma'am,” Fanny whispered, “but—this is the way to his—oh, wait a minute!” she cried, suddenly remembering something crucial. “I must prepare you for his——”

At this, Mrs. Pendennis gasped, stepped back, and let out a tiny shriek—like a cat discovering a canary in its cupboard—before quickly smothering it with a handkerchief. Too late. The damage was done.

“He’s been like this since yesterday,” Fanny said, teeth chattering like castanets in a Spanish dance.

And then, from Pen’s room—a door wide open like a stage curtain—came a sound so shocking it made the hair on your neck stand up. A horrid shriek of laughter. Then shouts. Then a full-blown college drinking song, sung with all the gusto of a man who’d just discovered gin and forgot he was dying. He thumped the wall with his fist, hollered “Hooray!” as if at a wine party, and generally behaved like a drunkard who’d just won the lottery.

“He doesn’t know me, ma'am,” Fanny said, voice cracking.

“Indeed,” Mrs. Pendennis replied, cool as ice. “Perhaps he’ll recognize his mother. Let me pass.” And with that, she pushed past Fanny like she was a doormat, marching down the dark passage toward Pen’s room. Laura sailed by too, silent as a ghost, and Major Pendennis followed, muttering something about “the state of the nation” or possibly “the state of the patient.”

Fanny sat down on a bench—formerly occupied by Pen’s printer’s devil, who’d spent many hours there waiting for ink to dry—broke down, and wept. She would have died for him. But they hated her. Not a word of thanks. Not a single “thank you, dear.” Just silence. Cold, cruel silence.

She sat there, lost in grief, until Doctor Goodenough arrived for his second visit. He found her still kneeling by the door, looking like a martyr who’d just given up hope.

“What, nurse? How’s your patient?” asked the doctor, cheerful as ever, though he knew better than anyone what a mess things were.

“Go ask them,” Fanny muttered. “They’re inside.”

“Who? His mother?”

Fanny nodded. Said nothing else.

“You must go to bed yourself, my poor little maid,” the doctor said kindly. “You’ll fall ill if you don’t.”

“Oh, mayn’t I come and see him? Mayn’t I? I—I love him so!” she begged, falling to her knees and clutching his hand so tightly it looked like she might pull off his sleeve. The sight melted the doctor’s heart. Tears misted his spectacles. He sighed.

“Pooh, pooh! Nonsense! Nurse, has he taken his draught? Any rest? Of course you can come and see him. So can I.”

“They’ll let me sit here, won’t they, sir? I’ll never make no noise. I only ask to stay,” Fanny pleaded.

The doctor called her a “stupid little thing,” patted her cheek, and bustled into the room.

Inside, Mrs. Pendennis had already claimed her throne—literally. She sat in a great chair beside Pen’s bed, Bible on lap, watch on table, bonnet and cloak neatly placed by the window. She’d already removed Fanny’s shawl and bonnet from Pen’s drawers and flung them onto the study table like evidence of a crime. She’d locked the door on the Major and Laura and declared herself the queen of the sickroom.

She’d feared Arthur wouldn’t recognize her—but lo and behold, he did. He smiled, nodded, and began talking nonsense about being back at Fairoaks. Laura, outside, heard every word. Each laugh was a dagger to her heart. *So it’s true*, she thought. *He was guilty—and with that creature!* A servant-maid! And she’d loved him! And now he was probably dying, raving, unrepentant. The Major mumbled a few words of comfort, but Laura didn’t hear them. She was too busy imagining Arthur’s last moments—probably shouting “Hooray!” while burning in hell.

The room was dismal. Until Dr. Goodenough arrived—like an angel with a lancet.

Doctors don’t just heal the sick. They heal the *family*. Their presence alone can calm a room full of panic. We’ve all waited for that carriage to clatter down the street. That moment when the wheels stop—*thud-thud-thud*—and you know: *It’s him.* You hang on every word. A smile? Relief. A sigh? Despair. You watch the doctor’s face like a hawk watches a mouse. Is there hope? Or is it over?

Mrs. Pendennis stared into his eyes like a woman reading her own future. If there was light—she’d breathe again. If not—she’d faint.

Goodenough examined Pen, confirmed the high fever, and declared immediate action necessary. Bleeding. Now. He consoled the mother with the best news he could give: “No reason to despair yet. Youth. Constitution. Strength. All in his favor.”

Then he took the Major aside into Warrington’s bedroom (now empty, sadly), for a private consultation.

“The case is critical,” he said. “Bleeding required. Must inform the mother.”

“And there was another young lady brought with her?” the Major asked, suspiciously. “Out of place in a sickroom.”

“And there was another woman still,” the Major added, lowering his voice. “The—the little person who opened the door. The one with the bonnet and shawl. Did you notice her? I just caught a glimpse as we passed in—and begad, she was uncommonly nice-looking.”

The doctor blinked. Smiled. In the midst of life-and-death drama, humor sneaks in like a thief in the night. Sometimes it’s the only thing that keeps you from crying.

“I have it,” said the doctor, returning to the study. He scribbled two notes, sealed one, picked up Fanny’s shawl and bonnet, and went out to find her.

“Quick, nurse,” he said. “Take this to the surgeon. Tell him to come instantly. Then go to my house in Hanover Square. Ask for Harbottle. Tell him to prepare this prescription. Wait until it’s ready. It may take time.”

So Fanny trudged off, notes in hand, to the apothecary in the Strand. The man came immediately—lancet in pocket, ready to bleed the poor boy. Then Fanny ran to the doctor’s house, where Harbottle was taking forever to mix the potion. By the time it was ready, the doctor was already back.

And from that day forward, poor Fanny never returned to nurse Arthur again. But for a few days, a small, sad figure could be seen lurking near Pen’s staircase—asking the apothecary, his boy, the laundress, and the doctor himself: “Is he better? Is he sleeping? Does he know me?”

On the third day, the doctor’s carriage stopped at Shepherd’s Inn. He entered the porter’s lodge and treated a little patient—Fanny Bolton—who had been suffering quietly in the shadows. And when he finally told her the crisis was over, that Arthur was recovering, her tears turned to joy.

Mr. Costigan, Esquire, late of Her Majesty’s service, saw the doctor’s carriage and commented on the horses. “Green liveries, bedad!” he said. “And as fine a pair of high-stepping bee horses as ever a gentleman need sit behind. No end to the pride and arrogance of doctors nowadays—though this one’s a good fellow, and a scientific character. He’s brought the poor little girl through her fever, Bows, me boy!”

From that day on, whenever Mr. Costigan saw Dr. Goodenough’s carriage, he saluted it like royalty—as if the doctor were the Lord Lieutenant and Costigan himself the hero of Phoenix Park.

Mrs. Pendennis, meanwhile, was so grateful she wanted to give Goodenough the silver-gilt vase—the pride of Fairoaks, once presented to John Pendennis by Lady Elizabeth Firebrace after her son recovered from scarlet fever. The vase featured Hippocrates, Hygeia, King Bladud, and a wreath of serpents—crafted by Messrs. Abednego of Milsom Street, with an inscription by Mr. Birch, tutor to the young baronet.

She decided this treasure belonged to Goodenough. But there was one favor she refused—even though he asked for it: to treat poor Fanny kindly. The doctor had learned enough during their conversations to know Fanny was innocent—just a heartbroken girl who’d stayed by Arthur’s side out of love, not mischief. But whenever he mentioned her name, Mrs. Pendennis’s face hardened like iron. The doctor realized it was hopeless. He stopped asking.

Ah, jealousy. The one illness no medicine can cure. Not poppy, not mandragora, not mesmerism, not even Dr. Simpson’s chloroform. It’s the disease of the heart, and it runs deep in ladies.

Now, some might ask: how did all these people fit into a set of chambers in the Temple? Three rooms, two cupboards, a passage, a coal-box? Well, almost everyone was out of town. Fashionable folks fled London like rats from a sinking ship. Even Percy Sibwright, the dashing lawyer from Baker Street, had left. His bedroom—once the most elegant retreat in Lamb Court—was now available for Miss Bell (aka Mrs. Pendennis) to rest.

Sibwright’s room was a marvel: a brass bed with pink-lined chintz curtains, a mignonette box by the window, boots arranged like soldiers, a museum of scent bottles, and portraits of ladies in distress—Medora lamenting Conrad, Princesse Fleur de Marie trapped in a convent cage, Dorothea washing her feet eternally. It was a gallery of romance, fit for a poet.

And years later, Laura confessed—with a blush and a laugh—that she’d once read a French novel in that very room. When her husband asked where she’d gotten it, she admitted: “In the Temple. In Percy Sibwright’s chambers.”

“And also,” she added, “I never confessed before: I opened his japanned box, took out that strange wig, put it on, and looked at myself in the mirror.”

Imagine if Sibwright had walked in! What would he have said? The enraptured rogue! His collection of disguised beauties would’ve been nothing compared to that living, breathing, wig-wearing wonder.

But those were younger days. When people were young. When Sibwright was still a bachelor. When Laura could play pranks without fear of consequences.

When she did it, Pen couldn’t have been very ill—otherwise, decorum would’ve prevented such tomfoolery.

But events had changed everything. Martha, the faithful servant from Fairoaks, had arrived. She’d been summoned by the Major, who wisely thought her presence would be comforting. And indeed, she became a pillar of strength—sleeping on Warrington’s straw mattress among his math books, like a guardian angel.

Meanwhile, Pen’s condition improved dramatically. The fever, subdued by blisters, potions, and a good bleeding, began to recede. His mind cleared. He kissed his mother, blessed her, called for Laura and the Major, thanked them warmly, and then—after being gently ejected by his nurse—sank into a glorious sixteen-hour sleep.

When he woke, he called out: “I’m hungry!”

Oh, the joys of convalescence! To go from loathing food to craving it like a starving lion. The pleasures fade with age, alas. But for now, it was pure bliss.

On that happy day, another arrival came—Warrington, fresh from Norfolk, having rushed back upon hearing of Arthur’s plight. He burst into the chambers with a cigar in his mouth and a carpet-bag under his arm, puffing smoke like a steam engine.

He found the Major sitting demurely in an easy chair, listening to a young lady reading Shakespeare aloud—softly, sweetly.

She stopped. Started. Put down her book.

Warrington blushed. Threw the cigar into the passage. Took off his hat. Dropped it. Rushed to the Major, seized his hand, and asked about Arthur.

The Major answered, voice trembling but cheerful. He told him of the crisis, the mother’s arrival, the young charge—Miss—

“You need not tell me her name,” Warrington said, eyes bright with emotion. “I know it. It’s Laura.”

And he reached out, took her hand.

Tenderness shone from under his rough eyebrows. “Arthur’s hero,” his eyes seemed to say. “The brave one. The kind one. Who traveled hundreds of miles to help his friend.”

“Thank you, Mr. Warrington,” Laura said simply, her face flushing crimson. She was glad the lamp was behind her.

As they stood there, the door to Pen’s room opened softly—just as Mrs. Pendennis always did—and Warrington saw another lady, who looked at him, then turned toward the bed, and whispered: “Hsh!” with a raised hand.

And so the scene remained—quiet, hopeful, full of secrets and tender hearts—while Arthur slept, and the world held its breath.
