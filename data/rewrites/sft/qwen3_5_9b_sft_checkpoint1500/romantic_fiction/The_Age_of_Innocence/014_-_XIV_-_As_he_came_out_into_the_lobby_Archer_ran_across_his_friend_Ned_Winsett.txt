As Archer stepped out into the vast, echoing space of the lobby, he bumped directly into Ned Winsett, the solitary soul among his circle of "intellectuals" whom Archer truly trusted to look past the surface chatter of the club and the chop houses. Across the room, Archer had glimpsed Winsett's weary, hunched silhouette and had noted earlier how his gaze had drifted toward the Beaufort box. Now, the two men exchanged a firm handshake. Winsett, ever eager for diversion, suggested a beer at a tiny German eatery around the bend. Archer, however, felt no spark for the kind of gossip that usually flowed from such joints, so he politely feigned a pressing need for domestic labor. Winsett chuckled, offering a wry salute to his own impending industriousness.

Together, they strolled through the air-filled hall. Soon, Winsett dropped the pleasantries to launch straight into his inquiry. "Tell me everything about the dark beauty in your exclusive box—the one sharing it with the Beauforts, yes? The one Lefferts seems utterly bewitched by."

Archer felt a flicker of irritation he couldn't quite name. Why did Ned Winsett care about Ellen Olenska's identity? And why link her so intimately with Lefferts? It was so unlike Winsett to display such prying curiosity, yet Archer recalled the man's profession. Was it journalism driving this interest?

"For an article, I hope not?" Archer asked, trying to lighten the mood with a laugh.

"Not for the press," Winsett replied quickly, shaking his head. "Just for me. Here's the truth: she's a neighbor of mine. A strange neighborhood for such stunning beauty to inhabit. She's been incredibly kind to my little boy. He chased his kitten into her garden and took a terrible fall. She dashed in, hatless, carrying him in her arms, his knee wrapped in beautiful bandages. Her sympathy and sheer grace were so overwhelming that my wife was completely flabbergasted and forgot to ask her name."

A warm, genuine glow spread through Archer's chest. There was nothing remarkable in the act itself—any decent woman would have helped—but it was precisely *Ellen* who would rush in bareheaded, cradling a stranger's child, and mesmerize a simple, hardworking woman like Mrs. Winsett into oblivion.

"That is Countess Olenska," Archer said softly. "A granddaughter of old Mrs. Mingott."

"Nonsense!" Winsett whistled, impressed. "Well, I never thought Countesses were so neighborly. The Mingotts certainly aren't."

"If you let them," Archer countered.

"And there it is again," Winsett sighed, falling back into their endless, circular debate about the stubborn refusal of the "clever" set to mingle with the fashionable elite. Both men knew better than to push the point further.

"How does a Countess happen to live in our section?" Winsett asked, breaking off the argument.

"Because she cares not a whit about where she lives—or about any of our petty social markers," Archer said, feeling a quiet, secret pride in his mental portrait of her.

"Mmm," Winsett murmured, perhaps thinking of grander, older worlds. "Well, here is my corner."

With a characteristic slump, the journalist slouched away across Broadway. Archer stood watching him retreat, lost in thought over Winsett's final words. Ned possessed those rare flashes of deep insight, the quality that made him so fascinating, yet it always puzzled Archer why Winsett accepted failure with such stoic resignation at an age when most men were still clawing their way upward.

Archer knew Winsett had a wife and child, though he had never laid eyes on them. Their meetings were confined to the Century Club or the haunts of journalists and theater-goers, like the German restaurant. Winsett had hinted that his wife was an invalid—a claim that could have meant physical frailty, or perhaps a lack of social polish, or even a wardrobe crisis regarding evening gowns. Winsett held a visceral hatred for social rituals; Archer, who dressed formally because he believed it cleaner and more comfortable, viewed this disdain as part of the tedious "Bohemian" affectation that made the fashionable class appear so naive and self-assured compared to the rest. Still, Winsett stirred something in Archer. Whenever Archer spotted the journalist's lean, bearded face and melancholy eyes, he would drag him out of his corner for a long, stimulating conversation.

Winsett was not a journalist by vocation. He was a pure man of letters, tragically born into a world that no longer needed literature. After publishing a single volume of brief, exquisite literary critiques—one hundred and twenty copies sold, thirty given away, and the rest ultimately destroyed by the publisher to make room for marketable trash—he had abandoned his true calling. Now, he worked as a sub-editor on a women's weekly magazine, where fashion plates and sewing patterns danced alongside New England love stories and ads for temperance beverages.

On matters of "Hearth-fires"—the magazine's name—he was endlessly entertaining. Yet beneath his humor lay the sterile bitterness of a young man who had tried and failed. His conversations forced Archer to measure his own life against Winsett's and realize how hollow his own existence seemed. Still, their shared intellectual curiosities made their talks electric, even if their exchanges mostly hovered within the bounds of a pensive dilettante's musings.

"The fact is, life isn't much of a fit for either of us," Winsett had once confessed. "I am down and out; there is nothing to be done. I have only one thing to produce, and there is no market for it here, and certainly not in my lifetime. But you are free and wealthy. Why don't *you* get involved? There is only one path: politics."

Archer had thrown his head back and laughed. There, instantly, appeared the unbridgeable chasm between men like Winsett and Archer's own kind. Everyone in polite society knew that in America, "a gentleman could not enter politics." Yet, he could hardly say that to Winsett, so he deflected with a shrug. "Look at the record of the honest man in American politics! They do not want us."

"Who are 'they'? Why don't you all unite and become 'they' yourselves?"

Archer's laughter lingered, turning into a faint, condescending smile. It was futile to prolong the discussion; everyone knew the tragic fate of the few gentlemen who had gambled their pristine linens in municipal or state politics in New York. Those days were gone. The nation belonged to the bosses and the immigrants, leaving decent people to retreat into sports or culture.

"Culture! Yes—if we actually possessed it! But there are only a few small, dying patches here and there, fading for lack of tending and cross-pollination: the last fragments of the old European tradition your ancestors brought with them. But you are a pitiful minority: you lack a center, competition, or an audience. You resemble the portraits on the walls of an abandoned house: 'The Portrait of a Gentleman.' None of you will achieve anything until you roll up your sleeves and dive into the mud. Or, God forbid, you could emigrate..."

Archer silently shrugged and steered the conversation back to books, where Winsett, if unsure, was always captivating. Emigrate! As if a gentleman could abandon his homeland! One could not do that any more than one could roll up one's sleeves and descend into the filth. A gentleman simply stayed home and abstained. But convincing a man like Winsett required a patience Archer did not possess, and that was why New York, with its literary clubs and exotic eateries, despite initially appearing as a dazzling kaleidoscope, ultimately revealed itself as a cramped box with a monotonous pattern, far inferior to the scattered, vibrant atoms of Fifth Avenue.

The following morning, Archer searched the city in vain for yellow roses. Because of this fruitless hunt, he arrived late at the office. He realized with a jolt that his tardiness altered nothing for anyone else, filling him with a sudden, sharp exasperation at the elaborate futility of his existence. Why not be, right then and there, on the sands of St. Augustine with May Welland? No one would be fooled by his pretense of professional busyness. In old-fashioned legal firms like Mr. Letterblair's, where large estates and "conservative" investments were managed, there were always two or three young men, comfortably off and devoid of professional ambition, who sat at their desks performing trivial tasks or simply reading newspapers for hours. Although it was considered proper to hold an occupation, the crude reality of making money was still viewed as derogatory, and the law, being a profession, was deemed a gentler pursuit than commerce. Yet, none of these young men harbored serious hopes of advancement or a genuine desire to strive; over many of them, the green mold of the perfunctory was already visibly spreading.

The thought chilled Archer. Could it be spreading over him, too? He had, indeed, other tastes and interests; he spent his vacations traveling Europe, cultivated the "clever people" May admired, and generally strove to "keep up," as he had once admitted wistfully to Madame Olenska. But what would become of this narrow margin of life where his real experiences unfolded once he was married? He had witnessed enough of other young men who had dreamed his dreams, perhaps less fervently, who had slowly sunk into the placid, luxurious routines of their elders.
