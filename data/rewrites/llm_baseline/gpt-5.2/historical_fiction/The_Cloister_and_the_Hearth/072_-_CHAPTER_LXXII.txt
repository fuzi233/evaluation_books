The Black Friars had once held Rome and Christendom by the throat, not by violence, but by voice—by trained tongues that could turn a market-place into a chapel and make a prince feel poor. Yet now the Order’s power ebbed. The Franciscans, brown and barefoot, took the people’s ear, and the Dominicans, in their black cloaks, felt the world sliding away from them like dough from an unfloured board.

Therefore the prudent heads of the convent were hungry—hungry as men are when a pantry thins—to lay hands on any rare gift, salt it, and send it out where it might preserve what was decaying. Word went quickly through the cloister that young Brother Clement—shaven, pale, newly vowed—had a mouth made for more than prayer: Latin came to him as naturally as breath; Italian, French, high and low Dutch, he moved among them as a cook among herbs, knowing which leaf to bruise and which to keep whole.

It was already spoken, half in admiration and half in policy, that he should be sent to England to preach and to write, to answer Rome as a well-tuned bell answers another across a valley.

But Jerome stood in the way.

“He has still the milk of the world in his veins,” said Jerome to the prior—Jerome whose voice, even in kindness, carried iron filings. “Let not new-born zeal and a meek face cheat us. Try him. Temper him. Better to bend green wood now than find, in the day of need, we have taken a reed for a staff.”

The prior, who had watched many fine promises turn brittle under strain, nodded. “It is well advised. Take him in hand thyself.”

So Jerome took Clement as a man takes a knife to a stone, not to spoil it, but to see if it holds an edge.

One day he led him out beyond the cloister walls, into the sun and noise of a field where young men played at their rough games, shouting and laughing, the sound of it running like a brook through the air. It was a place Clement had known in other days; Jerome knew it too, as a cook knows where the temptations are kept—where the fat hangs, where the sweetmeats lie.

They had not stood long before two citizens passed by, Pietro Vanucci and Andrea—companions of Clement’s former life. Their eyes slid over the two friars as over stones on a road. They did not know their dead friend beneath a tonsure and a habit.

Clement gave a tiny start, like a hand that has touched a hot dish by accident; then he dropped his eyes and said a paternoster, low and swift, as if he would cover the old warmth with prayer.

Jerome’s test came quietly. “Would ye not speak with them, brother?”

“No, brother.” Clement’s voice was steady, though his mouth had gone dry. “Yet it was good for me to see them. They remind me of sins I can never repent enough.”

“It is well,” said Jerome, and his face betrayed nothing. But his report, when he made it, was coldly in Clement’s favour, like watered wine—truth, yet without warmth.

Then Jerome set him to harsher airs.

He brought him to death-beds where the room smelled of sour linen and stale breath, of wax and fear; where the last words rasped out like the last scraping of a spoon in an empty pot. Clement bent over the dying, held their hands, moistened cracked lips, prayed as if prayer were broth to be sipped by a failing body. And after that Jerome took him into dungeons—holes where the air was thick as old stew, where darkness sat on the eyes, and men, coiled in rags and filth, lay like beasts in a pen, their voices half growl, half sob. The place tried Clement’s body hard; the stench clung to his habit and hair as smoke clings to a cook’s sleeve. Yet his soul gathered itself and went among those poor wretches that were not past comfort, and there he shone—gentle, patient, fearless of the foulness of flesh.

Jerome reported: the spirit willing, the flesh weak.

“Good,” said Anselm the prior. “His flesh is weak, but his spirit is willing.”

Yet Jerome had one trial more in store—one that was like the moment a man is forced to watch the killing of an animal, not in the clean economy of a kitchen where death serves life, but in a street where death is a spectacle and the crowd feeds on it with its eyes.

It was seen by many.

On a morning when the sun came up clear and indifferent over Rome, a principal street filled as a granary fills with grain. Heads packed the ways; children were hoisted up onto shoulders; vendors cried out as if it were a fair. The city’s noise—foot-shuffle, cough, the clack of sandals, the murmur of anticipation—made a living skin of sound. Even the air had a taste in it: dust, sweat, and the faint metallic tang that comes before blood is spilled.

The governor of Rome had been found at daybreak in his bed slaughtered—throat cut, temples bruised, his hand hacked off at the wrist as though he had tried to ward the blow. The murderer, traced to his servant, was to pay in kind.

Italian executions were not always cruel; but the city had decided this one must be exact, as if pain could balance a scale.

They brought the criminal first to the murdered man’s house and fastened him for half an hour to the wall, a thing for eyes to gnaw upon. Then they struck off his left hand. A new-killed fowl was split and bound round the stump—some physic of the doctors, a foolish poultice of warm flesh against bleeding; it looked, to any honest observer, like kitchen-work turned grotesque. Then, mutilated and bandaged, he was hurried to the scaffold.

A young friar was beside him—Brother Clement—earnest, affectionate, pressing the crucifix close, his voice thick with pity as he prayed with the man and for him, trying to give the dying a last mouthful of mercy.

The executioner, impatient, hauled the friar aside as one pushes away a child from a fire. In a moment the mallet fell; the culprit dropped; the knife drew a line from ear to ear.

The crowd cried out—not in joy, but in a sudden sick recoil, as if a gust of foul air had struck them.

Clement swooned.

A gigantic monk strode forward and carried him off like a child, his black sleeves swinging; and the crowd, denied its full meal of spectacle, began to buzz again, restless as flies.

Clement went back to the convent with his heart bruised. He confessed to the prior with tears he could not swallow.

“Courage, son Clement,” said Anselm. “A Dominican is not made in a day. Thou shalt have another trial. And I forbid thee to go to it fasting.”

Clement bowed, obedient. The prior spoke as a man who knows the body too has its law: a stomach empty makes the spirit more fragile, as a pot with a thin bottom cracks under heat.

He had not long to wait.

A robber was taken—no trembling servant, but a monster of cruelty who had killed for sport after robbing. Clement spent the last night in prison with him. The cell smelled of cold stone and old straw; outside, Rome’s sound ebbed and flowed like a sea against walls. Clement did not flinch from the man’s breath, nor from the stories that fell from his mouth, greasy with sin. He spoke to him as one speaks to a starving soul, not as a judge but as a cook might speak to a famished beggar—plainly, urgently, with the tender impatience of those who want to save what can still be saved.

In the morning he accompanied him to the scaffold. There, as rope and wood waited, Clement prayed so earnestly that the hardened ruffian wept—tears cutting tracks through grime. He embraced Clement. Clement embraced him too, though his flesh quivered with repugnance; he held the crucifix before the man’s eyes like a last light in a cellar.

They garotted the man. Clement, for a space, lost sight of the crowd entirely; he prayed aloud while that dark spirit struggled through its final choking. When the body sagged dead, the hangman raised a hatchet and began to quarter it on the spot.

Then—oh, the mystery of man’s heart!—the same people who had watched the taking of life with a kind of dull appetite cried out pitifully at each stroke upon the corpse that could feel nothing. Each chop drew a sound from the crowd as if the axe fell on their own bones. Clement shuddered too; but he stood firm, like a rock that vibrates yet will not be thrown down.

And at that moment Jerome’s voice cut in at his ear, sharp as a command in a kitchen when the fire is high.

“Brother Clement, get thee on that cart and preach to the people. Nay, quickly. Strike with all thy force on all this iron, while yet ’tis hot, and souls are to be saved.”

Clement’s colour came and went; his breath hurried. Yet he obeyed. With an ill-assured step he mounted the cart and looked out over that sea of heads. His throat felt parched as if he had swallowed ash; his heart hammered; his voice, when it came, trembled.

But as he spoke, the greatness of the occasion took him—this mingled smell of blood and sun, this crowd still raw with sensation, these faces turned up like bowls waiting to be filled. He told them the robber’s history—fresh from the man’s own lips in the prison—showing the slow steps by which folly becomes crime, the first small yielding that is like tasting a forbidden sweetness and then returning for more. He warned them not to set foot on the first rung of that ladder, because the last rung ends in rope and axe.

Then his voice strengthened. He thundered against shedders of blood; he pleaded for charity and pity; and he felt, to his own astonishment, the crowd’s pulse under his words, as a musician feels a lute answer his touch. He played on them sacredly: sobs, groans, the sudden hush—these were his proof. The air itself seemed to change; the street-noise, for once, became a kind of listening.

Jerome stared as one who goes to light a stick and fires a rocket.

After a time Clement saw Jerome’s astonishment and, finding no plain approval there, broke off abruptly and came down, humble as ever.

“It was my first endeavour,” he said, apologetic. “Your behest fell on me like a thunderbolt. Was I—did I—Oh, correct me, and aid me with your experience, Brother Jerome.”

“Humph!” said Jerome, doubtfully; and after long reflection he added, sullenly, “Give the glory to God, Brother Clement. My opinion is thou art an orator born.”

He reported as much at headquarters, half reluctantly—for Jerome, though disagreeable, was honest.

Not long after, another spectacle gave Jerome occasion to harden Clement further, though here cruelty wore the mask of law and ritual.

Julio Antonelli was accused of sacrilege: three witnesses swore they saw him come from the church at the very hour the candlesticks were stolen. Other witnesses proved an alibi as stoutly. Neither side could be shaken; so, in that doubt, Antonelli was permitted the ordeal by water, hot or cold.

The clergy who believed him innocent recommended the boiling water—an ordeal that, for those favoured, was not always as fierce as words made it. But Antonelli, poor wretch, had not the nerve for the steaming cauldron. He chose the cold.

So they brought him to the Tiber. The river’s voice was steady and indifferent, its surface catching the morning like a sheet of hammered metal. He took the sacrament, then they stripped him naked on the bank, where the crowd pressed close, their murmurs and cries swelling in the open air as if Rome itself were one great throat. They tied him hand and foot, to prevent those struggles by which a man, flinging his arms out of water, can sink his body.

Then they lowered him gently into the stream. For a moment he floated with just his hair above water.

A roar went up from both banks: guilty.

But the ropes were new; they drank the river, grew heavy, and he settled down.

Another roar: innocent.

They left him the appointed time, a little more than half a minute, then drew him up gurgling and gasping—his mouth working like a fish’s, his eyes wild. He screamed for mercy, though he had been granted it; the body, having tasted suffocation, does not reason. After the appointed prayers they dismissed him, cleared of the charge, trembling and alive.

During the whole Clement had stood on the bank and prayed, his lips moving with the river’s sound beneath them.

When it was over he thanked God aloud, his voice loud yet faintly quavering, as if the river had put its cold hand on his throat too.

And then, when the crowd began to drift away, that human tide receding with talk and laughter as if nothing had happened, Clement turned to Jerome.

“Brother,” he asked, “ought not the man to be compensated?”

“For what?”

“For the pain, the dread, the suffocation. Poor soul—he liveth, but hath tasted all the bitterness of death. Yet he had done no ill.”

Jerome’s eyes narrowed. “He is rewarded enough in that he is cleared of his fault.”

“But being innocent of that fault, yet hath he drunk Death’s cup, though not to the dregs; and his accusers, less innocent than he, do suffer nought.”

Jerome answered sternly, as if he would drive a nail into Clement’s soft places.

“It is not in this world men are really punished, Brother Clement. Unhappy they who sin yet suffer not. Happy they who suffer such ills as earth hath power to inflict; ’tis counted to them above, ay, and a hundred-fold.”

Clement bowed his head. “May thy good words not fall to the ground, but take root in my heart, Brother Jerome.”

Yet the severest trial Jerome gave him was not planned. It sprang, as the worst quarrels do, from an indulgent moment and a table where a thing lay open that ought perhaps to have stayed shut.

Jerome, softened for once, went with Clement to the lodgings of Fra Colonna. There upon the table lay a fair copy of “The Dream of Polifilo,” newly written out, the ink still rich and black, the letters standing up like raised crust on fresh bread. Colonna, poor man, was in the pride of his labour, like a chef who has perfected a sauce and cannot wait to have it tasted.

He pointed out what he called a master-stroke.

“For ages,” said he, “fools have been lavishing poetic praise on mortal women—mere creatures of earth, smacking palpably of their origin; sirens at the windows, where our Roman women in particular have by lifelong study learned the wily art to show their one good feature—be it but an ear or an eyelash—at a jalosy, and hide all the rest. Magpies at the door—Capre n’ i giardini, Angeli in strada, Sante in chiesa, Diavoli in casa. Then come I, and ransack the minstrels’ lines for amorous turns, not forgetting those Petrarch wasted on that French jilt Laura; and I lay you the whole bundle of spice at the feet of the only females worthy amorous incense—to wit, the Nine Muses.”

Jerome had been turning pages while he talked. He shut the book with a slap that sounded like a lid on a pot.

“By which goodly stratagem,” said Jerome, “you, a friar of St. Dominic, have produced an obscene book.” He dashed Polifilo down upon the table.

“Obscene? thou discourteous monk!” Colonna flushed as if scalded. He ran round, snatched the manuscript away as a housewife snatches bread from a dog, locked it up, and, trembling with mortification, tried to laugh it off. “My Gerard—pshaw! Brother What’s-his-name had not found Polifilo obscene. Puris omnia pura.”

“Such as read your Polifilo,” said Jerome, “—Heaven grant they may be few—will find him what I find him.”

Colonna swallowed the insult as one swallows a bitter herb—because to spit it out would be to admit its taste. Had he not been in his own lodgings, and a gentleman by birth as well as a scholar, there might have been a vulgar quarrel then and there. As it was he forced the talk into another channel, and brought forth a chrysolite the Cardinal Colonna had lent him. He put it into Clement’s hand, and Clement, who had the trained senses of one who has lived by touch and smell and sight, turned it to the light, watching its green-gold heart kindle.

Colonna began to speak, with the eager exactness of his kind, of the jewel’s virtues—how this stone could strengthen courage, that could preserve chastity, another guard from poison, another from drunkenness. He went the whole length of his age as a worshipper of jewels, believing in the moral flavours of dead stones as some believe certain herbs can cool anger or quicken love.

Jerome would not have it. He expostulated, contempt in every syllable, at the thought that a dead stone could confer valour, chastity, safety, temperance.

“The experience of ages proves they do,” said Colonna. “As to the last virtue you have named, there sits a living proof. This Gerard—pardon, Brother Thingemy—comes from the north, where men drink like fishes; yet was he ever most abstemious. And why? He carried an amethyst, the clearest and fullest-coloured e’er I saw on any but a noble finger. Where, in Heaven’s name, is thine amethyst? Show it this unbeliever!”

“And ’twas that amethyst made the boy temperate?” Jerome asked, with a thin smile.

“Certainly. Why, what is the derivation and meaning of amethyst? a negative, and methua—to tipple. Go to; names are but the signs of things. A stone is not called amethustos for two thousand years out of mere sport, and abuse of language.”

He went through the chief jewels—ruby, sapphire, emerald, opal—spicing each with anecdotes out of grave historians, as a cook seasons a dish with citations, certain that authority improves the flavour.

“These be old wives’ fables,” said Jerome. “Was ever such credulity as thine?”

It stung. Colonna, branded with that word—credulity—writhed; for nothing hurts a learned man more than to be told he is merely gullible. He fell silent. Jerome, mistaking silence for yielding, pressed on, urging him to blot out pagan lore, to study the lives of saints, to cleanse his mind as Christianity had cleansed the earth. In this zealous harangue he repeated, carelessly, some loose theological assertions that sat in the air like half-baked loaves.

Colonna’s pride, already sore from Polifilo, found its revenge. He burst out and flung at Jerome a mountain of crude, miscellaneous learning, sweeping from sculpture to sewers, from Doric columns to Roman roads, with that breathless momentum scholars use when they mean not to convince but to overwhelm.

“The heathen blotted out? Why, they hold four-fifths of the world. And what have we Christians invented without their aid? Painting? Sculpture? These are heathen arts, and we but pigmies at them. What modern mind can conceive and grave such god-like forms as did the chief Athenian sculptors—Dinocrates, Scopas, Timotheus, Leochares, Briaxis, Chares, Lysippus—and the immortal three of Rhodes that wrought Laocoon from a single block? What prince hath the genius to turn mountains into statues, as was done at Bagistan, and projected at Athos? What town the soul to plant a colossus of brass in the sea, for the tallest ships to sail in and out between his legs?

“Is it architecture we have invented? Here too we are but children. Can we match the Parthenon, and for grandeur and finish the theatres of Greece and Rome, or the prodigious temples of Egypt, where men walked awe-struck through avenues a mile long of sphinxes, each as big as a Venetian palace? Those columns of porphyry cut and polished like crystal—not rough-hewn like our puny work. Even now their broken pillars lie o’ergrown with acanthus and myrtle, sparkling still, flouting the slovenly art of modern workmen. Is it sewers, aqueducts, viaducts? Why, we have lost the art of making a road—lost it with the world’s greatest models under our very eye.

“Is it sepulchres? No Christian nation has erected a tomb the sight of which does not set a scholar laughing. Think of the Mausoleum, the Pyramids, the monstrous sepulchres of the Indus and Ganges—mountains without, mines of precious stones within. Ah, you have not seen the East, Jerome, or you could not decry the heathen.”

Jerome, who could endure a blow better than a lecture, cut in: these were material things; true greatness lay in the soul.

“Well then,” cried Colonna, “in the world of mind, what have we discovered? Geometry? Logic? We are all pupils of Euclid and Aristotle. Written characters—almost divine—we no more invented than Cadmus did. Poetry? Homer hath never been approached by us, nor Virgil, nor Horace. Tragedy or comedy? Poets, actors, theatres—all fell to dust at our touch. Have we revived them? Compare our miserable mysteries, frigid personification and dog Latin, with a Greek play—decoration costing a hundred thousand crowns—performed inside a marble miracle, the audience a seated city, and the poet a Sophocles.

“What then have we invented? Monotheism? The learned Greeks and Romans held it; even their poets were monotheists in their sleeves. Zeus estin ouranos, Zeus te gy, Zeus toi panta—saith the Greek; and Lucan echoes him: Jupiter est quodcunque vides, quocunque moveris.

“Their vulgar were polytheists; and what are ours? We have not invented invocation of saints. Our sancti answer to their Daemones and Divi; they prayed to deified mortals to intercede with higher divinity. The rude among them worshipped those they should have invoked. So do our mobs—by unbroken tradition. In holy writ is no polytheism of any sort.

“We have not invented so much as a variety of polytheism. The pagan vulgar worshipped deified mortals; each had his favourite, to whom he prayed ten times for once to the Omnipotent. Our vulgar worship canonized mortals; each has his favourite, to whom he prays ten times for once to God. Call you that invention? Invention is in the East.

“Among the ancient vulgar only mariners were monotheists; they worshipped Venus and called her Stella maris, Regina caelorum. Among our vulgar only mariners are monotheists; they worship the Virgin Mary and call her Star of the Sea, Queen of Heaven. Their new religion? An old doublet with a new button.

“Our vulgar make images and adore them—absurd; adoration is due from creature to creator; here man is creator, so statues ought to worship him, and would, had they brains enough to justify a rat in worshipping them. But even this abuse is ancient. The pagan vulgar made images, knelt before them, adorned them with flowers, offered incense, lighted tapers, carried them in procession, made pilgrimages—just to the smallest tittle as we do.”

Jerome, impatient, struck in: the most revered images in Christendom were made by no mortal hand, but had dropped from heaven.

“Ay,” cried Colonna, “such are the tutelary images of most great Italian towns. I have examined nineteen and made drafts. If they came from the sky, our worst sculptors are our angels. But my mind is easy. Ungainly statue or villainous daub fell never yet from heaven to smuggle bread out of capable workmen’s mouths.

“This is pagan, and arose thus: the Trojans feigned their Palladium fell from heaven, a wooden statue three cubits long. The Greeks carried the fib home, and soon it rained statues over all Greece and their Latin apes. One Palladium gave St. Paul trouble at Ephesus—Diana that fell from Jupiter: credat qui credere possit.”

Jerome demanded if he would cast profane doubts on the picture of Our Lady that, scarce a century gone, hung lustrous in the air over Rome and was taken down by the Pope and bestowed in St. Peter’s.

“I have no profane doubts,” said Colonna, and his voice turned sly with scholarship. “This is Numa’s shield revived by theologians with an itch for fiction but no talent—being not orientals. The ancile hung lustrous in the air over this very city till Numa took it down and hung it in Jupiter’s temple. Be just: swallow both stories or neither. The Bocca della Verità passes for a statue of the Virgin and convicted a woman of perjury the other day; it is in truth an image of Rhea, and the modern figment is but one of its ancient traditions. Swallow both or neither.

“Indeed we owe all our Palladiuncula, and all our speaking, nodding, winking, sweating, bleeding statues, to these abused heathens. Athenian statues sweated before Chaeronea; Roman statues in Tully’s day—Victory at Capua, Mars at Rome, Apollo outside the gates. Antony’s statue on Mount Alban bled from every vein before Actium. Others cured diseases. And all these marks and dents in stone made by soft substances—this saint’s knee, that saint’s finger—are heathen too. Hercules’ footprints were shown in Scythia. Castor and Pollux left hoof-prints at Regillum; a temple was built there, and the marks were seen in Tully’s day. Near Venice you may see a stone nearly cleft by St. George’s sword; yet he had but followed the old Roman who cut a whetstone with his razor.

“Kissing of images, and the Pope’s toe, is Eastern paganism. Egyptians had it of Assyrians; Greeks of Egyptians; Romans of Greeks; we of Romans—whose Pontifex Maximus had his toe kissed under the Empire. Druids kissed the High Priest’s toe a thousand years before Christ. Mussulmans, who profess to abhor heathenism, kiss the stone of the Caaba—pagan practice. Priests of Baal kissed their idols. Tully tells of a fair Hercules at Agrigentum whose chin was worn by kissing.

“The lower parts of the statue we call Peter are Jupiter. The toe is worn—not all by Christian mouths. Heathen lips laid there first, and ours have followed, as monkeys their masters. And that is why—down with the poor heathen!

“Pereant qui ante nos nostra fecerint.

“Our infant baptism is Persian, with the font and the signing of the child’s brow. Our three handfuls of earth on the coffin—dust to dust—is Egyptian. Our incense is Oriental, Roman, pagan; and the early Fathers died for refusing to touch it. Our holy water is pagan—see, here is a pagan aspersorium; could you tell it from ours? It stood in the same part of their temples, used as ours, in worship and purifications. They called it aqua lustralis. Their vulgar, like ours, thought a few drops could wash out sin; their men of sense smiled or sighed at such credulity. Ovid himself mocked it:

‘Ah nimium faciles, qui tristia crimina caedis
Fluminea tolli posse putetis aqua.’

“Thou seest the heathen were not all fools. No more are we. Not all.”

So the scholar’s torrent ran, filling the room with names and proofs, like a pantry flung open, every jar and sack emptied at once. Jerome stood stiff, Clement silent between them, hearing not only the words but the room’s changing sound—the sharp strike of pride against pride, the way Latin and Greek rang like hard coins on a table, the way the very air seemed to thicken with untranslatable meanings, each phrase carrying a world no other tongue could wholly hold.

And in that lodging, where a manuscript had been called obscene and a jewel made a battleground, Clement learned another lesson Jerome had not meant to teach: that languages themselves are power—tools that can feed or starve memory; that a dominant lexicon can bless or bruise; and that men, even vowed men, can turn learning into a cudgel as easily as a crowd can turn death into entertainment.
