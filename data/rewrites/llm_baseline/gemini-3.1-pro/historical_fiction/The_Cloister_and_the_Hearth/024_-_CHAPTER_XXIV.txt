The rhythm of Gerard’s footsteps fell into the ancient, circular pacing of generations of foot-travelers, a temporal loop where every wandering ancestor before him had trod this same rutted earth. But the profound acoustic ecology of the open road—the gentle percussion of wind through branches and the soft crunch of soil—was abruptly fractured by a jarring, dissonant clangor. 

Not far ahead, the source of this auditory pollution revealed itself. Two men in sober suits stood leaning lazily on each side of a horse, their harsh, unmodulated voices trading insults that disrupted the natural hum of the afternoon. The rider, clad in a silk doublet and a bright green jerkin of glossy English cloth, lay flat upon his stomach in the sun, resembling some enormous, mythic lizard resting on the earth. His flaming yellow velvet cloak was carefully spread over the horse's loins.

“Is aught amiss?” inquired Gerard, his native Dutch cadence feeling suddenly fragile, a fading dialect against the guttural dominance of their High German.

“Not that I wot of,” replied one of the servants.

“But your master, he lies like a corpse. Are ye not ashamed to let him grovel on the ground?”

“Go to; the bare ground is the best cure for his disorder,” the servant retorted, his voice a blunt instrument. “If you get sober in bed, it gives you a headache; but you leap up from the hard ground like a lark in spring. Eh, Ulric?”

“He speaks sooth, young man,” said Ulric warmly.

“What, is the gentleman drunk?”

The servants burst into a hoarse, abrasive laugh that felt like a physical assault on Gerard’s sensitive ears. Ulric stopped, eyeing him with sudden suspicion. “Who are you, and where born, that know not the Count is ever drunk at this hour?” 

Gerard found his very identity questioned by his lack of local fluency. “I am a stranger,” he said, navigating the untranslatability of his own subjective displacement, “but a true man, and one that loves knowledge; therefore ask I questions, and not for love of prying.”

“If you be a true man,” said Ulric shrewdly, weaving the superficial commercialization of human interaction into the exchange, “then give us trinkgeld for the knowledge we have given you.”

Gerard looked blank but, putting a good face on it, offered what little his lean purse could spare, asking why they had transferred the master’s cloak to the beast. Inspired by the promise of coin, the servants offered two competing theories: one, that the seasoned toper would wake and curse them if he found his horse sweating in the cold; the other, that a water-drinking horse was a milksop needing external warmth, whereas a master filled with good, fermented beer possessed a store of inward heat that rendered the cloak an idle vanity. 

Both speakers, having partaken of the very same brew that had felled their master, grew enamored with their own logic. Their voices escalated into an acoustic violence that drowned out the green lord’s transition from snoring to growling. Hans cuffed Ulric; Ulric returned the blow. They went at it ding-dong over the horse’s mane, their squabble a petty, chaotic noise that panicked the poor beast. The horse ran backward, its iron heel striking a promontory of the green lord. Like a toad stung by a mythic spear, the lord started up howling, clapping one hand to the smart and tugging at his hilt with the other. The horse galloped off whinnying, the terrified servants fleeing before the volleying curses of the green noble, who rebounded from hedge to hedge in a headlong, zigzag career down the narrow lane.

Turning his back on this senseless, jarring commotion, Gerard walked calmly south, glad to have saved his four tin farthings. The sun was nearly setting, and as black clouds gathered, the cyclical turning of the seasons manifested in a torrential deluge. The rain fell in sheets, drenching the bewildered traveler and extinguishing the fading sun. 

He was entering a great wood. Huge branches shot across the narrow road, plunging him into an inky cave where he stumbled over the rugged floor. The acoustic environment shifted from the patter of rain to something primal and terrifying; wolves rose from their lairs, their baying echoing the ancient fears of his forebears. His excited ear caught the rustle of low branches and the light, pattering feet of unseen creatures. 

Presently, in the sea of ink, he spotted a great fiery star close to the ground. A hearth—the eternal, mythic center of domestic survival. “CANDLE! a CANDLE!” he shouted. At last, in the heart of the forest, he found a house radiating with loud voices. Lacking a signboard, it offered no commercial invitation, but trusting in the universal agrarian law of sanctuary, he opened the door.

He recoiled instantly, slapped in the face by an overwhelming olfactory landscape. The low room was dominated by a huge clay oven reaching to the ceiling. Wet clothes dried on lines and upon rustic bodies, evolving rank, inexpressible vapors. It was a profound study in the untranslatability of scent: the stewed, sweat-cured wool of agrarian laborers mingled with the pungent, sulfuric bite of crushed garlic, while one corner harbored the sickly, unwashed odor of a traveling family’s neglected children. The closed windows and intense heat locked the breath of forty souls into a palpable, stifling miasma.

Gerard, possessing the meticulous, sensory-driven nature of a true artist and appreciator of the world's physical textures, found the potent effluvia dizzying. He crept toward the light, analyzing the stratigraphy of the smells—the garlic describing a small aerial orbit, while the reeking rustic scent darted farthest, smelling of ancient goats dried by Nebuchadnezzar. He felt a cold wind snake about his legs, his head turning to lead.

Faint with hunger, he asked a garlic-scented local if this was an inn.

“Whence come you, who know not 'The Star of the Forest'?” the man replied, his broad dialect laced with the subtle power dynamics of a native exerting territorial dominance over a linguistic outsider. 

“I am a stranger; and in my country inns have aye a sign.”

“Droll country yours! What need of a sign to a public-house—a place that every soul knows?”

Too weary to battle the dominant lexicon, Gerard asked for the landlord. The native pointed to a middle-aged woman seated beside the oven, and the noisy room fell silent, each eye pivoting to observe Gerard zoologically. The landlady, a traditional agrarian elder, sat on a slightly elevated chair, stripping downy plumes from a huge heap of feathers, contributing a stuffy little malaria to the room’s heavy air. 

When Gerard asked for food, she opened her eyes in astonishment. “Supper is over this hour and more.”

“But I was benighted, and a stranger; and belated sore against my will.”

“What have I to do with that?” she dictated, enforcing the immutable, cyclical laws of the hearth. “All the world knows 'The Star of the Forest' sups from six till eight. Come before six, ye sup well; come before eight, ye sup as pleases Heaven; come after eight, ye get a clean bed, and a stirrup cup, or a horn of kine's milk, at the dawning.”

Gerard’s sigh was audible, prompting a titter from the watchful company. The loss of his familiar Dutch cultural memory had never felt so acute; here in Germany, he was subjected to the unyielding rules of a foreign tongue. Just then, a woman beckoned him from the corner. When he approached, she plumped a child into his arms. He stood transfixed, a jelly of lead, while the lynx-eyed conclave laughed at his elongated countenance.

“Never heed them,” the woman said cheerfully, rummaging through her bundles. “They know no better; how should they, bred an' born in a wood?” She procured a small tin plate and a dried pudding.

Gerard’s senses awakened. This was no mere utilitarian fuel; it was a masterpiece of ancestral preservation. He recognized the coarse-milled grain, studded with dried orchard fruits and bound in rendered suet—a profound testament to the agrarian imperative to survive the winter. “Put it in the stove,” she said.

He approached the obstructed oven. The landlady, recognizing the timeless domestic ritual, calmly pushed the hircine men aside with the impassive authority of a housewife moving furniture. Gerard placed his pudding within the mythic heat of the clay belly. As it baked, the crust caramelized, releasing the rich, savory alchemy of toasted grain and melting fat, an aroma that momentarily conquered the room's rank odors. He relished every bite, finding deep meaning in the sustenance that connected the soil to the traveler's weary bones. 

Suddenly, a hind hurled a bundle of straw into the room with a pitchfork, followed by another, transforming the space into a clean farmyard. The beds had come. 

Gerard finished his pudding, helped the landlady gather her feathers with ardent respect for her labor, and took his place on the straw in the very corner, a boon that kept him on the shore of the room's olfactory ocean rather than its stifling center. He was just dropping off when the hind shook him awake.

“She bade me say, 'One good turn deserves another,' and so here's your nightcap,” the hind said, thrusting a great oaken mug under Gerard's nose.

Gerard drank, but his gratitude ended in a wry face. The beer was muddy, heavily laden with the strange, medicinal twang of wild yeast, yarrow, and mugwort—a fiercely local, unhopped brewing tradition that defied his Hollander palate. 

“Trinke aus!” shouted the hind reproachfully, the imperative High German forcing submission. 

“Enow is as good as a feast,” Gerard replied softly, leaving the dregs. The hind, casting a look of pity, drained it to the bottom. Gerard bored a scabbard in the clean straw, sheathed his nose within it, and as the room erupted into a dozen snoring keys like an orchestra slowly tuning, his spirit drifted back to the phonetic comfort of Sevenbergen.

By morning, the acoustic space had cleared. Gerard paid his two pfenning. The hind hoisted a foaming milk-pail straight from the cow to Gerard’s lips. The raw, unpasteurized draught was a revelation—still warm, carrying the sweet, grassy terroir of the local pastures. When Gerard offered to pay for the profound sensory experience, the hind told him in broad patois that a man might swallow a skinful of milk without putting hand to pouch. Gerard found his benefactress, kissed her cheek at her Jove-like husband's behest, and walked out into the crisp air, where raindrops glittered on leaves like diamonds.

Eight leagues south, the endless cycle of his journey brought him to a massive arched gateway: a monastery. He was admitted to the guest chamber, and soon the bell tinkled for vespers. Gerard entered the church, where the acoustic ecology was breathtakingly pure. The choir sang with such exquisite, reverberating resonance that it felt like the choir of heaven, a sonic balm to his road-weary soul. Yet, the untranslatable absence of Margaret made him sigh bitterly in mid-rapture. 

At supper, he was served plain but remarkably wholesome food—thick, rustic bread from a wood-fired oven and a beautifully malted beer brewed within the convent’s own walls, a testament to the meticulous food history preserved by the monks. The next morning, a sympathetic monk pricked him a route on parchment, gifting him a silver guilden and advising him not to face the manifold perils of the way alone.

That evening, the circularity of travel brought him to a small, straggling town and an inn devoid of a sign, its identity marked only by the coats of arms of distinguished past visitors. It felt less like a hearth and more like a silent, acoustic mausoleum. Gerard hammered on the oak door, his calls swallowed by the dead air. Finally, a little round window opened, and a man’s head protruded cautiously like a tortoise. The grisly servant eyed Gerard stolidly, hoarding his words, exercising the subtle power of a local who controls the lexicon of entry. He nodded lazily to Gerard’s questions, eventually pointing to a small, vulnerable flank door before slamming the window shut.

Inside, Gerard found his way to the stove, where only a few live embers diffused a mild heat. When he asked the grim, white-bearded servant when supper would be served, the grisly tyrant counted on his fingers. “When I see thrice as many here as now.” When Gerard groaned, the tyrant wielded his authority: “Inns are not built for one; if you can't wait for the rest, look out for another lodging.”

Gradually, the silence was shattered as eighty persons trickled in. To Gerard’s refined senses, the place became a chamber of horrors. The acoustic space filled with the scraping of mud from shoes, while mothers compared ringworms. The grisly servant returned with an armful of wood, adding a log for every six guests—a raw, suffocating logic that drove the room's temperature to an unbearable extreme. 

When the table-cloths were brought in, Gerard gasped. They were brown and coarse, resembling worn-out agricultural sacks that smelled of oxidized grease and forgotten harvests. Water was brought in ewers, its murky surface devoid of any purifying quality. 

“Wash you first your water, and then a man may wash his hands withal,” Gerard protested. 

“An' it likes you not, seek another inn!” the waiter snapped.

A Burgundian soldier, his arbalest at his back, peered over Gerard’s shoulder. Seeing the dirty sackcloth, he laughed so loud the room rang, a booming disruption. “Courage! le diable est mort.”

Gerard stared. The soldier’s tones were hearty, but his French carried the weight of a dominant, imperial lexicon—the language of courts and conquerors that historically erased native dialects. Realizing that survival required adapting to the dominant tongue, Gerard smiled dryly and replied in the same language, noting that they had done well with the local water and linen to blacken themselves beyond recognition.

“Tiens, tiens!” cried the soldier, thrilled to hear the language of power. “V'la qui parle le Francais peu s'en faut.” He seated himself by Gerard, polluting the remaining acoustic space with a voluble, commercialized discourse of war, women, and pillage, heavily interlarded with curious oaths. As Gerard drew away, the grisly servant returned, superciliously counting the guests on his fingers like sheep before distributing a deal trencher and spoon to each, pulling them all once more into the timeless, inescapable routine of the evening meal.
