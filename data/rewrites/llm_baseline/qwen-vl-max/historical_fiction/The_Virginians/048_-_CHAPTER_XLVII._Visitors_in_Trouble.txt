Mr. Gumbo’s kindness had softened the harsh edge of Harry Warrington’s pride, and his second night in the spunging-house passed with a measure of comfort he had not known before. At least one person—his loyal servant—stood by him, offering quiet companionship and practical care. Yet though this small act of devotion eased his heart somewhat, it did little to mend his bitterness toward the world at large. To Harry, England was a land of coldness and self-interest. His pious Aunt Warrington, his noble friend March, even his cynical cousin Castlewood—all had promised aid, only to fail him when he needed them most. He would not beg from any of them again, not even if it meant twenty years in prison. What fools he had been to trust their words, to believe their friendship! There was no true loyalty here, only calculation and indifference. He would leave this place. He would go to Germany and fight under the king’s banner, or return to Virginia and vanish into the forests, hunting all day, managing his mother’s lands, becoming her steward. He might marry Polly Broadbent or Fanny Mountain—any woman who offered honest affection—and live as a simple farmer. Anything, anything but remain among these English gentlemen whose fine manners hid nothing but emptiness.

With that resolve burning within him, Harry rose early the next morning, his face calm, his spirit still aflame. Gumbo was already waiting outside his chamber, having come from Bond Street with letters. “I wanted to bring more clothes,” the faithful man said, “but Mr. Ruff, the landlord, wouldn’t let me bring anything else.”

Harry glanced at the letters without much interest. One by one he opened them—each a bill, demanding payment. Then came the fourth: a letter from the landlord, cold and firm. He would allow no more of Mr. Warrington’s belongings to leave the house. Unless the debt was paid, he would sell every item and claim the money for himself. And Gumbo, too, must find another place to sleep.

The black man sighed. “I found shelter with friends at Lord Wrotham’s house,” he said quietly. “Colonel Lambert’s people, sir.”

He looked intently at Harry. “And Miss Hetty—she fainted when she heard you were taken in. Mr. Lambert, he’s a good man. This morning he told me, ‘Gumbo, tell your master if he needs me, send word, and I’ll come.’”

At the mention of Hetty, something stirred in Harry’s heart. He didn’t believe the story about her fainting—he was used to Gumbo’s dramatic flair—but the thought that she had suffered because of him touched him deeply. Yet when Gumbo spoke of Colonel Lambert, a shadow fell across Harry’s face.

“Send to Lambert?” he muttered, grinding his teeth. “The man who insulted me, who threw my gifts back in my face? Even if I were starving, I’d rather die than ask him for a crust!”

Soon after, dressed in his finest gown, Harry called for breakfast and sent Gumbo with a brief note to Mr. Draper in the Temple, summoning him immediately. The tone of the message was stiff, almost imperious—so much so that when Draper read it, he felt affronted. “It’s as if he’s writing to one of his slaves,” he grumbled, “not to a freeborn English gentleman.” He nearly refused to go. “What right has a man in a debtor’s prison to give himself such airs? Hang him!” But he went anyway.

When Draper arrived, he found Harry seated on his bed like a prince in exile, wrapped in a brocade dressing gown, his hair neatly arranged. He motioned Gumbo to fetch a chair.

“Excuse me, madam,” Draper said, turning to Madame de Bernstein, who had been listening from the doorway, “but such haughtiness—I’m not accustomed to it!”

“Sit down, please, Mr. Draper,” she replied with a smile. “And carry on with your report.” She found his indignation amusing. She liked her nephew precisely because he remained proud even in ruin.

The plan for the meeting had been discussed between her and Draper the previous day. Draper was competent—usually effective in handling difficult clients. But today, his own irritation clouded his judgment. He could not understand the young man before him, nor the strange mixture of arrogance and vulnerability that defined him.

Perhaps, dear reader, you are one of those gentle souls who have dealt with men of lower station. Have you not noticed how easily they are offended—how a glance, a tone, a silence can wound them, though they themselves may not know why? A man who is not a gentleman often offends without meaning to. You disapprove, perhaps, and he feels it instantly. He sees scorn where there is none, and responds with fury. You speak plainly, and he thinks you mock. You are indifferent, and he hates you all the more—for you do not care enough to hurt him.

“Gumbo, a chair for Mr. Draper,” Harry commanded, folding his robe tightly around his legs. “Sit down and let us talk business. Thank you for coming so promptly. Did you hear about this unfortunate turn?”

Draper nodded. “Bad news travels fast, sir. I was eager to offer my services as soon as I heard.”

“I’ve been imprudent,” Harry admitted. “I lived beyond my means. I gambled with men wealthier than myself, and a run of bad luck left me with debts totaling five hundred pounds.”

“Five hundred pounds now in the office,” Draper confirmed.

“I thought I could settle it yesterday by appealing to a few friends,” Harry continued. “But I was mistaken. I need your help to raise the funds as quickly as possible.”

Draper frowned. “Hm.”

“You say it can be done?” Harry pressed.

“It can be done,” Draper replied slowly. “But it will take time. To access your estate in England, we must wait for your father’s death to be proven, the trustees released—none of which can happen until we hear from Lady Esmond Warrington in Virginia. That may take six months.”

“And my Bristol agent?”

“He’s authorized to pay only two hundred pounds a year.”

“So I have no choice but to turn to my aunt, Madame de Bernstein?”

“She will do anything for you,” Draper said. “She has told me so repeatedly. If she gives her word, you can walk out of here today.”

“Then go to her for me, Mr. Draper,” Harry said. “I didn’t wish to burden my family, but I cannot endure this imprisonment any longer. Tell her where I am, what has happened. Disguise nothing. And say that I trust her kindness to release me from this—this disgrace.”

His voice cracked slightly, and he rubbed his eyes.

“Sir,” Draper said, studying him closely, “I was with her yesterday. We discussed your situation—the entire matter. I won’t call it disgraceful, but it is certainly unfortunate.”

“Does she know everything?” Harry asked sharply.

“Every detail,” Draper replied. “The pawned watches, the unpaid bills.”

Harry flushed crimson.

“It’s an unfortunate affair,” Draper continued. “Pawning items you never paid for—especially when it involves credit. It could lead to legal trouble. In court, barristers don’t care about rank. When they put a man on trial—”

“Good God, sir,” Harry interrupted, rising from the bed, “do you think a gentleman of my standing couldn’t take a watch on credit without intending to cheat?”

“Of course not,” Draper said, winking. “But the law doesn’t always see it that way.”

“That’s exactly what my aunt says—‘the most imprudent thing I ever heard of.’”

“Then you agree with her,” Harry said slowly, each word measured.

“I’m only doing my duty,” Draper said. “Like a doctor telling a patient he’s ill. I must warn you of the risks.”

“And yet you can’t help me pay the debt?” Harry demanded. “You’ve come only to tell me I may be accused of fraud?”

“Of obtaining goods under false pretenses? Yes, sir. I can’t help it. But I *can* help you avoid it—if you’re willing to accept certain conditions.”

Harry stared.

“There’s money in my pocket,” Draper said. “Not mine, but your aunt’s. She’s ready to pay the debt immediately—on one condition.”

“What is it?”

“She wants you to break off your engagement to Miss Maria Esmond.”

Harry’s face brightened.

“She believes the match will ruin you,” Draper continued. “She knows you gave your word, but she also knows the promise was made under duress. It was recovered—dropped at Tunbridge, as I understand.”

“Yes,” Harry said. “She still has it.”

“She does,” Draper confirmed. “And letters compromising her family were found as well. But you were away when they were discovered. No fault of yours.”

“And you want me to break off the marriage?”

“I want you to give me your word of honour,” Draper said, stepping forward. “Say you’ll withdraw. Say yes, and I’ll pay every shilling, take you out of here, and put you in a coach—either to your aunt’s or White’s Club—with two hundred pounds in your pocket. Say yes, and shake hands with me. Don’t waste your life behind bars!”

For a moment, Harry hesitated. The thought of freedom, of escape from this humiliation, was intoxicating. His feelings for Maria had long since faded. If his aunt could free him from this bond, how grateful he would be.

“Come, sir,” Draper urged, holding out his hand. “Don’t dither. If I’d married every woman I promised, I’d be like the Grand Turk or Captain Macheath!”

But Harry stepped back. He folded his robe tighter and turned away.

“Give me an hour,” he said. “I must think.”

Draper clenched his jaw. “Very well, sir. Very well.” He grabbed his hat, stormed out, muttering curses under his breath.

“Won’t take my hand, eh?” he growled as he descended the stairs. “Will tell me in an hour! I’ll show him what an hour is!”

Back in his chambers, Draper raged, berating his clerks, sending a message to the Baroness, then going through his usual routine—seeing clients, eating dinner—before finally making his way to Cursitor Street.

“The Fortunate Youth in his room?” he asked the bailiff’s assistant.

“Mr. Warrington is in his apartment,” the man replied, “but—”

And then he winked and touched his nose.

“But what?” Draper snapped.

“My name is Costigan,” the man said proudly. “My family is noble. My native city is Dublin, Mr. Six-and-eightpence!”

A strong smell of spirits filled the air.

“Confound you!” Draper roared. “Let me pass!”

“I can hear you perfectly,” the man retorted. “Except your h’s—you drop them like stones. And don’t call me names, or your fingers and my nose will become very familiar.”

Draper pushed past, but the bailiff blocked his path. “Walk in, sir!”

As he entered, a lady hurried down the stairs, her face covered by a capuchin and handkerchief. She gasped in alarm as Draper tried to follow.

“Keep your distance!” the bailiff shouted. “This way, madam!”

He slammed the door in Draper’s face.

That evening, at six o’clock, the old Baroness paced her drawing-room, her nerves frayed. She had delayed dinner, ignored her cook’s complaints, ordered extra dishes as if expecting guests. When a coach stopped at her door, she rushed to the window.

“Mr. Draper,” the servant announced.

He bowed deeply.

“Where is the boy?” she demanded. “I told you to bring him! How dare you come without him?”

“It is not my fault, madam,” Draper said. “Mr. Warrington refuses to come.”

And he recounted the interview—every word, every gesture, every refusal.
