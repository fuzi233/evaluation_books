I pushed to my feet to resume my journey, tramping mile after mile across the sun-scorched, barren plain. My chest ached for a mountain, even a tall jagged rock, whose peak might let me peer past the dismal flatlands and choked river channels to catch a glimmer of hope on the far edge of the world. But what good would even that foresight have done me? What dwells inside a person, not what lies stretched beyond their line of sight, is the true architect of what befalls them: the world’s work upon your soul is the event, not the sights you see. To foresee is not to understand, else the latent prophecy all men carry in their bones would surface far more often than it does.

The sun hung halfway down the western sky when I spotted a rugged rocky slope ahead, but by the time I neared its base that urge to climb had drained out of me, replaced by a overwhelming, bone-deep longing to lie down. The sun was nearly set by then, the air thickening with dusk, and at my feet lay a carpet of moss so soft and vividly green it might have been stitched for a king’s couch. I threw myself down, and weariness began to ebb at once: the moment my head touched the moss, I heard the third time the low rush of many hidden waters below me, weaving broken, ethereal harmonies as they tumbled over the stones of their long-buried channels. It was the loveliest chaos of raw musical material, that subterranean harp sending up gurgles and bell-like drips that tangled into overlapping, clashing melodies held together by a single unshakable refrain. What a masterpiece Händel might have spun from that endless, haunting sound!

As I lay listening, my eyes wandered up the steep rocky face above me, reading the carved mark left by a thousand-years-dead cataract that had once roared down this same slope, feeding the river channels I had followed all the way to its foot. My chest swelled at the thought of that splendid, roaring tumult, waves dancing in wild revelry as they fell, massing their thunder into one great organ roar in the pool below. But the hidden brooks soon lulled me to sleep, their lullabies winding through my dreams.

I woke before the sun cleared the horizon, and scrambled up the rock face at once to see what lay on the other side. To my dismay, there was nothing but a desert of the finest, powdery sand. Not a trace remained of the river that had once plunged down those cliffs: the fine drift had filled its old course level with the rest of the dreary expanse. Glancing back, I could now make out the two branches the old river had split into at the falls: one whose bank I had followed to the foot of this scaur, and the other I had crossed long before to reach the Evil Wood. I could see that wood faint and far off between the two old river beds on the horizon. Before me and to my left, the desert stretched past the edge of my sight, but far to the right the skyline lifted, the rise of land promising the forest my strange hostess had directed me to find.

I sat down to dig in my pocket for the half-loaf of bread I had carried away with me, and only then understood what the hostess had meant when she spoke of it not keeping. The bread had shrunk and hardened to a solid, heavy stone. I tossed it aside, and set out again.

Around noon I reached a scattering of tamarisk and juniper trees, then a handful of stunted firs. Thickets grew denser, firs grew taller, until I found myself in a full pine forest identical to the one where the Little Ones tended their found babies, and I realized I had wandered back to a far stretch of the same woods. But what did place matter, when everywhere felt the same as nowhere? I had not yet done anything in this world to turn any spot into a home, into somewhere that mattered. I was not truly alive yet, only dreaming that I lived, nothing more than a disembodied consciousness with a view of the world. I had been no more real than that in the old world I had left behind, but now I could see the truth of it plain as day. I swore to myself that if I caught even the faintest glint of that enchanted mirror that would send me back to my old life, I would turn miles out of my way to avoid it. Here I might finally learn to be something real, by doing something real. I could not bear the thought of waking up back in my old bed, knowing all my wanderings had amounted to nothing, a dozen half-starts with not a single end to show for them. The Little Ones would face whatever fate was waiting for them, I would never meet that terrible witch, the dead would ripen and rise without me. I would wander forever rather than end up with that empty, hollow wake-up.

I went deeper into the wood, weary, eager to rest somewhere among the trees. The trunks grew massive, spaced in almost perfect geometric order with wide, clear gaps between them. There was barely any undergrowth, and I could see long stretches in every direction. The forest felt like a vast, empty cathedral, solemn, silent, entirely uninhabited. I met no creature on two legs or four all that day, though every so often something swift and something slow would dart across the far space my eyes were fixed on, distant enough that they only amplified the strange sense of vast, empty quiet. I heard a few hidden birds, and saw dozens of butterflies: some painted with impossible, blazing hues, others a pure, dazzling white that seemed to glow in the dappled shade.

I came to a stretch where the pines thinned out, making room for flowering shrubs, and my heart lifted at the sight, hopeful that some dwelling was nearby. I followed the direction where roses grew thicker and thicker, starved for the sight and voice of another living soul, human or otherwise, any being I could even partially understand. What a terrible hell, I thought, to wander forever alone, trapped inside your own existence that never reaches outward, never widens itself by sharing life with another, bound tight by the thin cords of your own petty quirks, an eternal prisoner in the dungeon of your own skull. I was learning fast that it is impossible to even live for yourself alone, unless you are in the company of others — though sadly, in that case, living for yourself becomes far too easy. Evil is only ever a parasite on the tree of goodness, selfishness a leech that can only feed on shared life. Back in my old world, I had the habit of singing to myself when I was alone. Here, not a single crooning murmur ever crossed my lips. There I had never bothered to find a close friend; here, the clumsy affection of a half-wit would have felt like the greatest divine gift. “If only I had a dog to love,” I sighed, and stared back in wonder at my old self, who had preferred the company of a book or pen to a real man or woman, who would have chased away the author of a story he was enjoying just so he could get back to the words on the page. I had chosen the dead made of ink, not the living, the thought not the thinker. “Any living person,” I said to myself, “is worth more than the greatest book ever written.” I had neglected my living brothers and sisters back home, and now I was left with nothing but endless empty woods.

The pines thinned further, their trunks growing even larger, soaring like stone columns straining to hold up the sky. More varied trees appeared, the forest growing lusher, until the roses themselves grew into full trees, their blooms blazing with impossible splendour. Suddenly I spotted what looked like a huge castle, but its edges were so indistinct I could not be sure it was anything more than a random arrangement of tree trunks. As I drew nearer, the shape held, but its lines never sharpened, never grew more defined. When I stood right before it, I still could not tell what it was. It was no habitable house or castle, certainly, but it might be a ruin overgrown entirely with ivy and roses — though I could not spot a single fragment of stone wall anywhere through the foliage. Again and again I thought I glimpsed masonry, only for it to dissolve before my eyes. Could the ivy have wrapped itself around a giant edifice and slowly consumed it, its tangled branches retaining the shape of the walls it had devoured? I could make no certain sense of it.

Right before me was a rectangular empty gap, the ghost of a doorway with no door. I stepped through, and found myself in an open space like a giant great hall, its floor carpeted with grass and wild flowers, its walls and ceiling woven entirely of ivy, vine, and climbing roses. There could be no better place to spend the night. I gathered a pile of dry withered leaves, tucked them in a corner, and threw myself down on the makeshift couch. A red sunset flooded the hall, the night was warm, and I lay staring up at the living ceiling, tangled with twigs and foliage, peepholes to the sky far above. My eyes wandered through the web of branches until the sun set, and the dark crept over the world. The red roses turned black, until only the yellow and white blooms were visible, and when they too faded the stars hung through the leaves like living topazes, throbbing and sparkling with a dozen different colours. I was lying under the boughs of a tree straight out of Aladdin’s cave. Then I noticed the canopy was full of little nests, tiny heads peeking out of them every so often to chirp softly before vanishing again. For a little while there was rustling, quiet stirring, the soft little noises of baby birds saying their small bedtime prayers, until the dark deepened, every mother bird tucked her brood tight under her wings, and the whole bird nursery went still, drifting into sleep. A few more soft flutters made me look up: an owl sailed silently across the hall. I only caught a glimpse of it, but I felt the cool soft gust of its wings brushing the air more than once. The mother birds did not stir — they knew it hunted mice, not chicks.

Around midnight I woke completely, roused by a soft, muffled revelry. The noise was not loud, and it was not far off, it was right there, attenuate, thin as mist. My eyes were dazzled at first, and I could see nothing, until slowly they cleared. I was still lying on my pile of dry leaves, in a splendid hall of light. Before me, a crowd of gorgeously dressed men and women in soft, flowing robes danced, none of them able to see me. They wove their dances one after another, acting out the old story of all life: first meetings, burning passions, bitter partings. As a student of Shakespeare, I knew every traditional dance referenced in his plays, and I recognized several immediately: the slow, stately minuet, the solemn pavan, the skipping country hey, the quick, swirling coranto, the leaping lavolta. Their fine clothes were as ancient as the dances themselves. The moon had risen while I slept, shining down through the leafy roof, its light crisscrossed with so many shadows that at first I could barely make out their faces. But there was something very strange about them, and I sat up to get a better look.

Heaven help me, those were no faces. They were bare skulls, gleaming hard bone, jaws stripped of flesh, truncated noses, lipless bared teeth that would never smile again. Some of those teeth flashed white and sharp and murderous, others were fogged with the dark of decay, broken and gapped, stained the colour of the earth they had rotted in for centuries. Worse still, their empty eye sockets were not empty. Every single one held a living, lidless, glowing eye. In those ruined faces, eyes blazed, sparkled, glowed in every colour, every emotion: the proud dark lustrous eye, condescending to everything it rested on, was the most terrifying of all. The soft, languishing, amorous eye was the most repulsive. The dim, sad eyes, the ones that fit most naturally to the bony sockets, were so unbearably mournful they pulled at my heart no matter how much horror I felt at the sight.

I stood up and walked straight among the apparitions, desperate to understand what they were. Were they souls, or were their rhythmic dances nothing more than phantoms of long-dead people? Not a single one of them noticed me, did not break step for an instant. I was not real to them. But how real were they to each other? Surely they saw their companions just as I saw them, each a walking death with living eyes. Did they know how they looked to each other? Had they spent their whole lives using their faces as masks, hiding their true selves behind false expressions to seem what they were not, and so been stripped of their soft flesh forever, condemned to wander faceless in their bone until they repented? How long, I wondered, would they flaunt that facelessness, their eyes trapped in bone? How long would their terrible punishment last? Had they begun to love, to grow wise? Had they yet given in to the shame that had hunted them down? I heard no sound come from their bare mouths, no word spoken. Had they been stripped of speech because they spent their whole lives lying? Their eyes spoke, longing to be understood, but what was it they spoke — truth, or more lies? They seemed to know each other, surely they must have long grown accustomed to judging which skull was beautiful, which plain. When a dancer drifted close to me, they passed straight through my body like I was no more than mist, and I could tell, even without looking, whether it was a man or a woman that had walked through me. Many of the skulls still held their hair, no matter how fine or luxuriant it was, it looked horrid resting bare on the bone of the forehead and temple. Often the outer ear still clung to the bone, a pearl, opal, or diamond glimmering at its tip, nestled under dark brown locks, raven tresses, or pale gold curls, looking obscene and alien against the damp yellow or ivory white naked bone. I glanced down and saw their daintily arched insteps peeking out under their gowns, then glanced up and saw their soft plump shoulders, that narrowed halfway up the neck to the ridged bone of a bare skull.

The music grew wilder, the dance faster and faster. Eyes flared, jewels glittered, casting streaks of colour and fire over the pale grinning skulls that wove through the hall in a tangled, intricate maze of motion, until the dance stopped all at once, every eye turning to the same spot.

In the vine doorway stood a woman, perfect in form, perfect in poise, her skin glowing with warm, living colour, staring down at the company like a goddess on her pedestal. The dancers all froze like they had been cursed, locked into a new death by the sight of that living warmth. Dead things, her scornful glance said, I live.

Then, as if caught all at once by a gust of wind, they turned to their partners and burst back into dance, a new look in their eyes, once lonely, now lit with shared triumph. You too, they seemed to say, will soon be as weak as us. You will soon become exactly like us.

I turned back to the woman, and saw a small dark shadow spreading across her side. She had seen the shift in the dancers’ eyes, she looked down, understood exactly what their glowing eyes were saying. She pressed both her lovely hands to the dark shadow, let out a smothered, terrified cry, and fled.

The birds rustled in their nests, and a flash of pure joy lit every eye in the dancing skulls, when a warm wind roared through the hall, growing stronger as it swept across them, snuffing out every spark of warmth that clung to the apparitions. The low moon still glimmered faint and sickly on the horizon, and the dim, turbid glow of all their eyes gave me more than enough light to see what happened next. Every shape crumbled like a snowman caught in the sun. Flesh peeled off the bone in thin papery flakes, dropping like dirty snow from under their gowns and doublets. The clothes fell to the floor in rags and strips, and the bare, lank white skeletons stepped out of the decaying flesh and fabric, standing alone amid the litter on the flower floor. A faint rattling shiver ran through the whole company, and one by one the glowing eyes in their sockets went dark. The darkness closed around me, thick with the weight of loneliness. For a moment the dry leaves swept rustling all one way, then the wind died, and the owl glided silent through the quiet night.

I had not felt fear, not for a single second. It was true that anyone who crossed into this strange other world had to leave their fear behind, but I could not take any credit for my courage. No deliberate bravery was at work in me — I simply was not afraid. I did not know why I had no cause to fear, nor why I should have been afraid. I was not even afraid of fear, the most dangerous of all dangers. I stepped out of the vine hall back into the wood, ready to resume my journey. A new moon was rising over the treetops, and I turned my face to its light.
