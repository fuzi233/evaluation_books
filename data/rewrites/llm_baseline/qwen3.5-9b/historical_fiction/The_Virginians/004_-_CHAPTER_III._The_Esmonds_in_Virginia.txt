Colonel Henry Esmond, a veteran who had borne the rank of Colonel through the wars of Queen Anne's reign, found himself, at the conclusion of that turbulent era, embroiled in conspiracies aimed at restoring the Stuart line to the English throne. Fortunately for his safety, the nation ultimately embraced the House of Hanover. While many staunch opponents fled the realm, seeking exile across the seas, Colonel Esmond was advised by his friends to follow suit. Though he deeply regretted his role in the failed uprising, and recognizing that the new monarch, the Augustus Prince of Hanover, was a ruler of profound peaceability, his friends swiftly facilitated a reconciliation.

It is recorded that Colonel Esmond hailed from the distinguished English lineage of Castlewood, in the County of Hants. It was widely known that King James II and his son had once proffered the title of Marquis to both the Colonel and his father. Furthermore, the King had suggested that the Colonel might assume the Irish peerage inherited by his family, had it not been for a minor procedural oversight which the Colonel simply refused to rectify. Wearied by the ceaseless political strife of England and plagued by domestic troubles on the continent, the Colonel elected to settle in Virginia. There, he took possession of a substantial estate originally granted to his ancestor by King Charles I.

In this American haven, his daughter and grandsons were born, and eventually, his wife passed away. Upon her marriage, she had been the widow of the Colonel's cousin—the unfortunate Viscount Castlewood, who met his end in a duel with Lord Mohun near the twilight of King William's reign. True to tradition, the Colonel named his American residence "Castlewood," mirroring the ancestral home left behind in England. Indeed, every custom of Virginia was here lovingly modeled after English precedent. It remained a fiercely loyal colony; the Virginians proudly asserted that King Charles II had ruled over Virginia long before he ascended the throne in England. Both the English monarchy and the Church were revered with unwavering fidelity. The local gentry, allied with prominent English families, held their heads high above the Dutch merchants of New York and the mercantile Puritans of Pennsylvania and New England. Few places in the world were less inclined toward republicanism than this great province, which would later lead the historic revolt against the British Crown.

The Virginian gentry lived upon their vast estates in a manner almost feudal. Each property employed a multitude of hands—both purchased and indentured servants—who were entirely subject to the master's command. The land provided ample food, livestock, and game, while the great rivers teemed with fish. Ships laden with tobacco harvested from private wharves along the Potomac or James River sailed to London or Bristol, returning with English goods and manufactured items, the sole commodities the Virginian elite deemed worth cultivating. Hospitality was boundless; no stranger was ever turned away from their gates. The gentry traveled between homes in a state nearly indistinguishable from feudal homage.

At this time, the institution of slavery was not yet a subject of public debate. To own Black servants caused no offense among Virginian gentlemen, nor was the dominion exercised over the Negro race typically savage. Provisions were plentiful, and the enslaved population, though laboring hard, appeared content and rarely unhappy. One might have preached abolition to Madam Esmond of Castlewood with the same ease as advising her to let her horses roam free; she possessed no doubt that the whip and the corn-bag were essential tools for both beasts and men. Her father, possessing a skeptical mind on many matters, harbored private doubts, yet these never manifested as open dissent. Instead, he remained somewhat disaffected rather than rebellious. Once a participant in active life at home, he might formerly have craved its rewards, but in his later years, he showed little interest in them. Some unseen tragedy had cast a permanent melancholy over his existence. He was not unhappy—he was kind, affectionate, and even obsequious toward the women of his family, whom he rarely contradicted—but he suffered from a spiritual bankruptcy that his soul never fully recovered. He endured life rather than enjoying it, yet paradoxically, he was happiest in his final moments as he prepared to leave it.

Having lost his wife, his daughter assumed control of the Colonel's affairs. He surrendered his duties to her with total acquiescence; provided he retained his books and quiet, he desired nothing else. When guests arrived at Castlewood, he entertained them lavishly, displaying a pleasant, sarcastic wit. Yet, he felt no sorrow when they departed.

"My love, I shall not be sorry to go myself," he once told his daughter. "And you, though the most affectionate of daughters, will find comfort again in time. Why should I, who am so old, be romantic? You may, who are still a young creature." He spoke without intending all he said, for the lady he addressed was a pragmatic individual with little capacity for romance.

After fifteen years of prosperous residence on his great estate, the Colonel agreed to his daughter's ambitious plan to construct a mansion far grander and more enduring than the simple wooden dwelling in which he had resided, ensuring his heirs would possess a home befitting their noble name. Several neighbors had recently erected handsome residences; perhaps it was her ambition to secure higher social standing that drove this desire for improved quarters. Colonel Esmond, however, cared little for physical quarters or genealogical distinctions. Yet, his daughter held a lofty opinion of her lineage's merit and antiquity. In his serene, declining years, the Colonel grew exquisitely calm and good-natured, easily accommodating his child's peculiarities. He even assisted her with his considerable antiquarian knowledge and his proficiency in painting.

Knowledge of heraldry had once formed part of the education for most noble ladies and gentlemen. During her visit to Europe, Miss Esmond had diligently studied family histories and pedigrees, returning to Virginia armed with a trove of documents she accepted with grave authority, alongside the most edifying volumes then published in France and England concerning the noble science. These works satisfied her completely: the Esmonds were descended not only from Norman warriors who accompanied their victorious leader to England but also from native Englishmen of royal dignity. Two magnificent heraldic trees, painted by the Colonel's own hand, depicted the family sprouting from two illustrious sources: on one side, Emperor Charlemagne, rendered in plate armor with his imperial mantle and diadem; on the other, Queen Boudicca, portrayed in the light costume of an ancient British queen, wearing a prodigious golden crown, a fur-trimmed mantle, and a lovely, symmetrical figure tastefully tattooed with brilliant blue designs. From these two noble stocks, the family tree rose until it converged in the thirteenth century within the person of a fortunate Esmond who claimed descent from both.

Regarding the Warrington family into which she married, good Madam Rachel paid scant attention. She styled herself Esmond-Warrington but was universally addressed as "Madam Esmond of Castlewood" after assuming control of the domain following her father's death. It is feared that disputes over precedence occasionally troubled her temper. Although her father had possessed a Marquess's patent from King James—which he had burned and disowned—she frequently acted as if the document remained valid and in full force. She regarded the English Esmonds as inferior to her own branch and made no secret of asserting her superiority over the entire colonial aristocracy. Consequently, quarrels, angry words, and even occasional scuffles arose at the Governor's assemblies in Jamestown.

But why recall these petty squabbles? Are not the participants beyond the reach of conflict today, and has not the Republic ended such social inequalities? Before the establishment of Independence, no country was more aristocratic than Virginia. Thus, the Virginians whose history we chronicle were bred to hold the highest respect for home institutions, and the rightful king could not have found two more faithful subjects than the twin sons of Castlewood.

When the boys' grandfather died, their mother, in great state, proclaimed her eldest son, George, as the successor and heir to the estate. Harry, George's younger brother by merely thirty minutes, was constantly enjoined to respect his senior. The entire household was instructed to honor him, including the numerous Black servants, who formed a large and happy family, and the indentured servants from Europe, whose lot was managed as tolerably as possible under the Lady of Castlewood's governance. Within the whole family, there was scarcely a rebel except for Mrs. Esmond's faithful companion, Madam Mountain, and Harry's foster-mother—a devoted Black woman who could never comprehend why her child should not be placed ahead of his brother. She maintained that Harry was handsomer, stronger, and cleverer, though in truth, the twins bore little difference in beauty, strength, or stature.

In temperament, they were strikingly different, yet in appearance, they were so similar that, aside from the color of their hair, it would have been difficult to distinguish them. Lying in their beds, crowned with the vast ribboned nightcaps worn by our great ancestors, it was nearly impossible for anyone but a nurse or mother to tell them apart. Alike in form, they differed profoundly in spirit. The elder, George, was peaceful, studious, and silent; the younger, Harry, was warlike and noisy. Harry was slow to begin learning but quick once started, whereas George was diligent from the outset. No threat of the birch rod would provoke Harry to study in an idle mood, nor would it prevent George from assisting his brother.
