A heavy, unrelenting dark cloud settled over Gerard’s once noble, bright spirit. His pure, unmatched love for Margaret had long been his guiding polar star across every hardship and strange land; now that star had been snuffed out, and he drifted adrift on a sunless, hopeless sea.

He did not only suffer despair: he burned with sharp, biting exasperation that every bit of his self-denial, fortitude, years of peril, and steadfast virtue had been not just wasted, but counterproductive. It gnawed at him ceaselessly that if he had stayed home lazy, selfish, and unadventurous, he would never have left Margaret unprotected, and she would still be alive. The two poisons of grief and fury raged together in his young blood, maddening him and eroding every moral boundary he had ever held. He threw himself fiercely into every available pleasure, and in 15th century Rome, pleasure of the kind that numbs pain was almost indistinguishable from vice. He chased wine, gambling, and loose company, anything that could grant him an hour of fevered excitement and a single moment of escape from his thoughts, flinging himself into these excesses the way a man tired of life might run headlong into enemy musket fire. The large sum of money he had saved carefully for Margaret’s future gave him endless means for debauchery, and he soon became the leader of the very band of reckless rakes he had once kept strictly at arm’s length.

His heart rotted alongside his morals. He snapped at his kind old landlady when she tried to offer gentle, well-meaning warnings to pull him back from the edge, and finally moved to a distant, unfamiliar quarter of the city entirely to escape all remonstrance and every last reminder of his old, diligent life. Weeks of this debauchery left his hand too unsteady to produce the fine, precise calligraphy he had once prided himself on, and his patience for quiet craft vanished as his lazy, indulgent habits took root. He abandoned writing entirely, and took to painting portraits instead – though he neglected that work too, the second his idle rakish companions knocked on his door to drag him out drinking. He waded deeper and deeper into foul, mirey waters, chasing the sorry, empty prize of total oblivion.

It serves no purpose to lay out every coarse, vice-ridden scene Gerard took part in at this lowest point. What matters is to state the plain, unvarnished truth: he had become a rake, a drunkard, one of the wildest, most unmoored young men in all Rome. Those who soften or slur the wickedness of the formerly good do no favours to truth, or to struggling sinners who deserve no false, sugar-coated fables to rob them of their chance at redemption. Gerard never did anything by halves, and he was not half-hearted in his self-destruction either.

His old well-meaning acquaintances gossiped about his downfall constantly. His former landlady begged Teresa, her friend, to help track him down, and Teresa passed the story on to her husband Lodovico, the streetwise swordsman, asking him to keep an eye out for the lost young painter. “You would remember his face, Lodovico mio?” she asked. “A man in my line of work never forgets a face,” he told her, “least of all a benefactor’s. But you know I almost never leave the house by daylight.” Teresa sighed. “How long will that go on?” “Till some rich hot-headed cavalier runs his sword through me,” he said grimly. “They will never let a poor man like me turn to an honest trade.”

Pietro Vanucci, the painter who had never handled prosperity well, had been kicked out of his old lodgings a short while prior for coming home at all hours of the night. He ran into Gerard on the street, greeted him like an old friend, and soon moved into Gerard’s new house of loose company. He brought with him a sharp, stunningly beautiful teenage apprentice named Andrea, who ground his colours, served as his model, and was as quick-witted as a needle. Pietro feigned great warmth and friendship for Gerard, who – every last trace of old gentle sentiment burnt out of him – accepted the affection coldly. Pietro was not just a libertine, but a bitter misanthrope and open atheist, and the two men moved through the city in a strange, mismatched pair.

One day, after Gerard had all but ruined his health, stripped the bloom off his once famous good looks, and run through most of his savings, Pietro organized a grand, absurd day trip: a cruise up the Tiber in a galley pulled by buffaloes. Lorenzo de’ Medici had imported these massive beasts to Florence only three years prior, and they were a total novelty in Rome. Nothing would satisfy Pietro, the newly wealthy beggar-on-horseback, but to be towed upriver by the strange brutes. Their rules for the party were simple: every rake must bring a handsome woman as his guest, and anyone who failed to bring a pretty companion would pay a fine. The man who brought the loveliest guest of all would be crowned with laurel and named a public benefactor.

The whole wild crowd gathered at the embarkation point – all except Gerard. They waited, first patiently, then with growing irritation. Pietro laughed it off. “I heard him say he forgot to find a fardingale for his lady,” he joked. “The good lad is hunting for a beauty fine enough to hold her own among all these peerless dames. Be patient, ladies, he has a hard task ahead of him!”

At last they spotted Gerard in the distance, walking arm in arm with a tall, striking woman. “She is certainly long enough,” one of the assembled ladies snarked, squinting at her from afar. “Mercy, look how big her strides are,” added another. “Of course she hurries,” Pietro excused her. “She is eager to join such fine company.” But the second the pair drew close, every taunt died on their lips. Gerard’s companion was a peerless beauty, who outshone the entire boatload the way the rising sun snuffs out all the stars. She was tall but perfectly proportioned, straight as an arrow yet supple as a young panther, her face a perfect oval, her forehead pale, her cheeks rich olive with warm blood glowing just under the surface, and her glorious eyes framed by thick, silken long eyelashes that looked capable of sweeping men’s hearts away by the handful. She had saucy, full red lips and teeth polished white as ivory.

All the women on the boat were visibly deflated by the sight of her, while the men went wild with delight, cheering and waving their caps. One over-excited man even knelt on the boat’s gunwale and swore she must be divine. His girlfriend promptly grabbed his hair and wrenched it, while the “goddess” gave him a playful little kick, and he toppled backwards flat onto his back. She stepped right over him without deigning to glance his way, and took the best seat right at the prow. Pietro Vanucci sat frozen, gawking at her with his mouth hanging open like a dead codfish.

The drover shouted commands to the buffaloes, the ropes tightened, and the galley lurched upriver. The women grumbled about the strange beasts, complaining their horns were terrifying, their nostrils were ugly gaping holes. One hot-headed young gallant jumped ashore, drew his sword, and began goading the buffaloes to make them run faster, ignoring the drovers’ shouts of protest. The big beasts only snorted and flicked their tails, no faster than before, making the whole boatload roar with laughter – until he jabbed the patriarch old lead bull. The massive beast spun instantly, ran its two long horns straight through the man’s doublet, and tossed him 20 feet through the air in a great parabola, until he splashed sitting upright, still waving his sword blindly, into the yellow murk of the Tiber.

The women screamed in shock, all except Gerard’s beautiful companion, who swore under her breath, seized the helm, and steered the galley out to pull the sputtering man back into the boat, dripping and confused. He stared around, peevish and bewildered. “I cannot make head nor tail of that,” he muttered, which set the whole party laughing harder than before. Gerard’s lady teased him that he was enjoying his natural element, and advised him to go back to pricking the buffaloes until his wits caught up, to work the riddle out as slowly as lawyers climb to heaven, and buffaloes climb the Tiber.

The assembled cavaliers praised her wit as loudly as they praised her beauty – all except Pietro, who now sat coiled up in the corner, his face half covered, two bright eyes twinkling with barely contained mirth as he never took his gaze off her. She did not spare him so much as a glance, except a casual, unremarking sweep of her lashes now and then, as if he was no more notable than the wooden benches under them.

The fun broke apart when the humiliated ladies, furious that every man’s attention was fixed on the new arrival, began muttering insults about her under their breath. The beautiful girl had none of the quiet tact to rise above it, and she snapped back openly. “Not a single one of you wears beauty that was given by Nature,” she announced, pointing rudely in one lady’s face. “All that paint you wear is sold in every shop: cerussa white lead for your skin, stibium kohl for your eyes, purpurissum red for your cheeks. I use those things too, bless you – but I never paint my face with them.” She went on to mock their dyed horse-tail hair, their padded bosoms, their absurdly tall wooden chopine platform shoes that turned little stunted dwarfs into apparent giants, and rattled off the crude local rhyme about them: “tall of wood, fat of rags, red of dye, white of lime.”

The ladies shrieked back insults, calling her rude and vulgar, saying her figure was shaped like a coal shovel. Gerard, woken out of his dark, brooding reverie, told his companion sharply to hold her tongue, and stop being insolent. “The grave will close over your beauty just as it has over far fairer women than you,” he said. She pouted, and whispered back that she had only done it to make him laugh, that she saw how sad and distracted he was, and if she was really the great lady everyone took her for, she would do anything to chase his grief away. Gerard sighed, shook his head, and stroked her jet black hair as one might pat the head of an affectionate loyal dog.

Just then, a large galley drifting down the river got tangled in their tow ropes. Seated under a rich canopy on that passing vessel was Princess Claelia, attended by a whole retinue of gallants and servants. Gerard looked up, saw her, and his face flushed bright red with shame as he snatched his hand away from Andrea’s head. He remembered, not three months prior, he had turned down her passionate, refined, illicit offer of love – a temptation so great it might have melted an anchorite – just to stay loyal to Margaret. Now here he was, wallowing in this gutter of vice, ashamed to be seen by her.

Two of the Roman ladies, who had long suspected “the beauty” was no woman, seized a lapful of almonds and tossed the whole handful into her lap on a whim. She clamped her knees tightly shut by instinct, to catch the nuts before they fell through her legs. A woman, after all, would spread her knees wide to catch nuts in her apron. “Got you!” the lady who threw the nuts cried. “You are a man, or a boy! Confess it!” Andrea only casually cracked an almond between his teeth and said calmly, “They have found me out, Ser Gerard.”

Pietro burst into roaring, uncontrollable laughter. He announced that the “goddess” was none other than his 17-year-old apprentice Andrea, who ground his colours and posed for his paintings. All the men were furious they had been pranked, but the women swarmed around the beautiful boy, cooing over his eyes, his eyelashes, his perfect teeth, his rosy cheeks, vowing no one would harm a hair on his head – lavishing all their adoration on him to get revenge on their men for gawking at him all afternoon.

But Gerard was not laughing. The sight of Princess Claelia had torn his old, half-healed wound wide open. The brief, numbing fumes of his weeks of vice cleared away entirely. His health was broken, his savings almost gone, his old diligence, his innocence, his piety, all the balms that might have soothed his grief were thrown away forever. The gulf between him and the Margaret he loved was wider than any grave. The hot, wild fit of despair wore off, and the cold, heavy, empty despair settled in. He turned his back on all his rakish companions, wandered the streets alone, drank wine alone to paralyze the dark, gnawing remorse eating at his chest, and lay for hours stony-eyed and woebegone among the crumbling ruins of old Roman temples – more broken, more ruined, than any of the ancient stones around him.

At last, solitude, that dark, siren foe of diseased minds, pushed him to the final terrible edge. He wandered the banks of the dark Tiber at night, staring down at the slow, quiet black water, his disgust for the idea of slipping beneath its surface fading more with every passing day. There, he thought, was peace. There was escape. His old landlady, Teresa, Lodovico – all the people who cared for him – had no idea where he was, no idea how close he was to that final, fatal madness. There was no friendly voice, no kind smile, to step between him and the dark choice he was weighing.

But there was a pair of eyes fixed on him. Every step he took, every wandering hour, every return to his hovel, was tracked. It was not the eye of a friend. It was the eye of a mortal enemy, who meant to destroy him for good.
