Human existence requires the fiction of a starting line. Even science, that rigorous arbiter of truth, must invent a zero-point, a specific star in its endless trek, to declare "Time is Now." Its less precise cousin, Poetry, traditionally claims to begin in the middle; yet upon closer inspection, she merely follows suit. Both divide their units into vast fractions and launch *in medias res*, racing backward as quickly as forward. No memory can reach the primal dawn; whatever prologue we spin, whether celestial or terrestrial, is but a fragment of the pre-existing reality into which our tale steps.

Was she beautiful, or was she merely striking? What alchemy within her gaze transformed static features into a dynamic force? Did benevolence or malice reign supreme in those beams? Likely the latter. If goodness ruled, why did her effect stir unrest rather than serene contentment? Why did the urge to look again feel like a compulsion, a trap, rather than a willing surrender of the soul?

The woman posing these questions in Daniel Deronda's mind was engaged in gambling. Not in the sun-drenched ruins of antiquity, tossing copper coins amidst ragged limbs, but within a temple of modern luxury. The air here was thick with gilt moldings, somber hues, and the heavy, fleshy nudes of classical art—a perfect condenser for the breath of high society, a substance unavailable to lesser sorts in equal measure.

It was nearly four in the afternoon on a September day, the atmosphere brewed into a visible, golden haze. A profound silence hung over the room, shattered only by the soft clatter of chips, the gentle sweep of a brush, and the occasional, monotonous murmur of French, sounding as if spoken by a clockwork automaton. Two long tables hosted dense clusters of humanity, almost entirely focused downward upon the felt. The sole exception was a melancholy little boy. Clad in natural skin on his legs and calves, yet dressed in a fantastical costume above the waist, he stood gazing blankly toward the entrance. He resembled a masquerade advertisement pinned to the stage of a traveling circus, positioned directly behind a lady deeply engrossed at the roulette wheel.

Around this table, fifty or sixty souls gathered. In the outer rows sat mere spectators, though occasionally a woman would slip a five-franc note onto the table with a coquettish flutter, feigning curiosity about the fever of gambling. Those fully immersed in the game displayed a kaleidoscope of European types: the stoic Livonian, the fiery Spaniard, the languid Graeco-Italian, and various Germans. There was an aristocratic Englishman and a plebeian Englishman side by side, a stark testament to human equality. Beside the jeweled, white-fingered hand of an English countess rested a bony, yellow hand, resembling a crab, stretching a bare wrist to seize a pile of gold. That hand belonged to a woman whose face was a slight mutation of a vulture: gaunt, with deep-set eyes, grizzled brows, and hair combed with a frantic, ill-mannered haste. Near her sat another figure, prematurely aged, holding a shabby velvet reticule and occasionally poking her card with the pointed tip, her lips dry as parchment. Close by stood a respectable London tradesman, soft-handed and blonde, his hair parted with surgical precision. He carried the consciousness of circulars addressed to the nobility, holidays purchased with their patronage, and a leisure born of winning money in business to spend more lavishly in play. He reflected that Providence held no grudge against his amusement, remaining dispassionate enough to cease if the sweet taste of victory curdled into the sour rot of defeat. For the sin of gambling lies not in the wager, but in the loss. Though he bore the mannerisms of a merchant, in his pleasures he ranked with ancient title-holders. Beside him stood a handsome Italian, calm and statuesque, placing a fresh stack of napoleons from a newly opened bag. Within thirty seconds, these coins slid to an elderly, bewigged dame, her glasses pinching her nose. A faint, gleaming smile touched her lips, but the Italian remained impassive, secure in a system that placed his foot upon the neck of Chance, already preparing his next pile. Nearby, a man with the air of an emaciated beau, viewing life through a single monocle, held out a trembling hand for change. Perhaps it was not a rigid strategy, but a delusion of white crows, or the superstition that the eighth of the month was auspicious, fueling his fierce, tottering impetuosity.

Yet, despite the marked differences in their faces and hands, a uniform negativity permeated the assembly. Their expressions wore the mask of those who had eaten a fruit that bound their minds to a narrow, monotonous circle of action.

Deronda's initial impression of this scene, bathed in the dull, gas-choked light, was that the gambling of Spanish shepherd-boys seemed infinitely more enviable. Rousseau might be vindicated in claiming that art and science had served mankind poorly. But suddenly, the atmosphere shifted into drama. His attention seized by a young lady standing at an angle nearby, she was the final object of his gaze. She leaned toward a middle-aged woman seated at play, speaking English, before returning to the table. Instantly, she revealed the full stature of a graceful figure, possessing a face that, while perhaps not inspiring immediate admiration, demanded indifference be impossible.

An internal debate ignited within Deronda, sharpening his gaze from vague sensation to critical scrutiny. At moments, his eyes tracked the movement of her arms and hands as this problematic sylph bent to deposit her stake with an air of firm decision. Then, they snapped back to her face, steady and unbothered by observers. The sylph was winning. As her tapering fingers, delicately gloved in pale gray, adjusted the coins offered to her, she glanced around with a survey so cold and neutral it suggested art concealing inner exultation.

Then, her eyes met Deronda's. Instead of averting them, as she would have preferred, she became acutely aware of his arrestment. How long had he studied her? A darting sense told her he viewed her as inferior, measuring her from a height, regarding her as a specimen of lower order, distinct from the "human dross" surrounding her. This roused a tingling resentment, stretching the moment into conflict. It drained the blood from her lips, leaving them pale, though her cheeks remained untouched. She steeled herself with an inward defiance, turning back to the game without further sign. To Deronda, however, her gaze felt like an evil eye. Her stake vanished. It mattered not; she had been winning steadily since arriving with a few napoleons, accumulating a considerable reserve. Visions of a *cortège* worshipping her as a goddess of fortune swirled in her mind. Male gamblers achieved such supremacy; why not a woman? Her friend and chaperon, initially opposed, began to nod approval, offering the prudent counsel to stop and return to England. Gwendolen had dismissed this, insisting she sought the excitement of play, not the winnings. Thus, the present moment should have been the flood-tide of her experience. Yet, as her next stake was swept away, she felt the heat rising in her eyes. The certainty of that man's unwavering gaze pressed upon her like a torture. She resolved not to flinch, to play on as if indifferent to loss or gain. Her friend touched her elbow, suggesting they leave. In reply, Gwendolen laid ten louis on the same spot. She existed in a mood of defiant madness, where the mind loses sight of any goal beyond enraged resistance, foolishly including luck itself as an enemy to be defied. Since she was not winning spectacularly, the next best thing was to lose spectacularly. She controlled her muscles, masking any tremor. Every time her stake disappeared, she doubled it. Many watched her now, but she registered only Deronda's stare, sure he had not moved away.

Such dramas require no lengthy staging; development and catastrophe are measured by the turn of the hand. *"Faites votre jeu, mesdames et messieurs,"* intoned the automatic voice of destiny from behind the croupier's mustache and imperial. Gwendolen's arm stretched to deposit her last, pitiful heap of napoleons. *"Le jeu ne va plus,"* declared destiny. Within five seconds, Gwendolen turned from the table, but she turned resolutely toward Deronda, fixing her eyes upon him. An iron smile played in his eyes as their gazes collided; it was preferable that he keep his attention fixed on her than dismiss her as part of an indistinguishable insect swarm. Furthermore, despite his superciliousness, it was hard to believe he did not admire her spirit as well as her person. He was young, handsome, distinguished—an anomaly among the ridiculous, dowdy Philistines who would blight a gaming table with a sour look of protest. The universal conviction that we are admirable rarely yields to a single negative judgment. When members of Vanity's vast family find their performance received coldly, they instinctively believe that a little more of themselves will convert the dissident. Gwendolen operated on the assumption that she knew what was admirable and that she possessed it. This foundation, however, had received a disagreeable concussive blow, reeling slightly but refusing to collapse.

That evening, the room grew stiflingly hot, aglow with gas and the flowing trains of ladies' costumes.
