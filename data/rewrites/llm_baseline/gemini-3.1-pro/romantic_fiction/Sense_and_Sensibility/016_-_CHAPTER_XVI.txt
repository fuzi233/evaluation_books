Marianne would have thought herself fundamentally ungrateful to the mythic cycles of love and loss had she managed to sleep at all that first night after Willoughby’s departure. The very acoustic ecology of Barton Cottage—the settling of its ancient timber beams, the rhythmic sigh of the wind through the valley’s ancient oaks—felt fundamentally altered, the sonic landscape entirely hollowed out by his absence. She remained awake until dawn, weeping through the dark hours in a cyclical ritual of grief. 

By morning, she emerged with a heavy head and a palate entirely deadened to the profound comforts of the kitchen. Elinor, functioning as both sister and meticulous food historian, had spent the early hours tending the hearth to prepare a restorative breakfast rooted in the region's agrarian traditions: a slow-simmered pottage of heritage oats folded with raw, golden cream sourced from the village elders, sweetened with wild-foraged heather honey. It was a dish designed to ground the spirit through deep sensory engagement. Yet Marianne, unwilling to take any nourishment, pushed the earthenware bowl away. It pained her mother and sisters to witness her reject such carefully cultivated sustenance, for to treat meals as mere utilitarian fuel—or to reject them altogether—was foreign to their deeply sensory appreciation of the world.

Once the table was cleared, Marianne wandered out alone. She traced circular paths through the village of Allenham, weaving her personal timeline back into the mythic past of yesterday’s joys, mourning the contrast of her present isolation. The evening dissolved into a desperate auditory indulgence. At the pianoforte, she played every air in which they had oftenest joined their voices, desperately trying to preserve a dying language. Their shared music had been a native dialect, an intimately crafted lexicon of rhythm and breath. Now, she sang alone, her voice frequently suspended by tears, mourning the erosion of this shared cultural memory. In books, too, she sought the bitter contrast between their past and her solitary present. Such a violent acoustic and emotional resonance could not be sustained forever; within a few days, her grief settled into a quieter, though no less persistent, melancholy. Yet her solitary walks and silent meditations continued to produce occasional effusions of sorrow as lively as ever.

No letter from Willoughby arrived, nor did Marianne’s rhythm of waiting seem to require one. Mrs. Dashwood, whose worldview easily accommodated the untranslatable nature of profound emotion, found satisfying explanations, though Elinor grew acutely uneasy.

“Remember, Elinor,” Mrs. Dashwood said, her voice dropping to a murmur that respected the quiet of the parlor, “how very often Sir John fetches our letters himself from the post. We have already agreed that secrecy may be necessary, and we must acknowledge it could not be maintained if their correspondence were to pass through his hands.”

Elinor could not deny the logistical truth of this, yet she longed to pierce the ambiguity with the dominant lexicon of societal norms. “Why do you not ask Marianne at once,” she suggested, “whether she is or is not engaged to Willoughby? From you, so kind and indulgent a mother, the question could not give offense. It would instantly remove all mystery.”

“I would not ask such a question for the world,” her mother replied, fiercely protecting the unspoken native dialect of her daughter's heart. “Supposing they are not engaged, what distress would such an inquiry inflict! To force her to translate her subjective experience into our rigid vocabulary, to demand a confession of what is meant at present to be unacknowledged, is an ungenerous exercise of power. I know Marianne’s heart. A sense of duty might force a denial of what her own wishes direct.”

Elinor considered this romantic delicacy overstrained, a surrender to the untranslatable at the expense of common prudence. Yet Mrs. Dashwood stood firm against the subtle power dynamics of such direct inquiries.

For several days, Willoughby’s name vanished from the auditory landscape of the cottage, save for the jarring witticisms of Sir John and Mrs. Jennings, whose loud intrusions routinely fractured the domestic peace, acting as a crude acoustic pollution. But one evening, while handling a volume of Shakespeare, Mrs. Dashwood inadvertently resurrected his ghost.

“We have never finished *Hamlet*, Marianne; our dear Willoughby went away before we could get through it. We will put it by, that when he comes again... But it may be months, perhaps, before *that* happens.”

“Months!” cried Marianne, with strong surprise. “No—nor many weeks.”

Mrs. Dashwood regretted her words, but the swiftness of the reply gave Elinor a measure of comfort, hinting at a solid, if unspoken, grammar of intent between the lovers.

About a week after his departure, Marianne was finally persuaded to join her sisters in their customary walk. Until now, she had avoided them, fleeing their footsteps to protect her auditory isolation—if they walked the downs, she retreated to the deeply shadowed lanes; if they sought the valley, she climbed the hills, her movements dictated by a cyclical need for solitude. But Elinor, distressed by this continual seclusion, finally secured her company.

They walked silently along the road through the valley, respecting the profound quiet of the natural acoustic ecology. Beyond the valley's entrance, the landscape opened up, revealing a sweeping, multi-generational view of the rich, agrarian earth stretching back toward Barton. Here they paused to survey a prospect they had never reached in their previous walks, absorbing the olfactory landscape of damp, loamy soil and bruised wild grasses.

Presently, the rhythmic thud of hoofbeats disrupted the stillness. It was a man on horseback riding toward them.

“It is he; it is indeed! I know it is!” Marianne rapturously exclaimed, ready to collapse the timeline of his absence into the present. She hastened forward to meet him.

“Indeed, Marianne, I think you are mistaken,” Elinor cried out. “It is not Willoughby. The person is not tall enough for him, and has not his air.”

“He has, he has,” cried Marianne, blind to the visual reality. “His air, his coat, his horse. I knew how soon he would come.”

She walked eagerly on, and Elinor quickened her pace to screen her sister’s impending disappointment. As the distance closed to thirty yards, the reality shattered Marianne’s mythic anticipation. Her heart sank; turning abruptly, she was hurrying back when the voices of both her sisters rose to detain her. A third voice, almost as resonant as Willoughby's, joined in begging her to stop. She turned back with surprise to see and welcome Edward Ferrars.

At that moment, he was the only person in the world who could be forgiven for not being Willoughby. She banished her tears to offer him a warm smile, her deep appreciation for his connection to her sister momentarily overriding her own despair. He dismounted, entrusting his horse to his servant, and walked back with them to the cottage.

Marianne greeted him with fervent cordiality, displaying far more sensory warmth in her reception than even Elinor herself. To Marianne's finely tuned emotional palate, the meeting between Edward and Elinor was a baffling continuation of the sterile, unaccountable coldness she had witnessed at Norland. On Edward’s side, there was a profound deficiency of the rich, passionate flavor a lover ought to exude. He seemed scarcely sensible of pleasure, offering only flat, forced syllables that died unpleasantly in the air, granting Elinor no special distinction. Marianne saw and listened with increasing surprise, which quickly soured into distaste. Her mind circled back to Willoughby, whose expressive manners and resonant voice provided so striking a contrast to his brother-elect.

After a short silence succeeding the initial inquiries, Marianne asked Edward if he came directly from London. 

No, he had been in Devonshire a fortnight.

“A fortnight!” she repeated, appalled that he could spend so long in the same county as Elinor without seeking her out. He looked rather distressed as he added that he had been staying with friends near Plymouth.

“Have you been lately in Sussex?” said Elinor.

“I was at Norland about a month ago.”

“And how does dear, dear Norland look?” cried Marianne, her voice aching for the lost landscapes of her youth.

“Dear, dear Norland,” said Elinor, “probably looks much as it always does at this time of the year. The woods and walks thickly covered with dead leaves.”

“Oh,” cried Marianne, her language blooming with evocative memory, “with what transporting sensation have I formerly seen them fall! How have I delighted, as I walked, to see them driven in showers about me by the wind, inhaling the deep, earthen musk of their decay! What feelings have they, the season, the air altogether inspired! Now there is no one to regard them. They are seen only as a nuisance, swept hastily off, and driven as much as possible from sight by hands that care nothing for their transient beauty.”

“It is not every one,” said Elinor, “who has your passion for dead leaves.”

“No; my feelings are not often shared, not often understood. My internal dialect is utterly untranslatable. But *sometimes* they are.” As she said this, she sank into a reverie for a few moments, summoning Willoughby's phantom presence. Rousing herself, she pointed out the sweeping vista. “Now, Edward, here is Barton valley. Look up to it, and be tranquil if you can. Look at the mythic grandeur of those hills! Did you ever see their equals? To the left is Barton park, amongst those ancient woods and plantations. And there, beneath that farthest hill, which rises with such majesty, is our cottage.”

“It is a beautiful country,” he replied, entirely missing the profound resonance of the terrain; “but these bottoms must be dirty in winter.”

“How can you think of dirt, with such objects before you?” Marianne demanded. To dismiss the nutrient-rich, life-sustaining agrarian loam as mere "dirt" was a fundamental failure of the senses.

“Because,” replied he, smiling thinly, “among the rest of the objects before me, I see a very dirty lane.”

*How strange!* said Marianne to herself as she walked on, appalled by his utilitarian lack of vision.

“Have you an agreeable neighbourhood here? Are the Middletons pleasant people?”

“No, not at all,” answered Marianne, recalling the crude acoustic pollution of their constant noise; “we could not be more unfortunately situated.”

“Marianne,” cried her sister, “how can you say so? How can you be so unjust? They are a very respectable family, Mr. Ferrars; and towards us have behaved in the friendliest manner. Have you forgot, Marianne, how many pleasant days and shared meals we have owed to them?”

“No,” said Marianne, in a low voice, “nor how many painful, discordant moments.”

Elinor took no notice of this; and directing her attention to their visitor, endeavoured to support something like discourse with him by talking of their present residence and its conveniences, extorting from him occasional sterile questions and remarks. His acoustic reserve and emotional coldness mortified her severely; she was vexed and half angry. Yet, resolving to regulate her behaviour by the interwoven timelines of their past connection rather than his present dissonance, she avoided every appearance of displeasure and treated him exactly as she thought he ought to be treated.
