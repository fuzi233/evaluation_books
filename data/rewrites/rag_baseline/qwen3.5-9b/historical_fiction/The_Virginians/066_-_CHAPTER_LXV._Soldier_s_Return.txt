The noise of London was a mechanical thing, a grinding of wheels and shouting that sought to prove something to itself. They spoke of St. Casimir, a rear-guard action where five hundred men were cut down by ten thousand on a beach. They fired cannons at the Invalides until the powder ran dry, celebrating a disaster as if it were a harvest festival. It was a vulgar arithmetic of glory, adding up losses to make a sum called "victory."

Harry sat in the room, listening to the echo of his own absence. He had returned. The sea air had stained his cheeks brown, a permanent mark of survival. The ball that had whistled past his side-curl had missed, sparing the flesh beneath. He had shown the lapels of his coat to the enemy, yes, but only for the briefest instant, withdrawing into the shadow of the retreat. Lapels were reverses, he reminded himself; coats had them, and men wore them with a certain stoic indifference.

"We all had to run," Harry said, his voice flat, devoid of the drama the room demanded. "When the line broke, luck belonged to those who reached the boats first."

He recounted the slaughter. The French horse and foot had poured down onto the sand, mingling with the fleeing English, bayonets finding soft targets on the ground. Armytage had fallen ahead of him. Harry had picked up the dying man and staggered through the surf, carrying weight that threatened to crush his legs. The sailors in the boat had not flinched; the shots whistled around their ears, snapping oars and shredding flags, yet the officer commanded with the calm of a man drinking punch in Portsmouth.

"Sir John never made the ship," Harry finished. "The service lost a fine soldier. Miss Howe lost a true gentleman."

Hetty watched him, her eyes fixed on the gap between his words and the silence that followed. She thought of the King, of the command that had sent Armytage to his death. "Why did the King encourage him?" she asked, her voice trembling with a sentimentality that felt like a wound. "A gentleman cannot refuse such a quarter. Now he is dead!"

Her father nodded, a slow, rhythmic motion. "His Majesty will be in a deep state of grief."

"Now you are laughing!" Hetty cried out, the accusation sharp. "Do you mean the King feels nothing when a gentleman dies almost at his feet?"

"If I thought that, I would be for the Pretender," Harry replied, the words automatic, a shield raised against the storm of her emotion.

"The sauce-box would make a pretty little head for Temple Bar," the General murmured, watching Hetty's face. He saw the turmoil there—the remorse, the consternation, the gratitude that the danger was finally over. "No, my dear," he said gently. "Were kings to weep for every soldier, what a life you would make for them! I think better of his Majesty than to suppose him so weak. If Miss Hester Lambert got her Pretender, I doubt she would be any the happier. That family was never famous for too much feeling."

"But if the King sent Harry... actually to the war..." Hetty pressed.

"If Harry had fallen, the court would have gone into mourning," the General noted, observing the shift in the room's atmosphere. "As it is, gentlemen and ladies wore colored clothes yesterday."

"Why should we not make bonfires for a defeat and put on sackcloth after a victory?" George asked, trying to lighten the mood.

"I protest I don't want to thank Heaven for helping us burn the ships at Cherbourg," Harry said, his tone grave.

"Yes, you do, George," Hetty interjected, though she knew she had no right to speak. "Not that I have a right, and you aren't ever so clever. But when your country wins, you're glad—I know I am. When I run away before Frenchmen, I'm ashamed. I can't help it, though I did it."

She paused, looking at Harry. "'Tis a bad business," she continued, her voice dropping. "But 'tis lucky 'twas no worse."

"The story about the French is that their Governor, the Duke of Aiguillon, was rather what you call a moistened chicken," Harry said, turning his attention back to the mechanics of the war. "Our retreat might have been cut off entirely, only we were in a mighty hurry to move. The local militia behaved famously. There were many gentlemen volunteers with them, showing as they ought to do, in the front. They say the Chevalier of Tour d'Auvergne engaged in spite of the Duke's orders."

Officers had brought lists of prisoners and wounded to General Bligh and Lord Howe. Howe was a lord now, elevated by the news of his brother's death. He was a brave fellow, whether lord or commoner.

"And his sister, who was to have married poor Sir John Armytage..." Hetty sighed, the image of the future collapsing into the present. "Think what her state must be!"

"And his mother!" Mrs. Lambert cried.

Mrs. Lambert read the address in the papers aloud, her voice rising. "'Lord Howe being now absent upon the publick service, and Lieutenant-Colonel Howe with his regiment at Louisbourg, it rests upon me to beg the favour of your votes and interests that Lieutenant-Colonel Howe may supply the place of his late brother as your representative in Parliament.'"

"A laconic woman," George observed.

"How can sons help being brave who have been nursed by such a mother as that?" the General asked.

The two young men looked at each other.

"If one of us were to fall in defense of his country, we have a mother in Sparta who would think and write so too," George said.

"If Sparta is anywhere Virginia way, I reckon we have," Harry remarked.

"And to think that we should both of us have met the enemy, and both of us been whipped by him, brother!" he added, his penitence evident.

Hetty looked at him, and in her mind, she saw only the other day: Harry tottering through the water, his comrade bleeding on his shoulder, the enemy in pursuit, the shot flying round. It was she who had driven him into that danger. Her words had provoked him. Yet, now that he was returned, he never rebuked her. Except when asked, he scarcely spoke of his adventures at all. He was very grave and courteous with Hetty; with the rest of the family, especially frank and tender. But those taunts of hers had wounded him.

"Little hand!" his looks and demeanor seemed to say. "Thou shouldst not have been lifted against me! It is ill to scorn anyone, much more one who has been so devoted to you and all yours. I may not be over quick of wit, but in as far as the heart goes, I am the equal of the best, and the best of my heart your family has had."

This magnanimous endurance served him to regain in Miss Hetty's esteem the place he had lost during the previous months of inglorious idleness. The respect she paid to the brave transformed her. She was no longer pert in her answers, or sarcastic in her observations regarding his conduct. In a word, she was a humiliated, an altered, an improved Miss Hetty. And all the world seemed to change towards Harry, as he changed towards the world.

He was no longer sulky and indolent; he no more desponded about himself or defied his neighbors. The colonel of his regiment reported his behavior as exemplary and recommended him for one of the commissions vacated by the casualties. Unlucky as the expedition's termination was, it was fortunate to him.

His brother-volunteers, returning to St. James's Street, reported highly of his behavior. These volunteers and their actions became the theme of everyone's praise. Had he been a general commanding, slain in the moment of victory, Sir John Armytage could scarce have had more sympathy than that which the nation showed him. The papers teemed with letters about him; men of wit and sensibility vied with each other in composing epitaphs in his honor. The fate of his affianced bride was bewailed. She was, as we have said, the sister of the brave Commodore who had just returned from this unfortunate expedition and succeeded to the title of his elder brother, an officer as gallant as himself, who had just fallen in America.

My Lord Howe was heard to speak in special praise of Mr. Warrington, and so he had a handsome share of the fashion and favor which the town now bestowed on the volunteers. Doubtless there were thousands of men employed who were as good as they, but the English ever love their gentlemen, and love that they should distinguish themselves; and these volunteers were voted Paladins and heroes by common accord. As our young noblemen will, they accepted their popularity very affably. White's and Almack's illuminated when they returned, and St. James's embraced its young knights. Harry was restored to full favor amongst them. Their hands were held out eagerly to him again.

Even his relations congratulated him. There came a letter from Castlewood, whither Aunt Bernstein had by this time betaken herself, containing praises of his valor and a pretty little bank-bill as a token of his affectionate aunt's approval. This was under my Lord Castlewood's frank, who sent his regards to both his kinsmen and an offer of the hospitality of his country-house if they were minded to come to him.
