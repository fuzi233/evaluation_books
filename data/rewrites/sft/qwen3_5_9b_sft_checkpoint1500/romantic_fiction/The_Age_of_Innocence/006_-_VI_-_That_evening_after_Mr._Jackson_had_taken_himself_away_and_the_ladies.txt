That evening, following Mr. Jackson's departure and the ladies' retreat to their curtained sanctuary, Newland Archer ascended the stairs to his study with a thoughtful, heavy gait. As always, a watchful hand had tended the fire and trimmed the lamp, rendering the room—a cavern lined with volumes, adorned with bronze fencers on the mantel, and populated by photographs of masterpieces—singularly domestic and inviting.

As he sank into his leather armchair before the blaze, his gaze landed upon a large portrait of May Welland. It was a gift from the early, electric days of their courtship, and now it dominated the space where lesser likenesses had once resided. With a sudden, profound sense of reverence, Archer studied the frank brow, the serious eyes, and the playfully innocent mouth of the creature destined to be his soul's keeper. Yet, staring back at him through the guise of this familiar face, he saw something chilling: the terrifying product of the social machinery he worshipped. She was a young girl who knew nothing and expected everything, looking at him like a stranger. In that glance, the illusion shattered for him anew; marriage was not the safe harbor he had been taught to believe in, but a voyage across uncharted, storm-tossed seas.

The shadow of the Countess Olenska had already disturbed the sediment of his convictions, setting them adrift in dangerous currents. His own words, "Women should be free—as free as we are," echoed in his mind, striking at the root of a problem his world conveniently declared nonexistent. "Nice" women, no matter how wronged, never claimed the liberty he espoused, allowing generous men like himself to feign chivalry while secretly upholding the iron shackles of convention. This verbal generosity was merely a humbug masking the inexorable laws binding everyone to the old patterns. Here lay the terrible paradox: he was pledged to defend, on behalf of his betrothed's cousin, a conduct that, if committed by his future wife, would invite the thunders of Church and State.

Of course, the dilemma was purely hypothetical. Since he was not a blackguard Polish nobleman, it was absurd to speculate on his wife's rights if he *were*. But Newland Archer was too imaginative not to feel the chill of what might be. Could he and May truly know one another? Was it not his duty, as a "decent" gentleman, to conceal his past from her, and hers, as a marriageable girl, to possess no past to conceal? What if, driven by subtler, unseen forces, they tired of each other? Reviewing the marriages of his peers—the supposedly happy unions—he found none that resembled the passionate comradeship he envisioned. They were dull associations of material and social interest, cemented by ignorance on one side and hypocrisy on the other. Lawrence Lefferts, the high priest of form, had realized this enviable ideal most completely. He had crafted a wife so entirely suited to his convenience that, amidst his frequent adulteries, she wandered in smiling unconsciousness, claiming, "Lawrence was so frightfully strict," and blushing indignantly when anyone hinted at Julius Beaufort's "other establishment."

Archer tried to console himself with the thought that he was not quite such an ass as Larry Lefferts, nor May such a simpleton as poor Gertrude. But the distinction was one of intelligence, not of moral standards. In truth, they all inhabited a world of hieroglyphics, where the real thing was never spoken, acted, or thought, but only represented by arbitrary signs. Just as Mrs. Welland, knowing precisely why Archer had pressured her to announce May's engagement at the Beaufort ball, felt compelled to simulate reluctance and act as though her hand had been forced—much like the savage bride dragged from her parents' tent in accounts of primitive peoples—so too did the young girl remain the most inscrutable figure in this elaborate system of mystification. She was frank, poor darling, because she had nothing to hide; she was assured because she knew of nothing to fear. And with no better preparation, she was about to be plunged overnight into what people euphemistically termed "the facts of life."

The young man was sincerely, placidly in love. He delighted in her radiant health, her horsemanship, her grace, and the shy intellectual curiosity blooming within her under his guidance. She had progressed far enough to join him in mocking the *Idylls of the King*, though not yet to grasp the tragic beauty of *Ulysses* or the seductive danger of the *Lotus Eaters*. She was straightforward, loyal, and brave, possessing a humor proved chiefly by her laughter at *his* jokes. He suspected, deep within her innocently gazing soul, a warm glow of feeling waiting to be awakened. Yet, when he had completed his brief survey of her virtues, discouragement overtook him. All this frankness and innocence were merely artificial products. Untrained human nature was not naturally open; it was bristling with the twists and defenses of instinctive guile. He felt oppressed by this creation of factitious purity, so cunningly manufactured by a conspiracy of mothers, aunts, and grandmothers, simply because it was what he wanted—and what he felt entitled to exercise his lordly pleasure in shattering like an image made of snow.

These reflections carried a certain triteness, habitual to young men approaching their wedding day. Usually, they were accompanied by a sense of compunction and self-abasement, which Newland Archer felt no trace of. He could not deplore, as Thackeray's heroes so often did, that he lacked a blank page to offer his bride in exchange for her unblemished one. He could not escape the fact that, had he been raised as she had, neither of them would have been fit to navigate the world without being lost like the Babes in the Wood. Nor could he, despite his anxious cogitations, discern any honest reason—any reason unconnected with his own momentary pleasure or the passion of masculine vanity—for why his bride should not have been granted the same freedom of experience as himself.

Such questions drifted through his mind at such an hour, but he was acutely conscious that their uncomfortable persistence and precision stemmed from the inopportune arrival of the Countess Olenska. Here he was, at the zenith of his betrothal, a moment for pure thoughts and cloudless hopes, when he had been pitchforked into a coil of scandal that raised every problem he had wished to leave undisturbed. "Hang Ellen Olenska!" he grumbled, covering the fire and beginning to undress. He could not fully comprehend why her fate should bear so heavily on his own, yet he dimly sensed that he had only just begun to measure the risks of the championship his engagement had forced upon him.

A few days later, the bolt fell.

The Lovell Mingotts issued cards for what was known as "a formal dinner"—three extra footmen, two dishes per course, and a Roman punch served midway—headlined with the words "To meet the Countess Olenska." In accordance with the hospitable American fashion, which treated strangers as if they were royalty or their ambassadors, they extended the invitation.

Guest selection displayed a boldness and discrimination that the initiated recognized as the firm hand of Catherine the Great. Alongside immemorial standbys like the Selfridge Merrys, who were invited everywhere because they always had been, and the Beauforts, with whom there was a tenuous claim of relationship, sat Mr. Sillerton Jackson and his sister Sophy, who followed her brother's dictates. There were the Lawrence Leffertses, Mrs. Lefferts Rushworth (the lovely widow), the Harry Thorleys, the Reggie Chiverses, and young Morris Dagonet and his wife, a van der Luyden. Indeed, the company was perfectly assorted, comprising the little inner group that, throughout the long New York season, disported itself together daily and nightly with apparently undiminished zest.

Forty-eight hours later, the unbelievable occurred. Every single recipient of the Mingotts' invitation refused, save for the Beauforts and the venerable Mr. Jackson and his sister. The intended slight was sharpened by the fact that even the Reggie Chiverses, though of the Mingott clan, inflicted it. Furthermore, the uniformity of the notes was damning; in all of them, the writers "regretted that they were unable to accept," omitting the mitigating plea of a "previous engagement" that ordinary courtesy prescribed.

New York society, in those days, was far too small, and too scant in its resources, for every inhabitant—from livery-stable keepers to butlers and cooks—not to know exactly which evenings people were free. Thus, it was possible for the recipients of Mrs. Lovell Mingott's invitations to make their determination not to meet the Countess Olenska with cruel, unmistakable clarity.

The blow was unexpected, yet the Mingotts, true to their nature, met it gallantly. Mrs. Lovell Mingott confided the grievance to Mrs. Welland, who passed it to Newland Archer. Blazing with outrage at the affront, Archer appealed passionately and authoritatively to his mother. After a painful period of inward resistance and outward temporizing, she succumbed to his instances (as she always did). Immediately embracing his cause with an energy redoubled by her previous hesitations, she donned her grey velvet bonnet and declared, "I'll go and see Louisa van der Luyden."
