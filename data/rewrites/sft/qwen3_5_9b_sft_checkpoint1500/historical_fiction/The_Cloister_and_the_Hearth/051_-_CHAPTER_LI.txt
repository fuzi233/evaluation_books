For Margaret, the day was a paradox of sweetness and sorrow. It brought her a loyal companion, yet it delivered the crushing truth that Gerard stood utterly alone in that distant, strange land. Her hope, which Denys had nurtured, shattered instantly; she realized with a jolt that he would not return, nor had he ever truly intended to. The silence of his absence filled the room, heavy and absolute.

Yet, amidst this grief, Denys proved to be a balm. He embraced her with the warmth of a brother, calling her his "she-comrade" and filling the air with his characteristic formulas and boisterous cheerfulness. Margaret indulged him completely, treating him with a gentle, almost doting affection that she also reserved for Martin. She became a strict guardian of his manners, refusing to let him take a single meal in her house unless he observed proper decorum, and fiercely forbidding any coarse language. "It spoils you, Denys!" she would chide, her voice softening despite her sternness. "Good lack, to hear such ugly words issuing from so comely a head! Forbear, or I shall be angry, so be civil." Under this pressure, Denys would switch from his natural, rough politeness to a ludicrous display of affected refinement, the struggle between his innate nature and his adopted civility often ending in a comedic pantomime.

However, as the old saying goes, when it rains, it pours. Soon, others sought to court Margaret's friendship. She had sent a humble letter to Margaret Van Eyck, acknowledging her diminished status and promising to keep her distance, yet she could not erase the memory of her former benefactor's kindness. In her note, she recounted the myriad struggles she had endured to survive, and concluded with a desperate plea: "Do not betray my residence, least of all to his people. I loathe them; they drove him from me. Even when he is gone, their hearts turn away from me as though repenting their cruelty were impossible."

The Van Eycks were thrown into perplexity. Eventually, they made Reicht a confidante, and like water passing through a pipe, the secret flowed from Reicht to Catherine.

"Ay, and has she turned so bitter against us?" asked the good woman, Catherine, her voice tinged with regret. "She stole our son from us, and now she hates us for failing to rush into her embrace. Yet, surely it is a blessing she lives and remains no further than Rotterdam."

Meanwhile, across the water, the English Princess, now known as Countess Charolois, moved with stately grandeur through the northern duchy, accompanied by her stepdaughter, the young heiress of Burgundy, Marie de Bourgogne. The Old Duke, the most magnificent prince in Europe, poured out his splendor to honor them. Troops of dazzling knights and battalions of fair ladies, gorgeously attired, formed a retinue around the princesses. Minstrels, jongleurs, storytellers, bards, musicians, actors, and tumblers trailed behind, creating a carnival of noise and color. Fencing matches, dances, and unending merriment lit up every town they visited. Richart issued an invitation to all his people to gather in Rotterdam to witness the pageantry.

Days passed in Rotterdam, and eventually, Denys encountered Catherine accidentally on the street. After exchanging warm greetings, he crowed triumphantly about having found her "she-comrade." But Catherine quickly cooled his enthusiasm, pointing out that he would have found her far sooner by staying quietly at Tergou rather than wandering aimlessly through Holland. "And now that we are found, what is gained?" she asked sharply. "Her heart is set dead against us now."

"Oh, let that fly buzz around its ear," Denys insisted, undeterred. "Come with me to her house."

"No," Catherine refused firmly. "I am certain of an ill welcome. Those who come unbidden sit unseated." Instead, she suggested that Denys act as mediator, bringing the parties together for a reconciliation. He accepted the task with immense pomp and confidence, trotting off to Margaret.

"She-comrade," he announced breathlessly, "I met a friend of yours today."

"You looked into Rotterdam, and saw yourself?" Margaret teased, though her eyes betrayed a flicker of hope.

"Nay, 'twas a female, and one who seeks your regard. 'Twas Catherine, Gerard's mother."

"Oh, was it?" Margaret replied, her tone shifting instantly from curiosity to cold resolve. "Then you may tell her she comes too late. There was a time I longed and longed for her, but she held aloof in my hour of greatest need. So now, we shall remain as we have been."

Denys attempted to shake this resolution, coaxing her gently. But she grew bitter and sullen, impenetrable to his charm. He then resorted to scolding, and suddenly, she burst into hysterics.

Startled by this emotional collapse, Denys, off guard, allowed himself to be trapped into a solemn promise never to mention the subject again. Crestfallen, he returned to Catherine and relayed the reception of his overture. Catherine flared with anger and informed the family how poorly his efforts had been received. The family erupted, and what began as a misunderstanding quickly ignited into a fierce, burning feud. Only little Kate offered any excuse for Margaret, trying to bridge the widening gap.

The following day, another visitor arrived at Margaret's door. Inside, the household appeared military yet enslaved and degraded: Martin was elbow-deep in soapsuds, Denys ironing with clumsy ineptitude, and Margaret herself was plaiting ruffs. Yet, above the mundane labor, her gaze remained sharp, a mistress's eye surveying her raw recruits. Just then, an old man entered. He appeared venerable at first glance, but closer inspection revealed a face keen and wizened.

"Oh!" cried Margaret. With swift, instinctive motion, she turned her back on him, hiding her face with an invincible repugnance. "Oh, that man! That man!"

"Nay, fear me not," said Ghysbrecht calmly. "I come on a friend's errand. I bring you a letter from foreign parts."

"Do not mock me, old man," she murmured, turning slowly back to face him.

"Nay, see;" and he held out an enormous, crinkled envelope.

Margaret darted forward, snatching the letter. She clutched it with trembling hands, her eyes glistening with sudden, overwhelming emotion. It was Gerard's handwriting.

"Oh, thank you, sir, bless you for this!" she gasped, her voice breaking. "I forgive you all the ill you ever wrought me."

Pressing the letter tightly to her bosom with one hand, she glided swiftly from the room, leaving Ghysbrecht standing there. As she did not return, the old man departed, casting a final, unreadable scowl at Martha. For hours afterward, Margaret sat alone, clutching the letter, the weight of his absence momentarily lifted by the tangible proof of his existence.
