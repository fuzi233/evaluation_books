More than an hour after leaving the city hospital, Romola took a winding detour away from her usual path, looping farther west toward the Arno until she reached the riverbank well outside the Ponte Vecchio. She turned straight for the famous old bridge, intent on hurrying to San Stefano to search for Baldassarre. Dread coiled tight in her chest at the thought of what she might learn of him, yet she knew to abandon him would be to betray a sharp, unignorable claim he held on her conscience.

But when she neared the crossroads where the Por’ Santa Maria branched right and the road to Ponte Vecchio ran left, the crowd around her dropped to their knees all at once, and she followed suit without a second’s hesitation. The Great Cross of the Duomo was at the head of a procession moving toward them, and she would not be able to reach San Stefano until the full march had passed. When the Cross had faded from view and she stood back upright, the idle waiting hit her with a surge of bone-deep fatigue she had not noticed while walking with purpose.
A local hose vendor at her elbow greeted her warmly: “Madonna Romola, you must be worn through standing. My door is right here—Gian Fantoni will be proud to give you a seat. We all love God and the Frate as you do, this house is yours for the taking.”

Romola had grown used to this fraternal welcome from ordinary Florentine citizens over the past two years, faces she recognized from seeing them week after week at the Duomo. The quiet loneliness of her empty Via de’ Bardi home no longer felt like her true anchor; she felt far more at home inside the towered circuit of the city, where almost every street corner brought her a look of friendliness or quiet appeal. She stepped through the open door with gratitude, and the vendor led her up to an upper window where his stout Piagnoni-clad wife and three children made space for her behind bright, hanging draperies. From her vantage point, she had a clear view of the procession moving at a solemn crawl across the full length of the Ponte Vecchio, and out across the Arno Lung’ Arno all the way to the distant Santa Trinita bridge.

The procession moved in almost total stillness, no wailing hymn breaking its quiet plea for mercy—only the steady tramp of hundreds of feet, and the faint soft drag of wool robes against stone. The first ranks that passed under Romola’s window were the city’s youth: a long line of beardless Florentine boys bearing high above their midst a white statue of the Child Jesus, its golden halo glowing above its head, standing beside a tall cross laid out with thorns and iron nails.

After them came the mysterious Companies of Discipline, their ranks bound by secret oaths to self-chastisement and devout works, every inch of their bodies covered by shrouds save for their eyes. Every onlooker knew the forms walking past were ordinary Florentines of all ranks—shopkeepers, merchants, statesmen—who went about their public lives as usual by day, but no one could pick out a son, a husband, a father under their hoods. They had shed all individual identity to walk as living symbols of a shared vow, each company marked only by its coloured shroud and badge, their faces erased of all expression save quiet fellowship.

They were followed by wave after wave of monastic orders: first a white stream of reformed Benedictines, then the far longer grey line of the Franciscans, their waists bound with knotted cord, many shod in rough wooden zoccoli over bare feet, the largest and most zealous order in Florence at the time, many of them devoted to the poor and openly critical of the Dominicans. Next came the black-robed Augustinians of San Spirito, their faces sharper and more learned, the heirs to Boccaccio’s legendary library and long Florence’s most respected community of scholars, followed by the Carmelites in white over dark, then the all-black Servites, the famous local order founded seven hundred years prior by seven wealthy merchants who had abandoned all their profits to worship the Divine Mother.

At the sight of black mantles worn over white scapularies, the whole crowd’s pulse quickened, split between bitter hatred and fierce devotion: the Dominicans, the followers of Savonarola, were approaching. The order’s Fiesole branch marched first, their mantles coarse, most threadbare if not outright ragged, stripped to absolute poverty by the strict discipline their Prior of San Marco had enforced. At last, one figure in a mantle no more worn than the rest moved to the front of the line, a tonsured head above it that would have seemed unremarkable to a stranger who had never seen it stamped on bronze medals emblazoned with the Sword of God, or flanked by armed guards on the steps of the Duomo, or lit from within by the fire of preaching as it turned to face a rapt, screaming crowd of thousands.

No one dared break the stillness enough to cheer or jeer openly as Savonarola drew near, but every ear caught a mingled murmur of hissing that strained to be curses, and soft whispers that strained to be blessings. The hisses seemed loudest to the small group of his disciples at the crossroads, who dropped to their knees as if divine presence was moving past them, and the wave of silent homage rippled down the length of the street like a subtle shock, moving many to bend the knee even as others stood unmoved, their faces twisted with hatred. As Savonarola passed down the Por’ Santa Maria, Romola spotted a man at an upper window spit down directly at him.

More orders followed: the Frati Umiliati, the Humbled Brethren of Ognissanti, famous as the first workers to build Florence’s wool trade centuries before, then ranks of Vallombrosan Benedictines, their small variations of hood cut and dye a quiet nod to the generations of reformers who had marked shifts in piety with new robes. At last the line of shaven crowns ended, and the untonsured secular priests of the city marched past, followed by the full assembled ranks of Florence’s twenty-one incorporated Arts, their banners flying high to mark every distinct station of the city’s work, from the bakers who supplied its bread all the way up to its judges and notaries. Next came all the junior state officers, rising in rank until the secular line was broken by the Duomo’s canons, carrying a silver casket that held the relic of the head of San Zenobio, Florence’s immortal patron saint whose virtues were said to have saved the city from ruin a thousand years prior.

This was the heart of the procession. Behind the relic walked the archbishop under a rich embroidered canopy, and after him came the long-awaited hidden tabernacle: draped first in heavy brocade curtains around an outer painted shrine, and within that, the far older inner shrine no living person, nor any of their fathers, had ever opened. Inside rested the Pitying Mother, the Unseen Madonna, dug up out of the soil of L’Impruneta ages before, who had cried out the moment the spade struck her wooden form. For centuries the Madonna had never been carried to the Duomo without a stream of rich offerings borne before her, but today there were no gifts for the shrine, no torches lit, no precious veils laid out. By Fra Girolamo’s direct order, every donation that day went straight to the hungry poor, not to the altars. He had always preached that help for suffering living people mattered far more than offerings to distant divine powers, and temporary altars for alms had been set up outside every major church along the route. Florence was at her final point of extremity, and even the most pious knew the hidden Madonna cared more for the sound of a hungry child’s cry than gilded brocade. Many clung harder to the Frate’s public promise that deliverance would come than they did to the ancient miracle of the unseen statue, while his enemies waited in secret glee for the day his word would be proven false.

Slowly the tabernacle moved on, knees bending before it as it passed, the whole street wrapped in profound stillness. The procession was nearly at its end, the Priors and the Gonfaloniere waiting to bring up the rear, and Romola, half heavy with foreboding and half lifted by the warm, familiar fellowship she had learned to cherish in Florence over the last two years, sighed deep and sank back to her knees in utter languor.

Then a flash of bright colour burst between the houses, out over the distant Santa Trinita bridge.

Romola shot to her feet, leaning far out the window, her black veil slipping off her head so her golden hair caught the sunlight, the flush high on her cheeks glowing as if lit by a sudden spark. A roar burst from the crowd at the exact same instant: the last ranks of the procession froze, every face turning toward the distant bridge. A rider on a lathered bay horse galloped full tilt along the Arno embankment, foam streaked white along his horse’s sides from the hard ride, his cap flying loose by its red trailing becchetto, waving a green olive branch high over his head. A messenger. A messenger of good tidings. The crowd surged forward to meet him, seizing his horse’s reins, pushing and trampling in their eagerness to hear his news, and Romola recognized him in an instant. It was Tito, her husband, who had ridden out on a private embassy to Pisa only a few days prior.

No spark of joy lit her face. The first rush of expectation faded fast, and her only care was what relief he had brought for the starving city. The crowd’s teasing cries of “Give us hose-worthy news!” circled around him as he laughed off their questions, pushing his horse steadily forward until he reached the Gonfaloniere and Priors waiting at the crossroads. He bowed low, and announced clearly for all to hear: “Magnificent Signori! I bring joyful tidings: the French galleys, laden with corn and armed men, have docked safe in the port of Leghorn. A strong west wind held the enemy blockading fleet far out at sea.”

The words vibrated through the streets, a great shout ringing out all the way along the Arno, then another and another, the cheers spreading back up the length of the procession toward the Duomo, distant answering cries rippling outward like waves across a wide lake. For minutes no one could speak: even the Signoria lifted their caps, standing bareheaded before the rescue that had arrived from beyond any human power, that grace all generations call divine.

At last, as the order was given for the procession to move forward again, Tito smiled and added gracefully: “I must note that the reward of hose the Signoria may bestow for these tidings is no due to me. Another man rode far harder than I to bring the news—Meo di Sasso, whose horse broke down only a mile outside Signa. He would have stood here in my place if he could, and he will arrive in another hour to claim the honour he has earned, after bearing all the labour and missing the greatest joy.”
The crowd cheered at his modesty, the Signoria’s spokesman spoke a quick word of praise, and the end of the procession moved on. The great bell of the Palazzo Vecchio began to swing, ringing out to amplify the people’s joy, as Tito turned his horse to follow behind the march. Romola watched him adjust his cap, playing the part of the weary exhausted rider, and she felt almost certain he had looked up and seen her leaning out that window. Tito had always had a gift for seeing everything, without ever letting anyone else notice he had looked. She drew her black veil back over her head, and sat quiet, hidden in the shadow of the window nook, as the roar of Florence’s celebration swelled around her.
