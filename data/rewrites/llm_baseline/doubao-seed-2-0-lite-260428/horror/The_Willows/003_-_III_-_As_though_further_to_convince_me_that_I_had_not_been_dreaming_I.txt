As if the night’s uncanny encounter had not already been enough to shatter my grip on what was real, I lay awake for what felt like hours before any drowsiness found me at all. When sleep finally did come, it was thin and fragile, a mere paper crust stretched over a wide, thrumming core of full, unblinking alertness that never once let down its guard.
That second jolt to full consciousness hit me with a raw, unmistakeable lurch of terror. It was no howl of the gale, no thunder of the rising river that woke me. It was the slow, unhurried drift of something *towards* the tent, something that shrank the tiny sleeping part of my mind smaller and smaller till it dissolved entirely, leaving me bolt upright in my blankets, every muscle coiled, straining to listen.
Outside the canvas, there was a pattering. Thousands upon thousands of tiny, soft, scuttling footsteps, brushing, tapping, creeping. I realized I had been hearing it for hours, half-processed at the edge of my sleep, long before it dragged me fully awake. I sat there, wide as if I had never slept a wink, my breath coming in tight, shallow gasps as a dead, heavy weight settled over every inch of my skin. For all the swelter of the summer night, I was slick with icy clammy sweat, shivering so hard my teeth chattered. Something was pressing steadily inward against the tent walls, pressing down hard on the roof from above. Was it the wind’s unseen bulk? Rain? Spray blown off the river? I ran through a dozen plausible explanations in the span of ten heartbeats—then jumped to what felt like the obvious answer: the single tall poplar on the island had snapped half-way through, its bough caught in other branches, its leaves tapping on the taut canvas as it swayed, ready to tear free and crush us both at the next gust.
I wrenched the loose tent flap up and stumbled out, yelling for the Swede to follow.
When I stood full upright on the sand, the tent was clear. No hanging broken bough. No rain. No river spray. Nothing at all was approaching. A thin, cold grey light seeped through the willow branches over my head, gilding the sand in faint, ghostly streaks. Stars still crowded the dark sky directly overhead, and the wind howled loud enough to vibrate in my bones, but our campfire had burned down to cold, dead ash, and streaks of bruising pink bled up the eastern horizon through the trees. Hours had passed since I’d stood in that same spot the night before, watching the writhing column of figures rise from the sand, and the memory hit me now like a punch to the gut, vivid and cruel as any bad dream that clings long after you wake. The ceaseless, raging gale left me drained, my bones heavy with the lassitude of a wholly sleepless night, but my nerves fizzed with sharp, tireless dread that banished any thought of rest. The river had risen further still; its roar filled the whole island, and fine stinging spray pricked my bare chest through my thin sleeping shirt. But there was not a single tangible sign of anything that could possibly explain the rising terror knotting tight in my throat.
My companion had not stirred when I called, and I saw no reason to wake him now. I scanned the camp slowly, marking every object: the upturned canoe half-buried in drifted sand, the two bright yellow paddles propped against its hull, the provision sack and extra lantern dangling side by side from the poplar’s lowest branch. And all around us, wrapping the camp in its endless hissing throng: the willows, those uncountable, shaking, whispering willows. A bird let out its thin, sharp morning cry, and a flock of ducks whirred overhead through the twilight, their wings beating the air hard. Dry, stinging sand swirled around my bare ankles in the gale.
I circled the tent, then pushed a little way out into the brush to stare across the river at the far bank, and that same wordless, suffocating distress clamped down on my chest again. The unbroken sea of bushes stretched to the horizon, ghost-pale and utterly unreal in the wan pre-dawn light. I wandered soft-footed through the undergrowth, still turning that endless pattering sound over in my head, still puzzling over the heavy weight that had pressed down on the tent and woken me. It had to be the wind, I told myself: wind driving loose hot sand grains hard against the taut canvas, wind bearing down heavy on our fragile little canvas roof. But no matter how hard I tried to convince myself, my nervousness, my quiet unnameable malaise, grew sharper by the minute.
I crossed the narrow strip of shallow water to the far shore of the island, and saw how the gale had torn great chunks of sand away from the banks through the night, warping the whole familiar coastline into something new and unrecognizable. I dipped my hands and feet into the cold current, splashed water over my burning forehead. The first glow of sunrise was already bleeding over the horizon, and the air hummed with the sharp, fresh sweetness of the coming day. On my walk back, I deliberately passed right through the exact clump of bushes where I had watched that column of shadowy figures rise up into the air. Midway through the thick willow thicket, a vast, engulfing terror swallowed me whole. A great, dark, staggering figure blurred past me in the shadows, so close the edge of its trailing bulk brushed my shoulder. Someone had walked right past me—no doubt, no mistake. A hard, gusting blow of wind shoved me forward, and once I stumbled out into the open clearing, the weight of that terror receded as suddenly as it had fallen. The winds walk, I told myself, remembering old tales I’d heard from rivermen, great living presences that drift through the trees like ghosts. The fear hanging over me was something so vast, so entirely unlike any fear I had ever known, that it woke a raw, awe-struck wonder in me that fought to counteract its sharpest bite. When I climbed the high sandy knoll at the island’s center, and looked out over the wide stretch of river glowing crimson under the first streaks of sun, the wild, unearthly beauty of the view was so overwhelming it made my throat tight, like a cry was about to tear out of me.
The cry never came. My gaze drifted from the far bank back down to our little camp half-hidden in the willows, and I made a discovery so awful that all my prior terror of walking winds shrank to nothing next to it.
The landscape had shifted. It was not that my higher vantage point had changed my view. The willows had moved. Their positions relative to the tent, the tent’s position relative to them—something was irreversibly different. The bushes now crowded far, far closer, so close it made my skin crawl. They had crept nearer. Silent, slow, soft-footed over the shifting sands, drawing imperceptibly closer all night with unhurried, gentle movements, the willows had advanced while we slept. Had the wind moved them? Or had they moved on their own? I thought of that endless pattering of tiny feet outside the tent, the heavy weight that had pressed down on my chest and woken me screaming. I swayed on the sandy knoll like a loose reed, barely able to stay upright. There was no accident here. It was deliberate. Personal. Hostile intent hung thick in the air, and it froze me rigid where I stood.
Then the reaction hit. The idea was so absurd, so ridiculous, that I wanted to laugh out loud. But the laughter would not come any easier than the cry I had choked back a moment before. The sudden knowledge hit me like a cold bucket of water: they were not coming for our bodies first. The attack was coming for our minds, to warp what we saw, what we knew, what we could trust, and it was already well underway.
The wind buffeted me roughly, and the sun cleared the horizon fast— it was past four o’clock, and I realized I must have stood rooted on that little sand pinnacle for far longer than I knew, too afraid to climb back down and get close to the willows. I crept back to the tent slow and quiet, doing one last full circuit of the camp, and I will not lie to myself or to you: I took measurements. I paced off the distance between the nearest willow trunk and the tent’s canvas, marking every step on the warm sand, burning the exact shortest distance into my memory.
I crawled back into my blankets. My companion appeared to still be deeply asleep, and I was grateful for it. If I had no one to corroborate these strange, terrible experiences, I could force myself to deny them. With full daylight, I could convince myself it had all been a hallucination, a fever dream of the sleepless night, nothing more than my overactive imagination run wild. Nothing else disturbed the camp after that. I fell asleep almost instantly, utterly drained, every last scrap of my spent energy gone—even as I waited, every thrumming nerve on edge, to hear that uncanny multitudinous patter of tiny feet outside the canvas once more, or feel that heavy weight settle over my chest, making my breath impossible to draw.
