Mr. Home remained for two days. Throughout his stay, he could not be persuaded to venture outdoors. He spent his entire days seated by the fireplace, alternating between profound silence and engaging in conversation with Mrs. Bretton. Her chatter was perfectly suited to a man in his melancholy state—not overly sympathetic, yet congenial enough, sensible, and possessing a gentle, maternal warmth appropriate for someone slightly older than him.

Paulina, meanwhile, was a study in contrasts: simultaneously joyful and silent, industrious and observant. Her father often lifted her onto his knee, where she would remain until she sensed—or imagined—he grew restless. Then, with a dramatic flair, she would declare, "Papa, put me down; I shall tire you with my weight."

The heavy burden would slide to the rug, settling on the carpet or a stool right at her father's feet. There, the white workbox and the scarlet-speckled handkerchief would come into play. This handkerchief was intended as a keepsake for "Papa" and needed to be finished before his departure; consequently, the demand on the seamstress's industry was rigorous (she managed about twenty stitches in thirty minutes).

Evening restored Graham to the maternal roof (his days were spent at school), bringing a fresh wave of animation to the household. This liveliness was undiminished by the dynamic interactions between him and Miss Paulina.

A distant and haughty demeanor had resulted from the slight inflicted upon her during the first evening of his arrival. Her standard reply when addressed was, "I can't attend to you; I have other things to think about." When pressed to specify *what* things:

"Business."

Graham would attempt to capture her attention by opening his desk to display its varied contents: seals, bright sticks of wax, penknives, and a miscellaneous collection of engravings—some vividly colored—that he had accumulated over time. While this potent temptation was not entirely ineffective, her eyes, furtively raised from her work, cast numerous glances toward the writing table, cluttered with scattered images. An etching of a child playing with a Blenheim spaniel happened to flutter to the floor.

"Pretty little dog!" she exclaimed, delighted.

Graham prudently ignored the comment. Soon, she stealthily moved from her corner to examine the treasure more closely. The dog's large eyes, long ears, and the child's hat adorned with feathers were irresistible.

"Nice picture!" was her favorable critique.

"Well—you may have it," Graham offered.

She seemed to hesitate. The desire to possess was strong, but accepting it would compromise her dignity. No. She placed it down and turned away.

"You won't have it, then, Polly?"

"I would rather not, thank you."

"Shall I tell you what I will do with the picture if you refuse it?"

She half-turned to listen.

"Cut it into strips for lighting the taper."

"No!"

"But I shall."

"Please—don't."

Graham, unmoved by her pleading tone, retrieved the scissors from his mother's workbasket.

"Here goes!" he announced, making a menacing gesture. "Right through Fido's head, and splitting little Harry's nose."

"No! _No!_ NO!"

"Then come to me. Come quickly, or it is done."

She hesitated, lingered, but eventually complied.

"Now, will you have it?" he asked as she stood before him.

"Please."

"But I shall want payment."

"How much?"

"A kiss."

"Give the picture first into my hand."

As Polly said this, she looked somewhat faithless. Graham handed it over. She absconded as a debtor, darted to her father, and took refuge on his knee. Graham rose in mock wrath and followed. She buried her face in Mr. Home's waistcoat.

"Papa—papa—send him away!"

"I'll not be sent away," Graham replied.

Keeping her face averted, she extended her hand to ward him off.

"Then, I shall kiss the hand," he said; but at that very moment, it transformed into a miniature fist, dealing him payment in a small coin that was certainly not a kiss.

Graham, proving as shrewd in his own way as his little playmate, retreated, appearing thoroughly defeated. He threw himself onto a sofa, resting his head against the cushion, lying as if in pain. Polly, noticing his silence, peeked at him. His eyes and face were hidden behind his hands. Turning on her father's knee, she gazed at her foe with anxiety for a long time. Graham groaned.

"Papa, what is the matter?" she whispered.

"You had better ask him, Polly."

"Is he hurt?" (a second groan echoed.)

"He makes a noise as if he were," Mr. Home noted.

"Mother," Graham suggested feebly, "I think you had better send for the doctor. Oh my eye!" (silence resumed, broken only by sighs from Graham.)

"If I were to become blind——?" he added finally.

His tormentor could not bear the suggestion. She rushed to his side immediately.

"Let me see your eye: I did not mean to touch it, only your mouth; and I did not think I hit so _very_ hard."

Silence answered her. Her features contorted—"I am sorry; I am sorry!"

Then came emotion, faltering, leading to weeping.

"Stop trying that child, Graham," Mrs. Bretton interjected.

"It is all nonsense, my pet," cried Mr. Home.

Once more Graham snatched her up, and she again punished him; while pulling his lion-like hair, she called him—the "naughtiest, rudest, worst, untruest person that ever was."

On the morning of Mr. Home's departure, he and his daughter had a private conversation in a window recess; I heard part of it.

"Couldn't I pack my box and go with you, papa?" she whispered earnestly.

He shook his head.

"Should I be a trouble to you?"

"Yes, Polly."

"Because I am little?"

"Because you are little and tender. It is only great, strong people that should travel. But don't look sad, my little girl; it breaks my heart. Papa will soon come back to his Polly."

"Indeed, indeed, I am not sad, scarcely at all."

"Polly would be sorry to give papa pain; would she not?"

"Sorrier than sorry."

"Then Polly must be cheerful: not cry at parting; not fret afterwards. She must look forward to meeting again, and try to be happy meanwhile. Can she do this?"

"She will try."

"I see she will. Farewell, then. It is time to go."

"_Now_?—just _now_?"

"Just now."

She held up quivering lips. Her father sobbed, but she, I noticed, did not. After setting her down, he shook hands with everyone present and departed.

When the street door closed, she dropped to her knees at a chair with a cry—"Papa!"

It was low and prolonged, akin to a "Why hast thou forsaken me?" For several minutes thereafter, I perceived she endured agony. Within that brief interval of her infancy, she experienced emotions some never feel; it was woven into her constitution; she would have more such moments if she lived. No one spoke. Mrs. Bretton, being a mother, shed a tear or two. Graham, who was writing, lifted his eyes and gazed at her. I, Lucy Snowe, remained calm.

Left unharassed, the little creature did for herself what no one else could—struggled with an intolerable feeling and, sooner or later, suppressed it. That day she accepted solace from no one, nor the next; she grew increasingly passive afterward.

On the third evening, as she sat on the floor, worn and quiet, Graham entered and picked her up gently, saying nothing. She did not resist; rather, she nestled into his arms as if weary. When he sat, she rested her head against him; within minutes, she fell asleep. He carried her upstairs to bed. I was not surprised that the following morning, the first thing she demanded was, "Where is Mr. Graham?"

It happened that Graham was not coming to the breakfast table; he had exercises to write for that morning's class and had asked his mother to send a cup of tea into the study. Polly volunteered to carry it; she must be busy about something, looking after someone. The cup was entrusted to her, for if restless, she was also careful. Since the study faced the breakfast room across the passage, my eye followed her.

"What are you doing?" she asked, pausing on the threshold.

"Writing," Graham replied.

"Why don't you come to take breakfast with your mamma?"

"Too busy."

"Do you want any breakfast?"

"Of course."

"There, then."

She deposited the cup on the carpet, like a jailer passing a prisoner's pitcher of water through a cell door, and retreated. Shortly, she returned.

"What will you have besides tea—what to eat?"

"Anything good. Bring me something particularly nice; that's a kind little woman."

She went back to Mrs. Bretton.

"Please, ma'am, send your boy something good."

"You shall choose for him, Polly; what shall my boy have?"
