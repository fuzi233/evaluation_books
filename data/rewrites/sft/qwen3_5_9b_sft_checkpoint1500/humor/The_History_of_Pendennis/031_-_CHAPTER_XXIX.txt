The journey from the quiet, wooded shadows of the West to the roaring chaos of London was not merely a change of geography; it was a metamorphosis of spirit. As the 'Alacrity' coach thundered through the velvet darkness, shedding the gentle gossip of Clavering and the humble, humming routine of Fairoaks, Arthur Pendennis sat poised on the edge of destiny. With every mile the wheels churned, the young man's mind spun a kaleidoscope of futures. He was no longer the boy who had stumbled onto this road a year prior, heading to Oxford with a naive stride. Now, hardened by a single, stinging failure, he possessed a sharper edge, a deeper reservoir of courage, and a self-assurance that bloomed like a flower in frost.

Yet, beneath the ambition, a tender current flowed. His thoughts drifted, with a warmth that defied the nipping air, to his mother. She stood at home, likely awake, her prayers rising to the same stars he gazed upon. "O Heaven!" he whispered, clutching the fabric of hope. "Grant me the strength to be worthy of her love, to work honestly, and to resist the snares that wait." Her love was his talisman, a shield against the coming storm. But there was another shadow in his heart—a regret for Laura. He had pushed her away, convinced himself unworthy of her radiant, innocent nature. Like a prodigal child fleeing a church he once loved, he drew back from her spotless purity, admitting with a pang of shame that he was not a match for such a creature. Yet, strangely, he felt liberated by her rejection, as if stepping clear of a holy ground where he felt he had no right to tread.

Sleep eluded him until the pale fingers of dawn touched the windows. By the time the coach halted at the bustling breakfast stop at B--, the sun had burst forth, igniting the road with gold. The pace quickened, the horn blared, and the miles flew by in a blur. Pen smoked, joked with the guard, and mingled with the shifting throngs, his spirit buoyant. The atmosphere thickened with every instant; the final team of grey horses broke from H--, and the coach swept into the jaws of the great beast that was London.

"What a place!" Pen thought, his heart hammering a rhythm of anticipation. "Here begins my battle. Fight or fall—that is the law." From the roof of the carriage, he surveyed the sprawling metropolis with the feverish longing of a soldier on the eve of campaign.

Among the passengers, he struck up a conversation with a cheery gentleman in a shabby cloak, a reporter for *The Star* named Mr. Doolan. This man spoke of literature as if it were his native tongue, regaling Pen with tales of Tom Campbell, Tom Hood, and Sydney Smith, treating them as old friends. Passing Brompton, he pointed out Mr. Hurtle, the formidable reviewer, striding with his umbrella. "A Boniface man!" Pen declared, peering over the side. "I admire his works!" Doolan chuckled, confirming the reputation. Pen, already intoxicated by the allure of the pen, calculated wildly: if the famous novelist Wagg earned three hundred pounds a volume, perhaps he, too, could carve out a fortune of five thousand a year. For now, he believed in the magic of authors and editors, revering even Wagg, whose intellect seemed flawed to others but who was clearly a king in the realm of print.

As the coach pulled up at the gloomy precincts of the Gloster Coffee-House, the first face to greet him was that of Harry Foker. Foker emerged from behind an enormous cab-horse, a vision in white kid gloves and tight leather breeches, his chin sporting a tuft of hair that nature had generously bestowed. Beside him rode a diminutive cab-boy, knobby-kneed and vibrating with energy. Foker eyed the dusty coach, the smoking horses of the 'Alacrity,' with a twinkle in his eye. "What, Foker!" cried Pendennis. "Hullo! Pen, my boy!" shouted the other, waving his whip in a salute that spoke of old camaraderie. Pen, thrilled to be in the lion's den and surrounded by his peculiar friend, invited Doolan to dine with him at Covent Garden. He called a cab, rattled off to the coffee-house in high spirits, and was greeted with the familiar bustle of waiters and the polite bows of the landlord. He sought out the landlady, wishing he could shake hands with everyone, his pocket heavy with a hundred pounds and his heart lighter with purpose.

Dressed in his very best, Pen dined modestly on a pint of sherry, determined to exercise economy, before venturing to the adjacent theatre. The lights, the music, the sheer cacophony of the crowd enchanted him. These sights, new to the country boy, filled him with a giddy exhilaration. He laughed at the jokes, applauded the songs, and strutted through the lobbies with the confidence of someone belonging to the highest circles. What weary frequenter of London's pavements could not recall such youthful delusions?

There, amidst the revelry, was young Foker again, this time in the company of Grandy Tiptoff of the Household Brigade, Lord Tiptoff's brother, and Lord Colchicum, a venerable peer who had been a man of pleasure since the days of the first French Revolution. Foker dragged Pen into a private box, where sat a lady with the longest ringlets and the fairest shoulders—Miss Blenkinsop, the eminent actress of high comedy. Behind her, snoozing in a wig, sat her father, the "useful" and "veteran" Blenkinsop, a man who played the heavy father both on stage and in private life.

It was nearly midnight. Back at Fairoaks, Mrs. Pendennis slept, wondering if her Arthur had rested, while Laura, too, lay awake. Yesterday, as the coach rolled over silent commons, Pen had vowed to reform, his heart anchored at home. Now, however, the farce was proceeding with magnificent success. On stage, Mrs. Leary, clad in a hussar jacket and braided pantaloons, enchanted the audience with her archness and lovely figure.

Pen, new to the town, wanted to listen to Mrs. Leary, but the box was a hive of chatter. Tiptoff knew the origin of her maillot; Colchicum recalled seeing her in '14. Miss Blenkinsop informed Pen that she sang out of tune, a revelation that shocked Pen, who saw an angel and heard a nightingale. Then Hoppus appeared as Sir Harcourt Featherby. The gentlemen in the box declared him stale, and Tiptoff prepared to fling Miss Blenkinsop's bouquet to him.

"Not for the world!" cried the daughter of the veteran Blenkinsop. "Lord Colchicum gave it to me."

Pen recalled the nobleman's name, bowing and blushing as he offered thanks for being voted into the Megatherium Club at his uncle's request. "What, you're Wigsby's nephew, are you?" asked the peer, using the familiar nickname that made Pen blush. "We balloted you last Wednesday. Your uncle wasn't there."

Delighted, Pen professed his gratitude, delivering a handsome speech to which the peer listened through his double opera-glass. "Don't be always looking at that box, you naughty creature," chided Miss Blenkinsop. "She's a dev'lish fine woman, that Mirabel," said Tiptoff, adding, "though Mirabel was a d——d fool to marry her." "A stupid old spooney," agreed the peer.

"Mirabel!" cried Pendennis.

"Ha! ha!" laughed Harry Foker. "We've heard of her before, haven't we, Pen?"

It was Pen's first love. It was Miss Fotheringay. The year prior, she had been led to the altar by Sir Charles Mirabel, G.C.B., a diplomat who had negotiated the Peace of Pultusk. "Emily was always as stupid as an owl," remarked Miss Blenkinsop. "Eh! Eh! pas si bete," countered the old Peer. "Oh, for shame!" cried the actress, oblivious to the meaning.

And there he saw her again, his first love, and wondered how he could ever have loved her so deeply. Thus, on his first night in London, Arthur Pendennis found himself entangled with a club, an actress of genteel comedy, a heavy father of the stage, and a dashing society of jovial blades. Lord Colchicum, though stricken in years and bald, remained indefatigable in the pursuit of joy, boasting that he could drink as much claret as the youngest member of his circle. He patronized the arts in all languages, hosting dramatic professors from England, Italy, France, and the Opera, entertaining scores of young men of fashion who consorted with the greenroom's ladies.

The following day, Pen paid his entrance fees to the Megatherium Club, an operation that consumed exactly one-third of his hundred pounds. He took possession of the edifice, ate his luncheon with immense satisfaction, and sank into an easy-chair in the library, trying to read the magazines. He worried about the members staring at him, daring to keep their hats on in such refined rooms. Sitting down, he wrote a letter to Fairoaks on the club paper, declaring how comforting this place would be after his labors.
