The crowd had hardly rolled away toward the Piazza before Romola came down into the street and hastened to the steps of San Stefano. Cecco, drawn like the rest by that tide of bodies and bells, was gone; but Baldassarre stood where she had left him, alone against the church-door, the horn-cup in his hand as if it were the last vessel in the world that belonged to him.

Something in him had changed as if a shutter had been flung back. The vacant, half-wandering look of a mind that had returned only by fragments was gone; in its place there burned a fixed fierceness—no wild ranting, but the glare of a creature that has watched its prey pass safely beyond bars, and bites the iron with its eyes. When Romola came near and spoke, that look struck on her as though she were the very object of it. She started; then the thought came quickly—he had seen Tito.

And with that thought, against her will, there rose a thin, shamefaced hope—hope like the taste of water when one has been too long in dust: perhaps this old man was the guilty one; perhaps the dread, the secrecy, the inward recoil she had felt in her husband had not had this misery for its root. If she could bring Tito face to face with him—if she could know, and so either be set free from suspicion or be made sure of her fear.

“If you will come with me,” she said, steadying her voice as one steadies a bowl filled too near the brim, “I can give you shelter and food until you are quite rested and strong. Will you come?”

“Yes,” Baldassarre answered, and then, as if his words were for himself alone, he repeated with a hungry mutter, “I shall be glad to get my strength. I want to get my strength.”

“Come,” she said, inviting him to walk at her side; and she chose the way by the Arno toward the Ponte Rubaconte, where the air was wider, and fewer faces pressed near. Under the arch of the city’s glad sound, the river ran with a steady, indifferent swash against stone; and above it the bells pealed, rich and exultant, as if the very bronze had been fed and warmed.

“I think you are not a Florentine,” Romola said at last, trying to lead him by a small, safe thread. The bridge beneath them was thronged with the after-murmur of the procession, but here and there a pause opened, and the river’s voice crept in.

He looked round at her without speaking. Suspicion sat on him like a rough cloak; his bodily weakness made the fog in his mind thicker, not thinner, and he guarded his thought as a miser guards his last coin. But Romola’s gaze was not that of a questioner who would seize and use; it was the gaze of one who has carried burdens and learned reverence for pain. Slowly, as if against his will, he answered—

“No. I am no Florentine; I am a lonely man.”

She saw his reluctance, and dared not press him, lest he should turn away and be lost. Yet her mind would not be still. If he had been the wrong-doer, why this dread and secrecy? Why that hatred which had looked through him like a knife-blade? They went on in silence until they reached the entrance into the Via de’ Bardi. There Baldassarre turned on her with a sudden movement, as if some shock had passed through his nerves—some remembered turn of stone, some scent of a familiar lane, some old echo of footsteps.

A few moments after, Romola paused at the half-open door of the court and turned toward him. Before she could speak, he broke out—

“Ah! you are his wife.”

“Whose wife?” Romola said. The words were like the quick fall of a shutter; her heart leaped, and stood listening.

But it was impossible for Baldassarre, in that instant, to recall a name. The very force with which Tito’s image pressed on him seemed to drive out all speech that could label it. He made no answer; only looked at her with a strange fixedness, as if he would pin her to the wall with his eyes and read what he needed there.

Romola opened the door wide and showed him the court. Straw lay thick over the stones, and on it four or five sick people were stretched—bodies made small by hunger, faces sharpened, hands lying like broken twigs. Among them little children crawled or sat, tiny pale creatures biting straws and gurgling with the innocent industry of starving mouths, as if the straw itself could be turned to sweetness by persistence. The air was the mingled breath of sickness, damp straw, and scant living—yet threaded now with the far promise carried by sound: bells, bells, bells, beating the news into every alley.

“If you will come in,” Romola said, her voice trembling as if she feared to disturb that frail heap of lives, “I will find you a comfortable place, and bring you some more food.”

“No, I will not come in,” Baldassarre said. Yet he did not go. He stood arrested, weighted by impressions his mind was too confused to master—like a man in a dream who sees a door and cannot remember whether it opens toward safety or toward fire.

“Can I do nothing for you?” Romola said. “Let me give you some money that you may buy food. It will be more plentiful soon.”

She drew her scarsella, and held out her palm with several grossi—more than she would have offered to any other man in the same want; for his misery clung to her with a particular dread, and she tried to ease it as one eases a pain by pressing hard elsewhere.

He looked at the coins a little while; then he said, with the abruptness of a decision long prepared—

“Yes, I will take them.”

She poured them into his hand. He closed his fingers tightly over them as if to keep not merely the silver, but the power inside it.

“Tell me,” Romola began, almost as if she pleaded. “What shall you—”

But Baldassarre had already turned away. He walked toward the bridge again, and when he had passed it he went straight up the Via del Fosso, as if led by scent rather than sight. There was Niccolò Caparra’s shop, crowded with the bright and dark bodies of weapons—steel catching light, hilts worn smooth by hands, blades sleeping in rows as loaves sleep in a baker’s window, each with its own promise of use.

Niccolò himself was that moment in procession with the armourers of Florence; only an apprentice was in the shop—a lad whose eyes were on trade rather than on faces. Baldassarre’s hunger altered: it was not bread his gaze devoured first. His eyes chose what they wanted with the swift certainty of a starving man choosing a crust. The apprentice, less observant and less scrupulous than his master, took three grossi for a sharp hunting-knife without hesitation.

It was small enough to hide; small enough to seem almost harmless—yet its edge was keen as a confession. Baldassarre thrust it within the breast of his tunic and walked on feeling stronger, as if the blade were a bone returned to him. That sharpness might give deadliness to the thrust of an aged arm: at least it was a companion; it was a power in league with him, even if it failed. It would break against armour—but was armour always there? In those long months while vengeance had lain in prison, baseness might have grown forgetful and secure. The knife had been bought with the traitor’s own money. That was just.

Before he had taken the coins, he had known what he would do with them—buy a weapon. Yes, and if possible buy food too: food to nourish the arm that would grasp the weapon; food to nourish the body which was the temple of vengeance. When he had had enough bread, he should be able to think and act—to think first how he could hide himself, lest Tito should have him dragged away again.

With that need of hiding in his mind, he turned into the narrowest streets, where stone leaned toward stone, and the city’s great rejoicing became a muffled throb. He bought meat and bread, and sat down under the first loggia to eat. The bread was coarse, but to him it was substance, it was fuel for hatred; the meat, salt and dark, sat heavy on the tongue like an oath. Above, the bells swung into louder and louder peals of joy; the sound seized on him and made his body vibrate along with the air, yet to him it was not gladness but the voice of that strong world which was against him, ringing its triumph and leaving him outside it.

Romola watched him until he disappeared round the turning into the Piazza de’ Mozzi—half feeling that his departure was a relief, half reproaching herself for not pressing him harder, for not forcing the truth, for not learning whether there was in his lot any guiltless misery which she was not helpless to relieve. Yet what could she have done if the truth had proved to be a painful secret about her husband, added to the anxieties already weighing on her? Surely a wife might be permitted to desire ignorance of a husband’s wrong-doing—since she alone must not protest and warn men against him. But that thought caught in too many fine fibres of feeling to be followed now; she was weary, and weariness makes even a righteous tangle feel like a net over the face.

It was a time to rejoice. Help had come to Florence. She turned into the court to tell the good news to her patients on their straw beds. She closed the door behind her lest the bells should drown her voice; then she threw back the black drapery from her head so the women might see her better, and stood among them as if she were the messenger of something they could taste.

“Corn is coming,” she said. “The bells are ringing because they have brought the news.”

They all raised themselves to listen, each movement a slow expenditure. The children trotted or crawled toward her and tugged her black skirts, impatient at being so far from her face, as if her features were a warm loaf they needed near them. She yielded, weary as she was, and sat down on the straw. The little pale things peeped into her basket with the grave curiosity of hunger, pulled her hair down through their fingers, and the feeble voices around her murmured—

“The Holy Virgin be praised!”

“It was the procession!”

“The Mother of God has had pity on us!”

The words were old words—handed down like a household recipe whose measures are never written, only remembered by the tongue. In them, gratitude took the shape of names that had survived the ruin of many other names; for the poor, praise must often speak in the dominant lexicon that power has taught them, even when their own private sense of mercy has no exact translation.

At last Romola rose from the heap of straw, too tired to try and smile any longer.

“I will come by-and-by,” she said, turning toward the stone steps, “to bring you your dinner.”

“Bless you, madonna! bless you!” came the faint chorus, in much the same tone in which a few minutes before they had praised and thanked the unseen Madonna.

Romola cared a great deal for that music. Not the bells—those were too loud, too proud, too easily made to serve whatever master held the city’s rope. But the thin human sound in the court, the breaths shaping blessing, the children’s gurgles and small coughs, the rustle of straw as bodies shifted: that was a gentler sound-world, one that did not bruise the ear, one that asked rather than commanded. She had no innate taste for tending the sick and clothing the ragged, as some women have, to whom the details are welcome for their own sake like kneading dough or sorting herbs. Her early training had kept her aloof from such labours; without the inspiration of her deepest feeling, they would have been irksome, like work done in a foreign dialect that never lies easy on the tongue.

But they had become the one unshaken resting-place of her mind—the one narrow pathway where the light fell clear. If the gulf between herself and Tito, which only gathered a more perceptible wideness from her attempts to bridge it by submission, brought doubt whether the bond she laboured to be true to might itself be false—if she came away from her confessor, Fra Salvestro, or from contact with Savonarola’s disciples, with a sickening sense of their narrowness, and an almost impetuous reaction toward her old contempt for what she had once called superstition—she recovered firm footing here, among straw and fever and the plain needs that have no rhetoric.

Whatever else made her doubt, the help she gave to her fellow-citizens made her sure that Fra Girolamo had been right to call her back. According to his unforgotten words, her place had not been empty; it had been filled with her love and her labour. Florence had had need of her; and the more her own sorrow pressed upon her, the more gladness—stern, quiet gladness—she felt in the memories stretching through two long years: hours and moments when she had lightened the burden of life to others, giving not only alms but attention, not only bread but the recognition that their pain had a name in her mind.

All the ardour of her nature, which could no longer spend itself in a daughter’s tenderness or a wife’s trust, had transformed itself into an enthusiasm of sympathy with the general life. She had ceased to think her own lot could be happy—had ceased to think of happiness at all. The end of her days seemed to be the diminishing of sorrow, as one thins a broth so that many may have a portion, even if none be richly fed.

Her zeal was continually stirred to fresh vigour by Savonarola’s influence. In spite of the wearisome visions and allegories from which she recoiled when they came as stale repetitions from lips that did not burn with them, her strong affinity for his passionate sympathy and the splendour of his aims had lost none of its power. His indignation against abuses and oppression—those daily stories of Church and States—had kindled the ready fire in her also. His care for liberty and purity of government in Florence, and his constant reference of that immediate struggle to a wider regeneration, created in her a new consciousness: the great drama of human existence was not a distant pageant, but a living action in which her life had a part.

Through her daily contact with the less fortunate—through these courts where the voice was low and the air smelled of damp straw and thin broth—that consciousness grew firmer than a vague sentiment. It became a more definite motive of self-denying practice. She thought little of dogmas, and shrank from fixing her mind closely on the Frate’s prophecies of scourge and swift renewal. But she had submitted her mind to his and entered into communion with the Church because there she found an immediate satisfaction for moral needs that all her former culture had left hungry. Savonarola’s voice had given her a reason for living apart from personal enjoyment and personal affection; yet it was a reason that seemed to need feeding with forces beyond her own, and her use of the Church’s offices was like a watching and waiting—listening, as one listens in the dark for a returning step, if by any means fresh strength might come.

The pressing problem for Romola then was not controversy but endurance: to keep alive that flame of unselfish emotion by which a life of sadness might still be a life of active love. Her trust in Savonarola’s nature as greater than her own made a large part of the strength she had found, and that trust was not easily shaken. It is not force of intellect that causes ready repulsion from the aberrations and eccentricities of greatness—any more than it is sharp vision that makes the eye explore the warts on a face bright with human meaning; it is simply the negation of high sensibility.

Romola was so deeply moved by the grand energies in him that she listened patiently even when the words were strange to her inward speech—patiently, because in his living voice there was an earnestness that carried meanings no written phrase could translate. No soul is desolate while there remains a human being toward whom it can feel trust and reverence. Romola’s trust in Savonarola was like a rope stretched securely along a difficult path, making her step elastic while she grasped it. If it were suddenly removed, no firmness of ground could keep her from staggering—or perhaps from falling.
