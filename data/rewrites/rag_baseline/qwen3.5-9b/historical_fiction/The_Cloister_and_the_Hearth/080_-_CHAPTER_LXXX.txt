Brother Clement had labored in Basel for over a year, weaving his voice through the cobblestones and confessional booths of the city, when Jerome appeared before him. Dust coated the younger friar's robes, but his eyes burned with a triumphant clarity. "Glory be to God, Brother Clement," Jerome declared, his tone vibrating with anticipation. "You may now journey to England alongside me."

"I stand ready, Brother Jerome," Clement replied, his demeanor shifting instantly from weary scholar to eager pilgrim. "I have awaited your summons for months. In the quiet intervals between teaching and prayer, I have diligently cultivated the English tongue."

"Tactful indeed," Jerome acknowledged. He explained that his delay had been necessary to secure extraordinary faculties from the Pope: the authority to collect funds for the Church's cause in England and to administer sacraments within the secular monasteries. "Now, gird your loins," Jerome commanded, his voice hardening into steel. "Let us depart and strike a decisive blow for the Church, and against the encroaching influence of the Franciscans."

Thus began their pilgrimage down the Rhine, a river that served as both artery and boundary. In the bustling metropolises, they shared the pulpit, their voices merging in a chorus of reform. Yet in the smaller hamlets, they diverged, taking opposite banks of the winding waterway until they converged once more at a preordained meeting point. Both were masters of rhetoric, yet their styles operated on distinct frequencies. Jerome's oratory was noble and imposing, a towering edifice of logic, though occasionally constrained by rigid dogma and a monotony that risked alienating the common ear. Clement, conversely, possessed a fluidity that defied categorization. His speech was a tapestry of variety, tender and gently persuasive, curling around the listener's reason and heart like smoke in a draftless room. Yet, when the moment demanded, Clement ascended. He soared with a prophetic intensity that left Jerome grounded. *Vehemens ut procella, excitatus ut torrens, incensus ut fulmen*—violent as a storm, roused as a torrent, inflamed as lightning—he thundered, flashed, and swept all before him with the rushing waves of his eloquence.

Jerome possessed the scholarly humility to admit such disparity, citing five reasons why he withheld samples of Clement's fiery Latin: the difficulty of translation, the brevity of time, prior publication, the existence of superior imitators, and the literary axiom that while uniformity suits peas, it constitutes a vice in books. Nevertheless, Clement employed a subtle strategy: he secretly investigated the specific sins plaguing each town before ascending the rostrum. Jerome, however, remained unbending, scorning the detour required to tailor his message to local vices.

At a great town situated several leagues from the Rhine, they climbed the same pulpit in succession. Jerome addressed the vanity of dress, a theme dear to his heart. He spoke with biting satire, and the congregation listened with complacent nods, for vanity was not their primary failing. Then Clement stepped forward to confront their true demon: drunkenness. It was a festering wound in these lands, shielded by clerical hypocrisy and popular prejudice. Clement delivered merciless blows drawn from Holy Writ and grim reality. He identified alcohol not merely as a vice, but as the nurturing mother of all other crimes, particularly theft and murder.

He recounted a recent parricide committed by a man intoxicated, and described how a gang of drunkards had roasted one of their own comrades alive in a neighboring village. "Your late prince," Clement proclaimed, his voice rising to a crescendo, "is rumored to have died of apoplexy, yet you know the truth: he died of drink. Last month, one of your aldermen perished miserably, drowned in his own stupor within a muddy puddle."

His gaze swept the crowd, piercing the collective shame. "Your children's backs go bare so that you may fill your bellies with the poison that turns you into the worst of beasts—silly as calves, yet fierce as boars. This deprives your families of sustenance and drives your souls straight to hell. I tell you, your town, yes, your entire nation, would sink to the bottom of human degradation if your women drank as you do. How long will they remain temperate, resisting nature to oppose the example of their husbands and fathers? Vice never stands still. Amend yourselves, or watch them descend to your level. Already in Bohemia, women drink alongside men."

"How does a drunken woman appear?" he demanded, the question hanging heavy in the air. "Would you wish to see your wives, your mothers, reduced to such a state?"

A roar of horror erupted from the assembly. Medieval audiences had not yet mastered the art of passive amusement; they reacted viscerally. "That strikes home!" cried the friar, seizing the momentum. "Do you suppose it shocks the All-Pure God less to witness a man, His noblest creation, transformed into a drunken beast, than it shocks you, creatures of sin and unreason, to see a woman degraded into something no better than yourselves?"

Clement concluded with two stark tableaux: the chaotic wreckage of a drunkard's household versus the serene order of a sober man's life. The details were so vivid, so brutally true, that the women fell into a collective swoon of "Ohing" and "Ahing," murmuring, "Eh, but that is a true word."

The discourse ignited a frenzy. Hearers clustered in knots; the men bristled with indignation. The women, however, rallied behind them, openly siding with the accused against the preacher. A married man, they argued, had a divine right to a drop; he needed it to sustain the labor of feeding his family. They refused to trade their sturdy drinkers for weak-willed "milksops." The hypocrisy was palpable.

That very evening, a mob of men cornered nearly a hundred individuals near Brother Clement, stuffing his wallet with coins and plucking roses from their own heads to offer him, kissing his habit and blessing him for undertaking the arduous task of curing their "sots."

Jerome observed the scene with a furrowed brow. "Drunkenness is not heresy, Clement," he remarked critically. "To dedicate an entire sermon to it seems... earthly."

As they continued their journey, Jerome discovered to his astonishment that Clement's serments penetrated the populace far deeper than his own. They compelled listeners to listen, to think, to weep, and occasionally, to amend their ways. "He possesses the art of sinking to their peg," Jerome mused, watching the crowds react. "Yet he retains the capacity to soar high enough at times."

On the whole, the phenomenon puzzled Jerome, who harbored a secret sense of superiority over his tenderer brother. Eventually, after traveling approximately two hundred miles, the situation ceased to puzzle him and began to displease him. He struggled to suppress the sentiment as petty and unworthy. "Souls differ like locks," Jerome reasoned aloud to himself, attempting to rationalize the divergence. "Preachers must differ like keys, or the Church remains locked shut, unable to open for God to pass through. And certainly, this novice holds the key to these northern souls, being himself a northern man."

And so they descended slowly along the Rhine, sometimes drifting a few miles downstream, but mostly walking the banks, preaching, teaching, and hearing confessions in the scattered towns and villages. Finally, they reached Düsseldorf.

There lay the small quay where Gerard and Denys had once boarded their boat upstream. The friars disembarked upon it. The streets stretched out before them, familiar and unchanged, save for the presence of "The Silver Lion." Nothing had altered in the cityscape, yet everything had shifted within the traveler who walked through it barefoot. His heart was calm and cold, his hands folded across his breast, his eyes bent meekly toward the earth—a true son of Dominic and the Holy Church, returning to a world he knew intimately, yet forever changed by the fires he had kindled.
