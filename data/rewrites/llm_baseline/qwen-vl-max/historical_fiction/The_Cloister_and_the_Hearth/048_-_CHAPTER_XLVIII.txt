Catherine sat in quiet dread, her thoughts turning like a millstone as she reckoned the time until Gerard’s return—no more than a month, perhaps just a week. How could she face him, knowing she had failed to watch over his betrothed? The weight of that failure pressed upon her chest like a stone. And then there was Margaret—what had become of her? The uncertainty gnawed at Catherine, sometimes twisting into something worse: the fear that the girl might have taken her own life.

“Oh, Kate,” she murmured, her voice thick with sorrow, “if only she hadn’t gone and done such a thing!”

“Mother,” Kate replied gently, “she wouldn’t do that. She’s not wicked.”

“Ah, my dear,” Catherine sighed, “you don’t understand how young girls can be—impulsive, reckless, without anyone to guide them. They’ll throw themselves into water for a man who, the next day, would make them forget him in an instant. I’ve seen it happen—girls jumping in one moment, screaming for help the next, as if they didn’t know their own minds. And when their bodies ache with pain—oh, no creature suffers like a woman does—then their spirits snap. That patience they carry like a shield, stronger than any other soul save perhaps a donkey, vanishes all at once. And so they leap. That’s why I say men are monsters.”

“Mother!” Kate gasped.

“Monsters, I tell you,” Catherine insisted, “for building these endless canals, tempting poor women into ruin. They know we won’t cut our throats—we hate blood, and value our skin more than our lives. Hanging gives time to change one’s mind—the nail is driven, the rope tied, and still there’s thought. But a canal? It’s like stepping into bed. Once in, the water takes care of the rest, whether you want it or not. Look at me—I’m the mother of nine, yet I confess I once stood on the edge of our own canal, ready to jump.”

“No, mother,” Kate said, shaking her head, “I won’t believe it.”

“You may,” Catherine said firmly. “It was the first year we lived together. Eli and I were young, neither of us knowing the other’s weaknesses. We quarreled, pulled apart like two stubborn oxen. I ran off, crying, to tell some idle gossip what I should never have spoken aloud—not even to saints—but there was a canal right before me. Do they take us for ducks, I wonder? Oh, how inviting it looked! I said to myself, ‘Since he has cast me out, let him see me drowned. Then he’ll change his key. He’ll weep bitterly, and I’ll look down from heaven’—though I forgot I’d be on the other side—‘and see him mourn. Oh, how sweet that would be!’ And I was already stepping forward, barefoot on the cold stones, when suddenly I stopped. I remembered my new gown—nearly finished, fine linen, embroidered at the hem. So I turned back. When I returned, Eli said nothing about my tears, nor the quarrel. Instead, he said, ‘Let yon flea stick in the wall, my lass. Not a word I heard was true—except this: “I love thee.”’ Those were his very words. I remember them well—it was our first real fight. I threw my arms around his neck and wept, thinking of the canal. And he held me just as tightly, being a young man, warm and alive. That was better than drowning, better than spoiling my wedding dress and my new shoes—old John Bush made them, and now his nephew keeps the shop. And what was my grief compared to hers?”

Kate hoped, with all her heart, that Margaret loved her father deeply enough to stay by him, especially now that he was old and frail. “He is everything to her—father, mother, protector. She couldn’t leave him.”

“Not so,” Catherine replied. “When despair strikes, a woman forgets everything—family, duty, love. The sky seems black, the world cruel. I trust more in a child not yet born than in one nearing the grave to keep her safe. For surely, when a little innocent clings to our hearts, feeding from our veins, it goes against nature to end our lives.”

“Well then,” Kate said softly, “let us hold fast to hope.”

She added that perhaps their fears were too great, that likely Margaret was safe and well. Still, she ended with a solemn plea: “Please, mother, do not name the child’s sex. It brings ill luck, everyone says. As if girls aren’t born as often as boys.”

Her words drew a sharp retort. “Do you dare threaten me with a girl?!” Catherine exclaimed. “I don’t want another daughter while I have you. What use would a girl be to me? Can I sit her on my knee and see Gerard again, as I can with a son? No. I have decided. It is settled.”

“How can it be?” Kate asked.

“In my heart,” Catherine declared. “And if I am to be disappointed at last, it shall not be because you robbed me of hope first, telling me it will not be a child at all, but only a girl.”
