The temple guard’s sandaled feet scuffed the ruby dust of the outer gardens as they escorted me past terraces overgrown with the blood-red, thorn-studded blooms sacred to Issus, and there I found Xodar ringed by a baying horde of First Born nobles. The once-mighty Dator, stripped of his rank regalia, stood tall and unflinching as men with skins polished like obsidian slapped his cheeks, and women draped in iridescent silk spat at his bare feet, cursing him for a traitor to their eternal goddess.

The second they spotted me, their rage snapped toward me like a hunting calot’s jaws.
“Ah!” a burly black noble roared, baring his fangs. “This is the beast that bested Xodar bare-handed? Let us see if his luck holds against a true warrior of the First Born.”
The tallest of the women, her face painted with swirling gold patterns, laughed and called out, “Make him bind Thurid! Thurid is no fallen outcast cur—let the great Dator show this alien dog what it means to face a man who still walks in Issus’s favor!”
The cry of *Thurid! Thurid!* rose up from a dozen throats, and a new voice boomed over the din: “What is the meaning of this rabble?”

I turned to see him: a colossus of a black warrior, his harness crusted with rubies and platinum filigree, his sheathed long-sword heavy enough to cleave a green Martian war thoat in two, moving with the arrogant, unchallenged swagger of a man who had never known defeat. This was Thurid, highest ranked of all Issus’s fighting Dators.
Dozens of overlapping voices explained the game at once, and Thurid’s eyes slitted to narrow, venomous black shards as he rounded on Xodar. “Calot!” he hissed. “I always knew you carried the heart of a cowardly sorak in your rotting chest. For years you outmaneuvered me in Issus’s secret councils, but now your scabby true nature is bared to all. I spit on you, I spurn you with my foot—”

His boot swung hard for Xodar’s ribs, a deliberate, degrading kick meant to send the once-noble warrior sprawling in the dust. My blood had been simmering for minutes at the cowardly spectacle of a crowd tearing down a man who had once been their equal, and I saw red through a haze of righteous anger. I do not abide unfair persecution, and I acted before thought could catch up. My own foot snapped out, catching Thurid dead on the unprotected shin with a crack that made him howl and stagger, his kick flying wide of Xodar.

For a heartbeat the whole garden went dead silent. Then Thurid roared with rage and launched himself at my throat, exactly as Xodar had done on the deck of the First Born cruiser only a few days prior. The outcome was identical: I ducked under his flailing arms, drove a rock-hard right fist into the side of his jaw, and the 300-pound warrior spun like a child’s top, his knees buckling, crumpling unconscious at my feet in a cloud of ruby dust.

The crowd stared, gape-mouthed, first at the motionless Dator, then at me like they could not believe their eyes. “You asked me to bind Thurid,” I called out, loud enough for every last one of them to hear. “Behold!” I knelt, tore his own harness free, and bound his wrists and ankles tight with the leather straps. “Treat him exactly as you treated Xodar. Drag him before Issus, bound in his own regalia, so she can see there is a man here greater than her pet champions. I am John Carter, citizen of two worlds, Captain of Virginia, Prince of the House of Tardos Mors, Jeddak of Helium. Tell your goddess that I will meet any of her flower of warriors, naked-handed or armed with short-sword or long-sword, and beat them.”

The woman who had mocked me earlier whispered, awestruck, “Who are you?”
Before I could answer, the guard who had been tasked with escorting me back to the island stepped forward. “My orders are absolute,” he said, though there was no bite left in his tone, no trace of the contempt he had shown a prisoner only an hour before. “No more delays. Xodar—come with him.” For the rest of the march back to the great shaft that led down to the Sea of Omean, he walked a respectful pace behind me, his short-sword drawn, as if I was a Jeddak rather than a condemned slave.

Our journey back was uneventful. We descended the vast, echoing shaft in the same suspended car that had carried us up to Issus’s surface gardens, boarded the waiting submarine, and dove deep through dark, frigid water to traverse the tunnel that connected the outer world to the sunken sea’s vast cavern. We surfaced in the glass-smooth pool on the far side, then boarded a small, fast cruiser that carried us across miles of windless, phosphorescent green water to the Isle of Shador.

The island was nothing but bare gray rock, topped by a squat, windowless stone prison, guarded by half a dozen armed First Born. There was no ceremony to our incarceration. A guard dragged open the heavy oak door with a rusted iron key, we stepped inside, the door slammed shut behind us, and the lock grated with a sound that sent a wave of the same crushing hopelessness I had felt in the Therns’ Chamber of Mystery rolling over me. Back then Tars Tarkas had been at my side. Now I had no ally I could trust, no way to send word to my friends: neither the great Thark warrior nor Thuvia of Ptarth could possibly guess that I was trapped in a buried sea no surface dweller on Barsoom even believed existed. Even if they could, the full might of the First Born’s navy would tear any rescue fleet to shreds before it ever reached the cavern mouth. My situation looked impossible. But I straightened my shoulders, shoved the brooding despair away, and turned to survey my surroundings.

Xodar sat on a low stone bench, his head bowed, silent since Issus had stripped him of his rank. The prison was roofless, the walls rising thirty feet high, polished so smooth no hand or foot could grip them to climb. Halfway up, two small slits crisscrossed with iron bars let in faint traces of the cavern’s ubiquitous glow. The building was divided into half a dozen separate cells by 20-foot-high stone partitions, two of the connecting doors standing ajar. I wandered through the empty cells one by one, until in the furthest room I found a young red Martian boy asleep on the stone bench, his face relaxed, his limbs lean and graceful, his skin a pale, sun-kissed gold-red. There was something hauntingly familiar about his features, though I could not place his name. I did not wake him—sleep is the most precious boon a prisoner can have, and I had seen men turn feral over losing even a few minutes of it.

When I returned to our cell, Xodar had not moved. “Moping will do you no good,” I told him, clapping him on the shoulder. “There’s no shame in losing to John Carter. You saw me drop Thurid with one punch. You saw me kill three of your men on the cruiser deck. Why waste the rest of your days rotting here when two great fighters like us can win our freedom?”

He looked up at me, horrified. “You do not know what you say. Issus is omnipotent. She is omniscient. She hears every word you speak, reads every thought in your head. To even dream of defying her is sacrilege. She will strike you dead any second now, writhing in agony.”

I laughed. “Rot, Xodar. The red men of Barsoom have had the power to read thoughts for centuries. They also know how to shield their minds so no one can pry into them. I learned that trick decades ago. Your Issus cannot read my thoughts—she could not even read yours if you did not will it. When I knelt before her and pretended to gaze in awe at her ‘radiant holy face’? I thought she was the most hideous, vile old crone I had ever laid eyes on in my life.”

Xodar gasped, leapt to his feet, and lunged at me screaming “Blasphemer!” I did not want to hurt him, so I caught his wrist, swung my elbow under his chin, and bore him backward over my thigh, holding him helpless until the rage drained out of him. “I am not lying to you,” I said, setting him back on his feet. “Your goddess is no immortal. She is a mortal old woman, a fraud who has lied to you and every other First Born for centuries to keep you enslaved to her will. With your knowledge of this sunken realm and my experience fighting my way out of worse scrapes, we can escape.”

He said nothing, walked back to his bench, and sat lost in deep thought for hours. It was only when a soft sound came from the cell doorway that I looked up to see the young red Martian youth leaning against the frame, watching us with a curious smile.
“Kaor,” he said, using the traditional Barsoomian greeting.
“Kaor,” I replied. “What brings you to this prison?”
“I expect I’m waiting for my execution,” he said, grinning. “I’ve been here nearly a year, ever since I snuck a look at Issus’s so-called radiant face. I half expected to drop dead on the spot when I saw her: bloated, wrinkled, hideous, a fraud pretending to be the goddess of eternal life. No one could believe that garbage, not after seeing her.”
He told me he had been flying a one-man scout plane far south, chasing the legend of the Lost Sea of Korus, when his propeller had jammed over the southern ice cap. The First Born had swarmed him before he could make repairs, and he had fought his way through a dozen of them before they finally dragged him down. “I only regret that my mother will mourn me,” he said. “She mourned my father ten years ago, when he died before I was even born.”
I was about to ask him his father’s name when the heavy prison door creaked open. A burly guard dragged the boy back to his own cell, locked it, then came back to stand over us. “Issus commands that John Carter be given every leave to punish this fallen cur,” he said, jabbing a thumb at Xodar. “Heap every indignity you can think of on him, or you will be punished in his place.” The door slammed shut behind him.

When we were alone, Xodar lifted his head. “I have thought on every word you said, John Carter,” he said, quietly. “I see it now. All our faith, all our sacred rituals, all of it is a lie the priests and Issus have fed us for generations to steal our power. I renounce her. I am ready to fight. But how? These walls are solid polished rock, we cannot climb them, and there is no way out of this prison.”

I smiled. “I will scale that wall and carry you with me, easy. All I need from you is the best time to make the break, and a guide to the great shaft that leads up out of this cavern to the outer world.”
“Nighttime is our only chance,” Xodar said. “We are thousands of feet below the surface. The sun never reaches here. The glow you see, that faint phosphorescence, seeps out of the cavern dome rock. There is no darkness here at all. But at the hour when the outer world is dark, all the First Born sleep. Only a sleepy sentry keeps watch on the largest battleships, none at all on the small cruisers. The guards at the prison will be dozing.”
I shrugged. No darkness? That would make things trickier, but I had faced far worse odds. “We will rest first,” I said. “A solid plan will come to us when we wake.”

We stretched out on the cold hard stone floor, and fell asleep to the soft, endless lap of the windless Sea of Omean’s waves against the Isle of Shador’s rocky shores, the first spark of real hope we had known since we were dragged down into the sunken realm of Issus.
