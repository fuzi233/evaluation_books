The most distinguished guest at the dinner party was Mr. Murthwaite. Upon his return to England following his extensive travels, society had held him in high regard as a survivor of numerous perils, a man who had returned to recount his tales. He had recently declared his intention of revisiting the theaters of his past exploits and venturing into territories yet unmapped. This audacious disregard for personal safety, even for a second time, reignited the waning enthusiasm of his admirers. The odds seemed stacked heavily against his survival on this latest expedition; indeed, it is rare to encounter a celebrated figure at dinner without harboring a grim expectation that the breaking news of his demise might be the final word we hear of him.

Once the ladies had retired, leaving the gentlemen to their own devices in the dining room, the inevitable shift occurred. With the moderating influence of female company gone, the conversation naturally drifted toward politics—a staple of male discourse. Regarding this perennial national obsession, I confess I am perhaps the least "English" of Englishmen. Generally, I find political debate tedious and devoid of substance. Observing Mr. Murthwaite as the wine circulated, I noted he shared my sentiment. He managed the silence with considerable tact, sparing the host's feelings, yet it was evident he was preparing for sleep.

Seizing the moment, I decided to test whether a strategic mention of the Moonstone could rouse him, and if so, gauge his reaction to the latest developments regarding the alleged Indian conspiracy uncovered in my office.

"If I am not mistaken, Mr. Murthwaite," I began, "you knew the late Lady Verinder and were deeply involved in the peculiar sequence of events culminating in the loss of the Moonstone?"

The traveler woke instantly, asking who I was. I introduced myself, detailing my professional ties to the Herncastle family and recalling my unique role concerning the Colonel and his diamond during those earlier days. Mr. Murthwaite adjusted his position to shield himself from the rest of the company, focusing intently solely on me.

"Have you heard anything recently of the Indians?" he asked.

"I have every reason to believe," I replied, "that one of them visited my office yesterday."

Mr. Murthwaite, rarely astonished, was completely stunned by this admission. I recounted the encounters with both Mr. Luker and myself precisely as they had transpired.

"It is evident," I continued, "that the Indian's final question had a specific purpose. Why was he so eager to know the exact hour a borrower is permitted to repay a loan?"

"Do you truly fail to discern his motive, Mr. Bruff?"

"I am ashamed to admit my ignorance, Mr. Murthwaite—I cannot see it."

The traveler grew genuinely interested in probing the depths of my confusion. "Allow me to pose a question," he said. "Where does the conspiracy to seize the Moonstone currently stand?"

"I cannot say," I answered honestly. "The Indian plot remains a mystery to me."

"The Indian plot, Mr. Bruff, is a mystery only because you have not scrutinized it closely. Shall we review it together, tracing it from the drafting of Colonel Herncastle's will to the Indian's visit to your office? Given your position, it is crucial for Miss Verinder's interests that you grasp the full scope of this matter. Will you unravel the Indian's motive yourself, or shall I spare you the effort of investigation?"

Recognizing his practical intent, I immediately accepted the challenge.

"Very good," Mr. Murthwaite agreed. "First, consider the ages of the three Indians. They appear roughly the same age. Do you not think the man you encountered was in the prime of life? Certainly not forty. Now, recall when Colonel Herncastle arrived in England and devised his plan to secure his life. Without counting the years, it is clear that these current Indians are the successors of three others—high-caste Brahmins—who accompanied the Colonel to these shores. If they had merely replaced their predecessors, the matter would be trivial. Instead, they inherited the organization established by those forebears. While this network seems insignificant to us—comprising access to funds, the aid of shady English figures operating in London's underworld, and the covert support of a few compatriots working within the city—it is a foundation we must acknowledge.

"Now, based on your experience, what event first presented the Indians with an opportunity to seize the Diamond?"

I understood the reference. "The first opportunity," I replied, "was undeniably created by Colonel Herncastle's death. They would naturally learn of his passing."

"As expected. His death gave them their initial chance. Until then, the Moonstone remained secure in the bank's vault. You drafted the will leaving the jewel to his niece, and it was probated conventionally. As a lawyer, you understand the steps the Indians would take under English counsel."

"They would procure a copy of the will from Doctors' Commons," I stated.

"Precisely. An intermediary would obtain this document, revealing that the Moonstone was bequeathed to Lady Verinder's daughter and that Mr. Blake the Elder—or his appointee—would deliver it. Information regarding Lady Verinder and Mr. Blake would be easily acquired. The crux of the matter lay in timing: Should they strike while the diamond was being moved from the bank, or wait until it reached Lady Verinder's home in Yorkshire? The latter path was obviously safer. This explains why the Indians appeared at Frizinghall disguised as jugglers, biding their time. In London, their organization kept them informed; two agents tracked movements—one monitoring anyone leaving Mr. Blake's residence for the bank, and another bribing servants to gather gossip. These measures confirmed that Mr. Franklin Blake had visited the bank and identified him as the sole individual traveling to Yorkshire. You recall what ensued?"

I recalled that Franklin Blake had spotted one of the spies, thereby accelerating his departure to Yorkshire by several hours. Thanks to Old Betteredge's sound advice, he secured the diamond in the local bank at Frizinghall before the Indians could even approach the area. The logic held up perfectly. Yet, knowing the Indians were unaware of these precautions, why had they made no attempt on Lady Verinder's house throughout the intervening weeks prior to Rachel's birthday? I clarified that I was familiar with the accounts of the child and the ink stain, noting that explanations relying on clairvoyance held no weight for me.

"Neither does it hold weight for me," Mr. Murthwaite conceded. "What you describe as clairvoyance is merely a manifestation of the romantic sensibility inherent in the Indian character. It would be refreshing for them to cloak their arduous mission in a veil of the marvelous and supernatural. Their boy is undoubtedly susceptible to mesmeric influence; under such trance, he likely projected thoughts already present in the mesmerizer's mind. I have tested clairvoyance myself, but its manifestations rarely exceed this limit. The Indians do not analyze matters scientifically; they view their boy as a 'Seer' of the invisible. In this marvel, they find renewed vigor for their united purpose. However, in our inquiry, we rely strictly on rational deduction, tracing effects back to natural causes. Am I satisfying your expectations?"

"Not a doubt," I assured him, "though I await your rational explanation for the difficulty I just raised."

Mr. Murthwaite smiled. "That is the simplest difficulty of all. Your summary of the case is accurate. The Indians were unaware of Mr. Franklin Blake's actions with the diamond, evidenced by their immediate error on the first night of his arrival at his aunt's house."

"Their first mistake?" I echoed.

"Certainly! The blunder of allowing themselves to be ambushed on the terrace by Gabriel Betteredge. Yet, possessing ample time thereafter, they wisely refrained from approaching the house for weeks."

"Why, Mr. Murthwaite? That is precisely what puzzles me!"

"Because no Indian ever risks unnecessarily," he explained. "The clause in Colonel Herncastle's will informed them that the Moonstone would become Miss Verinder's absolute property on her birthday. Which course was safer: attacking the diamond while under the control of Mr. Franklin Blake, who had proven capable of detecting and outwitting them? Or waiting until it fell into the hands of a young girl, who would innocently display the magnificent jewel at every opportunity? Consider the Indians' own conduct as proof. After weeks of patience, they appeared on Miss Verinder's birthday and witnessed the Moonstone nestled in her bodice! Hearing the story of the Colonel and the diamond later that evening, I recognized the grave risk Mr. Franklin Blake had incurred—they surely would have attacked him had he not ridden back with others. Convinced of the even greater danger awaiting Miss Verinder, I advised adopting the Colonel's plan: destroy the diamond's identity by cutting it into smaller stones. Had the extraordinary disappearance that night not thwarted my counsel and paralyzed the Hindoo plot, subsequent action would have been impossible, especially after their imprisonment as rogues and vagabonds. The first act of the conspiracy ends there."

He paused, ensuring his explanation was satisfactory. "The first chance to seize the diamond was lost on the day they were imprisoned at Frizinghall. When did the second chance arise?"

"While they were still confined," he answered, producing a pocket-book. "A day or two before their release, the prison governor showed me a letter left for the Indians by Mrs. Macann, where they lodged. Delivered via regular post, the authorities noted the 'Lambeth' postmark and an address format unusual for English correspondence. Inside, written in a foreign tongue correctly identified as Hindustani, was a message requesting translation."
