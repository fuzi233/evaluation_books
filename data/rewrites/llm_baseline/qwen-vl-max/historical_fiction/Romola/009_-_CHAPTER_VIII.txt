To northern folk, the dawn of Midsummer brings no special meaning—only the soft light on dew-kissed grass, the fresh green shoots among ancient oaks, and the quiet meadow beyond the gate. But for the Florentine, especially one born into the turbulent soul of the fifteenth century, this morning was different. The sun rising over the Arno carried more than warmth—it bore the weight of tradition. The bells rang not merely with sound, but with purpose: they announced the great festival of San Giovanni, patron saint of Florence since long before memory, a day when the city’s pride and piety intertwined like threads in a rich tapestry.

San Giovanni had watched over Florence for centuries—since Queen Theodolinda of Lombardy first decreed his honor, and perhaps even earlier, as chronicler Villani claimed, back to the days of Constantine and Pope Sylvester. Then, the Florentines had cast aside their old god Mars, not without care. Though they dedicated their noble temple to God and the “Beato Messere Santo Giovanni,” they placed Mars respectfully atop a tower near the river, honoring him as a guardian whose power could not be lightly broken—lest the city suffer ruin. But by the 1400s, that reverence had faded. Mars had vanished from the banks of the Arno; the Republic now trusted not in spear and shield, but in wit, wealth, and coinage. And upon every gold florin, gleaming under the Tuscan sun, stood the image of San Giovanni.

The city had prospered under his protection. Yes, there had been bloodshed—the Guelfs and Ghibellines, the Blacks and Whites, the faithful and the heretical Paterini—but also triumphs. Florence had conquered cities once stronger than itself, most notably Pisa, whose marble towers were too proud, whose ships too honored along Mediterranean coasts. Now, Florence’s name echoed across Europe, even into Africa, celebrated for its pure gold, its dyes, its learning, its poets, and its bankers. A pope once quipped that Florentines were “the fifth element”—so essential they seemed to transcend nature. And though fortune favored stars and saints alike, much of Florence’s greatness was owed to San Giovanni, whose likeness graced the very currency of the land.

So it was fitting that his feast day should be marked with grandeur. The night before, the city had kept vigil in true Florentine fashion—dancing in the streets, jesters mocking, windows shattered, stones thrown—but above all, with marvels only Florence could devise. Thanks to Cecca, the brilliant engineer and architect, the heavens themselves seemed to descend. Saints floated on clouds, surrounded by cherubs, their halos glowing as in Perugino’s paintings. Giants, taller than any man, moved slowly through the narrow lanes—men balanced on stilts, masked and padded like Greek actors, carrying divine images with solemn grace. Clouds were woven cloth; saints, mere mortals supported by iron bars. Yet few thought of such mechanics. To the Florentine mind, these visions carried a sacred echo—was not holiness reflected in the act of creation?

And then came the demons—black, clawed, armed with thongs and whips—performing farces of mock punishment, tearing clothes, dancing wildly. It was their way of keeping vigil too, their own kind of devotion. The Tuscan spirit slipped easily from reverence to satire, as water flows around a stone. The saints had passed, paid homage at the gates of San Giovanni, and now the city waited for the next day’s glory.

On the morning of June 24, 1492, the sky was clear and bright, the air crisp with anticipation. In the Piazza della Signoria, the heart of Florence’s civic life, the procession began to gather. The Palazzo Vecchio loomed beside the Loggia di Orcagna, both silent witnesses to history. Above, the blue sky stretched like a canopy, pierced by the clangor of bells so fierce that even spirits would flee. Terraces and windows overflowed with faces; draperies hung from balconies, turning stone houses into festive theaters. The towers of the Bargello and the Badia stood watch, while below, banners, horses, and towering wax tapers—*ceri*—awaited their moment.

These *ceri*, some as tall as buildings, were hollow structures of wood and pasteboard, gilded and painted with scenes of warriors, maidens, beasts, and fruits. Inside, men spun them endlessly, creating a dizzying spectacle of motion and color. They were not just candles—they were symbols of faith, art, and civic pride, descended from the torches used in early Christian catacombs. And now, beneath a sky of blue, the people gathered in the Piazza del Duomo, where a vast canopy draped in blue cloth adorned with golden lilies and coats of arms formed a second heaven. Banners drooped like rainbows beneath it, offering shelter and splendor to those who leaned from windows or lined the pavement.

Among them sat Tito Melema, radiant in the morning light. He rested his elbow on the red-draped sill of the window above Nello’s shop, his head tilted back, curls brushed against his ear. His face held that calm charm—neither excitable nor dull, neither bitter nor boastful—a man beloved by many, yet never intrusive. Since his arrival two months prior, he had grown brighter, as if sunlight had finally found its way into his soul.

Behind him stood Nello, dressed in holiday finery, and beside him, Pietro Cennini—the scholar, not the practical Domenico. Around them, others chattered: Piero di Cosimo, the painter, grimacing at the bells’ relentless toll; Francesco Cei, the poet, making jests; and Cronaca, the architect, offering dry wisdom. Tito glanced down at the crowd, then up at the faces near him, when suddenly—just at the edge of the marble steps before the Duomo—he saw a face turned toward him.

It was a friar—tonsured, robed in black and white, common enough in Florence. But the look in his eyes… it held something deeper. Not anger, not curiosity, but recognition—fleeting, yes, but undeniable. Like a spark in the dark.

“Nello!” Tito called, quickly. “Look—there! That tall friar going up the steps. Do you know him?”

“One of the Frati Predicatori,” Nello replied, half-joking. “Why should I know the private lives of crows?”

“I’ve seen that face before,” Tito said, frowning slightly. “It’s unusual.”

“Fra Girolamo?” asked Pietro. “Too tall. And he doesn’t appear in public like this.”

“Besides,” added Francesco Cei, “that fire-breathing ‘hound of the Lord’ is not in Florence now. He’s taken Piero de’ Medici’s advice and gone on pilgrimage with his prophecies.”

“The Frate doesn’t rail at men,” said Cronaca. “Only at vice. If you think that attacks your poetry, Francesco, it’s not his fault.”

“He’s gone into the Duomo,” Tito murmured, watching the figure vanish. “No, not Fra Girolamo. He has a high nose, thick lip. This man… he’s different.”

“Enough with your descriptions!” Cennini interrupted. “Look—here come the horsemen!”

And indeed, the procession advanced. First, the banner of the Guelf party—a red eagle clutching a green dragon, crowned with a red lily—held aloft by a rider in white trappings. Around it rode the chief officers of the Guelf faction. “Our proudest banner,” Cennini declared. “It means Florentine will, the triumph of the popolani.”

“And which means,” Cronaca retorted, “the fat popolani triumphing over the lean—and the fattest over all.”

“Sententiousness,” Cennini warned. “Fra Girolamo’s sermons are spoiling you. Soon you’ll carve morality into your cornices.”

Tito smiled. “They’re fine riders,” he said. “But aren’t there foreigners among them? I see strange costumes.”

“Certainly,” Cennini replied. “There are Orators from France, Milan, Venice—behind them, English and German nobles. It’s custom for distinguished visitors to pay tribute to San Giovanni in the train of the gonfalon.”

“Florentine cavaliers ride well,” said Cennini proudly. “Better than those northern cut-and-thrust types whose minds are in their heels. And that Venetian—wouldn’t he be happier riding a dolphin?”

Piero di Cosimo, captivated by color, leaned forward. “The banners—ah! Velvet, satin, brocade—light dancing on fabric. Human beings are dull compared to form and hue. _Va_! Your talk is tame. Only passion lives in color.”

“Ay,” Nello laughed, “if Satanasso could paint, you’d sell your soul to learn his secrets.”

“But angels are poor at chiaroscuro,” Piero added. “See the Madonna Nunziata—how flat she looks!”

Now came the banners of Pisa and Arezzo. “Messire Pisano,” Cennini said, “don’t sulk. Carry your banner with grace. ‘Pisans false, Florentines blind’—but the second part no longer holds.”

Then followed the standards of Florence’s subject towns—Arezzo, Prato, Pistoia—each to be hung in the Baptistery until next year’s festival.

“They’re beautiful,” Tito said. “San Giovanni must be pleased. As Minerva was with her peplos. Though he wears little himself.”

“But those spinning towers!” he added, squinting. “I’d fall out the window from vertigo.”

The *ceri*—those colossal tapers—were indeed a marvel. Hollow, painted, carved, they rotated with inner figures, creating a hypnotic swirl. Some were so large they rolled on wheels. They were the people’s favorite, though critics mocked them as “tower-puppets” when poorly made.

“_Pestilenza_!” Piero muttered, pulling away. “Those circles drive me mad. Warn me when the last one passes.”

“You’ll want to see the contadini,” Nello said. “The peasants of Mugello and Casentino—Leonardo would sketch them a hundred times.”

“No,” Piero insisted. “I’ll wait for the car of the Zecca. I’ve seen enough clowns with tapers.”

Then it came—the car of the Zecca, the Mint. Once a wooden *cero*, it had evolved into a pyramidal triumphal car, drawn by two oxen in rich harness. Atop, thirty feet high, stood a living Saint John the Baptist—bare limbs, tiger-skin robe, golden halo, iron cross fixed in hand. He was not the gentle boy of later art, but the austere prophet of cloister walls.

This role was not prestigious. The actor received ten shillings, a fourteen-pound cake, two bottles of wine, and sweets—paid by the Zecca. From a basket lowered from a nearby window, he ate his share, tossed the rest to the crowd, and clutched the giant cake like a shield as the car lurched forward.

“There go the Masters of the Zecca!” Cennini shouted, pride swelling. “And there’s my brother—you see him, Melema?”

Behind came the Calimara—foreign cloth dealers, dignified elders. Then the Art of Money-changers, the backbone of Florence’s commerce.

And still, Tito’s gaze lingered on the space where the friar had stood. The moment had passed, but the glance remained—like a shadow in the sun.
