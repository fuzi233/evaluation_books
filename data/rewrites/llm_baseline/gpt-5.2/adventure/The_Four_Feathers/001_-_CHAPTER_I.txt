Lieutenant Sutch was the first of General Feversham’s guests to arrive at Broad Place. He came up from the station road a little after five, while June sunlight lay warm on the Surrey slope, and the old red-brick house glowed as if it had stored the day’s heat in its walls the way a baker stores heat in a brick oven—slow, steady, inexorable.

He crossed the hall with his familiar limp. The portraits of the Fevershams—row upon row of faces hard as old iron—rose to the ceiling, their varnish catching the light in cold glints. Even with the windows open the hall felt close, smelling faintly of wax polish and stale coal-dust, and under it all the dry, papery odour of ancient canvas. Sutch went out to the stone-flagged terrace at the back.

General Feversham sat there upright, boy-straight, looking south towards the Sussex Downs. He rose briskly when he heard the uneven step.

“How’s the leg?” he asked.

He was a small wiry man, white-haired, alert in body; but his face—bony, high-foreheaded—was as barren as a scraped plate. His steel-blue eyes looked at you without taking you in.

“It gave me trouble during the winter,” Sutch answered. “But that was to be expected.”

Feversham nodded, and for a space they let the silence stand. The ground fell away from the terrace into a wide plain—brown earth, emerald fields, dark clumps of trees. Voices rose thin and perfectly clear through the sunshine: a child’s shout, the metallic tap of a gate, a distant dog’s bark—those clean country sounds that settle the mind because they belong there. Far off towards Horsham, a train’s coil of white smoke unrolled and vanished among the trees; even from here you could imagine the faint rhythm of its wheels, a measured industrial pulse intruding and then passing on. On the horizon the Downs lifted, patched with chalk, like flour dusted over a board.

“I thought I should find you here,” Sutch said.

“It was my wife’s favourite corner,” Feversham replied, in the same even tone he might have used for weather. “She would sit here by the hour. She had a queer liking for wide and empty spaces.”

“Yes,” said Sutch. “She had imagination. Her thoughts could people them.”

Feversham glanced at him as though he had spoken in a dialect he didn’t care to learn. What he did not understand he habitually let slip, as if it had no claim to existence. He changed the subject at once.

“There will be a leaf out of our table to-night.”

“Yes. Collins, Barberton, and Vaughan went this winter. Well, we are all permanently shelved upon the world’s half-pay list as it is. The obituary column is just the last formality which gazettes us out of the service altogether.” Sutch eased his crippled leg, remembering—without any wish to—how fourteen years ago that day it had been crushed and twisted by the fall of a scaling-ladder.

“I am glad you came before the others,” Feversham continued. “I want your opinion. This day is more to me than the anniversary of our attack upon the Redan. At the very moment when we were standing under arms in the dark—”

“To the west of the quarries; I remember,” Sutch broke in, drawing a breath he had not known he held. “How should one forget?”

“At that very moment Harry was born in this house.” Feversham said it as he might have said a dispatch had arrived. “I thought, therefore, that if you did not object, he might join us to-night. He happens to be at home. He will, of course, enter the service, and he might learn something, perhaps, which afterward will be of use—one never knows.”

“By all means,” Sutch said, with an eagerness that surprised himself.

For these anniversary dinners were the only times he saw General Feversham, and he had never met Harry. For years Sutch had turned over, like a puzzling recipe whose ingredients refused to marry, the question of what in Feversham had drawn Muriel Graham—so quick of mind, so finely made, so unlike this man in every discernible way. He had never found an explanation. He had had to content himself with the fact that she had, for some reason beyond him, married this older soldier with his narrow certainties. Personal courage and indomitable self-confidence—those, indeed, seemed the only virtues in Feversham that ever rose to the surface.

Sutch, sitting with the June air on his face, went back in thought over twenty years to a London season when he had come home fresh from the China station, before the Crimea, before the Redan. He told himself—because he valued his own impartiality—that his curiosity about Harry was no more than the natural curiosity of a man disabled young who had made a hobby of observing people. He wanted to see whether the boy took after his mother or his father. That was all.

That night Harry Feversham came down to dinner.

The table shone under lamps and candles; silver threw back points of fire; glass held wine dark as blood. The air thickened steadily with tobacco smoke, which blued the candlelight and gave it an underwater blur. Yet under those heavy indoor smells—hot wax, cigars, warmed cloth—Sutch fancied he still caught the faint breath of the house itself: brick that had baked all day, and the ghost of pine from the dark woods outside.

Harry took his place among them and listened while older men talked.

The stories were all of that dark winter in the Crimea, and a fresh story began before its predecessor ended, like voices overlapping in a trench at night. There were deaths spoken of in clipped phrases; hazardous exploits reduced to a shrug; famine and snow recalled as a man recalls bad quarters. The telling was matter-of-fact, as though those experiences were packed away and labelled in some store-room of the mind, brought out only on anniversaries. The strongest comment was “That’s curious”; the most significant exclamation a laugh.

But the boy listened as though those incidents were happening now, inside the walls of the room. His eyes—his mother’s eyes, dark and too awake—moved from speaker to speaker and waited, wide open, until the last word was dropped. The changes of expression shot and quivered across his face so vividly that Sutch could have sworn the boy heard what was not being said: the drone of bullets, the sudden hammer of a charge, the scream of horses, the metallic rip of shell. When a major of artillery spoke of the long suspense between parading the troops and the first command to advance, Harry’s shoulders worked as though minute by minute he bore that delay upon his own back.

And then he did more than work his shoulders. He threw a single furtive, wavering glance backwards.

Sutch was startled; more than startled—he was pained. For this was Muriel Graham’s son. The look was too familiar to him. He had seen it on recruits in their first experience under fire too often to mistake it: the backward search for refuge, for permission, for someone else to decide. One picture sprang up with cruel distinctness: an advancing square at Inkermann; a tall big soldier rushing forward in eagerness, then stopping as if he understood he was alone and must meet alone the charge of a mounted Cossack. Sutch remembered the fatal wavering glance thrown back towards comrades, and the queer sickly smile with it. He remembered the consequence too: the man, though armed, receiving without effort a lance-thrust in the throat.

Sutch looked hurriedly about the table, afraid that General Feversham or any of the guests had noticed the same look on Harry’s face. But no one had eyes for the boy; each man waited too greedily for his own turn to talk. Sutch drew a breath of relief and turned back. Harry sat with elbows on the cloth, head propped between his hands, as if the bright room and its glitter had ceased to exist. He was building, from the clipped anecdotes, a whole world of cries and wounds, riderless chargers, men writhing in a fog of cannon-smoke. The least graphic phrase about the biting days and nights in the trenches made him shiver; even his face grew pinched, as though the iron frost were actually eating into his bones.

Sutch touched him lightly on the elbow.

“You renew those days for me,” he said. “Though the heat is dripping down the windows, I feel the chill of the Crimea.”

Harry roused as if waking.

“The stories renew them,” he said.

“No,” Sutch answered. “It is you listening to the stories.”

Before the boy could speak again, General Feversham’s voice cut sharply from the head of the table.

“Harry, look at the clock!”

All eyes turned at once upon him. The hands made the acutest of angles. It was close upon midnight. From eight o’clock—without a question, without a murmur—he had sat listening.

He rose, reluctant.

“Must I go, father?” he asked.

The guests intervened at once, a chorus—half indulgent, half approving. The talk, they said, was good for him: a first taste of powder which might stand him in good stead. Besides, it was his birthday. A youngster of fourteen didn’t sit those hours without a kick against the table-leg unless he was entertained. Let him stay.

For once General Feversham relaxed.

“Very well,” he said. “Harry shall have an hour’s furlough from his bed. A single hour won’t make much difference.”

Harry’s eyes went to his father’s face and stayed there with a curious steady gaze. To Sutch it seemed like a question spoken without words—an old, private dialect between mother and son that the father could not hear: Are you blind?

But General Feversham had already turned back to his neighbours, and Harry sat down quietly. Again he propped his chin upon his hands and listened with all his soul. Yet it was not entertainment that held him; it was compulsion, as though a spell had been laid upon him. His face grew unnaturally white, his eyes unnaturally large, while the candles burned lower and redder through the haze of smoke and the wine in the decanters sank.

Half the hour went so. Then General Feversham, jogged by the unlucky mention of a name, blurted out in his jerky fashion:

“Lord Wilmington. One of the best names in England, if you please. Did you ever see his house in Warwickshire? Every inch of ground you’d think would have a voice to bid him play the man, if only in remembrance of his fathers. It seemed incredible—mere camp rumour—but the rumour grew. If it was whispered at the Alma, it was spoken aloud at Inkermann, it was shouted at Balaclava. Before Sebastopol the hideous thing was proved.

“Wilmington was acting as galloper to his general. I believe upon my soul the general chose him for the duty so the fellow might set himself right. There were three hundred yards of bullet-swept flat ground and a message to be carried across. Had Wilmington toppled off his horse, why, there were the whispers silenced for ever. Had he ridden through alive, he earned distinction besides.

“But he didn’t dare. He refused! Imagine it. He sat shaking on his horse and declined. You should have seen the general. His face turned the colour of that Burgundy.” Feversham nodded at the bottle as though it were a proper measure of shame. “‘No doubt you have a previous engagement,’ he said—in the politest voice you ever heard. Just that, not a word of abuse. A previous engagement on the battlefield! For the life of me I could hardly help laughing.

“But it was a tragic business for Wilmington. He was broken, of course, and slunk back to London. Every house closed to him; he dropped out of his circle like a lead bullet you let slip into the sea. The very women in Piccadilly spat if he spoke to them; and he blew his brains out in a back bedroom off the Haymarket. Curious that, eh? He hadn’t the pluck to face bullets when his name was at stake, yet he could blow his own brains out afterward.”

Sutch glanced at the clock as the story ended. It was a quarter to one. Harry still had a quarter of an hour’s furlough, and that time was taken by a retired surgeon-general with a great wagging beard who sat nearly opposite the boy.

“I can tell you an incident still more curious,” the surgeon-general said. “The man in this case had never been under fire before, but he was of my own profession. Life and death were part of his business. Nor was he in any particular danger. It happened during a hill campaign in India. We were encamped in a valley, and a few Pathans used to lie out on the hillside at night and take long shots into the camp. A bullet ripped through the canvas of the hospital tent—that was all. The surgeon crept out to his own quarters, and his orderly discovered him half an hour afterward lying in his blood stone-dead.”

“Hit?” the major exclaimed.

“Not a bit of it,” said the surgeon-general. “He had quietly opened his instrument-case in the dark, taken out a lancet, and severed his femoral artery. Sheer panic, do you see, at the whistle of a bullet.”

Even on these men, hardened to horrors, the bald simplicity struck. There were half-uttered exclamations of disbelief. Men moved restlessly, as if their chairs had turned rough beneath them. Someone gulped his wine as though to wash the knowledge away. Another shook his shoulders, like a dog shaking water.

Only Harry sat perfectly still.

His hands were clenched on his knees; he leaned forward a little across the table towards the surgeon-general. His cheeks were white as paper. His eyes burned—burned with ferocity. He looked, suddenly, like an animal trapped, muscles gathered, body taut, as if he might spring.

Sutch felt a fear so sharp it was almost physical: that the boy meant to leap across the table and strike, not from anger alone but from some savage despair at what he had heard. He had already half lifted a restraining hand when General Feversham’s voice intervened, flat as ever, and Harry’s posture slackened as if a string had been cut.

“Queer incomprehensible things happen,” Feversham said. “Here are two of them. You can only say they are the truth and pray God you may forget ’em. But you can’t explain, for you can’t understand.”

Sutch, moved, laid his hand upon Harry’s shoulder.

“Can you?” he asked—then regretted it almost as soon as the words escaped. Yet they were spoken.

Harry’s eyes turned swiftly to Sutch and rested on his face—not with guilt, not with a boy’s flurry, but quietly, inscrutably. He did not answer. The question was answered, instead, by General Feversham’s scorn.

“Harry understand!” the general exclaimed, with a snort. “How should he? He’s a Feversham.”

That mutely asked question—Are you blind?—seemed to flare again between father and son, only now Sutch felt it himself. Never had he heard an untruth so demonstrably untrue. One look at the two proved it. Harry wore his father’s name, but he had his mother’s eyes, his mother’s broad forehead, his mother’s delicacy of profile, his mother’s imagination—that burdensome gift which makes sounds sharper, shadows deeper, and stories into living presences.

Perhaps it needed a stranger to see. Feversham had looked on his son’s face so long that it had ceased to signify.

“Look at the clock, Harry.”

The hour’s furlough had run out. Harry rose and drew a breath.

“Good night, sir,” he said, and walked to the door.

The servants had long since gone to bed. As Harry opened the door, the hall beyond gaped black, like the mouth of night. For a second he hesitated on the threshold, and almost shrank back into the lighted room as though peril waited in that void. And peril did—the peril of his own thoughts.

He stepped out and closed the door behind him.

Inside, the decanter went round again. Soda-water bottles popped. The talk returned to its accustomed groove. Harry was forgotten at once by all but Sutch.

Sutch, despite his professed detachment, was the kindliest of men. He had more kindliness than observation by a great deal. And there were special reasons—reaching back to Muriel Graham—that made the boy’s isolation tug at him like a hand on a sleeve. He sat for a while, profoundly disturbed, then rose on impulse, went to the door, opened it noiselessly, slipped out, and closed it without a click.

This is what he saw.

Harry Feversham stood in the centre of the hall holding a lighted candle high above his head. He was looking up towards the portraits of the Fevershams as they climbed the walls and disappeared into roof-darkness. The dining-room voices came muffled through the panels, a blurred chorus; but the hall itself was silent—so silent that the tiny sounds seemed magnified: the faint hiss of the candlewick, the soft tick of cooling timber, the minute creak of the boy’s sleeve as his arm strained upward. The flame flickered, and with it the light wandered across painted faces—glowing on a red coat, glinting on steel, brightening a button, then sliding away.

There was not one portrait that did not glisten with uniform colours, and there were many. Father and son, the Fevershams had been soldiers from the family’s beginning. Father and son, in lace collars and bucket boots, in Ramillies wigs and breastplates, in velvet coats with powder in their hair, in shakos and swallow-tails, in high stocks and frogged coats—they looked down upon the last of the line, summoning him to the same service.

They were men stamped from one mould. No change of fashion could disguise their kinship: lean faces, thin lips, firm chins, straight level mouths, narrow foreheads, steel-blue inexpressive eyes. Men of courage and resolution, no doubt, but without nerves, without subtleties, without imagination. Sturdy men—let it be said frankly—rather stupid. First-class fighting men, but not one of them a first-class soldier.

Harry saw none of that. To him they were terrible and portentous. He stood before them like a criminal before judges, reading condemnation in those cold eyes.

Sutch understood then why the candle flame flickered. There was no draught in the hall. The boy’s hand shook.

And finally, as if he heard a verdict in the silence and accepted it, Harry bowed to the portraits.

As he raised his head he saw Lieutenant Sutch in the doorway embrasure. He did not start. He uttered no word. He let his eyes rest on Sutch and waited.

It was Sutch, the grown man, who found himself embarrassed.

“Harry,” he said, and though awkward he had the tact to use the tone and language one would use not to a child but to a comrade, “we meet for the first time to-night. But I knew your mother a long time ago. I like to think I have the right to call her by that much misused word—‘friend.’ Have you anything to tell me?”

“Nothing,” said Harry.

“The mere telling sometimes lightens a trouble.”

“It is kind of you. There is nothing.”

Sutch was at a loss. The boy’s loneliness stood in the hall like a second darkness. He tried again, more practically, as a man offers what he has.

He took his card-case from his pocket. “You will find my address upon this card. Perhaps some day you will give me a few days of your company. I can offer you on my side a day or two’s hunting.”

A spasm of pain crossed Harry’s face—fugitive but unmistakable, like the flinch at an unexpected sharp noise. It passed as swiftly as it came.

“Thank you, sir,” he said monotonously. “You are very kind.”

“And if ever you want to talk over a difficult question with an older man, I am at your service.” Sutch made his voice formally steady, lest the boy’s sensitiveness should imagine laughter where there was none.

Harry took the card and repeated his thanks.

Then he went upstairs to bed.

Sutch waited uncomfortably in the hall until the candlelight diminished and disappeared along the stair, leaving only the portraits’ dim sheen and the muffled rise and fall of talk through the door. Something was amiss; of that he was very sure. There were words he should have spoken, but he had not found them—had not found the right tongue for what he meant, as if the thing itself lived in a dialect he did not possess.

He went back into the dining-room. With a feeling—foolish and yet urgent—that he might repair his omission, he filled his glass, called for silence, and stood.

“Gentlemen,” he said, “this is June 15th,” and there was applause and rapping on the table. “It is the anniversary of our attack upon the Redan. It is also Harry Feversham’s birthday. For us, our work is done. I ask you to drink the health of one of the youngsters who are ousting us. His work lies before him. The traditions of the Feversham family are very well known to us. May Harry Feversham carry them on! May he add distinction to a distinguished name!”

At once the company was on its feet.

“Harry Feversham!”

The name was shouted with such hearty goodwill that the glasses rang on the table. “Harry Feversham, Harry Feversham,” they cried again and again, while old General Feversham sat aflush with pride, his chest lifted as if he wore his own decorations anew.

And a boy, a minute afterward, in a room high up in the house, heard the muffled words of their chorus—heard the rhythmic thump of voices as one might hear a distant song carried through floorboards—and believed they were drinking his father’s health.

He turned over in bed and lay shivering.

In his mind he saw a broken officer slinking at night in the shadows of London streets. He pushed back the flap of a tent and stooped over a man lying stone-dead in his blood, an open lancet clenched in his right hand. And he saw that the face of the broken officer and the face of the dead surgeon were one face—one unescapable face.

His own.
