It was the penultimate day of Flatland’s 1999th year, the very first eve of our Long Vacation. I’d stayed up far too late chasing my favourite pastime, untangling a stubborn, unproven geometric theorem, and dragged myself to bed with the unsolved problem still whirring behind my eyelids like a half-spun compass. In that liminal space between wakefulness and rest, a dream unfolded.

I floated above a vast throng of thin, straight little lines I first mistook for crowds of Flatland women, dotted here and there with even smaller, glimmering luminous points, all moving back and forth along a single unbroken straight line, every last one of them shifting at exactly the same steady speed. At intervals, as they moved, a tangled, high-pitched chirping and twittering rose up from the whole crowd, but the second they all went still, absolute silence settled over the strange space.

I drifted over to the biggest of the lines I’d assumed was a woman, and called out to her. No answer. I called twice more, still nothing. Fed up with what felt like unforgivable rudeness, I moved my mouth to line up perfectly directly in front of where I guessed hers would be, blocking her path of motion, and shouted loud enough to make the air hum: “Madam, what is this gathering? What is this confused chirping, this endless, monotonous back-and-forth motion all along this one single straight line?”

“I am no woman,” the thin line snapped back. “I am the rightful Monarch of this entire world. And you—what business have you intruding into my realm of Lineland?”

I hurried to apologize for startling or offending his Royal Highness, introduced myself as a travelling stranger from a land beyond his ken, and begged him to walk me through the facts of his dominion. It was far harder than I expected to get the information I craved: the King kept defaulting to the assumption that everything obvious to him must be common knowledge to every other living soul, and that I was only pretending to be ignorant to tease him. But I kept at it with steady questions, and at last pried loose every fact of his strange world:

This poor, self-described “ignorant” Monarch was utterly convinced that the single straight line he called his Kingdom, the only place he’d ever existed, was the entire universe, all of Space itself. He could not move, could not see, outside that one narrow line, so he had no frame of reference for anything that lay beyond its edges. He’d heard my voice when I first called to him from above, but the sound had arrived in a way so completely outside his experience he’d refused to answer at first. He told me he “saw no one, yet heard a voice that seemed to come from somewhere inside my own guts”. Not until I pressed my mouth right up against the surface of his line-world had he even registered my presence properly, and all the sounds he’d heard before that had just felt like muffled thuds beating against what he called his inside, his stomach. He had no concept at all of the place I’d come from. Outside his line, there was not even empty blank space—blank implies space to be empty, after all. It was pure, unthinkable non-existence.

His subjects were all trapped on that same narrow line: the slightly longer lines were men, the tiny points were women. Every single one of them could only move back and forth along that one axis, and their field of vision was so limited that all they could ever see, no matter what they looked at, was a single point. A man, a woman, a child, a stray object—all registered as nothing but a dot to a Linelander’s eye. The only way they could tell sex or age apart was by the tone of a voice. Worse, every person occupied the full width of the tiny path that made up their universe, so no one could step to the right or left to let someone pass. You could never overtake anyone else in Lineland. Once you were neighbours, you were neighbours forever. Neighbourhood bonds there were as permanent as marriage is in Flatland—you stayed side by side until the day you died.

A life where all sight was limited to a single point, and all movement was trapped along one thin line, felt unimaginably dreary to me, but I was shocked to see how bright, cheerful, and lively the King was. I hesitated for a long time to ask the delicate question of how anyone in these impossible domestic circumstances could enjoy conjugal happiness, but at last I worked up the nerve and asked after the health of his family.

“My wives and children are well and happy,” he replied easily.

I was staggered. Earlier, when I’d looked down on Lineland from above, I’d counted no less than six full grown men standing directly between the King and the spots where his wives would have to be, no gaps to move around anyone. I said, “Forgive me, Your Majesty, but I cannot fathom how you can ever see or even reach your wives, when there are half a dozen people between you that you can neither see through nor walk past. Is proximity not a requirement for marriage, for the making of children, here in Lineland?”

“How could you ask such a ridiculous question?” the King sputtered. “If things worked the way you say, the whole universe would be empty in a generation. No no no! You don’t need to be physically close for two hearts to join. Bringing new children into the world is far too important a matter to leave it to the random accident of who happens to be standing next to you. You must know this already. But since you insist on playing the fool, I will explain it to you like you are the silliest, most newborn babe in all of Lineland. Listen closely: all marriages are sealed using the power of sound, the sense of hearing.

“You of course already know that every Man has two mouths, two voices, just as he has two eyes: a deep bass at one end of his line body, and a clear tenor at the other. I wouldn’t even mention this, but I’ve noticed I haven’t heard a second, higher tenor note out of you once this whole conversation.”

I told him I only had one single voice, and I’d never known any man to have two.
“That only confirms what I already suspected,” the King huffed. “You are no man. You are some sort of feminine monstrosity with an absurdly low bass voice, and ears that haven’t been educated at all. But let me go on.

“Nature itself has decreed that every man must marry two wives—”
“Wait, why two?” I cut in.
“You take this fake simplicity much too far,” he roared. “How could there ever be a perfectly harmonious union without the Four-in-One combination: the man’s bass, his tenor, and the two wives’ soprano and contralto voices?”
“What if a man wanted only one wife?” I asked. “Or three?”
“Impossible,” he said. “That’s as unthinkable as two plus one making five, or a human eye being able to see a full straight line all at once.”

He didn’t let me interrupt, and kept going. “Once every week, right in the middle, a Law of Nature forces every single one of us to move back and forth in a rhythmic, violent sway that lasts as long as it would take you to count to one hundred and one. Halfway through that hundred-and-one count, at the fifty-first pulse, every single person in the whole universe stops moving mid-sway, and pours out their richest, fullest, sweetest possible note. That is the exact moment all our marriages are made. The voices fit together so perfectly, bass matching contralto, tenor matching soprano, that two people can be twenty thousand leagues apart along the line and recognize their destined partner’s note instantly. No trivial little obstacle of distance can stop love from binding the three of them together. The marriage is sealed right there in that instant, and soon a set of three children—one boy, two girls, perfect balance—are born to join the population of Lineland.”
“Wait, always three?” I said. “So each pair of wives always has twins plus one?”
“Bass-voiced freak, yes!” the King shouted, furious. “How else could we keep the number of men and women perfectly balanced, if every boy wasn’t born with two girls alongside him? Are you trying to say you’ve never even memorized the alphabet of how nature works?” He stopped, seething too angry to speak, and it was a long time before I could coax him to keep talking.

“Of course, not every bachelor finds his perfect harmonic trio in the very first weekly marriage chorus. Most of us have to try many times. Very few lucky hearts hit perfect harmony the very first time they sing together. For most of us, courtship is long. The suitor’s bass might align perfectly with one wife’s soprano, but not the second’s contralto. Or none of the three voices line up at first. Or the two wives’ voices don’t quite blend right. But Nature made a way for that: every week’s collective chorus nudges the three would-be lovers closer and closer to perfect harmony. Every time they sing, every tiny little note of discord they notice makes the less-perfect singer adjust their voice, almost without noticing, to match the pitch of the others. After dozens of tries, after hundreds of tiny adjustments, one week, when the universal marriage chorus rings out across Lineland, the three far-apart lovers suddenly find themselves singing in perfect, seamless harmony. Before they even realize what’s happening, the wedded trio is wrapped in that perfect vocal embrace, and Nature celebrates one new marriage, and three new lives, born into the long thin line of our world.”
