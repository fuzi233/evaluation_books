Catherine, who had shed so many tears for poor old Martin, could not endure this latest blow with the stoicism required for the passing of an elder. Her attempts to soothe Margaret with trite consolations—that it was a blessed release, that the tree was fully ripened—fell flat. Even her pragmatic argument, urging that there were now but two mouths to feed and thus one burden less, struck a dissonant chord.

"Such cares are all the joys I possess," Margaret whispered, her voice trembling. "They fill this desolate heart, leaving it void and barren. Look at that empty chair! My bosom aches to see it. I loved him as if he were my own child, though he was my father. He, once so wise and learned, has become helpless as my infant self. Oh, empty chair! Oh, empty heart! Well-a-day!"

Her pious grief refused to be stifled. Catherine, humbled, fell silent, lowering her gaze. Days later, she finally confessed, speaking from her mind rather than her soul, "Do not listen to me, for I know not what I say. Yet, were I in thy place, I would mourn the old man exactly as thou dost."

Moved by this rare, genuine sympathy, Margaret clutched Catherine's arm, finding some comfort therein, whereas the hollow platitudes had offered none. Catherine's heart swelled with compassion. "My poor girl," she murmured, "you were not born to live alone. Consider me thy mother indeed. Do not squander thy youth on my son Gerard; either he lies dead or acts as a traitor. It breaks my heart to utter such words, yet what else can I see? Thy father is gone, and I cannot stand beside thee forever. Here stands an honest lad who has loved thee faithfully for many days. I would take both him and Comfort. Heaven sends us creatures to torment and comfort alike; without them, we are naught."

Seeing Margaret's face cloud with perplexity, Catherine pressed on, "Pray, art thou so blind as not to perceive it?"

"What? Who?"

"Why, surely, but Luke Peterson."

"Oh, Mother, our Luke? The boy who carries my basket?"

"Nay, he is past nineteen, a sturdy, hale young man. I have made inquiries, and all attest to his capability as a worker and his temperance. That is rare in a Rotterdam lad, who are often but half-man, half-sponge."

Margaret's lips curved into a smile for the first time in weeks. "Luke loves dried puddings deeply," she admitted softly. "'Tis because of them that he comes to court me here." Suddenly, her cheeks flushed crimson. "Yet, if I believed he sought the hand of thy son's wife—or should be—the moment I knew, I would show him the door."

"Nay, nay! For Heaven's sake, let me not stir up mischief. Poor lad! Folly cannot be bridled, bless thee, I wormed it out of him nearly a year ago."

"Oh, Mother, and you allowed it?"

"Well, I thought of thee. I reasoned to myself, 'If he is foolish enough to be her slave for naught, all the better for her. A solitary woman is lost without a man to fetch and carry her small burdens.'" Catherine paused, her expression shifting. "But now my mind is altered. I believe the best use thou mayest put him to is to marry him."

"So," Margaret protested, "his own mother opposes him, wishing to wed me to the first comer? Ah, Gerard, thou hast but me. I will not believe thee dead until I behold thy tomb, nor false until I see thee with another lover in thy hand. Foolish boy, I shall never be civil to him again."

Burdened by Catherine's meddlesome protection, Luke Peterson encountered a chill reception in the household where he had previously found kindness. Soon, finding himself largely ignored and then sharply rebuked, the gentle giant began to whimper. He approached Margaret, asking bluntly if he had done something to offend her.

"None at all," Catherine replied. "It is my fault. I am cursed. If thou wilt heed my counsel, keep out of my sight for a while."

"It is all because of me, Mistress Catherine," Luke stammered. "What have I done to turn her against me?"

"Nay, I meant all for the best," Catherine explained. "I told her I saw thou wast gazing toward her through a wedding ring, but she would not hear of it."

"There was no need to whisper that, wife; she knows I have courted her for this whole twelve months."

"Not I," Margaret interjected, shaking her head. "Or I should never have opened the street door to thee."

"But why not?" Luke argued gently. "I come here every Saturday night. That is precisely how the lads in Rotterdam court. If we sup with a lass on a Saturday, that is the wooing."

"Oh, that is the custom of Rotterdam, is it?" Margaret said, her tone sharpening. "Then next time thou comest, let it be on a Thursday or Friday. As for me, I thought thou camest only for my puddings, boy."

"I like thy puddings well enough," Luke conceded tenderly. "Thou maketh them better than mother does. But I like thee still better than the puddings."

"And therefore, thou hast seen the last of them," Catherine declared, her voice firm despite its gentleness. "How darest thou speak so to another man's wife, especially when that husband lies far away?" She concluded, "Thou needest not trouble thyself to visit here again, Luke. I can carry my basket myself."

"Oh, very well," Luke muttered. After a silence heavy with stupidity, he rose and said sorrowfully to Catherine, "Dame, I suppose I have lost my employment;" before departing.

However, the following Saturday, Catherine found him seated on the doorstep, sobbing openly. He confessed that he had grown accustomed to coming there, and every other place seemed alien to him. Catherine entered and informed Margaret. Margaret sighed, remarking, "Poor Luke; he might have earned her hand if he could but know his place and treat her as a wife should."

When this advice was passed to Luke, he hesitated. "Pshaw!" Catherine scoffed. "Promises are merely pie-crusts. Promise her the whole world, sooner than sit there like a fool. If thou but humoured her in everything, and then, if poor Gerard did not return to claim her, thou wouldest surely have her—in time. A lonely woman is ever to be wearied out, thou foolish boy."
