The morning sun cast a deceptive, golden glow over the Downs, masking the chill that lingered in the air. As Elinor and Marianne walked together, the younger sister delivered a piece of news that struck Elinor with the force of a physical blow. Despite her prior knowledge of Marianne's impulsivity and lack of foresight, the extravagance of the revelation shocked her nonetheless. With a radiant expression, Marianne announced that Willoughby had gifted her a horse—a stallion he had personally bred on his Somersetshire estate, perfectly suited to carry a woman.

Marianne spoke of the acquisition with unbridled delight, failing to register the logistical catastrophe she had inadvertently signed away. She ignored the fact that her mother had no intention of keeping a horse, nor did she consider the necessity of purchasing a second mount for the servant, hiring a rider, and constructing a stable to house them. Instead, she had accepted the gift without hesitation, recounting the event to her sister in rapturous tones. "He intends to dispatch his groom to Somersetshire immediately," she added, her eyes shining with anticipation. "Upon its arrival, we shall ride daily. You must share its use with me. Imagine, my dear Elinor, the sheer ecstasy of a gallop across these downs."

Elinor found herself violently resisting the urge to snap out of this fever dream of felicity and confront the harsh realities underpinning the transaction. The financial burden was negligible; her mother would surely approve of the addition to the household. Any horse would suffice for *him*; he could procure one at the park whenever needed. As for a stable, a mere shed would serve adequately. Yet, Elinor dared to question the propriety of accepting such a substantial gift from a man she barely knew.

"You are mistaken, Elinor," Marianne retorted, her voice warming with defensive passion. "In supposing I know Willoughby little. While I have not known him long, my acquaintance with him surpasses that of any other living soul, save yourself and Mother. Intimacy is not measured by time or opportunity, but solely by disposition. Seven years may fail to acquaint two individuals, whereas seven days can forge a bond deeper than a lifetime. I would deem it far greater impropriety to accept a horse from my brother than from Willoughby. Of John, I know little, despite our years of cohabitation; yet my judgment regarding Willoughby has been formed long ago."

Recognizing the futility of opposition on such a tender subject, Elinor wisely chose to withdraw. She knew her sister's temperament; resistance would only bind Marianne tighter to her erroneous convictions. However, Elinor attempted to subdue Marianne by appealing to her maternal affection, outlining the inevitable inconveniences and burdens this expansion of the establishment would impose upon their indulgent mother. Slowly, Marianne yielded. She promised not to tempt her mother into imprudent generosity by mentioning the offer and vowed to inform Willoughby upon their next meeting that the gift must be declined.

True to her word, when Willoughby visited the cottage later that day, Elinor heard Marianne express her regret in a low, disappointed voice, lamenting her inability to accept his present. She relayed the reasons for her change of heart, rendering further persuasion on his part futile. Nevertheless, his distress was palpable. After voicing his concern earnestly, he added, also in a hushed tone, "Yet, Marianne, the horse remains yours, though you cannot utilize it at this juncture. I shall retain it only until you are able to claim it. When you depart Barton to establish your own permanent home, Queen Mab shall await your arrival."

Miss Dashwood, overhearing the entire exchange, noted the specific cadence of his speech and the intimate use of her sister's Christian name alone. Instantly, she perceived a level of intimacy so profound, a meaning so explicit, that it signaled a perfect accord between them. From that moment, Elinor harbored no doubt that they were engaged; the only surprise was that such frank natures would allow their union to remain undiscovered by accident.

Later, Margaret provided corroborating evidence that illuminated the situation further. Willoughby had spent the previous evening with the family, and Margaret, having been left alone in the parlor with only the two of them, had observed details she deemed crucial. With a grave demeanor, she confided in her eldest sister. "Oh, Elinor!" she exclaimed. "I possess a secret concerning Marianne. I am certain she will marry Mr. Willoughby very soon."

"You have asserted this," Elinor replied dryly, "almost daily since their first encounter on High-church Down. We had not known each other a week before you were convinced Marianne wore his portrait around her neck; yet it transpired to be merely the miniature of our great-uncle."

"But this is entirely distinct," Margaret insisted. "I am positive they will wed soon, for he possesses a lock of her hair."

"Exercise caution, Margaret. It may belong to some great-uncle of *his*."

"It is undoubtedly Marianne's," Margaret countered. "I am nearly certain, for I witnessed him sever it. Last night, after tea, when you and Mother exited the room, they whispered furiously together. He appeared to beg something of her, then seized her scissors. Her hair cascaded down her back, and he snipped a long lock. He kissed it, folded it within a sheet of white paper, and deposited it into his pocket-book."

Given the specificity of these particulars and the authority of the source, Elinor could not withhold her credence, nor was she inclined to do so, as the circumstances aligned perfectly with her own observations. Margaret's sagacity, however, often manifested in ways unsatisfactory to her sister. One evening at the Park, when Mrs. Jennings aggressively demanded the name of the young man who was Elinor's particular favorite—a query that had long plagued her curiosity—Margaret responded by glancing at her sister and asking, "Must I not tell, may I, Elinor?"

This response naturally elicited laughter from everyone present, including Elinor, though the effort to smile was painfully strained. She suspected Margaret had selected a suitor whose name she could not utter with composure, thereby ensuring it became a perpetual jest among Mrs. Jennings' circle. Marianne sympathized with her sister's plight but ultimately exacerbated the situation by flushing crimson and angrily admonishing Margaret, "Recall that regardless of your conjectures, you possess no right to disseminate them."

"I never entertained any conjectures," Margaret retorted. "It was you who revealed it to me yourself."

This only intensified the merriment of the assembly, and Margaret was eagerly prodded to elaborate. "Oh! Pray, Miss Margaret, enlighten us completely," urged Mrs. Jennings. "What is the gentleman's name?"

"I must not reveal it, ma'am. Yet I know precisely who he is, and I know his location as well."

"Yes, yes, we can deduce his whereabouts; surely at his own residence at Norland. He is the parish curate, I presume."

"No, *that* he is not. He holds no profession whatsoever."

"Margaret," Marianne interjected with considerable heat, "you know this is a fabrication of your own imagination, and that no such individual exists."

"Well, then, he is recently deceased, Marianne, for I am convinced such a man once existed, and his name commences with an 'F.'"

At this critical juncture, Elinor felt immense gratitude toward Lady Middleton for interjecting, "It is raining exceedingly heavily." Although she suspected the interruption stemmed less from genuine concern for her than from her ladyship's aversion to the inelegant topics of ridicule that delighted her husband and mother, the diversion proved timely. Colonel Brandon, ever mindful of the sensitivities of others, immediately seized upon the subject of precipitation, and much discourse ensued on the matter.

Willoughby rose, opening the piano-forte and inviting Marianne to sit. Thus, amidst the concerted efforts of various guests to bury the topic, it was successfully laid to rest. Yet, Elinor found herself unable to recover so readily from the alarm into which the conversation had plunged her.

That evening, plans were formulated for a visit the following day to a magnificent estate located approximately twelve miles from Barton, owned by a brother-in-law of Colonel Brandon. Access to the property required his intervention, as the proprietor, currently abroad, had issued strict prohibitions against unauthorized entry. The grounds were lauded as exceptionally beautiful, with Sir John serving as a particularly ardent advocate; having organized excursions to the site at least twice annually for the past decade, he qualified as a discerning judge. The itinerary included a noble expanse of water, where sailing would constitute the primary amusement. Cold provisions were to be carried, and travel arranged via open carriages, adhering strictly to the conventional style of a complete party of pleasure.

For several members of the company, the undertaking appeared audacious, given the season and the fact that rain had persisted daily for the preceding fortnight. Consequently, Mrs. Dashwood, who was already suffering from a cold, was persuaded by Elinor to remain at home.
