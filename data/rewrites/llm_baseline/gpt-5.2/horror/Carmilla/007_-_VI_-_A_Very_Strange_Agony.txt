When we had drawn ourselves again into the drawing-room—where the order of the house reasserted itself with its accustomed gentleness, as if habit were a kind of prayer—we settled to our coffee and chocolate. The cups made their small domestic music: porcelain ticking against saucer, the soft pour, the faint sigh of steam; and behind it all the old chateau’s breathing—timbers contracting, a shutter giving its minute complaint, the wind worrying the ivy at the casement. It was the sort of sound-scape that, in country houses, keeps one’s nerves in tune; there were no iron wheels shrieking on rails here, no factory pulse, no street-vendors’ bawling to sell you what you did not need. Only the steady, human economy of a household that still knew how to warm milk without scorching it, and how to draw water without waking the entire valley.

Carmilla took neither coffee nor chocolate. The fragrance rose to her and seemed to pass through her like moonlight through gauze; but she sat with us, and her color had returned, and her manner was once more that languid, affectionate composure which, after her earlier agitation, felt almost like a lamp re-lit. Madame and Mademoiselle De Lafontaine joined, and the cards came out, and we made of the evening the innocent little theatre it commonly was—hands shuffling, paper whispering, an occasional laugh dropped like a sugar-lump into a cup.

When the game was over, papa entered for what he called his “dish of tea.” I can still hear the bright clink of the spoon in his glass and the small satisfaction in the phrase itself—old words, old turns of speech, which belong to families as recipes do, and which one day, I have sometimes thought, vanish as completely as a dialect vanishes when its last mouth is closed. He sat down beside Carmilla upon the sofa, and, with a care that tried to look like ease, asked whether she had heard from her mother since her arrival.

“No,” she answered simply.

“And do you know,” he continued, “to what address a letter would reach you at present?”

“I cannot tell,” she said, with a softness that concealed rather than confessed. Then, after a moment, in which I saw her fingers rest upon the silk of her dress as if she were smoothing a thought flat, she added, “But I have been thinking of leaving you. You have been already too hospitable and too kind to me. I have given you an infinity of trouble, and I should wish to take a carriage tomorrow, and post in pursuit of her. I know where I shall ultimately find her, although I dare not yet tell you.”

“But you must not dream of any such thing,” papa exclaimed at once—to my great relief, for the very idea of her departure struck the room like the sudden extinction of the fire. “We cannot afford to lose you so, and I will not consent to your leaving us, except under the care of your mother, who was so good as to consent to your remaining with us till she should herself return. I should be quite happy if I knew that you heard from her; but this evening the accounts of the progress of the mysterious disease that has invaded our neighborhood grow even more alarming; and my beautiful guest, I do feel the responsibility, unaided by advice from your mother, very much. Still I shall do my best; and one thing is certain—you must not think of leaving us without her distinct direction to that effect. We should suffer too much in parting from you to consent to it easily.”

She looked down, and that shy smile came, as if praise and kindness were things she had been denied and did not quite know where to lay. “Thank you, sir, a thousand times for your hospitality,” she answered. “You have all been too kind to me. I have seldom been so happy in all my life before as in your beautiful chateau, under your care, and in the society of your dear daughter.”

Papa, gallant in his old-fashioned way, kissed her hand. I saw the pleased light in his face, and I remember, absurdly, the scent of the tea rising anew as he leaned—bergamot and warm leaf—and how that comforting odor seemed to insist that we were safe because we were civilized, because we still observed the pretty ceremonies of home.

I accompanied Carmilla, as usual, to her room, and sat chatting while she prepared for bed. The ordinary little actions—the unpinning of hair, the smoothing of a garment, the folding away of lace—had in her a curious solemnity, as if each were part of some private rite, performed not for cleanliness or comfort, but for memory. Her hair, when loosed, fell in rich waves, and the brushing of it made a faint, intimate sound, a dry, silken rain. Outside, the night held its own choir: a far owl’s single note, and the more constant hush of leaves. In such quiet, one hears even one’s thoughts turning; perhaps that is why the mind invents dangers to give itself a louder occupation.

“Do you think,” I said at length, “that you will ever confide fully in me?”

She turned round and smiled, and the smile answered in place of words—tender, ambiguous, and, in some strange way, complete. She only continued to look at me.

“You won’t answer that?” I said, half sorry I had spoken. “You can’t answer pleasantly. I ought not to have asked you.”

“You were quite right to ask me that, or anything,” she replied. “You do not know how dear you are to me, or you could not think any confidence too great to look for. But I am under vows—no nun half so awfully—and I dare not tell my story yet, even to you. The time is very near when you shall know everything. You will think me cruel, very selfish; but love is always selfish—the more ardent the more selfish. How jealous I am you cannot know. You must come with me, loving me, to death; or else hate me and still come with me, and hating me through death and after. There is no such word as indifference in my apathetic nature.”

“Now, Carmilla, you are going to talk your wild nonsense again,” I said hastily, for her words did not merely mean: they pressed against the skin, like a hand too cold.

“Not I—silly little fool as I am, and full of whims and fancies; for your sake I’ll talk like a sage,” she said, with that sudden turn to playful docility. “Were you ever at a ball?”

“No,” I answered, surprised at the leap. “How you do run on. What is it like? How charming it must be.”

“I almost forget,” she murmured, as if she were trying to taste a flavor long ago lost. “It is years ago.”

I laughed. “You are not so old. Your first ball can hardly be forgotten yet.”

“I remember everything about it—with an effort,” she said slowly. “I see it all as divers see what is going on above them, through a medium—dense, rippling, but transparent. There occurred that night what has confused the picture and made its colours faint. I was all but assassinated in my bed—wounded here,” and she touched her breast with the tips of her fingers, not dramatically, but as one touches a scar one cannot translate into speech. “And never was the same since.”

“Were you near dying?” I asked.

“Yes—very. A cruel love—strange love—that would have taken my life. Love will have its sacrifices. No sacrifice without blood. Let us go to sleep now; I feel so lazy. How can I get up just now and lock my door?”

She had already slipped into bed, and lay with her tiny hands buried in her rich wavy hair beneath her cheek, her little head deep in the pillow. Her eyes glittered in the candlelight and followed me wherever I moved, with a shy smile I could not decipher—like a word in a language one has heard in childhood and forgotten, except for the ache it leaves.

I bid her good-night and crept from the room with an uncomfortable sensation, as if I were leaving behind not a friend, but a question.

I often wondered whether our pretty guest ever said her prayers. I certainly had never seen her upon her knees. In the morning she never came down until long after our family prayers were over; at night she never left the drawing-room to attend our brief evening prayers in the hall. If it had not casually come out in one of our careless talks that she had been baptised, I should have doubted her being a Christian. Religion was a subject on which I had never heard her speak one word. Had I known the world better, this neglect—or antipathy—would not have surprised me so much; but in our quiet life, words were weights, and the words one refused were sometimes heavier than those one spoke. Silence, too, can be a kind of dominion.

The precautions of nervous people are infectious, and persons of a like temperament are pretty sure, after a time, to imitate them. I had adopted Carmilla’s habit of locking my bedroom door, having taken into my head all her whimsical alarms about midnight invaders and prowling assassins. I had also adopted her precaution of making a brief search through my room, to satisfy myself that no lurking assassin or robber was ensconced. These wise measures taken, I went to bed.

A light burned in my room. This was an old habit of very early date, and nothing could have tempted me to dispense with it. The candle made a small pool of safety—its waxy odor, its faint crackle, the tiny hiss when the wick fed. Thus fortified, I might take my rest in peace.

But dreams come through stone walls. They brighten dark rooms and darken light ones, and their persons make their exits and their entrances as they please, and laugh at locksmiths.

I had a dream that night that was the beginning of a very strange agony. I cannot call it a nightmare, for I was quite conscious of being asleep; yet I was equally conscious of being in my room and lying in bed precisely as I actually was. I saw—or fancied I saw—the room and its furniture just as I had seen it last, except that it was very dark; and I saw something moving round the foot of the bed, which at first I could not accurately distinguish.

Soon, however, its shape declared itself: a sooty-black animal, like a monstrous cat. It seemed to me four or five feet long, for it measured fully the length of the hearthrug as it passed over it; and it continued to-ing and fro-ing with the lithe, sinister restlessness of a beast in a cage. There was a soundlessness to it that was worse than any cry. In the country, even terror generally has a voice—hoofbeats, a branch snapped, a dog’s warning. This thing moved without paying the night its proper noises, and the unnatural quiet tightened round my throat.

I could not cry out, though I was terrified. Its pace grew faster. The room seemed to thicken with darkness, darker and darker, as if someone were smothering the candle with a slow hand; until at last I could no longer see anything of it but its eyes—broad, watchful, and fixed, as if they were the only two lights permitted in my world.

I felt it spring lightly upon the bed.

Those eyes approached my face, and suddenly I felt a stinging pain, as if two large needles darted—an inch or two apart—deep into my breast.

I woke with a scream.

The room was lighted by the candle that burned there all through the night; and I saw a female figure standing at the foot of the bed, a little at the right side. It wore a dark loose dress; its hair was down and covered its shoulders. It was so still that a block of stone could not have been more still. There was not the slightest stir of respiration. Even the night’s ordinary sounds seemed to hold their breath, as if the house itself were listening and did not dare to speak.

As I stared, the figure appeared to have changed its place and was now nearer the door. Then, close to it, the door opened, and it passed out.

At once I was relieved—able to breathe and move, as though some weight had been lifted from my ribs. My first thought was that Carmilla had been playing me a trick, and that I had forgotten to secure my door. I hastened to it and found it locked as usual on the inside.

I was afraid to open it. I was horrified. I sprang back into bed, covered my head in the bedclothes, and lay there more dead than alive till morning.
