I followed the raven deep into the pine forest, the air thick with resin that clung to my throat like the breath of a half-asleep god. Neither of us spoke for long stretches, wrapped in the sacred, velvet gloom that swallowed sound whole until not even a footstep could echo. The trees swelled larger as we walked, trunks gnarled and scarred with centuries of unrecorded weather, their boughs curving into strange, whimsical shapes as if they had forgotten how to be ordinary pine a hundred lifetimes prior.

At last the forest thinned, its ranks breaking apart to reveal the wind-scoured stretch of open heath beyond. “You see that hawthorn?” my guide said, his ebony, iridescent beak jabbing toward the tree line.
I squinted. Where the wood faded into heath edge, there stood a shape that looked less like a plant than a hunched ancient man, his head frosted pure white as if dusted with snow out of season. “I see a gnarled old elder, with a great white head,” I corrected.
“Look again,” he rumbled. “It is a hawthorn.”
“It is certainly an ancient hawthorn,” I frowned, “but this is no time of year for it to bloom. There is no spring here.”
“The season for the hawthorn to blossom,” he replied, “is when the hawthorn blossoms. That tree grows in the ruins of the old church on your home farm. You were going to give your bailiff directions for that churchyard the morning the thunder struck, were you not?”
The memory hit me sharp and bright. “I was going to tell him to let it run wild, turn every inch of it into a tangle of rose bushes, and ensure the plough never touches within three yards of the graves.”

“Listen.” The raven went still, as if holding his breath, his feathers flattening against his body.
I did as he said. At first I thought I caught the sigh of a distant, melodic wind, then something softer: the ghost of hymns sung so long ago they had dissolved into pure, wordless joy. I could not be certain I heard anything at all, until the note of it hummed in my bones.
“They still gather there,” the raven said.
“Who? And what do they do?” I asked.
“Some of the congregation that once knelt in that old church, they still return to the ruins,” he said. “I do not think they will be able to stay much longer, though.”
“What draws them back now?”
“They lend each other the weight of their company to work their thoughts free, to hatch the feelings that cannot unfold in a busy, loud world. They talk soft, they sing old songs, and then the great, shared thought they have tended floats out of all their chests together, like a tall ship slipping out of a river at the highest high tide, bound for open sea.”
“Do they pray, too, alongside their singing?”
“No. They have learned every heart speaks to the divine best in its own quiet, unshared silence. Some people never stop praying, even when they think they are doing nothing at all.--Look! There goes one!”

He snapped his beak upward, pointing into the empty blue sky. A snow-white pigeon was ascending, beating its wings faster and faster, climbing the rungs of an invisible, ethereal stair I could almost see shimmering beneath the sunlight. Light burst off its feathers in trembling, rippling waves.
“I see a pigeon,” I said.
“Of course you see a pigeon,” the raven huffed. “Because the pigeon is there. But I see a prayer, flying straight home. I wonder what heart gave birth to it? Someone in my cemetery must have finally woken up.”
“How can a pigeon be a prayer?” I protested. “I understand it makes a lovely symbol, of course, but a live, breathing bird that steps straight out of a human heart?”
“It must puzzle you! It cannot fail to, with how little of the worlds beyond your own you have seen.”
“A prayer is a thought! Something spiritual!” I pressed.
“That is true. But if you understood even a single world outside the small mortal sphere you call home, you would understand your own far better. When a heart is truly, fully alive, it can think living, breathing things. There is one great Heart at the core of all creation, every one of whose thoughts is a strong, joyful creature, even whose dreams are full of new life. Some people lift their prayers heavy as stones off the ground, only to drop them straight back again. Others send their prayers skyward in living shapes, whatever creature matches the shape of the longing in their chest the best. Every living thing was a thought first, once, so they are made to be held and given back by those that think. When you say to that great Thinker ‘This is one of your thoughts, I am holding it clear and bright in my own heart now’ -- that is prayer. A word from one small heart, back to the big Heart that made all of them. --Look, there is another!”

This time he pointed down, toward the foot of a weathered granite boulder half-buried in heath. I knelt. There was a tiny flower growing there I had never seen the like of, its shape soft and trusting, its colour a pale, blushing rose, its scent sweet as a new dawn that had never been seen before, that tasted like both a forgotten memory and a brand new world. It looked almost like an anemone, its center glowing the soft, rich gold of honey.
“That is a prayer-flower,” the raven said.
“I have never seen a flower like that in my life,” I breathed.
“There is no other exactly like it. No two prayer-flowers ever grow the same,” he said.
“How do you know that is what it is?” I asked.
“By the look of it. The expression of it, in the curve of every petal. I cannot explain more than that. If you know it, you know it. If you do not, no list of facts will make you.”
“Can you not teach me how to spot them for myself?” I said.
“I could not. And if I could, what good would it do you? You would not know it for the thing itself, through your own eyes. Why bother learning the name of a thing if you never learn to see the thing itself? It is no one’s job but your own to pry your eyes open. But make no mistake: the whole business of the universe is to make a fool of you, until you finally admit you are one -- and then, at last, you can start to be wise.”

I stared down at the small, glowing flower, and I knew. It was unlike any bloom I had ever encountered in my old world, so strange and alive I could almost feel the quiet, gentle longing that had grown it, thrumming soft in its petals. A great, humbling awe washed over me, as I realized I was not looking at a weed. I was looking at a shadow of a prayer, made tangible, rooted in the dirt.
