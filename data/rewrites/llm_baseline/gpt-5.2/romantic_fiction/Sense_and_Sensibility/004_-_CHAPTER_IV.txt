“It is such a pity, Elinor,” Marianne said, as they sat with the after-supper quiet settling over Norland like a clean cloth smoothed upon a table, “that Edward should have no taste for drawing.”

Elinor, who had been folding a square of linen with the patient exactness that turns household work into a sort of thinking, looked up at once. “No taste for drawing? Why should you suppose it? He does not draw himself, it is true; but he takes real pleasure in watching other people’s hands do what his own have never been taught. That is not indifference. It is only want of practice. Had he been in the way of learning, I believe he would have done it well. His only fault is a distrust of himself—he will hardly offer an opinion on a picture because he fears to say too much, or the wrong thing. But there is a natural propriety in him, a simplicity of liking and disliking, that generally guides him right.”

Marianne, who never handled a pencil without expecting a flame of feeling in return, held her peace for fear of hurting her sister; but the measured approval Elinor described seemed, to her, as thin as broth made only with water—nourishing perhaps, yet without that rich perfume which alone, in Marianne’s mind, deserved the name of taste. Still, she loved Elinor the more for that very blindness which warmed her judgment of Edward; it was an affection that, like an old family recipe, altered the flavour of everything it touched.

“I hope, Marianne,” Elinor continued, and her voice had that careful gentleness which always tried to keep peace in a room, “you do not think him deficient in general taste. Indeed I am sure you cannot; for you are perfectly cordial to him—and if you truly held him in contempt, you could never be civil.”

Marianne coloured, and for a moment listened not only to Elinor but to the house itself: the low stir of embers in the grate, the distant closing of a door, the soft, living hush that belonged to a country evening when no wheels or iron clatter invaded it. In that calm, she could not bear to make a harsh sound with words.

“Do not be offended, Elinor,” she said at last, choosing her phrases as if choosing herbs—knowing one leaf too much could spoil a whole dish. “My praise cannot in every thing equal your sense of his merits. I have not had so many opportunities of understanding the minuter propensities of his mind—his inclinations and tastes—as you have. But of his goodness and his sense I have the highest opinion in the world. I think him every thing that is worthy and amiable.”

“I am sure,” Elinor replied, smiling with honest relief, “that his dearest friends could not be dissatisfied with such commendation. I do not see how you could express yourself more warmly.”

Marianne was glad to have pleased her. Elinor, once set upon Edward, went on as if she must finish the account, as a careful cook finishes her directions so that nothing may be misdone.

“Of his sense and goodness no one can doubt,” she said, “who has seen him often enough to draw him into unreserved conversation. His understanding and his principles are excellent; they are only concealed by that shyness which too often keeps him silent. You know enough of him to do justice to his solid worth. But those minuter propensities—as you call them—you have been kept from knowing so well as I have. He and I have, at times, been thrown a good deal together, while you have been wholly engrossed—on the most affectionate principle—by my mother. I have seen a great deal of him; I have studied his sentiments; I have heard his opinions on literature and taste. Upon the whole, I venture to say his mind is well informed; his enjoyment of books exceedingly great; his imagination lively; his observation just and correct; his taste delicate and pure. His abilities improve upon acquaintance as much as his manners and his person. At first sight his address is not striking, and his person can hardly be called handsome—till one perceives the expression of his eyes, which are uncommonly good, and the general sweetness of his countenance. At present I know him so well that I think him really handsome—or at least almost so. What say you, Marianne?”

Marianne’s eyes softened at the tenderness in her sister’s last question. “I shall very soon think him handsome, Elinor, if I do not now. When you tell me to love him as a brother, I shall no more see imperfection in his face than I now do in his heart.”

Elinor started, as if a sudden loud note had broken the room’s quiet harmony, and she regretted, instantly, the warmth into which she had allowed herself to fall. Edward stood very high in her opinion—too high, perhaps, to be safely talked of in a house where Marianne and their mother had that eager manner of believing, in which wishing becomes hoping, and hoping becomes certainty. Elinor required more than conjecture; she required knowledge, the sober kind that could bear being named aloud.

“I do not attempt to deny,” she said, recovering her composure, “that I think very highly of him—that I greatly esteem him—that I like him.”

Marianne’s whole spirit rose at the chill of the words. “Esteem him! Like him!” she cried. “Cold-hearted Elinor! Oh! worse than cold-hearted—ashamed of being otherwise. Use those words again, and I will leave the room this moment.”

Elinor could not help laughing; it came from her, not as mockery, but as steam rises when something too hot meets something too cold. “Excuse me,” she said, “and be assured I meant you no offence by speaking so quietly of my own feelings. Believe them stronger than I have declared; believe them, in short, such as his merit—and the suspicion, the hope, of his affection for me—may warrant without imprudence or folly. But farther than this you must not believe. I am by no means assured of his regard for me. There are moments when the extent of it seems doubtful; and until his sentiments are fully known, you cannot wonder at my wishing to avoid encouraging my own partiality by believing it—or calling it—more than it is. In my heart I feel little—scarcely any—doubt of his preference. But there are other points to be considered besides his inclination. He is very far from being independent. What his mother really is we cannot know; but from Fanny’s occasional mention of her conduct and opinions, we have never been disposed to think her amiable; and I should be much mistaken if Edward is not himself aware that there would be many difficulties in his way, if he wished to marry a woman who had not either a great fortune or high rank.”

It was as if Elinor had set before Marianne, not a romantic feast, but the plain bread of reality—good for sustaining, though it sat heavy at first. Marianne stared at her, astonished at how far she and her mother had let imagination run ahead, like a melody sung too fast.

“And you really are not engaged to him!” she exclaimed. “Yet it certainly soon will happen. But two advantages will proceed from this delay. I shall not lose you so soon; and Edward will have greater opportunity of improving that natural taste for your favourite pursuit which must be so indispensably necessary to your future felicity. Oh! if he should be so far stimulated by your genius as to learn to draw himself, how delightful it would be!”

Elinor had spoken her real mind, and now she felt the sting of it. She could not consider her partiality in so prosperous a state as Marianne imagined. There was, at times, a want of spirits about Edward which, if it did not denote indifference, spoke of something almost as unpromising. A doubt of her regard—supposing him to feel it—might give him uneasiness, but would hardly produce that dejection which so often attended him. A more reasonable cause lay in his dependent situation: a life lived under another’s will, as a household lives under a harsh and constant noise, never allowed the full comfort of its own quiet. His mother neither behaved to him in a way that made his home comfortable now, nor gave him any assurance that he might ever form a home for himself without submitting strictly to her views for his aggrandizement. With such knowledge Elinor could not be easy. She was far from depending, as her mother and Marianne did, on the certain result of his preference. Indeed, the longer they were together, the more doubtful seemed the nature of his regard; and sometimes—only for a few painful minutes—she believed it no more than friendship.

Whatever its limits, it was enough, once perceived by his sister, to make that sister uneasy—and, still more commonly, uncivil. Fanny watched affection as if it were a theft to be detected: the moment she suspected an attachment, she began to speak of it in the language of danger and capture, as though a young woman could “draw in” a man the way a market huckster draws in customers with bright talk and false sweetness.

She took the first opportunity of affronting Mrs. Dashwood. Her manner was all fine edge and polished hardness; and she talked, with expressive meaning, of her brother’s great expectations, of Mrs. Ferrars’s resolution that both her sons should marry well, and of the peril attending any young woman who attempted to draw him in. The words themselves were genteel; the tone was a blade.

Mrs. Dashwood could neither pretend not to understand, nor endeavour to be calm. Her answer marked her contempt; and she left the room immediately, her mind made up in an instant—that whatever inconvenience or expense might attend so sudden a removal, her beloved Elinor should not be exposed another week to such insinuations, not another day forced to live in an atmosphere where every sound had a sting in it.

In this fever of spirit, a letter was brought to her by the post—one of those small, ordinary packets that, in a household, can change the whole flavour of life as suddenly as a handful of salt dropped into a pot. It contained a proposal, and it arrived as if Providence itself had timed the delivery.

The offer was of a small house, on very easy terms, belonging to a relation of her own—Sir John Middleton, a gentleman of consequence and property in Devonshire. The letter came from Sir John himself, written with a frank friendliness that seemed to open the windows of the room and let in clean air. He understood she was in need of a dwelling; and though the place he offered was merely a cottage, he assured her that every thing should be done to it which she might think necessary, if only the situation pleased her. He pressed her, after giving particulars of the house and garden, to come with her daughters to Barton Park, his own residence, from which she might judge whether Barton Cottage—for the houses were in the same parish—could, by any alteration, be made comfortable to her.

The style of the letter, so sincerely accommodating, could not fail to give pleasure, especially at a moment when she suffered under the cold, unfeeling behaviour of nearer connections. She needed no time for deliberation. Her resolution formed as she read; and what, a few hours before, would have seemed the greatest objection—the distance, the very remoteness of Devonshire from Sussex—now became its first recommendation. To quit the neighbourhood of Norland was no longer an evil; it was an object of desire. It was a blessing compared with the misery of continuing her daughter-in-law’s guest; and to remove for ever from that beloved place would be less painful than to inhabit it, or even visit it, while such a woman was mistress there.

She wrote instantly to Sir John with thanks and acceptance, the pen moving quickly, as if the very scratch upon the paper could drown out the remembered sharpness of Fanny’s voice. Then she hastened to show both letters to her daughters, that she might be secure of their approbation before her answer was sent.

Elinor had always thought it more prudent for them to settle at some distance from Norland than immediately among their present acquaintance; on that head, therefore, she could not oppose her mother’s plan of removing into Devonshire. The house, too, as Sir John described it, was on so simple a scale, and the rent so uncommonly moderate, as to leave her no right of objection. And though the scheme brought no charm to her fancy—though it carried them farther from Norland than she wished—she made no attempt to dissuade her mother from sending the letter of acquiescence.
