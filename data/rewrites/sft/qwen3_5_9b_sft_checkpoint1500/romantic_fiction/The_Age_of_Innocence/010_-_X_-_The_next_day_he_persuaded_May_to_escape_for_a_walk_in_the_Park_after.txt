The following morning, Newland Archer managed to persuade May Welland to slip away for a stroll in Central Park, a venture undertaken immediately after luncheon. In the rigid, old-fashioned circles of Episcopalian New York, it was customary for daughters to accompany their parents to church on Sunday afternoons; yet Mrs. Welland, having that same morning been swayed by the urgent necessity of preparing a hand-embroidered trousseau complete with the requisite number of dozen linens, tacitly approved May's absence.

The day itself was a masterpiece of nature. Along the Mall, the bare, vaulting branches of the trees formed a canopy of lapis lazuli overhead, sheltering a carpet of snow that glittered like splintered crystal. It was a weather that seemed designed to ignite May's inner radiance, and she glowed with the fierce, vibrant energy of a young maple tree burning in the frost. Archer watched her with a swelling pride, intoxicated by the glances cast her way. The simple, intoxicating joy of possession seemed to clear away the lingering, confusing doubts that had recently plagued him.

"It's so delicious," May exclaimed, her voice light, "waking every morning to find lilies-of-the-valley blooming in one's room!"

"They arrived somewhat late yesterday," Archer admitted gently. "I hadn't quite the time in the morning..."

"But your remembering to send them each day makes me love them infinitely more than if you had placed a standing order for them to arrive punctually, like a strict music teacher," she countered, her eyes twinkling. "As I recall, Miss Gertrude Lefferts used to do this when she and Lawrence were engaged."

"Ah—yes, they certainly would!" Archer laughed, amused by her sharp wit. He glanced sideways at her cheeks, flushed and luminous like fruit, and felt a profound sense of security allow him to confess: "When I sent your lilies yesterday afternoon, I noticed some exceptionally gorgeous yellow roses nearby. I packed them off to Madame Olenska. Was that the right move?"

"How thoughtful of you!" May replied, her expression softening. "Anything of that sort always delights her. It is peculiar, however, that she did not mention receiving them. She lunched with us today and spoke at length about Mr. Beaufort sending her magnificent orchids, and Cousin Henry van der Luyden delivering a massive hamper of carnations from Skuytercliff. She appears so genuinely surprised by gifts. Do people not send flowers in Europe? She considers it such a charming custom."

"Oh, well, no wonder mine were completely overshadowed by Beaufort's," Archer murmured, a flicker of irritation crossing his mind. Then, guilt struck him; he realized he had forgotten to include a card with the roses, and the admission now sounded clumsy. He desperately wished to say, "I called on your cousin yesterday," but hesitated. If Madame Olenska had not mentioned his visit, bringing it up now would likely create an awkward silence. Furthermore, omitting it entirely gave their relationship an air of mystery that he found deeply unsettling. To distract himself from this internal turmoil, he steered the conversation toward their own lives, their future, and Mrs. Welland's obsession with a prolonged engagement period.

"If you call it 'long'!" May protested, adopting the traditional maidenly interrogation. "Isabel Chivers and Reggie were engaged for two years! Grace and Thorley for nearly a year and a half. Why aren't we considered well-off as we are?"

Archer felt a sudden flush of shame. He recognized the childishness of her inquiry, yet he understood why she asked it. She was approaching her twenty-second birthday, and he wondered at what precise age "proper" women began to articulate their own desires rather than echoing societal norms.

"Never, if we refuse to let them, I suppose," he mused aloud, recalling his explosive outburst to Mr. Sillerton Jackson: "Women ought to be as free as we men are..."

Soon, it would fall to him to remove the blindfold from this young woman's eyes and guide her gaze toward the wider world. But how many generations of women, shaped by the same traditions, had descended into the family vaults with their eyes still bound? A chill ran through him as he recalled the radical new theories in his scientific texts, specifically the famous case of the Kentucky cave-fish, which had lost its eyesight because it lived in eternal darkness. What if, when he finally urged May Welland to open hers, she gazed out at a world that offered nothing but blankness?

"We could be much better off," Archer suggested softly. "We could be entirely together—we could travel."

May's face illuminated instantly. "That would be lovely," she confessed. Her desire to wander was palpable. However, she quickly tempered her enthusiasm with a sober reality check. "But Mother would never understand us wanting to live so differently."

"As if the mere fact of doing things 'differently' wouldn't account for everything!" Archer insisted, taking the role of the devoted wooer.

"You *are* so original, Newland!" she exclaimed, her spirit soaring.

His heart sank. He saw clearly that he was uttering the very clichés that young men in his position were expected to recite, and that she was responding with the automatic, instinctive answers drilled into her by tradition—even to the point of labeling him "original."

"Original!" he repeated, shaking his head. "We are all as similar to one another as those dolls cut from the same folded sheet of paper. We are like patterns stenciled onto a wall. Cannot you and I carve out a path for ourselves, May?"

He had turned to face her, seized by the fervor of their debate, and her eyes met his with a bright, unclouded admiration.

"My goodness—" May laughed, the tension breaking. "Shall we elope?"

"If you wish it—"

"Do you truly love me, Newland? I am so happy!"

"And if we did—why not be happier?"

"We cannot act like characters in a novel, can we?"

"Why not? Why not? Why not?"

A faint weariness settled over her face. She knew perfectly well that they could not, yet the burden of producing a logical reason for her restraint felt heavy.

"I am not clever enough to argue with you," she conceded, seeking relief in a single, decisive word. "But that sort of behavior is rather... vulgar, isn't it?" she suggested, hoping the term would instantly extinguish the subject.

"Are you so terrified, then, of being vulgar?"

She appeared momentarily stunned by the question. "Of course I would detest it—and so would you," she rejoined, a trace of irritation returning to her tone.

Archer stood silent, tapping his walking stick nervously against the top of his boot. He sensed that she had indeed found the perfect key to lock the door on this dangerous topic. Lightened by the resolution, she continued cheerfully, "Did I tell you I showed Ellen my ring? She believes it possesses the most beautiful setting she has ever encountered. There is nothing comparable on the Rue de la Paix, she says. I do love you, Newland, for being so artistic!"

The next afternoon, before dinner, Archer sat smoking with a sullen air in his study. His sister, Janey, wandered in upon him. He had skipped his customary stop at the club on his way home from the office, exercising the legal profession common to wealthy New Yorkers in a leisurely fashion. Spirits low and temper frayed, a haunting horror of monotony—the sameness of waking at the same hour, sitting in the same chairs, hearing the same conversations—besieged his mind.

"Sammerness—sameness!" he muttered, the word echoing in his head like a mocking melody as he stared at the familiar, tall-hatted figures lounging behind the plate glass. Since he invariably visited the club at this time, he had returned home instead. He knew precisely what topics they would discuss and the specific roles each member would play. Of course, the Duke would be the central theme; though the recent arrival in Fifth Avenue of a golden-haired lady in a small, canary-colored brougham, drawn by a pair of black cobs (a vehicle generally attributed to Mr. Beaufort), would undoubtedly dominate the conversation. These "women"—as they were termed—were rare in New York; those who drove their own carriages even rarer. The appearance of Miss Fanny Ring in Fifth Avenue at the fashionable hour had sent shockwaves through society. Not a day prior, her carriage had passed Mrs. Lovell Mingott's, prompting the latter to ring the little bell at her elbow and command the coachman to return her home immediately. "What if it had happened to Mrs. van der Luyden?" people whispered to one another with a shudder. Archer could already imagine Lawrence Lefferts, at that exact moment, holding forth on the disintegration of social moors.

He raised his head irritably when Janey entered, then quickly bent over his book—Swinburne's *Chastelard*, newly published—as if to hide from her. She glanced at the writing desk, heaped high with volumes, opened a book of *Contes Drolatiques*, wrinkled her nose at the archaic French, and sighed, "What learned things you read!"

"Well?" he asked, as she hovered over him like Cassandra.

"Mother is furious."

"Furious? With whom? About what?"
