The frosty hush of the opera lobby wrapped around Newland Archer as he stepped out of the auditorium, and the first face he recognized through the crowd of fur-collared, top-hatted patrons was that of Ned Winsett—his only friend among what his sister Janey called his "clever set" who cared to dig past the lazy, banal chatter of club smoking rooms and chop-house dinners to talk about things that actually mattered. He’d caught a glimpse of Winsett’s shabby, round-shouldered back across the opera house earlier, and had noticed the journalist’s dark, curious eyes lingering on the Beaufort box where Countess Ellen Olenska sat.

The two men shook hands, and Winsett proposed they duck around the corner to a little German café for a cold bock. Archer, too tangled up in thoughts of Ellen to slip into the usual meandering conversation, declined on the excuse that he had work waiting for him at home. "Oh, fine, so do I," Winsett laughed. "I’ll be the Industrious Apprentice right alongside you." They strolled side by side down the lamplit sidewalk, and after a minute Winsett leaned in, curious. "Look, the real thing I wanted to ask—who’s that dark lady in that fancy Beaufort box? The one your friend Lefferts was practically drooling over?"

A sharp, unaccountable flicker of annoyance pricked Archer. Why was Ned Winsett, a man who never bothered with society gossip, asking after Ellen Olenska? And why tie her name to Lawrence Lefferts of all people? "You’re not after an interview for the papers, I hope?" he joked, a little too sharp.

"Not for the press, for me," Winsett said. "She’s my neighbor, you see—funny sort of neighborhood for a woman that beautiful to land in. My little boy was chasing his kitten down our front area the other day, tripped and cut his knee to ribbons. She came rushing out bareheaded, scooped him right up, carried him inside and bandaged him all gentle, so warm and sweet that my wife was too flustered to even ask her name."

A soft, warm glow spread through Archer’s chest. Any woman might have helped a hurt child, but it was so perfectly Ellen to not care a fig for her bonnet or what the neighbors might think, to rush in unthinking and kind, and leave the poor Winsetts too dazzled to remember to get her name. "That’s the Countess Olenska. She’s old Mrs. Mingott’s granddaughter."

Winsett whistled. "A countess? I never would’ve guessed countesses were that neighborly. The rest of the Mingotts certainly aren’t."

"They would be, if you gave them half a chance," Archer smiled, and the two slipped into their old familiar, unresolvable argument about how the city’s intellectual set deliberately avoided the upper crust, even as both of them knew there was no point in hashing it out again. "I wonder how a countess ends up living in our scrappy little block," Winsett mused.

"Because she doesn’t care a hang about any of our silly little social signposts, where the rest of us tiptoe around every rule," Archer said, a quiet, private pride curling in his chest.

"Huh. Seen far bigger, fancier places, I expect," Winsett shrugged. "Well, this is my corner." He slouched off across Broadway, leaving Archer standing on the curb, staring after him, turning that last line over in his head. Ned Winsett always had those sudden, sharp flashes of insight—they were the best part of him, and they always made Archer wonder why a man that bright had settled for quiet, uncomplaining failure when most men his age were still fighting to make something of themselves.

Archer had always known Winsett had a wife and son, but he’d never met them. They always ran into each other at the Century Club, or at those tiny, smoky restaurants full of journalists and actors. Winsett had mentioned his wife was an invalid, which might have been true, or might just have meant she had no taste for performative social niceties, or no nice evening gowns—very possibly both. Winsett loathed every stuffy social ritual with a ferocity Archer found almost ridiculous, but he still felt invigorated every time he talked to the man.

Winsett wasn’t a journalist by choice. He was a man born to write beautiful, delicate literary criticism, a hundred years too late for anyone to care. He’d published one small volume of essays once—only 120 copies sold, 30 given away, the rest pulped by the publishers to make room for more profitable books—before he’d given up and taken a dead-end sub-editor job at a women’s weekly called *Hearth-Fires*, where fashion plates and paper patterns ran side by side with saccharine New England love stories and ads for temperance tonics. He was endlessly funny complaining about the paper, but under the jokes there was a quiet, bitter emptiness of a man who’d given up on his dream too soon. Their conversations always left Archer staring down at the shape of his own life, all soft and comfortable and carefully planned, wondering how much of it he was actually living.

"Life doesn’t fit either of us," Winsett had told him once. "I’m down and out, nothing to be done about it. I’ve only got one thing I can make, and no one’s buying it, not in my lifetime. But you? You’re free, you’ve got money, you’ve got all the chances I never did. Why don’t you actually do something? Get into politics. That’s the only way."

Archer had laughed, amused. Everyone in his world knew a gentleman didn’t sully himself with American politics. He couldn’t say that outright to Winsett, though, so he dodged: "Look at what happens to honest men in American politics! They don’t want us there."

"Who’s ‘they’? Why don’t all of you band together and be ‘they’ yourselves?"

Archer’s smile had softened into a little condescending one. There was no point arguing. Everyone knew what happened to the few gentlemen foolish enough to dip their nice clean linen shirts into New York’s municipal politics. The day for that sort of thing was long gone, the city run by party bosses and immigrant constituents, leaving decent, respectable folks to retreat into sport, or culture, or the quiet, predictable rut of their inherited lives.

"Culture? If only we had real culture," Winsett had pushed back. "We’ve just got a few tiny, dying little pockets of it, left over from what your ancestors brought over from Europe, no tending, no competition, no audience. You’re all just portraits of gentlemen hanging on the walls of an empty, deserted house. You’ll never be anything at all until you roll up your sleeves and wade right into the muck. Or emigrate… God, if I could emigrate."

Archer had shrugged it off, turned the conversation back to books. Emigrate? A gentleman didn’t abandon his own country, any more than he waded into the grime of party politics. A gentleman stayed home, and kept himself apart. But even as he thought it, he’d realized that the bohemian, glittering little literary circles he moved in were no bigger, no less monotonous than the staid world of Fifth Avenue society—just two different tiny boxes, running on the same invisible rails.

The next morning, Archer tramped all over Manhattan searching for yellow roses, the bright, sunlit blooms Ellen had paused beside at the theatre, and came up empty. He arrived late to his stuffy law office, run by Mr. Letterblair, where most of the work was managing old wealthy families’ vast estates and "conservative" investments, and noticed no one even cared he was late. A sharp, hot exasperation bubbled up in his chest at the sheer useless pointlessness of the life he’d been set to inherit. Why wasn’t he on the sands of St. Augustine right now, with his fiancée May Welland? No one was fooled by his pretense of serious professional ambition. All the other young, well-off men at the firm did the same trivial little tasks, or sat at their desks reading the newspapers all day. The law was a respectable, gentlemanly profession, but none of them really wanted to advance in it, and Archer could already see the soft, green mould of routine creeping over all of their lives, slowly smothering any spark of real passion. He shuddered to think it might be creeping over him, too.

He sent a messenger with a note to Ellen Olenska’s house that afternoon, asking if he might call, begging her to leave her reply for him at his club. But there was no note waiting there, not that day, not the next. The silence stung him far more than it should have, and when he passed a florist’s window the following morning draped in a perfect cluster of yellow roses, he walked right past, leaving them there.

It wasn’t until the third day that her letter came, postmarked Skuytercliff—the van der Luydens’ remote, exclusive upstate estate, where visitors were rarely invited, and even the most privileged got no more than a chilly weekend stay.

"I ran away," she wrote, no fancy polite opening, no flowery preamble, the words as blunt and unguarded as she was. "The day after I saw you at the play. These kind friends the van der Luydens took me in. I wanted quiet, to think things through. You were right to tell me how kind they are. I feel so safe here. I wish that you were with us." She signed off with a spare, conventional "Yours sincerely," with no mention at all of when she planned to return to the city.

Archer stared at the note, his heart thrumming. What was Ellen running from? Why did she need to feel safe? His first silly thought was that some dark threat from her terrible old European husband had followed her across the ocean, then he laughed at himself. She was probably exaggerating, the way all women did, and her English was still a little stilted, like she was translating every word from French. *Je me suis évadée*—I escaped—might not mean anything more than that she’d run away from the endless, suffocating round of social calls and dinners society kept pushing on her.

He thought of how the van der Luydens, so rarely willing to open their gilded doors to anyone, had swept Ellen off to their private estate after rescuing her from the cold shoulders of society, just like the kind man in that French play he’d seen in Paris who kept rescuing the young stranger he’d pulled out of a glacier. They’d made rescuing Ellen their little gentle, stubborn mission now.

A sharp, bright disappointment coursed through him when he realized she was miles away from the city—then he remembered, all at once, that he’d turned down an invitation to spend the coming Sunday with the Reggie Chiverses at their house on the Hudson, barely a few miles south of Skuytercliff. He’d had his fill of their loud, boisterous parties, full of sleigh rides, ice boating, silly mild jokes and meaningless little flirting, and had planned to spend the quiet Sunday at home with the new stack of books his London bookseller had just sent him. But now, he walked straight to the club’s writing room, scribbled a hasty telegram to Mrs. Reggie, and told the servant to send it off immediately. He knew she’d never mind a last-minute change of plans—there was always an extra guest room waiting in her big, sprawling house. He didn’t stop to unpack what he was doing, the quiet, unnameable pull drawing him miles out of the city, just to be close to the place Ellen Olenska was staying.
