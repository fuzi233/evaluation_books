Mrs. Dashwood's surprise at Edward's arrival lasted only a fleeting moment; in her view, his return to Barton was the most natural occurrence imaginable. Yet, while her initial wonder quickly faded, her joy and warm regard remained deep and enduring. She greeted him with such genuine kindness that any lingering shyness, coldness, or reserve on his part dissolved instantly under the weight of her hospitality. Even before he crossed the threshold, these barriers had begun to crumble, completely overwhelmed by Mrs. Dashwood's captivating warmth. Indeed, it is impossible for a man to harbor true affection for either of her daughters without eventually extending that feeling to their mother. Consequently, Elinor found great satisfaction in watching Edward gradually regain his usual composure. His affections seemed to reawaken toward them all, and his concern for their welfare became evident once more.

However, despite his attentiveness, his praise of their home, and his admiration of the view, it was clear that Edward was not truly in high spirits. The entire family sensed this shift, and Mrs. Dashwood, interpreting his gloom as a result of his mother's stinginess, sat down to dinner with a heart indignant against all selfish parents.

"What are Mrs. Ferrars's plans for you just now, Edward?" she asked after the meal, as they gathered around the fire. "Are you still destined to be a great orator, whether you wish it or not?"

"No," Edward replied. "I trust my mother is finally convinced that I possess neither the talent nor the inclination for a public life!"

"But how, then, is your reputation to be secured? For surely you must achieve fame to satisfy your family. With no desire for expense, no affection for strangers, no profession, and no assurance, establishing a name might prove difficult indeed."

"I shall make no attempt," Edward said firmly. "I have no wish to be distinguished, and I have every reason to hope I never shall. Thank heaven, I cannot be forced into genius or eloquence."

"You lack ambition, I am well aware," Mrs. Dashwood noted. "Your desires are entirely moderate."

"As moderate as those of the rest of the world, I believe," Edward countered. "I wish, as everyone else does, to be perfectly happy; but, like everyone else, it must be in my own way. Greatness will not secure that for me."

"Strange that it wouldn't!" cried Marianne. "What possible connection do wealth or grandeur have with happiness?"

"Grandeur has but little to do with it," Elinor interjected gently, "but wealth has much."

"Elinor, for shame!" Marianne exclaimed. "Money can only provide happiness where nothing else exists. Beyond a comfortable competency, it offers no real satisfaction regarding one's own inner state."

"Perhaps," Elinor smiled, "we shall arrive at the same conclusion. *Your* competency and *my* wealth are remarkably similar, I daresay. Without them, as the world currently stands, we would both agree that every form of external comfort is missing. Your ideals are simply nobler than mine. Come, what exactly constitutes your competency?"

"Between eighteen hundred and two thousand pounds a year; not more than that."

Elinor laughed softly. "*Two* thousand a year! *One* is my wealth! I knew exactly how this conversation would end."

"And yet, two thousand a year is a very modest income," Marianne declared. "A family cannot reasonably maintain itself on less. I assure you, I am not extravagant in my demands. A proper household staff, perhaps two carriages, and hunting horses cannot be sustained on anything smaller."

Elinor smiled again, hearing her sister describe their future expenses at Combe Magna with such precision.

"Hunting horses!" Edward repeated. "But why must you have them? Not everyone hunts."

Marianne blushed as she replied, "But most people do."

"I wish," said Margaret, suddenly striking upon a novel idea, "that someone would grant us all a large fortune individually!"

"Oh, that they would!" Marianne cried, her eyes sparkling with animation and her cheeks glowing with the delight of such imagined bliss.

"We are all unanimous in that wish, I suppose," Elinor remarked, "despite the acknowledged insufficiency of wealth."

"Oh dear!" Margaret sighed happily. "How joyful I should be! I wonder what I would do with it!"

Marianne appeared to have no doubts whatsoever on that score.

"I should be quite puzzled to spend such a vast fortune myself," Mrs. Dashwood admitted, "if my children were all to be wealthy without my assistance."

"You must begin your improvements on this house," Elinor suggested. "Then your difficulties will soon vanish."

"What magnificent orders would travel from this family to London in such an event!" Edward mused. "What a glorious day for booksellers, music merchants, and print shops! You, Miss Dashwood, would issue a general commission for every new print of merit to be sent to you. As for Marianne, knowing her magnanimous soul, there would not be enough music in all of London to content her. And books! Thomson, Cowper, Scott—she would purchase them all over and over again. I believe she would buy up every single copy to prevent them from falling into unworthy hands; she would acquire every volume that teaches her how to admire an ancient, twisted tree. Would you not, Marianne? Forgive me if I am being slightly saucy. I was merely wishing to demonstrate that I have not forgotten our old debates."

"I love to be reminded of the past, Edward," Marianne responded warmly. "Whether it be melancholy or cheerful, I cherish recalling it. You will never offend me by speaking of former times. You are quite correct in guessing how I would spend my money—at least some of it. My loose cash would certainly be invested in expanding my collection of music and books."

"And the bulk of your fortune?" Edward pressed playfully. "Would that be laid out in annuities on the authors or their heirs?"

"No, Edward, I would have something else to do with it."

"Perhaps, then, you would bestow it as a reward on the person who penned the most able defense of your favorite maxim—that no one can ever fall in love more than once in their lifetime? Your opinion on that point remains unchanged, I presume?"

"Undoubtedly," Marianne affirmed. "At my age, opinions are tolerably fixed. It is unlikely that I shall now see or hear anything capable of altering them."

"Marianne is as steadfast as ever, you see," Elinor observed. "She has not changed at all."

"She has only grown a little more grave than she was," Edward added.

"Nay, Edward," Marianne said, turning to him. "_You_ need not reproach me. You are not very gay yourself."

"Why should you think so!" he replied, letting out a sigh. "But gaiety has never been a part of my character."

"Nor do I consider it a part of Marianne's," Elinor stated calmly. "I would hardly call her a lively girl. She is very earnest, very eager in everything she undertakes. She sometimes talks a great deal and always with animation, but she is rarely truly merry."

"I believe you are right," Edward conceded. "And yet, I have always considered her a lively girl."

"I have frequently caught myself making such errors," Elinor confessed. "I have totally misunderstood characters in various ways, imagining people to be far more gay or grave, or ingenious or stupid than they truly are. I can hardly explain why or how this deception originates. Sometimes we are guided by what they say of themselves, and very often by what others say of them, without taking the time to deliberate and judge properly."

"But I thought it was right, Elinor," Marianne insisted, "to be guided wholly by the opinions of others. I believed our judgments were given merely to serve those of our neighbors. That has always been your doctrine, I am sure."

"No, Marianne, never. My doctrine has never sought the subjugation of the understanding. All I have ever attempted to influence is behavior. You must not confuse my meaning. I admit, I have often wished you to treat our acquaintances with greater attention; but when have I advised you to adopt their sentiments or conform to their judgment in serious matters?"

"You have not been able to bring your sister over to your plan of general civility," Edward turned to Elinor. "Do you gain no ground?"

"On the contrary," Elinor replied, looking expressively at Marianne.

"My judgment," Edward continued, addressing Elinor, "is entirely on your side of the question; but I fear my practice aligns much more closely with my sister's. I never wish to offend, yet I am so foolishly shy that I often appear negligent when I am merely held back by my natural awkwardness. I have frequently thought that nature must have intended me for low company, for I am so ill at ease among strangers of gentility!"

"Marianne has no shyness to excuse any inattention on her part," Elinor pointed out.

"She knows her own worth too well for false shame," Edward retorted. "Shyness is merely the effect of a sense of inferiority in some way. If I could convince myself that my manners were perfectly easy and graceful, I would not be shy."

"But you would still be reserved," Marianne added sharply. "And that is worse."
