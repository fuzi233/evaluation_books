He stood upon the curbstone, submerged in misery, watching her retreat until she vanished from view. Yet, the moment she disappeared, a weight lifted from his spirit. She had granted him his freedom; while technically he had never truly surrendered it, the time for semantic quibbling had passed. He was free to act, and the path forward was suddenly clear. Lightness surged through him, transforming quickly into a jubilant celebration of his liberty. By the time he was halfway home, his decision was made.

The vicar of the parish where he resided lived merely ten minutes' walk from the square. Oleron turned toward his house. He required every scrap of intelligence regarding the old property—the insurance markings, the tilted "To Let" signs—and the vicar was the most probable source. With this preliminary hurdle cleared—*ah!* Oleron chuckled—a sense of anticipation bloomed. Things were about to unfold.

However, the information yielded was scant. The vicar confirmed the house was ancient, though Oleron knew that already. More intriguingly, he learned it was rumored to be haunted—a common whisper surrounding aged dwellings, the vicar noted with a sigh regarding the modern world's waning faith. Beyond that, the clergyman adopted a soothing tone, carefully gauging his words. Oleron smiled perceptively. "Leave my nerves out of your calculation," he remarked.

"How long has it stood vacant?"
"A dozen years, perhaps," the vicar replied.
"And the final occupant? Did you know him—or her?"

A subtle tingling prickled Oleron's nerves as he posed the gender query.
"Him," the vicar said. "A man. If memory serves, his name was Madley; an artist. A profound recluse, rarely venturing beyond the premises..." The priest paused, then burst into a candid confession. "Since you seek this truth, and since garbled rumors serve no purpose, let me speak plainly: Mr. Madley died there under peculiar circumstances. The post-mortem revealed an entirely empty stomach, yet he possessed money. His body was utterly wasted. Suicide was considered, though deliberate starvation is, to say the least, an unconventional method. An open verdict was issued."

"Ah!" Oleron responded. "Do you possess a comprehensive history of this parish?"
"No; only fragments. I maintain personal notes on the ecclesiastical records and registers, which I would gladly share, but the parish is vast, I have but one curate, and my leisure is limited..."

The conversation drifted to the parish's size and the vicar's busy schedule. Oleron thanked him, departed, and walked slowly home. His pace was deliberate; twice near the gate, he veered away, adding twenty minutes to his journey. He needed a state of absolute mental clarity, a receptivity akin to the morning he had sat down to write the sixteenth chapter of the first *Romilly*. Previously, the house's influence had struggled against the inertia of his previous works, requiring slow saturation. Now, however, he was light, unburdened, freed from both *Romilly* and its prototype. He was ready for the new entity: the unknown, coy, jealous, bewitching Beckoning Fair.

At 2:30 PM, he inserted his key into the Yale lock, entered, and shut the door.

His experiment succeeded instantly and miraculously. He could have screamed in triumph. Entering the room felt like escaping into a sanctuary. Just as in his early writing days, he felt a surge of ease, mastery, exhilaration, and release. The air seemed richer in oxygen; his own gravity appeared diminished, his footsteps lighter. The flowers in the bowls, the meadowsweet-hued panels and moldings, the polished floor, and the lofty, faintly starred ceiling seemed to laugh in welcome. Oleron laughed back, speaking aloud. "Oh, you're pretty, pretty!" he cooed.

He collapsed onto the couch, spending the afternoon like a convalescent awaiting a beloved guest—in a delightful vacancy, smiling dreamily, lifting drowsy, contented eyes to his enchanting surroundings. Darkness fell, bringing with it the nocturnal sounds of the old house. Yet, expecting a specific event proved futile. The same futility marked the following day, despite his efforts to maintain that sensitized, plate-like state of mind. Nothing registered. Whatever he sought seemed shy and demanding.

On the third day, comprehension dawned. A look of gentle mockery and cunning softened his features, and he chuckled. "Oho, oho! ... Well, if the wind blows from *that* quarter, we shall discover what else remains to be done. What is there, now? ... No, I shall not summon Elsie; we require no wheel to crush a butterfly. We shall not go to such extremes, my butterfly..."

Standing, thumbing his lean jaw, he suddenly crossed to the hall, retrieved his hat, and stepped outside. "My lady is coquettish, is she? Well, we shall test the efficacy of neglect," he chuckled, descending the stairs. He headed for a railway station, boarded a train, and spent the entire day in the countryside. Yes, Oleron believed he was the master of dealing with Fair Ones who beckoned, invited, and then retreated into shyness. He did not return until past eleven.

"Now," he murmured, walking the alleyway and feeling for his keys, "my Fair Beckoner!"

Inside his flat, he was composed, deliberate, and fiercely cautious. To signal immediate retirement, he lit a single candle. Pretending to yawn, he began his nightly rounds. First, the kitchen: a full moon cast a peacock-blue lozenge of light on the floor beside his candle. The window was bare, reflecting his movement and his own pale face. He closed the slightly ajar powder-closet door before removing his boots on the cushioned chair made from a folded harp bag. Next, the bathroom, where another sliver of blue moonlight sliced across the pipes. Finally, the seldom-used study, where he gazed at the silvered roofs across the square. Walking silently through the sitting-room, he entered the bedroom and placed the candle on the chest of drawers.

His face showed only fatigue, though he was more alert and cunning than ever. His small fireplace faced the chest of drawers holding the mirror; his bed and window occupied the other sides. Oleron lowered the blind, removed his coat, and stooped beneath the bed for his slippers. He felt certain, without cause, that the manifestation withheld for two days was imminent. Fear did not grip him; his sensory scale had flattened. Startling events were possible, but he was prepared.

His hand searched under the bed for the slippers. Suddenly, his heart leaped and paused in a nearly horrific rhythm. He had found the slippers, but he remained on his knees; without this support, he would have fallen. The bed was low; his head had turned sideways during the search. He maintained the pose until regaining composure. Rising, he discovered a drop of blood on his lower lip, bitten in his haste, and his watch had dislodged from his waistcoat pocket, dangling by its leather guard.

Before the watch ceased its oscillation, he was himself again. On the mantelpiece stood a portrait of his grandmother. He positioned himself before it, using the glass to view the steady candle flame burning behind him on the chest. Through the portrait glass, he saw the glancing lights from the beveled objects around the mirror. But he saw more. Amidst the static reflections, one gleam moved. Fainter than the others, it traveled up and down through the air. It was the reflection of the candle on Oleron's black vulcanite comb. Each downward sweep was accompanied by a silky, crackling rustle.

Oleron continued his performance. Feeling for the dangling watch, he wound it slowly. Pausing briefly, he emptied his trouser pockets, arranging pennies and halfpennies in a neat row on the mantel. The sweeping, minutely electric noise filled the room. Had he shifted his gaze, he could have aligned the dim, moving gleam of the comb to almost outline his grandmother's head. Any other head it might have traced was invisible.

Finishing with his pockets, Oleron feigned another yawn, driven not by resolve but by overwhelming curiosity, and swung abruptly around. The object being combed remained unseen, yet the comb did not stop. It had adjusted its angle, shifting slightly left. It swept regularly from a point over five feet high, vertically downward, to a spot just below the chest of drawers. Oleron acted admirably. He walked to the washstand, poured water, and began washing his hands. Removing his waistcoat, he continued his bedtime preparations. The combing persisted. He stood in thought, then his eyes twinkled again.

"Hm! ... I think I'll read for a quarter of an hour," he announced aloud.

He exited the room. After a couple of minutes, he returned. Silence had descended. Glancing at the chest of drawers, he saw the comb lying still between his removed collar and a pair of gloves. Without hesitation, he reached out and picked it up. It was an ordinary eighteenpenny comb from a chemist's card, possessing a definite specific gravity, no more capable of defying natural law than the planets keeping their orbits.
