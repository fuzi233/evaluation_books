The morning after that extraordinary Midsummer night unfolded like a promise fulfilled—a day so radiant and perfect it seemed nature itself had paused to celebrate. The sun rose with a smile, golden and unburdened, painting the sky in hues of molten amber. Roses bloomed as if scattered by an unseen hand, their petals drifting across the garden like whispered secrets. Dew-kissed hills glistened under the clear azure dome, and time moved forward with lightness, as though even the hours were dancing on tiptoe. It was, without question, one of the most beautiful days summer could offer. Yet I wonder—was I the only soul in the Rue Fossette who truly noticed?

For while others saw only the brilliance of the world outside, my thoughts turned inward, caught in the quiet aftermath of what had passed. A single absence now marked every corner of the house: a vacant seat at matins, an untouched coffee cup at breakfast, a bolster left neatly folded in a bed, still wearing its cap and nightgown. And when Ginevra Fanshawe’s music teacher arrived as usual, she found no pupil waiting—no sign, no word, no trace.

A frantic search began. Every room, every corridor, every hidden nook was scoured. But nothing remained but silence. Miss Fanshawe had vanished—gone as silently and completely as a star swallowed by night.

The staff were stunned; Madame Beck, especially, looked shattered. Her composure cracked, her face pale as marble. This was not just a loss—it was a blow to her pride, to her control, to the very order she had built around her life. How had it happened? No window was open, no glass broken, no door left ajar. Everything was locked, secure. The mystery deepened.

But I remembered. I recalled the soft closing of a certain heavy door—the one near the attic—drawn shut, yes, but never bolted. I remembered the thundering carriage that had passed in the night, the flicker of a handkerchief waved from a passing window. And then, the ghost in the garret—the nun who came to visit me in the dead of night.

Now, all the pieces fell into place.

I knew, beyond doubt, what had occurred.

When I shared my conviction with Madame Beck, she did not argue. She already knew of M. de Hamal’s pursuit of Ginevra—had discussed it long ago with Mrs. Cholmondeley, placing the burden of responsibility squarely upon her shoulders. Now, both women turned to M. de Bassompierre for answers.

By then, the Hôtel Crécy was already abuzz with news. Ginevra had written to her cousin Paulina, hinting at marriage. The de Hamal family had responded. M. de Bassompierre set out in pursuit—but too late.

A week later, a letter arrived for me. I read it slowly, carefully, for it held more than mere news—it carried the voice of a heart full of mischief, triumph, and love.

> I’m off! Gone like a shot. Alfred and I planned this almost from the start—we never meant to marry in the ordinary way. Alfred has too much spirit for that—and so do I. *Dieu merci!*
>
> You know, Alfred—who once called you ‘the dragon’—has seen so much of you lately that he’s actually grown fond of you. He hopes you won’t miss him now that he’s gone. He apologizes for any trouble he may have caused—especially that time he startled you in the grenier, catching you mid-letter. He couldn’t resist. But you once frightened *him* too—rushing in for a shawl just as he lit his cigar.
>
> Do you now understand? M. le Comte de Hamal was the nun in the attic. He came to see me. Here’s how: he enters the Athénée—where his nephews study—and from there, climbs our wall. Then, using the great tree over the grand berceau, he scales the buildings until he reaches the garret. One night, he fell, broke branches, nearly broke his neck, and barely escaped Madame Beck and M. Emanuel walking below.
>
> From the grande salle, the climb to the attic is easy. The skylight stays half-open for air—and that’s how he entered.
>
> I told him about our legend of the nun. That inspired his disguise. He wore the black gown and white veil—not just for fun, but to avoid being caught. He thinks you and M. Paul are excellent ghost-seers—brave men both. But I must say, I marvel less at your courage and more at your silence. How could you endure those visits—night after night—without screaming or alerting the whole house?
>
> And how did you like the nun as a bedfellow? *I* dressed her up—didn’t I do well? Did you shriek? I would’ve gone mad. But you—you’re made of iron and leather. You feel nothing. You’re a real old Diogenes.
>
> So, dear grandmother—aren’t you furious I ran away? I assure you, it was pure joy. I did it partly to spite Paulina and Dr. John—to show them that even I, with all my supposed wildness, could get married just as easily as they.
>
> M. de Bassompierre was furious at first—threatened legal action, said Alfred had kidnapped a minor. I had to play the melodrama—kneel, cry, drench three handkerchiefs. But he gave in. What could he do? I’m married. That’s that. He still insists it’s not legal because I’m not of age. As if that matters! I’m just as married as if I were a hundred. We’ll be married again properly—Mrs. Cholmondeley will oversee my trousseau—and there’s hope he’ll give me a decent portion.
>
> Only I wish he’d do it generously, without conditions. He wants Alfred to swear never to gamble again. They say he has a weakness for cards—but I don’t care. I know he’s my darling, my angel.
>
> Alfred’s genius in planning our escape was remarkable. He chose the fête night—when Madame Beck would be at the concert. I watched you leave the dormitory at eleven. How you returned alone, on foot—I can’t imagine. Was it you we met in the narrow Rue St. Jean? Did you see me wave my handkerchief from the carriage?
>
> Adieu! Rejoice in my happiness. Congratulate me. Believe me, dear cynic and misanthrope, yours, in the best of health and spirits,
>
>
> P.S.—Remember, I’m a countess now. Papa, mamma, and the girls at home will be thrilled. *‘My daughter the Countess!’* *‘My sister the Countess!’* Bravo! Sounds better than *Mrs. John Bretton*, doesn’t it?”

And so ended the tale of Ginevra Fanshawe’s flight.

The reader might expect some tragic reckoning, some bitter fall from grace. But life, as it often does, spared her the harshest consequences.

Later, near the end of her honeymoon, she visited Madame Beck and summoned me to the salon. She burst into my arms, laughing, radiant—her cheeks flushed, her curls fuller than ever. She wore a white bonnet, a Flanders veil, orange blossoms, and a dress that suited her perfectly.

“I’ve got my portion!” she exclaimed—always practical, always grounded in the tangible. “Uncle de Bassompierre is reconciled. He calls Alfred a ‘nincompoop’—but that’s just his Scotch bluntness. Paulina envies me. Dr. John is jealous—so jealous he might shoot himself! And I’m so happy—I think I have everything I want… except maybe a carriage and a hotel. Oh, and I must introduce you to *mon mari*!”

Alfred stepped forward from the inner salon, where he’d been speaking with Madame Beck. He offered me a polite apology for his ghostly visits, ending with a smile: “The best excuse for all my misdeeds stands right there,” he said, pointing to his bride.

She sent him back and turned to me, enveloping me in her exuberant energy. She showed me her ring proudly, calling herself “Madame la Comtesse” again and again, asking how it sounded. I said little. I gave her only the dry, reserved side of myself—just as she expected. She liked my sarcasm, my detachment. The more impassive I appeared, the louder she laughed.

Soon after, Alfred left the army—persuaded by family and friends to seek a quieter life. A diplomatic post was secured for him, and the couple departed abroad.

I thought she’d forget me. But she didn’t.

For years, letters came—irregular, impulsive, sometimes extravagant. At first, they spoke only of her and Alfred. Then, gradually, Alfred faded. In his place emerged a new figure: *Alfred Fanshawe de Bassompierre de Hamal*. A child—her son—whose every milestone became a spectacle. His first tooth, his first illness—each event was magnified into a crisis of epic proportions.

I received letters filled with agony, fear, and desperate pleas for sympathy. I wrote back with concern at first—then realized it was all theater. The child weathered each storm with heroic resilience. Five times he was said to be “in articulo mortis.” Five times he revived.

Over time, murmurs arose about debts, about financial strain. Alfred the First faced troubles—some of honor, some of pride. Ginevra, as before, never faced them alone. She called for help, demanded support, and somehow always found it.

She fought life not with strength, but with charm—with wit, with drama, with relentless appeal. And through it all, she suffered little.

Not because she was immune to pain—but because she never let it settle. She lived, laughed, loved, and fled—always fleeing toward the next adventure, the next escape, the next moment of joy.

And so, in the end, she remained—unbroken, unrepentant, and gloriously alive.
