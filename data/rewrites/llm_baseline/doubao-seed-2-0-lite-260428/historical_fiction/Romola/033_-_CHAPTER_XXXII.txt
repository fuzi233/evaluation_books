All Florence hummed with wild, bright excitement the next day: the French were leaving, at last. For Romola, wrapped in her heavy wool mantle on the high house loggia, there was a half-superstitious, dim hope tangled in her gladness—surely the strange new anxieties that had settled over her home with the foreign troops would vanish alongside them. The French had occupied the city barely eleven days, but in that short stretch Romola had known sharper unhappiness than in all her twenty-one years of quiet, book-filled life before. Tito had donned his hateful hidden armour the very day the invaders arrived, and though she could not articulate exactly why their departure should erase the fear that haunted him, the memory of the prisoner grasping his wrist, the image she had seen in Piero’s sketch, rose unbidden whenever she tried to puzzle it out. At the very least, when the French were gone, she would be rid of the heavy, oppressive foreign presence bound so tight to all her recent pain.

She lingered on the loggia long after the last plume of the French royal retinue had vanished over the bridges leading toward Porta San Piero, the gate that faced Siena and Rome. Even after the city gates slammed shut for the night, she stayed, letting the vibration of the great city-wide bell peal thrumb up through the stone paving under her feet, until dusk bled purple over the Arno. When at last she descended into her father’s library, she lit the tiny guttering oil lamp, resolved to shake off the day’s agitation and get back to work copying the catalogue of Bardo’s ancient manuscripts. Tito had left at dawn, and she had not expected him home for hours; she had planned to move to the little frescoed saloon, the walls painted with dancing nymphs and songbirds, as she did every evening since Tito had complained the library was too chill and gloomy.

To her utter surprise, she had barely set pen to paper when Tito stepped through the door. Her first lurch of thought was how forlorn he must feel in the vast shadowy room, the fire almost dead on the hearth, only the single small lamp burning far off at the end of the long oak table. She almost ran toward him.
“Tito, dearest, I did not know you would come so soon,” she said, voice soft and nervous, lifting her white arms to unwind the silk becchetto from his shoulders.
“Am I not welcome then?” he teased, with one of his old bright, easy smiles, pulling her close even as he playfully held his face back from hers.
“Tito!” she chided, in that soft, loving lilt she used for him, and he kissed her fondly, stroking her hair the way he had in the first months of their marriage. Romola quivered with delight. All the day’s tangled emotions had sharpened her sensitivity to that familiar, warm affection. It will come back, she told herself, the old happiness will return. He is like himself again.

Tito was working very hard to be like his old self; under his tunic, his heart was hammering with unspoken anxiety.
“If I had expected you so soon,” she said, as she helped him shed his outer wrappings, “I would have laid out a little feast to match the bells. I never meant to be here in the library when you came home.”
“Never mind, sweet,” he said carelessly. “Pay no mind to the fire. Come, sit here with me.”
The low carved stool tucked beside his chair was Romola’s usual seat for their evening talks, the same one she had sat on beside Bardo’s chair for years. She rested her arm on his knee, as she had once rested it on her father’s, and looked up at him, the unmentioned portrait of Bardo propped against the bookshelf at her back.
“I have never enjoyed the clang of the bells so much before, Tito,” she began. “I let them deafen me, shake me right down to my bones. I felt half like a Bacchante, possessed by some divine, wild joy. Are not the people so happy tonight?”
“Happy after that sour, pious Florentine fashion,” Tito said, shrugging. “But truth be told, no one left in Florence has much to rejoice over. There are three rival factions in the city all ready to tear each other’s throats out. If the Frate’s party grows strong enough to silence the other two, as looks likely, life here will be as cheerful as a funeral. They are already hatching plans for their Great Council, and soon the man who shouts sacred lauds the loudest will be the only one allowed to hold office. On top of that, the city is bled dry paying the ransom to the French king, and the war to take back Pisa will drain the last of our gold. Florence will be a paradise for fanatics who spend their evenings lashing themselves in crypts, but for anyone who loves ease and light? The exiles have it far better. For my own part, I have been thinking very seriously that we would be wise to quit Florence, my Romola.”

Romola started back, cold trembling seeping through her limbs. “Tito, how could we leave Florence? Surely you cannot think I could leave — not yet, not for a very long while.”
“That is just your secluded imagination weaving fantasies, my sweet,” he said, leaning to kiss her brow, his hand brushing her fair hair. “You lay false weight on a handful of tiny local ties. Before we married, I told you I always wished we were somewhere far from Florence. The people here are as sharp and biting as their spring winds. I like places where life moves softer, slower. It would do you good, too, to dip yourself in the soft waters of forgetfulness, and leave these narrow, petty duties behind.”
Romola felt his caress no more than if he had kissed a painted mask. She was too stunned by the gulf between their thoughts to even register his touch. “I do not cling to Florence because it is the pleasantest place in the world,” she said, quiet and tight. “I stay here because we must fulfill my father’s last wish. My godfather is seventy-one years old. We cannot dump that burden on him.”
“Those are the very superstitious mists clouding your mind, my Romola,” he said, his tone gentle but unyielding. “I have to look out for you, even against your own will. If those dear tender eyes of yours see falsely, I must see for them, and save you from wasting your life chasing impossible dreams.”

Romola sat silent, motionless. She could not pretend not to see where his words were leading: he meant to persuade her to let the library be locked away in some monastery, to cast aside the sacred promise made to her dead father. She was determined she would never submit her judgment of that duty to his. But the first shock of realizing that she and Tito were now openly divided, that the love she had trusted was built on nothing, crushed her into stillness. Tito mistook her silence for wavering, and went on in the same soft, remonstrating tone, arguing that preserving Bardo’s library as a single collection under his name was a pointless, petty goal, pointing to how the Medici’s great collection had been scattered, how it was better for all knowledge to spread across the whole civilized world than be hoarded for Florentine pride.

Romola’s silence broke, a cold, sharp contempt rising in her throat as she listened to the smooth, clever words that had no pulse for love, for faith, for the dead man whose whole life had been wrapped up in that library. “You talk of substantial good, Tito!” she said, her voice rising, a ring of scorn cutting through her words. “Are faithfulness, and love, and grateful memories no good? Is it no good that we should keep the silent promises people trust us to keep? Is it no good that a just life should be justly honoured? What good can clever talk do, if men harden their hearts against the people who loved them and depended on them? I would leave Florence, I would give up anything else, Tito — what else did I live for but my father and you? But I will not give up that duty. It was the yearning of his heart, and so it is mine.”

Tito’s face hardened. He could see he would never persuade her to agree. “I am sorry to hear you speak with such blind stubbornness,” he said, quiet. “It obliges me to give you pain. I foresaw your opposition, and since a quick decision had to be made, I avoided that obstacle, and acted without consulting you. A husband must sometimes act separately for his wife’s good, even when he has a wife as good as you.”
Romola looked at him, breathless, eyes wide with question.
“I mean,” he said, answering her unspoken look, “that I have arranged the transfer of all the books and all the antiquities where they will do the most good. The books have been bought by the Duke of Milan. The marbles, bronzes, and the rest are going to France. They will be protected by the stability of great powers, instead of rotting in a city that is always on the edge of ruin.”

Before he had finished speaking, Romola had sprung to her feet, standing over him, hands clenched tight, a flash of fierce, Bardo pride flaring in her eyes she had never felt before in her life. “You have sold them?” she whispered, as if she could not trust her own ears.
“I have,” Tito said, quailing a little under the scorching weight of her scorn.
“You are a treacherous man!” she said, her voice grating, sharp as shards of broken glass. She stood silent a minute, then turned away, agitated, saying, “It may still be stopped. I am going to my godfather.”

In an instant Tito leapt up, crossed the room, locked the library door, and slipped the key into his leather scarsella pouch at his belt. He was not angry — only very, very unwilling, to face this unpleasant scene, but he knew he had to subdue her before she could run off to Bernardo. He leaned against the marble pedestal under the bust of a grim old Roman statesman, feigning ease, but his olive cheeks had paled. No chain armour in the world could protect him from the moral dread thrumming in his chest: he had locked his wife’s rage and scorn in the room with him, and there was no escaping it.
“Try to calm yourself, Romola,” he said. “Messser Bernardo cannot reverse what I have done. There is no point in going to him. You would not want, if you were in your right mind, to air our private quarrels to a third party, to make your own husband look like a villain before an old man you care for.”

Romola did not sit down, her whole frame thrumming with impetuous, furious force. “Why can it not be reversed?” she demanded. “Nothing has even been moved yet.”
“The sale is signed and sealed with written agreement. The buyers, Count di San Severino and the Seneschal de Beaucaire, rode out with the King this morning on the road to Siena, and I hold the bonds for the purchase money.”
“If my father had suspected you would prove a faithless man,” Romola said, the bitter scorn spilling over before she could stop it, “he would have locked the library safely out of your reach. But death overtook him too soon, and you robbed him when you knew his ear was deaf, and his hands were stiff in the grave.” She paused, passion gathering in her throat, and burst out the question she had hidden away for weeks: “Have you robbed someone else, someone who is not dead? Is that the reason you wear that armour under your tunic?”

For a second Tito was cowed, terrified that the retribution he had feared was about to break. Then his resistance hardened. He told her she was mad with exaggerated grief, that any rational person would see he had done the wisest possible thing, that even Bernardo would agree if he thought about it clearly. When Romola protested that Bernardo would never agree, that they could chase the buyers down and offer to pay Tito back the full sum for the library, Tito coolly told her it was impossible: Bernardo would never sink three thousand florins more into the library, and he, Tito, would never allow the old man to do such a foolish thing anyway.
“Think carefully before you take any step to oppose me,” he warned her, voice calm and cold as a winter stream. “What will you gain by disgracing me before him? The library is sold, the event is irrevocable. And you are my wife.”

Romola stared at the ground for a long, quiet several minutes. When she spoke again, her voice was dead, cold, perfectly still. “I have one thing to ask.”
“Ask anything I can do without harming us both, Romola.”
“Give me the portion of the sale money that belongs to my godfather. Let me pay him what he is owed.”
“I must have your assurance first that you will not try to harm me, or speak of this to anyone.”
“Do you believe in assurances, Tito?” she said, a flicker of bitter smile crossing her face.
“From you, I do.”
“I will do you no harm. I will tell no one. I will say nothing to pain him or you. You are right. It is irrevocable.”
“Then I will do as you ask tomorrow.”
“Tonight, if possible,” she said. “So we never have to speak of this hateful thing again.”

He moved to the lamp, wrote out the draft for the sum, then came back and bent over her, pressing the paper into her hand. Now that he was sure she would not defy him, he leaned in to press a light kiss to her brow. She did not react at all, as if she had not felt his lips touch her skin. She listened as he turned the key in the lock, stepped out, and the heavy great door of the courtyard boomed shut behind him.
Then she stood up, as if a chain had snapped loose from around her chest. She stumbled over to Bardo’s propped portrait, fell on her knees on the cold stone floor before it, and burst into great, heaving sobs.
