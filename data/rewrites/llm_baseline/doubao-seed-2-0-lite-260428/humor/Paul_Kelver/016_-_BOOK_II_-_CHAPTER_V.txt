Over a chipped plate of sausages and stale bread that night, Dan and I spilled our respective life stories, and found we’d got more in common than we’d guessed. Dan had bailed out of school years before me, trotted off to Cambridge, and two years later his college career got cut short when his dad died – the old man had never properly recovered from a Mutiny wound picked up in India.
“You’d think I’d have been glad to ditch all the tedious lectures, right?” Dan said, grinning into his beer. “But it knocked me for six. Turns out I’m a born bookworm, and school had spent all those years making learning so mind-numbingly boring I’d never noticed. They herded us through the curriculum like a Cook’s tourist party being rushed through the National Gallery – the goal wasn’t to make us understand a single thing, just to make sure we could lie and say we’d ‘done’ the whole syllabus. At Cambridge I picked my own subjects, studied them my way. I fed on knowledge, not got force-fed it like a Strassburg goose being fattened for pâté.”
Back in London he’d landed a bank job, courtesy of his dad’s old mate who was chairman there. It paid just enough to keep him in tea and library fines, and left him spare time to study – but the endless, mind-numbing shuffling of slips of paper in a city counting house nearly drove him round the bend. The second his mother died, he shook the city dust out of his hair and joined a ramshackle travelling theatre troupe. He stuck it out 18 months, played every part going from Romeo to bumbling old Paul Pry, helped paint the scenery, and once spent three hours chasing a stray poster that kept flying back and sticking to his head. He swore billposting is the single most difficult art form ever invented by mankind, second only to making a decent cup of tea on a camp stove.
When he got bored of the stage he joined a circus, went by “Signor Ricardo, the fearless bareback equestrian” (he once fell off mid-performance and landed in the band’s tuba), and was one half of the “Daring Brothers Roscius” trapeze act (neither of the other two lads were named Roscius). Then he got a sudden hankering for respectability, waited tables in Ostend for three months, then took a job as a footman in a country house.
“Never know when these odd jobs’ll come in handy,” Dan mused. “If I ever get round to writing that high society novel everyone’s snapping up these days, I won’t be making up all the butler gossip from guesswork like every other Fleet Street hack. I’ll have the edge.”
He’d tried half a dozen other lines of work too: tramped around Ireland with a fiddle playing jigs for farmers’ pennies, toured Scotland giving a talk about Palestine with a set of half-broken magic lantern slides, worked as a billiards marker, had a short, chaotic stint as a schoolmaster, and for the last three months he’d been drama and music critic for a Sunday paper – the exact job I’d daydreamed about a hundred times.
“How on earth did you land that?” I asked, awestruck.
“Pure dumb luck,” Dan said. “I was sauntering down the Strand one afternoon skint, three shillings in my pocket, wondering if I’d have to go back to circus work. I walked into the first newspaper office I saw, marched up the first flight of stairs, opened the first unlocked door, and wandered straight into the editorial room. It was getting dark, and a tiny, baby-faced 26 year old bloke was sprinting around the place swearing his head off. He’d spilled the ink, lost all the matches, and snapped the bell pull clean off. I thought he was the office boy, and teased him a bit about it – turns out he was the editor. The only reason he had the job was his aunt owned the paper. I lit the gas for him, found him a new ink pot, and he immediately started complaining that every random hobo with a pamphlet on safety valves kept wandering straight up the stairs to pester him.
“Just then someone knocked on the door. The editor screamed that he wouldn’t see anyone, send every last pest away. So I went out to the ante-room, and for the next half hour I sent 12 very confused people packing. The last bloke turned up smoking a cigarette, looked at me like I was daft when I asked his name, and said ‘Idiot’. I almost wrote that down on the visitor’s slip before he started laughing and said he was the sub-editor, and it was press night, and he needed to know who was going to review the show at the Royalty that evening.
“Lightbulb moment! If the sub doesn’t know who’s supposed to go to the theatre, that means the drama critic slot is empty. I told him I was the new guy, grabbed the theatre ticket off the editor’s desk without asking, and went off to the show. No one’s ever figured out I appointed myself. The sub thinks I’m a distant nephew of the proprietress, the aunt thinks I’m the sub’s brilliant discovery, and everyone treats me like I’m made of gold. The salary’s tiny, but it’s enough. Rolling stone that I am, I’ve never seen the point of getting smothered in moss anyway. Much more fun staying loose enough to wander around and see stuff.”
Then he pitched his idea. Our current digs were garbage, and there were four perfect rooms in Queen’s Square going cheap. The last tenant had been a Polish revolutionary who’d stupidly decided to sneak back to Russia three months prior, and now he was getting free year-round accommodation in a Siberian camp. The landlord was Deleglise, a weird old engraver who lived in the rest of the house, who hated all elderly female domestic servants with a fiery passion. He and his 70 year old French housekeeper bickered 12 hours a day over who left the burnt toast on the counter, and he wouldn’t even let a charwoman step over his threshold. No landlady breathing down our necks, no one else’s piano thumping through the walls, the whole second floor to ourselves – for 30 shillings a week, half what it should have cost.
“I’m a decent cook,” Dan said. “Will you be our joint house-parlour-maid?”
I didn’t need any convincing. A week later we’d moved in, and we stayed there nearly two years. I know all the ladies reading will gasp and say two men left to their own devices can’t even put themselves to bed without making a mess, but we survived. No maids, no supervision, zero domestic female interference, and we never once starved. Dan could knock up a perfect omelette, a devilled kidney, a cup of coffee so good it’d make a bishop weep. The only problem was, every time we had guests over, he’d get overambitious and try fancy cooking he’d never done before.
His lemon sponge – which the cookbook promised would be a light, snow-white cloud of sweetness – came out looking like a miniature collapsed sand dune. It tore with a sad wet cardboard noise when you poked it with a fork. His famous manna cake was so dense and stodgy, one slice would keep you full for 12 hours. If the Israelites had been forced to eat that every day, I completely get why they were crying for Egyptian leeks. Moses must have had a cast iron stomach. His trifles weren’t even remotely light as air, his soufflés all our guests kept mistaking for plain steamed pudding, and his early attempts at pancake tossing were a public danger. Once he flung a pancake so high it stuck to the ceiling for three days before we noticed.
Dan was the most even-tempered man alive 23 hours a day. That one remaining hour? He turned into a raving lunatic. The oven was his sworn mortal enemy. Every time the bun came out hard as red sandstone, every time the custard got pimply, every time the mayonnaise tasted like hair oil, it was 100% the oven’s fault, and he’d yell at it so loud the neighbours banged on the ceiling. If I so much as offered a helpful tip, he’d hurl cold coffee grounds or a spoiled tomato at my head until I ran out of the kitchen.
For my part, I was a model housekeeper. I’ve always held that dust is a lovely delicate homey bloom, not something to be stirred up and spread all over the house. Leave it alone, it sits quiet and harmless on the bookshelf, no trouble at all. All the crumbs, cigarette butts, and loose bits of string go under the rug – perfect compromise, no one has to sweep them. Why wash a clean plate the cat has already licked spotless? Why make a bed that’s just going to get slept in again the same night? Women keep missing this obvious hack, and spend 10 times more work than they ever need to.
Needless to say I was terrible at needlework. Dan could fumble with a thimble, but I always had to hunt for ten minutes for the right finger to stick it on, and then it would slip and I’d prick myself half to death, so eventually I just started pushing needles through fabric using the edge of the door.
As Dan predicted, our comic opera ran all through winter, then got another run the next year, and our mate “Goggles” the comic even got a lead role. My salary went up to 35 shillings, sometimes 40 a week – I felt like a millionaire. I daydreamed I’d be a world-famous opera star one day, on 600 pounds a week, easily. Then old O’Kelly the singing teacher burst that bubble for me.
“Ye’re wasting your time, lad,” he said, patting my shoulder. “Ye’ve got a voice that’s thin enough to strain pasta through. Great for comic opera, sure, but grand opera? Not a chance.”
No bother, I thought. I could be a famous actor instead, the next Kean. Or write epoch-making novels in the mornings, run for parliament when I got bored of art, end up Prime Minister. Or maybe join the diplomatic service, retire as an ambassador. I was learning French and German at the museum, I could do anything.
And then, the best thing that ever happened to 22 year old me: my first short story got accepted for publication. I’d had a story fall through before, when the paper folded before it could print it, so this time I didn’t tell anyone, just snuck out every morning for weeks on publication day to hunt for it. Until one grey January day, I walked past a tiny newsagent on the Harrow Road, and saw the poster stuck to his window: *THE WITCH OF MOEL SARBOD: A LEGEND OF MONA, BY PAUL KELVER*. My actual name, in actual print.
My legs shook so bad I could barely walk into the shop. The grumpy bloke behind the counter fished a copy out from under a pile of other papers on the floor. I sat down on a bench in Paddington Green and read the whole 12 pages there, in the cold. The story was ridiculous, full of unpronounceable Welsh names I’d dug out of dusty lexicons at the British Museum, about a princess named Purity who kisses an evil witch to save her enchanted king, and ends up trapped as a witch on the mountain forever. My friends later told me it was garbage, that the prose was a weird cross between Pilgrim’s Progress and Old Moore’s Almanac, that the scenery was copied straight out of a local tourist guide.
I didn’t care. That tattered, badly printed story was my first baby. I held it in my hands and felt like the whole world was wide open. I’d be famous, people in countries I’d never visit would read my work, generations yet unborn would remember my name. That morning, standing in the soft grey London fog, I was 100% sure the future had just turned up, and it was all mine.
