Elinor observed the low spirits of her friend with a profound, resonant uneasiness. His visit afforded her but a partial satisfaction, for his own enjoyment of the agrarian quietude seemed fractured. It was evident that he was unhappy; she wished it were equally evident that he still distinguished her by the same affection she had once felt so certain of inspiring. Yet his preference fluctuated, his reserved manner contradicting in one moment the animated look of the preceding one. 

The dissonance followed him into the breakfast-room the next morning. The kitchen was alive with the deep, percussive *thump* of dough being kneaded and the rhythmic *hiss* of the hearth, a comforting acoustic ecology that usually settled the mind. The air was already heavy with the gustatory richness of toasted stone-ground barley and the sharp, bright olfactory note of bruised mint gathered from the damp garden. Marianne, ever attuned to the unspoken rhythms of courtship, soon left them to the quiet clatter of porcelain, retreating upstairs to grant them a space of intimacy. But before she was halfway to the landing, the parlor door opened. She turned, astonished to see Edward himself emerging. 

“I am going into the village to see my horses,” he announced, his voice tight, retreating from the sensory warmth of the room as though the silence between him and Elinor was an untranslatable dialect he could no longer parse. “I shall be back again presently.”

When Edward returned, the table was fully laid with a meticulous spread: butter churned that very dawn, weeping delicate beads of whey, and a compote of wild tart damsons cooked down with crystallized sugar—a generational recipe passed through the local matriarchs. He took his seat, breathing in the steam of the black Keemun tea, its smoky, malted fragrance seeming to soothe his frayed nerves. He spoke of his walk with fresh admiration. The village, situated high above the valley, had offered him a sweeping, circular view of the ancient landscape, a domestic mythos etched into the rolling earth.

Marianne leaned forward, eager to map his subjective experience. She began to describe her own fierce admiration for these scenes, questioning him minutely on the objects that had struck his senses.

Edward interrupted her, tearing a piece of warm, crusty bread. “You must not enquire too far, Marianne. Remember, I lack the dominant lexicon of the picturesque. I shall offend you by my ignorance if we come to particulars. I shall call hills steep, which ought to be termed ‘bold’; surfaces strange and uncouth, which ought to be ‘irregular and rugged’; and distant objects out of sight, which ought only to be ‘indistinct through the soft medium of a hazy atmosphere.’ You must be satisfied with the honest, grounded admiration I can give. I call it a very fine country—the woods seem full of fine timber, and the valley looks comfortable and snug, smelling of curing woodsmoke, with rich meadows and neat farmhouses. It answers my idea of a fine country because it unites profound beauty with true sustenance. I know nothing of the picturesque.”

“I am afraid it is but too true,” said Marianne, her eyes flashing; “but why should you boast of it?”

“I suspect,” said Elinor, tracing the rim of her teacup, “that to avoid one kind of affectation, Edward here falls into another. Because he sees many people pretend to an admiration of nature they do not possess, regurgitating a commercialized jargon, he affects a greater indifference in his own sensory viewing. He is fastidious and will have an affectation of his own.”

“It is very true,” said Marianne, spreading the dark, jammy damson compote over a thick slice of crumb. “The admiration of landscape has become a mere tyranny of jargon. Everyone pretends to feel and tries to describe it using the dominant vocabulary of those who first defined picturesque beauty. This imported lexicon erodes our cultural memory and our native dialects of joy. I detest jargon of every kind. Sometimes I have kept my most visceral feelings to myself, because the language required to describe them is so worn and hackneyed it strips the subjective experience of all meaning.”

“I am convinced,” said Edward, savoring the rich, tart sweetness of the fruit and the complex malt of the tea, “that you really feel all the delight in a fine prospect which you profess to feel. But, in return, your sister must allow me to feel no more than I profess. I like a fine prospect, but not on picturesque principles. I do not like crooked, twisted, blasted trees. I admire them much more if they are tall, straight, and flourishing. I am not fond of ruined cottages or nettles. I find profound pleasure in a snug farmhouse—where hearths are warm and sustenance is respected—and a troop of tidy, happy villagers please me better than the finest banditti in the world.”

Marianne looked with amazement at Edward, and with compassion at her sister. Elinor only laughed. 

The subject was continued no farther, and Marianne remained thoughtfully silent, chewing on the textures of the conversation until a new object suddenly engaged her attention. She was sitting by Edward, and in taking his tea from Mrs. Dashwood, his hand passed directly before her through a shaft of morning light. A ring, featuring a tight plait of hair in the center, caught the glare, conspicuous on his finger.

“I never saw you wear a ring before, Edward,” she cried, the sudden volume of her voice shattering the cottage's acoustic calm. “Is that Fanny’s hair? I remember her promising to give you some. But I should have thought her hair had been darker.”

Marianne spoke inconsiderately what she sensed without filter. But when she saw how much she had pained Edward, her vexation at her want of thought could not be surpassed by his own. He colored very deeply, a flush of profound embarrassment, and giving a momentary, panicked glance at Elinor, replied, “Yes; it is my sister’s hair. The setting always casts a different shade on it, you know.”

Elinor met his eye, looking conscious likewise. The truth resonated within her like a perfectly struck tuning fork. The hair was hers. The shade, the texture—she recognized it instantaneously as a physical remnant of her own being. Yet, where Marianne assumed it a freely given gift from Fanny, Elinor knew it must have been procured by some unrecorded theft or contrivance, a secret history woven into the gold. She was not in a humor to regard it as an affront. Affecting to take no notice, she instantly shifted the conversation to the careful sourcing of the tea leaves, internally resolving to catch every future opportunity to eye the hair and confirm her own physical artifact. 

Edward’s embarrassment settled into a grave absence of mind that lasted the whole morning. Marianne severely censured herself for the acoustic and emotional disruption she had caused, though she might have forgiven herself sooner had she known how little offense she had given her sister.

Before the middle of the day, the delicate sonic ecology of the cottage was obliterated by the arrival of Sir John and Mrs. Jennings. Their voices were a barrage of booming joviality and piercing invasive noise, a jarring contrast to the valley's agrarian quietude. With the assistance of his mother-in-law, Sir John was not long in declaring that the name of Ferrars began with an F. This prepared a future mine of loud raillery against the devoted Elinor, staved off only by the newness of their acquaintance with the guest. From some very significant looks, Elinor learned exactly how far their penetration, founded on Margaret’s instructions, had extended.

Sir John, feeling bound to contribute to their visitor's amusement, insisted on engaging them for both the evening and the next day. “You *must* drink tea with us tonight,” his voice bounced harshly off the low plaster ceilings, “for we shall be quite alone—and tomorrow you must absolutely dine with us, for we shall have a large party.”

Mrs. Jennings enforced the necessity. “And who knows but you may raise a dance,” she said. “And that will tempt *you*, Miss Marianne.”

“A dance!” cried Marianne. “Impossible! Who is to dance?”

“Who! why yourselves, and the Careys, and Whitakers to be sure.—What! you thought nobody could dance because a certain person that shall be nameless is gone!”

“I wish with all my soul,” cried Sir John, “that Willoughby were among us again.”

This, and Marianne’s sudden, visceral blush, gave new suspicions to Edward. “And who is Willoughby?” he asked in a low voice to Elinor, seeking refuge from the visitors' auditory assault in a localized murmur. She gave him a brief reply. Marianne’s communicative countenance offered the full translation.

Edward saw enough to comprehend not only the hints of the others, but the subtle expressions of Marianne’s that had puzzled him before. When their visitors finally departed, taking their auditory chaos with them, he went immediately to her side and whispered into her acoustic sphere, “I have been guessing. Shall I tell you my guess?”

“What do you mean?”

“Shall I tell you?”

“Certainly.”

“Well then; I guess that Mr. Willoughby hunts.”

Marianne was surprised and confused, yet she could not help smiling at the quiet archness of his manner. After a moment’s silence, she said softly, “Oh, Edward! How can you?—But the time will come I hope... I am sure you will like him.”

“I do not doubt it,” he replied, rather astonished at the warmth of her earnestness. Had he not imagined the unwritten lore of Mr. Willoughby to be a mere superficial joke founded on nothing, he would not have ventured to translate it aloud.
