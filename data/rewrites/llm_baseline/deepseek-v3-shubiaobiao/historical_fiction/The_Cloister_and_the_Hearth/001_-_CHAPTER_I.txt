Every day, unnoticed by the world, men and women perform great deeds, speak words of wisdom, and endure sorrows with quiet nobility. Most of these hidden heroes will remain unknown until that final hour when the great are humbled and the humble exalted. Yet even those recorded in dusty annals sleep forgotten, their lives reduced to cold, lifeless lines that stir no hearts. For history, without flesh and breath, is but a skeleton—striking the reader’s mind only to glance away, unremembered.

Thus, the truths of the past remain sealed to common folk, for chroniclers leave too much to imagination—and imagination is a rare gift. Here, then, the storyteller may serve as a bridge, breathing life into the long-dead.

In a musty chronicle, written in stiff and grating Latin, lies a brief, harsh account of two souls who lived and died unsung four centuries ago. Their names, etched without pity into that stern page, lie as forgotten as fossils in stone. Unjust in life, Fate remains unjust in death. Yet if I can but draw back the veil of those dry words, perhaps you will grant them, for a day, a place in your heart.

It was the middle of the fifteenth century—a time when Louis XI ruled France with cunning, Edward IV sat uneasy on England’s contested throne, and Philip the Good, having crushed his cousin Jacqueline and broken her spirit, reigned unchallenged in Holland. Here, our tale begins.

In the small town of Tergou lived Elias, a cloth and leather merchant, and his wife Catherine. They were prosperous, their trade thriving, yet earthly cares weighed upon them—not for want of coin, but for the burden and blessing of nine children.

When the little ones came, one after another, each was welcomed with joy, each a gift from Heaven. But as the years passed and the family grew, so too did the parents’ fears. In Holland, where prudence was second nature, reckless fathers were as rare as disobedient sons. And so, as the table stretched with each new mouth, Elias and Catherine often exchanged uneasy glances, whispering, *Who will feed them when we are gone?*

The younger children, trusting in the eternal return of supper as surely as the sunrise, could scarce contain their laughter at such worries. But the older ones, marked by Dutch solemnity, took the words to heart—some with resolve, others with darker thoughts.

One day, Richart, the second eldest, stood before his mother, his face calm but his voice firm. *"Send me to Amsterdam,"* he said. *"Let me learn the merchant’s trade. I am quick with numbers, and I would not be a burden."*

Catherine’s hands flew to her cheeks. *"Leave Tergou?"*

*"What are streets and stones to me?"* he replied. *"If I can leave the people, I can leave the place."*

*"Mother,"* he said gravely, *"if I can leave you, I can leave anyone."*

She wept, but Richart’s will was iron. And so, with a heart of granite and a face of sorrow, he departed, costing them nothing more than the last of their small hoard.

Two years passed. Richart found a place for his brother Jacob, and Jacob too left, slipping away after dinner one day. That evening, Elias whispered, *"Sit wider,"* and Catherine, who had vowed not to weep, broke her promise. *"The best always leave us,"* she sobbed.

*"Nay,"* said Elias, *"all are dear alike. But men are ungrateful by nature—and women foolish."*

*"Yet Richart and Jacob were the flower of us all,"* she insisted.

Again the little coffer was drained, and again they saved like ants, denying themselves to fill it anew. In time, two more children were settled—a son as a tailor, a daughter as a seamstress—but the cost left them nearly bare.

Now only the unwilling and the unable remained: Giles, the dwarf, half-witted and sharp-clawed, clung to by his mother; Little Catherine, pale and sweet, who moved on crutches and never complained; Sybrandt, the idle youngest; and Cornelis, the eldest, who waited like a vulture for his inheritance.

*"What will become of them when we are gone?"* the couple often sighed—until, at last, their prayers seemed answered. *"Thanks be to St. Bavon,"* they said, *"there is Gerard."*

Gerard was different. A scholar, a dreamer, destined for the Church. His hands were made for pen and parchment, not for trade. He spent his days bent over books, his nights tracing letters by candlelight, even when the burgomaster himself slept. The monks admired his skill, the old painter Margaret Van Eyck became his friend, and though his family barely understood his passion, they indulged it—for the Church would provide.

When Duke Philip offered prizes for the finest craftsmanship in the land, Gerard’s heart leaped. He would compete—illuminate a manuscript, prove his worth. His mother, after much fretting, gave him the coins for vellum and colors. His sister Kate prayed to the Virgin for his success.

And so, with hope in his heart and his tools in his pack, Gerard set out for Rotterdam—unaware that Fate, which had been so cruel to his obscure kindred, had not yet finished with them.
